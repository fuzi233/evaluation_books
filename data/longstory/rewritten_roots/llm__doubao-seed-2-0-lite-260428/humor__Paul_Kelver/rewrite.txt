Then she’d back into every room they were in, slam the door as hard as she possibly could to make sure they heard her coming, and sing at the top of her lungs down every dark corridor. No woman alive could have done more to give them fair warning, and for all that? They hated her guts. The harder she tried to be unobtrusive, the more annoyed they got. One day Mum found her dropping teaspoons at 5-second intervals outside the drawing room to signal her arrival, and snapped, “Fanny, stop being ridiculous, do you hate seeing Luke and me happy?” Aunt Fanny blinked all innocent and said, “Hate it? Nonsense! You two turtledoves are barely 70 between you, you can bill and coo all you like, you pretty dears.”

Dad once lost his entire temper with her when we were walking along the canal bank. He ranted so hard it felt like he thought *I* was personally responsible for Aunt Fanny existing, which was a real unfair burden to lay on a 10-year-old. He shook his fist at the sunset and yelled, “I swear to God I wish she had a nice little freehold cottage all to herself in the country and a fat private income!” Then immediately looked sheepish, mumbled, “Not that she’s not nice sometimes, mind,” and made me promise not to tell Mum what he’d said.

The other observer of this chaotic domestic comedy was Susan, our maid-of-all-work, the first in a long line of colourful characters who worked for us until Amy showed up (bless her heart). Susan was a stout, elderly woman who fell asleep at random intervals, which she blamed on “past troubles,” and was forever bragging that her heart was always in the right place no matter what her hands were doing. Any time she saw Mum and Dad sitting within three feet of each other, she’d flop down on the nearest chair and start weeping, because it reminded her of her own glorious old married days. No one could ever make head nor tail of Susan’s ex-husband, though. One Monday she’s crying into the brussels sprouts because she hasn’t had time to go water his grave at Manor Park Cemetery. Next Tuesday she’s ranting that if she ever gets her hands on the little rat’s scruff, she’ll throttle him. When I went, “Wait, I thought he was dead?” Susan shrugged and said, “So did I! That’s how artful he is.” Then next minute she’s threatening to gouge any man’s eye out who dares say he’s not a good man, and bragging that every time he shows up he brings her a bottle of gin for her headaches. I’d beat a hasty retreat back to my German grammar at that point, because Susan’s timeline gave me a headache worse than any gin could cure.

Aunt Fanny finally got fed up with her, when Susan was still fast asleep at 11am with what she called “new-ralgy” (neuralgia, obviously). “That lazy hussy,” Aunt Fanny snarled, “I’d boot her and her box out onto the doorstep and leave her so-called good heart there next to her if I had my way.” Which is exactly what happened a Saturday night. Mum came running upstairs white as a sheet, yelling for Dad to go get the doctor, because Susan was flat out on the kitchen floor gurgling and wouldn’t speak. Dad ran off to grab Dr Washburn, this giant bloke with a thick black beard who looked like he could pick up the entire house with one hand. He bounded down the kitchen stairs two at a time, shook the whole place, leaned over unconscious Susan, and then burst out laughing so loud the windows rattled. Grabbed a half-full bucket of water and dumped the whole lot over her. Susan woke up spluttering, and he went “Feeling better? Want another splash?” Then he herded the rest of the Kelvers out of the kitchen and slammed the door. We stood on the landing listening to Susan screaming blue murder at him, and him whooping and yelling “Go on! Give it to her! Bark louder, I love it!” Mum huffed, “What a horrid brute,” Dad said, “Wait till you get to know him, he’s great,” and Mum replied, “I never intend to get to know him at all.”

Ten minutes later he came upstairs, pushed Mum’s open purse away, and said the only payment he wanted was a glass of beer and a chop. He told her he was coming back in an hour to cook it himself, because he saw they didn’t have a servant anymore, and Susan had promised him she’d be gone in half an hour. Sure enough, Susan was out the door before he came back, running so fast you’d think the hounds were after her. No one in the house could sleep while Fighting Hal Washburn was around, not even me, who snuck down wrapped in a blanket pretending I’d woken up with a sudden fit of missing my mum and dad. The doctor diagnosed my very convincing made-up tummy ache (it felt like I’d swallowed the dome of St. Paul’s, obviously) with a very specific prescription: a tablespoon of golden syrup four times a day, and a whole ounce of sherbet straight on the tongue ten minutes before every meal. Best doctor who ever lived.

That night was magic. Everyone was having fun, even Aunt Fanny was cracking jokes. Hal had that magic effect on everyone—you left his company feeling like the smartest, funniest person alive. I sat on the fender staring at my mum, and the stories Hal told made me see a whole big bright world out there, full of adventures, where no one cared if your omelette was a bit leathery. I still don’t know if meeting Hal was the best or worst thing that ever happened to me, any more than a tree can tell you if the wild winds that bent it made it stronger or misshapen.

After Susan left, we spent months pretending we still had a maid. Dad would hear a client ring the bell, wait a polite minute, then open the door himself and yell down the stairs at the top of his voice asking where Jane had got to, why wasn’t she answering the door. Mum would wrap a thick veil around her face and walk miles to buy bread and milk so the neighbours wouldn’t see she didn’t have staff. They were embarrassed by their poverty, like it was a crime, but it wasn’t snobbery, it was just silly old misjudged self-respect. They’d share one egg for breakfast, both fighting to give the other the nourishing white, while the fancy sherry and biscuits were always out on the sideboard for visitors. I thought the whole game was brilliant. I’d keep watch at the gate while Dad scrubbed the doorstep at 10pm, wrapped in a bit of sacking so he looked like a very tall charwoman. We’d mess around in the tiny garden playing cricket with weird rules where you weren’t allowed to hit the ball left or right because you’d either break a neighbour’s window or lose it to a pack of street urchins who’d run off with it like it was manna from heaven. The only time things got tense once was when Mum said she was making an omelette for dinner, and Dad came back with the wood chopper pretending he needed to saw it in half, because the last omelette she made was so tough you could have used it as a boot sole. Mum started crying, and Dad panicked so bad apologising you’d think he’d burned the house down.

Me and Dad were best mates, you see. He was 40 going on 12, same as me. We’d get up at 6am and hike across the marshes to bathe in the muddy creek, then spend half an hour scrubbing mud off our legs when we got home. Sometimes we’d take the train up to Hackney and row a boat up the Lea all the way to Ponder’s End. Life was perfect, and we didn’t care one bit how thin the larder was, because all of us still had that big dumb bright hope that tomorrow was going to be better.

“LOUISA!” roared my father down the kitchen stairs loud enough to rouse the napping cat curled on the coal scuttle, “are you all asleep mid-afternoon? I just had to answer the front door myself, like some common man who doesn’t keep a whole household to yell for!” He stomped back to his office and slammed the door so hard the inkwell on his desk hopped an inch. Dad could deliver an award-winning performance of towering, righteous rage, the only catch being he never performed it for anyone but himself.

A quarter of an hour later his office bell jangled, sharp and bossy enough to make the half-peeled potato slip right out of my mom’s gloved hands. She was wrestling root vegetables in fancy wash-leather gloves that had never been designed for kitchen duty, and locked eyes with Aunt Fan, who was flinging peas out of their pods like she held a personal grudge against them. The bell went off again, even louder, loud enough that the canary in its cage did a full startled loop.

“Once for Louisa, twice for James, that’s the secret code, right?” Aunt Fan enquired around a mouthful of pea pod.

“You go, Paul—” Mom started, then her face flooded bright pink like she’d suddenly remembered 12-year-olds have no business relaying household excuses to a Very Important Visitor. Before I could even set down my math slate, she’d ripped off the gloves and pushed past me. “No, don’t stop your lessons! I’ll pop up myself!” she yelped, and vanished out the door like a hare that smelled a terrier.

A few minutes later the kitchen door creaked open a crack, and Mom’s hand snaked through the gap, waving at me like she was signalling a submarine to surface mid-raid.

“Walk on tiptoes!” she whispered, demonstrating so hard she nearly tripped over the doormat. We crept up the stairs, which of course took this as a personal invitation to snitch on us: every single step creaked so loud it sounded like a brass band playing a sneeze, no matter how carefully we placed our feet. We made it all the way to my parents’ bedroom, where the old oak wardrobe, relic of the days when we’d actually had money, hid my “better” weekday suit (we called it my best, to spare its feelings).

Mom cut off every single question I tried to ask that wasn’t “where’s my second sock?” and had me dressed in 90 seconds flat, right down to the brand new Blucher shoes that pinched my toes so bad I could barely feel them. She grabbed my hand, and we crept back down the stairs like a pair of burglars who’d forgotten the jam they came to steal. She herded me straight to the front door, pulled it open, and I went “Wait—don’t I need my cap?” but she just shook her head, slammed the door so it clicked nice and loud, and suddenly I got the bit: this whole silly pantomime was to make sure the Important Visitor thought we were far posher than we actually were. We tiptoed into dad’s office, and the big reveal was standing right there.

To be honest, as a kid I’d never thought dad’s “lawyer office” was real work. Our fancy drawing room was always kept polished for random drop-ins: Cowper’s poems lay face down on the wobbly loo table, a half-finished crochet throw was draped over the armchair like we did nothing all day but do elegant needlework, and every evening we’d pile all his legal paperwork under the sofa and turn the office into our playroom. Sometimes dad would play cricket in the back garden with me, or ninepins in the hallway when it rained, and the second he heard a knock at the front door he’d go sprinting back to his desk and pretend he’d been poring over important documents for hours. The fancy labelled tin boxes? “Sutton Hampden, Esq.” was mom’s sewing kit. “Drayton Estates” was where dad stashed his eating apples. “Mortgages” I later found out had nothing at all to do with the half-finished poetry scribbles I kept hidden under my mattress.

When we walked in, dad stood up so fast his hair got all rumpled (he had a habit of scrubbing at it when he was nervous) and he was making such a valiant effort to look stern and businesslike he just looked like a 12-year-old boy wearing his dad’s suit for a school play.

“This is the youngster,” dad said, yanking me over to the visitor. “Tall for his age, isn’t he?”

The guy was huge, fat and jolly, with a cigar that smelled like wet tar rolling from one corner of his thick lips to the other. He held out a big, fat, slightly grubby hand that wrapped around mine like a walrus’s flipper (I’d just seen a picture of a walrus in my natural history book, so the comparison was instant). He bopped me on the head gently with his other hand.

“Looks just like ‘is mum, blimey, don’t he?” he winked one of his tiny, bright, piercing eyes at dad. “That’s the highest compliment I can pay the little nipper, ain’t it?” His eyes were so sharp I was convinced he could see the lollipop I’d hidden in my pocket earlier, so I tried to think really hard about how nice and rich he was to impress him.

“Where you sending him to school? Eton? ‘Arrow?” he asked.

“We haven’t quite decided yet,” dad lied smoothly. “Educating him at home for now.”

“Take a tip from me, kid,” he said, pointing a chubby finger at me. “Learn all the book stuff you can. If I’d had the chance to be a proper scholar, I wouldn’t be here paying your dad a fortune to sort all my legal nonsense out. I’d do it all myself.”

“You seem to be doing very well without it,” dad laughed. And he did: his fat fingers were dripping with sparkly rings, and a massive gold cable chain was bobbing up and down on his huge waistcoat like a tugboat bobbing on the Thames.

“Could’ve done better,” he grunted. “I’d be able to talk about something other than property prices, and I wouldn’t drop all my aitches. My little girl, she’s proper posh, never drops an h in her life. Doesn’t take after me at all, freak of nature, that one.”

“You have a daughter?” Mom perked right up, she’d have bonded with a burglar the second he mentioned he had a kid. “You must bring her round to visit!”

“Great, that’s settled,” he said, shoving a shiny half-crown into my hand before my parents could protest. “Don’t waste it on missionaries or anything daft, spend it on a proper Christian baker or toy shop, yeah?” And out he went.

That half-crown was the first actual pocket money I’d ever had, and it caused absolute chaos. I tossed and turned in bed for three whole nights, tramped every street in the neighbourhood staring in shop windows, and every time I asked someone what I should spend it on they went “No, you have to learn to use your own commercial judgement!” like buying a bag of sweets and a new pencil was gonna turn me into a millionaire. The goody-two-shoes kids in my storybooks would’ve bought a present for their mom or given it to a homeless orphan, and I hated every single one of them for being so smug about it. Aunt Fan said she’d buy side-combs, which she was always losing, but that was no help.

Then I spotted it: in the plumber’s shop window, stacks of colourful stained glass squares, the kind you put in lavatory doors, with little stars in the middle. I bought a dozen, and the plumber was so chuffed he threw in two extra for free. I still don’t know why I bought them. Mom cried when she saw the whole pile. Dad went “But—what are you gonna do with them?” and I had no answer at all. Only Aunt Fan was nice about it: “If the kid fancies coloured glass, why shouldn’t he buy coloured glass? Tastes are different!” Then I cut my thumb so bad it gushed blood, and we threw the whole lot in the dustbin. Looking back now? I realize that half-crown was fairy gold. Every single person I ever met who got a handout from Noel Hasluck—who later everyone called the Napoleon of Finance—ended up blowing it on their own stupid version of coloured glass, and getting cut for the trouble.

Next time Hasluck knocked on the door, a proper maid in cap and apron opened it, and that was just the start of the changes. New shiny oilcloth covered the hallway floor, lace curtains went up in every window so all the neighbours could see we were getting rich, and dad started wearing his Sunday suit to work on weekdays, while the brand new fancy suits he’d bought lived in that old wardrobe we used to hoard all our good stuff in. Mom would drag out her old lilac silk dress and the Indian shawl that was so thin you could pull it through a wedding ring, and she’d hum little silly songs while she folded them up, all bright and happy like ten years of drudgery had fallen off her shoulders.

I remember one night, ages before we’d even met Hasluck, I woke up screaming from a nightmare: I’d fallen a million flights of stairs, ended up in the street in nothing but my nightshirt, tried to catch a bus and had no pockets to pay the fare, so the conductor was chasing me. I crept downstairs for a cuddle, and found the drawing room door glowing under the crack. I pushed it open, and there were mom and dad, both in their absolute best clothes—dad in buckled shoes and a frilled shirt I’d never seen before, mom wrapped in that thin Indian shawl, wearing her fancy ceremony cap that lived in a silk-lined wicker box 360 days a year. They jumped guiltily like two kids caught stealing jam. They’d snuck in there after washing the windows, lit the silver candlesticks, and were drinking sherry and eating sweet biscuits, talking about Byron’s poems and the Great Glass Palace in Hyde Park. I didn’t ask any questions. I still think that’s the nicest secret I ever kept.

After Hasluck turned up, dad was busy all day, off to the city to do rich people’s business, and mom had a million new social calls to make, so I was left to wander the streets all on my own. Mom wanted to make me stay in, but dad said “Don’t mollycoddle him, let him learn to be sharp.” Mom said “I don’t think sharp people are that nice, Luke. You’re not sharp at all, are you?” Dad thought for a minute and said “Well—sort of, in a business way,” and mom laughed and agreed.

I liked wandering the squalid narrow streets far more than the posh parks. The dim little back lanes were the twisted roots of that big forest of brick houses, full of mystery and quiet, full of people slipping past you never stopping. One day I heard a woman scream behind a curtained window, so I ran till I ended up on a half-built street that led out to the mudflats by the river. I sat down on the step of an empty house, and a little boy my own age came hopping up to me, moving like a water wagtail, and plopped down next to me.

He whispered, mouth full of what felt like wool: “Wot ’appens when yer dead?” I told him heaven was millions of miles away, no coming back. He said he’d touched a dead body once, stuck a pin in it, it didn’t feel nothing. He gave me a thump on the chest to be friends, the local way of saying pals, and I thumped him back. He swore me not to tell anything, gave me his old pocket knife, and hopped off across the mudflats towards the river. I yelled at him to stop, but he didn’t turn back. He sank deeper and deeper into the black mud, till the slime closed over him. I ran home late that night, and I never told anyone. That cured me of hanging around the back lanes.

But the crowded streets had their own problem: the gang of local urchins that hunted me like a pack of sparrows ganging up on one unlucky bird. It’s funny to watch, right? Except when you’re the one they’re chasing. I was a knight in my head, riding with Richard the Lionheart, duelling Sir Lancelot, buddies with Robin Hood—but every time they spotted me I ran. I’d hide in doorways, creep home, and cry my eyes out on my bed, furious at myself for being a coward. Turns out fairy tale courage doesn’t stick to you very well when a gang of eight-year-olds with sticky fists are yelling taunts and chasing you down the high street.

These days, East India Dock Road is nonstop chaos: tram bells jingle nonstop, omnibuses rattle so hard their rivets look ready to pop, and thousands of feet pound the pavement at the same time, like the whole city is late for a very important appointment. Back when I was a kid, though? It was practically a ghost street. On one side, there was the echoing miles-long dock wall, and on the other, random tiny houses dotted between vegetable patches, laundry lines strung between trees, and piles of rubbish that looked like they might have been there since the Romans popped by for a visit. Only one little leftover from those days still stands (or did, last time I wandered past): the single-story brick cottage right at the start of the bridge, the old toll-house. I remember that rickety little building like it was yesterday, because that’s exactly where my childhood fell right off my shoulders, leaving me sad, jittery, and staring at the big scary world like it had just shown up uninvited to my tea party.

I can’t explain it any clearer than that. That afternoon I’d trundled all the way to Plaistow to visit our family dentist. The guy set up shop in the middle of nowhere for a very good reason: the half-crown my mom sent me with never actually left my pocket.

“Hold that half-crown out in your hand the second you get in the door,” Mom would tell me, double-checking that the coin purse it was in was tucked all the way at the bottom of my knickerbockers so I couldn’t lose it, “but if he won’t take it? Bring it right back home, no questions asked.”

I think he was some distant cousin, if not exactly related, definitely the kindest man with a sharp pointy tool ever to exist in human history. I’d plop down in his fancy velvet dentist chair, he’d unroll his case of instruments and gush over the most brutal-looking one like it was a prize-winning puppy, begging me to pick my favorite. But the second I opened my mouth to say “uh, maybe the little one that doesn’t look like it could yank a tree root out?” a pair of pliers would zip out from under his sleeve and before I could even say “wait, hold on!” the bad tooth was out, no fuss no pain. Then we’d have tea.

He was an old bachelor, his house surrounded by a huge overgrown garden (Plaistow back then was a proper little country village, not the crowded mess it is now), and his housekeeper made jam and jelly out of all that fruit that tasted like pure heaven. Those teas were legendary. Half the time he’d tell me stories about Mom, back when she was a tiny kid — nothing at all like the sensible, no-nonsense mom I knew! A chaotic, prank-loving little gremlin, by the sound of him. I’d repeat all the tales back to Mom later, and she’d laugh and say she had no memory of any such nonsense, and scold me for gossiping about grown-ups, then immediately lean in and go “…wait, what else did he say about me, specifically?”

Tea done, he’d bring out his giant microscope, and it was like peeking through a door straight into fairyland. Pond dragons, tiny slimy monsters the size of a pinhead, all wiggling around. I decided he was a totally harmless magician, no questions asked. Looking back, I always figured his obsession with tiny tiny things made perfect sense, because he was a very little man, with the biggest heart that ever fit into a human chest.

I’d hand him the half-crown on my way out, “with mamma’s compliments”, he’d take it very formally, and the second I stepped outside his gate? I’d dig in my jacket pocket and there that exact same half-crown would be, grinning back at me like it had snuck out just to hang out. The first time I brought it back to him, and he looked at me with a perfectly straight face and said “Must be a different half-crown! Those things are slippery little rascals, they wander right back into pockets all the time. Happens every day.”

On that fateful day I was trundling home, I stopped on the bridge for a minute to watch the lazy barges drift slow between the piers. It was one of those quiet summer evenings where even the gross, grimy city air feels full of soft little whispers, and the second I walked through that white toll-gate? I felt like I’d left the little 10-year-old version of me leaning on the bridge railing, staring at the sun on the water. The feeling was so strong I actually turned around to check, half expecting to see him still there. It sounds daft, I know, but I swear that little kid never came back. He was gone, like he’d wandered off somewhere and forgot to return for his body.

I tried for weeks to play his games, dream his silly little dreams, but none of it felt real anymore. I was just a tall, thin ghost pretending to be a kid, and it made me sad enough to cry. I didn’t want to grow up! Could no one put a stop to this? I’d much rather stay small forever, running around the streets with my broomstick horse, not heading down this weird dark road no one would tell me anything about. Did I have to go forward? Slowly, over months and years, a new kind of me grew in his place, all these new silly feelings and weird daydreams, and that little Paul faded out of my life for good. I have to let him fade out of this book too, before he sticks around and messes up all the stories later. Before I say goodbye to him for good, though, let me get the rest of his best memories down on paper, so we can be properly quit of him.

First up, Aunt Fan. She’d be crouched over the kitchen fire, her skirt and crinoline rolled all the way up to her waist, leaving only her petticoat on for decency’s sake, swaying back and forth like a metronome while she stroked her own knees affectionately. I’d stand opposite her, waving a paper knife like a sword or riding a broomstick steed, yelling dramatic lines at the top of my lungs. Sometimes I was a knight and she was a wicked ogre, so she’d growl and curse dramatically, get “killed”, and immediately turn into the beautiful princess I was supposed to rescue. The rescuing part was always make-believe, though, because Aunt Fan would never get up out of that chair by the fire, she was way too busy rubbing her knees.

Other times, I’d grab the meat chopper and declare myself an Indian brave, so she’d be Laughing Water or Singing Sunshine, and we’d go off “scalping” the dinner potatoes together. When I was the Fairy Prince, though, she made a terrible Sleeping Beauty — that role was way too boring for her. Her absolute best role was Cannibal Chief, when I was Captain Cook sitting on top of an upturned table, and she’d smack her lips and go “I’ll skin him, hang him in the larder for a week, then he’ll be perfect — not too tough, not too gamey.” Aunt Fan was always incredible at adding the tiny, ridiculous details no one else thought of.

Don’t get me wrong, Aunt Fan wasn’t just humoring me to be nice. No, she was living for this stuff. Half the time she’d make up the game and the plot all by herself, and immediately cast herself as the heavy: dragons, wicked uncles, fussy wizards, random annoying fairies. She could have written a best-selling ogre cookbook, no contest. All the boring fairy tales just say “boil the prince, roast the princess for supper, stick them in a pie” — Aunt Fan invented whole new recipes. “We’ll stuff him with chestnuts, serve him on toast,” she’d direct me, “don’t forget the giblets, they make the best sauce.” For imprisoned maidens? “Don’t feed them too much fish, it ruins the taste when you roast them later.”

The stuff she’d turn people into! Kings’ sons, rightful princesses, she’d turn them into a glyptodon or a hippocrepian without a second thought, and later when I looked those weird ancient creatures up in the illustrated dictionary, I realized I’d never be able to kiss the transformed princess ever again, no matter what the fairy tale said. Warriors turned into plaice, queens into Brussels sprouts. She’d plan out every last detail of the big battle: sharpen the knives, get the mops and buckets ready to clean up the mess afterwards. Total realist, that woman.

Her dramatic death scene at the end of every afternoon was a whole production, groaning and writhing so hard it sometimes scared me. I’d cheer out loud when she did her final death gurgle, and only then would she let her skirt fall back down, like she was dropping the theatre curtain, and stand up to start making the tea.

Another memory: me, in my little glazed peaked school cap, strutting through the grimy streets explaining absolutely everything to a tiny curly-haired girl who stared up at me like I was the smartest person who ever lived. Every so often she’d run ahead, turn around, and look at me like I was a hero fresh off a horse, ever since the day she decided I was the most famous, impressive kid in Poplar.

I loved having her around, for way more reasons than she knew. First off, she stopped me from being a coward. With her next to me, I couldn’t run away from a random stray dog or a gang of older kids, we’d both go looking for adventure together — the constant chance of getting attacked makes walking way more fun than it is for boring grown-ups, after all. But that trusting little face next to me made me feel 10 feet tall, and in the quiet dark lanes I’d even let her hold my hand, which was peak kid chivalry back then.

The other perk? No one would shove me into the gutter when she was around. She had a vocabulary on her that could make a sailor blush. Any idiot who tried to walk straight through us like we weren’t there (everyone did that back then, no one bothered stepping around kids) would get a full earful. “Oi, glass-eye! Where d’you think you’re going? We’re not ghosts, can’t you see us?” If they tried to argue back, her tiny shrill voice would only get louder: “Garn, they ran out of heads when they made you! That thing on top of your shoulders is just a turnip!” No one ever dared smack her for it, it’d be like yelling at a terrier for barking, it just made the whole street laugh at them. Half the time I was the one staring at her like she was the hero, not the other way around. She’d wait for hours for me outside my house, her little face pressed to the iron railings, until I came out or waved no from my bedroom window, then she’d run off, the sound of her little feet pattering away making me feel sad for no reason.

I sort of liked her, but she never made it into my daydreams. That meant she was only a real-world friend, not a princess from my head. And honestly? Her dad ran a fried fish shop. I searched every fairy tale I owned, and not a single one had a princess whose dad sold battered cod out of a shopfront. If he’d been a wood chopper? Easy, that’s classic fairy tale stuff, she was clearly a lost royal baby the fairies accidentally dropped off there. Even a pork butcher? You could rebrand that as a swineherd, no problem. But fried fishmonger? There was no precedent. Our meetings got fewer and fewer, and once the glorious Barbara showed up (the girl with golden hair I’m getting to soon) I stopped hanging out with her entirely. I still see her in my head, though: that tiny little girl who thought I hung the moon. Some quiet summer afternoon soon I’m gonna walk into that little shop where she works now, go say hi. I traced her ages ago, I know she’s a mom with a whole bunch of kids, she’s gotten stout, and her tongue is even sharper than it was back then. But I know under all that grown-up stuff, I’ll still see that little curly-haired maid staring up at me like I’m a hero. It took me years to realize how rare it is to find someone who thinks you’re amazing just for existing. If we all wrote the real truth in our diaries, wouldn’t we admit our favorite thing about other people is how much they admire us? If you want me to be a good person? Tell me how great I am. Praise is sunlight, indifference makes you boring, and contempt turns you into garbage. That’s the truth, no one will admit it out loud.

Then there were all the “general” maids that cycled through our house. Back then, any woman who actually wanted to be a proper servant moved out to the rich west end, so the local maids were all girls running away from bad breakups, or mad at their families, or heartbroken and looking for a quiet place to hide instead of joining a convent. They kept quitting the second they found a new boyfriend or patched things up with the old one, so my poor mom was forever running around trying to find a new one. I was friends with all of them, since I had no other kid friends. Most of them weren’t very good at make-believe, but they’d always squeeze 10 minutes out of their mopping schedule to run around and play with me, which was way better for me than any fancy fantasy game. The bad part? They were absolutely blown away by how much random book learning I had, so they all thought I was some sort of tiny child prophet, and they’d listen to me ramble for hours, wide-eyed. Sometimes they’d ask Mom if they could bring me home to tea with them, just so all their friends could hear me talk. I’d stand on their kitchen table and hold forth, loving the sound of my own voice, and everyone would go “ain’t he as good as a whole book?” One old greengrocer even said I was “as good as a play”. I was totally convinced I was one of the immortals at 10 years old.

One girl named Janet stayed with us for months, and would have stayed forever, if she hadn’t sworn like a barge sailor. She was the only daughter of the captain of the barge Nancy Jane, that ran between Purfleet and Ponder’s End, and her conversation was equal parts terrifying and hilarious. “Janet!” Mom would yell, clapping her hands over her ears, “how can you say such terrible words?” “What words, mum?” “The things you just called the gas man!” “Him! Did you see him walk straight into my clean kitchen without wiping his boots? The — “ and before Mom could stop her, Janet would yell the whole curse again, completely confused why Mom was panicking.

We were best mates, so I took it upon myself to reform her. I told her straight up, blunt as anything, that if she kept swearing she’d go straight to hell. “Then where’s my dad going?” she asked immediately. I asked her if her dad swore. Turns out her dad made Janet’s swearing look like a nursery rhyme. “It’s the only way he knows how to talk!” she said. “He doesn’t mean anything by it.” I sighed, stuck to my guns, told her all swearers go to hell. But Janet wasn’t having any of it. “God send my sweet old dad to hell just cause he can’t talk like a prissy posh gentleman? Don’t you believe it, Master Paul. He’s got more sense than that.”

Those words stuck with me forever. Back then I was going through a whole phase of being absolutely terrified of hell, you see. All the stupid children’s books they gave you back then showed pictures of devils shoveling burning coals onto little sinners, who screamed forever. I’d burned my hand once pretty bad when a shovel of hot coals tipped on me, so I knew exactly what that felt like — but forever? A thousand years of burning, a million years of burning, and you still weren’t even a tiny bit closer to it stopping. I couldn’t sleep for weeks. The grown-ups all kept telling me “just don’t do bad things! Just repent! Just believe!” but it was so easy to do bad things, and no one could explain to me how to properly repent, or how to make sure you actually believed, or what the unforgivable sin even was. I was too scared to ask, because I was 100% sure I’d accidentally committed it when I snuck an extra biscuit last Tuesday. I’d kneel in the dark for hours, whispering over and over “I believe! I believe! I do believe!” then stand up, my knuckles white, wondering if I’d done it right. But whenever I got stuck thinking about all that nonsense, Janet’s voice would pop back into my head: “God’s got more sense than that.” And she was right.

If I had to pick one word to sum up Doctor Florret, headmaster of our respectable middle-class academy, it would be “correct”. The man was the exact, factory-issue specification of what a headmaster should be: large but not alarmingly large, stout but nowhere near fat, built to look like a walking advertisement for steady, comfortable respectability. His hands were white and perfectly shaped, his single diamond ring glinted just the right amount—no tasteless sparkles that would make the local vicar side-eye him, no tiny dud that looked like he fished it out of a Christmas cracker. He spoke of even the driest topics (rain, school dinner menus, the state of the cloakroom boots) in a tone that lent them the grandeur of a royal coronation speech. Clean-shaven, radiating benignity tempered by exactly the right amount of sensible discretion, he was less a person than a walking manual of “How To Be A Respectable Victorian Gentleman”.

Everyone and everything around him matched the brief: his wife was his feminine carbon copy, the sort of woman who could pour tea and scold a misbehaving boy with equal, perfectly calibrated politeness. Stare at the pair of them standing side by side and you’d half believe the old story about the rib was true, and some divine factory had cranked her out directly from one of his own to be his perfect helpmeet. His furniture was solid, heavy, built to last 200 years if it didn’t break your toe first, and so unobtrusive you’d never notice it till you tripped over it. His pictures hung on the walls without ever daring to draw attention to themselves, and you could set your pocket watch by the things he said: not the clock time, mind you, but the exact minute it was Proper To Say Whatever he was about to say. If you were ever stuck wondering what the “right” thing to do in any situation was, you only had to hang about till Doctor Florret did it, and instantly you’d realise every other possible choice on God’s green earth would have been absurdly wrong.

At the cost of four more or less wasted years, I learned his school’s teaching methods were also 100% correct—correct, that is, for every English schoolmaster who’d ever drawn breath since the days of Edward VI., not correct for overwrought 12-year-olds who’d much rather be doing literally anything else.

Heaven knows I tried. Boyhood ambition, the big messy kind that doesn’t know what it wants to be when it grows up, burned right through me. If I passed a ragged orator on a street corner yelling about Utopia at a crowd of passersby, I instantly saw myself as Demosthenes swaying mobs, the Prime Minister of England so beloved even the opposition papers were forced to print grudging praise of me. If I heard a distant military band, I was leading an army home from glorious war, perched on a horse I spent three whole maths lessons debating (snow white? Too easy to get mud on. Coal black? Too grim for a victory parade. I settled on dappled grey and was still arguing with myself about it when the bell rang). When I saw a sailor staggering out of a pub swinging his tankard, I was Columbus discovering a whole new continent, defeating the French, dying heroically at the peak of victory and getting buried in Westminster Abbey, then waking up half a second later to walk straight into a lamppost or step on some grumpy old man’s toes. If the other boys chattered about the Lord Mayor’s Show? I was a millionaire merchant prince, lending nobles millions from my counting house and marrying their daughters. If I heard a snippet of conversation about a new novel? I was a world-famous author, adored by everyone, no grumpy reviewers in sight. The only fly in all this grand glory? My handwriting was atrocious and spelling was a total mystery to me, writing pages full of blots and crossed-out words till I nearly gave up on being a famous author entirely.

No matter which grand path to fame I daydreamed about, though, I understood even in my foggy boy brain that education was the key I needed. So I pored over my textbooks till my eyes ached, sitting at my wobbly one-legged desk in my cold little bedroom, jumping every time my hair got too close to my single candle and started fizzling. On winter nights I’d wear my overcoat buttoned to the chin, wrap a blanket round my legs, and stuff my fingers in my ears to drown out the noise from the street below, droning “amo, amas, amat” over and over till my own voice sounded like a buzzing fly in my ear, and my head felt so small I’d pat it to make sure it was still attached to my shoulders.

Was I stupider than the average boy? Or is a ten to fourteen year old’s brain literally not built to handle the absurd workload our education system dumps on it?

“Latin and Greek,” Doctor Florret would purr in that smooth, correct voice of his, echoing the solemn croak of the entire Victorian establishment, “are excellent mental gymnastics.” My dear silly Doctor Florret, can’t you pull one single useful subject from the thousands that exist that will actually strengthen a kid’s brain instead of making it want to hide under the desk? You, fully grown, with a brain trained for decades to parse your own native language—go dig out a page of Browning, and parse every single line, break every word down into its parts. Would you call that fun for a tear-stained, ink-smeared little Tommy? It’s just as sensible as handing him 100lb dumbbells and telling him to build up his skinny little arms.

Half our time at school was wasted on total nonsense that none of us would ever use, all of us sons of clerks, shopkeepers, minor civil servants and small professional men who were going to spend our whole lives in the regular workaday world. I hated those old dead poets with every fibre of my being. Homer? I wished those fishermen who met him had asked him that stupid riddle about the thing they caught and didn’t catch a few years earlier, so he’d have dropped dead before writing a single line of the Iliad. Horace? Why couldn’t that shipwreck he wrote about have actually drowned him, like it would have done for any normal person not labelled a “classic”?

Until one blessed, glorious day, I found a magic talisman. Listen up, all you little overburdened schoolboys: don’t waste your pocket money on marbles or sweets, save up. Somewhere near Paternoster Row there’s a very clever bookseller who will sell you a little thing called a “translation key” for a few pence, that flings open the gates of all that stuffy old Greek and Latin nonsense and turns it into a story you actually understand. With that key, Aristophanes’ Frogs were my silly, goofy friends, and I wandered through Odysseus’ adventures like they were my own. Doctor Florret was delighted with my sudden “progress”, and for once it was real progress—I finally got to understand the story first, instead of wasting three hours picking apart each word for no reason.

I can’t take credit for the discovery, though. It was Dan—big Dan, strong Dan, soft-smiling Dan, the laziest, sharpest lad who ever hated useless work—who showed me the trick. Dan turned up in the Lower Fourth a term after me, and within a week he was handling Latin verse like he’d made a deal with the devil. We snuck off to a quiet corner of Regent’s Park once, he checked to make sure no one was within earshot, and spilled the beans.

“Don’t tell the rest of the lads,” he told me, “they’ll blab it, and then no one will get away with it.”
“But is that allowed?” I asked him, all nervous and proper.
“Look here, young ‘un,” he said, “what’s your dad paying all these school fees for? Are you here to play a silly game against the masters, or are you here to actually learn something? If it’s just a game, fine, we play by their rules. If it’s to learn? You’ve been in this class four months, I’ve been here a week, and I already know more Ovid than you do.” Which was 100% true.

So I thanked him, we shared our keys, and all the Latin I still remember to this day I owe to Dan. Even more than that, I owe him the common sense that balanced out all my silly, daydreamy nonsense—he was my best friend, and being the mate of big, wise Dan, who could read a bloke’s character in two seconds, is still one of my proudest achievements. He said he liked me because I was “jolly green”, a “rummy little mug”, and he’d never explain further, just ruffle my hair with his great big hand. I was a soft, shy little thing back then, pink and white, hair that would never lie flat no matter how much water I dumped on it, growing so fast I ached all over. But I never minded that walk, because Dan was always waiting for me at Chalk Farm station, and he’d walk back home with me in the afternoon too. When my back ached so bad it felt like someone had cut a chunk out of it, he’d sling his big arm round my shoulders, and leaning against him was as comfy as sinking into an armchair. Technically, Dan should have been in the Upper Fourth. He was six inches taller than every other boy in the Lower Third, years older, and the Doctor would yell at him in front of the whole class twenty times a week for wasting his potential. Old Waterhouse, the form master who secretly adored him, was genuinely upset that he wouldn’t move up. All the rest of us were a bit miffed that our hero was stuck down in our lowly class. The only person perfectly happy about it was Dan. The Lower Third suited him just fine. The Upper Fourth had nothing he wanted, and no one was going to make him go there.

He deliberately picked the very last seat in the back row, nearest the door, and fought to keep it no matter what. It meant he could be first out the door at playtime and last in when the bell rang, and he was so far back most teachers forgot he was even there. He was almost always bottom of the class, and he preferred it that way. Most of the tedious questions about participles, square roots, or meridians never even made it to him, snatched up by the swots waving their hands in the front row. The odd one that drifted back he’d just ignore, like it was an annoying fly buzzing near his ear.

Only once did he lose that perfect seat. Caught off guard, he accidentally blurted out the correct answer to a geography question, and before he knew it he was dragged up to the exposed front bench, fuming so bad if any of us had dared to whisper congratulations he’d have punched all our heads in. “Never said I was,” Dan muttered, staring longingly back at his empty little nook by the door. By the end of the day, he’d slithered his way back there again.

Dan gave him that bright, unapologetic smile of his. “Plenty, sir. It just isn’t yours, that’s all.”

“Can’t come in here,” the boy said.
“Why not?” Dan asked.
“‘Cos if you do, I’m gonna kick you,” he explained simply.

“You told me you had no money left!” one of the boys, Dudley, said, outraged.

As the end of year exams rolled around, I was a nervous wreck. My mum and dad never said a word, but I knew they were waiting anxiously for the results—Dad to see what I’d learned, Mum to see how hard I’d tried. I’d worked my socks off, but I knew prizes never went to the kid who knows the most stuff, they go to the kid who’s best at convincing the examiner they know all the stuff. Same rule applies to every prize after school, if you ask me.
“Are you going for any of the prizes, Dan?” I asked him, tramping over Primrose Hill one bright June morning. I knew the question was daft, but I wanted him to ask me about my own plans.
“They’re not giving away anything I particularly want,” he drawled. Looked at from that angle, school prizes really aren’t worth all the stress and sweat, are they?

Of the Shadow That Slipped Between the Man in Grey and the Lady With the Love-Lit Eyes

“There’s not a single thing missing, far as I’ve searched,” my mom announced, patting her apron like she was double-checking no silverware had vanished down the sofa cushions. “Mark my words, that’s the only explanation: she got spooked out of her wits and legged it.”

Dad froze mid-pour, decanter in one hand, unopened bottle of sherry in the other, blinking like he’d just been caught red-handed sneaking an extra pre-dinner glass. “Spooked? What on earth was there to scare her?”

“The whole idea!” Mom huffed. “She’s never waited on anyone in her life. Last night I found her blubbering over a tray, convinced she’d dump the entire soup tureen on a guest’s best waistcoat.”

“Uhh… what do we do?” Dad glanced at the mantel clock, face going pale. “They’ll be through the front door in less than an hour.”

“We’ll have no dinner at all,” Mom said, “unless I yank an apron on and haul every course up the stairs myself.”

“Where’s her home again? Ilford, right?”

“Yep.”

“Easy,” Dad said, brightening up. “We just play the whole thing off as a silly joke! No one’ll mind.”

Mom sat straight down on the nearest chair and started crying. The previous week had already been absolute hell. This was the first proper fancy dinner we’d ever hosted, the kind that started with anchovies and ended with ices from the confectioner’s down the road, and we’d spent every waking minute prepping. We’d scrubbed the house top to bottom, bought all those tiny useless frilly knickknacks that no normal household ever needs that Ostentation demands the second you invite a business contact over for a meal. Our kitchen range had straight up gone on strike like a union rep from the Boiler Workers’ Guild, refusing to bake so much as a scone until we called three plumbers to beg it to behave. The dusty old cookbooks we’d dug out from the back of the shelf had been argued with, cussed at, and half the ice recipes we tested had turned into runny cold custard disasters, but we’d finally nailed it. Victory was ours. Until the only maid we’d trained for a whole week had up and vanished, leaving a massive uncrossable chasm between the perfectly cooked dinner in the kitchen and the empty dining table. The bridge had bolted. No wonder mom was sobbing.

“Can’t we grab someone else last minute?” Dad asked.

My memory lingers on that soft, silly scene, because the days that followed got so sad, so fast. I swore I could sometimes see its outline in the dim air, feel a cold chill when it brushed past me. “Your own disinfectant.”

“Silly fool,” Teidelmann said, under his breath — but loud enough for everyone in the room, including me out in the hallway, to hear. The whole table went dead quiet for a full ten seconds before Hasluck burst out cackling.

“Fair enough!” Hasluck laughed. “He’s completely right, that’s exactly what I am. I’m so obsessed with talking shop I assume everyone else is too.”

He wasn’t even that much of a fool, though, because by the time I snuck back down the hall he was yelling about art, and Teidelmann was suddenly fully interested. Apparently Teidelmann was one of the best art judges in Europe, and he’d just bought six paintings from a young painter who’d be world famous soon for two thousand quid, and they’d be worth twenty grand someday. He said he’d never sell them, though. His widow would get the cash. A soft, musical little laugh drifted from the far corner of the table.

“It’s Anderson’s great big disappointment,” a soft, lazy, purring voice said, the laugh turned into words. “He’s spent thousands buying paintings of ragged little kids and badly dressed Madonnas, and I just can’t make myself care about any of them. Total waste of money, as far as I’m concerned.”

“But you’d pay thousands for a diamond to hang around your neck,” Dad pointed out.

“It makes my neck look nicer,” she purred back.

“Which is an even bigger waste of money,” Dad said, quiet, and she laughed that tinkly little laugh again.

“Who is she?” I whispered to Barbara. We were sat on the top of the stairs, splitting a shared jelly spoon to spoon.

“Second Mrs. Teidelmann,” Barbara whispered back. “Married him for his money. I don’t like her much, but she’s drop dead gorgeous.”

“Pretty as you?” I asked, disbelieving. We were sat with our backs to the light, so I couldn’t see her face.

“Oh, me!” Barbara scoffed. “I’m just a kid. Nobody looks at me like that, not unless they’re other little children like you.”

“Everybody thinks you’re beautiful,” I insisted.

“Who?” she asked, quick.

“Dr. Hal,” I said.

Her voice softened, a little happy. “Next time you see him, ask him what he honestly thinks of me, like you’re just curious for no reason. And don’t forget a single word he says this time. I’m curious.” It sounded like a silly task, but I never said no to anything Barbara wanted.

Turns out Barbara was far and away the star of the whole dinner anyway. Everyone was gushing over her: Mrs. Florret called her a perfect neat-handed Phillis, the Reverend Cottle’s wife — a broad, loud lady with eleven kids, eight of them boys — laughed and said “I’d never let a girl that pretty wait on my table, my sons would trip over each other trying to hand her the salt.” Dad accidentally called her “my dear” mid-plate-pass, and Hasluck snuck a silly little pinch to her elbow when no one was looking, and Barbara yelped “augh!” so loud Mom decided they might as well just tell everyone the whole story, rather than look weird. Everyone loved it even more, and when Barbara took off her apron but kept her cute little lace cap and came to sit down with the rest of the guests for dessert, every single person in the room was crowding her to say how clever and kind she was.

“That’s way better than being beautiful,” Mrs. Cottle said, to round off all the compliments.

“Kind hearts are more than coronets,” Dr. Florret said, perfectly on cue like he always did, and it never sounded annoying coming from him, just like the Amen at the end of a church service.

My aunt, of course, had one last observation to make, breaking the quiet lull. “When I was a girl,” she announced, “I used to stand in front of the mirror and tell myself ‘Fanny, you’ve got to be amiable.’ And I was! For ten whole years. No one can say I wasn’t.”

“Didn’t it pay off?” Hasluck asked, grinning.

“Got absolutely zero attention,” my aunt said, defiant.

Most of the rest of the dinner everyone argued about Dr. “Fighting Hal” Washburn’s big rant: he insisted all the respectable middle class folks were nothing but marionettes, dancing to the rules of etiquette, not their own actual feelings. He told a story about a patient of his whose wife was doing laundry in the front room when her husband died, and didn’t even stop wringing out her stocking to yell into the back room “Jim, you still there? Nope? Guess he’s dead.” No fake sobbing, no fainting on the doorstep. Dr. Florret said he’d much rather hang out with the polite, well-behaved dolls than the rough unfiltered East End folks. Dad chimed in with a story about a masked ball he went to in Paris when he was a student, where everyone acted exactly the same, and he bailed before midnight ‘cause he hated all the masks so much.

“That’s a shame,” the new Mrs. Teidelmann purred at him. “I’d have loved to see what they all looked like underneath.”

“I probably would’ve been disappointed,” Dad shrugged. “I’m with Dr. Florret. Sometimes the mask is the better part.”

Only a man who’d sat through far too many awkward dinners could have the audacity to state the blindingly obvious that “a dinner of herbs with love beats a fancy roast joint with hatred” loud enough for everyone to hear. It’s the kind of glaring commonplace no normal person dares utter, lest someone throw a boiled potato at your head.

Before we knew it, we were eating hot fancy joints every single day of the week, not just Sundays, and pudding turned up on the table so reliably it stopped feeling like the miracle we’d spent our whole childhood saving up for. It was like giving a kid unlimited lollipops: after three days they’re begging for dry toast. No more late-night strategy sessions scheming how to stretch Sunday’s roast into Monday’s hash, no more carefully slicing bread thin instead of breaking it like a normal human to make sure we had enough for Saturday’s bread pudding. We had no money worries left at all, but somehow the joy bailed right out the front door. A silent, uninvited third guest sat at every meal with us, so we stopped laughing and teasing each other the way we used to over our shared half-pound of slightly burnt sausages or two herrings that smelled strong enough to clear the house faster than a fire alarm. Instead we’d chat stiltedly about the weather and the price of turnips, never daring to name the thing hanging thick in the air between us.

Every now and then mum and dad would mumble half-heartedly about moving to Guilford Street, like two people poking a bruise just to check it still hurt. Inevitably one of them would say the quiet part out loud by accident, they’d snap at each other, and the aching months would drag on, the cloud over our table getting heavier by the week.

Then one day the news dropped: old Mr. Teidelmann had keeled over dead mid-account book in his counting house.

“You’re going to see her?” mum said.
“I’ve been sent for,” dad replied. “I have to, it’s probably important business.”
Mum laughed so bitterly I almost dropped my marble. I couldn’t work out why at the time, and dad stormed out the house. For the whole hours he was gone, mum locked herself in her room, and I snuck to the door a dozen times, hearing her sniffle. I was very confused. Old Teidelmann was the guy with the permanently gravy-stained waistcoat who pinched my cheek so hard it left a mark, why on earth would anyone cry over *him*?

After that, the new Mrs. Teidelmann started coming over far more often. Her black mourning clothes softened her sharp, bright eyes, made her look even prettier than usual. She was endlessly sweet to my mum, who suddenly looked plain, stilted and out of place next to this rustling silk-clad vision of elegance. To me she was kindness itself, never turning up without a tiny silly gift, or a plan for some treat that actually felt fun. She got me, in a way no grown up ever had: she brought me adventure novels with pirates and dragons, not the pious moralising books mum bought me that spent 30 pages explaining why skipping church was a sin. She talked about days out chasing ducks in the park instead of the boring missionary picnics mum kept trying to drag me to. Mum always painted my future as this narrow, walled alley, every inch lined with giant “MUST NOT” signs. Mrs. Teidelmann made life sound like a big sunny field where you could run wherever you wanted, no rules, no walls. I was half scared of her, but there was some stupid stubborn part of me that would’ve followed her all the way down the street no questions asked.

“Has he ever seen a pantomime?” she asked my dad one morning, wiggling her mouth at me playfully. My heart did a full backflip under the table.
Dad raised his eyebrows. “What do you think your mum will say, eh?”
My heart plummeted straight through my shoes. “Mum says theatres are very wicked places,” I mumbled. That was the first tiny crack in my head where I wondered if maybe mum was wrong about something.
Mrs. Teidelmann’s smile made that crack bigger. “Oh dear, then I must be the wickediest woman alive! I’ve always thought pantomimes are the most moral entertainment on earth. All the bad guys get sent packing to their just desserts so fast you barely have time to boo them. And we’d promise to leave before the clown steals the sausages, wouldn’t we Paul?”

They called mum in, and I couldn’t help but notice how tiny and pale she looked in her plain dark dress, standing stiff next to this glowing woman covered in lace and rosewater perfume.
“Let him come, Mrs. Kelver,” Mrs. Teidelmann begged in her soft, coaxing voice. “It’s *Dick Whittington*. The moral is practically the whole plot!”
Mum stood there twisting her hands over and over, that silly old nervous habit she had, her lips trembling. Even I thought this was a tiny, trivial treat, I couldn’t work out why she was so upset.
“I’m very sorry,” she said, voice sharp and tight. “It’s very kind of you, but I’d rather he didn’t go. When he’s older other people can judge for him, but right now I’m the one who guides him. Alone.”
“C’mon Maggie,” dad chipped in. “Things are different now than when we were kids.”
“That may be,” mum snapped. “It’s a very long time ago, isn’t it.”
“I didn’t mean it like that,” dad said with a short, awkward laugh.
“I know,” mum said. “I feel so old around all of you, so out of place. I’ve tried to change. I can’t.”
Mrs. Teidelmann was immediately all soft sweetness again. “No worries, I just thought he’d like it. His dad told me he worked so hard this last term.” She laid her hand on my shoulder, pulling me a little closer to her.
“It was very kind of you,” mum said. “I’d do anything to give him pleasure. Anything at all. He knows that. He understands.”
I could feel mum’s hand hovering an inch from mine, waiting for me to take it, but I was being a stupid sulky kid, so I pretended I didn’t notice. Mum turned and walked out of the room without another word.

She never mentioned the pantomime again. But the very next afternoon, she dragged me off to a local hall to see a magic lantern show and a conjurer. She’d put on a pretty dress she hadn’t worn in months, laughing and chatting like she did back in the old happy days. But I was too busy nursing my stupid grudge to care. Normally I’d have lost my mind watching a man pull a live rabbit out of a random stout uncle’s hat — the last person on earth you’d expect a rabbit to be hiding in his headwear — but all I could think about was the glittering big theatre I’d heard all the other boys rave about, the pantomime I’d missed. We trundled home through the dark muddy streets barely speaking. That night I woke half-asleep, and saw a white figure crouched at the foot of my bed. I yelped, and mum stood up from her knees.
She laughed that wobbly, broken little laugh, stroked my hair. “Silly dream I had, that a wicked magician spirited you away from me. I couldn’t find you anywhere, I was all alone in the world.” She hugged me so tight my ribs ached, and I fell back asleep in her arms.

Later that same holiday, mum and I went to visit Mrs. Teidelmann at her huge stone house in Clapton. She’d sent a note that morning saying she had crippling headaches, and was leaving England to travel in a few days, wanted to say goodbye. Dad waved the note about. “Of course you have to go. It’d look so rude not to turn up.”
Mum hesitated. “Do you think I should? What could she possibly have to say to me?”
“Just goodbye,” dad said.

The house looked like a boring grey prison from the street, but once you stepped through the door it was like walking straight into Aladdin’s cave. Ugly old thick-lipped Mr. Teidelmann had spent his whole life hoarding pretty things from every corner of the globe. Paintings were stacked propped against antique furniture, stretched unframed across ceilings, statues peeked out from every corner, priceless china, gold-tooled books, gem-framed miniatures were heaped everywhere. The only boring, cheap uninteresting thing that had ever lived inside those walls was old Teidelmann himself. Now he was shrivelled up in his coffin, under a fancy marble statue that made his random no-name cemetery a tourist spot.

The servant left us in Mrs. Teidelmann’s boudoir. She was asleep on the sofa, dressed in a loose lace gown, her dark hair spilled all over the pillows. Without her usual makeup she looked harder, a little rough around the edges, nothing like her perfect polished daytime self. Mum sat down by the window, her eyes fixed on the sleeping woman’s face, and no one spoke. The only sound was an old French clock on the mantelpiece ticking soft and muffled. Then Mrs. Teidelmann started mumbling in her sleep. At first it was just nonsense murmurs, then I heard her breathe, so quiet but so clear in the silent room: “Ah no, we must not. Luke, my darling.”

Mum stood up, her voice completely calm. “Go Paul. Wait for me downstairs.” She pushed me gently out the door, closed it soft behind me.
I hung around the hall for half an hour before she came down. We walked all the way home without a single word, her moving like she was sleepwalking, her eyes seeing nothing in front of her. Only when her hand touched the lock of our front gate did she snap back to reality. “You must not say a word about this, Paul. Do you understand? When people are half-asleep and delirious they say all sorts of nonsense that means nothing. Promise me you will never breathe a word to anyone.”
I promised.

From that day, mum’s whole personality shifted. No more sharp words, no more angry flashes in her eyes. Mrs. Teidelmann was gone for three months, dad wrote her letters all the time managing her affairs, but mum wrote her long secret letters too, writing them in the twilight by the window, refusing to turn on the lamp. “I think better in this light,” she’d say whenever dad tried to ring for a light, fussing that she’d strain her eyes. When Mrs. Teidelmann came back, mum was the first person to call on her, before dad even knew she was home. Everyone thought they were the closest of friends. Peace settled over our house again. Dad was tender and sweet to mum, mum looked after all his little whims exactly like the old days, but every week she got a little paler, a little more tired, the old spark was gone from her.

That summer in London was the sweltering sort, where the air smells like fried fish and horse manure, everyone lies awake at night huffing like overworked donkeys. Dr. Hal said there was nothing actually wrong with her, just weakness, that London air was slowly sucking the life out of her. “Get her out of the city,” he said.
Dad couldn’t leave work right then, so he sent us off to that old coastal village we’d loved when we were little, said he’d join us later if he could.
We climbed the rickety old stone tower that silly old Jacob had built on the headland, the one that looked like it was one strong gust away from tumbling into the sea. The village was exactly the same. Mrs. Fursey was still there, now bragging about me to all the neighbours like I was a prize turnip she’d grown against all odds. Anna, the grubby little girl who used to steal my marbles, was now a buxom wife who kept trying to sneak up and kiss me at random moments, so I had to spend every waking minute positioned behind a table or chair so I could make a quick escape. Old Chumbley was still cobbling shoes in his tiny little shop, chattering to every pair on his workbench like they were people: the tiny new baby shoes were impatient to go toddling, the fancy dress boots were sighing that they were late for a dance, the old worn out work boots were muttering “just a quick patch, mate, we don’t have far left to go.” All the old local blokes were still doing their exact same schtick: Joe the pedlar was still selling his wares with the exact same three jokes he’d been using for 40 years, Tom Pinfold was still scratching his head with that sad rejected fish hanging off his wrist, I swear it was the exact same fish he’d had when I was little. The tiny babies I used to bounce on my knee were now strutting around in knickerbockers acting like they were grown men, it was so annoyingly pretentious.

One afternoon, we were up on the tower’s top platform, mum leaning on the parapet staring at the faint distant hills, a smile on her face. “What are you thinking about?” I asked.
“Just stuff that happened over there, when I was a girl,” she said, nodding at the hills like they were an old gossip friend she shared secrets with.
“You lived there ages ago, didn’t you?” I said.
She laughed, that sharp, playful old laugh I hadn’t heard in months. “You’re very rude. It was only a minute ago.”
Then we heard a long, loud “coo-ee” drifting up from the pines. We looked down, and there was dad, waving his stick, climbing up the path. We both knew immediately something terrible had happened. He only ever got that bright, brave, lit-up look on his face when everything had gone completely pear-shaped, the same energy as an old Norse pirate spotting a gale heading straight for his ship.
He clattered up the rickety stairs, puffing a little, laughing. He stood next to mum for a minute, both of them staring out at the hills. “Remember this was our very first walk together, the day we got here,” he said. “We made all these big grand plans to get rich, fix all our problems.”
“The future always has a way of making its own plans for you instead,” mum said.
Dad laughed, a little bitter. “Yeah, well. I’m an unlucky beggar. I lost all your money as well as mine down that stupid mine. The damn pump jammed, and the whole thing went bust.”
“It’s fate, Luke,” mum said, quiet. “You couldn’t help it.”
“D’you remember that Mrs. Tharand?” mum said after a minute. “She came to see me the morning we left. Told me ‘of all the troubles in the world, money troubles are the easiest to bear.’ I thought that was a stupid thing for a rich woman to say to me back then. But I know now she was telling the truth.”
Dad grinned. “Also, I’ve quit working for Hasluck. Told him to his face he was little better than a thief.”
“What’d he say?” mum asked.
“Laughed, and said that was better than some people.”
Noel Hasluck was the most charming unprincipled man who ever caught the bus into the city every morning. His laugh was so deep, hearty and honest sounding he’d later admit it was worth thousands of pounds to him. His whole “business” philosophy was simple: the game is rigged, nine out of ten people at the table are sharpers like him, and the tenth fool doesn’t deserve to keep his money. Once I argued with him about the widows and orphans who lost their savings in his dodgy mines. He just patted my shoulder and laughed. “My dear lad, if you go looking for widows and orphans you’ll find them playing roulette at Monte Carlo, same as everyone else. They’re no more innocent than the rest of the gamblers. Besides — I’m an orphan myself, aren’t I?”

The eighteen months that followed—for the end crept up far softer and kinder than any of us had dared fear—were, I swear, the happiest my father and mother ever shared. If “happy” feels too light a word for it, call it the most beautiful, the least broken stretch of days two people could ask for. It felt as if Death had leaned in and tapped gently at our door, like a polite neighbor dropping off a note: “Not yet. You’ve got a little longer to be together. Only a little while.”

They laid aside every single silly, performative thing that had cluttered their lives before. No more straining to keep up appearances for the neighbors, no more panicking that the lady across the road would judge our mended curtains. All the tiny petty worries, the mean little vanities and petty grudges that turn ordinary people into smaller, crosser versions of themselves just fell away. They grew softer, kinder, more willing to cut the whole messy world a break. Their love spilled right over the edges of their own small home, wrapping around every stray cat, every peddler at the door, every grumpy passing stranger. Looking back now, I swear most of us are fools to spend all our energy running from Death like he’s a bogeyman. He’s God’s own messenger, the only fellow who’s seen the full scope of life, right? He doesn’t come to scare you. He leans in and whispers: “Only a little longer left together. Is it worth being cross over burnt toast? Is it worth hoarding all your kind words for no reason at all?”

That soft warm graciousness wrapped around every corner of our house, even my Aunt Fan. One afternoon, not long after we got back from Devonshire, she dropped her knitting, fixed both of us with a steely stare, and announced out of nowhere: “I’m a beast of an old woman.”

“Now don’t say that, Fan,” my mother fussed.
“What’s the point of telling me not to say it when I’ve already said it?” Aunt Fan snapped back.
“It’s just your tone, that’s all,” Mum explained. “People tend to think you’re being disagreeable.”
“They’d be daft not to,” Aunt Fan huffed.
“Oh, you don’t really mean half the sharp things you say, do you?” Mum said, smiling.
“Stuff and nonsense,” Aunt Fan snorted. “Of course I mean ’em. I like being disagreeable. I like watching people squirm. Makes ’em think.”
Mum laughed. “I’m sure you could be just as pleasant if you tried, Fan.”
“I *could*,” Aunt Fan muttered to herself, a faint vindictive glint in her eye. “I tried it once before, you know. Fell flatter than a pancake left out in the rain. Nobody even noticed. And it didn’t do anybody any good. A disagreeable person is at their absolute best when they’re disagreeable. But I could do it again. Easy. Look at all the soft idiots who pull off being nice every day.”

Mum thought she was joking. She was very, very wrong.
Dad was the first one to pick up that something was off. “Everything all right with poor old Fan?” he asked one evening, three days after Aunt Fan had put her new plan into action. “Nothing bad happened to her, did it?”
“Not that I know of,” Mum said, biting back a laugh.
“Her whole vibe is weird,” Dad said, frowning. “Weird as anything. Creepy, almost.”
Mum told him then that Aunt Fan was making a deliberate effort to be agreeable. Dad laughed for a full minute, then went a little wistful. “Oh, I almost wish she wouldn’t. We were all used to her being snappy. It was comforting. She was way more fun when she was calling the postman an idiot.”

But Aunt Fan was a woman of iron will, and she’d barely settled into her new nice persona when Mr. James Wellington Gadley wandered into our lives. Properly, he’d been christened Wellington James back in 1815, but he’d figured out halfway through his school days that being named after a war hero was more burden than blessing for a very ordinary, slightly round little clerk, so he’d flipped the order. He was a bit pompous, but the softest, simplest old man you could ever meet, absurdly proud of his job as head clerk at Stillwood, Waterhead and Royal, the old, once-fancy law firm that’d just taken Dad on as an assistant. The firm dated back to the days of King George, had handled half the aristocracy’s messiest secrets (and more than a few very shady deals back in the day), and even though old Mr. Stillwood, the last surviving partner, refused to take on new clients and openly admitted the business was slowly dying, it still carried a name for rock-solid respectability. Dad thought he’d won the lottery getting the job.

Gadley had started there as an office boy back in the firm’s glory days, and he still genuinely believed they were the most important legal office within a mile of Lombard Street. His favorite hobby was retelling all the firm’s old unsolved mysteries, all names redacted: Mr. Y, Lady X, “a foreign country that shall remain nameless,” “a town not 20 miles from where we’re sitting right now.” Most people yawned and made excuses to leave when he started on one of his stories, but Aunt Fan hung on every word, especially if it involved potential murder. The very first time they spoke, Gadley leaned in, wide-eyed, and said “Now, why would a man abandon his wife for 18 whole years, never send a single word, then suddenly show back up out of nowhere? I’ve never cracked that one.”
Aunt Fan didn’t even blink. “To poison her slow. So he could marry the new girl.”
“Marry the new girl? But what new girl?” Gadley spluttered.
“The one who told him she’d never speak to him again unless he came back a free man,” Aunt Fan said, like it was the most obvious thing in the world.
“Wait a minute,” Gadley said, gawping. “His wife *did* die a few weeks after he got back, and he *did* remarry.”
“Told you,” Aunt Fan said, sipping her tea.
Every single one of Gadley’s old unsolved cases got the Aunt Fan treatment after that. She was convinced every unnamed spinster in the West Country had burned down a house to destroy a will, every wealthy old uncle had been smothered in his sleep by his ungrateful nephew. “We’re all miserable sinners,” she’d say cheerfully, when Gadley protested she was being too harsh. “Most of us just never get the chance to be properly bad, that’s all.” Gadley was absolutely fascinated. “Miss Davies, you ought to have been a solicitor’s wife!” he’d declare, to his mind the highest possible honor a woman could achieve. Aunt Fan sniffed and said “Chances are I would’ve been, if any of them had ever asked.”

The only problem with Gadley was his stomach, which stuck out a full foot in front of him without him seeming to notice at all. It was a gentle but unrelenting battering ram. He’d lean in closer and closer as he got interested in a story, slowly driving you backwards around the room until you were pinned in a corner, at which point he’d blink and wonder why you kept moving away. The very first night he visited us, he and Aunt Fan were chatting, he inched his chair forward, she inched hers back until she was perched on the very edge of the seat, and one final nudge sent her tumbling right onto the floor. She didn’t yell. She didn’t even complain. Next time he came over, she was sitting by the fire darning stockings. He started inching forward as usual, but this time she didn’t move an inch. Ten minutes later, Gadley yelped, sprung back, and stared at the tiny new pinprick in the thigh of his trousers. He never figured out what happened. For all their future visits, there was a full two feet of empty space between their chairs, no exceptions.

Aunt Fan, now fully committed to being nice, decided the best way to do agreeable was nonstop over-the-top flattery. The trouble was, she laid it on so thick everyone thought she was being sarcastic. Dad would wander home and tell a silly little story about a stray goose that stole his sandwich at the office, and Aunt Fan would throw her hands in the air and go “MY GOODNESS WHAT A WONDERFUL STORYTELLER! SUCH NATURAL WIT! NO TRAINING AT ALL, WHAT A GIFT!” Dad would just blink and say “All I said was the goose bit my notebook.” When Mum came down in a newly retrimmed old bonnet, Aunt Fan would step back and pretend to gasp: “Don’t tell me you’ve got a 14 year old son! You look like a frisky 17 year old girl running off to a picnic!” Any spoiled vanity that had ever gotten out of hand in any of us would’ve been cured after a week of Aunt Fan’s “nice” comments. Every once in a while, when the effort of being perky and peppy got too much for her, she’d vanish down to the kitchen to be properly rude to Amy, our new maid. Amy never minded. Amy was a philosopher whose whole worldview boiled down to “there’s only 24 hours in a day, and everything ends eventually.” “Lord bless you, mum,” she’d say to Mum, when Mum apologized for Aunt Fan snapping at her, “if I wasn’t listening to her yell, I’d probably be burning the bread or dropping a plate. Everything’s all the same once it’s over.”

Amy had come to us saying she was only a temporary hire, because she was about to get married in a few weeks. A month later, when Mum asked how the wedding plans were going, Amy blinked like Mum was being ridiculous. “Oh, that one? Turns out I didn’t want him quite as bad as I thought I did. I’m taking my time getting to know the new fella. Better change your mind before it’s too late, than be stuck regretting it afterwards, that’s what I always say.” Amy had a new “Dearest” every few months, a rotating cast of butchers, bakers, soldiers, sailors, all of whom were “poor dears who just need someone to take care of them.” We never bothered learning their names, just called all of them Dearest, so we never got confused. “How’s Dearest doing this week?” Mum would ask her on Sunday evenings, and Amy would rattle off how he’d had a cold, or won a prize at the fair, and the wedding was always “in the spring, mum, when the weather turns nice.”

It took Amy less than a week to worm our secret out of Mum, and then she took over the whole house completely. “You leave all the scrubbing, the cooking, the errands to me, mum,” she ordered. “You two have got better things to do.” And just like that, we didn’t have to worry about a single trivial thing ever again. Barbara Hasluck started popping over even more often, too, bringing baskets of fruit and flowers her dad sent along, laughing so loud she chased every last shadow out of the house. Dad started looking younger, lighter, like all the weight he’d carried for 20 years had fallen off his shoulders. One summer night, he and Barbara and I walked her to Poplar Station, when Dad wasn’t tired. “I like my walking companions tall and slight,” Barbara said, tucking her arm through his. “The silly young lads don’t know how to flirt properly. You can come along too, Paul, you’re our gooseberry.” Dad pretended to sneak a kiss to her hand before Mum could see, and the three of us tramped down the East India Dock Road, all quiet and soft in the moonlight.
“I spent my whole life thinking I was a total failure,” Dad said quietly. Barbara gave his arm a tiny, gentle squeeze. “But these last few months? I’ve realized I’m the luckiest man alive. It’s like I was lost in a dark wood, panicking I’d never find my way out, and then I turn a corner and I’m exactly where I needed to be all along. All that time I thought I was wandering, I was walking straight home. Who ever would’ve thought?”

I’ve always suspected Barbara and her sneaky old softie of a dad arranged all that, of course. Barbara came over one day waving a birthday cheque old Mr. Hasluck had given her, saying “Uncle Luke, help me invest this! Dad’s no good, he’d dump all my money on some silly dodgy railway scheme.” And then she’d “let” Dad talk her into buying Argentino railway shares, which of course went up, then sell them at exactly the right time to buy Calcuttas, which went up even more. Dad kept muttering that it felt an awful lot like gambling, and that when he was gone, Paul, you put all that money aside to do good, don’t use it for yourself, it needs to be cleaned up a bit. (It turned out to be the kind of luck that always stuck to old Hasluck, and did far more good than harm anyway.)

The silliest, most wonderful trick Barbara pulled off was talking my two very Puritan, play-hating parents into going to a proper 19th century melodrama: *A Voice from the Grave, or The Power of Love*, five acts, 13 separate dramatic tableaux, full of ghosts and secret murders and long-lost heirs. Dad argued that you couldn’t judge a thing you’d never seen, and Mum admitted she’d once gotten in massive trouble as a girl for reading Sir Walter Scott by moonlight, so they caved. The four of us stood on the corner of Pigott Street waiting for the omnibus, the summer air thick and warm.
“Lovely night,” Dad said, grinning. “Let’s go the whole hog. We’ll sit up on top.”

Where have all my childhood crushes on my little boy friends run off to? Tubby the golden-haired, Langley with the dented schnoz, Shamus stout of heart but wobbly of leg (easy to tackle, impossible to ever force to admit he lost a fight), scrawny Neal, and fat Dicky everyone called "Dicky Dick", Ballett the crier who'd burst into tears if he scraped his knee, and Beau Bunnie who owned 17 frayed neckties and insisted on fighting in spotless black kid gloves. All the rest of you, whose names I can't dig up but whose grubby little faces I could pick out of a crowd of a thousand adults, where did you go? What corners are your silly ghosts loitering in? If someone had told 10-year-old me there'd come a day I'd never hear your wild whoop of greeting, never feel the inky smudge of your handshake, never bicker and scrap and make up with you over stolen jumbles, I'd have sat down on the curb and cried right then.

I swear I saw Tubby last month, in fact. He's got bushy sideburns now, his once-golden curls are thin and grey, and he's hurrying down Throgmorton Street chained to a black leather briefcase like a overworked dachshund. I nearly hailed him, but I was late for a train and had to stop for a shave on the way. He stared at me funny for a second, like he half-recognised me too. Gallant old Shamus, who once fought a bigger boy for half an hour just to stop him skinning a live frog? They say he's an Income Tax Assessor now. A man so skeptical no expense claim gets past him, a paragon of zero charity and boundless disbelief. May Providence kindly assign him a different postal code than mine.

That's Time for you, the handyman of the universe, bustling around tidying everything up. He drapes grass over dead volcanoes, turns ancient rubble into garden soil, patches up the beech bark lovers carved their initials into, and turns all those feral little boys you knew as a kid into boring middle-aged men clutching briefcases.

I started out my school career as the official class pariah, and that was the first real heartache I ever carried. Grown folks are great at lying to themselves: at 40, most of us will swear we're at least a little bit popular, because no one could bear to go through life convinced no one likes them. But kids haven't learned that handy self-protective lie yet. I knew exactly how unpopular I was, no delusions to hide behind, and I brooded over it so hard I'd go hide in the playground hedge and chew on grass.

One day the most important person in the entire lower school sauntered up to me: head of the Lower Fourth, tall, beaky-nosed, son of a Edgware Road draper who thought he was the second coming of the Duke of Wellington. (He ended up a low-level civil service clerk, poor bloke, but to us he was royalty.)
"Can you run?" he demanded.
"Yeah," I said. Running was the only thing I was actually good at, word must've leaked.
"Then run twice around the playground as fast as you can. Go."
I balled my fists and bolted. I was so thrilled he'd even spoken to the class outcast I ran so hard my lungs felt like they were going to fly out my throat. When I skidded to a stop in front of him, gasping, he nodded.
"Why don't the other lads like you?" he asked bluntly.
Oh, what I could've said! I could've begged him for the secret of popularity, told him I'd do anything to get the other kids to stop falling quiet when I walked up, stop kicking me out of their games. I'd swagger like Tommy till my sides ached, I'd steal their balls and jump on their backs like Dicky, I'd fling my arms around their necks and do all their math homework and take all their detentions, if only they'd like me even a little bit. But all I mumbled, sullen, was "They do. Some of 'em do."
"Don't lie," he said. "You know they don't. Now listen: I'm giving you a shot. We're running hare and hounds next Saturday. You can be one of the two hares. Don't tell anyone yet, just turn up at the Spaniards on Hampstead Heath at half two. But you better run like the devil, yeah?"
He walked off before I could answer, and joy came sprinting at me so fast I almost fainted. That's the big break moment for everyone, isn't it? The politician getting a congratulatory pat on the back from his party leader after his first speech, the playwright getting a note asking him to come meet the producer, the new subaltern getting called over by his commanding officer. The sun breaks through the fog, and you think the whole world's yours.

I didn't say a word at school, but the second I burst through my front door I exploded. "I'M GOING TO BE A HARE! Only two hares, and they picked me 'cause I run the fastest! Can I have a blue and white striped jersey? And new running shoes? We start Saturday!"
We bought the jersey and the shoes that very same afternoon, because I was convinced if we waited one more day the last striped jersey in all of London would be gone, and every shoe shop in the city would be sold out of running soles. That night I put the whole getup on and admired myself in the mirror for ten minutes straight. All week I practiced leaping over every chair in the house, and I'd slide down the banisters instead of using the stairs, much to my mother's horror. Dad backed me up, saying the honour of the Lower Fourth was on the line, but mom added a whole new line to her nightly prayers begging God not to let me break my neck.

Saturday came. Everyone met at the turnpike outside the Spaniards, none of them paying me any mind, assuming I'd just come to watch. I had my regular clothes on over my jersey, hugging the secret surprise to my chest that I was the second hare, that I'd be the hero of the whole run.
Then the Duke, our Wellington, announced it. "Kelver's the other hare."
Silence.
"Oh come on, we don't want him," one kid yelped. "He's a total muff."
"He can run," the Duke said.
"Then he can run home," someone else snickered, and everyone laughed.
The Duke got mad. "Any more lip and you'll be the one running home, do you hear me? Kelver, you ready?"
I'd already started unbuttoning my jacket, but my hands dropped to my sides. "I don't want to come if they don't want me," I mumbled. The tears were pricking my eyes and I couldn't make them go away. I turned my face away, refused to move.
"Fine, don't then," the Duke snapped, and handed the paper trail bag to some other kid. "Hurry up, it'll be dark before we even start."
The two hares bolted off, and I drifted away, hiding my face. Someone yelled "Cry-baby!" but the Duke growled at him to leave me alone. I wandered over to the big cedar trees.
I heard the hounds whoop and take off five minutes later. How was I supposed to go home? Admit my mom and dad that I'd been shunned, that the big honour I'd bragged about had vanished? I wandered off, slipped my jacket and shirt off, tucked them under my arm, and became my own hare and hounds. I chased myself all over Hampstead Heath, alone, in the fine cold rain that had started falling.
I still see that pathetic, ridiculous little kid, jogging doggedly through the mud. Mile after mile he runs, that little idiot, jumping over ditches and occasionally face-planting straight into them, scrambling through dripping hedges, clambering over tarry fences and slimy wooden palings. He stops every few minutes to wipe his eyes on his grubby handkerchief, tuck his bundle of clothes tighter, and duck behind hedges whenever he sees a farmer or a walker pass by. He runs all the way through Bishop's Wood, past the graveyard bottom that's got a railway screaming through it now, down sloppy lanes that are now lined with identical cheap suburban villas, all the way to Seven Sisters Road station, soaked to the bone, covered in mud. He takes off his soggy fancy jersey, and goes home to Poplar, and lies shamelessly about what a jolly afternoon he had, and how he was so fast none of the hounds even came close to catching him.
You poor dumb little brat. Popularity's just a shadow. Chase it, and it'll always run faster than you. Turn your back on it and walk toward the sun, and it'll follow you all the way home.

Telling that lie started a whole habit, though. I'd sneak out every Saturday, run miles and miles alone, scattering torn up bits of paper behind me to pretend I was laying a proper hare trail, so my conscience wouldn't sting for lying. My mom would coo "They never catch you, dear?" and I'd say no, never even get near me. She was so proud of her fast little son. I even started hearing imaginary hounds behind me sometimes, and I'd sprint faster, for a second forgetting I was all alone.
The only person who never made me feel left out was Dan. His friendship was a rock in the middle of all that loneliness. To this day, Dan always roots for the losing team, defends the guy everyone else is picking on, hates crowds more than anything. "You've got no opinions!" a club friend complained to him once. Dan just shrugged. "I hate crowds." He never asked for anything back for being my friend. When I came crying to him after the other kids were mean to me, he never even gave me annoying unsolicited advice. When I finally got popular later and didn't need him quite as much, he wasn't even hurt. He was like a fountain, made for giving water, and didn't care if anyone ever thanked him.

And popularity did come, all of a sudden, after I'd completely stopped trying for it. Groups of kids would fight over who got me to walk home with them, like I was a prize at a fair. I didn't get it, till Norval, the second-best fighter in the lower school, asked me to walk to Camden Town with him, and I demanded to know why everyone suddenly liked me.
"'Cause you're a funny bloke," he said. "You say the funniest things."
It hit me like a slap to the face. All the school story books I read said the hero won everyone's love for being brave, honest, good at cricket, a leader. None of the book heroes were the class clown, the guy everyone gathered around to laugh at. I didn't want their laughter. I wanted their admiration. But I was so desperate to be liked I went along with it. I practiced silly jokes, thought up goofy little bits, twisted every serious thought I had into a punchline.
I got all the popularity I'd ever wanted, but I was never happy. I'd trade every single laugh I ever got for just the respect everyone gave stuttering Jerry when he hit his century and won our school match against Highbury. I'd get up an hour early every morning to practice throwing balls at broomsticks stuck in the yard, I spent weeks trying to learn to walk on stilts so I could be the fastest stilt-walker in school, and it broke my heart for months when even fat Tubby could outrun me on them.
There was a Sixth Former named Wakeham who was dead jealous of my joke skill. He was 15, going to little girls' socials at Gospel Oak, and he was convinced if he was funny all the girls would like him. But he was the most boring, solemn boy on the entire planet.
One afternoon he pulled me into an empty corner of the playground, eyes wide. "Kelver, you are so funny. You think you could teach me how to do it?"
I was just about to say absolutely not, when I thought of something. Wakeham had a superpower: he could stick two fingers in his mouth and blow a whistle so loud it would make dogs a quarter mile away panic, and make every grown up within ten feet leap two feet in the air. Old men would cuff him round the head for doing it, bus drivers would flick him with their whips, even other kids would throw rocks at him. I was desperate to learn it.
We made a deal. I'd teach him to be funny, he'd teach me the whistle.
For two whole weeks we tried. He did every goofy face and bad joke I told him, and it landed every time with the grace of a sack of potatoes. He showed me every whistle trick he had, and the best I could do was a tiny, reedy little peep that couldn't even startle a sleeping sparrow.
"Think anyone would laugh at that?" Wakeham would ask, after his funniest ever joke that fell flatter than a pancake. I'd have to say no.
"Think anyone could hear that whistle from ten feet away?" I'd wheeze, red in the face.
"Depends if you were already listening for it," he'd say kindly, not wanting to hurt my feelings.
We called the whole thing quits after a fortnight.
"I guess being funny's just something you're born with," I told him, to cheer him up.
"I guess the roof of your mouth just isn't the right shape for the whistle," Wakeham said, to cheer me up.
Neither of us ever got the skill we wanted. But we both got the lesson: the stuff you chase the hardest is never the stuff that fits you.

IN WHICH PAUL GETS SHIPWRECKED AND Tumbled Headfirst Into The Deep, Murky Waters Of Grown-Up Life

My father, displaying the kind of absurd punctuality that only the very dead can pull off, breathed his last on the exact morning of his own birthday. None of us had been expecting the final curtain to fall for another few weeks at least, so for the first quarter hour we hadn’t the foggiest the show was over.

“I left him dozing,” my mother whispered, slipping back into the parlour in her dressing gown, soft as a mouse. “Washburn gave him a sleeping draught last night. No use rousing him now.”

So we huddled round the breakfast table, talking in tones so quiet the toast nearly fell asleep, too. Our little Poplar house was about as soundproof as a paper bag – you could hear someone dropping a teacup three doors down, never mind through our own thin partitions. Later my mother crept upstairs, me tiptoeing two paces behind her, and eased the bedroom door open a crack.

The blinds were still drawn, the room dim as a coal cellar. My mother stood there for what felt like half an hour, ear pressed to the doorjamb, listening. The first costermonger of the day drifted past – a girl, by the sound of her – yelling at the top of her lungs: “Watercreases! Fine fresh watercreases, going with your breakfast, a penny a bunch, watercreases!” And further off, a hoarse teenage lad was wailing like a lost seagull: “Mee-ilk, mee-ilk-oi!”

Inch by inch my mother pulled the door wider, and we snuck inside. He was lying there eyes still closed, lips just parted. I’d never seen death before, and I couldn’t wrap my head around it at first. All I noticed was that he looked younger than I’d ever seen him look when he was alive, like he’d shed ten years overnight. Slowly, slowly it sank in: he was gone. For days, weeks afterwards, I’d spot his step behind me on the high street, hear his voice calling my name, catch a glimpse of his face in a crowd, and sprint over to greet him – only to stand there gawping, confused, as some random bearded grocer or pipe-smoking carpenter stared back at me like I was a lunatic. At first, funny enough, I felt no pain at all.

For my mother, it barely registered as a separation at all. Her quiet, unshakable faith had never once entertained fear or doubt. He was just waiting for her, that was all. In God’s good time they’d meet again, no tears needed. The days did drag on, of course – the house always feels a bit emptier when the man who tells all the bad jokes and fixes the wobbly chairs isn’t around – but that was the worst of it. She’d talk about him constantly, smiling at the memory of his daft little quirks and silly habits, not as if he was some fossil stuck in the past, but as if he was leaning over the garden fence just out of sight, waving her over.

We stayed on in that old house, forever muttering about moving out, even as that great greedy brick monster that was London crept closer every year, crunching up every nice thing between its ugly cement teeth. Fields, gardens, oak trees, cosy old cottages that looked like they had stories spilling out of their windows, soft hedgerows that hid the grime beyond – every last little spot that still smelled like sunshine got gobbled up one by one, leaving nothing but endless rows of smoky, sooty terraces, the East End’s little ghetto for the working poor that just kept spreading, dreariness piling on top of dreariness until it felt like the whole place was one big grey catacomb. On misty afternoons, when we knew the sun was shining somewhere outside that maze of slums, we’d trudge out house-hunting in the suburbs that had not yet been eaten alive – back when Highgate was a tiny little market town connected to civilisation only by a single rickety coach that trundled through leafy Holloway; back when Hampstead had rows of ancient red brick houses, and from the windy heath you could see London’s domes and spires peeking over distant farms and woods; back when Wood Green was surrounded by pastures, where labourers in smock coats argued about politics and ale under spreading elm trees; back when Hornsey was little more than an ivy-covered church and one single grass-lined lane. Every time we’d find the “absolute perfect place for us”, pick it apart from every angle, decide it was flawless… and then dither.

“We’ll sleep on it,” my mother would say. “No rush. For all kinds of silly little reasons, I’ll be sorry to leave Poplar.”

“What reasons, mum?”

“Oh, no particular reason, Paul. We’ve lived here so long, that’s all. It’ll feel like yanking a tooth, leaving this old house.”

We’re all of us just clingy vines, aren’t we? We twist our tendrils round whatever wall is closest, no matter if it’s a castle keep or a pigsty, and the rain and sun only make the grip tighter. You remember old Sir Walter Scott, dying in Italy, hurrying home as fast as he could for fear he’d draw his last breath without breathing the damp mist off his beloved Scottish hills? I knew an old woman once, who got carted out of her slum attic to the seaside to get fresh sea air… and just pined away for her little hovel. She was a wife, a mother, a widow, lived there her whole life, till the alley’s smoky reek smelled sweeter than lavender, and the neighbours yelling at each other at 2am sounded like the voices of her family. Who are we to argue with that, save the God who made us?

So the old house held us tight, till first my aunt, then my mother, were gone, and I’d said goodbye to Amy, crying in the middle of half-packed crates, before I finally walked out the front door for good.

My aunt died exactly as she lived: grumbling the whole way.

“You’ll all be glad to be rid of me!” she huffed, dropping for the first and only time in my memory straight talk, no snarky detours. “And I can’t say I’m all that gutted to go myself. This life never was what I’d ordered.”

Poor old soul! That was only two weeks before the end. I don’t reckon she realised how close the finish line was, or she might’ve bothered being a bit sentimental.

“Don’t be daft,” my mother said. “You’re not dying.”

“Why talk such rubbish?” my aunt snapped back. “I’ve got to die some time. Why not now, when all the arrangements are already sorted? It’s not like I’m having a grand old time.”

“I’m sure we do all we can for you,” my mother said.

“I know you do,” my aunt replied. “I’m a burden. Always have been.”

“Burden’s not the word for it,” my mother corrected her.

“Well what word would you use?” my aunt shot back. “Sunbeam? Have I been a sunbeam in this house? I’ve been a nuisance to everyone. That’s what I was born for. It’s my speciality.”

My mother wrapped her arms round the grumpy old thing and kissed her forehead. “We’d miss you terribly,” she said.

“Good! I hope you do!” my aunt said. “It’s the only half-decent thing I’ve got to leave you lot.”

My mother laughed.

“Might even have been good for you, Maggie,” my aunt mumbled, softening a little. “If there weren’t cantankerous old devils like me knocking about, gentle patient sorts like you would never get any practice at being patient. For all you know, I’ve been a blessing in disguise.”

I can’t honestly say I ever caught myself wishing she was back – but we absolutely did miss her. Missed all the little jokes we’d crack about her odd habits, missed all the laughs at her grumpy, prickly little ways. It takes all sorts to make a world, as the saying goes. If all of us perfect, easy-to-get-along types were left, we’d all be so bored we’d start picking fights just to liven things up.

Amy, for one, truly did regret her passing.

“You never know what you’ve got till it’s gone,” Amy sighed.

“I’m so glad you grew fond of her,” my mother said.

“See, mum,” Amy explained, “I was one of seven kids. A little bit of yelling every now and then does a body good. I reckon I’ll lose the use of my tongue soon if no one around here picks a fight with me.”

“You’re getting married in a few weeks, remember,” my mother reminded her.

Amy just glumly shook her head. “Men are useless things, aren’t they? The best of ’em can’t stand a bit of backchat. Half the time you’re just getting into a good argument, you turn round and find they’ve put their hat on and snuck out the door.”

My aunt gone, Barbara off to finishing school abroad (old Mr Hasluck called it “learning how to ape the nobs proper”), mum and I were left all alone. I’d left school by then, and taken a no-salary job with Mr Stillwood, the idea being I’d study to be a solicitor.

“You’ve hit the jackpot, my boy,” old Mr Gadley assured me. “Starting your career at Stillwood, Waterhead and Royal is a golden ticket anywhere. It’ll mark you out as a proper respectable sort, no question.”

Mr Stillwood himself was so old and frail he looked like he might blow over in a strong gust of London smog. It was a wonder a man as rich as he hadn’t retired decades earlier.

“I keep meaning to!” he explained to me a few days after I started at the office. “When your poor father came to see me, he told me straight he hadn’t got more than a few years left. ‘Mr Kelver,’ I told him, ‘don’t you worry on that score. I’ve got a few loose ends tied up in the office I’d like to sort out first, and you can help me with those. The second they’re done, I’m retiring!’ And here I still am, plodding along. I’m like that old hackney coach horse Mr Weller tells us about, you know? If you pulled the shafts out from under him he’d just collapse on the spot. So I jog on, one slow step at a time.”

He’d married late in life, to a woman much younger than him, a common sort of girl who’d dragged in her wake a whole horde of greedy, broke relatives who all thought they were owed a fancy living. No wonder he preferred the quiet peace of Lombard Street to his own home, where the noise never stopped. We’d see his wife every now and then. She was the sort of loud, bossy woman who went through life convinced that if she yelled loud enough and made enough of a fuss, she was “putting things to rights”. She was absurdly ashamed of her humble origins, and tried to hide them under a posh duchess act that her sycophantic friends told her was perfectly aristocratic – but in reality, it just came off like a barmaid on a Sunday trying to pretend she’d never served a pint in her life. Old Gadley was the only person alive who wasn’t scared of her. On the contrary, he kept her terrified of him, speaking to her with a brutal honesty that was always extremely refreshing. He’d known her back when she was a urchin playing in the gutter, and he’d always taken it as a personal insult that she’d wormed her way into the old, respected firm of Stillwood & Co.

Her story was a strange one. Mr Stillwood, a bored, well-off bachelor pushing 40, had first spotted her when she was a scrawny blonde little kid, caked in dirt, playing in the gutter. To this day he’s kicking himself over it, but it was young Gadley, walking home with his boss from Westminster, who drew Stillwood’s attention to her by boxing her ears – because the little brat had slapped him across the face with a sprat she’d grabbed off a passing fishmonger’s cart. On a random impulse, Stillwood took the kid by the hand, dragged her off kicking and screaming to her father’s place of business – a tiny little coal shed down Horseferry Road. The arrangement he made there amounted to buying the child off her parents, practically. He sent her off to boarding school abroad, and the coal shed was closed up. Ten years later, when she came back a tall, handsome young woman, he married her – and learned the hard way what everyone always says about what’s bred in the bone. You can scrub it, dress it up in silk, teach it to speak posh, but it always comes back out.

Her constant complaint about her husband was that he was “only a solicitor”, a profession she considered terribly common. Nothing riled old Gadley more than hearing her spout that nonsense.

“It’s not fair on the girls!” I heard her yell at him once. I was working in the next room, the door ajar, and she talked so loud at all times you could hear her in the next postcode. “What proper gentleman is ever going to marry the daughter of a City attorney? I told him years ago, he ought to retire and get himself a seat in the House of Lords.”

“Funny, that’s exactly what your old dad was going to do every time he lost a sack of coal or a bag of potatoes in his little shop,” Gadley grunted back.

Mrs Stillwood called him a “low brute” in her poshest duchess tone, and swept out of the room in a huff.

Old Stillwood himself was never all that fond of the law, to hear him talk.

“I’m not at all sure you made a wise choice going into law, Kelver,” he said to me once. “It makes you look at humanity the same way a doctor looks at a patient – like everyone’s fundamentally sick. You start suspecting every single person you meet is a rogue. The only people that ever cross our desks are the ones that are up to no good – all roguery, trickery, hypocrisy. It rots your character a bit, Kelver. We breathe nothing but sin all day. I sometimes wonder if it’s catching.”

“It doesn’t seem to have infected you, sir,” I told him. The firm of Stillwood, Waterhead and Royal was famous across the whole legal world for being as honest as the day is long, old-fashioned rectitude and all.

“I hope not, Kelver, I hope not,” the old man smiled. “Though, you know, sometimes I catch myself wondering… what if I’m a scamp and I don’t even know it? Rogues always manage to talk themselves into believing they’re perfectly honest, don’t they? No scamp ever thinks he’s a scamp.”

His words gave me a little jolt then, because on Gadley’s advice I’d talked my mother into putting all our small savings into Mr Stillwood’s hands to invest, and it had boosted our tiny income no end. But when I looked into his soft, smiling eyes, the silly fear vanished instantly.

He laughed, and laid a hand on my shoulder. “There’s one person you should never trust, Kelver – yourself. Nobody on this whole planet can do you half as much harm as you can do yourself.”

We saw more and more of Washburn, after that. We both called him Hal, now. He’d slipped past my mother’s shyness with his soft, strong hands, and settled right into our family like he’d always been there. Mum treated him like some absurdly bearded overgrown boy who’d somehow wandered into her care without noticing, and I looked up to him like the coolest, most chaotic big brother anyone ever had.

“You rest me, Mrs Kelver,” he’d say, lighting his pipe and sinking into the big worn leather armchair we kept waiting for him by the fire. “Your even voice, your soft eyes, your quiet hands – they soothe the savage beast in me.”

“A man ought to test all his grand ideas against two things,” he’d say, drifting smoke curling around his head as he looked between the two of us. “The face of a good woman, and the ear of a young man – sorry Paul, not quite a child anymore. A good woman’s face is bright sunlight. Under the gaslamps, you can’t tell a diamond from a bit of paste. Hold it up to the sun, and you know instantly if it’s real. And the kids! They’re the soft waiting earth, and we fling all our silly grand thoughts onto them. Is it chaff and dust, or real living seed? Wait and watch. I dump all my cleverest thoughts on you, Paul. To me they seem brilliant, deep, original. You swallow them, and then forget them instantly. Turns out they were rubbish. Then I say something that sticks with you, that grows. Ah! That one was alive, that was a real seed. The earth only keeps what’s true.”

“You ought to get married, Hal,” my mother would say. It was her cure for every ill a man ever had.

“I would, Mrs Kelver,” he told her once, “I’d march down the aisle tomorrow if I could marry half a dozen women. I’d be the perfect husband for six different wives. I’d neglect one for five straight days, and be a total nuisance to her on the sixth.”

From anyone else, my mother would have taken that as an insult to the whole female sex, but Hal’s smile could sugar any kind of pill.

“I’m not one man, Mrs Kelver. I’m six different men rolled into one. If I married just one woman, she’d end up married to six separate husbands. That’s way more than any poor woman can handle.”

“Have you never fallen in love, even once?” my mother asked.

“Three of me have. But every single time, the other five of me outvoted him.”

“Are you sure six is enough?” my mother asked, grinning.

“Just the right number. There’s one part of me that wants to worship a woman madly, abjectly, grovel at her feet like a troubadour before his queen of song, eat her slipper, drink the water she washed her hands in, beat myself with a cane outside her window, die for a single kiss of her glove that she tosses down with a laugh. She has to be scornful, cruel, indifferent. There’s another part of me that wants to cherish a soft, tender little thing, whose face lights up when I walk in the door

“Room to let, suitable for a single gentleman, no weird hidden surprises (mostly).” Sometimes when I’m feeling a little daft, I’ll loiter outside that same scuffed old terraced lodging house I knew as a lad and knock on the door, just for the bit. It takes forever to creak open, and there she is: the legendary urchin “slavey,” unchanged by the decades, not a day older, not an inch taller. Morning slavey has her scrawny red, dish-scored arms tucked up tight under a stained apron, afternoon slavey has a lopsided frilly cap perched on her touzled hair, round bewildered eyes like she just got caught swiping a jam tart off the counter. Then there’s the “easy chair” — and let’s be clear, I disavow all responsibility for that adjective. The second three mates turn up for tea? Suddenly everyone dives straight for that bed, no one will touch the easy chair at gunpoint. Makes total sense.

That room does not let you pretend the bed doesn’t exist: it’s the main character, the star of the show, it will not be ignored. Its only rival is the straw-coloured washstand, glowering defiantly from the corner like “Yeah I’m tiny, but I’m here, and you can’t pretend I’m not.” The rest of the furniture is a pair of chairs that don’t even pretend to be easy, they’re just hard as nails, a tiny chest of light-painted drawers with white china handles, topped with a dinky little looking glass, and a wobbly home-made four-legged table that takes up all remaining floor space except for the three square feet reserved for the poor sod who’s going to live there. For the rest of it, you lads haven’t changed a bit. I wave to you from the misty street. God rest you, gallant lonely boys, making the best of joyless days, scraping by genteel on twelve, fifteen, even eighteen shillings a week (absolute plutocrats, the lot of you), tucking a bit of that away to send home to your old mum in the country, who brags to all the neighbours about her “gentleman” son who’s got book learning and works “in the City.” May nothing you dismay. And we had our moments, didn’t we? The Saturday night trip to the theatre, hanging outside the doors with all the other drama nerds chatting about Florence and Kate, our favourite actresses whose photos are taped to our mantelpieces, turning our dinky little room into a shrine. Would you trade that feeling for the rich bloke in the stalls who saunters in ten minutes late like he owns the place? Never. My very first lodging was an attic on the other side of Blackfriars Bridge, three shillings a week if you wanted cooking privileges, half a crown if you didn’t. I bought a methylated spirit stove with a kettle and a frying pan, and opted for the no-cooking deal, saved sixpence a week.

Back then, there was a tiny brass plate nailed to a house in Farringdon Street near the Circus, announcing the Ludgate News Rooms on the third and fourth floors, admission one penny. Every morning the place was packed with a seedy bunch of us: clerks, shopmen, skilled artisans, travellers, warehousemen, all of us out of work. Most were young, but there were a few older blokes mixed in, the quietest, saddest ones in that whole whispering army of down-at-heel blokes. We split into two groups: the newbies, cheery and cocky, who flitted from newspaper to newspaper picking job ads like they were choosing treats off a fancy menu, then wrote replies like they were doing the employer a massive favour. “Dear Sir, in reply to your ad in today’s Standard, I shall be pleased to accept the vacant post. The old stagers didn’t buzz around. They sat there hour after hour writing steadily, methodically, every day a little less hopeful. “Sir, your advertisement in today’s Daily Telegraph — I am X years old, my qualifications are modest, I will work as hard as I can to satisfy you, I won’t ask a high starting salary, you can contact me any time that’s convenient.”

Out of all of them, I got three or four curt replies telling me to turn up for an interview. But the crippling shyness that’d dogged me as a kid was back, worse than ever. Shy little kids people can sympathise with, but a shy young bloke? Everyone just assumes you’re a daft idiot. I came off awkward, stupid, sullen. The harder I tried to act normal, the ruder and more out of place I seemed. Every interview ended the exact same way: “Thanks, we’ve got a couple more blokes to see, we’ll write to you,” and I never heard back.

My few pounds of savings dwindled no matter how hard I guarded them. Looking back now, it’s easy to laugh at those struggles, because I know how it all turned out, happy ending and all. But back then? I had no clue if my life was going to be a comedy or a tragedy. Some days I was dead sure it was going to end badly. I felt tiny, helpless, like the whole huge world was going to trample right over me, never even noticing I was there.

Even the self-inflicted melodramatic suffering of shy, over-sensitive 20-something homebodies eventually runs its course, thank goodness. Winter rolled around, and I threw myself headfirst into work—there’s no better drowning pool for all your dumb, self-pitying woe than a good solid work habit. My tiny garret “apartment” got cozy fast: a handful of glowing coals in the grate, two guttering tallow candles on the rickety table, a stack of cheap paper, and a new bottle of ink. What else does a would-be writer need, anyway? I was fully convinced I was about to be the great medium for all the spooky, brilliant story voices banging around in the ether—though in hindsight, the ether clearly left its front door locked the whole time.

London, with its ten million weirdos, romances, tragedies, and silly little jokes, was right there at my fingertips, just waiting for me to look at it and write it down honestly. But did it ever occur to me I could write stories about the actual people I saw walking down the street every day? Of course not. All my terrible tales starred cottage maidens, gruff yeoman farmers, and windmill backdrops, or remote moated granges, or craggy mountains I’d never once seen in my life. I’m fairly sure every single 19-year-old wannabe fiction writer goes through this specific phase of brain rot.

All of my stories dripped with soft, mournful melancholy. Back then, sentiment was all the rage, and like every newbie creator ever, I copied the trend so hard you could see the smudge marks. The worst part? Being a heroine in my story was basically a death sentence. No matter how bouncy her curly hair was (which I was sure was the official marker of top-tier vitality), no matter if she had “dancing eyes” or “ringing laughter” or the grace of a baby deer in the first chapter, the poor lass was guaranteed to drop dead of consumption before page 15, buried under a blanket of snowdrops and early primroses (I’d memorized a little illustrated pamphlet called *Our Garden Favourites* for that very specific detail) that grew in her tiny churchyard like they’d been force-fed fertilizer. If the graveyard was near the coast, the local sea breezes would make a beeline straight for it to gallivant around disrespectfully, no other graveyard allowed. Then 50 years later, a white-haired sad guy would show up with a handful of small kids, drag them to her grave, and tell them the whole miserable story—what a terrible Saturday outing that must have been for the toddlers.

Every once in a while, to mix things up a bit, I’d kill off the leading man instead. He’d always give a grand final speech on his deathbed going, “Eh, it’s fine, this is for the best.” No snowdrops or primroses for him, though, sorry: he’d be planted under a rude stick cross in the most deserted patch of African veldt imaginable, or halfway across the Sahara. I’d pop into the British Museum reading room for 20 minutes to fact-check what those places looked like, scribble the descriptions down, and call it a day.

Those little fledgling stories were so scrawny and sad. I’d send them flying out of my garret nest full of hope, and every single one of them would come fluttering back to me, crumpled, stained, crumpled, and clearly beat up by the post office. The only person who ever read them and cried over them was me.

I tried every trick in the book to get them accepted. Sometimes I’d tuck them into an envelope with a curt, tough-guy business note like “Take it or leave it, chump.” Other times I’d scrawl a pathetic, sobbing appeal begging the editor to give my poor little baby a chance. Sometimes I’d name a price of five guineas, demand a crossed cheque, and act like I was a big shot professional. Other times I’d try to trick them by offering my work for free, like it was a free sample of dodgy pickles from a travelling salesman, how could they turn down a free sample? Sometimes I’d get snarky, slip in a pre-stamped return envelope and write a note daring the editor to reject me. Other times I’d lay my whole heart out, admit I was a total beginner, and beg them to tell me exactly what my faults were—if they even had any, obviously.

Not a single one found a home. They’d be gone for a month, or a week, or two whole days, and then they’d boomerang right back. I never lost a single manuscript, which at a certain point got so monotonous I found myself actively wishing the post office would lose one just to shake things up.

I grew to hate the poor little slavey who’d burst into my room, grinning like she’d caught me doing something embarrassing, and hold the rejected manuscript out between her apron-covered thumb and finger. Her constant little sniff sounded to me like unmasked contempt. If I flew down the stairs the second I heard the postman’s knock and grabbed the envelope straight from his hands, I swore he was snickering at me behind his mustache. I’d tear the envelope open, yell at the stupid story, fling it straight into the fire—then dive to fish it out before it was more than scorched, smooth the crumpled pages out, and reread the whole thing to make sure it was still brilliant. There was no way the editor had actually seen it, obviously. Some lazy, jealous underling had tossed it straight into the rejection pile out of spite. I’d sent it to the wrong magazine! It arrived on the exact day the editor’s dog died! Any day now, their real triumph would come. I’d rewrite the first and last pages, stuff it in a fresh envelope, and send it out again, brimming with brand new stupid hope.

While all this was going on, I’d decided that if I was going to be a successful famous genius, I couldn’t just hide in my garret writing sad stories. I had to learn how to be cool in social settings too. The two big sins I had the most trouble forcing myself to enjoy? Smoking and drinking. This isn’t me bragging about being virtuous, by the way. This is me admitting my body is a total coward. My brain was fully game to be a cool bohemian artist, but my flesh was weak as wet toast. Back in school, I’d tried smoking a cigarette once or twice just to impress the other boys, and every single time I’d ended up hunched over the school toilet heaving my guts up. I quit trying for a while, and told everyone holier-than-thou that smoking was bad for growing boys, even though I knew perfectly well I was just a big baby making excuses. Same with drinking: I’d snuck sips of our family’s decanted claret now and then, and fully agreed with St. Paul that a little wine is for your stomach’s sake—but only as medicine, never for fun. I noticed even full grown men made horrible faces when they drank my mom’s home brewed wine, so I told myself that liking strong drink was a skill, not a natural talent, and I could put it off a little longer.

But now I was a grown man living on my own, my education in being a bohemian could not be delayed any further. A quarter of a century ago, if you claimed to be a genius and didn’t smoke and drink way more than was healthy, everyone would write you off as a total fraud no questions asked. I was pretty sure I was a genius, though not 100% certain—but at the very least, I could nail the smoking and drinking part. I set to it systematically. Smoking, I learned, was best practiced on Saturday nights, so I had all of Sunday to go for long walks and shake the dizziness out of my system. After enough patience and willpower, I pulled it off: I could smoke a whole cigarette and look like I was actually enjoying it, no heaving required. Most lads would have stopped there, but I’m nothing if not an overachiever. Cigarettes conquered, I moved straight onto cigars. I still remember my first one—most people do. It was at a smoking concert in the Islington Drill Hall, and my friend Minikin had dragged me along. I couldn’t tell if the swimming in my head was from the cigar or the overcrowded, overheated room full of smoke, so I made my excuses and snuck out to the little courtyard. There it was 100% the stupid cigar making me want to die. My first instinct was to yank it out of my mouth and fling it as far across the yard as I could. Bells were her whole life. She spent every second of the day listening for a doorbell to ring. Conversations were just something to do to kill time while waiting for the next interruption.

There was no Mr. Peedles around the house, at least not for Mrs. Peedles—though he was still alive somewhere, last we heard. He’d been general utility man back at the Princess’s, a strapping handsome fellow in his day, the kind who’d get led astray by any pretty minx that batted her eyes at him. Mrs. Peedles spoke very bitterly about general utility men, saying they were useless at their jobs. On weekdays, she wore the same plain black dress and plain black cap, no frills at all. But on Sundays and holidays? She transformed completely. She’d kept almost all of her old stage wardrobe, right down to the paste-studded shoes and the tinsel necklaces. Suddenly you’d see her padding around the landing looking like a very lumpy Hermia in classic flowing Greek robes, or a very bulgy Lady Teazle in heavy brocade, receiving her fellow washed-up former actor friends on Sunday evenings, reminiscing about the old days before their careers broke. Or she’d drape an old mackintosh over all the finery to hide it from the rude street strangers, and go visit her few old friends. Her cheeks would flush pink, her eyebrows would darken and arch again, all the old liveliness coming back. The only clues that it was still the same woman were her thin grey hair and her work-worn, rough hands. The change wasn’t just surface level, either. The drab, tired Mrs. Peedles hung up on a hook behind the kitchen door, like a discarded rag. Out of the old wardrobe, along with the silks and satins, came out Miss Lucretia Barry, a little aged, a little faded, but still perfectly “arch.”

Next door to my room lived an old law-writer and his wife. They were ancient, and miserably poor through no fault of their own. All those schoolbook maxims about hard work paying off? The world is full of that boring, stubborn bad luck that wears you down until you stop fighting. Their story was so simple and sad it was almost boring. He’d been a schoolmaster, she’d been a pupil teacher, they married young and everything went great for a few years. Then they both came down with typhoid from bad drains. No moral, no big dramatic lesson to be learned, just bad drains. They clawed their way back out of debt, just to lose it all again when they helped a poor friend who’d once helped them. You couldn’t blame them for that, right? They couldn’t have just left him to rot. And that’s how it went, over and over, one small disaster after another. Now they were just tired, patient, working as hard as they could, so used to bad luck they didn’t even care anymore, just happy if they got enough bread and tea to get by. Their only luxury was an occasional sip of beer or spirits. Half the time he couldn’t get any work, and then they’d go hungry. Law work always got handed out late at night, to be finished and dropped off at the office first

The sun blared through my garret window like a nosy neighbour shoving their face against the glass at full volume, and I jolted awake so fast I nearly launched myself right off the lumpy straw mattress. The low roar of London streets below was already in full swing, loud and unapologetic, reminding me the rest of the world had already finished their first cuppa, ticked off two errands, and was openly judging me for still horizontal. I fumbled for my watch on the wobbly dressing table that looked one sneeze away from collapsing entirely, only to find it had vanished. My clothes were strewn across the floor like a toddler’s discarded toy dump, one boot perched snootily on the armchair by the fireplace like a retired aristocrat, the other nowhere to be seen – almost certainly hiding to avoid being associated with whatever mess I’d made the night before.

My skull felt like it had swollen to twice its normal size overnight. For half a second I idly wondered if I’d accidentally swapped heads with Minikin while drunk, and forgotten to grab my own back; this new top-heavy monstrosity was throbbing so hard, every pulse felt like a small man with a hammer was doing construction work behind my eyebrows.

Then the previous night’s memories hit me all at once, and I snapped fully awake. The nearby church steeple started chiming, and I counted carefully. Eleven o’clock. I leapt out of bed – and immediately plopped straight down onto the floor. The whole room was still waltzing, you see. Not whirling, not violently spinning, just staggering slowly in circles like it was also nursing a hangover and trying not to knock over the furniture after a 12-hour all-night dance party. I crawled to the washbasin, missed twice, finally dunked my throbbing head in cold water, hauled my trousers on with three failed attempts, and collapsed into the beat-up armchair to untangle the mess, starting from the here and now and working backwards.

I was very obviously ill. Something had definitely disagreed with me.
“That terrible strong cigar,” I whispered to myself weakly. “I never should’ve touched it. And that stuffy little crowded room. Also I’ve been working far too hard. I definitely just need more exercise, that’s all.”
I congratulated myself briefly for being so sharp I couldn’t be bamboozled by my own bad decisions – until my inner snark cut in.
“Nonsense, you berk,” it snapped. “Stop lying. You were completely, utterly, disgracefully drunk.”
“Not *drunk*,” I whined. “That’s such a rude, coarse word! I’ve heard some people just can’t handle sweet champagne. It’s my liver. My liver’s delicate. Let’s call it a liver issue, please.”
“Drunk,” my inner voice insisted, unrelenting. “Hopelessly, vulgarly drunk. Drunker than any bloke named ‘Arry stumbling home after a Bank Holiday fair.”
“It’s my first time,” I mumbled pitifully.
“It’s your first ever chance to get properly wasted, you mean,” it shot back.
“I’ll never touch a drop again!” I promised.
“Sure you will,” it retorted. “That’s the exact line every drunk in every temperance pamphlet uses right before they sneak a flask out of their coat pocket next weekend. You’re 19, you don’t even have the excuse of being an old, tired man. What if this becomes a habit? What if you end up a ragged, staggering sot dying alone in a ditch?”

I dropped my heavy head into my hands and groaned. Every single Sunday school temperance tale I’d ever sat through came flooding back, and I could vividly picture myself stumbling down a path of ruin: disappointing my mom, making my dad’s face go pale, getting that scornful, withering look from Barbara that made her whole mouth curl, and having Hal mock me so mercilessly for being a weak idiot I’d never live it down. Shame and panic mixed together to make me feel even worse.
“I’m leaving this house right now!” I yelped out loud, dramatic as only a 19 year old can be. “I’ll get far away from this terrible unwholesome vibe, wipe all of last night from my memory, start a whole new fresh life –”
And then the memory that had been skulking at the back of my brain stepped front and centre. Miss Rosina Sellars. The barmaid. 160 pounds of very determined woman. What was she doing here? What right did she have to ruin my new, sober, regenerated life?
“The right of your brand new fiancée,” my inner voice said, with a dry little cackle.
“Did I actually propose to her?” I whispered, horrified.
“Let’s not go into the details,” it replied. “We don’t want to relive them. But that’s the end result.”
“I was not myself! I didn’t know what I was doing!”
“Yeah, well no one’s ever fully in their right mind when they do stupid things, but you still have to deal with the consequences. Half a dozen people saw you do it, and she’s not the kind of woman who’ll let a perfectly good ‘foolish proposal’ slip through her fingers. You’re gonna end up married, living in two tiny rooms, meeting all her weird relatives who all think she’s the fanciest member of the family – which tells you everything you need to know about them. Great start to your grand ambitious career as a famous writer, genius.”
“What the hell do I do?” I asked.
“Beats me,” it answered.

I spent the entire rest of the day holed up in my room, door locked, too ashamed to even face Mrs Peedles or the 12 year old slavey who brought my bread and butter round. At dusk, when I felt slightly less like I’d been run over by a cart, I snuck out to the nearby coffee shop for a sad meal of unsugared tea and a kipper. But then another guy sat down opposite me and ordered hot buttered toast, which smelled far too rich for my fragile stomach, so I bolted before the waiter even brought it out.
At 8pm Minikin turned up from the office to see what the hell had happened. I was too ashamed to lie, so I told him the whole sordid truth.
“Thought as much,” he said, nodding. “Looks like you had an A1 bender, mate.”
“I’m glad it’s over,” I told him earnestly. “It’s a lesson I’ll never forget. Nothing’ll make me touch a drink again.”
“Right,” Minikin said, grinning. “Nothing beats a proper all-night drunk to make you feel holier than a preacher fresh off a three-hour sermon. I’ve done it myself, I know.”
I was desperate for advice, and even though he was younger than me, Minikin was shockingly worldly. “The worst part isn’t the hangover,” I confessed. “I accidentally got engaged.”
“Quiet little you? No one’s more feral than the shy ones when they finally cut loose,” he commented. “Nice girl?”
“I don’t know!” I moaned. “I just know I don’t want to marry her. How do I get out of this?”
Minikin plucked his removable glass left eye out and started polishing it on his handkerchief – his little ritual for when he was thinking hard, like he could pull inspiration right off the glass. “Is she the sort of girl who’ll fight tooth and nail to make you keep your promise?”
I nodded. That was a perfect description of Rosina Sellars.
“Does she know how little you earn?”
“She knows I live up here in an attic and boil my own eggs for dinner half the time,” I said.
Minikin glanced around the tiny, messy room. “Wow. She must really fancy you, then.”
“She thinks I’m a genius who’s gonna be famous one day,” I explained. “She’s willing to wait for me.”
Minikin popped his polished glass eye back into his head. “Then just let her wait. There’s ages before you have to do anything about it.”
“But she’s a barmaid!” I wailed. “She’s gonna make me walk her round the park on Sundays, meet all her cousins, go to her family tea parties! I can’t do that. And when I actually do get successful? She’s never letting go. This is a disaster!”
“How’d you even propose anyway?” he asked.
“I have no idea,” I groaned. “I don’t remember a single second of it until I woke up today.”
“Any witnesses?”
“Half a dozen of them, all of her friends.”
The door flew open without a knock – Jarman, obviously, the man who had never heard of a door that required prior warning. He didn’t do his usual loud hello, just crossed the room, shook my hand so hard my arm wiggled, and looked at me sombre. “You look like you survived a shipwreck,” he said. “This your mate?”
I introduced them.
“Pleased to meet you,” Jarman said.
“Glad to hear it,” Minikin replied. “Doesn’t look like you’ve got much else to be pleased about in life.”
“Don’t mind him,” I told Jarman. “He was born that rude. It’s a medical condition.”
“Gifted, that is,” Jarman said, nodding. “I should hire myself out as a professional party disrupter, that talent’d make me a fortune tramping round suburban evening dos. Shame you weren’t here last night, you could’ve saved our lad a whole world of trouble. He told you he accidentally got engaged?”
I nodded, confirming I’d spilled everything.
“Alright then, you’ve got a sharp practical eye,” Jarman said, leaning forward to stare at Minikin’s glass eye. “What d’you reckon we oughta do with him? Dose of poison and bury him in the back garden? Or tie a brick to his ankle and toss him off Waterloo Bridge?”
“Wait a second,” I cut in. “There’s no way she actually thinks we’re properly engaged, is there?”
“Not a single tiny sliver of a doubt, mate,” Jarman said cheerfully. “As far as she’s concerned, you two are already picking out the china for your wedding registry.”
“I’ll just tell her straight I was completely wasted at the time,” I said. “She’ll see that makes the proposal invalid, right?”
Jarman blinked at me like I’d just suggested I could fly to the moon. “And how d’you plan on proving that? You think telling her you loved her is proof you were too drunk to know your own name? Any normal person might buy it, but not her! You think she spent three months hunting for a clever, upwardly mobile fiancée just to let him wiggle out of it because he was a bit tipsy? If every girl gave their man back the engagement ring just because he was too drunk to propose properly, all the engagement shops in London would close down tomorrow, wouldn’t they?”
Minikin nodded in complete agreement.
Jarman, now that he’d successfully bantered with Minikin and decided he liked him, pulled his chair right up next to him and started plotting like they were planning a bank heist. “Look, this ain’t some ordinary bloke who can just tell her to get lost and never see her again. This lad’s gonna be the next Poet Laureate one day, right? You think when the Prince of Wales invites him over to dinner at Marlborough House he wants to turn up with a 160 pound barmaid who drops aitches left and right and doesn’t know which end of the soup spoon to drink from?”
“Fair point,” Minikin said. “That does put a spanner in the works.”
“So we need to get him out of this,” Jarman said, frowning. “And I swear to God, I almost can’t think of how.”
“Minikin suggested he do a runner,” I offered.
“Not a bad plan!” Jarman mused. “But where you gonna run? She’s sharp, that one. She’d track you down across three counties no problem. She’d sniff you out like a bloodhound.”
“Change his name? Get a new job?” Minikin suggested.
“Need references for jobs,” Jarman pointed out. “He’s got none. She’d find him in a week.” He jumped up so fast his chair scraped the floor. “I’ve got it! THE STAGE!”
“Can he act?” Minikin asked.
“Course he can! You only need to not make a total fool of yourself, that’s it! No references needed, no one asks you what you did last month, everyone there’s running away from something! Perfect hiding spot!” Jarman yelled, excited now. “Can you sing?”
I said I could warble a little, if no one was listening.
“Sing something now!” Jarman said. “Quick! Wait a second –” He snuck out of the room, tiptoed down the landing, knocked on Rosina’s door, and came back grinning. “She’s not home yet! Sing like your freedom depends on it, because it does!”
I sang *Sally in Our Alley*, but every time the name Sally came out of my mouth I pictured Rosina’s broad, flushed face, and my voice got wobbly. But Jarman gave me a thumbs up at the end. “It’s not grand opera, but it’s more than good enough for the back row of a cheap economy chorus where they don’t care what you sound like as long as you don’t fall off the stage. Tomorrow you go find O’Kelly, the theatre guy I know. He’s a good sort, he’ll sort you out with a spot in the next show. She’ll never find you in that crowd.”
I was too tired and hungover to feel hopeful, but I thanked them both, and promised I’d think about it properly the next day when my head stopped hurting enough to make rational decisions. They said goodnight, Minikin heading down first, and Jarman snuck back in one more second.
“Consols are a bit low this week,” he whispered, hand on the door. “If you need a few quid for train fare or a new hat, don’t go selling your whole book collection. I’ve got a couple of pounds spare I can lend you. Suck a couple lemons tonight, you’ll feel brand new tomorrow. See you later, mate.”
I did exactly what he said, and by morning my head was almost clear. I turned up to the office on time, and my boss Mr Lott let me off with a two shilling deduction from my weekly pay for missing half the previous day – far better than getting fired. I’d planned to go see O’Kelly that evening, sent him a note saying I was coming round, but got a reply back that he was out, and I’d have to wait till next Friday. I moped back to the lodgings, feeling suddenly lonely and miserable, the old familiar dark hollow homesickness creeping back. The thought of no one on earth caring if I lived or died suddenly made Rosina, my eager new fiancée, seem weirdly like a lifeline. At least she cared about me, even if it was mostly because she wanted to marry a famous writer and move her family up in the world.
That’s when there was a tiny tap at my door. The little slavey was standing in the dim passage holding a crumpled pink, scented envelope. It was addressed to “Paul Kelver Esquire”, handwriting messy but surprisingly neat, full of crossed out words. I lit my candle, and read it:
“Dear Mr Paul,
I heard you got took ill right after the party. I fear you work too hard and you will make yourself poorly and then I will be very cross with you. If you are better now I am going for a walk and you can come with me if you are a good boy. Lots of love.
Your affectionate ROSIE.”
For half a second I felt all soft and stupid. My first ever love letter! A little mist swam in my eyes, and I pictured her face, all warm and soft, her big white fleshy arms held out to me. I grabbed my coat, pulled my boots on in a panic, and banged on the door of her second floor back room, hands shaking.
“Who’s there?” her sharp voice called.
“It’s me! Paul.”
“Oh, wait a minute, dear!” Her voice went all sweet and syrupy. There was a sound of scurrying feet, drawers banging shut, rustling fabric, then she called out “You can come in now, dear.”
I walked in. The small room smelled of smoky lamp, and all I could see at first was Rosina, standing with her arms over her head, pinning her hat to her straw coloured hair.
And all of a sudden, every last bit of my gushy soft feeling died. She wasn’t even ugly, really, but the whole room, her whole vibe, reeked of that cheap common coarseness that made me cringe. The same finicky snobbery that made me send a plate of chops back if the waitress who served them had dirty fingernails was now fixated entirely on her rough red hands, dotted with two or three little warts that were impossible to miss, and the caked-on powder she’d dabbed on her face in a hurry.
“You’re a very naughty boy,” she said, finishing pinning her hat on. “Why didn’t you come visit me at dinner time? I’ve half a mind not to kiss you.”
I prayed she’d skip the kiss, but no, she leaned in and pressed her flowery, powdery cheek to mine. It tasted exactly like those pale, thin, cardboardy dough cakes the school porter’s wife used to sell us at four a penny back when I was a kid.
We trundled down the stairs to the kitchen, where she paused and yelled shrilly for Mrs Peedles. “Me and Mr Kelver are just going for a little walk, I won’t need any supper tonight! Good night!”
“Oh good night dear,” Mrs Peedles panted. “Hope you two have fun. Is Mr Kelver there?”
“He’s just round the corner being shy,” Rosina sniggered.
“Aww, the shy ones are always the wildest once you get them home, that’s my experience,” Mrs Peedles whispered back.
We headed out down Blackfriars Road, and she immediately slipped her arm through mine. “I’ve never liked flirty boys,” she explained. “That’s why I picked you right off – you were so quiet, and everyone could tell you were a proper gentleman. I’ve had hundreds of offers,

“Quit jittering,” O’Kelly told me, patting my shoulder like I was a spooked cart pony, “and don’t try to blow the rafters off. You’ve got a perfectly serviceable little tenor, but it’s not loud enough to rouse a napping seagull. Keep your head on, open your mouth like you’re yawning at a bad sermon, and you’ll be grand.”

It was 11 a.m., and we were huddled in the mouth of a narrow, smelly alley that led to the theatre’s stage door. For two whole weeks O’Kelly had been drilling me, an experience that had been far more stressful for him than it was for me. His wife, Mrs. O’Kelly, was a thin, sharp-faced woman I’d only caught glimpses of from Belsize Square while loitering waiting for O’Kelly’s secret signal, and she was dead set against any music that wasn’t hymn-adjacent. In a well-meaning attempt to yank O’Kelly away from his “sinful” love of secular tunes, she’d hauled their perfectly good piano out to be sold and replaced it with an American organ that sounded like a grieving walrus being stepped on.

That was the only instrument we had to practice on. And while O’Kelly, who was a proper musical genius, had wrangled that droning beast into accompanying “Sally in Our Alley” without making it sound like a funeral march for a dead goldfish, he’d only barely succeeded. By the end of our two weeks of drills, my version of the sweet old ballad was so mournful you’d leave the room wondering if the groom-to-be had a secret opium habit and Sally was already planning to elope with the village baker. You could practically hear the audience whispering: “This engagement is not going to end well.”

“Lighter! Less woe-is-me, you daft lad!” O’Kelly would beg, as the organ wheezed and groaned like it was begging for a mercy killing.

Once we nearly got caught red-handed, when Mrs. O’Kelly came home early from her district visitors’ committee meeting. O’Kelly stuffed me behind a stack of geranium pots in the tiny first-floor conservatory, and I crouched there shaking while she grilled him like a suspect at a police station.

“William, don’t lie to me. That was not a hymn.”
“Ah, darlin’, so much depends on the rhythm—let me show ye what I mean—”
“William, stop making a mockery of sacred music. And I distinctly heard the word ‘Sally’ when I walked in the door.”
“Salvation, me love! I was singing about Salvation!”
“Your enunciation is never that muddled, William.”
“Bit of a sore throat, pet. All that hymn-singing wears a man out.”
“Your voice sounded perfectly fine. Let’s just drop this subject.”

O’Kelly was only too happy to agree. “She’s a bit of a tough nut when you’re fit as a fiddle,” he explained later, wiping sweat off his brow, “but if you’re sick? She’s the tenderest nurse that ever lived. When I had typhoid three years back, she didn’t sleep more than two hours a night looking after me. Never saw anything like it. Trouble is, I’m such a annoyingly healthy bastard she never gets the chance to dote on me. If I could just be a chronic invalid, she’d wait on me hand and foot. As it is—” He plonked a discordant chord on that wretched organ, and we went back to singing about Sally.

Back to that morning outside the stage door. “Meet me at the Cheshire Cheese at one,” O’Kelly said, shaking my hand. “If this gig doesn’t work out, we’ll find you something else. I already talked to Hodgson, the manager, and he’s half expecting you. Good luck!”

He trundled off, and I pushed through the stage door, where a tiny round-eyed man with a nose curved like a parrot’s beak was stuck in a glass booth, looking like an angry sparrow in a cage. He squawked at me to state my business, I showed him my letter of audition, and he spat out directions so fast I almost choked: “Up the passage, across the stage, down the corridor, first floor, second door right!” and slammed his window shut so hard the glass rattled.

I trundled along the passage, and to my shock I wasn’t even a little bit starstruck. It reminded me of a story my dad once told me, about a young Crimean War soldier he’d met who got asked what he thought about his first ever battle charge. The lad said all he could think about the whole time was that he’d left the canteen beer tap running, and he couldn’t get the thought out of his head. That was me: I was too busy worrying I’d forget the words to Sally to be impressed by backstage glitz. All the theatre magic evaporated the second I stepped onto the boards. The whole thing was just a grown-up kid’s dollhouse: chipped paint, wobbly flats, costumes that looked like they’d been cobbled together out of old curtains. Not that I’m knocking theatre! It’s a lovely thing, but it’s still just a fancy toy, same as dressing dolls and putting them to bed in their little dollhouse rooms. It’s all practice for when we finally grow up, isn’t it?

I got lost in the maze of hallways backstage, but eventually followed the sound of chattering voices to a big bare room full of 20 or 30 people sitting on beat-up settees. They were all different ages and sizes, but every single one of them had that unmistakeable theatrical glow: the men had no facial hair and way too much hair on their heads, the women all had impossibly pink-and-white complexions and eyes so bright they looked lit from inside. Everyone called each other “my love” and “old chappie,” like they were all related, and I nearly apologized for walking in on a family reunion until I realized no one cared. I squeezed into an empty seat next to a tiny, boyish-looking man with big fish-like eyes, and asked him if this was the waiting room for new chorus members for the upcoming comic opera.

He stared at me up and down for so long that a giant broad-shouldered bloke sitting nearby, who’d overheard me, took it upon himself to answer. “Aye, it is. And that arsehole Butterworth who’s supposed to be auditioning us is keeping us waiting for no damn reason. Bloody rude, if you ask me.”

The tiny fish-eyed man turned to him, his voice a deep rumbling contralto. “I think it’s exceedingly bad manners for a gentleman to butt in and answer a question that was directed specifically at me.”
“Sorry, mate. I thought you were asleep.”
“And it’s even worse manners to tell a perfectly awake man you thought he was asleep.”
“Sir,” the big guy said, propping himself up on his giant umbrella like he was Samuel Johnson himself, “I’m not rearranging my entire personality to suit your weird little rules of etiquette.”
“Fair enough,” the tiny man said. “But you’re still making a fool of yourself.”

“Does anyone even know what this comic opera is supposed to be about?” a bright little lady at the other end of the room piped up.
“Has anyone ever known what a comic opera is about?” an older, world-weary woman shot back.
“I once asked the author of the last one I was in,” a tired-looking man in the corner said. “He told me, ‘If you’d asked me at the start of rehearsals I might’ve been able to tell you, but I’ll be damned if I can remember now.’”
“Bet there’s a drinking chorus for blokes somewhere in it,” a good-looking guy in a velvet coat said.
“And if we’re real lucky,” added the thin blonde lady, “the heroine will pour her whole sad love life out to us, and we’ll all gasp and go ‘ooooh’ like we care.”

The door popped open, and a name got called. An older lady everyone called Gertie got up and left. “Poor old Gertie,” the blonde lady said sympathetically. “I heard she once had a lovely voice, back in the day.”
“Reminds me,” the big guy next to me said, “of when young Bond first came to London. He applied to Alexander Lee, the guy who ran the Strand Theatre, for a chorus spot. Lee heard him sing two lines and jumped up, said ‘Thanks, that’s all, goodbye.’ Bond knew his voice was good, so he asked Lee what was wrong. Lee yelled at him: ‘Do you think I hire a chorus to make my principals sound like tone-deaf donkeys?’”
“Given the company we’re in right now,” the fish-eyed man said coldly, “that anecdote is in terrible taste.” Everyone else nodded along like they agreed.

For the next half hour the door kept opening and closing, swallowing people one by one until the room got quieter and quieter, everyone’s nerves getting thicker than cigarette smoke. Finally, a voice yelled: “MR. HORACE MONCRIEFF!” We all glanced around to see who that poor sod was, until it hit me like a brick: that was me.

“Glad you finally remembered who you are,” the pale-faced young man who was calling names said, when I stumbled over to the door. “Try not to lose yourself again, we’re running behind schedule.”

He led me past an empty, sad-looking refreshment bar where the once-fresh sandwiches were all curled up, into a room where a miserable-looking man was slouched at the piano. Next to him was tall, handsome Mr. Hodgson, the theatre manager, who was flipping through a huge stack of letters and never once looking up. A big burly guy who later turned out to be the opera’s “English adapter” (read: guy who translated the French libretto and changed three jokes to make them worse) was trying very hard not to laugh at the theatre’s lead low comedian, who had a weird habit of never looking at the person he was talking to—his eyes were always darting somewhere else, like he was looking for a lost cockroach.

“Moncrieff?” Hodgson said, still staring at his letters. The pale guy nodded for me.
“Go on, then,” Hodgson said.
“What’s your song?” the sad pianist mumbled.
“‘Sally in Our Alley,’” I said.
“What are you?” Hodgson asked, still not looking up.
“Tenor,” I said, remembering O’Kelly’s instructions. “Not a *loud* tenor, though.”
“Terrible idea to ever hire a full tenor for chorus, am I right?” the restless-eyed comedian said, staring right at me while talking to the burly adapter. “Have you ever even tried to fit one?” Everyone laughed, except the sad pianist, who huffed out a single mechanical “ha” without cracking a smile.

“Alright, off you go,” the pianist said, and started the wheezy accompaniment.
When I finished the first verse, Hodgson didn’t even glance up. “Tell me when he’s supposed to start singing, would you?”
“He’s got a nice little voice,” the pianist said, “he’s just nervous.”
“Audiences tend to prefer voices they can actually hear,” Hodgson said. “That’s all I’m getting at.”

I belted out the second verse like I was trying to yell at a ship three miles out at sea. The burly adapter said he could definitely hear me, the comedian joked we’d have to put out a notice for the audience to shut up while I sang. Suddenly my voice decided it was too shy to perform, and it vanished right down into my boots, like a skittish cat hiding under the sofa. I was panicking so hard I thought I’d pass out.

“Your voice is great, it’s fine,” the pianist whispered, patting my arm. “Just belt the last bit out as hard as you can.”

My voice immediately zoomed back up my throat and out my mouth. “Thirty shillings a week, half pay for rehearsals,” Hodgson said, still scribbling notes on his letters. “If that’s alright, Catchpole here will give you your contract. If not, thanks for stopping by.”

I signed the papers in 30 seconds flat, the comedian called me Boanerges (which means “sons of thunder,” apparently) and wished me good luck, and I stumbled back out onto the street grinning like an idiot. My first ever theatre gig!

Our first rehearsal call was the following Monday at 2 p.m. The whole theatre was buzzing. The principals had just finished their own run-through, chatting in a huddle off to the side. Over a chipped plate of sausages and stale bread that night, Dan and I spilled our respective life stories, and found we’d got more in common than we’d guessed. Dan had bailed out of school years before me, trotted off to Cambridge, and two years later his college career got cut short when his dad died – the old man had never properly recovered from a Mutiny wound picked up in India.
“You’d think I’d have been glad to ditch all the tedious lectures, right?” Dan said, grinning into his beer. “But it knocked me for six. Turns out I’m a born bookworm, and school had spent all those years making learning so mind-numbingly boring I’d never noticed. They herded us through the curriculum like a Cook’s tourist party being rushed through the National Gallery – the goal wasn’t to make us understand a single thing, just to make sure we could lie and say we’d ‘done’ the whole syllabus. At Cambridge I picked my own subjects, studied them my way. I fed on knowledge, not got force-fed it like a Strassburg goose being fattened for pâté.”
Back in London he’d landed a bank job, courtesy of his dad’s old mate who was chairman there. It paid just enough to keep him in tea and library fines, and left him spare time to study – but the endless, mind-numbing shuffling of slips of paper in a city counting house nearly drove him round the bend. The second his mother died, he shook the city dust out of his hair and joined a ramshackle travelling theatre troupe. He stuck it out 18 months, played every part going from Romeo to bumbling old Paul Pry, helped paint the scenery, and once spent three hours chasing a stray poster that kept flying back and sticking to his head. He swore billposting is the single most difficult art form ever invented by mankind, second only to making a decent cup of tea on a camp stove.
When he got bored of the stage he joined a circus, went by “Signor Ricardo, the fearless bareback equestrian” (he once fell off mid-performance and landed in the band’s tuba), and was one half of the “Daring Brothers Roscius” trapeze act (neither of the other two lads were named Roscius). Then he got a sudden hankering for respectability, waited tables in Ostend for three months, then took a job as a footman in a country house.
“Never know when these odd jobs’ll come in handy,” Dan mused. “If I ever get round to writing that high society novel everyone’s snapping up these days, I won’t be making up all the butler gossip from guesswork like every other Fleet Street hack. I’ll have the edge.”
He’d tried half a dozen other lines of work too: tramped around Ireland with a fiddle playing jigs for farmers’ pennies, toured Scotland giving a talk about Palestine with a set of half-broken magic lantern slides, worked as a billiards marker, had a short, chaotic stint as a schoolmaster, and for the last three months he’d been drama and music critic for a Sunday paper – the exact job I’d daydreamed about a hundred times.
“How on earth did you land that?” I asked, awestruck.
“Pure dumb luck,” Dan said. “I was sauntering down the Strand one afternoon skint, three shillings in my pocket, wondering if I’d have to go back to circus work. I walked into the first newspaper office I saw, marched up the first flight of stairs, opened the first unlocked door, and wandered straight into the editorial room. It was getting dark, and a tiny, baby-faced 26 year old bloke was sprinting around the place swearing his head off. He’d spilled the ink, lost all the matches, and snapped the bell pull clean off. I thought he was the office boy, and teased him a bit about it – turns out he was the editor. The only reason he had the job was his aunt owned the paper. I lit the gas for him, found him a new ink pot, and he immediately started complaining that every random hobo with a pamphlet on safety valves kept wandering straight up the stairs to pester him.
“Just then someone knocked on the door. The editor screamed that he wouldn’t see anyone, send every last pest away. So I went out to the ante-room, and for the next half hour I sent 12 very confused people packing. The last bloke turned up smoking a cigarette, looked at me like I was daft when I asked his name, and said ‘Idiot’. I almost wrote that down on the visitor’s slip before he started laughing and said he was the sub-editor, and it was press night, and he needed to know who was going to review the show at the Royalty that evening.
“Lightbulb moment! If the sub doesn’t know who’s supposed to go to the theatre, that means the drama critic slot is empty. I told him I was the new guy, grabbed the theatre ticket off the editor’s desk without asking, and went off to the show. No one’s ever figured out I appointed myself. The sub thinks I’m a distant nephew of the proprietress, the aunt thinks I’m the sub’s brilliant discovery, and everyone treats me like I’m made of gold. The salary’s tiny, but it’s enough. Rolling stone that I am, I’ve never seen the point of getting smothered in moss anyway. Much more fun staying loose enough to wander around and see stuff.”
Then he pitched his idea. Our current digs were garbage, and there were four perfect rooms in Queen’s Square going cheap. The last tenant had been a Polish revolutionary who’d stupidly decided to sneak back to Russia three months prior, and now he was getting free year-round accommodation in a Siberian camp. The landlord was Deleglise, a weird old engraver who lived in the rest of the house, who hated all elderly female domestic servants with a fiery passion. He and his 70 year old French housekeeper bickered 12 hours a day over who left the burnt toast on the counter, and he wouldn’t even let a charwoman step over his threshold. No landlady breathing down our necks, no one else’s piano thumping through the walls, the whole second floor to ourselves – for 30 shillings a week, half what it should have cost.
“I’m a decent cook,” Dan said. “Will you be our joint house-parlour-maid?”
I didn’t need any convincing. A week later we’d moved in, and we stayed there nearly two years. I know all the ladies reading will gasp and say two men left to their own devices can’t even put themselves to bed without making a mess, but we survived. No maids, no supervision, zero domestic female interference, and we never once starved. Dan could knock up a perfect omelette, a devilled kidney, a cup of coffee so good it’d make a bishop weep. The only problem was, every time we had guests over, he’d get overambitious and try fancy cooking he’d never done before.
His lemon sponge – which the cookbook promised would be a light, snow-white cloud of sweetness – came out looking like a miniature collapsed sand dune. It tore with a sad wet cardboard noise when you poked it with a fork. His famous manna cake was so dense and stodgy, one slice would keep you full for 12 hours. If the Israelites had been forced to eat that every day, I completely get why they were crying for Egyptian leeks. Moses must have had a cast iron stomach. His trifles weren’t even remotely light as air, his soufflés all our guests kept mistaking for plain steamed pudding, and his early attempts at pancake tossing were a public danger. Once he flung a pancake so high it stuck to the ceiling for three days before we noticed.
Dan was the most even-tempered man alive 23 hours a day. That one remaining hour? He turned into a raving lunatic. The oven was his sworn mortal enemy. Every time the bun came out hard as red sandstone, every time the custard got pimply, every time the mayonnaise tasted like hair oil, it was 100% the oven’s fault, and he’d yell at it so loud the neighbours banged on the ceiling. If I so much as offered a helpful tip, he’d hurl cold coffee grounds or a spoiled tomato at my head until I ran out of the kitchen.
For my part, I was a model housekeeper. I’ve always held that dust is a lovely delicate homey bloom, not something to be stirred up and spread all over the house. Leave it alone, it sits quiet and harmless on the bookshelf, no trouble at all. All the crumbs, cigarette butts, and loose bits of string go under the rug – perfect compromise, no one has to sweep them. Why wash a clean plate the cat has already licked spotless? Why make a bed that’s just going to get slept in again the same night? Women keep missing this obvious hack, and spend 10 times more work than they ever need to.
Needless to say I was terrible at needlework. Dan could fumble with a thimble, but I always had to hunt for ten minutes for the right finger to stick it on, and then it would slip and I’d prick myself half to death, so eventually I just started pushing needles through fabric using the edge of the door.
As Dan predicted, our comic opera ran all through winter, then got another run the next year, and our mate “Goggles” the comic even got a lead role. My salary went up to 35 shillings, sometimes 40 a week – I felt like a millionaire. I daydreamed I’d be a world-famous opera star one day, on 600 pounds a week, easily. Then old O’Kelly the singing teacher burst that bubble for me.
“Ye’re wasting your time, lad,” he said, patting my shoulder. “Ye’ve got a voice that’s thin enough to strain pasta through. Great for comic opera, sure, but grand opera? Not a chance.”
No bother, I thought. I could be a famous actor instead, the next Kean. Or write epoch-making novels in the mornings, run for parliament when I got bored of art, end up Prime Minister. Or maybe join the diplomatic service, retire as an ambassador. I was learning French and German at the museum, I could do anything.
And then, the best thing that ever happened to 22 year old me: my first short story got accepted for publication. I’d had a story fall through before, when the paper folded before it could print it, so this time I didn’t tell anyone, just snuck out every morning for weeks on publication day to hunt for it. Until one grey January day, I walked past a tiny newsagent on the Harrow Road, and saw the poster stuck to his window: *THE WITCH OF MOEL SARBOD: A LEGEND OF MONA, BY PAUL KELVER*. My actual name, in actual print.
My legs shook so bad I could barely walk into the shop. The grumpy bloke behind the counter fished a copy out from under a pile of other papers on the floor. I sat down on a bench in Paddington Green and read the whole 12 pages there, in the cold. The story was ridiculous, full of unpronounceable Welsh names I’d dug out of dusty lexicons at the British Museum, about a princess named Purity who kisses an evil witch to save her enchanted king, and ends up trapped as a witch on the mountain forever. My friends later told me it was garbage, that the prose was a weird cross between Pilgrim’s Progress and Old Moore’s Almanac, that the scenery was copied straight out of a local tourist guide.
I didn’t care. That tattered, badly printed story was my first baby. I held it in my hands and felt like the whole world was wide open. I’d be famous, people in countries I’d never visit would read my work, generations yet unborn would remember my name. That morning, standing in the soft grey London fog, I was 100% sure the future had just turned up, and it was all mine.

Back when I was half-starving in my garret turning out dreary potboilers for Lott & Co. at three ha’pence a line, I steered well clear of old Hasluck, Barbara’s dad. The man was the jolliest, most generous soul alive, but he had zero patience for feelings he couldn’t wrap a slice of roast beef around. If he’d caught wind of my straits, he’d have clapped me on the back, stuffed a five-pound note in my pocket, and set me up behind a desk at his warehouse before I could stammer a single protest – and a man in the throes of ridiculous youthful romance will choose crusts and cold tea over a steady wage every time, if it means he doesn’t have to admit he’s struggling.

I can’t even explain the snobbery of it. Hasluck’s meat and tallow business was no more honest than Lott & Co.’s rag-and-bone hack writing racket. It wasn’t that he was Barbara’s father, neither. That good soul and his homely, loud-voiced wife might as well have been the park keeper and his charwoman for all connection I drew between them and Barbara. She wasn’t a girl who came from a Mile End Road butcher’s household. She was the sort of being that wandered out of forgotten Greek groves, hanging around marble shrines with winged sandals on her feet. There’s no accounting for that sort of instinct, you know. It’s the same instinct that makes you refuse to ask a bobby for directions, even when you’ve been lost in a suburb for two hours, poring over a waterlogged Continental Bradshaw that’s definitely got a misprint on page 472, just because you’re stubborn enough to swear you can work the route out all by yourself. I’ve no doubt one of my distant ancestors was a grumpy caveman who lived alone in his own hovel, made his own lumpy stone hammers, and skipped the tribe’s war dances just to caper all by himself behind a bush.

But once I’d actually sold a novel, got my name on a book cover, and could stand on my own two feet without looking like I was begging for a handout? I wrote to Hasluck that very week, and had his reply the next morning, scrawled in his messy handwriting, telling me Barbara was just back from four years traipsing around Europe, that she’d be over the moon to see me, and that his front door on Stamford Hill was propped wide open for me any hour of the day or night.

I’d never breathed a word to a single soul about that boyhood crush of mine, not even my mum. She liked Barbara well enough, but she’d chatter on about how the girl needed to stop leaving her bonnet strings untied and eat more bread and dripping, the way you’d chat about the new girl next door. It was like listening to someone discuss the flaws in the architecture of your private holy shrine, and it hurt worse than stepping on a drawing pin in bare feet. Most nights after work, I’d wander all the way up Stamford Hill, loop around the big red brick house hidden behind its overgrown garden walls, hover like a lost ghost by the iron gate for half an hour, then trudge back down to my garret feeling like I’d just been to church. Once a copper stopped me, thought I was casing the silver, and marched me halfway to the police station before I mumbled something about visiting a holy site. He told me to “go visit it on a Sunday like a normal Christian” and sent me on my way.

Four years of Barbara being gone had turned that silly boyhood crush into something even dafter. I’d built her up in my head into a vision so perfect she was more real to me than the actual scuffed-knee girl I’d grown up playing with. The idea of getting close enough to see her with hair in curl papers, or hear her complain about a chilblain on her heel, was enough to make me shudder. I didn’t want to kiss her, or take her to the music hall, or have her be my wife. That was far too vulgar. I just wanted to worship her, from a respectable distance, like she was a stained-glass window. I didn’t even get jealous thinking about her marrying someone else. My little temple I’d built around her was mine, no one else could get in there to mess it up.

I waited a full week after getting Hasluck’s letter before I turned up at the house, just to seem nonchalant. It was a golden late afternoon in June. The footman was a supercilious chap who looked like he’d never touched a mutton chop in his life, and when he told me Barbara was out in the garden I waved him off, saying I’d find her myself. I knew exactly where she’d be: that sunny nook at the bottom of the garden, surrounded by yew hedges clipped into lopsided Dutch-style animals, half of which looked more like confused geese than anything else.

She was there, reading a book, exactly as I’d pictured. For a second I forgot how to breathe. She was taller now, carried herself like a queen, not the scruffy 14-year-old I’d first seen hanging around my dad’s office. I stood there in the shadow of the yew for ten minutes, staring like an idiot, before she turned round and spotted me. I didn’t even think about it. I walked over, knelt down on the path, and kissed her outstretched hand like I was a medieval knight greeting his liege lady.

She laughed, not unkind, with just a flicker of amusement at how much of a dolt I looked. “So you’re still in love with me, Paul? I wondered if you’d be.”

“Wait – you knew about that? Back when I was just a spotty 16-year-old?” I spluttered.

“I’d have to have been as blind as a bat not to,” she said. “You kept bringing me dandelions you’d picked from the side of the road and blushing every time I said hello. Hardly subtle.”

We chatted, and I told her exactly how I felt, all daft and flowery: how I wanted to be her loyal courtier, never ask for her love, just pick up the gloves she dropped, kneel at her feet, be her obedient servant for ever. She laughed, and decided that was a splendid idea, that she’d be my queen, she’d rule over me, and nothing she ever did could possibly be wrong. I agreed, of course. I’d have agreed that two plus two made seven if my queen had told me so.

She then broke the news she was going to marry a Spanish count, who was as broke as only a grand Spanish nobleman can be, who’d give her the title and half-ruined mountain castle she wanted, and her dad would pay all the bills. She didn’t love him, she said. Queens don’t do love matches, they do political alliances. Then she dropped the real bombshell: the man she actually did love was Hal, that big lunk we’d both known since we were kids. The one who’d got locked up in Paris with her during the siege.

She made me promise I’d think her choice was right, that I’d never stop worshipping her, and I did. I never did think less of her, even years later, when I ran into Hal half-dead in that absurd alpine hotel up in the Julier Pass. The room had once been a chapel, had a giant green German stove that was so overheated my moustache started to steam the second I walked in. He was propped up in bed, coughing his lungs out, and he told me the whole sordid, sad, silly story: how they’d run off together, how it had all gone wrong, how he’d finally let her go back to the count when he saw she was miserable eating cheap claret and burnt mutton chops, how neither of them had been the hero or the villain, just two daft fools who’d forgotten that grand love poetry doesn’t pay the rent or cure chilblains.

By the end of it all, the only thing I could think was that love, for all its grand glory, is mostly just a lot of very human, very silly mistakes, and I wouldn’t trade a single second of it for all the boring, sensible, non-romantic happiness in the world.

Old Deleglise’s Sunday suppers are already well-trodden ground for memoir hounds, so I’ll skip the flowery overviews you’ve no doubt read elsewhere. Picture the man himself, tricked out head to toe in pristine linen, red-faced and dead serious as he maneuvers around his gleaming cast-iron stove, while his guests hover round a massive oak table stacked so high with cut glass and silver that old Madeline, his white-capped housekeeper, flutters like a distracted sparrow trying to polish all the spots. The crowd that waited for Deleglise to haul out his steaming latest culinary triumph? Writers, actors, painters, musicians, even the occasional unapologetic professional jester. The second the great man turned back to the room beaming, holding a savory roast that smelled better than a summer morning, the hush of whispers would burst into a roar of laughter loud enough to rattle the windowpanes. Their witticisms have all been quoted in a hundred biographies by now, and if their old jokes feel a little flat to you? That’s not their fault—humor goes out of style faster than last year’s top hat.

I won’t bore you rehashing second-hand anecdotes you can find elsewhere. What matters for this story are the chaotic afternoon At Homes Dan and I hosted on the second floor of our creaky old Georgian house on quiet, pleasant Queen Square. To make sure the day didn’t end in total disaster, we came up with a brilliant, foolproof system: we were each the other’s sole inspector. I judged Dan’s cooking, and Dan judged my housework.

“Too much soda,” I’d declare, prodding the cake he’d set down in front of me.
“You absolute mutt!” he’d squawk. “It’s a soda cake! It’s supposed to taste of soda.”
“I know what it’s called,” I’d say, holding firm. “It’s just not supposed to taste like nothing but soda. This thing’s 90% fizz, no cake at all. You bite into it and it’s basically a solidified Seidlitz powder. You can’t serve people soda pop masquerading as cake for afternoon tea!”

Dan would fume so hard his ears turned pink, but that soda cake got tossed straight in the bin. His cooking was the one thing he’d fight to the death defending, no matter how bad it was. He’d eat his most catastrophic failures later when no one was looking, pretending they were the greatest thing he’d ever tasted. I watched him once polish off a sponge cake so undercooked the center was runny custard, raving the whole time that dry sponge cake was just overbaked rubbish, that good sponge was *supposed* to be syrupy. Ten minutes later he was sat in the corner silently chugging brandy out of a wine glass like he’d just survived a poison attack.

Then it would be Dan’s turn to pick at my work. “These knives aren’t clean,” he’d say, swiping his finger across the blade to leave a perfect grey smudge.
“They are clean! You just dragged your grubby finger over it!” I’d protest.
“All you did was wipe the crumbs off,” he’d argue. “And besides, we don’t even have knife polish.”
“Sure we do, the tin’s under the sink,” he’d say. “Also, over-polishing ruins knives! Takes all the edge right off. We’re gonna need these sharp to cut through the lemon buns you baked that are harder than paving stones.”
“Over-cleaning! You don’t take any pride in this flat at all!” he’d whine.
“Good Lord, I work from dawn till dusk!” I’d yell back. “Your cooking makes everyone lazy, it gives you indigestion bad enough you can barely stand up!”

But Dan wouldn’t stop nagging until I dragged the knife polish out and scrubbed every last blade until they shone bright enough to use as mirrors. It wasn’t all bad, I suppose—once a week the whole flat actually got properly tidied up, which was more than it got the rest of the time. After lunch we’d send the cook and house maid off for the rest of the afternoon, leaving the two of us, dressed in our best stiff collars, to wait for guests.

Our crowd was half Dan’s journalist mates, half my theatre friends. Sometimes Minikin and Jarman would tag along, and even Mrs Peedles would turn up once in a while, red-faced and breathless from running up three flights of stairs. Left to my own devices I never would have invited them—they ran a shabby lodging house in Blackfriars, for goodness sake, I thought they’d stick out like sore thumbs next to our fancy writer and actor friends. But Dan, the second he met them, brushed all my objections right off.

“My dear stuffy old Lord Chamberlain,” he’d say, grinning, “one ounce of actual weird, unscripted originality is worth a ton of boring, predictable convention. You can find a hundred perfectly proper, cookie-cutter middle-class couples sipping tea all over London. These three? They’d make a party memorable. Here’s the plan: we never mention Mrs Peedles runs a lodging house. We’ll introduce her as *Lady P*, the dear, droll old City widow whose husband got knighted in some crowd ceremony and forgot to tell anyone. Her funny anecdotes about loud third-rate actors? They’re stories about Earl Blank and the Baroness de Dash. Jarman? He’s the exiled heir to a South American blacking fortune, rolling in cash. Minikin? He’s a world-famous German professor of chemistry. That weird glass eye he has? He lost it in an experiment. His rudeness? That’s the famous tortured genius edge everyone adores. We’ll even get him a yard of bright red ribbon for fourpence at the haberdashery to wear across his shirt front, and call him Herr Professor. Everyone will just write off his bad grammar and weird accent as ‘authentic foreign charm’. They’ll be our party lions, I swear. We’ll invite all the boring middle-class schmucks to meet them.”

And Dan actually went through with it, much to my constant quiet panic. Jarman ate the bit up, and every single guest was completely fooled. The more he swaggered, the more he bragged about his imaginary mines and plantations, the more impressed everyone was. The journalists would crowd around him, waving their half-baked plans for new newspapers that they swore would get 300,000 readers overnight if he just tossed a couple thousand pounds at it, and I had to clamp a hand over my mouth not to laugh. But when I watched all the actresses from my theatre troop flock around him, luring him into corners, batting their eyelashes, and even pulling each other’s bonnet ribbons fighting over who got to sit next to him, my conscience pricked me. I was terrified the whole joke was going to blow up in our faces. Luckily Jarman was far more sensible than most rich people (a fact that makes perfect sense now he wasn’t actually rich) and he never got swept off his feet. He laughed his way through the whole winter of being fussed over, and eventually married the plain, nice eldest daughter of a small blacking factory owner. Now he’s fat, pink-cheeked, and the proud owner of that same factory, and every time we run into each other he looks like the happiest man alive on God’s green earth.

Minikin was far more work. Every once in a while a guest would turn up who actually knew a little bit of science, so we had to explain the Professor *never* talked shop at parties. Someone else would know German and try to practice with him, so we said he’d sworn a vow not to speak a word of his native tongue for his whole stay in London, to practice his English. People kept remarking that his knowledge of British working class slang was shockingly good for a foreigner. The worst was when he’d be outright rude: once a lady asked him what he thought of English women, and he said he thought most of them were brain-dead silly. A friend of Dan’s who was a pretty big editor pointed at his red ribbon and asked what honor it was for, and Minikin said “I got this for fourpence down at the shop on the corner.” We had to cobble together a backstory that he’d been heartbroken by a faithless lover years ago, and everyone sighed and nodded and forgave him immediately. The ladies thought he was tragic, the men joked that the girl must have been an idiot to turn down someone with a fourpenny ribbon. Minikin took a *lot* of explaining.

Lady Peedles, meanwhile, everyone decided must be the widow of some very important City knight. They snickered at her behind her back, but to her face they were practically fawning over her, calling her “my dear Lady Peedles” every five minutes. Soon “my friend Lady Peedles” was a phrase everyone at the theatre tossed around loudly, like they’d known her their whole lives. My own social stock went through the roof when everyone decided I was her presumptive heir, so people kept bringing me free drinks and inviting me to dinners hoping I’d put in a good word for them.

The fishy-eyed little comic actor was another regular at our afternoons. Dan won his undying loyalty just by never laughing at him when he spoke, which was a new experience for the poor guy.
“I like talking to you,” he told Dan one afternoon. “Everyone else bursts out cackling if I so much as say it’s a nice day. On stage I don’t mind— I’m paid to be funny. I saw a workman stop at my poster in the theatre lobby last week, right? He looked at it, and burst out laughing so hard he could barely breathe, going ‘That little nutter just makes me laugh even looking at him!’ That’s fine! I want the gallery guys to think I’m funny. But off stage? It drives me mad. If I’m at a dinner and ask someone to pass the mustard, they never hand it over, they just start cackling. I don’t want to be laughed at while I’m eating my dinner! I just want my mustard! Sometimes it makes me so angry I could throw the table over.”

“I get it,” Dan said, all sympathy. “The world never remembers that a person is a whole collection of things, not just the one bit everyone likes best. I was at a dinner once once, and the guest of honor was this super famous Hebrew scholar. At tea time, a girl called Miss Henman dropped a pat of butter on the floor. The great scholar boomed out ‘Why is Miss Henman like a caterpillar? Because she makes the butter fly!’ And no one could believe the old serious guy had told a pun. Dead silence for a full minute, then the hostess said all grave ‘Oh, do you really think that?’”

“If I walked into a room full of people and told them my mother had been run over by an omnibus,” the little actor said, “they’d all think it was the funniest story they’d heard all year.” He was playing the lead in the hit opera that season, pulling in all the crowds, but he was miserable. “I’m not even a comic actor at heart!” he sighed. “I could play Romeo, I’ve got all the feeling for it, I’d do it *so* well. There’s all this poetry in my head. But what’s the point, with this stupid face of mine that makes people laugh the second they see it?”

“Hey now, none of that,” Dan said. “Paul here’s got the same problem. At school all he ever wanted to do was be good at football, the same as every other lunkhead, but he couldn’t kick a ball three feet without tripping over it. Everyone loved him because he could tell a story better than anyone else in the whole school, but instead of writing the funny stories God clearly intended him to write, he’s writing these dreary grim dramas about sad princesses trapped in Welsh caves. It’s not bad, but a hundred other people could write that stuff better.”
“ You don’t get it,” the little man said, shaking his head. “The worst, most terrible tragedian that ever lived would never swap places with the best clown on the stage. The court jester got a great salary, sure, and was probably twice as smart as every soldier in the palace. But every single one of those oafish guards looked down on him. Every bloke in the gallery who pays a shilling to laugh at me thinks he’s smarter than me. But he’d worship the ground some terrible blank verse spouter walked on.”
“Does it actually matter what some bloke in the gallery thinks of you?” Dan asked.
“Of course it does!” the little man said. “Because we think the same thing! If I could make five pounds a week playing the juvenile lead, I’d never put on a scratch wig and do a pratfall again.”
“I don’t buy that,” Dan said. “We’re all entertainers, same troupe. One guy makes you shiver with ghost stories, one guy makes you laugh so hard your sides hurt. The tragedian shows you how wicked you are, the clown shows you how ridiculous you are. Both lessons matter. Which one’s the more important? No one can say.”
“Ah, I’m no philosopher,” the little man sighed.
“Me neither,” Dan said, grinning. “And I’m no comic actor about to get a hundred quid a week. We all just want the other bloke’s cake, don’t we?”

O’Kelly was another permanent fixture. He’d had to give up his old attic in Belsize Square, no matter how much incense he burned, how many times he claimed the whole building was full of rats and bad drains. His wife was too sharp to fool.
“That woman’s a miracle,” O’Kelly would groan, half admiring half miserable. “You can’t trick her for a second.”
“Why don’t you just stand up to her?” we’d say. “Just say you’re going to smoke, and do it.”
“Ah, it’s her whole theory, lads,” he’d explain. “She says the home’s a temple, that if we keep it pure it’ll eventually turn me into a proper decent man. It’s a beautiful idea, if you think about it.” But he kept a special pipe and his own favorite tobacco jar in the houses of six different smoking friends across London, and one sat permanently on our mantelpiece.

By spring, temptation was gnawing at his very good but very wobbly resolve. The Signora, the famous tightrope dancer he’d been mad about years ago, was back in England performing at Ashley’s Theatre. O’Kelly would sit in silence for ages, puffing so hard at his pipe you could barely see him through the smoke, or go off on long rants about how perfect his wife Mrs O’Kelly was. “If any woman in the world could turn me into a model husband,” he’d mumble, sounding like he didn’t quite believe it, “it’s that woman.”
It was a Saturday afternoon, I’d just got home from the matinee. “I swear,” O’Kelly went on, “I don’t think that woman has ever done a single wrong thing in her whole life, or missed a single good one.”
“Maybe she’s done little tiny wrong things you just don’t know about,” I suggested, thinking that might make him feel better.
“I wish I could believe that!” he said. “Not big wrong, mind. Just a little tiny bit naughty. I think I’d like her even more if she had. Don’t get me wrong, I respect that woman more than I can say. You don’t know her. A month ago, I was running late for our 7am prayers, right? Seven o’clock struck, and she still wasn’t up. For one unworthy second I was *glad*—I thought she’d finally overslept. I ran up to her room, and the door was wide open, her bed was already made. She walked in five minutes later. She’d got up at four o’clock that morning to go welcome a boatload of East African native missionaries arriving at Waterloo Station. She’s a saint. I don’t deserve her.”
“Look, don’t dwell on that,” I said.
“I can’t help it!” he moaned. “I never will be good enough for her.”

To distract him from this very dangerous line of thinking, I steered the conversation to champagne.
“Most folks prefer it dry,” O’Kelly said. “Me? I like it with just a little hint of fruit, nothing too sharp.”
“Hey, there was that champagne you were crazy for back when we were younger, right?” I said.
“I think I know the one,” he said. “It wasn’t half bad, for how cheap it was.”
“Do you remember where we got it?” I asked.
“Bridge Street,” he said. “Not far from the Circus.”
“It’s a lovely evening out,” I said. “Let’s go for a walk.”

We found the shop, half wine seller half office, and it hadn’t changed a bit. I ordered a bottle, we sat down. I called for a glass of claret, and O’Kelly drank the entire bottle. By the time he was done, he was glowing with righteous resolve, swearing he was going to be worthy of his saintly wife. Walking down the Embankment, he announced he was going to buy an alarm clock that very evening to make sure he never missed 7am prayers again. We got to the corner of Westminster Bridge, and he suddenly went completely still. I looked where he was staring, and there was a big poster of the Signora, standing on one leg on a tightrope high over drawn mountains, her face perfectly recognizable. I cursed my terrible luck, but O’Kelly just put a fatherly hand on my shoulder.
“Me dear Paul,” he said, all sincere and glowing, “there are the pretty laughing girls you want to dance with, and then there are the sainted women who lead us up. Always up.” He marched straight into the American general store nearby, and bought an alarm clock that the shopkeeper swore would wake an Egyptian mummy from the dead, for six shillings elevenpence. He waved me goodbye, hopped on the Hampstead bus, and I wandered back to the theatre alone.

My dad blew every penny he had buying me a fancy established practice, big house, carriage and pair, white-haired butler—everything perfect, except me. I quit London once with drums thumping in my chest and victory flags flapping around my brain, the sort of grand dramatic send-off a 20-something lad with delusions of knighthood and a play in his pocket swears he’s earned. A little over a year later, I slunk back on a foggy, drippy December evening, properly defeated — and that’s nothing, you can bounce back from a loss with a pint and a new plan. No, I was disgraced. The kind of disgraced you don’t just brush off. I returned to London like it was a big, overcrowded haystack I could lose myself in, planning to waste my unravelling days unnoticed until I could summon the nerve to finally put an end to the dead, useless life I’d accidentally built for myself.

I had been ambitious, back when I still thought I was clever. I’d wanted to make a name for myself, carve out a spot in the world — and not just any spot, mind you. I was going to be the good great man. The world wouldn’t just admire me, it would respect me. I’d be Galahad, not Launcelot: the knight without fear, and far rarer, without reproach. Turned out I was just a spineless, backboneless little fighter who folded the first time evil tapped him on the shoulder, a creature ruled by petty cowardice who jumped every time the devil cracked his tiny whip, happy enough to snuffle around the trough with the pigs.

I’d learned my “genius theatrical partner” Urban Vane was just a regular, unremarkable swindler. That “original play” of his? He’d nicked it straight off the desk of a well-known dramatist he’d met hanging around Deleglise’s kitchen. When the dramatist fell ill, Vane had popped round every other day with broth and flattery, and the second the poor guy left for Italy to recover (everyone thought he’d cark it there, which would’ve made the theft airtight), Vane swiped the whole manuscript. We were halfway through a run in some tiny northern town when word reached us: the guy wasn’t dead. He’d bounced back and was already on a boat home to England.

Vane broke the news to me over a bottle of wine, puffing on his cigar like he was telling me a funny anecdote about a stray cat he’d seen steal a fish off a market stall, not admitting we were both about to get caught for grand theft script. Six months of hanging around with Vane hadn’t quite killed off every last scrap of decency in me — thank the Lord for small mercies — but it had definitely wilted all its leaves, so I didn’t blow up nearly as hard as I should have.

The man was clever, in the way a guy who can lie his way out of a train fare is clever. It still baffles me that he didn’t work out earlier that honesty is the easiest scam for people with two brain cells to rub together. Last I heard he’s making thousands now running perfectly legitimate “respectable” businesses, the sort of upstanding citizen people invite to charity galas. Chicanery in the blood has to burn off eventually, I suppose. You meet Urban Vanes in every line of work: they start out as unapologetic scamps, then 10 years later you run into them and everyone’s calling them an ornament to their profession. We all sow our wild oats, it just depends what soil they land in.

At first I did storm, of course. Vane just sat there smiling like I was a toddler throwing a tantrum over a broken toy.

“Your language, my dear Kelver,” he said, once I’d run out of swear words, “might wound me if I considered you any sort of authority on this tedious morals business. Everyone preaches one thing and does the other, I’ve seen it a hundred times. It’s sad, sure, but no one’s sermons interest me anymore. You’re furious I stole another man’s ideas? It happens every single day. You were happy enough to take top billing on this play, weren’t you? Not 200 lines of it are yours, and they’re very good lines, but they don’t make a full five-act drama. Vane told me he didn’t want his own name on it, remember? Private family reasons, you said! You jumped at the chance to call yourself a famous playwright with zero effort.”

That angle had never occurred to me. “But you said it was translated from the French!” I stammered. “Adaptors put their own names to plays all the time, that’s just the industry custom!”

He waved cigar smoke in my face like I was being very silly. “Oh, I know. It’s always made me laugh. Our sick friend the dramatist we’re all so pleased is not dead? He’s done that exact thing at least twice, and made a pile of money off it. I’d bet good money this very play he wrote is mostly stolen from someone else too. What’s the difference between him nicking from a foreigner and me nicking from him? The only difference is the copyright laws are so daft stealing from a Frenchman is totally legal and everyone claps you for it, while stealing from a living Englishman makes people side-eye you over tea.”

“And the consequences are going to be nasty,” I said, panicking.

He laughed. “You mean the consequences for you, dear boy.”

I stared at him, dumbfounded.

“Don’t look so shocked,” he said, patting my shoulder. “You can’t be that thick. The original manuscript registered at the Lord Chamberlain’s office? All in your handwriting. You visited the sick playwright right alongside me. You’ve managed every single stop on this tour, all the contracts, all the bookings. No one’s going to believe you were just the stooge.”

All the anger drained right out of me, replaced by cold, dumb fear. “But you said it was just a formality! You said—”

He looked genuinely surprised I’d bought that line. That was the worst part. I suddenly felt like the biggest idiot in the whole north of England. “I’m sorry, Kelver. I genuinely thought you were a man of the world, and that you just chose not to ask questions.”

To my everlasting shame, fear was the only feeling in my whole body. “You’re not going to pin the whole thing on me?” I pleaded.

He stood up and gave my shoulder a friendly little squeeze. And I was grateful for it, the pathetic wretch — he was the only person I thought I could turn to for help.

“Don’t be daft, it’s not that bad,” he said. “He’ll just think he lost the manuscript. Half the writers I know can’t even remember what ideas they’ve had and what they’ve only thought about having. None of the cast are going to say anything, it’s not their problem. We’ll rewrite the whole thing in a fortnight. Change all the character names, keep the one scene that’s actually good, tweak every line so even if he saw it he’d just think we saw a snippet of his idea and beat him to staging it. No one will ever know.”

For a few weeks after that I kept hearing the voice of my last remaining good angel, telling me I should go find the dramatist, confess everything, take whatever lumps came to me. But Vane was glued to my elbow 24/7, dropping little reminders that if I ratted him out, he’d tell everyone I was the lying little thief who’d tried to frame him, and every theatre director in the country would blacklist me before I turned 25. And then he’d spin the other side: what harm were we actually doing? The play had only run in a handful of tiny towns where no one would remember it in six months. The original guy would put on his version and make a fortune, ours would vanish without a trace. The whole “world is fair and honest” thing was just a bunch of bourgeois conventions, anyway. The only real rule was don’t get caught.

So I caved. I fell right out of my own good graces, and I worked side by side with Vane to rewrite that stolen play, every line I wrote feeling like it was being dragged out of my skin. Even now I’m glad I didn’t enjoy a single second of it. I tried to beg Vane to let me write a completely new, original play from scratch, offered to give it to him for free, but he wouldn’t hear of it. “Later, dear boy, later. This one’s making us money right now, why fix what isn’t broken?”

Three weeks later we had a cobbled-together mess that was technically original as far as dialogue and characters went, and some of it was even decent. In any other circumstances I might have been proud of it. Instead, I felt like a burglar tiptoeing past a constable every single night. When the audience laughed at my jokes, I didn’t feel clever, I just felt like I’d cheated them. There was one grand, dramatic line the hero spits at the villain: “Yes, I love her. But there’s something I love far more — my own self-respect!” Our juvenile lead, a man who spent most of his time hiding from bailiffs sent after him by his very angry ex-wife for unpaid alimony, would yell that line while slapping his chest like it was a treasure chest full of virtues, and bring the house down every single night. Listening to the roaring applause, I’d sit in the wings and shudder, because I’d written that exact line while hiding under my desk terrified someone would break down the door and arrest me.

Vane had been bugging me for weeks to play the bit part of the vicious old rascal in the play, and once my willpower had completely dried up I agreed. It was a zippier part than the noble country lad I’d been playing, but every cheap, leering laugh I got from the crowd chipped another little piece of decency off of me. I started leaning into the gross little suggestive gags Vane suggested, making the part as unpleasant as we could get away with, and he kept telling me I was a genius, that I “understood human nature.” Worst of all, I started to like Vane.

Looking back now, he was just a regular, boring scammer with no interesting quirks at all, but to my dumb 20-something self he seemed like the coolest, most enlightened man alive. He’d wander around acting like he was far too clever to bother following all the silly little rules us regular chumps stuck to, and call anyone who had a scrap of basic decency “suburban” or “bourgeois” like that was the worst insult you could possibly come up with. He was one of those guys who rants about Hedonism and how all morality is just made up nonsense, the exact sort of boring edgelord you get cornered by at a house party at 2 a.m. that everyone rolls their eyes at now, but I was a kid who’d never heard anyone talk like that before, and I ate it all up. For a while I actually thought being a dissolute layabout was a sign you were a great artist.

Of course, the whole “mysterious elegant villain who does evil for aesthetic pleasure” schtick is great in novels, but in real life they’re just delusional fools. And for all his talk, Vane turned out fine in the end — I’ve heard he’s an excellent husband and dad, a kind boss, the sort of guy who hosts fantastic dinner parties. He genuinely thought he was doing me a favour, “freeing me from the chains of convention.” Idiot.

Then there was our leading lady, a big, handsome, brusque woman who’d started out as a music hall singer, then run off with a very promising young sensitive novelist who’d thrown his whole career away for her. By the time we met her, she was already bored of him. He was hanging around our troupe playing tiny, terrible bit parts, too wrapped up in being in love with her to leave even when he wasn’t getting paid.

Vane decided it’d be a great little scheme to set us two up. He told her I was a rich, upcoming young writer who could keep her in all the fancy gowns she wanted, and told me she was madly in love with me. I wasn’t attracted to her beyond thinking she looked as nice and robust as a well-fed cart horse, but my stupid, fragile vanity ate the whole thing up. Just like I’d forced myself to learn to smoke cigars and drink whiskey because I thought it made me look like a real writer, I started trying to force myself to fall for her, convinced that all great artists had to be a little bit of a cad. The fact her lingering touches made me feel cold instead of flustered, the fact her so-called “sultry” smiles just annoyed me, felt like a personal failure on my part.

Then fate stepped in, thank God, and saved me from making a complete fool of myself. The dramatist we’d stolen the play from released his version earlier than anyone expected, and word got around our tiny touring circuit that some other troupe had already been running a nearly identical knockoff. People started asking questions. Vane kept saying “it’ll blow over,” but it was very much not blowing over.

We were playing in a tiny town in the north of Ireland, and I was the one who always handed out salaries on a Friday night after the show. I hadn’t seen Vane all day, and the second I got out of my costume the box office guy handed me a crumpled note. Vane had grabbed all the evening’s takings and legged it on the 7:50 train. He wrote he regretted the inconvenience, but life was full of little comedies and you shouldn’t take any of them seriously, and he was sure we’d meet up soon and laugh about the whole mess over a drink.

I looked up from the note and saw every member of the troupe staring at me, all their faces full of suspicion. I tried to tell them the truth, but none of them believed it. Vane had spent months making sure every single one of them thought I was the sole manager and the only person in charge. Half of them were talking about giving me a “good hiding,” but I was so numb and listless at that point I don’t think I would have noticed if they’d beaten me within an inch of my life. Someone said I was obviously just very drunk, and everyone bought it. There were no more trains out of town that night, so they told me to go back to my lodgings and they’d come “sort things out” first thing in the morning.

I got back to my tiny rented room and found our leading lady sitting by my fire, waiting for me. Her husband was off working as our advance agent in the next town, so there was no one else around.

“Well?” she said, giving me the one smile she’d been telling me for months was absolutely soul-shattering. It had never once made my pulse speed up.

“Vane ran off with all the week’s takings,” I said, and laughed. “There’s no money for anyone’s salaries, no money for anyone’s fares back to London.”

She stood up so fast she knocked her chair over. “Wait a second. Are you the rich idiot Vane told me you were, or just his broke little accomplice?”

“Both,” I said. “No money attached. The whole tour was his idea, he just put my name on the paperwork because he didn’t want his respectable family to find out. He stole the whole play, I was just the idiot who wrote the transcript for the Lord Chamberlain.”

She sat back down and laughed so loud the walls shook, long and loud and genuinely amused. “Well I’ll be damned! He’s the first man who’s ever managed to take me for a ride. I never would’ve signed on for this rickety grift if I’d known what a crook he was.” She stood up to leave, then stopped leaning on the doorframe, cackling too hard to leave. “You know why I really turned up here tonight? You’d asked me to run away with you last night, remember? I came to tell you yes.”

I stared at her. That felt like it had happened 100 years ago. I’d completely forgotten the whole stupid, dramatic speech I’d slurred at her outside the stage door after three whiskeys.

She snort-laughed again. “Well, thank God I decided to sleep on it! What a lucky escape for both of us. Goodnight, kid.”

She paused at the step. “Got any money to get yourself out of here?”

I dug in my pocket. “One pound seventeen shillings. It’s either enough to get me the fare back to London, or buy a dinner and a secondhand revolver. Haven’t decided which yet.”

“Go back to London and pull yourself together, you’re just a kid,” she said, and left.

I stuffed a handful of my things in a tiny bag and walked 30 miles straight through the night all the way to Belfast, then caught the first ferry back to England. I rented a tiny, anonymous garret in Deptford, where I was unlikely to run into a single person I’d ever met before. I scraped by for four months teaching sixpence half-hour singing lessons, 18 pence a week evening French and German classes to ambitious teen shop boys (may God forgive my terrible accent), and doing bookkeeping for local tradesmen. I never wandered outside my tiny neighbourhood, and whenever I saw a familiar London street corner in the distance I’d turn and run back to my little corner of dim, smoky Deptford. For months I existed in a sort of numb, dull daze, eating, sleeping, walking for hours and staring at the ground, not really seeing a single thing going on around me. I was half-convinced I’d already died and no one had bothered to tell me.

It took me three full, grueling years to earn that first proper industry handshake from a newspaper sub-editor. For the first six months of that stretch I was holed up in Deptford, where the docks and the dockside pubs brimmed with perfect fodder for essays, stories, and punchlines of every stripe—even the sad, tear-jerking kind that made readers blow their noses into their newspapers. But I owed two hundred quid (give or take a few pennies, once I’d stopped lying to myself about the total), so I wasn’t about to waste good material on making strangers snivel. I left the sad stuff to the mopey writers who enjoyed weeping over other people’s misfortunes, stuck strictly to my own patch, and only hauled armfuls of straight-up laughter to market to sell.

At first I sent every single finished manuscript straight to Norah, who got them typed up, deducted the copying cost, cashed the editor’s check, and sent me whatever change was left. My early earnings were tiny, but Norah was a shark of an agent, and before long my pay packets were swelling so fast that old Dan Brian, the tightfisted editor who’d first published me, got thoroughly huffy. He wrote me a pained, grumpy note saying my work was “fine, not exceptional,” and that even though he wanted to help out a friend of Norah’s, business was business—he couldn’t possibly pay that much, not when he had proprietors to answer to. Norah shot back a curt third-person reply agreeing fully that business was indeed business, and that if Dan couldn’t afford top-tier funny, the three other editors she was equally chummy with absolutely could, and she’d be sending the half-finished manuscript she was holding right over to them if he didn’t reply by return post. Dan, realizing he’d met his match, grumbled so loud I bet his office walls shook, and paid up. He never admitted he had any clue my pen name “Jack Homer” was me, but he never tried to lowball us again, anyway. Norah made a point of sending a solid chunk of my work to rival publications on purpose, too, and soon “Jack Homer” was a hot commodity everyone was fighting over. I’d sit at my wobbly rickety table, cackling while I wrote, and the drab fourth wall of my crappy garret would dissolve to show me all the bigger, better places I was gonna get to one day.

Still, it was slow going. Humor, it turns out, is the worst kind of diva of a coworker. She won’t be rushed, refuses to show up on a schedule, spends 90% of her time napping and getting her hair done, and if you badger her for a joke when she’s not in the mood, she’ll vanish for three whole days without a text, only to saunter back at 2am with a perfect one-liner and no apology. To pay the bills I had to add straight journalism to my workload, and boy was I bad at the whole “burst into the editor’s office uninvited to pitch a story” schtick. Fate never cuts nervous people any slack—if I’d tried that, the editor would’ve definitely been out, or if he was in, he’d have booted me out on my ear before I got three words out. Even the legendary roguish Robert Macaire himself, dressed in my only frayed coat, would’ve apologized for existing before he even crossed the threshold. So I joined the ranks of the penny-a-liners—three halfpence a line, to be annoyingly precise. Me and half a dozen other ragged, shabby blokes (some lads like me trying to climb the ladder, some old blokes who’d already fallen off it) hung around inquests, camped outside police courts, sprinted after fire engines, got giddy over random street crashes, and prayed for murders to happen so we could make rent. We were basically a pack of well-meaning vultures, scratching a living off other people’s bad luck. Every once in a while we’d make half a quid by writing up a scandal for the papers, and even more often make a whole crown by keeping our mouths shut about said scandal.

“Gentlemen,” our designated spokesman would whisper hoarsely, trundling back to our little table at the coroner’s court, “looks like the corpse’s brother-in-law is real keen on this not making the papers, if we can help it.” Suddenly we’d all be taking the most detailed, diligent notes known to man, cross-examining witnesses like barristers. Five minutes later our guy would drift back, wiping his mouth like he’d just had a pint down the pub. “Nah, lads, this case ain’t interesting at all. Five bob apiece sound fair?” Sometimes, if we were feeling especially proud of our noble calling, we’d dig our heels in and demand ten.

My dumb sense of humor gave me a huge leg up on the rest of the crew, too. Once, 12 jurors were trying to work out how a random Lascar sailor had dropped dead out of nowhere. It was the most obvious thing in the world: a plumber working on the roof of a tiny two-story house had slipped, and landed right on top of him. The plumber walked away with a couple of bruises, the sailor was stone dead before anyone could say “watch your step.” The plumber’s workmate, a great witness, told us the whole story:
“I was fixin’ a gas pipe on the first floor, right? Jim, the plumber, was up on the roof. He yells down the chimney askin’ if I’m ready for him. I yells back ‘Ready and waitin’.’ He says ‘Right, I’m comin’ in through the window.’ I yells ‘Hold on, I’ll go move the ladder for you.’ He says ‘Nah, it’s fine, I can reach it.’ I says ‘No you can’t, it’s on the other side of the chimney.’ He says ‘Nah, I can get round.’ Next thing I know I hear him go SMACK! All the other hacks wrote up the story with the boring, truthful headline “Sad Accident,” which no one would ever stop to read. I called mine “Plumber In A Hurry – Fatal Result,” wrote barely a paragraph about the poor dead sailor, and dedicated the rest of the piece to warning plumbers everywhere that rushing through work to save five seconds is the dumbest possible move you could ever make. It made me a local legend. The sub-editor of a big evening paper even rolled up to the press room in his shirtsleeves, walked all the way over to me, and shook my hand like I’d just won the Nobel Prize for news. “That’s exactly the stuff we want,” he told me. “Light touch, a little bit of fun.” After that, I was pulling jokes out of burning buildings (I really, really hope no one’s insurance lapsed because of it), mining street brawls for silly little gags, and digging punchlines out of even the most horrific disasters imaginable, and for once, everything was going my way.

I never once went near that old Poplar house, even though I passed within spitting distance of it all the time. Back then I was basically two different guys stuffed inside one coat, constantly fighting. My whole goal was to nurture the new, practical, go-getter Paul who was building a decent career, and chase off that weird sappy twin of mine, the old teenage Paul with the sad faraway eyes who still lived in my head. Sometimes I’d glance in my cracked cheap mirror and see his wistful, yearning face staring back at me, begging me to go visit the old house. But I’d shake my head at him stern, because I knew what he was up to. If I let him call the shots, he’d lead me winding through all the old familiar streets, drag me past the broken railings, down the weedy path, through the dented cracked front door, up the creaky stairs to that tiny garret we used to sit in daydreaming all those stupid, useless big dreams. “C’mon,” he’d whisper, “it’s so close! Let’s quietly push the chest of drawers out of the way, lift that broken window sash real soft, prop it open with the Latin dictionary, lean our elbows on the sill, and listen to all the tired old city voices calling out in the dark.” But I was too sharp to fall for that. “Later,” I’d tell him. “When I’ve made it big, when I’m tough enough to ignore all your mushy nonsense. Then I’ll come with you, if you’re still hanging around, you silly sentimental oaf. We’ll dig up all those old dumb impossible dreams, and laugh at how stupid we were.” Then he’d vanish, and the new practical, wide-awake Paul would nod at me approvingly in the mirror, ready to go write another funny paragraph.

But I caved to one silly, sentimental temptation. By then my career was steady, I didn’t have any reason to hide anymore, so I decided to move west to a nicer neighborhood. I wasn’t planning to go anywhere fancy, but one day I was walking down Guildford Street and spotted that creeper-covered corner house my dad had once talked about renting, years back. There was a To Let card stuck in the fanlight, so I knocked and enquired. The third floor bed-sit was empty, and the second the chatty landlady opened the door, I recognized it instantly. “This shall be your room, Paul,” my dad’s voice echoed so clearly behind me that I turned around, half-expecting to see him standing there, before I remembered he was long gone. “You’ll be quiet here, we can hide the bed and washstand behind a nice screen.” My dad got his way, after all. It was a sunny, cozy little room, looking out over the hospital gardens. I did exactly what he’d suggested, stuck a screen in front of the bed and washstand, and some evenings I swear I could hear him turning the doorknob, poking his head in to ask how I was getting on. “All good,” I’d call back, and it felt like he was proud of me. I kept thinking about that thing he’d told me once: “You’ve got to be the practical one, Paul, and get on with it. I was always a bit of a dreamer. I planned to do such huge great things in the world, but I guess I aimed too high. I wasn’t practical.” When I’d asked him if it wasn’t better to aim high, he’d fidgeted in his chair and said “It’s all so confusing. You aim high and you don’t hit anything, but then it feels a waste to aim low and hit it, when you’ve got all those big ideals in your head. I don’t know why life works like that. Maybe none of us ever really understand.”

I’d been publishing work under my own name for a few months when a letter got forwarded to me by an editor, addressed to my care. It was a stiff, formal invite from Mrs. Sellars, the mom of the girl I’d once almost run off with, asking me to her daughter’s wedding to a Mr. Reginald Clapper. I’d barely even thought about the whole Lady Ortensia incident in ages, but it was nice to know it had all worked out fine for her. I also figured sending me the invite meant she wanted me to see I hadn’t left her heartbroken, so I splurged on the nicest wedding present I could afford, dug my only good frock coat and light grey trousers out of the back of the wardrobe, and trundled off to Kennington Church the next Thursday.

The ceremony was already halfway over when I got there. I tiptoed up the aisle, about to slip into an empty pew, when a big calloused hand clamped onto my sleeve. “We’re all packed in here,” O’Kelly whispered, grinning, “just enough spot for you.” I squeezed his hand as I squeezed past, and plopped down between the Signora and Mrs. Peedles. Both of them were crying: the Signora was leaking slow, pretty tears that clung to her cheeks like they didn’t want to roll off, Mrs. Peedles was sobbing so hard she made gurgling noises like a bottle of water being tipped upside down. “The service is so beautiful,” the Signora murmured, squeezing my hand. “I would so, so love to get married someday.” “Darlin’,” O’Kelly whispered back, snatching her other hand and kissing it behind his prayer book where no one could see, “maybe you will, one day.” The Signora smiled at him through her tears, but sighed and shook her head.

Mrs. Peedles, dressed in what I’m pretty sure was Queen Elizabeth’s third best old gown (the dim November light made it hard to be 100% sure), sobbed that weddings always reminded her how fleeting love was. “Poor dears!” she blubbered. “Well, there’s no telling, I s’pose. Maybe they’ll be happy. I hope they are. The groom looks harmless enough.” Jarman leaned over past Mrs. Peedles to pat my shoulder. “Cheer up, mate,” he advised me. “Don’t go wearin’ your heart on your sleeve. Try and smile.”

Back in the vestry I ran into a dozen old faces. Mrs. Sellars was even stouter than I remembered, propped up on a chair I couldn’t even see under her, greeting me so warmly and asking after my health so tenderly that I was confused until I realized she’d heard I was making good money now. Groom Reginald Clapper was a tiny, energetic guy who was clearly 100% convinced he’d won the lottery by marrying Ortensia. He shook my hand so hard my arm ached, said he was over the moon to meet me, and told me three separate times I had to come over to their house whenever I wanted, no invitation needed. I got the distinct impression he thought he was being unbelievably generous by even letting me in the front door. The new Mrs. Clapper curtsied to me, and the whole crowd around us murmured approvingly. She looked even prettier than I remembered, or maybe my taste had just gotten less fussy. She said she hoped we could be friends, too, and before I knew it she’d leaned over and kissed me on the cheek, then hugged her mom and started crying, saying everyone was just so nice to her.

Her brother George, who used to be all lanky and thin, was now filling out a bit, and struggling to move his hands in a pair of giant white kid gloves. He supervised me signing the wedding register, then leaned over to whisper sympathetically in my ear, “Better luck next time, old cock.” The fat young lady (or maybe it was the thin young lady from before who’d put on a bunch of weight, I couldn’t tell for sure) who’d once said I was the best guy Ortensia could’ve picked, told me to my face she’d said it a dozen times before the wedding, but no one had listened to her. Cousin Joseph, easy to spot because of his watery red eyes, looked way less chirpy than I remembered him. “Take my tip,” he told me, pulling me off to the side, “and stay the hell out of marriage.” “You’re speaking from experience?” I asked. “I love a good joke as much as the next bloke,” Joseph said, “but when it comes to buckets of water being dumped on your head at 2am, and never getting a quiet minute to yourself—” Mrs. Sellars yelled that the wedding breakfast was at 11, so everyone trundled off to the restaurant, and Joseph’s rant got cut short. “See you later, maybe,” he mumbled, pushing past me.

I walked there with Uncle and Aunt Gutton, not because I wanted to, but because Uncle Gutton had clamped his hand around my arm and refused to let go. “C’mon, young man,” he boomed, as soon as we were far enough behind the rest of the crowd, “if you wanna yell at me for being a git back then, go right ahead. I won’t mind, it’ll probably do you good.” I told him that was the last thing I wanted. “Oh, fine then,” he said, looking a little disappointed. “If you’re willing to forgive and forget, I am too. I never liked you, you know, I told Rosie that straight out. Said you could either be smarter than you looked, or a bigger idiot than I thought, and odds were on the latter. Told her to go find herself a full grown bloke who knew what he was doing.” I told him his advice had been perfect. “Well that’s weird,” he said, looking confused, “I was half expecting you to call me every name under the sun. Fair play, though, you’re a sensible chap. Got over it, just like I told Rosie you would. Young men in storybooks never die of broken hearts, even if for a month or two they feel like sticking their head in a water butt to drown the sadness out. Why, I was head over heels in love three separate times before I married your aunt here.” Aunt Gutton sighed and said she was afraid blokes never took heartbreak half as seriously as they should. “They’ve got a living to earn, don’t they?” Uncle Gutton shot back. We all agreed there was no point moping over lost love, and as we walked into the restaurant, Uncle Gutton clapped me on the back and shook my hand, saying he’d been wrong about me, and I’d turned out way better than he ever expected.

Minikin was already waiting for us, saying he’d bailed on the second half of the church service because he only had limited time off work, and the food was way better than hymns. There were about twenty of us total, half old friends of Ortensia from her acting days, half coworkers of Reggie’s from the City office. I climbed that grindstone slow, steady, and determined to not let my gaze drift off the path an inch, all silly daydreams tossed straight in the bin. Trouble was, the sappy, sad-eyed little dreamer version of me kept popping up like a stray cat begging for treats, trying to lure me away from spreadsheets and copy deadlines to mope about unappreciated poetry, half-baked political speeches, and dramatic essays full of very heavy opinions that no one asked for. If I hadn’t been so single-mindedly obsessed with paying back every last penny I owed to win back my self-respect — and more than that, to feel Norah’s strong, warm white hand back in mine, a craving that got so sharp it felt like a physical ache in my chest every evening — that silly little dreamer would’ve definitely won, and I’d have ended up a very broke, very unhelpful person whose only talent was annoying everyone around him with deep thoughts. Every once in a while, when my comedy brain took a holiday and refused to write a single funny line, I’d scribble sad little stories just for my own amusement. They never went anywhere, but I was making solid steady money, and by the time my last debt was ticked off the list, I’d built up a name for myself as the guy who could turn any boring premise into a laugh.

All my practical friends immediately lost their minds. “Are you mad?” they squawked. “You’re gonna throw away a guaranteed fortune for a maybe, barely-scraping-by shot at serious writing? You know you’re good at the funny stuff. The serious stuff? That’s starting your whole career over from day one!”
“You’ll never make it,” the experienced, wise old heads agreed, patting my shoulder like I was a toddler who’d tried to eat a whole jar of pickles. “The public already decided you’re the funny guy. Once a clown, always a clown. It’s like if a guy who does pratfalls at the music hall decided he was gonna play Hamlet. It could be the best Hamlet the world’s ever seen, and the audience would just snort into their popcorn or walk out, waiting for him to trip over Yorick’s skull.”

Me and my mate Goggles — the guy with the fishy, permanently wide eyes who was dead set on being a great tragedian — would sneak off to the club’s quietest corner to mope and pour our hearts out to each other. He’d ramble for 45 minutes about his very carefully thought out vision of Romeo, which was probably very good, but I couldn’t for the life of me picture him pining after Juliet without accidentally knocking over the balcony. I nodded and cheered him on anyway, it was the price I paid for getting to rant at him later about my own grand plan to write giant, world-changing serious literature that would fix all of humanity’s problems.
“Maybe in the next life,” Goggles laughed one evening, yanking his coat on as the clock hit seven, “I’ll be the big Shakespearean star, and you’ll be the world-famous serious poet. Right now? Like our mate Brian says, we’re both sinning our mercies. Turns out showing up and paying your rent is the most important thing in life, who knew?”
He tapped me on the chest, his face so deadly serious it looked absurd. “Take my advice, marry a nice sweet girl. I did — she’s twice my size, and she’s the best thing that ever happened to me. You don’t actually understand what life is for until the kids come along and explain all the riddles to you.”
I sank back into my armchair by the fire, staring at the flames. Wife, kids, a real home? Wasn’t that the actual point of being alive, instead of chasing all those misty morning dream ghosts that vanish as soon as the sun comes up? I could picture it: the sweet, sharp woman who was my partner in every stupid thing, the tiny kids running around the house yelling questions that would make all my big dumb serious ambitions look totally useless.

Then Hodgson, the theatre manager, turned up at my place one night waving a letter. “I want you to write me a comic opera,” he said. “The public’s sick to death of the same boring French translations, over and over. I want something English, fresh, new and original.”
“English is easy,” I told him, “but if I were you I wouldn’t beg too hard for new and original. You might actually get it, and then you’ll be very disappointed.”
He snort-laughed. “You know what I mean! Just something we can stick on the program that says ‘new and original’. It’s a big break for you, everyone’s starting to talk about your work, I know you can pull it off.”
I’d already written a handful of short farces and one-act plays that did great, but headlining a whole night’s entertainment was terrifying — it could make your career, or blow it up so bad no one would hire you for years. I didn’t tell him that I already had a whole four-act serious drama stuffed in my desk drawer, full of all the heavy humanity and feeling I’d been dying to show off to prove I wasn’t just the joke guy.
“C’mon, it’s easy,” Hodgson urged me. “Write our star Atherton lots of silly physical gags, give Gleeson a pretty waltz number to sing, and for Duncan’s part just make sure she gets to change her fancy dress every 15 minutes. That’s all anyone wants.”
“Also,” he added, “we’re all hiring a coach to drive out to Richmond this Sunday. Leave the theatre at 10:30. You can come along, hang out with all the cast, work out what each of them is actually good at, get all their ideas. Then next week we’ll sign the contract and get to work.”
I couldn’t say no. It was too good a shot, even if I knew if it went well I’d be stuck as the resident jester forever. But it turns out writing comic opera pays extremely well.

A little crowd of street kids had gathered on the Strand to watch us leave. Our leading lady jumped out of her cab 15 minutes late, panicking. “Oh no — is everyone alright? Did the theatre burn down?”
“Nah, they just all came to see you,” Hodgson said, not looking up from his stack of letters.
“Good god, then let’s go right now,” she yelped.
We hauled her up to the top deck first, with three of us holding her hand while she pretended to be far too delicate to climb the coach ladder. Then our low comedian Charlie Atherton had to show off: he faked missing his footing three times, sliding right back down into the doorman’s arms every go. The street kids went wild, cheering and laughing, so on the third try he crawled up the ladder on his hands and knees, and then immediately plopped down right on top of the tenor, who just smiled like he’d been expecting it.
For the first dozen buses we passed, Charlie stood up and took a deep, dramatic bow every single time, then fell over straight into the tenor or my lap. Everyone on the coach thought it was hilarious — everyone except the tenor and me, that is. Once we got past Charing Cross and no one was paying attention to us anymore, he finally sat still and brushed dust off his knees.
“People always ask why I do this stuff off stage,” he said. “It’s just fun, for me. The other week I was on the train coming up from Birmingham, and at Willesden I jumped out the door the second the ticket collector opened it, and ran down the platform as fast as I could. Fifty people got up and started chasing me! I only stopped and told them I had a first class ticket for a second class seat once they caught me. People think I do it for attention — nah, it’s just fun to be an idiot sometimes.”
“Sounds like a lot of extra work to amuse yourself,” I said.
“Exactly what my wife says,” he sighed. “She gets such awful headaches, she never comes out anywhere with me. That’s why she’s not here today, poor thing.”

By the time we got to the Richmond hotel, we’d had Charlie balance the coach horn on his nose to amuse passersby, stop the whole rig to ask a girls’ boarding school for change for sixpence, and halt traffic at the bottom of Richmond Hill to try and talk a deaf old man in a bath chair into racing us up the hill for a shilling. Everyone on the party was cackling — except Hodgson, who was buried in his letters, and a fancy newly-divorced lady who made a hundred quid a week off her very notorious reputation.
We wandered into the garden, and Charlie spotted a very dignified old man sipping vermouth at a table. He froze like he’d just seen a ghost, then ran straight over, grabbed the poor guy’s hand, and shook it like they were long lost brothers. “Uncle George! I can’t believe it! How’s dear old Aunt Martha? Is she doing well?”
The old guy blinked, completely confused. “Excuse me, I don’t —”
“Wait don’t tell me — Aunt Martha’s not dead, is she?!” Charlie grabbed his shoulders, making a horrified face so dramatic passersby started gathering.
“I have no idea who you are,” the old man snapped, very annoyed. “My name’s not Steggles, whoever you think I am.”
“Terribly sorry, wrong person!” Charlie said, chugging the rest of the old guy’s vermouth in one go and sprinting away. The rest of us went over to apologize, and told him that was Charlie Atherton, the famous comic.
“Oh yeah?” he growled. “Tell him if I want his stupid tomfoolery I’ll pay for a theatre ticket, thank you very much. Don’t give it away for free out here.”

Lunch was even more chaos. Charlie kissed the back of the bald waiter’s head pretending he thought it was the leading lady’s face, asked for hot mustard and water, pretended to steal the silverware, and when the finger bowls came out he took off his coat and told all the ladies to turn around, no peeking. Then after lunch he dragged me off alone down a quiet path.
“About this new opera,” he said, all serious now. “None of that stale old garbage everyone does. Give me something new. My idea: I’m a nice young bloke, in love with a girl. Her dad’s a grumpy apoplectic old idiot who hates me, thinks I’m a total fool. But I’m not actually an idiot, right? Then in the second act I disguise myself — first as an organ grinder with a silly accent, then a policeman, then a fancy toff. Give me lots of room to do bits, not the same old jokes everyone’s seen a hundred times. And — put a little sad stuff in there. A tiny scene where I can make ‘em feel a little, not just laugh. Make the audience leave going, ‘Wait Charlie made me cry? I never knew he had it in him!’ You get what I mean?”
I promised him I did.

Next the leading lady dragged me off to a secluded bench overlooking the river. “About my part,” she said, pouting and laying her very pretty hand on mine. “I’m so sick of playing the boring simple village maiden. I want a flirty, fun part. I swear I can flirt! Test me! Make the whole plot revolve around me. Women are way more interesting than men, right? The whole show will be a hit if it’s all about me.”
I told her I thought that was brilliant, I’d do my best.
Then the tenor cornered me on the balcony over tea. “Listen,” he said, glancing around to make sure Charlie wasn’t nearby, “the public doesn’t want nonstop clowning anymore. Give them a real human love story. I get paid the same whether I’m on stage five minutes or an hour, but my fans come to see me! They want a proper interesting lover character. That’s the secret of a hit.”
Last of all, quiet little Marmaduke Trevor, the guy who had the tiny throwaway part as the Duke of Bayswater, snuck over to me like he was sharing state secrets. “You might not remember me, Mr. Kelver, but I had a huge hit in America once doing a bit with a soda-water syphon. If you could just slip a tiny little scene in for me to do that bit, it’ll make the whole show way funnier, I promise.”
I kept that soda-water scene in, even when everyone complained it didn’t fit. The critics whinged about it later, but Trevor was right, it always got a big laugh.

The drive back to London was even more chaotic than the way there: Charlie honked the coach horn as loud as he could to make every bus driver mad, and then stopped at Hammersmith to stand on the back seat and sell all of our hats to passersby at rock bottom prices, pretending he was a market cheap jack.
“Got all the ideas you needed off them?” Hodgson asked me, when we were all saying goodbye.
“I think I’m gonna go hide out in the Yorkshire Wolds and write the whole thing away from everyone,” I told him. “No interruptions.”
“Smart call,” he said. “Too many cooks spoil the broth. Just get it done before autumn.”
I had a great time writing it up there, and read the whole finished script out loud to the full cast once we got back to London. The whole room went dead silent when I finished. The leading lady was the first to speak: “Is that clock right? Because if it is I can still just catch my train if I run right now.”
Hodgson checked his watch. “It’s probably a little fast.”
“I hope so!” she said, and bolted.
The tenor was next. “Oh, I remember that famous hit opera at the Philharmonic, right? Everyone thought it was total garbage five minutes before opening night! Anyway I gotta run, I have a train too.”
Marmaduke Trevor snuck over and squeezed my hand. “Don’t give up,” he whispered. Charlie Atherton, the last one left, told Hodgson “I think I can make some bits of this work, if I get to rewrite most of it.”
Hodgson and I stared at each other once they were all gone. “It’s never gonna make money,” he said. “It’s clever, but the box office will hate it.”
“How do you know?” I asked.
“I never make mistakes,” he said, grinning. “I’ve put on plenty of flops before, that’s how you learn. We’ll hack it into shape at rehearsals, you’ll see.”
Their idea of “hacking it into shape” was hacking it to total pieces.
“Cut this whole scene!” Charlie would say, scribbling straight through five pages of my script.
“But that’s the scene where the hero escapes prison and meets the girl he loves!” I protested.
“Pfft, we can do that in two lines. ‘Hallo, you escaped prison?’ ‘Yep. The girl’s gonna marry me.’ Done! Who needs the rest?”
Then the leading lady would pop up. “Hodgson says I don’t fall in love with Peter, I fall in love with John. We cut Arabella out, I can sing all her songs.”
“But John was supposed to be in love with Arabella!”
Then the tenor would drag me into a corner. “Write me a pretty moonlit balcony scene with the leading lady at the top of act one. It’ll be great.”
“Act one opens with a morning hunting scene! No one goes hunting by moonlight.”
“So we change the hunting scene to a novelty moonlight hunt! Everyone’s sick of regular hunting scenes.”

I put up with this for a whole week, telling myself they were the experts, they must know better than me. By the end of the week I knew they didn’t. And I lost my temper — which I recommend every new playwright do immediately, by the way, it’s extremely cathartic. I grabbed two copies of the script: the first one was my neat, original, perfectly coherent draft, the second was the Frankenstein monster mess everyone had scribbled all over, cut, rewritten, flipped upside down, that no human being on earth could possibly make sense of. I dumped both on Hodgson’s desk.
“This first one’s yours,” I said, shoving the scribbled mess across to him. “If you can make any sense of it, you’re welcome to produce it. This other one is mine. You put it on exactly as I wrote it, or we tear up the contract right now.”
He argued with me for 40 minutes, yelling about lost money, my reputation, everything. Then he called me a stubborn, pig-headed idiot, threw the Frankenstein script straight in the wastepaper basket, stomped out to the cast, and yelled “We’re doing the damned opera exactly as Kelver wrote it. And if any of you complain, you’re all fired.”
Everyone rolled their eyes. Hodgson stayed away from rehearsals for a while, then came back, watching us all with a very sad, resigned look on his face. “This show’s gonna flop so bad,” he told me. “I’m spending 12 to 15 hundred quid just to teach you a lesson. Once it bombs, you’ll write me the crowd-pleaser we need, we’ll make all the money back.”
“Or what if it works?” I grinned.
“Dear boy,” he said, lighting a cigar, “I never make mistakes.”
