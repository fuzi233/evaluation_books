The abrupt ending of Colonel Brandon’s stay at the Park—so neatly cut off, so firmly sealed up behind the steadiness of his manner—set Mrs. Jennings to wondering as a kettle sets itself to sing the moment it feels heat. For two or three days her mind went on at it, never quite boiling over into certainty, yet never cooling into sense. She had that lively appetite for the movements of her acquaintance which some people reserve for dinner; and having once tasted a mystery, she could not leave it untasted.

“Something very melancholy must be the matter, I am sure,” she insisted, seasoning every sentence with a new conjecture, as if she were trying sauces upon the same plain joint. “I could see it in his face. Poor man! I am afraid his circumstances may be bad. Delaford was never reckoned above two thousand a year, and his brother left things sadly involved—sadly! I do think he has been sent for about money matters, for what else can it be? I would give anything to know the truth. Perhaps it is Miss Williams—and, by the bye, I dare say it is, because he looked so conscious when I mentioned her. May be she is ill in town; nothing more likely, for I have a notion she is always rather sickly. I would lay any wager it is about Miss Williams. Yet, it is not so very likely he should be distressed in his circumstances now, for he is prudent, and must have cleared the estate by this time. I wonder what it can be! May be his sister is worse at Avignon, and has sent for him over. His setting off in such a hurry seems very like it. Well, I wish him out of all his trouble with all my heart—and a good wife into the bargain.”

Thus Mrs. Jennings talked, her opinions shifting with each fresh guess, as a cook’s hand will alter its measures—more of this, less of that—till the whole bowl becomes a new thing, though the ingredients remain much the same.

Elinor, though sincerely concerned for Colonel Brandon’s welfare, could not give her whole attention to the matter, nor lend her imagination to the same restless speculation. The circumstance, in her judgement, did not justify so long a ferment. Her mind was already occupied—more quietly, but more deeply—by another silence far more extraordinary.

It was the silence of Marianne and Willoughby.

That they should know this subject must interest their family—that it could not but touch upon their common life—was obvious. Yet day after day went by, and not one frank word acknowledged what their every look, their every tone, their every habitual turn toward each other proclaimed. Their attachment did not merely exist; it spoke continually in the smallest domestic acts, like a savour that rises from the hearth and will not be denied. And still, on the very point where words would have been simplest, there was nothing.

Elinor tried to reason with it. She could easily imagine that marriage might not be immediately within their power; though Willoughby was independent, there was no certain proof that he was rich. Sir John had rated his estate at six or seven hundred a year; and yet Willoughby lived with a freedom of expense that such an income could hardly satisfy, and he had himself often complained—half in jest, half in earnest—of his poverty. But secrecy—this particular, needless, almost theatrical sort of secrecy—seemed to her wholly unaccountable. It concealed nothing, and yet it affected concealment. It was so unlike the open ardour of Marianne, so unlike the confident animation of Willoughby, that sometimes—only sometimes—a doubt entered Elinor’s mind: were they in truth engaged? And that doubt, slight as it was, checked her from questioning Marianne, for she would not draw from her sister either a confession prematurely forced, or an explanation that might wound.

In every other respect, Willoughby’s behaviour was as expressive of attachment as a man could make it. To Marianne, he brought all the distinguishing tenderness his heart could supply—an attention that had not only passion in it, but that delighted, with a lover’s vanity, in anticipating her wishes. To Mrs. Dashwood and Elinor he was affectionate as a son and brother: quick to perceive, eager to oblige, pleased with their little habits, and laying himself out to be necessary. The cottage appeared to have become his home—not by any right of possession, but by that stronger right with which the heart occupies a place. Many more hours were spent there than at Allenham; and if no general engagement collected them at the Park, the exercise that called him out in the morning was almost certain to end at Barton, where the rest of the day would be passed—he at Marianne’s side, his favourite pointer stretched contentedly at her feet, the room warmed by talk and by that comfortable stir of small domestic life.

One evening in particular, about a week after Colonel Brandon had left the country, Willoughby’s spirits seemed unusually unguarded, his feelings more ready to pour themselves out upon whatever lay nearest. The house was gathered as usual; the air held that mixed perfume of woodsmoke, candles, and the faint, homely trace of supper cleared away—warm bread and tea-leaves, a ghost of butter, a lingering sweetness from whatever preserve had been set out. Outside, the wind made a low, constant sound through the bare branches, and now and then a gust pushed at the cottage as if to listen; within, the crackle of the fire answered it. It was the sort of soundscape that steadies the mind without one’s knowing it—natural, various, and never harsh; unlike those louder, harder noises Elinor had sometimes heard in towns, where wheels, hammers, and shrill calls seemed to grind down thought itself and leave one’s nerves raw, as if the world had mistaken clamour for life.

It was then that Mrs. Dashwood happened to mention, with her usual cheerful confidence, her design of improving the cottage in the spring. She spoke of it as one might speak of adding a little spice to an old family dish—something to make it yet more agreeable, yet still the same.

Willoughby started at the very word.

“What!” he exclaimed. “Improve this dear cottage! No. That I will never consent to. Not a stone must be added to its walls, not an inch to its size, if my feelings are regarded.”

“Do not be alarmed,” said Elinor, quietly, “nothing of the kind will be done; for my mother will never have money enough to attempt it.”

“I am heartily glad of it,” he cried. “May she always be poor, if she can employ her riches no better.”

“Thank you, Willoughby,” Mrs. Dashwood returned, laughing, yet touched. “But you may be assured that I would not sacrifice one sentiment of local attachment of yours—or of any one whom I loved—for all the improvements in the world. Depend upon it, whatever unemployed sum may remain when I make up my accounts in the spring, I would even rather lay it uselessly by than dispose of it in a manner so painful to you. But are you really so attached to this place as to see no defect in it?”

“I am,” said he. “To me it is faultless. Nay, more, I consider it as the only form of building in which happiness is attainable; and were I rich enough I would instantly pull Combe down, and build it up again in the exact plan of this cottage.”

“With dark narrow stairs and a kitchen that smokes, I suppose,” said Elinor.

“Yes,” he cried, in the same eager tone, as if even inconvenience, when allied to affection, became dear. “With all and every thing belonging to it—in no one convenience or inconvenience about it should the least variation be perceptible. Then, and then only, under such a roof, I might perhaps be as happy at Combe as I have been at Barton.”

“I flatter myself,” Elinor replied, “that even under the disadvantage of better rooms and a broader staircase, you will hereafter find your own house as faultless as you now do this.”

“There certainly are circumstances,” said Willoughby, “which might greatly endear it to me; but this place will always have one claim of my affection which no other can possibly share.”

Mrs. Dashwood looked with pleasure at Marianne. Marianne’s eyes, bright and fixed, were turned toward him with an expression that needed no interpreter; she understood him, and, perhaps more dangerous still, she believed herself understood in return.

“How often did I wish,” he went on, “when I was at Allenham this time twelvemonth, that Barton cottage were inhabited! I never passed within view of it without admiring its situation, and grieving that no one should live in it. How little did I then think that the very first news I should hear from Mrs. Smith, when I next came into the country, would be that Barton cottage was taken; and I felt an immediate satisfaction and interest in the event which nothing but a kind of prescience of what happiness I should experience from it can account for. Must it not have been so, Marianne?” He spoke the last words lower, for her alone, as if he were placing something delicate into her hands—something meant to be felt rather than repeated.

Then, returning at once to his former tone, he addressed Mrs. Dashwood again. “And yet this house you would spoil? You would rob it of its simplicity by imaginary improvement! And this dear parlour, in which our acquaintance first began, and in which so many happy hours have been since spent by us together—you would degrade it to the condition of a common entrance, and everybody would be eager to pass through the room which has hitherto contained within itself more real accommodation and comfort than any other apartment of the handsomest dimensions in the world could possibly afford.”

Mrs. Dashwood again assured him that no alteration of the kind should be attempted.

“You are a good woman,” he replied warmly. “Your promise makes me easy. Extend it a little farther, and it will make me happy. Tell me that not only your house will remain the same, but that I shall ever find you and yours as unchanged as your dwelling; and that you will always consider me with the kindness which has made everything belonging to you so dear to me.”

The promise was readily given; and Willoughby’s manner through the whole of the evening declared at once his affection and his happiness—so overflowing, so certain, that it seemed as natural and inevitable as the fire’s warmth in the room, as the steady undertone of wind outside that made the cottage feel, by contrast, the safer and more intimate.

“Shall we see you tomorrow to dinner?” said Mrs. Dashwood, when at last he rose to go. “I do not ask you to come in the morning, for we must walk to the Park, to call on Lady Middleton.”

He engaged to be with them by four o’clock; and with that assurance, and with Marianne’s eyes following him to the door, the evening closed as such evenings often do—quietly, familiarly—while, beneath the ordinary sounds of a household settling itself, Elinor’s mind returned to its uneasiness: the strange silence that still held its own, like an unspoken word that will not translate itself into any language at all.
