When we finally sighted the coast of Atlantis, Tob—guided by his modern instruments—had navigated with astonishing precision. Yet, even so, we still had ten more days of sailing ahead, retracing our course until we reached the narrow sea arm that led to the great city of Atlantis, the heart of the continent. The moment we caught the first breath of land on the wind—the scent of earth and greenery—it was as if the gods themselves had breathed life back into us. For weeks, our journey across the endless ocean had taken its toll. Many of our sailors had died; others lay broken with scurvy, weakened by salt-ridden food and relentless storms. But now, at last, the smell of land stirred something deep within them. Those who had been mere shadows, unable to move even under lash, suddenly stirred. They rose, helped rig the sails, manned the oars, and found strength once more—not just to live, but to fight.

Since my fall from power in Yucatan, I had carried a quiet dread about what awaited me in Atlantis. Tatho had assured me that all would be well, but doubt lingered. Would Empress Phorenice welcome me—or would she have my head severed before I stepped ashore? Yet I had not dwelled on it. My fate was in the hands of the gods. If she chose to end me, then so be it. The Sun, my lord above all, watched over me. And so, during the voyage, I kept my mind clear, turning inward to study the deeper mysteries, free from fear or anxiety.

But when we neared the twin headlands that marked the entrance to the inland waters—just two days from the capital—we were met by another navy. They waited in a sheltered bay, their ships anchored calmly, yet scouts perched high on the cliffs shouted warnings as we rounded the point. Instantly, they set sail to block our path.

Of our original fleet, only five vessels remained. The rest had been lost to storms or abandoned, their crews dead from disease. The enemy had three sleek warships and three powerful galleys, each bristling with oars. Their hulls were black and polished, gleaming under the sun. Ours were battered, weed-choked, scarred by storm and time. We flew the banners of Tatho and Deucalion openly, proud of our origin. They came without flags, like thieves ashamed of their lineage.

It was clear they meant to stop us by force. That was no surprise—no law ruled the seas. One ship could attack another with impunity, especially if it returned laden with plunder. So, without hesitation, Tob ordered our ships into battle formation. The other captains followed, drawing close to form a tight squadron. We had no desire to surrender—not now, not when home was so near.

The sun blazed overhead, powering our machines with fierce energy. The two fleets closed rapidly, the enemy galleys holding back to maintain position. But when we were less than a hundred ship-lengths apart, they halted. One galley broke ranks, flying green branches from her masts—a sign of parley.

Unusual, yes—but we were too weakened to risk a fight we couldn’t win. So we stopped too. Tob raised a dried branch in place of greenery, signaling our willingness to talk.

The galley approached, swung around, and backed until its stern scraped against our rail. A man climbed aboard—dressed in fine clothes, young and healthy, untouched by the sea’s cruelty. He looked around with a sneer at our ragged crew, then spotted Tob.

“Old pot-mate,” he said, “your woman waits for you in Atlantis, with four brats at her heels. I saw her not half a month ago.”

Tob’s eyes narrowed. “You didn’t come out here to give me news of home,” he said. “I’ve shared enough wine with you, Dason, to know your tricks.”

“I wanted to remind you that your home still stands,” Dason replied. “Your wife and children are waiting.”

“And I never forget it,” Tob said grimly. “That’s why I’ve sailed through every pirate and storm without giving up. A man with a family doesn’t take slavery lightly.”

“Oh, I know you’re tough,” Dason said. “And I’ll admit, if it comes to blows, we’ll lose men before we sink you and your crippled ships. But our orders are clear. We want what you’re carrying—and if you cooperate, you may go home unharmed.”

“You’ve come to the wrong man for a plucking,” Tob laughed. “We took nothing from Yucatan. No treasure, no goods. Just food and water. We left as we came—bare. Search the holds if you like. You’ll find only sick men. Nothing worth stealing. You can take only blows—and we’ll give them freely.”

Dason smiled coldly. “I’m glad you value your cargo so low. Because that’s exactly what I must take back with me—if you want to reach Atlantis alive.”

Tob frowned. “Speak plainly. I’m no scholar of riddles.”

“It’s obvious,” Dason said. “You’re bringing Deucalion back to Atlantis to strengthen Phorenice. But we oppose her. And we won’t let her gain reinforcements. We want Deucalion—for ourselves.”

“And if I refuse?”

Dason glanced at our shattered fleet. “Then you all go to the depths. And instead of Deucalion standing before the gods alone, he’ll drown with a ragged band of dying men at his heels.”

Tob chuckled. “I doubt it. But we’ll see. As for handing over Deucalion—that’s impossible. First, if I return without him, Tatho will hunt me down and kill me slowly. Second, I’ve seen Deucalion slay a sea monster. He’s a man worth protecting—even from Tatho. Third… you still owe me for our last drinking bout. I’ll see you in the underworld before I give you anything until you pay.”

Dason shrugged. “I hope you drown easily. And that woman of yours—I’ve always had a fancy for her. I’ll find a use for her.”

Tob’s face darkened. “I’ll cut your throat for that, you son of a European! Get off this deck or I’ll do it now. Go! You father of monkey children! Leave me to fight you fairly—or I’ll crush your insides and make your crew wear them as necklaces!”

Dason retreated.

At once, Tob activated the engine of the *Bear* and bellowed orders. Though weak, the crew responded swiftly. Our five ships surged forward, converging on one of the enemy vessels. Unprepared, it tried to turn and flee instead of meeting us head-on. In that moment, all five of our ships rammed it broadside, tearing through its hull. It sank before we could disengage.

Now both sides stood equal—five ships each. But the advantage didn’t last. The enemy galleys formed a line. Their boatswains cracked whips, and the slaves bent to their oars. One of our ships was pierced and sunk, its crew drowned before they could escape.

Then came the battle—fierce, brutal, and unrelenting. Ships grinded together like living beasts. Arrows flew. Axes slashed. Swords thrust. Fire was hurled from both sides. The flames burned flesh and wood alike. Above, giant carrion birds circled, waiting. Below, man-eating fish swarmed to devour those who fell.

Yet our sailors fought with indomitable courage. Death held no terror for them—they had faced worse on the open sea. They fought not just for survival, but for glory. The cry of “Tob!” echoed across the decks, rallying our forces. On the enemy side, shouts of “Dason!” grew louder as their commanders fell.

I had seen many battles in my life—fought for the Priestly Clan, carved out colonies, survived wars. But this—this was unlike any. The instability of the sea, the smoke, the fire, the sky full of hungry birds, the water crawling with predators—it was enough to break the bravest warrior. Yet these sailors laughed as they died.

Our numbers dwindled. By the end, only Dason’s galley and the *Bear* remained. We were outnumbered, outnumbered, and overwhelmed. Boarders stormed the *Bear*, turning the deck into a battlefield. But the fire from our own ship had spread. Smoke poured from every crack. The *Bear* was burning.

Tob roared, “They can stay and fry if they like—but we’re taking the galley! Guard Deucalion! Follow me!”

“Tob!” the crew shouted, drunk on battle fever. Even I joined in.

He charged forward, axe raised, carving a path. Ten of us reached the galley, flames lashing at our backs. We turned and hacked at anyone who followed, cutting loose the grappling lines. The sea pushed the *Bear* away.

On the galley, the rowers—slaves—watched impassively. Only a few of the enemy had scrambled aboard. Three fought fiercely and had to be killed. Five dropped their weapons and were chained to the oar benches. Only Dason remained.

He had surrendered, arms cast aside, face sullen. Tob approached him, laughing.

“Ho, pot-mate Dason,” he said. “You spoke so proudly of my woman in Atlantis. Now I’ll give you a choice: I’ll take you home and tell her everything—before the whole crew, most of whom are dead now—and leave her to decide your fate. Or… I’ll deal with you myself.”

Dason bowed. “Thank you for the chance.” He knelt, offering his neck.

Tob raised his axe and struck. He mounted the head on the galley’s prow. Then threw the body overboard. A great bird swooped down, seized it, and flew off toward the cliffs.

Now we were free. The galley offered fresh meat, fruit, and water. The slaves rowed hard toward Atlantis. In the after-castle, a skin of wine remained. I filled a horn, poured some at Tob’s feet.

“My man,” I said, “you’ve shown me a fight.”

“Thanks,” he said. “And I know you’re a judge. It was good while it lasted. My men were mostly sick—but they fought with honor. I’ve lost Tatho’s navy, but I think Phorenice will right that wrong. If her enemies went to such lengths to stop Deucalion, she’ll surely reward me for delivering him—bruised and bloody, but alive.”

“The gods will decide,” I said. “As they have decided that I must go on to Atlantis.”

Tob held a horn of wine, ready to pour at my feet. But he changed his mind and drank it.

“Fighting is thirsty work,” he said.

I placed my hand on his bloodied arm.

“Tob,” I said, “whether I regain power or die tomorrow, only the gods know. But hear me now: if I ever have the chance to repay you, I will not forget how you served me—or how you fought today.”

Tob refilled his horn, splashed it at my feet.

“That’s good enough,” he said. “My woman and brats will never want again. Let the Lord Deucalion go to the block indeed!”
