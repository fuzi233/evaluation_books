The summer heat was oppressive, a heavy blanket that seemed to press down on the very walls of the pensionnat. Georgette, Madame Beck's youngest daughter, had fallen violently ill with a fever. In a frantic display of precaution, Désirée and Fifine were whisked away to the countryside with Bonne-Maman, leaving the school vulnerable to contagion. The medical situation had become critical; Madame Beck, ignoring the return of her former physician, Dr. Pillule, summoned Dr. John, an Englishman, to take up residence within the school's division.

It was a bold move. Dr. John was soon attending to the proud Blanche de Melcy and the coquettish Angélique. While I observed a certain satisfaction in the doctor's demeanor at this mark of confidence, I knew that in a house of nuns and confessions, such a presence was dangerous. Rumors spread like wildfire through the gossiping schoolgirls and whispering kitchens, reaching the ears of parents who wrote letters of remonstrance. Madame Beck, however, was not a woman to be undone by a slip. Though a "little Jesuit" in her methods, she displayed a terrifying competence. She met the alarmed parents with a charming, easy grace, a "rondeur et franchise" that disarmed severity.

"Ce pauvre Docteur Jean!" she would chuckle, rubbing her soft white hands. "Such a dear young man! The best creature in the world!" She spun a tale of her own children's adoration for him, framing his employment as a temporary necessity for Blanche and Angélique's migraines. The parents, charmed by her maternal affectation, fell silent. The girls themselves joined in, loudly praising the physician, and Madame emerged from the crisis with flying colors, her reputation enhanced.

Yet, I could not fathom why she risked her standing so openly for Dr. John. The consensus of the house—pupils, staff, and servants alike—was that she intended to marry him. The disparity in their ages seemed irrelevant to them; it was simply to be. Appearances supported this theory. Madame, usually dressed in simple morning disarray, now appeared in full toilette: auburn braids, silk dresses, and laced brodequins, looking fresh as a flower for his early visits. Was she hoping to secure a comfortable future for him with her savings? Did Dr. John suspect her? He often left her presence with a mischievous smile, his eyes alight with a masculine vanity that suggested he encouraged hopes he never truly intended to fulfill. Or perhaps he did intend them. They were on excellent terms, and in a world where few marry for love, we waited to see what would happen.

What Dr. John waited for, I could not discern. His manner had changed; he was no longer merely attentive but expectant, vigilant, and absorbed. He seemed to range far beyond my understanding.

One morning, Georgette's fever worsened. She was peevish, crying out, "Minnie, Minnie, me very poorly!" I waited anxiously for the doctor, but when the bell rang and he was admitted, he did not ascend to the nursery as usual. Ten minutes passed, then twenty. The corridor remained empty. Where was he?

Driven by worry, I descended to investigate. The hallway was deserted. I listened intently. In the rooms nearby, pupils practiced instruments and sang barcarolles, their voices filling the air with "fraîchë" and "brisë." But in the small cabinet adjacent to the corridor—the portress's private room—I heard a different sound. A giddy, treble laugh mingled with a man's voice, soft and deep, uttering a desperate plea: "For God's sake!"

Then Dr. John emerged. His eyes were shining, but not with triumph; his fair English cheeks were flushed, and his brow bore the lines of baffled torture and anxiety. I stood behind the slightly ajar door, hidden in the shadows. He seemed mortified, his soul wounded by some cruel injustice. Who could hold such power over him? Madame was in her chamber; the room belonged to Rosine Matou, the portress—a pretty, fickle, and mercenary grisette. Surely, it was not her hand that had dealt this blow?

Before I could ponder further, Rosine's voice cut through the air, clear and sharp, singing a lightsome French tune. I glanced inside. She sat alone at a table, dressed in a smart pink jaconas gown, trimming a tiny blond cap. The room was bright with a July sunbeam, containing only goldfish in a globe and potted flowers.

A problem presented itself, but duty called. I ascended the stairs to inquire about Georgette's medicine.

In the nursery, the scene had shifted. Dr. John sat by the little patient's bed, having soothed her into a composed slumber. Madame Beck stood before him, discussing his health with exaggerated concern, noting his pallor and thinness. He listened with good-natured indifference, dismissing her worries as "trop bonne."

"What do you think, Miss Lucie?" Madame asked, turning to me. "Is he not paler and thinner?"

Rarely did I speak more than a monosyllable in his presence, content to remain the neutral figure he perceived me to be. Yet, this moment demanded a response. I chose my words carefully, making them significant.

"He looks ill at this moment," I replied, my voice steady. "But perhaps it is owing to some temporary cause: Dr. John may have been vexed or harassed."

I did not watch his face for confirmation, but I sensed the weight of my words. As Georgette asked for sugar water in broken English, Dr. John finally noticed that I spoke his tongue. He paused, as if considering a remark, but held his tongue.

When he finally rose to leave, bidding Madame good morning with a courteous yet dismissive air, the transformation in Madame was instantaneous. The animation vanished from her face, replaced by a stony, stern expression. She sighed—a deep, singular sound that seemed to drain the color from the room.

As the bell rang for morning school, she moved to a dressing table. In the mirror, she saw a single white streak among her nut-brown tresses. With a shudder, she plucked it out. In the harsh summer light, the texture of her youth had faded; the contours of her face had softened into something older. For the first time, I felt pity for Madame Beck. A calamity had struck her, and the hag of Disappointment greeted her with a grim "All-hail."

But what of Rosine? My confusion regarding the portress was profound. I passed her cabinet five times that day, seeking to understand the secret of her influence. She was pretty, young, and well-dressed—qualities sufficient to distract a young man like Dr. John. Still, I could not help wishing he were my brother, or that he had a sister to admonish him. I quickly discarded this thought as foolish; someone might as well preach to Madame about her physician.

Madame, I realized, was preaching to herself. She did not behave weakly or ridiculously. Lacking strong passions to overwhelm her, she relied on her genuine good sense and her demanding business to maintain her composure. She had matched an Apollyon of a predilection, fought a good fight, and, for now, overcome it. Brava, Madame Beck.
