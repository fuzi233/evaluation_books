While Madame de Bernstein found her amusement in the card rooms of Tunbridge Wells, scolding her niece and pursuing her games with the same sharp wit that had long characterized her, young Harry Warrington embraced his idleness with the unapologetic zeal of youth. At twenty, every pleasure is fresh, every diversion a delight. The world is still a place of eager anticipation, where the next ball, the next hunt, the next bottle shared with good company holds the promise of unspoiled joy.

Harry had become something of a prince among the fashionable idlers of the Wells. His pockets were deep, his laughter ready, and his skill at cards, dice, and outdoor sports marked him as a man worth knowing. He dined at the best ordinaries, discoursed with French waiters in their own tongue, and could debate the merits of Burgundy versus Bordeaux with the discernment of a seasoned connoisseur. Yet for all his extravagance, there was a shrewdness in him—a Virginian practicality—that kept him from sinking into the ruinous excesses of some of his companions.

His reputation as a gallant had preceded him, though not all the tales were true. When the scheming mother of the opera singer Cattarina—she of the earlier scandal—came weeping to his door, lamenting her daughter’s debts and her father’s imprisonment, Harry listened with polite skepticism. But when the woman, with a leer, suggested that certain *favors* might settle the matter, his revulsion was swift and final. He showed her the door with a cold dignity that would have done credit to a plantation master dismissing a dishonest overseer.

Nor did he suffer fools gladly at the gaming table. When Captain Batts, a notorious sharp, pressed him for another round of piquet and grew quarrelsome at Harry’s refusal, the young man met him with cool defiance. "I play only with gentlemen who pay their debts," said Harry, and the Captain, finding no weakness to exploit, slunk away, leaving his own markers unpaid behind him.

But it was in defense of others that Harry’s true mettle shone. When a drunken chairman insulted old Miss Whiffler on her way to the assembly, Harry knocked the fellow into the gutter without a second thought. And when the gossipmonger Hector Buckler repeated slanders about Harry and the opera dancer in the pump-room, Harry confronted him publicly, declaring the tale a lie and demanding satisfaction should Buckler dare repeat it.

Word of these exploits reached Colonel Wolfe, who had once doubted Harry’s character. Now, convinced of the young man’s integrity, he rode to the Lambert household to set the record straight. The joy that news brought was immeasurable. Hetty danced, Theo played the harpsichord with uncharacteristic brilliance, and even Dr. Boyle, their evening guest, raised a glass to Harry’s vindication.

"What shall we do now, my dear?" Colonel Lambert murmured to his wife, a mischievous glint in his eye.

"The very thing!" she exclaimed, grasping his meaning at once.

Young Charley, home from school, looked between them. "What thing?"

"Why, celebrate with supper, of course!" laughed the Colonel. And so they did, toasting repentance for those who had doubted, and to the enduring goodness of a certain young Virginian.

Thus ended Harry’s idle days at Tunbridge Wells—days of laughter, folly, and, beneath it all, the unshaken honor of a gentleman.
