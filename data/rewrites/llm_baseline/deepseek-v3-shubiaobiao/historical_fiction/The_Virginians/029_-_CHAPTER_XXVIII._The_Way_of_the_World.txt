In the genteel bustle of Tunbridge Wells, young Harry Warrington found himself an object of considerable fascination. Within days of his arrival, whispers of his Virginian wealth and recent gambling successes spread through the spa town like wildfire. Matrons eyed him with speculative interest, their daughters blushed over their teacups, and even the local tradesmen bowed deeper when he passed. His servant, Gumbo, became a minor celebrity himself, plied with ale and gossip by those eager for tales of his master’s exploits.

Harry bore his sudden notoriety with amused detachment. Accustomed to the indulgent admiration of his home, he accepted the attention without vanity, though not without pleasure. He had been raised to believe in his own consequence, and Tunbridge’s flattery only confirmed what he already knew: he was a gentleman, and the world was his to enjoy.

Yet enjoyment, as he soon discovered, came with its own perils. The society of the Wells was a whirl of cards, races, and whispered scandals—a world where reputations were made and unmade between one hand of whist and the next. Harry, with his open purse and open heart, proved an eager participant. He rode with the gentlemen, wagered with the lords, and drank with the officers, all while his aunt, Madame de Bernstein, watched with indulgent amusement.

It was she who encouraged his forays into fashion, selecting lace for his cuffs and jewels for his attire. She delighted in his triumphs, whether over a rival at tennis or across the card table. But not all observers were so charmed. His friendship with the vivacious Cattarina, a French dancer of questionable reputation, raised eyebrows—and when he was seen escorting her through the public walks, the whispers grew louder.

“Boys will be boys,” Madame Bernstein remarked airily to her scandalized niece, Maria. “I’d rather he sow his wild oats now than turn into a dullard.”

Yet not everyone dismissed Harry’s behavior so lightly. Colonel Wolfe, the stern and principled soldier, watched with deepening disapproval. When he confronted Harry one evening, his words were blunt.

“You keep company with men who disgrace their rank and women who disgrace their sex,” Wolfe said coldly. “I will not pretend to tolerate it.”

Stung, Harry defended himself. “Cattarina is harmless! She’s alone here, with no one to speak for her. Must I abandon her because small minds gossip?”

Wolfe’s expression darkened. “Do you truly not know what they say of you? That you won her in a wager? That you parade her like a trophy?”

The accusation struck like a blow. Harry had heard the murmurs, of course—the sly insinuations at the card table, the arch glances when he passed. But to hear them laid bare by a man he respected was another matter entirely.

Wolfe, seeing his dismay, relented slightly. “I tell you this because I believe you are better than your reputation. But reputations, once tarnished, are not so easily mended.”

Harry left the conversation shaken. For the first time, he saw himself as others did: not as the fortunate young Virginian, but as another reckless wastrel in a town full of them. That night, he drank more than he intended, laughed louder than he felt, and lost more than he should have at the faro table.

Dawn found him bleary-eyed and hollow-chested, the taste of champagne gone stale in his mouth. He had come to Tunbridge seeking amusement. Now he wondered—was this truly the life he wanted? Or was there something more?

The answer, if it existed, remained just out of reach.
