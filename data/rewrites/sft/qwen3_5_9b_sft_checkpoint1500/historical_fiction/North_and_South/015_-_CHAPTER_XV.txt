*"Thought fights with thought; out springs a spark of truth
From the collision of the sword and shield."*
—W. S. Landor

"Margaret," her father said the following morning, "we must return Mrs. Thornton's call tomorrow. Your mother feels unwell and cannot walk so far, but you and I will go this afternoon."

As they strolled together, Mr. Hale gently broached the subject of Mrs. Hale's health, his tone veiled with a quiet anxiety that finally pierced through Margaret's composure.

"Have you consulted a physician, Margaret?" he asked softly. "Did you send for one?"

"No, Papa. You mentioned sending for him when I was ill, and I am perfectly well now. But I fear Mother is seriously indisposed. If only I knew a competent doctor, I would request him to visit us today."

She spoke plainly, driven by a need to break her father's stubborn denial. Yet, his mood had shifted. With a despondent sigh, he confessed his deepest dread.

"Do you suspect a hidden ailment? Is she truly gravely ill? Has Miss Dixon offered any clues? Oh, Margaret! I am terrified that our arrival in Milton has undone her. My poor Maria!"

"Oh, Papa, do not imagine such horrors," Margaret protested, shocked. "She is merely unwell. Many endure temporary ailments; with proper care, they recover stronger than before."

"But has Miss Dixon said anything?"

"No. You know how she enjoys wrapping trifles in mystery. Her secrecy about Mother's health has worried me, though likely without cause. As you noted recently, I tend toward fanciful imaginings."

"I trust you do, though I admit I sounded annoyed then. I would rather you indulge in fantasies concerning your mother's health! Do not fear voicing them. Moreover, let us inquire of Mrs. Thornton regarding a reputable physician. We shall not squander funds on anyone lacking distinction. Come, we turn up Marlborough Street."

To Margaret, the street offered no glimpse of grandeur suitable for Mrs. Thornton. Her son's demeanor usually masked his domestic circumstances, yet unconsciously, Margaret had envisioned a residence matching her own stature. Instead, Marlborough Street revealed long rows of modest cottages and blank walls.

"He assured me he resides here," Mr. Hale remarked, looking perplexed. "Perhaps it reflects some remaining frugality."

"There are many passersby; let me ask."

Margaret queried a neighbor, who directed them near the mill, pointing out the factory lodge at the end of a long, barren wall. The lodge itself resembled a standard garden gate, flanked by massive iron portals for heavy wagons. Beyond lay a vast rectangular courtyard bordered by business offices on one side and the colossal mill on the other. Inside, the relentless clanking of machinery and the thunderous groans of the steam engine created a deafening roar, suffocating the silence within the enclosure.

Opposite the industrial sprawl stood a handsome stone house, its facade blackened by soot yet meticulously maintained. Paint, windows, and steps gleamed with scrupulous cleanliness. Built fifty or sixty years prior, it bore the marks of age: stone copings, narrow windows, and dual staircases leading to the front door, all framed by iron railings. To Margaret, accustomed to the countryside, the situation baffled. Why would anyone, capable of maintaining such perfection amidst the factory's turmoil, settle for this urban cacophony instead of a quieter rural retreat?

Standing on the steps, Margaret barely registered her father's voice. The courtyard, bounded by the silent, dead wall, seemed a grim antechamber to the drawing-room. Upon ascending the old-fashioned stairs, they were ushered into a space that appeared untouched since the furniture had been carefully bagged centuries ago. The walls were a faded pink and gold; the floral-patterned carpet was concealed beneath a stark, glazed linen runner. Lace curtains draped the windows, and every chair and sofa was shrouded in delicate nets or knitted covers. Alabaster statues sat on pedestals behind glass shields, immune to dust.

In the center, beneath a chandelier wrapped in protective cloth, stood a large circular table. Neatly bound books lined its polished surface like colorful spokes of a wheel. Every object reflected light; nothing absorbed it. The room possessed a painfully spotty, speckled quality—a sterile brilliance that unsettled Margaret. It suggested immense labor and care, yet not for comfort or tranquility, but solely for ornamentation and the preservation of that ornament from decay. It was a display of icy, snowy discomfort.

Before Mrs. Thornton's arrival, the couple conversed in hushed tones, acutely aware of the room's acoustic sensitivity. Finally, she entered, rustling in handsome black silk. Her muslins and laces matched the pristine whiteness of the room's decor, though perhaps lacked its artistic soul.

Margaret explained her mother's inability to join them, but fearing her father's anxieties, she delivered a vague, clumsy account. To Mrs. Thornton, this implied merely a temporary, fanciful indisposition of the Hales' sort—one easily dismissed if motivation were absent—or perhaps a slight illness allowing the call to be postponed. Reminded of the horses hired for her own visit and Fanny's instruction to pay strict respect to the family, Mrs. Thornton drew herself up, slightly offended, offering little sympathy or credence to the statement of Mrs. Hale's condition.

"How fares Mr. Thornton?" Mr. Hale inquired. "His hurried note yesterday concerned me."

"My son is rarely ill," Mrs. Thornton replied smoothly. "When he is, he neither complains nor uses it as an excuse for idleness. He told me he lacked leisure to read with you last night, sir, though I am certain he regrets it. He values your companionship."

"I am sure the sentiment is mutual," Mr. Hale agreed warmly. "Seeing his enthusiasm for classical literature rejuvenates me."

"While classics are delightful for those with leisure, I confess it defied my judgment when my son resumed his studies," Mrs. Thornton countered, her tone sharp. "The time and place of our existence demand all his energy. Classics suit those who loaf away their days in the country or colleges. A Milton manufacturer must devote his entire mind and power to the work of today. That is my conviction." She added this final clause with "the pride that apes humility."

"But surely, if the mind focuses too long on a single object, it becomes rigid, incapable of embracing diverse interests," Margaret interjected.

"I fail to comprehend what you mean by 'stiff and rigid.' Nor do I admire those 'whirligig' characters who abandon one passion today for another tomorrow. Diversity of interest suits not the life of a Milton manufacturer. He requires one supreme desire, directing all his purposes toward its fulfillment."

"And that desire is—" Mr. Hale prompted.

Mrs. Thornton's sallow cheeks flushed, her eyes lighting up. "To uphold and maintain a high, honorable station among the merchants of his nation—and indeed of Europe. The name of John Thornton of Milton is known and respected by all men of business. Naturally, it remains unknown in fashionable circles," she continued, with a sneer. "Idlers, gentlemen and ladies alike, know little of a Milton manufacturer unless he enters parliament or marries a lord's daughter."

Both Mr. Hale and Margaret felt a distinct, awkward sensation; they had never heard of this celebrated figure until Mr. Bell wrote to introduce him. The proud mother's sphere of influence differed vastly from theirs, whether the Harley Street socialites or the clergy of the south. Margaret's face, despite her efforts to remain neutral, betrayed a sense of distance.

"You think you've never heard of my wonderful son, Miss Hale?" Mrs. Thornton challenged. "You believe I am an old woman obsessed with Milton, and that my own crow is the whitest ever seen?"

"No," Margaret replied with spirit. "It may be true I'd scarcely heard of Mr. Thornton before arriving in Milton. But since then, I have heard enough to respect and admire him, and to recognize the truth in your words."

"Who spoke to you of him?" Mrs. Thornton asked, softening slightly yet guarding against any rival accounts.

Margaret hesitated, disliking the authoritative query. Mr. Hale stepped in.

"It was Mr. Thornton himself who revealed his character to us, was it not, Margaret?"

Mrs. Thornton straightened. "My son does not recount his own deeds. Pray, tell me, Miss Hale, from whose account did you derive your favorable impression? A mother is naturally curious and eager for praise of her offspring."

"It was largely what Mr. Thornton *withheld*, Miss Hale," Margaret responded carefully. "More than what he said, it was his reticence regarding his past life, as recounted by Mr. Bell, that compelled us to feel the depth of your pride."

"Mr. Bell! What does he know of John? He leads a lazy life in a drowsy college." Mrs. Thornton sighed. "Still, I thank you, Miss Hale. Many a young lady would shrink from lending an old woman the satisfaction of hearing her son praised."

"Why?" Margaret asked, staring intently.

"Because, I suppose, they possess consciences telling them how swiftly they become advocates for their suitors, should they harbor designs on his heart."

A grim smile played on her lips; she enjoyed Margaret's frankness and perhaps realized she had interrogated too freely. Margaret burst into laughter, a merry sound that grated on Mrs. Thornton's nerves, sounding absurdly incongruous in the stiff atmosphere.
