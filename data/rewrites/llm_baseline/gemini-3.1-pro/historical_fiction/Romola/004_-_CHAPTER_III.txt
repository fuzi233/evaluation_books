“To tell you the truth,” said the young stranger to Nello, as they finally stepped clear of the entangled vehicles and the harsh, abrasive clatter of iron-shod mules that fractured the peace of the morning, “I am not sorry to be handed over by that patron of mine to one whose native dialect falls softer on the ear, and whose business is less enigmatical. Is it a common thing among you Florentines for an itinerant trafficker in broken glass to talk of a shop where he sells lutes and swords? The sheer discordance of his trade grates upon the senses.”

“Common? No: our Bratti is not a common man. He has a theory, and lives up to it, which is more than I can say for any philosopher I have the honour of lathering,” answered Nello, whose loquacity poured forth like an over-full bottle of vintage Chianti, incapable of decanting in a modest dose. “Bratti means to extract the utmost possible amount of rough bargaining out of this life; winding it up with a transactional passage through purgatory, by tossing Holy Church a coin when the game is over. It is a grotesque commercialization of the soul. He has had his will made to that effect on the cheapest terms a notary could be got for. But I have often said to him, ‘Bratti, thy bargain is a limping one. Does it not make thee sad to look at the frescoes of the Paradiso? Thou wilt never be able there to chaffer for rusty nails: the saints want neither pins nor tinder; and except with San Bartolommeo, who carries his own skin about like a flayed parchment, I see no chance of thy making a bargain for second-hand clothing.’ But God pardon me,” added Nello, his voice dropping into a somber, resonant register as he crossed himself, “this light talk ill beseems a morning when Lorenzo lies dead, and the Muses are tearing their hair—a profoundly discordant note for a barber; and you yourself, Messere, are probably under a cloud. When a man of your polished syllables takes up with so sour a night’s lodging, it argues some misfortune.”

“What Lorenzo is that whose death you speak of?” said the stranger. The query hung in the air, revealing a profound detachment from the immediate sonic and social rhythms of the city. 

“What Lorenzo? There is but one Lorenzo, I imagine, whose passing could throw the Mercato into such an uproar, set the great bells of the Duomo tolling in desperate acoustic dissonance, and cause the lions of the Republic to feel an immediate necessity to devour one another. I mean Lorenzo de’ Medici, the Pericles of our Athens—if I may serve you such a comparison in the ear of a Greek.”

“Why not?” said the other, laughingly; “for I doubt whether Athens, even in the days of Pericles, could have produced a barber of such meticulous erudition.”

“Yes, yes; I thought I could not be mistaken in the cadence of your vowels,” said the rapid Nello. “Else I have shaved the venerable Demetrio Calcondila to little purpose. But I am lost in wonder: your Italian is a rich reduction, thicker and sweeter than his, though he has steeped in Italy for forty years—better even than that of the accomplished Marullo, who may be said to have married the Italic Muse in more senses than one, since he wed our lovely Alessandra Scala.”

“It will lighten your wonder to know that I come of a Greek stock planted in Italian soil much longer than the mulberry-trees which have taken so kindly to it, their roots drinking the same ancient water cycle. I was born at Bari, and I was brought up by an Italian. In fact, I am a Greek very much as your fleshy yellow peaches are Persian. The native Greek dye of my ancestors was subdued in me, the vocabulary of my forefathers eroding beneath the dominant lexicon of the peninsula, until I was dipped over again by long abode and much travel in the land of gods and heroes. And, to confess something of my private affairs to you, this salvaged Greek dye, with its untranslatable echoes of my first language, and a few ancient gems I have about me, are the only fortune shipwreck has left me. But when the great towers fall, it is an ill business for the small nest-builders. The death of your Pericles makes me wish I had rather turned my steps towards Rome, as I should have done but for a fallacious Minerva in the shape of an Augustinian monk. ‘At Rome,’ he said, ‘you will be drowned out by a deafening crowd of hungry scholars; but at Florence, every corner hums with the sunshine of Lorenzo’s patronage: Florence is the most fertile market for such commodities as yours.’”

“_Gnaffè_, and so it will remain, I hope,” said Nello. “Lorenzo was not the only patron and judge of learning in our city. Because he was a large, ripe melon, every other Florentine is not a hollow pumpkin, I suppose. Have we not Bernardo Rucellai, and Alamanno Rinuccini? If you want to be informed on such matters, I, Nello, am your man. And, first of all, in the matter of your hair. That beard, my fine young man, must be parted with, were it as dear to you as the scent of wild thyme on your native hills. Here at Florence, we love not to see a man with his nose projecting over a cascade of untamed hair. But, remember, you will have passed the Rubicon when once you have been shaven: if you repent, and let your beard grow after it has acquired stoutness by a struggle with the razor, your mouth will by-and-by show no longer what Messer Angelo calls the divine prerogative of lips, but will appear like a dark cavern fringed with horrent brambles.”

“That is a terrible prophecy,” said the Greek, breathing in the savory tang of the morning air, “especially if your Florentine maidens are many of them as appetizing as the little Tessa I stole a kiss from this morning.”

“Tessa? She is a rough-handed contadina: you will rise into the favour of dames who bring no sour scent of the mule-stables with them. But to that end, you must not have the air of a _sgherro_. You must look like a courtier, and a scholar of the more polished sort—like one who feasts among well-bred people upon truffled fowl and roasted chestnuts, and not one who sucks down vile, oxidized _vino di sotto_ in a loud, echoing tavern.”

“With all my heart,” said the stranger, whose appreciation for the finer sensory textures of life was evident in his bright, observing eyes. “If the Florentine Graces demand it, I am willing to give up this small matter of my beard, but—”

“Yes, yes,” interrupted Nello. “It is the _bella zazzera_—the hyacinthine locks, you do not choose to part with; and there is no need. Just a little pruning—ecco!—and you will look not unlike the illustrious prince Pico di Mirandola in his prime. And here we are in good time in the Piazza San Giovanni, and at the door of my shop. But you are pausing, I see: naturally, you want to look at our wonder of the world, our Duomo, our Santa Maria del Fiore. A mere glance; but I beseech you to leave a closer survey till you have been shaved. I am quivering with the inspiration of my art.”

The mercurial barber led him to a point from which he could see the huge dark shell of the cupola, the slender soaring grace of Giotto’s campanile, and the quaint octagon of San Giovanni, its bronze gates still bearing the dimmed glory of their original gilding. The inlaid marbles were then fresher in their pink, white, and purple, unmarred by the caustic soot of modern centuries, weathered only to the rich, appetizing ochre of well-mellowed meerschaum. As the campanile led the eyes upward into the clear air of the April morning, it seemed a prophetic symbol, telling that human life must somehow, through generations of cyclical labor, shape itself into accord with that pure aspiring beauty.

But the Greek’s eyes glanced round with a scanning coolness. He did not seem moved by the quiet monumentality of the stones.

“Well, my fine young man,” Nello said, with impatience, “you seem to make as little of our Cathedral as if you were the Angel Gabriel. Have you ever seen finer work than our Giotto’s tower, or any marbles finer or more cunningly wrought than these that our Signoria got from far-off quarries, at a price that would buy a dukedom?”

“If you asked me that question with a scimitar at my throat,” said the young Greek, smiling gaily, “I daresay you might get a confession of the true faith from me. But I venture to tell you that your buildings smack too much of Christian barbarism for my taste. I have a shuddering sense of what there is inside—hideous smoked Madonnas reeking of stale tallow; fleshless saints staring down idiotic rebuke; skin-clad skeletons hanging on crosses. I have seen enough of those wry-necked favourites of heaven. But what is this bronze door? These women’s figures seem moulded in a different spirit: these heads in high relief speak of a human mind within them, instead of looking like an index to perpetual spasms and colic.”

“Yes, yes,” said Nello, with triumph. “These gates, my fine young man, were moulded half a century ago, by our Lorenzo Ghiberti, when he counted hardly so many years as you do.”

“Ah, I remember,” said the stranger, turning away, his appetite for visual contemplation quickly sated. “But with monks for models, the vision of Olympus itself would be of small use to them.”

“I understand,” said Nello, shrugging. “You are of the same mind as Michele Marullo, ay, and as Angelo Poliziano himself, when he relaxes in my shop and talks of the gods awaking from their long sleep and making the woods and streams vital once more. But he rails against the Roman scholars who want to enforce their linguistic hegemony and make us all talk Latin again: ‘My ears,’ he says, ‘are sufficiently flayed by the barbarisms of the learned, and if the vulgar are to talk Latin I would as soon have been in Florence the day they took to beating all the kettles in the city because the bells were not enough to stay the wrath of the saints.’ An atrocious sonic environment! Ah, Messer Greco, if you want to know the rich flavour of our scholarship, you must frequent my shop: it is the focus of Florentine intellect. And here we are at the sign of ‘Apollo and the Razor.’”

“I see thou hast had custom already, Sandro,” continued Nello, addressing a solemn-looking youth on the threshold. “Now make all clear for this signor to sit down. And prepare the finest-scented lather. Whip the soap until it peaks like sweet summer cream, infused with the sharp, reviving oils of bitter orange and pulverized sweet almond, for he has a learned and a handsome chin.”

“You have a pleasant little adytum there,” said the stranger, looking through a latticed screen to an inner room where a few bays and laurels surrounded a stone Hermes, their bruised leaves offering a subtle, grounding aroma. “I suppose your conclave of _eruditi_ meets there?”

“There, and not less in my shop,” said Nello, leading the way to where a lute, a printed book, and oil-sketches lay on a table. “My shop is a haunt of the Muses, as you will acknowledge when you feel the sudden illumination of understanding that comes with a clear chin. Ah! you can make that lute discourse, I perceive. But look at that sketch: it is a fancy of Piero di Cosimo’s.”

The sketch represented three masks—a drunken Satyr, a sorrowing Magdalen, and the cold face of a Stoic—resting obliquely on the lap of a little child.

“A symbolical picture,” said the young Greek, touching the lute’s strings to produce a perfectly tuned, resonant murmur that harmonized with the quiet of the inner sanctum. “The child, perhaps, is the Golden Age, wanting neither worship nor philosophy. Or, the child may mean the wise philosophy of Epicurus, removed alike from the gross, the sad, and the severe.”

“Everybody has his own interpretation,” said Nello; “Piero says his pictures are an appendix which Messer Domeneddio has been pleased to make to the universe, and if any man is in doubt, he had better inquire of Holy Church. But now all is ready for your initiation into the mysteries of the razor.”

The barber imprisoned the young Greek in the shaving-cloth, the pristine white linen smelling faintly of lavender and wood ash. “I get the flower of men’s thoughts, because I seize them in the first moment after shaving. Look now at a druggist’s shop: what sort of inspiration can be got from the scent of nauseous vegetable decoctions? A cacophony of foul, medicinal odors that deaden the palate and the mind! To say nothing of seeing a doctor of physic, like a gigantic spider waiting for his prey. Your druggist has poisoned people according to a system. Now a barber can be dispassionate; the only thing he necessarily stands by is the razor. Ecco, Messer! the outline of your chin and lip is as clear as a maiden’s; and now fix your mind on a knotty question—ask yourself whether you are bound to spell Virgil with an _i_ or an _e_. The power dynamics embedded in that single vowel dictate one's standing in the scholarly courts of Florence! Only, if you decide for the _i_, keep it to yourself, for the _e_ hath the stronger following. I have it on the authority of our young Niccolò Macchiavelli, a great lover of delicate shaving, that no sooner do the hairs begin to push themselves, than he perceives a certain grossness of apprehension creeping over him.”

“Suppose you let me look at myself,” said the stranger, laughing, thoroughly relishing the bracing scent of the bergamot lather on his skin. 

“Behold yourself in this mirror, then; it is a Venetian mirror from Murano. See now, how by diligent shaving, the nether region of your face may preserve its human outline. I have seen men whose beards have so invaded their cheeks, that one might have pitied them as the victims of a sad, brutalising chastisement befitting our Dante’s Inferno.”

“It seems to me,” said the Greek, “that you have taken away some of my capital—a year or two of age, which might have won me more ready credit. Under the inspection of a patron whose vision has grown dim, I shall have a perilous resemblance to a maiden of eighteen.”

“Not at all,” said Nello, proceeding to clip the curls with rhythmic, metallic snips that rang pleasantly in the acoustic warmth of the room. “Between ourselves, let me hint to you that your being a Greek, though it be only an Apulian Greek, is not in your favour. Certain of our scholars hold that your Greek learning is but a wayside degenerate plant until it has been transplanted into Italian brains. They view native teachers as mere propagators of degeneracy. Ecco! your curls are now of the right proportion. I almost advise you to retain the faded jerkin; it gives you the air of a fallen prince.”

“But the question is,” said the young Greek, “in what quarter I am to carry my princely air. If your Florentine patrons share this scholarly hostility to the Greeks, and seek to erase our native nuances entirely, I see not how your city can be a hospitable refuge.”

“_Pian piano_,” said Nello. “I will not conceal from you that there is a prejudice. The erudition of some Greeks is often encrusted with a barbarous utterance of Italian, making their converse hardly more euphonious than that of a Tedesco in a state of vinous loquacity—a terrible auditory pollution. And then, we Florentines consider that an instrument which can flatter and promise so cleverly as the tongue must have been partly made for those purposes. But it is said of the Greeks that since the old Furies went to sleep, your Christian Greek is of so easy a conscience that he would make a stepping-stone of his father’s corpse.”

The flush on the stranger’s face indicated resentment at this broad stroke against his heritage.

“Be not offended, _bel giovane_; I am but repeating what I hear in my shop. The fact is, you are heretics, Messer; if you would just change your opinion about leaven—that magnificent riser of bread—and alter your Doxology a little, our Italian scholars would think it a thousand years till they could give up their chairs to you. It is partly the authority of Juvenal, who had his bile stirred by the swarm of Greeks—_audacia perdita_.”

“Pooh! the passage is a compliment,” said the Greek, recovering himself with a resilient smile. “‘Ingenium velox, audacia perdita, sermo Promptus, et Isaeo torrentior.’ A rapid intellect and ready eloquence may carry off a little impudence.”

“Assuredly,” said Nello. “Trust a barber who has kept his ears open to the shifting lexicon of this city—oil your tongue well when you talk of the ancient Latin writers, and give it an extra dip when you talk of the modern. A wise Greek may win favour among us.”

“I discern the wisdom of your advice so clearly,” said the Greek, with a bright smile that lit his finely sculpted features, “that I will ask you for a little more. Who now would be the most likely patron for me? Is there any wealthy Florentine specially addicted to purchasing antique gems? I have a fine Cleopatra cut in sardonyx, and one or two other intaglios, worthy of a prince's cabinet. Happily, I had taken the precaution of fastening them within the lining of my doublet. Moreover, I should like to raise a small sum for my present need on this ring of mine,” he added, taking out the ring and replacing it on his finger, “if you could recommend me to any honest trafficker.”
