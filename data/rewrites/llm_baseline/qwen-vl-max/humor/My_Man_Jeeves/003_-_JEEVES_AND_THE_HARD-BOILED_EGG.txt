There are moments—usually early in the morning, when I’m half-asleep and sipping tea like a man who’s just discovered it’s a liquid—when I’ve caught myself wondering what on earth I’d do if Jeeves ever decided to leave me. It’s not that he’s particularly attached to me; in fact, I suspect he views me more as a slightly amusing inconvenience than a human being with feelings. But still, the thought of life without him is enough to make my soul shiver.

Now, don’t get me wrong—I’m not saying he’s *my* best friend or anything sentimental like that. He’s just… very good at everything. Like, for instance, putting on a shirt. You know how some valets fumble with studs? Not Jeeves. He slides them into place with the precision of a surgeon performing an operation on a tiny clockwork mouse. It’s unnerving. And yet, oddly comforting.

He’s also the only person I can count on when things go sideways—which, let’s face it, they tend to do whenever I’m involved. Whether it’s a crisis involving a stolen hat, a misplaced monocle, or a sudden desire to grow a moustache (more on that later), Jeeves always appears, calm as a cucumber, with a plan that somehow makes sense even though it probably shouldn’t.

Take the case of Bicky and his uncle—the Duke of Chiswick, otherwise known as “the hard-boiled egg.” That’s what they called him in England: a man so frugal he once refused to buy a new pair of socks because the old ones were still technically wearable. If you asked me, he was less a nobleman and more a walking savings account with a title.

It all began after I’d been in New York for a few months. I came home late one night, feeling rather tired and vaguely guilty about having skipped dinner with the club, when Jeeves handed me my final drink with the news:

“Mr. Bickersteth called twice, sir, while you were out.”

“Oh?” I said, taking a sip of whisky. “And?”

“He appeared a trifle agitated, sir.”

“Pipped?”

“That impression, sir.”

I didn’t mind much—Bicky was always pipped, usually because he’d spent the last of his allowance on something ridiculous like a monkey costume or a set of fake teeth. But this time felt different. And anyway, I had my own troubles: namely, the moustache.

Yes, I’d grown one. A little thing, really—just a faint shadow above my lip—but it had sent Jeeves into a state of near-melancholy. He couldn’t stand it. Every time I walked into a room, he’d look at me like I’d committed a crime against fashion. It wasn’t personal, of course. He simply believed in symmetry and elegance. And apparently, facial hair doesn’t fit into either category unless it’s carefully groomed, which mine most certainly was not.

So we’d been living in silence, me with my moustache, him with his disapproval. It was tense. Like two people sharing a train compartment but refusing to acknowledge each other’s existence.

Then came Bicky.

He showed up looking like someone had run over him with a steamroller. His eyes were sunken, his suit was askew, and he smelled faintly of despair.

“Halloa, Bertie!” he croaked. “Jeeves told you I was trying to reach you?”

“Indeed he did,” I said. “What’s the matter, old chap?”

“I’m in a hole,” he said, sinking into a chair. “A deep one.”

I glanced at Jeeves, who stood motionless, hands behind his back, as if waiting for instructions from a superior officer.

“Jeeves,” I said, “this is serious. Can you help?”

“Certainly, sir,” he replied, as though he’d already written the solution in invisible ink on the ceiling.

Bicky looked at him like he was a miracle worker.

“It’s about my uncle,” he said. “He’s coming tomorrow. The Duke of Chiswick.”

I blinked. “The *Duke*?”

“Yes,” said Jeeves, calmly. “His Grace.”

“You’re related to a duke?” I said. “That’s news.”

“Not directly,” said Bicky. “My father was his sister’s husband. Captain Rollo Bickersteth of the Coldstream Guards. Dead now. Left nothing.”

Ah. That explained why Bicky was perpetually broke. He had a duke for an uncle, yes—but one who treated money like it was made of glass and could crack if you looked at it too hard.

“Anyway,” Bicky continued, “the old boy wanted me to go to Colorado and learn ranching. I said no. Horses bite, Bertie. They’re vicious. I hate them. So instead, I cabled and told him I’d found a great business opportunity in New York. He wrote back saying fine, carry on. I’ve been here ever since, pretending I’m making a fortune.”

“And now he’s coming to visit?”

“Exactly. And I’ve been telling him I live in luxury. In a flat. With servants. All that jazz. But I’m staying in a boarding house. What am I supposed to do?”

I turned to Jeeves.

“What should he do?”

Jeeves paused. Then spoke.

“If I may suggest, sir, perhaps Mr. Bickersteth could borrow your flat.”

“Borrow it?” I said. “You mean… pretend it’s his?”

“Precisely, sir. With your permission, I could assume the role of his valet. You would be his guest. His grace would occupy the second spare bedroom. And a wireless message could be sent to inform him of the change of address.”

Bicky stared.

“You mean… I could tell him I own this place?”

“Absolutely, sir.”

We both stared at Jeeves. He gave a small nod, as if confirming a fact everyone already knew.

“Well,” said Bicky, “that’s clever. How do you think of these things?”

“I believe it has something to do with the shape of my head,” Jeeves replied. “It sticks out at the back.”

Bicky laughed. “I’ll take your word for it.”

The next morning, I woke early to greet the arrival of the Duke. Ocean liners, as anyone who’s ever been near one knows, arrive at unearthly hours. By nine o’clock, I was dressed, had my tea, and was leaning out the window, watching the street below.

And then—chaos.

A taxi pulled up. Out stepped a man in a top hat, waving his arms like a conductor leading a symphony of outrage. He was arguing with the cab driver over the fare.

“The trip from the dock to here would have cost eightpence in London!” he shouted.

The cabby shrugged. “This is New York, pal. Eight shillings.”

The Duke looked like he’d been punched in the stomach.

I called to Jeeves.

“The Duke’s arrived.”

“Yes, sir.”

Sure enough, Jeeves opened the door and ushered in the Duke of Chiswick, who looked like he’d just survived a war.

“How do you do, sir?” I said, stepping forward. “Your nephew met you at the dock, but you must have missed him. I’m Wooster. Great pal of Bicky’s. I’m staying here. Would you like a cup of tea?”

He sank into an armchair.

“Does this flat belong to my nephew Francis?”

“Absolutely.”

“It must be terribly expensive.”

“Well, yes,” I said. “Everything costs more here. Especially taxis.”

He sighed. “A terrible country! Nearly eight shillings for a short ride! Iniquitous!”

Jeeves brought the tea. The Duke took a sip and nodded slowly.

“Have you any idea how much he pays for this place?”

“About two hundred dollars a month,” I said.

“Forty pounds!” he gasped. “In England, that would pay for a modest cottage!”

I saw where this was going. He was suspicious. And rightly so. Poor old Bicky wasn’t exactly known for his financial acumen.

“So,” I said, “you might say Bicky has changed. New York brings out the best in people. Something in the air. Makes them sharp. Efficient. Businesslike. He’s doing quite well now. Everyone says so.”

“Really?” said the Duke. “What does he do?”

“Oh, just business,” I said. “Like Carnegie. Or Rockefeller. Big names.”

I left before he could ask another question.

Later that evening, I returned to find Bicky sitting in a chair, staring blankly at the wall, cigarette dangling from his mouth.

“Rotten luck,” he muttered. “He thinks I’m doing well. So he canceled my allowance.”

“What?”

“He said I don’t need it anymore. I’m making my own way.”

I felt sorry for him.

“We must do something,” I said.

Jeeves appeared, as if summoned by a silent bell.

“Sir?”

“Any ideas?”

“Yes, sir. Since we last spoke, I’ve considered a new approach.”

“Go on.”

“His grace is a prominent personage. Americans love shaking hands with prominent personages. Why not offer introductions—for a fee?”

Bicky snorted. “You want us to charge people to shake hands with my uncle?”

“I have an aunt,” said Jeeves, “who paid five shillings for a young man to bring a movie star to tea. It boosted her social standing.”

Bicky hesitated.

“If you think it could work…”

“I believe it will, sir.”

And so we launched the “Duke of Chiswick Experience.” We advertised locally. We offered handshake opportunities for $2.50. We even printed leaflets.

But nobody bit.

Until Jeeves stepped in.

One morning, he entered with tea and a smile.

“Might I speak to you, sir?”

“It’s off,” I said. “No one wants to meet the Duke.”

“I believe I’ve arranged it, sir.”

“Who?”

“Eighty-seven gentlemen from Birdsburg, Missouri.”

I dropped my tea.

“Birdsburg?”

“Yes, sir. Missouri.”

“How?”

“I sent a letter to the local newspaper. I mentioned that His Grace is visiting and is available for handshakes. The response was immediate.”

I sat back, stunned.

“Eighty-seven men from Missouri are coming to shake hands with my uncle?”

“Precisely, sir.”

And so they did.

They arrived in a convoy of wagons, wearing hats shaped like corn cobs, and demanded their introductions. The Duke, though bewildered, played along. And by the end of the week, Bicky had earned enough to cover his rent—and then some.

As for me? I learned a valuable lesson: never underestimate the power of a man who can turn a grumpy duke into a cash cow.

And Jeeves? Well, he just smiled, poured me another cup of tea, and said, “I believe the market is beginning to respond, sir.”

Which, of course, meant nothing to me—but sounded impressive.
