Martin’s clever little scheme woven for his own private amusement had been strung with all the deft precision of a spider spinning its web, but the unforgiving, red-armed housewife we call Fate has never spared even the most finely spun plans of schemers far older and wiser than him. This time, her broom was hewn straight from the tough, stubborn fibres of Robert Moore’s own iron will, newly sharpened through weeks of slow recovery, and nothing could stop it sweeping all other carefully laid arrangements out of his path.

He defied the formidable Mrs. Horsfall first, to her endless astonishment: one morning he refused to let her dress him, the next he turned away her specially brewed coffee and declared he would take breakfast with the rest of the Yorke family, and by week’s end he had barred her entirely from his bedchamber. The very same day, over the squawking protests of every woman in the house, he stuck his head out the front door into the crisp, frosty air. The next morning, he limped after Mr. Yorke to his counting house, and calmly requested an envoy to fetch a chaise from the Red House Inn. He was returning home to Hollow’s Cottage that very afternoon, he said, and no amount of arguing would shift him.

Far from opposing him, Mr. Yorke laughed and aided him, even as Mrs. Yorke wailed the foolhardy act would be his death. Moore spoke scarcely a word all the while, but his purse did all his talking for him: he pressed generous coins into every servant’s palm, and handed Mrs. Horsfall a sum so handsome it wiped every last memory of his earlier defiance clean away. The pair parted the best of friends.

He soothed the kitchen staff first, then made his way to the parlour to appease Mrs. Yorke, a far trickier task. She sat huffing in her chair, sunk deep in grievances over man’s base ingratitude, until he leaned over her, his pale, gaunt face still holding traces of quiet worn beauty, his hollow eyes soft with a shy, earnest smile.
“Goodbye,” he said, and the smile glistened and melted a little, for he was still too weak to master every small stirring of emotion.
“And what do you mean to leave us for?” she grumbled. “We’ll nurse you back to full strength, do anything in the world for you, if you’ll only stay a little longer.”
“Goodbye,” he repeated, and added gently, “You’ve been a mother to me. Won’t you give your wilful stray son one embrace?”
Half protesting, she let him lean in to kiss first one cheek, then the other, in his foreign fashion, and found herself kissing him back. “What a burden I’ve been to you,” he murmured.
“Worse than a wilful boy at that,” she sniffled. “Who on earth will nurse you at that tiny cottage? Your sister Hortense knows no more about tending an invalid than a five year old child.”
“Thank God for that,” he laughed low. “I’ve had more than enough of being coddled to last me a lifetime.”

The two little Yorke girls — Jessie red-eyed with tears and Rose quiet and grave — came in then, and he herded them out to the hall to kiss them, to pet their soft hair, knowing full well Mrs. Yorke would be annoyed to see him lavish affection on anything that wasn’t her. The boys were loitering by the chaise waiting for him when he stepped outside; he had no farewell words for them, only nodded to Mr. Yorke before he climbed in. “You’ll be glad to be rid of me,” he said. “That shot of mine turned your fine house into a hospital for weeks. Come visit me soon at the cottage.” He drew the carriage glass shut, and the chaise rumbled away down the lane.

Half an hour later, he alighted at his own garden wicket, paid off the driver, and leaned against the wood for a long minute, catching his breath and thinking. Six months ago, he had walked out this very gate a proud, angry, bitterly disappointed man, convinced his whole life was unraveling at the seams. Now he came back sadder, wiser, still weak on his feet, but untormented. The world around him was cold and grey, but quiet — and now that he no longer dreaded the thought of ruin, he had nothing left to fear. He knew the bankruptcy he had spent so many sleepless nights dreading would come, in six months’ time at the latest, but it no longer felt like dishonour. He could work beside Joe Scott, earn an honest living with his own two hands, and if the worst came, he and Louis would sail for America, plant a new life in the woods there. A quiet, unnameable thought tugged at the back of his mind: would anyone else come with them? He had no right to ask, not yet.

He pushed open the door and stepped inside. It was late afternoon, the sky heavy with frozen clouds, the frost biting sharp enough to freeze the mill dam over, the whole Hollow wrapped in stillness. Hortense came bustling over the second she saw him, ready to help him off with his heavy cloak. “I’m glad to be home at last,” he told her, the words soft — words he had never spoken of the small, cramped cottage before, that had always felt to him like a prison.

He sat by the fire, then got up and walked to the window, then paced back again. “This parlour looks so clean, so bright,” he said.
“I had the whole house scrubbed top to bottom while you were gone,” Hortense told him proudly.
“Then we ought to have a guest for tea,” he said, “to see all your hard work. Not Miss Mann — it’s too cold for her to walk all this way. Not Miss Ainley, she lives too far. Send Harry Scott to the rectory, tell Caroline Helstone we’d be honoured if she’d come spend the evening with us.”

Hortense fussed a little that it was too late, but he was firm, and ten minutes later Harry was off with a hasty pencil-scrawled note. Before Sarah had time to fret about her overdone toast, Caroline was through the kitchen, stopping to pet the new tortoiseshell kitten curled on the hearth, to murmur to the canary that the firelight had startled on its perch, to chat a minute with the cook before she tripped up the stairs to take off her bonnet and furs. She came back down with her soft curls neatly smoothed, her merino dress spotless, her little work bag hanging from her wrist, and the two cousins greeted each other with the quiet, gentle warmth of people who had ached to see each other for months. The new lamp burned bright, the singing tea urn hummed, and a soft, delicate pleasure, like the scent of wild rose, hung light in the small parlour.

“I am glad to be home,” Moore said again, as they all sat round the table. Hortense chattered most of the meal, gushing over how Caroline’s cheeks had filled out, how her colour had come back — it was true. There was a new lightness to her, all the old heavy depression and fear and loneliness melted away, as if she had drunk deep of a cordial of perfect heart’s ease.

After tea, Hortense suddenly remembered she had not sorted through her dress drawers in a month, and trotted off upstairs to tend to that long deferred task, leaving the two of them alone. Caroline took up the conversation easily, her voice softer than ever, with a new, sweet lilt to it, her face lighting up with animation he had never seen there before.
“Caroline, you look like you’ve heard nothing but good tidings lately,” he said, after watching her for several quiet, enchanted minutes.
“Do I?” she smiled.
“I sent for you this evening thinking you’d cheer me up,” he admitted. “But you’ve cheered me far more than I ever dared hope.”
She told him all about her mother, Mrs. Pryor — how the weeks of quiet nursing had bound them closer than she had ever dreamed possible, how she spent all her days sewing for her, walking with her, listening to all her mother’s quiet stores of wisdom, falling more in love with her every single day. Then she told him she was glad he was well, and glad they were friends again, after she had once feared they never would be.

He tried to tell her about his old foolish plan to marry Shirley for her fortune, to warn her he was far from perfect, but Caroline laughed and said she already knew all about it — she and Shirley had stayed up the whole night together at the rectory, talking by the glimmer of starlight and reflected snow. She told him Shirley’s heart was a shrine, pure as snow, warm as flame, strong as death — that no suitor of hers, not even Sir Philip Nunnely, had ever won her favour, because Shirley was already in love, secretly, deeply.

Moore teased her, bumbling through guesses that it was old Mr. Helstone, then the curate Mr. Hall, making Caroline giggle until he pretended he was far too weak to guess any more, and begged her to whisper the name in his ear. She leaned close, and breathed the name against his ear, and he gave a surprised little start, then a quiet laugh, just as Hortense came back into the parlour, and Sarah followed to say Fanny had come from the rectory to walk Caroline home.

Caroline was putting on her shawl at the foot of the stairs when he caught her hand for a last whispered moment. “Must I call Shirley a noble creature now?” he asked her.
“Of course you must, if you want to tell the truth,” she said.
“Must I forgive her?”
“Forgive her? You were the one in the wrong, silly Robert!” she chided, eyes sparkling.
“Must I at length love her downright, Cary?” he teased, his thumb brushing soft over her knuckles.
Caroline looked up at him, half fond, half petulant, and tugged her hand away.
“Indeed you must not. The very idea is quite absurd.”
And with that, she stepped out the door, leaving him leaning against the stair rail, smiling to himself in the warm lamplight, for the first time in years no dread weighing heavy on his chest, only a soft, bright, unnameable hope stirring in the space he had long thought was empty.
