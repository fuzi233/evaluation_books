On the eighth day after that fateful night, Romola stood at the edge of the Mediterranean, gazing out over the gentle swell of the sea just beyond what was then a quiet fishing village—Viareggio. Once more she had fled from Florence, and this time no voice called her back. She wore the grey robes of a religious woman, not as a disguise now, but as a mantle of surrender. In her sorrow, the distinction between truth and pretense had blurred; she cared little whether she was seen as who she was or something else. The old ties that once bound her—love, duty, identity—had snapped under the weight of disillusionment.

She had believed in marriage as a sacred bond, a union so profound it transcended mere choice. But now even that conviction had crumbled. What good was a vow if its foundation was illusion? Had she not tried to live by those promises only to find them impossible? And so, once again, the urge to escape rose within her—not as rebellion, but as exhaustion. Yet freedom offered no relief, only another kind of suffering. For a woman who feels that her deepest relationship was nothing more than a mistake, there is no redemption. She has lost not just love, but purpose—the crown of her life.

And now, even the vision that had sustained her—some grand design, some noble end that gave meaning to endurance—had vanished. The world seemed chaotic, effort futile. Every act felt like pulling at tangled threads. Fellowship, once a source of strength, now appeared selfish and exclusive. Where once she saw nobility, she now saw contradiction.

Her faith in Savonarola, the man she had once regarded as a living saint, had turned to ashes. He had been her hero—not for stoic silence, but for his fierce, self-giving love. But now, stripped of reverence, she saw only flaws: the rigidity, the hypocrisy, the way his reform became less about God’s kingdom and more about power in Florence. His words, once inspiring, now rang hollow. “The cause of my party is the cause of God’s kingdom”—she heard only pride beneath that phrase. Perhaps such declarations always carry a tinge of egoism. Yet they are also the breath of conviction, the fire that drives great deeds forward. Without them, life sinks into timidity, into care for only what is near, while ignoring the larger truths that elevate us.

But Romola could not see beyond her pain. Her grief had clouded her judgment. Anyone who has ever loved deeply and then lost that love will understand how such a blow can shake not just trust in another person, but belief in goodness itself. When the highest human ideals fall, we begin to doubt our own worth. We stop believing in the better part of ourselves, because it too seems tainted by the same brokenness.

Romola felt pity wither inside her. She had given so much—cared for others when few cared for her. Was it not enough? She had striven toward the unattainable, and now she was spent. All she wanted was peace—simple rest, free from thought, free from decision. She remembered the dreams of her youth: floating in water like a nymph, drifting without care. Now, the sea before her seemed to offer that dream. The waves shimmered golden under the setting sun, calling softly. Could she lie down upon them, sleep, and let death come gently?

She could not kill herself—not yet. Life still pulsed strong within her. But she longed for it to end.

Near where she stood, the shore curved inward, sheltering a small boat moored in the cove. A memory stirred—one from Boccaccio’s *Decameron*, read in secret when her father slept. It told of Gostanza, a young woman heartbroken by love, who did not have the courage to take her life, but instead rowed out to sea, wrapped her head in cloth, and waited for disaster. She wished to be destroyed, not by her own hand, but by fate.

Romola had never thought much of that story—until now. As she walked along the beach, she saw another boat approaching, carrying one man. He landed, pulled his vessel ashore, and began unpacking his catch. He was likely the owner of the smaller boat as well. Soon he would leave, and the chance would vanish.

“Is that little boat yours?” she asked, stepping forward.

He looked up, startled by the tall figure in grey, and bowed slightly. “Yes, Sister,” he said. “It’s old, barely seaworthy, but someone might mend it.” He smiled. “By the blessing of San Antonio, my fishing has prospered. I’ve a newer boat now—once belonged to Gianni, who passed on. But I haven’t sold this one yet.”

Romola asked the price. While he bent over his gear, she slipped the money into his satchel, which lay beside his half-eaten meal. Then she watched him fold his sail, asking how he’d set it to go out to sea. She paced, waiting, until finally he gathered his things and walked away.

The light faded. Gold bled into dusk. The sea grew dark. No sound but the soft lap of waves.

There was no tide here to help carry her off, but a faint breeze stirred from the land. She stepped into the boat, unfurled the sail as she had learned in a fleeting lesson, and secured it. It caught the wind. That was all she needed.

She cast off the moorings and rowed slowly outward, until the land was gone, the sky deepened, and stars began to appear—like distant hearts beating across the vastness.

At last, she rested. She pulled back her cowl, removed the kerchief binding her hair, and folded both beneath her head as a pillow. The boat rocked gently. The night air brushed her face. She was alone—truly alone. Free from duty, from expectation, from the crushing burden of choice.

Had she found the dream of her childhood? No. Memories clung to her like wings broken in flight—memories of love, of connection, of moments when she felt known. Even pain had carried warmth. But now, in this solitude, there was no comfort. The heavens offered no message. The stars shone coldly, like eyes that saw her but did not recognize her.

With a great sob, she wished she were slipping into death.

She drew the cowl over her face, choosing darkness. Then, in the stillness, she felt herself in a grave—but not at rest. She reached out, touched hands—familiar, beloved—and whispered, “Wake up.”
