Denys was positioned squarely between his companions, a precaution taken to prevent him from attempting flight in his state of madness. Carried away by an agony of grief and remorse, he raged against heaven and earth, alternating these outbursts with a silence so gloomy and sinister that his guards felt compelled to keep him disarmed. His torment stemmed from a bitter realization: for Denys's sake, Gerard had forsaken the direct German road to Rome, leaving the young man stranded alone in the heart of Burgundy. "He has parted from me," Denys cried, his voice breaking, "and now he may finally listen to reason. Poor wretch, he cannot bear solitude!"

They intercepted their leader just outside the town, halting the entire cavalcade at the *Tête d'Or*. The young landlady, though preoccupied with the Count and the Bastard, spotted Denys and asked anxiously where his young companion had gone. With a sudden burst of sorrow, Denys recounted everything, imploring her to send someone after Gerard. "Now that he is separated from me, he might heed my counsel," he pleaded. "Poor creature, he hates loneliness." The landlady merely tossed her head. "I fear I have shown him too much kindness already," she replied, turning slightly red. "Will you not?" "Certainly not," Denys snapped. Whereupon he unleashed a torrent of curses upon her. She turned her back, retreating while whimpering that she was unused to such treatment, yet she immediately ordered her stablehand to saddle two mules.

Denys rode north with his troop, silent and dejected, unaware that the truthful young woman had donned riding gear in less than forty minutes. Driven by urgency, she spurred away with her servant in the exact opposite direction. That evening, after a grueling march, the Bastard and his men arrived at "The White Hart." Their entry created a commotion, and it took some time before Manon recognized her old friend amidst the crowd. Upon spotting him, her face flushed, yet she offered no greeting, her demeanor suggesting a growing disdain for the humble origins through which she had risen. Denys observed her coldness but could not muster a smile; the inn itself seemed to mirror the memory of Gerard.

Before nightfall, the wind shifted. Manon entered the room and beckoned him with a single finger. Rising sullenly, accompanied by his guards, Denys followed her to a secluded corner. "I wish to speak privately," she whispered. "Do you grant me a favor?" he asked aloud, his tone sharp. "No," he declared loudly. "I am not in the mood to show kindness to man or woman. If I were to bestow a kindness, it would be upon a dog—and even then, only if I could help it."

"Alas, good Archer," Manon said humbly, "you once did me a great service, you and your pretty companion." "Indeed, Dame, you did," Denys conceded. "Well then, for his sake—what is required?" "You know my tale," she continued. "I was once unfortunate, but now I am respected. Yet today a woman spat in my face regarding him. As long as he hangs there, this shame will remain. I desire him taken down; alas!"

"With all my heart," Denys replied. "But I dare ask no one else. Will you do it?" "Not I, even were I not a prisoner," he stated firmly. At this stern refusal, the gentle Manon sighed, clasping her hands in despair. Denys reassured her, noting that there were soldiers of lesser standing who would accept half a crown for such a task. It was merely a matter of coin; if she could provide two angels, he would find two men to perform the dirty work at "The White Hart." Though the offer was unpalatable, Manon reflected that soldiers were transient creatures, drunk tonight and likely dead by tomorrow. Softly, she agreed, instructing him to send them out. "But pray tell them it is for a friend," she urged. "Let them not believe it is for me; I should sink into the earth. Times have changed."

Denys readily found warriors eager to earn an angel each so easily. He dispatched them and, dismissing the matter with contempt, sat brooding over his lost friend. Soon, Manon and the hired men reached an agreement, save for the timing of payment. The armored men itched for their reward, seeking it in advance. Manon hesitated, doubting their word. "Come with us, or watch from a distance," she suggested. "Me?" Manon exclaimed in horror. "I would sooner die than witness it." "Yet you would have done so," Denys countered gently. "Yes, for my need is great. Times have changed."

She had already forgotten her earlier advice to Denys. An hour later, the unpleasant reminder of their caterpillar-like existence ceased to mar the respectable matron's public life, and the grim eyes of the past stopped casting malignant glares over the white hind's clover field. In total, she secured herself an average husband and a fine house-dog, outliving everyone around her.

When the troops returned from executing the medieval maxim "Off with the old love," they were shocked to find the marketplace thronged with people—a stark contrast to its emptiness an hour prior. Conscience struck them hard; this was the result of meddling with the dead. However, the bolder of the two, emboldened by the darkness, crept forward alone. He stealthily merged into a gathering crowd and soon returned to his companion, speaking in a tone of reproach that lacked strict logic: "You foolish fool, it is nothing but a miracle."
