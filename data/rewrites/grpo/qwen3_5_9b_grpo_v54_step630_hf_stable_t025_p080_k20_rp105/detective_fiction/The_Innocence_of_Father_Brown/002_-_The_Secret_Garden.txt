The Secret Garden

Aristide Valentin, the Chief of the Paris Police, was late for his dinner, and his guests began to gather in the entrance hall before he arrived. They were reassured by his confidential servant, Ivan, an old man with a scar and a face as grey as his moustaches, who always sat at a table in the hall—a space hung with weapons. Valentin's house was peculiar, perhaps even celebrated, for its architecture held a peculiar police value: there was no ultimate exit from the house except through the front door, guarded by Ivan and the armoury. The garden behind was large and elaborate, with many exits from the house into it, but no exit from the garden to the world outside. A tall, smooth, unscalable wall with special spikes ran all around it, creating a perfect trap for a man in whom a hundred criminals had sworn to kill.

Ivan explained that their host was detained for ten minutes, making last arrangements about executions—duties repulsive to him yet performed with precision. Valentin was ruthless in pursuit but mild in punishment, a humanitarian freethinker who made mercy even colder than justice. When he finally arrived, dressed in black with a red rosette, he went straight to his study, which opened onto the grounds. He locked his official box and stood at the open door, regarding the garden with a wistfulness unusual for such a scientific nature. Recovering quickly from this mood, he entered the drawing-room to find his guests. He saw Lord Galloway, the choleric English Ambassador; his wife, slim and superior; their daughter, Lady Margaret, pale and elfish; the opulent Duchess of Mont St. Michel and her daughters; Dr. Simon, the French scientist with his characteristic wrinkles; and Father Brown of Cobhole. Most notably, he saw Commandant O'Brien, an Irish gentleman in the French Foreign Legion, a dashing yet melancholy figure who had known the Galloways in his youth.

Valentin, however, was not specially interested in them. He was expecting Julius K. Brayne, a multi-millionaire whose colossal endowments to various religions had amused the press. Brayne was a man ready to pour money into any intellectual vessel, particularly one he deemed "progressive." His solid appearance in the room was as decisive as a dinner bell. He was a huge fellow, clad in complete evening black, with white hair brushed back like a German's and a face red, fierce, and cherubic, marked by a dark tuft under his lip.

The Galloways were genial enough, provided Lady Margaret did not take the arm of the adventurer O'Brien. She had not, having gone in with Dr. Simon. Yet Lord Galloway was restless, stung by the thought that O'Brien might be signaling to his daughter. After dinner, when the younger men melted away to smoke in the conservatory, Galloway grew undiplomatic. He argued with Brayne and Valentin until boredom set in, then sought the drawing-room. He lost his way for several minutes, hearing the voices of the doctor and priest arguing about science and religion. When he finally opened the salon door, he saw only one thing: Commandant O'Brien and Lady Margaret were absent.

Driven by a senile and passionate suspicion, Galloway rushed to the back of the house, where he found a servants' entrance opening onto the garden. The moonlight, now clear after the storm, lit up the lawn. There, striding toward the study door, was a tall figure in blue: Commandant O'Brien. He vanished through the French windows, leaving Galloway in an indescribable temper. The moonlight maddened the diplomatist, who tripped over a stone and began running and bellowing. His shouts brought Dr. Simon to the study door.

"A corpse in the grass—a blood-stained corpse!" Galloway cried.

Valentin entered, his demeanor instantly transforming from concerned host to businesslike detective. "Strange," he said, "that I should hunt mysteries all over the earth and now one settles in my own back-yard." Guided by Galloway, they found the body sunken in deep grass: a very tall, broad-shouldered man lying face-down, his head severed from his body by a clean cut. A scarlet serpent of blood crawled from the wound.

"He is dead," Dr. Simon confirmed, lifting the head. It was a ponderous, yellow face, resembling a wicked Roman emperor. Underneath the body, they saw the white gleam of a shirt-front defaced with blood. Valentin examined the ground, finding only a few chopped twigs. "Twigs," he said gravely, "and a total stranger with his head cut off."

Suddenly, Galloway pointed to a small figure near the garden wall. It was Father Brown, who meekly remarked, "There are no gates to this garden, do you know." Valentin, though cross at the sight of the cassock, agreed. He proposed keeping the matter private for now, ordering everyone to stay in the house until noon while he investigated.

Ivan, the scarred detective, arrived with eager eagerness to examine the remains. He lifted the head and gasped, "Do you know this man, sir?" Valentin replied indifferently, "No." They carried the corpse to a sofa in the study and returned to the drawing-room. Valentin questioned the group, noting that Mr. Brayne and Commandant O'Brien were missing. He summoned them.

O'Brien entered defiantly, claiming he had been admiring the moon. When Valentin asked for his sword, Ivan returned with an empty scabbard. Lady Margaret then stepped forward, declaring that O'Brien had been asking her to marry him and that she swore he was innocent. Lord Galloway tried to intimidate her, but she retorted that if he had murdered someone, who else could have witnessed it?

Then Father Brown spoke, asking about the length of Mr. Brayne's cigar. Valentin, realizing the significance, ordered Ivan to fetch Brayne. Instead, Ivan reported that Brayne had fled the house, throwing his hat, coat, and a blood-streaked cavalry sabre fifty yards up the road to Paris. Valentin returned the sword to O'Brien, clearing him of suspicion. The atmosphere lightened, though the riddle of the death remained.

O'Brien and Dr. Simon discussed the impossibilities: why use a clumsy sabre when a knife would suffice, why no outcry, how the victim entered the sealed garden, and how Brayne escaped. Dr. Simon added a fifth difficulty: the cuts on the neck appeared to be made after death, suggesting the head was severed post-mortem.

Father Brown arrived and announced a second murder: another beheading, with the second head found bleeding into the river nearby. O'Brien felt sick at the thought of Brayne's monomania. In the library, they found the second head, battered and wet, alongside the first. Father Brown examined it closely, noting a specific chip in the left ear. "He had exactly that chip," he said. "It is Brayne."

Valentin scoffed, suggesting Brayne might be converting to Catholicism to gain the priest's favor. O'Brien intervened, warning Valentin against slander. Valentin dismissed them, promising to write to the authorities. Ivan then revealed that the body in the garden was identified as Arnold Becker, a German criminal recently executed, and his twin brother, Louis Becker, had also been guillotined.

Father Brown suddenly clutched his temples, overwhelmed by the realization. "I see half," he cried, "but I only see half." He composed himself and addressed the group, answering their five questions. He explained that the murderer needed a sabre to distract the victim with tricks before the silent slash. He claimed there was no strange man in the garden. When asked how Brayne entered and exited, Brown replied, "He didn't get out of the garden... Not completely."

Finally, Brown revealed the truth: the body in the garden was not Becker. The murderer had hacked off Becker's head, flung it over the wall, and attached the severed head of Louis Becker to the body of Arnold Becker, creating a false identity. The real murderer was Aristide Valentin. He had argued with Brayne, lured him into the sealed garden, and committed the murder using Becker's head to frame the situation. Valentin had taken Becker's head home in his official box.

Driven by this revelation, they rushed into Valentin's study. The great detective sat at his desk, appearing too occupied to hear their entrance. Dr. Simon noticed a small box of pills at Valentin's elbow and realized the truth: Valentin was dead in his chair, a suicide born of his fanaticism to break the superstition of the Cross.
