In the quaint village of Clavering, nestled near the ancestral home of Fairoaks, a tapestry of relationships had long been woven, intricately connected by ties of kinship and friendship. It was here that Mr. Samuel Huxter, a bon vivant, had forged lasting bonds with the locals, regaling them with tales of high society and the fashionable circles he frequented in London. His mother, Mrs. Huxter, had welcomed young Mr. Hobnell, a former pupil at the Grammar School, into their home, where he would often indulge in the sweet scents of rose-water and the taste of tamarind-pots from the surgery.

As fate would have it, Mr. Hobnell's life became intertwined with that of Miss Sophy Huxter, whom he later married, and together they established themselves at the Warren, a rural estate a few miles from Clavering. The family had long been yeomen and farmers, cultivating the land with dedication and care. Upon Mr. Hobnell's father's passing, his son inherited the estate, and with it, the title of Squire Hobnell. He transformed the old farmhouse into a grand, whitewashed mansion, complete with spacious stables and a piano in the drawing-room, where he would host lavish gatherings and keep a pack of harriers.

As the years passed, the Hobnells solidified their position as county gentry, and Sam Huxter, in London, would often boast about his brother-in-law's estate, his hunting dogs, and his hospitality to his friends at Bartholomew's. Every year, Mr. Hobnell would journey to London, taking rooms at the Tavistock, where he and Sam would indulge in the city's pleasures, attending Ascot, the theatre, Vauxhall, and the convivial taverns surrounding Covent Garden. In the midst of this whirlwind, Mr. Hobnell would don a new bonnet and shawl for his wife, relinquishing the city's charms for the rustic pursuits of the countryside upon his return.

Through their correspondence, Sam Huxter kept his relative informed of the latest metropolitan news, receiving in return the bounty of hares, partridges, and clouted cream sent by the squire and his kindly wife. Mr. Hobnell's presence was a beacon of joy in the Warren, where his songs, jokes, and antics brought laughter and warmth to all who gathered around him.
