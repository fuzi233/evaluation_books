Denys caught Gerard as he fell, lessening the impact, but it was the dying bear beneath them that truly broke his descent. Gerard’s head and shoulders struck the thick fur, and Denys wrenched him free before the beast could stir. She was past harming them now—her breath came in ragged gasps, her limbs twitched weakly, and soon she lay still. Denys propped Gerard against her soft bulk, fanning him until his senses returned.

Groggy, Gerard recoiled at the touch of fur and rolled away with a cry.

"Courage," said Denys, grinning. *"Le diable est mort."*

"Is it dead? Truly dead?" Gerard peered from behind a tree, his earlier bravery now eclipsed by a cold, trembling dread.

Denys laughed, tugging the bear’s ear, prying open her jaws, even thrusting his head inside in mocking bravado. Gerard turned away, sickened.

"What now?" Denys teased. "You climb like a squirrel, then tumble like a drunkard just when the fight is won?"

"I swooned, I think."

"Why?"

Gerard wiped his face, grimacing. "Her blood—the stench of it. I cannot abide blood."

Denys smirked. "A fine soldier you’d make. Yet here you stand, unharmed, while your enemy lies gutted."

"You mock, but I saw you pale as a ghost beneath that tree."

Denys flushed. "A man may tremble for his friend."

Gerard, moved, embraced him without a word.

Denys, uncharacteristically softened, ruffled Gerard’s hair. "Women faint, not men—yet here you kneel like a monk at vespers."

Gerard dropped to his knees. "Should we not thank Him who saved us?"

Denys hesitated, then knelt beside him, hands folded in the manner of his homeland, face solemn. When they rose, Gerard’s eyes shone with quiet joy.

"God will reward your piety, Denys."

*"Bah!"* Denys waved a hand. "I did it to please you. Still, ’twas well-prayed—fit for a bishop. Now, with the saints on our side, *marchons!"*

Two steps forward, and Denys halted. "The cub—"

Gerard shuddered. "Leave it."

"You’re right. The hour is late, and the beast weighs heavy. Besides, its sire may yet seek vengeance." He paused, eyes widening. "You’re wounded!"

"I am not."

"You *are!*" Denys dropped to inspect Gerard’s leg, fingers tracing the stiffening limb. "Quick, before it locks!"

Gerard hobbled, wincing. "Who frets now?"

Denys muttered, "I mocked you for fainting—yet you bore a wound without complaint. I’ve a black tongue and a blacker heart."

"Denys—"

"You lie."

Denys chuckled ruefully. "Your kindness shames me."

The pain worsened until Gerard could barely walk, then not at all. He sank to the ground. "Leave me. I cannot go on."

Denys hauled him into the woods, pressed a crossbow into his hands. "If rogues come, shoot first. Honest men keep to the road; fools parley." Then he snatched Gerard’s axe and sprinted back the way they’d come.

Alone, Gerard wavered between pain and dreams of Margaret—how sweet to one day tell her of these trials! His thoughts were shattered by shouts, the *thunk* of an arrow striking a tree.

Battle cries filled the air, though from where, he couldn’t tell. Hooves pounded, steel clashed, and strange thunderous cracks split the night, followed by drifting smoke. Then—silence.

When Denys returned, hauling the bear’s pelt, Gerard cried out in relief.

"Were you in the battle?"

Denys blinked. "What battle?"

Gerard described the phantom clash.

Denys chuckled. "Fever breeds visions. Once, with a cracked skull, I saw armies clash in a cart-shed."

Gerard pointed to an arrow embedded in a tree.

Denys sobered. "English. Drawn to the ear, not the breast. A skirmish, then—but no affair of mine." He fashioned a bed of leaves, draped the bear-skin over them, and they slept.

At dawn, Gerard woke, ravenous.

Denys yawned. "Sleep dines."

"I *cannot* sleep for hunger!"

They trudged on, Gerard leaning on his axe and Denys. The moon revealed the wood’s edge—and a gibbet, its grim cargo swaying in the breeze. Beneath it, a fire burned, tended by a slumbering figure.

Denys stoked the flames, ignoring Gerard’s unease. The watcher woke, startled, then offered wine—Rhenish, rich and smooth. Gerard drank, the warmth steadying him, while Denys feasted, jesting macabrely with the stranger.

The man spun a tale of outlaws betrayed, hanged by soldiers disguised as merchants. He toasted each dangling corpse, his words slurred with wine and spite.

At Denys’s urging, the man helped Gerard walk, though Gerard recoiled at touching a thief.

Near Dusseldorf, the man balked. "Go further, and you deliver me to the noose."

Denys tossed him coins. "For your trouble—and a prayer, since you lack one."

Gerard obliged—in Latin, a swift, sharp plea for divine justice. The thief, satisfied, vanished into the dark.

As they walked, Gerard murmured, "That she-bear saved us both—by God’s will."

Denys grunted. "Aye. And may He guide us to softer beds next time."
