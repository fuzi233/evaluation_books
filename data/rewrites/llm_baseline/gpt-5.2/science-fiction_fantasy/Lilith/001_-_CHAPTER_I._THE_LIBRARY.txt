When I came down from Oxford, my head was still full of lectures and long corridors of talk, but my hands—unused hands for a man suddenly responsible for acres and tenants—ached for something simpler than argument: a thing you could weigh, smell, and set in order. My father had died when I was a child, my mother not long after; the estate passed to me like a loaf pulled from an oven while the baker’s back is turned—still steaming, but not made by my own labor. I knew next to nothing of the people whose name I carried. Only this: many of them had been readers, hoarders of thought, the way some families hoard seed.

If I inherited anything, it was the habit of searching for connections. I had been trained toward physical science and its neat instruments, but I could never stop myself from tasting for metaphors the way a cook tastes a broth—lifting a spoon, letting it cool, looking for what you can’t name but know when it’s missing. A theory, I learned, can be as easily over-salted as a stew: one handful too many of certainty, and the whole pot is spoiled. Still, I kept reaching for more.

The house was old enough to have learned, over generations, the domestic trick of swallowing its own additions. Nowhere was that more evident than in the library. It had begun before printing, when words were copied by hand on skin and vellum, and it had gone on accumulating in every age, taking on whatever the living believed was worth preserving. It had done what old kitchens do: expanded sideways into pantries, larders, sculleries; only here the shelves were the cupboards, and the books were the jars—some plainly labeled, some so dusted and darkened that you had to scrape a finger across the spine to raise their names.

It had absorbed room after room until most of the ground floor belonged to it. The chief room was large, book-lined nearly to the ceiling; from it the library overflowed into odd chambers of different shapes, linked by doors, arches, short passages, and steps that went up and down as if the house itself were a mind wandering from topic to topic. The place had its own sound: a continual hush stitched together from the soft sigh of paper, the faint creak of old boards, the little settling clicks of wood as the weather changed. Even the rain, when it came, had to lower its voice to speak through such thick walls.

I spent most of my time there. Not because I disliked the fields or the stables—indeed, there is a clean satisfaction in the dull thud of a fork into a heap of hay, in the blunt honesty of soil—but because the library was the estate’s true granary of inheritance. I read the sciences, old and new, interested less in what men called knowledge than in how they had arrived at it, what they had been hungry for, what had been fed to them, and what had poisoned them. Ptolemy and Dante, the two Bacons, Boyle—these were to me like old recipes written in a dead hand: imperfect, sometimes dangerous, but smelling faintly of the first fires.

On a gloomy day in August, toward evening, I sat in my usual place with my back to one of the windows and a book open before me. It had rained most of the morning and afternoon. The rain had its own music—good rain, steady and unhurried, not the metallic rattle of a storm whipped by wind. Yet even good rain, drumming long enough, can make the mind drowsy. When the sun at last dropped toward the horizon, the clouds parted in front of it and let in a sudden blade of light.

I rose and looked out. In the middle of the great lawn, the fountain’s feathering column caught the sunset and filled with red glory, as if the water itself had been warmed to wine. The air smelled rinsed, the way a scoured wooden table smells when the cloth is lifted: clean grain, damp earth, and a faint green from the bruised grass.

I turned back to my chair, and the same ruddy light caught the only picture in the room—a portrait set oddly alone in a kind of shallow shrine sunk into the shelves. I knew it was the likeness of one of my ancestors, but I had never paused to ask why it hung there rather than in the gallery with the other faces that belonged, in the usual way, to family pride. Now, in that direct sunlight, the painting woke. For the first time I seemed truly to see it: the dry, attentive look of the eyes; the suggestion of a mouth that had known both restraint and appetite. For the first time it seemed to answer my gaze, not like canvas, but like a person who has been waited on too long.

With the light from the portrait still bright in my eyes, something—no clear thought, more like a prickling along the scalp—made me glance toward the farther end of the room. There, in that comparative dusk, I saw, or thought I saw, a tall figure reaching up a hand to a shelf.

The next instant the impression collapsed like a mirage when you blink. There was no one. I scolded my own nerves for their tricks; after sudden brightness, the eye invents shadows. I sat down again and bent over my reading, determined not to become a superstitious householder frightened by his own furniture.

But a moment later I had occasion to consult a certain volume. I rose, walked to its place, and found only a gap in the row where it ought to have stood. At once the image returned: the tall figure, the lifted hand, precisely at that spot. I searched about, behind other books and along the shelves, half laughing at myself. The volume was nowhere.

The next morning, however, there it was—exactly where it belonged, as if it had never moved. No servant in the house had any reason to want it; the title was not the sort of thing one borrowed for amusement. I tried to dismiss the matter as some muddle of my own doing, but the clean absence and clean return made that hard. It felt like setting a loaf to cool and turning your back for a breath, only to find it sliced when you return, the knife put away, no crumbs on the board.

Three days later, something stranger happened.

In one of the walls was a low, narrow door to a closet where some of the oldest and rarest books were kept. The door was thick and heavy, framed so it projected slightly, and one of my long-ago predecessors had played a harmless trick with it: he had crossed it with shallow shelves filled with nothing but book-backs, so that at a glance the door disappeared into the surrounding wall of learning. The deception was made more amusing by the titles painted there—some comically invented, some the names of books long lost, as if the family would rather keep the idea of them than admit the emptiness.

I had always liked that masked door. It was the sort of whimsy an old house carries the way a kitchen carries a notch in the table from a careless cleaver—domestic history made visible. To complete the illusion, some ingenious workman—or perhaps the same fanciful ancestor—had slid into the top of one row a portion of a thin volume, so thin it could lie between the shelf and the one above. A considerable part of it had been cut away diagonally, and the remnant fixed there with one open corner projecting beyond the false backs. The binding was limp vellum, with that faint animal scent old parchment keeps, like clean leather warmed by hands. If you lifted the corner, you could see that it was manuscript upon skin—ink browned with age, the strokes made by a human wrist that had been dust for centuries.

That evening, as I read, I happened to raise my eyes, and my glance fell upon the masked door. At once I saw that the mutilated volume—if volume it could be called—was gone.

I felt an unreasonable anger, as if someone had taken a spoon from a set I used daily, though I had never once read a line of that fragment. The indignation was not for the object itself but for what it represented: an intrusion into the order of my household, into the quiet consent between the dead and the living that keeps old places sane. I rang the bell.

The butler came—an older man whose whole life seemed to have been trained into discretion, as if he had been brined in family service. I asked him if he knew what had become of it. His face, which was usually steady, lost its color. He assured me he did not.

I trusted him more than I trusted my own eyes. He had been in the family since my father was eight years old; faithfulness had become his second nature. Yet even as he denied knowledge, something in his hesitation suggested he knew where the question led, and would rather not walk there.

In the afternoon I was again in the library. The rain had stopped; the house held a damp-cool silence that made the slightest sound—page turned, chair shifted—seem too loud. I came upon a passage that demanded thought, lowered the book, and let my gaze wander as one lets a simmering pot be while the flavors marry.

In that same moment I saw, as plainly as I have ever seen a man’s back, a slender old figure in a long dark coat, shiny with wear at the shoulders and elbows, moving away from me. He was in the act of slipping through the masked door into the closet beyond.

I crossed the room in a rush. The boards gave a few sharp complaints under my feet—too loud in that careful hush. The door was shut. I pulled it open at once and looked into the closet. It was small, lined with shelves, crowded close with volumes bound in skins and cloths of every hue time can dim. There was no other exit. There was no one.

A chill moved through me—not the theatrical fear of a tale told by the fire, but a practical uneasiness, like finding the pantry unlatched in the night and not knowing whether wind or thief has been there. I told myself my eyes had played their earlier trick again. I went back to my chair and opened my book, trying to recover the thread of thought.

But I could not. A mind that listens for a footstep cannot hear a paragraph.

Presently I glanced up, needing to assure myself that I was indeed alone. I started to my feet again—for there, in its place on the sham shelf, was the mutilated vellum fragment.

I went to it and laid hold of the projecting corner. I pulled. It was firm, fixed as it had always been, as if it had never been removed. There was no looseness, no sign of tampering, not even a scuff on the false backs around it. The thing was as immovable as the notion of it had become.

Bewilderment, when it is complete, feels strangely physical. My mouth was dry; my skin prickled. I rang for the butler again.

When he came, I told him everything: the gap on the shelf, the book’s return, the missing vellum, the figure disappearing through the door, the fragment restored as if welded in place. He listened without interrupting, and when I had finished, he answered with the air of a man setting down at last a covered dish he has been carrying too long.

He said he had hoped the old gentleman would be forgotten. It was well no one but myself had seen him. He had heard a good deal of the matter when first he came into service, but by degrees it had ceased to be mentioned, and he had been careful never to allude to it.

“The place was haunted by an old gentleman, then?” I said, hearing my own voice sound thin in that large room.

At one time, he replied, everybody believed it, but the fact that I had never heard of it seemed to show the business had died away into silence, as household talk does when it threatens the peace. My grandfather, he said, would never hear a word on the subject. If any servant mentioned it, dismissal would come without warning. Old Sir Ralph, he added, believed in nothing he could not see or lay hold of; he called it a pretext of the maids for running into the arms of the men.

No maid, the butler said, had ever claimed to see the apparition; but a footman once left the place because of it. An ancient woman in the village—one of those who remember other people’s grandfathers by the sound of their steps—had told him a legend concerning a Mr. Raven, long ago librarian to “that Sir Upward whose portrait hangs there among the books.”

Sir Upward, the old woman said, had been a great reader—not only of wholesome books, but of strange, forbidden, and evil ones. In those readings Mr. Raven—who, she hinted, was likely the devil himself—had encouraged him. Then, suddenly, both Sir Upward and Mr. Raven disappeared. Sir Upward was never again seen or heard of, but Mr. Raven continued to show himself, at uncertain intervals, in the library.

Some believed he was not dead. Others, the old woman among them, found it easier to believe a dead man might revisit the world he had left than that a man might go on living for hundreds of years and still be a man. The butler had never heard that Mr. Raven meddled with anything in the house, but he might consider himself privileged where books were concerned.

How the village woman had learned so much, the butler could not tell. Yet the description she gave—slender, tall, old, dark coat worn glossy with time—matched exactly what I had seen.

“I hope it was but a friendly call on the part of the old gentleman,” he concluded, attempting a troubled smile that did not quite reach his eyes.

I told him I had no objection to any number of visits from Mr. Raven. If the library had its own customs, I could live with them. But I said it would be well he kept to his resolution of telling nothing to the servants; a household has enough noises of its own without adding the echo of fear.

Then I asked him whether he had ever seen the mutilated vellum out of its place. He answered that he never had and had always thought it a fixture.

With that, as if to reassure both of us by the honest test of touch, he went to the fragment and gave it a pull. It did not budge. It lay there as it always had, the corner inviting the curious finger, the old ink hidden just beyond the reach of certainty, as if the house kept its secrets not with locks and keys but with a finer, older power: the power to make what is real sound like a story, and what is a story press back upon the senses as real.
