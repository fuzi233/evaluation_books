The Countess Olenska had said "after five"; and at half-past the hour, Newland Archer rang the bell of the peeling stucco house. A giant wisteria choked the feeble cast-iron balcony above, a verdant strangler around the entrance of the home she had rented from the vagabond Medora, far down West Twenty-third Street.

It was a peculiar neighborhood for a woman of her station. Her neighbors were small dressmakers, bird-stuffers, and "people who wrote." Further down the disheveled street, Archer recognized a dilapidated wooden house at the end of a paved path, where a journalist named Winsett resided—a man Archer had occasionally encountered. Winsett did not invite guests, yet he had once pointed out his home during a late-night stroll, prompting Archer to shiver and wonder if the humanities were so poorly housed in other capitals.

Madame Olenska's dwelling was saved from this squalid appearance only by a fresh coat of paint on the window frames. As Archer approached the modest front, a dark thought crossed his mind: the Polish Count must have stripped her of her fortune as thoroughly as he had of her illusions.

The young man had endured an unsatisfactory day. He had lunched with the Wellands, hoping to whisk May away for a walk in the Park afterward. He craved the solitude of her company, wanting to tell her how enchanting she had looked the previous night, how proud he felt, and to urge her to hasten their marriage. But Mrs. Welland had firmly reminded him that the round of family visits was far from over. When he hinted at advancing the wedding date, she raised reproachful eyebrows and sighed, "Twelve dozen of everything—hand-embroidered."

Packed into the family landau, they rolled from one tribal doorstep to another. When the afternoon's circuit finally ended, Archer parted from his betrothed with the sensation of being paraded like a wild animal cunningly trapped. He supposed his anthropological readings colored his view, making him see this natural display of family affection as coarse. Yet, remembering that the Wellands did not expect the wedding until the following autumn, and picturing the years of confinement that lay ahead, a damp chill settled in his spirit.

"Tomorrow," Mrs. Welland called after him, "we'll do the Chiverses and the Dallases." He realized she was methodically alphabetizing through their two families, and they were only in the first quarter.

He had intended to mention the Countess Olenska's summons—the command, rather—to May. But in the brief moments they shared, he had more pressing matters to discuss. Furthermore, it struck him as absurd to bring it up. He knew May particularly desired him to be kind to her cousin; hadn't that wish hastened their engagement? It gave him an odd sensation to reflect that, but for the Countess's arrival, he might have been, if not still a free man, at least less irrevocably pledged. But May had willed it so, and he felt strangely relieved of further responsibility—and therefore at liberty, if he chose, to visit her cousin without telling her.

Standing on Madame Olenska's threshold, curiosity seized him. He was puzzled by the tone of her summons; he concluded she was less simple than she appeared.

The door opened to reveal a swarthy, foreign-looking maid with a prominent bosom beneath a gay neckerchief, whom he vaguely imagined to be Sicilian. She welcomed him with a flash of white teeth. Answering his inquiries with a head-shake of incomprehension, she led him through the narrow hall into a low, firelit drawing-room. The room was empty, and she left him for an appreciable time to wonder if she had gone to find her mistress or if she hadn't understood his purpose, perhaps thinking he was there to wind the clock—the only visible specimen of which had stopped. He knew the southern races communicated in the language of pantomime, and he was mortified to find her shrugs and smiles so unintelligible. Finally, she returned with a lamp. Archer, meanwhile, had constructed a phrase from Dante and Petrarch, evoking the answer: *"La signora è fuori; ma verrà subito,"* which he interpreted as, "She's out—but you'll soon see."

What he saw, illuminated by the lamp, was the faded, shadowy charm of a room unlike any he had known. He knew the Countess had brought some of her possessions with her—"bits of wreckage," she called them—and these were represented by a few small, slender tables of dark wood, a delicate Greek bronze on the mantelpiece, and a stretch of red damask nailed over the discolored wallpaper behind a couple of Italian-looking pictures in old frames.

Newland Archer prided himself on his knowledge of Italian art. His boyhood had been saturated with Ruskin, and he had devoured the latest books: John Addington Symonds, Vernon Lee's *Euphorion*, the essays of P. G. Hamerton, and the wonderful new volume *The Renaissance* by Walter Pater. He spoke easily of Botticelli and Fra Angelico with a faint condescension. But these pictures bewildered him; they were unlike anything he was accustomed to seeing when traveling in Italy. Perhaps, too, his powers of observation were impaired by the strangeness of finding himself in this empty house, where apparently no one expected him. He regretted not telling May Welland of the Countess's request and felt a disturbance at the thought that his betrothed might arrive to find him sitting there, waiting alone in the dusk at a lady's fireside with an air of intimacy.

But since he had come, he meant to wait. He sank into a chair and stretched his feet toward the logs.

It was odd to be summoned in that manner and then forgotten, yet Archer felt more curious than mortified. The atmosphere of the room was so distinct from anything he had ever breathed that self-consciousness vanished in the sense of adventure. He had been in drawing-rooms hung with red damask and pictures "of the Italian school" before; what struck him was how Medora Manson's shabby hired house, with its blighted background of pampas grass and Rogers statuettes, had been transformed by a turn of the hand and the skillful use of a few props into something intimate, "foreign," subtly suggestive of old romantic scenes and sentiments. He tried to analyze the trick, searching for clues in the grouping of chairs and tables, in the fact that only two Jacqueminot roses—of which nobody ever bought less than a dozen—sat in the slender vase at his elbow, and in the vague, pervading perfume that was not what one put on handkerchiefs, but rather the scent of a far-off bazaar, composed of Turkish coffee, ambergris, and dried roses.

His mind wandered to the question of what May's drawing-room would look like. He knew Mr. Welland, who was behaving "very handsomely," already had his eye on a newly built house in East Thirty-ninth Street. The neighborhood was considered remote, and the house was constructed of a ghastly greenish-yellow stone that the younger architects were beginning to employ as a protest against the brownstone that coated New York like a cold chocolate sauce; but the plumbing was perfect. Archer would have liked to travel, to postpone the housing question; but though the Wellands approved of an extended European honeymoon—perhaps even a winter in Egypt—they were firm on the need for a house for the returning couple. The young man felt his fate was sealed: for the rest of his life, he would go up every evening between the cast-iron railings of that greenish-yellow doorstep, passing through a Pompeian vestibule into a hall with wainscoting of varnished yellow wood. Beyond that, his imagination could not travel. He knew the drawing-room above had a bay window, but he could not imagine how May would deal with it. She submitted cheerfully to the purple satin and yellow tuftings of the Welland drawing-room, to its sham Buhl tables and gilt vitrines full of modern Saxe. He saw no reason to suppose she would want anything different in her own house; his only comfort was the reflection that she would probably let him arrange his library as he pleased—which would, of course, be with "sincere" Eastlake furniture and plain new bookcases without glass doors.

The round-bosomed maid came in, drew the curtains, pushed back a log, and said consolingly, "Verra—verra." When she had gone, Archer stood up and began to wander about. Should he wait any longer? His position was becoming rather foolish. Perhaps he had misunderstood Madame Olenska—perhaps she had not invited him after all.

Down the cobblestones of the quiet street came the ring of a stepper's hoofs; they stopped before the house, and he caught the opening of a carriage door. Parting the curtains, he looked out into the early dusk. A street-lamp faced him, and in its light he saw Julius Beaufort's compact English brougham, drawn by a big roan, and the banker descending to help out Madame Olenska.

Beaufort stood, hat in hand, saying something which his companion seemed to negate; then they shook hands, and he jumped into his carriage while she mounted the steps.

When she entered the room, she showed no surprise at seeing Archer there; surprise seemed the emotion she was least addicted to.

"How do you like my funny house?" she asked. "To me, it's like heaven."

As she spoke, she untied her little velvet bonnet and tossed it away with her long cloak, standing before him with meditative eyes.

"You've arranged it delightfully," he rejoined, alive to the flatness of his words but imprisoned by convention in his consuming desire to be simple and striking.

"Oh, it's a poor little place. My relations despise it. But at any rate, it's less gloomy than the van der Luydens'."

The words gave him an electric shock. Few rebellious spirits would dare to call the stately home of the van der Luydens gloomy. Those privileged to enter it shivered there and spoke of it as "handsome." But suddenly, he was glad she had given voice to the general shiver.

"It's delicious—what you've done here," he repeated.

"I like the little house," she admitted. "But I suppose what I like is the blessedness of its being here, in my own country and my own town; and then, of being alone in it." She spoke so low he hardly heard the last phrase, but in his awkwardness, he took it up.

"You like so much to be alone?"

"Yes; as long as my friends keep me from feeling lonely." She sat down near the fire, said, "Nastasia will bring the tea presently," and signed to him to return to his armchair, adding, "I see you've already chosen your corner."

Leaning back, she folded her arms behind her head and looked at the fire under drooping lids.

"This is the hour I like best—don't you?"

A proper sense of his dignity caused him to answer, "I was afraid you'd forgotten the hour. Beaufort must have been very engrossing."

She looked amused. "Why—have you waited long? Mr. Beaufort took me to see a number of houses—since it seems I'm not to be allowed to stay in this one." She appeared to dismiss both Beaufort and himself from her mind and went on, "I've never been in a city where there seems to be such a feeling against living in *des quartiers excentriques*. What does it matter where one lives? I'm told this street is respectable."

"It's not fashionable."

"Fashionable! Do you all think so much of that? Why not make one's own fashions? But I suppose I've lived too independently; at any rate, I want to do what you all do—I want to feel cared for and safe."

He was touched, as he had been the evening before when she spoke of her need of guidance.

"That's what your friends want you to feel. New York's an awfully safe place," he added with a flash of sarcasm.

"Yes, isn't it? One feels that," she cried, missing the mockery. "Being here is like—like—being taken on a holiday when one has been a good little girl and done all one's lessons."

The analogy was well meant but did not altogether please him. He did not mind being flippant about New York but disliked hearing anyone else take the same tone. He wondered if she did not begin to see what a powerful engine it was, and how nearly it had crushed her. The Lovell Mingotts' dinner, patched up in extremis out of all sorts of social odds and ends, ought to have taught her the narrowness of her escape; but either she had been all along unaware of having skirted disaster, or else she had lost sight of it in the triumph of the van der Luyden evening. Archer inclined to the former theory; he fancied that her New York was still completely undifferentiated, and the conjecture nettled him.

"Last night," he said, "New York laid itself out for you. The van der Luydens do nothing by halves."

"No: how kind they are! It was such a nice party. Every one seems to have such an esteem for them."

The terms were hardly adequate; she might have spoken in that way of a tea-party at the dear old Miss Lannings'.

"The van der Luydens," said Archer, feeling himself pompous as he spoke, "are the most powerful influence in New York society. Unfortunately—owing to her health—they receive very seldom."

She unclasped her hands from behind her head and looked at him meditatively.

"Isn't that perhaps the reason?"

"The reason—?"

"For their great influence; that they make themselves so rare."

He colored a little, stared at her—and suddenly felt the penetration of the remark. At a stroke, she had pricked the van der Luydens, and they collapsed. He laughed and sacrificed them.

Nastasia brought the tea, with handleless Japanese cups and little covered dishes, placing the tray on a low table.
