While we were still recovering from the shock of what had just happened, and examining the chilling relics of the Place of Death, Gagool had slipped away into her own dark ritual. Somehow—though she moved with surprising agility when she chose—she had climbed onto the massive stone table where Twala lay beneath the drip, as if to inspect his state, or perhaps for some deeper purpose of her own. Good whispered that she might be checking how he was “pickling,” but it seemed more likely she was paying homage.

She leaned over, pressed her lips against the cold, lifeless mouth of the king as though greeting an old friend, then hobbled back across the table, pausing now and then to mutter words toward the shrouded figures—words we couldn’t catch, but which carried the weight of ancient ceremony. Once done, she settled herself directly beneath the White Death, squatting low, and began a strange, guttural chant, as if offering prayers to something far darker than mere death.

The sight of this twisted woman praying to the very force of evil sent a chill through us all. We hurried our search.

“Now, Gagool,” I said in a hushed voice—no one dared speak loudly in such a place—“lead us to the chamber.”

Without hesitation, she scrambled down from the table.
“My lords are not afraid?” she asked, grinning up at me with yellowed teeth.

“Lead on,” I replied.

“Good, my lords,” she croaked, limping around to the rear of the great stone figure. “Here is the chamber. Let my lords light the lamp and enter.” She set the gourd of oil on the floor and leaned against the cave wall.

I pulled out a match—one of the few we had left—and lit the rush wick. Holding it aloft, I scanned the rock face before us. There was nothing—just solid stone. Then Gagool let out a cackle.
“The way is there, my lords. _Ha! ha! ha!_”

“Do not jest with us,” I said sharply.

“I jest not,” she replied, pointing. “See!”

As she did, the lantern’s light caught movement—a massive slab of rock slowly rising from the ground, disappearing into the ceiling above. It was wide enough for a door, ten feet high, five feet thick, and must have weighed twenty or thirty tons. It rose with eerie silence, balanced by unseen counterweights—perhaps like a modern window mechanism, only carved from mountain stone centuries ago. How it was triggered, we didn’t see; Gagool avoided the spot entirely. But I suspected a hidden lever, pressed just so, shifting balance and lifting the monolith inch by inch.

It rose slowly until it vanished completely, leaving behind a black maw in the rock.

Our hearts pounded. The entrance to Solomon’s treasure chamber was open at last.

Would it be a trick? Or would Da Silvestra’s tale prove true? Were these the riches that could make us kings of the world?

We stood frozen, breathless.

“Enter, white men from the Stars,” Gagool called, stepping forward. “But first, hear your servant, Gagool the old. The bright stones you will see were dug from the pit guarded by the Silent Ones. They were stored here long before I was born—by whom, I know not. Only once has this chamber been entered since those who hid them fled. The legend spread, yes, but no one knew the secret of the door… until a white man came from beyond the mountains—perhaps also ‘from the Stars’—and was welcomed by the king of that time. He sits yonder,” she pointed to the fifth king on the table of the dead.

“And it came to pass that he and a woman journeyed here. By chance, she discovered the secret of the door—search a thousand years, and you would never find it. So they entered. The man found the stones, filled a goat-skin with them, and as he left, he took one more—a large one—in his hand.”

She paused.

“Well,” I asked, voice trembling, “what happened to Da Silvestra?”

Gagool flinched.
“How do you know the name of the dead man?” she snapped. Then, without waiting: “None can say. But the white man fled—frightened. He dropped the skin and ran, holding only the one stone. The king took it—the same stone Macumazahn removed from Twala’s brow.”

“Has no one entered since?” I asked, peering into the darkness.

“None, my lords. Only the door’s secret remains. Every king has opened it—but none have entered. There is a saying: whoever enters dies within a moon. Just as the white man died upon the mountain, so shall others die here. _Ha! ha!_ Mine are true words.”

Our eyes met. A cold dread washed over me. How could she know so much?

“Enter, my lords,” she urged. “If I speak truth, the goat-skin lies on the floor. And if death follows entry, you shall learn soon enough. _Ha! ha! ha!_” She shuffled through the doorway, carrying the lamp.

I hesitated.

“Confound it!” Good burst out. “Here goes. I’m not letting an old witch scare me.” He stepped in, followed by Foulata—though she trembled violently, clearly terrified. We followed.

A few yards down the narrow passage, Gagool stopped.
“See, my lords,” she said, holding the light. “Those who hid the treasure fled in haste. They meant to seal the way—had time to stack blocks two feet high, but not finish. Look—stones ready, mortar, trowels—tools like those used today.”

Foulata gasped, pale and weak. “I cannot go farther,” she whispered. “I feel faint.”

We sat her down on the half-built wall, placed the basket of provisions beside her, and continued.

After another fifteen paces, we reached a painted wooden door—open. Whoever had been here last hadn’t closed it.

Across the threshold lay a goat-skin bag, bulging with pebbles.

“Hee! hee!” Gagool snickered. “What did I tell you? The white man fled in haste—dropped the woman’s bag. Look inside—you’ll find a water-gourd among the stones.”

Good bent down. The bag was heavy, jingling.
“By Jove,” he breathed, “it’s full of diamonds.”

He was right. The thought alone was staggering.

“Go on,” Sir Henry said impatiently. “Here, old lady—give me the lamp.” He took it, stepped through the door, and raised it high.

We crowded in after him, forgetting the bag for a moment.

And there we stood—inside King Solomon’s treasure chamber.

At first, the dim light revealed only a small room, roughly ten feet square, carved from living rock. Then, piled high to the arched ceiling, we saw rows of elephant tusks—hundreds of them, first-quality ivory. Enough wealth to make any man rich forever. Perhaps this was the source of Solomon’s famed throne of ivory.

On the opposite wall stood about twenty wooden boxes, painted red—like ammunition crates, but larger.

“There are the diamonds!” I exclaimed. “Bring the light!”

Sir Henry held it close to the top box. Its lid was cracked, rotted by time. I reached in—not diamonds, but gold coins—strange shapes, stamped with what looked like Hebrew letters.

“Ah,” I said, pulling one out. “We won’t go home empty-handed. At least two thousand pieces per box. Eighteen boxes. This must’ve paid the workers.”

“Looks like that’s it,” Good said. “No diamonds unless Da Silvestra packed them all in his bag.”

“Look yonder,” Gagool croaked. “In the darkest corner—there is a recess, three stone chests. Two sealed, one open.”

Before I could translate, I asked: “How do you know this? No one’s been here since the white man.”

“Ah, Macumazahn,” she mocked, “the watcher from the stars. Do you not know some live long? Some see through rock? _Ha! ha! ha!_”

“Curtis,” I said, pointing. “Check that corner.”

“Hullo!” he yelled. “There’s a niche—look! Great heavens!”

We rushed to the alcove shaped like a bow window. Three stone chests stood against the wall—two closed, one open.

“_See!_” Curtis cried, holding the lamp over the open chest.

At first, the silvery glow blinded us. When our eyes adjusted, we saw it: uncut diamonds—most large, many huge. I picked some up. The soapy texture confirmed it. Real diamonds.

I dropped them, stunned.
“We’re the richest men in the world,” I whispered. “Monte Christo was a fool compared to us.”

“We’ll flood the market,” Good said.

“First, we’ve got to get them out,” Sir Henry reminded us.

We stood, pale-faced, staring at each other, the lantern flickering between us. It felt less like triumph and more like conspiracy.

“Hee! hee! hee!” Gagool cackled behind us, darting like a bat. “There are the bright stones you love—take them! Run them through your fingers! _Eat_ them! _Drink_ them! _Ha! ha!_”

At that, I burst out laughing—so hard I couldn’t stop. The others joined in, though none knew why. We laughed wildly over the treasure that had waited thousands of years—for *us*. The miners who dug them, the overseer who stored them—none ever saw their value. Solomon never touched them. David never claimed them. Da Silvestra barely escaped with a handful.

But we had them. Millions in diamonds, thousands in gold and ivory—all ours.

Then the laughter faded.

“Open the other chests,” Gagool croaked. “Take your fill, white lords. _Ha! ha!_ Take your fill.”

We obeyed. We broke the seals—felt a pang of sacrilege—then lifted the lids.

Hooray! The second chest was full to the brim. The third, only a quarter full—but every stone was a gem: none smaller than twenty carats, some the size of pigeon eggs. Many were slightly yellow, “off-coloured,” as they’d say at Kimberley.

But I noticed something else—the look on Gagool’s face. As she crept silently from the chamber, slithering down the passage like a snake, her eyes burned with hatred.

Then—suddenly—cries echoed through the tunnel.

“_Oh, Bougwan! Help! Help! The stone falls!_”

“Leave go, girl! Then—”

“_Help! Help! She stabbed me!_”

We sprinted down the passage.

The rock door was closing—slowly, inexorably. Only three feet from the floor.

Near it, Foulata and Gagool struggled. Blood soaked Foulata’s leg. She clung to the witch, who fought like a wild animal.

Then—Gagool broke free. Foulata collapsed. The old hag threw herself to the ground, twisting through the narrowing crack.

She made it—just—under the stone.

Then—*crunch!*

The thirty-ton slab descended. It crushed her. A scream tore from her throat—long, agonizing—then silence. The door sealed shut.

We hurled ourselves against it—too late.

Four seconds. That was all.

We turned to Foulata. She was dying.

“Ah! Bougwan, I die,” she gasped. “She crept out—I didn’t see her. The door began to fall. Then she came back—through the falling stone—I saw her and caught her. She stabbed me. And now—_I die_, Bougwan.”

“Poor girl,” Good sobbed, kneeling beside her. He kissed her again and again.

“Bougwan,” she whispered, “is Macumazahn there? It grows so dark…”

“Here I am, Foulata.”

“Macumazahn, be my tongue. I cannot speak to him. Say… I love him. And I am glad to die, because I know he cannot carry such as I am—sun cannot mate with darkness, white with black.

Say… since I saw him, sometimes I felt a bird in my heart—ready to fly away and sing elsewhere. Even now, though I cannot move, my heart does not die. It is full of love—could live ten thousand years and still be young.

Say—if I live again, I may see him in the Stars. And I will search them all—even if I am still black, and he… still white.

Say—nay, Macumazahn, say no more. Just that I love—oh, hold me closer, Bougwan—_oh! oh!_”

“She’s gone,” Good murmured, tears streaming. “She’s dead.”

“Don’t let that trouble you,” Sir Henry said quietly. “You’ll soon join her.”

“What?” Good stammered.

“You don’t see it?” Sir Henry said grimly. “We’re buried alive.”

Until he spoke, we hadn’t fully grasped it. But now it hit us—Gagool had planned this. She’d known the door would trap us. Her mockery about eating and drinking diamonds—now it made sense. Someone had tried it before. Da Silvestra had fled. Now we were trapped—with the treasure we’d come for.

“This won’t do,” Sir Henry said hoarsely. “The lamp will burn out. Let’s find the spring.”

We searched frantically—hands in blood, feeling the walls, the floor, the sides. Nothing. No lever. No knob.

“It doesn’t work from inside,” I said. “If it did, Gagool wouldn’t have risked crawling under. She knew it was triggered from outside.”

“At least,” Sir Henry said with a dry laugh, “retribution was swift. Hers was worse than ours.”

We gave up. Returned to the chamber.

I spotted the basket of food near the unfinished wall. I picked it up and brought it in.

Then we carried Foulata’s body into the chamber, laid her beside the gold boxes.

We sat back against the stone chests, backs to the treasure.

“Let’s divide the food,” Sir Henry said. “Make it last.”

We did. Four tiny meals each. Enough for maybe two days. Two gourds of water—each holding a quart.

“Now,” Sir Henry said grimly, “let us eat and drink. For tomorrow, we die.”

We ate a small bite of dried meat, drank a sip of water. Not hungry, but needed it. Afterward, we stood and examined every inch of the chamber—walls, floor, ceiling. No exit. No secret passage. None existed. A treasure vault wasn’t meant to be escaped from.

The lamp flickered. The fat was nearly gone.

“Quatermain,” Sir Henry said, “what time is it?”

I checked my watch. Six o’clock. We’d entered at eleven.

“Infadoos will miss us,” I said. “He’ll search tomorrow.”

“But he doesn’t know the door’s secret,” Sir Henry replied. “No one does. Not even if he finds it. Five feet of solid rock—nothing short of dynamite could break it. All the Kukuana army couldn’t breach it. My friends, we must accept fate. The hunt for treasure has ended many lives. We shall join them.”

The flame dimmed further. Then—suddenly—it flared.

For a moment, it illuminated everything: the towering pile of ivory, the gold boxes, Foulata’s corpse, the goat-skin full of jewels, the glimmering diamonds, and the three of us—pale, exhausted, sitting in silence, awaiting death by starvation.

Then the flame sank.

And died.
