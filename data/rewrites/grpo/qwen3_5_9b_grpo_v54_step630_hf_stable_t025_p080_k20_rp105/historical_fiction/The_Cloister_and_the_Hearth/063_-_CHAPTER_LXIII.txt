A heavy shadow descended upon the mind of a noble soul. What had once been his guiding star, a pure and unrivalled love for Margaret, was now extinguished. He drifted aimlessly across a dark and hopeless sea. Yet, his torment was not merely despair; it was a searing exasperation at the futility of his own sacrifices. Every act of self-denial, every moment of fortitude, every peril endured, and every virtue displayed felt like a cruel joke, for they had served no purpose. The bitter truth gnawed at him: had he remained idle and selfish at home, he might have saved Margaret's life.

These twin poisons, rage and regret, churned within his young blood, driving him to madness. He threw himself violently into pleasure, seeking oblivion. In those days, pleasure was indistinguishable from vice. Wine, women, gambling—anything that could procure an hour of excitement or a moment of forgetting—he pursued with the desperate frenzy of a man rushing into enemy fire because he has grown weary of living.

The substantial savings he had gathered for Margaret now funded his debauchery. Soon, he became the leader of a circle of loose companions he had previously avoided. His heart rotted alongside his morals. He grew sullen toward his old landlady, rejecting her gentle advice and warnings, until he finally moved to a different part of the town to escape her remonstrances and the painful memories they evoked. As time passed, his hand grew unsteady; he could no longer write to satisfy his need for connection. His patience eroded as the habits of pleasure took root. He abandoned the art of writing and turned to painting portraits in color, though he neglected this craft whenever his idle companions called for him.

Thus, he dove into foul waters, searching for a worthless shell of oblivion. It was not necessary to detail the coarse scenes of vice in which this unfortunate young man now indulged. The essential truth remains: he had become a rake, a debauchee, and a drunkard, one of the most wicked and reckless youths in Rome. Those who hide the sins of the virtuous or soften the edges of wickedness rob the despairing sinner of any hope of redemption.

The man was not made for half-measures, nor was his vice partial. His former humble female friends whispered of his decline. His old landlady told Teresa that he was going to ruin and begged her to find his whereabouts. Teresa relayed the sad story to her husband, Lodovico, asking him to search for the young man's current abode. "If you remember his face, Lodovico mio?"

"Teresa, a man of my station never forgets a face, especially that of a benefactor. But you know I rarely venture abroad in daylight."

Teresa sighed. "And how long will this last, Lodovico?"

"Until some cavalier drives his sword through me. They will not allow a poor fellow like me to pursue any honest trade."

Pietro Vanucci, who bore prosperity far worse than adversity, had been ejected from their old lodging for keeping late hours. Meeting Gerard in the street, he greeted him warmly and soon secured quarters in the same house. He brought with him a lad named Andrea, who ground his colors and served as both pupil and model—a youth of striking beauty and sharp wit.

Pietro had not entirely forgotten the past and professed a warm friendship for Gerard. Gerard, however, in whom all warmth of feeling seemed dead, accepted the other's friendship with cold indifference. It was a strange alliance. Pietro was not only a libertine but also a half-misanthrope and an open infidel. Together, they formed a couple with little in common, a rare phenomenon indeed.

One day, having undermined his health, stripped himself of his beauty, and squandered most of his money, Vanucci organized a festive party to ascend the Tiber in a boat pulled by buffaloes. Lorenzo de' Medici had introduced these beasts to Florence three years prior, but they were novelties in Rome. Nothing satisfied the "beggar on horseback," Vanucci, except to be drawn upstream by the brutes.

Each libertine was required to bring a lady, who must be handsome, or face a fine. The one presenting the most beautiful companion would be crowned with laurel and hailed as a public benefactor, interpreting the question "Who is the good man?" in their own twisted way. They procured a splendid galley and twelve buffaloes. All the libertines and their female associates gradually assembled at the embarkation point, but Gerard was absent.

They waited for him, first patiently, then with growing impatience. Vanucci offered an excuse. "I heard him say he forgot to procure a fardingale. Comrades, the good lad is hunting for a beauty worthy to stand among these peerless ladies. Consider the difficulty, ladies, and be patient!"

Finally, Gerard appeared in the distance, holding a woman by the hand.

"She is long enough," one of the women critiqued from afar.

"Gemini! Look at her steps," said another. "Oh, it is wise to hurry into good company," Pietro muttered as an excuse.

But when the pair approached, satire choked in their throats. Gerard's companion was a peerless beauty; she outshone the entire boatload like the rising sun extinguishes the stars. She was tall but not towering, straight as a dart yet supple as a young panther. Her face was a perfect oval, her forehead pale, her cheeks a rich olive flushed with eloquent blood, and her glorious eyes framed by long, thick silken lashes seemingly designed to sweep away sensitive hearts. Her lips were saucy red, her teeth the whitest ivory.

The women were visibly depressed by this wretched sight; the men were in ecstasy. They received her with loud shouts and waving caps, and one enthusiast even knelt on the boat's gunwale, hailing her of divine origin. However, his mistress pulled his hair, and the goddess delivered a sharp kick; simultaneously, he lay supine. The peerless creature skipped over his body without deigning him a glance, taking her seat at the prow. Pietro Vanucci sat in a collapse, glaring at her, his mouth agape like a dying codfish.

The drover spoke to the buffaloes, tightened the ropes, and they moved upstream.

"What do you think of this new beef, mesdames?"

"We have never seen such vile, ill-favored monsters, with their nasty horns that make one afraid, and their foul nostrils cast skyward. Holes they are, not nostrils."

"Signorina, the cattle are a gift from Florence the Beautiful. Would you look a gift beef in the nose?"

"They are so dull," objected a lively lady. "I went up the Tiber twice as fast last time with only five mules and an ass."

"Nay, that can be remedied," cried a gallant. Jumping ashore, he drew his sword. Despite the drivers' protests, he went down among the dozen buffaloes, goading them.

They snorted and whipped their tails but made no faster progress. At this, the boatload laughed loudly and for a long time. Finally, he goaded a patriarch bull, who instantly turned on the sword. With a furious jerk of his prodigious neck, he sent the man flying over his head into the air. He described a bold parabola and fell sitting, unconsciously waving his glittering blade, into the yellow Tiber. The laughing ladies screamed and wrung their hands, except for Gerard's fair companion. She uttered something very much like an oath, seized the helm, and steered the boat out. The gallant surfaced sputtering, gripped the gunwale, and was drawn in, dripping wet.

He glared around him, confused. "I do not understand that," he said, somewhat peevishly, puzzled and thus appearing discontented. Finding, by some strange accident, that he was not slain—his doublet perforated rather than his body—they began to laugh even louder.

"What are you cackling at?" the spark remonstrated. "I wish to know how it is that one moment a gentleman is out there pricking African beef, and the next moment—"

Gerard's lady. "Disporting in his native stream."

"Tell him not, a soul of you," cried Vanucci. "Let him solve his own riddle."

"Confound you all. I might puzzle my brains until doomsday and never find it out. Also, where is my sword?"

Gerard's lady. "Ask the Tiber! Your best way, signor, will be to do it over again; and, in a word, keep pricking African beef until your mind receives light. So shall you comprehend the matter by degrees, as lawyers mount heaven and buffaloes the Tiber."

Here, a chevalier remarked that the last speaker transcended the sons of Adam in wit as much as she did the daughters of Eve in beauty.

At this, and indeed at all their compliments, Pietro Vanucci's conduct became peculiar. He had ceased staring and gaping bewildered; now he sat coiled like a snake, his mouth muffled, two bright eyes fixed on the lady, twinkling and scintillating most comically.

He did not appear to interest or amuse her in return. Her glorious eyes and lashes swept him calmly at times, but she scarcely distinguished him from the benches and things.

Soon, the unanimity of the party suffered a momentary check. Mortified by the attention the cavaliers paid to Gerard's companion, the ladies began to pick her to pieces in whispers and aloud.

The lovely girl then revealed that, while rich in beauty, she was poor in feminine tact. Instead of revenging herself through the men, she allowed herself to overhear and openly retaliate against her detractors.

"There is not one of you who wears Nature's colors," she said. "Look here," pointing rudely in someone's face. "This is the beauty sold in every shop. Here is cerussa, here is stibium, and here purpurissum. Oh, I know the articles, bless you; I use them every day—but not on my face, no thank you."

Here Vanucci's eyes twinkled nearly out of sight.

"Why, your lips are colored, and the very veins in your forehead: not a charm but would come off with a wet towel. And look at your great coarse black hair like a horse's tail, drugged and stained to look like tow. And then your bodies are as false as your heads and your cheeks, and your hearts I trow. Look at your padded bosoms, and your wooden-heeled chopines to raise your little stunted limbs and deceive the world. Skinny dwarfs you are, cushioned and stultified into great fat giants. Aha, mesdames, well is it said of you, grande-di legni: grosse-di straci: rosse-di bettito: bianche-di calcina."

This drew a rejoinder. "Away, vulgar toad, telling the men everything. Your coarse, ruddy cheeks are your own, and your little handful of African hair. But who is padded more? Why, you are shaped like a fire-shovel."

"You lie, malapert."

"Oh, the well-educated young person! Where did you pick her up, Ser Gerard?"

"Hold thy peace, Marcia," said Gerard, awakened from a gloomy reverie by the raised trebles. "Be not so insolent! The grave shall close over thy beauty as it hath over fairer than thee."

"They began," said Marcia petulantly.

"Then be thou the first to leave off."

"At thy request, my friend." She then whispered to Gerard, "It was only to make you laugh; you are distraught, you are sad. Judge whether I care for the quips of these little fools or the admiration of these big fools. Dear Signor Gerard, would I were what they take me for? You should not be so sad."

Gerard sighed deeply and shook his head. But touched by the earnest young tones, he caressed the jet-black locks, much as one strokes the head of an affectionate dog.

At this moment, a galley drifting slowly downstream got entangled for an instant in their ropes; for, the river turning suddenly, they had shot out into the stream, and this galley came between them and the bank. In it sat a lady of great beauty under a canopy, with gallants and dependents standing behind her.

Gerard looked up at the interruption. It was Princess Claelia.

He blushed and withdrew his hand from Marcia's head.

Marcia was filled with admiration. "Aha! ladies," she said, "here is a rival if ye will. Those cheeks were colored by Nature, like mine."

"Peace, child! Peace!" said Gerard. "Make not too free with the great."

"Why, she heard me not. Oh, Ser Gerard, what a lovely creature!"

Two of the females had been putting their heads together and casting glances at Marcia for some time. One of them now addressed her.

"Signorina, do you love almonds?"

The speaker held a lapful of them.

"Yes, I love them; when I can get them," said Marcia pettishly, eyeing the fruit with ill-concealed desire; "but yours is not the hand to give me any, I trow."

"You are much mistaken," said the other. "Here, catch!" And suddenly threw a double handful into Marcia's lap.

Marcia brought her knees together by an irresistible instinct.

"Aha! You are caught, my lad," cried she of the nuts. "'Tis a man; or a boy. A woman still parts her knees to catch the nuts the surer in her apron; but a man closes his for fear they should all fall between his hose. Confess, now, didst never wear a fardingale ere to-day?"

"Give me another handful, sweetheart, and I'll tell thee."

"There! I said he was too handsome for a woman."

"Ser Gerard, they have found me out," observed the Epicaene, calmly cracking an almond.
