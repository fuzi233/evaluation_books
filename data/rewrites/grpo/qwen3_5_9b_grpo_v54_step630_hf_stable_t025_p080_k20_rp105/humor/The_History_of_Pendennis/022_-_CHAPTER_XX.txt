Just before Mr. Foker departed for Oxford, a certain gentleman arrived at Boniface College. He hailed from Cambridge, having been expelled for a row with the tutors, and his name was Horace Bloundell. The young wags dubbed his ancestral home "Bloundell-Bloundell Hall, Bloundell-Bloundellshire," a title that sounded grander than reality. Yet, Bloundell's lineage was genuine; he was of the ancient Suffolk Bloundells. It was likely this pedigree, combined with the fact that their Master, Dr. Donne, was also a Suffolk man, that secured Bloundell a spot at Boniface after St. George's and a couple of others had rejected him.

A family living was at stake, and Bloundell needed it desperately. While serving in a dragoon regiment, his third brother—the original beneficiary of the living—had fallen ill and died. Determined to swap his crimson pantaloons and black shako for the black coat and white cravat of the clergy, Bloundell set out to secure his future. Though his initial plans at Cambridge had stumbled, the ex-dragoon was undeterred. He simply shifted his battlefield.

By Pen's second year, Major Pendennis visited his nephew and was introduced to the university's elite: the gentle Lord Plinlimmon, the gallant Magnus Charters, the sly Harland, the intrepid Ringwood (known as "Rupert" for his brave blunders), the republican Broadbent (a Bristol dissenting firebrand), and finally, Mr. Bloundell-Bloundell, who had already claimed his place among the select.

The Major, though baffled by Harland's Greek quotes and Broadbent's dirty shoes, loved the company. He was particularly delighted with the stories he told, which were fresh to Oxford ears despite being old news in London. The students listened with honest laughter and respect—a rarity in the city. However, Mr. Bloundell wore a sneer. He once dared to correct the Major on a detail of an anecdote, boasting of a version he'd heard at a club. The Major, with a melancholy grace, accepted the correction, thanking Bloundell for his "accuracy." Later, Bloundell openly mocked the Major as a "regular old trencherman" and a "fogy."

Sympathies are usually mutual. As the Major noted, dislike is like a bad smell; if someone abuses you, you naturally dislike them. Consequently, the Major declared Bloundell "ineligible."

"Bless my soul, sir!" Pen laughed. "He's the most popular man in the university! We elected him Barmecides the first week. He's from an excellent family, descended from Richard's Blondel, bearing a harp in chief and the motto *O Mong Roy*."

"A man may have a fine coat of arms and still be a tiger," the Major retorted, chipping his egg. "That man is a tiger. I'll wager he left his regiment in bad odor. Look at his rings and jewelry—that's 'Scamp' written on his face. Avoid him."

Pen ignored the warning. He was eager to impress, viewing a knowledge of wines and cooking as the mark of a sophisticated roue. He wanted to be an "Admirable Crichton" of dining. Soon, his rooms were besieged by waiters and dish-covers, a stark contrast to the quiet student life he had once known.

Guardian advice fell on deaf ears. Young men crave the appearance of worldly wisdom, and Bloundell, a self-styled *Lovelace*, was the perfect guide. The college tutors noticed the moral tone dropping; Bloundell was called "Captain Macheath" by Broadbent, who predicted he'd hang. Foker, wisely cautious, merely hinted that Bloundell was a dangerous partner at whist but a terrible risk to bet against.

Pen, however, possessed an insatiable appetite for pleasure. He called it "seeing life," quoting Terence and Shakespeare to justify his hedonism. One night, after a supper party, Bloundell produced a pair of dice from his waistcoat pocket and placed them in a green wine glass. He tossed them, sang the chorus of *Robert the Devil*, and the group, including Pen, abandoned their caps for the game. By midnight, they were rolling dice in the glass until Pen shattered it after throwing six mains.

From that night, Pen plunged into the delights of hazard. Bloundell would visit in the morning, rattling the bones in a felt-lined box to avoid the tutors. Once, they were nearly caught by Mr. Buck, who heard "Two to one on the caster" through an open window. Inside, however, Pen was pretending to coach Ringwood on Homer, asking gravely about the navigability of the River Scamander.

Bloundell also shared his "legends." He spoke of winning twelve thousand francs in Paris and sleeping with Napoleon coins in his boot. Pen, glistening with excitement, respected the man who could pull off such coups. When Pen mentioned his love for Miss Fotheringay, Bloundell laughed it off, comparing her to a notorious actress and listing the men who had wasted fortunes on her. Pen, trusting implicitly, felt his heart broken. How could he love a "worthless impostor"?

During an Easter break, Pen and Bloundell visited London. They stayed at a hotel in Covent Garden and frequented a military club, where Pen drank pale ale with mustachioed officers. One evening, Pen sat in the boxes of the Museum Theatre, separated from the Major by a mere plank. Miss Fotheringay was starring.

It wasn't the first time Pen had seen her; he had visited her in Chatteris the previous year. Now, watching her perform, the illusion faded. The light was gone from her eyes. Her voice, once thrilling, now sounded coarse and mechanical. The loud sobs and sighs he had once adored seemed like a cheap imitation. He realized he had loved a dream, not a woman. He felt humiliated and lonely, running to the stage door only to find she didn't recognize him.

That night, they supped uproariously, and Pen woke with a splitting headache, having spent all his ready money.

The narrator notes that Pen's own confessions reveal the truth of his downfall. He was an easy mark for rogues. Finch the money-lender charged him a hundred percent, and even the college cook, his devoted admirer, sent bills he couldn't pay. Yet, Pen retained a generous streak. He would leave a party to visit a sick friend and, in his third year, faced a levee of creditors. When a poor tradesman's daughter, Mary Frodsham, came to plead for help due to her father's illness, Pen pawned his watch and all his jewelry except two old buttons belonging to his father. He rushed to her shop, weeping and begging forgiveness.

This act was less virtue than weakness; he still stood by the prints he had sold to pay her, leaving his mother to suffer the consequences of his follies.

Tutor Mr. Buck had long assumed Pen was wealthy, seeing him with knights of the Garter at St. James's. He was thunderstruck when Pen confessed his massive debts. Pen, terrified of hurting his mother, begged for time, lamenting that his uncle's insistence on high society had ruined him. He slunk away from parties, becoming a gloomy figure in a battered cap, the talk of freshmen's wine parties.

Finally, the Degree Examinations arrived. While many of the men Pen had mocked for their hobnailed shoes passed with honors, the lists were published. Where was Pen the superb, the wit, the dandy, the poet? Where was the widow's darling? The rumor swept through the university: Pendennis of Boniface had been plucked.
