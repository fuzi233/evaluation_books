The Hammer of God

Perched atop a steep hill, the village of Bohun Beacon seemed to rise like a solitary mountain, its church spire piercing the sky like a crown. At the base of the church stood the smithy—always glowing with fire, littered with hammers and iron scraps—and across a rough crossroads lay “The Blue Boar,” the village’s only inn. It was here, at dawn’s first silver light, that two brothers met on the street—one beginning his day, the other ending it.

The Reverend and Honorable Wilfred Bohun, devout and solemn, walked toward the church for early prayer. His elder brother, Colonel Norman Bohun, sat outside the inn in evening dress, sipping what could be called either his last drink of Tuesday or his first of Wednesday—no one cared much which. The colonel didn’t care much either.

The Bohuns were among the few aristocratic families whose lineage stretched back into the Middle Ages; their banner had once flown over the Holy Land. But noble blood did not guarantee noble character. Aristocrats, more often than not, followed fashion rather than tradition. The Bohuns had been rakes under Queen Anne, dandies under Victoria. By the modern age, they’d decayed into drunkenness and vanity, and whispers of madness began to follow them. Colonel Bohun embodied this decline—tall, striking, with startling yellow hair and deep-set blue eyes that seemed almost black. His face bore a permanent sneer, etched by furrows from nose to jaw, and he wore a pale yellow coat so odd it looked like a dressing gown. On his head perched a wide-brimmed green hat, likely some exotic souvenir picked up without thought. He took pride in wearing such absurd clothes—and in making them look stylish.

His brother, the curate, shared the same golden hair and refined features, but dressed in plain black, clean-shaven, and deeply serious. Wilfred lived for religion—or so it seemed. Yet some, especially the blacksmith (a Presbyterian), suspected it was less devotion and more a love of Gothic beauty that drew him to the church. They said he haunted its crypts, galleries, even the belfry—not to worship, but to commune with silence and shadow. Still, his piety was real enough. And now, as he approached the church through the smithy yard, he paused when he saw his brother’s intense gaze fixed in the same direction.

He assumed the colonel was drawn to the church—but then realized the only alternative was the smithy. The blacksmith, Barnes, was known to have a beautiful wife, and rumors had circulated about her. Wilfred cast a suspicious glance toward the shop. The colonel rose, laughing.

“Good morning, Wilfred,” he said. “Like a good landlord, I’m watching over my people. I’m going to call on the blacksmith.”

“The blacksmith is out,” Wilfred replied flatly. “He’s at Greenford.”

“I know,” the colonel said, still grinning. “That’s why I’m calling on him.”

Wilfred frowned. “Norman, are you ever afraid of thunderbolts?”

“What do you mean?” asked the colonel. “Is your hobby meteorology?”

“I mean,” Wilfred said, not looking up, “do you ever think God might strike you down in the street?”

“Oh,” the colonel replied, “your hobby’s folk-lore.”

“And yours,” Wilfred shot back, stung, “is blasphemy. But if you donー’t fear God, you should fear man.”

The colonel raised an eyebrow. “Fear man?”

“Yes,” Wilfred said firmly. “Barnes is the strongest man within forty miles. You’re no coward, but he could throw you over that wall.”

The colonel’s expression darkened for a moment—then broke into laughter, revealing sharp, dog-like teeth beneath his thick mustache. “Then,” he said lightly, “it was wise for the last of the Bohuns to come out partly armored.”

With that, he removed the green hat, revealing it was lined with steel—a light helmet torn from a war trophy in the family hall. Wilfred recognized it instantly.

“It was the nearest hat,” the colonel said airily. “Always the nearest hat—and the nearest woman.”

“The blacksmith is away,” Wilfred said quietly, turning to enter the church, crossing himself as if casting off something foul. He sought refuge in the cool shadows of the Gothic nave, hoping to cleanse his mind. But fate had other plans.

As he stepped inside, he saw a figure rise from prayer near the altar. It was Mad Joe—the village idiot, nephew of the blacksmith. A dark, slouching young man with a pale face, dark hair, and mouth perpetually open, Joe was known for nothing except his silence. Yet here he was, kneeling in the church—something no one had ever seen before. What prayers had he offered? Wilfred couldn’t imagine.

He watched as Joe left the church, only to see his brother laugh and toss coins into Joe’s open mouth, aiming carefully as if playing a game.

The sight—this grotesque blend of cruelty and idiocy—shook Wilfred deeply. He climbed to the gallery, seeking solace beneath a stained-glass window depicting an angel holding lilies. There, amid the soft blues and silvers, he tried to forget the world below.

Half an hour later, Gibbs, the village cobbler, found him. Gibbs was an atheist, and his presence in church was unusual. He came breathless, urgent.

“You must excuse me, sir,” he whispered hoarsely, “but we thought you should know at once. Something dreadful has happened. Your brother—”

Wilfred clenched his hands. “What devilry has he done now?”

“Sir,” Gibbs coughed, “I’m afraid he’s done nothing. He won’t do anything. He’s dead.”

The curate rushed downstairs, following Gibbs through a winding stairway to the yard. There, beneath the morning sun, lay a scene of horror. A man in evening dress sprawled face-down in the dirt, skull shattered like glass. Blood fanned out in a starburst pattern. Wilfred knew it was his brother—down to the rings on his fingers.

“My brother is dead,” he gasped. “What does this mean? What is this horror?”

Silence fell. Then Gibbs spoke: “Plenty of horror, sir. But not much mystery.”

“What do you mean?” Wilfred demanded.

“Only one man in forty miles could have struck such a blow,” Gibbs said. “And he had the most reason to.”

The doctor, a tall man with a black beard, interrupted nervously. “We mustn’t jump to conclusions. But I can confirm what Mr. Gibbs says. That blow—no ordinary man could have done it. Fragments of bone were driven into the body and ground like bullets into mud. It was the hand of a giant.”

He paused, adjusting his spectacles. “The good news is—it clears most suspects. If any normal man were accused, he’d be acquitted as easily as a child accused of stealing Nelson’s column.”

“That’s what I say,” Gibbs insisted. “Only one man could’ve done it—and he’d have done it. Where’s Simeon Barnes?”

“He’s at Greenford,” Wilfred said.

“More likely in France,” muttered Gibbs.

“No,” said a quiet voice. “He’s coming up the road now.”

All turned. From the distant path, walking slowly with a hammer on his shoulder, came Simeon Barnes, the blacksmith. A massive man with deep, dark eyes and a thick beard, he moved with calm authority. Behind him walked two men from Greenford.

“My God!” exclaimed Gibbs. “There’s the hammer he used!”

“No,” said the inspector, a sensible-looking man with a sandy mustache. “That hammer is over there, by the church wall. We’ve left everything as it was.”

They all turned. The hammer was small, nearly invisible among the others. But on its edge—blood and yellow hair.

The short priest, Father Brown, stepped forward and studied it silently.

“Mr. Gibbs was wrong,” he said quietly. “There *is* a mystery. Why would such a man use such a small hammer for such a blow?”

Gibbs waved it off. “Forget the hammer! What do we do with Barnes?”

“Leave him alone,” Father Brown said calmly. “He’s coming of his own accord. Those two men with him are honest folks from Greenford—they’re here about the revival meeting.”

Indeed, the smith rounded the corner and entered his yard. He stopped. The hammer dropped from his hand.

The inspector stepped forward. “I won’t ask if you know anything. But I must arrest you in the King’s name—for the murder of Colonel Norman Bohun.”

“Not bound to say anything,” Gibbs chimed in, thrilled by the legal formality. “They have to prove it. They haven’t even proven it’s the colonel.”

“That won’t wash,” the doctor murmured to Father Brown. “I was his physician. I know his hands—he had identical second and third fingers. That’s the colonel.”

The blacksmith stared at the corpse, then said calmly: “If Colonel Bohun is dead… then he’s damned.”

“Don’t say anything!” Gibbs cried, dancing with glee. “You’re free to stay silent!”

The smith turned, his voice low and fierce. “It’s well for you infidels to dodge like foxes—because the world’s law favors you. But God guards His own. You’ll see today.”

He pointed at the body. “When did this dog die in his sins?”

“Moderate your language,” the doctor said.

“Moderate the Bible’s language,” the smith replied, “and I’ll moderate mine. When did he die?”

“I saw him alive at six this morning,” Wilfred said.

“God is good,” the smith said. “I have no objection to being arrested. But you may regret arresting me. I’ll leave court without stain. You may not leave with your career intact.”

The inspector looked at him anew. The smith continued: “Two men from Greenford will swear I was in the mission committee room all night—from midnight to dawn. Twenty people in Greenford saw me. If I were a heathen, I’d let you fail. But as a Christian, I give you your chance—will you hear my alibi now or in court?”

The inspector hesitated. “Of course—I’d be glad to clear you now.”

The smith returned to his friends. Each gave testimony that no one doubted. The evidence was solid. Simeon Barnes was innocent.

A heavy silence settled. Wilfred broke it, turning to Father Brown: “You seem very interested in that hammer, Father.”

“Yes,” said Father Brown. “Why is it so small?”

The doctor spun around. “By George—that’s true! Who would pick a tiny hammer when ten larger ones are nearby?”

He leaned close to Wilfred. “Only someone who can’t lift a big one. It’s not courage—it’s strength. A bold woman could kill with a light hammer. She couldn’t lift a heavy one.”

Wilfred stared. Father Brown listened, head tilted slightly, truly curious.

The doctor went on: “These fools always assume the husband hates the lover. But nine times out of ten, it’s the wife. Look at her!”

He gestured toward the red-haired woman on the bench. Her tears had dried. Her eyes glowed with a strange, electric fury.

Wilfred shuddered. Then Father Brown, brushing ash from his sleeve, said: “You’re like many doctors—your mental science is clever. But your physical science is impossible. No woman could smash a skull like that. And notice—there’s an iron helmet scattered like glass. Look at her arms.”

Silence again.

The doctor sighed. “Well, maybe I’m wrong. But no man would pick a small hammer if he could use a big one.”

Wilfred clutched his head. Then dropped his hands. “That’s it—the word I needed. ‘No man but an idiot would pick the small hammer.’”

The doctor blinked. “Well?”

“Well,” Wilfred said, trembling, “no man but an idiot did.”

Everyone froze.

“The lunatic,” Wilfred said, voice rising. “Mad Joe. He was praying in the church this morning. A madman’s prayers—maybe upside down. Maybe he prayed before killing. I saw him with my brother. My brother was mocking him.”

“By Jove!” the doctor said. “This makes sense. But how—?”

Wilfred was feverish. “Don’t you see? It explains both riddles—the small hammer and the great blow. The smith could’ve made the blow, but wouldn’t choose the small hammer. The wife would pick the small hammer, but couldn’t deliver the blow. But a madman—yes. He might pick any hammer. And in a fit of rage, a lunatic can have the strength of ten men.”

The doctor exhaled. “By golly—you might be right.”

Father Brown had watched him closely. Now, with quiet respect, he said: “Mr. Bohun, your theory holds water. It’s unassailable. But I must tell you—on my knowledge—it’s not the truth.”

And with that, he walked away, staring again at the hammer.

The doctor muttered, “That fellow knows more than he lets on. Popish priests are deucedly sly.”

“No,” Wilfred said, exhausted. “It was the lunatic. It was the lunatic.”

The group drifted apart. Then voices came from the other side. The blacksmith was speaking loudly:

“I hope I’ve convinced you, Mr. Inspector. I’m strong, yes—but I couldn’t have thrown my hammer from Greenford. It hasn’t wings to fly half a mile over hedges.”

The inspector laughed. “No, you’re cleared. Though it’s one of the oddest coincidences I’ve seen. Now, help us find a man as big and strong as you. You might be useful—just to hold him. Any idea who it could be?”

And the silence returned—thick, expectant, waiting for the next clue.
