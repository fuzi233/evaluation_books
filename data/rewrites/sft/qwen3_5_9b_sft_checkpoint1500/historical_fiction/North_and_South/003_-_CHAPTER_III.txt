"Mr. Henry Lennox." The name had lingered in Margaret's thoughts but a fleeting moment prior, recalling his polite inquiry regarding her domestic duties. It was, as the French proverb suggests, *parler du soleil et l'on en voit les rayons*; and a sudden, radiant warmth spread across Margaret's countenance as she lowered her workbasket and stepped forward to greet him. "Tell Mamma, Sarah," she directed gently, "that Mamma and I have countless questions for you concerning Edith. I am deeply grateful for your visit."

"Was I not bound to return?" he asked, lowering his voice from the cheerful register he had previously employed.

"But news of you had traveled only as far as the remote Highlands," Margaret observed, "leading me to believe Hampshire itself remained untouched by your presence."

"Oh!" he replied with a lighter cadence, "our young pair were engaging in such whimsical pranks, courting every conceivable hazard—scaling mountains, navigating lakes—that I genuinely believed they required a guardian angel. And indeed, they did; they utterly surpassed Uncle Edmund's capacity for oversight, keeping the old gentleman in a state of perpetual alarm for sixteen hours daily. Upon realizing their inability to trust themselves alone, I deemed it my solemn duty to remain until I had safely escorted them aboard at Plymouth."

"You have visited Plymouth? Oh! Edith never mentioned that. Naturally, her letters have been a blur of haste recently. Did they actually depart on Tuesday?"

"They certainly sailed, liberating me from a heavy burden of responsibility. Edith entrusted me with numerous messages for you. I believe I retain a diminutive note from her somewhere... yes, here it is."

"Oh! Thank you," Margaret exclaimed. Yet, a fleeting impulse stirred within her—to read the letter privately, unseen by another gaze. Seizing this pretext, she excused herself, claiming she must inform her mother anew that Mr. Lennox was present, suggesting perhaps Sarah had erred in her initial notification.

Once Margaret had departed, Mr. Lennox commenced his customary scrutiny of the surroundings. The modest drawing-room appeared at its finest, bathed in the streaming light of the morning sun. The central casement of the bow window stood open, allowing clusters of roses and scarlet honeysuckle to spill teasingly around the sill; the small lawn below burst forth with verbenas and geraniums of dazzling hues. Yet, the brilliance without rendered the interior colors pale and somewhat diminished by comparison. The carpet showed signs of age, far from new; the chintz curtains bore the marks of frequent washing. The entire apartment, smaller and shabbier than anticipated, served merely as a humble backdrop and frame for Margaret, who stood before it with a queenly poise. He picked up one of the volumes resting on the table—it was the *Paradiso* of Dante, bound in proper antique Italian vellum with gold tooling. Beside it lay a dictionary and several pages filled with words transcribed in Margaret's handwriting. They formed a somber list, yet somehow, he found himself drawn to examine them. Lowering the book with a sigh, he remarked, "The living appears as limited as she described. It strikes me as peculiar, considering the Beresford lineage belongs to a respectable family."

Meanwhile, Margaret had located her mother. Today marked one of Mrs. Hale's fitful intervals, wherein existence seemed fraught with difficulty and hardship; consequently, Mr. Lennox's arrival cast a shadow over her composure, though she secretly felt honored that he deigned to pay a visit.

"It is most unfortunate!" she lamented. "We are dining early today, subsisting solely on cold meats so the servants may finish their ironing. Yet, naturally, we must invite him to dine—he is, after all, Edith's brother-in-law. And your father is in such a low spirits this morning regarding some matter—I know not what. I entered the study moments ago to find him with his face buried in the desk, hands covering it. I assured him the air of Helstone agrees with neither him nor myself, yet he suddenly raised his head and beseeched me to cease speaking ill of Helstone, declaring he could not endure it further; if there exists one place he loves upon earth, it is Helstone. Nevertheless, I am convinced the atmosphere remains damp and relaxing."

A sensation akin to a thin, cold cloud drifted between Margaret and the sunlight. She had listened with patience, hoping her mother's venting might bring solace, but now it was time to redirect attention back to Mr. Lennox.

"Papa holds a high regard for Mr. Lennox; they connected wonderfully at the wedding breakfast. I am certain his presence will lift Papa's spirits. And need not trouble yourself regarding the dinner, dear Mamma. Cold meat suits a lunch perfectly, particularly when viewed through the lens of Mr. Lennox, who will likely perceive a two o'clock repast as precisely that."

"But what shall we do with him until then? It is barely half-past ten."

"I shall request he accompany me for a sketching excursion. I am aware he possesses artistic talents, and this will remove him from your immediate vicinity, Mamma. Now, do enter promptly; he would find it exceedingly strange otherwise."

Mrs. Hale removed her black silk apron and smoothed her features. She presented a picture of genteel refinement as she welcomed Mr. Lennox with the cordiality reserved for one almost kin to the family. He evidently anticipated spending the day and accepted the invitation with a joyful alacrity that left Mrs. Hale wishing she possessed additional cold beef. He expressed delight at everything, particularly at Margaret's suggestion of a joint sketching trip, and declined with evident pleasure to disturb Mr. Hale, anticipating their reunion at dinner. Margaret produced her drawing supplies for his selection; once the paper and brushes were duly chosen, the pair departed in the merriest spirits imaginable.

"Now, pray, pause here for a brief moment," Margaret instructed. "These cottages haunted me relentlessly during those rainy fourteen days, reproaching me for failing to capture them."

"Before they collapsed and vanished forever. Truly, if they merit a sketch—and they are undeniably picturesque—we ought not delay until next season. However, where shall we sit?"

"Oh! You might have emerged directly from chambers in the Temple, rather than residing two months in the Highlands! Observe this magnificent trunk of a tree, abandoned by the woodcutters in the perfect position for illumination. I shall drape my plaid over it, creating a veritable forest throne."

"With your feet submerged in that puddle, serving as a regal footstool!" he interjected playfully. "Wait, I shall shift slightly, allowing you to approach closer from this direction. Who inhabits these cottages?"

"They were constructed by squatters fifty or sixty years hence. One stands uninhabited; the foresters intend to demolish it immediately following the demise of the remaining occupant, a poor old soul! Look—there he stands. I must address him. He is so deaf you will overhear all our confidences."

The elderly man stood bareheaded beneath the sun, leaning upon his cane at the threshold of his dwelling. His stern features softened into a slow, gentle smile as Margaret approached and addressed him. Mr. Lennox swiftly incorporated both figures into his sketch, concluding the landscape with a subtle nod to their presence—as Margaret later realized, amidst the gathering of waterskins and discarded papers, as they displayed their works to one another. She laughed and blushed; Mr. Lennox watched her countenance intently.

"Now, I deem that treacherous," she declared. "I scarcely imagined you intended to make Old Isaac and me the subject of your art when you requested I inquire about the cottages' history."

"It proved irresistible. You cannot conceive the strength of the temptation. I hardly dare confess how greatly I shall cherish this sketch."

He was uncertain whether she had caught the latter sentiment before she retreated to the brook to cleanse her palette. Returning, she appeared somewhat flushed yet radiated innocence and unconsciousness. He rejoiced in this, for the remark had slipped from his lips unbidden—a rarity for a man like Henry Lennox, who meticulously premeditated his actions.

The atmosphere of home resumed its brightness upon their return. The clouds hovering over her mother's brow had dissipated under the auspicious influence of a brace of carp, graciously presented by a neighbor. Mr. Hale had concluded his morning rounds and awaited his visitor just outside the wicket gate leading to the garden. He appeared a complete gentleman, clad in a somewhat threadbare coat and a well-worn hat. Margaret felt a profound, tender pride in her father; she always marveled at how favorably he impressed strangers. Yet, her keen eye detected traces of unusual disturbance upon his face, disturbances that were suppressed but not entirely dispelled.

Mr. Hale requested inspection of their sketches.

"I suspect you have rendered the tints on the thatch somewhat too dark, have you not?" he observed, returning Margaret's work and extending his hand for Mr. Lennox's, which the latter hesitated to accept for a fleeting instant.
