The June sun blazed over the square, casting sharp shadows as a murmuring crowd gathered before the old brick house. Its red walls bore the ghostly imprints of antique insurance marks, and the "To Let" signs swayed like grim pendulums above the broken gate. Two constables barred the narrow alley, holding back the tide of onlookers. Women lingered at the edges, their whispers fluttering like moth wings as they angled for a better view of the house’s shuttered windows. The children had been hidden away, doors locked tight against the unseen horror.

A gaunt man with a hooked nose held court near the front, his voice low but urgent.

"I saw her face, clear as day," he insisted to the knot of listeners. "White as death itself. No mistaking it. I told my wife—'Jane, this ends now. We’ll have no part in it.' And from that moment, she never set foot past that gate again."

A murmur rippled through the crowd. Someone dared to ask, "Did she ever... *see* anything?"

The gaunt man’s eyes darkened. "What was seen stays sealed. 'Keep thy tongue from speaking evil,' as the Good Book says."

Nearby, a pudgy, wide-eyed man strained to be heard over the murmurs. "She took the key from me—three ones, three threes—111333!" His words dissolved into the din as others traded their own fragments of rumor.

"Shut your gobs," a burly laborer growled. "She ain’t been found yet. Here come the inspectors—we’ll know soon enough."

The officers pushed through, their presence hushing the crowd. The frantic little man rushed forward, babbling about the key, but they brushed him aside.

"Anyone been inside?" one inspector asked the constable.

"No, sir."

"Right. Brackley, with us. Smith, hold the gate. Squad’s on the way."

The three men disappeared into the alley, their footsteps swallowed by the oppressive silence of the house. The staircase groaned under their weight, dead leaves crackling underfoot.

"No need to knock," one muttered. "Break the glass."

A gloved fist shattered the door panel, the latch clicked, and the door swung inward.

"*Christ*—" one inspector choked, recoiling. "Open the windows. Now."

Outside, the crowd watched as the red blinds were torn up, the windows thrown wide. The stench that rolled out was thick, cloying—a miasma of rot and something worse.

Inside, the bedroom blind rose, flooding the room with cruel light. On the bed, a skeletal figure flinched, shielding his eyes. The man was barely recognizable—waxen, hollow, his mind adrift.

"Where is she?" one officer demanded, looming over him. "Elsie Bengough. Where?"

The man on the bed only shook his head, his lips moving soundlessly.

"Get him up. Carefully."

In the kitchen, the other inspector waded through ankle-deep decay—crushed petals, withered stems. Then he saw it: a trail swept clean, leading to the corner cupboard. The hatch slid open with a whisper.

And then—

A shape. Swaddled in faded cloth, slumped forward. Too large. Too *wrong*.

The inspector stepped back.

He didn’t touch it.

Instead, he forced the cupboard door shut, but not before a scrap of checkered fabric caught, jutting out like a final plea.

Outside, the murmurs had become a roar. A four-wheeler pulled up, a stretcher waiting. The emaciated man was half-carried, half-dragged toward it, his mind too broken to comprehend the shouts, the hatred, the rock that struck the cab.

The last thing he saw, before the door closed, was the "To Let" sign among the privet hedges.

Then darkness.

The cab rolled away.

The other vehicle turned toward the mortuary.

And the house stood silent, waiting for the next tenant.
