Only a truly great man would dare to utter something so blindingly obvious. Once upon a time, a modest Sunday roast was a luxury. Now, joints of meat appeared daily, and pudding became so frequent it lost all charm—another joy of life reduced to mere routine. We ate without restraint, no longer scheming how to stretch yesterday’s scraps into tomorrow’s meal. No more breaking bread with careful hands, mentally tallying crumbs for Saturday’s pudding.

Yet the old proverb failed us, for now, despite full bellies, laughter had fled the table. An unseen guest sat among us, stifling our chatter. No more teasing over half a pound of sausages or the scent of fresh herrings. Instead, we spoke in stiff, hollow voices of things that meant nothing.

Moving to Guilford Street would have been simple. Occasionally, in the lifeless tones they now used for everything but *the* subject, my parents discussed it—until some stray word would ignite the buried anger, and the months dragged on, the shadow between them deepening.

Then came the news: old Teidelmann had dropped dead in his counting house.

*"You’re going to her?"* asked my mother.

*"I’ve been sent for,"* said my father. *"It may be business."*

My mother laughed—a bitter sound I didn’t understand then—and my father stormed out. For hours, she locked herself away, and if I pressed my ear to the door, I was sure I heard crying. That she grieved so for old Teidelmann puzzled me.

After that, Mrs. Teidelmann visited more often. Mourning softened her—or seemed to. Her eyes lost some of their hardness. She was always sweet to my mother, who, beside her, seemed awkward and graceless. To me, she was unfailingly kind, arriving with books that *actually* interested me, not the dreary moral tales my mother favored, or planning treats that felt like treats, not dutiful lessons in virtue.

*"Has he ever seen a pantomime?"* she asked one morning, quirking her mouth in a way that made my heart leap.

My father raised his brows. *"What would your mother say?"*

*"She thinks theatres are wicked,"* I muttered—the first doubt I’d ever voiced about my mother’s judgments.

Mrs. Teidelmann’s smile deepened it. *"Dear me, I must be terribly wicked then. I’ve always found pantomimes quite moral. All the villains get their comeuppance so neatly. And we’d leave before the Clown stole the sausages, wouldn’t we, Paul?"*

My mother was summoned. She looked small and plain beside Mrs. Teidelmann’s rustling elegance.

*"Let him come,"* the lady coaxed. *"It’s Dick Whittington—such an uplifting tale."*

My mother stood silent, fingers twisting. *"No,"* she said at last, her voice grating. *"When he’s older, others may decide. For now, he follows me—alone."*

*"Surely there’s no harm,"* my father protested. *"Times change."*

*"Do they?"* said my mother. *"I wouldn’t know. I seem too old for this world."*

Mrs. Teidelmann yielded gracefully. *"I only thought to please him. He’s worked so hard."* Her hand lingered on my shoulder, warm and approving.

*"You’re very kind,"* said my mother. *"I would do anything for him—anything I could."* Her hand groped for mine, but I, nursing my grudge, refused to take it.

She didn’t mention it again. Yet the very next afternoon, she took me herself to a magic-lantern show and a conjurer in a drafty hall. She wore her prettiest dress, laughed brightly—but I, sulking, barely noticed the open-mouthed sleeper swallowing rats or the rabbit in the old man’s hat. All I saw was the glittering pantomime I’d been denied.

We walked home in silence through the greasy twilight. That night, half-waking, I thought I saw a white figure crouched at my bed’s foot. I cried out—and my mother rose from her knees.

*"A silly dream,"* she said, her laugh broken. *"A wicked magician stole you. I couldn’t find you. I was alone."*

Her arms tightened around me until I slept again.

Near the holidays’ end, we visited Mrs. Teidelmann at her Clapton mansion—a dull house outside, but inside, a treasure-cave. Old Teidelmann’s hoard spilled over every surface: paintings, statues, gem-bound books. Now he lay in his coffin under a famous tomb, and his daughter-in-law reclined in lace on a sofa, asleep.

We waited. The French clock ticked softly. Then—clear as a struck bell—she murmured in her sleep: *"Ah no, we must not. Luke, my darling."*

My mother rose, pale but composed. *"Go, Paul. Wait downstairs."*

Half an hour later, we left. All the way home, she walked like a ghost. At our gate, she woke. *"Never speak of this,"* she said. *"Delirium means nothing."*

From that day, she changed. No more anger, only quiet smiles. When Mrs. Teidelmann returned months later, my mother was the first to call. They became inseparable—or so it seemed. Peace returned; my father softened, my mother faded.

*"She needs air,"* said Dr. Hal. *"This city is killing her."*

So we returned to the village, climbed again to Jacob’s tower. One afternoon, my father came striding through the pines, his eyes alight—the old Viking look he wore when trouble loomed.

*"I’ve broken with Hasluck,"* he said. *"The man’s a thief."*

*"I thought you would,"* said my mother.

*"He laughed,"* said my father. *"Said that’s better than some."*

Noel Hasluck—kind to me, ruthless to the world. *"Business,"* he’d say, *"is a game of cheats. The sharpest win."* Once, I asked about the widows and orphans.

*"My dear boy,"* he chuckled, *"the last ‘widow’ who wept over her losses sold out at a profit—to her brother-in-law. Besides, I’m an orphan too."* His laugh boomed, rich with honesty. It was worth thousands to him.

But that was Hasluck’s way. And now, my father stood beside my mother on the tower, gazing at the hills where their youth had been.

*"We planned our future here,"* he said.

*"The future plans for us,"* she replied.

*"I’ve been unlucky,"* he admitted. *"Lost your money too."*

*"Fate,"* she said. *"Not you."*

*"If only that damned pump hadn’t jammed,"* he muttered.

She smiled. *"Remember Mrs. Tharand? ‘Of all troubles, money’s easiest to bear.’"*

*"I wish I believed that,"* he said.

The silence stretched. Then he laughed. *"Well, we’ll manage."*

But my mother was already far away, listening to echoes only she could hear.
