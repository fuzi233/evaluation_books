The correspondence from George Warrington confirms a peculiar stability: despite the Lord's prior maneuvers involving Harry, the bond between the Earl and his cousin remained intact, governed by a rigid code of kinsmanship. George required nothing more than access, introduction, or a seat in the House of Lords to witness the debates; his cousin was perpetually available to facilitate these desires, provided the transaction did not impinge upon the Earl's own standing. This utility extended to securing a position for George himself, a prospect the Virginian embraced with fervor. His reluctance to return to Virginia was not merely sentimental. The idleness of a planter's life held no allure unless coupled with autonomy—a condition impossible in the colony where he ranked as the most neglected among his mother's subjects. He foresaw a domestic trajectory defined by wearisome routine, tedious subordination, and the inevitable friction of shared quarters with a young bride. In contrast, London offered congenial society and the faint, intoxicating possibility of distinction. Thus, the Earl's patronage was accepted, even if the Earl's own provision for his brother had historically been scant.

Lord Castlewood, however, displayed a grace toward George's engagement to Miss Lambert that belied his reputation. He attended the Lamberts, praising the union to the parents and the young lady alike, cultivating an atmosphere so agreeable that the family's charitable nature filtered out his notorious past. They constructed a narrative wherein his presence was a temporary reprieve from the storm, a harbor where a worldling sheds his coat of wickedness to breathe. Perhaps he was genuinely better in their company, shedding the cynicism of his trade until the moment he stepped back into the hall. The Lambert elders, observing this performance, seemed satisfied. When Castlewood spoke of his inability to compete with innocence, claiming he could not learn goodness at his age, he did so with a melancholy precision that resonated with the matrons. He posited that Fate held the reins, that some souls were predestined angels while others, like him, were condemned to vacillation. Yet, Mrs. Lambert, reading the prayers in his eyes, refused to accept this fatalism. She argued that there was always an hour left for hope, a sentiment that softened the hard edges of his confession. He acknowledged the truth of her kindness, bowing with a grace that suggested a man aware of his own fragility, knowing that tears were often a debauch of sentiment, yet unable to suppress the genuine feeling that arose in their proximity.

This dynamic evolved into a sustained intimacy. The Ladies of Castlewood frequented the Lamberts' drawing rooms, engaging in card assemblies and games that the aristocracy deemed acceptable precisely because bishops and peers participated. Mrs. Lambert, finding solace in the admiration her daughters garnered, became a regular guest at these routs. Meanwhile, the Lambert matriarch, ever the architect of alliances, turned her attention to her son, Jack. With Harry absent in the colonies, she envisioned a new arrangement: a wealthy, handsome wife for Jack from the Dutch family recommended by Madam Esmond. She pressed this upon the General, ignoring his dismissal of her as a goose, arguing that breaking Hetty's heart was preferable to letting her son remain unmarried. The General, however, saw only the absurdity of sacrificing his daughter's happiness for a political convenience.

Parallel to these domestic machinations was the arrival of the Dutch family, specifically Miss Lydia. Initially confined to the lower circles of London, she ventured outward with her grandfather, seeking to expand her sphere. She encountered Mr. Draper of the Temple, who viewed her as a prize, and Mr. Drabshaw, a Quaker nephew who regarded her arrival with alarm. George Warrington, wishing to honor his mother's neighbors, hosted an entertainment where Miss Lydia's presence commanded immediate attention. She possessed the foot of a fairy and the flashing eyes of a huntress, carrying a plaintive accent from home that, rather than marking her as foreign, added a layer of charm to her speech. Whether one favored the Cockney imitation, the northern burr, or the Irish warble, the consensus was that beauty dictated the value of the voice. After six months of instruction at the Kensington Academy, Miss Lydia returned to her grandfather's care, eager to navigate the narrow world of society. The Lambert family welcomed the benevolent old man, unaware that the intricate web of engagements and ambitions was tightening around them, with George and Theo already bound, rendering Madam Esmond's matchmaking efforts moot, and leaving Mrs. Lambert to devise her own schemes amidst the shifting tides of London life.
