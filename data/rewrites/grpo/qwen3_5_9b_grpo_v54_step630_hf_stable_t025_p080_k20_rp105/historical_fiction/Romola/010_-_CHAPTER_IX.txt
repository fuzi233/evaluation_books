A Man's Ransom.

The Piazza was alive with the heat of the midday sun, and Tito moved swiftly through the throng, his steps carrying him from the square toward the Corso degli Adimari. Though he offered only a casual answer to Nello's inquiry regarding his recent encounter, a quiet longing stirred within him. As he navigated the crowd, his mind drifted briefly to the pair of blue eyes he had seen gazing up from beneath the white linen hood of a country woman at the festival. He knew well that face belonged to Tessa, yet he chose silence. Why should Nello concern himself with such trivialities? Tito possessed a natural aptitude for reticence, a talent that operated without conscious intent. Like many who find concealment effortless, he sometimes hid things that required no hiding at all, such as the mere sight of a flock of crows.

However, this fleeting thought of Tessa was quickly overshadowed by a more persistent memory: the face of the friar, pale and fanatical, who had stared at him with an intensity that felt irrecoverably linked to his own past. Why had that sickly figure singled him out? Where, in all his travels, had he seen such a visage before? It was folly to dwell on such vague, cobweb-like impressions; they demanded to be swept away. By the time he turned from the Corso into a narrow side street, his thoughts had shifted entirely. He cared only that the sun burned high and that the procession had delayed him from his intended visit to the room in the Via de' Bardi. He knew his arrival was anxiously awaited there.

In his imagination, he rehearsed the scene of his entrance: the blind man's face, usually obscured, glowing with a diffuse joy like light filtering through a translucent lamp; the faint, transient flush on Romola's cheeks and neck, which added a delicate, feminine charm to her inherent majesty, enhanced by a boyish frankness in her gaze and smile. Together, they were the finest companions, circling the blind man's chair. She appealed to him constantly, and he informed her, yet he felt a strange subjugation to Romola, bowing before the simplicity of her nature. For the first time, he experienced a loving awe in the presence of noble womanhood—a reverence akin to the ancient worship of a great goddess, whose power was deeper and more primordial than mere knowledge. They had never been alone, and he could conjure no probable image of love between them. He could only imagine wildly, though he knew it impossible, that one day Romola would speak her love.

He recalled a day in Greece, leaning against a sun-drenched wall, when a small peasant girl with dark eyes had crept closer until she shyly asked for a kiss, offering her round olive cheek with innocent trust. Tito was accustomed to love arriving in such unsought ways. But Romola's love would never come thus; would it ever come at all? Yet, it remained the highest apple upon which his mind had fixed itself. In his fresh youth, though not yet passionate, he was deeply impressionable. It was as inevitable that he should feel love for Romola as it was for white irises to reflect in a sunlit stream. He possessed no vanity, yet he held an intimate sense that she stood far above him. Many men have felt this same distance before a large-eyed, simple child.

Nevertheless, Tito had achieved rapid success. Some men might have grown presuming, or at least entertained the hope of becoming Romola's husband, or even convinced her father, Bartolommeo Scala, of such a future. His first meeting with the secretary had indeed marked the beginning of growing favor. Politian, professor of both Greek and Latin in Florence, found himself without a rival since the departure of Demetrio Calcondila to Milan. Friends wished to teach the renowned scholar humility, and it was agreed that Tito should be supported in a Greek chair, following the precedent set by Lorenzo the Magnificent for Calcondila. There was nothing invidious in this arrangement, only a zeal for true learning and the education of Florentine youth.

Tito sailed under the fairest breeze. He convinced fair judges that his talents matched his fortune, wearing his good luck with such ease and lack of pretense that no offense was taken. He was destined for the best Florentine society, a circle where fine plate abounded and the Signory permitted the wearing of rich brocade. Where else would a handsome young scholar be welcomed, one who could play the lute and sing a gay song? His bright face, easy smile, and liquid voice seemed to bring a holiday to the city, making the work-worn and the sad ashamed of their own gloom. He was a professor likely to render the Greek classics amiable to the sons of great houses.

This was not all his fortune; he had sold his jewels, keeping only one ring, and now held five hundred gold florins. Yet, the moment he first possessed this sum marked the crisis of the first serious struggle his facile, good-humored nature had ever known. An importunate thought, previously seen only as a shadow, suddenly grasped him. He was forced to pause and decide: would he surrender to duty, or offer a refusal with irrevocable consequences?

It was in the room above Nello's shop, which Tito now rented, that the elder Cennini handed him the final portion of the funds on behalf of Bernardo Rucellai, the buyer of the two precious gems.

"Ecco, giovane mio!" the respectable printer and goldsmith declared. "You now possess a pretty little fortune. If you will take my advice, let me place your florins in a safe quarter where they may increase, rather than slipping through your fingers for banquets and follies common among our Florentine youth. It has become the fashion of scholars, especially when, like our Pietro Crinito, they believe their scholarship needs scenting and embroidery, to squander with one hand until they must beg with the other. I have brought you the money, and you are free to make a wise or unwise choice; I shall see which way the balance tips. We Florentines hold no man a member of an Art until he has shown his skill and been matriculated; and no man is matriculated to the art of life until he has been well tempted. If you decide to put your florins out to usury, let me know tomorrow. A scholar may marry, and should have something in readiness for the *morgen-cap*. Addio."

As Cennini closed the door, Tito turned, the smile fading from his face, and fixed his eyes on the table where the florins lay. He made no other movement, standing with his thumbs in his belt, looking down in a transfixed state, his consciousness concentrated on some inward image.

"A man's ransom!" Who had said that five hundred florins was more than a man's ransom? If, under this midday sun on some hot coast far away, a man somewhat aged, yet possessing high thoughts and a passionate heart—a man who had once rescued a boy from beggary and cruelty, raising him tenderly as a father—were now toiling as a slave, hewing wood and drawing water, perhaps struck down because he lacked agility? If that man were thinking, "Tito will find me; he need only carry our manuscripts and gems to Venice; he will raise money and never rest until he finds me"? If that were certain, could Tito, seeing the price of the gems before him, say, "I will stay in Florence, fanned by soft airs of promised love and prosperity; I will not risk myself for his sake"? No, surely not, *if it were certain*.

But nothing was certain. The galley had been captured by a Turkish vessel on its way to Delos, known only by the report of the companion galley that had escaped. There had been resistance, probable bloodshed, and a man seen falling overboard. Who were the survivors, and what had befallen them amidst the multitude of possibilities? Had not Tito himself suffered shipwreck and narrowly escaped drowning? He had good cause to feel the omnipresence of casualties threatening all projects with futility. Rumors of pirate settlements in Delos were unreliable. What would likely happen if he left Florence for Venice, obtained authoritative letters, and set out for the Archipelago? Why, that he himself would be seized, spend all his florins on preliminaries, and become again a destitute wanderer with no gems to sell.

Tito had a clearer vision of this disastrous result than of the moment he might find his father and carry him deliverance. It would surely be an unfairness for him, in his full ripe youth, to whom life had hitherto held some of the restraint of a school, to turn his back on promised love and distinction, perhaps never to be visited by that promise again. "And yet," he told himself, "if I were certain that Baldassarre Calvo was alive, and that I could free him by whatever exertions or perils, I would go now—now that I have the money. I would go now to Bardo and Bartolommeo Scala and tell them the whole truth."

He did not explicitly tell himself that if those two men knew the truth, he would have no alternative but to search for his benefactor, the rightful owner of the gems, whom he had always equivocally spoken of as "lost." He did not acknowledge that Greeks of distinction had made sacrifices, undertaken repeated voyages, and sought aid from crowned and mitred heads to free relatives from Turkish slavery. Public opinion did not regard this as exceptional virtue.

This was his first real colloquy with himself. He had followed the impulses of the moment, one of which had been to conceal half the truth. He had never considered this part of his conduct long enough to face the consciousness of his motives for concealment. What was the use of telling the whole? It was true, the thought had crossed his mind several times since leaving Nauplia that it was a great relief to be quit of Baldassarre, and he would have liked to know *who* had fallen overboard. But such thoughts spring inevitably from a relationship that has become irksome. Baldassarre was exacting and had grown stranger as he aged; he constantly scrutinized Tito's mind to see if it met his own exaggerated expectations. Age—the age of a thickset, heavy-browed, bald man beyond sixty, whose intensity and eagerness in the grasp of ideas had long taken the character of monotony and repetition—could be viewed from many angles without being found attractive. Such a man, stranded among new acquaintances, unless he possessed the philosopher's stone, would hardly find rank, youth, and beauty at his feet. The feelings that gather fervor from novelty would be of little help in making the world a home for dimmed and faded human beings. Unless they retained a love rooted in memories, distilling perpetually the sweet balms of fidelity and forbearing tenderness, they were widowed of all affection.

But surely such memories were not absent from Tito's mind? Far in the backward vista of his remembered life, when he was only seven years old, Baldassarre had rescued him from blows, taking him to a home that seemed like opened paradise, filled with sweet food and soothing caresses, all centered on Baldassarre's knee. From that time until the hour they parted, Tito had been the sole center of Baldassarre's fatherly cares.

He had been docile, pliable, quick of apprehension, and ready to acquire: a very bright, lovely boy, a youth of splendid grace, who seemed quite without vices, as if that beautiful form represented a vitality so exquisitely poised and balanced that it could know no uneasy desires, no unrest—a radiant presence for a lonely man to have won for himself. If he was silent when his father expected a response, he did not look moody; if he declined some labor, he flung himself down with such a charming, half-smiling, half-pleading air that the pleasure of looking at him made amends to one who had watched his growth with a sense of claim and possession. The curves of Tito's mouth held ineffable good humor. And then, the quick talent to which everything came readily, from philosophical systems to the rhymes of a street ballad caught up at a hearing! Would anyone have said that Tito had not made a rich return to his benefactor, or that his gratitude and affection would fail on any great demand?

He did not admit that his gratitude had failed; but *it was not certain* that Baldassarre was in slavery, nor certain that he was living.

"Do I not owe something to myself?" Tito said inwardly, with a slight movement of his shoulders, the first he had made since turning to look down at the florins. "Before I quit everything and incur again all the risks of which I am even now weary, I must at least have a reasonable hope. Am I to spend my life in a wandering search? *I believe he is dead.* Cennini was right about my florins: I will place them in his hands tomorrow."

When, the next morning, Tito put this determination into action, he had chosen his color in the game and given an inevitable bent to his wishes. He had made it impossible that he should not from henceforth desire it to be the truth that his father was dead; impossible that he should not be tempted to baseness rather than that the precise facts of his conduct should not remain forever concealed.

Under every guilty secret is hidden a brood of guilty wishes, whose unwholesome, infecting life is cherished by the darkness. The contaminating effect of deeds often lies less in the commission than in the consequent adjustment of our desires—the enlistment of self-interest on the side of falsity. On the other hand, the purifying influence of public confession springs from the fact that by it the hope in lies is forever swept away, and the soul recovers the noble attitude of simplicity.

Besides, in this first distinct colloquy with himself, the ideas which had previously been scattered and interrupted had now concentrated. The little rills of selfishness had united to make a channel, so that they could never again meet with the same resistance. Hitherto, Tito had left in vague indecision the question whether, with the means in his power, he would not return to ascertain his father's fate.
