“Come in!” Trent called out, his voice crisp and alert.

Mr. Cupples stepped into the sitting room of the hotel, the evening light casting long shadows across the worn furnishings. It was the same day the coroner’s jury had returned their verdict—without leaving the box—declaring the death a result of “a person or persons unknown.” Trent stood near the window, his eyes fixed on a photographic negative resting in an enamelled dish. He moved it slowly, studying it with intense concentration. His face was pale, and his hands trembled slightly as he worked.

“Sit down,” he said, gesturing toward the sofa. “The chairs are relics—bought at auction after the fall of the Holy Inquisition in Spain. This one’s actually quite comfortable.”

He held up the negative, tilting it to catch the fading daylight. “This is a good one,” he remarked, adjusting his head for better judgment. “Washed enough now. Let’s let it dry and clear this mess away.”

As Trent busied himself clearing the table—basins, bottles, racks, boxes—Mr. Cupples picked up each item, examining them with quiet curiosity.

“That’s hypo-eliminator,” Trent said, catching him sniffing a bottle. “Useful when you’re racing against time. But don’t drink it—I wouldn’t risk it. It removes sodium hypophosphite, but I’d wager it could remove a man too.”

After arranging the last object on the cluttered mantel, Trent sat down on the edge of the table, facing Mr. Cupples.

“The beauty of a hotel room,” he mused, “is that it doesn’t distract. No charm, no comfort—just function. Perfect for work. Have you ever been in this room before?”

“No,” replied Cupples.

“I have—hundreds of times. It follows me around England like a familiar ghost. If they gave me another room in some distant inn, I’d feel lost. Look at this tablecloth—the ink stain from Halifax. That hole in the carpet? Ipswich. And the glass over ‘Silent Sympathy’—they’ve repaired it. I threw a boot at it in Banbury. This is where I do my best thinking. Today, since the inquest, I’ve finished several excellent negatives. There’s a decent darkroom downstairs.”

“The inquest,” said Cupples, suddenly remembering. “I came to thank you for looking after Mabel earlier. I didn’t expect her to be affected. She seemed so composed—so strong. I thought she could manage alone while I listened to the evidence. I’m glad she found someone to help her. She’s back to herself now.”

Trent said nothing at first, his hands tucked into his pockets, a faint frown creasing his brow.

“I was just getting to the really interesting part,” he said finally. “Would you like to see a little high-class detective work? The kind old Murch should be doing right now. Maybe he is. But I hope not.”

He rose abruptly and vanished into his bedroom. Moments later, he returned carrying a large drawing board loaded with odd objects.

“Let me introduce you to these,” he said, placing them carefully on the table. “An ivory paper knife. Two pages torn from my own diary. A bottle of toothpaste. A walnut case. Some of these need to go back to a bedroom at White Gables before nightfall. That’s the sort of man I am—nothing stops me. I took them this morning, while everyone was at the inquest. Some might think it peculiar. Now—there’s one thing left on the board. Can you tell me what it is, without touching it?”

Cupples leaned forward. “It’s a glass bowl. Looks like a finger-bowl. Nothing unusual about it.”

“I can’t see much either,” Trent admitted. “And that’s exactly why it’s interesting.”

He handed Cupples a small fat bottle. “Pull the cork. Do you recognize the powder inside?”

“It’s grey powder,” Cupples said, squinting. “They give it to babies. Mercury and chalk, I suppose?”

“Exactly. Great stuff. Now—while I hold the bowl sideways over this sheet of paper, pour a little of it onto the surface here.”

Cupples did as instructed. The powder fell evenly.

“Perfect,” Trent said. “Sir Edward Henry himself couldn’t have done better. You’ve done this before, haven’t you?”

“I haven’t,” Cupples insisted. “I’m completely baffled. What did I just do?”

Trent returned the excess powder to the bottle. “Now I’ll brush the powdered area gently with this camel-hair brush. Look again.”

Cupples peered closely.

“How curious!” he exclaimed.

“Two large grey fingerprints,” Trent said. “They weren’t there before. The human hand, even when clean, leaves moisture—especially under stress. When it touches cold glass, it leaves traces. This bowl was handled recently by someone with damp fingers.”

He sprinkled more powder. “Here’s the thumb mark—excellent impression. And this—index finger. A single-spiral whorl, symmetrically arranged. The second finger—simple loop, staple core, fifteen ridge counts. I know because I have the exact same prints on this negative.”

He held it up to the dimming sunlight, pointing with a pencil. “See the bifurcation? The scar near the center? They match perfectly. An expert would swear in court these marks came from the same hand.”

“But where did you take the prints?” Cupples asked, wide-eyed.

“From the inside of the left pane of the front window in Mrs. Manderson’s bedroom. I couldn’t bring the window, so I photographed it—using black paper on the other side for contrast. The bowl? It’s from Manderson’s room. That’s where he kept his false teeth at night. I brought that one.”

“So these aren’t Mabel’s prints?”

“Impossible,” Trent said sharply. “They’re twice the size of any print she could make.”

“Then they must belong to her husband.”

“Possibly. But let’s test it again.”

Whistling softly, Trent opened a squat bottle of dense black powder.

“Lamp-black,” he explained. “Hold a piece of paper for a few seconds—this will reveal your fingerprint pattern.”

With tweezers, he lifted one of the diary pages and showed it to Cupples—no marks visible. Then he dusted both sides lightly with the powder, shook off the excess, and held it out.

On one side, two distinct black fingerprints appeared—identical to those on the bowl and the negative.

He turned the paper over. On the reverse, a bold thumbprint matched the grey one on the glass.

“Same hand,” Trent said, laughing shortly. “I knew it. Now I know.”

He walked to the window, staring out.

“I know,” he repeated, quietly, almost to himself. His voice was low, bitter.

Cupples watched him, stunned.

“I still don’t understand,” he said after a moment. “I’ve heard of fingerprinting, but I don’t see how finding Manderson’s prints helps us—”

Trent cut him off, returning quickly to the table.

“I’m sorry, Cupples,” he said, his tone firm. “When I started this, I meant to include you every step of the way. But now I can’t say more. Not yet. I’ve discovered something that could cause terrible consequences if anyone else learns it.”

His face hardened. He struck the table once.

“It’s unbearable. Until now, I hoped I was wrong. I may still be. But there’s only one way to find out—and I must face it.”

He smiled suddenly, easing the tension.

“Don’t worry—I won’t dwell on tragedy. I’ll tell you everything soon. For now—look, I’m not done with the powder bottles.”

He pulled over one of the battered chairs and sat down, picking up the ivory paper knife.

“Let’s test this blade,” he said.

Cupples, still reeling, leaned forward, handing him the lamp-black bottle.
