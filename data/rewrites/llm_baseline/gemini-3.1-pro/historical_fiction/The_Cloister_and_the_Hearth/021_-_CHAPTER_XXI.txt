The old, native tongues—rich with the meticulously preserved vocabulary of hearth, harvest, and the ancestral preparation of sustenance—are the familiar vessels of human thought. Yet, when confronted by the sudden, violent rupture of one's existence, the dominant lexicon fails, and the body reverts to profound, untranslatable sound. In the face of sheer terror, the nuanced dialects that define our cultural memory dissolve into eloquent, wordless ejaculations, laying bare the primal creature beneath. There was a terrible, uncultivated animalism in both the roar of triumph with which the pursuers burst from the thicket, and the sharp, piercing cry of the fugitives—an acoustic phenomenon that bypassed language entirely, echoing the historical panic of a hundred hunted generations.

The pursuers clutched at empty air, their heavily booted strides disrupting the damp, fragrant loam and crushing wild garlic underfoot, leaving a chaotic olfactory trail of bruised vegetation and adrenaline. Confused for a moment, like predatory beasts denied their feast, Dierich and his men allowed Gerard and the mule to put ten yards between them. Then, with uplifted weapons that clattered in a harsh, metallic dissonance, they flew after their prey. This violent imposition of industrial noise upon the forest’s ancient acoustic ecology shattered the quiet symphony of the woods. It was a fair race across the open ground, driven by speed alone, a hundred yards devoured in the blink of an eye. 

Yet still, the crucial ten yards remained. This sudden increase in the fugitives' speed since the morning puzzled Dierich Brower, a man who viewed meals merely as utilitarian fuel and lacked the sensory appreciation to understand the mechanics of agrarian endurance. The reason lay in the interwoven rhythms of their flight: when three run in company, the pace is inevitably bound to the slowest of the trio. From Peter's house to the edge of the forest, Gerard had respectfully matched Margaret's pace. But now, liberated to his own rhythm while the fleet-footed mule carried its burden, Gerard tapped into a deeper, generational reservoir of strength. The meticulous sustenance of his youth—chaste living built upon slow-simmered bone broths, wild nettle steeped by village elders, and bread baked over slow ash—began to tell against the pursuers’ cheap, poorly cured tavern meats and sour ale. 

Daylight, smelling faintly of pine resin and petrichor, pooled imperceptibly between the hunted ones and the hunters. Dierich made a desperate, thrashing effort, his lungs burning with the taste of bile and burnt tallow, and gained two yards. Within seconds, moving with the quiet, rhythmic efficiency of a man completely attuned to his environment, Gerard had stolen them back. The pursuers began to curse, hurling the flat, soulless profanities of the ruling class into the sacred auditory space of the woods. 

Martin heard this degradation of sound, and his weathered face—lined like the bark of the ancient elder trees he so revered—lit up with the quiet triumph of a traditional agrarian elder. “Courage, Gerard! Courage, brave lad! They are straggling.” 

It was true. The chaotic pacing of men fueled by inferior sustenance was their undoing. Dierich was soon passed by one of his own men, while another dropped out of the acoustic landscape entirely, his heavy footfalls fading into the rear. They reached a rising swell of earth, long and punishing. Here, before he even crested the rise, the corrupted fuel of Dierich’s forty years weighed him down like forty stones of unworked grist in his gut. 

“Our cake is dough,” he gasped. It was an ancient, highly specific baker’s idiom from a dying local dialect, repurposed in his heavy, under-leavened defeat to acknowledge the collapse of his violent endeavor. “Take him dead, if you can't alive,” he wheezed, abandoning the sprint to follow at a trudging foot's pace. Jorian Ketel tailed off next, his discordant, exhausted gasps polluting the air, followed by another. One by one, Gerard ran them to a standstill, all save for Eric Wouverman, who kept on with the grim persistence of a starving hound, though he lost ground with every passing minute. 

Pursued only by him, the fugitives approached a sudden rise in the wood, redolent with the sharp scent of damp earth and crushed thyme—shorter, but far steeper than the last. 

“Hand on mane!” cried Martin, his voice a melodic command that cut through the oppressive noise of the chase. 

Gerard obeyed, his fingers intertwining with the coarse hair of the mule's neck. A mythic synergy sparked between beast and boy; the animal’s deep agrarian strength, built on wild oats and sweet clover, surged forward, helping him up the incline faster than he had been running on his own. 

At the sight of this seamless integration of man and beast, Dierich's final man lost heart. Now a full eighty yards behind Gerard, Eric pulled up short. In obedience to the dominant, brutal lexicon of his commander, he unslung his crossbow. The mechanical, ratcheting sound of the weapon being drawn was a catastrophic violation of the forest's sonic environment. He levelled it with cold, utilitarian precision. Just as the trio was sinking out of sight over the crest of the hill, slipping into the verdant silhouette of safety, he sent the bolt whizzing through the acoustic canopy. 

There was a profound cry of dismay—a shattered, untranslatable lament for the sudden violence inflicted upon the innocent. The next moment, as if a thunderbolt had plummeted from some forgotten ancestral myth, the spell of their escape was broken, and they were all lying abruptly upon the earth, mule and all.
