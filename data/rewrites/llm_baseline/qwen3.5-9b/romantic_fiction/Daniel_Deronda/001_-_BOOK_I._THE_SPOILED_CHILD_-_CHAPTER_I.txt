Men require a fiction to begin anything. Even science, that rigorous measurer of reality, must invent a starting point; it selects a specific star in its endless journey and declares, "Here, at this precise moment, Time is Zero." Its less precise counterpart, Poetry, is traditionally said to begin in the middle. Yet, upon closer inspection, their methods are remarkably similar. Science counts backward as well as forward, dividing its units into billions, and even with its clock hand pointing to Zero, it truly launches *in medias res*. No amount of looking back can reach the absolute origin. Whether our prologue unfolds in heaven or on earth, it remains merely a fragment of the vast, presupposed fact from which our narrative springs.

Was she beautiful, or not? What secret combination of form and expression lent such dynamic power to her glance? Did the good or the evil genius reign within those beams? Likely the latter; otherwise, why did her presence evoke unrest rather than serene charm? Why did the desire to look again feel like coercion rather than a willing surrender of the soul? These were the questions swirling in Daniel Deronda's mind as he observed the woman before him. She was currently engaged in gambling—not in the open air beneath a southern sun, tossing copper coins against a crumbling wall with ragged limbs, but within one of those magnificent establishments that centuries of enlightenment have crafted for such pleasures at a steep price. The venue was adorned with heavy gilt moldings, dark tones, and plump nudes, creating a dense condenser for the breath of humanity's elite, a mixture rarely available to those of lesser station.

It was nearly four o'clock on a September afternoon, the air thick with a visible haze. Deep silence hung over the room, punctuated only by the soft clatter of chips, a gentle sweep of the dealer's cloth, and the occasional monotone murmur of French, sounding as if spoken by a sophisticated automaton. Two long tables hosted packed crowds, all eyes fixed downward except for one. That solitary exception was a melancholy little boy, dressed in a fantastical costume save for his bare legs and feet. He stood behind a lady deeply engrossed at a roulette table, staring blankly toward the doorway with the vacant gaze of a costumed child advertising an itinerant show.

Around the table, fifty or sixty people were gathered. Spectators sat in the outer rows, occasionally dropping a five-franc note with a simpering air, merely curious about the thrill of the game. Those fully immersed in high-stakes play displayed a remarkable variety of European types: Livonian, Spanish, Greek-Italian, and various German strains, alongside English aristocrats and commoners alike. Here was a stark admission of human equality. The jeweled, manicured fingers of an English countess hovered inches from a bony, yellow, claw-like hand gripping a stack of coins. That hand belonged to a figure with a square, gaunt face, deep-set eyes, grizzled brows, and ill-combed hair that resembled a slight mutation of a vulture. Nearby sat a prematurely aged woman, withered like an artificial flower after its brief bloom, clutching a shabby velvet reticule and occasionally popping the tip of her card-pricker into her mouth. Close to the countess also stood a respectable London tradesman, blonde and soft-handed, his hair meticulously parted. Conscious of circulars addressed to the nobility, he enjoyed fashionable holidays and the company of the gentry. Unlike the true gambler whose passion overrides appetite, this man sought only a well-fed leisure, alternating between making money in business and spending it ostentatiously in play. He reasoned that Providence approved of his amusement, ready to quit if the sweet taste of winning turned sour. To him, the vice of gambling lay solely in losing money. Though he retained the bearing of a merchant, in his pleasures he ranked with ancient titled families.

Beside him stood a handsome Italian, calm and statuesque, reaching across to place a fresh pile of napoleons onto the table. Within minutes, the pile was pushed toward an elderly woman wearing glasses that pinched her nose. A faint, gleaming smile touched her lips, but the Italian remained impassive, confident in his infallible system that placed his foot firmly on the neck of Chance, already preparing the next wager. Similarly, a man resembling an emaciated beau or a spent libertine, peering at life through a single eyeglass, extended his trembling hand for change. Perhaps it was not a rigid system, but a delusion of white crows or the superstition that the eighth of the month was lucky, driving his fierce yet tottering impulses.

Despite their individual differences, every player wore a uniform negativity of expression, like a mask. It was as if they had all consumed a root that forced their minds into a narrow, monotonous loop of action. Deronda's initial reaction to this scene of dull, gas-choked absorption was that the gambling of Spanish shepherd boys seemed far more enviable. One might almost justify Rousseau's claim that art and science had served mankind poorly.

Suddenly, however, the atmosphere became dramatic. Deronda's attention seized by a young lady standing nearby, the last object of his gaze. She was bending to speak English to a middle-aged woman seated at the table, then straightening up to resume play. She revealed the full stature of a graceful figure, possessing a face that might not immediately inspire admiration but certainly demanded notice. An internal debate sparked in Deronda, sharpening his scrutiny until it drifted far beyond the vague warmth of undefined attraction.

At moments, his eyes tracked the movement of her arms and hands as this enigmatic sylph deposited her stake with an air of decisive choice. Then they returned to her face, steady and focused on the game, seemingly unaffected by observers. The sylph was winning. As her slender, pale-gray gloved fingers adjusted the coins pushed toward her, she glanced around with a survey so cold and neutral it suggested artifice masking inner triumph. But during this scan, her eyes met Deronda's. Instead of averting them as she intended, she felt an unpleasant awareness that he was arresting her gaze.

A fleeting sensation told her he was measuring her, looking down upon her as an inferior, distinct from the human dross surrounding her. He appeared to belong to a realm outside and above her, examining her as a specimen of a lower order. This provoked a tingling resentment that stretched the moment in conflict. It did not flush her cheeks but drained the color from her lips. Controlling herself with an inward defiance, she offered no further sign of emotion save for that pallor before turning back to the game. Yet, Deronda's gaze seemed to act as an evil eye. Her stake vanished.

It mattered little; she had been winning since arriving with a few napoleons and possessed a substantial reserve. She began to believe in her own luck, as others had begun to believe in hers. Visions arose of a *cortège* following her, worshipping her as a goddess of fortune and watching her play as a divine omen. While such tales existed for male gamblers, why not for a woman seeking supremacy? Her friend and chaperon, initially opposed to her playing, now approved, offering prudent advice to stop and return to England. Gwendolen Harleth dismissed this, stating she cared for the excitement, not the winnings. Under this assumption, the current moment should have marked the peak of her gambling fervor.

Yet, when her next stake was swept away, she felt her eyes heating up. The certainty of that man still watching her felt like a tightening pressure, becoming increasingly torturous. This only fueled her resolve not to flinch, to continue playing as if indifferent to loss or gain. Her friend touched her elbow, suggesting they leave. In response, Gwendolen placed ten louis on the same spot. She entered a mood of defiance where the mind ignores all ends beyond the satisfaction of enraged resistance, foolishly including luck itself as an object of that defiance. Since she wasn't winning spectacularly, the next best option was to lose spectacularly. She controlled her muscles, showing no tremor in her mouth or hands. Every time her stake disappeared, she doubled it.

Many now watched her, but the only observation she registered was Deronda's. Though she never looked directly at him, she was certain he hadn't moved away. Such dramas unfold quickly; development and catastrophe can often be measured by nothing clumsier than the ticking of the clock hand.

"Faites votre jeu, mesdames et messieurs," announced the automatic voice of destiny from behind the croupier's mustache and powdered wig. Gwendolen stretched her arm to deposit her final, meager heap of napoleons. "Le jeu ne va plus," declared destiny. Within five seconds, Gwendolen turned from the table, but she turned resolutely with her face toward Deronda, locking gazes with him.

An ironic smile played in his eyes as their目光s met, yet it was preferable that he had kept his attention fixed on her than ignored her as one of an anonymous insect swarm. Moreover, despite his superciliousness and irony, it was hard to believe he didn't admire both her spirit and her person. He was young, handsome, and distinguished in appearance—not one of those ridiculous, dowdy Philistines who felt compelled to frown with sour disapproval as they passed the gaming tables. The general conviction that we are admirable rarely yields to a single negative; when members of Vanity's large family find their performance received coldly, they tend to believe that adding more of themselves will sway the dissenting observer. In Gwendolen's mindset, it was assumed she knew what was admirable and that she herself was admired. This foundation had suffered a disagreeable shock, reeling slightly, but proving difficult to overthrow.
