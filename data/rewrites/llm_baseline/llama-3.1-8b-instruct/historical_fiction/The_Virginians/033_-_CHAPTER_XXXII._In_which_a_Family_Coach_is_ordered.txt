As the harvest season drew to a close, the Oakhurst family found themselves with a rare opportunity to take a leisurely journey to Tunbridge Wells. With the corn still unripe and the farm horses idle, Colonel Lambert proposed a most delightful excursion. He suggested that they pack up the ancient family coach, a magnificent vehicle said to have carried King George I in his coronation procession, and set off towards Westerham, where they would meet up with their dear friend Colonel Wolfe along the way.

The children erupted into joyful excitement at the prospect of this adventure, and Mamma, pleased by the idea, eagerly agreed to the plan. The girls quickly gathered their finest clothes, including delicate calamancoes, intricately embroidered mantuas, and sparkling solitaires, while Mamma retrieved her own treasured gowns from the presses, reserved for special occasions. The Colonel donned his finest laced hat and waistcoat, and Charley proudly wore a holiday suit that had once belonged to his father, though Mrs. Lambert couldn't help but feel a pang of nostalgia as she remembered the happy day it was worn on her wedding.

The family's horses, adorned with ribbons and flowers, led the way, with Chump, the gentle old cart-horse, taking the lead as unicorn. Brian, the loyal coachman, sat atop the box, accompanied by young Charley, while the precious clothes were carefully stowed away in imperials on the roof. The Colonel's pistols were discreetly placed in the carriage pockets, and the blunderbuss hung securely behind the box, within easy reach of Brian's experienced hand.

Their journey passed uneventfully, with no highwaymen to trouble them and no inns demanding exorbitant tolls from the Colonel's slender purse. They arrived at the lodgings arranged by Colonel Wolfe, a modest but comfortable abode opposite Madame Bernstein's, where the sound of laughter and music drifted out into the evening air. As they settled into their rooms, the young ladies, Hetty and Theo, watched with fascination as the elegant guests departed from the Baroness's doorstep, their powdered wigs and brocaded dresses catching the fading light of day.

Colonel Wolfe regaled the ladies with stories of the various personages they saw, and the girls felt as though they were attending the party themselves, watching the festivities through the open windows of the Bernstein residence.
