Across the way, dear reader, if you’ll kindly step with us, we find ourselves at Lord Wrotham’s residence—lent by his lordship to the General and his small household party, those same individuals we met earlier at Oakhurst and Tunbridge Wells. James Wolfe has promised to join them for dinner, but as it happens, he is currently occupied with Miss Lowther, whose glance holds more value in his eyes than any delicacy prepared by Lord Wrotham’s finest cook or the sumptuous dessert that awaits us. You, gentle reader, will make the sixth guest. Feel free to take Mr. Wolfe’s seat—he won’t be coming. As for me, I shall remain at the sideboard, observing and reporting.

Observe first the joy on the women’s faces! When Harry Warrington was seized by bailiffs, I had meant to tell you how Mrs. Lambert, upon hearing of his misfortune, rushed to her husband, pleading, beseeching, demanding that Martin lend a hand. “Forget his rebelliousness from the other day—forget his anger when his gifts were returned; anyone would be angry, especially a spirited boy like Harry! Forget our poverty, forget that every spare penny goes to the boys at college—there must be a way to help him. Didn’t you rescue Charles Watkins two years ago? He paid you back every farthing. And you made an entire family happy—blessed be God! Mrs. Watkins still prays for you and blesses you daily. I believe everything has prospered for us since then. And I’m certain your kindness helped make you a major-general—no doubt about it,” she insisted, full of fervor.

Martin Lambert, ever ready to do a kind deed, needed little convincing. He resolved to seek out James Wolfe and post bail for Harry. Taking his hat and bidding farewell, he squeezed Theo’s hand—she seemed to sense his purpose (or perhaps their mother had already spilled the secret)—and kissed little Hetty’s flushed cheek before stepping out of the room, though not before being followed by his wife.

Alone with him, Mrs. Lambert could no longer contain herself. She threw her arms around his neck, kissed him a hundred times over, called down blessings from heaven, wept freely upon his shoulder—so overcome with emotion that she was discovered in this tender state by old Mrs. Quiggett, the housekeeper, who was bustling through the halls. The sight, I imagine, startled her greatly.

“We’ve had a disagreement and are making up! Don’t go spreading gossip, Mrs. Quiggett!” the gentleman said, walking off.

“Well, I never!” exclaimed Mrs. Quviggett, bursting into a shrill, cackling laugh, like an ancient cockatoo—indeed, she bore a striking resemblance to such a bird: white-haired, hooked-nosed, long-lived, and perpetually animated. “Well, I never!” she repeated, shaking her sides until her keys and ribs clattered together.

“Oh, Quiggett!” sobbed Mrs. Lambert, “what a man he is!”

“You’ve been quarreling, have you, mum, and now making up? That’s good.”

“Quarrel with him? He never told a bigger lie. My General is an angel, Quiggett. I’d worship him. I’d fall at his boots and kiss them, I truly would! There’s never been a man so good as my General. What have I done to deserve such a husband? How dare I possess such a noble soul?”

“My dear,” replied the old housekeeper, “I think there’s a pair of you.” Then, with a twinkle in her eye, she added, “Now, what would you like for supper?”

When Lambert returned late that evening, bearing news that Harry was free and his brother had come to his rescue, the household erupted into jubilation. If Mrs. Lambert had thought her husband an angel before, what could she call him now? If she had wanted to embrace his boots in the morning, what deeper expression of devotion might she offer in the evening? Little Hetty quietly crept to her father, silently drinking a sip from his glass. Theo and her mother glowed with happiness, radiant as twin moons.

After supper, at a silent signal, all four knelt together—offering solemn yet joyful homage, a prayer of repentance and renewal, filled with the purest delight angels might know. Just then, a loud knock echoed at the door. Who could it be? Lord Wrotham was miles away in the country. It was past midnight—their talk had carried on long into the night. But Mrs. Lambert knew instantly who stood outside.

“This is George,” said a young man, ushering in another. “We came from Aunt Bernstein’s. We couldn’t sleep without thanking you too. You dear, dear, good—” His words trailed off. Aunt Lambert was already embracing Harry. Theo snatched up Hetty, pale as death, and held her close, breathing life back into her. George Warrington stood with his hat in hand. When Harry’s reunion was complete, George stepped forward and kissed Mrs. Lambert’s hand. The General passed a hand across his eyes. I assure you, they were all wrapped in tenderness and joy. Generous hearts often feel most deeply when wrong is forgiven, peace restored, and love returns after being lost.

“We came from Aunt Bernstein’s,” Harry explained. “We saw lights here, and couldn’t go to bed without saying goodnight to all of you. Could we, George?”

“A famous nightcap, indeed,” said the General with a smile. “When shall you dine with us? Tomorrow?”

“No,” said George, “we must visit Madame Bernstein tomorrow.”

Then the next day? Yes, they would come then—and that very day is the one we write of. This is the dinner where, in place of Lieutenant-Colonel James Wolfe—absent on private business—you, dear reader, have just been invited to sit.

To sit down—and why, pray? Not merely for a feast of empty promises, no—but to hear **MR. GEORGE ESMOND WARRINGTON’S ACCOUNT**, which he is about to deliver. Here they all sit—not in Lord Wrotham’s grand dining hall, but in the cozy study or parlour ahead. The cloth has been removed, the General has given the King’s health, the servants have left, and the guests sit in quiet expectation. After a moment of hesitation and a blush, Mr. George begins:

“I remember well, at our General’s table, how the sharp-witted Philadelphia agent—whose cleverness we admired at home—voiced the very concerns that later proved justified by the disaster. ‘Of course,’ he said, ‘once your Excellency’s troops reach Fort Duquesne, such a weak outpost cannot withstand your general, your army, your artillery.’ But he asked, ‘Have you considered the difficulty of reaching it? Your march will be through nearly untouched woods, over roads you must build yourself. Your line stretches four miles long. Such a slender column, moving through dense forest, will be vulnerable to constant attacks—front, rear, flank—from enemies you cannot see, men trained in ambush.’

‘Psha!’ the General scoffed. ‘The savages may frighten your raw American militia’—as if acknowledging Washington’s presence at the table—‘but the Indians will never trouble His Majesty’s regulars.’

‘I sincerely hope not,’ Mr. Franklin sighed. Naturally, the General’s companions sneered at the postmaster—a civilian, they thought, meddling beyond his understanding.

We underestimated the Indians. Our commander dismissed them. Our officers insulted the native chiefs accompanying us, behaving outrageously toward their women. Only seven or eight remained with our force. Had two hundred stood before us on that fateful 9th of July, the outcome might have been different. They could have repelled the French Indians, prevented the panic and surprise that followed. Now we know the French had even begun preparing to surrender, never imagining resistance possible. Even the French Indians protested against attacking such overwhelming numbers.

I was with the General when the firing began. One aide-de-camp after another was sent forward. At first, our advance guard responded bravely, cheering with spirit. But soon our fire slackened, while single shots rang out from behind every tree and bush. Men fell one after another. We marched in order—skirmishers ahead, colors and two small guns in the center, baggage guarded at the rear—over open ground for a mile or two, flanked by thick tangled woods.

After a short time, enemy fire opened from both sides. Men dropped rapidly—officers more than soldiers. At first, we cheered and returned fire. Our guns fired into the woods, seeming to silence the hidden Frenchmen. But then the rifle shots began again. Our men halted, huddled together despite shouts and orders to advance. They fired wildly into the brush—useless, of course. Those in front fled back, wounded and terrified. They reported five thousand Frenchmen and a legion of shrieking Indian devils ahead, scalping our fallen. We heard their cries from the surrounding woods as our comrades fell.

No amount of urging could make them move forward. Aide-de-camp after aide-de-camp was sent—none returned. Finally, it was my turn. I was sent with a message to Captain Fraser of Halkett’s regiment—one I would never deliver.

I had barely advanced thirty yards when a rifle ball struck my leg. I fell immediately. I saw Indians and Frenchmen rush forward, the former screaming war cries, the latter as fierce as their allies. I was stunned to see how few whitecoats there were—no more than a score passed me. In truth, fewer than fifty fought in the battle that shattered two of Britain’s bravest regiments.

One man—half Indian, half French, wearing moccasins and a white coat with a cockade—saw me lying there and turned back, musket raised, intent on killing me and looting my body. I had my fusil, given to me by Harry before the campaign—it had fallen near me. I grabbed it and shot him dead at six yards. I was saved—for the moment—but bleeding heavily, fainting. I nearly passed out trying to reload. The gun slipped from my grasp, and my hand went limp.

I was barely conscious, the screams and gunfire echoing dimly, when I saw an Indian approaching. He was rifling the Frenchman I’d killed, tearing open his coat, searching his pockets. Then he scalped him, knife bloody in his mouth, and came toward me.

I saw him through a haze, as in a dream. I could not move. He placed his knee on my chest, seized my hair with one bloodied hand, lifted my head, and in doing so revealed a French officer advancing behind him.

‘Good God!’ I cried. ‘A moi, Florac! C’est Georges! Aide-moi!’

He froze, ran to me, laid a hand on the Indian’s shoulder, and ordered him to stop. But the savage didn’t understand French—or refused to. He gripped my hair tighter, waved his knife, signaling Florac to leave me to him.

‘Ah, canaille, tu veux du sang? Prends!’ Florac cursed, and with a grunt, plunged his sword into the Indian’s chest. He fell over me, dead.

Florac looked around. ‘Eh! La belle affaire! Where are you wounded? In the leg?’ He bound my leg tightly with his sash. ‘Others will kill you if they find you here. Ah, tiens! Put on this coat and hat with the white cockade. Call out in French if anyone passes. They’ll think you’re one of us. You’re Brunet of the Quebec Volunteers. God guard thee, Brunet! I must go forward. It’s a general rout, and all your redcoats are fleeing, my poor boy.’

What a rout it was! What a day of shame for England!

Florac’s rough bandage stopped the bleeding. He helped me lean against a tree, loaded my fusil, and placed it within reach. He gave me the gourd of the French soldier I’d killed—the drink inside refreshed me greatly. He marked the tree and the terrain so he could return, then hurried forward. ‘You see how much I care for you, George,’ he said. ‘I stay behind in a moment like this.’

I don’t recall telling you, Harry, that Florac owed me something. I won money from him at cards in Quebec—only because he begged me to play—and there was trouble paying. I forgave his debt and lit my pipe with his note of hand. You see, sir, you’re not the only gambler in the family.

At dusk, after the pursuit ended, Florac returned with two Indians, each carrying fresh scalps. He told them I was a wounded Frenchman, his brother, captured early in the day. They laid me on a blanket and carried me groaning, with Florac beside me. Had he abandoned me, they would have left me for dead, looted me, and added my scalp to theirs. He promised brandy at the fort if they brought me safely. I remember little of the journey—my wound was agony—I fainted several times. Eventually, we reached the fort. I was taken to the officer’s log cabin and laid on Florac’s own bed.

Thank heavens I was unconscious. I had arrived as a wounded French soldier. Later, I learned that during my delirium, the few British prisoners taken that day were dragged under the walls of Duquesne by their Indian captors and burned, tortured, butchered—before the eyes of the garrison.

As George speaks, a shiver of horror runs through the listeners. Theo takes Hetty’s hand, alarmed. Harry slams his fist on the table. “The bloody, murderous, red-skinned villains! There’ll be no peace until every last one is hunted down!”

“They offered a hundred and thirty dollars per Indian scalp in Pennsylvania when I left home,” George says calmly. “Fifty for women.”

“Fifty for women, my love?” the Colonel exclaims, lifting his wife’s hair. “Did you hear that, Mrs. Lambert?”

“The murderous villains!” Harry shouts again. “Hunt them down, sir! Hunt them down!”

“I don’t know how long I lay fevered,” George continues. “When I regained consciousness, Florac was gone. He’d been sent on a mission against an English fort in Pennsylvania, claimed by the French. In Duquesne, only thirty Europeans remained. The place could have been retaken a dozen times—if anyone had dared return after defeat.

My old enemy, ague-fever, returned as I lay by the river. It’s a miracle I survived. But for a kind half-breed woman in the fort, who pitied me and cared for me, I would never have recovered. My poor Harry would have become, as he imagined yesterday, our grandfather’s heir, our mother’s only son.

I remembered how Florac had placed my money, watch, and trinkets under my pillow when he laid me in his bed. When I woke, they were gone. A gruff old sergeant—the only officer left—told me, cursing, that I was lucky to be alive. My white cockade and coat had saved me from the fate others deserved.

When I recovered, the fort was nearly empty. The Indians had left, rich with plunder. Most French regulars had gone north on expeditions. Florac had left me in charge of an invalided sergeant. Monsieur de Contrecoeur had joined one of these missions, leaving an old lieutenant named Museau in command.

Museau had long served in the colonies, far from France. His record there was likely poor, and knowing promotion in France favored nobility, he saw no future. He had taken my guineas, I suppose, along with my watch—I once saw it pinned to his chest.

Monsieur Museau and I became fairly friendly. I told him I could be exchanged or sent home, and my mother would pay handsomely for my ransom. This idea stirred his greed. When a trapper arrived in winter, while I still lay ill, Museau agreed I could write home—but only in French, only if he approved it, and only if I claimed I was in Indian hands and wouldn’t be ransomed unless ten thousand livres were paid.

I protested—I was a prisoner of His Most Christian Majesty, entitled to treatment as a gentleman and officer. But Museau swore the letter would go, or he’d throw me out or hand me to his savage allies. He forbade the trapper to speak with me except in his presence. Life and liberty are precious. I resisted briefly, but weakened by fever, I wrote the letter he demanded. The trapper took it, promising to deliver it to my mother in Virginia in three weeks.

Three weeks passed. Six. Twelve. No messenger returned. Winter came and went. The plantations near the fort—where French soldiers had cleared fields and planted gardens, peach and apple trees—bloomed in spring. Heaven knows how I endured.

When I grew stronger, I drew portraits of the soldiers, the half-breed woman and her child (Museau’s child), and Museau himself—though I admit I flattered him terribly. An old guitar remained in the fort. I played French airs, sang, and tried to win favor with my captors. The months dragged on. The messenger never returned.

Finally, word came: the trapper had been shot by British Indians in Maryland. My hope of ransom vanished. Museau grew furious—especially when his sergeant accused the Indian woman of favoring me, which, I believe, she did. I was always gentle with her, grateful to her. My small talents amazed her. I was sick and unhappy—always claims to a woman’s heart.

A captive weakened by illness, a cruel jailer, and a young woman moved by his plight—surely you expect some tragic tale to unfold? You, Miss Hetty, are about to guess she saved me?

“Why, of course she did!” cries mamma.

“What else is she good for?” says Hetty.

“You, Miss Theo, have already painted her as a dark beauty—isn’t that right? A swift huntress—”

“Diana with a baby,” says the Colonel.

“—Who roams the plains with her nymphs, brings down game with unerring aim, queen of the forest—and I see by your expressions you think I’m madly in love with her?”

“Well, I suppose she’s interesting, Mr. George?” Theo says, blushing.

“What think you of a dark beauty, the color of new mahogany, with long straight black hair, usually oiled with a pomade unpleasant to approach, small eyes, high cheekbones, a flat nose sometimes pierced with a ring, rows of glass beads around her tawny throat, her cheeks and forehead tastefully tattooed, fond of finery, and passionately devoted to—oh! Must I confess?”

“For coquetry. I know what you’re going to say!” Miss Hetty interrupts.

“For whisky, my dear Miss Hester—in which appetite my gaoler also indulged. Often, when I was in favor with Monsieur Museau, I sat by and watched him and the woman drink together until they could barely hold the mug. These evenings included singing, dancing, quarreling, knocking things over. And when I was in favor, I was allowed to join them—for Museau, jealous of his dignity, refused his men access to his private revels.

While the trapper’s fate remained uncertain, and Museau and I expected ransom payment, I was treated reasonably. I was allowed to walk the fort, even venture into nearby fields and gardens—always keeping my parole, returning before gun-fire. I practiced a bit of deception, which I hope you’ll forgive. Once my leg healed—the bullet exited in winter, after pain and inflammation—I chose to walk as if still crippled. I hobbled with sticks, moaning Ah! and Oh! every minute, hoping one day I might finally run.
