The most notable guest at the dinner party was Mr. Murthwaite. His return to England after years of travel had stirred considerable interest—society admired him as a man who had survived perilous adventures and lived to tell the tale. Now, he announced his intention to return to India, venturing into regions untouched by Western explorers. This bold decision reignited public fascination with the adventurer. The odds against his survival were plainly stacked against him. It is rare indeed to dine with a distinguished figure and yet feel that the next news of him might very well be of his death.

When the ladies withdrew, leaving the men in the dining room, I found myself seated beside Mr. Murthwaite. With all guests English, it was inevitable that politics would dominate the conversation once the social restraint of the women was lifted. On this topic, I must confess, I am among the least English of Englishmen. Political discourse strikes me as tedious and unproductive. Glancing at Mr. Murthwaite as the first round of wine circulated, I noticed he seemed equally disengaged—though politely so, mindful of his host’s feelings. In fact, he appeared ready to doze off.

I decided to test whether mentioning the Moonstone might rouse him—and if it did, what his reaction would be to the latest twist in the Indian conspiracy, revealed in the quiet confines of my office.

“If I’m not mistaken, Mr. Murthwaite,” I began, “you knew the late Lady Verinder, and took an interest in the strange chain of events surrounding the loss of the Moonstone?”

He woke instantly, asking who I was. I told him of my professional connection to the Herncastle family, including my role during Colonel Herncastle’s time and my involvement with the Diamond itself.

Mr. Murthwaite turned fully in his chair, shielding himself from the rest of the company—Conservatives and Liberals alike—and fixed his attention on me, plain Mr. Bruff of Gray’s Inn Square.

“Have you heard anything lately about the Indians?” he asked.

“I have every reason to believe,” I replied, “that one of them visited me in my office yesterday.”

Mr. Murthwaite, usually impervious to surprise, was visibly startled. I recounted what had happened to Mr. Luker and to myself—just as I’ve related here.

“It’s clear,” I added, “that the Indian’s final question had a purpose. Why would he be so eager to know when a borrower typically repays a loan?”

“Is it possible you don’t see his motive, Mr. Bruff?” he asked.

“I’m ashamed to admit it—but I truly don’t.”

He leaned forward, intrigued by the depth of my ignorance. “Let me ask you one thing: What is the current state of the conspiracy to reclaim the Moonstone?”

“I can’t say,” I confessed. “The Indian plot remains a mystery to me.”

“The mystery,” he said, “exists only because you haven’t examined it closely. Shall we trace it together—from the moment you drafted Colonel Herncastle’s will, to the moment the Indian came to your office? Given your position, it may matter greatly to Miss Verinder that you understand this clearly. So—will you uncover the Indian’s motive yourself? Or shall I spare you the effort?”

I recognized at once the practical intent behind his offer. I chose the former.

“Very well,” he said. “Let’s begin with the ages of the three Indians. I can confirm they appear roughly the same age. You’d agree the one you saw was not yet forty?”

“Not forty,” I agreed.

“Then consider this: When Colonel Herncastle returned to England, you helped devise a plan to preserve his life. That was years ago. At their current ages, these Indians must be successors to three others—high-caste Brahmins—who followed the Colonel here. These men have inherited not just their predecessors’ roles—but also their network in this country.”

He paused. “Don’t be alarmed. The organization is modest by our standards—perhaps no more than access to funds, the services of certain shadowy Englishmen living on the fringes of London’s foreign scene, and the quiet support of a few fellow countrymen and believers scattered through the city’s many offices and trades. Nothing grand—but worth noting. It may come into play later.”

He continued. “Now, what event gave the Indians their first real chance to seize the Diamond?”

I understood the reference. “Colonel Herncastle’s death,” I said. “They would have known of it immediately.”

“Exactly. Until then, the Moonstone was secure in the bank. You drew the will leaving the jewel to his niece. It was probated as usual. As a lawyer, you know what the Indians would do next—with English help.”

“They’d obtain a copy of the will from Doctors’ Commons,” I said.

“Precisely. One of those shady figures I mentioned would get it for them. That copy would reveal the Diamond was left to Lady Verinder’s daughter—and that Mr. Blake, or someone appointed by him, would deliver it. Information about such individuals would be easy to gather. The only dilemma for the Indians: should they act while the Diamond was being moved from the bank—or wait until it reached Yorkshire?”

“The latter would be safer,” I said. “That explains their appearance at Frizinghall, disguised as jugglers.”

“In London, they used their network—two men would suffice. One to follow anyone leaving Mr. Blake’s house for the bank. Another to bribe servants and learn the household’s plans. They discovered Franklin Blake had gone to the bank—and that he was the only one going to visit Lady Verinder.”

I recalled how Franklin Blake spotted a spy in the street, hastened his journey, and—thanks to Betteredge’s advice—deposited the Diamond in the local bank before the Indians could reach him.

But here lay the puzzle: If the Indians believed the Diamond was in Lady Verinder’s home, why didn’t they attempt a raid before Rachel’s birthday?

I added that I’d heard of the boy and the ink drop, but dismissed any supernatural explanation.

“Nor do I,” Mr. Murthwaite said. “The idea of clairvoyance is merely a romantic fantasy for them—a way to make their dangerous mission seem more sacred. Their boy is sensitive to hypnotic influence. Under it, he reflects the thoughts of whoever controls him. I’ve tested such claims—never beyond that point. To them, the boy is a seer. It gives meaning to their quest. But we need not concern ourselves with mesmerism or mysticism. Our inquiry is rational. Have I satisfied you so far?”

“Without doubt,” I replied. “But I still await your explanation for the delay in their attack.”

He smiled. “It’s the simplest thing. Let me accept your premise: the Indians didn’t know what Franklin Blake had done. And indeed, they made their first mistake—the night he arrived. They were surprised by Gabriel Betteredge lurking near the terrace.”

“They realized their error,” I said. “And never approached the house again.”

“Because,” he said, “no Indian takes unnecessary risks. Your clause in the will stated the Moonstone would pass to Miss Verinder on her birthday. Which path was safer? Attacking while it was under Franklin Blake’s control—after he’d already outwitted them? Or waiting until it was in the hands of a young girl who would proudly wear it?”

“Clearly the second,” I admitted.

“And so they waited. On her birthday, they arrived—and found the Moonstone in her dress. My own sense of danger led me to recommend cutting the gem into fragments, as Colonel Herncastle had done. But its sudden disappearance nullified that plan. The Indians were arrested the next day—imprisoned as vagabonds. The first phase ends there.”

He paused. “Before we proceed, have I answered your question satisfactorily?”

He had. His insight into Indian psychology—and his singular focus—had clarified what had long puzzled me.

“Now,” he continued, “the second chance arose—not after their release, but *while* they were imprisoned.”

He opened his pocketbook to a specific page. “I was staying with friends at Frizinghall at the time. A few days before their release, the prison governor brought me a letter. It had been delivered to Mrs. Macann, the woman who housed the Indians, by regular post. The postmark was ‘Lambeth.’ The address, though written in correct English, was oddly formatted. Inside, it was in Hindustani. They sought my help translating it. I copied both the original and my translation.”

He handed me the book. The address read:
“To the three Indian men living with the lady called Macann at Frizinghall in Yorkshire.”

Below, in Hindi characters, followed the translation:
“In the name of the Regent of the Night, whose seat is on the Antelope, whose arms embrace the four corners of the earth.
Brothers, turn your faces to the south, and come to me in the street of many noises, which leads down to the muddy river.
The reason is this.
My own eyes have seen it.”

No date. No signature.

I returned it, baffled.

“I’ll explain the first line,” he said. “In Hindu myth, the moon god is a four-armed deity riding an antelope—one of whose titles is ‘Regent of the Night.’ A clear, if veiled, reference to the Moonstone.”

“What did the Indians do?” I asked.

On the day of their release, they went straight to the railway station and boarded the first train to London. No one watched them—Lady Verinder had ended all inquiries. They vanished into the capital.

“What happened next?” he asked.

“They harassed Mr. Luker,” I said, “loitering around his house in Lambeth.”

“Did you read Mr. Luker’s report to the magistrate?”

“Yes.”

“He mentioned dismissing a foreign worker suspected of theft—and possibly colluding with the Indians.”

The implication was clear. “The workman had tried to steal one of Mr. Luker’s Oriental treasures,” I said. “The Moonstone.”

“Exactly. The letter was from that man—writing to warn the Indians. He’d seen the Diamond in Mr. Luker’s possession.”

So the Indians had learned where it was. But how?

“By now,” I said, “they knew it was pledged. And they wanted to know when it could be redeemed.”

“Ah!” he said. “You’ve grasped it.”

“Their third chance,” I exclaimed, “comes when the pledge expires!”

“Precisely. They assume it’s pledged. They want to know the earliest date it can be reclaimed—that’s when it will leave the bank’s protection.”

“Mr. Luker told them it’s a year,” I said.

“And you confirmed it,” he added. “So they know the timeline.”

We calculated: the Diamond likely reached London in late June ’forty-seven. Now it was ’forty-eight. In a year—June ’forty-nine—it could be redeemed.

“That date,” he said, “may be crucial. I’ll be thousands of miles away. But you—Mr. Bruff—should mark it. Be in London then.”

“You think something serious will happen?” I asked.

“I’d rather face the fiercest fanatics in Central Asia than walk into a bank carrying the Moonstone,” he replied. “They’ve failed twice. I believe they won’t fail a third time.”

That was his final word on the matter.

Coffee was served. Guests rose and dispersed. We joined the ladies upstairs.

I made a note: _June, ’forty-nine. Expect news of the Indians toward the end of the month._

With that, I pass the pen to the next narrator.
