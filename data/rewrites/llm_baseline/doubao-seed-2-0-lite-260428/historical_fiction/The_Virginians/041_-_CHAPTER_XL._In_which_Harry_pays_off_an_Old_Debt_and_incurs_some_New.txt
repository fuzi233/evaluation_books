By the end of the season, the little party from Tunbridge Wells was heartily sick of the spa’s quiet amusements and all too eager to strike out for new destinations. Madame de Bernstein had already set her sights on Bath for the autumn, where there were far more card tables, a busier crowd, and far more life to be found than the stagnant spa could offer, and she planned to stop off only at a handful of country house visits on the road there. Harry, with no great grace to hide his reluctance, agreed to ride with Lady Maria and Parson Sampson ahead of the main party, bound for the old Esmond family seat at Castlewood.

They passed the turn-off for Oakhurst village, and the warm, hospitable home where Harry had been treated like a favoured guest not long before. Lady Maria launched into a stream of sharp, cutting remarks about the two pretty Misses Oakhurst, their mother’s obvious determination to hook the wealthy young Virginian as a son-in-law, and how brazen the girls had been to set their caps at him the moment he stepped through the door. Stung and cross, Harry declared he would ride straight past his old friends’ gate rather than subject them to her ladyship’s unprovoked insults, and he stayed haughty and sullen for the rest of the day’s journey to the roadside inn where they stopped a few miles further on.

At supper, none of Lady Maria’s softest smiles could thaw his black mood, not even the ready, well-practised tears she summoned at will to try and soften him. To every one of her querulous, sentimental remarks, he growled out a surly, monosyllabic reply, and she was forced to retire to her chambers without ever getting the private tete-a-tete she had been craving: that stubborn old chaplain Sampson stayed planted in the parlour armchair as if he had received explicit orders to never leave the two of them alone. Harry bowed her out with stiff, perfect courtesy, called the landlady and her personal maid to see her up the stairs, and closed the door behind her.

Ten minutes later, a horse galloped out of the inn-yard at full tilt. An hour after Lady Maria had gone to bed, her maid Betty came back down to fetch her mistress’s bottle of smelling salts, and found Parson Sampson alone, puffing on a clay pipe. Where was Mr Warrington? He’d stepped out for a moonlight walk, Sampson said vaguely, as if no one could possibly be expected to keep track of where the young gentleman wandered. The next morning, Harry was back in his saddle beside Lady Maria’s carriage, his brow still dark, his black mood unbroken, barely speaking a dozen words for the whole rest of the journey. Lady Maria’s mind raced: *He must have found out I lied about stealing that little silken case*, she thought to herself.

The truth of it was, half a day earlier, as they climbed that steep hill three miles outside Oakhurst on the Westerham road, Lady Maria had been leaning heavily on Harry’s arm, pouring out her most overwrought sentimental vows, protests, and gushings of undying love. The more fawning and fond she grew, the colder Harry turned. Looking up into his face, the summer sun lit up hers, and for all its careful powder and preservative creams, the faint lines and wrinkles of forty years were plain to see. To Harry, the weight of her arm resting on his felt like a lead drag he would have to carry for the rest of his life, a dreary, suffocating prospect. He cursed the hot night, the spiced wine, and the stupid, reckless pledge he had made while half drunk, tying him irrevocably to this woman he did not love.

Maria chattered on, quoting scraps of sentimental plays, calling him over and over again her champion, her Henrico, her preserver, swearing her Molinda would be faithful to him until her dying day. She pulled a small silken case from the neck of her gown, holding it up to his face. “Have I not thy precious image, thy hair, thy loving note here?” she sighed. “It shall go with me to the grave – it will, sir, if my Henrico ever treats me unkindly.”

Harry jolted so hard he nearly wrenched his arm right out of her grasp. He had thought Madame de Bernstein had burned every last scrap of that stolen hair and that foolish old note, when she had destroyed the case right in front of him back at Tunbridge. That was the exact moment Lady Maria felt her first twinge of remorse: not that she had lied, but that she had been caught lying, a far more powerful spur to repentance than any pious instruction.

When they arrived at Castlewood, Harry’s bad mood did not lift. The Earl was away from home, the rest of the family out visiting, and the only member of the household they found on the grounds was Will Esmond, who trundled back up the drive from partridge shooting just as their party reached the gate. He went dead pale when he saw Harry, and Harry favoured him with nothing more than a surly, cold scowl of recognition.

Over supper that night, they made a show of civility at first, with Lady Maria sitting between them. Will made vague noises about how he’d done well at the recent local race meeting, until Harry leaned forward. “And you brought my horse back sound, I hope?” he asked, evenly.

“Your horse? What horse?” Will blustered.

“The brown thoroughbred I won from you at cards the night before you rode away from Tunbridge,” Harry said, stern and steady. “You remember that horse, Mr Esmond.”

“Mr Warrington, I perfectly well remember playing you for a horse, which my servant handed over to you the day you left the spa,” Will said.

“Mr Sampson, you were present at our game. Will you be umpire for us?” Harry said, turning to the chaplain with surprising gentleness.

“I must confirm that the wager was laid for the brown thoroughbred, sir,” Sampson said, looking away awkwardly.

“Well, he got the other one,” Will said, with a surly grin. “Sold it for thirty shillings! A broken-kneed old rip, that was more than it was worth.”

Harry kept up his perfect, polite smile for as long as Lady Maria remained in the room, pressing her to have more chicken, making small, easy conversation. When she rose to retire, he bowed her to the door with all the courtly grace he could muster, closed it behind her, and then called all the servants out of the supper parlour. He turned the heavy iron key in the lock, and pocketed it.

The click of the lock made Will jump. He was slouched over his punch, eyeing Harry askance, and swore out loud. “What the hell do you think you’re playing at?”

“I expect there’s going to be a quarrel,” Harry said, blandly. “No use making the servants stand gawking at a disagreement between gentlemen of quality.”

“Quarrel? What quarrel?” Will snapped, his hand closing around the big silver-handled carving knife on the table.

“Mr Sampson, you witnessed that I staked fifty guineas against Will’s brown horse, did you not?” Harry asked.

Will roared and denied it, but Harry cut him off. “Don’t take me for a fool just because I was raised in Virginia,” he said, stepping forward. “Hands off, chaplain, you stand back and see fair play!”

Harry never got the chance to finish his insult. Will hurled a full wine bottle straight at his head. Harry ducked just in time, and the heavy glass flew across the room, smashing straight through the face of a portrait of an ancient Esmond ancestor, before shattering against the wainscot and splashing a full pint of good port wine all over Sampson’s face and his neatly powdered wig. “Good heavens, gentlemen, I beg you to stop this madness!” the chaplain wailed, dripping red wine off his periwig.

Neither of the two men paid him any mind. Will dropped the empty bottle, seized the carving knife, and lunged straight at Harry’s chest. Harry had learned his boxing at the Marybone gardens, and he caught Will’s right wrist in his left hand, landing a sharp, heavy punch square on Will’s nose that sent him reeling backwards into the oak panelling. Will dropped the knife, which Harry kicked under the table out of reach.

Will had been to bare-knuckle fights at Hockley-in-the-Hole too, and he gasped for breath, then charged forward headfirst like a battering ram, aiming to drive his skull straight into Harry’s stomach. Harry had seen that trick before, both at the London fights and amongst the enslaved boxers on his mother’s Virginia estate. He skipped easily to the side, and landed a tremendous blow to Will’s right ear that sent him crumpling to the floor, his head cracking hard against the heavy oak table leg.

“Chaplain, you bear witness this was a fair fight,” Harry said, still quivering with the rush of combat, but striving to steady his voice. He pulled the key from his pocket and unlocked the parlour door, where half a dozen curious servants had clustered, listening to the crash of glass and the shouts. They burst in to find two men covered in red: the chaplain soaked in port wine, and the Honourable Will Esmond splayed unconscious on the floor in his own blood.

“Someone unlace his neckcloth – he may be dying,” Harry said. “Gumbo, fetch a fleam and bleed him. Wait, he’s coming round.” Will’s good eye fluttered open first, the other already swollen shut from the first punch. He gasped, then started swearing so loudly the parlour windows rattled.

“Looks like he’s on the mend,” Harry said dryly. The servants, who had never had a good word to say for the cruel, violent Will Esmond, laughed. “Gumbo, ask him if he’d like a second round?”

Will cursed them all, lashing out at Gumbo when he leaned over him. “Nobody here dares touch you, sir?” Harry said, straightening his lace ruffs. “That’s what I thought.” The servants murmured their agreement, none of them moving to interfere on Will’s behalf. Will staggered to his feet, wiping the blood off his face with a napkin, and skulked off to bed, trailing a string of curses after him.

The next morning, Harry sent Will a playful note, teasing him that if he was heading for London he could ride ahead and scare highwaymen on Hounslow Heath with his bruised, battered face. Then a maid brought a gushing, tear-stained letter from Lady Maria, begging her dearest Henrico never to be cruel to his Molinda again, declaring she had not slept a wink all night. Harry wrote a formal, carefully spelled letter to the Earl of Castlewood explaining the full circumstances of the fight, told Maria that he could not possibly stay under their roof after bruising and bloodying the master’s brother, and took his leave. The second he crossed Castlewood Bridge, his black mood vanished entirely. He put his horses to a gallop, singing to them as they flew ten miles an hour down the road, nodding at pretty girls standing by cottage gates, chucking roadside inn landladies under the chin. He was sick of Tunbridge’s petty gossip, silly sentimental games, and stuffy card parties. He was ready for London, for St James’s, for Newmarket, for all the amusements of a gentleman of quality.

By the time he rolled into London, the forty-four pounds he had left when he left Tunbridge was all but spent. He told himself it made perfect sense to sell five hundred pounds of his share of the family legacy stock: with a little luck at the tables, he would make it all back in no time. A gentleman of his rank, he reasoned, could not possibly keep the right company or appear properly in society on a paltry two hundred pounds a year. He moved out of the cheap Bedford Coffee House, took elegant, expensive lodgings in Bond Street, stabled his two horses at a nearby livery yard, and set tailors, mercers, and shoemakers to work making him a full new wardrobe. He laid aside his mourning, appeared in a laced waistcoat and a cocked hat trimmed with silver, had his hair powdered, and drove his fancy phaeton in the park Ring. Reports of his vast Virginian fortune had preceded him to the city, and every man of fashion was curious to meet the wealthy young colonial heir. Lord March had already written his name down for membership at White’s Chocolate House, even before he was old enough to be formally balloted in. At one-and-twenty, with a pocket full of cash, Harry was determined to drink deep of every pleasure London had to offer.

Among the unpublished papers of the late Lord Orford, one letter addressed to General Conway survives, copied out in Madam Esmond’s neat handwriting and marked *Mr. H. Walpole’s account of my son Henry at London*. It runs as follows:
> ARLINGTON STREET, Friday Night.
> My dear General,
> I have torn myself away from three weeks of devotion to our Lady of Strawberry, my old joints all wracked with rheumatism, to pay London a visit. Why shouldn’t an old man have his rattle, same as any other elderly baby? The Norfolk party was full of talk this week about some sort of young Virginian “savage” they met at Tunbridge, an offshoot of that disreputable Esmond family, where all the men are gamblers and spendthrifts, and all the women – well, your wife is reading over your shoulder right now, so I shan’t say the word. You know the old Baroness Bernstein, once Mrs Tusher, that notorious old beauty who flipped sides from the Pretender to the House of Hanover without a second thought? Her great-nephew is the young man in question.
> We ran into him at Vauxhall two nights ago, with that silly little busybody Jack Morris hanging off his arm. You never saw a creature so delighted by fireworks, or so ready to huzzay at a screaming singer on the stage! He’s the lad who won three hundred guineas off my nephew and Lord Rockingham at White’s the night before, and the whole gardens were buzzing about him. Jack introduced him to the singer after the show, and the big colonial boy blushed all the way up to the eyes and gave her a bow so formal you would have thought he was at St James’s court.
> Later, over our punch and chicken, I was regaling Tony Storer with a few of the old Esmond family scandals, including tales of that elderly beauty Lady Maria Esmond and her old flirtation with the late Prince of Wales, when the young “savage” himself stepped out from behind the next box, his face scarlet with rage, his hand on the hilt of his sword. He demanded to know which of us had dared malign his family, and I was forced to stammer out a full apology before he would even think of putting his blade away. What a creature! We shall see a great deal more of him, mark my words.
