The next day, Mrs. John Dashwood visited Mrs. Jennings and her daughters, and the visit yielded a surprising result: even the woman hosting her sisters proved worthy of her attention. As for Lady Middleton, she emerged as one of the most charming women in the world. A strange, cold-hearted symmetry existed between the two households; they were drawn together by a mutual attraction born of shared superficiality and a profound lack of genuine understanding. While these mannerisms secured Mrs. John Dashwood's approval from Lady Middleton, they repelled Mrs. Jennings, who viewed her as merely a proud, unaffectionate woman who barely spoke to her husband's sisters. During the fifteen-minute visit to Berkeley Street, Mrs. Jennings noted that Mrs. Dashwood spent at least seven and a half minutes in absolute silence.

Elinor wished intensely to inquire whether Edward was in town, though she chose not to ask. Nothing would have prompted Fanny to volunteer the information until she could confirm that Edward's marriage to Miss Morton was finalized or that her husband's hopes for Colonel Brandon were realized. She believed the couple remained so deeply attached that they required constant separation in word and deed. However, the intelligence Elinor sought soon arrived from another source. Lucy came promptly to claim Elinor's sympathy for her inability to see Edward, who had arrived in town with the Dashwoods. Fear of detection prevented him from visiting Bartlett's Buildings, and though their mutual longing to meet was palpable, they were restricted to correspondence. Yet, Edward ensured they knew of his presence in town by calling at Berkeley Street twice. Twice his calling card was discovered upon their return from morning engagements. Elinor was pleased that he had called, and even more pleased that she had missed him.

The Dashwoods were so delighted with the Middletons that, despite their usual reluctance to host, they decided to invite them to dinner. Soon after forming this acquaintance, they extended an invitation to dine at their residence in Harley Street, where they had leased a fine house for three months. The sisters and Mrs. Jennings were included, and John Dashwood carefully secured Colonel Brandon, who, always eager to be near the Miss Dashwoods, accepted their eager civilities with some surprise but considerable pleasure. They intended to meet Mrs. Ferrars, though Elinor could not ascertain if her sons would attend. Nevertheless, the prospect of meeting her was sufficient to engage Elinor's interest. Though she could now face Edward's mother without the acute anxiety that once plagued such introductions, viewing her with perfect indifference regarding her own merit, her curiosity about Mrs. Ferrars' character remained as vivid as ever.

This anticipation was soon intensified, more powerfully than pleasantly, by news that the Miss Steeles would also be present. Having impressed Lady Middleton so thoroughly, and rendered so agreeable by their assiduity, that though Lucy lacked elegance and her sister was not even genteel, Sir John was as ready as Lady Middleton to invite them to spend a week or two in Conduit Street. Conveniently, the Miss Steeles arranged their arrival to coincide with the Dashwoods' party. While their connection to Mrs. John Dashwood as the nieces of her late brother's caretaker might not have secured them a seat at her table, as Lady Middleton's guests, they were welcome. Lucy, who longed to be personally known to the family to gauge their characters and navigate her own difficulties, had rarely been happier than when she received Mrs. John Dashwood's card. For Elinor, the effect was diametrically opposed. She immediately resolved that Edward, living with his mother, must be invited to a party hosted by his sister, and the thought of seeing him for the first time since their parting in the company of Lucy filled her with dread.

These apprehensions, perhaps not entirely grounded in reason or truth, were alleviated not by Elinor's own reflection but by Lucy's goodwill. Believing she was inflicting a severe disappointment, Lucy informed Elinor that Edward would certainly not be in Harley Street on Tuesday, hoping further to soothe her by persuading her that Edward was absent solely due to his extreme affection for her, which he could not conceal when they were together. The crucial Tuesday arrived, destined to introduce the two young ladies to this formidable mother-in-law. "Pity me, dear Miss Dashwood!" cried Lucy as they ascended the stairs together, for the Middletons arrived immediately after Mrs. Jennings, and they all followed the servant simultaneously. "There is no one here but you who can truly feel for me. I declare I can hardly stand. Good gracious! In a moment I shall behold the person upon whose happiness my entire existence depends—that is to be my mother!" Elinor could have provided immediate relief by suggesting they were about to behold Miss Morton's mother rather than her own, but instead, she assured Lucy with great sincerity that she pitied her, to the utter amazement of Lucy, who, though genuinely uncomfortable, hoped to appear an object of irrepressible envy to Elinor.

Mrs. Ferrars was a small, thin woman, upright to the point of formality in her bearing and serious to the point of sourness in her aspect. Her complexion was sallow; her features were small, devoid of beauty and naturally lacking expression. Only a fortunate contraction of the brow saved her countenance from the disgrace of insipidity, lending it instead the strong characteristics of pride and ill-nature. She was not a woman of many words, proportioning her speech strictly to the number of her ideas. Of the few syllables that escaped her lips, not one fell to the share of Miss Dashwood, whom she eyed with the spirited determination of disliking her at all costs. Elinor could no longer be distressed by such behavior. A few months prior, it would have hurt her exceedingly, but Mrs. Ferrars held no power to distress her now. Indeed, the disparity in her treatment of the Miss Steeles, a difference seemingly engineered to humble them further, only amused Elinor. She could not help smiling at the graciousness displayed by both mother and daughter toward the very person—Lucy Steele, who was particularly distinguished—who, had they known the truth, they would have been most anxious to mortify. Meanwhile, Elinor, possessing comparatively little power to wound them, sat pointedly slighted by both.

Yet, as she smiled at this misapplied graciousness, she could not reflect on the mean-spirited folly from which it sprang, nor observe the studied attentions with which the Miss Steeles courted its continuation, without thoroughly despising all four. Lucy was in a state of exultation at being so honorably distinguished, while Miss Steele desired nothing more than to be teased about Dr. Davies to achieve perfect happiness. The dinner was grand, the servants numerous, and everything bespoke the mistress's inclination for display and the master's ability to sustain it. Despite the ongoing improvements to the Norland estate and the fact that its owner had once been within a thousand pounds of selling at a loss, no symptom of indigence appeared—not even a poverty of conversation, which was considerable. John Dashwood had little of value to say, and his wife even less. This was not peculiar disgrace, however, for most of their visitors suffered from similar disqualifications for agreeability: a lack of sense, natural or acquired; a lack of elegance; a lack of spirits; or a lack of temper.

When the ladies retired to the drawing-room after dinner, this conversational poverty became particularly evident. The gentlemen had supplied the discourse with some variety—politics, enclosing land, and breaking horses—but the topic died quickly thereafter. Only one subject engaged the ladies until coffee was served: the comparative heights of Harry Dashwood and Lady Middleton's second son, William, who were nearly the same age. Had both children been present, the matter might have been settled easily by measurement; but with only Harry there, it devolved into conjectural assertion on both sides. Everyone felt entitled to be equally positive in their opinion and to repeat it ad infinitum. The positions were as follows: the two mothers, though each privately convinced her own son was taller, politely decided in favor of the other. The two grandmothers, with equal partiality but greater sincerity, were equally earnest in supporting their own descendants. Lucy, hardly less anxious to please one parent than the other, deemed both boys remarkably tall for their age and could not conceive of any difference whatsoever. Miss Steele, with greater address, gave credit to each boy as fast as she could. Elinor, having once expressed her opinion in favor of William, thereby offending Mrs. Ferrars and Fanny further, saw no necessity to enforce it with further assertion. Marianne, when called upon, offended everyone by declaring she had no opinion to offer, as she had never considered the matter.

Before leaving Norland, Elinor had painted a very pretty pair of screens for her sister-in-law, which, now mounted and brought home, adorned the current drawing-room. Catching the eye of John Dashwood as he followed the other gentlemen into the room, these screens were officiously handed to Colonel Brandon for his admiration. "These are done by my eldest sister," he said. "As a man of taste, you will, I dare say, be pleased with them. I do not know whether you have ever seen any of her performances before, but she is generally reckoned to draw extremely well." The Colonel, though disclaiming all pretensions to connoisseurship, warmly admired the screens as he would have admired anything painted by Miss Dashwood. With the curiosity of the others naturally aroused, they were circulated for general inspection. Mrs. Ferrars, unaware they were Elinor's work, particularly requested to examine them.
