The air in Mr. Tartar's chambers possessed a sterility that bordered on the supernatural. It was a vacuum where dust dared not settle, a realm where the concept of grime had been exiled forever. The floors were scrubbed to a blinding whiteness, suggesting that the very London blackmen had been liberated from their labor and sent adrift into the ocean. Every brass fixture, from the smallest latch to the largest telescope mount, burned with a metallic luster, reflecting the room's inhabitants back at themselves like distorted mirrors. There was no speck, no stain, no organic residue to mar the purity of his collection.

His sitting room resembled the bridge of a frigate; his bathroom, a sterile dairy; his sleeping chamber, a meticulously organized seed merchant's warehouse. Even his cot, suspended in the center, seemed to breathe in rhythm with the ticking of unseen clocks. Belongings were not merely stored; they were quartered. Maps claimed their specific alcoves, books their shelves, brushes their hooks, boots their racks, clothes their drawers, and case-bottles their niches. Nothing was wasted. Every shelf, bracket, and drawer was engineered with mathematical precision to accommodate items that would otherwise have nowhere to exist. His silver service lay on the sideboard with such exactitude that a misplaced salt spoon would scream betrayal. His toiletries sat on the dressing table in a formation so rigid that a toothpick of slovenly intent could be identified from a distance.

Curiosities from distant shores—stuffed birds, dried fish, repolished reptiles, ancient arms, shells, seaweed, coral memorials—were displayed in their designated sanctuaries. Each object occupied a space it could not possibly share with another. Paint and varnish waited invisibly, poised to erase any stray fingerprint that might dare to breach the seal. It was a vessel kept immaculate, a ship of order sailing through a chaotic world. On this bright summer day, a neat awning stretched over his garden, rigged with the competence of a seasoned sailor, lending the entire scene a maritime completeness. It was a garden that belonged to the stern windows of a ship, ready to sail gallantly if only Mr. Tartar had raised his voice through the speaking trumpet slung in the corner and ordered the anchor hove up.

Mr. Tartar, conducting the tour of this floating citadel, embodied the spirit of his domain. When a man rides a hobby that refuses to shy away from anything, there is a peculiar charm in watching him laugh at the absurdity of his own creation. Being cordial, earnest, and genuinely fresh, he appeared at his brightest during this inspection. Rosa, though treated with the deference due to the First Lady of the Admiralty, perceived the scene differently. She saw a sunburnt sailor finding joy in his own delusions, a man half-laughing, half-rejoicing in the mechanics of his life. As the inspection concluded, he bowed her out of his admiral's cabin, gesturing toward the garden with the hand that had once held Mr. Crisparkle's life in its grip.

"Helena! Helena Landless! Are you there?"

"Who speaks to me? Not Rosa?" A second face, equally handsome, emerged from the foliage.

"Yes, my darling!"

"Why, how did you come here, dearest?"

"I—I don't quite know," Rosa replied, her cheeks flushing. "Unless I am dreaming!"

Blushes bloomed in the country of the magic beanstalk. Why did they bloom? Their faces were alone with the flowers, yet the color persisted.

"I am not dreaming," Helena smiled, her expression steady. "I should take more for granted if I were. How do we come together—or so near together—so very unexpectedly?"

Unexpectedly indeed, amidst the dingy gables of P.J.T.'s connection and the salt-tolerant blooms. Rosa, waking from her reverie, hurriedly recounted the circumstances of their meeting, explaining the why and wherefore with breathless urgency.

"And Mr. Crisparkle is here," Rosa concluded rapidly. "And, could you believe it? Long ago he saved his life!"

"I could believe any such thing of Mr. Crisparkle," Helena returned, her face flushing with admiration.

More blushes erupted in the beanstalk country.

"Yes, but it wasn't Crisparkle," Rosa corrected sharply.

"I don't understand, love."

"It was very nice of Mr. Crisparkle to be saved," Rosa said, "and he couldn't have shown his high opinion of Mr. Tartar more expressively. But it was Mr. Tartar who saved him."

Helena's dark eyes fixed intently on the bright face among the leaves. "Is Mr. Tartar with you now, dear?"

"No; because he has given up his rooms to me—to us, I mean. It is such a beautiful place!"

"Is it?"

"It is like the inside of the most exquisite ship that ever sailed. It is like—it is like—"

"Like a dream?" Helena suggested softly.

Rosa nodded, inhaling the scent of the flowers.

After a brief silence, during which Helena seemed to harbor a silent compassion for someone unseen, she spoke slowly. "My poor Neville is reading in his own room; the sun is too bright on this side just now. I think he had better not know that you are so near."

"Oh, I think so too!" Rosa agreed readily.

"I suppose," Helena continued, her tone doubtful, "that he must know by-and-by all you have told me; but I am not sure. Ask Mr. Crisparkle's advice, my darling. Ask him whether I may tell Neville as much or as little of what you have told me as I think best."

Rosa retreated into her state-cabin to pose the question. The Minor Canon gave his verdict freely: Helena's judgment was hers to exercise.

"I thank him very much," Helena said when Rosa returned. "Ask him whether it would be best to wait until any further maligning or pursuit of Neville by this wretch discloses itself, or to try to anticipate it: I mean, to discover if any such activity occurs in the shadows around us?"

The Minor Canon struggled to offer a confident opinion. After two or three failed attempts, he suggested consulting Mr. Grewgious. Helena acquiesced. With an unsuccessful attempt at feigned indifference, the Minor Canon crossed the quadrangle to P.J.T.'s and delivered the message. Mr. Grewgious adhered strictly to the principle that if one could steal a march upon a brigand or a wild beast, one ought to do so. He viewed John Jasper as precisely that combination of brigand and wild beast.

Thus advised, Mr. Crisparkle returned to report to Rosa, who in turn informed Helena. Steadfastly pursuing her train of thought at the window, Helena inquired, "We may count on Mr. Tartar's readiness to help us, Rosa?"

Oh yes! Rosa thought shyly, believing she could almost guarantee it. Should she ask Mr. Crisparkle? "I think your authority on the point is equal to his, my dear," Helena said sedately. "You needn't disappear again for that."

"You see, Neville," Helena reflected further, "knows no one else here; he hasn't even exchanged a word with anyone else. If Mr. Tartar would call to see him openly and often; if he would spare a minute for the purpose, frequently; if he would even do so, almost daily; something might come of it."

"Something might come of it, dear?" Rosa repeated, her face a study of perplexity as she surveyed her friend's beauty. "Something might?"

"If Neville's movements are truly watched, and if the purpose is to isolate him from all friends and acquaintances, wearing his daily life out grain by grain—which seems to be the threat to you—does it not appear likely," Helena reasoned, "that his enemy would somehow communicate with Mr. Tartar to warn him off from Neville? In which case, we might not only know the fact, but learn from Mr. Tartar the terms of the communication."

"I see!" Rosa cried, darting back into her state-cabin.

Soon her face reappeared, flushed with excitement. She had told Mr. Crisparkle, who had summoned Mr. Tartar. Mr. Tartar—who was waiting now, should she require him—had declared his immediate readiness to act as suggested, commencing his task that very day.

"I thank him from my heart," Helena said. "Pray tell him so."

Confused between the flower garden and the cabin, Rosa delivered the message and returned with further assurances from Mr. Tartar, standing wavering in a divided state between Helena and him. This proved that confusion is not always awkward; sometimes, it presents a very pleasant appearance.

"And now, darling," Helena said, mindful of the caution restricting their current interview, "we will part. I hear Neville moving. Are you going back?"

"To Miss Twinkleton's?" Rosa asked.

"Yes."

"Oh, I could never go there again. I couldn't, indeed, after that dreadful interview!"

"Then where _are_ you going, pretty one?"

"Now I come to think of it, I don't know," Rosa admitted. "I have settled nothing at all yet, but my guardian will take care of me. Don't be uneasy, dear. I shall be sure to be somewhere."

(It did seem likely.)
