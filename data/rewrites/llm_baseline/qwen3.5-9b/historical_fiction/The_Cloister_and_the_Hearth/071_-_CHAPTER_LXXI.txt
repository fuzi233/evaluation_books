"As is the race of leaves, so is that of men." In this same year, while a great soul budded quietly within a humble tailor's home in Rotterdam, a mighty figure met his end with spectacular fanfare. Philip, Duke of Burgundy, Count of Holland, and many other titles, lay dying in his bed at Bruges.

In those days, the poor fell ill and recovered according to nature's whims; but for the wealthy, sickness was often a sentence of doom. In an era where one physician could kill three patients before curing a single one, the stakes were terrifyingly high. The Duke's ailment, unnamed in its time though we now know it as diphtheria, was a ravaging disease. Given the Duke's advanced age, the attending physicians—led by the learned Dr. Remedy—decided upon bloodletting. The Duke grew pale and icy, a sign that the treatment was failing.

Desperate, Dr. Remedy turned to the arcane remedies of science. "Alas!" he cried. "We must skin an ape immediately and affix it to the Duke's chest!" State officials scrambled frantically through the city, hunting for the specific primate required to counteract the madness of humanity. Yet, perdition awaited them; there were no apes to be found. Buffaloes, lizards, Turkish captives, and leopards abounded, but none possessed the requisite qualities.

"Why, there used to be an ape about here," one official noted hopefully. "I saw him standing just outside." Indeed, there had been one, until a hungry mastiff had torn the lively creature apart over a stolen meal—a grim fulfillment of the ancient human adage: *Be of your own generation.*

Faced with this emergency, the Seneschal cast desperate eyes around the room, and soon hope returned to his countenance. "Look here," he whispered urgently. "Surely this will suffice; it is entirely ape-like, save for the doublet and hose."

"Nay!" protested the Chancellor irritably. "Princess Marie would have us hanged. She holds this creature in high regard." At that moment, our friend Giles strutted through the room, unconscious of the gravity of the situation, dressed in shimmering cloth of gold.

Impatient with the delay, Dr. Remedy ordered a dog skinned instead. "A dog is the next best thing to an ape," he declared, adding the strict condition, "but it must be a dog of a single color." Thus, a liver-colored dog was skinned and pinned, still beating, against the Sovereign's chest. The Duke drew his final breath.

Philip the Good, thus dispatched by the methods of his time, left behind thirty-one children. Somehow, one emerged legitimate to reign in his stead. The benevolent Duke had provided for nineteen of the others; the remainder were forced to make their own way. Flemish chronicles claimed the deceased prince descended from the Kings of Troy through Thierry of Aquitaine and the ancient rulers of Franconia. Yet, such lineage offered no true distinction; I cannot find a single prince of that day who did not trace his roots back to Troy. To call someone "of Priam" was merely medieval code for "son of Adam."

The Duke's body was transported to Burgundy and interred in a magnificent black marble mausoleum at Dijon. Holland mourned his passing, unaware that within its borders, something far more famous was being born that very year. That judgment has since been overturned. Today, people gaze with reverence at the tailor's house where the great birth of the fifteenth century occurred. No one knows, nor does anyone care, in which house the good Duke breathed his last. Why? For Dukes like Philip the Good come and go, leaving mankind neither richer, nor wiser, nor better than they found it.

But when, once every few centuries, a child such as Margaret's son is brought into the world, behold! A human torch ignited by heavenly fire; and let there be light, thundering from pole to pole.
