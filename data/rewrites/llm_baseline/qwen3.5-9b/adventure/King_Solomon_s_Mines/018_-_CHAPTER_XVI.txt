It was already dark on the third night after the events of the previous chapter when our party established camp at the foot of the "Three Witches"—a triangular cluster of mountains marking the start of Solomon's Great Road. Our group comprised Quatermain, Sir Henry, and Good, accompanied by Foulata, who attended to us, particularly to Good. With us was Infadoos, and carried in a litter was Gagool, the witch, whose mutterings and curses echoed from within her enclosure throughout the day. A contingent of guards and attendants rounded out the expedition.

The mountains themselves, or rather the three distinct peaks resulting from a singular geological upheaval, formed a triangle with its base facing us: one peak to our right, one to our left, and the central summit directly ahead. I shall never forget the sight those three towering spires presented in the early dawn of the following morning. High above, soaring into the azure sky, twisted wreaths of snow clung to their summits. Below the snowline, the peaks were stained a deep purple by heather, a color shared by the wild moors climbing the slopes toward them. Directly before us, the white ribbon of Solomon's Great Road stretched uphill for about five miles before abruptly terminating at the foot of the central peak. It ended there.

I need not detail the surge of intense excitement that gripped us as we commenced our ascent that morning. Finally, we were approaching the legendary mines responsible for the tragic deaths of the old Portuguese Dom three centuries prior, my unfortunate friend, his ill-fated descendant, and, as we feared, George Curtis, Sir Henry's brother. Could we, after enduring so much, escape a similar fate? As the fiendish Gagool had prophesied, evil often befell those who sought treasure. Walking up that final stretch of beautiful, heather-fringed road, a creeping superstition took hold of me, and I suspect it did for Good and Sir Henry as well.

For an hour and a half, we hurried upward, our eagerness driving us faster than the bearers carrying Gagool's hammock could manage. The witch eventually piped out to halt us, thrusting her hideous, shriveled face between the grass curtains of her litter.

"Walk more slowly, white men," she rasped, her gleaming eyes locking onto ours. "Why will ye run to meet the evil that shall befall you, ye seekers after treasure?" She emitted that terrible, bone-chilling laugh that always sent a shiver down my spine, momentarily draining our enthusiasm. Yet, undeterred, we pressed on.

Suddenly, the landscape revealed itself: a vast, circular chasm with sloping sides, plunging three hundred feet or more into darkness and spanning nearly half a mile in circumference.

"Can't you guess what this is?" I asked Sir Henry and Good, who stared in stunned silence at the abyss. They shook their heads. "Then clearly, you have never visited the diamond diggings at Kimberley. Rest assured, this is Solomon's Diamond Mine." I pointed to the exposed layers of stiff blue clay clinging to the grass-covered slopes. "Look at the formation; if we descended, we would undoubtedly find 'pipes' of soapy brecciated rock. And observe," I continued, indicating a series of worn, flat stone slabs arranged on a gentle slope beneath an ancient watercourse carved into the bedrock. "If those are not washing tables used to separate the ore, I am a Dutchman!"

At the precipice of this immense pit—the location marked on the old Dom's map—Solomon's Great Road forked, curving around the void to support its crumbling edges against landslides. Driven by insatiable curiosity to identify the three towering shapes visible across the gulf, we advanced along the path. As we drew nearer, we realized they were colossal statues. Before us sat the three "Silent Ones," revered with terror by the Kukuana people.

Only when we stood close did we grasp their full majesty. Upon massive pedestals of dark rock, adorned with crude symbols of phallic worship, sat three colossal figures separated by forty paces, overlooking the road that spanned sixty miles of plain toward Loo. Two were male, one female; each measured approximately thirty feet from head to pedestal. The female form, nude and possessing severe yet striking beauty, bore crescent-shaped horns atop her head, though centuries of exposure had damaged her features. The two males were draped in garments. The statue to our right possessed a terrifying, devilish visage, while the one to our left wore a serene expression that felt dreadfully unnatural—a calm born of inhuman cruelty, as Sir Henry noted, reminiscent of ancient myths regarding powers capable of watching human suffering without pity.

These three statues formed an awe-inspiring trinity, sitting in eternal solitude, gazing forever across the plains. Wondering who had shaped them or dug the pit, I recalled the Old Testament accounts of King Solomon worshipping strange gods: Ashtoreth, the goddess of the Zidonians; Chemosh, the god of the Moabites; and Milcom, the god of the Ammonites. Suggesting these figures might represent those fallen deities, I turned to Sir Henry, a scholar of classics.

"Hmm," Sir Henry replied thoughtfully. "There may be truth in that. Ashtoreth of the Hebrews was known as Astarte to the Phoenicians, great traders during Solomon's era. Astarte, later becoming Aphrodite for the Greeks, was depicted with horns resembling a half-moon. Notice the distinct horns on the female figure's brow. Perhaps these colossi were commissioned by a Phoenician official managing the mines. Who knows?"

Before we could finish our examination, Infadoos approached, saluting the "Silent Ones" with his spear. He asked if we intended to enter the "Place of Death" immediately or wait for midday. Since it was barely eleven o'clock and curiosity burned within us, we decided to proceed instantly, suggesting we take provisions in case we were delayed. Gagool's litter was brought forward, and she stepped out, casting an evil grin at us before hobbling toward the sheer rock wall rising eighty feet ahead, leading up to the snow-capped peak.

Following her to a narrow, solidly arched portal resembling a mine entrance, Gagool awaited us with that sinister smile.

"Now, white men from the Stars," she cackled, "great warriors Incubu, Bougwan, and Macumazahn the wise—are ye ready? Behold, I am here to serve my Lord the King and show you the store of bright stones. Ha! ha! ha!"

"We are ready," I replied. "Good, good! Strengthen your hearts to bear what lies ahead. Comest thou too, Infadoos, thou traitor to thy master?"

Infadoos frowned. "Nay, I do not enter. It is not for me. But thou, Gagool, curb thy tongue and beware how thou dealst with my lords. Should a hair of them be harmed, thou shalt die fifty times over as a witch. Hearst thou?"

"I hear, Infadoos," she sneered. "Thou hast always loved big words; even as a babe, thou threatenedst thine own mother. But fear not, I live only to serve the King. Many kings I served until they served me. Ha! ha! I go to look upon their faces once more, and Twala's as well! Come, here is the lamp." She produced a large gourd filled with oil and fitted with a rush wick.

"Art thou coming, Foulata?" Good asked in his affected Kukuana dialect.

"I fear, my lord," the girl whispered timidly.

"Then give me the basket."

"Nay, my lord, whither thou goest, I go also."

*The deuce you will!* I thought. *That may prove awkward if we never escape.*

Without hesitation, Gagool plunged into the dark passage, wide enough for two abreast. We followed the sound of her voice, trembling slightly, until a sudden rustle of wings startled us.

"Hullo! What's that?" Good cried. "Someone hit me in the face!"

"Bats," I reassured him. "On you go."

After perhaps fifty paces, the passage grew faintly luminous. Within moments, we entered what must be the most wondrous place ever beheld by living eyes. Imagine the nave of the grandest cathedral you have ever seen, windowless yet dimly lit from above by shafts piercing the vaulted ceiling a hundred feet overhead. Nature's cathedral exceeded any human construction in loftiness and width. Yet, its size was secondary to its splendor. Running in rows down its length were gigantic pillars of ice-white material—huge stalactites. Some were twenty feet in diameter at the base, rising delicately to the distant roof; others were still forming. On the rock floor lay broken columns resembling ruins of Greek temples, while high above, the tips of massive icicles hung from the ceiling.

As we gazed, we heard the rhythmic *plop* of water falling from the distant icicles onto the columns below. On some, drops fell only once every few minutes. Calculating the time required to grow a pillar eighty feet tall at such a rate yielded an incomprehensible duration. Indeed, evidence of this slowness was etched into one pillar: a crude carving of a mummy with an Egyptian god seated beside it, likely made by an ancient laborer.
