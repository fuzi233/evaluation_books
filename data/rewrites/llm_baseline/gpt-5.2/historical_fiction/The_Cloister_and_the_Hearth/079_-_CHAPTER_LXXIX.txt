Margaret had wept for old Martin as one weeps for a tree that has stood long in the yard and made a kindly shadow; but this new felling smote her nearer, for it fell across the very hearth. Catherine, bustling with the good-will that loves to mend sorrow by handling it, tried her homely cordials.

“He is at rest,” she said; “he himself called it a ripe tree. And see now—there be but two mouths to feed, child; one pot the lighter.”

Margaret turned on her with wet eyes that had no anger in them, only a deep amaze, as if Catherine had spoken in some foreign tongue and mistaken the meaning of her own words.

“Such cares are all the joys I have,” she answered. “They fill my desolate heart; and now my heart seems void as well as waste. Oh, empty chair—my bosom it aches to see thee. Poor old man! how could I love him by halves? I that did use to sit and look at him and think, ‘But for me thou wouldst die of hunger.’ He—so wise, so learned erst—was got to be helpless as my own sweet babe; and I loved him as if he had been my child instead of my father. Oh, empty chair! Oh, empty heart! Well-a-day! well-a-day!”

Her tears fell as naturally as broth into a bowl, and would not be denied. The room itself seemed to listen: the small hush between the tick of the clock and the soft crack of settling wood; the far-off street noises dulled by the thick walls—sounds that had once been a comfort, because Martin’s breath had made a third, familiar sound among them. Now there was a gap in the household’s music; and the very silence had a sharpness, like a knife laid down on the table.

Catherine stood abashed. Her ready sayings, that were meant to serve for all griefs as salt serves for all meats, lay flat and tasteless here. At last she dropped her eyes, and, after a day in which she had watched Margaret move about the house like one who lays trenchers for a ghost, she came to her with a simpler voice.

“I speak to thee out o’ my head, and not out o’ my bosom,” said she. “Thou dost well to be deaf to me. Were I in thy place, I should mourn the old man all one as thou dost.”

Then Margaret caught at her as drowning folk catch a plank, and kissed her; and that one bit of true sympathy did her more good than all the earlier comfort. The commonplaces did none.

After this, Catherine’s heart—being soft under its meddlesomeness—yearned over the young wife. She watched her eat little, and yet still set food out as if three must dine; watched her ladle soup into an extra porringer by sheer habit, and then, remembering, stop short with the spoon trembling above the pot. Margaret’s grief took the form of household work; she clung to it as to a native tongue in a strange country, because it was something she could still speak without thinking.

One evening Catherine found her in the act of laying the board. She had taken down a platter that Martin had liked, and stood with it in her hands as if it were heavier than baked clay ought to be. Catherine came close and spoke low, as women do when they would put hard counsel into kindly words.

“My poor girl,” said she, “you were not born to live alone. I have got to look on you as my own daughter. Waste not thine youth upon my son Gerard. Either he is dead—or he is a traitor. It cuts my heart to say it; but who can help seeing it? Thy father is gone, and I cannot always be aside thee. And here is an honest lad that loves thee well this many a day. I’d take him and Comfort together. Heaven hath sent us these creatures to torment us and comfort us and all; we are just nothing in the world without ’em.”

Margaret stared, as if Catherine had slipped from plain Dutch into Latin.

Then Catherine, taking her perplexity for blindness, pushed on in her practical fashion.

“Why, sure you are not so blind as not to see it?”

“What? Who?” Margaret’s voice was small, like one that has been called out of sleep.

“Who but this Luke Peterson.”

“What—our Luke? The boy that carries my basket?”

“Nay, he is over nineteen, and a fine healthy lad. And I have made inquiries for you; and they all do say he is a capable workman, and never touches a drop; and that is much in a Rotterdam lad, which they are mostly half man, half sponge.”

The first smile that had visited Margaret in many days came and went, faint as the smell of cinnamon when the lid is lifted and clapped down again.

“Luke loves dried puddings dearly,” said she, and the kitchen seemed for a moment to warm with the memory of nutmeg and currants, of bread soaked and sweetened, pressed, steamed, and sliced. “And I make them to his mind. ’Tis them he comes a-courting here.”

She stopped, colouring suddenly to the roots of her hair, as if her own tongue had betrayed her into another dialect than she meant to speak.

“But if I thought he came after your son’s wife—that is, or ought to be—I’d soon put him to the door.”

“Nay, nay,” cried Catherine, frightened at the mischief her own hands had raised. “For Heaven’s sake let me not make mischief. Poor lad! Why, girl, Fancy will not be bridled. Bless you, I wormed it out of him near a twelvemonth agone.”

“Oh, mother—and you let him?”

“Well,” said Catherine, half ashamed and half stubborn, “I thought of you. I said to myself, ‘If he is fool enough to be her slave for nothing, all the better for her. A lone woman is lost without a man about her to fetch and carry her little matters.’ But now my mind is changed, and I think the best use you can put him to is to marry him.”

Margaret drew herself up, not in pride so much as in loyalty that had become the last structure standing in her desolation.

“So then,” said she bitterly, “his own mother is against him, and would wed me to the first comer. An, Gerard, thou hast but me. I will not believe thee dead till I see thy tomb, nor false till I see thee with another lover in thine hand. Foolish boy—I shall ne’er be civil to him again.”

Thus Luke Peterson, who had been coming and going with the basket like a tame creature of the house, met now a cold look where he had found gentle speech. At first he took it for some chance weather of the women’s minds; but coldness that lasts turns the stoutest heart into pulp. By-and-by, when Margaret spoke little, and then spoke short and sharp, the great soft fellow fell to whimpering, and at last asked her plump, with that blunt honesty that is a child’s weapon in a man’s hand, whether he had done aught to offend her.

“Nothing,” said Margaret, though her tone cut. “I am to blame. I am curst. If you will take my counsel you will keep out of my way awhile.”

“It is all along of me, Luke,” put in Catherine quickly, not able to bear the sight of his hurt.

“You, Mistress Catherine?” Luke gaped as if struck. “Why, what have I done for you to set her against me?”

“Nay,” said Catherine, half coaxing, half scolding, “I meant all for the best. I told her I saw you were looking towards her through a wedding ring. But she won’t hear of it.”

“There was no need to tell her that, wife,” Luke cried, with a sudden flare of misery. “She knows I am courting her this twelvemonth.”

“Not I,” said Margaret hotly; “or I should never have opened the street door to you.”

“Why, I come here every Saturday night,” said Luke, amazed. “And that is how the lads in Rotterdam do court. If we sup with a lass o’ Saturdays, that wooing.”

“Oh—that is Rotterdam, is it?” Margaret’s mouth tightened; the words were Dutch, but the meaning was not hers at all. “Then next time you come, let it be Thursday or Friday. For my part, I thought you came after my puddings, boy.”

“I like your puddings well enough,” said Luke, swallowing. “You make them better than mother does. But I like you still better than the puddings.”

The very mention of puddings—of the raisins she had picked over, the suet she had chopped fine, the spice she had grated till the air was full of warm sweetness—might once have softened her; food had always been her way of giving love a body. But now the words struck her as an invasion of the one sacred thing she had left: her name as Gerard’s wife.

“Then you have seen the last of them,” said she. And then, not loudly, but with a firmness that shut a door, “How dare you talk so to another man’s wife, and him far away? You need not trouble yourself to come here any more, Luke; I can carry my basket myself.”

“Oh—very well,” said Luke.

He sat a little while, silent and stupid, as if his thoughts were heavy as wet wool. Then he rose, looked once at Catherine with the face of a chastised dog, and said sadly, “Dame, I daresay I have got the sack,” and went out.

Yet habit is a chain that holds even when it galls. The next Saturday, as evening settled and the street’s noises thinned to the occasional footfall and a distant call, Catherine, opening the door, found him on the step, his great shoulders shaking, blubbering outright. He told her, between sobs, that he had got used to come there; and every other place seemed strange.

Catherine went in and told Margaret. Margaret listened, and her mouth softened against her will. She sighed.

“Poor Luke,” said she. “He might come in for her—if he could know his place, and treat her like a married wife.”

When Catherine carried back this message, Luke’s face brightened, then fell into doubt. He hesitated, as if the terms were a riddle spoken in a language he had not learnt.

“Pshaw!” cried Catherine, impatient with nicety. “Promises are pie-crusts. Promise her all the world, sooner than sit outside like a fool, when a word will carry you inside. Now you humour her in everything; and then, if poor Gerard come not home and claim her, you will be sure to have her—in time. A lone woman is aye to be tired out, thou foolish boy.”
