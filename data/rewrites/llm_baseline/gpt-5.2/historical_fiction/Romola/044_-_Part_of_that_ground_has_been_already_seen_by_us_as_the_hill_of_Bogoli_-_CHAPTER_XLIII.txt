Romola, on her way back from the hospital, chose the longer road, letting her steps draw a wide curve toward the river as if the Arno’s damp breath might cool the fever of her thoughts. The streets had begun to smell of late-autumn smoke and sour wine lees, and from open doors came the faint, homely scents of boiled cabbage and yesterday’s bread warming on a tile—poor comfort, yet comfort still. She meant to reach the Ponte Vecchio and hasten to San Stefano in search of Baldassarre. She shrank from learning more—each new fact about him seemed like another thorn pressed into the flesh of duty—yet to leave him unlooked-for felt like abandoning a claim that had fastened itself near her heart.

But when she came to the meeting of the roads, where the Por’ Santa Maria would open at her right and the bridge lie to her left, the current of the crowd caught her and turned her. A hush fell as if a hand had been laid upon the city’s mouth; then, as one body, people dropped to their knees. Romola sank with them, her skirts gathering dust, her palms pressed together before she had time to decide.

The Cross was passing—the great Cross of the Duomo, borne high and steady like a mast in a windless sea.

When it had gone by and she rose, the change from kneeling to standing made her aware of her fatigue as sharply as a cramp. Walking had kept her mind occupied; standing left her to feel her own weight, her limbs, the slight trembling that came after long strain. Beside her, a shopkeeper—one of the hose-vendors whose face she had seen again and again beneath the Duomo vaulting—leaned toward her with the familiar, almost familial confidence Florence now gave her.

“Madonna Romola, you will be weary of standing. Gian Fantoni will be glad to give you a seat in his house. Here—his door is close. Let me open it. What! He loves God and the Frate as we do. His house is yours.”

It was a strange thing: the house in the Via de’ Bardi had become less “home” to her than this ringed body of streets, towers, and bells, where at every turning she met a look that asked help or offered fellowship. She let herself be guided through the open door on her right-hand. Up a narrow stair that smelled of wool and warm oil, she was led to an upper window. There a stout woman, with three children pressed close to her skirts, all in the plain garb of the Piagnoni, made space with reverence, as if Romola were not merely a woman but an emblem of the city’s own penitence. Bright lengths of drapery hung below the sill—cloth for hose, cloth for cloaks—like strips of captured sunset.

From that corner she could see the procession moving in solemn slowness over the Ponte Vecchio, and beyond it the river flowing out toward Santa Trinita. The air held a trembling of sound, but not the expected chanting. No wail rose, no hymn braided itself through the streets. There was only the measured tramp of feet, and the faint sweep of woollen garments brushing against one another—an austere music, as if Florence had decided that even her sorrow must be economical, saving breath as she saved flour.

The first footsteps that passed within her sight were young: a long train of Florentine youth, fresh faces beardless as peeled almonds, bearing high among them the white image of the youthful Jesus. A golden glory shone above his head; he stood by the tall cross where thorns and nails lay ready. Their shoulders moved in time like men carrying heavy sacks of grain—silent labor in a season when every household knew the precise weight of want.

After those came the Companies of Discipline, mysterious as a masked night. Each wore a garb that hid the entire head and face save for the eyes. Everyone knew these shrouded forms were citizens—men who on other days counted coin, weighed wool, argued contracts, drew up laws—yet here no one could be singled out as son or husband or father. Their individuality had been laid down like a tool at the door. They walked as symbols of a shared vow. Each company bore its color and badge, but all were swathed in the same obliterating cloth, leaving only the gaze, the bare fact of human sight, and the wordless fellowship of moving together.

Compared with these blanked figures, the monks seemed almost startlingly personal, even under tonsure and frock—faces with their own histories written in the mouth and brow. First flowed the white of reformed Benedictines; then the far longer stream of the Frati Minori, Franciscans all in grey, knotted cord at their waists, some with wooden _zoccoli_ beneath bare feet, the clack of those soles a thin percussion against the stones. There were many of them in Florence—zealous lovers of mankind, and, in that age, often haters of the Dominicans. After the grey came the black of the Augustinians of San Spirito, with more cultivated human faces above it—men who had inherited Boccaccio’s library and been, when learning was rarer, the most learned company in Florence. Then the white over dark of the Carmelites; and again the unmixed black of the Servites, that Florentine order founded by seven merchants who had forsaken gain for the Divine Mother.

Now the crowd’s breath seemed to tighten. Hearts beat faster, in hatred or in love, for over the bridge came a stream of black mantles over white scapularies. Everyone knew: the Dominicans.

Those of Fiesole passed first—black parted by white, one after another, tonsured head after tonsured head. Their mantles were coarse, many threadbare, some almost ragged; the Prior of San Marco had drawn the fraternities under his rule into strict poverty and harder discipline. Still expectation held, suspended like a pot over a low flame. Then at last, among the line of black and white, one mantle—only a little more worn than the rest—seemed singled out by a force that was not cloth and stitch. Above it was a tonsured head that might not have struck a stranger as remarkable, had it not already been fixed on bronze medals, with the sword of God on the reverse; had it not been seen ringed by an armed guard on the way to the Duomo; had it not been transfigured by the inward fire of the orator when it turned upon a multitude.

Savonarola.

No one dared break the stillness with a cry that would rise above the solemn tramp and the hush of garment-sweep; yet his ear, and every ear near him, caught a mingled sound—slow hissing that longed to become curses, and low murmurs that longed to become blessings. Perhaps he heard the balance tilt toward hatred. Two or three of his disciples near the front dropped to their knees as if divinity itself were passing. The movement ran like a subtle shock along the street-sides: some remained rigid, but most bent the knee and bowed the head.

Hatred, too, found its gesture. As Savonarola went up the Por’ Santa Maria, Romola saw, from an upper window, a man spit down upon him.

The procession did not end with him. More monks followed—the Frati Umiliati from Ognissanti, with their old glorious tradition of being among the earliest workers in the wool-trade; then Vallombrosans and other Benedictine varieties, reminding any instructed eye by niceties of form and color that in earlier ages of abuse, reformers had risen and marked a change of spirit by a change of garb. At last the shaven crowns ceased, and there came the train of secular priests.

Then, in long array, followed the twenty-one incorporated Arts of Florence, banners floating above them in proud declaration that each had its distinct function—from the bakers of bread to the judges and notaries. The word “bread” was not an abstraction in those days; it was the remembered weight of loaves, the sting of flour dust, the smell of starter kept alive like a child through lean weeks, the sound of crust cracking under a knife that had become careful and thin. Even from Romola’s window, the city’s hunger seemed to sit behind every banner like an uninvited guest.

After the Arts came the secondary officers of State, from lesser to greater, until the line of secularities was broken by the Canons of the Duomo. They carried a sacred relic: the very head of San Zenobio, enclosed in silver—the immortal bishop whose virtues were held to have saved Florence perhaps a thousand years before. Here was the nucleus of the procession. Behind the relic came the archbishop in a gorgeous cope, canopy held above him; and after him the hidden Image.

Hidden first by rich curtains of brocade enclosing an outer painted tabernacle, and within that by a more ancient tabernacle that had not been opened in the memory of living men, nor in the memory of their fathers. In that inner shrine was the image of the Pitying Mother, found long ago in the soil of L’Impruneta—found, they said, with a cry as the spade struck it. It was a story told as naturally as any household tale: a spade, a stone, a voice from earth. Myth and labor, miracle and digging, braided into one.

In former times the unseen Image had scarcely ever been carried to the Duomo without rich gifts borne before it—veils and curtains and mantles offered by emulous men and communities. People loved to recite such lists: gold, silk, jewels, the sumptuous vocabulary of devotion. And the richest tale of all was that of the poor abbess and her nuns who had no money for materials, and so—so the story went—wove a mantle of gold brocade with their prayers, embroidered and adorned it with prayers, and saw it presented in the great Piazza by two beautiful youths who spread white wings and vanished into the blue.

But today there were no gifts before the tabernacle. No donations were to be given except to the poor. That had been Fra Girolamo’s counsel: not gifts to invisible powers, but help to visible need. Altars had been raised before churches at various points, and there the oblations for the poor were laid down. Not even a torch was carried. The hidden Mother, surely, cared less for torches and brocade than for the wail of hungry people.

Florence was at extremity. She had exhausted her own strength, spent her own cunning, and could only wait for something beyond her reach—something that, in every age, has been called divine. The Frate in the torn mantle had said help would certainly come. Many faint hearts clung more to his word than to any virtue of the unseen Image. Yet there were fierce hearts, too, who held secret rejoicing that his word might be proved false. Even in prayer, power struggles moved like rats beneath a pantry floor—quick, unseen, always hungry.

Slowly the tabernacle advanced; knees bent; the air thickened with silence. The priests and chaplains from L’Impruneta stirred no party passion in the onlookers. The procession was about to close with the Priors and the Gonfaloniere. That long train of companies and symbols—silent music that stirred the mind as a chorus stirs it—was passing out of sight, and now only a faint yearning hope wrestled with habitual despondency.

Romola’s heart had been swelling—half foreboding, half with the enthusiasm of fellowship that the last two years had made habitual to her, like the very consciousness of clothing to a vain woman. She gave a deep sigh, as if released from a long inward strain, and remained on her knees from sheer languor.

Then, sudden as a knife-flash between shutters, something bright-colored darted into view on the distant bridge.

In the same instant Romola sprang up, stretched her arms, and leaned from the window. The black drapery fell from her head; the gold of her hair caught the light; her face flushed as if an inner lamp had flared. A shout burst forth at once. The last troops of the procession paused. Faces swung as one toward the distant bridge.

The bridge was already behind the newcomer: a horseman drove at full gallop along the Lung’ Arno. His bay horse ran so hard the foam streaked its sides white; his cap flew loose by his red _becchetto_; and in his hand he waved an olive-branch.

A messenger—good tidings. The olive-branch spoke before any mouth could.

But the people would not wait for the message to arrive in orderly fashion. They surged to meet him, seized his rein, pressed in, jostling and trampling. Romola, peering down through the bright cloth and the crush of bodies, recognized the rider.

It was Tito—her husband—sent a few days earlier to Pisa on a private embassy.

The recognition did not brighten her eyes anew. Her first impulse of expectation had already spent itself; what ruled her now was the need to know whether the news meant bread—real bread, ground grain, ovens lit again—relief that could be portioned into meals.

“Good news!” voices cried up at him, hoarse with hope. “Best news!” “News to be paid with hose!”—_novelle da calze_—men shouted, as if the old jest about rewarding a messenger could conjure a full granary.

“Good news,” Tito returned vaguely, smiling into their faces as they tugged his horse forward, until he gained the spot at the meeting of the roads where the Gonfaloniere and Priors awaited him. There he checked the horse, bowed low, and spoke with clear ceremony:

“Magnificent Signori! I have to deliver to you the joyful news that the galleys from France, laden with corn and men, have arrived safely in the port of Leghorn, by favour of a strong wind, which kept the enemy’s fleet at a distance.”

The words seemed to quiver through the stones and up the walls, vibrating along the streets like sound in a vaulted church. A shout tore the air, rolled along the river, broke and rose again—one wave after another. The joy ran down the line of the procession toward the Duomo; and from farther off came answering cries, fainter but obedient, like the intermediate plash across a great lake when the whole water has taken one impulse.

For minutes there was no more speaking. The Signoria themselves lifted their caps and stood bare-headed, as if before something that had come from beyond their own contrivance—from that region people named “divine” when they could not bear to call it mere chance.

At last, when the signal was given for the procession’s end to move forward again, Tito added, smiling still, his tone graceful as if laying a garnish on the truth:

“I ought to say, that any hose to be bestowed by the Magnificent Signoria in reward of these tidings are due, not to me, but to another man who had ridden hard to bring them, and would have been here in my place if his horse had not broken down just before he reached Signa. Meo di Sasso will doubtless be here in an hour or two, and may all the more justly claim the glory of the messenger, because he has had the chief labour and has lost the chief delight.”

It was well phrased—the necessary statement offered as generosity. After a word of reply from the _Proposto_, the dignified extremity of the procession moved on. Tito turned his horse’s head to follow it. Already the great bell of the Palazzo Vecchio began to swing, giving the people’s joy a larger voice—metallic, insistent, filling the air with a sound that would seep into courtyards and kitchens, into rooms where women measured the last handful of meal and listened with a fear that had become habitual.

Now that his attention was no longer held by urgent ceremony, one might have expected him to look up and recognize Romola at the window. But he seemed occupied with his cap, which, as the eager crowd led his horse, he managed to seize and set straight, while his right hand remained encumbered with the olive-branch. He wore a becoming air of weariness after exertion.

Romola made no sign. She did not lean farther out, did not call, did not lift a hand to catch his glance. She drew her black drapery over her head again and sat perfectly still.

Yet she felt almost sure Tito had seen her. He had that power of seeing everything—of letting nothing escape him—without seeming to see it at all.
