Little Gerard was barely three months old when a messenger came spurring hot from Tergou, mud splattered up to his knees, to fetch Catherine home. "Go back at once," she told him shortly, wiping flour from her wrists. "Tell them I will not stir—they have Kate to tend to all their silly needs."

The man departed, and Catherine turned back to Margaret, her face softening as she rested a calloused hand on the young mother’s shoulder. "There, lass, I *must* go after all. The house is all at sixes and sevens, this is the third time they’ve sent begging me to come back, and if I delay till the morrow, my old man will trudge over himself and drag me home by the ear, with a sharp flea in it for good measure."

She rattled off every trick she had learned across forty years of tending fussy infants, warned Margaret of colic, of teething fevers, of the strange little ills that creep up on a babe unlooked for, and pressed a heavy purse of silver into her palm. Margaret thanked her but refused the money. Catherine insisted, then grew sharp with frustration when Margaret would not budge. Every excuse Margaret made was so gentle, so reasonable, that Catherine rejected them out of hand—they felt thin, unwomanly, to her no-nonsense mind.

"Come now, out with your real heart, when we’re parting like this, and mayhap we’ll never see one another’s faces again," she said.

"Oh mother, do not say that!" Margaret protested.

"Alack, lass, I’ve seen too many partings that never met again. It creeps into my head every time now. When I was your age, I never thought of such things—we all thought we’d live forever, back then. So out with it."

Margaret sighed. "Well then, I would not have told you otherwise. Your Cornelis looked at me the day you first brought me to Tergou, and said plain as day, ‘So you’ve come to share with us, eh mistress?’ I told him I would never do such a thing. But mother, I will take no grudged bounty from any household. Not unless I saw my child starving, and heaven alone knows what I might do then. Give me only your love—for that I prize above all silver, and they do not grudge me that, as far as I can tell. Not one stiver of your coin will I carry out of your house."

"You foolish lass," Catherine huffed. "If it were me, I’d take it just to spite him."

"No you would not," Margaret smiled. "We are apples off the very same tree."

Catherine yielded with good grace, but when the final moment of parting came, they clung to each other, tears running down both their faces.

No sooner had Catherine’s boots vanished down the lane than the baby began to wail, sharp and inconsolable. Margaret bounced him, sang to him, rocked him, and could not hush him, till she suspected a stray pin. She stripped off his swaddling and searched between his clothes and his soft skin, and found a fine gold angel coin digging into his backbone.

"There now, little Gerard," she laughed through her tears. "I thought granny gave in far too sudden." She wrapped the coin in a scrap of fine linen, tucked it at the very bottom of her strongbox, and told the wide-eyed baby that she could be just as stubborn and clever as his granny, when she chose.

When Catherine got back to Tergou, she told Eli all about Margaret’s stubborn pride, and how she had outwitted it by hiding the coin. Eli only nodded, and said Margaret was right, and Catherine was wrong. Catherine tossed her head, but Eli sat quiet for a long time, turning the thought over in his mind.

Margaret had no shortage of small domestic anxieties. She still had two old men to feed, and could no longer work the long hours at her ironing and illumination that she had before the baby came. It was all she could do to keep the house clean, tend to little Gerard, and cook hot meals for all three of them. But she had a little sum of coin laid by, and she would lean over the cradle every evening and whisper to her boy that his father would be home to help them long before that money ran out. With that bright hope, and the sweet treasure of her son in her arms, she spent several soft, happy months.

But time wore on, and Gerard did not come. Stranger still, there was no letter, no word, no rumor of him at all. Her mind grew heavy with disquiet. Contrary to her usual practical, steady nature, she would sink into long sad reveries, sighing silent to herself while she stitched or stirred the pot. All the while, her little store of coin was melting away, little by little. One day she opened her cupboard and found it bare, and looked across at the two old men sitting by her hearth. Her heart swelled with pity for them, and she leaned down to the baby in his cradle and asked him how she could ever let grandfather and poor old Martin go without a hot meal. She went to her strongbox and pulled out Catherine’s hidden gold angel, and a tear of gentle, mortified shame fell right on the bright face of the coin as she unfolded the linen.

She sent Martin out to change it for smaller coin. While he was gone, a French gentleman came to her door with one of the art dealers who had offered her a pittance for her illumination work some months prior. The Frenchman told her he was sent by his sovereign, the Duchess of Burgundy, to collect the very finest masterpieces of local craft for her private Book of Hours. Margaret showed him her two best small works, and he fell utterly in love with the tiny panel she had painted of wild strawberries, creeping clover, and little wood violets, the very piece Margaret Van Eyck herself had taught her to shade. He offered her a sum of gold so large she could scarce believe her ears.

"Do not mock my need, good stranger," she said, shaking her head. "There are three mouths in this house, and none but me to fill them."

"I do not mock you, fair mistress," he said warmly. "My princess gave me strict charge: ‘Seek out the best craftsfolk, do not drive hard bargains, make them content with you, and you content with them.’ I pay you what your work is worth, no less."

A moment later Margaret was on her knees, kissing little Gerard all over his soft face, showering the four new gold pieces over his swaddling chest again and again, chattering the whole story out to him in her broken, giddy Dutch. "Oh, what a good princess, isn’t she? We will pray for her, won’t we my little lambkin, when you are old enough to say your prayers?"

Just then Martin trundled in the door, furious. "They would not change that gold angel! I swear they think I stole it off some poor traveler."

"I am beholden to you for trying," Margaret said hastily, snatching the coin from his hand, wrapping it back in its linen scrap, and tucking it away in the bottom of her strongbox once more, safe and secret.

Before that unexpected windfall ran out, Margaret was back at her ironing and starching again. But right in the middle of her busy work, Martin fell sick. He lay ill for nine days, then died. Nearly all her new gold went to bury him properly, decently, no pauper’s grave for the old man who had sat by her hearth every day.

He was gone, and his empty wooden chair sat by the fireside, for he had always preferred the warmth of her hearth to the sun outdoors once the meddlesome old busybodies left them be. Margaret would not let anyone else sit in that chair. Every time she let her gaze linger on its empty seat, it cost her a hot, quiet tear.

Now there was no one to carry her heavy bundles of laundered linen home from the great houses across town. To do it herself, she would have to leave little Gerard in the care of a neighbor. But she would not trust her most precious treasure to any mortal, and she could not bear to be parted from him for hours at a stretch. So she put out word that she was looking for a boy to carry her laundry basket every Saturday and Monday.

A plump, fresh-cheeked youth named Luke Peterson turned up at her door, blushing so hard his ears were pink, twisting his wool bonnet between his fingers. He looked no older than fifteen, though he was already eighteen. "Would a man not serve your turn just as well as a boy?" he asked.

Before he spoke, Margaret had been thinking to herself that this boy would do perfectly. But she laughed, and took his cue. "Nay, a man might ask for more coin than I can afford to pay."

"Not I!" Luke said warmly. "Mistress Margaret, I’m your neighbor, and I make a fine wage at the coopering trade. I can carry your basket before or after my day’s work, and welcome. You don’t need to pay me anything at all. We aren’t strangers, you know."

"Why, Master Luke, I know your face, but I cannot recall that we ever exchanged a single word before today," Margaret said, puzzled.

"Oh but we did!" he said. "Don’t you remember? One day you were trying to carry your heavy baby and a full pail of water all at once, and I said ‘Give me the baby to carry.’ And you said ‘Nay, I’ll give you the pitcher, and keep my bairn myself.’ So I carried the full pail all the way back to your door, and you took it from me right here, and you said ‘I’m muckle obliged to you, young man,’ in that soft sweet voice you have, not sharp the way everyone else on this street speaks to a body."

"I do remember that now, Master Luke. And it was the very least I could say," Margaret smiled.

"If you will say as much every time I carry your basket, I won’t care how far I bear it, or how heavy it is," he said.

"Nay, nay," Margaret said, coloring faintly. "I will not impose on your good nature. You are young, and you are very kind. What say I give you your supper every Saturday night when you bring the linen home, and a warm dawn breakfast every Monday morning? Will that make us even, a little?"

"As you please," Luke mumbled, red as a cherry. "Only don’t go telling people I asked for two measly meals just to carry a basket."

With chubby, good-hearted Luke’s timely help, and the strength Heaven lent the hardworking young mother to balance all her many troubles, the household ran smooth for a little while. But Margaret’s heart grew heavier and heavier with every week of Gerard’s long, strange silence. Suspense, that cruel mental torturer, began to tear at her heart with its hot pincers, till she cried out, loud and vehement, "Oh that I could only know the worst!"

While she was in that state, one day she heard a heavy, unfamiliar step climbing the stairs. She started, trembling. That’s no step I know, she thought. Ill tidings.

The door opened, and Eli stood there, grave and gentle, an unexpected visitor. Margaret stared at him in silence, her agitation rising by the second.

"Girl," he said, "the skipper is home from Rome."

Margaret gasped, clutching the edge of the table. "One word. Is he alive?"

"Surely I hope so. No one has seen him dead."

"Then someone must have seen him alive!" she cried.

"No, girl. No one has seen him, dead or alive, these many past months in Rome. My daughter Kate thinks he has wandered off to some other city, far away. She bade me tell you that thought."

"Ay, like enough," Margaret said gloomily, staring over at the cradle. "Like enough. My poor babe."

The old man, his voice thin and tired, asked her for a morsel to eat—he had walked all the way from Tergou fasting. Margaret pitied him even through the fog of her own agitation, and she cooked him a hot meal, trembling the whole time, hardly knowing what she stirred. Before he left, he laid his hand soft on her head.

"Be he alive, or be he dead," he said, "I look on you as my own daughter. Is there nothing I can do for you today? Think hard, lass."

"Ay, old man," Margaret whispered. "Pray for him. And pray for me."

Eli sighed, and trundled slowly, heavily down the stairs. Margaret listened half-stupidly to his footsteps, till the last sound of them faded away. Then she sank down moaning beside the cradle, and drew little Gerard tight to her bosom.

"Oh my poor fatherless boy," she sobbed. "My fatherless boy."
