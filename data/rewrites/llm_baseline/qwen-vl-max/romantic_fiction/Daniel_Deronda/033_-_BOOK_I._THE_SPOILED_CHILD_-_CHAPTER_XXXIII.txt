“No man,” a Rabbi once declared, “may turn the bones of his father and mother into spoons”—a statement meant to stir moral reflection, not practical debate. The idea of such transformation was absurd, not because it lacked utility, but because it offended against something deeper: sentiment. And sentiment, though invisible in the market, holds sway over the heart. It does not command those who do not believe that emotions are among the truest riches of life.

Deronda, meanwhile, had turned from the polished streets of London to quieter corners—those parts where ordinary Jewish families lived their lives. He wandered through neighborhoods thick with shops, synagogues, and faces that spoke of hardship and endurance. He walked without purpose, yet with intent: to feel, to see, to know. Why did he not simply go to a respected Rabbi or community elder and ask about a woman named Cohen, her son Ezra, and a lost daughter called Mirah? He had considered it—but only after Christmas. For now, the weight of reality pressed upon him more than ever before.

Though Deronda often found beauty in the everyday, when personal longing stirred within him, he too felt the cold grip of actuality—the unyielding world that refuses to conform to our dreams. Enthusiasm thrives on ideals; it can endure garlic in medieval halls, admire ancient processions, even find nobility in suffering centuries past. But when ideals become flesh—when they walk beside us in plain clothes, with common features—they lose their glow. We may dream of finding Mirah’s family in Cordova, surrounded by scholars and poets under Moorish skies, but when we stand in St. Mary Axe, where shop signs are grim and faces weary, the romance fades. Yet, paradoxically, the deepest poetry lies not in escaping reality, but in embracing it—seeing the divine in the mundane, the sacred in the squalid.

And so Deronda walked, not expecting much, but hoping for a glimpse of truth. He saw each Jew as a possible echo of Mirah—her mother, perhaps, her brother. He carried with him a quiet dread: what if the woman he found were not noble, not kind, not worthy of Mirah’s memory? And worse—what if the same collision awaited *him*? What if his own origins, hidden beneath layers of silence, held secrets just as painful?

He did not long to meet anyone in particular. When he passed a shop and glanced at the name above the door, he breathed easier when it wasn’t “Ezra Cohen.” In fact, he secretly hoped Ezra Cohen did *not* run a shop. Wishes, people say, carry weight. If you wish hard enough against something, fate may grant it anyway. Or if you desire it, it will never come. Such superstition amused Deronda, but he could not deny the strange pull of hope.

One morning, tired of Holborn’s noise, he stepped into a narrow side street. A hansom cab approached, and he paused to hail it—then stopped short. In the window of a small shop, gleaming in silver, were ornate clasps, finely chased. He thought of Lady Mallinger—how she might love them turned into a bracelet. Then he looked further. The shop was a pawnbroker’s, specializing in jewelry, lace, and other curious things labeled _bric-à-brac_. A sign read: *Watches and Jewelry Exchanged and Repaired.*

Inside, a young man appeared at the door, smiling warmly. “Good day, sir.”

Deronda returned the greeting, but already he was turning away—unwilling to be drawn into sales talk. Across the street, he beckoned the cabman. From there, he saw the name: **Ezra Cohen**.

It could be any one of a hundred Ezra Cohens. But this one was young—about thirty—and stood exactly the age Mirah’s brother might have been when she was still a child. Still, Deronda told himself firmly: no evidence existed. Even if this man *were* Mirah’s brother, and even if the mother were dead, it was not *his* duty to reveal it. Was it fair to impose his own sense of justice on another’s life?

But then came the thought: Mirah would want to know. She would need to know whether her mother lived or died, whether her brother was alive. How could he decide her future based on his own feelings? Wasn’t this the very thing he resented in his own upbringing—the absence of clear truth, the burden of hidden truths that shaped a life without consent?

For now, he decided, he had not discovered anything real. He would return—not to confront, but to learn. He intended to buy the clasps for Lady Mallinger and use the visit as an excuse to observe.

But Sir Hugo delayed him. The baronet, preparing a speech on a controversial legal matter, insisted Deronda help him research. Every day, they argued—passionately, evenly matched. Sir Hugo admired Deronda’s clarity, though he disagreed with him.

“Confound it, Dan! Why don’t you speak up in public? You’re wrong, I know. You won’t win. But you’ve got the power—the heavy artillery of the country is against you. That’s *exactly* why you should step forward. When I was your age, I’d have taken it. It would throw you into relief. Be in opposition to me here and there—it would make you stand out. Seize an opportunity like this, and you could be in Parliament in no time. And you know that would please me.”

“I’m sorry not to do what pleases you, sir,” said Deronda. “But I can’t see politics as a profession.”

“Why not? If you’re not born into public life, you must make your way into it. The country’s business must go on. And it won’t if everyone treats politics like prophecy, demanding divine calling. You can’t wait for a call from heaven or your constituents.”

“I don’t want to live off opinions—especially borrowed ones. Not that I blame others. Many better men than I climb platforms and praise themselves. They give their word of honor to a party.”

“Then let me tell you, Dan—a man who rejects all humbug is a three-cornered, impractical fellow. There’s bad humbug, yes. But there’s good humbug too—the kind that oils the wheels of progress. To rule men, you must rule them through their beliefs. I agree with the Archbishop of Naples who led a procession of St. Januarius during a plague. You can’t fight popular ignorance with orders. Action needs a little acting.”

“One may yield to necessity,” said Deronda. “But there’s a difference between saying, ‘In this case, I must wear foolscap and grin,’ and buying foolscap and practicing grinning. I believe true public service must keep an ideal in sight. Otherwise, success becomes confusion. If I entered politics, I might mistake my fame for justice.”

After this exchange—jarring, yet thoughtful—Deronda set out again for Ezra Cohen’s shop.

This time, he entered from the opposite end of the street. As he walked, he hesitated. The questions that haunted him now were not political, but personal: how far could he justify concealing kinship? Was it wise—or moral—to hide a truth that might bring comfort to Mirah?

He paused before a second-hand bookshop, its window filled with books from Homer to railway novels. Among them lay the autobiography of Salomon Maimon, a Polish Jew whose life had stirred many minds. Deronda took it, intending to pay inside.

Instead of the usual dusty clerk, he saw a man seated on a stool, reading the *Times*. His face was startling—pale, yellowish, almost carved from ivory. His features were sharp, not large, but deeply expressive. His eyes held a distant, searching gaze. It was a face that seemed to belong to another time—perhaps to a prophet in exile, or a poet of old Hebrew days. The man looked worn, as if years of struggle had left their mark, not on the body alone, but on the soul.

When he lowered the paper and met Deronda’s eyes, the stranger’s expression shifted—suddenly alert, questioning.

“You are perhaps of our race?” he whispered, gripping Deronda’s arm tightly.

Deronda flushed. He shook his head gently. “No.”

The hand released. The fire in the man’s eyes dimmed. He retreated into silence, offering the book with distant politeness. “Half-a-crown, sir. Mr. Ram will accept it.”

Deronda paid and left, feeling oddly humbled—as if some high authority had dismissed him.

He entered Ezra Cohen’s shop, where the young man greeted him with a smile. “Mother! Mother!” he called. “Coming, sir—coming directly.”

A woman in her fifties emerged—plain, yes, but not repulsive. Her hair was neatly done, her earrings large, her presence warm. She smiled at Deronda. “How can I serve you, sir?”

“I’d like to see the silver clasps in the window—the larger ones.”

She reached for them, but her son interrupted. “I’ll get them, mother—I’ll reach ’em.” He handed them to Deronda with a grin. “Mother’s too proud. She wants to do everything herself. That’s why I called her. When there’s a gentleman customer, I daren’t do otherwise.”

Deronda examined the clasps carefully.

“They’re only three guineas, sir,” said the mother.

“First-rate workmanship,” added the son. “Worth twice the price. I got them cheap from Cologne.”

At that moment, two new customers arrived. “Addy!” called the son.

From the back came a young woman in a bright blue dress, coral jewelry, her hair piled high. She carried a baby with dark curls, which she placed on the counter. The child stared around with unnerving intelligence. Beside her were a six-year-old boy and a younger girl—both with black eyes, black hair, faces more Semitic than their parents’. The boy, Jacob Alexander Cohen, marched forward, hands in pockets, and studied Deronda with a boldness that surprised him.

“What’s your name, sirrah?” Deronda asked.

“Jacob Alexander Cohen,” he replied, proudly. “Named after my grandfather—he sells knives and razors and scissors. He gave me this knife.” With quick fingers, he opened two blades and a corkscrew.

“Is that safe?” Deronda asked the grandmother.

“He’ll never hurt himself, bless you!” she said, watching him with pride.

“Have *you* got a knife?” Jacob asked, stepping closer.

“Yes,” said Deronda, pulling out a small penknife. “Do you want to see it?”

The boy leaned in, his voice hoarse with the weight of generations—already sounding like a merchant grown old in trade.

Deronda stood there, holding a penknife, facing a child who knew nothing of the world beyond his shop, yet carried within him the echoes of a people whose history was written in silence and survival.

And somewhere, in the quiet hum of the shop, a question stirred—no longer just about Mirah, but about identity, belonging, and the cost of truth.
