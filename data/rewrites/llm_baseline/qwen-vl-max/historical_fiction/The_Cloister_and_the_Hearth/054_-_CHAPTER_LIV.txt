When she came to herself, her head rested on Catherine’s arm, and the honest half of the family—those she had entered like an intruder—gathered around her, speaking words of comfort in their rough, homely way. Giles roared at her, “Don’t take on like that! Gerard is alive and well—or he couldn’t have written this letter, the biggest man ever penned, and,” as he thought, “the most beautiful, moving, and smallest-written letter yet!”

“Ay, good Master Giles,” Margaret sighed faintly, “he is alive. But how can I know what has befallen him since? Oh, why did he leave Holland to wander among strangers fierce as lions? And why didn’t I drive him from me sooner than part him from his own flesh and blood? Forgive me, you who are his mother!”

She gently withdrew from Catherine’s arm and made a feeble attempt to slide off the chair onto her knees. After a brief struggle against stronger hands, she found herself cradled once more in Catherine’s embrace. Then, with trembling fingers, she handed the letter to Eli and whispered, soft but sweet, “I will trust it now from my hand. In truth, I am little fit to read further—and loth to leave my comfort.” She wound her other arm tightly around Catherine’s neck.

“Read it, Richart,” said Eli. “Thine eyes are younger than mine.”

Richart took the letter. “Well,” he said, “such writing I never saw. He writes with a needle’s point—and clear into the bargain. Why isn’t he in my counting-house in Amsterdam instead of wandering about yonder?”

“When I came to myself,” the letter began, “I was seated in the litter, my good merchant holding my hand. I babbled things I don’t remember, then shuddered in silence. He put a horn of wine to my lips.”

Catherine murmured, “Bless him! bless him!”

Eli hushed her: “Whisht!”

“And I told him what had happened. He wanted to see my leg. It was badly sprained, swollen at the ankle; all my points were broken—I could barely keep up my hose. I said, ‘Sir, I shall be a burden to you, I fear, and can make no harmony now; my poor psaltery is broken.’ And I grieved over my broken music, my companion through so many weary leagues. But he patted my cheek and told me not to fret. He raised my leg upon a pillow and tended me like a kind father.”

“January 19.—I sit all day in the litter, for we press forward with haste. At night, the good, kind merchant sends me to bed and will not let me work. Strange! Whenever I fall in with men like fiends, God sends me a good man or woman right after, lest I turn away from humanity. Oh, Margaret! How strangely mixed they are, and how old I feel compared to who I was three months ago. And lo! If good Master Fugger hasn’t gone and bought me a new psaltery!”

Catherine interrupted, “Eli, my man, if that merchant comes our way, let us buy a hundred ells of cloth from him—no haggling!”

Eli nodded firmly. “That I will, take your oath on it!”

As Richart prepared to read on, Kate glanced at her mother, blushed faintly, and pulled a piece of embroidery from beneath her apron. With her head lowered just a little more than necessary, she began to sew. Her mother, seeing this, drew out a similar piece from her pocket and joined her. Both women now revealed their secret works—baby caps, more than half finished. The sight told its own story. Horror! They were shaped and delicately stitched like tiny monks’ cowls.

“January 20.—Laid up in the litter, nearly blind, but halting to bait. Suddenly, the Lombardy plains burst upon me. Oh, Margaret! A land flowing with milk and honey—rolling slopes, noble rivers, cheerful meadows, delightful orchards, blooming gardens. Though winter, it feels warmer than beloved Holland in midsummer. It makes the wanderer’s face shine and his heart leap with joy at such kindness from the earth. Here grow vines, cedars, olives, and plenty of cattle—but only three goats for every sheep. The draught oxen wear white linen around their necks, and standing beside dark green olive trees, each one is a picture. The people, especially the women, wear delicate straw hats adorned with flowers and leaves imitated in silk, threaded with silver. Today we crossed a river in a chained ferry-boat. On either bank stood a windlass, and a single man turned it to draw our whole company across. I admired it, being a stranger. We passed some country folk. An old woman looked at a young girl, then covered her face with her hand and held out her crucifix like a knight brandishing his sword in tourney, fearing the evil eye.”

“January 25.—Safe in Venice. A place whose strange and surpassing beauty you know from mariners’ tales. Do you remember how Peter used to fill our ears with stories while we sat beneath the table, always talking of this sea-throned, peerless city—shaped like a bow, its great canal and palaces built on piles, its watery ways thronged with scores of gilded boats; and that marketplace of nations, orbis, non urbis, forum, St. Mark’s Square, his place? And his statue with priceless jewels in his eyes, and the lion at his gate? But I, lying in pain by my window, cannot yet see any of these wonders—only a dull street, fairly paved, and houses with oiled paper and linen instead of glass, which seems rude; and passers-by whose habits and gestures strike me as excessive. So, not to miss my daily comfort of whispering to you, I will turn inward and bind my sheaves of wisdom gathered by travel. For I love you so deeply that no treasure pleases me unless shared with you—and what treasure is better or lasting than knowledge? Thus I have learned, Sir Footsore, that every nation has its own wisdom and its own folly. I think, if a great king or duke walked as I have, seeing with his own eyes, he might gather the flowers and avoid the weeds of each people, and return home to set his own folk upon Wisdom’s hill.

“The Germans in the north are churlish, yet frank and honest; in the south, kindly and honest too. Their chief fault is drunkenness, carried even to contempt for sober men. They say, ‘If you can’t drink and eat, you can’t serve any master well.’ In England, the common folk drink deeply, but the respectable hold excess in disrepute. They drink a health or two out of courtesy, not gluttony, and still sweeten their wine. Their drinking is solemn—little mirth, little talk—just business, crying ‘Be joyful!’ Their best drinking game is ‘Kurlemurlehuff,’ where one touches glass, beard, table in turn, interlacing whistles and finger-snaps in such intricate order it takes Hercules to master it, yet delights the watcher. Their drunkards, by advice of German physicians, sleep with pebbles in their mouths—for just as a boiling pot must have its lid slightly open, so must these fleshy wine-pots vent their inner heat. Despite this, many die suddenly from drink; yet it’s a matter of religion to cover it up, excuse it, blame some innocent illness. Still, it’s more custom than nature, for their women join the drinkers, but only stand a moment and seem to kiss the cup—they are truly temperate in food and drink, modest and virtuous, faithful wives and friends to their husbands—far above our Holland lasses, who, when maids, ask the men the question, and when married, lord it over them. Take the wife in Tergou, near our door. One came to the house seeking her husband. She said, ‘You won’t find him—he asked my leave to go out this afternoon, and I gave it.’”

Catherine exclaimed, “True enough! True enough! That was Beck Hulse, Jonah’s wife. This is what comes of a woman marrying a boy!”

“In the south, where wine flows, the nobility drink themselves bare; not so in the north—there, beer may burst a noble’s body before melting his lands. They are quarrelsome, but it’s the liquor, not the mind; for they’re not revengeful. If they make a bad bargain drunk, they honor it when sober. They keep their windows bright and judge a man by his clothes. Whatever fruit or grain grows by the roadside, pick and eat it—the owner will say, ‘Welcome, honest man.’ But pluck a grape from the roadside, and your life is in danger—it’s eating what Heaven gave to be drunk. The French speak much fairer, though not nearly so true-hearted. Sweet words cost them nothing. They call it *payer en blanche*.”

Denys laughed. “Les coquins! Ha! ha!”

“Yet courtesy is in their hearts, yes, in their very blood. They often say, ‘Give yourself the trouble of sitting down.’ Such phrases show the direction of the wind. Also, at a public spectacle, if you wish to leave your seat without losing it, tie your napkin round the bench—no Frenchman or woman will sit there, preferring to keep it for you.”

Catherine declared, “Gramercy! That is manners. France for me!”

Denys rose and placed his hand gracefully over his breastplate.

“Yet they say things in jest that are neither courteous nor pleasant—‘Le diable t'emporte!’ ‘Allez au diable!’ and so forth. But I doubt they mean no harm—likely just habit. Moderate in drinking, they mix water with wine and sing and dance over their cups, making enchanting company. They are careful not to drink from another’s cup. In war, the English gain the upper hand in the field; but the French excel in siegecraft—witness Orleans, where they besieged their besiegers and punished them cruelly with double and triple culverines; and many other sieges this century. Above all nations, they flatter their women—and yet despise them. No. She may rule them. They often hang female criminals instead of drowning them decently, as others do. Furniture in their inns is walnut—only deal in Germany. French windows are ill-designed: the lower half is wood, opens; the upper half is glass, fixed—so servants cannot clean it. German windows are all glass, movable, shining far and wide like diamonds. In France, many humble homes are unglazed. Once I saw a Frenchman pass a church without uncovering his head—a thing I never witnessed in Holland, Germany, or Italy. At many inns, they show travelers their sheets to prove they are clean, and warm them at the fire before them—a commendable custom. They receive guests kindly, like hosts; yet mostly cheat them, sometimes even cut their throats. They plead harsh and tyrannical laws as excuse. And indeed, their law thrusts its nose into every platter, its finger into every pie. In France, gentlemen wear hats and furs indoors, and go abroad lightly clad. In Germany, they don hat and fur cloak to go out, but sit bareheaded and light-clad by the stove.

“The French do not mix men and women in assemblies, as we Hollanders do. Around their preachers, women kneel in rows, men stand behind. Their harvests are rye, flax, and wine. Three mules for every horse, and whole flocks of sheep as black as coal.

“In Germany, snails are red. I lie not. The French buy minstrels, but breed jests and make their own mirth. The Germans foster fools with ear-caps, who mimic madness to provoke laughter—a sorrow that calls for pity, not amusement. In this, I believe the lighter nation wiser than the grave German. What say you? Alas, you cannot answer me now.”

Denys leaned forward. “My foul mouth! Is that so written, Master Richart?”

Richart nodded. “Ay, in truth—see for yourself.”

Denys inspected the letter gravely. “I read it quite differently.”

“How so?” asked Richart.

“Humph! Ahem—why, just the opposite.” He added, “It’s tricky work reading these black scratches men agree to call words. And I suspect you clerks go by guess, worthy sir. My foul mouth! This is the first time I’ve heard it mentioned. Eh, mesdames?”

But the women did not seize the chance. Margaret blushed and said nothing. The others bent silently over their work, each with a smile almost hidden. Denys studied their faces long and carefully. Satisfied, he turned with a tone of injured, patient innocence and said, “Proceed, Master Richart.”

“The Italians are polished and subtle. They judge a man not by his clothes, but by his speech and gesture. Here, Sir Chough cannot pass for a falcon gentle, as I did in Germany, pranked in my noble servant’s feathers. Wisest of all nations in their remarkable temperance with food and drink. Most foolish to search strangers entering their borders and hinder them from bringing in money. They should invite it, like other nations, and let travelers take it out freely. Also, here in Venice, the ladies turn their black hair yellow by sun and art—to be wiser than Him who made them. You enter no Italian town without a bill of health, though there is no plague in Europe. This fuss is for extortion. Innkeepers cringe, fawn, cheat, and in the countryside, murder you. Yet they give clean sheets—if you pay. Delicate in eating, they abhor putting their hands in the dish; rather, they use a crust or something else. There’s even a tale of a cardinal in Rome who arms his guest’s left hand with a small bifurcated dagger to hold meat, while his knife cuts it. But I think this, too, is to be wiser than Him who made the hand so supple and strong.”

Eli nodded. “I’m with you, my lad.”

“They suffer greatly from the itch. Ointment for it—*unguento per la rogna*—is sold at every corner of Venice. From my window, I saw a urchin sell it to three noblewomen in silken trains and two velvet knights.”

Catherine remarked, “Italy, my dear, I advise you wash your body in the tub on Sundays; then you may put your hand in the plate on Thursday without offense.”

“Their bread is lovely white. Their meats they ruin by sprinkling cheese over them—O, perversity! Their salt is black—without a lie. In commerce, these Venetians are masters of land and sea, governing their territories wisely. Only one flaw I find—the same a learned friar once cast at Plato’s republic: that here women are encouraged to venal frailty and pay a tax to the State, which, not content with silk and spice and other honest goods, must trade in sin. Twenty thousand such Jezebels live in Venice and Candia, pampered and honored for drawing strangers to the city, many in princely palaces of their own. But here, I think, the wise signors of Venice forget King David’s word: ‘Except the Lord keep the city, the watchman wakes in vain.’ Also, in religion, they hang their cloth according to the wind—now with the Pope, now with the Turk—but always with the god of traders, Mammon. Can such a flower, cankered as it is, bloom forever? But since I speak of flowers, none may deny them their skill in making roses and gilliflowers bloom out of season. In summer, they nip certain budding roses and withhold water. Then in winter, they dig around these discouraged plants and insert cloves, and with great art raise sweet-scented roses, bringing them to market in January. And I first learned this art from a cow. She grazed buds in summer, and they sprouted at Yule. Women have sat in doctors’ chairs at their colleges. But the one who sat in St. Peter’s was a German. Italy also excels in artful fountains and figures moved by water, enacting life. Next to it stands Augsburg, where they harness the foul knave Smoke to good Sir Spit, and he turns stout Master Roast. But lest any place boast, two towns in Europe scorn giddy fountains and bring water tame through pipes to every citizen’s door, where he fills his vessels by turning a cock. One is London, watered for many years by pipes from Paddington, a neighboring city; the other, the fair town of Lubeck. Also, the fierce English are reported wise in driving wolves from their lands—fairly banishing those marauders into the mountains. But nowhere—in France, Germany, or Italy—is a traveler safe after sundown. I hear of no glazed house in all Venice—only oiled linen and paper; behind these barbarian eyelets, wooden shutters. Their name for a cowardly assassin is ‘a brave man,’ and for a harlot, ‘a courteous person’—as if a woman’s worst vice and a man’s worst vice were virtues. But I pray God for little Holland that an assassin may still be called an assassin, and a harlot a harlot, until Doomsday—and then let others gloss foul faults with silken names if they can!”

Eli sighed. “He should have been a priest, save your presence, my poor lass.”

“January 26.—Sweetheart, I must be brief, and tell you only part of what I’ve seen, for today my journal ends. Tonight it sails to you, but I, unhappy, not with it—tomorrow, in another ship, to Rome.

“Dear Margaret, I took a hand-litter and was carried to St. Mark’s Church. Outside, toward the marketplace, stands a noble gallery, and above it four famous horses, cast in brass by ancient Romans, seeming all in motion, ready to leap down upon the beholder at the next step. Around the church are six hundred pillars of marble, porphyry, and ophites. Inside lies a treasure greater than at St. Denis, Loretto, or Toledo. Here, a jeweled pitcher given the seigniory by a Persian king; the ducal cap blazing with jewels, crowned with a diamond and a chrysolite, each as big as an almond; two golden crowns and twelve golden stomachers studded with gems from Constantinople; a monstrous sapphire; a great diamond from a French king; a prodigious carbuncle; three unicorns’ horns. But what are these compared to the sacred relics?

“Dear Margaret, I stood and saw the brazen chest that holds the body of St. Mark the Evangelist. I saw with my own eyes and touched his ring, and his gospel written by his own hand—and all my travels seemed light. For who am I that I should see such things? Dear Margaret, his sacred body was first brought from Alexandria by merchants in 810, but not valued then. Between 829, when this church was built, and 1094, the very place where it lay was forgotten. Then holy priests fasted and prayed many days seeking light—and lo! The Evangelist’s body broke through the marble at midnight and stood before them. They fell to the earth; but in the morning, they found the crevice it had burst through, and peering within, saw him lying. Then they took and laid him in his chest beneath the altar, and carefully replaced the stone with its miraculous crack—which crack I saw, and it shall remain a monument while the world lasts. Afterward, they showed me the Virgin’s chair—of stone—and her picture, painted by St. Luke, very dark, features now scarcely visible. This image, during droughts, they carry in procession and it brings rain. I wish I had not seen it. Item: two pieces of marble stained with John the Baptist’s blood; item: a fragment of the true cross and of the pillar to which Christ was bound; item: the rock struck by Moses, still wet to this hour; also a stone Christ sat upon preaching at Tyre—but some say it is the one Jacob laid his head upon, and I agree, for our Lord never preached at Tyre. Leaving the church, they showed me the state nursery for children of those aphrodisian ladies, their favorites. In the outer wall was a broad niche—should the child be small enough to squeeze through alive, it falls into a net inside, and the state takes charge; but if too large, the mother must take it home again, where it is likely to be *mali corvi mali ovum*. Coming out, we met them carrying in a corpse, feet and face bare. I learned then this is Venetian custom—and surely no other town will ever rob them of it, nor of what follows. On a great porphyry slab in the piazza stood three ghastly heads, rotting and fouling the air, dangerous in their hot summers, likely to spawn a plague. These were traitors to the state, and a heavy price—two thousand ducats—was set on each head. Their kin killed them and brought all three to the slab, selling blood and faith alike. No state buys heads so many, nor pays so high a price for that sorry merchandise. But what I most admired was the alabaster gallows across from the Duke’s palace, erected expressly to hang him alone for the least treason to the state—and there it stands in his sight, whispering *memento mori*. I pondered, and owned these signors my masters, who allow no man—not even their sovereign—to rise above the common weal. Nearby, on a wall, workmen were finishing, by order of the seigniory, a stone effigy of a tragic and enormous act from last year—yet it looks innocent on the wall. Here two gentlefolk whisper together, swords at their sides. Four brothers conspired on either side to poison the other two, to divide their land in half instead of quarters. At a mutual banquet, two drugged the wine, two poisoned a marchpane—so effectively that the same afternoon, four ‘brave men’ lay around one table, writhing in agony, cursing each other and themselves, and perished miserably. The land, for which they lost their immortal souls, passed to another family. And why not? It could not go to a worse.

“But O, sovereign wisdom of proverbs! How truly they point to each nation’s fault.

“Quand Italie sera sans poison
Et France sans trahison
Et l'Angleterre sans guerre,
Lors sera le monde sans terre.”

Richart explained the verse to Catherine, then continued reading aloud.
