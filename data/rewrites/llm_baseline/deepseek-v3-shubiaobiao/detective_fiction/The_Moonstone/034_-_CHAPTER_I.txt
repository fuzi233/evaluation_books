When Miss Clack set down her pen, it falls to me, Mr. Bruff, to take up the tale. My part in these events is not without significance, for there are shadows I alone can illuminate—secrets which, until now, have remained hidden.

First, consider the matter of Miss Rachel Verinder’s broken engagement. The truth of it lies in my hands. And as for Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite’s sudden withdrawal of his suit? That, too, is a riddle I have unraveled.

Second, fate—whether kind or cruel, I cannot say—entangled me in the affair of the Moonstone. I stood face-to-face in my office with an Oriental gentleman of imposing demeanor, none other than the chief of those three Indians who have haunted this business. The following day, I crossed paths with Mr. Murthwaite, the renowned traveler, and our discourse upon the Diamond cast a long shadow over all that followed. These are my credentials.

But let us begin where the story does: at the deathbed of my late friend and client, Sir John Verinder.

Sir John, for all his virtues, possessed a most human reluctance to confront his own mortality—at least, while in good health. The making of his will was a duty he deferred until illness finally compelled him. Lady Verinder and I pressed upon him the necessity; he conceded the point, yet took no action until his final hours. Then, at last, I was summoned to his bedside.

I found him drowsing, but he roused himself when I entered.

"Ah, Mr. Bruff," said he. "Let us be brief. Then I may sleep again."

Pen, ink, and paper in hand, I waited.

"I leave everything to my wife," he declared. "That is all." And with that, he turned away as if to dismiss the matter entirely.

I was forced to disturb him.

"To be clear," I asked, "you bequeath the entirety of your estate—land, investments, all of it—unconditionally to Lady Verinder?"

"Yes," said Sir John, impatient. "Must you make it so tedious? Everything to my wife. There is my Will."

Had this been any other client, I might have urged reconsideration. But Lady Verinder was no ordinary woman. She was not only trustworthy—as all good wives ought to be—but possessed the rare competence to manage such a trust. The will was drawn, executed, and Sir John returned to his rest.

In widowhood, Lady Verinder proved herself worthy of his faith. She consulted me at once regarding her own will, displaying such sound judgment that my role was merely to frame her wishes in legal terms. Her daughter’s future was secured with wisdom and affection.

Years passed before that will was disturbed. Then, in the summer of '48, came grim news: Lady Verinder was under a death sentence. She wished to review her will once more.

The provisions for Rachel remained untouched, but minor legacies required adjustment. To avoid confusion, I drafted a second will—identical in substance to the first, save for the appointment of a guardian under my advisement. Miss Clack, ever officious, witnessed its execution.

Three weeks later, an anomaly arose. My proctor informed me, with some intrigue, that Lady Verinder’s will had already been examined at Doctors’ Commons—by none other than Mr. Smalley, of Skipp and Smalley.

This was peculiar indeed. The will was uncontestable. Who, then, had an interest in scrutinizing it?

A firm such as Skipp and Smalley held little power over me. My clerk was Smalley’s brother, and their firm had long subsisted on the crumbs of my practice. A pointed inquiry soon revealed the truth: the client behind this curiosity was Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite.

That name told me all I needed to know.

Now, let me lay bare the contents of Lady Verinder’s will as plainly as I knew them myself. Rachel’s inheritance was a life interest only—no more. The principal remained beyond her reach, or that of any husband. She would have homes, an income, but no power to raise funds against the estate.

This discovery left me in a quandary. Rachel, whom I held in the highest esteem, was on the brink of marriage to a man I had always suspected of mercenary motives. Now, the truth was clear: Godfrey sought her for her money. But if that money was locked away, would he still want her?

I resolved to see Rachel at once.

At Brighton, I found her listless, resigned.

"You may speak of my engagement," she said, when I broached the subject, "if you have nothing better to discuss."

"Tell me, Miss Rachel," I ventured, "is your heart truly in this match?"

"I marry out of despair," she replied. "A stagnant happiness is all I hope for."

Such words hinted at deeper sorrows, but I pressed on.

"And Mr. Ablewhite? Is his heart as indifferent as yours?"

"He claims it is not. I suppose I must believe him."

The cruelty of my task struck me then. How could I tell her that the man she meant to marry cared only for her fortune? Yet tell her I must.

When I did, she walked in silence, her grip on my arm tightening, her face pale. At last, she spoke.

"Contradict any rumors of my marriage," she said. "It will not proceed."

"But have you considered the difficulty of withdrawing?" I asked. "Is there no one you might consult?"

"No one," she said.

The loneliness in her voice moved me deeply. I advised her to confront Godfrey with the truth, to force his hand. But Rachel, proud even in humiliation, refused.

"I believed in him," she said. "To call him a deceiver to his face would be to degrade myself. I will not do it."

Her resolve was unshakable. She would end the engagement, but without accusation.

Before I could receive word from her, old Mr. Ablewhite came to me in a fury, demanding explanation. His son, he said, had been dismissed—and had accepted it without protest.

The reason was plain: Godfrey needed money, and quickly. Rachel’s inheritance could not serve his purpose. And so, he let her go.

As for Mr. Ablewhite senior, his temper foretold ill for Rachel. But that, as they say, is another chapter.
