Following the serendipitous arrival of the olive-bearing courier—a favor unbidden by fate—Tito's agenda shifted toward matters of deliberate design. Pausing before the Palazzo Vecchio, he waited for the return of the *Dieci*, the board managing external and military affairs, to formally present the fruits of his clandestine journey to Pisa. This mission served as a prologue to an open embassy led by Bernardo Rucellai, aimed at securing a peaceful accord between Florence, Emperor Maximilian, and the League.

Tito's aptitude for diplomacy had been thoroughly vetted. As he delivered his findings with exhaustive detail and precise articulation, Bernardo del Nero, then a member of the *Ten*, could not suppress his admiration. Yet, beneath this professional courtesy, Bernardo's ancient hostility toward Tito had resurfaced and deepened following the sale of the library. Though Romola had remained silent regarding the transaction, leaving Bernardo to interpret her quietude as a command not to broach the subject, the elder man was certain that violating her father's wishes had inflicted a profound, blighting sorrow upon her. Observant as ever, Bernardo detected further signs that her marriage lacked happiness.

"Ah," he murmured internally, "this undoubtedly explains her turn toward Fra Girolamo and the *Piagnoni*—a shift I never anticipated from her nature. Women, unhappy and barren, inevitably drift into either madness or a religion so straitened they bear the weight of heaven alone on their shoulders. And as for my poor child Romola, as I often warned, the excessive cramming of Latin and Greek has left her scarcely more than a woman who spends her days pricking her fingers with needles. As for her husband, a tool with a smooth handle, employed wherever needed, I pray that the Tornabuonis and their ilk do not find their fingers severed. Well, well, *solco torto, sacco dritto*—many a full sack comes from a crooked furrow; and he who commands only honest men shall receive scant wages."

Holding firmly to the conviction that the moral qualities of political agents could not be sifted, Bernardo abstained from any overt interference against Tito. Beyond the sacred privacy owed to Romola, Bernardo possessed no direct accusation save Tito's Greek origin and his personal dislike of the man. The duplicity of feigning allegiance to the popular government while harboring Medicean sympathies was common to Tito and half the Medicean faction alike, though Tito practiced it with superior skill. Thus, Bernardo maintained a cold reserve, which Tito returned with scrupulous, distant respect. To the citizens of Florence, any service rendered to Romola's husband was viewed as an acceptable tribute to her godfather.

Once discharged at the Old Palace, Tito felt the day's official duties complete. Exhausted and scorched by the long ride, he chose not to return home immediately. There lingered in his satchel (*scarsella*) and in his mind certain burdens he wished to shed, yet he must do so with such guile that his intentions appeared accidental rather than sought. Walking from the palace with an air of languid indifference, he drifted toward the Piazza del Duomo. Although the procession had concluded, the bells still rang, and the populace moved restlessly through the streets, craving a more tangible outlet for their jubilation. While the new discipline, proclaiming Christ the special King of the Florentines and mandating Christian pleasures, sought to curb excess, a secret, vibrant hunger persisted in many youths for the vigorous catharsis of stone-throwing.

As Tito traversed the square, he could not evade recognition as the bearer of the olive branch. To dispel the inevitable ovation, particularly the barrage of eager inquiries, he adeptly deflected attention by announcing that Meo di Sasso, the true messenger from Leghorn, was imminent and could surely be found near the Porta San Frediano. He offered far more knowledge than he actually possessed.

Liberated from these importunities, Tito entered the Piazza del Duomo, casting wide, careless glances around the space while secretly scanning for a presence that might offer a desired opportunity. The termination of the procession at the Duomo promised an unusual concentration of loungers and conversationalists near Nello's shop, and as he had hoped, the crowd was thick. Near the north gates of the Baptistery, a group leaned against the iron rails. Spotting precisely what he sought, Tito affected an even greater indifference, pretending to recognize the tallest figure in the group only by chance as he passed, tossing the end of his *becchetto* over his left shoulder with a casual flick.

Yet the tall, broad-shouldered individual thus greeted looked like a man of considerable consequence. He wore a richly embroidered tunic, displaying a lavish amount of linen in the latest French fashion, and at his belt hung a sword and poniard of exquisite craftsmanship. His hat, adorned with a red plume, seemed a defiant protest against the somber gravity imposed upon Florentine dress by the *Piagnoni*. Indefinable traces of youth accentuated the coarseness of his face and the substantial diameter of his waist. His eyes held a rude, desecrating stare toward all men and things, an offense to a refined mind akin to a foul odor or a glaring light.

He and his companions, likewise young men clad in expensive attire and bearing arms, exchanged jokes with an ostentatious laughter designed to prove that their mirth remained unassailed despite any underlying suspicions. There were ample grounds for such suspicion; this broad-shouldered man with the red feather was Dolfo Spini, leader of the *Compagnacci*, or Evil Companions. These were the dissolute young men of the old aristocracy, enemies of the Mediceans and the popular government, but above all, bitter foes of Savonarola. Dolfo Spini, heir to the great house with the loggia over the Santa Trinita bridge, had organized these youths into an armed band, swearing themselves champions of extravagant suppers and the sinful pleasures of the flesh against the reforming pietists who threatened to render the world so chaste and temperate that existence itself would become unbearable, save for the misery of the alternative. Earlier that morning, he had loudly proclaimed that Florence faced famine and ruin solely due to its blind adherence to the Frate's counsel, asserting that salvation lay only in joining the League and driving the Frate from the city—to send him to Rome, where he rightfully belonged, obeying the Pope's summons long overdue. Consequently, it was suspected that Messer Dolfo Spini's heart burned with no pure joy at the unexpected succors arriving in apparent fulfillment of the Frate's prophecies. Furthermore, the laughter ringing out anew as Tito joined the group near Nello's door failed to dispel this suspicion. Leaning against the doorpost in the center of the circle was a close-shaven, keen-eyed figure named Niccolò Machiavelli. Though young, he had already penetrated the minute secrets of human egoism.

"Messer Dolfo's head," Machiavelli remarked, "is more of a pumpkin than I thought. I gauge a man's dullness by the devices he trusts to deceive others. Your dullest beast of all is he who grins and claims he minds not a whit just after having his shins kicked." Smiling as the circle opened to admit Tito, he continued, "If I were a trifle duller, now, I should pretend to be fond of this Melema, who holds a secretaryship that would fit me perfectly—as if ill-paid Latin could love better Latin that is better paid! Melema, you are a pestiferously clever fellow, very much in my way, and I am sorry to hear you have secured another stroke of good luck today."

"Questionable luck, Niccolò," replied Tito, placing a friendly hand on Machiavelli's shoulder. "I have gained nothing from it yet but being grabbed and breathed upon by wool-combers, while I am as soiled and battered by the ride as a letter-carrier from Bologna."

"Ah! You crave a touch of my art, Messer Oratore," said Nello, stepping forward at the sound of Tito's voice. "I perceive yesterday's stubble on your chin. Come, come—consign yourself to the priest of all the Muses. Sandro, quick with the lather!"

"In truth, Nello, that is precisely what I most desire at this instant," said Tito, taking a seat. "That was why I turned my steps toward thy shop instead of returning home directly after completing business at the Palazzo."

"Yes, indeed, it is not fitting that you should present yourself to Madonna Romola with a rusty chin and a tangled *zazzera*. Nothing that lacks delicacy ought to approach the Florentine lily; though I see her constantly wandering like a sunbeam among the rags lining our corners—or perhaps she resembles a moonbeam now, for I noted yesterday, when I encountered her, that she looked as pale and worn as that fainting Madonna of Fra Giovanni's. You must attend to this, my bel erudito: she observes too many fasts and vigils in your absence."

Tito gave a melancholy shrug. "It is too true, Nello. She has been depriving herself of half her proper sustenance every day during this famine. But what can a husband do? Her mind has been ignited into flame. A husband's influence is powerless against the Frate's."
