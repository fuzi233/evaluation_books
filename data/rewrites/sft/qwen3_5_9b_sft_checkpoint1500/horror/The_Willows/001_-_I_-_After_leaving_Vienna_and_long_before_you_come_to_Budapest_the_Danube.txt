After departing Vienna, and long before you reach Budapest, the Danube slips into a realm of absolute solitude and desolation. Here, its waters abandon any semblance of a main channel, spreading outward in a chaotic fan that transforms the countryside into a swamp stretching for miles. Vast seas of low willow-bushes blanket the ground, and on the great maps, this forsaken expanse is rendered in a fluffy, fading blue, marked only by the large, straggling word *Sümpfe*—marshes.

In times of high flood, this great acreage of sand and shingle beds is nearly submerged, but in the ordinary season, the bushes bow and rustle in the free winds, displaying their silver leaves to the sun in an ever-shifting plain of bewildering beauty. These willows never achieve the dignity of trees; they possess no rigid trunks. They remain humble bushes with rounded tops and soft outlines, swaying on slender stems that tremble at the slightest breeze. Supple as grasses and constantly shifting, they create the illusion that the entire plain is alive and moving. The wind sends waves rising and falling over the whole surface—not waves of water, but waves of leaves, green swells like the sea, until the branches turn and lift, revealing undersides silvery-white against the sun.

Joyful in slipping beyond the control of the stern banks, the Danube wanders at will among an intricate network of channels intersecting the islands with broad avenues where waters pour with a shouting sound. It creates whirlpools, eddies, and foaming rapids; it tears at the sandy banks, carries away masses of shore and willow-clumps, and forms new islands innumerable, which shift daily in size and shape, possessing at best an impermanent life, for the floods eventually obliterate them completely.

Properly speaking, this fascinating phase of the river's life begins shortly after leaving Pressburg. We, in our Canadian canoe with a gypsy tent and frying-pan aboard, reached it on the crest of a rising flood about mid-July. That same morning, when the sky was reddening before dawn, we had slipped swiftly through the sleeping city of Vienna, leaving it merely a patch of smoke against the blue hills of the Wienerwald an hour or two later. We had breakfasted below Fischeramend under a grove of birch trees roaring in the wind, then swept on the tearing current past Orth, Hainburg, Petronell (the ancient Roman Carnuntum of Marcus Aurelius), and so under the frowning heights of Thelsen on a spur of the Carpathians, where the March steals in quietly from the left and the frontier between Austria and Hungary is crossed.

Racing along at twelve kilometers an hour soon brought us well into Hungary. The muddy waters—a sure sign of flood—drove us aground on many a shingle-bed and twisted us like a cork in sudden belching whirlpools before the towers of Pressburg showed against the sky. Then the canoe, leaping like a spirited horse, flew at top speed under the grey walls, navigated the sunken chain of the Fliegende Brucke ferry safely, turned sharply to the left, and plunged on yellow foam into the wilderness of islands, sandbanks, and swamp-land beyond—the Land of the Willows.

The change came suddenly, as when a series of biograph slides snaps down on a street and shifts without warning into the scenery of a lake and forest. We entered the land of desolation on wings, and in less than half an hour there was neither boat nor fishing-hut nor red roof, nor any sign of human habitation within sight. The sense of remoteness from the world of mankind, the utter isolation, the fascination of this singular world of willows, winds, and waters, instantly laid its spell upon us both. We allowed ourselves to joke that we ought by rights to have held some special passport to admit us, and that we had audaciously come without asking leave into a separate little kingdom of wonder and magic—a kingdom reserved for those who had a right to it, with unwritten warnings to trespassers scattered everywhere for those with the imagination to discover them.

Though it was still early afternoon, the ceaseless buffetings of a tempestuous wind made us weary. We began casting about for a suitable camping-ground. But the bewildering character of the islands made landing difficult; the swirling flood carried us ashore and then swept us out again. The willow branches tore at our hands as we seized them to stop the canoe, and we pulled many yards of sandy bank into the water before the wind, with a great sideways blow, shot us into a backwater where we managed to beach the bows in a cloud of spray. Then we lay panting and laughing on the hot yellow sand, sheltered from the wind, under a scorching sun and a cloudless blue sky, surrounded by an immense army of dancing, shouting willow bushes closing in from all sides, shining with spray and clapping their thousand little hands as if to applaud our success.

"What a river!" I said to my companion, thinking of all the way we had traveled from the source in the Black Forest, and how he had often been obliged to wade and push in the upper shallows at the beginning of June.

"Won't stand much nonsense now, will it?" he replied, pulling the canoe a little farther into safety up the sand, and then composing himself for a nap.

I lay by his side, happy and peaceful in the bath of the elements—water, wind, sand, and the great fire of the sun—thinking of the long journey behind us and the great stretch before us to the Black Sea, and how fortunate I was to have such a delightful and charming traveling companion as my friend, the Swede.

We had made many similar journeys together, but the Danube, more than any other river I knew, impressed us from the very beginning with its *aliveness*. From its tiny bubbling entry into the world among the pinewood gardens of Donaueschingen until this moment when it began to play the great river-game of losing itself among deserted swamps, unobserved and unrestrained, it had seemed to us like following the growth of some living creature. Sleepy at first, but later developing violent desires as it became conscious of its deep soul, it rolled, like some huge fluid being, through all the countries we had passed, holding our little craft on its mighty shoulders, playing roughly with us sometimes, yet always friendly and well-meaning, till at length we had come inevitably to regard it as a Great Personage.

How, indeed, could it be otherwise, since it told us so much of its secret life? At night we heard it singing to the moon as we lay in our tent, uttering that odd sibilant note peculiar to itself, said to be caused by the rapid tearing of pebbles along its bed. We knew the voice of its gurgling whirlpools, suddenly bubbling up on surfaces previously calm; the roar of its shallows and swift rapids; its constant steady thundering below all mere surface sounds; and that ceaseless tearing of its icy waters at the banks. How it stood up and shouted when the rains fell flat upon its face! And how its laughter roared out when the wind blew upstream and tried to stop its growing speed! We knew all its sounds and voices, its tumblings and foamings, its unnecessary splashing against bridges, that self-conscious chatter when there were hills to look on, the affected dignity of its speech when it passed through little towns far too important to laugh, and all those faint, sweet whisperings when the sun caught it fairly in some slow curve.

It was full of tricks, too, in its early life before the great world knew it. There were places in the upper reaches among the Swabian forests where it elected to disappear through holes in the ground, to appear again on the other side of porous limestone hills and start a new river with another name; leaving so little water in its own bed that we had to climb out and wade and push the canoe through miles of shallows.

A chief pleasure, in those early days of its irresponsible youth, was to lie low, like Brer Fox, just before the little turbulent tributaries came to join it from the Alps, and to refuse to acknowledge them when they arrived, running for miles side by side with the dividing line well marked, the levels different, the Danube utterly declining to recognize the newcomers. Below Passau, however, it gave up this particular trick, for there the Inn comes in with a thundering power impossible to ignore, pushing and incommoding the parent river so severely that there is hardly room for them in the long twisting gorge that follows, forcing the Danube to hurry itself with great waves and much dashing to and fro to get through in time. During that fight, our canoe slipped down from its shoulder to its breast, having the time of its life among the struggling waves. But the Inn taught the old river a lesson, and after Passau it no longer pretended to ignore new arrivals.

This was many days back, of course, and since then we had come to know other aspects of the great creature. Across the Bavarian wheat plain of Straubing, she wandered so slowly under the blazing June sun that we could well imagine only the surface inches were water, while below there moved, concealed as by a silken mantle, a whole army of Undines, passing silently and unseen down to the sea, very leisurely too, lest they be discovered.
