The house had fallen into a profound silence. Dinner was done, the noisy hours of recreation were over, and darkness had wrapped the building in its cloak. In the refectory, a single, quiet lamp burned in the study, casting a gentle glow as the externes had returned home, leaving the clanging doors and clamorous bells hushed for the night. Madame Beck was safely settled in the *salle-à-manger*, sharing the evening with her mother and a circle of friends. Seizing this moment of absolute stillness, I slipped into the kitchen. With a specific purpose in mind, I begged a candle for half an hour. My request, "Goton," was met with immediate and cheerful acceptance; he chuckled, offering me two candles if I desired them—"Mais certainement, chou-chou!" I took my light and ascended silently to the dormitory.

Disappointment, sharp and sudden, seized me upon entering. A pupil lay in bed, indisposed. As I drew nearer, recognizing amidst the frills of a muslin nightcap the familiar, rumpled form of Mistress Ginevra Fanshawe, my spirits sank further. Though she appeared supine, I knew better. Her "chiffonnée" figure suggested a ruse; the slight twitching of her eyelids betrayed a sly vigilance, watching for "Timon." She was not to be trusted with solitude. I needed privacy, a space to read my precious letter in peace, unobserved.

Resigned, I turned back toward the classes. Yet, bad luck, that relentless companion, awaited me below. The classrooms were undergoing their weekly cleansing by candlelight: benches stacked atop desks, the air thick with dust, and coffee grounds—used by the local maids instead of tea leaves—darkening the floorboards. It was a scene of hopeless confusion. Baffled but not defeated, I withdrew, determined to find solitude somewhere.

I located a key I knew well, climbed three flights of stairs in succession, and reached a dark, narrow, silent landing. I pushed open a worm-eaten door and dove into the deep, black, icy garret. Here, no one would follow, no one interrupt—not even Madame herself. I shut the door behind me, placing my light upon a dilapidated, mold-covered chest of drawers. Wrapping a shawl around me against the biting chill, I took the letter. Trembling with sweet, feverish impatience, I broke its seal.

*"Will it be long?"* I thought, rubbing my eyes to clear the silver haze of a southern shower. *"Will it be short?"*
It was long.

*"Will it be cool?"* *"Will it be kind?"*
It was kind.

Against my braced, disciplined expectations, it seemed excessively kind. To my famished, longing thoughts, it appeared perhaps kinder than it truly was. So little I had hoped for, so much I had feared; the sheer fullness of this delight tasted like a rare fruit, a sweetness many pass through life without ever tasting. The poor English teacher, huddled in a frost-bitten garret by a guttering candle, reading a letter that was merely "good-natured"—nothing more, yet everything—felt happier than queens in their palaces.

Of course, happiness born of such shallow origins could not endure. Yet, while it lasted, it was genuine and exquisite: a bubble, yes, but a bubble filled with sweet honey-dew. Dr. John had written at length, with evident pleasure and a benignant mood, dwelling with sunny satisfaction on scenes he had witnessed, places we had shared, conversations we had held—all the trivial, cherished subjects of our recent halcyon weeks. But the core of my joy lay in the conviction, generously imparted by his blithe language, that these words were poured out not merely to content *me*, but to gratify *himself*. A gratification he might never again desire, perhaps never again seek—a hypothesis approaching certainty regarding the future. But for this present moment, there was no pain, no blot, no want. It was full, pure, perfect, deeply blessing me. A passing seraph seemed to have rested beside me, leaning toward my heart and resting a softening, cooling, healing wing upon its throbbing beat. Dr. John, you pained me afterward; let me freely forgive every ill, for the sake of that one, dear, remembered good.

Then, the question arose: Are there wicked things, not human, which envy human bliss? Are there evil influences haunting the air, poisoning it for man? What was near me?

Something in that vast, solitary garret sounded strangely. Most surely, I heard a stealthy footfall on the floor, a gliding motion emerging from the black recess haunted by the malefactor's cloaks. I turned. My light was dim; the room was long. But as I lived! In the middle of that ghostly chamber stood a figure, stark black and white: skirts straight and narrow, black; head bandaged and veiled, white.

Say what you will, reader—tell me I was nervous, mad, or unsettled by the excitement of the letter; affirm that I dreamed. I vow, by whatever oath binds me, that I saw there, in that room, on that night, an image like a NUN.

I cried out, sickened. If the shape had approached, I might have swooned. Instead, it receded. I fled for the door. How I descended the stairs, I know not. By instinct, I shunned the refectory and hurried toward Madame's sitting-room. I burst in.

"There is something in the garret! I have been there; I saw something. Go and look at it, all of you!"

I cried, "All of you," because the room seemed full of people, though in truth there were but four: Madame Beck, her mother, Madame Kint—who was frail and visiting—and her brother, M. Victor Kint, along with another gentleman. This stranger was conversing with the old lady, his back to the door.

Mortal fear and faintness had drained the color from my cheeks, leaving me deadly pale. I felt cold, shaking. They all rose in consternation, surrounding me. Urging them to the garret, I pointed out the sight of the gentlemen as a source of hope, a tangible aid. I turned to the door, beckoning them to follow. They tried to stop me, but I insisted they must see what I had seen—something strange standing in the middle of the garret. Then, suddenly, I remembered my letter, left on the drawers with the light. That precious letter! Flesh or spirit must be defied for its sake. I flew up the stairs, hastening the faster as I knew I was followed; they were obliged to come.

Lo! When I reached the garret door, all within was dark as a pit. The light was out. Fortunately, someone—perhaps Madame, with her usual calm sense—had brought a lamp from the room. Speedily, as they ascended, a ray pierced the opaque blackness. There stood the quenched *bougie* on the drawers; but where was the letter? Now I looked for *that*, and not for the nun.

"My letter! My letter!" I panted, plaintive, almost beside myself. I groped on the floor, wringing my hands wildly. Cruel, cruel doom! To have my bit of comfort preternaturally snatched from me, ere I had well tasted its virtue!

I knew not what the others were doing; I could not watch them. They asked questions I did not answer; they ransacked every corner, prattling about disarranged cloaks, a breach or crack in the skylight. "Something or somebody has been here," was sagely averred.

"Oh! They have taken my letter!" cried the groveling, groping monomaniac.

"What letter, Lucy? My dear girl, what letter?" a known voice whispered in my ear. Could I believe that ear? No. I looked up. Could I trust my eyes? Had I recognized the tone? Was I now looking on the face of the writer of that very letter? Was this gentleman near me in this dim garret John Graham—Dr. Bretton himself?

Yes: it was. He had been called that very evening to prescribe for an access of illness in old Madame Kint; he was the second gentleman present in the *salle-à-manger* when I entered.

"Was it *my* letter, Lucy?"

"Your own: yours—the letter you wrote to me. I had come here to read it quietly. I could not find another spot where it was possible to have it to myself. I had saved it all day—never opened it till this evening: it was scarcely glanced over: I *cannot bear* to lose it. Oh, my letter!"

"Hush! Don't cry and distress yourself so cruelly. What is it worth? Hush! Come out of this cold room; they are going to send for the police now to examine further: we need not stay here—come, we will go down."

A warm hand took my cold fingers, leading me down to a room where a fire burned. Dr. John and I sat before the stove. He talked to me and soothed me with unutterable goodness, promising me twenty letters for the one lost.
