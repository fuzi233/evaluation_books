Christmas Eve in Cloisterham. The town is alive with quiet festivity, its usual stillness softened by the season’s warmth. A few unfamiliar faces wander through the streets—visitors from beyond the city walls, men and women once children of Cloisterham who return after years away, only to find their hometown diminished, as if time had shrunk it down like a garment left too long in the wash. For them, the chime of the Cathedral clock and the cawing of rooks from the tower echo not just in the air but in memory—the sounds of childhood, now tinged with nostalgia. Some have even dreamed, on their deathbeds far from home, that their floors were scattered with autumn leaves from the elm trees in the Close, proof that early impressions never truly fade.

The season has touched every corner. Red berries glimmer in the lattice windows of Minor Canon Corner; Mr. and Mrs. Tope carefully tuck sprigs of holly into the carved stalls of the Cathedral, as though decorating the very robes of the Dean and Chapter. Shops overflow with holiday goods—currants, raisins, spices, candied peel, and moist sugar piled high. There’s a sense of indulgence in the air: a massive bunch of mistletoe hangs in the greengrocer’s doorway, and at the pastrycook’s, a small Twelfth Cake—so modest it might be mistaken for a Twenty-fourth or Forty-eighth Cake—awaits raffle, priced at one shilling per ticket. Public entertainments are in full swing: a waxwork exhibit, once admired by the Emperor of China, can be seen during Christmas Week alone at the bankrupt livery-stable-keeper’s shop up the lane; and a new comic pantomime is set to debut at the Theatre, advertised by a life-sized portrait of Signor Jacksonini the clown, grinning awkwardly and asking, “How do you do tomorrow?” In short, Cloisterham is stirring—but not all corners share in the cheer. The High School and Miss Twinkleton’s school remain quiet. The scholars have returned home, each secretly smitten with one of Miss Twinkleton’s pupils (though none of the girls suspect it), and only the maids flutter behind the windows of the latter establishment. It’s observed that these young women grow more lively when left to represent their sex alone—more spirited, perhaps, than when sharing the role with others.

Tonight, three people are meant to meet at the gatehouse. How does each pass the day?

Neville Landless, freed from his studies for the holiday by Mr. Crisparkle—whose own nature is no stranger to the joy of a break—spends the morning in his room reading and writing with intense focus until well past noon. Then he begins to tidy: clearing his desk, arranging books, burning stray papers. He makes a thorough sweep, ordering drawers and destroying every scrap except those tied directly to his education. Once done, he turns to his wardrobe, selects a few essentials—including sturdy shoes and socks—and packs them into a new knapsack bought yesterday in the High Street. Alongside it, he places a heavy walking stick, strong in the grip, iron-shod. He tests it, swings it, balances it, then sets it aside beside the pack.

Dressed and ready to leave, he steps out of his room—only to pause and return for the stick. On the staircase, he meets Mr. Crisparkle emerging from his own chamber. Neville retrieves the stick, and Mr. Crisparkle, pausing, takes it from him with a smile.

“How did you choose this?” he asks.

“I don’t know much about sticks,” Neville replies. “I picked it for its weight.”

“Too heavy,” says Mr. Crisparkle. “Much too heavy.”

“To rest upon during a long walk?”

“Rest upon?” Mr. Crisparkle repeats, adopting a walking stance. “You don’t rest on it—you balance with it.”

“I’ll learn with practice,” Neville says. “I’ve never lived in a walking country.”

“True,” Mr. Crisparkle agrees. “But get into shape, and we’ll take a few miles together. I wouldn’t let you go anywhere now. Will you be back before dinner?”

“I don’t think so—we dine early.”

Mr. Crisparkle nods brightly, bids him farewell, and gives off an unmistakable air of confidence.

Neville goes to the Nuns’ House and requests that Miss Landless be told her brother is there by appointment. He waits at the gate, not crossing the threshold—he’s bound by his word not to come near Rosa.

His sister arrives promptly, mindful of their shared pledge. They greet warmly, avoid lingering, and begin walking toward the higher countryside.

“I won’t tread on forbidden ground, Helena,” Neville says, once they’ve walked a way. “But I must speak of—what shall I call it?—my infatuation.”

“Perhaps you’d better not,” she says. “You know I can hear nothing.”

“You can hear what Mr. Crisparkle has heard—and approved.”

“Yes. I can hear that much.”

“Well, then. I’m not only unsettled myself, but I feel I’m unsettling others. How do I know that, without my presence, you—and the rest of that old party, save our guardian—might not be dining happily at Minor Canon Corner tomorrow? Probably you would be. I see clearly enough that I’m not held in high regard by the old lady, and I understand how burdensome I must seem to her orderly household—especially now, when I must be kept apart from one person, avoided near another, and already carry a reputation that precedes me with yet another. I’ve spoken gently to Mr. Crisparkle—knowing his selfless ways—but I’ve said it. What I’ve stressed most is that I’m fighting a battle within myself, and that a change of scene may help me win it. So, since the weather is clear and firm, I’m going on a walking journey, and plan to remove myself from everyone’s path—my own included, I hope—tomorrow morning.”

“When will you return?”

“In two weeks.”

“And alone?”

“I’m better off alone—even if you were the only one who could bear me.”

“Mr. Crisparkle fully agrees?”

“Fully. At first, he thought it might be a brooding scheme, harmful to a mind like mine. But last Monday night, we took a moonlit walk to talk it over. I laid out the truth plainly: I need to conquer myself, and after tonight, it’s better I’m away than here. I’d likely run into people walking together, and that would do no good—nor help me forget. In two weeks, that risk will pass. And if it returns later, I can go again. Besides, I truly believe fresh air and hard exercise will help. You know Mr. Crisparkle values such things highly—both for body and mind—and he wouldn’t uphold one standard for himself and another for me. When convinced I was serious, he agreed. With his full approval, I leave tomorrow morning—early enough to be gone from the streets and out of earshot of the bells when the townspeople go to church.”

Helena considers this and finds it wise. She trusts Mr. Crisparkle’s judgment, but she also sees merit in it herself—a healthy effort, a real attempt at self-improvement. She feels sorry for him, leaving on Christmas Eve alone, but knows encouragement matters more than pity. She gives it freely.

Will he write?

Every other day. He’ll tell her all his adventures.

Will he send clothes ahead?

“No,” he says. “Travel like a pilgrim—with wallet and staff. My wallet—my knapsack—is packed and ready. And here is my staff!”

He hands it to her. She echoes Mr. Crisparkle’s comment: “Too heavy.” She asks what wood it is.

“Ironwood.”

Up to this point, Neville has been cheerful. Perhaps the need to present his plans in the best light has lifted his spirits. Or perhaps, having succeeded, he now feels a drop. As dusk falls and the city lights flicker on, he grows quieter.

“I wish I weren’t going to that dinner, Helena.”

“Dear Neville, does it matter? It will be over soon.”

“Soon!” he repeats, darkly. “Yes. But I don’t like it.”

There might be a moment of awkwardness, she suggests, but only a moment. He’s certain of himself.

“I wish I felt as sure of everything else,” he says.

“How strange you sound! What do you mean?”

“I don’t know, Helena. Only that I don’t like it. There’s a strange dead weight in the air.”

She points to the coppery clouds beyond the river. “The wind is rising.”

He speaks little more until they part at the Nuns’ House gate. She doesn’t enter immediately, watching him walk away. Twice he passes the gatehouse, hesitating. Finally, as the Cathedral clock strikes quarter past one, he turns sharply and hurries inside.

And so _he_ ascends the postern stair.

Edwin Drood spends the day alone. Something deeper than he realized has vanished from his life. Last night, in silence, he wept for it. Though the image of Miss Landless still lingers in his thoughts, it is not the girl he once imagined—she is stronger, wiser, more grounded than he knew. He thinks of her with a quiet regret for his own shortcomings, for not having taken her seriously sooner, for not valuing her more. If he had treated his future differently—not accepted it as a given, but sought to improve it—perhaps they could have been something more. Yet despite this sorrow, youth’s vanity still holds the image of Rosa in his mind.

That look she gave him when they parted—what did it mean? Did she see beneath his thoughts, into their hidden depths? No, he decides—it was not that. It was sharp, curious, almost startled. He cannot interpret it, though it was deeply expressive.

Now he waits only for Mr. Grewgious. Once he sees him, he will leave. Until then, he walks the ancient city and its edges, recalling times when Rosa and he wandered here as children, proud and engaged. Poor children, he thinks, with a touch of sadness.

His watch has stopped. He stops at the jeweller’s to have it wound. The man, sensing an opportunity, brings out a bracelet—perfect for a young bride, especially one petite. Edwin looks at it coldly. The jeweller shifts to rings—gentlemen’s signets, plain and dignified, often chosen at marriage. Some have the wedding date engraved inside. A responsible memento.

Edwin ignores them. “I wear only my watch and chain—my father’s—and my shirt-pin.”

“I knew that,” the jeweller replies. “Mr. Jasper came in the other day for a watch-glass. I showed him these items, saying, ‘If he should wish to give a gift to a gentleman relative—’ But he smiled and said he had an inventory in his head of everything his relative ever wore: just the watch, chain, and pin.”

Still, the jeweller insists, times change. “Twenty minutes past two, Mr. Drood—I’ve set your watch. Don’t let it run down.”

Edwin takes it, fastens it, and leaves, thinking: *Dear old Jack. If I made a crease in my neckcloth, he’d notice.*

He wanders aimlessly, passing the hours. Cloisterham seems different today—reproachful, as if judging him for not living up to it. But it’s not anger—it’s sorrow. His usual carelessness is replaced by a wistful attention to familiar sights. Soon he’ll be gone—maybe forever. Poor youth. Poor youth.

As twilight deepens, he walks Monks’ Vineyard. He’s paced back and forth for half an hour by the Cathedral chimes, and only now, as darkness settles, does he notice a woman crouched near a wicket gate in a corner. The gate opens onto a narrow, seldom-used path. She must have been there all along—yet he’s only just seen her.

He approaches, and by the light of a nearby lamp, sees her—haggard, chin resting on her hands, eyes fixed forward in a glassy stare.

Always kind-hearted, and especially so tonight, he bends down.

“Are you ill?”

“No, deary,” she answers, without looking at him, her gaze unblinking.

“Are you blind?”

“No, deary.”

“Lost? Homeless? Faint? Why stay here in the cold so long?”

Slowly, stiffly, she focuses on him. Then a film crosses her face, and she begins to tremble.

He straightens, steps back, staring in dread. He knows her.

*Good heavens!* he thinks. *Like Jack that night!*

She lifts her eyes to his and whimpers: “My lungs is weakly; my lungs is dreffle bad. Poor me, poor me, my cough is rattling dry!” She coughs, violently.

“Where are you from?”

“Come from London, deary.” (Her cough tearing through her.)

“Where are you going?”

“Back to London, deary. I came here, looking for a needle in a haystack, and I ain’t found it. Look’ee, deary; give me three-and-sixpence, and don’t you be afeard for me. I’ll get back to London then, and trouble no one. I’m in a business.—Ah, me! It’s slack, it’s slack, and times is very bad!—but I can make a shift to live by it.”

“Do you eat opium?”

“Smokes it,” she replies, struggling. “Give me three-and-sixpence, and I’ll lay it out well, and get back. If you don’t give me three-and-sixpence, don’t give me a brass farden. And if you do give me three-and-sixpence, deary, I’ll tell you something.”

He counts the coins and hands them over. She clutches them tightly, rises with a croaking laugh.

“Bless ye! Hark’ee, dear genl’mn. What’s your Chris’en name?”

“Edwin.”

“Edwin, Edwin, Edwin,” she repeats, trailing off drowsily. Then suddenly: “Is the short of that name Eddy?”

“It is sometimes called so,” he replies, color rising in his cheeks.

“Don’t sweethearts call it so?” she asks, pondering.

“How should I know?”

“Haven’t you a sweetheart, upon your soul?”

“None.”

She starts to move away, murmuring, “Bless ye, and thank’ee, deary!” But he stops her.

“You were to tell me something. You may as well.”

“So I was, so I was. Well, then. Whisper. You be thankful that your name ain’t Ned.”

He stares at her. “Why?”

“Because it’s a bad name to have just now.”

“How a bad name?”

“A threatened name. A dangerous name.”

“The proverb says threatened men live long,” he says lightly.

“Then Ned—so threatened is he, wherever he may be while I am a-talking to you, deary—should live to all eternity!” she whispers, leaning close, her finger trembling before his eyes.

Then she pulls back, mutters, “Bless ye, and thank’ee!” and vanishes toward the Travellers’ Lodging House.

It’s not a comforting end to a dull day. Alone, in a secluded place, surrounded by relics of the past, the encounter sends a chill through him. He heads for the brighter streets, deciding not to mention it tonight—but to tell Jack tomorrow, only as a curious coincidence. Of course, just a coincidence.

Yet the words linger. As he walks another mile, the woman’s voice seems to echo in the wind, in the sky, in the river, in the flickering lights. Even the Cathedral chime, striking suddenly as he enters the gatehouse archway, carries a solemn resonance.

And so _he_ climbs the postern stair.

John Jasper enjoys a more pleasant day than either guest. With no music lessons during the holidays, his time is free except for Cathedral duties. Early, he visits shops, ordering treats his nephew enjoys. “My nephew won’t be with me long,” he tells the dealers, “so I must spoil him.”

On his errands, he calls on Mr. Sapsea and mentions that dear Ned and that “inflammable young spark” of Mr. Crisparkle’s are dining at the gatehouse today—to reconcile.

Mr. Sapsea is not fond of the young man. “Un-English,” he declares. And once labeled Un-English, a thing is forever lost.

Jasper regrets hearing this. He knows Mr. Sapsea never speaks without meaning, and that he’s often right. By remarkable chance, Mr. Sapsea is exactly that opinion.

Jasper sings beautifully that day. In the heartfelt plea to have his heart inclined to keep the law, he astonishes his fellows with his voice—melodic, controlled, flawless. He handles difficult music with greater skill than ever. Usually prone to rush, today his timing is perfect.

This ease likely comes from inner peace. His throat is slightly tender—he wears a thick black silk scarf around his neck, both in choir robe and everyday dress. But his composure is evident. Mr. Crisparkle notices it as they leave Vespers.

“I must thank you, Jasper,” he says. “What pleasure I’ve had hearing you today. Beautiful! Delightful! You couldn’t have surpassed yourself unless you were wonderfully well.”

“I _am_ wonderfully well.”

“Nothing uneven,” Mr. Crisparkle observes, gesturing smoothly. “Nothing unsteady, nothing forced, nothing avoided—everything masterfully done, with complete self-control.”

“Thank you. I hope so, if it’s not too much to say.”

“One would think,” Mr. Crisparkle continues, “you’ve tried a new medicine for that occasional ailment of yours.”

“No, really? That’s well observed. Because I have.”

“Then stick to it,” Mr. Crisparkle says, clapping him on the shoulder. “Stick to it.”

“I will.”

“I congratulate you,” he adds, stepping out of the Cathedral. “On all accounts.”

“Thank you again. I’ll walk with you to the Corner if you don’t mind. I have time before my guests arrive—and I want to speak to you. I think you’ll be pleased to hear it.”

“What is it?”

“We spoke the other evening of my black humours.”

Mr. Crisparkle’s face softens with concern.

“I said I’d make you an antidote,” Jasper continues. “You said you hoped I’d burn them.”

“And I still hope so, Jasper.”

“With every reason! I mean to burn this year’s Diary at year’s end.”

“Because you—?” Mr. Crisparkle brightens.

“You anticipate me. Because I’ve been out of sorts—gloomy, bilious, brain-oppressed. You said I was exaggerating. I was.”

Mr. Crisparkle beams.

“I didn’t see it then, because I _was_ out of sorts. But now I’m healthier, and I admit it with real pleasure. I made much of a little—that’s the truth.”

“It does me good,” Mr. Crisparkle exclaims, “to hear you say it!”

“A man in a monotonous life,” Jasper says, “with nerves or stomach out of balance, dwells on an idea until it loses proportion. That was my case. So I’ll burn the record when the book is full—and start anew with a clearer vision.”

“This is better,” Mr. Crisparkle says, stopping at his door to shake hands, “than I could have hoped.”

And so _he_ goes up the postern stair.
