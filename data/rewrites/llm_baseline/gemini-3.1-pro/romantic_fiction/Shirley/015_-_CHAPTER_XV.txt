The morning light steeped the oak parlor in the color of clarified butter as Shirley reflected on the previous evening’s gathering. For her, the orchestration of a feast was a profound communion, an ancient ritual where the careful alchemy of heritage ingredients might coax out the hidden truths of her guests. 

"I rather like to entertain a circle of gentlemen," Shirley mused to Caroline, her hands resting near a bowl of wild plums. "It is amusing to observe how they navigate a judiciously concocted repast. For ourselves, the subtle notes of these choice, cellar-aged wines and the meticulous culinary architecture of our dishes are layered with generational memory. But gentlemen often retain a bizarre, utilitarian *naïveté* about food, viewing it as mere fuel, save when they surrender to the sensory experience with the decent self-government of our admirable rectors. I watch Moore sometimes, trying to decipher his palate. Yet he lacks that uninhibited, childlike simplicity. He eats without tasting the history on the plate. Did you ever find his accessible point, Caroline? You have studied his rhythms more closely than I."

"It is not, at any rate, that of my uncle and Dr. Boultby," Caroline replied, a soft smile rising. Left to her own devices, she would never have breached the subject, but the invitation to speak of her cousin drew her into the current. "But I truly do not know what it is. I have never attempted to watch Robert without my scrutiny being instantly baffled; I always find he is already watching me."

"There it is!" Shirley exclaimed, her voice ringing like struck crystal in the quiet acoustic ecology of the room. "You cannot fix your eyes upon him but his gaze flashes back, registering your frequency. He is never off his guard, never yielding to the moment. Even when he looks away, his thoughts are dissecting your own, tracing your words and actions back to their ancestral source. Oh, I know that sort of character—it piques me singularly. How does it affect you?"

The sharp, sudden modulation of Shirley’s inquiry was a familiar tempest. Caroline, having long learned to parry these abrupt shifts in tempo, softly deflected. "Pique you? In what way does it pique you?"

"Here he comes!" Shirley interrupted herself, rising swiftly toward the window, her ear catching a disruption in the gravel’s crunch outside. "A diversion. I never told you of a superb conquest I have made lately—achieved entirely without my intention. There is the bell." The heavy, industrial clang of the brass clapper ruptured the manor’s harmonious quiet, a jarring acoustic intrusion. "And, by all the fragrant spices of the earth, there are two of them! Do they never hunt except in pairs? You may have one, Lina. Listen to Tartar!"

From the hollow, vaulted space of the ancient hall, the black-muzzled, tawny dog gave tongue. It was a deep, mythic resonance, a bellow that reverberated against the centuries-old oak panels like muttered thunder—the organic, protective frequency of the estate itself.

"Listen!" Shirley laughed. "You would think it the prelude to a bloody onslaught. They do not know old Tartar’s dialect. His uproars are all sound and fury, signifying nothing to those who understand his tongue."

A sudden, violent clamor shattered the air. "Down, sir, down!" barked a high, imperious voice, lacking all local inflection, followed by the vicious, unnatural crack of a cane. It was an assault on the sonic environment, immediately answered by a scutter, a yell, and a deafening tumult.

"O Malone, Malone!"

"Down! down! down!" the abrasive voice shrieked again.

"He really is worrying them!" Shirley cried, the levity draining from her. "They have struck him. A blow is an untranslatable insult to him; it is an indignity he will not absorb."

She swept into the hall. The visual chaos mirrored the acoustic violence. One gentleman was fleeing up the polished oak staircase, scrambling for sanctuary in the upper chambers, while the other, Malone, backed wildly toward the stairfoot, flourishing a knotty stick and shouting in a discordant rhythm. Tartar bayed and howled, his canine honor offended, while a cluster of servants spilled from the kitchen, their flour-dusted aprons a testament to interrupted domestic rhythms. The dog sprang; the second gentleman abandoned his retreat and rushed after his comrade. The first was already sealed inside a bedroom, holding the heavy timber door against his fellow—terror stripping away all veneer of civilized fraternity. The other fugitive threw his weight against the wood, desperate to breach the barrier.

"Gentlemen," Shirley projected, her silvery, vibrating tone cutting through the dissonance, restoring a measure of acoustic sanity. "Spare my locks, if you please. Calm yourselves! Come down! Look at Tartar; he will not harm a cat."

She knelt, her fingers sinking into the dog’s tawny ruff. Tartar crouched at her feet, his bulldog eyes smoldering with a dull, ancient fire, his nostrils drawing in the complex scents of the hall. He was a creature of stubborn, phlegmatic truth, devoted only to Shirley and to John, the man who meticulously prepared his daily sustenance. To the rest of the world, he was indifferent, unless his boundaries were violently breached by the strike of a stick. 

"Mr. Malone, how do you do?" Shirley called up to the gallery, her face lit with mirth. "That is not the way to the oak parlor; that is Mrs. Pryor's domain. Request your friend Mr. Donne to evacuate. I shall receive him in a lower room."

"Ha! ha!" Malone offered a hollow, forced laugh that died awkwardly against the tapestries. He leaned over the massive balustrade. "Really, that animal alarmed Donne. He is a little timid," he added, stiffening his posture and attempting a trim walk to the stairhead. "I thought it better to follow, to reassure him."

"It appears you did. Well, come down, if you please. John," she turned to the manservant, "go upstairs and liberate Mr. Donne. Take care, Mr. Malone; the historic oak is slippery."

The wood, smoothed by generations of passing feet, indeed offered no purchase to the clumsy. Malone slipped, his stately descent ruined by a frantic, creaking clutch at the banisters. Tartar, sensing the arrhythmic, unwarranted *éclat* of the man's stumble, rumbled a low growl. Recovering his footing, Malone passed the dog in suppressed, sullen rage, pushing into the parlor before his host. He glared at both women, his hands curling as though he wished to grip them to death, completely devoid of the sensory grace the room demanded.

Shirley’s amusement softened into pity, and Caroline, a soul far too steeped in quiet empathy, offered no smile at his mortification. Tartar was dismissed to the kitchens. Peter Augustus Malone was gradually soothed by Shirley’s masterful tones—frequencies capable of gentling a raging bull. Realizing his lack of leverage, he shifted his tactics, attempting to integrate into the parlor’s harmony. He sought to transform his tempestuous arrival into charming fascination, smoothing his aura from a roaring March lion to a docile lamb.

Yet his unease persisted. Taking a chair near the door—shunning both Shirley’s offered sofa and Caroline’s warm fireside—he spoke in fitful, intensely commonplace bursts. His sighs punctuated the air like rhythmic, discordant exhaust. Seeking to project ease, he drew forth an ample silk handkerchief, folding the red-and-yellow fabric with bizarre, utilitarian energy. He whipped it, banded it, and, in a display of utterly original awkwardness, wrapped it firmly around his crossed, athletic Irish legs. This mechanical pantomime, repeated as if worthy of an encore, drove Shirley to the window to conceal her silent laughter, while Caroline turned her head, letting her long curls veil her mantling smile. Caroline was edified by his abrupt tactical shift: realizing Caroline’s modest inheritance paled against the deep, rooted wealth of the Fieldhead estate, Malone’s homage wheeled entirely toward the heiress. He approached courtship not as an organic merging of souls, but as a calculated harvest.

Eventually, John succeeded in coaxing Mr. Donne from his refuge. As Donne presented himself at the oak-parlour door, he bore no trace of shame. He was a man of densely self-satisfied nature, entirely insulated from the rich, sensory world. He lacked the inner fire to blush, his blood too tepid to mount to his cheek. He was an arrogant, decorous slip of the commonplace, harboring the wooden notion that he might simply woo Miss Keeldar by formal visitation and a written proposal. He viewed Fieldhead not as a living lineage of earth and hearth, but merely as a place where he could command servants, consume the finest meats and wines without tasting their origin, and be a great man. 

"A very dangerous dog that, Miss Keeldar," Donne announced in an injured, grating tone. "I wonder you should keep such an animal."

"Do you, Mr. Donne? Perhaps you will wonder more when I tell you I am very fond of him."

"I should say you are not serious. Can't fancy a lady fond of that brute—'tis so ugly—a mere carter's dog. Pray hang him."

"Hang what I am fond of!"

"And purchase in his stead some sweetly pooty pug or poodle—something appropriate to the fair sex." 

His usage of "sweetly pooty" hung in the air like a foul odor, a symptom of the superficial, dominant lexicon trying to overwrite the robust, muscular dialect of the moors. 

"Perhaps I am an exception," Shirley noted cleanly.

"Oh, you can't be, you know. All ladies are alike in those matters. That is universally allowed."

"Tartar frightened you terribly, Mr. Donne. I hope you won't take any harm."

"That I shall, no doubt. When I *sor* him"—his pronunciation a sterile, imported corruption of the native tongue—"about to spring, I thought I should have fainted."

"Perhaps you did faint in the bedroom; you were a long time there."

"No; I bore up that I might hold the door fast. But what is that? Your man told a falsehood! The dog is there!"

Outside the glass garden door, Tartar stalked the pavement, stiff and black-muzzled, whistling a half-strangled breath inherited from the mythic bulldog lines of his ancestry.

"There are other visitors coming," Shirley observed with the profound coolness of one attuned to the landscape. Tartar sprang toward the gate, bellowing with explosive force. Shirley quietly opened the door, chirruping a specific, melodic command. Instantly, the beast’s roar died, and he lifted his heavy head to be patted by the new arrivals.

"What! Tartar, Tartar!" rang a cheery, boyish voice, vibrating with genuine warmth. "Don't you know us? Good-morning, old boy!"

Little Mr. Sweeting, whose innate good nature allowed him to navigate the world without fear of beast or man, stepped through the gate, his hands naturally seeking the guardian’s ruff. His vicar, Mr. Hall, followed. To Mr. Hall, the dog offered a deep, snuffing inhalation, reading the elder man's olfactory signature—a blend of old parchment, moorland wind, and quiet righteousness—and deemed him harmonious. Tartar withdrew to the sun-warmed stone, indifferent to Sweeting's further play. 

Shirley advanced, greeting Messrs. Hall and Sweeting cordially. They brought news of successful subscriptions for the parish fund. Mr. Hall’s eyes beamed with benign, generational wisdom behind his spectacles, his face handsome with agrarian goodness. When Caroline ran out, pressing both her hands into his, his serene, affectionate gaze enveloped her—an untranslatable communion of shared history.

They drifted into the garden, leaving the sterile curates behind. The breeze, a master musician, rustled the ancient trees and freshened the women’s cheeks. The olfactory landscape was intoxicating: profusely blooming heritage flowers exhaled perfumes that had drifted over these lands for centuries. Shirley gathered handfuls of the delicate, splendid blossoms, instructing Caroline to arrange a nosegay for Mr. Hall. Caroline settled onto the steps of a summer-house, her lap a vibrant canvas of petals, while the vicar leaned gently on his cane beside her.

Unwilling to be entirely inhospitable, Shirley eventually convoyed the neglected pair from the oak parlor, guiding a fearful Donne past the snoring Tartar. Miss Keeldar impartially offered the curates flowers. They accepted with jarring awkwardness. Malone clumsily balanced the fragile blooms against his shillelah. Donne’s "Thank you!" was a fatuous, arrogant sound, assuming the heiress was merely paying homage to his perceived superiority. Sweeting alone understood the gesture, accepting his posy like a meticulous custodian of beauty and tucking it nattily into his buttonhole.

As reward for his sensory appreciation, Shirley entrusted Sweeting with a hospitable commission. The young curate’s eyes sparked. He darted toward the courtyard kitchens, intimately familiar with the domestic rhythm of the estate. Soon he reappeared, acting as a devoted food historian, bearing a round table beneath the spreading arms of the cedar tree. He collected garden-chairs, forming a circular space of communion. A parlor-maid arrived with a napkin-covered tray, and Sweeting’s nimble fingers assisted in arranging the crystal and silver.

The impromptu luncheon was a testament to Fieldhead's bounty: cold, slow-poached chicken steeped in ancestral herbs; ham cured in the smoke of autumnal applewood; and rustic tarts weeping with the dark, jammy juices of early summer fruit. While Donne and Malone consumed the feast merely as utilitarian fodder, their mechanical engagement with knife and fork contributing nothing to the table’s vivacity, the others partook in a joyous, sensory celebration. Amidst the wilderness of fragrant flowers, under a vibrant sky, the communion of Shirley, Caroline, Mr. Hall, and David Sweeting banished all ungenial dullness.

During the meal, Mr. Hall invoked the approaching Whitsuntide—the grand, united Sunday-school tea-drinking, a cyclical, multi-generational gathering of the parishes. He hoped Miss Keeldar would make her first public appearance at this muster of elated hearts. Shirley, ever attuned to the orchestration of festive, communal joy, assured him they might dispose of her as they pleased.

"And," Caroline murmured, the cadence of her voice intimate and rooted, "you will promise to come to my table, and to sit near me, Mr. Hall?"

"I shall not fail, *Deo volente*," he promised. He turned to Shirley, weaving the timeline of the past into the present. "I have occupied the place on her right hand at these monster tea-drinkings for six years. They made her a teacher when she was but twelve. The first time she had to 'take a tray' and pour the tea in public, there was a piteous, silent panic. I observed the speechless terror, the porcelain cups shaking in her little hands, the urn overflowing. I came to her aid, took my seat, and managed the slop-basin and the steeping leaves for her, like any village elder."

"I was very grateful to you," Caroline said softly.

"You were. You thanked me with an earnest sincerity rarely found in little ladies, who often absorb kindness as if they were carved of wax and wood rather than living flesh and nerve. She shadowed me the rest of the evening across the grounds; she followed me into the vestry, and would have mounted the pulpit with me had I not secured her in the rectory pew."

"And he has been my friend ever since," Caroline affirmed.

"And always sat at her table, handing the cups. The next service I render will be to marry her to some curate or mill-owner. But mind, Caroline, if he is not a man capable of honoring the little girl who walked hand-in-hand with me over Nunnely Common, I will not officiate."

"The caution is useless. I shall live single, like your sister Margaret."

"You might do worse. Margaret is content with her books and her brother. Should the day come when Briarfield is yours no longer, the old maid and bachelor at Nunnely vicarage will give you tender welcome."

"There are your flowers," Caroline said, holding out the selected nosegay. "You do not typically care for bouquets, but give this to Margaret. Only—keep this wild forget-me-not I pulled from the grass. And let me take three of these blue blossoms for my souvenir." 

She opened a small book with an enameled cover and a silver clasp, pressing the flowers between the pages to immortalize their brief, organic life, writing in pencil: *To be kept for the sake of the Rev. Cyril Hall, my friend. May —, 18—.*

The Reverend, in turn, placed his sprig safely within the leaves of a pocket Testament, his marginalia reading simply, *Caroline*.

"Now," he smiled, oblivious to the shallow, localized jokes the curates muttered at the table’s far end. "I trust we are romantic enough. Miss Keeldar, you may laugh at this trait of exaltation in a gray-headed vicar, but I am so accustomed to the requests of this young friend, I cannot refuse her."

"He is naturally rather sentimental," Caroline noted. "I know what pleases him."

"That you should be good and happy? Yes. May God preserve your blessings of peace and comparative innocence. For in His sight, none are spotless. When human tongues flatter you, remember that in the eyes of her Maker, Mary Ann Ainley—a woman whom neither glass nor lips have ever panegyrized—is fairer and better than either of you. You young things, wrapped in the sweetness of earthly existence, scarcely live as Christ lived. But she, with meek heart and quiet reverence, treads close in those ancient steps."
