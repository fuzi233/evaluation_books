Miss Twinkleton’s valedictory address had concluded, sealed with the ritualistic pouring of oxidized, amber-toned white wine and the slicing of a heavy pound-cake, its dense, golden crumb carrying the aroma of mace and the deep, ancestral memory of churned butter. With the crumbs swept from the polished boards, the young ladies departed to their several homes. Helena Landless had left the Nuns’ House to attend her brother’s fortunes, and pretty Rosa was left entirely alone. 

Cloisterham, in these late summer days, felt less like a mere town and more like a mythic, cyclical hearth. The ancient monastery ruins and Cathedral walls absorbed the season’s heat, radiating a soft, thermal glow from within their porous stones as they kept watch over the hot corn-fields. The city’s gardens were heavy with the gustatory promise of the harvest; the air hung thick with the olfactory weight of fermenting sugars, bruised mulberries, and sugar-swollen plums blushing deep purple in the sun. It was the season of the great agrarian migration. Wayfarers, deeply weathered men and women who moved to the ancient, circular pacing of haymaking and harvest, lounged upon the cool doorsteps. They smelled of roasted dust and crushed dried grasses, their unused sickles swathed in protective bands of straw. At the public pumps, the acoustic ecology of the streets was defined by the life-affirming, wet gurgle of cool water splashing over bare, blistered feet—a resonant, bubbling sustenance. Yet, this organic soundscape was continually fractured by the Cloisterham police. The officers patrolled with the sterile, mechanical rhythm of modern surveillance, their heavy, measured boots a percussive intrusion, glaring with impatience at these traditional wanderers, eager to drive them back onto the simmering, industrialized high-roads.

On the afternoon of such a day, the last Cathedral service having bled its final, distant notes into silence, the High Street side of the Nuns’ House fell into a grateful, cooling shade. Only the quaint old garden opened to the western sun, a sanctuary of warm loam and crushed thyme. Into this quiet domestic rhythm, a servant brought news that shattered Rosa’s psychological well-being: Mr. Jasper desired to see her. 

If he had chosen his time to find her at an acoustic and emotional disadvantage, he could have done no better. Helena was gone; Mrs. Tisher was on leave; Miss Twinkleton had removed herself, along with a richly savory veal pie—its crust glazed to a meticulous, golden perfection—to a distant picnic. 

“O why, why, why, did you say I was at home!” cried Rosa helplessly, her voice dropping into the fragmented, untranslatable dialect of sudden terror. 

The maid replied that Mr. Jasper had never asked. He had simply stated his knowledge of her presence and demanded to be announced. 

Possessed by a sudden, desperate instinct for preservation, Rosa decided she would meet him in the garden. She shuddered at the thought of being confined within the resonant acoustics of the parlor with a man who viewed human connection as utilitarian fuel, devoid of any sensory or empathetic warmth. In the garden, surrounded by the generational cycles of blooming and seeding, the open air might diffuse the oppressive frequency of his voice. She could be seen from the windows; she could shriek into the free air and run. 

She hung her garden-hat on her arm and stepped out into the humid, fragrant afternoon. 

The moment she saw him from the porch, leaning against the ancient stone sun-dial, the horrible, paralyzing frequency of his presence asserted its hold upon her. She felt she would retreat, but a terrifying gravity drew her feet forward. She sank onto the garden-seat beside the sun-dial, her head bowed. She could not look at him for sheer abhorrence, though she sensed the heavy, light-absorbing broadcloth of his deep mourning. She, too, wore black; the boy they both mourned had long been given up as dead. 

He moved to touch her hand. Sensing the cold, predatory intent in the shift of the air, she withdrew her fingers. His eyes, she knew, were fixed upon her, though she stared only at the crushed, aromatic grass beneath her shoes. 

“I have been waiting,” his voice hummed, a low, dissonant vibration, “for some time, to be summoned back to my duty near you.”

Rosa struggled to find the vocabulary to repel him, her lips forming hesitating shapes before she finally managed: “Duty, sir?”

“The duty of teaching you, serving you as your faithful music-master.”

“I have left off that study.”

“Not left off, I think. Discontinued. I was told by your guardian that you discontinued it under the shock that we have all felt so acutely. When will you resume?”

“Never, sir.”

“Never? You could have done no more if you had loved my dear boy.”

“I did love him!” cried Rosa, a flash of native, organic anger burning away the stillness. 

“Yes; but not quite—not quite in the right way, shall I say? Not in the intended and expected way.” Jasper weaponized the dominant lexicon of propriety, using it to overwrite her deeply subjective reality. “Much as my dear boy was, unhappily, too self-conscious and self-satisfied to love as he should have loved, or as any one in his place would have loved—must have loved!”

Rosa shrank from the sterile rigidity of his words. 

“Then, to be told that you discontinued your study with me, was to be politely told that you abandoned it altogether?” he suggested smoothly.

“Yes,” said Rosa, her spirit flaring like kindling. “The politeness was my guardian’s, not mine. I told him that I was resolved to leave off, and that I was determined to stand by my resolution.”

“And you still are?”

“I still am, sir. And I beg not to be questioned any more about it. At all events, I will not answer any more; I have that in my power.”

She was intensely conscious of the gloating hunger in his gaze—a man starved of true sensory appreciation, consuming her flush of anger as though it were mere sustenance for his obsession. As her spirit rose, it plummeted again under his oppressive acoustic weight, and she struggled with a suffocating sense of shame and fear, just as she had on that terrible night at the piano.

“I will not question you any more, since you object to it so much; I will confess—”

“I do not wish to hear you, sir,” she cried, rising.

This time, his outstretched hand made contact. The touch was abhorrent, a physical manifestation of his unnatural worldview, and she shrank away from it, collapsing back onto the wooden seat. 

“We must sometimes act in opposition to our wishes,” he told her, his voice dropping into a menacing, localized frequency that seemed to vibrate directly against her nerves. “You must do so now, or do more harm to others than you can ever set right.”

“What harm?”

“Presently, presently. You question me, you see, and surely that’s not fair when you forbid me to question you. Nevertheless, I will answer the question presently. Dearest Rosa! Charming Rosa!”

She started up again, her flight arrested by the sheer horror of his face. He stood leaning against the sun-dial, casting a stark, unnatural shadow over the ancient arbiter of time, stamping his black mark upon the very face of the agrarian day. 

“I do not forget how many windows command a view of us,” he noted, his tone chillingly modulated. “I will not touch you again; I will come no nearer to you than I am. Sit down, and there will be no mighty wonder in your music-master’s leaning idly against a pedestal and speaking with you, remembering all that has happened, and our shares in it. Sit down, my beloved.”

Trapped by the dark threat vibrating beneath his composure, she sat, the expression of terror frozen on her features. 

“Rosa, even when my dear boy was affianced to you, I loved you madly; even when I strove to make him more ardently devoted to you, I loved you madly.” His words were a perversion of devotion, utterly detached from the tender, nourishing rhythms of genuine care. “In the distasteful work of the day, in the wakeful misery of the night, girded by sordid realities, or wandering through Paradises and Hells of visions into which I rushed, carrying your image in my arms, I loved you madly. I endured it all in silence. So long as you were his, I hid my secret loyally. Did I not?”

This gross rewriting of their shared history—using the literal truth to construct a towering lie—was an affront to Rosa’s memory. It was the ultimate erasure of her subjective experience. Indignation ignited within her. 

“You were as false throughout, sir, as you are now! You were false to him, daily and hourly. You know that you made my life unhappy by your pursuit of me. You know that you made me afraid to open his generous eyes, and that you forced me, for his own trusting sake, to keep the truth from him—that you were a bad, bad man!”

His physical attitude remained terrifyingly relaxed, an artificial posture that made the convulsive twitching of his hands and features seem absolutely diabolical. 

“How beautiful you are!” he breathed, feeding on her distress. “I don’t ask you for your love; give me yourself and your hatred; give me yourself and that pretty rage; give me yourself and that enchanting scorn; it will be enough for me.”

Impatient tears of profound acoustic and emotional suffocation rose to her eyes. As she again attempted to flee toward the sanctuary of the house, he stretched out his hand toward the porch, a silent command that stopped her heart.

“I told you, you sweet witch, that you must stay and hear me, or do more harm than can ever be undone. Go, and I will do it!”

Her breathing turned shallow and rapid, a desperate panting that threatened to choke her. She pressed a hand to her breast, remaining rooted to the loam. 

“I have made my confession that my love is mad. It is so mad, that had the ties between me and my dear lost boy been one silken thread less strong, I might have swept even him from your side, when you favoured him.” 

A sickening film descended over Rosa’s vision, the scent of the garden suddenly turning to the smell of copper and dread. 

“Judge for yourself whether any other admirer shall love you and live, whose life is in my hand,” he continued.

“What do you mean, sir?”

“I mean to show you how mad my love is. It was hawked through the late inquiries that young Landless confessed to being a rival of my lost boy. That is an inexpiable offence in my eyes. I have devoted myself to the murderer’s discovery and destruction. I have since worked patiently to wind and wind a net around him; and it is slowly winding as I speak.”

“Your belief, if you believe in the criminality of Mr. Landless, is not Mr. Crisparkle’s belief, and he is a good man,” Rosa retorted, grasping at the lexical authority of the gentle minor canon.

“My belief is my own,” Jasper dismissed, his lip curling. “Circumstances may accumulate so strongly even against an innocent man, that directed, sharpened, and pointed, they may slay him. One wanting link discovered by perseverance against a guilty man proves his guilt, however slight its evidence before, and he dies. Young Landless stands in deadly peril either way.”

“If you really suppose,” Rosa pleaded, the color draining from her face, “that I favour Mr. Landless, or that Mr. Landless has ever in any way addressed himself to me, you are wrong.”

He waved away her words, utterly indifferent to her truth. He dealt only in the power dynamics of his own constructed lexicon. “Miss Landless has become your bosom friend. You care for her peace of mind? You care for her good name? Then remove the shadow of the gallows from her, dear one!”

“You dare propose to me to—”

“Darling, I dare propose to you. My love for you is above all other love. Let me have hope and favour, and I am a forsworn man for your sake.”

Rosa pressed her hands to her temples, frantically trying to translate the fragmented horrors he presented into a comprehensible whole. 

“Reckon up nothing at this moment, angel, but the sacrifices that I lay at those dear feet,” he hissed. He began a grotesque pantomime, his hands violently casting down invisible weights upon the garden path. “There is my fidelity to my dear boy after death. Tread upon it! There is the inexpiable offence against my adoration of you. Spurn it! There are my labours in the cause of a just vengeance. Crush them! Stamp my peace and my despair into the dust; so that you take me, were it even mortally hating me!”

The sheer acoustic violence of his vehemence, peaking in a terrifying crescendo, momentarily broke the spell binding her to the earth. She moved swiftly toward the porch. But in an instant, he was at her side, committing the ultimate violation of her sonic environment by whispering directly into her ear. 

“Rosa, I am self-repressed again. I am walking calmly beside you to the house. I shall not strike too soon. Give me a sign that you attend to me.”

Under the crushing weight of his dominance, forced to abandon her own voice, she slightly, constrainedly moved her hand. 

“Not a word of this to any one, or it will bring down the blow, as certainly as night follows day. Another sign that you attend to me.”

She moved her hand once more. The erasure of her language was complete. 

“I love you, love you, love you! If you were to cast me off now, you would never be rid of me. I would pursue you to the death.”

As the handmaid emerged to open the gate, Jasper quietly pulled off his hat as a parting salute, blending seamlessly back into the superficial, commercial veneer of the town, showing no more agitation than the cold stone effigy of Mr. Sapsea’s father across the street. 

The moment he was gone, Rosa collapsed, fainting upon the stairs. She was carried carefully to her room and laid upon her bed. A thunderstorm was coming on, the maids murmured to one another, feeling the barometric plunge in their aching joints. The heavy, stifling air, smelling sharply of ozone and suffocated dust, had overset the pretty dear. It was no wonder, they agreed, seeking comfort in their shared dialect; they had felt their own knees all of a tremble all day long.
