Madame de Bernstein was scarcely less delighted than her two Virginian nephews when the final interview between Harry and Lady Maria drew to its close. George sent her a brief billet recounting every detail of the meeting that very same evening, and before long Lord Castlewood—whose visits to his aunt were notoriously rare—arrived at her door to pay his respects, and spoke openly of all that had transpired. No man was better than his lordship at striking a frank, unguarded tone when it suited his purpose, and now that the match between Maria and Harry was well and truly ended, there was no need for pretense or hypocrisy. The game was played, the final move made: he saw no harm in recalling its stratagems, finesses, and small, clever schemes.
“She is my own sister,” he declared, with a show of warm affection. “She will not get many more chances as good as this to marry and settle her future. I may not have approved of the match in every particular, and I may have pitied your ladyship’s young Virginian favourite, but such a stroke of good fortune was not to be thrown away, and I was bound to stand by my own flesh and blood.”
“Your candour does your lordship infinite honour,” Madame de Bernstein replied dryly, “and your devotion to your sister is positively edifying!”
“Nay, we have lost the game, and I speak with no rancour in my heart. It is you, who won, that have no cause to bear malice,” Lord Castlewood said, bowing with easy grace.
The Baroness protested she had never been in better humour in her life. “Confess it now, Eugene: that visit Maria paid Harry at the spunging-house, that dramatic, tender little scene where she gave back all his gifts—was that stroke of genius yours?”
“Pity for the young man, and a sense of what was due from an affianced lady to her lover in misfortune—sure those are motives sufficient to explain her conduct,” Lord Castlewood replied, affecting a demure innocence before he shrugged, and owned he had indeed advised his sister to call on Mr. Henry Warrington. “And we should have won, in spite of all your ladyship’s interference,” he added, “had not the elder brother risen from the grave to make his appearance. I have been doing my best to console my poor Maria, in fact, by pointing out how very lucky it is, on the whole, that we lost.”
“Suppose she had married Harry, and then your cousin George had turned up alive after all?” the Baroness pointed out.
“Effectivement!” cried Eugene, taking a pinch of scented snuff. “As the grave chose to give up its dead, let us be thankful it disgorged them in the nick of time! I must own that Mr. George Warrington seems a man of sound sense, no more selfish than any other eldest son and man of the world. My poor Molly had fancied he might be a greenhorn, like his younger brother, you see. She fondly hoped he would be inclined to share and share alike with Twin junior, and she was so infatuated with the young man, I do believe she would have taken him even then. ‘Harry Warrington with half a loaf might do very well,’ I told her, ‘but Harry Warrington with no bread at all, my dear!’”
“No bread?” the Baroness asked.
“No bread but a spot at his elder brother’s side table. The man said as much to me, plain as day.”
“What a hard-hearted wretch!” Madame de Bernstein exclaimed.
“Ah, bah! I show you my cards, aunt, cartes sur table! Mr. George only did what every other man in his position would have done, and we have no right to be angry with him, not really. Molly acknowledged as much herself, after her first burst of tears was over, and I had brought her to listen to reason. The silly creature! To be so wild about a young lad half her age!”
“‘Twas a real passion, I almost do believe,” said Madame de Bernstein. “You should have heard her take leave of him. C'etait touchant, ma parole d'honneur! I cried, right there before George, I could not help myself. The young fellow with muddy stockings, his hair falling over his eyes, flings himself into our dinner party, makes his offer to Molly in a frank, noble manner, in very fine language too, and then she replies. Begad, it put me in mind of Mrs. Woffington in that new Scotch play Lord Bute’s man wrote—Douglas, what d’ye call it? She clings round the lad, bids him adieu in heartrending accents, steps out of the room in stately despair, no more chocolate for her, thank you. If she had made a mauvais pas, no woman alive could have retreated from it with more dignity. ‘Twas a masterly defeat, we were starved out of our position, but we retired with all the honours of war.”
“Molly will not die of this disappointment,” my lord’s aunt observed, sipping her spiced tea. My lord grinned, showing his yellow teeth. “He, he! She has had this very malady very severely two or three times before, and recovered perfectly. It does not kill, as your ladyship well knows, not at Molly’s time of life.”
How should her ladyship know? She had not married Doctor Tusher until she was well advanced in years, nor become Madame de Bernstein until even later. Old Dido, the poet remarks, was no stranger to misfortune, and so learned to have compassion on the wretched.
Folk in the small world brawl and feud, and keep up their quarrels for years, refusing to make peace. But the people of the great world are surely wiser in their generation. They fall out, they cease calling on each other, then they make amends and meet again, no awkward questions asked. A stray prodigal, or even a stray puppy-dog, is welcomed back under the terms of an unspoken amnesty, no matter where he has been roaming. For six whole months, ever since the Castlewoods and Madame de Bernstein began their battle for possession of poor Harry Warrington, the two branches of the Esmond family had kept entirely apart. Now, that the quarrel was settled, they were free to meet again as if no difference had ever divided them: Madame de Bernstein drove her great gilded coach to Lady Castlewood’s rout, the Esmond ladies appeared smiling at the Baroness’s weekly drums, and they all professed to love each other every bit as dearly as they had done before the feud began.
“So, sir, I hear you have acted like a hard-hearted monster to your poor brother Harry!” the Baroness cried, delighted, tapping George playfully with her ivory cane.
“I only acted on your ladyship’s hint, to see whether our kinsfolk wanted Harry for himself, or for the fortune everyone thought he had,” George replied, flushing bright red.
“Nay, Maria could not marry a penniless fellow who had nothing at all, when his own elder brother declared he would not give him a penny!”
“I did it for the best, madam,” George insisted, still blushing.
“And thou didst, thou little hypocrite!” the old lady cackled in delight.
“Hypocrite, madam! And what makes you say that?” Mr. Warrington asked, drawing himself up with great offended dignity.
“I know all, my infant!” the Baroness switched to French. “Thou art the very image of thy grandfather. Come, that I embrace thee! Harry has told me everything, how thou hast divided thy little patrimony evenly with him!”
“It was but natural, madam. We have shared hearts and purses since the day we were born. I only feigned that hard-heartedness to call those people’s bluff,” George said, his eyes glistening.
“And thou wilt divide the great Virginia estate with him too?” the Baroness pressed.
“I do not say that. It would not be just,” Mr. Warrington replied. “The land must pass to the eldest born, and Harry would not have it otherwise. It may be I die young, or our mother outlives us both. But half of everything that is mine is his, and it must be remembered he only fell into debt because he was misled to believe he was the heir.”
“Ah! It is a knight of old, it is Bayard, it is the old grandfather come back to life!” Madame de Bernstein declared to her waiting-woman as she retired for the night. That evening, when the two lads took their leave, she only offered Harry two fingers in polite parting, but pressed her rouged painted cheek to George’s, who blushed as deep as that well-dyed rose for very bashfulness at the favour.
Harry Warrington was the least envious soul alive, and had always honoured his brother as his chief, his guide, and his better, so it was no surprise that a heavy sense of humiliation and disappointment weighed on him after he was deposed from his lofty position as the Fortunate Youth, heir to boundless Virginian territories. His friends at Kensington might vow over and over that they would love him all the better after his fall; Harry bowed low and thanked them earnestly, but he could not fail to notice, when he accompanied George to the grand state dinner Lord Castlewood threw for his newly-found kinsman, that George was the centre of everyone’s attention. All the compliments, all the small, tender little attentions, were lavished on him, while no one paid Harry any mind at all—save poor Maria, who followed him all evening with wistful, heavy eyes, that seemed to carry dismal, silent reproach, blaming him for the very thing she had done.
“Ah!” those eyes seemed to say. “It is all very well for you, Harry, to accept the freedom I gave you, but I never, sir, intended for you to be quite so pleased to be let off.” She had set him free, and yet she could not forgive him for taking her at her word. She would not marry him, and yet she would have liked to keep him pining after her. Oh, my young friends, how delightful is the beginning of a love affair, and how undignified, sometimes, the end! What a romantic vista opens before young Damon and young Phillis, or middle-aged lovers of the same, when they declare their artless affections to each other, twine their arms around each other’s waists, and survey that charming pays du tendre stretching out before their feet! Linked together, they will wander down its paths until extreme old age. There may be rough rocks and roaring rivers in the way, but will not Damon’s strong true love carry his sweetheart safely over them? There may be dragons and dangers in the path, but shall not his courageous sword cut them all down? Then at evening, they will rest cuddled together like two pretty babes in the wood, moss for their couch, stars for their canopy, their arms for each other’s pillows! That is the wise, perfect plan young folk draw up when they set out on the journey of love—and, alas, they have not walked a mile before they come to a great solid wall, and find they must turn back. They are squabbling with the post-boy at Barnet, the very first stage on the road to Gretna Green, and suddenly Strephon has no money in his pocket, or there is papa waving a great whacking horsewhip, who drags the young lady back, locks her up in the schoolroom to cry her eyes out. The parting is heart-breaking at the time, but years later, when she has married the banker and borne him eight children, and he has become either a prosperous barrister or a seedy scapegrace who has gone bankrupt two or three times, when they meet again by chance, is not the whole situation faintly ridiculous? Nevertheless, I hope no young man ever falls in love while doubting the eternity of his passion. It is only when a man has had a second or third amorous attack that he begins to grow doubtful. Some women are romantic to the very end, and from eighteen to eight-and-fifty, for all I know to the contrary, are always half expecting their hearts to break. In short, when you have been in love and are in love no longer, when the King of France, with twenty thousand men, colours flying and music playing, all the pomp of war, marches up the hill and then proceeds to march right back down again, he and you are in a very absurd position.
That was exactly what Harry Warrington felt, no doubt, when he went to Kensington and met those melancholy, reproachful eyes of his cousin Maria. Yes, it is a foolish position to be in. But it is also melancholy to look up at a house you once lived in, and see black empty casements where once the welcome fires blazed. Melancholy? Yes, but ha! how bitter, how absurd, to pass by that number 13 and look up sentimentally, only to see somebody else grinning out of the window, on the best possible terms with the landlady. I always feel a little aggrieved, even at an inn I frequent, when I see other people’s trunks and boots lined up outside the rooms that were once mine. Did those muddy vulgar highlongs rest their feet on the sofa where I once reclined? Kick you out of my sight, you clumsy things!
So, considering his tenancy of Maria’s affections was well and truly over, and if her heart was not let to a new tenant yet, it was at any rate up for let, Harry never felt easy in his cousin’s company, nor she in his. He found they had nothing to say to each other that was worth hearing, and Maria’s softest accents and most melancholy pouts were far less agreeable to him now than the red lip of a well-loved white clay pipe filled with his own Virginia tobacco. So when George went to call at Kensington, Harry rarely cared to tag along, and made excuses of other engagements.
At his uncle’s house in Hill Street, the poor lad fared no better, and the virtuous folk who lived there barely paid him any attention at all. The ladies did not scruple to tell their servants to deny themselves when he called. He could scarcely believe such insincerity was possible, after all their former caresses, their warm welcomes, their repeated vows of undying affection—until one day, sitting with the Lamberts for an hour after he had called on his aunt, he saw her chairmen arrive with the empty sedan, and his aunt step out of it to go inside, not even blushing when he bowed to her from the opposite window. To be denied by his own father’s brother’s wife! To find that door which once had opened so wide to welcome him now slammed in his face! He would never have believed such coldness possible, simple honest Harry declared. He might have thought the door-knocker had a tender heart, and was not made of brass. My Lady Warrington’s virtuous, unsmiling face was no more changed than the polished brass head of that very knocker when she passed him by.
“Oh, Aunt Lambert, Aunt Lambert!” he cried, with his usual impetuosity. “Did you ever see such heartless behaviour? What have I done to offend her?”
“Do we treat you any differently, my dear Harry?” Aunt Lambert asked, glancing sideways at her youngest daughter. “The rest of the world may turn cold, but we do not belong to it. You may come to us whenever you please, in perfect safety.”
“This house is different from every other house I go to,” Harry replied. “I do not know how to say it, but I always feel quiet, and happy somehow, the moment I step over your threshold.”
“Quis me uno vivit felicior? aut magis hac est Optandum vita dicere quis potuit?” General Lambert called out, turning to his eldest son, the college student home for Easter holidays. “Do you know where I got those lines, Mr. Gownsman?”
“From Catullus, sir,” the scholar replied.
“Got no such thing, sir,” the General laughed. “I got them from my favourite Democritus Junior—old Robert Burton, who has supplied hundreds of indifferent scholars with far more learning than they would ever dig up on their own.” Burton and Montaigne, indeed, were the good General’s two favourite authors in all the world.
