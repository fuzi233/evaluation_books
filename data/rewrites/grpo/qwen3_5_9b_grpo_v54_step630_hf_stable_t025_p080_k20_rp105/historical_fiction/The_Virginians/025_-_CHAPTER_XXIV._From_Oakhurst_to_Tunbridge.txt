Mrs. Lambert stood by the roadside, her lily-white handkerchief pressed to her lips as a final token of farewell to the departing travelers. Beside her, her daughters watched the two gentlemen pace leisurely along the first few hundred yards of the road, their figures growing smaller until a clump of lime trees obscured them from view. Behind that green screen, the good matron had watched countless departures: husbands marching to battle, sons bound for school, each vanishing into the distance to return only when Heaven's will decreed. The leisure of home life fostered a tender fidelity in women; with the men gone, the house fell silent, filled only with the ache of absence. There was the vacant room where the boy had slept, the faint impression of his carpet bag still lingering on the bed, his whip hanging in the hall, his fishing rod and basket—mute memorials of brief pleasures. At dinner, the cherry tart remained untouched, for the boy had eaten his share hours ago, sandwiched between his choking sisters. Evening prayers were said without his voice joining the responses, and midnight silence descended, leaving the mother wakeful, counting the hours until the next letter might arrive. Those rustling limes formed a protective barrier between the domestic sphere and the wider world. Mrs. Lambert often became silent and contemplative when walking near these trees, wishing to carve the names of her loved ones into the grey, silvered trunks amidst the knots of true love, a custom of the time. Her daughter, Miss Theo, possessed an elegant turn for verse and composed a poem for the trees, which her delighted mother later submitted to a periodical.

"Now we are out of sight of the ladies," Colonel Lambert said, tipping his hat as the gentlemen trotted past the lime trees. "I know my wife watches from her window until we round this corner. I hope we shall see the trees and the house again, Mr. Warrington; and with the boys at home, there may be better sport for you."

"I never wish to be happier than I have been, sir," replied Mr. Warrington warmly. "I feel as if I am leaving behind old friends."

"The friend at whose house we shall sup tonight has a son who is also an old friend of our family," the Colonel continued. "My wife, an inveterate marriage-monger, would have arranged a match between him and one of my girls, but the Colonel has chosen to fall in love with someone else."

"Ah!" sighed Mr. Warrington.

"Others have done the same before Agamemnon," the Colonel added with a laugh.

"Is the gentleman's name Aga...?" the young traveler inquired meekly, having missed the name.

"No, his name is James Wolfe," the Colonel corrected, smiling. "He is still young, or what we call so, being scarce thirty. He is the youngest lieutenant-colonel in the army, barring a few scores of our nobility who take rank before us common folk."

"Of course, of course!" the Colonel's young companion remarked with true colonial notions of aristocratic precedence.

"I have seen him commanding captains who were thirty years his senior, men with neither his merit nor his good fortune. Yet, lucky as he is, no one envies his superiority, for most acknowledge him as our superior. He is beloved by every man in our old regiment, knows them all, and is a good scholar as well as a consummate soldier, a master of many languages."

"Ah, sir!" Harry Warrington sighed with great humility. "I feel I have neglected my youth sadly. I have come to England but an ignoramus. Had my dear brother been alive, he would have represented our name and our colony far better than I can. George was a scholar, a musician, capable of conversing with the most learned men in our country. Do you know, sir, I am glad to have come home, and to you especially, if only to learn how ignorant I am."

"If you know that well, 'tis a great gain already," the Colonel smiled.

"At home, especially since we lost my brother, I used to think myself a mighty fine fellow, and no doubt the folks around flattered me. I am wiser now—or I hope I am, though perhaps I am only bragging again. You see, sir, the gentry in our colony know very little, except about dogs, horses, betting, and games. I wish I knew more about books and less about them."

"Nay. Dogs and horses are very good books in their way; we can read much truth from them. Some men are not made to be scholars, yet may be worthy citizens and gentlemen in spite of their ignorance. What call have all of us to be especially learned or wise, or to take a first place in the world? His Royal Highness commands, Martin Lambert is colonel, and Jack Hunt, who rides behind yonder, was a private soldier and is now a very honest, worthy groom. If we all do our best in our station, it matters not whether that be high or low. Nay, how do we know what is high and what is low? Perhaps Jack's currycomb, my epaulets, or His Royal Highness's baton may prove equally significant. When I began life, I dreamed of success and honor; now I think of duty, and those folks we parted with just hours ago. Let us trot on, else we shall not reach Westerham before nightfall."

At Westerham, the two friends were welcomed by their hosts: a stately matron, an old soldier whose memories and services dated back fifty-four years, and his son, the Lieutenant-Colonel of Kingsley's regiment, currently stationed at Maidstone. Harry looked with curiosity at this officer, who, though young, had seen extensive service and earned a high character. There was little beauty in his face; he was very lean and pale, with red hair and high cheekbones. Yet, he possessed a fine courtesy toward elders, a cordial greeting to friends, and an animation in conversation that caused listeners to forget, and even admire, his homely features.

"Mr. Warrington is going to Tunbridge?" the hostess asked. "Their James will bear him company." She whispered something to Colonel Lambert at supper, eliciting smiles and knowing winks from the officer. He called for wine and toasted "Miss Lowther." "With all my heart," cried the enthusiastic Colonel James, draining his glass to the very last drop. His mother whispered to her friend how James and the lady were planning a match, noting that she came from the famous Lowther family of the North.

"If she were the daughter of King Charlemagne," Lambert declared, "she is not too good for James Wolfe, or for his mother's son."

"Mr. Lambert would not say so if he knew her," the young Colonel declared.

"Oh, of course, she is the priceless pearl, and you are nothing," the mother cried. "No, I am of Colonel Lambert's opinion. If she brought all of Cumberland as a jointure, I should say it was my James's due. That is the way with them, Mr. Warrington. We tend our children through fevers, measles, whooping-cough, and smallpox; we send them to the army and cannot sleep for worry; we break our hearts parting with them, having them home only for a week or two a year, or maybe ten years. And after all our care, there comes a lass with bright eyes, and away goes our boy, caring not a fig for us afterwards."

"And pray, my dear, how did you come to marry James's father?" the elder Colonel Wolfe asked. "And why didn't you stay at home with your parents?"

"Because James's father was gouty and needed someone to care for him, I suppose; not because I liked him a bit," the lady answered. Thus, with easy talk and kindness, the evening passed.

On the morrow, Colonel Lambert, expressing much kindness and friendship for his late guest, handed the young Virginian over to Mr. Wolfe's charge and turned his horse's head homeward, while the two gentlemen sped toward Tunbridge Wells. Wolfe was eager to reach the destination; Harry Warrington was perhaps less so. Indeed, as Lambert rode toward his home, Harry's thoughts followed him with longing desire for the parlor at Oakhurst, where he had spent three days in happy calm. Mr. Wolfe agreed with all of Harry's enthusiastic praise of the Lambert family. "To have such a good name and to live such a life as Colonel Lambert's," Wolfe said, "seems to me now the height of human ambition."

"And glory and honor?" Warrington asked. "Are those nothing? Would you give up the winning of them?"

"They were my dreams once," the Colonel answered, his ideas of happiness now different. "Now my desires are much more tranquil. I have followed arms since I was fourteen. I have seen almost every kind of duty connected with my calling. I know all the garrison towns in this country and have served wherever there was work to be done during the last ten years. I have done nearly the whole of a soldier's duty, except the command of an army, which can hardly be hoped for by one of my years. Now, methinks, I would like quiet, books to read, a wife to love me, and some children to dandle on my knee. I have imagined such an Elysium for myself, Mr. Warrington. True love is better than glory; and a tranquil fireside, with the woman of your heart seated by it, is the greatest good the gods can send us."

Harry imagined the picture his comrade described. He said "Yes" to the remark, but his assent was not cheerful, for his companion noted the expression on his face.

"You say 'Yes' as if a fireside and a sweetheart were not particularly to your taste," Wolfe observed.

"Why, look you, Colonel, there are other things a young fellow might like to enjoy. You have had sixteen years of the world; I am but a few months away from my mother's apron-strings. When I have seen a campaign or two, or six, as you have; when I have distinguished myself like Mr. Wolfe and made the world talk of me, then I may think of retiring from it."

To these remarks, Mr. Wolfe, whose heart was full of a very different matter, replied with a further encomium of the joys of marriage and a special rhapsody on the beauties and merits of his mistress—a theme intensely interesting to himself, though perhaps not so to his hearer, whose views on married life, if he entertained any, were somewhat melancholy and despondent. A pleasant afternoon brought them to the end of their ride, unaccompanied by accident or incident, save perhaps for a mistake Harry made a few miles from Tunbridge Wells, where two horsemen stopped them. Harry, pistol in hand, was about to charge, supposing them to be highwaymen. Colonel Wolfe, laughing, bade him reserve his fire, explaining that these were merely innkeepers' agents, not robbers (except in their calling). Gumbo, whose horse ran away at that critical moment, was brought back after much bawling from his master, and the two gentlemen rode into the little town, alighted at their inn, and then separated, each seeking the ladies he had come to visit.

Mr. Warrington found his aunt installed in handsome lodgings, attended by a guard of London lacqueys in her anteroom and following her chair when she went abroad. She received him with the utmost kindness. His cousin, Lady Maria, was absent upon his arrival; it is unknown whether the young gentleman was unhappy at not seeing her, whether he disguised his feelings, or whether Madame de Bernstein took note of them.

A beau in a richly figured suit—the first of his kind Harry had seen—and two dowagers with voluminous hoops and plenty of rouge were visiting the Baroness when her nephew made his bow. She introduced the young man to these personages as her nephew, the young Croesus from Virginia, of whom they had heard. She spoke of the immensity of his estate, as large as Kent and infinitely more fruitful. She mentioned how her half-sister, Madam Esmond, was called Princess Pocahontas in her own country. She never tired of praising mother and son, their riches, and their good qualities. The beau shook the young man's hand, delighted to make his acquaintance. The ladies praised him so loudly to his aunt that the modest youth was fain to blush at their compliments. They departed to inform the Tunbridge society of his arrival, and the little place soon buzzed with accounts of the wealth, good breeding, and good looks of the Virginian.

"You could not have come at a better moment, my dear," the Baroness said to her nephew as her visitors departed with many curtseys and congees. "Those three individuals have the most active tongues in the Wells. They will trumpet your good qualities in every company they go. I have introduced you to a hundred people already, and Heaven help me! have told all sorts of fibs about the geography of Virginia to describe your estate. It is a prodigiously large one, but I am afraid I have magnified it. I have filled it with all sorts of wonderful animals, gold mines, spices; I am not sure I have not said diamonds. As for your negroes, I have given your mother armies of them, and in fact represented her as a sovereign princess reigning over a magnificent dominion. So she has a magnificent dominion: I cannot tell to a few hundred thousand pounds how much her yearly income is, but I have no doubt it is very great. And you must prepare, sir, to be treated here as the heir-apparent of this royal lady. Do not let your head be turned. From this day forth, you are going to be flattered as you have never been flattered in your life."

"And to what end, ma'am?" the young gentleman asked. "I see no reason why I should be reputed so rich or get so much flattery."

"In the first place, sir, you must not contradict your old aunt, who has no desire to be made a fool of before her company. And as for your reputation, you must know we found it here almost ready-made upon our arrival.
