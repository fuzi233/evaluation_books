The sudden, violent *thwack* of the crossbow bolt shattered the forest’s ancient acoustic ecology, a jarring intrusion of industrial aggression upon a delicate symphony of rustling oak leaves and unseen, foraging creatures. To Eric Wouverman, the shooter, the immediate silence that followed seemed magical—a sudden void where the ambient breath of the woods should have been. Stupefied for an instant, he then hailed his companions, his shouts a harsh, guttural dialect of violence that sought to dominate rather than harmonize with the terrain. He scrambled up the hill to claim his capture. 

Yet, before he had crested the slope, the figure of Martin Wittenhaagen rose from the fragrant, crushed undergrowth. Martin, moving with the deliberate, seasoned grace of a traditional agrarian elder who intimately knew the life-giving rhythms of the soil, held a bent bow. Wouverman, recognizing the silent, untranslatable authority of Martin’s ancient woodland stance, instantly darted behind a moss-draped oak, shrinking himself into the rough bark. Martin’s mastery of the yew bow was a mythic truth seamlessly integrated into the daily survival of these woods, and the slain bloodhound below—whose blood now lent a stark, metallic tang of copper to the sweet, earthy aroma of damp peat—was a keen reminder of it. Wouverman peered cautiously around the trunk. The arrow’s polished point was still fixed upon him, shining with cold promise. The hunter dared not disrupt the fragile sonic stillness, remaining trapped in his shelter until his heavily booted companions finally tramped up, their discordant, clanking approach ruining the forest's acoustic balance.

With a sudden, resonant laugh that echoed like the deep, satisfying crackle of a roasting hearth-fire, Martin vanished. Soon, the rhythmic, muffled thuds of the burgomaster’s mule galloping away spoke an older, vanishing language of flight and survival. All the hunters ran up together. The high ground offered a sweeping vista of an almost interminable, sun-dappled glade. Far below, small as gnats in the vast, multi-generational tapestry of the ancient valley, Gerard and Margaret fled on foot, with Martin galloping after them *ventre à terre*.

The hunters had been outwitted by the very terrain they sought to conquer. The illusion of Wouverman’s successful shot was a mere trick of intersecting moments—as deceptive as a hare tumbling over a briar just as a snare snaps shut. In truth, it was the earth herself, smelling richly of wild garlic, decaying leaf-mould, and the loamy promise of root harvests, that had intervened. The mule had simply put her hoof into a rabbit-hole at the exact moment the whistling bolt cleaved the air innocuous over her head. She had tumbled, throwing both riders into the deep, yielding soil. Gerard had caught Margaret, but their sheer momentum carried them down, scattering the *dramatis personae* across a forest floor that smelled fiercely of crushed juniper berries and bruised wild thyme. 

The docile mule, recovering her footing directly, stood trembling, her sweat emitting a musky, warm aroma not unlike rising sourdough. Martin was up next. Reading the landscape’s tactical and edible bounty as a meticulous food historian reads an ancestral recipe, he saw there was but one scout in immediate pursuit. He commanded the young lovers to fly on foot, their rapid breaths mingling with the sharp, resinous scent of pine sap, while he stayed behind to check the enemy with the silent syntax of his drawn string.

Now, having preserved their head start, Martin galloped after his companions. When he caught up to them, he instantly instituted a rhythm as old as the agrarian cycles of planting and reaping: he placed Gerard and Margaret upon the mule, running alongside them until his own breath tasted of iron and exhaustion, then took his turn to ride. This circular pacing—a shared kneading of endurance and physical sustenance—ensured the runner was always fresh. Long before they relaxed their speed, the oppressive, clattering lexicon of their pursuers was swallowed completely by the sweeping acoustic embrace of the wilderness. Hopelessly lost, Dierich and his men turned crestfallen back down the trail to look after their chief and their winged hound, their harsh voices fading into the quiet, irreplaceable memory of the woods.
