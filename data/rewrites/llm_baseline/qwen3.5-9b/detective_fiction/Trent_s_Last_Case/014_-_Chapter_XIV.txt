"If you insist," Trent said, his voice carrying a distinct note of resignation, "I suppose you will have your way. But I had much rather compose this missive when you are not within earshot. However, if I must proceed, fetch me a tablet whiter than a star, or a sheet of note-paper unblemished by your address. Do not mistake this for mere formality; I am making a profound sacrifice. I have never felt less inclined toward correspondence in my entire life."

She smiled and handed him the stationery. "Very well. What shall I say?" he asked, his pen hovering uncertainly above the pristine page. "Shall I compare him to a summer's day? Or... what *should* I say?"

"Simply say what you need to say," she suggested helpfully.

He shook his head vigorously. "The sentiment I've been desperate to express to every man, woman, and child I've encountered for the last twenty-four hours is: 'Mabel and I are betrothed, and all is gas and gaiters.' But that certainly lacks the gravitas required for a letter of strictly formal, indeed, sinister character. I have managed only as far as 'Dear Mr. Marlowe.' What follows?"

"I am enclosing a manuscript," she prompted, "which I believe you might find relevant."

"Do you realize," he countered, "that in that sentence there are only two words exceeding a single syllable? This letter is designed to impress, not to induce comfort. We require vocabulary of considerable length."

"I fail to see the necessity," she replied. "While I acknowledge it is customary, I wonder why. I have received countless letters from attorneys and businessmen; they invariably open with phrases like 'with reference to our aforementioned communication' and continue in that vein until the end. Yet, when I meet them face-to-face, they do not speak thus. It strikes me as absurd."

"It is hardly absurd to them," Trent stated, setting the pen down with an air of relief and rising to his feet. "Allow me to elucidate. Our nation, being somewhat reluctant to exercise its intellect, manages quite adequately with a remarkably small and simple lexicon. Consequently, long words are anomalous. Like all anomalies, they tend to be either comically ridiculous or dreadfully solemn. Consider the phrase 'intelligent anticipation.' Were such a construct uttered in any other European country, it would pass unnoticed. Here, however, it has become a proverb; we all chuckle upon hearing it in a speech or reading it in a headline, regarding it as a pinnacle of wit. Why? Merely because it comprises two lengthy terms. The concept itself is as mundane as cold mutton. Then there is 'terminological inexactitude.' We still roar with laughter at that! The entire jest rests solely on the elongation of the words. It is identical when we seek to appear profoundly serious; we signal our gravity by resorting to polysyllabic obscurities. When a solicitor begins a sentence with 'pursuant to the instructions previously conveyed to our representative,' he feels he has earned his six-and-eightpence. Do not scoff! It is undeniably true.

"Now, consider the Continental mindset. They are perpetually preoccupied with concepts themselves. As a result, every shopkeeper or peasant possesses a vocabulary in daily usage that remains entirely Greek to the vast majority of Britons. Some years ago, I dined with a friend—a Parisian cab driver. We ate at a grimy little establishment opposite the central post office, surrounded by cabbies and porters. The conversation was lively and universal, yet it occurred to me that a London cabman would have felt distinctly out of his depth. Terms like 'functionary,' 'unforgettable,' 'exterminate,' and 'independence' flew across the table with alarming frequency. And these were merely ordinary, boisterous, red-faced drivers. Mind you," he added hastily as she moved to retrieve his pen, "I mention this solely to illustrate my point. I am not suggesting cabmen should aspire to intellectualism. I align with Keats: 'Happy is England, sweet her artless cabmen, enough their simple loveliness for me.' But when we turn to the collective industrial brainpower of the nation..."

"Oh, no, no, no!" interrupted Mrs. Manderson sharply. "I know nothing at the moment save that your discourse must cease immediately if we are to progress with this letter to Mr. Marlowe. You shall not evade this task. Come!"

She placed the pen firmly into his palm. Trent regarded it with visible distaste. "Be warned," he said dejectedly, "not to dampen my spirits. Believe me, men who remain silent are far more difficult to endure than those who chatter. 'O have a care of natures that are mute.' I confess I am avoiding this composition. It borders on indecency. To draft such a letter while seated in the same room as you creates a dissonance of moods."

She guided him back to his chair before the desk and gently urged him to sit. "Very well, but please attempt it. I wish to witness your handwriting, and I desire it sent forthwith. You claim you must uncover the truth; if so, let it be done swiftly. I would be content to leave matters as they stand, but since you feel compelled to investigate, I want resolution now. You possess the ability if you choose. Write it, and I shall dispatch it the instant it is finished. Do you not occasionally feel that intense urge—to thrust the agonizing letter into the postbox, to cast it adrift so it cannot be recalled, rendering further fretting futile?"

"I shall comply with your wishes," he conceded, turning to the paper, which he dated from his hotel.

Mrs. Manderson glanced down at his bowed head, a soft light in her eyes, raising a hand as though to smooth his unruly hair, but she withdrew it. Moving silently to the piano, she began to play very softly. Ten minutes passed before Trent broke the silence.

"If he elects to reply stating he will say nothing?"

Mrs. Manderson looked over her shoulder. "Naturally, he dares not adopt that stance. He will speak to forestall your denunciation."

"But I have no intention of doing so anyway," she insisted. "You would forbid it—you explicitly said so; moreover, I refuse if you object. The evidence is too tenuous now."

"But," she laughed lightly, "does poor Mr. Marlowe know that you will not?"

Trent sighed. "What extraordinary constructs are codes of honor!" he murmured absently. "There are actions I would perform without hesitation, yet which would disgrace you if executed—such as administering a black eye to anyone who grossly insults me, or swearing violently after striking my shin in a darkened room. And now, you calmly advise me to bluff Marlowe via a tacit threat I do not intend to utter—a maneuver that even hell's most abandoned fiend would never employ in the drunkenness of guilt—well, regardless, I shall not do it."

He resumed writing, and the lady, wearing an indulgent smile, returned to her quiet accompaniment. Shortly thereafter, Trent announced, "Finally, I am his faithfully. Would you care to review it?"

She crossed the dim room, switching on a reading lamp beside the desk. Leaning affectionately against his shoulder, she read the following:

>
> You will perhaps recall that we met, under unfortunate circumstances, in June of last year at Marlstone. On that occasion, acting in my capacity as a representative of a newspaper, it was my duty to conduct an independent inquiry into the circumstances surrounding the death of the late Sigsbee Manderson. I undertook this investigation and arrived at specific conclusions. You may deduce from the enclosed manuscript—which was originally drafted as a dispatch for my publication—what those conclusions were. For reasons requiring no elaboration, I decided at the eleventh hour neither to publish them nor to communicate them to you; consequently, they are known to only two individuals besides myself.
>
> Quite recently, however, facts have come to my attention which have compelled me to alter my decision. I do not imply that I intend to publish my findings, but rather that I have resolved to approach you and request a private statement. Should you possess information capable of casting the matter in a different light, I can conceive of no valid reason for withholding it.
>
> Accordingly, I await your response indicating when and where I may call upon you, unless you prefer the interview to occur at my hotel. In either event, I desire that Mr. Cupples, whom you will undoubtedly remember and who has reviewed the enclosed document, be present as well.
>
> Faithfully yours,
>

"What a remarkably stiff letter!" she observed. "I am convinced you could not have rendered it stiffer within your own chambers."

Trent slid the letter and its enclosure into a long envelope. "Indeed," he agreed. "I believe it will cause him to sit bolt upright. Now, this matter must not risk failure. It would be prudent to entrust it to a special messenger with explicit orders to deliver it personally. If he is absent, it should not be left."

She nodded. "I can arrange that. Wait here briefly."
