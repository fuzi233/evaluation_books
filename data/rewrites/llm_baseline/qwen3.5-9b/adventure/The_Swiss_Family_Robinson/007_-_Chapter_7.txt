The following day, our first task was to return to the Calabash Wood to retrieve the sledge carrying the dishes, bowls, and baskets we had crafted. Only Fritz accompanied me; I instructed the other boys to stay with their mother, believing that a large party was unnecessary for this specific mission.

As we passed through the grove of evergreen oaks, we spotted our sow feasting on acorns. She showed no sign of distress from the scare we had caused her the previous day; in fact, she seemed unusually friendly, perhaps viewing us as her rescuers from the savage dogs. The woods teemed with birds, undisturbed by our approach—until Fritz fired his gun, bringing down a magnificent blue jay and two parakeets, one scarlet and the other green and gold. Just as Fritz began reloading, an odd, rhythmic noise startled us. It sounded like a muffled drumbeat, immediately raising our suspicions of nearby savages.

We crept forward with extreme caution, hiding among low bushes and thick undergrowth until we reached an open clearing. There, standing atop a fallen log, was a striking bird roughly the size of a rooster, colored a rich chestnut brown and mottled with dark brown and grey. Tufts of velvety black feathers, glossed with green, adorned its shoulders. The bird strutted and wheeled in a bizarre, stately manner, ruffling its wings and erecting its tail and neck feathers. After performing for a while to the amusement of a flock of similar-looking birds lacking these distinctive ruffs, the "drummer" spread its tail like a fan, stiffened its wings, and began beating them in rapid, short strokes. The sound grew louder, mimicking distant thunder, as the whirring wings created a swirling cloud around him. Occasionally, the wingbeats struck the decaying, hollow stump beneath him, amplifying the drumming effect.

I watched with intense fascination when a shot rang out from behind me, abruptly ending the performance. My impulsive son had transformed a delightful comedy into a tragic necessity. The enthusiastic musician fell dead from his perch, and the crowd of admirers scattered in panic. I was deeply annoyed by the cruelty of interrupting such a rare spectacle and reproached Fritz for firing without permission. I was certain we had killed a Ruffed Grouse, a fine specimen indeed.

We secured the bird on the ass, which waited patiently for our return, and continued our journey. The sledge remained safely where we had left it. Since it was still early, I decided to pursue my original intent: exploring the line of cliffs and rocky hills that ran along the entire visible coastline. I hoped to find an opening leading inland or confirm definitively that we were isolated on this stretch of coast.

Leaving the Calabash Wood, we traversed terrain abundant with manioc, potatoes, and unfamiliar plants. Pleasant streams watered the fertile soil, offering views that were both open and agreeable. My attention was caught by bushes laden with small, white berries that looked like wax and were notably sticky when touched. Recognizing the botanical name *Myrica cerifera*, I excitedly explained to Fritz that by melting and straining these berries, we could create candles. This prospect would greatly please his mother, who disliked retiring to rest merely as the sun set. While the resulting wax would be slightly more brittle than beeswax, it burned well and emitted a pleasant fragrance. With the ass helping us, we quickly gathered enough berries to fill a large canvas bag and pressed on.

Shortly thereafter, we encountered another natural wonder: a colony of birds, about the size of yellowhammers but with plain brown plumage, sharing a single dwelling. Their nests were clustered densely around the trunk and branches of a solitary tree, covered by a roof of grass, straw, and fibers that shielded them from rain and sun. Openings dotted the irregular structure, resembling rooms in a communal house. Twigs protruded from the walls, serving as perches and lookout points for the young. The entire establishment resembled a giant bath sponge. Thousands of feathered inhabitants swarmed in and out, including many beautiful little parrots that seemed to vie for space with the rightful owners.

Eager to inspect the nests closer, Fritz climbed the tree, hoping to steal a hatchling. His fingers explored several empty holes until, finally, he reached deep into a nest and received an unexpected rebuke. A powerful beak seized his finger and bit sharply, forcing him to withdraw with a cry and shake his hand to soothe the pain. Undeterred, he grabbed the offending bird, ignored its shrieks, stuffed it into his pocket, and zipped up his coat. However, he was immediately pursued by the captive's relatives, who darted from other holes, screeching and pecking at him in fury.

Fritz's prize was not one of the sociable grosbeaks—the true residents—but a charming, small green parrot. Delighted with his acquisition, he decided to tame and teach it to speak, keeping it securely imprisoned in his pocket for the moment.

This unique colony sparked a lively discussion about social behavior in nature. I pointed out examples ranging from beavers to insects like bees, wasps, ants, and corals, marveling at their collective efforts. Fritz shared his observations of ants, correcting me gently regarding the terminology of ant larvae versus eggs. I expanded on this, describing the massive, fortress-like mounds built by foreign ants, their internal cities of streets and galleries, and their formidable enemies. I detailed the life of the anteater (tamanoir), explaining how its elongated, toothless head and sticky tongue functioned as a specialized tool to devour millions of ants, twisting them onto its tongue and pulling them into its jaws. These stories captivated Fritz and held our attention for a significant portion of our walk.

Eventually, we arrived at a grove of tall trees with broad, thick leaves bearing round, fig-like fruits that tasted sour and harsh. Fritz noticed gummy resin oozing from cracks in the bark, reminiscent of the cherry-tree gum he loved collecting back home. He scraped some off, attempted to soften it, and was amazed to find it elastic, snapping back to its original shape.

"Oh, Father!" he exclaimed. "Look! This gum is elastic! Could it be India rubber?"

"What!" I cried, eager to see it. "That would be a discovery of immense value! You are absolutely right!"

"Why so valuable, Father?" Fritz asked, noting he had only seen it used for erasing pencil marks.

"India rubber," I explained, using the term *caoutchouc*, "is a milky resinous juice that flows from certain trees when tapped. Native to Brazil, Guiana, and Cayenne, the locals originally used it to coat earthen flasks, creating durable, dark-colored bottles. They also drew decorative figures on the wet resin. Today, Europeans buy these bottles cut into pieces for erasers. But the possibilities are endless! I intend to try manufacturing boots and shoes from it."

Our exploration led us soon after to the coconut wood, revealing the bay and the great promontory we named Cape Disappointment, which had marked the limit of our travels. Passing through, I noticed a smaller palm species I hadn't seen before. One had been snapped by the wind, exposing a pith with a distinct mealy texture. I was convinced this was the famous sago palm. Inside the pith, I found fat worms or maggots, recalling reports that they were eaten as a delicacy in the West Indies. Curiosity getting the better of me, I lit a fire, skewered a handful of them with salt, and roasted them. The rich fat dripping from them smelled irresistible, banishing any hesitation. I ate one like a pat of butter on a potato and found it delicious, encouraging Fritz to join in. This novel addition complemented our dinner of baked potatoes perfectly.

On our way back, a dense thicket blocked our path, so we diverted toward the sugar-brake near Cape Disappointment. We harvested a substantial bundle of sugarcane, adding it to the load alongside the bag of wax berries. Upon returning to the Calabash Wood, we transferred everything to the sledge. The ass, relieved of its burden, happily dragged the load.

We arrived at Falconhurst in the evening to a warm welcome. Our tales were listened to with great interest, and our findings eagerly examined, especially the pretty green parakeet, which enchanted the younger boys. An excellent supper awaited us, enjoyed with grateful hearts. Later, ascending to our tree-castle and securing the ladder, we retired to rest after a tiring day.

The idea of candle-making inspired the boys immediately. By the next morning, "candle" was the topic of every conversation during breakfast. Eager to begin, I declared, "Then let us become chandlers." Though I harbored doubts about lacking animal fat for a bright flame and feared forgetting the precise manufacturing steps, I hid these concerns. Under my guidance, the boys worked diligently. They stripped the berries, melting the green, sweet-smelling wax in a large iron vessel over the fire. Skimming the pure wax into a separate pot, we repeated the process until we had enough liquid. Using wicks prepared by my wife, I dipped them repeatedly into the molten wax, handing them to Fritz to hang on a bush to dry.

Though the initial coating was thin, multiple dips resulted in sturdy, respectable candles. Once the wax supply was exhausted, we hung the candles in a cool, shaded area to harden. That same night, three hours after sunset, Falconhurst was brilliantly illuminated for the first time. We were all thrilled with our success.
