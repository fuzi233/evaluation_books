February 27th dawned with a crisp, decisive air, the currents of the Florentine populace surging unmistakably toward San Marco. It was the final morning of the Carnival, yet while rumors swirled of a grand Bonfire of Vanities staged before the Old Palace, the true gravity of the day anchored the hearts of the city elsewhere. In front of the church, a semicircular barrier held back a swelling tide of humanity, a wall of bodies pressed tight enough to resist the frantic pushing of latecomers seeking entry. Within this confined space, the Dominican brethren assembled in silence, their black mantles a uniform sea against the chaotic energy outside.

Above the church door, a temporary wooden pulpit stood stark and empty. It was destined for a man who had been cast down from the very heights of the Duomo, shunned by the ecclesiastical order, and declared anathema by the citizenry upon penalty of excommunication. This man, who had dared call the Pontiff a thief who bought his chair and a tyrant whose curses were merely broken swords, had proclaimed that obedience to the Papal command was not only unlawful but a sin against Christian life. Yet, despite the Vatican's terrifying threats of divine wrath and interdicts if Florence did not renounce this "pestilential schismatic," the crowds flocked to San Marco. They communed with his hand, their faith suspended between a desperate hope for salvation and a terrifying dread of judgment. Was it possible that this friar, far from Rome, held sway over heavenly lightning greater than the official successor of Saint Peter? If God had chosen him as a prophet to rebuke a High Priest clothed unworthily, surely the signs would be undeniable. For weeks, the people had asked silently, *"What miracle?"* The promise of commerce ruin, the shaking of Italy's balance, the looming shadow of an interdict—the stakes had grown too high for mere rhetoric. Now, the question gnawed at Savonarola: *What proof?* His faith, once iron-clad, trembled under the weight of public scrutiny, wavering between the certainty of yesterday and the uncertainty of tomorrow, yet his voice remained unbroken, speaking for a crowd that demanded a sign before they would follow.

To meet this impatience, the wooden platform had been raised. As the usual friars in black took their places, chanting softly, the multitude's gaze did not yet fix on the empty wood. Instead, eyes darted from face to face, searching for the familiar features of the inner circle, noting which monks hailed from noble houses and which bore kinship with the artisans and shopkeepers forming the bulk of the crowd. Only when the choir was fully seated and the psalmody began to swell did a murmur ripple through the throng: *"Fra Girolamo must be coming now."*

It was not the sound of the chants, but the sudden, profound silence that followed, a paling light falling over thousands of upturned faces, that signaled the arrival. With the last note fading, the pulpit was no longer void. A figure, cloaked entirely in black, stepped forward and knelt, head bowed, face hidden. To the eager multitude, the time seemed to stretch into eternity as the monk knelt. The stillness was absolute, charged with a strange, electric awe that unnerved even those ready to stone him; their arms grew weak, unable to raise stones against a man who appeared to commune directly with Heaven.

Then, a subtle vibration ran through the crowd, like a passing wind or the rustle of dry leaves, as if the air itself were waiting. The friar rose, turning slowly toward the sea of faces, and partially drew back his cowl. The monotony of the chant had ceased, swallowed instantly by the force of Savonarola's flashing glance. He looked around, his eyes burning with intensity, silencing the square. Stretching out his hands, they seemed transformed from tools of labor into vessels of pure sensitivity, delicate and translucent, emanating a speech of the soul that masked his fierce, passionate visage. Lines of deep weariness etched his brow and mouth, marking forty-four years of struggle.

As he extended his hands, a wave swept through the front ranks. Men and women fell to their knees. Some knelt with fervor, others with hesitation, fearing that to kneel before an excommunicated man might invite immediate divine judgment. A third group stood firm, their hearts filled with scorn for what they called a theatrical deception, an ambitious comedy played before an impotent audience. Yet, beneath the hatred, there lay a helpless admiration for a fashion that had already conquered them.

Then the voice broke forth, clear and low, uttering the ancient words of absolution: *"Misereatur vestri."* More sank to the earth. As the tone rose, growing louder and clearer, the number of upright heads dwindled rapidly. At the climax, *"Benedicat vos omnipotens Deus,"* the words erupted into a masculine cry, a trumpet blast ringing to the extremities of the piazza, as if protesting the grip of a demon trying to stifle the blessing. Under the sound, every head bowed.

Savonarola himself collapsed onto his knees, hiding his face, consumed by a temporary exhaustion. These great jets of emotion were the price of his existence; he had told the people long ago, "Without preaching I cannot live." But it was a life that shattered him piece by piece.

Moments later, as some began to rise, most remained kneeling, their faces fixed on him. In his hands, he held a crystal vessel containing the consecrated Host.

"You remember," he addressed them, his voice carrying over the hush, "three days ago I besought you. When I hold this Sacrament before you, I ask you to pray fervently to the Most High. Pray that if this work of mine does not come from Him, He will send a fire to consume me, that I may vanish into eternal darkness, away from the Light which I have hidden with my falsity. Again, I beseech you. Make that prayer. Make it *now*."

A breathless silence fell. Perhaps no one truly prayed, save those driven by obedient habit, but every consciousness was seized by the overwhelming sense that Savonarola himself was praying. His voice was not loud, yet it cut through the wide stillness like a knife.

"Lord," he whispered, the words rising in a crescendo, "if I have not wrought in sincerity of soul, if my word cometh not from Thee, strike me in this moment with Thy thunder! Let the fires of Thy wrath enclose me!"

He ceased, standing motionless, the sacred mystery clutched in his hand, eyes lifted to heaven, his whole aspect trembling with a feverish excitement. The world around him froze. Sunlight, previously piercing the grey mist of the square in fitful streaks, suddenly parted. With a gentleness akin to a smile, a stream of brilliance poured down, illuminating the crystal vase. The light spread, washing over Savonarola's face with a mild, glorious radiance.

An instantaneous shout tore through the Piazza: *"Behold the answer!"*

The warmth of the sun thrilled through the friar's frame, merging with the roar of the crowd. It was his final moment of untroubled triumph. In a rapturous vision, he saw a grander stage yet to come, where he would stand before all Christendom, sealed as the messenger of supreme righteousness, charged anew with divine strength. But the vision lasted only a heartbeat before it dissolved.

Within seconds, Savonarola turned and vanished into the sanctuary, retreating from the glare of the square. As the sunlight returned to its impartial, mundane scattering over clean and unclean alike, the silence broke. Voices erupted in a confused clamor, certain dissonant notes and varying scales of laughter revealing that the previous kneeling had been nothing more than a submission to a spell, hostile and scornful in its heart.

"It seems to me the plaudits are giving way to criticism," observed Tito, leaning from an upper loggia opposite the church, his gaze sharp and analytical. "Nevertheless, it was a striking moment, was it not, Messer Pietro? Fra Girolamo is a man who reminds us that there was a time when the monk's frock was a symbol of power over the human mind, rather than merely the keeper of keys to women's cupboards."

"Assuredly," replied Pietro Cennini, watching the dispersing crowd with a thoughtful expression. "And until I have seen proof that Fra Girolamo possesses far less faith in God's judgments than the common rabble—and indeed, considerably more—I shall not believe that he would dare to brave Heaven in this manner if his soul were laden with a conscious lie."
