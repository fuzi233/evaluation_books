At six o'clock, the spectacle of the new Charlemagne's arrival in Florence concluded, leaving the populace with a mixture of exhaustion and awe. From the turreted rooftops, where observers watched the winding column of troops against the backdrop of green hills, the event appeared as a triumphal tableau. Without the sun to glare upon the banners or the dust to obscure the steel, the army emerged with terrifying clarity: tall, rugged Scottish archers; Swiss halberdiers, massive and menacing; agile Gascon footmen poised for climbing; and cavalrymen who resembled knight-errants mounted on warhorses. It was a force designed solely to crush the enemies of God, a certainty that transformed the display of martial splendor into a source of genuine comfort. Nobles, knights, and pages of noble birth paraded in satin scarves embroidered with pious symbols, jeweled sword hilts, and brocaded mantles, all crowned by a transcendent canopy borne aloft by select youths above the Most Christian King. As an old diarist noted, simply to witness this *fù gran magnificenza* was a privilege.

For the Signoria, however, the ceremony had been a logistical nightmare. Stationed on platforms before the gates, they were forced to march out precisely timed to meet the guest, only to find their orator, Messer Luca Corsini, rendered speechless by the sudden onset of rain. The precipitation created a chaotic urgency among men and horses alike, breaking the rhythm of his prepared Latin address and reducing the scholarly city to offering a clumsy, impromptu welcome in French. This breakdown provided Tito Melema with a critical opening. Positioned behind the Signoria amidst the throng of officials, Tito seized the moment when Soderini demanded someone speak French. Hesitating dignitaries retreated, but Tito stepped forward instantly. With the natural grace of a man accustomed to command, he delivered the requisite few words in the name of the Signoria, allowing the King to pass without further delay. When later praised for his composure—a trait that had notably deserted him during the morning's crisis—he dismissed the accolade with characteristic modesty, deflecting credit to Francesco Gaddi. In the end, vanity proved the ultimate test of character; Tito's popularity stemmed from his ability to navigate chaos while others faltered.

Yet, the day's proceedings yielded other disappointments. The Florentine procession of clergy and laity had been bottlenecked, forced onto narrow backstreets to reach the Cathedral, far from the ceremonial route. Furthermore, the young monarch, though draped in the magnificent canopy, appeared less like the legendary Charlemagne and more like a grotesque caricature. His leg was too thin, resting awkwardly on cloth of gold and pearls; his mouth was a reptilian slit, and his head possessed an exorbitant, unnatural outline. Yet, amidst the blaze of rubies and the black velvet of the canopy, these flaws were negligible distractions. The crowd roared "Francia, Francia!" with fervor proportional to the sheer opulence of the canopy, which they subsequently tore apart as spoils of war, adhering to ancient custom. Royal lips touched the altar, and despite the mishaps, the King and his retinue were lodged safely in the Palace of the Via Larga, while the terrified soldiery were encamped in the open fields of Prato.

By eight o'clock, the business of the day was done, yet the streets of Florence presented a startling transformation under the November stars. Gone was the usual gloom, broken only by feeble lamps before saintly images. Now, every house displayed suspended lights, casting a uniform glow that allowed pedestrians to traverse the city with the security of daylight. Another instance of *fù gran magnificenza*.

Tito Melema walked these illuminated thoroughfares, heading home from the Piazza di Santa Croce via the Via dei Benci. Having endured a day of concealed anxiety, he had managed to slip away unnoticed from the post-dinner festivities. Alone now, he resolved to confront his circumstances with rational adjustment, hoping to shed the childish fears that plagued him. He particularly regretted his inability to recognize Baldassarre earlier; had he succeeded, the outcome might have been more favorable, sparing his father unnecessary distress. Though he preferred Baldassarre's prosperity, the path was now closed. Conflict was impossible; survival was the only imperative.

His thoughts were interrupted by a sound that cut through the evening air—not the muffled noise of revelry, but the sharp, rhythmic clang of an anvil. Startled, Tito quickened his pace. The sound signaled hope. He recognized the source: the workshop of Niccolò Caparra, a renowned destination for those seeking exquisite ironwork. "What drives the giant to work so late?" Tito wondered. "All the better," he thought. "I can attend to this small matter tonight rather than tomorrow."

Preoccupied, he paused briefly before the workshop, drawn by the sight within. The wide doorway, situated at the truncated angle of a large block of houses, was topped by a loggia with fluted tile roofing and supported by stone columns bearing rough capitals. Inside, bathed in the red glow of the forge, stood the imposing silhouette of Niccolò. His massive frame moved with rhythmic power, his huge arms rising and falling to shape metal, occasionally revealing the firm line of his mouth and powerful brow. Flanking him were two smaller, darker figures—one tending the anvil, the other pumping the bellows—emphasizing the master smith's superior bulk.

Tito entered the doorway, his form a stark contrast to the fiery interior. He waited in silence, knowing speech was futile until Niccolò acknowledged him. Only after the smith had hammered an axe head to its perfect edge and tossed it aside did Niccolò turn. He nodded with a blunt, good-natured gesture, resting his hammer on his hip.

"What is it, Messer Tito? Business?"

"Undoubtedly, Niccolò," Tito replied smoothly. "Otherwise, I would not dare interrupt your work outside of hours, which I interpret as a sign of urgent demand."

"I've been at it all day," Niccolò grunted, wiping sweat from his brow. "Making axes and spearheads. Every fool who passes my shop sticks his head in asking if I'll go see the King of France and his soldiers. I tell them no. I don't wish to see their faces; I'd rather see their backs."

"Are you forging arms for the citizens, then? Something better than rusty scythes and spits in case of trouble?"

"We shall see," Niccolò said, his tone shifting to a sharper edge. "Arms are good, and Florence will likely need them. The Frate predicts we regain Pisa, and I agree with him. But I'd like to know how that promise holds water if we aren't supplied with quality weapons. The Frate sees far ahead; he understands sense, not the nonsense of those who think they can catch birds by blinking at them."

"You're a bit of a Medicean, Messer Tito Melema," Niccolò added, his gaze piercing. "Ebbene! So was I, in my time, before the cask began to run sour. What brings you here?"

"Merely to inquire about the price of that fine coat of mail I noticed hanging here recently. I intend to purchase it for a specific individual who requires protection beneath his doublet."

"Let him come and buy it himself," Niccolò replied bluntly. "I am particular about my clientele. I require knowledge of who stands to wear my goods."

"I understand your scruples, Niccolò. But this is defensive armor; it cannot harm anyone."

"True," the smith conceded, though his expression remained guarded. "But it may embolden the wearer to inflict harm. Besides, this piece is not mine; it is the fine work of Maso of Brescia. I would be loath to see it protect the heart of a scoundrel. I must know the identity of the purchaser."

"Well, then, to be perfectly frank, Niccolò mio, I want it myself," Tito stated, sensing persuasion was futile. "I anticipate a journey soon, and you know the perils of travel in these times. Do you suspect me of treason against the Republic?"

"No, I know no ill of you," Niccolò said, returning to his straightforward manner. "But do you possess the funds? You frequent my shop enough to know my signs; you've seen the burning account books. I trust no one. The price is twenty florins, discounted for its second-hand condition. Unlikely you carry such a sum. Perhaps we can arrange payment for tomorrow."

"I happen to have the money," Tito countered, recalling his recent winnings at play. "I will transport the armor home with me."

Niccolò reached down and lifted the finely wrought coat, which collapsed into a bundle barely larger than two handfuls. After counting the florins across his palm, he handed over the armor.

"There," he said. "Take the coat. It is designed to foil swords, daggers, and arrows. Personally, I would never don such a garment. It is akin to carrying fear within one's bosom."

The words struck Tito with unsettling resonance, yet he masked his reaction with a smile. "Ah, Niccolò, we scholars are all cowards. Wielding a pen does not thicken the arm as your hammer does. Addio!"

Tito folded the armor beneath his mantle and hurried across the Ponte Rubaconte, the weight of the garment a silent testament to the dangers awaiting him.
