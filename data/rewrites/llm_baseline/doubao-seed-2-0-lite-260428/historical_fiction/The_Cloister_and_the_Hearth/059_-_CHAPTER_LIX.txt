Happy is the man who has two strong cables to pull him through life: merit, and the favour of women. Oh, that I, like our Gerard, possessed such a "chain of ladies" to hoist me upward! I should be prose laureate, or professor of the then-fashionable spasmodic school of writing, or some such distinguished post, in no time. While I wait for that good fortune, let me sketch for you the singular Fra Colonna.

The true revivers of ancient learning and philosophy were two writers of fiction themselves: Petrarch and Boccaccio. Their work did not win great, public, immediate success in their own lifetimes, but they sowed good seed that never perished, quickening in the soil for generations while it waited for sunshine. From their day onward, Italy never lacked at least one native scholar fluent in Greek, and every learned Greek who landed on her shores was welcomed as a brother. By the end of the 14th century, Poggio, Valla, and the elder Guarino were born; and early in the 15th century, Florence under Cosmo de Medici hummed as a nest of Platonists. These scholars, led by the Greek-born Gemistus Pletho, began writing in the 1440s to argue Aristotle out of his place of honour: for few minds are broad enough to do full justice to great A without dealing great B a slanderous injustice. Theodore Gaza defended Aristotle with moderation; George of Trebizond attacked him with ferocious bitterness, and struck back at Plato in turn. Then Cardinal Bessarion, another Greek by birth, stepped in to oppose George and his Aristotelian idol in a treatise titled *Against the Slanderer of Plato*. Pugnacity, wise or unwise, is always a mark of vitality.

Born with no taste for petty controversy in that zealous age, Francesco Colonna, a young Florentine noble, lived only for the arts. At twenty he took Dominican orders, seeking nothing but quiet study. He withdrew from idle company, faction fights, and the humming, stinging chaos of the human hive, to find refuge with Saint Dominic and the Nine Muses. An eager student of languages, paintings, statues, chronology, coins, and monumental inscriptions, these last artefacts slowly loosened his faith in the popular myths peddled as official history. He travelled the East for many years, returning home laden with treasures: master of several rare manuscripts, fluent in Greek and Latin, Hebrew and Syriac.

He found his country had not stood still. Other lettered princes besides Cosmo de Medici had risen: Alfonso, King of Naples, Nicolas d'Este, Lionel d'Este, and many more. Above all, his old friend Thomas of Sarzana had been elected Pope, and had lent enormous new force to the spread of letters: he amassed 5000 manuscripts in the Vatican Library, and commissioned Poggio to translate Diodorus Siculus and Xenophon's *Cyropaedia*, Laurentius Valla to translate Herodotus and Thucydides, Theodore Gaza to translate Theophrastus, George of Trebizond to translate Eusebius and several treatises of Plato, and dozens of other similar projects. The friar found Plato and Aristotle had called a truce to their quarrel, but Poggio and Valla were at daggers drawn over tiny points of grammar, burning with that bitter *odium philologicum* that makes the lives of true book lovers feel like paradise. He settled back in his native land, his days passing as a rosy, unbroken dream.

No one is happier than the man of versatile tastes, so long as he does not have to earn his bread by his hobbies -- and Fra Colonna was versatility incarnate. He knew seven or eight languages, a little mathematics, could write verse passably, paint a little, model clay, sing a little, pluck a lute passably well, and best of all, could recognise and genuinely enjoy true excellence in every one of those fields. For that last trait alone, he deserved all the happiness he had. Gauge the intellects of your own acquaintances, and you will find precious few whose minds are not deaf, blind, or completely dead to at least one great art or science, so that "wisdom at one entrance quite shut out." Worse still, the conceited fools who lack that breadth of taste will often parade the holes in their intellects as badges of pride instead of blushing for them.

A zealot for art, the friar was a naïve sceptic in matters of religion. In every age, a small handful of men hold opinions that belong to a different era, past or future. His doubt was as simple and unassuming as his artistic enthusiasm: he looked on the religious ceremonies of his own day much the way his idols, the pagan philosophers, looked on public worship of gods and dead heroes -- harmless mummeries, good for keeping the populace content. And he drew a droll, unplanned distinction here: any Christian ceremony he traced back to purely pagan origins he respected, out of sheer reverence for antiquity, even though had he lived in the days of that original pagan rite, he would have scorned it from the heights of his own philosophy.

Fra Colonna was utterly charmed by his new scribe Gerard, and with free run of half the noble palaces in Rome, he sang his praises so loudly that patrons soon came knocking to poach the young Dutchman away. "But I am so happy with you, father," Gerard protested.

"Fiddlestick about being happy tucked away with me," Fra Colonna waved him off. "You cannot afford to be happy. You must learn to be a man of the world -- that is the great lesson I drum into all the young. The Montesini family can pay you three times what I can, and they shall too -- by Jupiter!" And the friar set a very steep price on Gerard's pen work, which the Montesini accepted without a single murmur. Manuscript copying fetched far higher rates back then than printed authors would earn for centuries after the invention of the press. Gerard received three hundred crowns just for transcribing Aristotle's treatise on rhetoric.

The great are endlessly affectionate to their pets, for as long as the fancy lasts, and in the height of the craze for Greek manuscripts, the handsome, careful scribe soon became the favourite of Roman high society: nobles of both sexes caressed him like a pampered lapdog. It would have turned a vain man's head, but the shrewd Dutchman saw the steel hand beneath the velvet glove, and never presumed on their favour. Even so, it was a proud day for him when he and Fra Colonna found themselves seated at the table of his current patron, Cardinal Bessarion. They sat nearly a mile down from the head of the table, but no matter: there Gerard saw roast pheasants dished up still covered in their feathers, as if they had just flown out of a coppice instead of off the spit, and chickens cooked sealed inside glass bottles, so tender they melted like peaches. But the greatest novelty that electrified Gerard far more than any modern reader could imagine were the napkins, surpassingly fine, folded into cocked hats, birds' wings, and fluttering fans instead of lying flat on the table.

After dinner, the tables were taken apart and carried away, and lo, under each one was a second table set out heaped high with sweetmeats. The Roman signoras and signorinas fell on them and gormandised, while the men eyed the sugar confections with reasonable suspicion. "But dear father," Gerard objected, "I do not see those two-pronged daggers men say his excellency puts in every guest's left hand."

"Nay, that is Cardinal Orsini's silly invention, that peevish little instrument for his guests to fumble their meat withal," Colonna snorted. "I hear one man, in a great hurry, skewered his tongue to his own palate with it. O tempora, O mores! The ancients, reclining godlike at their feasts, would have spurned such pedantries instantly."

As soon as the ladies had tired themselves playing among the sugarplums, those tables too were swept away, and all the guests sat lined up against the wall. Then two clerics entered, ducking and scraping, carrying lutes, knelt at the cardinal's feet, and sang the daily office, then retired with deep obeisance. The cardinal answered their respect by fingering his skullcap the way the later Iron Duke of Wellington used to tip his hat, and the whole company dispersed. Gerard had just dined with a cardinal who had thrice come within a hair's breadth of being elected Pope.

But greater honour was still in store for him. One day the cardinal sent for him, praised the flawless beauty of his script, and took him in his own coach to the Vatican, up a private stair to a luxurious little chamber with a great oriel window. It was fitted out with inkstands, slanted writing frames, and every tool of a scribe's trade. The cardinal whispered a word to a courtier, and soon the Pope's private secretary arrived carrying a glorious, grimy old manuscript of Plutarch's Lives. Soon Gerard was seated alone copying it, awe-struck, half-delighted at the thought that his Holiness would one day hold his work and read it. The papal inkstands were all gilded and glorious on the outside, but the ink inside was thin, vile rubbish. But Gerard always carried good homemade ink in a dirty little inkhorn slung at his belt; he knelt for a moment to pray for a firm, skilful hand, and set to work.

One whole side of his room was almost occupied by a massive heavy curtain, divided down the centre, its ample folds overlapping. For a long while Gerard resisted the growing impulse to peep through the gap, but at last it overpowered him. He left Plutarch half-written, stole across the matted floor, gathered up the folds of the curtain gently with his fingers, and poked his nose through the chink -- right against the cold steel of a halbert. Two fully armed soldiers were standing behind the curtain, their glittering weapons crossed in a triangle. Gerard drew swiftly back, but in that instant he heard the soft murmur of voices, and caught a glimpse of a whole group of people cringing before some hidden figure on the other side. He never repeated the attempt to pry through that guarded curtain, though he found his eyes drawn to it again and again. Every hour or so an ecclesiastic would peep in, stare hard at him, chill his blood, and leave without a word. It was all gloomy, mechanical, lifeless.

The next day a gentleman, richly armed, bounced in and glared at him. "What is going on here?" he demanded. Gerard told him he was writing out Plutarch, with the help of the saints. The spark said he did not know any Signor of that name. Gerard explained the circumstances of time and space that had deprived Signor Plutarch of the honour of the young noble's acquaintance. "Oh! One of those old dead Greeks they make such a coil over."

"Ay, signor, one of them who, being dead, yet live."

"I understand you not, young man," the noble said, drawing himself up with all the dignity of complete ignorance. "What did the old fellow write? Love stories? Merry tales, like Boccaccio?"

"Nay, lives of heroes and sages."

"Soldiers and popes?"

"Soldiers and princes."

"Will you read me of them some day?"

"And willingly, signor. But what would my employers say, were I to break off work?"

"Oh never heed that! Know you not who I am? I am Jacques Bonaventura, nephew to his Holiness the Pope, and captain of his guards. I came here to look after my fellows. I trow they have turned them out of their own room to make space for you." He hurried away after that.

This lively companion soon got into the habit of running in on the little chamber, and finding Gerard excellent company, he popped in constantly, chattering trivial court gossip while Gerard wrote the immortal lives. One day he came in a changed, moody man, threw himself into a chair, and cried, "Ah, traitress! traitress!" Gerard inquired after his ill, but only got the same reply: "Traitress! traitress!" So Gerard went back to writing Plutarch. After a minute Bonaventura sighed, "I am melancholy, and for Our Lady's sake read me a story out of Ser Plutarcho, to soothe my bile. In all that Greek is there nought about lovers betrayed?" Gerard read him the life of Alexander. He got so excited he marched up and down the room, threw his arms around the scribe, and vowed to shun "soft delights," that bed of nettles, and follow glory forever.

Who so happy now as Gerard? His art was honoured, fabulous prices were paid for it; in a year or two he would return by sea to Holland, his pockets heavy with good store of gold, and set up with his beloved Margaret in Bruges, or Antwerp, or dear Augsburg, and end their days in peace, and love, and healthy, happy labour. His heart never strayed an instant from her. In his prosperity he did not forget poor Pietro. He took Fra Colonna to see his friend's masterpiece. The friar inspected it severely and closely, fell on the artist's neck, and carried the painting to one of the noble Colonna family, who gave a noble price for it. Pietro moved down to the first floor, and lived like a gentleman. But Gerard remained in his garret. To increase his expenses would have been to postpone his return to Margaret. Luxury had no charms for that single-hearted young man, when set against the joy of seeing his love again.

Jacques Bonaventura made him acquainted with crowds of other gay young fellows of the papal court. They loved him, and sought to entice him into vice and reckless spending. But he begged humbly to be excused, and escaped that temptation. But a far greater trial was waiting for him just around the corner.
