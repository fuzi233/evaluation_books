"Old longings nomadic leap,
Chafing at custom's chain;
Again from its brumal sleep
Wakens the ferine strain."

Buck did not read the newspapers, nor would he have known that a storm was gathering—not just for him, but for every tide-water dog of strong muscle and warm, long hair stretching from Puget Sound to San Diego. Men, groping blindly in the Arctic dark, had stumbled upon a yellow metal. Steamships and transport lines were booming the news, and thousands of men were rushing north. They wanted dogs, specifically heavy ones with powerful muscles to toil and thick furs to withstand the frost.

Buck lived in a sun-drenched domain in the Santa Clara Valley, the estate of Judge Miller. The house stood back from the road, half-hidden among trees that framed glimpses of a wide, cool veranda circling its four sides. Gravelled driveways wound through sprawling lawns beneath the interlacing boughs of tall poplars. Behind the house, the scale was even grander: great stables where a dozen grooms and boys gathered, rows of vine-clad servants' cottages, endless outhouses, long grape arbors, green pastures, orchards, and berry patches. There was even a pumping plant for the artesian well and a massive cement tank where the Judge's boys plunged to cool off in the heat.

Over this vast demesne Buck reigned. Born here and living here for four years, he was the undisputed sovereign. While other dogs existed—Toots, the Japanese pug, and Ysabel, the Mexican hairless, who rarely ventured outdoors; or the score of fox terriers who yelped fearful threats at them from behind windows—they were insignificant. Buck was neither a pampered house-dog nor a kennel-bound animal. He swam in the tank, hunted with the Judge's sons, escorted the daughters, Mollie and Alice, on twilight rambles, and lay at the Judge's feet before roaring library fires. He carried the grandsons on his back, rolled them in the grass, and guarded their footsteps through wild adventures down to the stable yard and beyond. Among the terriers he stalked with imperial disdain, ignoring the pugs entirely, for he was king over all creeping, crawling, and flying things on Judge Miller's place, humans included.

His father, Elmo, a massive St. Bernard, had been the Judge's inseparable companion, and Buck seemed destined to follow in his father's footsteps. Though lighter than his sire, weighing only one hundred and forty pounds due to his mother, Shep, a Scotch shepherd, Buck possessed a dignity born of good living and universal respect. He carried himself royally. For four years, he had lived as a sated aristocrat, proud and slightly egotistical, insulated by his privileged position. Yet, he had avoided the fate of a mere pampered pet. Hunting and outdoor delights had kept his muscles hard and his frame lean, while his love for the cold tub had served as a tonic and health preserver.

It was the fall of 1897, the year the Klondike strike dragged men from across the globe into the frozen North. Buck, however, remained oblivious to the shifting tides of the world. He did not know that Manuel, one of the gardener's helpers, was an undesirable acquaintance. Manuel suffered from a singular vice: a love for the Chinese lottery. Worse still, he possessed a fatal weakness—a blind faith in a gambling system. Such a system demands capital, yet the wages of a gardener's helper barely covered the needs of a wife and numerous children.

On a memorable night, while the Judge attended a meeting of the Raisin Growers' Association and the boys organized an athletic club, Manuel committed his treachery. No one saw him and Buck slip away through the orchard, pretending on a simple stroll. Except for a solitary stranger, no one witnessed their arrival at the little flag station known as College Park. There, the stranger spoke with Manuel, and coins chinked between them.

"You might wrap up the goods before you deliver 'em," the stranger said gruffly. Manuel doubled a piece of stout rope around Buck's neck, beneath his collar. "Twist it, an' you'll choke 'm plentee," Manuel advised, and the stranger grunted affirmation.

Buck accepted the rope with quiet dignity. He trusted the men he knew and credited them with a wisdom surpassing his own. But when the loose ends were handed to the stranger, Buck growled menacingly. He had merely intimated his displeasure, believing in his pride that an intimation was a command. To his shock, the rope tightened, cutting off his breath. In quick rage, he sprang at the man, who met him halfway, grappled him by the throat, and with a deft twist threw him onto his back. The rope tightened mercilessly. Buck struggled in a fury, tongue lolling, chest heaving futilely. Never in his life had he been so vilely treated, nor so enraged. Yet his strength ebbed, his eyes glazed, and he knew nothing when the train was flagged and the two men tossed him into the baggage car.

The next moment, he was dimly aware of a throbbing tongue and the violent jolting of a conveyance. The hoarse shriek of a locomotive signaled a crossing. Having ridden in baggage cars often with the Judge, he knew the sensation. Opening his eyes, he found within them the unbridled anger of a kidnapped king. The man lunged for his throat, but Buck was too quick. His jaws clamped onto the hand, refusing to release until his senses were choked out once more.

"Yep, has fits," the man muttered, hiding his mangled hand from the baggageman drawn by the struggle. "I'm takin' 'm up for the boss to 'Frisco. A crack dog-doctor there thinks he can cure 'm."

That night's ride ended in a shed behind a saloon on the San Francisco waterfront.

"All I get is fifty for it," the man grumbled, "and I wouldn't do it over for a thousand, cold cash." His hand was wrapped in a bloody handkerchief, his right trouser leg ripped from knee to ankle.

"How much did the other mug get?" the saloon-keeper demanded.

"A hundred," was the reply. "Wouldn't take a sou less, so help me."

"That makes a hundred and fifty," the saloon-keeper calculated. "And he's worth it, or I'm a squarehead."

The kidnapper unwrapped the bloody bandages to inspect his lacerated hand. "If I don't get the hydrophoby—"

"It'll be because you was born to hang," the saloon-keeper laughed. "Here, lend me a hand before you pull your freight."

Dazed, suffering intolerable pain in his throat and tongue, with the life half-throttled from him, Buck attempted to face his tormentors. But he was thrown down and choked repeatedly until they succeeded in filing the heavy brass collar from his neck. The rope was removed, and he was flung into a cage-like crate.

There he lay through the weary night, nursing his wrath and wounded pride. He could not comprehend the chaos. What did these strange men want with him? Why keep him pent in this narrow box? A vague sense of impending calamity oppressed him. Several times, when the shed door rattled open, he sprang to his feet, expecting the Judge or the boys. Each time, however, it was the bulging face of the saloon-keeper peering in by the sickly light of a tallow candle. Each time, the joyful bark trembling in Buck's throat twisted into a savage growl.

The saloon-keeper left him alone, and in the morning, four men entered to pick up the crate. More tormentors, Buck decided; they were evil-looking, ragged, and unkempt. He stormed and raged at them through the bars. They only laughed and poked sticks at him, which he promptly assailed with his teeth until he realized that was exactly what they wanted. Sullenly, he lay down and allowed the crate to be lifted into a wagon. Thus began his passage through many hands. Clerks in the express office took charge; he was carted in another wagon; a truck carried him, mixed with boxes and parcels, onto a ferry steamer; he was trucked off the steamer into a great railway depot, and finally deposited in an express car.

For two days and nights, this express car was dragged at the tail of shrieking locomotives. Buck neither ate nor drank. In his anger, he had met the first advances of the express messengers with growls, and they retaliated by teasing him. When he flung himself against the bars, quivering and frothing, they laughed and taunted him, growling and barking like detestable dogs, mewling, flapping their arms, and crowing. It was all very silly, he knew, but therefore the more outrage to his dignity, and his anger waxed to a fever pitch. He did not mind the hunger so much, but the lack of water caused severe suffering, fanning his wrath. High-strung and finely sensitive, the ill treatment had thrown him into a fever, fed by the inflammation of his parched, swollen throat and tongue.

He was glad for one thing: the rope was off his neck. That had given them an unfair advantage; now that it was gone, he would show them. They would never get another rope around his neck. Upon that, he was resolved. For two days and nights he neither ate nor drank, accumulating a fund of wrath that boded ill for whoever first fell foul of him. His eyes turned bloodshot, and he was metamorphosed into a raging fiend. So changed was he that the Judge himself would not have recognized him. The express messengers breathed with relief when they bundled him off the train at Seattle.

Four men gingerly carried the crate from the wagon into a small, high-walled backyard. A stout man in a red sweater that sagged generously at the neck emerged and signed the book for the driver. That was the next tormentor, Buck divined, and he hurled himself savagely against the bars. The man smiled grimly and brought a hatchet and a club.

"You ain't going to take him out now?" the driver asked.

"Sure," the man replied, driving the hatchet into the crate to pry it open.

An instantaneous scattering occurred among the four men who had carried it in, scrambling to safe perches atop the wall to watch the performance.

Buck rushed at the splintering wood, sinking his teeth into it, surging and wrestling with it. Wherever the hatchet fell on the outside, he was there on the inside, snarling and growling, as furiously anxious to get out as the man in the red sweater was intent on getting him out.

"Now, you red-eyed devil," the man said, having made an opening sufficient for Buck's body. He dropped the hatchet and shifted the club to his right hand.

And Buck was truly a red-eyed devil as he drew himself together for the spring, hair bristling, mouth foaming, a mad glitter in his bloodshot eyes. Straight at the man he launched his one hundred and forty pounds of fury, surcharged with the pent passion of two days and nights. In mid-air, just as his jaws were about to close, he received a shock that checked his body and brought his teeth together with an agonizing click. He whirled over, hitting the ground on his back and side. He had never been struck by a club in his life and did not understand. With a snarl that was part bark and more scream, he was on his feet again and launched into the air. Again the shock came, crushing him to the ground. This time he was aware it was the club, but his madness knew no caution. A dozen times he charged, and as often the club broke the charge and smashed him down.

After a particularly fierce blow, he crawled to his feet, too dazed to rush. He staggered limply, blood flowing from his nose, mouth, and ears, his beautiful coat sprayed and flecked with bloody slaver. Then the man advanced and deliberately dealt him a frightful blow on the nose. All the pain he had endured was nothing compared to the exquisite agony of this. With a roar almost lionlike in its ferocity, he hurled himself at the man again. But the man, shifting the club from right to left, coolly caught him by the under-jaw, wrenching downward and backward. Buck described a complete circle in the air, then crashed to the ground on his head and chest.

For the last time he rushed. The man struck the shrewd blow he had purposely withheld, and Buck crumpled up, knocked utterly senseless.

"He's no slouch at dog-breakin', that's wot I say," one of the men on the wall cried enthusiastically.

"Druther break cayuses any day, and twice on Sundays," was the reply of the driver as he climbed onto the wagon and started the horses.

Buck's senses returned, but not his strength. He lay where he had fallen, watching the man in the red sweater.

"'Answers to the name of Buck,'" the man soliloquized, quoting from the saloon-keeper's letter announcing the consignment. "Well, Buck, my boy," he continued in a genial voice, "we've had our little ruction, and the best thing we can do is to let it go at that. You've learned your place, and I know mine. Be a good dog and all 'll go well and the goose hang high. Be a bad dog, and I'll whale the stuffin' outa you. Understand?"

As he spoke, he fearlessly patted the head he had so mercilessly pounded. Though Buck's hair involuntarily bristled at the touch of the hand, he endured it without protest. When the man brought him water, he drank eagerly, and later bolted a generous meal of raw meat, chunk by chunk, from the man's hand.

He was beaten—he knew that—but he was not broken. He saw, once for all, that he stood no chance against a man with a club.
