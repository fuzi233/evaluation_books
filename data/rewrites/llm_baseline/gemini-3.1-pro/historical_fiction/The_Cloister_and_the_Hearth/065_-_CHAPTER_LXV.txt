Two days after the tempestuous scene that shattered his spirit, the heavens above Rome cracked open, unleashing a thunderstorm of mythic fury that felt less like weather and more like an ancestral judgment. The torrential rain, smelling sharply of ozone and bruised dust, drove Gerard into a low hostelry tucked beneath the crumbling arches of an ancient street. He sought refuge not just from the wet, but from the deafening, fractured echoes of his own mind. 

He called for wine, and the innkeeper brought a heavy clay flagon of unaged Trebbiano. It was a rustic pour, sharply acidic on the tongue, tasting of damp cellar earth, crushed grape stems, and the wild, unpruned vines of the Campagna. For generations, agrarian elders had pressed such harvests by foot, preserving the cyclical rhythm of the seasons in every amphora. Yet Gerard, a man who once revered the profound, communal meaning of such sustenance, now drank only to obliterate the present. As the rain battered the thatch outside, he drank until the sharp vinegar notes faded into a dull, oxidized stupor, resting his heavy head upon his hands. His nostrils filled with the rich olfactory landscape of the hearth—the scent of spilled wine, white wood-ash, and the heavy, dripping fat of a mutton haunch roasting slowly over spitting olive-wood coals. 

In course of time, the hostelry began to fill. The muffled, rhythmic acoustic sanctuary of the rain was entirely entirely overwhelmed by an oppressive, discordant din. The screech of dragging wooden benches, the clatter of trencher plates, and the rough, barking laughter of the Roman underclass created a toxic sonic environment that battered Gerard’s fragile psychological state. It was the commercialized noise of desperate survival, and it pulled him unpleasantly from his daze.

Beneath the roaring clamor of the tavern, an insidious, whispering undercurrent caught his ear, a low frequency that seemed to corrupt the very air. Two figures sat nearby. One was a pardoner, dressed with a clean, modest precision. The other, reeked faintly of stale garlic and sour sweat; his slouched hat was pulled deep over his brows to hide his face, marking him as a creature who shunned the honest light of day. They were driving a dark bargain in the market of sin, trading in a dominant, institutional lexicon that commodified the soul.

The man in the slouched hat complained of the soaring prices of pardons. "If they go up any higher, we poor fellows shall be shut out of heaven altogether," he muttered. 

The pardoner denied the charge, his voice a smooth, commercial drone. "Indulgences were never cheaper to good husbandmen." When asked who these husbandmen were, the pardoner replied, "Why, such as sin by the market, like reasonable creatures. But if you will be so perverse as go and pick out a crime the Pope hath set his face against, blame yourself, not me!"

To prove his point, the pardoner unrolled a parchment. The dry, sterile crinkle of the vellum cut through the warm, fat-sizzling acoustics of the room. From it, he read aloud a calculated scale of forgiven crimes. It was a lexicon of death, an institutional language that systematically erased the raw, visceral truth of human transgression, translating the spilling of blood into neat, numerical values. It was a list too grotesque in its administrative coldness to recount in full, but its essence was clear: the murder of a layman was priced far cheaper than any minor infraction against the Church hierarchy. 

Through minor concessions, the bargain was closed. Coins clinked—a hollow, metallic sound that polluted the auditory space—buying celestial forgiveness for a murder yet to be committed. The word 'assassination' was never uttered, suffocated completely beneath the dominant lexicon of the pardon. 

The transaction buzzed like a trapped hornet in Gerard's ear. He did not lift his head from the sticky, wine-soaked timber; he only listened with a heavy, uncomprehending stupor. However, when the merchants of grace rose and separated, Gerard half-raised his head, his nostrils flaring at the retreating scent of wet wool, scowling at the slouched hat. 

"If Margaret were alive," he muttered, the syllables of her name tasting like cold ashes on his tongue. Her name was a beautiful, native dialect now dead, rendering the vastness of his subjective grief entirely untranslatable to the world. "I'd take thee by the throat and throttle thee, thou cowardly stabber. But she is dead; dead; dead. Die all the world; 'tis nought to me: so that I die among the first."

When he finally stumbled home, the storm had passed, leaving the cobbled timeline of the city slick and smelling of wet stone and wild street-fennel. Across the narrow way, pacing briskly to and fro, was the man in the slouched hat. He had circled back through the evening like a mythic specter of doom integrating itself into the domestic shadows. 

"Why, there is that cur again," thought Gerard. But his grief had deafened him. The subtle, acoustic warning of the assassin's pacing footfalls registered no meaning, for the cultural memory of his own will to live had long since eroded to nothing.
