—*Hamlet*

The spring of 1582-3 was bitter in Ireland. A cutting March wind swept across the bogs, where winter’s frost still clung in patches of rust and gold. Amidst the bleak expanse, a solitary knoll rose, crowned by gnarled oaks, their twisted limbs like the grasping fingers of some ancient spirit. Beneath them, a dark pool shivered in the wind, its surface rippling as if in answer to the mournful cry that echoed across the waste.

A company of English soldiers pressed forward, their boots sinking into the spongy earth. They were hardened men, veterans of Smerwick and countless skirmishes since. At their head rode two captains—one in battered armor, the other resplendent in gilded cuirass and plumed helmet, a stark contrast to the shaggy pony that carried him. Bound by a cord to one of the pikemen trudged their guide, a bare-legged Irish kerne, his eyes darting between fear and defiance.

“A pleasant country, is it not, Captain Raleigh?” the armored man—Amyas Leigh—remarked dryly.

Raleigh’s eyes gleamed. “It could be. With prudence, justice, and labor, this land might flourish. The soil is rich beneath the peat. Who knows what treasures lie in these rocks? Gold, silver—perhaps another Peru.”

Amyas scoffed. “It’ll take more than a plough to tame this wilderness. Sword and shot first, then law.”

Raleigh shook his head. “These Irish lords we hunt—they were once Normans, as English as we are. What corrupted them? Power without restraint? If I had but a grant of this land, in seven years I’d make it bloom.”

“And would you stay to see it?”

“I would. And you, Amyas, could be my steward.”

Amyas laughed. “I’d sooner haul lime in Devon than rot here another year. There’s a curse on this land.”

“The only curse is men’s greed and idleness,” Raleigh countered. “Root out the thorns, and wheat will grow.”

A wail cut through the wind—long, trembling, like a soul in torment.

“An Irish howl,” muttered Amyas.

“Or a banshee,” said the ancient, crossing himself. “They say she cries before death comes.”

Raleigh’s lips curled. “Then let us see whose death she foretells.”

Dismounting, he, Amyas, and Yeo the arquebusier splashed toward the knoll. There, beneath the oaks, a girl crouched over a corpse—a priest, his limbs stiff with starvation. She screamed again, tearing at her hair, heedless of the strangers.

Yeo rifled the dead man’s pouch. “Papers, Captain. Latin.”

Raleigh scanned them, then stiffened. “Nicholas Saunders.”

Yeo recoiled. “The legate?”

“The same.”

The girl shrieked as Yeo kicked the body. Raleigh checked him. “Enough. He’s paid his dues.”

They left her there, wailing over the man who had dreamed of reclaiming Ireland for Rome—now food for crows on a desolate moor.

An hour later, another Englishman stood by the corpse—Eustace Leigh, his face lined with years of intrigue. Around him, wild kernes muttered prayers.

He gazed at Saunders’ wasted form. *So ends the great legate—starved like a beggar.* Yet to Eustace, it was martyrdom.

“Blessed Saunders,” he whispered. “Pray for me, that I may endure as you did.”

They buried the priest hastily. There was no time for rites. The English were hunting Baltinglas; Fitz-Eustace would flee to Spain. And Eustace?

He turned his steps homeward—back to Devon, where none would think to look for him.

As they rode, Amyas eyed Raleigh. “You’ve changed your tune about Ireland.”

Raleigh laughed. “Perhaps. But I’ve another tune now—Newfoundland.”

“Newfoundland?”

“Aye. Humphrey Gilbert’s venture. I mean to sail with him—and you, if you’ve the stomach for it.”

Amyas grinned. “Better icebergs than bogs.”

Raleigh’s gaze turned westward. “There are fortunes to be made, Amyas. And glory. Elizabeth’s England is too narrow for me.”

The wind howled, carrying the banshee’s cry away into the dusk. Somewhere, Eustace Leigh trudged toward exile, and the ghost of Saunders rotted under Irish soil. But for Raleigh, the future lay beyond the horizon—where new worlds waited.
