It was, as Mrs. Archer smiled to Mrs. Welland, a magnificent occasion for a young couple to host their inaugural grand dinner.

Since establishing their household, the Newlands had entertained frequently, albeit informally. Archer enjoyed hosting intimate gatherings, and May received them with the radiant hospitality her mother had taught her. Her husband often mused if she would have ever extended an invitation without social pressure, but he had long since surrendered the battle to liberate her true nature from the mold of tradition and upbringing. In New York, it was deemed essential for affluent young couples to engage in casual entertaining, and a union between a Welland and an Archer carried a double burden of adherence to these customs.

Yet, a formal dinner party—a spectacle involving a hired chef, borrowed footmen, Roman punch, imported roses from Henderson's, and menus printed on gilt-edged cards—was a venture of a different magnitude. As Mrs. Archer noted, the Roman punch was the deciding factor, not merely for its taste, but for the manifold implications it carried: it signaled canvasbacks or terrapin, two distinct soups, hot and cold sweets, low-cut dresses with short sleeves, and guests of significant standing.

Such occasions were invariably intriguing when a young couple issued their first invitations in the third person, and their summons was rarely declined, even by the most sought-after. Still, it was a genuine triumph that the van der Lydens, at May's specific request, had stayed on to attend the farewell dinner for Countess Olenska.

On the afternoon of the great day, the two mothers-in-law occupied May's drawing-room. Mrs. Archer meticulously wrote out the menus on thick, gilt-edged Bristol board from Tiffany's, while Mrs. Welland supervised the placement of palms and standard lamps.

Archer arrived late from his office to find them still there. His mother-in-law was focused on the name-cards for the table, and Mrs. Welland was deliberating the effect of moving the large gilt sofa closer to create another conversational corner between the piano and the window. They informed him that May was in the dining room, inspecting a mound of Jacqueminot roses and maidenhair fern arranged on the long table, and positioning baskets of Maillard bonbons in openwork silver between the candelabras. A large basket of orchids, sent from Skuytercliff by Mr. van der Lyden, stood prominently on the piano. Everything, in short, was precisely as it should be for such a considerable event.

Mrs. Archer ran her sharp gold pen thoughtfully over the guest list, ticking off names. "Henry van der Lyden—Louisa—the Lovell Mingotts—the Reggie Chiverses—Lawrence Lefferts and Gertrude—(yes, I suppose May was right to include them)—the Selfridge Merrys, Sillerton Jackson, Van Newland and his wife. (How time flies! It seems only yesterday he was your best man, Newland)—and Countess Olenska—yes, I believe that covers everyone..."

Mrs. Welland surveyed her son-in-law with affection. "One cannot say, Newland, that you and May are not giving Ellen a truly generous send-off."

"Oh, well," said Mrs. Archer, "I understand May wants her cousin to assure people abroad that we aren't quite barbarians."

"I am sure Ellen will appreciate it," Mrs. Welland replied cheerfully. "She was due to arrive this morning, I believe. It will certainly make a charming final impression. The eve before sailing is usually so dreary."

Archer turned toward the door, and his mother-in-law called out, "Go in and peek at the table. And try not to let May tire herself too much." But he feigned not to hear, springing upstairs to his library. The room stared back at him like an alien countenance wearing a polite grimace; it had been ruthlessly "tidied" and prepared, through a judicious distribution of ashtrays and cedar-wood boxes, for the gentlemen to smoke.

"Ah, well," he thought, "it won't last long"—and proceeded to his dressing room.

Ten days had elapsed since Madame Olenska's departure from New York. During this period, Archer had received no communication from her save for the return of a key, wrapped in tissue paper and sent to his office in a sealed envelope bearing her handwriting. This retort to his last appeal might have been viewed as a masterstroke in a familiar game; however, the young man chose to interpret it differently. She remained fighting against her fate, but she was leaving for Europe and would not return to her husband. Therefore, nothing prevented him from following her. Once he took the irrevocable step and proved to her that it was irreversible, he believed she would not send him away.

This confidence in the future steadied him to play his part in the present. It kept him from writing to her or revealing, through any sign or act, his misery and humiliation. To him, it appeared that in this deadly silent game, the trumps still lay in his hands, and he waited.

Nevertheless, there had been moments of sufficient difficulty. Such as when Mr. Letterblair, the day after Madame Olenska's departure, summoned him to discuss the details of the trust Mrs. Manson Mingott wished to establish for her granddaughter. For two hours, Archer examined the terms of the deed with his senior, vaguely sensing that if he had been consulted, it was for reasons other than the obvious one of kinship; and that the conclusion of the conference would reveal it.

"Well, the lady can't deny that it's a handsome arrangement," Mr. Letterblair summarized after muttering over a summary of the settlement. "In fact, I'm bound to say she's been treated rather handsomely all round."

"All round?" Archer echoed with a touch of derision. "Do you refer to her husband's proposal to give her back her own money?"

Mr. Letterblair's bushy eyebrows rose a fraction of an inch. "My dear sir, the law is the law; and your wife's cousin was married under the French law. It is to be presumed she knew what that entailed."

"Even if she did, what happened subsequently..." But Archer paused. Mr. Letterblair had laid his pen-hand against his big corrugated nose and was looking down it with the expression assumed by virtuous elderly gentlemen when they wish their juniors to understand that virtue is not synonymous with ignorance.

"My dear sir, I have no wish to extenuate the Count's transgressions; but—but on the other side... I wouldn't put my hand in the fire... well, that there hadn't been tit for tat... with the young champion...." Mr. Letterblair unlocked a drawer and pushed a folded paper toward Archer. "This report, the result of discreet inquiries..." Then, as Archer made no effort to glance at the paper or repudiate the suggestion, the lawyer somewhat flatly continued: "I don't say it's conclusive, you observe; far from it. But straws show... and on the whole, it's eminently satisfactory for all parties that this dignified solution has been reached."

"Oh, eminently," Archer assented, pushing the paper back.

A day or two later, responding to a summons from Mrs. Manson Mingott, his soul underwent further trial.

He found the old lady depressed and querulous.

"You know she's deserted me?" she began immediately; and without waiting for his reply: "Oh, don't ask me why! She gave so many reasons I've forgotten them all. My private belief is that she couldn't face the boredom. At any rate, that's what Augusta and my daughters-in-law think. And I don't know that I altogether blame her. Olenski is a finished scoundrel; but life with him must have been considerably gayer than it is in Fifth Avenue. Not that the family would admit that: they think Fifth Avenue is Heaven with the Rue de la Paix thrown in. And poor Ellen, of course, has no idea of going back to her husband. She held out as firmly as ever against that. So she's to settle down in Paris with that fool Medora.... Well, Paris is Paris; and you can keep a carriage there on next to nothing. But she was as gay as a bird, and I shall miss her." Two tears, the parched tears of age, rolled down her puffy cheeks and vanished into the abysses of her bosom.

"All I ask is," she concluded, "that they shouldn't bother me any more. I must really be allowed to digest my gruel...." And she twinkled a little wistfully at Archer.

It was that very evening, upon his return home, that May announced her intention of giving a farewell dinner to her cousin. Madame Olenska's name had not been spoken between them since the night of her flight to Washington; and Archer looked at his wife with surprise.

"A dinner—why?" he interrogated.

Her color rose. "But you like Ellen—I thought you'd be pleased."

"It's awfully nice—you putting it in that way. But I really don't see—"

"I mean to do it, Newland," she said, quietly rising and going to her desk. "Here are the invitations all written. Mother helped me—she agrees that we ought to." She paused, embarrassed yet smiling, and Archer suddenly saw before him the embodied image of the Family.

"Oh, all right," he said, staring with unseeing eyes at the list of guests she handed to him.
