*"My heart revolts within me, and two voices
Make themselves audible within my bosom."*

Margaret returned home to find two letters awaiting her—one a note for her mother, the other, postmarked from abroad, thin and crisp, unmistakably from Aunt Shaw. As she turned it over in her hands, her father entered abruptly.

"Your mother has retired early—the heat must have fatigued her. What did the doctor say? Dixon mentioned he spoke with you."

Margaret hesitated. The gravity in her father’s expression deepened.

"He doesn’t believe her condition is serious?"

"Not presently. She requires care, he said. He was kind—promised to call again to assess her progress."

"Only care? He didn’t suggest a change of air? Nothing about the city’s smoke affecting her?"

"No, not a word." Her voice was quiet. "Though he seemed… concerned."

"Doctors always do. It’s their manner."

Yet Margaret saw the unease beneath his dismissive tone. The specter of fear had taken root, though he fought it, circling back to the subject restlessly throughout the evening, as if repetition might dispel the dread.

"Aunt Shaw writes from Naples," Margaret ventured, lifting the letter. "She’s moved to Sorrento—the heat was intolerable. Though I doubt Italy pleases her."

"Did the doctor mention anything about diet?"

"Nourishing and digestible. Mamma’s appetite is fair."

"Strange he should mention it at all."

"I asked him."

A pause. Then Margaret added, half-smiling, "Aunt Shaw sends coral ornaments, but fears Milton’s dissenters won’t appreciate them. She confuses all dissenters with Quakers, doesn’t she?"

"If your mother expresses any wish—anything at all—you must tell me. I fear she keeps silent." His voice tightened. "Engage that servant Mrs. Thornton recommended. A competent maid would free Dixon to attend her constantly. With proper care, we can restore her. This heat, the strain of managing without help—rest will mend her. Don’t you think?"

"I hope so," Margaret murmured, her sorrow so palpable her father pressed her cheek.

"You’re too pale. Take care, or the doctor will be needed for you next."

But he could not settle. All evening, he stole upstairs on tiptoe, returning each time with fragile reassurance.

"She’s awake now—smiled at me, just as she used to. She feels refreshed, wants tea. Where’s that note? I’ll read it to her while you prepare the tray."

The note was an invitation from Mrs. Thornton—a dinner on the twenty-first. Margaret was surprised when her parents resolved to accept, despite the day’s grim tidings. Yet her mother clung to the prospect with frail determination, brightening at the thought of an outing.

"If she wishes it, we’ll go gladly," Mr. Hale insisted the next morning, watching Margaret pen the acceptance. "She wouldn’t insist unless she felt stronger—would she?" His hands trembled. "Margaret?"

To deny him hope felt cruel. His own stubborn refusal to entertain fear kindled a spark in her.

"She does seem better since last night. Her eyes are clearer."

"God bless you," he breathed. "But was it the sultry air yesterday that made everyone ill? An ill-chosen day for the doctor’s visit."

He left for his lectures—an engagement at the Lyceum, where he would speak on Ecclesiastical Architecture, a subject dear to him, if not to Milton’s laborers. The struggling institution was grateful for his unpaid expertise.

That evening, the Thorntons convened.

"Who has accepted for the twenty-first?" John inquired.

Fanny produced the replies. "The Slicksons, Collingbrooks, Stephenses—Browns decline. Hales will attend, though the mother is indisposed. Macphersons, Mr. Horsfall, Mr. Young."

"Strange they’d accept if she were truly unwell," Fanny remarked.

"I said *far from well*, not *dying*," John snapped. "They may not realize." Then he recalled—Margaret, at least, knew the truth.

"Perhaps they appreciate the advantage of meeting the Stephenses and Collingbrooks," Mrs. Thornton suggested.

John stiffened. "That’s not their motive."

Fanny laughed nervously. "How you claim to understand these Hales, yet deny *us* any insight. Are they so unlike anyone else?"

He ignored her, though the jab rankled.

Mrs. Thornton mused, "He seems a decent man, though unsuited for trade. She’s a trifle delicate, and the girl—" She frowned. "Proud, somehow. Not rich, yet carries herself as if above her company."

"She can’t even play the piano," Fanny added.

"Must she meet *your* standards?" John retorted.

His mother intervened. "Fanny meant no harm. If you’d cease defending Miss Hale, we might find reason to like her."

Fanny muttered, "*I* never could."

John paced, willing the candles to be lit, the conversation ended. But his mother kept to her frugal habits.

At last he halted. "Mother—I wish you *would* like her."

"Why?" Her eyes sharpened. "You’re not thinking of *marrying* her? A penniless girl?"

"She wouldn’t have me," he laughed shortly.

"No, I think not," his mother agreed. "She laughed outright when I praised her for defending you. Saucy creature! Where would she find better?"

"Since we agree, trust my motives. I foresee hardship for her—lack of guidance. If the time comes, be her friend." He turned to Fanny. "And you—understand that any hint of my attachment would offend her as much as me. More, perhaps."

Mrs. Thornton snorted. "I’ll tolerate her for your sake. But her pride galls me. One moment I near like her; the next, I detest her."

"If she scorns me," John said coldly, "I’m no boy to cringe. I can laugh at it."

"Then laugh at *her* and her airs!"

Fanny yawned. "Must we talk of her? I’m weary of it."

John’s voice turned bitter. "Shall we discuss something cheerier? A strike, perhaps?"

Mrs. Thornton leaned forward. "Have the hands walked out?"

"Hamper’s men have. Mine stay for fear of prosecution. I’d have punished every one who broke contract."

"The law costs would outweigh their worth," his mother scoffed.

"Yet I’d have shown them my word is iron. They know me. Slickson’s men are out—he won’t spend to punish them. We’re in for a fight."

"Are there many orders?"

"Plenty. They know it—but not the full truth."

"What do you mean?"

As candles were lit, Fanny took up her endless needlework, gazing vacantly.

"The Americans flood the market. Our only hope is to produce cheaper. Fail, and we all starve—masters and men. Yet these fools demand wages from three years past, blind to the fines Dickinson extracts. If the old combination laws still stood—" He broke off, exasperated. "Must we beg the Spinners’ Union for labor at *their* price? Drive us away, and they’ll find no work at all."

"Bring in Irish hands," his mother said flatly. "Teach them who’s master."

"I will, if this lasts. But it’s risk and expense."

"Then why host a dinner now?"

"Necessary. Horsfall won’t stay long; the others are owed."

He resumed pacing, jaw clenched. Fanny prattled inconsequentially, earning terse replies. At ten, the servants filed in for prayers—a chapter from the Old Testament, read sternly by Mrs. Thornton.

When she bid him goodnight, her gaze held no softness, yet the intensity of her silent blessing steadied him. Alone, he paced still, the strike upending his plans. Fools, led by Slickson’s cunning—playing at yielding to embolden them.

At two o’clock, the guttering candles mirrored his smoldering resolve.

"They’ll have a fortnight. No more. If sense doesn’t prevail, I’ll bring in Irish hands. Let them learn whom they defy."

The strike would break them before it broke him.
