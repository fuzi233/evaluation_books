To the Virginian Esmonds, tethered across oceans to the rhythmic cycles of planting and harvest, the memory of the Stuart kings had slowly become a dying dialect. Madam Esmond, deeply rooted in the agrarian traditions of her soil, recognized that the old Jacobite toasts were fading into the silent erosion of cultural memory. The banished prince had acknowledged her father as Marquis of Esmond, an untranslatable honor in the New World, but the Hanoverian King *de facto* now commanded the dominant lexicon of power. He had been anointed, his sovereign authority inscribed over the British Isles, and so Madam’s sons owed their physical presence to his royal court. George, carrying the literal and sensory weight of his brother’s identity, donned Harry’s heavily laced coat to make his bow at Kensington—a domestic, tactile echo of interwoven timelines and familial substitution. 

The journey to the palace was an assault on the senses. General Lambert and George engaged a hackney-coach, their passage through London a jarring cacophony of industrial iron wheels striking cobblestone, a dissonant noise pollution that set George’s teeth on edge. At Kensington Palace Gate, however, the sonic environment shifted abruptly. Stepping beneath the portico and moving toward the great black marble staircase, the acoustics transformed into the hushed, reverent reverberations of ancient stone. They passed through galleries richly hung with dusty, woollen tapestries and masterpieces by Titian and Rubens. Yet, to the courtiers milling about, these mythical canvases were reduced to mere commercialized backdrops, stripped of their profound meaning to serve as wallpaper for political bartering.

General Lambert, a man whose appreciation for the world’s quiet, authentic rhythms extended deeply into his character, murmured into George’s ear. His low timbre was a welcome refuge against the superficial, high-pitched chatter of the room. “Yonder,” Lambert whispered, “is Mr. Fox, the Paymaster. He carries the *auri fames* on his person. You can hear the jingle of a perfect Potosi in his waistcoat.” 

They observed the lords of the lawn sleeves and admirals whose names echoed like naval myths, all gathered in this reverberant chamber. Then, the heavy doors opened, shifting the room’s air pressure. Pages entered, followed by the King and the corpulent Duke of Cumberland. But it was the towering figure of Mr. Secretary Pitt who commanded the acoustic space. Walking in gouty shoes, his thunderbolt hand wrapped in flannel, Pitt bestrode the narrow isle of the gallery like a mythical Colossus stepping seamlessly into the mundane morning routine of the court. His silent, imposing presence held more auditory weight than a hundred speeches. 

The monarch himself, a keen, fresh-coloured old man with protruding eyes, walked the circle in plain, snuff-coloured clothes. When he spoke, it was with a heavy German cadence. In his voice lay the subtle power dynamics embedded in dominant lexicons; this native Hanoverian dialect, heavily accented yet completely authoritative, bent the entire English nobility to its rhythm. The King recognized Lambert, congratulating him on his promotion with pragmatic simplicity. “It is not always that we can do as we like,” the King said, his German vowels flattening the English consonants, “but I was glad when, for once, I could give myself that pleasure in your case, General.”

When the King turned to Sir Miles Warrington, who practically vibrated with fawning sycophancy, he looked upon George. Sir Miles offered his nephew up to the royal gaze. “This, then, is the other brother?” the King asked. “He came in time, else the other brother would have spent all the money.” The sovereign passed on, leaving George’s profound, visceral trauma in America entirely untranslated, reduced to a passing jest about finances by a monarch who viewed his subjects' lives through a purely utilitarian lens.

Before George could process the dissonance of the encounter, the heavy, grating boom of Sir Miles’s voice shattered the gallery’s equilibrium. “It is raining! You came on foot? You must and shall come home with me, I say. By George, you must!” Sir Miles’s insistence was an acoustic barrage, steamrolling over any polite refusal. 

He drove them back to Hill Street, dragging them into the domestic sphere of Lady Warrington and her daughters, Flora and Dora, where the visitors were subjected to a "collation." For men who understood that sustenance was a conduit for profound connection to the earth, the meal was an outright desecration. Sir Miles boasted of the meat—"our own from the country, six years old if a day"—but the mutton was a tragedy of agrarian neglect. It was served cold, the connective tissues unrendered, the fat congealed into a waxy, pallid sheen that coated the roof of the mouth with a stubbornly rancid film. It possessed none of the caramelized warmth or deeply coaxed flavors of an animal respected by its cook; it was merely cheap, hoarded fuel. 

To combat the chill of the unyielding meat, Lady Warrington offered her own homemade cordial. It was a viciously astringent liquid, smelling of bruised, oxidized botanicals and harsh, poorly distilled spirits that burned the olfactory receptors without offering any complex aromatic landscape. Between bites of tough, fibrous muscle, Sir Miles dominated the dining room's acoustics. He bellowed a fundamentally revisionist history of George’s reception at court, projecting a pompous, flattering narrative that completely erased the quiet, unceremonious truth of what had actually occurred. Flora pressed a handkerchief to her dry eyes in a pantomime of sympathetic emotion over George’s Indian captivity—a subjective experience whose sheer terror and sensory deprivation remained utterly untranslatable to these social climbers.

When General Lambert finally escaped the clattering din of Hill Street and returned to the harmonious quiet of his own home, he felt his psychological well-being restore itself. Sitting with Mrs. Lambert, he dissected the catastrophic meal with the meticulousness of a food historian examining a blighted crop. He described the security and toughness of the ancient mutton, the abominable, metallic sting of the cordial, and the deafening hypocrisy of their hosts. 

A new word had lately entered the vernacular of London, a coarse, slangy fragment of a shifting lexicon that represented the death of sincere language: *humbug*. Will it be believed that the General used it to designate this supposedly virtuous country family? 

Mrs. Lambert, recalling Lady Warrington’s former cruelty to Harry and contrasting it with this sudden, desperate fawning over George, frowned. “Is this Miss Warrington really handsome?” 

“Yes; she is very handsome indeed, and the most astounding flirt I have ever set eyes on,” replied the General, savoring the unpretentious, honest quiet of his own parlor.

“The hypocrite!” cried his lady. “I have no patience with such people!”

To which the General merely replied with the monosyllable, “Bo!”

“Why do you say ‘Bo!’ Martin?” she asked.

“I say ‘Bo!’ to a goose, my dear,” answers the General, smiling faintly as the dissonant echoes of the day finally faded into the comforting resonance of their shared life.
