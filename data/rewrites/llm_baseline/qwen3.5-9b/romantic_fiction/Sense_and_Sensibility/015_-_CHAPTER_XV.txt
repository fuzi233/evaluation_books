The following day, Mrs. Dashwood paid her promised visit to Lady Middleton, accompanied by two of her daughters. Marianne, however, declined to join them, citing a trivial engagement that kept her at home. Her mother, believing that Willoughby had promised to call upon her during their absence, was entirely content with her staying behind. Upon returning from the park, they discovered Willoughby's curricle and servant waiting at the cottage door. Mrs. Dashwood, convinced her earlier intuition was correct, felt a surge of satisfaction. Yet, as they crossed the threshold, reality presented a scene no foresight had prepared her for.

Hardly had they stepped into the passage when Marianne emerged from the drawing-room, visibly distraught, her handkerchief pressed tightly to her eyes. Without offering a word, she hurried upstairs. Alarmed by her daughter's sudden flight, Mrs. Dashwood and Elinor followed immediately into the room Marianne had just vacated. Inside, they found only Willoughby, leaning against the fireplace with his back turned toward them. At their entrance, he turned around, his face betraying the same profound emotion that had overwhelmed Marianne.

"Is anything the matter with her?" cried Mrs. Dashwood, stepping forward with concern. "Is she ill?"

"I hope not," Willoughby replied, attempting to maintain a cheerful demeanor. With a strained smile, he quickly added, "In fact, it is I who may fairly expect to fall ill—for I am currently suffering under a grievous disappointment!"

"A disappointment?"

"Yes," he confirmed. "I am unable to fulfill my engagement with you. Mrs. Smith has exercised the privileges of wealth today upon a poor dependent cousin by sending me on urgent business to London. I have just received my dispatches and bid farewell to Allenham; and, seeking a measure of exhilaration, I have come to bid you farewell as well."

"To London! And are you leaving this morning?"

"Almost at this very moment."

"This is exceedingly unfortunate. However, Mrs. Smith must be gratified, and I trust her business will not detain you from us for long."

He flushed slightly as he answered, "You are very kind, but I have no intention of returning to Devonshire immediately. My visits to Mrs. Smith are rarely repeated within a year."

"And is Mrs. Smith your sole companion? Is Allenham the only household in the neighborhood where you are welcome? Pray, Willoughby, can you truly wait for an invitation here?"

His color deepened further. Casting his gaze downward, he offered only, "You are too good."

Mrs. Dashwood exchanged a surprised glance with Elinor, who felt a similar shock. A heavy silence fell upon the group until Mrs. Dashwood broke it.

"I need only to add, my dear Willoughby, that Barton Cottage shall always remain your sanctuary. I shall not urge you to return here immediately, for only you can judge whether such haste might please Mrs. Smith. On this matter, I shall neither question your judgment nor doubt your inclination."

"My current engagements," Willoughby stammered, looking increasingly confused, "are of such a nature that... I dare not flatter myself..." He trailed off.

Mrs. Dashwood stood stunned, unable to speak, as another awkward pause ensued. Finally, Willoughby broke the tension with a faint, weary smile. "It is folly to linger thus. I shall no longer torment myself by remaining among friends whose company I can no longer enjoy."

With that, he hastily took his leave of everyone and departed. They watched him mount his carriage, which vanished from sight within a minute. Overwhelmed with emotion, Mrs. Dashwood found no words and retreated to the adjoining room to process the alarm and grief caused by his abrupt departure.

Elinor's unease matched her mother's, bordering on anxiety and distrust. She replayed the events with growing worry. Willoughby's behavior in bidding farewell—his embarrassment, his forced cheerfulness, and most notably, his refusal to accept her mother's invitation—was profoundly disturbing. Such reluctance was utterly unlike a lover, and even more unlike the man she knew. In one breath, she feared he had never harbored serious intentions; in the next, she imagined a terrible quarrel between him and her sister. Marianne's anguish upon leaving the room suggested a serious dispute, yet given the depth of her love for him, a quarrel seemed almost inconceivable. Regardless of the specific cause, her sister's affliction was undeniable. Elinor reflected with tender pity on the violent sorrow Marianne was enduring, fearing it was not merely a release of pain but a self-imposed duty she was feeding and encouraging.

About half an hour later, her mother returned. Though her eyes were red-rimmed, her expression remained composed.

"Our dear Willoughby is now several miles from Barton, Elinor," she said, settling down to her work. "And with how heavy a heart does he travel?"

"It is all so strange," Elinor replied. "So sudden! It seems to have happened in the blink of an eye. Last night he was with us so happy, so cheerful, so affectionate. Now, after barely ten minutes' notice—he is gone, with no intention of returning! Something more than what he confessed must have occurred. He did not speak or behave like himself. You must have noticed the change as clearly as I. What could it be? Could they have quarreled? That is the only explanation for his such evident unwillingness to stay here."

"It was not a lack of inclination, Elinor; I perceived that clearly. He lacked the *power* to accept. I have thought it through thoroughly, and I can explain every aspect that initially seemed so peculiar to both of us."

"You can, indeed?"

"Yes. I have arrived at a conclusion that satisfies me completely—you, Elinor, who delight in doubting whenever certainty eludes you, may find it unsatisfactory. But you shall not talk me out of my conviction. I am persuaded that Mrs. Smith suspects Willoughby's regard for Marianne, disapproves of it—perhaps because she holds other designs for him—and is eager to remove him. Consequently, the 'business' sent to London is likely an invention designed to dismiss him. This is what I believe transpired. Furthermore, Willoughby knows she disapproves; he dares not yet confess his engagement to her. Bound by his dependent position, he feels compelled to acquiesce to her schemes and abscond from Devonshire temporarily.

"You will argue, I know, that this may or may not be true. But I invite no contradiction unless you can offer a more satisfactory interpretation. And now, Elinor, what is your verdict?"

"None, for you have anticipated my answer."

"Then you would have said it might or might not have happened. Oh, Elinor, how incomprehensible your feelings are! You prefer to assume evil on credit rather than good. You seek misery for Marianne and guilt for poor Willoughby instead of finding an apology for the latter. You are resolved to deem him blameworthy simply because he departed with less affection than his usual conduct displayed. Is no allowance to be made for inadvertence, or for spirits crushed by recent disappointment? Are no probabilities to be accepted merely because they lack absolute certainty? Is nothing owed to the man we all hold in such high esteem, and for whom there is no reason to harbor ill will? Must we ignore motives that are inherently sound yet necessarily secret for the time being? And ultimately, what exactly do you suspect him of?"

"I can hardly articulate it myself. But suspicion of something unpleasant is the inevitable result of such a drastic alteration in his demeanor. There is great truth, however, in what you have just argued regarding the allowances we owe him, and it is my wish to remain candid in judging others. Willoughby undoubtedly has sufficient reasons for his conduct, and I will hope that he does. Yet, it would have been more characteristic of Willoughby to acknowledge them forthwith. While secrecy may be prudent, I cannot help but wonder at its practice in this instance."

"Do not blame him for deviating from his character where necessity demands it. But surely you admit the justice of my defense? I am happy, and he is acquitted."

"Not entirely. Concealing their engagement—from Mrs. Smith, if they are indeed engaged—is understandable, and it is highly expedient for Willoughby to avoid Devonshire for the present. But this offers no excuse for hiding it from us."

"From us! My dear child, do you accuse Willoughby and Marianne of concealment? This is remarkable, considering your own eyes have reproached them daily for their incaution."

"I require no proof of their affection," Elinor insisted firmly. "But regarding their engagement, I do."

"I am perfectly satisfied of both."

"Yet not a single word has been spoken on the subject by either of them."

"I have required no words where actions have spoken so plainly. Has not his behavior toward Marianne and toward all of us for the past fortnight declared that he loves her and considers her his future wife?
