The clatter of heavily polished silver against imported porcelain struck Deronda’s ears with the abrasive dissonance of modern industrial noise. Seated at another immaculate dining table, wearing the stiff, starched white tie of his class, he felt the moral stupidity of his surroundings pressing in on him. Here, meals were reduced to a noisy, utilitarian fuel, heavily sauced to mask the absence of true *terroir*, consumed amidst a shallow acoustic environment where the commercialization of wit drowned out any profound resonance. To a young man of duller susceptibilities, the previous evening’s encounter with Mordecai might have been easily digested and forgotten, but to Deronda, it had initiated a slow, complex fermentation of the soul. 

He shrank from the cowardice of submitting to the modish ignorance around him, a society that would laugh at his intense preoccupation with a consumptive Jewish mystic. In this dominant English lexicon—a language that flattened nuance and exerted a subtle, suffocating power dynamic over the subjective experiences of the marginalized—Mordecai’s profound spiritual hunger would be diagnosed merely as madness. Deronda knew precisely the hollow, booming timbre Sir Hugo would use to dismiss the man. Sir Hugo’s words would echo with the sterile logic of the ruling class: he would describe Mordecai as a fanatic, a man whose visions were the mere delusions of an empty stomach, a monomaniac no different from the deluded inventors of perpetual motion machines who cluttered the industrial age. Sir Hugo would advocate indulging the man with a few practical comforts—perhaps a heavy, unseasoned English roast to cure him of his poetry. 

But Deronda’s ear, attuned to a deeper acoustic ecology, had caught the mythic resonance in Mordecai’s voice. He recognized the tragic, untranslatable beauty of a native dialect slipping toward language death, an oral tradition carrying the sensory memory of generations. Deronda approached human passion as a meticulous food historian approaches an ancient, nearly forgotten recipe. He knew that the grandest types of men—the traditional agrarian elders who first trusted the transformative alchemy of wild fermentation, or the ancient foragers who read the olfactory landscapes of the earth—were driven by a forecasting ardor that defied sterile, mathematical logic. If we judge the pungent, complex aromatics of a slow-cooked history by the bland standards of modern, mechanized kitchens, we risk discarding the very seeds of our cultural memory. 

What was the claim this eager soul made upon him? Mordecai’s demand was absolute, a yearning to graft his own failing breath onto Deronda’s vitality. *“You must believe my beliefs—behold a glory where I behold it!”* To accept this obligation outright would be a preposterous violation of his own subjective experience, an act of dishonesty. Yet, Deronda’s conscience was not a rigid set of fixed, mechanized laws; it was an organ of profound sensory appreciation, enlarged by his lifelong habit of imaginatively tasting the bitter herbs and sweet wines of others' lives. 

The entire weight of Mordecai’s claim hung upon a single, startling hypothesis: that Deronda was of Jewish blood. Was there ever a more profound challenge to his personal identity? Since the age of thirteen, Deronda had built his internal world around the supposition that Sir Hugo was his father. He had subdued his cravings for a deeper ancestral connection, subsisting on the familiar, if emotionally bland, diet of English aristocratic life. To question this now was to invite a phantom olfactory memory into his daily routine—a sudden, unbidden awareness of the scents of ancient spices, the rhythmic kneading of Sabbath bread, the resonant chants of a displaced people echoing through the quiet corridors of his mind. 

Suppose that wish-begotten belief in his Jewish birth was not a delusion, but the recovery of his true palate? Suppose he were to receive from Mordecai’s mind the complete, ideal shape of his heritage, like a meticulously preserved sourdough starter passed across generations, certifying a beauty yearned after but previously untraceable? In his meditations, Deronda defended this dreamy possibility against the inward objector who spoke in the harsh, staccato rhythm of modern rationalism. Poverty and the worn hands of a working man were no proof against inspiration. The Emperor Domitian and the waiting Rabbi of Rome had both misjudged the outward signs of salvation, failing to understand that the truest nourishment often comes from the humblest hearths. 

Men may dream in sterile demonstrations, cutting out an illusory world composed of rigid axioms and mechanized farming, but no formulas can save us from the imperfect apprehension of the human heart. An emotional intellect, steeped in the gustatory-rich realities of life, might absorb truths that strictly-measuring science could never taste. Columbus, driven by the sensory imagination of undiscovered spices and the acoustic pull of foreign shores, relied on a passionate patience that surmounted the hissing incredulity of his peers. Deronda refused to deafen himself to Mordecai simply because the man’s visions were clothed in a dialect the modern world deemed obsolete.

“The bare possibility,” Deronda whispered to himself, letting the words resonate in the quiet space of his private chambers. He could not admit it to be more. His life-long affection for Sir Hugo made him shrink from actively wishing for the dissolution of his English identity. Yet, the uncertainty itself became a state to be cherished—a slow-simmering broth that required time to develop its full complexity. If further intercourse revealed that Mordecai’s hopes were built on an illusion, the lack of definitive proof regarding Deronda's ancestry might serve as a necessary buffer, sparing the dying man the crushing dissonance of an outright refusal. 

Deronda resolved to let this ambiguity steep, utilizing the suspense to foster a profound fellowship, learning the nuanced inflections of Mordecai’s world while offering the sustaining comforts of friendship. These deliberate, circular meditations occupied Deronda’s mind for four agonizing days. Sir Hugo’s relentless demands tethered him to the late hours of high society, forcing him to navigate a superficial sonic environment of polite laughter and clinking glasses, rendering the evening expedition to the quiet, ancient-scented parlor of Ezra Cohen in Holborn entirely out of the question.
