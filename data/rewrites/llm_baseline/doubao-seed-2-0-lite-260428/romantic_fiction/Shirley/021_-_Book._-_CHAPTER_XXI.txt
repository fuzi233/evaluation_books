The smouldering tension of last night’s mill riot hung soft as woodsmoke over Fieldhead Hall that morning, even as pale sun broke through the haze gilding the distant moors. Downstairs, Shirley was still locked in heated, eager conversation with Robert Moore, the mill owner who had stood his ground against two hundred rioters hours before, while Caroline Helstone slipped quietly back up the staircase to find Mrs. Pryor.

Her governess sat huddled by the bedroom window, shoulders hunched tight, and the faint, almost invisible line of pain around her mouth told Caroline all she needed to know: Shirley’s sharp, hasty temper earlier that morning had cut deeper than anyone could have guessed. To a stranger, Mrs. Pryor would have seemed entirely unmoved by the small, tender attentions Caroline offered—steeping her favourite chamomile tea, tucking a soft wool shawl around her shoulders, murmuring quiet little anecdotes to make her smile—but Caroline knew better. She saw the way the older woman’s shoulders softened, the way her fingers curled gently around Caroline’s wrist when she passed the cup, and knew the comfort was sinking in, mending the quiet hurt.

“I have never had enough confidence, enough firmness of purpose,” Mrs. Pryor said at last, her voice thin and wobbly. “Surely by now Miss Keeldar knows I would never do anything but strive to do what is right, no matter how much it troubles me. The riot, the alarms of the night, the sudden demand to make a choice for another person… it puzzled me. I dared not act rashly. I can only pray my hesitation did no real harm.”

A soft, tentative knock rapped at the door. Caroline opened it a crack, and there was Shirley, standing in the gallery like a repentant child who’d smashed a beloved ornament, her face flushed with shame, her usual boldness nowhere to be found.

“How is she?” Shirley whispered, almost too quiet to hear.

“Her spirits are low,” Caroline told her gently.

“I was horrid to her,” Shirley burst out, all contrition. “Ungenerous, ungrateful, insolent. I snapped at her for nothing but her own overabundance of conscientiousness. Tell her I am sorry, tell her I beg her forgiveness—please, Caroline.”

Caroline delivered the message with a heart full of gladness, and Mrs. Pryor rose to her feet, trembling a little. She hated public shows of affection, dreaded reconciliations as all timid souls do, but she faltered to the door and said, “Come in, my dear.”

Shirley swept inside on a burst of impulsive warmth, wrapping her arms around her governess and kissing her cheek so hard it left a pink mark. “You have to forgive me,” she insisted. “I cannot bear even a single misunderstanding between us. I would be utterly lost without you.”

“There is nothing to forgive,” Mrs. Pryor replied, patting her back gently. “Let us forget the whole incident. It only proves what I have long known: I am not fit to face moments of crisis.”

That quiet, bitter little conviction clung to her long after, no matter how much Shirley and Caroline teased and laughed with her to chase it away. She could forgive Shirley for her outburst, but she could not forgive herself for being weak.

Shirley was barely settled before a servant summoned her downstairs again. The first visitor was her rector, Mr. Helstone, bright-eyed and giddy with the thrill of the night before, ready to shower her with both a lively welcome and a playful reprimand for the reckless way she’d thrown herself into the fray. He expected both, and took both in perfect good humour.

In all his excitement, he quite forgot to ask after his niece. The rioters, the mill, the magistrates, the heiress herself filled all his thoughts, and he rambled on about the part he and his curate had played defending the Hollow.

“Everyone who disapproves of us will be spitting mad over this,” he said, laughing. “But I defy every last gossiper. I was there only to uphold the law, to stand my ground as a man and a Briton—those roles sit perfectly well alongside the duties of a clergyman, if you ask me. And your tenant Moore? He has won my full approval. Never have I seen a cooler, more determined man. He was fully prepared for the riot before it even happened, and once his plans won the day, he had the good sense not to abuse his victory. Half the magistrates are so frightened now they’re baying for cruel, excessive punishment, but Moore restrains them all with incredible prudence. He’s been unpopular in these parts for years, but mark my words: the tide is about to turn. The whole parish will soon realize how much they’ve underestimated him, and he’ll drop that gruff, reserved air he’s worn around us all to show the kind, gracious man he really is.”

He was just about to make a half-joking, half-serious remark about the obvious, unspoken partiality everyone had noticed between Shirley and her talented tenant when the door bell rang. He caught sight of the new visitor: white-haired, truculent old Hiram Yorke, his old bitter political enemy, and Helstone snatched up his hat, bade Shirley a hasty goodbye, and fled before they could exchange a single hostile word.

Mr. Yorke was in no gentle mood, and he let loose a torrent of blunt Yorkshire invective about the previous night’s events, lashing out at the rioters, the magistrates, the soldiers, and most of all the two “demon parsons” who’d dared to fight alongside the mill workers.

“The Church is in a fine mess now,” he grumbled. “What’s the world coming to when parsons go swaggering around with guns, killing men far honester than themselves?”

“What would you have had Moore do?” Shirley challenged, immediately leaping to her tenant’s defence. “Let them burn his mill down? He’s got enough courage for ten men, but no man can fight two hundred angry rioters alone.”

“He had the soldiers, those poor fools who sell their blood for a shilling a day.”

“You abuse soldiers as much as you abuse clergymen!” Shirley laughed, heated now, pacing the oak parlour floor. “Everyone in a red coat is trash to you, everyone in a black coat is a swindler. According to you, Moore should have just stood by and let his livelihood be destroyed, let himself be murdered, and every gentleman in the parish should have stood by and watched and done nothing.”

“If Moore had treated his workers right from the start, they never would have turned on him,” Yorke argued.

“That’s easy for you to say!” Shirley cried, cheeks flushing bright pink. “Your family’s lived at Briarmains for six generations! Everyone here has known you for fifty years, you know all their little quirks and prejudices. Moore came here a stranger, poor, friendless, with nothing but his own wits and his hard work to build a life. It’s no crime that he couldn’t turn his naturally quiet, grave nature into the jolly, familiar demeanour everyone expected overnight. Why should he be left defenceless against a mob for being a little too reserved? Why should anyone who stood by to help him be called a villain?”

“Calm down, lass,” Yorke said, grinning, amused by her fiery defence. He’d never seen her so eager, so bright, and the fact she was defending Robert Moore so fiercely pleased him more than he’d ever admit.

“Calm down?” Shirley tossed her head. “I will not sit here and listen to dangerous nonsense! I like you well enough, Mr. Yorke, but half your principles are utter rubbish. All that cant about hating every parson, hating every soldier, pitting class against class, all those tyrannies you dress up as ‘liberty’? I want no part of it. You think you’re a great philanthropist, but Mr. Hall, the quiet gentle parson of Nunnely, is a far better friend to the people than you will ever be.”

Yorke would never have tolerated that speech from a man, or from most women, but Shirley was honest, sharp, and pretty, and her temper was a delight to him. “What else have you got to say for yourself?” he asked, when she paused to catch her breath.

“I could talk all day!” she laughed, still striding back and forth across the parlour rug. “All your extreme political views are nothing but opposition talk—you’d drop every last one of them the second you had any real power. You’d do exactly what Moore did, if you were in his shoes. I’m sick of everyone judging each other so harshly, no one giving anyone else an inch of grace. I think you’re a fine, honest old Yorkshireman, but you’re harsh, narrow, and far too unforgiving.”

Yorke stood up, brushing dust off his coat, and fixed her with a sharp, knowing grin. “When is the wedding, then?”

Shirley stared, colour rising in her cheeks, but her eyes didn’t flinch. “Whose wedding?”

“Yours and Robert Gérard Moore’s. Everyone’s talking about it, aren’t they? The wealthy heiress of Fieldhead marrying the poor hardworking mill tenant.”

Shirley drew herself up, her face glowing. “Is that your revenge for me arguing with you? Would the match be so bad, then? Is he not good enough for me?”

“Moore’s a gentleman,” Yorke said immediately. “His blood is as old and as fine as yours or mine.”

Shirley smiled, bright and proud. “That he is. There’s no inequality there, then, is there? No one could say we don’t suit. Thank you for your approval. Will you give me away when I stop being Miss Keeldar to become Mrs. Moore?”

Yorke stared at her, completely baffled. He couldn’t tell if she was teasing or dead serious, if her bright eyes were laughing or burning with earnest feeling. “I don’t understand thee at all, lass,” he said, turning for the door.

Shirley laughed, the sound light and warm. “Don’t worry. You’re not the only one who’s confused. As long as Moore understands me, that’s all that matters, isn’t it?”

“Moore can sort his own business out from now on,” Yorke said, shaking his head. “I’ll not meddle in it anymore. Good day, Miss Shirley.”

The day was soft and fair, thin clouds dimming the sun just enough to take the edge off the heat, and Caroline had convinced Mrs. Pryor to slip on her bonnet and light summer shawl for a walk up the far end of the Hollow, leaving Shirley to her callers. The glen narrowed here, the sides rising steep and thick with scrub oak and bramble, the mill stream gurgling and foaming over stones, rushing past gnarled old roots as if it was in a hurry to get somewhere no one else could follow. Half a mile from the mill, they stepped off the path into quiet: no footprints marred the wild rose blooms, no sound of men or machinery broke the hum of bees and the song of dozens of small birds. Bluebells spangled the shade like scattered bits of sky, tiny white star flowers dotted the grass, and the whole place felt like a secret no one but the trees had ever witnessed.

Mrs. Pryor loved quiet walks. She avoided high roads, hated crowded lanes, but with Caroline beside her, she had no fear at all. The tension melted out of her shoulders, her face softened, and for the first time Caroline could remember, she talked freely, pointing out different birds in the branches, naming every tiny wild flower that grew at their feet, talking about the botany she had studied years before, long before she came to Fieldhead. They stopped to sit on a mossy grey rock half way up the green hill that rose above the ravine, and Mrs. Pryor looked around at the familiar view, talking softly of how the valley had changed over the decades, her descriptions gentle and vivid, full of a quiet love for the land that no one else would have guessed she held.

She turned to Caroline then, brushing a stray curl back from the girl’s pale forehead, her voice softer than Caroline had ever heard it. “I do hope this sweet hill air does you good, my dear. I wish I could see a little more colour in your cheeks—were you ever rosy and bright, as little girls are supposed to be?”

“I was, once,” Caroline smiled. “Two years ago, I looked in the glass and saw a round, rosy face, not the pale thing I see now. Back when I had no cares at all.”

“Do you fret over the future?” Mrs. Pryor asked, the words coming out slow, as if she was fighting her natural shyness to say them. “Don’t. Let the morrow look after itself.”

“It is not the future that troubles me,” Caroline said, her voice dropping almost to a whisper. “The present is heavy, sometimes. Too heavy. I long to run away from it all.”

She could see Mrs. Pryor struggling to ask if her uncle was unkind to her, and Caroline hurried to reassure her. “Uncle is kind enough. We never quarrel. No one is cruel to me. But sometimes… sometimes I feel as if I am not living at all, just enduring the days, marking time. Since you and Shirley came to Fieldhead, I have been happier, but… there is a reason I cannot name, a secret I cannot tell anyone, that makes me ache to leave this place, and forget every last person in it.”
