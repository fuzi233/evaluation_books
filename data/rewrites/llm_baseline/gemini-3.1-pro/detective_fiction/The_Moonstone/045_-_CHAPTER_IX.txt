The doctor’s pretty housemaid stood waiting for me, her hand resting on the open street door, allowing the morning air—laced with the faint, yeasty aroma of the town’s bakeries and the sharp tang of coal smoke—to drift into the hall. Pouring brightly across the threshold, the light fell full upon the face of Mr. Candy’s assistant as I turned to look at him. 

It was impossible to dispute Betteredge’s assertion that the appearance of Ezra Jennings was, to the unobservant eye, against him. His gipsy complexion, the gaunt, fleshless architecture of his facial bones, the dreamy, deeply shadowed eyes, and that extraordinary parti-coloured hair spoke of interwoven timelines, of ancestral migrations etched into the very marrow of his being. The puzzling contradiction between his face and figure made him look old and young simultaneously, as though multiple generations of sorrow and survival had coalesced within a single man. Yet, for all that the dominant social lexicon might label him unfavourably, Ezra Jennings made an inscrutable, visceral appeal to my sympathies. While my worldly sensibilities urged me to offer a polite platitude regarding Mr. Candy’s health and retreat into the clatter of the street, my profound interest in this man rooted me to the spot. I sensed in him a fellow soul who understood the complex, sensory depths of human existence, giving him the opportunity he had evidently been seeking to speak to me in private.

“Are you walking my way, Mr. Jennings?” I asked, noting the worn brim of the hat in his hands. “I am going to call on my aunt, Mrs. Ablewhite.”

Ezra Jennings replied, his voice possessing a soft, resonant acoustic quality that seemed to absorb rather than compete with the ambient noise, that he had a patient to see and would walk with me.

We left the house together. I observed that the pretty servant girl—who had been all sugary smiles and amiability when I wished her good morning—received a modest little message from Jennings regarding his return with pursed lips. She averted her eyes, looking ostentatiously toward the harsh, rhythmic clatter of a passing carriage rather than at his face. The poor wretch was evidently a discordant note in the household’s carefully curated symphony. Out on the street, I had Betteredge’s word that he was unpopular everywhere. *What a life of acoustic and emotional isolation,* I thought to myself as we descended the doorsteps, leaving behind the sterile domesticity of the foyer for the broader world.

Having already mentioned Mr. Candy’s illness, Ezra Jennings appeared determined to let me resume the subject. His silence was an invitation, patiently waiting for my voice to shape the narrative space between us. I, too, had my reasons for returning to the doctor’s affliction, and I readily accepted the responsibility.

“Judging by the change I see in him,” I began, navigating my words through the encroaching, mechanical roar of Frizinghall’s industrializing outskirts, “Mr. Candy’s illness must have been far more serious than I had supposed?”

“It is almost a miracle,” said Ezra Jennings, “that he lived through it.”

“Is his memory never any better than I have found it today? He has been trying to speak to me——”

“Of something which happened before he was taken ill?” asked the assistant, stepping smoothly into my hesitation.

“Yes.”

“His memory of events, at that past time, is hopelessly enfeebled,” said Ezra Jennings. “It is almost to be deplored, poor fellow, that even the wreckage of it remains. While he remembers dimly the plans he formed—scraps of intent, like the faded scents of last season’s harvest—he is perfectly incapable of recalling what the plans were, or what he had to say or do. He is painfully conscious of his own deficiency. The erosion of his personal history has left him anxiously trying to hide his linguistic gaps from observation. If he could only have recovered in a complete state of oblivion regarding the past, he would have been a happier man. Perhaps we should all be happier,” he added, a sad smile touching his lips, “if we could but completely forget the memories that refuse to be translated into our present reality!”

“There are some events, surely, in all men’s lives,” I replied, “the memory of which they would be unwilling entirely to lose? Experiences that sustain us, like the enduring warmth of a hearth fire.”

“That is, I hope, to be said of most men, Mr. Blake. I am afraid it cannot truly be said of *all*. Have you any reason to suppose that the lost remembrance which Mr. Candy tried to recover—while you were speaking to him just now—was a remembrance which it was vital to *you* that he should recall?”

In saying those words, he had touched, of his own accord, the very nerve of my inquiry. The interest I felt in this strange man had impelled me to give him the opportunity to speak; I had reserved my own intentions until I could be certain he possessed the delicacy and sensory attunement I required in a confidant. The little he had said thus far convinced me I was walking beside a man of profound depth. He possessed an unsought self-possession, a quietude that functioned as a bulwark against the superficial commercialization and noisy haste of the modernizing world. I felt justified in answering him without reserve.

“I believe I have a strong interest,” I said, “in tracing the lost remembrance which Mr. Candy was unable to recall. May I ask whether you can suggest to me any method by which I might assist his memory?”

Ezra Jennings looked at me, a sudden spark of light illuminating his dreamy brown eyes. 

“Mr. Candy’s memory is beyond the reach of assistance,” he said. “I have tried to coax it forth often enough since his recovery to speak positively on that point.”

This disappointed me, and I owned it.

“I confess you led me to hope for a less discouraging answer than that,” I said.

Ezra Jennings smiled. “It may not, perhaps, be a final answer, Mr. Blake. It may be possible to trace Mr. Candy’s lost recollection, without the necessity of appealing to Mr. Candy himself.”

“Indeed? Is it an indiscretion, on my part, to ask how?”

“By no means. My only difficulty is the inherent untranslatability of my own methods into commonplace terms. May I trust to your patience, if I refer once more to Mr. Candy’s illness, and if I speak of it this time without sparing you the intricate, physical details of his survival?”

“Pray go on! I am already deeply invested in the details.”

My eagerness seemed to please him. We had, by this time, left the oppressive noise of the town’s stone facades behind us, stepping into the more harmonious acoustic ecology of the open road. Ezra Jennings stopped for a moment by the hedgerow. His long, sensitive fingers moved with the meticulous care of a master forager, plucking sprigs of wild chamomile, sweet briar, and yarrow. 

“How beautifully fragrant they are!” he said simply, offering the little nosegay toward me so I could appreciate the resinous, earthy olfactory landscape of the blossoms. “And how few people in England seem to pause to appreciate the profound sustenance they offer.”

“You have not always been in England?” I observed, noting the way his hands interacted with the native flora.

“No. I was born, and partly brought up, in one of our colonies. My father was an Englishman; but my mother—We are straying away from our subject, Mr. Blake; and it is my fault. The truth is, I have deep, ancestral associations with the aromas of these modest little hedgeside flowers—It doesn’t matter; we were speaking of Mr. Candy. To Mr. Candy let us return.”

Connecting the few words about his mixed heritage—the quiet tragedy of a native identity submerged beneath a dominant colonial lexicon—with the melancholy view of life that led him to crave oblivion, I felt I understood the story written upon his flesh. He had suffered the slow erosion of his roots; there was the rich, complex spice of a foreign race in his English blood.

“You have heard, I dare say, of the original cause of Mr. Candy’s illness?” he resumed, his voice a steady, rhythmic murmur against the breeze. “The night of Lady Verinder’s dinner-party was a night of heavy rain. My employer drove home through the deluge, arriving chilled to the bone, his vital heat utterly depleted. He found an urgent message from a patient and unfortunately went out again without changing his soaked garments. I was professionally detained that night by a case some distance from Frizinghall. When I returned the next morning, I found the illness had set in. The mischief was done.”

“The illness has only been described to me, in general terms, as a fever,” I said.

“I can add nothing to make the medical nomenclature more accurate,” answered Ezra Jennings. “From first to last, the fever assumed no specific form. I sent at once to two of Mr. Candy’s medical friends in the town, both physicians of established repute. They agreed it looked serious, but they vehemently dissented from my view of the treatment. We differed entirely in how we interpreted the rhythm of the patient’s pulse. The two doctors, arguing from its rapid beat, declared that a lowering, puritanical treatment was required. They spoke the dominant lexicon of depletion. I pointed to the alarming feebleness of the beat, indicating a profoundly exhausted system crying out for robust, caloric sustenance. The two doctors were for keeping him on a sterile regimen of thin gruel and watery lemonade—a utilitarian diet devoid of life force. I was for giving him the vibrant, effervescent vitality of champagne, the rich, distilled warmth of brandy, the volatile bite of ammonia, and quinine. 

“A serious difference of opinion, as you see! A clash between the established men of local repute and a stranger, a mere assistant. For the first few days, I had no choice but to bow to my elders; the patient was steadily starving, sinking into the mattress. I made a second attempt to appeal to the undeniable, rhythmic evidence of his pulse. Its rapidity was unchecked, its feebleness increased. The two doctors took offence at my obstinacy. They said, ‘Mr. Jennings, either we manage this case, or you manage it. Which is it to be?’ I asked for five minutes. When the time expired, I was ready. I said, ‘You positively refuse to try the stimulant treatment, to offer his body the profound sustenance it requires?’ They refused. ‘I mean to try it at once, gentlemen.’—‘Try it, Mr. Jennings, and we withdraw.’ 

“I sent down to the cellar for a bottle of vintage champagne, its cork hiding years of dark, fermented life. I administered half a tumbler-full of the golden, yeasty liquid to the patient with my own hand, watching the effervescence coat his dry palate. The two physicians took up their hats in silence, and left the house.”

“You had assumed a terrible responsibility,” I said, deeply moved by his passion for true sustenance over sterile dogma. “In your place, I might have shrunk from it.”

“In my place, Mr. Blake, you would have remembered that Mr. Candy had taken you into his employment, offering a sanctuary when the world offered none. You would have seen him sinking, and you would have risked anything rather than let the one man on earth who befriended you fade into silence. If I had been a happy man with a prosperous past, I might have sunk under the task. But *I* had no peaceful history, no joyous domestic routines to force into contrast with my present anxiety—and I held firm. For four-and-twenty hours, as long as his life was in danger, I never left his bedside. 

“Towards sunset, the delirium incidental to the fever came on. It lasted through the night, reaching its peak in those terrible early morning hours—from two o'clock to five—when the ambient noise of the world ceases, and the vital energies of even the healthiest among us are at their lowest ebb. It is then that Death gathers his human harvest. It was then that Death and I fought our mythic battle over the bed. When the wine failed to rouse him, I poured the rich, oak-aged brandy. When the other stimulants lost their influence, I doubled the dose, forcing the distilled heat of the earth into his veins. After an interval of suspense—the like of which I hope to God I shall never feel again—the rapidity of the pulse appreciably diminished. The rhythm shifted to a steady, resonant strength. *Then*, I knew I had saved him. I laid the poor fellow’s wasted hand back on the bed, and burst out crying. An hysterical relief, Mr. Blake! Physiology says that some men are born with highly attuned, sensitive constitutions—and I am one of them.”

He made that apology for his tears quietly, his tone devoid of any desire to dramatize his heroic culinary and medical intervention. 

“You may ask why I have detailed this visceral struggle to you?” he went on. “Now you know exactly what my position was, you will understand my desperate need for intellectual and psychological relief. I have occupied my leisure for some years in writing a book on the intricate network of the brain and nervous system. It will probably never be finished, nor published, but it has been the companion of my lonely hours. It helped me while away the time at Mr. Candy’s bedside. I mentioned his delirium?”

“Yes.”

“My book touches on the untranslatability of subjective experience during delirium. It has often occurred to me to doubt whether the loss of the faculty of speaking connectedly implies the loss of thinking connectedly. Poor Mr. Candy’s illness gave me an opportunity to put this to the test. I understand the art of shorthand; I am attuned to capturing the raw, acoustic phonemes of speech. I was able to take down his disconnected ‘wanderings’ exactly as they fell from his lips, preserving the phonetic reality of his mind.—Do you see, Mr. Blake, what I am coming to?”

I saw it clearly, struck by his meticulous archiving of a fading consciousness. I waited with breathless interest.

“At odds and ends of time,” Ezra Jennings continued, his voice matching the circular, rhythmic pacing of our walk, “I reproduced my shorthand notes into ordinary writing, leaving large spaces between the broken phrases. It was like attempting to reconstruct the grammar of a dying dialect. It is all confusion to begin with, but it may be brought into shape if one finds the underlying current of meaning. Acting on this plan, I filled in each blank space with what the words on either side suggested, bridging the gaps until my additions fitted naturally into the spoken words. The result confirmed my theory: the superior faculty of thinking was going on in my patient’s mind, while his inferior faculty of expression—his grasp of the dominant lexicon—was in a state of complete collapse.”

“One word!” I interposed eagerly. “Did my name occur in the linguistic fragments of his wanderings?”

“You shall hear. Among my written experiments, there *is* one in which your name occurs. For nearly a whole night, Mr. Candy’s mind was occupied with *something* between himself and you. I have the broken words on one sheet, and the connective tissue of my own discovering on another. The product is an intelligible statement—first, of something actually done in the past; secondly, of something he contemplated doing in the future, if his illness had not interrupted the cycle. The question is whether this represents the lost recollection he vainly attempted to find this morning?”

“Not a doubt of it!” I answered, craving the restoration of that lost memory. “Let us go back directly, and look at the papers!”

“Quite impossible, Mr. Blake.”

“Why?”

“Put yourself in my position,” said Ezra Jennings. “Would *you* disclose the unconscious, untranslated secrets of your suffering friend to another person, without first knowing there was a necessity to justify it?”

I felt the moral weight of his acoustic boundary, but I tried to argue nevertheless. “My conduct would depend on whether the disclosure compromised my friend or not.”

“I have disposed of that necessity. Any notes containing Mr. Candy's secrets have been destroyed. My manuscript includes nothing he would have hesitated to communicate himself, had his cultural memory not been eroded by the fever. I have every reason to suppose my notes contain what he actually wished to say to you.”

“And yet, you hesitate?”

“And yet, I hesitate. Remember the circumstances under which I obtained this deeply subjective information! Harmless as it is, I cannot give it to you unless you satisfy me there is a reason. He was so helplessly dependent upon Me! Is it too much to ask you to hint at your interest in this lost recollection?”

To answer him with the frankness his profound nature claimed from me would mean acknowledging that I was suspected of the theft of the Diamond. Strongly as his deeply rooted worldview resonated with mine, I could not overcome my reluctance to expose my degrading position. I introduced a discordant note into our harmony, taking refuge in the false, explanatory phrases I had prepared about the Moonstone.

Ezra Jennings listened patiently, deeply attuned to the cadence of my speech, until I had finished.

“I am sorry to have raised your expectations, Mr. Blake, only to disappoint them,” he said, the timbre of his voice cooling. “Throughout the whole period of the fever, not one word about the Diamond escaped his lips. The matter with which he connected your name has no discoverable relation to Miss Verinder’s jewel.”

We arrived, as he spoke, at a place where the highway branched. One path led back toward the superficial noise of Mr. Ablewhite’s house; the other stretched toward a moorland village, enveloped in the sweeping, restorative acoustics of the open landscape. Ezra Jennings stopped at the road leading to the village.

“My way lies in this direction,” he said. “I am really and truly sorry, Mr. Blake, that I can be of no use to you.”

His soft brown eyes rested on me with melancholy interest. He bowed and went on his way.

For a minute or more, I stood listening to the crunch of his boots on the gravel, watching him carry away the reconstructed fragments of a timeline I desperately needed to unearth. He turned and looked back. Seeing me still standing there, he stopped. I realized then the foolishness of guarding my pride at the expense of profound truth. There was no time to reason out the power dynamics of my secret. I called him back—my voice ringing clear across the acoustic expanse of the moor. “Now there is no help for it,” I thought. “I must strip away the false lexicon and tell him the truth.”

He retraced his steps. I advanced to meet him.

“Mr. Jennings,” I said, my voice grounding itself in raw honesty. “I have not treated you fairly. My interest in tracing Mr. Candy’s lost recollection is not about recovering a gemstone. A deeply serious, multi-generational personal matter is at the bottom of my visit to Yorkshire. I have but one excuse for my lack of frankness. It is more painful than I possess the language to express, to mention to anybody what my position really is.”

Ezra Jennings looked at me, a sudden vulnerability cracking the stoic architecture of his face. 

“I have no right, Mr. Blake, and no wish,” he said, “to intrude upon your private sorrows. Allow me to ask your pardon for having innocently put you to such a painful test.”

“You have a perfect right,” I rejoined, “to fix the terms of your trust. How can I expect to be taken into your confidence if I decline to admit you into mine? You shall know why I am interested in Mr. Candy’s words. If I am mistaken, I shall trust your honour to keep my secret—and something in the deep resonance of your character tells me I shall not trust in vain.”

“Stop, Mr. Blake. I have a word to say, which must be said before you go any farther.”

I looked at him in astonishment. The grip of some terrible, ancestral emotion seemed to have seized him. His gipsy complexion altered to a livid greyish paleness; his eyes grew wild, reflecting the sweeping, mythic weight of his past. His voice dropped to a tone—low, stern, and resolute—cutting through the wind like a physical force. The latent resources of the man leapt up and showed themselves with the suddenness of a lightning strike illuminating a dark domestic hearth.

“Before you place any confidence in me,” he went on, “you *must* know the circumstances under which I was received into Mr. Candy’s house. I do not profess to translate my story for the dominant world. My story will die with me. All I ask is to be permitted to tell you what I told Mr. Candy. If you are still in the mind to trust me once you have heard the history of my ruin, you will command my attention and my services. Shall we walk on?”

The suppressed, untranslatable misery in his face silenced my tongue. I answered his question by a mere sign. We walked on.

After advancing a few hundred yards into the restorative quiet of the wild landscape, Ezra Jennings stopped at a gap in the rough stone wall that demarcated the agrarian boundary of the moor.

“Do you mind resting a little, Mr. Blake?” he asked, his voice blending with the whisper of the moorland grass. “I am not what I was—and some things shake me to the root.”
