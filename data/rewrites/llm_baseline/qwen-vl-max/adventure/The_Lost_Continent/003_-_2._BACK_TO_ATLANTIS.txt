Tatho’s words lingered in my mind long after the night had fallen, like a flame that refuses to be extinguished. I couldn’t help but reflect on how deeply I had immersed myself in governing Yucatan—so completely, in fact, that I’d lost touch with what was happening back in Atlantis. For years, it had been evident that the sailors who crossed the seas spoke cautiously, their tongues bound by unspoken laws. Only the news the Empress permitted seeped beyond her borders. Yet I had never sought to pry beneath the veil. My duties in the colony consumed me, and I respected the orders of others—even when they silenced truth. It was not in my nature to torture men simply because they followed commands, even if those commands kept secrets from me.

But now, the iron discipline of the Priestly Clan left no room for hesitation. As custom dictated, I had been stripped of office without warning. From that moment, all records, authority, and power passed to my successor. Though I might remain as a guest in the pyramid I once called home—allowed to witness one final sunrise—I was strictly ordered to leave Yucatan at once and return to Atlantis without delay.

Tatho, though, showed himself to be a man of loyalty. Before dawn broke, he stood beside me, offering every resource at his disposal. I asked little. “A ship to take me home,” I said, “and I shall owe you my gratitude.”

He looked surprised. “You may have one, if you wish. But my vessels are worn from long voyages and need careening. If you take them, your journey will be slow. Why not take your own navy? They’re anchored in harbor now—I saw them when we arrived. Strong ships, well-built and proud.”

“But they are not mine,” I replied. “That fleet belongs to Yucatan.”

“Ah,” he said, smiling faintly, “but Deucalion—you *are* Yucatan. Or rather, you were yesterday, and for twenty years before that.”

I understood his meaning, but it did not please me. “The ships belong either to private merchants or to the state,” I said stiffly. “I cannot claim even a ten-slave galley as my own.”

Tatho shrugged. “Perhaps you know your principles best. But to me, it seems reckless for a man in your position not to maintain a strong navy of his own. You never know when a recall might come—and without preparation, a lifetime’s work could vanish in an hour.”

“I have no fear for my fortune,” I answered coldly.

“Of course not,” he said. “Because you know I am your friend. But had another been appointed viceroy, you might have found yourself stripped bare. Few men resist the temptation of wealth already waiting in coffers they’ve helped fill.”

“Lord Tatho,” I said, “it is clear we no longer share the same values. The wealth I’ve gathered here would hardly tempt any man. I wear the clothes you see me in now. I possess a small box of medicines—nothing more. Three slaves: two scribes and one sturdy European savage who cooks and draws my bath. My salary has been nothing more than a soldier’s ration. And if anyone under my rule has ever been unjustly taxed—even a single ounce of bronze—I ask you, as a final favor, to hang that man for me. Let him die as a liar and thief.”

Tatho stared at me, his expression unreadable. “I do not know whether to admire you or pity you. To be astonished or despise. We heard tales of your integrity in Atlantis—your justice, your sternness—but I swear by the old Gods, no one imagined you carried this far. Money is power, Deucalion. With money, you can move mountains. Without it, you are crushed by the first misstep.”

“The Gods will decide my fate,” I said.

“Possibly,” he replied. “But I prefer to shape mine myself. I came here not to follow your example. I shall govern Yucatan wisely—yes—but also for Tatho, the viceroy. I brought my navy of eight ships, a personal guard, my wife, her women, and her slaves. All must be supported. Why should it be otherwise? A people should pay for their ruler.”

“We shall not agree,” I said. “You hold the power now. Use it as you see fit. If it meant anything, I would beg you to treat these people with mercy. They have grown dear to me.”

“You’ve grown tired of me,” he said, “and I regret it. But even to keep your respect—something I value above all else—I cannot act as you did. It would be impossible. Do not judge all men by your own standard. A Tatho is not a colossus like a Deucalion. And besides… I have a wife and children. They must be provided for—even if I neglect myself.”

“And there,” I said, “you give me the advantage. I have no wife to burden me.”

He caught my tone quickly. “It seems you have nothing that makes life worth living. No wife, no children, no riches, no cook, no retinue, no finery. I must say, old friend, you are strangely ignorant about some things. For instance—you don’t know how to dine. On every day of our long voyage, I promised myself that your feast would make up for all the plain sea fare. I expected a grand banquet. But what did I get? A farmer’s meal. You told me how it was prepared. Your European slave may be strong and loyal—but he’s a demon in the kitchen. By the Gods, I’ve eaten better on campaign!”

“I know this is a frontier settlement,” I said, “without refinements. But if the deer, fish, and other resources of this land are not used better than they have been, I shall consider it my duty to fry a few cooks in grease—to teach them better skills.”

“Deucalion,” he said, “have you forgotten what it means to have taste? Have you no regard for your dignity? Look at your clothes! You dress like a shepherd. Not a jewel, not a gaud to brighten you.”

“I eat,” I said coldly, “when hunger calls. I wear this robe until it wears out. Excessive feasting and many garments are luxuries I never desired. But enough. Our differences run deep. You have changed, Tatho. Perhaps I have too. These changes creep in silently with time. Let us part now, and remember only the friendship we shared twenty years ago. That memory has always tasted sweet to me.”

Tatho bowed. “So be it. And I still charge myself to you for that ship. Dawn is near. It would not be proper for the former ruler to walk through the city streets in daylight after his dismissal.”

“So be it,” he said. “You shall have my navy. I wished you had asked for more.”

“No,” I said. “Not the navy. One small ship. Believe me, more would be wasted.”

“Oh,” he said, “now I shall play the tyrant. I am viceroy now, and I will have my way. You may go with nothing—though I cannot help that. But you shall not leave unattended.”

And so, with no choice, I sailed aboard the *Bear*, Tatho’s personal vessel, escorted by the rest of his fleet. But departure was delayed. The ships lay moored at the inner harbor, drained of supplies, crews exhausted. To launch them immediately would have been suicide. Instead, courtesies were observed—the *Bear* was hauled into the entrance basin and anchored in the fairway, where she and her companions took on wood, water, cured meats, and fish, refitted swiftly and thoroughly.

For the first time in twenty years, I found myself free from labor. Officially, I had already left Yucatan. I could not interfere with the ship’s operations—sea matters belonged solely to the Mariners’ Guild, protected by royal decree. So I spent my days watching the quays, the harbors, the palaces, and pyramids I had helped build stone by stone. I gazed across the fields and grain lands beyond the walls, longing for the dense forests we had cleared so slowly, field by field. Would Tatho continue this work? I hoped so—despite his selfish words.

By day or night, I turned my thoughts to the higher mysteries, the sacred studies of the Priestly Clan—no books, no instruments, no temples needed. Just mind and spirit.

The navy was refitted with remarkable speed. Never before had ships been provisioned, caulked, and remanned so swiftly for an ocean crossing. Barely a month after arriving, they set sail eastward, over the hills and dales of the open sea.

No rowing slaves from Europe were taken now—modern humanity forbade the old custom of letting them eat each other during long voyages. Sails alone were unreliable. But modern science had discovered how to harness solar energy. When the Sun shone clear, machines aboard the ships drew seawater forward and expelled it forcefully at the stern, propelling the vessel forward—even against the wind. This secret knowledge was guarded closely by mariners.

Navigation had also advanced. No longer did sailors rely solely on starry nights. A small device—a balanced image with an arm pointing steadily toward the Southern Cross—was placed at the bow of every ship. By adjusting angles, navigators could steer with precision. Other tools allowed accurate positioning on the vast ocean. Modern sailors trusted less in gods and more in their own strength and wits.

Yet even today, tradition held sway. Before sailing, the crew would wash and dress in their finest clothes, go ashore solemnly, and visit a minor temple—perhaps one whose god was disliked by the common folk. There, they made lavish sacrifices, chanting and offering gifts. Then came a feast in honor of the deity. Drunk and bloated, they returned to the ship, ready to face the sea.

My journey was unlike any I had known. We did not hug the coasts. Instead, we cut straight across the gulf, passing the Carib Islands as if they were signposts. We stopped only twice to replenish—each time, the natives brought wood, water, and fruit freely, so great was their submission. No fights, no resistance. A testament to Atlantis’s growing power and its most distant colony’s strength.

Then onward, boldly, across the endless ocean. No prayers to guide us. No offerings to appease the gods. One might criticize such impiety—but admiration rose for their courage and skill. The dangers of the sea were the gods’ to deal, and men could only endure.

We faced storms. The mariners fought them with grim endurance. Twice, blazing stones fell from the sky, hissing into the sea beside us—harmless, yet terrifying. And the great sea beasts hunted us, as they always had.

Only once did we suffer real loss. Three massive sea lizards attacked the *Bear*—the ship I traveled upon—at once. It happened at midday, when the Sun blazed at full strength. Our solar machines hummed with power, driving the ship forward faster than a man could walk on land.

Three beasts emerged over the horizon—two smaller females on one flank, a giant male on the other. They beat the waves with their flippers, their necks rising like masts. Their jaws chomped hungrily. The air reeked of foulness.

Our navy formed a line, but these creatures no longer hunted alone. Like men, they had learned to fight in packs.

Captain Tob urged me to retreat to the after-castle. He was responsible to Lord Tatho for my safety. He knew the beasts would kill some of the crew. If I were harmed, he feared he would be forced to surrender himself to them—or worse, face Tatho’s wrath.

But I refused. A man gains no wisdom by avoiding danger. This attack was new to me. I wanted to learn.

I wrote a letter to Tatho explaining my decision—though he seemed indifferent. Then I stayed in my seat beneath the awning.

The beasts surged forward. Arrows bounced off their thick hides. Fireballs barely singed them. Only twenty axe blows delivered together could drive one back—and even then, only by sheer force, leaving no mark.

Man has long struggled against beast for dominion. Only in Atlantis, Egypt, and Yucatan has he dared to stand firm. In Europe and Africa, beasts rule. Men hide in caves and treetops, fleeing like prey.

On the oceans, beasts are masters—unchecked.

Yet here, on this desolate sea, though these lizards were new to me, it was thrilling to test my warcraft against their brute strength.

From the beginning, survival demanded action. Hiding below deck was useless—if the beasts couldn’t reach men above, they’d smash the ship apart. So battle had to be met head-on, until the beasts were satisfied and left.

Thus, I found myself in a one-sided fight—and felt the joy of combat once more.

After delivering a dozen heavy axe blows—useless as striking a wall—I found a lance. That changed everything.

Their eyes were small, deep-set in bony sockets. I judged them vulnerable.

I stepped into view, offering myself as bait.

The beast lunged—like a falling stone.

I sidestepped and drove the lance into its eye.

Thank the Gods I had trained with the lance until aim became instinct.

The blade pierced true. The shaft snapped in my hand.

The beast recoiled, blinded and roaring, thrashing the water with its flippers.

In a cascade of foam, I saw it bend its long neck, rubbing its head against its back—still impaled, suffering agony, but unable to dislodge the spear.

Eventually, it gave up and fled—quickly vanishing into the distance.

The other two beasts had also departed—but not so peacefully.

Tob, seeing my resolve, deliberately pushed a sailor into the jaws of each, ensuring they were sated and gone.

It was clear Tob feared for his skin. I smiled inwardly, thinking of the threats Tatho must have issued to protect me.

It is comforting, after all, when old friends still care enough to watch over you.
