"Well, I suppose we must," Mrs. Thornton sighed, the weight of necessity settling heavily on her shoulders.

The decision to visit the Hales had required a diplomatic campaign on the part of Mr. Thornton. Persuading his mother to abandon her usual protocol of "heavy state" visits—a mode of travel involving hired carriages and a specific, solemn demeanor—had proven difficult. While Mr. Thornton had offered to provide a carriage, Mrs. Thornton remained steadfast in her refusal to accept permanent horses. For her, transportation was a matter of occasion: hired cabs for the rare social engagements, and only the finest equines for the truly grave matters of life and death.

Currently, however, she found herself in a precarious position. She had recently utilized borrowed horses for three days straight, a maneuver that had successfully exhausted the patience and resources of every acquaintance within a twenty-mile radius. Now, with her local network depleted, she faced the prospect of traveling to Crampton, a distance too great for comfortable walking. When she pressed her son regarding the cost of hiring a cab, her tone suggested that the mere idea of paying for such a convenience was absurd.

"Why should I incur this expense?" she had reasoned aloud. "There is no utility in cultivating intimacy with every teacher and master in Milton. Next, he will expect me to call upon Fanny's dancing instructor's wife!"

"And so I would, Mother," Mr. Thornton replied gently, "were Mr. Mason and his wife friendless in this strange city, much as the Hales are."

"Oh! Do not speak so hastily," she countered, her eyes narrowing. "I intend to depart tomorrow. My sole purpose today is to ensure you understand the gravity of the situation."

"If you are leaving tomorrow, I shall arrange for the horses immediately."

"Nonsense, John. One would think you were made of pure gold."

"Not entirely, yet. However, regarding the horses, my resolve is absolute. Recall the last time you ventured out in a cab; you returned home suffering from a severe headache caused by the violent jolting."

"I never complained of it, I assure you," she retorted.

"No," he agreed with a touch of pride. "My mother is not a woman given to complaining. Precisely because she does not complain, I feel compelled to watch over her health with greater vigilance."

He turned his gaze toward his daughter, who sat nearby. "As for Fanny, a measure of hardship would actually benefit her constitution."

"She is not forged from the same material as you, John," Mrs. Thornton stated firmly. "She could not withstand such rigors."

Mrs. Thornton fell silent. Her final remark touched upon a subject that mortified her deeply. She harbored an unconscious disdain for weakness, particularly in those areas where both she and her son possessed strength. Fanny was fragile precisely where her parents were robust. Mrs. Thornton was rarely inclined toward lengthy philosophical debates; her sharp intellect and iron will allowed her to bypass internal deliberation and arrive instantly at conclusions. Instinctively, she knew that nothing could fortify Fanny to endure suffering patiently or confront adversity with courage. Though this realization stung her, it transformed into a peculiar, pitying tenderness in her demeanor—a behavior reminiscent of how mothers treat their frail, sickly offspring.

To an outside observer, unaware of the family dynamics, Mrs. Thornton's conduct might have appeared to indicate a far deeper affection for Fanny than for John. Such an interpretation would have been profoundly mistaken. The boldness with which mother and son exchanged uncomfortable truths revealed a profound trust in the solidity of their respective characters. In contrast, Mrs. Thornton's uneasy gentleness toward her daughter stemmed from a desire to conceal Fanny's poverty amidst the grand virtues she herself possessed and highly valued in others. This shame exposed a lack of security in her maternal affection. She addressed her son solely as "John," reserving endearments like "love" and "dear" exclusively for Fanny. Yet, her heart gave thanks for her son day and night, and she walked proudly among her peers largely due to his existence.

"Fanny dear," Mrs. Thornton announced, breaking the tension, "I shall hire horses for the carriage today to visit the Hales. Would you not like to stop by Nurse's house first? It lies in the same direction, and she delights in seeing you. You could visit her while I attend to Mrs. Hale."

"Oh, Mamma, it is such a long way, and I am so tired," Fanny protested.

"Tired of what?" Mrs. Thornton asked, her brow furrowing slightly.

"The weather, I suppose. It feels so oppressive."

"Could not you ask Nurse to come here? The carriage could fetch her, allowing her to spend the remainder of the day with us, which I am certain she would appreciate."

Mrs. Thornton paused, setting down her embroidery and appearing to deliberate. Finally, she spoke. "It would be a considerable distance for her to walk back at night."

"Oh, I shall send her home in a cab. I simply forgot to consider her needs."

Just then, Mr. Thornton entered the room, preparing to depart for the mill.

"Mother," he said, "need I remind you that if there is any minor comfort you can offer Mrs. Hale, considering her condition, please extend it willingly?"

"If I can ascertain what she requires, certainly. However, having never suffered illness myself, I am somewhat unfamiliar with the particular whims of invalids."

"Well, here stands Fanny," Mr. Thornton pointed out, gesturing to his daughter. "She is perpetually afflicted. Perhaps she can suggest something suitable."

"I do not suffer from ailments constantly," Fanny snapped pettishly. "Moreover, I am not accompanying Mother. I have a headache today and shall remain indoors."

Mr. Thornton's expression darkened with annoyance. His mother's eyes were fixed intently on her needlework, stitching away industriously.

"Fanny," he commanded authoritatively, "I require you to go. It will prove beneficial to you rather than detrimental. Please comply with this request without further discussion."

Having delivered this ultimatum, he exited the room abruptly. Had he lingered even a moment longer, Fanny likely would have burst into tears at his imperious tone, even when couched in polite phrasing. Instead, she merely muttered under her breath.

"John always acts as if I imagine myself to be ill, when I am perfectly certain I am not. Who exactly are these Hales that warrant such excessive fuss?"

"Fanny, do not speak disparagingly of your brother," Mrs. Thornton admonished softly. "He possesses valid reasons, or he would not urge us to make this visit. Hasten and prepare yourself."

However, the brief skirmish between son and daughter did little to improve Mrs. Thornton's disposition toward the Hales. Her possessive heart replayed her daughter's query: *Who are they, that he is so eager for us to bestow our attention upon?* The question hung in the air like a burdensome refrain, lingering long after Fanny had dismissed the matter entirely in the delightful anticipation of admiring her new bonnet in the mirror.

Mrs. Thornton was inherently shy. Only in recent years had she possessed the leisure to engage in societal activities, and even then, she derived satisfaction primarily from hosting dinners or critiquing the culinary arts of others. Venturing out to cultivate acquaintanceship with strangers was a wholly different endeavor, one that left her distinctly ill-at-ease. Consequently, she appeared unusually stern and forbidding as she crossed the threshold into the Hales' modest drawing-room.

Margaret Hale was occupied with embroidering a delicate piece of cambric, intended for a garment for Edith's unborn child. To Mrs. Thornton, this activity represented "flimsy, useless labor." Conversely, she held Mrs. Hale's double knitting in higher regard, deeming it practical and sensible. The room itself was cluttered with various trinkets and ornaments, tasks that demanded significant time to clean—a luxury scarce for families of limited means. These observations formed the backdrop of Mrs. Thornton's stately conversation, punctuated by the standard platitudes that many recite while effectively blinding their senses to reality.

Mrs. Hale, meanwhile, exerted considerable effort in her responses, captivated by a strand of antique lace adorning Mrs. Thornton's attire. As she later confided to Dixon, it was "old English point, not manufactured for seventy years, impossible to purchase. It must be an heirloom, proving she descended from distinguished ancestors." Thus, the possessor of such ancestral treasures deemed herself worthy of more than the languid exertion required merely to be agreeable to a guest.

Soon, Margaret, straining her mental faculties to converse with Fanny, overheard her mother and Mrs. Thornton descending into an interminable discourse on domestic service.

"I presume you are not musical," Fanny remarked, scanning the room. "I observe no piano."

"I hold a fondness for fine music, though I lack the skill to perform it myself. Furthermore, neither my father nor my mother places much value on it; consequently, we sold our old instrument upon arriving here."

"How can you possibly subsist without one? It seems almost essential to life," Fanny mused.

*Fifteen shillings per week, with three shillings saved,* Margaret thought internally. *Yet, she must have been exceedingly young. She likely has forgotten her own youthful experiences, though she ought to recall those times.*

A subtle chill permeated Margaret's demeanor as she responded next. "You host excellent concerts here, I believe."
