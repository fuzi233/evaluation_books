“What was it you heard at Bamangwato about my brother’s journey?” Sir Henry asked, when I paused to tamp down the tobacco in my pipe. The ember caught; the smoke came sweet and sharp, like sun-warmed veld and cured leaf.

“I heard this,” I said, and the words tasted of dust and distance. “And I’ve not spoken it to any living soul till to-day. I heard he was starting for Solomon’s Mines.”

“Solomon’s Mines?” Captain Good and Sir Henry said together, as though the name itself had snapped like a whip in the air.

“Where are they?” Good demanded.

“I don’t know,” I answered. “I know where they’re said to be. Once I saw the peaks of the mountains that border them, blue as iron in the heat-haze; but there was a hundred and thirty miles of thirsty desert between me and those teeth of rock. And I’m not aware any white man ever got across it, save one. Still—perhaps the best I can do is tell you the legend as I know it. But first: you’ll give me your word not to repeat a syllable of it, without my leave. I’ve my reasons for asking.”

Sir Henry’s head dipped in assent; his eyes didn’t.

“Certainly, certainly,” said Good, quick as a man who smells a story the way a cook smells yeast rising.

“Well,” I began, “elephant hunters are mostly rough—men who don’t set store by anything but powder, water, and the ways of Kafirs. Yet now and then you meet one who listens—who stoops, like a careful gleaner after harvest, and gathers old talk from old mouths. He’ll sit by a cooking-fire and take down a proverb as though it were a rare spice, because he knows you can’t buy such things with money later, once the tongue that holds them is dead.

“It was such a man who first gave me Solomon’s Mines—near thirty years ago. His name was Evans. He was killed the following year by a wounded buffalo and lies near the Zambesi Falls. I can hear even now the night he told me: the mopane logs popping like small rifles as their sap burst, the far cough of a lion, and the steady, comforting music of men chewing mealies and talking low—sound that keeps a camp human.

“I was speaking to Evans of certain workings I’d found while hunting koodoo and eland in what’s now Lydenburg district. There’s a broad wagon-road cut out of solid rock, clean as if a giant chisel had shaved it; it runs to the mouth of a gallery. Inside were stacks of gold quartz piled ready for roasting—set out with the neatness of a cook laying firewood for baking, which shows the workers left in a hurry, abandoning their work mid-recipe, as it were. And about twenty paces in, the gallery is built across with masonry—fine work, squared and fitted so tight you’d struggle to press a knife-blade between the stones.

“‘Ay,’ Evans said, looking into the coals as if he read there, ‘but I’ll spin you a queerer yarn than that.’ And he told me of a ruined city in the far interior, which he believed to be Ophir of the Bible. Others, more learned, have said the same since. I listened open-eared—I was young, and the thought of ancient hands drawing treasure from a land now fallen into darkness took hold of my imagination as smoke takes hold of cloth.

“Then, sudden as a jackal’s yelp, Evans said, ‘Lad, did you ever hear of the Suliman Mountains, away to the north-west of the Mushakulumbwe country?’

“I told him no.

“‘Ah,’ he said, ‘that’s where Solomon really had his mines—his diamond mines, I mean.’

“‘How do you know that?’ I asked.

“‘Know it? Why, what is “Suliman” but Solomon with the edges worn off by other men’s mouths? Besides,’ he went on, ‘an old Isanusi—witch doctoress—up in Manica told me. She said the people beyond those mountains were a branch of the Zulus, speaking a dialect of Zulu—same root, different flavour, like sorghum beer brewed in a new pot. Finer and bigger men even. And among them live great wizards who learned their art from white men when “all the world was dark,” and they have the secret of a mine of bright stones.’

“I laughed at it then—though it clung to me, the way a strong smell clings to a blanket. Diamonds weren’t found yet at the Fields, and the whole thing seemed no more solid than campfire smoke. Then Evans went off and was killed, and for twenty years I never thought on it again.

“Yet twenty years later—long enough for a child to grow and a man to go grey—I heard something more. I was up beyond Manica at a place called Sitanda’s Kraal, a miserable hole where even hunger seemed to have settled and built itself a hut. You could get next to nothing to eat; no proper meal to anchor a man—only stringy fowl and sour mealie porridge doled out grudgingly, while the flies sang over it like a curse. There was little game. I had fever and lay about in that half-world where sound comes and goes as if through water: the thin crying of women, the dull clack of pestles in mortars, and, underneath, my own pulse drumming too loud in my ears.

“One day a Portuguese arrived with one companion, a half-breed. Now, I know the low-class Delagoa Portugee well enough—no greater devil unhung, living on the misery of others in the shape of slaves. But this man was different: tall and thin, large dark eyes, and curling grey moustachios; he had the air of the polite doms you read about, the kind who can bow even when they mean to cut your throat later.

“We spoke awhile—his broken English and my little Portuguese fitting together poorly, like mismatched crockery. He said his name was José Silvestre and that he had a place near Delagoa Bay.

“When he left next day with his half-breed, he took off his hat as though he stood in a Lisbon doorway instead of African dust.

“‘Good-bye, señor,’ he said. ‘If ever we meet again I shall be the richest man in the world, and I will remember you.’

“I was too weak to laugh much, but I did. And I watched him strike out for the great desert to the west, wondering if he was mad, or what hunger of the mind was driving him—because men will cross whole countries for what they cannot eat, while refusing to cross a river for a pot of good stew.

“A week passed and I beat the fever down. One evening I sat on the ground before my little tent, chewing the last leg of a miserable fowl I’d bought from a native for a strip of cloth worth twenty fowls. The skin was tough and tasted of smoke and old grease, but I worried it like a dog because that’s what hunger teaches you. The sun went down red as a forge over the desert; the air held its heat and the insects’ whining seemed to write lines around my head.

“Then I saw a figure on the rising ground opposite—about three hundred yards off—apparently a European, for it wore a coat. It crawled on hands and knees, got up, staggered a few yards, fell, crawled again—like a man trying to climb out of the world by inches.

“I sent one of my hunters. Presently he came in with the stranger—what was left of him.

“Who do you suppose it was?”

“José Silvestre,” said Good.

“Yes. José Silvestre—or rather his skeleton with a little skin stretched over it. His face was bright yellow with bilious fever; his eyes looked too big for his skull, as if they’d eaten the rest of him. Only parchment-skin, white hair, and bones that stuck up sharp beneath.

“‘Water! for the sake of Christ, water!’ he moaned. His lips were split; his tongue, thrust between them, was swollen and blackish.

“I gave him water with a little milk in it—milk, thin and precious there—and he drank in great gulps, two quarts or more, without drawing breath, as if he meant to swallow the whole desert back down. I would not let him take more. Then fever caught him again and he raved—Suliman’s Mountains, diamonds, the desert—words broken and repeated until they were nothing but sound.

“I carried him into my tent and did what I could, which was little. I knew how it must end. About eleven he grew quieter. I lay down for a short rest and slept.

“At dawn I woke, and in the half-light saw Silvestre sitting up—gaunt as a fencepost—and gazing out towards the west. The air was cold enough to make the canvas sweat. Then the first ray of sun shot across the wide plain and struck the far crest of one of the tallest Suliman peaks, more than a hundred miles away. The light touched it like a finger pointing.

“‘There it is!’ he cried in Portuguese, thrusting out his long thin arm. ‘But I shall never reach it—never. No one will ever reach it!’

“Then he stopped, and you could hear the camp waking: a child’s cough, the rattle of a pot, a bird testing its voice. He seemed to gather himself as if tying a knot in the last of his strength.

“‘Friend,’ he said, turning his head with effort, ‘are you there? My eyes grow dark.’

“‘Yes,’ I answered. ‘Yes. Lie down now and rest.’

“‘Ay,’ he said, ‘I shall rest soon. I have time to rest—all eternity. Listen: I am dying. You have been good to me. I will give you the writing. Perhaps you will get there if you can live to pass the desert, which has killed my poor servant and me.’

“He groped inside his shirt and brought out what I took for a Boer tobacco pouch made of sable-skin—Swart-vet-pens. It was tied with a strip of hide, a rimpi. His fingers trembled; he couldn’t loosen it. He handed it to me.

“‘Untie it,’ he said.

“I did, and drew out a torn bit of yellow linen with rusty writing on it. Inside that rag was a paper.

“He spoke feebly—his voice thinning like broth stretched too far.

“‘The paper has all that is on the linen. It took me years to read. Listen: my ancestor, a political refugee from Lisbon, one of the first Portuguese who landed on these shores, wrote that when he was dying on those mountains no white foot ever pressed before or since. His name was José da Silvestra, three hundred years ago. His slave, who waited on this side, found him dead and brought the writing home to Delagoa. It has been in the family ever since, but none cared to read it till I did. I have lost my life over it—but another may succeed and become the richest man in the world—the richest man in the world. Only give it to no one, señor. Go yourself.’

“Then he wandered again, talking to shadows, and within an hour it was done.

“God rest him. He died quietly. I buried him deep, with big boulders on his breast so the jackals wouldn’t have him. Then I came away.”

Sir Henry leaned forward. “But the document?”

“Yes,” said Good. “What was in it?”

“Well, gentlemen—if you like, I’ll tell you. I’ve never shown it to anyone except a drunken Portuguese trader who translated it for me and had forgotten the whole business by morning. The original rag is at my home in Durban, with poor Dom José’s translation. But I have the English rendering in my pocket-book, and a facsimile of the map—if map it can be called. Here it is.”

And I read:

“I, José da Silvestra, who am now dying of hunger in the little cave where no snow is on the north side of the nipple of the southernmost of the two mountains I have named Sheba’s Breasts, write this in the year 1590 with a cleft bone upon a remnant of my raiment, my blood being the ink. If my slave should find it when he comes, and should bring it to Delagoa, let my friend (name illegible) bring the matter to the knowledge of the king, that he may send an army which, if they live through the desert and the mountains, and can overcome the brave Kukuanes and their devilish arts, to which end many priests should be brought, will make him the richest king since Solomon. With my own eyes I have seen the countless diamonds stored in Solomon’s treasure chamber behind the white Death; but through the treachery of Gagool the witch-finder I might bring nought away, scarcely my life. Let him who comes follow the map, and climb the snow of Sheba’s left breast till he reaches the nipple, on the north side of which is the great road Solomon made, from whence three days’ journey to the King’s Palace. Let him kill Gagool. Pray for my soul. Farewell.

JOSÉ DA SILVESTRA.”

When I finished and showed the copy of the map—drawn, so the tale went, by a dying hand with blood for ink—there was silence. Even the ship’s small noises seemed to step back a pace: the faint groan of wood, the distant clink of metal, the hush between waves.

“Well,” Captain Good said at last, “I’ve been round the world twice and put in at most ports, but may I be hung for a mutineer if ever I heard a yarn like that out of a story book—or in it either.”

“It’s a queer tale, Mr. Quatermain,” Sir Henry said slowly. “You’re not hoaxing us? It is sometimes thought allowable to take in a greenhorn.”

The words pricked me. I cannot abide being taken for one of those foolish fellows who think lying is wit.

“If you think that, Sir Henry,” I said, pocketing my paper, “there’s an end to the matter.” And I rose.

Sir Henry put his big hand on my shoulder—heavy, steady. “Sit down, Mr. Quatermain,” he said. “I beg your pardon. I see well enough you do not wish to deceive us. But it sounded so strange I could hardly believe it.”

“You shall see the original map and writing when we reach Durban,” I answered, mollified. For, indeed, it was not wonderful he should doubt. “But I’ve not told you about your brother.

“I knew the man Jim who went with him—a Bechuana by birth; a good hunter, and for a native, clever. The morning Mr. Neville was starting I saw Jim by my wagon cutting up tobacco on the disselboom. He worked the knife careful, making the leaf into fine, even shreds as though he respected it; the smell of it rose warm and familiar, a small comfort before a long trek.

“‘Jim,’ I said, ‘where are you off to this trip? Is it elephants?’

“‘No, Baas,’ he answered. ‘We are after something worth much more than ivory.’

“‘And what might that be?’ I asked, curious. ‘Is it gold?’

“‘No, Baas—something worth more than gold,’ and he grinned.

“I asked no more. A man doesn’t like to lower his dignity by seeming inquisitive, though my mind chewed at it like tough meat.

“When Jim finished his tobacco he said, ‘Baas—’

“I took no notice.

“‘Baas—’ again.

“‘Eh, boy, what is it?’ I asked.

“‘Baas, we are going after diamonds.’

“‘Diamonds! Then you are steering the wrong way. You should head for the Fields.’

“‘Baas, have you ever heard of Suliman’s Berg?’—Solomon’s Mountains, Sir Henry.

“‘Ay.’

“‘Have you ever heard of the diamonds there?’

“‘I have heard a foolish story, Jim.’

“‘It is no story, Baas. Once I knew a woman who came from there, and reached Natal with her child. She told me—she is dead now.’

“Now that, gentlemen, struck me more than Evans’s talk, because it came with the everyday weight of a man mentioning mealies or weather—no flourish, no selling. And there’s power in a thing said plain, in a tongue that has carried it from hearth to hearth. When a story lives by being spoken, it changes like soup in a pot—thickening, taking on the hand of each cook—until no man can swear what the first taste was. Yet still it feeds.

“‘Your master will feed the aasvögels, Jim,’ I said, ‘if he tries to reach Suliman’s country—and so will you, if they can pick anything off your worthless old carcass.’

“He grinned. ‘Mayhap, Baas. Man must die. I would rather like to try a new country myself; the elephants are getting worked out about here.’

“‘Ah, my boy,’ I said, ‘you wait till the “pale old man” gets a grip of your yellow throat, and then we shall hear what tune you sing.’

“Half an hour later I saw Neville’s wagon move off. Presently Jim came back running, dust on his shins, breath whistling.

“‘Good-bye, Baas,’ he said. ‘I didn’t like to start without bidding you good-bye, for I daresay you are right, and that we shall never trek south again.’

“‘Is your master really going to Suliman’s Berg, Jim, or are you lying?’

“‘No,’ he said. ‘He is going. He told me he was bound to make his fortune somehow, or try to; so he might as well have a fling for the diamonds.’

“‘Oh!’ I said. ‘Wait a bit, Jim. Will you take a note to your master and promise not to give it to him till you reach Inyati?’—a hundred miles off.

“‘Yes, Baas.’

“So I took a scrap of paper and wrote: ‘Let him who comes… climb the snow of Sheba’s left breast, till he reaches the nipple, on the north side of which is Solomon’s great road.’

“‘Now, Jim,’ I said, ‘when you give this to your master, tell him he had better follow the advice on it implicitly. You are not to give it to him now, because I don’t want him back asking me questions I won’t answer. Now be off, you idle fellow; the wagon is nearly out of sight.’

“He took the note and went. And that is all I know of your brother, Sir Henry; but I am much afraid—”

“Mr. Quatermain,” Sir Henry broke in, and his voice had the quiet hardness of a man who has already eaten his resolve and swallowed it. “I am going to look for my brother. I am going to trace him to Suliman’s Mountains, and over them if necessary, till I find him—or until I know that he is dead. Will you come with me?”

I am a cautious man—timid, if you like. The proposal chilled me as though someone had poured cold water down my back. To undertake such a journey seemed like stepping into the mouth of death; and besides, I had a son dependent on me. A man who has to put food into another’s mouth cannot gamble his life lightly, whatever romance may be served up with the tale.

“No, thank you, Sir Henry,” I said. “I think I would rather not. I am too old for wild-goose chases. We should only end like poor Silvestre. I have a son dependent on me, so I cannot afford to risk my life foolishly.”

Sir Henry and Captain Good looked disappointed—Good with the face of a man whose stew has been snatched off the fire before it’s finished.

“Mr. Quatermain,” Sir Henry said, “I am well off, and I am bent upon this business. You may put the remuneration for your services at whatever figure you like in reason, and it shall be paid over before we start. Moreover, I will arrange that if anything untoward happens to us—or to you—your son shall be suitably provided for. You will see from this how necessary I think your presence. Also, if by chance we reach this place and find diamonds, they shall belong to you and Good equally. I do not want them. That promise is worth nothing at all, of course; and the same would apply to any ivory. You may make your own terms with me, Mr. Quatermain. And I shall pay all expenses.”

“Sir Henry,” I answered, “it is the most liberal proposal I ever had, and not to be sneezed at by a poor hunter and trader. But it’s the biggest job I’ve come across, and I must take time to think it over. I will give you my answer before we get to Durban.”

“Very good,” he said.

Then I wished them good-night and turned in. And as the ship’s sounds wrapped themselves round my sleep—the soft insistence of water, the faint creak of timbers—I dreamt of long-dead Silvestre and the diamonds, bright as salt in the sun, locked away behind the white Death.
