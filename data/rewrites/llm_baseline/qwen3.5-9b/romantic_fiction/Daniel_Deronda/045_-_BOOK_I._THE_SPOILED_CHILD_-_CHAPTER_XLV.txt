Behold the lady's carriage halt its progress,
With powdered lackeys and a charming bay;
She sweeps the matting, treads the crimson stair,
Her sole arduous function: "To be there."
Like Sirius rising o'er the silent sea,
She hides her heart in lustre, loftily.

So the Grandcourts arrived in Grosvenor Square, timely enough to receive a card for the musical gathering at Lady Mallinger's. There were matters of business that had compelled Sir Hugo to anticipate the arrival of his ill-favored nephew. It was merely the third evening since their return, yet Gwendolen made a rather distracted acquaintance with her new ceilings and furnishings, her mind preoccupied with the certainty that she would soon speak to Deronda again, and that she would finally meet the Miss Lapidoth who had endured so much, and was deemed "capable of submitting to anything in the form of duty."

Gwendolen had recalled nearly every word Deronda had spoken concerning Mirah, particularly that phrase which she repeated to herself with bitter intensity. She possessed an ill-defined consciousness that her own submission was something vastly different. Had anyone pointed it out to her, she would have been forced to admit that what she submitted to could not possibly take the shape of duty. Rather, it was submission to a yoke imposed upon her by an action she was ashamed of, worn with a strength of selfish motives that left no weight for duty to bear.

The drawing-rooms in Park Lane, adorned in white, gold, and pale crimson, were agreeably furnished and not yet crowded with guests. Before Mr. and Mrs. Grandcourt entered, more than half an hour of instrumental music had been succeeded by an interval of mingled movement and chatter. Klesmer was present with his wife, and in his generous regard for Mirah, he proposed accompanying her in a rendition of Leo's *"O patria mia,"* a piece he had previously recommended as more distinctive of her character than better-known selections. He was already seated at the piano, and Mirah stood there conspicuously when Gwendolen, magnificent in her pale green velvet and poisoned diamonds, was ushered to a seat of honor, well in view of them.

With her long sight and self-command, she possessed the rare ability to distinguish persons and objects instantly upon entering a full room. As she turned her gaze toward Mirah, she did not neglect to exchange a bow with Klesmer as she passed. The smile seemed to flash back like lightning to that morning when it had been her ambition to stand where the "little Jewess" now stood—to survey a grand audience from the higher rank of her talent—instead of finding herself one of the ordinary crowd in silk and gems, whose utmost performance was limited to admiration or criticism.

"He thinks I am in the right road now," murmured the lurking resentment within her.

Gwendolen had not caught sight of Deronda during her entrance. Seated now, engaging in chat with Sir Hugo, she glanced around with careful ease, bowing a recognition here and there, yet fearful lest an anxious-looking exploration in search of Deronda be observed by her husband and subsequently rebuked as something "damnably vulgar." Yet, all traveling, even that of a slow, gradual scan of a room, carries a liability to undesired encounters. Among the eyes that met Gwendolen's, forcing her into a slight bow, were those of the "amateur too fond of Meyerbeer," Mr. Lush, whom Sir Hugo continued to find useful as a half-caste among gentlemen. He stood near her husband, though Sir Hugo turned a shoulder toward him, pretending to listen to Lord Pentreath.

How was it that, at this precise moment, for the first time, a disagreeable sensation darted through Gwendolen—the idea that this man knew all about her husband's life? He had been banished from her sight, according to her will, and she had been satisfied; he had sunk entirely into the background of her thoughts, screened away by the agitating figures that kept up an inward drama in which Lush had no place. Suddenly, he reappeared at her husband's elbow, and there sprang up in her, like an instantaneously fabricated memory in a dream, the sense of his connection with the secrets that made her wretched.

Conscious of the effort required to turn her head away, she tried to continue her wandering survey as if she had seen nothing of more consequence than the picture on the wall, until she discovered Deronda. But he was not looking toward her. She withdrew her eyes without securing any recognition, consoling herself with the assurance that he must have seen her arrive. In truth, he was not standing far from the door with Hans Meyrick, whom he had carefully included in Lady Mallinger's guest list. Both were slightly more anxious than comfortable lest Mirah should not be heard to advantage. Deronda even felt himself on the brink of betraying emotion; Mirah's presence now linked with crowding images of what had gone before and what was to come after—all centering in the brother he was soon to reveal to her. He had escaped as soon as he could from the side of Lady Pentreath, who had spoken in her violoncello voice: "Well, your Jewess is pretty—there's no denying that. But where is her Jewish impudence? She looks as demure as a nun. I suppose she learned that on the stage."

Deronda was beginning to feel on Mirah's behalf something akin to what he had once felt for himself in his seraphic boyhood, when Sir Hugo asked him if he would like to become a great singer. An indignant dislike arose in him at the suggestion of her being remarked upon in such a free and easy manner, as if she were an imported commodity disdainfully purchased by the fashionable public. He winced the more because he knew Mordecai would feel that the term "Jewess" was taken as a sort of stamp, like the lettering on Chinese silk.

In this susceptible mood, he saw the Grandcourts enter and was immediately addressed by Hans regarding "that Vandyke duchess of a beauty." Deronda felt a transient renewal of his first repulsion from Gwendolen, as if her beauty and her failings were to blame for the undervaluing of Mirah as a woman—a feeling somewhat like class animosity, which affection for what is not fully recognized by others, whether in persons or poetry, rarely allows us to escape. To Hans, who admired Gwendolen with his habitual hyperbole, Deronda answered with a sarcasm that was not quite good-natured: "I thought you could admire no style of woman but your Berenice."

"That is the style I worship—not admire," said Hans. "Other styles of women I might make myself wicked for, but for Berenice I could make myself—well, pretty good, which is something much more difficult."

"Hush," said Deronda, under the pretext that the singing was about to begin. He was not so delighted with the answer as might have been expected, and was relieved by Hans's movement to a more advanced spot.

Deronda had never before heard Mirah sing *"O patria mia."* He knew well Leopardi's fine Ode to Italy (where Italy sat like a disconsolate mother in chains, hiding her face on her knees and weeping), and the few selected words were filled for him with the grandeur of the whole, seeming to breathe an inspiration through the music. Mirah singing this made Mordecai more than ever one presence with her. Certain words not included in the song nevertheless rang within Deronda as harmonies from the invisible: *"Non ti difende Nessun de' tuoi! L'armi, qua l'armi: io solo Combatterò, procomberò sol io"*—"Do none of thy children defend thee? Arms! bring me arms! alone I will fight, alone I will fall." These seemed the very voice of that heroic passion which is falsely said to devote itself in vain when it achieves the godlike end of manifesting unselfish love. That passion was present to Deronda now as the vivid image of a man dying helplessly, far removed from the possibility of battle.

Mirah was equal to his wishes. While the general applause sounded, Klesmer gave a more valued testimony, audible only to her: "Good, good—the crescendo better than before." But her chief anxiety was to know that she had satisfied Mr. Deronda; any failure on her part this evening would have pained her as an especial injury to him. Of course, all her prospects were due to what he had done for her, yet this occasion of singing in the house that was his home brought a peculiar demand. She looked toward him in the distance, and he saw that she did; but he remained where he was, watching the streams of emulous admirers closing round her, until presently they parted to make way for Gwendolen, who was taken up to be introduced by Mrs. Klesmer.

Easier now regarding "the little Jewess," Daniel relented toward poor Gwendolen in her splendor, and his memory returned, with some penitence for his momentary hardness, over all the signs and confessions that she too needed a rescue—one much more difficult than that of the wanderer by the river—a rescue for which he felt himself helpless. The silent question, "But is it not cowardly to make that a reason for turning away?" became the form in which he framed his resolve to approach her on the first opportunity and show his regard for her past confidence, despite Sir Hugo's unwelcome hints.
