"A lie is not like a blow with a curtal axe," someone muttered—perhaps it was Sybrandt. "True enough; we can foresee, to some extent, the fallout of a physical strike. But a lie knows no bounds. Its nature is to spread far beyond human reckoning. In the ordinary course of events, a single falsehood has often cost a life or devastated several families. And so, in this tale, what catastrophic consequences flowed from that one heartless fabrication!"

Yet, ironically, the architects of the deceit reaped little reward. The brothers, who concocted the story merely to reduce the number of claimants to their father's estate, watched as the gentle Gerard stepped into the void they created, occupying the central place in their mother's heart. Worse still, one day Eli openly declared that, since Gerard was lost—and presumed dead—he had already arranged via will for both little Gerard and Margaret, the widow of his poor son. At this revelation, a silent warning passed between the outcasts. Cornelis felt the words die on his lips: *Gerard is most likely alive.* But catching his mother's gaze, he stifled them in time.

Ghysbrecht Van Swieten, the other co-conspirator in that web of untruth, was now a dying man. He sensed the end drawing near, the moment when all his worldly wealth would slip from his grasp while his crimes clung eternally to his soul. Too wise to fully delude himself, he had never escaped the periodic storms of remorse. When he delivered Gerard's letter to Margaret, he had compromised. *"I cannot surrender land and money,"* his giant Avarice had commanded. *"I will cause her no unnecessary pain,"* his dwarf Conscience had replied. Thus, after tampering with the seal and discovering there was not a single word regarding the deed, he brought it to her personally, claiming credit for the gesture—a moral offset, calculated on a scale common where the self-accuser serves as judge.

The birth of Margaret's child struck him with fresh horror, casting his treachery in a cruel new light. If his forged letter were to take effect, he would be condemning the honor of a woman who was the daughter of one friend, the granddaughter of another, and from whom he was unjustly withholding her inheritance. These gnawing thoughts, arising during the twilight of life when bodily strength fades and memories of old friendships resurface, filled him with gloomy terrors. Yet, he dared not confess. The local physician was a man of integrity who would have forced him to purge his guilt, but for Ghysbrecht, Avarice was an ingrained habit, while penitence remained but a fleeting sentiment.

It was on such a day, returning from the town hall to his home, that he discovered a woman waiting in the vestibule, cradling a child. Veiled deeply, he assumed she harbored shame and addressed her with condescending authority. Instantly, she lowered her veil and met his gaze squarely. It was Margaret Brandt. Her sudden arrival and intense demeanor startled him, betraying a confusion he could not hide.

"Where is my Gerard?" she cried, her chest heaving. "Is he alive?"

"For all I know," Ghysbrecht stammered. "I pray he is, for your sake. Pray, enter this room. The servants—"

"Not a step," Margaret interrupted, seizing his shoulder with the fierce energy of a distressed woman. "You hold the secret that shatters my heart. Why does my Gerard not come? Why has he sent not a single line in these many months? Answer me, or the whole town shall know, along with your servants. My misery is too profound to be treated as a jest."

In vain, he insisted he knew nothing of Gerard. She countered that those who had escorted her to him had spun a different narrative.

"You do know why he neither comes nor writes," she stated firmly.

At this, Ghysbrecht paled further, yet summoning a facade of dignity, he retorted, "Would you believe those two knaves against a man of worship?"

"Which two knaves?" she pressed sharply.

He faltered. "Did you not say...? There I am, a poor, broken old man, my memory failing. You come here and confuse me so that I speak nonsense."

"Yes, sir, your memory is shaken, or surely you would not be my enemy. My father saved you from the plague when no other would approach; he was always your friend. My grandfather Floris aided you in your early poverty and loved you, man and boy. Three generations of us you have known; here stands the fourth. This is the grandson of your old friend Peter and the great-grandson of your old friend Floris. Look down upon his innocent face and recall theirs!"

"Woman, you torture me," Ghysbrecht sighed, collapsing onto a bench.

Seeing her advantage, Margaret knelt before him and placed the boy on his lap. "This fatherless babe belongs to poor Margaret Brandt, who has never wronged you, and comes from a lineage that loved you. Nay, look at his face. It will move you more than any words I could speak. Saints of heaven, what could a destitute girl and her infant have done to erase the memory of your own youth, when you were as guiltless as he is now, gazing up at you and imploring you to return his father?" Holding the child aloft with her arms beneath him, she smiled beneath the old man's weary eyes.

Ghysbrecht cast a wild look of anguish upon the child, then at the kneeling mother, before springing up and shrieking, "Away! Ye pair of adders!"

Stung, his soul granted his aged limbs a momentary surge of vigor. He paced rapidly, wringing his hands and clutching his white hair. "Forget those days? I forget everything else. Oh, woman, woman! Whether sleeping or waking, I see only the faces of the dead, hear only their voices, and soon I shall join them. There, there, what is done is done. I am in hell. I am in hell."

Unnatural force led to total collapse. He staggered, and without Margaret's support, would have fallen. With one freed arm, she steadied him as best she could, crying out for aid. Two servants rushed forward and carried him away in a state bordering on faintness. The last sight Margaret had of him was his ancient, furrowed face, pale and helpless, with white hair hanging over the servant's elbow.

"Heaven forgive me," she whispered. "I fear I have killed the poor old man."

Thus, this attempt to pierce the tormenting mystery left it as obscure, if not darker, than before. As she reflected on every word spoken, her suspicion hardened: Ghysbrecht certainly knew something about Gerard. "And who were the two knaves he claimed had performed a good deed and told me? Oh, my Gerard, my poor abandoned baby, you and I are wading in deep waters."

The journey to Tergou drained funds she could scarcely spare, and a customer fled owing her money. Once more, she was forced to reveal Catherine's angel. Strangely, however, as she descended the stairs carrying it, she found loose silver on the table accompanied by a written note: *For Gerard, his wife.*

She collapsed with a cry of astonishment upon reading the message, which quickly swelled into a cry of joy. "He is alive. He sends this through some friendly hand." She kissed the paper repeatedly and tucked it safely into her bodice.

Time passed, bringing no direct news of Gerard. Yet, roughly every two months, a small sum of silver appeared in the household. Sometimes it lay on the table; once it was thrown through the bedroom window in a purse; another time, it was found at the bottom of Luke's basket. The giver—or his agent—was never caught. Catherine disclaimed involvement; Margaret Van Eyck swore she played no part; even Eli denied knowledge. Whenever the money arrived, Margaret would tell little Gerard, "Oh, my poor deserted child, you and I are wading in deep waters."

She allocated at least half of this modest, yet vital, supply to dress little Gerard above his station in life. "If it truly comes from Gerard, he shall see his boy presentable." Soon, the mothers in the street began to sneer, particularly those with children dressed in rags.

Months rolled on, and a deadly sickness of the heart replaced the sharper pains of suspense. She returned to her initial belief: *Gerard must be dead. She should never see her boy's father again, nor secure her marriage prospects.* This final grief, previously softened by Eli and Catherine's acceptance of her engagement, now surged forth with renewed intensity. Others viewed her story with growing skepticism. Often, she lamented over her son's illegitimacy. "Is it not enough for us to be bereaved? Must we be dishonored as well? Oh, that we had never been born."

A shift occurred within Peter Brandt. His mind, clouded for nearly two years, seemed to clear; he experienced intervals of lucidity. During these times, he and Margaret spoke of Gerard until his clarity faded again. One day, after an absence of several hours, Margaret found him conversing with Catherine in a manner unseen since his paralytic stroke.

"Eh, girl, why must you be out?" she asked.

"But indeed, I have told him all; and we have been weeping together over your troubles."
