Twelve years had passed since I laid Captain John Carter, of Virginia, to rest in the quiet solitude of the old cemetery at Richmond. His body rested beneath a stone vault, sealed with an open casket and a mechanism that could only be operated from within—strange instructions, I always thought, for a man who seemed to defy death itself. And yet, there he lay, or so I believed.

I had read his manuscript many times—the tale of a man without memory of childhood, whose age was unknown, who remembered dandling ancestors of my own bloodline upon his knee, and who claimed to have spent ten years on Mars. A warrior among green men, a prince among red ones, he had fought across the deserts and canyons of Barsoom, won the heart of Dejah Thoris, Princess of Helium, and ruled beside her as a prince of Tardos Mors. He had returned to Earth by accident—hurled through space by some cosmic force—and left behind a world that could not comprehend his story.

But now, twelve years later, I stood again at the edge of doubt. Was he truly dead? Or had he returned to Mars, to find the great atmosphere plant still failing, the skies growing thin, the people choking under a dying sky? Had he found Dejah Thoris alive, waiting in the royal gardens? Or had he arrived too late, only to face a world already lost?

I was lost in these thoughts one sweltering August evening when Ben, my loyal servant, handed me a telegram. My hands trembled as I tore it open:

“Meet me tomorrow, Hotel Raleigh, Richmond.

The words burned into me. I did not hesitate. The next morning, I boarded the first train bound for Richmond. Within hours, I was standing before the door of Room 12 at the Raleigh, where I found him—John Carter—standing tall and unshaken, as if no time had passed at all.

He rose as I entered, his smile warm and familiar, his eyes sharp and clear. He looked unchanged—still the man of thirty, strong and proud, with a presence that commanded the room. Only the lines etched deep into his face spoke of battles fought and worlds crossed.

“Well, nephew,” he said, “do you feel like you’re seeing a ghost—or just the aftereffects of Uncle Ben’s juleps?”

“Juleps, I reckon,” I replied, laughing despite myself. “But maybe it’s just seeing you again. You’ve been back to Mars, haven’t you? Tell me—Dejah Thoris? Is she well? Did you find her?”

He nodded slowly. “Yes, I’ve been back. But the story is long—too long to tell now. I’ve learned the secret, nephew. I can travel between planets at will. The void is no longer a prison. But my heart remains on Barsoom, where my wife and son await me.”

He gestured to a thick portfolio resting on the table. “These are my notes—written during the past three months I’ve spent on Earth. You’ll find everything there. The truth. The whole truth. I know you believe. I know the world will not—but one day they will.”

His voice softened. “I came to see you because I care for you. Because I know this life ends for you soon. I have died three times, and tonight I die again—though not as you understand death. It means nothing to me. Even the therns of Barsoom, those ancient keepers of secrets hidden in the Mountains of Otz, do not truly know what lies beyond. I’ve tested it. Nearly died doing so. But you’ll learn it all in my writings.”

That night, we walked together to the cemetery. At the entrance of his tomb, he turned to me, gripping my hand tightly.

“Good-bye, nephew,” he said. “I may never see you again. I cannot leave them—my wife, my son—while they live. On Mars, lifespans stretch beyond a thousand years. I am bound to them now.”

Then he stepped inside. The heavy door closed behind him. The bolts slid home with a final, echoing click. The lock snapped shut.

And I never saw Captain John Carter again.

But here, in the pages that follow, is the story of his return to Mars—what he wrote during his brief time back on Earth, the account of his journey, his triumphs, and his sorrows. It is not the full tale—much I have omitted, much I dared not reveal. But you will find the truth of his second quest for Dejah Thoris more wondrous than the first—a tale of love, war, and a world teetering on the brink of oblivion.

I stood upon the edge of a bluff, overlooking the Hudson River on a clear, cold night in early March, 1886. The river below flowed like a ghostly shadow, silent and gray beneath the stars—no longer the vibrant artery of life it once had been, but now a spectral remnant of Earth’s past. And as I gazed across its still waters, I felt it again—the magnetic pull of Mars, the red god of war, my true home. For ten long years I had waited, arms outstretched to the heavens, begging the ancient forces that had once carried me across the void to return me to Dejah Thoris, the woman I loved more than life itself.

Not since that fateful night in 1866—when I stood outside an Arizona cave, my body seemingly dead within—had I felt this call so strongly. Then, I had been pulled from Earth into the unknown, reborn on another world. Now, once more, I reached for the crimson eye of Mars, praying with every fiber of my being that the power would come again.

And then—suddenly—the world tilted. A wave of nausea surged through me. My vision blurred, my knees buckled, and I collapsed onto the edge of the cliff. But just as quickly, clarity returned. And with it, the memory: the cold darkness of the cave, the eerie moans echoing from unseen depths, the paralysis that had held me fast. Once more, I fought against the invisible chains binding my will. Then—*click*—a sharp sound, like a wire snapping. And I was free.

I stood naked beside the lifeless form of John Carter, his body still warm with blood, yet inert. Without looking back, I turned toward Mars, raised my hands, and waited.

No sooner had I done so than I was gone—shooting through space faster than thought could follow. There was the same moment of unbearable cold and total blackness I’d known before. Then, light.

I opened my eyes beneath a blazing sun, lying in the shade of a vast forest canopy. I was no longer on Mars—but where? The air was thin, the gravity lighter. I lay on a carpet of red grass, surrounded by trees unlike any I had ever seen. Their trunks were colossal—some over a hundred feet wide—and their bark gleamed like polished metal. The foliage shimmered in impossible colors: cobalt, gold, violet, scarlet. Birds, winged yet silent, flitted among the branches, their forms so alien they seemed painted by dreams.

Beyond the trees, I saw something even stranger—an open sea, its surface glinting under the fierce sun. This was not Mars. Or was it?

I rose—only to stumble violently into the air, landing hard on my face. My Earth-born muscles, unaccustomed to the weaker pull of this world, reacted wildly. But I laughed at myself, rising again. Soon, I adjusted. The balance came back. I walked slowly down the gentle slope toward the water.

The landscape was unnaturally orderly. The grass was cut short, like a manicured lawn. The trees were pruned to uniform height—fifteen feet—creating a forest that looked less like nature and more like a grand hall built by giants. It was clear: this place was cultivated. Civilized. And if I was to find people here, I might be welcomed—not as a savage, but as a prince.

As I neared the edge of the forest, I glimpsed a wide meadow between the trees and the sea. To my left stretched the endless ocean. Ahead, a hazy line marked the distant shore. To my right, a great river flowed from towering cliffs, its banks stained deep red, emptying into the sea.

But what caught my attention was not the land or the water—it was movement.

A group of creatures moved slowly across the meadow near the riverbank. They were tall—ten to twelve feet high—and strangely humanoid in shape. Yet their limbs were wrong. Their arms were short, thick, and flexible, writhing like serpents. They moved them across the ground, brushing the grass.

One approached close enough for me to see clearly.

Its body was hairless, a sickly blue-gray, except for a white band circling its single eye—a pale, lifeless orb, no pupil, no iris. Its nose was a ragged, inflamed hole, like a fresh wound. Below it, no mouth. Just smooth skin.

Its head was covered in thick, jet-black hair—each strand as thick as a worm. As it moved, the hair writhed, alive with motion, crawling over its skull like a nest of living things.

Its legs were human-shaped, but monstrous—feet three feet long, flat and broad. And from its armpits hung two tiny versions of itself, each about six inches long, suspended by slender stalks growing from their heads.

I stared in horror. These were not men. Not animals. Something else entirely.

Then, the herd turned as one. A shrieking wail echoed from the cliffs. Every hair on their heads stood rigid—each strand a separate ear, listening. They all faced a leader, who began to move toward the bluffs, the rest following in a strange, leaping gait—great bounds, like kangaroos, but far more powerful.

I hesitated—then followed.

They moved toward the river’s source, where boulders littered the meadow. As I crested one, I saw them.

Surrounding a small group of green Martians—men and women of Barsoom.

My heart leapt. *I was on Mars.*

These were the wild tribes of the dying planet—towering warriors with tusks jutting from their jaws, eyes set sideways, antennae-like ears atop their heads, and secondary arms sprouting from their hips. Their hides were glossy green, their ornaments marking different hordes. And yet, they stood together—back to back—facing a common enemy.

No weapons of fire. Only swords and daggers.

The plant men charged.

The leader bounded forward, then leapt high above the group. His tail whipped downward like a scythe—crushing a warrior’s skull like an eggshell.

The others circled, leaping in tandem, their tails sweeping through the air. Two more fell.

Only one warrior and two women remained.

The warrior fought bravely, swinging his sword with furious precision. He slashed through one plant man from chin to groin. But the second struck with its tail—both women crumpled.

Now only he remained.

He turned and ran—toward the forest, seeking shelter.

I watched, my blood boiling. This warrior had fought with honor. And I—John Carter—could not stand by.

I leapt from the rock, grabbed a fallen long-sword, and sprinted after the herd.

I reached them just as the warrior was cornered against a boulder. He stood defiant, back to stone, facing a ring of monstrous foes.

They did not see me.

I struck.

Four fell before they knew I was there.

The warrior turned—his eyes wide—and then smiled.

We fought side by side. Our blades carved through flesh and bone, slicing through the viscous sap that oozed from their wounds—thick, green, sticky, like the juice of crushed caterpillars. Tails lashed. Talons tore. One creature landed on my back, its lips sucking at the wounds it had made.

I fought off one attacker, while the warrior cleaved through another. We stood back-to-back, fighting with the ferocity of born killers.

Then—above us—came the cry again.

I looked up.

On a ledge high on the cliff stood a figure—humanoid, but strange. He waved one hand toward the river, pointing at us with the other.

And then I saw it.

From every direction—forest, plain, riverbanks—more plant men poured in. Hundreds. And among them—new creatures, running on four legs or two, faster than any animal I’d known.

“It will be a great death,” I said.

The warrior grinned. “We may die—but we shall die as warriors should. John Carter.”

I turned—startled.

He called me by name.

And then I saw him.

Tars Tarkas.

Jeddak of Thark.

The greatest warrior of the green men.

My old friend.

My brother in battle.

And now—my ally once more.

Tars Tarkas and I stood before the massive boulder, our breath ragged, surrounded by the twisted remains of the monstrous creatures we had just felled. But there was no time for words or reflection. From every direction down the broad valley, a terrifying tide surged forward—hordes of grotesque beings drawn by the eerie cry echoing from a lone figure atop the cliff.

“Run!” Tars Tarkas roared. “To the cliffs! That’s our only hope. There may be a cave, a narrow ledge—we can hold it against this mindless swarm.”

We sprinted across the crimson grass, my pace adjusted to keep pace with his heavier stride. Three hundred yards separated us from the cliffs—a distance that might as well have been a mile given the relentless pursuit behind us. As we raced, he called to me to go ahead, to scout for shelter. It was sound advice—every second counted.

I pushed myself beyond human limits, leaping and bounding toward the cliff face until I reached its base in seconds. The cliffs rose sheer from the flat valley floor, untouched by erosion or fallen stone. Only scattered boulders hinted at any decay. My first glance up revealed nothing but smooth, unyielding rock—no footholds, no crevices. Only where the strange herald stood did a faint shadow suggest any break in the wall.

To my right, the forest pressed close, its towering canopy meeting the cliff at its very foot. To the left, the cliff stretched unbroken into distant mountains that enclosed the valley like a prison. A river burst from beneath the cliffs a thousand feet away, but escape there seemed impossible. So I turned back to the trees.

The cliffs soared five thousand feet above, their surface dull yellow in the shade. Sunlight hadn’t yet touched them, but when it did—when the golden rays struck—the entire face ignited. It shimmered with burnished gold, flaming red, emerald green, and gleaming white. Veins of solid gold ran through the rock, interrupted only by outcroppings of ruby, emerald, and diamond. It was a treasure beyond imagining—yet now it felt like a tomb.

Then I saw them—dark patches high on the cliff, near the treetops. Caves. Openings. Possible refuge—if we could reach them.

Only one path led there: through the forest beside the cliff. I knew I could climb the trees. But Tars Tarkas? His bulk and weight made climbing nearly impossible. Martians rarely climbed; their world offered few vertical challenges, and they preferred long, easy routes around hills rather than up them.

But there was no other choice. We moved toward the nearest tree.

Our pursuers were closing fast. The fastest among them—a giant plant man—lunged forward, claws extended, mouth gaping with blood-sucking jaws. He was fifty yards ahead of the others. I shouted for Tars Tarkas to climb while I dealt with the beast.

But I underestimated both the creature’s cunning and the speed of its kin.

As I raised my sword, the monster stopped. My blade sliced empty air. Then—its thick tail lashed like a grizzly’s arm, knocking me to the ground. In an instant, it was on me, jaws snapping. I grabbed two writhing tentacles, fighting with every ounce of strength. Earthly muscles and agility gave me the edge—but not enough time.

Above the creature’s shoulder, I glimpsed the full horror: the horde had arrived. And now I saw what they were.

Great white apes of Barsoom.

I’d known them before. They were the most feared beasts on Mars—twelve feet tall, walking upright, with a second set of arms midway down their bodies. Their eyes were close-set, their ears high and wide, their snouts like gorillas’. And atop their heads, bristling hair. They looked almost human—just too large, too terrible.

And now they came, roaring, snarling, shrieking, purring—a wave of madness crashing over me.

Fangs tore into my flesh. Cold lips sucked at my veins. I fought back, rising to my feet, shortening my grip on the sword to use it like a dagger. For a brief moment, I stood clear—then the apes returned.

Tars Tarkas had seen. He dropped from the branches and joined me. Back-to-back, we fought as we had countless times before.

Swords flashed. Apes charged. Plant men leapt from above, tails lashing. But even the finest fighters cannot hold off endless numbers. Step by step, we fell back—until we reached the great tree.

Then Tars Tarkas let out a cry.

“Here is shelter—for one,” he said, pointing to a hole at the base, about three feet wide.

“In you go,” I ordered.

“No,” he replied. “My body is too large. You can slip in.”

“We die together,” I insisted. “You go in first. I’ll cover your retreat.”

He hesitated. No Thark ever turned from battle. But he obeyed.

“Quickly,” I urged. “Or we both fall.”

He dropped to the ground and forced himself into the hole. At once, the horde descended on me.

My sword flew—green with plant juice, red with ape blood—cutting through one after another. I fought like I never had before. But they overwhelmed me. I fell again.

Then strong hands gripped my ankles. I was dragged into the hollow.

Tars Tarkas fought at the entrance. A plant man clung to my chest. I drove my sword through him.

Inside, I lay wounded, panting. The tree was hollow—fifty feet across. The floor was hard and worn. Above, a faint glow. An opening.

I explored. A rough ladder led upward—horizontal bars spanning the shaft. I climbed, the light growing brighter. Five hundred feet up, I reached the top. A large limb stretched out, worn smooth—clearly used by others.

I didn’t step out. Instead, I returned to Tars Tarkas.

Together, we ascended. I pulled the ladder up behind me, handing it to him. He carried it higher, wedging it between bars. I dislodged each bar as I passed, sealing the way behind us—preventing pursuit.

At the top, I stepped out onto the limb. It angled gently toward the cliff, ending just above a narrow ledge at the mouth of a cave.

The branch bent under my weight. When I reached the end, it swayed, barely two feet from the ledge. Below, five hundred feet of scarlet sward. Above, five thousand feet of glittering cliff.

The cave wasn’t among those I’d seen from below—lower, perhaps a thousand feet down. But it would do.

I returned to Tars Tarkas. Together, we crawled along the branch. But our combined weight lowered it too far. The cave was now out of reach.

So we devised a plan: Tars Tarkas would return, leaving his longest leather strap. When the branch rose again, I’d enter. Then he’d pull himself up.

It worked. We stood together on the ledge—on a dizzying balcony overlooking the valley.

Below, the apes feasted on the corpses. Herds of plant men grazed, keeping the sward trimmed like a lawn. The silence was broken only by wind and distant cries.

We decided to explore the cave. It was likely part of a hidden passage—perhaps leading beyond this nightmare valley.

Inside, we found a tunnel—twenty feet high, five feet wide, arched overhead. No light. We walked slowly, hands touching walls, clasping each other to avoid separation.

Eventually, we hit a barrier—not rock, but something hard and wooden. I felt a button—like a Martian door. Pressed it.

The door slid open. Beyond, a dim chamber. Empty.

I stepped in. Tars Tarkas followed.

We stood silent, surveying the room. Then—behind us—the door clicked shut.

I lunged for it. My fingers scraped against the unyielding wood. No lever, no knob. Just silence.

Then—laughter.

Cold, cruel, mocking—echoing from nowhere and everywhere.

From unseen lips, a voice laughed in the dark.

For a long moment after the dreadful laugh faded into silence, Tars Tarkas and I stood frozen in the dimly lit cavern, ears straining, eyes scanning every shadow. No sound followed. Nothing moved within sight.

Then Tars Tarkas let out a low chuckle—soft, dry, the kind his people often gave when confronted with horror. It wasn’t madness; it was the genuine pleasure they took in things that made Earth men shudder or weep. I’d seen them roll on the ground laughing at the agonies of women and children during the Great Games, those monstrous spectacles of Martian cruelty.

I looked up at him, a smile tugging at my own lips. Here, more than ever, there was no room for fear—only resolve.

“What do you make of this?” I asked. “Where in the world are we?”

He blinked at me, surprised.

“Where are we?” he echoed. “Do you really not know, John Carter? You’re standing upon Barsoom.”

“That’s all I can guess,” I replied. “And if not for you and those great white apes, I wouldn’t even have guessed that. The things I’ve seen today—nothing like anything from the Barsoom I knew ten years ago, nor anything from Earth either.”

“No,” I said, “I don’t know where we are.”

Tars Tarkas leaned forward, his green eyes narrowing. “Since the day you opened the atmosphere plant’s gates—after the keeper died and the engines failed, and all Barsoom began to suffocate—where have you been? Your body was never found. Men searched for years. The Jeddak of Helium and his granddaughter, your princess, offered rewards so vast that princes joined the hunt.”

He paused. “There was only one explanation: you had taken the final journey down the River Iss, to await Dejah Thoris in the Valley Dor, by the shores of the Lost Sea of Korus.”

“She still lives?” I interrupted, my voice tight with hope. “I didn’t dare ask—I feared I might be too late. She was dying when I left her in Tardos Mors’ gardens that night. So weak… I barely believed I’d reach the plant in time. But she lives?”

“She lives,” Tars Tarkas said flatly.

“I still don’t know where we are,” I reminded him.

“We are exactly where I expected to find you—both of us,” he said. “Years ago, you heard of the woman who taught me what green Martians hate most: love. The woman who taught me to feel it. Tal Hajus killed her for it. I believed she waited for me by the Lost Sea.”

“You taught me what friendship meant—something I never knew before,” he continued. “And I thought you, too, wandered freely in the Valley Dor, free from war and bloodshed.”

“So I waited. And when the years passed without word from you—when Dejah Thoris clung to the hope that you’d return from Earth—I finally gave in. A month ago, I began my pilgrimage. And now you see its end. Do you understand now?”

“The River Iss flows into the Lost Sea of Korus in the Valley Dor?” I asked.

“This is the valley of peace, love, and rest,” Tars Tarkas said. “The place every Barsoomian dreams of reaching at life’s end—a paradise.”

His tone was cold, bitter. The disappointment was written deep in his face. To learn that the sacred destination of generations was nothing but a trap—filled with beasts that devoured pilgrims—was a blow that could break even the fiercest Thark.

I laid a hand on his shoulder.

“I’m sorry,” I said. There was nothing else to say.

“Think of it,” he murmured. “Countless billions have walked this river, believing in peace, only to meet death at the claws of monsters. There’s an old tale of a red man who returned from the Valley Dor—alive. He spoke of beautiful lands, yes, but also of terrible creatures that attacked each traveler as they reached the shore, devouring them where they hoped to find salvation. The ancients killed him for blasphemy. Anyone who returns from the River of Mystery must die.”

“And now we know it’s true,” I said. “But what good does it do us? Even if we escape, we’ll be branded blasphemers. We’re caught between the certain jaws of the thoat and the mad zitidar—we can’t run from either.”

“Earth men would say we’re between the devil and the deep sea,” I added, forcing a smile.

“There’s nothing left but to face what comes,” I said. “At least we’ll take many of them with us. White ape, plant man, green warrior, red man—anyone who kills us will pay dearly. For I am John Carter, Prince of Tardos Mors. And you are Tars Tarkas, Jeddak of Thark. Let them count their dead.”

He laughed—deep, real laughter, rare among his kind. I laughed too, relieved by the absurdity of our plight.

“But tell me,” he said, “if you haven’t been here all these years—where have you been? How did you come to be here now?”

“I went back to Earth,” I said. “Ten Earth years I’ve spent praying to return to this harsh, cruel world. Though it’s brutal, I love it more than the world that birthed me. Ten years I lived in doubt—whether Dejah Thoris still lived. Now, at last, I know she does. But fate has cast me into the one place on Barsoom where escape seems impossible—and if it were possible, the cost would destroy any hope of seeing her again.”

I paused. “Only half an hour ago, I stood beneath the moonlight on Earth’s eastern shore, beside a wide river. That’s where I came from. I’ve answered you. Do you believe?”

“I believe,” Tars Tarkas said. “Though I cannot understand.”

As we spoke, I scanned the chamber. It stretched two hundred feet long, half as wide, carved from golden rock. A single radium light in the ceiling cast a pale glow over polished surfaces of ruby, emerald, and diamond embedded in the walls. The floor was hard, worn smooth as glass. Two doors—one sealed behind us, the other across the room—were the only openings.

I stepped toward the far door, hand outstretched to find the control.

Then—again—the mocking laugh erupted, so close it made me flinch. My fingers tightened on my sword hilt.

From the far corner, a hollow voice droned:

“There is no hope, there is no hope. The dead do not return. Nor is there resurrection. Hope not—for there is no hope.”

We turned sharply—no one there. A chill ran down my spine. My hair bristled like a dog’s at night when it sees what humans cannot.

I walked toward the voice—but it had vanished. Then, from the opposite end, another voice shrieked:

“Fools! Fools! Do you think to defy the laws of life and death? Would you cheat Issus, Goddess of Death, of her due? Did not her messenger, the ancient Iss, carry you to the Valley Dor on her leaden breast?

“Do you think Issus will surrender her own? That you can escape where only one soul has fled in all time?

“Return the way you came—to the merciful maws of the Tree of Life’s children or the gleaming fangs of the white apes. There lies swift relief.

“But if you persist—through the Golden Cliffs, past the Holy Therns’ fortresses—Death will catch you. A death so terrible even the Therns, who created both life and death, turn away from its horror and cover their ears against the screams.

“Go back, fools. Go back!”

Then—once more—the awful laugh.

“Uncanny,” I muttered.

“What now?” Tars Tarkas asked. “We can’t fight air. I’d rather face foes whose flesh I can feel my blade cut—know I’m selling my life dearly before I fall into oblivion. That seems the fairest eternity any mortal may hope for.”

“If we can’t fight air,” I said, “then air can’t fight us. I’ve faced thousands of warriors, fought battles with tempered steel. I won’t flee from wind. Nor will you.”

“But unseen voices may come from unseen beings with invisible blades,” he warned.

“Rot,” I snapped. “Those voices come from living things. Blood flows in their veins—just like ours. Their invisibility proves they’re mortal—probably cowardly ones. Think you, Tars Tarkas, that John Carter will run from a coward who dares not face a blade?”

I shouted loud enough for them to hear. I was tired of this game.

Silence followed.

Then—behind me—a soft, creeping sound.

I spun around.

A banth—ten-legged, snarling, massive—slithered toward me.

The banth roams the hills near Mars’ dead seas. Hairless, save for a bristling mane, it has long, needle-like fangs and enormous green eyes. Its tail lashed its yellow sides. When it saw me, it let out a roar that freezes prey in terror.

It lunged.

But I was ready.

Steel met flesh. I drove my blade through its heart.

I turned—Tars Tarkas was fighting his own banth.

No sooner had he dispatched it than another leapt across the chamber at me.

For nearly an hour, beast after beast emerged from nowhere—springing from thin air.

Tars Tarkas grinned. At last, something real to fight. I, too, welcomed the distraction—better than ghostly voices.

Their howls of pain, their bleeding wounds—they were mortal. Real.

And I noticed: they only appeared when our backs were turned. They never materialized from nothing. They came through hidden doors.

Tars Tarkas wore a small mirror on his harness—a common Martian accessory. As he stood over a fallen beast, I glanced at it—and froze.

In the reflection, I saw a section of the wall shift—pivoting on hinges. The floor beneath it rotated too, like a card placed upright on a coin. A beast sat on the other side—now revealed as the panel swung open.

But what caught my eye was beyond the opening.

A well-lit chamber. Chained prisoners—red Martians. Beasts like the ones attacking us. And a man—white-skinned, yellow-haired, human in form—operating the mechanism.

I turned to face my next foe, heart lighter.

“Watch the wall at your end,” I whispered to Tars Tarkas. “They’re using secret doors.”

We stayed facing opposite ends—no more attacks.

Then I had a plan.

I backed close to him, whispering my idea. He grunted agreement.

We reversed positions: I advanced while he retreated, until we were ten feet from the secret door.

I stopped him. “Don’t move until I signal.”

Then I turned my back to the door—feeling the gaze of our enemy burn through me.

I looked at the mirror.

The golden wall began to move.

I signaled. I lunged through the receding panel.

Tars Tarkas followed.

I landed in the other chamber—facing the man from the mirror.

He was my height, strong, Earth-born in appearance. Sword, dagger, revolver—fully armed.

I had only my long-sword.

He drew his revolver before I touched the ground.

But my sword swept upward—sending it flying.

He drew his long-sword. We clashed.

He was skilled—experienced. I hadn’t fought in ten years.

But I fell into rhythm quickly. Within minutes, he was bleeding from a dozen wounds.

“Who are you, white man?” he hissed. “You’re not Barsoomian. Not like us.”

“Maybe I’m from the Temple of Issus,” I guessed.

“Fate forbid!” he gasped, his face pale beneath the blood.

I filed that away. His fear suggested the temple held power—or danger—he respected.

But now, I had to kill him.

I did—within seconds.

As his body hit the floor, a prisoner screamed:

“Turn! Behind you!”

I whirled.

Another man—same race—crept from a dark passage, sword raised.

Tars Tarkas was gone. The secret door was closed.

I wished he were here. I was exhausted. Twenty-four hours without food or sleep. Hours of battle. My strength waned.

I had no choice.

I charged.

He danced back—parrying, sidestepping. Outlasting me.

He was better than the first. He led me in a desperate chase—nearly defeating me.

My vision blurred. I stumbled.

He forced me backward—onto the corpse of his comrade.

My heel struck it. I fell—head hitting the stone.

The impact cleared my mind.

Pain flared. Rage surged.

I rose—my hand brushing cold metal.

A weapon.

I awoke to the sound of battle—clashing metal, snarling beasts, and the ragged breath of a man struggling for life. For a moment, I could not place myself or the world around me. Then, through the blank wall beside me, came the unmistakable signs of violence: shuffling feet, growls like those of starving wolves, the jingle of armor, and heavy, labored breathing.

I rose quickly, scanning the dim chamber where I had been cast. On the far side, chained to the stone, were prisoners—men and women of Mars—watching me with eyes wide with curiosity, fury, shock, and something else… hope. Among them stood a young woman, pale copper in hue, her face sharp and noble, her gaze fixed upon me. She was unadorned, no jewelry, no insignia—yet there was dignity in her bearing that spoke of high birth, now stripped by captivity.

She was the most striking creature I had ever seen—not merely beautiful, but radiating a quiet strength that seemed to hum beneath her stillness. Her people, I knew from rumor, were the red Martians—tall, graceful, nearly divine in form, though their skin bore the warm glow of polished bronze. But she wore no mark of rank, only chains—and yet, even in bondage, she carried herself as one who commanded respect.

Then the sounds beyond the wall snapped my mind into focus. That was Tars Tarkas—my friend, the mighty Thark—facing death alone in some hidden chamber. My blood surged. Without hesitation, I lunged toward the wall, throwing my weight against what I believed to be a secret door. It did not yield. The gold was impenetrable.

I searched frantically for the mechanism—a hinge, a lever, anything—but found nothing. Just as I raised my longsword to strike at the metal, the girl called out.

“Save your blade, O Warrior,” she said. “It will serve you better when it is needed. This door yields not to force, but to knowledge.”

I turned to her. “Do you know how to open it?”

“Yes,” she replied. “Release me, and I shall let you pass. The keys to my bonds lie on the corpse of your first foe.”

“Then I will free you,” I said, already moving to the dead thern’s body. There were many keys on the oval ring at his belt, but she picked one swiftly—the correct one—and freed herself. Then, without delay, she stepped forward and inserted a slender, needle-like key into a tiny hole in the wall. Instantly, the panel swung open, and the floor beneath me shifted, carrying me into the next chamber.

There stood Tars Tarkas, back pressed against the corner, bleeding from a dozen wounds. Around him crouched six massive beasts—banths—bloodied and snarling, waiting for an opening. Their claws had torn his hide, and his arms were nearly shredded. Yet he stood firm, his green eyes blazing with defiance.

As I entered, he smiled—a grim, weary smile. Whether it was relief or mockery at my own battered state, I could not tell.

I drew my sword, ready to join the fight—but a hand touched my shoulder. I turned.

The girl had followed me. “Wait,” she whispered. “Leave this to me.”

Before I could stop her, she stepped forward—unarmed, defenseless—into the midst of the snarling monsters. Close to them, she spoke a single word in Martian. The creatures froze. Then, in a flash, they turned and bowed their heads before her, slinking to her feet like whipped dogs.

She spoke again—softly, urgently—and led them one by one through the secret panel, vanishing into the next room. When the last had gone, she turned to us, smiled, and passed through after them.

We stood in silence.

Tars Tarkas broke it. “I heard the fighting beyond the wall. I did not fear for you until I heard the gunshot. I knew no man alive could stand against you with steel alone. But the shot… that told me you were unarmed. And then I lost all hope.”

I told him what had happened—how I had fought, how I had killed two therns, how I had come to this place.

Then we sought the other secret panel—the one opposite the one she had used. We tried every trick, every pressure point, but it would not budge. Frustration mounted. We felt certain escape lay beyond it.

Just as we began to despair, the panel opened silently. The girl stood there once more.

“Who are you?” she asked. “And why do you seek to flee the Valley Dor? You have chosen death.”

“I have not chosen death,” I answered. “I am not of Barsoom. I am John Carter, Prince of Helium, Jeddak of Tardos Mors. And this warrior beside me is Tars Tarkas, Jeddak of the Tharks. We are not bound by your superstitions. We seek to leave this cursed place—and take others with us.”

She studied me. “I have heard of you. Long ago. They said you vanished—no pilgrimage, no trace. Some believed you died. Others thought you fled. But none knew.”

“And you?” I asked. “Who are you? Why are you imprisoned, yet able to command these beasts?”

“I am a slave,” she said. “Fifteen years in this hell. Now, they tire of me. They fear my knowledge. They have sentenced me to die.”

“To die how?” I asked.

“The Holy Therns eat human flesh—but only that which has been drained of blood by the plant men. I am to be given to one soon. Had you not come, I would have perished within hours.”

I stared. “So the ones I killed—they were not Holy Therns?”

“No. Lesser therns. The Holy Therns dwell on the outer slopes of these mountains, above the valley. Their palaces are connected by labyrinthine tunnels. Through them pass slaves, beasts, and the lesser priests. Many born here have never seen daylight. They are kept to serve the therns—to provide sport and food.”

“And you?” I asked. “How did you end up here?”

“I was taken from the river Iss. A Holy Thern saw me from the balcony above and claimed me. The law says all who reach the Valley Dor belong to the plant men and apes. But if one escapes the creatures for a time, the therns may claim them. And sometimes, a Holy Thern will seize a victim by force—even over the rights of the beasts.”

She paused. “There is a temple—Issus. Some say it is the true paradise. That after death, the soul enters it. But no one returns from within its golden walls. No one knows what lies there. To the therns, it is heaven. To us, a myth.”

“And you believe it?” I asked.

She shook her head. “No. But they do. And so I must die.”

“Not if we can help it,” I said. “We will escape. And you will come with us.”

Her eyes widened. “To be executed by my own people? To bring shame upon my house? A prince should not suggest such folly.”

Tars Tarkas watched me closely. I could feel his gaze—waiting.

“We have a right to live,” I said. “We know this is no paradise. The Valley Dor is a lie. The Holy Therns are not holy. They are mortals—cruel, selfish, and no wiser than we. If we stay, we accept their deceit. If we go, we carry the truth. Even if no one believes us, we must try. For the sake of others who may fall into this trap.”

She looked at me long and hard. Then she nodded. “I never thought of it that way. But yes—I would give my life a thousand times to save one soul from this horror. I will go with you.”

Tars Tarkas stepped forward. “To the gates of Issus, or to the bottom of Korus. North or south, I follow where John Carter leads. I have spoken.”

“Then come,” I said. “We begin now.”

She led us back through the secret door, into the chamber of prisoners. Ten in total—men and women—all chained. After a brief explanation, they agreed to join us, though their faces showed fear and doubt.

Thuvia—her name, I learned—freed them all. Tars Tarkas and I stripped the dead therns of their weapons—swords, daggers, and two revolvers. We gave them to our companions. Thuvia took one of the guns.

With her guidance, we moved through winding corridors, vast chambers carved from living rock, steep inclines, and shadowed alcoves. We paused at every sound. Our goal: a storeroom filled with arms and ammunition.

“Even if we reach the surface,” she warned, “the Holy Thern’s reach is long. His temples are everywhere. If we escape, they will know. Death awaits us wherever we go.”

We traveled for an hour—then entered a great hall. There stood a man in leathery robes, adorned with jewels and a golden circlet upon his brow. At its center glowed a gem—an inch across, shimmering with nine colors: the seven of Earth’s prism, and two unknown, radiant hues.

I recognized it instantly—the same stone I had seen on the old man at the atmosphere plant. One of only two known in all Mars. A symbol of power, of authority.

The thern saw us. His eyes narrowed. “Stop!” he cried. “What is this, Thuvia?”

She raised her revolver. “Beast.” And fired.

He fell without a sound.

She turned to me. “After all these years… I am avenged.”

Then she stared at me. Her eyes widened. “Prince… look. Fate is kind. See the resemblance between him and you?”

Indeed, the man was my height, his features close to mine. Only his hair was long and golden, while mine was black and short.

“What of it?” I asked.

She knelt, removed the circlet, then lifted the entire scalp from the dead man’s head—skin, hair, and all. She placed the yellow wig over my head, crowned me with the circlet.

“Now don his harness,” she said. “You are Sator Throg—Holy Thern of the Tenth Cycle. No one will question you.”

I hesitated. “They are bald?”

“Yes,” she said. “From birth. Their ancestors had golden hair, but now they are born without a strand. The wig is sacred. To appear without it is disgrace.”

In moments, I stood clad in the robes of a Holy Thern. Two prisoners carried the corpse ahead of us, as we continued to the storeroom—reaching it without further incident.

Thuvia used the keys from the first thern to unlock the chamber. Inside, we armed ourselves—swords, guns, ammunition. By then, I was spent. I collapsed onto the floor, bidding Tars Tarkas to rest as well. I ordered two of the freed prisoners to watch.

And in seconds, I was asleep.

I do not know how long I slept on the cold stone floor of the storeroom, but it must have been many hours. My rest was shattered by sudden cries of alarm—shrieks that tore through the silence like knives. Before my eyes could fully open, before my mind had grasped where I was, a burst of gunfire erupted, echoing wildly through the underground tunnels in a thunderous chain of reverberations.

In an instant, I was on my feet.

At the far end of the chamber, a dozen lesser therns stood silhouetted in the doorway, rifles still raised. On the ground lay the lifeless forms of my companions—every one except Thuvia and Tars Tarkas, who, like me, had been sleeping and thus avoided the first volley. As I rose, the therns lowered their weapons, their faces twisted with shock, fear, and confusion.

I stepped forward, voice ringing with authority. “What is this?” I demanded, fury blazing in my chest. “Is Sator Throg to be slain by his own servants?”

“Mercy, O Master of the Tenth Cycle!” one of them stammered, stepping back. The others shuffled toward the door, as if hoping to vanish from the presence of a god.

Thuvia leaned close, her whisper barely audible. “Ask them what they were doing here.”

I turned to face them. “Why are you here? What brings you into this place?”

“We were sent by the Father of Therns,” one replied, glancing nervously at Tars Tarkas. “Two outsiders have escaped into the domain of the therns—a white man with black hair, and a giant green warrior. We were ordered to capture or kill them.”

Thuvia stepped forward, pointing to Tars Tarkas. “Here is one.” Then she gestured toward the body near the door. “And if you look closely at this dead man, you may recognize the other. It was left to Sator Throg and his slaves to finish what your guards failed to do—we killed one and captured the other. For that, Sator Throg granted us our freedom. And now, in your ignorance, you’ve killed all but myself—and nearly killed the mighty Sator Throg himself.”

The therns looked ashamed, terrified. Their eyes darted between each other, then toward me.

“Shouldn’t you take these bodies to the plant men?” Thuvia asked me quietly. “Then return to your posts?”

“Yes,” I said. “Do as she says.”

As they gathered the corpses, I noticed something strange. One of the men hesitated when he reached Sator Throg’s body. He stared at the face—then glanced at me out of the corner of his eye. A flicker of suspicion crossed his features. But he said nothing. When he lifted the body, he shot another glance at me—this time sharper, more deliberate. And as he turned to leave, I caught a glimpse of his profile: a sly, triumphant smile playing across his lips.

Only Thuvia, Tars Tarkas, and I remained.

The therns’ reckless fire had ended any hope my comrades might have had of escape. Once the last of the grim procession vanished, Thuvia urged us to move again.

She had seen the same look in the man’s eyes. “It bodes ill for us, Prince,” she whispered. “Even if he dared not accuse you directly, there are those above who hold power enough to demand answers. And if they investigate… we are doomed.”

I shrugged. Death seemed inevitable. I had rested from sleep, but I was still weak from blood loss. My wounds burned. No healing was possible—not here. I longed for the miraculous salves of the green Martian women. In an hour, they would have made me whole. But here, I was helpless. Hopelessness settled over me like a shroud.

Then, a thought struck me—the Holy Thern’s golden crown, its long, flowing yellow tresses stirred by a faint breeze. Could it still be the key to freedom? If we moved quickly, perhaps we could escape before the alarm spread.

“What will he do first?” I asked Thuvia.

“He’ll go straight to Matai Shang, the Father of Therns,” she replied. “He may wait for an audience, but since he’s a thorian—high among the lesser therns—he won’t be kept long. If Matai Shang believes him, within an hour the entire fortress will be searching—courts, gardens, temples, corridors.”

“So we must act within the hour,” I said. “What is the fastest way out of this hell?”

“Straight up the cliffs, Prince,” she answered. “Through the gardens to the inner courts, then across the temples to the outer court. From there, the ramparts. But—O Prince—it is impossible. Even ten thousand warriors could not break free from this cursed place.”

She spoke with quiet certainty. “Since time began, the therns have built layer upon layer of defenses around the Mountains of Otz. A continuous wall of impregnable fortifications circles the slopes. Within the temples, a million warriors stand ready. The courts and gardens teem with slaves, women, children. There is no hiding. No escaping.”

“If there is no other way,” I said, “then we face it.”

“Perhaps we should wait until night?” suggested Tars Tarkas. “By day, there’s no chance.”

“Night offers slightly better odds,” Thuvia admitted. “But the ramparts are well guarded—even more so than by day. Fewer people wander the courts, though.”

“What time is it?” I asked.

“It was midnight when you freed me,” she said. “We reached the storeroom two hours later. You slept fourteen hours. It must be nearly sundown now. Come—let’s find a window.”

She led us through winding passages until we came upon a narrow opening overlooking the Valley Dor. To the right, the sun was setting—a vast, crimson disc sinking below the western peaks of Otz. Below us, the Holy Thern stood on his balcony, wrapped tightly in his scarlet robe, bracing against the sudden chill of nightfall.

On Mars, the air is thin. It holds no heat. Days are scorching; nights are freezing. And unlike Earth, there is no twilight. The moment the sun dips below the horizon, light vanishes—like a lamp extinguished in a room. Darkness falls instantly, absolute and crushing.

Then came the moons—two great, ghostly orbs hurtling low across the sky, casting eerie shadows over the land.

Beneath the eastern banks of Korus, the crimson grass shimmered under the dying light. Trees stretched tall, their leaves trimmed neatly—now I understood why I’d once thought this grove belonged to a civilized people. But no—these were the feeding grounds of the plant men.

They stood on their toes, tails coiled high, talons plucking every leaf. Herds roamed beneath the trees, feeding in silence.

Then, from the base of the cliffs, a canoe emerged from the mountain mouth—carrying a dozen souls from the outer world. They were red Martians, noble and cultured, the dominant race of Barsoom.

The herald on the balcony below saw them first. He raised his head, leaned far over the railing, and let out a piercing, unearthly wail.

Instantly, the plant men surged from the grove—great, grotesque creatures leaping across the valley in monstrous bounds. The newcomers had landed on the sward. They tried to fight. But it was futile. Silence followed—then the terrible sound of tearing flesh, of countless mouths drinking deep from veins.

I turned away, sickened.

“Their part ends quickly,” Thuv:ia murmured. “The great white apes come next, to feast on the flesh. Look—they’re coming.”

Sure enough, a dozen massive white beasts ran across the valley, drawn by the scent of blood.

Then the sun vanished. Darkness swallowed everything.

Without delay, Thuvia guided us along the winding path upward, deeper into the cliffs. Twice, great banths blocked our way—ferocious predators roaming the galleries. But each time, Thuvia spoke a single word, low and commanding. The beasts slunk away, growling in submission.

“You command them as easily as you command these beasts,” I said, smiling. “If you can do that to every obstacle, what danger remains?”

She laughed, then shivered. “I don’t know how. When I first came here, I angered Sator Throg. He threw me into a pit full of banths. But when they lunged at me, something in my voice—something I cannot explain—made them cower. Instead of killing me, they bowed. Sator Throg found it amusing. He kept me to train them. Now I know them all by name. These are scavengers—many prisoners die in chains. The banths clean the mess.”

She paused. “Above, they’re kept in pits. The therns fear them. That’s why they rarely come down here—especially at night.”

An idea struck me. “Why not bring some banths with us? Let them run ahead—draw attention.”

She smiled. “That might work.” She began to hum a low, singsong tune—half purr, half command. As we moved, soft padded footsteps echoed behind us. Then, from the shadows, a pair of glowing green eyes appeared. A tawny form crept forward. Growls and snarls filled the air as more banths joined us.

Thuvia spoke to each one. They walked beside us like trained hounds—though their jowls dripped saliva, their eyes fixed hungrily on Tars Tarkas and me.

Soon, fifty of the beasts surrounded us. Two flanked Thuvia like guards. Others brushed against my bare legs. The silence was broken only by our footsteps—naked human feet and padded paws. The walls gleamed with precious stones. Dim radium bulbs cast pale light. The air was thick with tension.

It was a surreal procession: a woman leading, a green warrior towering above, a man crowned with holy gold, and fifty hungry predators following in silence.

I shall never forget it.

We approached a large chamber, brighter than the corridors. Thuvia halted us. She crept forward, peered inside, then signaled us to follow.

Inside lay a horrifying collection—hybrids, offspring of mixed races: red Martians, green Martians, whites, and therns. Years of confinement had twisted their forms. Many were blind, maimed, deformed. They sprawled across the floor in heaps, motionless—like corpses.

They reminded me of the illustrations in Dante’s *Inferno*. This was no kingdom—it was a true hell, populated by lost souls, damned beyond redemption.

We navigated carefully, the banths sniffing at the helpless forms, growling with hunger. We passed other chambers, similarly filled with horrors. Some held chained prisoners, others wild beasts.

“Why do we see no therns?” I asked.

“They avoid the underworld at night,” Thuvia explained. “That’s when the banths hunt. The therns fear the creatures they’ve bred. Sometimes prisoners turn on them. A thern never knows when a shadow might become a killer.”

“By day, it’s different,” she added. “Then the corridors are alive—guards patrol, slaves carry supplies. But I brought you through hidden paths. Still, we might meet one yet.”

But we reached the upper galleries unseen.

Thuvia stopped at the foot of a steep climb. “Above,” she said, “is a doorway to the inner gardens. I’ve brought you this far. Beyond lies four miles of danger—guards, temples, gardens, ramparts. Every inch watched.”

I frowned. “Why such a massive force? This place is feared by all—no one would dare approach.”

She opened the door. “They fear the black pirates of Barsoom, Prince. May our ancestors protect us from them.”

The door swung wide. The scent of living things filled the air. Cool night wind touched my skin. The banths sniffed the new odors—then surged forward, racing across the gardens beneath the glow of the nearer moon.

Suddenly, a cry rang out—from the temple roofs. An alarm. It spread rapidly, echoing from tower to tower, from court to rampart, fading into the distance like a distant scream.

Tars Tarkas drew his long-sword. Thuvia clung to my side, trembling.

“What is it?” I asked the girl.

She pointed upward, her hand trembling slightly. I followed her gesture and saw them—dark shapes drifting across the sky like phantoms, weaving through the air above the temple, the courts, and the gardens. Then came flashes—bright bursts of light—and a thunderous roar that shook the very ground beneath my feet. From the ramparts, answering fire erupted in quick succession, streaks of flame cutting through the night as defenders returned volley for volley.

“The black pirates of Barsoom,” Thuvia whispered, her voice tight with dread. “O Prince.”

Above us, their flying machines circled in wide arcs, descending steadily toward the thern forces below. Each craft spat death—round after round of bullets tearing into the temple guards, while from the fortifications, rifles fired through shielded muzzles, their shots arcing upward into the darkness. The smaller fliers swooped low, darting like hawks among the defenders, while larger vessels hovered high, dropping bombs from their underbellies that exploded against the sacred walls.

Then, suddenly, they surged forward in unison—a signal must have been given. In a reckless charge, the nearest pirate ships plunged straight down into the heart of the thern ranks. Before their craft even touched the ground, their crews leapt out—silent, swift, and deadly—as if summoned by some ancient fury.

I had never seen such warfare. I had thought the green Martians the fiercest warriors in the universe, but these black raiders fought with a savagery beyond imagination. They moved like demons unleashed, their swords flashing in the twin moonlight, their cries echoing across the garden like the wails of the damned.

The scene unfolded in stark clarity beneath Mars’ luminous moons. Golden-haired, pale-skinned therns clashed with ebony-skinned foes in brutal hand-to-hand combat. Here, a knot of fighters trampled a bed of glowing pimalia flowers; there, a curved blade pierced a thern’s chest, leaving him dead at the base of a ruby statue carved from living stone. Elsewhere, a dozen therns pressed a single pirate back onto an emerald bench, its surface shimmering with intricate diamond inlays depicting forgotten myths of Barsoom.

Nearby, Thuvia stood beside Tars Tarkas and me. The tide of battle had not yet reached us, but occasionally the fighting swept close enough for us to see clearly—the grim faces, the bloodied blades, the desperate struggle.

And the pirates—they fascinated me. I had heard whispers of them in my youth, stories passed among the people of Helium—tales so wild they seemed more myth than truth. It was said they dwelled upon Phobos, the lesser moon, emerging only once every few generations to descend upon Barsoom in terror. Wherever they went, they left destruction in their wake: temples burned, cities sacked, women taken captive. And worse—rumor claimed they sacrificed their prisoners to a dark god in grotesque rites, ending with cannibalistic feasts.

Now, at last, I saw them.

They were tall—easily six feet or more—with sharp, noble features and eyes large and deep-set, their irises pure black, their whites unnaturally bright. Their bodies were strong and well-proportioned, almost identical in build to mine, to the therns, to any human. But their skin—oh, their skin—was like polished ebony, gleaming under the moonlight. Strange, perhaps, for a man born in the South to say, but it lent them a terrible beauty.

Yet their hearts were not beautiful. Not one bit.

Their thirst for blood was monstrous. They fought not for victory, but for slaughter. For sport. For glory in the carnage.

Scattered about the garden lay their sleek, black fliers—dozens of them—abandoned, untouched. The therns made no move to destroy them. Why? I would soon learn.

From time to time, a pirate would emerge from a temple, clutching a young woman in his arms. He’d sprint toward his ship, comrades rushing to cover his retreat. The therns would charge to rescue her, and instantly the two sides would collide in a whirlwind of steel and fury. But always, it seemed, the pirates won. The girl, unharmed, would vanish into the night on the deck of a speeding vessel.

“Do you understand now, O Prince,” Thuvia said, her voice low, “why a million soldiers guard the Holy Therns day and night?”

“This,” she continued, “is what I’ve witnessed a hundred times in fifteen years as a prisoner. Since time began, the black pirates have raided the therns. But they never finish them. Never attempt to wipe them out. As though they keep them alive—not for conquest, but for conflict. For sport. They take weapons, ammunition, and slaves… but never do they destroy the race.”

I looked around. “Why don’t they attack the fliers? If they destroyed these craft, the raids would end—or at least the pirates wouldn’t be so bold. Look how carelessly they leave them! As if they’re safe in their own hangars.”

“The therns dare not,” Thuvia replied. “Once, long ago, they tried. They burned several fliers. The next night, a thousand great black battleships surrounded the Mountains of Otz. For an entire lunar cycle, they bombarded the temples, gardens, and courts. Every thern who wasn’t killed fled underground. That was the last time they dared touch a pirate ship.”

She paused. “They live only by the mercy of the blacks. They know full well that another mistake could mean extinction.”

Just then, a new force entered the fray—one neither side expected.

The banths—those massive, lion-like beasts we had freed earlier—had been startled by the noise, the shouting, the gunfire. But now, driven by hunger and rage, they charged.

One burst from a thicket, roaring, talons slashing through flesh. Its cry echoed like thunder, and instantly the rest of the pack surged forward—no longer afraid, no longer passive. They tore into the battle, indifferent to side, devouring men without mercy.

Panic spread. Therns and pirates alike turned to face the same enemy. The beasts were too powerful—too fast. They crushed men beneath their weight, ripped throats with jaws like iron, and flung bodies aside like rag dolls.

It was horrifying. And yet—my mind snapped to attention.

This chaos was our chance.

The therns were distracted. The pirates were overwhelmed. Now, more than ever, escape was possible.

I scanned the garden. Hundreds of fliers littered the grounds—unmanned, unguarded. A perfect opportunity.

I knew every kind of Martian aircraft. For nine years, I had sailed with the navy of Helium. I had piloted one-man scouts and commanded warships that dwarfed Earth’s greatest fleets. To think was to act.

I grabbed Thuvia’s arm. “Tars Tarkas—follow me!”

We slipped through the confusion toward a small flier, farthest from the fight. Within seconds, we were aboard. I reached for the starting lever, my thumb pressing the repulsion button—the Martian invention that allowed flight in Mars’ thin air.

But nothing happened.

The craft trembled, but didn’t rise.

Then I understood.

It was a two-man flier. Its energy tanks held just enough power for two average men. Tars Tarkas—huge, powerful, heavy—was too much. His weight anchored us.

Behind us, shouts erupted. A dozen pirates broke from the melee, charging toward us.

“We’re discovered!” I thought.

I pressed the button again—harder this time—locking it in place. I set the lever to maximum speed. Then, as the pirates lunged forward, I stepped off the deck, sword drawn.

“Thuvia!” I shouted. “Run!”

A scream cut through the air—her voice, fading into the din.

“O my Prince… I would rather die with you—”

But the rest was lost in the roar of battle.

I fought alone now.

My sword flashed—once, twice, three times. Blood sprayed. Pirates recoiled. I was stronger than them—stronger than any man on Mars. My strength, inherited from my Virginia ancestors, gave me an edge. I wove a deadly net of steel around me. They pressed close, but couldn’t reach me. Respect flickered in their eyes.

“You fight like a Dator,” one growled. “If not for your yellow hair and white skin, you’d be worthy of the First Born.”

“I am no thern,” I said. “I am from another world.”

I was about to offer an alliance—fight with them against the therns—when something struck me hard between the shoulders.

I stumbled. Then, over my head, a second object flew past—smashing into a pirate’s face, knocking him unconscious.

I turned. A thick chain dangled from a large flier hovering just fifty feet above—slowly rising. The anchor swung downward, passing over the heads of my attackers.

A chance.

I leapt—not away, but *over* them—clearing their line with a bound that left them stunned. Then, another leap—just high enough to grab the anchor as it rose.

I hung there, swinging through the branches of the garden trees, the pirates screaming beneath me.

The ship drifted west, then south. Soon, I was carried beyond the Golden Cliffs, soaring over the Valley Dor. Below, the Lost Sea of Korus sparkled in the moonlight, vast and silent.

I climbed carefully onto the anchor, sitting astride it. Was the ship empty? Abandoned? Or friendly?

I hoped so.

With caution, I began to climb the chain toward the deck.

My hand reached the rail. I grasped it—then froze.

A black face appeared over the side.

Eyes blazing with hatred stared into mine.

And I knew—this was no accident.

This was no rescue.

This was a trap.

For a heartbeat, the black pirate and I stood frozen, locked in a silent duel of stares. Then, a cold smile curled his lips—handsome, cruel, and utterly confident—as an ebony hand emerged from above the deck edge, revealing the hollow, unblinking eye of a revolver aimed directly at my forehead.

The gun’s barrel was icy against the night air. At the same instant, my free hand lunged forward, fingers closing around the throat just inches away. The pirate’s finger tightened on the trigger.

“Die, cursed thern!” he hissed—only for the words to choke in his throat as my grip tightened.

The hammer clicked—empty.

Before he could reload, I wrenched him over the rail. He had no choice but to drop the weapon and grasp the deck’s edge with both hands. My fingers clamped hard, cutting off sound, breath, and hope. We struggled in silence—a war of flesh and will—him fighting to break free, me determined to drag him into the abyss.

His face turned blue. His eyes bulged. He knew death was near unless he broke free.

With a final, desperate surge, he threw himself back onto the deck, releasing the rail to tear at my hands with both fists. That split second was all I needed.

I drove him down with a single, brutal thrust. His body tumbled over the side, nearly yanking me with it. But I held fast—because one cry from those lips would summon his comrades. So I kept my grip, choking, squeezing, until his frantic kicks slowed, then stopped.

Then, I let go.

He vanished into the black water below.

I climbed back to the rail and peered up. The nearer moon had set, but the distant satellite cast a silver glow across the deck. In that light, I saw six or eight black men sprawled in sleep. Near a rapid-fire cannon, huddled at the base, was a young white woman—bound, her eyes wide with terror.

They locked onto mine.

A wave of relief flooded her features—like finding a lost star in the dark. Her gaze fell upon the jeweled circlet atop my head, the stolen insignia of a Holy Thern. She didn’t speak. Instead, her eyes warned me: *Beware the sleepers.*

Silently, I crossed the deck. She nodded, urging me closer. As I bent, she whispered, “Free me.”

“I can help you,” she said. “And you’ll need every advantage when they wake.”

“Some will awaken in Korus,” I replied, smiling.

She understood. And her smile—cold, sharp, cruel—chilled me more than any blade.

Cruelty is expected in a monster’s face. But when it flickers across a goddess’s perfect features—when beauty and malice coexist—it becomes something monstrous.

I freed her.

“Give me a revolver,” she whispered. “It silences faster than your sword.”

I gave it to her.

Then I turned to the work ahead. No mercy now. These were not men who deserved honor. They were predators.

Stealthily, I approached the nearest sleeper. When he woke, he was already falling—into the void beneath. His scream echoed faintly from the depths.

The second stirred as I touched him. I hurled him overboard—but his cry brought the rest to their feet.

Five pirates rose.

The girl fired. One dropped, dead.

The others charged—swords drawn.

She dared not shoot again, fearing to hit me. But I saw her move—cat-like, silent—toward the flank of the attackers.

They were on me.

For a few minutes, I fought harder than ever before. Space was too tight for movement. It was stand-and-deliver combat.

At first, I took more blows than I dealt. But then—I slipped under one man’s guard. He collapsed.

The others redoubled their fury. Steel clashed, sparks flew, and then—crunch—the dull snap of bone beneath my sword.

Three remained. But the girl was closing in.

Then—everything happened at once.

They rushed me, intent on forcing me over the rail. The girl fired.

My sword arm moved twice.

One man fell—bullet through the brain.

Another’s sword clattered across the deck, tumbling over the edge.

The third—my blade buried to the hilt in his chest, three feet protruding—died screaming, wrenching the weapon from my grasp.

Disarmed, I faced the last.

He smiled—confident, naked, bare-handed. His muscles rippled beneath his glossy black skin. He thought me easy prey.

I let him come close.

Then I ducked under his arms, stepped right, pivoted on my left foot—and slammed a fist into his jaw.

He fell like a felled ox.

Behind me, a silvery laugh rang out.

“You are no thern,” said the girl. “Not for all your golden hair or the harness of Sator Throg. Never before has any man fought like this on Barsoom. Who are you?”

“I am John Carter,” I replied. “Prince of the House of Tardos Mors, Jeddak of Helium.”

“And whom,” I added, “do I have the honor of serving?”

She hesitated.

Then she asked, “You are no thern. Are you an enemy of the therns?”

“I’ve been in their lands for a day and a half,” I said. “Every moment, I’ve been hunted—by men, by beasts. I had no quarrel with them. But can you blame me for not loving them now?”

She studied me long and hard—as if reading my soul.

Finally, she said, “I am Phaidor, daughter of Matai Shang, Holy Hekkador of the Therns, Father of Therns, Master of Life and Death upon Barsoom, Brother of Issus, Prince of Life Eternal.”

At that moment, the man I’d knocked out began to stir.

I sprang to him, stripped his harness, bound his hands and feet, and tied him to a heavy gun carriage.

“Why not the simpler way?” Phaidor asked.

“What simpler way?” I replied.

She shrugged, gesturing as though casting something overboard.

“I am no murderer,” I said. “I kill only in self-defense.”

She looked at me, puzzled.

She couldn’t understand.

Neither had Dejah Thoris. To her, mercy was noble—even dangerous. On Barsoom, mercy meant weakness. Every life lost meant less for the dying world.

But there was a difference here.

Phaidor didn’t mourn the man’s survival. She regretted the thrill she’d missed.

The prisoner regained consciousness. He was handsome, strong, intelligent—his features so finely carved even Adonis might have envied him.

The ship drifted aimlessly. Time to take control.

I guessed the Valley Dor lay far south. From the stars, I could tell. But without Martian charts, I could only approximate.

Northward—toward the settled lands—was my course.

I gripped the helm. The repulsive rays engaged. The cruiser lifted—soaring into space.

Speed lever maxed. We raced northward, rising higher and higher above the valley of death.

As we passed over the thern territories, flashes of gunfire lit the darkness below. The battle raged—silent to us, lost in the thin air.

Cold bit deep. Breathing grew hard.

Phaidor and the prisoner watched me.

Then she spoke.

“Unconsciousness comes quickly at this altitude,” she said calmly. “Unless you want us all dead, drop—now.”

No fear in her voice. Just fact.

I dropped the ship.

Just in time.

Phaidor fainted.

The prisoner lost consciousness.

Only I remained—alive by will alone.

We descended, low over the foothills of the Otz. Air thickened. Warmth returned.

The prisoner opened his eyes.

Then Phaidor.

“It was close,” she said.

“It taught me two things,” I replied.

“What?”

“That even Phaidor, daughter of the Master of Life and Death, is mortal,” I said, smiling.

“There is immortality only in Issus,” she replied. “And Issus is for the thern race alone. Thus, I am immortal.”

A fleeting grin crossed the prisoner’s face.

I didn’t understand then.

Later, I would. And she would, too—in horror.

“If the other thing you’ve learned,” she continued, “leads to such wrong conclusions as the first, you’re no richer in wisdom.”

“The other,” I said, “is that our dusky friend here isn’t from the nearer moon. He nearly died at a few thousand feet above Barsoom. Had we flown five thousand miles to Thuria, he’d have been frozen memory.”

Phaidor stared at him in shock.

“If you’re not from Thuria,” she demanded, “then where?”

He shrugged, looking away.

She stamped her foot.

“The daughter of Matai Shang does not accept unanswered questions. A lesser being should be honored to be noticed by one born to eternal life.”

He smiled—wicked, knowing.

“Xodar, Dator of the First Born of Barsoom, gives commands, not receives them.”

He turned to me. “What do you intend for me?”

“I’m taking you both to Helium,” I said. “No harm. The red men are kind and generous. But if they listen to me… no more pilgrimages down the River Iss. The myth will end.”

“Are you of Helium?”

“I am Jeddak of Helium,” I said. “But I am not of Barsoom. I am of another world.”

Xodar studied me.

“I believe you’re not of Barsoom,” he said. “None here could defeat eight of the First Born alone. But why wear the golden hair and jeweled circlet of a Holy Thern?”

He spat the word *holy* with irony.

“I forgot them,” I said. “Spoils of conquest.” With a sweep of my hand, I removed the disguise.

When Xodar saw my short black hair, his eyes widened.

“No bald pate,” he murmured. “You are truly of another world.”

He paused. “With the skin of a thern, the hair of a First Born, and the strength of a dozen Dators… even Xodar must acknowledge your supremacy. Though never would I bow to a Barsoomian.”

“Traveling ahead of me, friend,” I said. “I know your name is Xodar. But what are the First Born? What is a Dator? And why, if you were conquered by a Barsoomian, could you not admit it?”

“The First Born,” he explained, “are the ancient black race. I am a Dator—one of their princes. Our lineage traces back to the Tree of Life, twenty-three million years ago, in the heart of the Valley Dor.”

“For ages, its fruit evolved—from plant to animal. At first, the fruit moved independently, still attached by stems. Then brains developed. They thought. They judged. Reason was born.”

“Eventually, the tree bore tiny plant men—still hanging by stems from their heads. The buds contained four life forms: the plant man, a sixteen-legged worm, the ancestor of the white ape, and the primordial black man.”

“When the bud burst, the plant man dangled. The others fell—hopping, trapped in shells. For eons, they lived within their prisons, hopping across Barsoom, scattered by rivers and seas.”

“Billions died before the first black man broke free. Curiosity led him to open others. Barsoom was peopled.”

“Our bloodline remains pure. All other life—worm, ape, beast—descended from those other sections. The therns?” He smiled bitterly. “They’re mere evolved white apes. A lower order.”

“Only one race is truly immortal—the black men.”

“The Tree of Life is gone. But before it died, the plant men detached themselves. Now they reproduce like plants, but their minds remain simple. Their brains are smaller than your little finger. They live on vegetation and animal blood, driven only by instinct. They feel no fear. That’s why they’re terrifying in battle.”

I wondered why he told me this—why a captive, bound and powerless, would reveal such secrets.

Then I saw it.

His eyes—just for a fraction of a second—darted past me.

Toward the stern.

I turned.

And froze.

A massive battleship—dark, silent, unlit—loomed behind us.

Closer than I could have imagined.

And it was coming.

Now I understood why the black pirate had kept me captivated with his tale. For miles, he’d sensed the approach of rescue—had it not been for that one fateful glance, the battleship would have loomed directly above us in moments. The boarding party, already swinging from the ship’s keel, would have swarmed over our deck, crushing my fragile hope of escape into dust.

I was no novice to aerial combat. Instinct took over. I reversed the engines and dropped the tiny craft a full hundred feet. Above me, I saw the dangling figures of the boarding crew as the battleship raced overhead. Then, with a sharp upward surge, I pushed the speed lever to its maximum. Like a bolt from a crossbow, my sleek vessel lunged forward, its steel prow aimed straight at the whirring propellers of the colossal warship above.

If I could strike them, even once, the giant would be crippled—buying me time. At that instant, the sun rose over the horizon, revealing a hundred grim, dark faces peering over the stern of the battleship. A roar of fury erupted from their throats. Orders were bellowed—but it was too late. With a thunderous crash, we rammed the propellers.

The impact jolted me violently. I reversed the engine immediately, but my prow was wedged deep into the hole I’d torn in the battleship’s stern. I hung there for only a second before tearing free—but that second was enough. Black figures flooded onto our deck like a tide.

There was no fight. Not really. There was no room to fight. We were overwhelmed by sheer numbers. Swords flashed toward me, but a command from Xodar halted them. “Secure them,” he ordered, “but do not harm them.”

Several pirates had already freed Xodar. Now, he personally disarmed me and bound my wrists. He believed the restraints secure—had I been Martian, they might have held. But I smiled inwardly. These ropes were as weak as cotton thread. When the time came, I could snap them without effort.

They bound the girl as well, then fastened us together. Meanwhile, our craft was brought alongside the disabled battleship. Soon, we were transferred to her deck.

A thousand black warriors manned this engine of destruction. Their decks teemed with them, pressing forward as far as discipline allowed to gawk at their captives. The girl’s beauty drew vulgar comments and crude jokes. It was clear these so-called supermen were far less noble than the red men of Barsoom—lacking refinement, honor, or chivalry.

My short black hair and thern complexion sparked curiosity. When Xodar told his companions of my fighting prowess and strange origin, they crowded around, eager with questions. The fact that I wore the harness and armor of a dead thern—one killed by someone in my own party—convinced them I was an enemy of their ancient foes. That placed me in better standing among them.

All the blacks were strikingly handsome, powerfully built. The officers stood out in dazzling splendor. Many harnesses were encrusted with gold, platinum, silver, and gemstones so thick the leather beneath was hidden. The commanding officer’s harness was a solid mass of diamonds—blazing against his ebony skin like stars on midnight.

The scene was breathtaking: the proud men, the barbaric finery, the polished skeel wood decks, the richly grained sorapus cabins inlaid with jewels and metals in intricate patterns, the burnished handrails, the gleaming guns.

Phaidor and I were taken below. Still bound, we were thrown into a small compartment with a single porthole. As our guards left, they barred the door behind us. We heard the clatter of tools—repairing the broken propellers—and through the porthole, we saw the ship drifting southward.

For a long while, neither of us spoke. Each lost in thought.

I wondered about Tars Tarkas and Thuvia. Even if they escaped pursuit, they’d eventually fall into the hands of either red or green Martians. As fugitives from the Valley Dor, they faced only swift, brutal death. How I wished I’d gone with them.

I knew I could convince the intelligent red men of Barsoom of the cruel lie they’d been fed—a superstition born of ignorance and fear. Tardos Mors would believe me. I was certain. And he’d act on it. Dejah Thoris would believe me—no doubt about that. And there were hundreds of red and green warriors who would follow me into hell itself. Like Tars Tarkas, they would follow where I led.

My only danger? If I ever escaped the blacks, I might fall into the hands of unfriendly Martians. Then I’d face execution without trial. But the odds of escaping the blacks seemed slim. Very slim.

We were chained together by a rope allowing only three or four feet between us. Inside the cell, we sat on a low bench beneath the porthole—the only furniture. The bench was made of sorapus. The walls, floor, and ceiling were carborundum aluminum—light, strong, impervious—common in Martian warships.

As I sat, lost in thought, my eyes fixed on the porthole, level with my gaze. Then I turned to Phaidor.

She was staring at me—with an expression I’d never seen before. She was beautiful. Then her lids lowered, and I noticed a faint blush across her cheek.

She must’ve felt embarrassed—caught looking at a lesser being, I thought.

“Do you find studying the lower orders fascinating?” I asked, laughing.

She looked up, nervously, then laughed softly. “Oh, very much—especially when they have such excellent profiles.”

I flushed—but didn’t show it. I sensed she was teasing me. And I admired her courage—finding humor on the road to death.

“Do you know where we’re going?” she asked.

“To solve the mystery of the afterlife, I suppose,” I replied.

“I’m headed to something worse,” she said, shuddering.

“What do you mean?”

“I can only guess,” she answered. “No thern maiden stolen by black pirates has ever returned to tell the tale. They never take men prisoner—that suggests the fate of women is worse than death.”

“Is that not just retribution?” I couldn’t help asking.

“What do you mean?”

“Don’t the therns themselves do the same to those who take the pilgrimage down the River of Mystery? Wasn’t Thuvia a slave for fifteen years? Is it unjust that you suffer what you inflict?”

“You don’t understand,” she said. “We therns are holy. To be a slave among us is an honor. If we didn’t save some of the fools who float down that river, they’d all be devoured by plant men and apes.”

“But don’t you encourage the superstition?” I pressed. “That’s your greatest sin. Why foster such a cruel deception?”

“All life on Barsoom exists to serve the therns,” she said. “How else could we live? Would a thern stoop to labor?”

“Then you eat human flesh?” I asked, horrified.

She looked at me with pity. “Of course. As man eats beasts, so gods eat men. The Holy Therns are the gods of Barsoom.”

I recoiled. I imagine I showed it.

“You’re an unbeliever now,” she said gently. “But if we escape the pirates and return to Matai Shang’s court, I think we can show you the truth. And…” She hesitated. “…perhaps find a way to keep you… one of us.”

Her eyes dropped. A faint color tinged her cheeks.

I didn’t understand. Not then. Dejah Thoris often called me a simpleton. Maybe she was right.

“I fear I’d repay your father’s hospitality poorly,” I said. “The first thing I’d do as a thern would be to station guards at the mouth of the River Iss—to guide the deluded back to the outer world. And I’d spend my life destroying the plant men and the great white apes.”

She stared at me, truly shocked. “No! You mustn’t say such things—such sacrilegious thoughts! If they ever learned you believed this, if we returned to the temples… they’d kill you. Not even I…” She stopped, blushing again. “…not even I could save you.”

I said nothing. It was useless. She was more deeply trapped in superstition than any Martian I’d known. They believed in love, peace, eternal joy. The therns worshipped monsters—plant men and apes—as homes for the spirits of their ancestors.

Then the door opened. Xodar stepped in.

He smiled at me—a kind smile, not cruel or vengeful.

“Since you cannot escape,” he said, “why keep you locked away? I’ll cut your bonds. Come on deck. You’ll see something rare—something no one outside the First Born and their slaves knows: the underground entrance to the Holy Land. The true heaven of Barsoom.”

“And a lesson for this thern daughter,” he added. “She’ll see the Temple of Issus. Perhaps Issus will embrace her.”

Phaidor lifted her head proudly. “What blasphemy is this, dog of a pirate?” she snapped. “Issus would destroy your entire race if you ever neared her temple.”

“You have much to learn, thern,” Xodar replied, smirking. “And I don’t envy you how you’ll learn it.”

On deck, I was stunned. The ship sailed over an endless expanse of ice and snow—stretching as far as sight could reach. Only one explanation: we were above the south polar ice cap. No life below. Even the fur-bearing beasts were absent here.

Xodar stood beside me. “What course?” I asked.

“A little west of south,” he said. “You’ll see Otz Valley soon. We’ll skirt it for a few hundred miles.”

“The Otz Valley?” I gasped. “Isn’t that where the therns’ domains lie—the ones I just fled?”

“Yes,” Xodar said. “You crossed this ice field last night during your chase. Otz Valley lies in a vast depression at the south pole—thousands of feet below the surrounding land, like a great bowl. A hundred miles north rise the Otz Mountains, circling the inner Valley of Dor. At its heart lies the Lost Sea of Korus. On its shore stands the Golden Temple of Issus—the Land of the First Born. That’s where we’re headed.”

As I looked, I began to grasp why only one had ever escaped from the Valley Dor. Crossing this frozen wasteland alone would be impossible.

“Only by airboat,” I murmured.

“That’s how one escaped long ago,” Xodar said. “But none has ever escaped the First Born.”

Pride laced his voice.

We reached the southern edge of the ice barrier. It ended abruptly in a sheer cliff thousands of feet high. Below stretched a flat valley—broken by hills, forests, and rivers formed by melting ice.

We passed over a deep canyon-like rift running from the ice wall across the valley.

“That’s the River Iss,” Xodar said. “It flows beneath the ice, below the valley floor. But here, its canyon is open.”

Soon I spotted what looked like a village. I pointed.

“It’s a village of lost souls,” Xodar laughed. “This strip between the ice and the mountains is neutral ground. Some pilgrims turn off the River Iss, climb the canyon walls, and settle here. Slaves sometimes escape the therns and come here, too.”

“They don’t pursue them,” he continued. “No escape possible from this outer valley. And they fear our cruisers too much to leave their lands. We leave them alone—they have nothing we want, and aren’t strong enough to fight us worthily. Several villages exist, but they’ve grown little in centuries—always warring among themselves.”

We turned slightly north of west, leaving the valley of lost souls. Then, over our starboard bow, I saw a black mountain rising from the ice.

Not tall—flat-topped. Xodar had left us. Phaidor and I stood alone by the rail.

She hadn’t spoken since we came on deck.

“Is what he said true?” I asked.

“In part,” she replied. “The outer valley—yes. But the Temple of Issus being in the center of *his* land? That’s false. Or… if it’s true…”

She hesitated. “It can’t be. Because if it is, then for ages my people have been led to torture and death—not to the beautiful Life Eternal we’ve been taught.”

“As the outer Martians were lured to the Valley Dor,” I said, “perhaps the therns themselves were lured to a similar horror. A terrible retribution—but just.”

“I can’t believe it,” she whispered.

“We shall see,” I said.

And we fell silent—approaching the black mountain, which somehow felt tied to the truth we sought.

As we neared the dark, truncated cone, the ship slowed. Then, cresting the summit, I saw it: a massive circular pit, yawning wide, its bottom lost in darkness. The diameter was over a thousand feet. Smooth, black basalt walls rose around it.

The ship hovered above the center—then slowly descended into the abyss. Lower and lower, until darkness swallowed us. Lights flared, casting a dim glow. The battleship sank deeper—into what felt like the very heart of Barsoom.

For nearly half an hour we fell. Then the shaft ended—abruptly—in the dome of a vast subterranean world.

Below us, waves rose and fell on a buried sea. Phosphorescent light illuminated the scene. Thousands of ships dotted the water. Tiny islands bore strange, colorless plants.

The battleship settled gently on the surface. Its air propellers had been retracted. Smaller, more powerful water propellers extended—spinning as the ship resumed its journey, gliding smoothly over the new element.

Phaidor and I stood dumbfounded. Neither had ever dreamed such a world existed beneath Barsoom.

Most vessels were warships. A few lighters and barges—no great merchantmen like those that sailed between cities above.

“Here is the navy’s harbor,” a voice said behind us. We turned. Xodar watched us, amused.

“This sea,” he said, “is larger than Korus. It receives water from the upper sea. To prevent overflow, we have four pumping stations—forcing excess water back north, into the reservoirs the red men use to irrigate their fields.”

A sudden understanding struck me. The red Martians had always believed the water spouting from their reservoir walls was a miracle. None had ever discovered the source. Now I knew: the pumps.

We passed several islands—strange, circular buildings, roofless, with barred windows midway up. Guards patrolled. Prisons. Clearly.

Then, ahead, a larger island appeared. Our destination. The ship docked.

Xodar signaled us to follow. With a dozen officers, we approached a large oval structure.

“You’ll soon see Issus,” he told Phaidor. “We bring prisoners to her. Sometimes she chooses slaves—handmaidens. None serves her longer than a year.” His lips curled into a grim smile.

Phaidor clung to me—no longer the proud daughter of the Master of Life and Death, but a frightened girl in the hands of ruthless enemies.

Inside the building—roofless—we found a long pool of water, set below the floor. Near one end floated a dark, odd-shaped object—either a creature or a raft.

Xodar called out in a foreign tongue. A hatch opened. A black seaman emerged.

“Tell your officer,” Xodar commanded, “that Dator Xodar, with officers and men, escorting two prisoners, wishes transport to the gardens of Issus beside the Golden Temple.”

“Blessed be the shell of thy first ancestor, most noble Dator,” the man replied. “It shall be done.” He raised both hands, palms backward—the universal salute of Barsoom—and vanished.

Moments later, an officer in dazzling regalia appeared. He welcomed Xodar. We followed him aboard—below deck.

The cabin stretched across the ship, with portholes along both sides, below waterline. Once everyone was inside, hatches sealed. The ship vibrated—its machinery purring.

“Where are we going in such a tiny pool?” Phaidor asked.

“Not up,” I said. “I noticed the building is roofless, but covered with metal grating.”

“Then where?” she asked.

“Down,” I replied. “By the look of this craft, we’re descending.”

She shuddered. Deep water was a terror to Martians—even one born near Mars’ last sea.

Soon, the sensation of sinking became real. Water rushed past the portholes. Eddies swirled in the dim light.

She gripped my arm. “Save me!” she whispered. “Save me, and every wish of yours shall be granted. Anything the Holy Therns can give—yours. Phaidor—” She stumbled. “Phaidor is already yours.”

I felt sorry for her. I placed my hand over hers.

She glanced around—then, swiftly, threw her arms around my neck and pulled my face to hers.

Her confession—forced from her by fear—struck me like a blade to the heart. It stirred something deep within, yet it also filled me with shame. I had said or done something careless, some word or gesture that led her to believe I returned her affection. But such feelings were not mine to give. I had never been one for courtship or tender words; my life had been shaped by battle, survival, and duty. Romance was not my forte, nor did I seek it in the perfume of a glove too small for my hand or the fragile beauty of a wilted flower.

So now, faced with this young woman—radiant, proud, trembling with emotion—I found myself utterly unprepared. A thousand times I would have preferred to face the monstrous hordes of the dead sea bottoms than stand before her and speak the truth. Yet there was no escape.

Gently, I disentangled her hands from around my neck, still holding them in mine as I spoke. I told her plainly of my love—for Dejah Thoris alone. Among all the women I had known across two worlds, none had ever touched my soul as she had. Not even the goddesses of Mars could compare.

Her reaction was instant and terrible.

She sprang up like a wounded tigress, breathing hard, her face twisted into something monstrous. Her eyes burned with fury.

“Dog,” she hissed. “Blasphemer! Do you think Phaidor, daughter of Matai Shang, begs for your attention? She commands. What is your petty passion for that lowly creature from your past life compared to the love I have given you?”

She advanced, her voice rising. “I have honored you with my devotion, and you cast it aside. Ten thousand deaths—each more hideous than the last—would not cleanse the insult you’ve brought upon me. That woman you call Dejah Thoris shall die the most agonizing death imaginable. You have signed her sentence.”

Then, turning her venom toward me: “And you—your fate is slavery. The lowest servant beneath the feet of the goddess you dared defy. You will endure torments until you crawl before me, begging for death. And when I grant it, I will watch from the golden cliffs as the great white apes tear you apart.”

It was all laid out—her revenge, complete and chilling. I stood stunned, watching the divine beauty unravel into a creature of pure malice. Then, without malice of my own, I pointed to the nearest porthole.

She had forgotten where we were. Forgotten the dark, churning waters outside.

The moment she saw them—black and endless—she collapsed onto a bench, burying her face in her arms. Her sobs came like those of a child, not a goddess.

We sank deeper. The glass of the portholes grew warm under the pressure of the water. We were far beneath the surface of Mars. Then, abruptly, our descent stopped. The propellers whirred to life, pushing us forward through a narrow, rocky tunnel. Light from our ports and a powerful searchlight at the bow revealed jagged walls, like the throat of some ancient beast.

Minutes passed. The engines fell silent. We halted—and then began to rise.

Soon, light flooded in from above. When the hatch opened, Xodar entered with his men.

“Come,” he said.

We stepped out into a subterranean vault. In its center lay our submarine, floating calmly, only its black back visible above the water. Around the pool ran a stone ledge, and beyond it, the cave walls rose sharply, forming an arch overhead. Along the edge, dim doorways led into shadowed corridors.

Our captors guided us to one of these passages, leading us to a steel cage suspended in a shaft that stretched upward into darkness. It was an elevator, operated by magnetic force at the top—familiar enough from other parts of Barsoom. As soon as the doors closed, we shot upward at dizzying speed, slowed only by the weak gravity of Mars.

We emerged into a scene so breathtaking it stole my breath.

It was a garden of impossible beauty. Scarlet lawns stretched beneath ivory-trunked trees heavy with purple blooms. Paths wound between crushed rubies, emeralds, turquoise, and even diamonds. At the center stood a temple of burnished gold, carved with intricate patterns that shimmered in the light. But no Earth tongue could capture the true colors—hues unknown, radiations unseen, jewels that glowed with inner fire.

Even I, raised in the opulence of a Jeddak’s court, felt awed.

Phaidor stared, wide-eyed. “The Temple of Issus,” she whispered.

Xodar watched us with a grim smile—half amusement, half triumph.

The gardens teemed with black men and women in rich garments, attended by red and white slaves. This was their world—the First Born, the sacred race who had taken the princesses and gods of others to serve as their own.

We walked toward the temple, passing armed guards at the entrance. Xodar spoke briefly to an officer, who entered with him. After a time, they returned.

“Issus wishes to see the daughter of Matai Shang,” the officer announced, “and the strange being from another world—once Prince of Helium.”

We were led through endless halls of dazzling design—corridors lined with gemstone mosaics, chambers adorned with celestial carvings. Finally, we reached a vast chamber at the heart of the temple.

An officer approached a massive door at the far end. He signaled, and it opened. Another noble emerged. We were then instructed to kneel, backs turned to the room.

“Do not turn your heads,” the guard warned. “Or you die.”

We crawled forward on hands and knees—two hundred feet, it seemed—until we were halted.

“Let them rise,” came a thin, wavering voice behind us. Though frail, it carried the weight of command.

We stood—but still faced away.

“The woman pleases me,” the voice said after a pause. “She shall serve me for her allotted time. The man—return him to the Isle of Shador, along the northern shore of the Sea of Omean.”

Then: “Let the woman turn and look upon Issus—knowing that those of the lower orders who gaze upon her radiant face live only one year.”

I watched Phaidor from the corner of my eye. Her skin turned ashen. Slowly, almost against her will, she turned.

She stood close to me—close enough that her bare arm brushed mine. I felt the tremor run through her.

What could be so terrifying, I wondered, to move such a goddess?

“It must be divine splendor indeed,” I thought, “to shake one so beautiful.”

“Let the woman remain,” Issus commanded. “Remove the man. Go.”

A hand gripped my shoulder. I knelt again, crawling backward from the presence.

It was my first meeting with a deity. And I must admit—my greatest impression was how absurd I looked, dragging myself across the floor on my elbows.

Once outside, the doors shut behind us. I rose.

Xodar joined me.

“You spared my life,” he said after a silence. “When you could have killed me. If I could, I’d help you. I can make your life here less unbearable. But your fate is sealed. You will never return to your world.”

“What is my fate?” I asked.

“It depends on Issus. As long as she does not summon you, and reveal herself to you… you may live for years. In relative peace. I can arrange that.”

“And if she calls me?”

“She often uses men of the lower orders for sport. A fighter like you—perfect for the monthly rites. Men fight men. Men fight beasts. For Issus’ pleasure. And to feed her.”

“She eats human flesh?” I asked—calmly. For I had already learned of the Holy Therns, and knew that nothing in this place would surprise me.

“She eats only the finest—Holy Therns, red Barsoomians. The rest—well, they go to our tables. Animals are eaten by slaves. And she has… other dainties.”

I didn’t understand then. I thought the horror had reached its peak.

But I had much to learn.

We were near the gardens when an officer caught up to us.

“Issus wishes to see the man again,” he said. “The girl tells her he is wondrously beautiful—and fought seven of the First Born alone. Bare-handed, he bound Dator Xodar with his own harness.”

Xodar stiffened.

He did not like the idea of Issus knowing of his defeat.

We followed the officer back to the chamber.

Again, we knelt. Again, we crawled.

Again, Issus bid me rise.

Silence. Then the same voice, droning:

“Let the man turn and look upon Issus—knowing that those of the lower orders who gaze upon her radiant face survive but one year.”

I turned.

And what I saw made my blood freeze.

Between me and the dais stood a phalanx of armed guards. On the throne—a black woman, ancient, grotesque. No hair. No teeth save two yellow fangs. Sunken eyes burning from hollow sockets. Skin cracked like dried leather. Emaciated limbs. A body warped by age and decay.

This was the “radiant vision” of Issus, Goddess of Life Eternal.

Around her stood slaves—among them, Phaidor, pale and trembling.

“This is the man who slew seven of the First Born—and bound Xodar with his bare hands?” Issus squeaked.

“Yes, most glorious one,” replied the officer. “He is.”

“Bring Xodar.”

He was dragged in.

Issus stared at him, her eyes blazing.

“And *you*—a Dator of the Immortal Race?” she shrieked. “For bringing disgrace upon us—you shall be stripped of rank. From this day, you are slave of slaves. Fetch and carry for the garden servants. Remove his harness. Cowards wear no trappings.”

Xodar stood rigid. His frame did not tremble. A soldier tore off his armor.

“Begone!” she screamed. “Begone! But instead of serving in the gardens—serve this slave who conquered you in prison. Take him away! Out of my sight!”

With head high, Xodar left.

Issus rose, turned, and departed through a side exit.

To me, she said: “You will return to Shador for now. Later, Issus will test your strength. Go.”

And she vanished.

Only Phaidor remained.

As I started to follow my guard, she ran after me.

“Oh, don’t leave me here!” she begged. “Forgive me, my Prince. I didn’t mean the things I said. Take me with you. Let me share your imprisonment. Please—let us live together. We’ve both seen Issus. We’ll die in a year. Let us spend it together—whatever joy remains.”

Her words tumbled out, frantic, pleading.

“If it was hard for you to understand me,” I said, “can you not understand that it may be just as hard for me to grasp your ways? I do not wish to hurt you. I honor the gift you gave me. But what you ask… cannot be.”

“I am not dead,” I continued. “While I live, my heart belongs to one woman—Dejah Thoris, Princess of Helium. When I die, my heart will stop. What comes after—that I know not. But I am no more wise than Matai Shang, nor Issus herself.”

She stared at me, silent. No anger now—only sorrow.

“I do not understand,” she whispered.

Then she turned and walked slowly toward the door Issus had used.

In moments, she was gone.

And I was left standing in the silence, wondering what fate awaited me on the Isle of Shador.

The guard led me through the outer gardens of Issus’s domain, where I found Xodar surrounded by a jeering throng of noble blacks. They spat at him, slapped his face, and hurled insults like stones. Their voices rose in mockery, their hatred plain in every gesture. When I stepped into view, their attention shifted—curiosity now replacing contempt.

“Ah!” one shouted. “So this is the creature who defeated the mighty Xodar with bare hands. Let us see how it was done!”

A woman with dark eyes and sharp features laughed. “Let him bind Thurid,” she suggested. “Thurid is a noble Dator. Let him show the dog what it means to face a true man.”

“Thurid! Thurid!” the crowd echoed, calling his name as though he were a champion.

Then came the sound of heavy footsteps. Turning, I saw a towering black warrior approach—Thurid, adorned in gleaming ornaments and armed with pride. He moved with the confidence of power.

“What now?” he barked. “What do you want from Thurid?”

A dozen voices explained the challenge. Thurid turned to Xodar, his eyes narrowing to slits of venom. “Calot,” he hissed. “I always thought your heart was that of a sorak—a cowardly beast. You’ve beaten me in secret councils before, but here, in the field of battle, where men are truly tested, your weakness has been laid bare for all to see. Calot, I spurn you!” And with that, he raised his foot to kick Xodar.

My blood boiled.

For minutes, I had watched these once-proud warriors abuse a fallen comrade—not because he failed in battle, but because he fell out of favor with Issus. I didn’t love Xodar, but I could not stand by while justice was trampled underfoot. Cowardice and cruelty ignite something deep within me—anger that blurs vision, clouds reason, and drives action without thought.

I stood beside Xodar as Thurid swung his leg. The former Dator remained still—calm, dignified, accepting whatever they threw at him. But I did not. My fist shot out, striking Thurid hard on the shinbone. He stumbled, cursing, and I seized the moment.

Before he could recover, he lunged for my throat—just as Xodar had done aboard the cruiser. I ducked beneath his arms, then drove a crushing right into the side of his jaw. His head snapped sideways. He spun like a top, knees buckling, and collapsed at my feet.

Silence followed—then shock.

The nobles stared at the fallen Dator, then at me, as if I had just defied the laws of nature. One of them whispered, “You asked him to bind Thurid… behold!”

I knelt beside the unconscious warrior, tore off his harness, and bound his limbs tightly. “As you have treated Xodar,” I said, rising, “now treat Thurid. Take him before Issus, chained in his own gear, so she may see there is one among you greater than the First Born.”

“Who are you?” the woman asked, her voice trembling.

“I am John Carter of Virginia,” I replied. “Prince of Tardos Mors, Jeddak of Helium. A citizen of two worlds. Bring this man to Issus—and tell her that as I have humbled Xodar and Thurid, so too can I humble any of her champions. With bare hands, sword, or blade—I challenge the finest fighters of her realm.”

The guard who had escorted me stepped forward. “Come,” he said. “My orders are clear—no delay. Xodar, follow.”

His tone held no scorn. In fact, it carried a new respect. He had seen me defeat a Dator with ease, and though I was a slave, he walked behind me now, short-sword drawn, as if protecting me rather than guarding me.

The return journey to the Sea of Omean passed quietly. We descended the same shaft we had risen by, entered the submarine, and plunged deep beneath the surface world. Through tunnels and chambers, we finally emerged near the hidden pool that linked Omean to the Temple of Issus. From there, a small cruiser carried us across the dark waters to the distant Isle of Shador.

There stood a stone prison, guarded by half a dozen blacks. No ceremony marked our imprisonment. One guard unlocked the door with a massive key. We entered. The door closed. The lock clicked shut.

And with that sound, the same cold dread returned—the same despair I’d felt in the Chamber of Mystery beneath the Holy Therns. Then, Tars Tarkas had been with me. Now, I was utterly alone.

I thought of him—of Thuvia, of the great Thark, of their fate. Had they escaped? Could they find me? Even if they knew where I was, how could they reach this buried sea, beneath the watchful eye of Issus’s navy?

No. Hope was gone. Yet I refused to surrender to it. I stood up, shook off the weight of despair, and began to explore my cell.

Xodar sat on a low stone bench, head bowed, silent since his fall from grace. The prison was open to the sky above, its walls thirty feet high, barred windows halfway up. Thick partitions divided the space into separate chambers. Only one other prisoner lived here—a young red Martian boy, sleeping peacefully on a stone slab.

I leaned over him. There was something familiar about his face—something hauntingly familiar—but I couldn’t place it. His features were delicate, his body graceful, his skin unusually pale for a red man. Still, he was clearly of Barsoom’s noble race.

I didn’t wake him. Sleep in prison is sacred. I’d seen men turn savage when robbed of it.

Returning to my cell, I found Xodar unchanged—still lost in silence.

“Man,” I said, “you gain nothing by sitting here like a broken statue. It’s no shame to be bested by me. You saw me take down Thurid. You saw me kill three of your guards on the deck of the cruiser.”

“I wish you had killed me then,” he muttered.

“Come now,” I said. “We’re not dead. We’re strong. Why not fight for freedom?”

He looked at me, stunned. “You don’t understand,” he whispered. “Issus is omnipotent. She hears every word. She knows every thought. To dream of defiance is sacrilege.”

“Sacrilege?” I scoffed. “Rot, Xodar.”

He leapt to his feet. “Her curse will strike you down! You’ll writhe in agony and die!”

“Do you really believe that?” I asked.

“Of course! Who would dare doubt?”

“I do,” I said. “And I deny it. You say she reads your thoughts. But the red men have long known how to shield their minds. I learned that secret years ago. And another—none can read mine. Not even the most powerful mind on Barsoom. So if she could read my thoughts, she’d have heard me think she was the most hideous creature I’d ever seen.”

He froze. “What?” he whispered.

“I mean it,” I said. “When she ordered me to gaze upon her holy visage, I looked—and saw only ugliness. A grotesque face, a swollen belly—nothing divine. To call such a being ‘Goddess of Life Eternal,’ ‘Mother of the Moon’—it’s madness.”

He stared at me, horrified. Then he lunged.

But I didn’t strike back. I caught his wrist, twisted his arm, and used my elbow to drive him backward across my thigh. He hung there, helpless, glaring up in rage.

“Xodar,” I said gently, “let us be friends. We may be trapped together for months. I didn’t mean to offend you—but I can’t believe a god who tortures her followers, who demands blind worship, who kills for a glance.”

“You speak blasphemy,” he breathed.

“And yet,” I continued, “she hasn’t struck me dead. Nor has she rescued you. That tells me she’s not divine. She’s mortal. Old. Weak. And once we escape her clutches, she cannot harm us.”

I helped him to his feet. He didn’t attack again. He walked to the bench and sat, staring into space.

Later, I heard soft footsteps. A young red man stood in the doorway, watching us.

“Kaor,” I said, greeting him in the manner of the red Martians.

“Kaor,” he replied.

“What are you doing here?”

“I await death,” he said, smiling bravely. “I looked upon Issus nearly a year ago. I should have dropped dead. But I didn’t. Instead, I saw her—her face, her figure. By my first ancestor, never has such a grotesque being existed. And they call her Goddess of Life, Mother of the Moon? Ridiculous.”

“How did you come to be imprisoned?”

“I was flying a scout southward, seeking the Lost Sea of Korus. My propeller jammed. I landed to repair it. Before I knew it, the sky was black with fliers. A hundred First Born leaped from their ships. They charged with swords drawn. But I fought. I gave them a taste of my father’s blade. I fought well.”

“Your father is dead?”

“He died before I was born. But I’ve been happy. My only sorrow is that my mother must mourn me—as she has mourned my father for ten years.”

“Who was your father?” I asked.

He opened his mouth—but then the outer door creaked open. A burly guard entered.

“Back to your quarters,” he ordered the youth, locking the door behind him.

“To you,” the guard said to me, “this cowardly slave shall serve. If he fails, beat him. Issus commands that you heap upon him every indignity you can imagine.”

He left.

Xodar still sat with his face in his hands.

I placed a hand on his shoulder. “You heard her command,” I said. “But you don’t need to fear me. I won’t abuse you. You’re a brave man. If you choose to suffer, that’s your choice. But I would fight.”

He looked up. “I’ve been thinking,” he said. “About what you said. About Issus. I’ve pieced together things I once dared not think. I believe now—she is not divine. She is a fraud. Like the Holy Therns, like the First Born—all of them use religion to control us. Lies built for power. I’m ready to break free. But what good will it do? They are strong. We are trapped.”

“I’ve escaped worse,” I said. “And while I live, I’ll never give up hope of escaping this island, this sea.”

“But we can’t get past these walls,” he insisted, pounding the rock. “Look at this surface—smooth, polished. Nothing to grip. Impossible to climb.”

I smiled. “That’s not the hardest part. I’ll scale it—if you help me. Use your knowledge of their customs. Tell me when it’s safest. Guide me to the shaft that leads from this sea to the sky above.”

“Nighttime,” he said. “It’s the only chance. Then the guards sleep. On the battleships, only a few watch. On smaller vessels, none at all. The light remains constant—it’s always day here, because sunlight never reaches us. The glow comes from the rocks above. But at night, above ground, people sleep. Here, it’s always the same.”

“It makes escape harder,” I admitted. “But what’s the joy in an easy victory?”

“We’ll sleep on it,” Xodar said. “Perhaps a plan will come with morning.”

So we lay down on the cold stone floor and slept—the sleep of men worn out by battle, betrayal, and the weight of a world that seemed bent on crushing them.

The dawn light crept through the narrow bars of our cell as Xodar and I began to lay the groundwork for escape. First, I made him sketch a map of the southern polar regions on the stone floor—using only a buckle from my harness and the sharp edge of the gem I’d taken from Sator Throg. Though crude, it was enough to give me a sense of direction: Helium lay far to the north, beyond the vast wastes, and Omean loomed close at hand, its hidden entrance near the shore.

Then came a second map—this one of Omean itself, with Shador clearly marked, along with the breach in the dome that led to freedom. I studied both until every line was burned into memory.

From Xodar, I learned the rhythms of the guards—their shifts, their patrols. At night, only one man walked the perimeter, pacing slowly around the prison, a hundred feet out, his movements sluggish, each circuit taking nearly ten minutes. That meant five whole minutes when each side of the building stood unwatched.

“This knowledge,” Xodar said, “will help us once we’re free—but none of this matters unless we can get out first.”

“We will,” I told him, smiling. “Leave that to me.”

“When?” he asked.

“The first night a ship is moored near Shador,” I replied.

“But how will you know?” he countered. “The windows are too high.”

“Not so,” I said. “Look!”

I leapt to the opposite window, scanning the harbor beyond. Several small vessels bobbed close to shore, and two large battleships sat anchored just yards away.

*Tonight,* I thought, about to speak—when suddenly the door swung open.

A guard stepped inside. If he saw me at the window, our chances would vanish. Martian law was strict; they’d chain me if they suspected my Earth-born strength could let me leap over walls or scale bars.

He stood facing the center of the room, his back to me. Above me, five feet up, ran the top of a partition wall separating our cell from the next. That was my only hope.

If he turned, I was done. And I couldn’t drop—I’d land directly on him.

“Where is the white man?” the guard bellowed at Xodar. “Issus demands his presence.” He began to turn.

I scrambled up the iron grate, found purchase on the sill, then launched myself onto the partition’s ledge. The metal scraped against stone as I slid over.

“What was that?” the guard roared.

I dropped silently into the adjacent cell.

“Where is the white slave?” he called again.

“I do not know,” Xodar answered. “He was here when you entered. I am not his keeper—go look elsewhere.”

The guard muttered something unintelligible, then unlocked another cell door. I waited, listening—then heard it shut behind him. I sprang back across the partition and landed beside Xodar.

“You see now how we’ll escape?” I whispered.

“I see *you* might,” he said, “but I cannot jump like you. How will I cross these walls?”

We heard the guard moving between cells. Then, after a while, he returned to ours. His eyes locked on me—and widened in shock.

“By the shell of my first ancestor!” he thundered. “Where have you been?”

“I’ve been here since yesterday,” I said calmly. “You put me in this cell. I was here when you entered. Perhaps you should check your eyes.”

He stared, half furious, half relieved.

“Come,” he said. “Issus commands your presence.”

He led me outside, leaving Xodar behind. We joined other guards and the red Martian youth from another cell on Shador. The journey to the Temple of Issus repeated itself. The guards kept us apart—no chance to talk.

But the boy’s face haunted me. Where had I seen him? There was something deeply familiar in his bearing, his voice, the way he moved. I felt certain I knew him—even though I knew I hadn’t met him before.

At the gardens of Issus, we were not led to the temple, but toward a massive wall rising a hundred feet into the sky. Through an arched gate, we entered a wide plain surrounded by lush forests. Crowds of blacks strolled forward, mingling with plant men and great white apes—treated like pets, pushed aside with swords or rough hands, cowering in fear.

Our destination: a vast amphitheatre at the edge of the plain, half a mile beyond the garden walls. Through a smaller entrance, we passed beneath the seating into a barred enclosure where prisoners huddled under guard—some in chains, others merely terrified.

Now, at last, I could speak to the red youth.

“What is this gathering?” I asked. “Are we to fight for entertainment—or something worse?”

“It is part of the monthly rites of Issus,” he said. “Blacks cleanse their sins in the blood of outsiders. If a black dies, it means disloyalty—a sin without pardon. If he survives, he is cleared of the charge. The fights vary—we may face beasts, warriors, or be matched against numbers of blacks.”

“And if we win?” I asked.

“Freedom?” he laughed. “No. Only death offers true freedom. No one leaves the domains of the First Born. If we fight well, we live longer. If not…” He shrugged. “We die in the arena.”

“And you’ve fought often?”

“Many times. A hundred black devils I’ve slain in nearly a year. My mother would be proud—she knows not how I honor my father’s name.”

“My father was—”

“Come, calots!” a guard shouted. “To the slaughter!”

We were shoved toward the incline leading down to the arena below.

Like all Barsoom arenas, this one was carved into the earth. Only the highest seats rose above ground. The arena itself sank deep below. Just beneath the lowest tier, barred cages lined the pit’s edge. Into one of these we were herded—though the youth was placed elsewhere.

Directly across from me sat the throne of Issus. She lounged there, surrounded by a hundred slave maidens adorned in jewels. Rich cloths covered her dais. Three ranks of armed soldiers stood stiffly before her, elbows touching. Behind them, nobles glittered in gold and gems, their foreheads marked with rank.

On both sides of the throne, the stands teemed with people—men and women alike, dressed in elaborate harnesses. Each black had slaves—drawn from conquered lands, or the outer world. The First Born were noble by birth. Even the lowliest soldier was a god. They did no labor. Men fought—it was sacred duty. Women did nothing. Slaves bathed them, dressed them, fed them. Some even had slaves who spoke for them. One woman sat blindfolded, her slave narrating the battle.

The first event: the Tribute to Issus. Ten young women—beauties from Jeddak courts and thern temples—had served Issus for a year. Now, they would pay the price of divine favor with their lives.

A huge black entered the arena with them. He inspected each, prodding ribs, testing limbs. Finally, he chose one and led her before Issus. He spoke—words I couldn’t hear. Issus nodded. The black raised his arms in salute, seized her wrist, and dragged her through a small door beneath the throne.

“Issus will dine well tonight,” a prisoner muttered.

“What do you mean?” I asked.

“That was her dinner,” he said. “Did you not see how carefully he picked the plumpest?”

I cursed the monster on the throne.

“Do not rage,” my companion said. “You’ll see worse—if you live a month among the First Born.”

I turned in time to see a cage door swing open. Three monstrous white apes burst into the arena. The girls huddled together, trembling. One fell to her knees, pleading to Issus.

But Issus leaned forward, eager for the spectacle.

The apes charged.

A wave of fury surged through me. The cruelty of this goddess—this mindless tormentor—ignited every fiber of my being. Red haze swam before my eyes.

The guard stood idle before my cage. Why bar it? These women were already doomed.

One blow sent him unconscious. I snatched his long-sword and leapt into the arena.

The apes were upon the girls. In two mighty bounds, I reached the center.

Silence fell—then erupted into wild cheers from the cages.

My sword whirled. One ape collapsed, headless, at the girls’ feet.

The others turned on me. From the corner of my eye, I saw guards rushing across the sand. Then a figure broke from a nearby cage—the red youth.

He paused, sword raised.

“Men of the outer world!” he shouted. “Let us make our deaths meaningful! Join this warrior! Turn today’s tribute into revenge that will echo through ages! The racks hold blades—take them!”

He bounded toward me. From every red-held cage, a roar went up. Guards fell beneath surging mobs. Cages emptied. Prisoners rushed for the weapon racks, stripped them bare, and stormed the arena.

The apes had fallen before the guards reached me. The youth raced past, striking from behind. I charged from the front.

We fought like demons—me sidestepping thrusts, driving my blade into hearts and throats. The youth moved with impossible speed—leaps and bounds like those of my own Earth-born strength.

His sword flashed beside me. A grim smile played on his lips. He taunted enemies. He fought with the same fierce precision I knew so well.

It was that resemblance—vague, yet undeniable—that stirred something deep within me. Respect. Pride. Affection.

We were fighting hard when Issus’ personal guards stormed the arena. They came in waves, roaring. Prisoners swarmed to meet them.

For half an hour, chaos reigned. The arena became a whirlwind of steel, screams, and blood. The youth fought beside me, relentless.

Slowly, I formed the prisoners into a loose circle around the girls. I ordered fifty to fall back and protect them.

“Stay until I return,” I commanded.

Then, turning to the rest, I roared, “Down with Issus! To the throne! We take vengeance where it belongs!”

The youth echoed the cry. “To the throne!”

As one, we surged forward—over corpses, through dying foes—toward the throne.

Hordes of First Born fighters poured from the audience to stop us. We cut them down like paper.

“Some of you to the seats!” I yelled. “Ten of us can reach the throne.”

Prisoners broke left and right, vaulting the wall into the stands. The amphitheatre erupted in shrieks and clashes.

Side by side, the youth and I fought to the foot of the throne. Guards and nobles closed in, shielding Issus, who now screamed orders and curses.

Her slaves trembled—unsure whether to pray or flee. Some snatched swords from the dead and attacked—only to be cut down. Martyrs to a lost cause.

We fought shoulder to shoulder—like Tars Tarkas and I once had. No mercy. No retreat.

Man by man, they fell. But more came.

Then—cries from the stands: “Rise, slaves!” “Rise, slaves!”

The female slaves rose—armed with daggers, swords, ornaments, teeth, and fists. They attacked their masters. A dagger, stained crimson, waved high. A sword drawn from a corpse. A heavy bracelet used as a club.

It was terrifying—and glorious.

But we were still fighting.

Only one thin line of men remained between us and Issus. Her face was blue with terror. Foam clung to her lips. She could not move.

Only the youth and I remained standing. The others had fallen.

I was struck—my sword jammed in the chest of a Dator. I pulled free, grabbed his blade—just as a hand gripped the arm of my attacker. The youth lunged, stabbing him.

Then I saw who had saved me.

Phaidor—daughter of Matai Shang.

“Fly, my Prince!” she cried. “It’s over. All in the arena are dead. Only you and this youth remain. Your men in the stands are falling. The women—few survive. For each of you, ten thousand blacks await. Break for the sea of Korus. Use your strength. Reach the Golden Cliffs. Tell Matai Shang what happened. He will shelter you. Together, you may save me. Fly—while there’s still a chance!”

But that was not my purpose. And I would not trade one tyranny for another.

I looked at the throne. At Issus. At the blood-stained sand.

This was not flight.

This was war.

For a heartbeat, I stood frozen as the mob surged forward. The first wave shoved me back—two steps, then my foot caught nothing but emptiness. I had stepped into the same pit that had swallowed Issus. For a moment, I teetered on the edge, heart pounding, breath gone. Then, with the boy still clutched tightly in my arms, we plunged backward into the abyss.

We struck a smooth, polished chute, and above us, the opening sealed shut as if by magic. We tumbled down unharmed, landing in a dimly lit chamber far beneath the arena. As I pushed myself up, the first thing I saw was Issus—her face twisted in fury—staring at me through the iron bars of a grated door on the side wall.

“Rash mortal!” she hissed. “You shall pay for your blasphemy in this cursed cell. Here you will rot alone, in darkness, beside the festering corpse of your accomplice—until madness and hunger drive you to devour the maggots that once were a man.”

Her voice died. In the next instant, she vanished, and the weak light faded into utter blackness.

“Pleasant old lady,” came a quiet voice beside me.

I turned sharply. “Who speaks?”

“It’s me—your companion. Proud to have fought beside the greatest warrior ever to wear armor on Barsoom.”

“I’m glad you’re alive,” I said. “That wound on your head… I feared it might be fatal.”

“A mere scratch,” he replied. “Only stunned me.”

“Maybe it would’ve been better if it had killed you,” I muttered. “Now we’re trapped—starving, thirsty, and probably doomed.”

“Where are we?” he asked.

“Beneath the arena,” I answered. “We fell through the same shaft that took Issus when she was within our grasp.”

He let out a low chuckle—relief mixed with amusement. Then, reaching out into the dark, he pulled me close and whispered into my ear.

“This is perfect,” he murmured. “There are secrets beneath the secrets of Issus—even she doesn’t know them.”

“What do you mean?”

“I worked with the slaves last year, helping rebuild these underground tunnels. At that time, we uncovered an ancient network of corridors and chambers long buried. The black overseers explored them, taking some of us along. I know every turn, every passage.”

“There are miles of tunnels under the gardens and temple,” he continued. “One path leads down to the lower levels—directly to the water shaft that connects to Omean. If we can reach the submarine unnoticed, we might escape into the sea. There are islands where the blacks never go. We could survive there—for a while. And who knows what might happen next?”

He spoke softly, wary even here of hidden ears. So I answered in the same hushed tone.

“Lead us back to Shador,” I whispered. “Xodar is there. We planned to escape together. I can’t abandon him.”

“No one should desert a friend,” he said. “Better to be caught than leave him behind.”

Then he began feeling his way across the floor, searching for the trapdoor leading to the tunnels below. After a while, he called to me with a soft, “S-s-t.” I crept toward the sound and found him kneeling at the edge of an opening.

“There’s a ten-foot drop,” he whispered. “Hang from your hands—then release. You’ll land safely on sand.”

I lowered myself into the void. The darkness was absolute—no hint of light, no shape, no depth. It was total night, deeper than any cave I’d ever known. For a moment, I hung suspended, paralyzed by the fear of falling into nothingness. Even though he’d told me it was only ten feet, the sensation was like hanging over an endless chasm.

Then I let go.

Four feet down—I landed softly on cushioned sand. The boy followed.

“Climb onto my shoulders,” he said. “I’ll replace the trap.”

Once done, he took my hand and led me forward, moving slowly, cautiously, testing each step. We descended a steep incline.

“Soon,” he said, “we’ll see light. At the lower levels, the same phosphorescent rock that lights Omean glows here.”

Never would I forget that journey through the pits of Issus. Though no danger threatened, the air thrummed with silent adventure. The darkness hid much—but my mind painted grander things: visions of ancient peoples, forgotten civilizations, their wars and rituals, their desperate struggle against the green hordes rising from the drowned seas. They had fled higher and higher, building temples and fortresses atop the world’s highest peaks, shielding themselves behind walls of myth and fear.

Once, Barsoom had held three great races: the blacks, the whites, and the yellows. The green men were savages of the deep waters, but as the seas shrank and resources dwindled, the stronger races clashed—until the greens, now swarming from the sinking lands, overwhelmed the others. The survivors mingled—blacks, whites, yellows—and from their union arose the red men of today.

I had always believed the old races were extinct. But in just four days, I’d seen thousands of whites and blacks. Could remnants of the yellow race still exist somewhere on Mars?

My thoughts were cut short by the boy’s voice.

“At last—the light!” he breathed.

Ahead, a faint glow shimmered in the distance. As we drew closer, it brightened until we emerged into well-lit corridors. Our pace quickened. Soon, we reached the end of a tunnel—opening directly onto the ledge surrounding the submarine pool.

The vessel lay moored, its hatch open. The boy raised a finger to his lips, then tapped his sword—warning me to be silent. We crept forward, dropped silently onto the deck, and crawled on hands and knees toward the hatch.

A glance below revealed no guard. With catlike stealth, we slipped into the cabin.

No one stirred.

I quickly secured the hatch. The boy moved to the pilot house, pressed a button—and the submarine sank into swirling water, descending into the depths.

Still, no alarm. No shouts. No rush of feet.

I searched every cabin—empty. Not a soul aboard.

It felt almost too good to be true.

When I returned to the pilot house, the boy handed me a sheet of paper.

“This might explain why the crew is gone,” he said.

It was a radio message:

> *The slaves have risen. Come with all men you can gather. Too late to reach Omean. They are massacring everyone in the amphitheatre. Issus is under threat. Haste.*

“Zithad is Dator of Issus’s guards,” the boy explained. “We gave them quite a scare—one they won't soon forget.”

“Let’s hope it’s the beginning of the end,” I said.

“Only our ancestors know,” he replied.

We reached the submarine pool in Omean without incident. Now we faced a new problem: how to get past the guards.

I had an idea.

“What’s the name of the officer in charge?” I asked.

“Torith,” he said. “He was on duty this morning.”

“And the submarine commander?”

“Yersted.”

I found a blank dispatch form and wrote:

> *Dator Torith: Return these two slaves at once to Shador.*

“Simple enough,” I said, handing it to him with a smile. “Let’s see how well it works.”

“But our swords!” he protested. “How do we explain those?”

“We can’t explain them,” I said. “So we leave them behind.”

“You’re putting us back in the hands of the First Born—unarmed!”

“It’s the only way,” I insisted. “I’ll find a way out of Shador. Once free, we’ll arm ourselves again. This world is full of warriors.”

“As you say,” he replied, smiling. “I’d follow no other leader with more confidence. Let’s test your plan.”

We stepped out of the submarine, left our swords behind, and walked boldly toward the guard post.

At sight of us, the soldiers rushed forward, rifles raised.

I held out the message. One guard took it, read it, then handed it to Torith, who was stepping out of his office.

Torth studied it, then looked at us suspiciously.

“Where is Dator Yersted?” he demanded.

My heart sank. I’d forgotten to sink the sub.

“He ordered an immediate return to the temple landing,” I said.

Torth hesitated—then turned toward the pool, as if to verify. For one terrible second, I thought he’d see the empty craft and expose us. But he didn’t. Perhaps the order seemed plausible. Or perhaps he simply couldn’t believe two slaves would surrender so willingly.

“The uprising—were you involved?” he asked.

“All were involved,” I said. “But it failed. The guards crushed most of us.”

He nodded. “Take them to Shador.”

We boarded a small boat and soon arrived at the island. There, I was reunited with Xodar. The boy was taken to his own cell.

And behind locked doors, we were prisoners once more—of the First Born.

Xodar listened in stunned silence as I recounted the events of the arena at the rites of Issus. His disbelief was palpable, even though he had already questioned her divinity. Yet to hear that someone could stand before her with a sword drawn and escape unscathed—without being torn apart by divine fury—struck him as impossible.

“It’s the final proof,” he said at last, his voice low and heavy. “No more is needed to shatter every shred of my belief in Issus. She is not a goddess. She is merely an old woman, wielding immense power through deception—through lies that have kept her people, and all of Barsoom, in ignorance for ages.”

“She may be mortal,” I replied, “but she still commands this place. That means we must leave the moment we can.”

“I hope you find such a moment,” Xodar said with a dry laugh. “For I’ve never seen one where a prisoner of the First Born escaped.”

“Tonight will do,” I told him.

“The night falls soon,” he said. “How can I help?”

“Can you swim?” I asked.

“No slimy silian of Korus’ depths knows water better than I,” he answered proudly.

“Good,” I said. “The red man likely cannot. There’s scarcely enough water in their world to float a boat. So one of us must carry him across the sea to our ship. I’d hoped to go beneath the surface, but I doubt he could endure it. Even the bravest among them fear deep water—none of their ancestors have seen a river or lake in generations.”

“The red man comes with us?” Xodar asked.

“Yes.”

“It’s wise. Three swords are better than two. Especially when the third belongs to one as fierce as this youth. I’ve seen him fight in the arena many times. Never before have I seen anyone who seemed unbeatable—until I saw you. You two move alike—same grim smile, same contempt in your eyes, same rhythm in your strikes. It’s uncanny. There’s a resemblance—almost like father and son.”

“Perhaps,” I said. “But he is strong. Together, we’ll make a hard trio to defeat. If only Tars Tarkas were here—we could cross Barsoom from end to end, even if every warrior on the planet rose against us.”

“He will,” Xodar said. “When they learn where you come from. That’s another lie Issus has spread—the idea that you’re from beyond the stars. The Holy Therns serve as her puppets, just as ignorant of her true nature as the people of Barsoom are of the outer world. Her decrees arrive written in blood on strange parchment. The fools believe they’re divine messages, delivered by supernatural means. They find them on guarded altars—no one could enter without being seen. For years, I’ve carried those messages myself. There’s a secret tunnel from Issus’ temple to Matai Shang’s. Dug long ago by slaves, so hidden no thern ever knew it existed.”

“The therns have temples across the civilized world,” he continued. “Priests unseen preach of the Mysterious River Iss, the Valley Dor, the Lost Sea of Korus—urging the people to take the pilgrimage. It brings wealth to the therns and new slaves to their ranks.”

“So they collect the labor and riches the First Born need,” I said.

“Exactly. Occasionally, the First Born raid directly. Then they take royal women, capture battleships, steal artisans—so they can copy what they cannot build themselves.”

“We are a race that does nothing,” Xodar said. “We pride ourselves on idleness. To work is forbidden. That is the role of others—to live so we may enjoy luxury. Fighting is all we do. Without it, we’d outnumber all life on Barsoom. As far as I know, none of us die naturally. Our women live forever—until we grow tired of them. Only Issus is immune to death. She has lived for countless centuries.”

“And would the others live forever too—if not for the pilgrimage?” I asked.

“I now believe they are the same species as we. And I hope to fight for them one day—to atone for the sins I’ve committed under false teachings.”

As he finished, a haunting call echoed across the waters of Omean. I recognized it—I’d heard it the night before. It marked the end of the day, when the men of Omean laid out their silks and fell into dreamless sleep.

Our guard came to check on us once more. He did his duty quickly, then left, slamming the heavy door behind him. We were alone.

I waited a few moments—long enough for him to return to his quarters—then climbed onto the grated window ledge. From there, I scanned the sea. About a quarter-mile offshore, a massive battleship loomed. Closer to shore, several cruisers and solo scouts bobbed in the dark water. Only the battleship had a watch. I saw him clearly on the upper deck, spreading his sleeping silks. Soon, he lay flat on his back, motionless. Discipline here was lax—understandable, since no one outside knew of Omean, the First Born, or this hidden sea.

I descended and spoke with Xodar, pointing out the ships.

“There’s one,” he said, “my own vessel—built for five. It’s the fastest thing on Mars. If we can reach it, we can flee.”

He described its engines, its design. In his explanation, I noticed a gear mechanism Kantos Kan had taught me during our time in Zodanga’s navy. Only Helium’s ships used that system. And suddenly I understood—this craft had been stolen from Helium. Xodar wasn’t exaggerating its speed. Nothing on Mars could match the fliers of Helium.

We decided to wait an hour—long enough for all stragglers to rest. In the meantime, I would bring the red youth to our cell.

I climbed the partition wall, balancing carefully. At the next cell, I found the boy sitting on his bench, staring up at the glowing dome above. When he saw me perched above, his eyes widened. Then a grin broke across his face—knowing, appreciative.

I leaned down, ready to drop beside him. But he held up a hand.

“Wait,” he whispered. “Catch my hand. I’m close to making it. I’ve tried every day. Soon I’d have done it.”

I lay flat on the wall, reaching down. He sprinted from the center of his cell and leapt upward. I grabbed his hand—and pulled him to the top.

“You’re the first jumper I’ve ever seen among the red men,” I said.

He smiled. “It’s not strange. I’ll tell you why later.”

Together, we returned to Xodar’s cell. We talked, made plans, and swore a solemn oath: we would fight to the death for each other. We knew that even if we escaped the First Born, the world might still rise against us—superstition is a powerful weapon.

I would navigate the ship. If we reached the outer world safely, we would head straight for Helium.

“Why Helium?” the youth asked.

“I am a prince of Helium,” I said.

He looked at me strangely, but said nothing. I wondered at the time—but the moment passed.

“Come,” I said. “Now is as good a time as any.”

I climbed the partition again, the boy beside me. I unbuckled my harness, joined the straps, and lowered them to Xodar below. He caught them and climbed up.

“How simple,” he laughed.

“The balance is simpler,” I replied.

Then I stood atop the outer wall, peering over to spot the sentry. I waited five minutes. Then he appeared—slow, deliberate, walking his beat. I watched until he turned the corner, disappearing from view.

The moment he vanished, I pulled Xodar to the top. I handed him one end of the strap. He slid down. The boy followed.

They walked slowly toward the water—about a hundred yards, past the guardhouse filled with sleeping soldiers.

After they’d taken a dozen steps, I dropped to the ground and followed. As I passed the guardhouse, I hesitated—dozens of swords lay inside. If we were to survive, we’d need them.

I glanced back. Xodar and the youth were already in the water, clinging to rings along the dock, mouths just above the surface, waiting.

The temptation was strong. I crept toward the door, pressed it open just enough to see—twelve guards sprawled on their silks, breathing deeply. At the far end, a rack held swords and firearms.

I pushed the door wider. A hinge groaned. One guard stirred. My heart stopped. I cursed myself—foolish to risk everything now.

But there was no turning back.

I sprang silently beside him, hands poised near his throat. I waited—what felt like an eternity. Then he rolled over, resumed his sleep.

I slipped between the bodies, reached the rack. I drew a sword—its scabbard scraped loudly. I froze, expecting alarms. But no one moved.

I took a second—silently. The third clanked. I braced myself for attack—but still, no one stirred. Either they were deep sleepers—or the noise was less than it seemed.

I turned to the revolvers. I could carry only one. Already burdened, I took one. Then I saw it—a window beside the rack, open, leading directly to the dock, twenty feet from the water.

I grinned. A perfect exit.

But as I stepped toward it, the opposite door opened. An officer stood there—eyes locked on mine.

We both raised our revolvers. The shots rang out together—like a single blast.

I felt the wind of his bullet brush my ear. He crumpled. I didn’t know where I hit him. Didn’t matter.

I dove through the window. Water closed over my head. Moments later, we were swimming toward the small flier, a hundred yards away.

Xodar carried the boy. I bore three swords. I’d dropped the revolver. Though we were strong swimmers, the weight slowed us. I swam beneath the surface. Xodar surfaced often to let the youth breathe. Miraculously, we weren’t seen.

We reached the boat. All aboard before the battleship’s watch, roused by the gunfire, spotted us.

An alarm gun boomed—deep, thunderous—echoing under Omean’s dome.

Instantly, thousands awoke. Decks teemed with warriors. Alarms were rare here—now chaos reigned.

We cast off before the echo faded. In seconds, we rose from the sea.

I lay flat on the deck, hands on controls. Xodar and the youth lay behind me, prone—minimizing resistance.

“Rise high,” Xodar whispered. “They won’t fire heavy guns—they’d damage their own ships. If we’re high enough, our hulls can withstand rifle fire.”

I obeyed. Below, hundreds leapt into the water, scrambling for boats. Larger vessels surged after us, but stayed on the surface.

“A little to your right!” Xodar shouted. “There are no compass points here—every direction is north.”

The noise was deafening—rifles, shouts, propellers churning.

I hadn’t dared push full speed—afraid of missing the shaft. But even so, we raced faster than any flier had ever gone.

Smaller craft began rising toward us.

“THE SHAFT! DEAD AHEAD!” Xodar yelled.

I saw it—a black maw in the glowing dome.

A ten-man cruiser rose directly ahead, blocking our path. It angled sharply, intent on grappling us.

No choice. I feigned passing over it—causing it to rise steeper. Then, as we neared, I screamed: “Hold tight!”

I threw the flier into maximum speed, deflecting the bow horizontally—straight at the cruiser’s keel.

At impact, I angled upward.

The collision shattered the cruiser. It flipped backward, crew tumbling into the sea. The ship plunged, propellers spinning, down to the ocean floor.

Our bow crumpled. We nearly flew overboard. I grabbed the rail. Xodar held on. The boy would have fallen—had I not caught his ankle.

The ship veered wildly. I regained control, nose horizontal, aiming again for the shaft.

The crash had slowed us. Now, a hundred scouts closed in.

Xodar warned: ascending by buoyancy alone would let them catch us. Faster ships lacked large tanks—so they couldn’t rise as fast.

We were doomed unless we acted.

I always believed there’s a way through obstacles. If you can’t go over, under, or around—then go *through*.

I couldn’t outrun them. But I could try something else.

“Reverse?” Xodar cried. “For your ancestor’s sake, reverse! We’re at the shaft!”

“Hold tight!” I screamed. “We’re going straight up the shaft!”

The words barely left my lips when we swept beneath the black opening.

I yanked the bow up, slammed the speed lever to full. Clutching a stanchion and the wheel, I hung on—praying.

Behind me, Xodar gasped. Then laughed. The boy laughed too—something I couldn’t hear over the wind.

I looked up—no stars. Only darkness.

Then down—below, a shrinking circle of light—the mouth of Omean, glowing with phosphorescence. I steered by it—keeping it centered. A thread of survival.

We were in the shaft for seconds—maybe forty. Our speed must have been two hundred miles per hour. Martian fliers are swift.

Then—out.

Darkness. No moons. No stars.

I blinked. Then understood. Summer at the south pole. The ice cap melted. Clouds—unknown elsewhere on Barsoom—blocked the sky.

I stared ahead. We were free.

My son! The words struck me like a thunderclap. I rose slowly, my heart pounding in my chest, and turned to face the young man before me. Now, seeing him clearly, I understood why his presence had stirred such a deep, inexplicable recognition within me. His features bore the unmistakable grace of his mother—Dejah Thoris—but shaped by strength and masculine resolve. His grey eyes, sharp and intelligent, were mine. They held not just my likeness, but something deeper—an echo of the soul.

He stood before me, hesitation warring with hope in his expression.

“Tell me of your mother,” I said, my voice thick with emotion. “Tell me everything about the years I’ve been denied—denied her, denied you.”

With a cry of joy, he rushed forward and threw his arms around my neck. In that moment, as I held him close, tears welled in my eyes—tears I would have buried had I not been so utterly overwhelmed. I felt a lump rise in my throat, the kind that makes even the strongest man seem foolish. But I did not regret it. A long life has taught me that a man may weep for love and loss, yet still stand firm in battle, in duty, in honor.

“You are taller than I imagined,” he said, stepping back, his gaze searching my face. “Your bearing, your manner, the way you wield a sword—my mother spoke of them often. But even with all this proof, I could not believe it… until you spoke. You spoke of her first. No one else would have thought of her so immediately. Only the man she loved—only *you*—would have done that.”

“For many years,” I replied, “I have carried her image in my mind. Not a day passed without her memory burning bright. Tell me—how is she?”

“She has not changed,” he said. “Some say she has grown more beautiful, though how that could be, I cannot imagine. Yet when she thinks I’m not watching… her face grows sad. She dreams of you, Father. All of Helium mourns with her. Her grandfather’s people revere her—and they revere you too. They speak of you as the savior of Barsoom, the warrior who unlocked the gate of life when all seemed lost.”

Each year, on the anniversary of that day you raced across a dying world to open that portal, they hold a great festival in your name. There is joy—yes—but also sorrow. For they know you are gone. And no one can truly share the happiness you gave them. Across all Barsoom, no name is greater than John Carter.”

“And what name did your mother give you?” I asked.

“The people of Helium wanted to name you after me,” he said. “But Mother refused. She said you and she chose a name together, and that your wish must come first. So the name she called me—the name I carry—is one made from both of ours: *Carthoris*.”

Xodar had been at the helm during our conversation. Now he called out, his voice tense.

“She’s dropping fast, John Carter,” he warned. “We were rising steeply, so it wasn’t noticeable. But now that I’m trying to level off, the bow is sinking. The wound has ruptured one of the forward ray tanks.”

I checked the damage myself. It was worse than I feared. Not only was the ship listing dangerously, forcing us into an unnatural angle, but we were losing repulsive energy rapidly. If we didn’t act soon, we’d be floating stern-up, helpless and doomed.

We had slowed slightly, lulled by the illusion of safety. Now I took the wheel again and opened the engine wide. The craft surged forward, racing north at breakneck speed. Meanwhile, Carthoris and Xodar worked frantically at the breach in the hull, trying to seal the leak—though their efforts were futile against the escaping rays.

It was still dark as we crossed the northern edge of the ice cap and emerged from the cloud cover. Below us lay a classic Martian landscape—rolling ochre plains once covered by ancient seas, low hills, silent ruins of forgotten cities, and the towering remains of mighty architecture, now home only to ghosts and the great white apes.

The ship grew harder and harder to stabilize. The bow sank lower with each passing minute. We were forced to shut down the engine to avoid plunging to the ground.

As dawn broke, casting light over the land, our vessel gave one final lurch—then tilted violently, half-turning on its side. The deck angled sharply, and we clung to rails and stanchions. When it became clear we couldn’t survive the fall, we unfastened our harnesses from the rings along the sides.

Then, in a heartbeat, the deck rose nearly vertical. We dangled in our leather straps, feet dangling a thousand yards above the cracked earth.

I was near the controls. Reaching out, I touched the lever that governed the repulsion rays. The ship responded—slowly, gently—beginning to descend toward the ground.

Half an hour passed before we landed. North of us rose a range of hills—our best chance for concealment from any pursuers. An hour later, we found shelter in the wind-scoured gullies, where wild flowering plants bloomed in the arid soil. Here, among the milk-giving shrubs—those strange plants that feed the green hordes—we found sustenance. We were starving, and this was a blessing.

Beneath a cluster of these shrubs, hidden from sight, we laid down to sleep—for me, the first rest in days. This was the fifth day since I had awoken on Barsoom, torn from my cottage on Earth to the valley of Dor, a place of beauty and horror alike. In all that time, I had slept only twice—once in the therns’ storehouse.

Mid-afternoon found me waking to a sudden warmth on my hand. Someone gripped it tightly, then covered it with kisses. I jolted awake—to see Thuvia’s face, radiant and tearful.

“My Prince!” she cried, her voice trembling with joy. “It’s you! I thought you were dead. My ancestors have blessed me—I have not lived in vain!”

Her voice roused Xodar and Carthoris. The boy looked at her in surprise, but she saw only me. She reached for my neck, ready to embrace me, but I gently pulled away.

“Come, Thuvia,” I said, calming her. “You’re overcome. You forget yourself—and you forget that I am married to the Princess of Helium.”

“I forget nothing,” she replied softly. “You’ve never spoken of love to me, nor do I expect you to. But I cannot help loving you. I would not take Dejah Thoris’s place. My greatest desire is to serve you, my Prince, as your slave. That is my honor, my dream, my joy.”

I am not one for courtship or sentiment. Yet I felt a deep discomfort, a sense of awkwardness I had never known before. Though Martian custom allows men to keep female slaves, I had always chosen male servants. I valued honor and dignity—above all, respect.

“And when I return to Helium,” I told her, “you will go with me—not as a slave, but as an honored guest. There, noble youths will vie for your hand. One of the finest will marry you. Let go of this mistaken devotion. I value your friendship, Thuvia—but nothing more.”

“You are my master,” she said simply. “It shall be as you wish.” But there was sadness in her voice.

“How did you come here?” I asked. “And where is Tars Tarkas?”

“He is likely dead,” she whispered. “A horde of green warriors attacked us. He fought bravely—but they were too many. The last I saw, they carried him bleeding into the city where they had launched their attack.”

“You’re not certain?” I asked. “And which city?”

“It lies just beyond these hills. Our airship—your noble sacrifice to let us escape—was poorly navigated. We drifted aimlessly for two days. Then we abandoned it and walked. Yesterday, we crossed these hills and found the dead city. We entered its streets, heading toward the center—when we saw a band of green warriors approaching.”

Tars Tarkas stepped forward. They saw him, but not me. He pulled me into a doorway and ordered me to hide.

“There will be no escape for me,” he said. “These are the Warhoon of the South. Once they see my metal, it will be death.”

Then he stepped out to meet them.

Ah, my Prince—what a fight! For an hour, they swarmed around him. The dead piled up like a hill. But finally, they overwhelmed him—pushing one another onto him until he could no longer move. He fell. They rolled over him like a wave. When they carried him away, I think he was already gone.

“We must confirm this,” I said. “I cannot leave Tars Tarkas in the hands of the Warhoon. Tonight, I will enter the city and find him.”

“And I will go with you,” said Carthoris.

“So will I,” added Xodar.

“No,” I replied firmly. “This requires stealth. One man alone may succeed. More would draw attention. I will go alone. If I need help, I’ll return for you.”

They protested, but obeyed. The sun was already sinking. Soon, darkness would fall.

With final instructions given, I bid them farewell and set off at a steady run toward the city.

Emerging from the hills, I saw the nearer moon streaking across the sky, casting silver light upon the ancient metropolis. The city rested on gentle foothills—once part of a vast sea. Because of this, I slipped into the streets unseen.

Green warriors rarely occupy more than a few blocks around the central plaza. And since they travel across the dead sea floors, entering from the hills is usually safe.

Once inside, I stayed in shadow. At intersections, I paused to check for patrols before darting across. I reached the edge of the inhabited area without detection.

The sounds of thoats and zitidars—grunting, squealing—filled the courtyards. These familiar noises stirred something deep within me—a sense of belonging. It reminded me of my early days in Korad, when I first wooed Dejah Thoris.

At the corner of the first square, I saw warriors emerging from buildings—heading toward a massive structure in the center of the plaza. My knowledge of green Martian customs told me this was either the chieftain’s quarters or the audience hall. Something important was happening—possibly related to Tars Tarkas’s capture.

To reach it, I had to cross a full square, a broad avenue, and part of the plaza. Around me, animals roared from every courtyard. Many warriors were present—likely several communities of the Warhoon horde.

Passing undetected was difficult. But if I was to save Tars Tarkas, I needed to press on.

I approached from the south, now standing at the corner of the avenue and the first street south of the plaza. The buildings on the south side were dark—no lights. I decided to enter through one of them.

I moved silently through the deserted structure, reaching the inner courtyard near the east wall. Inside, a herd of thoats and zitidars grazed on the ochre moss. A breeze blew from the northwest—no danger of scent.

Crawling beneath the balconies, I followed the shadows until I reached the north end of the courtyard. Above, three floors were lit. Beyond that, darkness.

I couldn’t pass through the occupied rooms. My only path was up. I climbed the wall, grasping the stone railing of the second-floor balcony. I pulled myself up.

Through open windows, I saw green Martians squatting on furs, grunting—using their telepathic minds to converse.

Then a warrior entered.

“Come, Tan Gama,” he called. “We are taking the Thark before Kab Kadja. Bring another.”

The warrior rose and left with two others.

If I could follow them, I might rescue Tars Tarkas—or at least learn where he was held.

To my right was a door leading into the building. I stepped inside—a long, dark corridor lined with apartment doors.

I hadn’t gone far when I saw the three warriors ahead. They turned right, disappearing from view. I ran after them.

At the far end was a spiral stairway. They descended. I knew from past experience that prisoners were kept underground. The Warhoon used dungeons—deep pits beneath their cities.

I followed the flicker of their torch down into the labyrinth below.

After a hundred yards, they turned right through a doorway. I hurried forward—just in time to see them removing chains from a figure bound to the wall.

Tars Tarkas.

They dragged him out quickly—too quickly. I nearly stumbled into their light. But I sprinted ahead, keeping just outside their torch’s glow.

I expected them to return the same way. But instead, they turned directly toward me.

No choice—keep moving. I ran deeper into the darkness, following the faint torchlight.

Eventually, I came to a junction—five corridors branching from a single point. I went straight, but suddenly, the light vanished behind me.

I stopped. Silence. Utter silence.

They had taken another path.

I retraced my steps, feeling my way through total blackness. After what felt like hours, I reached the junction—counting five corridors. None showed light.

Then I heard it—a shuffling sound, faint but distinct.

I turned. Behind me, in the endless dark, I saw them.

Eyes.

Not one pair—two. Then three. Glowing red, unblinking, staring from the void.

No shape. No form. Just eyes—burning, silent, watching.

My blood turned to ice.

I remembered the dungeon—where I had seen a corpse dragged away by unseen things. Where eyes had glowed in the dark, dragging the dead from my grasp.

Now, here, in these pits—these same eyes.

I gripped my sword. Slowly, I backed away.

But the eyes advanced. Still no sound—except the shuffling, like bones dragging across stone.

Then—more eyes. From the right. The left. Behind.

Trapped.

And still, they watched.

Silent.

Unseen.

Only the eyes.

And the dark.

And the slow, creeping dread of knowing I was not alone.

In the depths of Mars, something older than war, older than memory, waited in the dark.

And it had seen me.

I came to with a dull throb in my forehead and the faint glow of light creeping through the corridor. The eyes—those terrible, watchful eyes—were gone. I had been unconscious only seconds, but it was enough. My fall had left me unharmed save for a shallow bruise where my skull had struck the stone floor. I rose quickly, heart pounding, searching for the source of the illumination.

It came from a torch held by one of four green warriors advancing rapidly down the passage. They hadn’t seen me yet. I didn’t hesitate. I slipped into the nearest side corridor, keeping close to the wall, my breath shallow. This time I wouldn’t wander too far from the main route—not like before, when I’d lost Tars Tarkas and his guards in the labyrinth.

The party passed the opening where I crouched, their footsteps echoing in the silence. Relief washed over me. I hadn’t been discovered. And better still—these were the same warriors I’d followed into the pits earlier. Tars Tarkas and his three guards. I fell in behind them, unseen, as they approached the cell where the great Thark had been imprisoned.

Two of the guards remained outside while the one with the keys entered to rechain Tars Tarkas. As soon as they turned away, I moved forward, a plan already forming in my mind. There was no other way if I was to get him out—and take him back to my camp in the hills.

I crept near the door, sword raised high above my head, gripping it with both hands. I would strike the moment the jailer stepped out—quick, silent, decisive. I didn’t want to think about what came after. But there was no choice.

Then I heard footsteps approaching the doorway.

A minute later, Tars Tarkas emerged—now wearing the armor of a Warhoon chief—holding the torch that lit his path. Behind him, at a dozen paces, came John Carter, Prince of Helium.

The two guards who’d been stationed outside had just begun ascending the spiral runway when Tars Tarkas appeared. “Why so long, Tan Gama?” one called.

“I had trouble with a lock,” Tars Tarkas replied. “And now I’ve left my short-sword in the Thark’s cell. Go on ahead—I’ll return for it.”

“As you wish, Tan Gama,” the other said. “We’ll see you above.”

Tars Tarkas nodded, turning as if to retrace his steps—but he waited only until the two vanished up the stairs. Then I joined him. We extinguished the torch and crept toward the spiral incline leading to the upper levels.

At the first floor, the hallway ended halfway across the building, forcing us to cross a rear chamber filled with green Martians before reaching the inner courtyard. Our only option was to reach the second floor—the same passage I’d used earlier.

We ascended cautiously. Voices drifted from the room above, but the hall remained dark. No one saw us as we reached the top.

Together, we made our way down the long corridor, emerging onto the balcony overlooking the courtyard—unseen.

To our right, a window opened into the room where I’d first seen Tan Gama and his companions. Now they were gathered inside, speaking in low tones.

“What could be delaying Tan Gama?” one asked.

“He surely couldn’t be this long fetching his shortsword from the Thark’s cell,” another replied.

“His short-sword?” a woman interrupted. “What do you mean?”

Tan Gama left his short-sword in the Thark’s cell,” explained the first. “He told us to wait at the runway while he returned.”

“But Tan Gama wore no short-sword tonight,” the woman said. “It broke in today’s battle with the Thark. He gave it to me to repair. See?” She drew the weapon from beneath her furs.

The warriors froze.

“There’s something wrong here,” one snapped. “I suspected it when he left us. His voice sounded strange.”

“We must go to the pits!”

We didn’t wait. I fastened my harness into a single strap and lowered Tars Tarkas to the courtyard below. A moment later, I dropped beside him.

We’d spoken barely a dozen words since I’d struck down Tan Gama and seen the shock on the Thark’s face.

“By now,” he said, “I should have learned to wonder at nothing you do, John Carter.”

That was all. But I knew he understood—how I’d risked everything for him. He didn’t need to say it. He was the first to greet me on Mars twenty years ago—charging at me with spear raised, hatred burning in his eyes, riding his thoat across the dead sea bottom beyond Korad. And now, among all beings of two worlds, none was a truer friend than Tars Tarkas, Jeddak of the Tharks.

In the shadows beneath the balcony, we discussed our next move.

“Five of us now,” I said. “Thuvia, Xodar, Carthoris, and ourselves. We’ll need five thoats.”

“Carthoris?” he asked.

“My son. I found him in Shador’s prison, on the Sea of Omean, in the land of the First Born.”

“I know not these places,” Tars Tarkas replied. “Are they upon Barsoom?”

“Upon and beneath,” I said. “But wait—we must escape first. Then I’ll tell you the strangest tale any Barsoomian has ever heard.”

We reached the great gates at the far end of the courtyard—the only way to bring the thoats into the avenue beyond.

Stealing five of these beasts was no small task. Thoats are wild, fierce creatures, tamed only by cruelty. Their nature is as savage as their masters. Ten feet tall at the shoulder, sleek and hairless, dark slate on back and sides, fading to yellow at their massive, padded feet, with pure white bellies. Their broad, flat tails complete the image of a fearsome war mount.

They’re guided by thought alone—no reins, no bridles. Our goal: find two that would obey our minds.

As we approached, the beasts snorted and squealed, circling us, necks arched, jaws gaping. They sensed our unfamiliar scent. The noise would draw attention—if it lasted.

But I managed to reach one. Before it could react, I was astride its glossy back. Moments later, Tars Tarkas mounted another. Together, we drove the others toward the gates.

He threw open the barriers while I held the rest in check. Then we rode through into the avenue, leaving the gates unsecured, and raced northward.

Our escape had been miraculous. And fortune held. We passed the outer ruins of the dead city without a sound of pursuit.

At our camp, a low whistle—our signal—alerted the others. They met us with joy, their faces alight.

No time was wasted in recounting our tale. Tars Tarkas and Carthoris exchanged formal greetings—yet I could feel the bond between them. The boy loved the Thark, and the Thark, the boy.

Xodar and Tars Tarkas were formally introduced. Thuvia was lifted onto the calmest thoat. Xodar and Carthoris mounted two more. We set off eastward.

At the edge of the city, we veered north. Under the twin moons, we crossed the dead sea bottom in silence, fleeing the Warhoons and the First Born—toward unknown dangers.

By midday, we halted to rest. The thoats were hobbled, feeding on the ochre moss that served as both food and drink. Thuvia volunteered to keep watch while the others slept.

I had closed my eyes barely a moment when her hand touched my shoulder.

“Arise, O Prince,” she whispered. “There is a dark line on the horizon—many pursuers.”

She pointed behind us. I looked—and saw it: a thin, shadowy column growing larger.

I woke the others. Tars Tarkas, towering above the rest, scanned the distance.

“It is a large force,” he said. “Riding hard.”

We sprang to our mounts, freed them, and fled again—northward, at full speed.

For the rest of the day and through the night, we raced across the ochre wastes. The pursuers grew closer—slowly, relentlessly.

By dusk, we could make out their green forms. Through the night, we heard the clank of their armor, the thunder of their thoats.

At dawn, the sun revealed them—less than half a mile behind.

A roar of triumph erupted from their ranks.

Ahead lay a range of hills—the far shore of the dead sea. If we reached them, our chances would improve.

But Thuvia’s thoat, though carrying the lightest load, was failing.

I rode beside her when suddenly it stumbled, lurching into mine. I saw it collapsing. Without hesitation, I pulled her from its back and swung her onto mine—behind me, her arms wrapped tightly around my waist.

But the added weight slowed my beast. The others could not go faster than the slowest.

We were united—though of different lands, colors, races, religions—one even from another world.

Yet none would abandon another.

We neared the hills. But the Warhoons were closing fast. Hope faded.

Thuvia and I were in the rear, lagging.

Suddenly, I felt her lips brush my shoulder—a soft, warm kiss.

“For thy sake, O my Prince,” she murmured.

Then her arms slipped from around me—and she fell.

I turned. She had leapt from my back, deliberately placing herself in the path of the pursuing horde—believing that removing her weight might save me.

Foolish child. She should have known me better.

I turned my thoat, urging it after her.

Carthoris must have seen it too. By the time I reached her, he was already there—leaping from his mount, throwing her onto it, cracking the beast’s rump with the flat of his sword. Then he turned to mine, trying to spur it forward.

My heart swelled with pride. The boy had sacrificed himself—for me.

But the Warhoons were upon us.

Tars Tarkas and Xodar had noticed our absence and charged to our aid.

All seemed lost.

But then—above us—a thunderous explosion.

A shell burst among the Warhoons. A hundred fell. Riderless thoats stampeded. Warriors were trampled. Chaos erupted.

They looked upward, seeking the source—then fled in panic.

We turned to see what had caused it.

Just clearing the hilltops, a colossal battleship floated through the air—majestic, silent, powerful.

Her bow gun fired again. Another shell tore through the retreating ranks.

As she drew nearer, I could scarcely contain myself.

On her bows—glowing in the morning light—was the golden crest of Helium.

As Carthoris, Xodar, Tars Tarkas, and I stood in awe before the magnificent vessel that loomed above us, a second ship crested the hilltop, followed swiftly by a third. The air shimmered with motion as a dozen one-man fliers launched from the upper decks of the nearest warship, diving like silver birds toward the ground. In moments, armed sailors surrounded us, their weapons drawn. An officer stepped forward, his eyes scanning our group—then they locked onto Carthoris.

With a gasp of recognition, he rushed forward, placing his hands on the boy’s shoulders. “Carthoris, my Prince!” he exclaimed, his voice thick with emotion. “Kaor! Kaor! Hor Vastus greets the son of Dejah Thoris, Princess of Helium, and her husband, John Carter. Where have you been? All Helium has mourned your absence. Since the day you vanished, calamity has fallen upon our great nation.”

“Grieve not, Hor Vastus,” Carthoris replied, his voice steady. “I return not alone—but with one whom all Barsoom once loved best: John Carter, Prince of Helium, savior of our world!”

The officer turned, his gaze sweeping over me—and then froze. His breath caught. “John Carter…” he whispered, his face pale. A flicker of pain crossed his eyes, but he did not press further. He knew the unspoken truth: that I had returned from beyond the River Iss, from the shores of the Lost Sea of Korus, from the Valley Dor itself. That I had walked through death and come back alive was a secret too sacred to force from my lips.

Yet still, he knelt. With a gesture that spoke volumes, he unbuckled his sword and laid it at my feet. On Mars, such an act meant more than loyalty—it meant surrender. It was the oath of fealty: body, soul, life, honor—all pledged to the one who now held the blade. No man had ever offered me such devotion. There was only one answer.

I bent, picked up the sword, kissed the hilt, and fastened it back upon him. “Hor Vastus,” I said, laying a hand on his shoulder, “you know your heart better than any. I may need your sword, but swear this: I will never ask you to draw it save for truth, justice, and righteousness.”

“That I knew, my Prince,” he answered, eyes bright. “Even before I cast it down.”

As we spoke, more fliers darted between the ships and the ground. Soon, a larger craft descended—a boat capable of carrying a dozen. It landed gently beside us. From its deck stepped an officer who saluted Hor Vastus. “Kantos Kan commands that you be brought immediately to the _Xavarian_.”

We boarded the vessel, but as I looked around, I realized Thuvia was missing. None had seen her since Carthoris sent her thoat racing into the hills—hoping to spare her from danger. Without hesitation, Hor Vastus dispatched a dozen scouts in every direction, desperate to find her.

On the _Xavarian_, Kantos Kan awaited us. My old comrade had risen to command Helium’s entire navy, yet he remained the same brave warrior who once shared a dungeon with me. He greeted Carthoris and Tars Tarkas with joy, but said nothing of where we had been. Like Hor Vastus, he dared not ask.

Then, quietly, he drew me aside. “For a year, Carthoris has been lost,” he said. “Dejah Thoris has grieved—her heart broken. When you disappeared, she suffered terribly. But when her son vanished… she began to fade. Her father and grandfather led expeditions to find him, but no word has come back. Rumors say they perished.”

He paused. “Zat Arras has renewed his demands for her hand. She despised him, feared him. But now, with both her father and grandfather gone, he holds power. Six days ago, he had a private audience with her. The next day, she vanished—along with Sola, Tars Tarkas’ daughter, and a dozen of her guards.”

He looked at me grimly. “They left no word. We believe she has taken the final pilgrimage—to the icy bosom of Iss. And her servants went with her. Zat Arras commands this fleet. He searches for her, but I fear it is already too late.”

My heart sank. Then came the news of Thuvia’s disappearance—another burden. I had vowed to return her to her people. Now, both women were lost.

Before I could speak, a flier arrived from Zat Arras’ flagship. The officer bore a message: “Bring the prisoners before me.”

Kantos Kan read it, then turned to me. “He demands we bring you to him. He is supreme in Helium—but it would be more fitting if he came to greet the savior of Barsoom.”

I smiled. “You know well, my friend, that Zat Arras hates me. Nothing would please him more than to humiliate and kill me. Let us go—see if he has the courage.”

We boarded a small flier. As we approached Zat Arras’ ship, his face showed no warmth—not even for Carthoris. Cold. Proud. Unyielding.

“Kaor, Zat Arras,” I said.

No reply.

“Why were these men not disarmed?” he asked Kantos Kan.

“They are not prisoners,” Kantos Kan replied. “Tars Tarkas is Jeddak of Thark, ally of Tardos Mors. The other is a friend of the Prince of Helium.”

“It is not enough,” Zat Arras snapped. “Where have you been, John Carter?”

“I have just returned from the Valley Dor,” I said. “From the Land of the First Born.”

He exhaled sharply. “Ah! So you admit it—you’ve returned from Iss?”

“I have returned from a land of lies and torment,” I said. “From a valley of death. We escaped the clutches of monstrous deceivers. I am back to save Barsoom—this time, from true annihilation.”

“Cease, blasphemer!” he roared. “Do not expect to save yourself with horror-filled lies—”

But he did not finish. One does not call John Carter a coward or a liar lightly.

Before anyone could react, I was on him—hand gripping his throat. “Come I from heaven or hell, Zat Arras, I remain the same John Carter. And no man calls me such names and lives—unless he apologizes.”

I bent him backward across my knee. His face turned purple. “Seize him!” he gasped.

A dozen officers lunged forward. Kantos Kan stepped close. “Desist,” he whispered. “If you fight, we all fall into chaos. My men will stand with you. A mutiny could spark revolution. For Tardos Mors, for Helium—stop.”

I released Zat Arras. Turned my back. “Come, Kantos Kan. The Prince of Helium returns to the _Xavarian_.”

No one stopped us. Zat Arras stood trembling, white-faced. Some officers looked at him with scorn. One, a veteran of Tardos Mors’ court, murmured as I passed: “Count me among your soldiers, John Carter.”

I nodded. We returned to the _Xavarian_ in silence.

Fifteen minutes later, orders came: proceed to Helium.

Our journey was quiet. Carthoris and I were lost in thought. Kantos Kan worried about what might happen if Zat Arras enforced the ancient law—death for those who returned from Dor. Tars Tarkas grieved for Sola. Xodar, ever the outlaw, shrugged. “Let us hope we die with red blood on our blades,” he said.

It was a simple wish—one likely to come true.

As we neared Helium, I saw division among the crew. Some gathered near us. Others kept their distance—afraid, perhaps, of the sin we carried. To return from Dor was sacrilege. To speak of it was heresy. We were blasphemers in their eyes—even if they still honored us personally.

Kantos Kan refused to talk of the therns or the First Born. “I risk enough by standing with you,” he said. “Do not ask me to hear what I’ve always believed was the greatest lie.”

I understood. Soon, the choice would be made. In Helium, we would face judgment. If Tardos Mors had not returned, Zat Arras would rule. To oppose him was treason. Yet many would follow me—despite god, man, or devil.

But my mind was consumed by Dejah Thoris. I saw her in the darkness—chained, tormented, facing horrors beyond imagining. The plant men. The white apes. I covered my face, trying to shut out the nightmare.

At last, we reached Helium—the city marked by its towering scarlet spire. As we circled downward, I saw the streets below filled with a vast, silent crowd. They had been warned by radio-aerogram.

From the _Xavarian_, we transferred to a smaller flier—bound for the Temple of Reward. This was where justice was given: heroes crowned, criminals condemned. We were taken directly to a chamber overlooking the Avenue of Ancestors, five miles long, stretching to the Gate of Jeddaks.

Below, the people stood packed tight—silent, solemn. Many wept when they saw us at the window.

Late in the afternoon, a messenger arrived. “Zat Arras commands,” he said, “that you be tried by a council of nobles tomorrow at the first zode—about 8:40 Earth time.”

And so we waited.

In the silence of the temple room, the weight of the coming trial pressed upon us. The fate of Barsoom hung in the balance. And somewhere, in the frozen wastes of Iss, my beloved princess may already be gone.

The morning dawned with the weight of fate pressing upon us. Moments before the hour set for our trial, a company of Zat Arras’s officers appeared at our quarters, their swords gleaming under the pale Barsoom sun. We were marched in pairs to the great temple hall—a place of ancient rites and sacred judgment. As we entered, the air thickened with tension. The broad Aisle of Hope stretched before us, flanked by ranks of Zodangan soldiers standing like stone sentinels. Guards marched ahead and behind us, their eyes cold, their weapons ready.

We reached the central platform—the Throne of Righteousness—where thirty-one judges sat in a solemn circle, their faces grim beneath the high vaults of the temple. I looked among them, searching for a familiar face, a sign of mercy. But there was none. Nearly all were men of Zodanga, the very people who had once bowed to my sword when the green hordes swept through their cities. And now, they would be my judges.

The coliseum was packed—thousands of Heliumites, nobles and commoners alike, silent as death as we stepped onto the platform. No sound escaped the vast crowd until I stood before the Pedestal of Truth, where I was to answer for my return from the dead.

Zat Arras, seated in the golden chair of the presiding magistrate, rose and called my name.

“John Carter,” he declared, “take your place upon the Pedestal of Truth. You shall be judged impartially, according to your deeds—and you shall know the reward you have earned.”

He turned to the audience, his voice echoing across the chamber.

“Know this, O judges and people of Helium: John Carter, once Prince of Helium, claims to have returned from the Valley Dor, from the Temple of Issus itself. He has spoken blasphemy against the Sacred Iss, against the Lost Sea of Korus, against the Holy Therns, even against Issus, Goddess of Death and Life Eternal. And you see him here—alive—when he should be dead. For on Barsoom, the dead do not return. Those who defy this law must be made dead forever. Judges—your duty is clear. What shall be the fate of John Carter?”

A single voice broke the silence.

“Death!”

Then, from the crowd, a man leapt to his feet—Kantos Kan, commander of the Helium fleet. He raised his hand high and roared:

“Justice! Justice! Justice!”

Before anyone could stop him, he burst past the guards and stormed onto the platform.

“What justice is this?” he demanded, turning to Zat Arras. “The accused has not been heard! He has not been allowed to speak for himself or call witnesses! In the name of Helium, I demand fair treatment for the Prince!”

The cry erupted again—“Justice! Justice!”—and the roar shook the very stones of the temple. Zat Arras, furious but powerless, glared at me.

“Speak,” he snarled. “But do not profane the holy things of Barsoom.”

I stepped forward, facing the crowd, my voice ringing above the heads of the judges.

“Men of Helium,” I said, “what justice can I expect from Zodanga? None. I do not ask it. I speak not for myself—but for you. For your wives, your daughters, your children yet unborn. I speak to save them from the horrors I have seen in the Temple of Issus—the monstrous plant men, the white apes of Dor, the lust of the Holy Therns. From the cold embrace of Iss, who steals women from their homes, their love, their lives.

You know my story. I came from another world, enslaved by the green men, tortured, scorned—yet I rose to stand beside the highest of Barsoom. I have never lied. I have never spoken ill of your faith, though I did not understand it. And every man here owes his life to one act of mine—when I gave up my own happiness, my Princess, so that you might live.

So let me speak. Let me be believed. Let me serve you—just as I saved you from death, now I beg you to let me save you from the false afterlife of Dor and Issus.

I speak to you, Heliumites. When I am done, let Zodanga have its way with me. Zat Arras has taken my sword—I am no threat to them. Will you listen?”

A noble from the audience shouted, “Speak, John Carter, Prince of Helium!”
And the entire hall echoed with the cry—“Speak!”

For two hours, I told them what I had seen. I spoke of the Temple of Issus, of the rituals, of the suffering, of the lies that masked horror beneath piety. I warned them of the dangers that awaited if they continued to blindly follow the Therns.

When I finished, Zat Arras rose again.

“My nobles,” he said, “you have heard the blasphemer. He was given chance to prove innocence—he chose instead to slander the sacred. What is your verdict?”

One judge stood. “Death to the blasphemer!”
In an instant, all thirty-one raised their swords—unanimous, unyielding.

The crowd stirred. A low murmur began, growing louder. Kantos Kan raised his hand, silencing them.

“You have heard the sentence Zodanga would impose on Helium’s greatest hero,” he said, calm and steady. “It may be the duty of Helium to accept it. But I, Kantos Kan, head of the navy, refuse. Here is my answer.”

With that, he unbuckled his sword and cast it at my feet.

Then, like a wave, men surged forward—soldiers, citizens, nobles—all breaking through the Zodangan lines. A hundred blades clattered down beside me. One by one, I took each sword, kissed the hilt, and returned it to its owner.

“Come,” Kantos Kan said. “We escort John Carter to his palace.”

We turned toward the stairs.

“Stop!” Zat Arras bellowed. “No prisoner leaves the Throne of Righteousness!”

But the soldiers of Helium did not obey. From every side, swords flashed. Men advanced on the Zodangans.

Someone cried: “Tardos Mors is dead! A thousand years to John Carter, Jeddak of Helium!”

I saw the danger—the city teetered on civil war.

“Hold!” I shouted, leaping back to the Pedestal of Truth. “No man move! One sword drawn here today could ignite a war that will tear Helium apart. Brothers against brothers. Fathers against sons. No life is worth that cost. I will submit to Zat Arras’s judgment—if it means peace. Let us compromise. Delay this trial until Tardos Mors returns—or Mors Kajak. If neither returns in a year, then we hold a new trial. There is precedent.”

I turned to Zat Arras.

“If you are not a fool, you will take this chance. Once those swords are drawn, no power on Barsoom can stop what follows. What say you?”

Zat Arras hesitated—then raised his voice to the crowd.

“Stay your hands, men of Helium,” he shouted, trembling with rage. “The court’s sentence stands. But the day of retribution is not yet set. By virtue of the prisoner’s rank and service, I grant a reprieve—one year, or until the return of Mors Kajak or Tardos Mors. Disperse quietly.”

No one moved. They stared at me, waiting.

“Clear the temple,” Zat Arras ordered his officer.

I stepped to the edge of the platform and pointed toward the main gate.

“Go,” I said. “Leave.”

As one, they turned and filed out—silent, angry, but obedient. Only Kantos Kan and a hundred loyal men remained.

“Come,” he said. “We escort you to your palace, my Prince.”

We walked through the temple, past scowling guards, through the great hall of judgment, into the sunlight beyond. The streets of Helium were crowded—people parted to make way. Swords fell at my feet. Slaves knelt, kissing my hands.

“Ah, master,” one whispered, “if only our divine Princess were here…”

Tears stung my eyes. I turned away.

Carthoris wept openly as the slaves embraced him. Then, for the first time, Tars Tarkas learned that Sola had accompanied Dejah Thoris on her final journey. I had not told him. But now, the truth was spoken.

He stood silent—his green face unreadable. Yet I knew the grief within him, as deep and fierce as my own. For though he was a Thark, he bore the heart of a man—full of love, loyalty, sorrow.

That evening, we gathered in the great dining hall of the Prince of Helium. Over a hundred of us, including members of my court. The table was triangular, as custom demanded—three sides, three places. Carthoris and I sat at ours. At the third side, Dejah Thoris’ chair stood empty, draped with her wedding finery, her jewels shimmering in the candlelight. A slave stood behind it, waiting—just as he had always done.

Tars Tarkas sat at his elevated seat, massive and still. Kantos Kan sat at my right. Hor Vastus, at Carthoris’. The room was quiet. No laughter, no song. Only the memory of Dejah Thoris—her voice, her smile, her presence—echoed in the silence.

Then, from far off, came shouting—many voices, rising in confusion. A slave rushed in.

“A great crowd approaches the gates!”
Another burst in, wild-eyed, laughing and screaming.

“Dejah Thoris is found! A messenger from the Princess!”

I did not wait. With a single leap, I cleared the table and landed on the balcony. Below, on the scarlet lawn, a thoat galloped toward the palace. Its rider—Sola.

I ran to meet her.

“Where is the Princess?” I demanded.

She slid from the beast, tears streaming down her green cheeks.

“O my Prince! She is gone forever. Even now, she may be a captive on the lesser moon. The black pirates of Barsoom have stolen her.”

Once inside the palace, I led Sola to the grand dining hall. After she had greeted her father in the formal manner of the green men, she began to recount the tale of Dejah Thoris’s pilgrimage and capture.

“Seven days ago,” she said, “after her audience with Zat Arras, Princess Dejah Thoris tried to slip away from the palace under cover of night. Though I had not heard the outcome of her meeting with the Jeddak, I could see the anguish etched upon her face—something terrible had transpired. When I found her creeping out, I knew at once where she meant to go.

Without delay, I roused a dozen of her most loyal guards and told them of my fears. Without hesitation, they pledged themselves to follow our beloved princess—no matter how far, no matter the danger. Together, we set off after her into the darkness, bound for the Sacred Iss and the Valley Dor.

We caught up with her just beyond the palace walls. Woola, her faithful hound, was by her side—but no one else. When we confronted her, she feigned anger and ordered us back, but this time we refused to obey. She saw our resolve, and when she realized we would not let her walk alone into the final pilgrimage, she broke down in tears, embracing each of us. Then, together, we journeyed southward into the night.

The next day, we came across a herd of small thoats. We mounted them and pressed on swiftly, covering great distances due south. On the morning of the fifth day, we sighted a massive fleet of battleships sailing northward. They spotted us before we could hide, and soon we were surrounded by a swarm of black warriors.

The princess’s guard fought valiantly, but they were outnumbered and overwhelmed. One by one, they fell. Only Dejah Thoris and I remained unharmed.

When she realized she was in the hands of the black pirates, she attempted to take her own life. But one of them snatched her dagger away. Then they bound both of us so tightly that we could not move our hands.

The fleet continued north, carrying us with it. There were twenty large battleships, along with several swift cruisers. That evening, one of the smaller ships returned—its prow stained with blood and bearing a prisoner: a young red woman they claimed to have captured near a range of hills, right beneath the noses of a red Martian fleet.

From fragments of conversation, we learned the blacks were hunting a group of fugitives who had escaped them earlier. The arrival of the captive seemed significant—her interrogation by the fleet commander lasted long into the night. Later, she was brought to our cell and chained beside us.

She was breathtakingly beautiful. Her name was Thuvia, Princess of Ptarth. She told Dejah Thoris that many years before, she had taken the voluntary pilgrimage from her father’s court. And then she asked who Dejah Thoris was.

When she heard the answer, she dropped to her knees and kissed the princess’s bound hands. She told her that only that morning, she had been with John Carter, Prince of Helium—and Carthoris, his son.

Dejah Thoris could not believe it at first. But as Thuvia recounted all the strange adventures that had befallen her since meeting John Carter, and spoke of the tales he and Carthoris and Xodar had shared about their journeys in the Land of the First Born, the truth became clear.

‘Who else,’ Dejah Thoris whispered, ‘on all Barsoom could have done such deeds?’

And when Thuvia confessed her love for John Carter—his loyalty, his courage, his devotion—the princess wept. She cursed Zat Arras and the cruel fate that had driven her from Helium mere days before her beloved lord returned.

‘I do not blame you for loving him, Thuvia,’ she said. ‘Your affection is pure, I can see it in your eyes. I would trust no less than that.’

The fleet sailed nearly to Helium, but last night, they turned south again—evidently realizing John Carter had slipped through their grasp.

Then, a guard came to our cell and dragged me to the deck.

‘There is no place for a green man in the Land of the First Born,’ he said coldly.

With that, he shoved me violently over the edge of the ship.

I thought I was falling to my death—thousands of feet above the ground. But a miracle saved me. The vessel was moving slowly, and as I plunged into the darkness, I struck not empty air, but a soft mass of vegetation—only twenty feet below.

I lay there all night, bruised but alive. At dawn, I saw the world stretched out beneath me—a vast sea floor and distant hills. I was atop the highest peak of a mountain range. The fleet had grazed the crest in the dark, and the guard had believed he was casting me into oblivion.

To the west, I found a great waterway. It belonged to Helium. There, I procured a thoat and made my way back—just as you know.”

For a long while, silence filled the hall. Dejah Thoris, held captive by the First Born? The thought chilled me. But then, like fire rekindled, the old flame of defiance surged within me. I rose, sword raised, and vowed aloud to reach her, rescue her, and avenge her suffering.

At once, a hundred swords leapt from scabbards. A hundred warriors stood upon the table, swearing allegiance to my cause.

My plans were already forming. I thanked each of them, then left Carthoris to entertain the others. I retreated to my private chamber with Kantos Kan, Tars Tarkas, Xodar, and Hor Vastus. There, under dim lantern light, we laid out the strategy.

Xodar was certain that Issus would choose both Dejah Thoris and Thuvia to serve her for a year.

“For that time,” he said, “they will be safe. And we will know exactly where to find them.”

Kantos Kan and Xodar would oversee the refitting of a fleet to sail into Omean. The former would dispatch vessels to Hastor, a distant Heliumetic city, where they could be modified in secret. Xodar, familiar with the mechanics of water propellers from years of repairing captured ships, would supervise the engineering.

Six months, we estimated, would be needed—long enough to keep Zat Arras unaware of our preparations.

Kantos Kan warned that Zat Arras now craved power more than ever. He would not welcome Dejah Thoris’s return—her presence threatened his ambitions. With me and Carthoris gone, nothing would stand between him and the throne.

“There is a way,” Hor Vastus declared suddenly, “to stop him forever.”

“What?” I asked.

He smiled. “I shall whisper it here… but one day, I will shout it from the dome of the Temple of Reward, to a cheering crowd below.”

“What do you mean?” Kantos Kan asked.

“John Carter, Jeddak of Helium,” Hor Vastus murmured.

My companions’ eyes lit with hope. But I shook my head.

“No,” I said. “Not yet. Not until Tardos Mors and Mors Kajak are truly gone. If I am still here, I will help the people of Helium choose their next leader fairly. Whomever they choose, I will swear my sword to. But I will not claim the title myself. Until then, Tardos Mors is Jeddak, and Zat Arras is his representative.”

“As you wish,” Hor Vastus said. “But—what was that?”

He pointed toward the window overlooking the gardens.

Before anyone could speak, he was already sprinting to the balcony.

“There he goes!” he shouted. “The guards! Below! The guards!”

We followed, and saw a figure dart across the lawn and vanish into the shrubbery.

“He was on the balcony when I first saw him!” Hor Vastus cried.

“Quick—follow him!”

We searched the grounds with every guard for hours. But the intruder was gone—no trace remained.

“What do you think?” Tars Tarkas asked Kantos Kan.

“A spy from Zat Arras,” he replied. “That’s always been his style.”

“He’ll have something interesting to report,” Hor Vastus laughed.

“I hope he only heard us talking about a new Jeddak,” I said. “If he overheard our plan to rescue Dejah Thoris, it will spark civil war. Zat Arras will try to stop us. And if he does, I will oppose him—even if it means turning against Tardos Mors himself. If it plunges Helium into bloodshed, I will still go forward. Nothing will stop me now—except death. And if I fall, swear to me that you will continue the search. Bring her home safely to her grandfather’s court.”

Each man placed a hand on the hilt of his sword and swore the oath.

We decided the remodeled battleships would be sent to Hastor, where Kantos Kan could manage the operations under the guise of routine naval work. From there, the fleet would remain hidden until we were ready to strike.

Kantos Kan and Xodar would handle the ships. Tars Tarkas would contact Thark and gauge the green men’s mood toward his return. If favorable, he would gather a vast army to send via transport directly to the Valley Dor and the Temple of Issus, while our fleet attacked the First Born’s vessels in Omean.

Hor Vastus would organize a secret force—men sworn to follow me wherever I led. To man a thousand battleships and transports, we’d need over a million soldiers. His task was immense.

After they left, I bid Carthoris goodnight. I was exhausted. I bathed and climbed onto my sleeping silks, eager for rest—the first true night’s sleep I’d known since returning to Barsoom.

But even that was denied me.

How long I slept, I do not know. I woke abruptly to find six powerful men upon me—my mouth gagged, my limbs bound. Before I could react, they had secured me completely.

They said nothing. The gag silenced any cry.

Silently, they lifted me and carried me toward the door. As we passed the window, the farther moon cast its silver glow. I saw that each man wore his face wrapped in silk—I could not recognize a single one.

They led me into the corridor and stopped at a hidden panel in the wall—one that opened into the tunnels beneath the palace.

I doubted anyone outside my household knew of it. Yet the leader stepped forward without hesitation. He touched a concealed button, and the panel swung open.

He stepped aside as we entered. Then he closed it behind us.

Down through the winding passageways we went. At last, we reached a heavy iron door. The leader tapped it with his sword—three sharp blows, a pause, three more, another pause, then two.

The door slid open. I was pushed into a brightly lit chamber where three richly clad men sat.

One turned toward me, a cruel smile twisting his thin lips.

It was Zat Arras.

“Ah,” said Zat Arras, “to what fortunate circumstance do I owe the pleasure of this unexpected visit from the Prince of Helium?”

As he spoke, one of my guards removed the gag from my mouth. But I remained silent—standing tall, eyes fixed on the Jed of Zodanga. My expression, I knew, carried the weight of scorn, a quiet defiance that burned in my gaze.

The room fell still. All eyes shifted between me and Zat Arras. Then, slowly, a flush crept across his face—anger rising like smoke from a smoldering fire.

“You may go,” he said to the men who had brought me. When only his two companions and I remained, he turned to me again, his voice cold as ice, measured and deliberate, each word chosen with deadly precision.

“John Carter,” he began, “by custom, by law, and by the judgment of an impartial court, you are condemned to die. The people cannot save you—I alone hold the power to spare you. You are at my mercy. I can kill you—or set you free. And if I choose death, no one would ever know.”

“If you return to Helium under the terms of your reprieve, it is unlikely the people will demand your execution. You may walk free within two minutes—if you agree to one condition: Tardos Mors shall never return to Helium. Neither shall Mors Kajak or Dejah Thoris. Helium must choose a new Jeddak within the year—and that Jeddak shall be Zat Arras. Say you will support my cause. That is the price of your freedom. I am done.”

I knew well the cruelty that lived beneath Zat Arras’ calm exterior. If I died, he might very well ascend to the throne unchallenged. But if I lived, I could still pursue the search for Dejah Thoris. If I refused, not only would I fail to stop him—but I might seal her fate. She would be forced into the arena of Issus, torn apart by beasts, her life snuffed out before she even knew what was coming.

For a moment, doubt flickered. But only a moment.

The daughter of a thousand Jeddaks would rather die than submit to such dishonor. And John Carter would stand beside her, even in death.

I turned to Zat Arras.

“There can be no alliance,” I said, “between a traitor to Helium and a prince of the House of Tardos Mors. And I do not believe, Zat Arras, that the great Jeddak is dead.”

Zat Arras shrugged.

“It will not be long,” he replied, “before your opinions matter to no one—not even yourself. So make the most of them while you can. I will give you time to reflect upon my generous offer. Tonight, you shall descend into the silence and darkness of the pits. There you will ponder your choice. Know this: if you fail to accept within a reasonable time, you will never see light again. Nor will you know when the hand reaches through the dark with the dagger that ends your last chance to taste warmth, freedom, and joy once more.”

He clapped his hands. Guards returned.

He gestured toward me.

“To the pits,” he said. No more.

Four men escorted me from the chamber, a radium light illuminating the way. We descended through endless tunnels, deeper and deeper beneath Helium’s heart.

At last we stopped in a dim chamber. Rings were embedded in the walls, chains fastened to them—many ending in human skeletons. One guard kicked aside a brittle frame, unlocked a massive padlock from a chain around what had once been a man’s ankle, and snapped the iron band around my own leg.

Then they left—taking the light with them.

Darkness swallowed everything. For a few minutes, I heard the faint clatter of their gear, fading into silence. Soon, there was nothing—only blackness and the bones of the forgotten.

How long I stood, listening, I don’t know. Eventually, I sank to the stone floor, leaned my head against the wall, and slept.

It must have been hours later when I awoke to find a young man standing before me. In one hand, a light. In the other, a bowl of gruel—the common fare of Barsoom’s prisons.

“Zat Arras sends greetings,” he said. “And commands me to tell you that though he knows of the plot to make you Jeddak of Helium, he still refuses to withdraw his offer. You need only ask me to inform him you accept—then you shall be free.”

I shook my head.

He said nothing more. Placed the food at my side, then turned and left—taking the light with him.

Twice a day, for many days, he came. Always the same message. Always the same silence.

I tried to speak with him—about anything—anything but the prison. But he refused. After a while, I gave up.

For months, I searched for a way to send word to Carthoris. I scraped at the chain, link by link, hoping to wear through it. Perhaps I could follow the youth back through the tunnels, find a way out.

But my thoughts were haunted by Dejah Thoris. I feared Carthoris was also imprisoned. Or worse—dead.

Zat Arras’ spy had overheard our conversation about choosing a new Jeddak. And just moments before, we’d spoken of rescuing Dejah Thoris. It was likely he knew every detail. Carthoris, Kantos Kan, Tars Tarkas, Hor Vastus, Xodar—they might all be dead or captured.

I decided to try one last thing.

When the youth returned, I studied him closely. He was handsome—much like Carthoris. And though his clothes were ragged, his bearing was noble. A contradiction that intrigued me.

I spoke carefully.

“You’ve been kind during my imprisonment,” I said. “And since I know my time is short, I wish to show my gratitude. Your service has made this place bearable. You’ve brought my food without delay, kept it clean, never harmed me. You’ve shown dignity and care. That means more than gold to me.”

“I want to give you something,” I continued. “In the guardroom of my palace, there are fine harnesses. Choose one you like—it’s yours. All I ask is that you wear it so I may know my gift reached you. Tell me you’ll do it.”

His eyes lit. He glanced from his worn garments to my own rich armor. For a moment, he hesitated—long enough for my heart to freeze.

“And if I went to the Prince’s palace with such a request?” he said at last. “They’d laugh. And throw me into the avenue. No, it cannot be. Though I thank you. If Zat Arras suspected such a thing, he’d cut out my heart.”

“There’s no danger,” I urged. “By night, you could bring a note from me to Carthoris, my son. Read it first, to ensure it harms no one. He will be discreet. Only we three need know. It’s simple. Harmless. No one could fault it.”

Again, he paused.

“And there is a jeweled short-sword I took from a northern Jeddak,” I added. “When you take the harness, ask Carthoris for that too. With it, you’ll be the finest warrior in all Zodanga.”

“Bring writing materials next time,” I said. “We’ll have you dressed fit for your birth.”

He turned and left—silent, thoughtful.

I sat, waiting. Would he accept? If he did, it meant Carthoris lived. If he returned in my armor and with the sword, it meant Carthoris had received my message—and knew I was alive.

Days passed. Then, finally, I heard footsteps.

My breath caught. Was it Parthak?

No. Another man brought food—and writing materials.

My heart soared. I had succeeded. I feigned surprise, then picked up the tools. Quickly, I wrote a brief order: *Deliver to Parthak any harness he chooses, and the jeweled short-sword.* That was all. But it meant everything.

I left the note open on the floor. The youth picked it up and left.

I estimated I’d been in the pits for three hundred days. Time was running out. If Dejah Thoris was still alive, her end was near. Those chosen by Issus lived no longer than a year.

The next visit, I waited anxiously. Would Parthak return, adorned in my gear?

But it wasn’t him.

“What happened to Parthak?” I asked.

He wouldn’t answer. Left without a word.

Days passed. A new jailer appeared—still silent, still refusing to speak.

I could only guess why Parthak was gone. But I knew it had something to do with the note. If he’d wanted to please Zat Arras, he could have delivered it and claimed loyalty. Instead, he’d vanished.

Thirty days had passed since I gave the note. Three hundred and thirty days since my capture. Thirty days remained before Dejah Thoris would be summoned to the arena.

The image tore at me—her beautiful form torn by white apes, dragged through dirt, her body broken and devoured by nobles. I buried my face in my arms. Tears threatened, despite my resolve. Such horror should not be real. Yet reason screamed it was true.

I nearly lost my mind—until I heard approaching footsteps.

A new determination rose within me. I would escape. Kill my jailer with a ruse. Trust fate to carry me beyond these walls.

I threw myself to the floor, pretending death—convulsions, perhaps. When he knelt beside me, I would grab his throat and strike with the chain, gripped tight in my hand.

The steps grew closer. He halted. A muttered curse. Then he knelt.

I opened my eyes.

And struck.

The blow fell as planned—swift, brutal. But in that instant, I saw his face.

Carthoris.

God! What cruel fate had brought my son here, at this moment? To strike him down—my own flesh and blood—without knowing!

A wave of agony washed over me. I collapsed, unconscious, across his body.

When I awoke, a cool hand pressed to my forehead. I didn’t open my eyes. Memories swirled—confused, painful.

Then came the truth: I had killed my son.

I dared not look.

Who tended me? Carthoris must have come with someone else. But no—when I finally opened my eyes, there he was—alive, bruised, but breathing.

I pulled him into my arms. A prayer rose from my soul, echoing across the dying world of Mars—a cry of gratitude to the Eternal Mystery.

He had been unconscious for a time. How long, he didn’t know.

“How did you find me?” I asked.

“Through your clever plan,” he said. “You sent the note through Parthak. Until he came for his harness and sword, we thought you dead. When I read your message, I gave him his choice of armor—and later, the jeweled blade. But once I fulfilled my promise, I began questioning him. He refused to speak. Loyal to Zat Arras to the end.”

“We offered him freedom—or the pits. Information in exchange. He refused. So we moved him into the pits, where he remains.”

“No threats, no bribes worked. His only reply: ‘When Parthak dies, no man can say, “A traitor is gone to his deserts.”’”

“Then Xodar devised a plan. We had Hor Vastus chained beside him—dressed as a Zodangan soldier. For fifteen days, Hor Vastus endured the darkness. Slowly, he won Parthak’s trust. Today, thinking he spoke to a friend, Parthak revealed your cell.”

“I found the plans of the pits among official documents. Getting here was harder. The entrances between sections are guarded. I slipped past a Zodangan guard—left him behind, soulless.”

“And here I am,” he finished, laughing. “Just in time to be nearly killed by you.”

As he spoke, he worked the lock of my fetters. With a triumphant cry, he dropped the chain.

I stood—free at last after nearly a year.

He handed me a long-sword and a dagger. Together, we began the journey back.

At the edge of Zat Arras’ pits, we found the guard’s body—unnoticed. To delay discovery, we carried it away, hiding it in a side cell beneath a neighboring estate.

After half an hour, we reached the pits beneath our own palace. Soon, we emerged into the audience chamber—where Kantos Kan, Tars Tarkas, Hor Vastus, and Xodar waited, anxious.

No time for stories. I needed to know: had our plans held?

“It took longer than expected,” Kantos Kan said. “Secrecy slowed us. Zat Arras’ spies are everywhere. But I believe no word reached him.”

“This night, at Hastor, a fleet of a thousand battleships lies ready—each equipped to sail air and water. Five ten-man cruisers, ten five-man scouts, a hundred one-man scouts per ship. One hundred and sixteen thousand vessels in total.”

“At Thark, nine hundred troopships wait—carrying the green warriors of Tars Tarkas. Seven days ago, all was ready. We waited, hoping you might be rescued in time to lead the expedition. It was wise to wait, my Prince.”

“And Thark,” I asked, “how do they treat one who returns from Iss?”

“They sent fifty chieftains,” Tars Tarkas replied. “We are a just people. When I told them the truth, they agreed: their actions would mirror Helium’s toward you. Meanwhile, I resumed my throne to gather allies. Two hundred and fifty thousand warriors now fill Thark—from north to south, from a hundred hordes. They await my command. They ask only loot and transport home when the war ends.”

“And you, Hor Vastus?”

“A million veteran fighters from Helium’s waterways man the ships,” he said. “Each sworn to secrecy and loyalty. Not enough from any one region to raise suspicion.”

“Good!” I said. “Now, Kantos Kan—can we go to Hastor at once? Before tomorrow’s sun?”

“We should lose no time,” he replied. “Already, Hastor questions the purpose of such a fleet. I wonder how Zat Arras has not learned of it. A cruiser waits above your dock. Let us leave—”

A sudden burst of gunfire erupted from the palace gardens.

We rushed to the balcony—just in time to see a dozen guards vanish into the shadows, chasing a fleeing figure. Below, on the scarlet sward, others knelt over a motionless body.

We watched as they lifted the figure and carried it into the chamber.

It was a red man—prime of life—wearing plain metal. A common soldier—or a spy.

“Another of Zat Arras’ agents,” said Hor Vastus.

“Likely,” I replied. “Remove the body.”

“Wait!” Xodar called. “Prince, ask for a cloth and some thoat oil.”

I nodded.

Two hours after leaving my palace in Helium—around midnight—Kantos Kan, Xodar, and I arrived at Hastor. Carthoris, Tars Tarkas, and Hor Vastus had already proceeded directly to Thark aboard another cruiser.

The transports were to depart immediately, moving slowly southward. The fleet of battleships would catch up by the morning of the second day.

At Hastor, everything was ready. Kantos Kan’s meticulous planning had ensured perfect coordination. Within ten minutes of our arrival, the first ship of the fleet lifted from its dock. Then, one after another—at a rate of nearly one per second—the massive vessels rose gracefully into the night sky, forming a long, slender line that stretched for miles toward the south.

It wasn’t until we entered Kantos Kan’s cabin that I thought to ask the date. Until then, I hadn’t been certain how long I’d spent imprisoned in the darkness of Zat Arras’s pits. When Kantos Kan told me, a sharp pang of despair struck me—I had miscalculated the time. Three hundred and sixty-five days had passed. It was too late to save Dejah Thoris.

The mission was no longer one of rescue—but of revenge. I did not remind Kantos Kan of the grim truth: before we could even hope to breach the Temple of Issus, the Princess of Helium would be gone. As far as I knew, she might already be dead. I didn’t know exactly when she had first beheld Issus.

What use was there in burdening my friends with my personal grief? They had shared enough sorrow with me already. From now on, I would bear it alone. So I said nothing. The expedition still held meaning—if only we could reveal to the people of Barsoom the cruel lie that had deceived them for ages, perhaps thousands of lives could be spared each year from the horror awaiting those who completed the voluntary pilgrimage.

If we could open the fair Valley Dor to the red men, we would have accomplished much. And beyond the Mountains of Otz, between them and the ice barrier, lay vast fertile lands needing no irrigation. This lowland, at the bottom of a dying world, was the only naturally productive region on Barsoom. Here fell dew and rain, here flowed an open sea, here water was plentiful—yet it remained the domain of savage beasts, guarded by the wicked remnants of two once-great races. If I could break through the religious superstition that barred the red men from this El Dorado, it would be a worthy tribute to my beloved’s noble spirit. I would serve Barsoom again, and her sacrifice would not be in vain.

On the morning of the second day, we raised the great fleet of transports and their escort ships just as dawn broke. Soon we were close enough to exchange signals. I should mention that radio-aerograms are rarely used in wartime or for secret dispatches, because whenever one nation develops a new cipher or wireless device, its neighbors work tirelessly to intercept and decode the messages. After centuries of such espionage, nearly every method of wireless communication has been compromised. No nation dares transmit important messages this way.

Tars Tarkas reported all was well with the transports. The battleships advanced to take position ahead, and the combined fleets moved slowly over the ice cap, skimming low to avoid detection by the therns whose territory we approached.

Far ahead, a thin line of one-man air scouts screened us from surprise. Flanking us on both sides, with a smaller contingent trailing twenty miles behind, they formed a protective perimeter. For several hours, we advanced toward the entrance to Omean when one scout returned from the front to report that the cone-shaped summit of the entrance was in sight. Almost simultaneously, another scout raced in from the left flank.

His speed signaled urgency. Kantos Kan and I waited on the forward deck—the equivalent of an earthly battleship’s bridge. Before his tiny flier had fully settled on the flagship’s landing deck, he was already climbing the stairs to where we stood.

“A great fleet of battleships, south-south-east, my Prince!” he shouted. “Thousands—possibly more—and they’re bearing down on us!”

“The thern spies weren’t idle in John Carter’s palace,” Kantos Kan remarked to me. “Your orders, Prince?”

“Dispatch ten battleships to guard the entrance to Omean,” I commanded. “They are to allow no hostile vessel to enter or leave the shaft. That will trap the First Born’s fleet.”

“Form the rest of the battleships into a large V, apex pointing south-south-east. Order the transports, protected by their convoys, to follow closely behind. Once the point of the V reaches the enemy line, the formation must open outward at the apex. Each wing of the V shall engage the enemy fiercely and drive them back, creating a passage through their ranks. The transports must surge through at full speed to reach a position above the temples and gardens of the therns.”

“There, they shall land and deliver to the Holy Therns a lesson in warfare they will never forget. I had hoped to stay focused on the main objective, but we must end this attack once and for all. Otherwise, peace will elude us while our fleet remains near Dor, and our chances of returning to the outer world will diminish greatly.”

Kantos Kan saluted and relayed my orders to his aides. In moments, the fleet reformed as directed. The ten assigned to Omean sped off, while the troopships and escorts gathered tightly, preparing for the charge.

Full speed ahead was given. The fleet surged forward like hunting greyhounds. Moments later, the enemy ships came into view—a ragged line stretching endlessly in both directions, three deep. Our sudden assault caught them completely unprepared. It was as unexpected as lightning from a clear sky.

Every part of my plan unfolded flawlessly. Our massive ships sliced through the enemy line. Then the V opened wide, revealing a broad channel through which the transports raced toward the glistening temples of the therns, now clearly visible beneath the sun.

By the time the therns recovered, a hundred thousand green warriors were already storming their courts and gardens. Another hundred and fifty thousand leaned from low-flying transports, unleashing their uncanny marksmanship upon the defenders atop the ramparts and within the temple walls.

Above, the two great fleets clashed in a titanic aerial battle, far above the chaos below. Slowly, the Helium battleships closed their ends, forming a circle around the enemy—a hallmark of Barsoomian naval warfare.

The ships circled each other in tight spirals, maintaining high speed to make themselves difficult targets. Broadside after broadside, they fired into the thern vessels. The enemy tried to break the formation, but it was like trying to stop a buzz saw with bare hands.

From my vantage beside Kantos Kan, I watched ship after ship of the enemy plunge into the abyss—a sickening dive signaling total destruction. We maneuvered our death-circle higher, hovering above the gardens where our green allies fought.

The order was given: the green warriors were to re-embark. They ascended slowly into the center of the ring.

Meanwhile, the therns’ fire had nearly ceased. They had seen enough. They were eager to let us go in peace. But our escape was not to be so simple. As we began to move again toward Omean, a dark line appeared on the northern horizon—a fleet of warships.

We couldn’t guess their origin or destination. When they drew close enough, Kantos Kan’s operator received a radio-aerogram. He handed it to me.

“Kantos Kan,” it read. “Surrender, in the name of the Jeddak of Helium. You cannot escape.” Signed: *Zat Arras*.

The therns must have intercepted and decoded the message almost instantly. Realizing we faced a new threat, they renewed their attacks.

Before Zat Arras reached firing range, we were already locked in fierce combat with the thern fleet. As soon as he came near, he unleashed a torrent of heavy shells. Ship after ship staggered under the relentless bombardment.

It couldn’t last. I ordered the transports to descend once more into the thern gardens.

“Wreak your vengeance to the fullest,” I sent to the green allies. “For by nightfall, none will remain to avenge you.”

Soon, I saw the ten battleships sent to guard Omean—returning at full speed, their stern batteries blazing continuously. Only one explanation: they were being pursued by another enemy fleet. Well, the situation could not worsen. The expedition was doomed. No man who had joined it would return across the ice cap. How I wished I could face Zat Arras one last time with my longsword!

He was the cause of our failure.

As I watched, their pursuers emerged swiftly into view—an enormous fleet. For a moment, I couldn’t believe my eyes. Then I realized: the most catastrophic disaster had struck. The fleet I saw was none other than the navy of the First Born—the very force meant to be trapped inside Omean. A chain of misfortunes! What terrible fate shadowed me, that I should be thwarted at every turn in my search for my lost love?

Could it be the curse of Issus? Was there truly some malignant power in that monstrous corpse? I refused to believe it. With shoulders squared, I rushed below deck to join my men in repelling boarders from a thern craft that had grappled our side.

In the frenzy of hand-to-hand combat, my old courage flared anew. As thern after thern fell beneath my blade, I felt we might yet triumph—even from apparent defeat.

My presence inspired the crew. They attacked the white soldiers with terrifying ferocity. Within moments, we turned the tide. Soon, we swarmed onto their decks. I saw their commander leap from the bow—his surrender signal.

Then I rejoined Kantos Kan. He had observed the battle below and seemed struck by a new idea. He gave a swift order. Moments later, the colors of the Prince of Helium unfurled from every point of the flagship. A mighty cheer erupted from our ship, echoed by every vessel in our fleet as they hoisted my banner.

Then Kantos Kan executed his masterstroke. A signal, visible to every sailor in the sky, was strung aloft:

Soon, the Prince’s colors flew from one of Zat Arras’s ships. Then another. On some, fierce clashes raged between Zodangan sailors and Heliumetic crews. But eventually, the banners of Helium soared above every vessel that had followed Zat Arras—except his flagship.

Zat Arras had brought five thousand ships. The sky was black with three colossal fleets. Now it was Helium versus the field. The battle devolved into countless individual duels. Maneuvering was impossible in that crowded, fire-ravaged sky.

Zat Arras’s flagship neared mine. I could see his thin face from afar. His Zodangan crew poured broadsides into us—we answered with equal fury. Closer and closer the ships drew, until only yards separated them. Grapplers and boarders lined the rails.

We prepared for the final clash.

A single yard apart, the first grappling irons flew. I charged to the deck, determined to lead the boarding party. As the vessels met with a jolt, I forced through the lines and leapt onto Zat Arras’s deck—first among us.

Behind me, a roaring tide of Helium’s finest stormed aboard. Nothing could resist them in their battle rage.

The Zodangans collapsed under the wave. As my men cleared the lower decks, I surged forward to the main deck—where Zat Arras stood.

“You are my prisoner,” I declared. “Yield, and you shall have mercy.”

For a moment, I couldn’t tell if he would surrender or draw his sword. He hesitated—then dropped his arms and ran to the opposite side of the deck. Before I could reach him, he hurled himself headfirst over the rail into the endless void below.

And so died Zat Arras, Jed of Zodanga.

The battle raged on. The therns and blacks had not united against us. Wherever thern and First Born ships met, they fought each other. In this, I saw our salvation.

I sent word—through any channels safe from interception—that all our vessels were to withdraw rapidly, positioning themselves west and south of the fighting. I dispatched an air scout to the green warriors in the gardens to re-embark, and to the transports to join us.

I instructed my commanders: when engaged, lure the enemy quickly toward a ship of their ancient foe, and maneuver them into conflict—freeing us to retreat. The tactic worked perfectly. Just before sunset, I saw the remnants of my once-mighty fleet gathered twenty miles southwest of the ongoing carnage between the blacks and whites.

Now, I transferred Xodar to another battleship and sent him with all the transports and five thousand battleships directly overhead to the Temple of Issus. Carthoris, Kantos Kan, and I took the remaining ships and headed for the entrance to Omean.

Our new plan: launch a combined assault on Issus at dawn. Tars Tarkas and his green warriors, guided by Xodar, would land in the gardens or surrounding plains. Carthoris, Kantos Kan, and I would lead the smaller force from Omean through the underground passages beneath the temple—routes Carthoris knew well.

Now I learned why my ten ships had fled from the shaft’s mouth. When they arrived, the navy of the First Born was already emerging—twenty vessels had surfaced. Though they fought valiantly, the odds were too great, and they were forced to flee.

With caution, we approached the shaft under cover of darkness. At several miles out, I halted the fleet. Carthoris went ahead alone in a one-man flier to scout.

Half an hour later, he returned: no patrols, no sign of the enemy. We advanced silently toward Omean.

At the mouth of the shaft, we paused briefly for all vessels to take position. Then, with the flagship, I plunged into the black depths. One by one, the others followed.

We gambled everything on reaching Issus through the subterranean route. We left no guard at the shaft—nor could we have. Even if we had, we lacked the strength to withstand the vast navy of the First Born had they returned.

Our safety depended on boldness—believing the First Born would not realize an enemy fleet was entering Omean until too late.

That proved true. Four hundred of my five hundred ships rested safely on Omean’s surface before the first shot was fired. The battle was brief and fierce, but the outcome was certain: the First Born, lulled by false security, had stationed only a few ancient, obsolete hulks to guard their harbor.

At Carthoris’s suggestion, we landed our prisoners on two larger islands under guard. Then we towed the captured First Born ships to the shaft, wedging several firmly inside the great well. We activated their buoyancy rays, allowing the rest to rise and block the passage as they collided with the vessels already lodged there.

Now we believed the returning First Born would be delayed. We had ample time to reach the tunnels leading to Issus.

My first action: I personally led a strong force to the island housing the submarine. I took it without resistance.

Inside the pool, I found the submarine. I placed a strong guard on it and the island, staying to await Carthoris and the others.

Among the prisoners was Yersted, commander of the submarine. He recognized me from my three voyages with him during my captivity.

“How does it feel,” I asked, “to have the tables turned? To be prisoner of your former captive?”

He smiled—a grim, knowing smile.

“It won’t last long, John Carter,” he replied. “We’ve been expecting you. We are prepared.”

“So it seems,” I said. “You were ready to surrender without a fight.”

“The fleet must have missed you,” he said. “But it will return to Omean—and then, things will be different—for John Carter.”

“I don’t know that the fleet has missed me,” I said—but he didn’t understand. He only looked confused.

“Many prisoners travel to Issus in your grim craft, Yersted?” I asked.

“Very many,” he nodded.

“Do you remember one called Dejah Thoris?”

“Yes,” he replied. “Her beauty was unmatched. And because she was the wife of the first mortal ever to escape from Issus through all the ages of her godhood. And Issus remembers her best—as the wife of one and mother of another who dared raise hands against the Goddess of Life Eternal.”

I shuddered. I feared what cowardly revenge Issus might take on the innocent Dejah Thoris for her husband’s and son’s sacrilege.

“And where is Dejah Thoris now?” I asked—knowing he would say the words I dreaded, yet unable to resist hearing them from one who had seen her recently. It felt like bringing her closer.

“Yesterday,” Yersted said, “the monthly rites of Issus were held. I saw her—sitting in her usual place at the foot of Issus.”

“She is not dead, then?” I cried.

“No,” he replied. “It has been less than a year since she saw the divine glory of the radiant face of—”

“No year?” I interrupted.

“No,” he insisted. “Not more than three hundred and seventy or eighty days.”

A light exploded in my mind. How foolish I had been! I barely contained my joy. I had forgotten—Martian years are vastly longer than Earth years! The ten Earth years I had spent on Barsoom equaled only five Martian years and ninety-six days. Martian days are forty-one minutes longer than ours, and their year contains six hundred and eighty-seven days.

I am in time! I am in time! The words echoed in my mind—until I must have spoken them aloud, for Yersted shook his head.

“In time to save your Princess?” he asked. Without waiting, he added, “No, John Carter. Issus will not give up her own. She knows you are coming. Before a single vandal foot enters the Temple of Issus—if such a calamity should occur—Dejah Thoris will be taken forever from any hope of rescue.”

“You mean she will be killed merely to stop me?” I asked.

“Not necessarily,” he replied. “But consider the Temple of the Sun. That is where they will take her. It lies deep within the inner court of Issus’s temple—a small spire rising above the great towers. Beneath it, in the ground, lies the main structure: six hundred and eighty-seven circular chambers, stacked one below the other. Each chamber is accessed by a single corridor through solid rock from the pits of Issus.

“As the entire Temple of the Sun rotates once per Barsoom’s orbit around the sun, only once each year does the entrance to each chamber align with the corridor—its only connection to the outside world.”

I stared at him, understanding dawning. The timing of the ritual, the alignment—it meant she was still alive. And perhaps… just perhaps… I could still reach her.

Yersted’s warning left no room for delay. I had to reach the Temple of Issus before Tars Tarkas’s forces struck at dawn. Once inside, I was certain I could overpower the guards and rescue Dejah Thoris—provided I could bring my army with me. The moment Carthoris and the others joined me, we began moving our men through the submerged passage leading from the submarine pool to the pits beneath the temple. The journey required many trips, but eventually every soldier stood together again, ready for the final leg of our quest.

Five thousand strong, we were seasoned warriors of the fiercest race among the red Martians. But only Carthoris knew the hidden tunnels, so we couldn’t split into multiple attacks as we might have wished. Instead, we decided he would lead us swiftly toward the heart of the temple.

Just as we prepared to leave the pool and enter the corridor, an officer drew my attention to the water surrounding the submarine. At first, it seemed merely disturbed—like something massive stirred beneath the surface. I suspected another sub was closing in on us. But soon, it became clear: the water level was rising—not fast, but steadily. Soon it would spill over the edges of the pool and flood the chamber floor.

For a moment, I didn’t grasp the full horror of what was happening. It was Carthoris who understood immediately—its cause, its purpose.

“Haste!” he shouted. “If we wait, we’re all dead. They’ve shut down Omean’s pumps. They mean to drown us like rats. We must reach the upper levels before the flood overtakes us—or we’ll never make it.”

“Lead on, Carthoris,” I called. “We follow.”

At my order, the young prince leapt into the corridor, and the soldiers followed in disciplined columns of two, each company advancing only when their dwar—captain—gave the signal.

By the time the last unit filed out, the water already lapped at their ankles. The men were uneasy—unaccustomed to water beyond drinking or bathing. The sight of it rising around them filled them with instinctive dread. Yet they held their ground, a testament to their courage and training.

I was the last to leave the submarine chamber. As I stepped into the corridor, the water reached my knees. The passage itself was flooded to the same depth—the floor was level with the chamber, and there was no rise for many yards.

Our march was swift, but not swift enough. The water climbed behind us, gaining ground. I realized why: as the waters rose toward the dome’s apex, the space narrowed, causing the rate of rise to accelerate.

Before the column could reach safety, I knew the tide would surge forward in overwhelming force—dooming half our men.

Then I saw it—a side corridor branching off to the right, sloping sharply upward. The water now swirled around my waist. Men ahead were losing control, panicking.

Something had to be done—now.

I raised my voice to a roar. “Call back the last twenty-five utans! There’s a way out! Turn and follow me!”

My command was obeyed—nearly thirty utans turned, bringing about three thousand men back into the flood. As the first dwarf entered, I warned him: “Listen closely. Do not step out into the open or leave the pits until I join you. If I don’t come… know that I died before reaching you.”

He saluted and passed on. The men streamed past me, entering the rising corridor.

The water rose to my chest. Men stumbled, fell, were swept away. I pulled some up, but alone I couldn’t save them all. Others vanished beneath the churning current, never to rise again.

Then Gur Tus, a brave soldier and captain of the 10th utan, joined me. Together, we held the line, keeping the terrified troops in order and rescuing many who otherwise would have drowned.

Djor Kantos, son of Kantos Kan and padwar of the fifth utan, arrived next. From then on, not a single man was lost in the transfer.

When the final unit passed, the water surged to our necks. We stood hand-in-hand, holding our ground until the last man was safe.

Beyond the fork, the path ascended sharply. Within a hundred yards, we stood above the flood.

For a few moments, we pressed onward, hoping the climb would soon carry us to the upper pits and the temple.

But then came a cry—“Fire!”—from far ahead. Then screams, shouts of command. A report returned: “They’ve set fire to the pits ahead.”

“We’re trapped—flames in front, flood behind.”

“Help, John Carter—we’re suffocating!”

A wave of thick smoke rolled back from the fire, blinding us, choking us. We stumbled backward, retreating from the inferno.

There was no choice but to find another route. Fire and smoke were far more deadly than water. I seized the first gallery leading upward from the smoke-choked tunnel.

I stepped aside as the soldiers rushed through. Nearly two thousand passed rapidly. Then the flow stopped.

But I wasn’t sure everyone had escaped. I ran forward into the flames, driven by duty. The air was hot, thick, unbearable. But I pressed on until the firelight revealed the corridor ahead—clear of Helium’s soldiers. What lay beyond? I could not say. No one could survive that seething hell of burning chemicals.

Satisfied, I turned back.

To my horror, the exit was blocked. A massive steel grating had descended across the mouth of the corridor—sealed from above.

Now I understood. The First Born knew our every move. The attack on our fleet yesterday, the timing of the pumps’ failure, the fire in our path—none of it was chance. This was a trap, carefully laid. And now, this gate sealed me between fire and flood.

Invisible eyes watched us at every turn. How could I hope to rescue Dejah Thoris if my enemies never showed themselves?

I cursed myself—why had I fallen into such a scheme? I should have kept our force united, attacked the temple from the valley, relied on our strength and numbers. That would have been better.

But now, the smoke drove me deeper into the dark, back toward the rising waters. My men had taken the last torch. This corridor was unlit—no phosphorescent rock here. That meant I was near the upper pits, beneath the temple.

Soon, I felt water lap at my feet. Smoke choked me behind. My suffering grew unbearable.

There was only one choice: choose the easier death. I moved forward, swimming into the blackness, waiting for my head to strike the ceiling—my final resting place.

But then—unexpectedly—I hit a wall before the water reached the roof. I explored. Yes—it was the main corridor. Still, there was air above.

I turned, swimming back toward where Carthoris and the lead units had gone half an hour earlier.

Stroke after stroke, my spirits lifted. I was nearing dry land. I would feel solid ground again. I would reach the temple. I would see Dejah Thoris once more.

But just as hope soared, my head struck stone. The worst had come. I’d reached a drop—where the tunnel plunged downward beneath the waterline.

Somewhere ahead, it rose again. But how far? I could not return. Not now. I had gone too far.

Only one desperate hope remained.

I took a deep breath, dived beneath the surface, and swam through the ink-black, icy depths.

Time and again, I surfaced—only to meet the crushing weight of rock above. My lungs burned. My strength faded.

I knew I could not go back. Death waited. I felt his cold breath on my skin.

One final effort. I rose—weak, gasping—my body screaming for air.

And then—life.

Fresh air rushed into my starving lungs. I was saved.

A few strokes brought me to solid ground. I scrambled out, racing along the corridor, searching for any doorway—any way into the temple.

If I couldn’t save Dejah Thoris, I would avenge her. No life would satisfy me except that of the monster responsible.

Sooner than expected, I found an opening—on the right side of the corridor, likely leading to one of the temple’s chambers. I didn’t care which. Any entrance would do.

Without hesitation, I climbed the steep incline and pushed open the door.

It swung slowly inward. Before it could slam shut, I lunged through.

The chamber was brightly lit, though dawn had not yet broken. A lone figure lay on a low couch at the far end—apparently asleep.

From the rich hangings and luxurious furnishings, I guessed it belonged to a priestess—perhaps even Issus herself.

My blood surged. What if fortune had placed the creature alone, defenseless, in my hands? With her as hostage, I could demand anything.

Cautiously, I approached—silent, stealthy. Halfway across the room, the figure stirred.

I sprang forward.

She rose—and faced me.

Terror flashed across her face—then disbelief—then joy.

My heart pounded. Tears welled in my eyes. Words choked in my throat.

I opened my arms—and embraced her.

Dejah Thoris, Princess of Helium, was mine again.

“John Carter, John Carter,” she wept, her head resting against my shoulder, “I still cannot believe what I see. When Thuvia told me you had returned to Barsoom, I listened—but I could not grasp it. It seemed too great a joy for one who had endured so long in silent sorrow. And when I finally understood it was true, and learned the horror of my prison, I dared not hope you could reach me here.

Day after day passed, moon after moon, without a whisper of your return. I gave myself over to fate. And now—now that you are here—I still find it hard to trust my eyes. For hours, I’ve heard the clash of battle within this temple. I did not know what it meant, but I clung to a fragile hope—that it might be Helium’s warriors, led by my Prince.

And tell me—what of Carthoris? Our son?”

“He was with me only moments ago, Dejah Thoris,” I answered. “It must have been his men you heard fighting within these halls.”

“Where is Issus?” I asked suddenly.

Dejah Thoris shrugged. “She sent me under guard to this room just before the fighting began. She said she would summon me later. She was furious—and afraid. I’ve never seen her like this—shaken, trembling, almost broken. Now I understand why. She must have known you were coming—Prince of Helium—to demand justice for the imprisonment of your Princess.”

The sounds of combat echoed through the temple—clashing steel, shouts, pounding feet. I knew I had to join the fight. But I could not leave Dejah Thoris. And I could not take her into the chaos. Then I remembered the pits beneath us—the very tunnels from which I’d emerged. Why not hide her there until I could return?

I explained my plan. For a moment, she held me tighter.

“I cannot bear to be separated from you, even for a second, John Carter,” she whispered. “I tremble at the thought of being alone again—where that monster might find me. You don’t know her. No one can imagine her cruelty unless they’ve watched her every act for half a year. It took me nearly all this time to even accept what I’ve seen.”

“I won’t leave you,” I said.

She was quiet a moment. Then she drew my face to hers and kissed me.

“Go, John Carter,” she said. “Our son is out there. The soldiers of Helium are fighting for the Princess of Helium. Where they are, you must be. I cannot think of myself now—only of them, and of your duty. Hide me in the pits. Go.”

I led her to the door through which I had entered. I pressed her close, then—though my heart broke—guided her across the threshold. I kissed her once more, and closed the door behind her.

Without delay, I rushed toward the loudest noise.

I crossed half a dozen chambers before reaching the battlefield. Black warriors blocked the entrance to a vast hall, trying to stop red men from advancing toward the sacred inner sanctum. Emerging from behind, I found myself behind the black line. Without hesitation, I charged across the chamber, sword raised.

As I struck the first blow, I roared: “For Helium!”

The reds outside heard me. They surged forward, shouting, “John Carter! John Carter!” The blacks faltered. Before they could recover, the reds broke through.

What followed—had it been recorded—would be etched in Barsoom’s history. Five hundred men fought, black against red. No quarter asked, none given. It was survival itself, a brutal test of will and strength. We all knew this battle would shape the future of our world.

With Carthoris at my side, I fought not just for the red men—but for their freedom from a monstrous lie.

Back and forth we clashed. Blood pooled ankle-deep. Dead bodies littered the floor, so thick we stood upon them as we fought.

Then, as we advanced toward the great windows overlooking Issus’s gardens, I saw something that filled me with triumph.

“Look!” I shouted. “Men of the First Born—look!”

Fighting ceased. Every eye turned to where I pointed.

Across the gardens, a wavering line of black warriors held their ground. But beyond them—a tide of green warriors on mighty thoats, pushing them back.

From the rear, a figure rode forward—grim, fierce, commanding. Tars Tarkas, Jeddak of Thark. He raised his forty-foot lance, metal-shod. His legion mirrored him.

Twenty yards between them and the black line.

One word from Tars Tarkas—and with a thunderous cry, the green horde charged.

The black line shattered. The beasts and riders burst through. Red men surged after.

The green surrounded the temple. The red stormed inside.

We turned to resume our battle—only to find our enemies gone.

My mind flashed to Dejah Thoris.

“Carthoris!” I called. “I’ve found your mother. Come!”

We ran—my son beside me, survivors behind.

I burst into the chamber where I’d left her.

Someone had been there. A silk lay on the floor. A dagger. Metal ornaments—torn off in struggle.

Worst of all—the door to the pits stood slightly open.

I lunged forward, flung it wide.

Dejah Thoris was gone.

I screamed her name. No answer.

For an instant, I teetered on the edge of madness. Rage consumed me.

“Issus!” I roared. “Where is Issus? Search the temple—but no man harms her except John Carter. Carthoris—where are her chambers?”

“This way!” the boy yelled, dashing ahead.

I followed, urging him faster.

We reached a massive carved door. Carthoris burst through—then I.

Inside—Issus’s throne room. Slaves reclined. Soldiers stood in ranks.

We didn’t wait. I slashed two in the front rank. Then, using my momentum, I crashed through the rest and leapt onto the dais.

Issus cowered, trying to flee into a trap behind her.

This time, I wouldn’t be fooled.

Before she rose, I seized her arm. As guards rushed forward, I drew my dagger, pressing it to her chest.

“Back!” I commanded. “Back! One black foot on this platform—and the dagger ends her.”

They hesitated. Then an officer ordered them back.

From the corridor, a thousand red men poured in—Kantos Kan, Hor Vastus, Xodar.

“Where is Dejah Thoris?” I demanded.

Her eyes darted wildly. She hadn’t yet grasped that the temple had fallen. That her power was gone. That her lies were exposed.

Then, from the highest noble—the high priest—the prime minister—came a desperate plea:

“Issus, Goddess of Death, of Life Eternal! Arise! Strike down the blasphemers! Save your people! Daughter of the Lesser Moon—only you can save us! We await your will. Strike!”

And then—she snapped.

A shrieking madwoman writhed in my grip. Biting, clawing, laughing with a sound that froze blood.

The slaves screamed. She spat at them, frothing.

I shook her violently.

“Where is Dejah Thoris?”

She mumbled—then grinned, cunning flashing in her eyes.

“Dejah Thoris? Ah—yes. And Thuvia. And Phaidor, daughter of Matai Shang. Each loves John Carter. Ha-ah! How amusing. Together, they will meditate in the Temple of the Sun—for a year. But before the year ends… no more food. Ho-oh! What divine entertainment.” She licked her lips. “No more food—except each other. Ha-ah! Ha-ah!”

Horror seized me. My Princess—condemned to starvation, to cannibalism.

I trembled with rage.

Like a terrier shaking a rat, I shook her.

“Countermand your orders!” I roared. “Recall them! Or you die!”

“It is too late,” she giggled. “Ha-ah!”

My dagger rose—aimed at her heart.

But something stopped me.

I would not stain my hands with her blood.

Instead, I turned to the gathered First Born.

“You have seen today,” I declared, “the impotence of Issus. The gods are false. Issus is not a god—she is a cruel old woman who deceived you for ages. Take her. John Carter, Prince of Helium, refuses to soil himself with her blood.”

And I hurled her—from her throne—into the arms of her betrayed people.

I spotted Xodar among the officers.

“Lead me to the Temple of the Sun,” I ordered.

Without waiting, I raced from the chamber—Xodar, Carthoris, Hor Vastus, Kantos Kan, and others following.

We sped through inner halls, until we stood in the central court—a vast circular space paved with translucent white marble.

Before us rose the golden Temple of the Sun, adorned with diamonds, rubies, sapphires, emeralds, and gems unknown on Earth.

“This way,” Xodar said, pointing to a tunnel beside the temple.

Just as we stepped toward it, a deep roar erupted from the Temple of Issus.

A red warrior burst from a gate.

“The blacks have set fire to the temple!” he shouted. “It burns in a hundred places. Haste to the outer gardens—or you’ll perish!”

We saw smoke pouring from windows. Above the minaret, a growing cloud of smoke.

“Go back!” I cried. “You—return! Xodar—point the way. Leave me. I will reach my Princess.”

“Follow me, John Carter,” Xodar replied—and dashed into the tunnel.

I followed, plunging through tier after tier of galleries.

At last, we reached a level passage. Ahead—a lit chamber.

Massive bars blocked the way.

But beyond—Dejah Thoris. With her—Thuvia and Phaidor.

When she saw me, she rushed to the bars.

The chamber had already begun its slow rotation. Only part of the opening remained aligned.

The gap was closing. Soon—just a crack. Then nothing.

And for a whole Barsoomian year—no light, no air, no escape.

“Xodar!” I cried. “Can anyone stop this thing? Is there someone who knows the secret of these bars?”

“None I can reach in time,” he said. “But I’ll try. Wait here.”

He vanished.

I stood with Dejah Thoris, holding her hand through the bars.

Thuvia stepped back—knowing we needed privacy.

But Phaidor came near.

“John Carter,” she said softly, “this may be the last time you see any of us. Tell me—you love me. Let me die happy.”

“I love only the Princess of Helium,” I replied quietly. “I’m sorry, Phaidor—but I’ve always told you the truth.”

She bit her lip. Turned away. But I saw the hatred in her eyes—directed at Dejah Thoris.

She stood apart, but not far enough.

We spoke in hushed tones.

The gap narrowed.

Above—echoes of battle. Smoke drifted down.

Then—shouting at the end of the corridor.

“Come back, John Carter!” a voice cried. “Even the pits are burning!”

Dozens of men burst through the smoke—Carthoris, Kantos Kan, Hor Vastus, Xodar.

“There is no hope,” Xodar said. “The keeper of the keys is dead. His keys are gone. Our only chance is to extinguish the fire—and pray that your Princess survives the year. I’ve brought food. Once the crack closes, no smoke will reach them. If we act fast, they may live.”

“Go,” I said. “Take them. I stay. I shall remain here—beside my Princess—until death ends my pain. I do not wish to live.”

As I spoke, Xodar tossed cans into the cell.

The gap was now less than an inch wide.

Dejah Thoris leaned as close as possible, whispering words of courage.

Then—beyond her—I saw Phaidor’s face twisted in fury.

Her eyes met mine.

“Think not,” she hissed, “that you can cast aside Phaidor’s love so easily. Nor dream of holding Dejah Thoris again. Wait the long year—but know this: when it ends, it will be *my* arms that welcome you—not hers. Behold—she dies!”

She raised a dagger.

Then—Thuvia stepped between them.

A wave of smoke blotted the scene.

A single scream tore through the air.

Then—silence.

The smoke cleared.

Only a blank wall remained.

The last crack had sealed.

For a full year—no one would know what happened within that dread chamber.

They urged me to go.

“In a moment, it will be too late!” Xodar cried. “There’s barely a chance we’ll survive. I’ve ordered the pumps—five minutes, and the pits will flood. If we don’t move now, we drown like rats.”

“Go,” I said. “Leave me. Let me die here. When they carry her body out next year—let them find her lord waiting.”

After that—my memory fades.

I seem to have struggled with many men.

Then—lifted from the ground.

Carried away.

I never asked what happened.

Nor has anyone spoken of it.

Not since that day.

Ah—if only I knew…

Whether the assassin’s blade pierced one fair breast or another—

Only time will tell.
