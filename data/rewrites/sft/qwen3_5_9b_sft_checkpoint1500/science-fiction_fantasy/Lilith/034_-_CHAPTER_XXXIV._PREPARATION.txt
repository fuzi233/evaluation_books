With victory secured, the woman of Bulika turned her gaze to the city behind them. She spoke at length of its defenselessness, condemning the wickedness of its princess and the cowardice of its people. Within days, the chatter of the children filled the air, though they possessed no true concept of what a "city" entailed. It was then that the design of the woman first revealed itself to me, though the deeper motives remained hidden.

To Lona—and to me—the notion of seizing this place proved irresistible. The children's faculties were blossoming with terrifying rapidity, presenting no insurmountable barrier to success. I knew the terrible Lilith, whether woman or leopardess, possessed one fatal vulnerability: her doom lay through her daughter, and the ancient prophecy haunted the minds of the citizens. Whatever risks the enterprise entailed, they were justified by the stakes. If successful, the Little Ones, drawn from a mere crowd of children, would swiftly evolve into a youth of righteous stature. Would they not rule the wicked with an iron rod, redeeming the nation?

Yet, I must confess, I harbored personal ambitions alongside these grand designs. It seemed just that Lona ascend the throne her mother had occupied, and natural that I serve as her consort and minister. I envisioned spending my life in her service; between us, bolstered by the core of the Little Ones, what noble state might we not build? A foolish dream also crossed my mind—a gem trade between the two worlds—but happily, I recognized its impossibility and potential harm.

Recalling Adam's plea, I proposed to Lona that finding water might accelerate the children's growth. She deemed it prudent to postpone the search, fearing unknown consequences, trusting instead that time would inevitably impose a new necessity upon them.

"They are what they are without it!" she declared. "Once we hold the city, we will seek water!"

Thus, preparations commenced. We marshaled our merry troops, reviewing them constantly. Lona focused on logistics, while I drilled the little soldiers. I taught them to throw stones, wielded unfamiliar weapons, and forged them into warriors. The primary challenge was rallying them instantly to the flag at the signal. Most carried slings; the larger boys bore bows and arrows. The older girls wielded aloe-spikes—strong as steel, sharp as needles, mounted on sturdy shafts, formidable in their simplicity. Their sole role was to support those too young to fight.

Lona had grown significantly, yet she remained unaware of it. She was, as ever, the tallest. Her hair had lengthened, and she approached womanhood, yet none of childhood's fleeting beauties had faded from her. When we reunited after our long separation, she laid down her infant, embraced me silently, and clung to me with a glow of pure joy. The child whimpered, and she sprang to lift him instantly into her bosom. To witness her with any thoughtless, obstinate, or irritable toddler evoked the image of a tender grandmother. I felt as though I had known her forever—from before time began. I scarcely recalled my mother, but in my mind's eye, she resembled Lona; if I imagined a sister or a child, Lona's face invariably emerged. Every thought of mine flew to her. She was my heart's wife. Though she rarely sought me, she was perpetually within earshot. Whatever I did or thought, I referred to her, rejoicing in the belief that while she worked in absolute independence, she thrived most near me. She never neglected the smallest child, and my love for her sharpened my sense of duty. To love her and to fulfill my duty seemed inseparable, not merely parallel paths. She might suggest actions or query my counsel, yet she never appeared to believe I cared for anything save what *must* be done. Her love flowed over me—not in caresses, but in a closeness of recognition akin to the devotion of a divine beast.

I never spoke to her of her mother.

The woods teemed with birds, their plumage a splendor that compensated for the absence of flowers, which clearly required water to bloom. Everywhere, glorious feathers drifted in the forest air. This inspired me to weave a garment for Lona from them. Gathering the plumes, I bound them in overlapping rows. She watched with evident appreciation, never asking what I fashioned, but waiting expectantly for the result. Within a fortnight, the work was complete: a long, loose mantle to fasten at the throat and waist, with openings for the arms.

I rose and draped it upon her. She stood, removed it, and placed it at my feet—I suspected a sense of propriety drove her. I lifted it again, showing her how to pass her arms through. She smiled, gazed briefly at the feathers, stroked them gently, then laid it down beside her once more. When she departed, she carried it with her, and I saw it no further for several days. Eventually, she returned wearing it, accompanied by another garment she had crafted similarly from the dried leaves of a tough evergreen. These leaves possessed the strength of leather and the appearance of scale armor. I donned mine immediately, and henceforth, whenever we rode, we wore these garbments.

Indeed, on the forest's outskirts, a troop of full-grown horses had appeared one day. Unalarmed by creatures of such distinct form, I quickly befriended them, training two of the finest for Lona and myself. Already accustomed to riding smaller mounts, her delight was immense when she first looked down from the back of a giant beast; the horse, proud of his burden, responded with grace. We exercised them daily until obedience became instinctive and fear vanished. Thus, they joined our parades and marches.

The undertaking occasionally seemed foolhardy, yet the woman of Bulika's confidence—real or feigned—dispelled my hesitation. The princess's magic, she insisted, would fail against the children. As for any forces the princess might summon, our animal allies ensured our superiority. She claimed readiness to face any two men of Bulika with a simple stick, confessing only a slight fear of the leopardess. I, however, felt prepared for her. Yet, I shrank from bringing *all* the children.

"Would it not be wiser," I asked, "if you remained in the forest with your baby and the smallest of the Little Ones?"

She replied that she relied heavily on the impression their presence would make on the women, particularly the mothers.

"When they see the darlings," she said, "their hearts will be swept away. I must be there to encourage them to stand! If any hardiness remains in the place, it will be found among the women!"

"You must not encumber yourself," I urged Lona, "with any of the children; you will be needed everywhere!"

There were two other babies besides hers, and even atop a horse, she often cradled one.

"I do not recall ever being without a child to tend," she answered. "But when we reach the city, it shall be as you wish!"

Her confidence in one who had failed so unworthily shamed me. Nor had I initiated the movement, nor grounds to oppose it. I had no choice but to lend my utmost aid. For myself, I was ready to live or die with Lona. Her humility and trust humbled me, and I threw myself heartily into her purpose.

Our path lay across a grassy plain, sparing the need for fodder for the horses and the two cows accompanying us for the infants. However, the elephants required provision. Although the grass suited them, it grew short, and with their slender trunks, they could not gather enough for a single meal. Consequently, we tasked the entire colony with gathering grass and making hay, sufficient for the elephants to carry for several days, supplemented by fresh harvests at every halt. For the bears, we stored nuts; for ourselves, we dried abundant fruit. Having captured and tamed several more large horses, we loaded them and the elephants with provisions, preparing to depart.

Then Lona and I conducted a general review, and I addressed them. I told them I had learned much of their origin and knew where they came from. "We did not come from anywhere," they cried, interrupting me. "We are here!"

I explained that every one of them had a mother, like the mother of the last baby. I believed they had been brought from Bulika when so small they could not remember. There, a wicked princess feared babies so deeply and sought to destroy them that their mothers had fled, leaving them where the tyrant could not find them. Now, we journeyed to Bulika to find their mothers and liberate them from the giantess.

"But I must warn you," I continued, "there is danger ahead. To take the city, we may have to fight fiercely."

"We can fight! We are ready!" shouted the boys.

"Yes, you can," I returned. "And I know you will: mothers are worth fighting for! Only remember, you must all stay together."

"Yes, yes! We'll look out for each other!" they answered. "No one shall touch one of us but his own mother!"

"You must heed every command given by your officers immediately!"

"We will, we will!--Now we're ready! Let us go!"

"One final thing," I added. "When you strike, ensure it is a full, swinging blow. When you shoot an arrow, draw it fully to the head. When you sling a stone, hurl it strong and straight."

"That we will!" they cried with jubilant, fearless shouts.
