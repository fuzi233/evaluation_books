From this day forward, my existence settled into a rhythm devoid of variety, a steady beat that Madame Beck, with her characteristic approval of my social circle, found entirely acceptable. Indeed, her regard for me had evolved from simple respect into a calculated distinction. She possessed a worldly wisdom that was neither fawning nor weak; her pursuit of self-interest was measured, her grasp on gain calm and considerate. Without ever risking my contempt as a time-server, she displayed a shrewd tact in noting that those associated with her establishment should frequent acquaintances capable of elevation rather than those prone to deterioration. She rarely praised me or my friends directly, yet on one occasion, seated comfortably in the garden sun with coffee and the Gazette, she granted me leave of absence with a gracious flourish: "Oui, oui, ma bonne amie: je vous donne la permission de cœur et de gré. Your work here has always been admirable, filled with zeal and discretion; you have every right to enjoy yourself. Go out as much as you wish. As for your choice of company, I am pleased; it is wise, dignified, and praiseworthy." With that, she closed her lips and returned to her paper.

It is a minor circumstance, yet significant: around this time, the triply-enclosed packet of five letters vanished from my bureau. My initial reaction was blank dismay, but I quickly summoned patience. "Wait peaceably," I whispered to myself. "They will return." And they did. They had merely visited Madame's chamber for a brief inspection; having passed their scrutiny, they returned duly and truly, found intact the following day.

I wonder what Madame Beck thought of my correspondence? How did she evaluate Dr. John Bretton's epistolary powers? Did she appreciate the pithy, sound, and occasionally original opinions set forth in an easy, spirited style? How did she view the genial, half-humorous vein that brought me such delight, or the few kind words scattered sparingly, like gems in unfabled beds? Oh, Madame Beck! How did these things appear to you?

I suspect the five letters found favor in her eyes. One day, after she had "borrowed" them from me—a suave little woman deserving of suave terms—I caught her examining me with a steady, contemplative gaze, puzzled but not malevolent. During the brief interval between lessons, when the pupils played in the courtyard, she and I remained alone in the first classe. Meeting her eye, her thoughts forced their way through her lips. "Il y a," she said, "quelque chose de bien remarquable dans le caractère Anglais." "How, Madame?" I asked. She laughed softly, repeating the word "how" in English. "Je ne saurais vous dire 'how,' but finally, the English have their own ideas regarding friendship, love, and everything else. But at least, there is no need to monitor them," she added, trotting away like a compact little pony. "Then I hope," I murmured to myself, "you will graciously let my letters alone for the future."

Alas, a sudden rush of tears dimmed my vision, blotting out the schoolroom, the garden, and the bright winter sun. I remembered that those letters, such as she had read, would never come to me again. I had seen the last of them. That goodly river, whose banks I had sojourned and whose waves had trickled to my lips, was bending to another course, leaving my little hut and field forlorn and sand-dry to pour its wealth far away. The change was right, just, and natural; yet I loved my Rhine, my Nile; I had almost worshipped my Ganges, and I grieved that the grand tide should roll estranged, vanishing like a false mirage. Though stoical, I was not quite a stoic; drops streamed fast on my hands and desk. I wept one sultry shower, heavy and brief.

Soon, however, I reasoned with myself: "The Hope I am bemoaning suffered and made me suffer much; it did not die until it was full time. Following an agony so lingering, death ought to be welcome." Welcome I endeavored to make it. Long pain had made patience a habit. In the end, I closed the eyes of my dead, covered its face, and composed its limbs with great calm.

Yet, the letters must be put away, out of sight. Those who have undergone bereavement jealously gather and lock away mementos; it is unbearable to be stabbed to the heart each moment by a sharp revival of regret.

One vacant holiday afternoon, intending to consider their final disposal, I discovered with a strong impulse of displeasure that the packet had been tampered with. The ribbon securing it had been untied and retied; other symptoms revealed that my drawer had been visited. This was too much. Madame Beck herself was the soul of discretion, possessing a brain and judgment as sound as any human head; knowing the contents of my casket was unpleasant but bearable. However, the idea that she had ventured to communicate this information to others—that she might have amused herself with a companion over documents most sacred to me—shocked me cruelly. I feared she had shared them, and I even guessed her confidant. Her kinsman, M. Paul Emanuel, had spent the previous evening with her; she was accustomed to consulting him on matters broached to no one else. That very morning, in class, he had favored me with a glance borrowed from the actress Vashti—a blue, lurid flash of anger. He, I believed, was not apt to judge me with tolerance or candor; I had always found him severe and suspicious. The thought that these mere friendly letters had fallen, and might fall again, into his hands jarred my very soul.

What should I do to prevent this? Where in this strange house could I find security or secrecy? Where could a key be a safeguard, or a padlock a barrier?

The attic? No, I disliked the grenier. Most boxes and drawers there were mouldering and unlocked; rats gnawed through the decayed wood, and mice nested among the litter. My dear letters, though "Ichabod" was written on their covers, might be consumed by vermin, the writing obliterated by damp. No, the grenier would not do.

While pondering this problem, I sat in the dormitory window-seat. It was a fine frosty afternoon; the winter sun, already setting, gleamed pale on the tops of the garden-shrubs in the "allée défendue." A great old pear-tree—the nun's pear-tree—stood up a tall, dryad skeleton, grey, gaunt, and stripped. A thought struck me, one of those queer, fantastic notions that sometimes visit solitary people. Putting on my bonnet, cloak, and furs, I went out into the city.

Bending my steps toward the old historical quarter of the town, whose hoar and overshadowed precincts I always sought in melancholy moods, I wandered from street to street until I found myself before a broker's shop, an ancient place full of ancient things. What I wanted was a metal box to be soldered, or a thick glass jar stoppered and sealed hermetically. Amongst miscellaneous heaps, I found and purchased the latter.

I rolled my letters, wrapped them in oiled silk, bound them with twine, and placed them in the bottle. I engaged the old Jew broker to stopper, seal, and make it air-tight. While obeying my directions, he glanced at me suspiciously from under his frost-white eyelashes, believing some evil deed was on hand. In all this, I felt a dreary something—not pleasure, but a sad, lonely satisfaction. The impulse controlling me was similar to that which had induced me to visit the confessional. With quick walking, I regained the pensionnat just at dark, in time for dinner.

At seven o'clock the moon rose. At half-past seven, when the pupils and teachers were studying, Madame Beck was with her mother and children in the salle-à-manger, the half-boarders were gone, Rosine had left the vestibule, and all was still—I shawled myself, took the sealed jar, and stole out through the first-classe door, into the berceau and thence into the "allée défendue."

Methusaleh, the pear-tree, stood at the further end of this walk, near my seat. He rose up, dim and gray, above the lower shrubs. Though very old, he was of sound timber, save for a deep hollow near his root, hidden partly by ivy and creepers. There I meditated hiding my treasure, but I was not only going to hide a treasure; I meant also to bury a grief. That grief over which I had lately wept, as I wrapped it in its winding-sheet, must be interred.

I cleared away the ivy and found the hole, large enough to receive the jar. I thrust it deep within. In a tool-shed at the bottom of the garden lay the relics of building materials left by masons repairing the premises. Fetching a slate and some mortar, I placed the slate on the hollow, secured it with cement, covered the hole with black mould, and finally replaced the ivy. This done, I rested, leaning against the tree, lingering like any other mourner beside a newly-sodded grave.

The air of the night was very still, yet dim with a peculiar mist that changed the moonlight into a luminous haze. In this air, or this mist, there was some quality—electrical, perhaps—that acted strangely upon me. I felt as I had felt a year ago in England, on a night when the aurora borealis streamed across the heavens, watching that mustering of an army with banners, the quivering of serried lances, the swift ascent of messengers from below the North Star to the dark, high keystone of heaven's arch. I felt, not happy, far otherwise, but strong with reinforced strength.

If life be a war, it seemed my destiny to conduct it single-handed. I pondered how to break up my winter-quarters, to leave an encampment where food and forage failed. Perhaps another pitched battle must be fought with fortune; if so, I had a mind to the encounter: too poor to lose, God might destine me to gain. But what road was open? What plan available?

On this question I was still pausing when the moon, so dim hitherto, seemed to shine out somewhat brighter. A ray gleamed even white before me, and a shadow became distinct and marked. I looked more narrowly to make out the cause of this well-defined contrast appearing suddenly in the obscure alley: whiter and blacker it grew on my eye; it took shape with instantaneous transformation. I stood about three yards from a tall, sable-robed, snowy-veiled woman.

Five minutes passed. I neither fled nor shrieked. She was there still. I spoke. "Who are you? and why do you come to me?" She stood mute. She had no face—no features; all below her brow was masked with a white cloth, but she had eyes, and they viewed me.

I felt, if not brave, yet a little desperate; and desperation will often suffice to fill the post and do the work of courage. I advanced one step. I stretched out my hand, intending to touch her. She seemed to recede. I drew nearer; her recession, still silent, became swift. A mass of shrubs, full-leaved evergreens, laurel and dense yew, intervened between me and what I followed. Having passed that obstacle, I looked and saw nothing. I waited. I said, "If you have any errand to men, come back and deliver it." Nothing spoke or re-appeared.

This time there was no Dr. John to whom to have recourse; there was no one to whom I dared whisper the words, "I have again seen the nun."

Paulina Mary sought my frequent presence in the Rue Crécy. In the old Bretton days, though she had never professed herself fond of me, my society had soon become to her a sort of unconscious necessity. I used to notice that if I withdrew to my room, she would speedily come trotting after me, opening the door and peeping in with her little peremptory accent: "Come down. Why do you sit here by yourself? You must come into the parlour."

In the same spirit she urged me now: "Leave the Rue Fossette," she said, "and come and live with us. Papa would give you far more than Madame Beck gives you."

Mr. Home himself offered me a handsome sum—thrice my present salary—if I would accept the office of companion to his daughter. I declined. I think I should have declined had I been poorer than I was, and with scantier funds, more stinted prospects. I had not that vocation. I could teach; I could give lessons; but to be either a private governess or a companion was unnatural to me. Rather than fill the former post in any great house, I would deliberately have taken a housemaid's place, bought a strong pair of gloves, swept bedrooms and staircases, and cleaned stoves and locks, in peace and independence. Rather than be a companion, I would have made shirts and starved.

I was no bright lady's shadow—not Miss de Bassompierre's. Overcast enough it was my nature often to be; of a subdued habit I was: but the dimness and depression must both be voluntary—such as kept me docile at my desk, in the midst of my now well-accustomed pupils in Madame Beck's first classe; or alone, at my own bedside, in her dormitory, or in the alley and seat which were called mine, in her garden: my qualifications were not convertible, nor adaptable; they could not be made the foil of any gem, the adjunct of any beauty, the appendage of any greatness in Christendom. Madame Beck and I, without assimilating, understood each other well. I was not her companion, nor her children's governess; she left me free: she tied me to nothing—not to herself—not even to her interests.
