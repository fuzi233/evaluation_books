A ragged sigh escaped the dying chest of Amos Cadwalader, a sound louder than any spoken word. It spoke of a grief raw and bleeding, an agony that refused to heal despite thirty years having passed since the terrible hour Felix described.

Felix echoed the sentiment, his voice urgent. "It was dusk when I told my tale. From deepening shadow until dawn, we sat motionless, eyes locked upon one another's faces, sleepless and unrested. Then we sought out John Poindexter."

"And how did he receive us?" Thomas asked, leaning forward.

"Had he fled, we might have found mercy in his absence," Felix replied. "But he met us openly, quietly, with the detached indifference of a man who cannot measure emotion because he is incapable of feeling it himself. His opening words revealed this truth immediately: *'Spare yourselves, spare me all useless recriminations. The girl is dead; I cannot call her back. Live your lives—eat, drink, spend, acquire. There are but a few years left anyway. Why dwell on ancient sorrows?'* His final remark was a victory cry: *'When a man cares for nothing and no one, it is futile to curse him.'*"

"That was it!" Amos gasped, clutching his sheets. "That was the secret of his power. He cared for nothing, loved no one—not even himself. We felt the blow strike us down, bending us low. Yet, before we left his house and the town, you and I, Thomas, swore an oath. We vowed that someday, somehow, we would make that cold heart bleed. We promised to cause that impassive nature to suffer exactly as he had made us suffer, regardless of his apparent happiness or the security of his fortune. That was thirty years ago, and the oath remains unfulfilled."

Felix paused. Thomas lifted his head, eager to speak, but the old man silenced him with a sharp gesture.

"Some men forget in a month; others in a year," Amos continued, his voice trembling with feverish intensity. "Neither I nor Felix have ever forgotten. When you were born—I had remarried, seeking renewed joy—I felt, though I knew not why, that Evelyn's avenger had arrived. And when, a year later, we learned that God had seemingly forgiven John Poindexter's sins—or perhaps merely delayed judgment—and granted him a child after eighteen barren years of marriage, I looked upon your beautiful face. In those features, I saw, or believed I saw, the instrument destined to execute the vengeance we had dedicated our lives to achieving."

"You grew," Amos pressed, ignoring Thomas's growing unease. "Your ardent nature, your generous temper, and your quick mind promised a brilliant adulthood. When your mother died, leaving me a widower for the second time, I hesitated no longer. I devoted you entirely to the purpose for which you seemed born. Thomas, do you remember the start of that journey that took you far from me? Do you recall how I carried you on my shoulders along a dusty road? Until we reached the outskirts of his home, I lifted you up, pointed to the distant gables looming black against the twilight gold, and dedicated you to the destruction of whatever happiness might ever bloom beneath that infant's smile?"

Thomas recoiled in horror at the memory. "Father..."

"I did not think you could forget!" Amos interrupted, seizing Thomas's arm with desperate strength. "And now, the moment has come to fulfill that promise. I call upon you again, Thomas. Avenge our griefs. Avenge your sister. *Poindexter's girl has grown to womanhood.*"

At the implication hidden in those words, Thomas flinched as if struck. But the old man misread his terror. Clinging to his arm, he spoke with passionate fervor. "Court her! Win her! They do not know you. To them, you will simply be Thomas Adams, not Thomas Cadwalader. Gather this budding flower to your bosom, and then—oh, a man must love his child! Through her, we hold the key to his heart. Make her suffer. She is but a simple country girl, while you have lived in Paris. Make her suffer, and if, in doing so, you cause him to tremble, then believe me when I tell you I watch over you from the grave I am about to enter. Be happy, for you will not have lived in vain, nor will I have died in vain."

Amos paused, fighting for breath, but his iron will triumphed over mortality, casting a spell that confused Thomas's instincts and turned him into a puppet of accumulated hatred—a rage that had taken his father and brother twenty-five years to forge, yet filled Thomas completely in a single hour.

"I am dying," Amos panted. "I shall never live to see your triumph. I shall never witness John Poindexter's eyes cloud with the agonies that rend the entrails and make a man question the existence of God. But I shall know it in the realm where I am going. No mound of earth can hold my spirit down when John Poindexter feels his doom approaching. I shall be conscious of his anguish and rejoice. And when, in the depths of darkness to which I descend, he comes faltering along my path—"

"Boy, boy, you were reared for this," Amos urged, his voice rising. "God made you handsome; fate has made you strong; you have forged yourself into intelligence and accomplishment. All you must do is reveal yourself to this country girl, and you will become the master of her will and affection. Once hers, remember *me*. Remember *Evelyn!* "

Never before had Thomas witnessed such consuming passion. It swept him along in a torrent of fire against which he struggled uselessly. Raising his hand in what he intended as a response, he tried to speak but choked on his own hesitation. Misinterpreting his silence, the old man cried bitterly, "You are mute! You dislike the task; perhaps you are virtuous—you who have spent years alone and free in Paris. Or perhaps you possess instincts of honor, habits of generosity that blind you to wrongs that have screamed to heaven for justice longer than your entire lifetime. Thomas, Thomas, if you fail me now—"

"He will not fail you," a calm, suave, and insinuating voice cut in.

It was Felix. Thomas shuddered; he had momentarily forgotten his brother, but hearing those words forced him to turn toward the man who had pledged to guarantee the completion of this unholy deed. Standing there was a figure who commanded attention, admiration, and yes, fear. He was not larger than Thomas, but the frenzied will evident on his father's lips manifested instead as quiet, inflexible resolve in the gray eyes fixed upon him. A steel band seemed to constrict Thomas's heart, yet he simultaneously recognized the elegance and perfect training of the man before him. Even the aura of poverty that clung to his father vanished in Felix's presence. Dressed impeccably, Felix stood as the superior elder brother in every regard demanded by a Parisian gentleman. Behind him, a heavy curtain of dark green serge stretched from floor to ceiling, throwing his pale features into stark relief. For an instant, Thomas wondered if the drapery was staged specifically to create this dramatic effect.

But the demand in his brother's face stole his focus. Bowing his head, Thomas stammered, "I am at your command, Felix. I am at your command, Father. I cannot say more. Only remember that I never saw Evelyn; she died before I was born, and that I—"

Felix's voice broke in, kind yet measured. "Perhaps there is an obstacle we have not considered. Perhaps you already love another woman and intend to marry her. If so, it need be no impediment—"

"No!" Thomas erupted, indignation surging through him. "My heart is whole, save for a few fleeting fantasies that are rapidly fading into dreams." He seemed to have aged decades since stepping into the room.

"Your heart will remain undisturbed," Felix noted smoothly. "I visited the girl myself a year ago. She is as pale as a snowdrop and as listless. You will not be required to recall the radiant smiles of Parisian beauties to resist her charms."

"She must be made to taste the full intoxication of hope," Felix continued, his voice clear and cutting. "To realize despair, she must first experience every delight of satisfied love. Do you possess the skill and the heart to play a role requiring patience, dissimulation, courage, subtlety, and that fusion of will and implacability that feeds on tears and grows stronger through the suffering of its victim?"

"I have the skill," Thomas murmured, "but—"

"You lack the incentive," Felix finished.

"Well, well, we must allow patience for your doubts and hesitations," Felix conceded. "Our hate has been nurtured by memories of a girl you claim never to have seen. Look, then, Thomas. Look at your sister as she was, as she is to us. Look at her, and imagine her despoiled, murdered, and forgotten by Poindexter. Have you ever gazed upon a more moving countenance, or one where beauty fights a sharper prophecy of woe?"

Uncertain of what awaited him, fearing he might meet her ghost, Thomas followed the direction of his brother's raised hand. Where moments prior the dismal curtain had blocked the view, there now burned a blaze of light. Within it stood a charming yet tragic figure, such as no gallery in all Europe had ever displayed
