Chafing at custom's chain;
Again from its brumal sleep
Wakens the ferine strain."

Buck did not read the newspapers, nor would he have known that a storm was gathering—not just for him, but for every tide-water dog of strong muscle and warm, long hair stretching from Puget Sound to San Diego. Men, groping blindly in the Arctic dark, had stumbled upon a yellow metal. Steamships and transport lines were booming the news, and thousands of men were rushing north. They wanted dogs, specifically heavy ones with powerful muscles to toil and thick furs to withstand the frost.

Buck lived in a sun-drenched domain in the Santa Clara Valley, the estate of Judge Miller. The house stood back from the road, half-hidden among trees that framed glimpses of a wide, cool veranda circling its four sides. Gravelled driveways wound through sprawling lawns beneath the interlacing boughs of tall poplars. Behind the house, the scale was even grander: great stables where a dozen grooms and boys gathered, rows of vine-clad servants' cottages, endless outhouses, long grape arbors, green pastures, orchards, and berry patches. There was even a pumping plant for the artesian well and a massive cement tank where the Judge's boys plunged to cool off in the heat.

Over this vast demesne Buck reigned. Born here and living here for four years, he was the undisputed sovereign. While other dogs existed—Toots, the Japanese pug, and Ysabel, the Mexican hairless, who rarely ventured outdoors; or the score of fox terriers who yelped fearful threats at them from behind windows—they were insignificant. Buck was neither a pampered house-dog nor a kennel-bound animal. He swam in the tank, hunted with the Judge's sons, escorted the daughters, Mollie and Alice, on twilight rambles, and lay at the Judge's feet before roaring library fires. He carried the grandsons on his back, rolled them in the grass, and guarded their footsteps through wild adventures down to the stable yard and beyond. Among the terriers he stalked with imperial disdain, ignoring the pugs entirely, for he was king over all creeping, crawling, and flying things on Judge Miller's place, humans included.

His father, Elmo, a massive St. Bernard, had been the Judge's inseparable companion, and Buck seemed destined to follow in his father's footsteps. Though lighter than his sire, weighing only one hundred and forty pounds due to his mother, Shep, a Scotch shepherd, Buck possessed a dignity born of good living and universal respect. He carried himself royally. For four years, he had lived as a sated aristocrat, proud and slightly egotistical, insulated by his privileged position. Yet, he had avoided the fate of a mere pampered pet. Hunting and outdoor delights had kept his muscles hard and his frame lean, while his love for the cold tub had served as a tonic and health preserver.

It was the fall of 1897, the year the Klondike strike dragged men from across the globe into the frozen North. Buck, however, remained oblivious to the shifting tides of the world. He did not know that Manuel, one of the gardener's helpers, was an undesirable acquaintance. Manuel suffered from a singular vice: a love for the Chinese lottery. Worse still, he possessed a fatal weakness—a blind faith in a gambling system. Such a system demands capital, yet the wages of a gardener's helper barely covered the needs of a wife and numerous children.

On a memorable night, while the Judge attended a meeting of the Raisin Growers' Association and the boys organized an athletic club, Manuel committed his treachery. No one saw him and Buck slip away through the orchard, pretending on a simple stroll. Except for a solitary stranger, no one witnessed their arrival at the little flag station known as College Park. There, the stranger spoke with Manuel, and coins chinked between them.

"You might wrap up the goods before you deliver 'em," the stranger said gruffly. Manuel doubled a piece of stout rope around Buck's neck, beneath his collar. "Twist it, an' you'll choke 'm plentee," Manuel advised, and the stranger grunted affirmation.

Buck accepted the rope with quiet dignity. He trusted the men he knew and credited them with a wisdom surpassing his own. But when the loose ends were handed to the stranger, Buck growled menacingly. He had merely intimated his displeasure, believing in his pride that an intimation was a command. To his shock, the rope tightened, cutting off his breath. In quick rage, he sprang at the man, who met him halfway, grappled him by the throat, and with a deft twist threw him onto his back. The rope tightened mercilessly. Buck struggled in a fury, tongue lolling, chest heaving futilely. Never in his life had he been so vilely treated, nor so enraged. Yet his strength ebbed, his eyes glazed, and he knew nothing when the train was flagged and the two men tossed him into the baggage car.

The next moment, he was dimly aware of a throbbing tongue and the violent jolting of a conveyance. The hoarse shriek of a locomotive signaled a crossing. Having ridden in baggage cars often with the Judge, he knew the sensation. Opening his eyes, he found within them the unbridled anger of a kidnapped king. The man lunged for his throat, but Buck was too quick. His jaws clamped onto the hand, refusing to release until his senses were choked out once more.

"Yep, has fits," the man muttered, hiding his mangled hand from the baggageman drawn by the struggle. "I'm takin' 'm up for the boss to 'Frisco. A crack dog-doctor there thinks he can cure 'm."

That night's ride ended in a shed behind a saloon on the San Francisco waterfront.

"All I get is fifty for it," the man grumbled, "and I wouldn't do it over for a thousand, cold cash." His hand was wrapped in a bloody handkerchief, his right trouser leg ripped from knee to ankle.

"How much did the other mug get?" the saloon-keeper demanded.

"A hundred," was the reply. "Wouldn't take a sou less, so help me."

"That makes a hundred and fifty," the saloon-keeper calculated. "And he's worth it, or I'm a squarehead."

The kidnapper unwrapped the bloody bandages to inspect his lacerated hand. "If I don't get the hydrophoby—"

"It'll be because you was born to hang," the saloon-keeper laughed. "Here, lend me a hand before you pull your freight."

Dazed, suffering intolerable pain in his throat and tongue, with the life half-throttled from him, Buck attempted to face his tormentors. But he was thrown down and choked repeatedly until they succeeded in filing the heavy brass collar from his neck. The rope was removed, and he was flung into a cage-like crate.

There he lay through the weary night, nursing his wrath and wounded pride. He could not comprehend the chaos. What did these strange men want with him? Why keep him pent in this narrow box? A vague sense of impending calamity oppressed him. Several times, when the shed door rattled open, he sprang to his feet, expecting the Judge or the boys. Each time, however, it was the bulging face of the saloon-keeper peering in by the sickly light of a tallow candle. Each time, the joyful bark trembling in Buck's throat twisted into a savage growl.

The saloon-keeper left him alone, and in the morning, four men entered to pick up the crate. More tormentors, Buck decided; they were evil-looking, ragged, and unkempt. He stormed and raged at them through the bars. They only laughed and poked sticks at him, which he promptly assailed with his teeth until he realized that was exactly what they wanted. Sullenly, he lay down and allowed the crate to be lifted into a wagon. Thus began his passage through many hands. Clerks in the express office took charge; he was carted in another wagon; a truck carried him, mixed with boxes and parcels, onto a ferry steamer; he was trucked off the steamer into a great railway depot, and finally deposited in an express car.

For two days and nights, this express car was dragged at the tail of shrieking locomotives. Buck neither ate nor drank. In his anger, he had met the first advances of the express messengers with growls, and they retaliated by teasing him. When he flung himself against the bars, quivering and frothing, they laughed and taunted him, growling and barking like detestable dogs, mewling, flapping their arms, and crowing. It was all very silly, he knew, but therefore the more outrage to his dignity, and his anger waxed to a fever pitch. He did not mind the hunger so much, but the lack of water caused severe suffering, fanning his wrath. High-strung and finely sensitive, the ill treatment had thrown him into a fever, fed by the inflammation of his parched, swollen throat and tongue.

He was glad for one thing: the rope was off his neck. That had given them an unfair advantage; now that it was gone, he would show them. They would never get another rope around his neck. Upon that, he was resolved. For two days and nights he neither ate nor drank, accumulating a fund of wrath that boded ill for whoever first fell foul of him. His eyes turned bloodshot, and he was metamorphosed into a raging fiend. So changed was he that the Judge himself would not have recognized him. The express messengers breathed with relief when they bundled him off the train at Seattle.

Four men gingerly carried the crate from the wagon into a small, high-walled backyard. A stout man in a red sweater that sagged generously at the neck emerged and signed the book for the driver. That was the next tormentor, Buck divined, and he hurled himself savagely against the bars. The man smiled grimly and brought a hatchet and a club.

"You ain't going to take him out now?" the driver asked.

"Sure," the man replied, driving the hatchet into the crate to pry it open.

An instantaneous scattering occurred among the four men who had carried it in, scrambling to safe perches atop the wall to watch the performance.

Buck rushed at the splintering wood, sinking his teeth into it, surging and wrestling with it. Wherever the hatchet fell on the outside, he was there on the inside, snarling and growling, as furiously anxious to get out as the man in the red sweater was intent on getting him out.

"Now, you red-eyed devil," the man said, having made an opening sufficient for Buck's body. He dropped the hatchet and shifted the club to his right hand.

And Buck was truly a red-eyed devil as he drew himself together for the spring, hair bristling, mouth foaming, a mad glitter in his bloodshot eyes. Straight at the man he launched his one hundred and forty pounds of fury, surcharged with the pent passion of two days and nights. In mid-air, just as his jaws were about to close, he received a shock that checked his body and brought his teeth together with an agonizing click. He whirled over, hitting the ground on his back and side. He had never been struck by a club in his life and did not understand. With a snarl that was part bark and more scream, he was on his feet again and launched into the air. Again the shock came, crushing him to the ground. This time he was aware it was the club, but his madness knew no caution. A dozen times he charged, and as often the club broke the charge and smashed him down.

After a particularly fierce blow, he crawled to his feet, too dazed to rush. He staggered limply, blood flowing from his nose, mouth, and ears, his beautiful coat sprayed and flecked with bloody slaver. Then the man advanced and deliberately dealt him a frightful blow on the nose. All the pain he had endured was nothing compared to the exquisite agony of this. With a roar almost lionlike in its ferocity, he hurled himself at the man again. But the man, shifting the club from right to left, coolly caught him by the under-jaw, wrenching downward and backward. Buck described a complete circle in the air, then crashed to the ground on his head and chest.

For the last time he rushed. The man struck the shrewd blow he had purposely withheld, and Buck crumpled up, knocked utterly senseless.

"He's no slouch at dog-breakin', that's wot I say," one of the men on the wall cried enthusiastically.

"Druther break cayuses any day, and twice on Sundays," was the reply of the driver as he climbed onto the wagon and started the horses.

Buck's senses returned, but not his strength. He lay where he had fallen, watching the man in the red sweater.

"'Answers to the name of Buck,'" the man soliloquized, quoting from the saloon-keeper's letter announcing the consignment. "Well, Buck, my boy," he continued in a genial voice, "we've had our little ruction, and the best thing we can do is to let it go at that. You've learned your place, and I know mine. Be a good dog and all 'll go well and the goose hang high. Be a bad dog, and I'll whale the stuffin' outa you. Understand?"

As he spoke, he fearlessly patted the head he had so mercilessly pounded. Though Buck's hair involuntarily bristled at the touch of the hand, he endured it without protest. When the man brought him water, he drank eagerly, and later bolted a generous meal of raw meat, chunk by chunk, from the man's hand.

He was beaten—he knew that—but he was not broken. He saw, once for all, that he stood no chance against a man with a club.

Buck's first day on the Dyea beach was a descent into a living nightmare. In a single, violent hour, he had been yanked from the soft, sun-drenched heart of civilization and hurled into the raw, primordial chaos of the Yukon. There was no leisure here, no lazy afternoons to be bored, only a relentless, terrifying urgency. Peace, rest, and safety were nonexistent concepts; instead, there was only confusion and constant motion, where every heartbeat threatened life and limb. Survival demanded an unceasing vigilance, for these were not the gentle dogs and polite men of the city. These were savages, bound by a single, brutal code: the Law of Club and Fang.

Buck had never witnessed a dog fight like the ones unfolding before him. His first lesson was bought with the life of a friend, a vicarious tragedy that spared him the immediate cost. Curly, a friendly soul who had attempted to court a massive husky nearly twice her size, paid the price for her naivety. Without warning, the giant lunged, teeth clashing with a metallic snap, and then Curly was gone. The wolfish creature had struck and leaped away, but the fight was only beginning. Thirty or forty other huskies converged in a silent, intent circle, their jaws watering with anticipation. They waited for the moment the giant would tire. When he finally met Curly's charge with a chest thrust that sent her tumbling, the pack moved as one. They closed in, snarling and yelping, burying the screaming, agonized Curly beneath a bristling mass of bodies until she was nothing but a limp, torn shape in the bloody snow.

The speed of the slaughter left Buck reeling. He saw Spitz, the alpha, panting with a scarlet tongue that seemed to laugh at the carnage. He saw François, swinging an axe, join three men with clubs to scatter the remaining attackers. Within two minutes, the last of Curly's tormentors were driven off, but the half-breed stood over her corpse, cursing in a language Buck could not understand. The scene burned itself into Buck's memory, a recurring nightmare that haunted his sleep. There was no fairness here. Once you fell, you were finished. Vowing never to go down again, Buck fixed his gaze on Spitz, and from that moment, a bitter, deathless hatred took root in his heart.

Before the shock of Curly's death could fully settle, François strapped Buck into a harness. It was the same gear used on the horses at home, and the command to pull was the same. Though his dignity was crushed by the indignity of becoming a draft animal, Buck was too wise to rebel. He buckled down with a will, hauling François on a sled into the forest and returning with a load of firewood. François was stern, his whip ensuring instant obedience, while Dave, an experienced wheeler, nipped Buck's hindquarters whenever he erred. Spitz, the leader, growled sharp reproofs or cunningly shifted his weight in the traces to jerk Buck into line. Buck learned quickly, mastering the commands "ho" and "mush," learning to swing wide on bends and avoid the wheeler on steep descents.

By afternoon, Perrault arrived with two new additions: Billee and Joe, brothers as different as day and night. Billee was excessively good-natured, wagging his tail in appeasement even as Spitz's teeth scored his flank. Joe, however, was sour and introspective, a malignant eye and perpetual snarl marking him as a danger. Spitz immediately began to thrash them, driving the wailing Billee to the edge of the camp. But Joe was different; he whirled to face Spitz, mane bristling, lips writhing, jaws snapping with diabolical gleam. It was the incarnation of belligerent fear, so terrifying that Spitz was forced to retreat, covering his own discomfiture by attacking the harmless Billee instead.

Later that evening, Perrault brought in Sol-leks, an old, gaunt husky with a battle-scarred face and a single, warning eye. Like Dave, Sol-leks asked for nothing and gave nothing, commanding respect simply by his presence. Yet, Buck discovered a fatal flaw: Sol-leks hated being approached on his blind side. Unknowingly guilty of this offense, Buck was slashed across the shoulder to the bone when Sol-leks whirled upon him. From that day forward, Buck kept his distance from Sol-leks' blind side, and their uneasy comradeship remained unbroken.

That night, Buck faced the great problem of sleeping. The tent, glowing warmly in the candlelight, seemed inviting, but entering it earned him a bombardment of curses and cooking utensils from Perrault and François. Driven out into the biting cold, Buck lay on the snow, the frost gnawing at his wounded shoulder. Shivering miserably, he wandered among the tents, only to find that the cold was universal. Savage dogs rushed at him, but he bristled and snarled, learning fast, and they let him pass.

Desperate, Buck returned to his team-mates, only to find them missing. Confused and forlorn, he circled the tent until his forepaws sank into the snow. Something wriggled beneath him, and a friendly yelp reassured him. There, curled in a snug ball under the surface, was Billee. Warmth radiated from the small dog, a bribe for peace that Buck accepted. He learned the secret: dig a hole, curl up, and let your body heat create a sanctuary. Exhausted, Buck dug his own pit, filled it with warmth, and slept soundly, wrestling with bad dreams.

Morning brought a new terror. Snow had fallen during the night, burying Buck completely. Pressed against the white walls of his den, a primal fear seized him—the fear of the trap. It was a regression to the instincts of his wild ancestors, a civilized dog suddenly terrified of confinement. His muscles contracted spasmodically, his hair stood on end, and with a ferocious snarl, he bounded straight up into the blinding daylight, shaking the snow from his fur. As François hailed him, Perrault nodded approvingly. "Dat Buck for sure learn queek as anyt'ing," the driver cried.

Within an hour, three more huskies joined the team, bringing the total to nine. Before long, they were harnessed and swinging up the trail toward the Dyea Cañon. Buck found a strange comfort in the work; the hardship did not depress him, but rather ignited a fierce eagerness in the entire team. Even Dave and Sol-leks, usually passive, were transformed by the harness. They were alert, active, and fiercely irritable if anything slowed their progress. The toil of the traces was their supreme purpose.

Buck was placed between Dave and Sol-leks for instruction. Both were apt teachers, correcting errors with sharp teeth. Dave was fair, nipping only when necessary, while François's whip backed him up. When Buck tangled in the traces and delayed the start, both Dave and Sol-leks flew at him, administering a sound trouncing. The resulting tangle was worse, but Buck learned his lesson quickly. By day's end, he had mastered his work, and the nagging ceased.

The journey was grueling. They climbed the Cañon, crossed glaciers and snowdrifts hundreds of feet deep, and traversed the great Chilcoot Divide, the forbidding barrier between salt and fresh water. That night, they pulled into the huge camp at Lake Bennett, where thousands of goldseekers were building boats. Buck made his snow-hole and slept, only to be roused early in the dark to hit the trail again. For days, they broke their own trail, working harder and moving slower. Perrault traveled ahead, packing the snow with webbed shoes, while François guided the sled.

Day after day, Buck toiled in the traces, breaking camp in the dark and sleeping in the snow. He was ravenous. His ration of a pound and a half of sun-dried salmon vanished instantly, leaving him suffering from perpetual hunger pangs. The other dogs, lighter and born to this life, managed on a pound and stayed in good condition. Buck, however, lost his fastidiousness. A dainty eater, he found his unfinished ration stolen by his mates. Unable to defend it, he adapted. Hunger compelled him to eat as fast as they, and eventually, he learned to take what did not belong to him.

He watched Pike, a clever thief who slyly stole slices of bacon when Perrault's back was turned. Buck duplicated the feat the next day, getting away with the whole chunk. A great uproar ensued, but Buck was unsuspected, while Dub, an awkward blunderer, was punished for Buck's misdeed. This first theft marked Buck as fit to survive the hostile North. It signaled his adaptability and the decay of his moral nature. In the Southland, under the law of love, respecting private property was noble; in the Northland, under the Law of Club and Fang, such sentiment was a fatal weakness. Buck did not steal for joy, but for survival, out of respect for the club and the fang. He acted because it was easier to do so than to starve.

His development was rapid. His muscles hardened like iron, and he became callous to pain. He achieved an internal economy, extracting every last particle of nutriment from anything he could eat, building his body into the toughest tissue imaginable. His sight and scent sharpened, his hearing became acute enough to detect peril in his sleep, and he learned to bite ice from his toes or break thick scum from water holes with his forelegs. Most notably, he developed the ability to scent the wind, forecasting storms a night in advance.

Instincts long dead woke within him. The domesticated generations fell away, replaced by vague memories of wild dogs ranging in packs through primeval forests. He learned to fight with the quick wolf snap, a skill stamped into his heredity. On still, cold nights, when he pointed his nose at a star and howled long and wolf-like, it was his ancestors, dead and dust, pointing their noses at the same stars and howling down through the centuries. Their cadences voiced their woe and the meaning of the cold and dark. Thus, as a token of life's puppetry, the ancient song surged through him, and he came into his own again, driven by the yellow metal found in the North and the desperate needs of men like Manuel, whose wages could not feed his family.

The primal beast within Buck was not merely present; it was expanding, swelling under the brutal crucible of the trail until it nearly overflowed his frame. Yet this transformation remained a secret, a silent metamorphosis. His newfound cunning granted him a terrifying poise, a deliberate control that kept him aloof from conflict. He did not seek fights; he engineered their avoidance. There was no rashness in his movements, no precipitate action, and even in the simmering, bitter hatred that burned between him and Spitz, he betrayed no impatience. He shunned offense with the patience of a predator waiting in the tall grass.

Conversely, Spitz, sensing a dangerous rival in the Southland dog, could not resist the urge to display his teeth. He actively bullied Buck, constantly probing for the spark that would ignite a fight destined to end in death for one of them. For weeks, this war of nerves held, thwarted only by an accident of fate.

They made camp on the desolate shore of Lake Le Barge, a bleak and miserable spot chosen by driving snow and a wind that cut like a white-hot knife. Darkness had forced them to grope for shelter, leaving them with no choice but to build their fire on the ice itself, beneath a perpendicular wall of rock. Their tent had been discarded at Dyea for the sake of speed, leaving them to eat supper in the dark, illuminated only by the flickering driftwood fire that melted through the ice beneath them.

Close against the sheltering rock, Buck had constructed a nest, snug and warm enough to make him reluctant to leave it when François distributed the thawed fish. But when he returned, the nest was occupied. A warning snarl revealed the intruder: Spitz. Until now, Buck had tolerated his enemy's provocations, but this trespass crossed a line. The beast within him roared. He sprang upon Spitz with a fury that startled them both, particularly Spitz, who had calculated Buck as a timid giant who survived only on weight and size.

François, shocked by the sudden eruption, cried out to Buck, "Give it to him! Give it to him, the dirty thief!"

Spitz was equally eager, circling back and forth in a frenzy of rage, seeking a chance to spring. Buck matched him, cautious yet desperate for the advantage. It was in this stalemate that the unexpected occurred, an event that would project their struggle for supremacy far into the future, past weary miles of trail and toil.

An oath from Perrault, the thunderous impact of a club on a bony frame, and a shrill yelp of pain heralded the breaking forth of pandemonium. The camp was suddenly revealed to be alive with skulking, furry forms—starving huskies, perhaps a hundred of them, who had scented the food from an Indian village. They had crept in while Buck and Spitz fought, and when the two men sprang among them with stout clubs, the invaders showed their teeth and fought back. Driven mad by the smell of food, they were a terrifying force. Perrault found one with its head buried in the grub-box; his club landed heavily on its gaunt ribs, capsizing the box. Instantly, a score of famished brutes scrambled for the bread and bacon. The clubs fell upon them unheeded. They yelped and howled under the rain of blows but struggled madly until the last crumb was devoured.

Meanwhile, the astonished team-dogs burst from their nests only to be set upon by the fierce invaders. Never had Buck seen such dogs. They were mere skeletons, draped loosely in draggled hides, with blazing eyes and slavered fangs. Hunger-madness made them irresistible. The team-dogs were swept back against the cliff at the first onset. Buck was beset by three huskies, and in a trice, his head and shoulders were ripped and slashed. The din was frightful. Billee cried as usual. Dave and Sol-leks, dripping blood from a dozen wounds, fought bravely side by side. Joe snapped like a demon, once crunching down through the foreleg of a husky. Pike, the malingerer, leaped upon a crippled animal and broke its neck with a quick flash of teeth. Buck grabbed a frothing adversary by the throat and was sprayed with blood when his teeth sank through the jugular. The warm taste goaded him to greater fierceness. He flung himself upon another, feeling teeth sink into his own throat simultaneously. It was Spitz, treacherously attacking from the side.

Perrault and François, having cleared their share of the camp, hurried to save their sled-dogs. The wild wave of famished beasts rolled back before them, and Buck shook himself free. But only for a moment. The men were compelled to run back to save the remaining grub, allowing the huskies to return to the attack on the team. Billee, terrified into bravery, sprang through the savage circle and fled over the ice. Pike and Dub followed on his heels, with the rest of the team trailing behind. As Buck drew himself together to spring after them, he saw Spitz rush upon him with the evident intention of overthrowing him. Once off his feet and under that mass of huskies, there was no hope. But he braced himself for the shock of Spitz's charge, then joined the flight out onto the lake.

Later, the nine team-dogs gathered in the forest for shelter. Though unpursued, they were in a sorry plight. Not one was without four or five wounds; some were grievously injured. Dub's hind leg was badly damaged; Dolly, the last husky added at Dyea, had a torn throat; Joe had lost an eye; and Billee, the good-natured one, cried and whimpered throughout the night with an ear chewed to ribbons. At daybreak, they limped warily back to camp to find the marauders gone and the two men in bad tempers. Fully half their grub supply was gone. The huskies had chewed through sled lashings and canvas coverings, eating Perrault's moose-hide moccasins, chunks of leather traces, and even two feet of lash from François's whip.

"Ah, my friends," François said softly, looking over the wounded, "maybe it makes you mad dogs, so many bites. Maybe all mad dogs, sacredam! What do you think, eh, Perrault?"

The courier shook his head dubiously. With four hundred miles of trail still between him and Dawson, he could ill afford to have madness break out among his dogs. Two hours of cursing and exertion got the harnesses into shape, and the wound-stiffened team set off, struggling painfully over the hardest part of the trail they had yet encountered.

The Thirty Mile River was wide open, its wild water defying the frost. It was only in the eddies and quiet places that the ice held. Six days of exhausting toil were required to cover those thirty terrible miles, accomplished at the risk of life to dog and man. A dozen times, Perrault, nosing the way, broke through ice bridges, saved only by the long pole he carried, which he held so that it fell across the hole made by his body. But a cold snap was on, the thermometer registering fifty below zero. Each time he broke through, he was compelled for very life to build a fire and dry his garments.

Nothing daunted him. It was because nothing daunted him that he had been chosen for government courier. He took all manner of risks, resolutely thrusting his weazened face into the frost and struggling on from dim dawn to dark. He skirted the frowning shores on rim ice that bent and crackled underfoot, upon which they dared not halt. Once, the sled broke through with Dave and Buck, and they were half-frozen and nearly drowned by the time they were dragged out. The usual fire was necessary to save them. They were coated solidly with ice, and the two men kept them running around the fire, sweating and thawing, so close they were singed by the flames.

At another time, Spitz went through, dragging the whole team after him up to Buck, who strained backward with all his strength, his forepaws on the slippery edge while the ice quivered and snapped all around. Behind him was Dave, likewise straining, and behind the sled was François, pulling till his tendons cracked.

Again, the rim ice broke away before and behind, leaving no escape except up the cliff. Perrault scaled it by a miracle, while François prayed for just that miracle; and with every thong and sled lashing and the last bit of harness rove into a long rope, the dogs were hoisted, one by one, to the cliff crest. François came up last, after the sled and load. Then came the search for a place to descend, ultimately made by the aid of the rope, and night found them back on the river with a quarter of a mile to the day's credit.

By the time they reached the Hootalinqua and good ice, Buck was played out. The rest of the dogs were in like condition, but Perrault, to make up lost time, pushed them late and early. The first day they covered thirty-five miles to the Big Salmon; the next, thirty-five more to the Little Salmon; the third, forty miles, bringing them well up toward the Five Fingers.

Buck's feet were not so compact and hard as the feet of the huskies. His had softened during the many generations since his last wild ancestor was tamed by a cave-dweller or river man. All day long he limped in agony, and once camp was made, he lay down like a dead dog. Hungry as he was, he would not move to receive his ration of fish, which François had to bring to him. Also, the dog-driver rubbed Buck's feet for half an hour each night after supper, sacrificing the tops of his own moccasins to make four for Buck. This was a great relief, and Buck caused even the weazened face of Perrault to twist into a grin one morning when François forgot the moccasins and Buck lay on his back, his four feet waving appealingly in the air, refusing to budge without them. Later, his feet grew hard to the trail, and the worn-out foot-gear was thrown away.

At the Pelly one morning, as they were harnessing up, Dolly, who had never been conspicuous, went suddenly mad. She announced her condition by a long, heartbreaking wolf howl that sent every dog bristling with fear, then sprang straight for Buck. He had never seen a dog go mad, nor did he have any reason to fear madness; yet he knew that here was horror, and fled away from it in a panic. Straight away he raced, with Dolly, panting and frothing, one leap behind. Nor could she gain on him, so great was his terror, nor could he leave her, so great was her madness. He plunged through the wooded breast of the island, flew down to the lower end, crossed a back channel filled with rough ice to another island, gained a third, curved back to the main river, and in desperation started to cross it. And all the time, though he did not look, he could hear her snarling just one leap behind. François called to him a quarter of a mile away, and he doubled back, still one leap ahead, gasping painfully for air and putting all his faith in that François would save him. The dog-driver held the axe poised in his hand, and as Buck shot past him, the axe crashed down upon mad Dolly's head.

Buck staggered over against the sled, exhausted, sobbing for breath, helpless. This was Spitz's opportunity. He sprang upon Buck, and twice his teeth sank into his unresisting foe, ripping and tearing the flesh to the bone. Then François's lash descended, and Buck had the satisfaction of watching Spitz receive the worst whipping yet administered to any of the teams.

"One devil, dat Spitz," remarked Perrault. "Some dam day heem keel dat Buck."

"Dat Buck two devils," was François's rejoinder. "All de tam I watch dat Buck I know for sure. Lissen: some dam fine day heem get mad lak hell an' den heem chew dat Spitz all up an' spit heem out on de snow. Sure. I know."

From then on, it was war between them. Spitz, as lead-dog and acknowledged master of the team, felt his supremacy threatened by this strange Southland dog. And strange Buck was to him, for of the many Southland dogs he had known, not one had shown up worthily in camp or on the trail. They were all too soft, dying under the toil, the frost, and starvation. Buck was the exception. He alone endured and prospered, matching the husky in strength, savagery, and cunning. Then he was a masterful dog, and what made him dangerous was the fact that the club of the man in the red sweater had knocked all blind pluck and rashness out of his desire for mastery. He was preeminently cunning, capable of biding his time with a patience that was nothing less than primitive.

It was inevitable that the clash for leadership should come. Buck wanted it. He wanted it because it was his nature, because he had been gripped tight by that nameless, incomprehensible pride of the trail and trace—that pride which holds dogs in the toil to the last gasp, which lures them to die joyfully in the harness, and breaks their hearts if they are cut out of it. This was the pride of Dave as wheel-dog, of Sol-leks as he pulled with all his strength; the pride that laid hold of them at break of camp, transforming them from sour and sullen brutes into straining, eager, ambitious creatures; the pride that spurred them on all day and dropped them at pitch of camp at night, letting them fall back into gloomy unrest. This was the pride that bore up Spitz and made him thrash the sled-dogs who blundered or shirked. Likewise, it was this pride that made him fear Buck as a possible lead-dog. And this was Buck's pride, too.

"Eh? Wot I say? I spik true w'en I say dat Buck two devils." François's voice cracked with excitement the next morning as he discovered Spitz vanished and Buck covered in fresh wounds. He dragged the massive dog to the firelight, pointing out the gaping rips. "Dat Spitz fight lak hell," Perrault observed, inspecting the carnage. "An' dat Buck fight lak two hells," François replied, his eyes gleaming. "An' now we make good time. No more Spitz, no more trouble, sure."

As Perrault packed the camp gear and François began harnessing the team, a tense drama unfolded at the lead. Buck trotted instinctively to the spot Spitz had occupied, but François, unaware of the shift, brought Sol-leks forward to the coveted position. In François's judgment, Sol-leks was the finest lead-dog remaining. Instantly, Buck sprang upon Sol-leks in a fury, driving him back and seizing the place.

"Eh? eh?" François cried, slapping his thighs in delight. "Look at dat Buck. Heem keel dat Spitz, heem t'ink to take de job."

"Go 'way, Chook!" he commanded, but Buck refused to budge. François grabbed the dog by the scruff of the neck; though Buck growled a low, threatening rumble, the driver dragged him aside and shoved Sol-leks back into the lead. The old dog bristled, clearly afraid of the newcomer. François was obdurate, but when he turned his back, Buck instantly displaced Sol-leks again, who was too willing to comply.

François's patience snapped. "Now, by Gar, I feex you!" he roared, returning with a heavy club.

Buck remembered the man in the red sweater and retreated slowly, refusing to charge when Sol-leks was brought forward. Instead, he circled just beyond the range of the club, snarling with bitter rage. He had learned the wisdom of clubs; he watched the weapon carefully, ready to dodge if thrown. François went about his work, calling for Buck to return to his old place beside Dave. Buck retreated two steps, then three. François followed, and Buck retreated again. Finally, François threw down the club, assuming the dog feared a beating. But Buck was in open revolt. He did not want to escape a thrashing; he wanted the leadership. It was his by right. He had earned it, and he would not settle for less.

Perrault intervened. Together, they ran Buck about for nearly an hour, hurling clubs at him. He dodged every throw. They cursed him and his lineage, every hair on his body and drop of blood in his veins, but he answered only with snarls, keeping just out of reach. He did not flee; he circled the camp, signaling plainly that once his desire was met, he would come in and be good.

François sat, scratching his head in frustration. Perrault checked his watch and swore; time was slipping away, and they should have been on the trail an hour ago. Defeated, François approached Sol-leks and called to Buck. Buck laughed—a sharp, barking sound unique to dogs—yet kept his distance. François unfastened Sol-leks's traces and moved him back. The team stood harnessed in an unbroken line, ready for the trail. There was no place for Buck save at the front. Once more François called, and once more Buck laughed and held his ground.

"T'row down de club," Perrault commanded.

François complied. Immediately, Buck trotted in, laughing triumphantly, and swung around into position at the head of the team. His traces were fastened, the sled broken out, and with both men running, they dashed onto the river trail.

Highly as the dog-driver had valued Buck with his two devils, he soon realized he had undervalued him. At a single bound, Buck assumed the duties of leadership. Where judgment, quick thinking, and swift action were required, he proved superior even to Spitz, whom François had never seen equaled.

Buck excelled not just in speed, but in enforcing the law. Dave and Sol-leks accepted the change without complaint; their business was to toil mightily in the traces, and they did not care who led as long as order was maintained. Billee, the good-natured dog, could lead if he kept order. However, the rest of the team, having grown unruly during Spitz's final days, were swiftly licked into shape by Buck.

Pike, who pulled at Buck's heels and never exerted more weight than forced upon him, was shaken repeatedly for loafing. Before the first day ended, he was pulling harder than ever in his life. That first night in camp, Joe, the sour dog, was punished roundly—a feat Spitz had never achieved. Buck simply smothered him with superior weight, cutting him until he ceased snapping and began to whine for mercy.

The team's tone improved immediately. It regained its old-time solidarity, leaping as one dog in the traces. At the Rink Rapids, two native huskies, Teek and Koona, were added, and the celerity with which Buck broke them in left François breathless.

"Nevaire such a dog as dat Buck!" he cried. "No, nevaire! Heem worth one t'ousan' dollair, by Gar! Eh? Wot you say, Perrault?"

Perrault nodded. They were ahead of the record, gaining ground daily. The trail was in excellent condition, well-packed and hard, with no new snow to contend with. The temperature hovered at fifty degrees below zero throughout the trip. The men rode and ran by turns, keeping the dogs on the jump with infrequent stops.

The Thirty Mile River was largely iced over, allowing them to cover in one day what had taken ten days coming in. In a single run, they made a sixty-mile dash from the foot of Lake Le Barge to the White Horse Rapids. Across Marsh, Tagish, and Bennett—seventy miles of lakes—they flew so fast that the runner towed behind the sled at the end of a rope. On the last night of the second week, they topped White Pass and dropped down the sea slope with the lights of Skaguay and the shipping fleet at their feet.

It was a record run. For fourteen days, they averaged forty miles a day. Upon reaching Skaguay, Perrault and François threw chests up and down the main street, deluged with invitations to drink, while the team became the center of a worshipful crowd of dog-busters and mushers. Soon, three or four western bad men attempted to clean out the town, only to be riddled like pepper-boxes, shifting public interest elsewhere. Then came official orders. François called Buck to him, threw his arms around the dog, and wept. That was the last of François and Perrault. Like other men, they passed out of Buck's life forever.

A Scotch half-breed took charge of him and his mates, and in company with a dozen other teams, they started back over the weary trail to Dawson. It was no longer a record run, but heavy toil each day with a heavy load behind, for this was the mail train carrying word from the world to those seeking gold under the shadow of the Pole.

Buck did not like it, but he bore up well, taking pride in the work like Dave and Sol-leks, ensuring his mates did their fair share. Life became monotonous, operating with machine-like regularity. Each morning, cooks turned out, fires were built, and breakfast eaten. Then, while some broke camp, others harnessed the dogs, and they were under way an hour before dawn. At night, camp was made, flies pitched, wood cut, and water carried. To the dogs, feeding was the highlight of the day, followed by an hour of loafing with the other fifty-seven dogs. Among them were fierce fighters, but three battles with the fiercest brought Buck to mastery; when he bristled and showed his teeth, they stepped aside.

Best of all, perhaps, he loved to lie near the fire, hind legs crouched, fore legs stretched out, head raised, eyes blinking dreamily at the flames. Sometimes he thought of Judge Miller's big house in the sun-kissed Santa Clara Valley, the cement swimming-tank, Ysabel the Mexican hairless, and Toots the Japanese pug. But oftener, he remembered the man in the red sweater, the death of Curly, the great fight with Spitz, and the good things he had eaten or would like to eat. He was not homesick. The Sunland was dim and distant; such memories held no power over him. Far more potent were the memories of his heredity, giving things he had never seen a seeming familiarity. The instincts, which were merely the memories of his ancestors becoming habits, had lapsed in later days but now quickened and became alive again.

Sometimes, as he crouched by the fire, it seemed the flames belonged to another fire. As he sat by this other fire, he saw a different man: shorter of leg, longer of arm, with stringy, knotty muscles. His hair was long and matted, his head slanted back under it. He uttered strange sounds and seemed terrified of the darkness, clutching a stick with a heavy stone tied to the end. He was nearly naked, a ragged, fire-scorched skin hanging partway down his back, but his body was covered in hair, matted into a thick fur across his chest and shoulders. He did not stand erect but leaned forward from the hips, his legs bent at the knees. There was a peculiar springiness, almost catlike, and a quick alertness as of one living in perpetual fear.

At other times, this hairy man squatted by the fire with his head between his legs, elbows on his knees, hands clasped above his head as if shedding rain. Beyond that fire, in the circling darkness, Buck saw many gleaming coals, two by two, always two by two, which he knew to be the eyes of great beasts of prey. He heard the crashing of their bodies through the undergrowth and the noises they made at night. Dreaming there by the Yukon bank, with lazy eyes blinking at the fire, these sounds and sights made the hair rise along his back and stand on end across his shoulders and up his neck, till he whimpered low and suppressedly, or growled softly. The half-breed cook would shout, "Hey, you Buck, wake up!" Whereupon the other world would vanish, the real world returning to his eyes, and he would get up, yawn, and stretch as though asleep.

It was a hard trip, the heavy work wearing them down. They arrived in Dawson short of weight and in poor condition, needing at least a week's rest. But in two days, they dropped down the Yukon bank from the Barracks, loaded with letters for the outside. The dogs were tired, the drivers grumbling, and snow fell every day, creating a soft trail with greater friction and heavier pulling. Yet the drivers remained fair, doing their best for the animals.

Each night, the dogs were attended to first. They ate before the drivers, and no man sought his sleeping-robe until he had tended to the feet of his dogs. Still, their strength waned. Since the beginning of winter, they had traveled eighteen hundred miles, dragging sleds the whole weary distance. Buck stood it, keeping his mates up to work and maintaining discipline, though he too was very tired. Billee cried and whimpered regularly in his sleep each night. Joe was sourer than ever, and Sol-leks was unapproachable.

But it was Dave who suffered most. Something had gone wrong with him. He became morose and irritable, making his nest at once when camp was pitched, refusing to get on his feet until harness-up time in the morning. Sometimes, in the traces, when jerked by a sudden stop or straining to start, he cried out with pain. The driver examined him but found nothing. All the drivers became interested, discussing his case at meals and over their last pipes. One night, they held a consultation. He was brought to the fire and pressed and prodded until he cried out many times. Something was wrong inside, but they could locate no broken bones.

By the time Cassiar Bar was reached, he was so weak that he fell repeatedly in the traces. The Scotch half-breed called a halt and took him out of the team, fastening the next dog, Sol-leks, to the sled. His intention was to rest Dave, letting him run free behind. Sick as he was, Dave resented being taken out, grunting and growling while the traces were unfastened, whimpering broken-heartedly when he saw Sol-leks in the position he had held and served so long. For the pride of trace and trail was his, and, sick unto death, he could not bear that another dog should do his work.

When the sled started, he floundered in the soft snow alongside the beaten trail, attacking Sol-leks with his teeth, rushing against him, trying to thrust him off into the soft snow on the other side, striving to leap inside his traces and get between him and the sled, all the while whining and yelping with grief and pain. The half-breed tried to drive him away with the whip, but Dave paid no heed to the stinging lash, and the man had not the heart to strike harder. Dave refused to run quietly on the trail behind the sled, where the going was easy, but continued to flounder alongside in the soft snow, where the going was most difficult, till exhausted. Then he fell, lying where he fell, howling lugubriously as the long train of sleds churned by.

With the last remnant of his strength, he staggered along behind till the train made another stop, when he floundered past the sleds to his own, standing alongside Sol-leks. His driver lingered to get a light for his pipe from the man behind, then returned and started his dogs. They swung out on the trail with remarkable lack of exertion, turned their heads uneasily, and stopped in surprise. The driver was surprised too; the sled had not moved. He called his comrades to witness the sight.

Thirty days after leaving Dawson, the Salt Water Mail arrived at Skaguay, dragging Buck and his weary companions into the town like a dying procession. They were reduced to shadows of their former selves; Buck's one hundred and forty pounds had evaporated to one hundred and fifteen, while his mates, though lighter to begin with, had shed even more precious substance. Pike, the habitual liar who once feigned a lame leg to escape work, now limped with genuine agony. Sol-leks hobbled, and Dub suffered from a wrenched shoulder blade. Their feet were raw and sore, striking the snow with a heavy, thudding rhythm that jarred their bones and compounded their exhaustion. This was not the fatigue of a single hard day's labor, recoverable in hours, but the slow, relentless draining of months of toil. Every muscle, fiber, and cell was spent; there was no reserve strength left, no power of recuperation. In less than five months, they had traversed two thousand five hundred miles, resting only five days during the final eighteen hundred. They were on their last legs, barely managing to keep the traces taut, stumbling on the downgrades just to avoid being run over by their own sled.

"Mush on, poor sore feets," the driver coaxed them as they tottered down the main street. "Dis is de las'. Den we get one long res'. Eh? For sure. One bully long res'."

The drivers expected a long stopover, confident in their own endurance after covering twelve hundred miles with merely two days' rest. But the Klondike was congested with mail, swelling to Alpine proportions, and official orders dictated that fresh Hudson Bay dogs replace the worthless ones. The current team, deemed unfit for the trail, was to be sold.

Three days passed, deepening their despair. On the morning of the fourth, two men from the States appeared and purchased the team, harness and all, for a song. They introduced themselves as Hal and Charles. Charles was a middle-aged man with watery eyes and a mustache that twisted aggressively upward, defying the drooping lip beneath it. Hal was a boy of nineteen or twenty, armed with a Colt's revolver and a belt bristling with cartridges that announced his sheer callowness. Both were out of place in the North, their presence a mystery that defied understanding.

Buck watched the transaction, sensing the Scotch half-breed and the mail drivers fading from his life. He was driven to the new owners' camp, a slovenly affair of half-stretched tents and unwashed dishes, where he encountered a woman named Mercedes, Charles's wife and Hal's sister. As the new family began to pack, their lack of business sense was evident. The tent was rolled into an awkward bundle three times too large; dishes were packed dirty; Mercedes fluttered in their way, offering endless, futile advice. When they attempted to load the sled, she insisted on moving items from front to back and back to front, creating a mountainous, top-heavy load that threatened to capsize.

"You've got a right smart load as it is," warned a neighbor, advising against taking the tent. "Undreamed of!" cried Mercedes in dainty dismay. "How could I manage without a tent?"

"It's springtime," the man replied gently.

She shook her head, and Charles and Hal, ignoring the warnings, lashed the overloaded sled. When the dogs sprang against the breast-bands, they strained hard but could not budge the anchor-like sled. Hal prepared to whip them, but Mercedes intervened, snatching the whip from his hand. "The poor dears! Promise you won't be harsh," she pleaded.

"Precious lot you know about dogs," Hal sneered, resuming his cruelty. "They're lazy. You've got to whip them."

"They're weak as water," another man observed. "They need a rest."

"Rest be blanked," Hal spat.

Mercedes, overwhelmed by the sight of pain, dropped to her knees before Buck, embracing him. "You poor, poor dears," she sobbed. "Why don't you pull hard? Then you wouldn't be whipped." Buck, feeling too miserable to resist her pity, accepted her touch as part of the day's misery.

Finally, a bystander advised breaking out the frozen runners. Hal complied, and the sled forged ahead, only to overturn on a steep turn, spilling half its contents. The dogs, angered by the abuse and the unfair load, broke into a run, scattering the remaining gear down the street. Kind citizens gathered the belongings and the dogs, offering blunt advice: half the load and twice the dogs were needed to reach Dawson.

Reluctantly, the family pitched the tent and began the process of elimination. Canned goods were laughed at as luxuries; blankets were deemed unnecessary. Mercedes wept as her possessions were discarded, eventually stripping herself bare and then attacking her husband's and brother's belongings. Despite cutting the load in half, they still possessed a formidable bulk. Desperate for numbers, they bought six outside dogs—pointers, a Newfoundland, and mongrels—who knew nothing of the trail. Added to the original six and two huskies from the Rink Rapids, the team numbered fourteen. But these new dogs were useless, spirit-broken or bone-thin, adding weight without power.

The men were cheerful and proud of their fourteen-dog team, unaware that one sled could not carry enough food for fourteen dogs. They calculated their needs with a pencil, ignoring the realities of the trail. The team started the next morning dead weary, led by Buck, who felt bitterness rising in his heart. He had covered this distance four times before, and now he faced it again, jaded and tired. He sensed he could not depend on these incompetent men or the woman. They were slack, disorganized, and incapable of learning. They took half the night to pitch a sloppy camp and half the morning to rearrange the load, often failing to make ten miles a day.

To compensate for their inefficiency, they overfed the dogs, hastening the day when starvation would begin. The outside dogs, unused to famine, had voracious appetites. When the supply ran low, Hal cut the rations even further, trying to force faster travel. But the dogs could not move faster, and the heavy load sapped their strength.

Dub was the first to fall victim to their incompetence. His untreated injury worsened until Hal shot him with the Colt's revolver. The outside dogs, receiving half the ration of the huskies, died quickly. The Newfoundland went first, followed by the pointers and mongrels. The remaining huskies were mere skeletons, insensible to the lash or the club. Pain was distant and dull; they were barely living, bags of bones with faint sparks of life fluttering within.

Billee fell and could not rise; Hal hacked him down with an axe. Koona followed. Only five remained: Joe, too far gone to be malicious; Pike, crippled and half-conscious; Sol-leks, one-eyed and mournful; Teek, fresh and beaten; and Buck, leading the team but blind with weakness, keeping the trail only by feel.

Spring had arrived, bringing a blaze of sunshine and the great murmur of awakening life. The Yukon broke free from its ice, and the land burst into green. Yet, amidst this vibrant renewal, the two men, the woman, and the starving huskies staggered like wayfarers to death. Mercedes wept and rode the sled, weighing one hundred and twenty pounds on the backs of the weak animals. Hal swore innocuously; Charles's eyes watered wistfully.

They reached John Thornton's camp at the mouth of the White River. The dogs collapsed as if struck dead. Thornton, whittling an axe handle, listened to Hal's triumphant claim that they had made it despite warnings of rotten ice. "The bottom's likely to drop out at any moment," Thornton said tersely. "Only fools... could have made it."

"We'll go on to Dawson," Hal declared, uncoiling his whip. "Get up there, Buck!"

The team did not rise. Blows fell, and Sol-leks, Teek, and Joe crawled to their feet. Pike struggled painfully. But Buck lay where he had fallen. The lash bit into him, but he neither whined nor struggled. He had made up his mind not to get up. A vague feeling of impending doom had taken hold, stronger than the pain. He sensed disaster close at hand on the thin ice ahead. The spark of life within him flickered and nearly went out.

Suddenly, John Thornton sprang upon Hal, hurling him backward. "If you strike that dog again, I'll kill you," Thornton choked out.

"It's my dog!" Hal retorted, drawing his knife.

Thornton stood between them, rapping Hal's knuckles with the axe handle until the knife fell. He cut Buck's traces with two swift strokes. Hal, distracted by his hysterical sister and the near-dead dog, did nothing. Moments later, they pulled away down the river. Buck watched them go: Pike leading, Sol-leks at the wheel, Mercedes riding the loaded sled, and Hal guiding at the gee-pole.

Thornton knelt beside Buck, searching for broken bones. Finding only bruises and terrible starvation, he looked up as the sled, a quarter of a mile away, suddenly tipped. The back end dropped into a rut, the gee-pole jerked into the air, and a section of ice gave way. Mercedes screamed. Charles stumbled back. Dogs and humans vanished into a yawning hole. The bottom had dropped out of the trail.

Thornton and Buck looked at each other. "You poor devil," Thornton said softly. Buck licked his hand.

John Thornton had spent the previous winter freezing his feet, a hardship his partners had met with kindness, leaving him to recover while they ventured upstream to harvest saw-logs for Dawson. By the time Buck stumbled upon him, Thornton was still limping, though the warming spring had soon erased the ache. Lying by the riverbank, watching the water flow and listening to the birdsong, Buck slowly reclaimed his strength.

Rest is a profound healer after three thousand miles of travel. Buck grew lazy as his wounds knit, his muscles swelling and flesh returning to cover his bones. He was not alone in this idleness; Thornton, and his two dogs, Skeet and Nig, were all waiting for the raft that would carry them downstream. Skeet, a small Irish setter, had bonded with Buck during his weakest moments, unable to resent her early advances. Possessing a natural healing instinct, she washed and cleansed his wounds daily, a ritual Buck soon came to anticipate as much as Thornton's presence. Nig, a massive black hound-deerhound mix with laughing eyes and boundless goodwill, shared this affection, though with less overt display.

To Buck's astonishment, these dogs showed no jealousy. They seemed to radiate the same expansive kindness as John Thornton. As Buck recovered, they dragged him into ridiculous games, with Thornton joining in, and through this play, Buck emerged from convalescence into a new existence. For the first time, he knew genuine, passionate love. It was a feeling entirely absent from his days in the sun-drenched Santa Clara Valley. With the Judge's sons, it had been a working partnership; with the grandsons, a pompous guardianship; with the Judge himself, a stately friendship. But it was John Thornton who ignited a feverish, burning adoration, a madness of the heart.

This man had saved his life, certainly, but he was also the ideal master. While others tended their dogs out of duty or business necessity, Thornton cared for them as if they were his own children, compelled by an instinct he could not suppress. He remembered every kind word and cherished long conversations with them, which he called "gas." He had a habit of gripping Buck's head roughly between his hands, resting his own forehead against Buck's, shaking him back and forth while hurling insults that Buck interpreted as terms of endearment. There was no greater joy for Buck than this rough embrace and the sound of murmured oaths; with each jerk, his heart seemed to shake loose from his chest in ecstasy. When released, Buck would spring to his feet, mouth grinning, eyes eloquent, throat vibrating with unspoken sounds, standing motionless until Thornton would reverently cry, "God! you can all but speak!"

Buck had a unique way of expressing love, akin to pain. He would seize Thornton's hand in his mouth, biting down so fiercely that the flesh bore the mark of his teeth for hours. Just as Buck understood the oaths as love words, Thornton understood this feigned bite as a caress.

Most often, however, Buck's love was expressed through adoration. While he thrilled at Thornton's touch, he rarely sought it. Unlike Skeet, who nudged Thornton's hand incessantly, or Nig, who rested his great head on Thornton's knee, Buck preferred to adore from a distance. He would lie for hours at Thornton's feet, alert and eager, gazing up into his face, studying every fleeting expression and movement. Sometimes he would lie further back, watching the man's outline and the subtle shifts of his body. Often, the intensity of Buck's gaze would draw Thornton's head around, and they would lock eyes in silent communion, their hearts shining out of their gazes.

For a long time after his rescue, Buck could not bear to lose sight of Thornton. From the moment he left the tent to when he returned, Buck followed at his heels. His transient masters in the Northland had bred in him a fear that no master could remain permanent. He feared Thornton would fade away as Perrault, François, and the Scotch half-breed had done. Even in his dreams, this fear haunted him; he would shake off sleep and creep to the tent flap to listen to his master's breathing.

Yet, despite this overwhelming love, the primitive strain awakened in him by the Northland remained active. Faithfulness and devotion, born of fire and shelter, were his, but so too were his wildness and cunning. He was a creature of the wild, come to sit by Thornton's fire, rather than a dog of the soft Southland stamped by generations of civilization. Because of his great love for Thornton, he could not steal from him, but from any other man in any other camp, he hesitated not an instant. His cunning allowed him to evade detection.

His face and body were scarred by the teeth of many dogs, and he fought as fiercely as ever, perhaps more shrewdly. Skeet and Nig were too good-natured for quarrels, besides belonging to Thornton, but any strange dog, regardless of breed or valor, quickly acknowledged Buck's supremacy or found itself struggling for its life. Buck was merciless. He had learned the law of the club and the fang, never foregoing an advantage or drawing back from a foe he had set upon the path to death. He had learned from Spitz and the fighting dogs of the police and mail that there was no middle course. To show mercy was a weakness; mercy was often mistaken for fear, and such misunderstandings led to death. The mandate was kill or be killed, eat or be eaten, a law from the depths of time that he obeyed.

He was older than the days he had seen and the breaths he had drawn. He linked the past with the present, and the eternity behind him throbbed through him in a mighty rhythm, swaying with the tides and seasons. Sitting by Thornton's fire, a broad-breasted dog with white fangs and long fur, the shades of all manner of dogs—half-wolves and wild wolves—hovered behind him. They urged him, tasting the meat he ate, thirsting for the water he drank, scenting the wind with him, listening to the sounds of wild life in the forest, dictating his moods and directing his actions. They lay down to sleep with him, dreamed with him, and became the stuff of his dreams.

So peremptorily did these shades beckon him that each day, mankind and the claims of mankind slipped farther from him. Deep in the forest, a call sounded, mysteriously thrilling and luring. Whenever he heard it, he felt compelled to turn his back on the fire and the beaten earth, plunging into the forest, onward and onward, knowing not where or why. He did not wonder, for the call sounded imperiously. Yet, whenever he gained the soft, unbroken earth and the green shade, the love for John Thornton drew him back to the fire.

Thornton alone held him. The rest of mankind was nothing. Chance travelers might praise or pet him, but he remained cold beneath it all, walking away from anyone too demonstrative. When Thornton's partners, Hans and Pete, arrived on the long-awaited raft, Buck refused to acknowledge them until he knew they were close to Thornton. After that, he tolerated them in a passive way, accepting their favors as if he were granting them in return. They were of the same sturdy type as Thornton, living close to the earth and thinking simply. Before they swung the raft into the big eddy by the saw-mill at Dawson, they understood Buck and his ways, refusing to demand the intimacy Skeet and Nig enjoyed.

For Thornton, however, his love seemed to grow endlessly. He was the only man who could put a pack upon Buck's back during summer travel. Nothing was too great for Buck when Thornton commanded. One day, having grub-staked themselves from the raft's proceeds and left Dawson for the headwaters of the Tanana, the men and dogs sat on the crest of a cliff dropping straight down to naked bedrock three hundred feet below. Thornton sat near the edge, Buck at his shoulder. A thoughtless whim seized Thornton, and he drew Hans and Pete's attention to the experiment he intended. "Jump, Buck!" he commanded, sweeping his arm over the chasm. The next instant, he was grappling with Buck on the extreme edge, while Hans and Pete dragged them back to safety.

"It's uncanny," Pete said after they caught their breath.

Thornton shook his head. "No, it is splendid, and it is terrible, too. Do you know, it sometimes makes me afraid."

"I'm not hankering to be the man that lays hands on you while he's around," Pete concluded, nodding toward Buck.

"Py Jingo!" Hans added. "Not mineself either."

It was at Circle City, before the year ended, that Pete's apprehensions were realized. "Black" Burton, a man of evil temper and malice, had been picking a quarrel with a tenderfoot at the bar when Thornton stepped in good-naturedly. Buck, as usual, lay in a corner, head on paws, watching his master's every move. Burton struck out without warning, straight from the shoulder. Thornton was spun backward, saving himself only by clutching the bar's rail.

Those watching heard neither bark nor yelp, but a roar. They saw Buck's body rise into the air as he leaped from the floor to Burton's throat. The man saved his life by instinctively throwing out his arm but was hurled backward to the floor with Buck on top. Buck loosened his teeth from the arm and drove in again for the throat. This time, the man only partly blocked, and his throat was torn open. Then the crowd surged upon Buck, driving him off. While a surgeon checked the bleeding, Buck prowled up and down, growling furiously, attempting to rush in and being forced back by an array of hostile clubs. A "miners' meeting" called on the spot decided the dog had sufficient provocation, and Buck was discharged. But his reputation was made, and from that day his name spread through every camp in Alaska.

Later, in the fall, he saved John Thornton's life in quite another fashion. The three partners were lining a long, narrow poling-boat down a treacherous stretch of rapids on the Forty-Mile Creek. Hans and Pete moved along the bank, snubbing with a thin Manila rope from tree to tree, while Thornton remained in the boat, poling its descent and shouting directions. Buck, on the bank, worried and anxious, kept abreast of the boat, his eyes never off his master.

At a particularly bad spot, where a ledge of barely submerged rocks jutted into the river, Hans cast off the rope. As Thornton poled the boat out into the stream, Hans ran down the bank with the rope's end to snub the boat once it cleared the ledge. The boat flew downstream in a current as swift as a mill-race, and Hans checked it with the rope, checking too suddenly. The boat flipped over, snubbing to the bank bottom-up, while Thornton, flung sheer out, was carried downstream toward the worst part of the rapids—a stretch of wild water where no swimmer could live.

Buck sprang in instantly. Three hundred yards later, amid a mad swirl of water, he overhauled Thornton. When he felt the man grasp his tail, Buck headed for the bank, swimming with all his splendid strength. But the progress shoreward was slow; the progress downstream was amazingly rapid. From below came the fatal roaring where the wild current went wilder, rent in shreds and spray by rocks thrusting through like the teeth of an enormous comb. The suck of the water as it took the beginning of the last steep pitch was frightful, and Thornton knew the shore was impossible. He scraped furiously over a rock, bruised across a second, and struck a third with crushing force. Clutching its slippery top with both hands, he released Buck and, above the roar of the churning water, shouted: "Go, Buck! Go!"

Buck could not hold his own and swept downstream, struggling desperately but unable to win back. When he heard Thornton's command repeated, he partly reared out of the water, throwing his head high as if for a last look, then turned obediently toward the bank. He swam powerfully and was dragged ashore by Pete and Hans at the very point where swimming ceased to be possible and destruction began.

They knew that the time a man could cling to a slippery rock in the face of that driving current was a matter of minutes. They ran as fast as they could up the bank to a point far above where Thornton hung on. They attached the line used for snubbing the boat to Buck's neck and shoulders, careful not to strangle him or impede his swimming, and launched him into the stream. He struck out boldly, but not straight enough. He discovered the mistake too late when Thornton was abreast of him, a mere half-dozen strokes away, while Buck was carried helplessly past.

Hans promptly snubbed with the rope as if Buck were a boat. The rope tightening on him in the sweep of the current, he was jerked under the surface and remained there until his body struck the bank and he was hauled out. Half-drowned, Hans and Pete threw themselves upon him, pounding breath into him and water out of him. He staggered to his feet and fell down. The faint sound of Thornton's voice came to them; though they could not make out the words, they knew he was in his extremity. His master's voice acted on Buck like an electric shock. He sprang to his feet and ran up the bank ahead of the men to the point of his previous departure.

Again the rope was attached, and he was launched. Again he struck out, but this time straight into the stream. He had miscalculated once; he would not be guilty a second time. Hans paid out the rope, permitting no slack, while Pete kept it clear of coils. Buck held on until he was on a line straight above Thornton, then turned, and with the speed of an express train headed down upon him. Thornton saw him coming, and as Buck struck him like a battering ram, with the whole force of the current behind him, he reached up and closed with both arms around the shaggy neck. When Buck secured sixteen hundred dollars for John Thornton in a single afternoon, he unlocked a future that would take them far from the safety of the Klondike. That sum paid off Thornton's debts and funded a journey eastward, chasing a legend older than the country itself: the Lost Mine. It was a place steeped in tragedy, marked by an ancient, crumbling cabin and the dying testimonies of men who had found gold unlike any other in the Northland, only to vanish. Now, Thornton, accompanied by his partners Pete and Hans and a team of dogs including Buck, turned their sleds toward the East, following an unknown trail where others had failed or perished.

They traveled seventy miles up the Yukon, swung into the Stewart River, and pressed on until the river became a mere streamlet threading through the jagged peaks of the continent's backbone. Thornton was a man of few words and no fear, moving through the wilderness with the ease of an Indian. He hunted for his meals, carrying only salt and a rifle, trusting that the land would provide if he kept moving. For Buck, this life of boundless wandering was pure delight. Weeks passed in steady travel, followed by weeks of camping where men dug fires into frozen muck and dogs loafed by the warmth. They rafted across blue mountain lakes, navigated unknown rivers in slender boats, and endured the shifting seasons.

Summer brought blizzards across divides and the midnight sun on naked mountains, yet they found strawberries and flowers in glacier shadows as fair as any in the South. Autumn revealed a weird, silent lake country where wildfowl had once lived but now only chilling winds and lonely waves remained. Through another winter, they tracked the obliterated paths of those who came before. At one point, they found a path blazed through the forest leading to the Lost Cabin, but it began and ended nowhere, a mystery as enduring as the man who made it. Later, amidst the rotting shreds of an abandoned hunting lodge, Thornton discovered a long-barreled flint-lock, a relic of the Hudson Bay Company days, offering no clue to its former owner.

Eventually, spring returned, and they found not the cabin, but a shallow placer in a broad valley where gold glittered like yellow butter. They worked tirelessly, sacking the dust and nuggets into moose-hide bags and piling them outside their spruce-bough lodge like firewood. With the gold secured, the dogs' work diminished to occasional hauling of meat. Buck spent long hours by the fire, and in the quiet, the vision of the short-legged, hairy man returned with renewed frequency.

In those visions, fear was the dominant emotion. Buck watched the man sleep restlessly, his head between his knees, eyes darting into the darkness. Whether walking a beach gathering shellfish or creeping through the forest, the man was perpetually vigilant, ready to run at the first sign of danger. He moved with supernatural agility, swinging from limb to limb, a creature at home in the trees as much as on the ground. Closely tied to these memories was the Call, a sound resonating in the depths of the forest that stirred a strange, sweet gladness and wild yearning within Buck. He would pursue it, sniffing the cool wood moss or crouching behind fallen trunks, driven by an irresistible impulse he could not reason.

One night, the Call became distinct—a long-drawn howl, unlike any dog sound, yet familiar to Buck's soul. He sprang from sleep, nostrils quivering, and dashed through the sleeping camp into the woods. Approaching the cry, he slowed, stalking carefully until he found an open space where a long, lean timber wolf stood erect, nose to the sky. Buck approached with a mixture of threat and friendliness, a truce between wild beasts. The wolf fled, and Buck chased him into a blind channel where the creek was barred by a timber jam. The wolf whirled, snarling and bristling, but Buck circled him, offering no attack. Suspicious and afraid due to the size difference, the wolf darted away, and the chase resumed. Time and again, the wolf was cornered only to dash free, but Buck's persistence eventually won. The wolf sniffed his nose, and they played together in a nervous, half-coy manner.

Seeing the wolf move off purposefully, Buck followed, running side by side through the sombre twilight up the creek bed and across the bleak divide. On the opposite slope, they entered a level country of great forests and streams. Buck was wildly glad, knowing he was answering the Call, running toward its source with his "wood brother." Old memories surged, and he felt the stirrings of a world he had once known. They stopped to drink, and Buck remembered John Thornton. He sat down, and the wolf returned, encouraging him to follow. But Buck turned back. For an hour, the wild brother whined softly beside him, then sat and howled a mournful sound that faded into the distance.

Buck returned to camp and threw himself upon Thornton in a frenzy of affection, licking and biting him until Thornton shook him back and forth, cursing him lovingly. For two days, Buck refused to leave Thornton's sight, but the Call grew imperious. Haunted by the memory of the wild brother and the land beyond the divide, Buck began to wander again. The wolf did not appear, and the mournful howl was silent.

Buck started sleeping outdoors, venturing away for days at a time. He crossed the divide and wandered into the land of timber and streams, seeking the wolf in vain. He killed a large black bear blinded by mosquitoes and fought off a dozen wolverines quarreling over the kill. The blood-longing intensified. He became a creature of pure instinct, a killer living by his own strength, proud and magnificent. His movements were those of a wolf, his intelligence a blend of shepherd and St. Bernard, sharpened by experience. He was in full flower, vibrating with vitality, his muscles coiled like steel springs.

"Never was there such a dog," Thornton said one day as the partners watched Buck march out of camp. "When he was made, the mould was broke," Pete agreed.

But within the secrecy of the forest, Buck transformed instantly. He became a passing shadow, crawling on his belly like a snake, striking with lethal precision. He killed to eat, preferring his own kills, and took delight in the hunt, sometimes letting prey escape in a game of cat-and-mouse. As fall approached, moose descended from the higher valleys. Buck targeted a great bull, wounded by an arrow, leading a band of twenty. He isolated the bull, driving him into paroxysms of rage while the younger bulls tried to protect their leader. Buck employed the patience of the wild, clinging to the flank of the herd, irritating the young bulls and driving the wounded bull mad with helpless fury.

For four days, Buck never left his prey, denying him rest, water, or browse. The great bull, weighing over three hundredweight, stood with lowered head, facing death at the teeth of a creature whose head barely reached his knees. Finally, Buck pulled the bull down. Rested and refreshed, he turned his face toward camp, heading home with a certitude of direction that surpassed any human compass.

As he neared, the land felt different, alive with a new stir. Birds and squirrels chattered of it. Three miles from camp, he found a fresh trail leading straight to the settlement, sending his neck hair bristling. The forest was pregnant with silence; birds had fled, squirrels hidden. Suddenly, his nose jerked as if gripped by a force. He followed the scent into a thicket and found Nig, dead, an arrow protruding from his body. A hundred yards further, he found another sled dog thrashing in a death-struggle.

From the camp came the sound of voices singing a sing-song chant. Buck slid to the edge of the clearing and saw Hans lying on his face, feathered with arrows. Then he looked toward the spruce-bough lodge and saw the truth. A gust of overpowering rage swept over him. Passion usurped cunning and reason. For the last time in his life, he lost his head, driven by his great love for John Thornton.

The Yeehats were dancing about the wreckage when they heard a fearful roar. Buck, a live hurricane of fury, hurled himself upon them. He ripped the throat of the chief open, then tore into a second man without pausing. He plunged into their midst, tearing and destroying, moving with such speed that they shot one another in their panic. Screaming of the Evil Spirit, they fled into the woods, dragging their wounded. Buck pursued them, a fateful day for the tribe.

Exhausted by the pursuit, Buck returned to the desolated camp. He found Pete dead in his blankets and Thornton's desperate struggle fresh on the earth. By a deep pool, head and forefeet in the water, lay Skeet, faithful to the last. The pool, muddy and discolored from sluice boxes, hid what it contained: John Thornton. Buck followed his trace into the water, where no trail led away.

All day, Buck brooded by the pool, mourning the void that food could not fill. Yet, when he contemplated the carcasses of the Yeehats, he felt a pride greater than any before. He had killed man, the noblest game, in defiance of the law of club and fang. Night fell, and a full moon bathed the land in ghostly light. As Buck mourned, he heard a faint, sharp yelp, followed by a chorus of similar sounds growing closer. These were the sounds of the other world. The Call sounded more luringly than ever. John Thornton was dead; the last tie was broken.

Hunting the migrating moose, a wolf pack had invaded Buck's valley. They poured into the moonlit clearing where Buck stood motionless. Awed by his stillness and size, they hesitated until the boldest leaped. Buck struck, breaking the wolf's neck instantly. Three others tried, drawing back with slashed throats. Panic seized the pack, and they charged pell-mell. Buck pivoted on his hind legs, snapping and gashing, presenting an unbroken front until he was forced back to a gravel bank where he came to bay, protected on three sides.

For half an hour, the wolves drew back, tongues lolling, fangs showing in the moonlight. Then, a long, lean, gray wolf advanced cautiously. Buck recognized the wild brother from his previous journey. They touched noses and whined softly. An old, gaunt wolf came forward, and after a preliminary snarl, they sniffed noses. The old wolf sat, pointed his nose at the moon, and broke out the long wolf howl. The others joined in, and the Call came to Buck in unmistakable accents. He sat and howled.

When the howling ceased, the pack crowded around him, sniffing in a half-friendly, half-savage manner. The leaders lifted the yelp and sprang away into the woods. The wolves swung in behind, yelping in chorus, and Buck ran with them, side by side with the wild brother, yelping as he ran.

Here may well end the story of Buck. Years later, the Yeehats noted a change in the breed of timber wolves; some were seen with splashes of brown on their heads and a rift of white down their chests. More remarkably, they spoke of a Ghost Dog that ran at the head of the pack, possessing cunning greater than their own, stealing from camps and slaying dogs. Tales grew of hunters failing to return, found with throats slashed and wolf prints in the snow larger than any wolf's. Each fall, they avoided a certain valley, fearing the Evil Spirit had chosen it for an abiding-place.

Yet, in the summers, a visitor came to that valley, unknown to the Yeehats. It was a great, gloriously coated wolf, like yet unlike any other. He crossed from the timber land to an open space where a yellow stream flowed from rotted moose-hide sacks, sinking into the ground. There he would muse, howling once, long and mournfully, before departing. But he was not always alone. When the long winter nights came and the wolves followed their meat into the lower valleys, he could be seen running at the head of the pack through the pale moonlight, leaping gigantic above his fellows, his great throat a-bellow as he sang the song of the younger world, the song of the pack.
