Life wears many masks: sometimes she glides through marble halls, crowned with jewels, never glancing down at those who serve her; other times, she trudges through shadowed alleys, reeking of cheap spirits, screaming amidst the chaos of the desperate. Yet tonight, she arrived not as either extreme, but as a practical, efficient matron, her mind sharp and cheerful, ready to find abundance within the smallest of things.

Mrs. Meyrick's home was quiet, a sanctuary separated from the city's roar. The front parlor overlooked the river, the rear garden offered green views, allowing the windows to stand open even as Mrs. Meyrick read aloud to her daughters. Inside the small, double room, a single lamp and two candles burned. One candle illuminated a separate table where Kate sat, sketching designs for a publisher; the lamp served not only the storyteller but also Amy and Mab, who were stitching satin cushions intended for "high society" gatherings.

From the outside, the house appeared narrow and worn, the bright light filtering through the holland blinds revealing a heavy, old-fashioned frame. Yet within these grim walls lay a culture untainted by vulgarity. In foggy London, poverty often stripped away the need for ostentatious display, leaving behind a purity that rich houses rarely achieved. Here, the grand spectacles of the world were merely distant curiosities, sparking neither envy nor futile striving.

The Meyrick household clung to this specific dwelling because every object remained exactly where it belonged. For Mrs. Meyrick, the furniture and engravings were relics of her marriage; for the girls, they were as familiar and essential as the stars of the Great Bear seen from their back windows. Mrs. Meyrick had sacrificed much comfort to preserve a few cherished prints her husband loved, filling the narrow walls with scenes and portraits that the children knew by heart. The chairs and tables were old friends, far superior to anything a broker might sell cheap. Despite their scarcity, the space allowed for a life of wide-ranging taste, open to the heights of music, painting, and poetry. Even during times of deepest want, before Kate earned her living, they likely managed without a live-in servant, yet they maintained a fastidious standard that refused to accept the coarse selfishness and petty squabbles depicted in the so-called "literary photographs" of fashionable life.

The family possessed unique quirks, inherited streaks of eccentricity from both parents, their minds resembling medieval houses with unexpected recesses and sudden staircases leading to new vistas. However, they were bound together by a triple knot: familial love, a shared admiration for noble works and actions, and a habit of diligent industry. Hans's desire to fund a more luxurious lifestyle for them had been firmly rejected, saving everyone from the regret of his artistic ambitions clashing with financial security—a triumph that would eventually force him to abandon his fellowship. Instead, they laughed at his caricatures and accepted his pursuit of art as a natural gift, unhindered by material needs. Their reward was a grand treat of opera-going whenever Hans visited.

That evening, observing the group, one could scarcely wish for any change. They were all petite, perfectly scaled to their miniature rooms. Mrs. Meyrick, reading from a French volume, was a lively little woman, half-French, half-Scotch, with a speech so articulate it seemed to illuminate the listener's understanding. Though nearing fifty, her hair, mostly gray and tucked beneath a Quaker-style net cap, rippled softly above brown eyebrows that matched her bright eyes. Her black dress, buttoned in rows like a priest's cassock, fitted a neat, barely five-foot figure.

Her daughters mirrored her spirit, though Mab possessed Hans's light hair and fair skin, marked by a bossy, irregular brow and other peculiarities that recalled him. From the tight coils of their hair, pinned *à la Chinoise*, to their gray skirts—a Puritan defiance of the era's fashion which demanded fuller silhouettes—they were compact and distinct. If they were wax figures, they might fit easily into a fashionable lady's travel trunk. Their faces seemed packed with words, as if their minds had been shelled like horse-chestnuts to reveal a bright core. The only large entity in the room was Hafiz, the Persian cat, lounging regally on a brown leather chair back, blinking occasionally to ensure the smaller creatures caused no trouble.

Mrs. Meyrick held Erckmann-Chatrian's *Histoire d'un Conscrit*. Having just concluded the reading, Mab dropped her embroidery, leaning forward with intense focus.

"I believe that is the finest story in the world," she declared.

"Of course, Mab!" Amy replied. "It is the last one you've heard. Whatever pleases you is the best, right now."

"It isn't really a story," Kate corrected gently. "It's a piece of history magnified by a powerful telescope. We see the soldiers' faces... no, it's more than that. We can hear everything. We can almost feel their hearts beating."

"I don't care what you label it," Mab said, tossing her thimble aside. "Call it a chapter in Revelation. It makes me want to do something good, something magnificent. It fills me with sorrow for everyone. It reminds me of Schiller—I want to wrap the world in my arms and kiss it. I must kiss you instead, little mother!" She flung her arms around her mother's neck.

"When you're in that mood, Mab," Amy chided, "your work suffers. Finishing your cushion without staining it would count as doing something good."

"Oh—oh—oh!" Mab groaned, stooping to retrieve her tools. "I wish I had three wounded conscripts to nurse."

"You'd spill their beef tea while talking," Amy teased.

"Poor Mab! Don't be too harsh," the mother interceded. "Hand me the embroidery, child. You continue with your zeal, and I'll resume the pink and white poppies."

"Well, Ma, I suppose you're more biting than Amy," Kate remarked, tilting her head to study her drawing.

"Oh—oh—oh!" Mab cried again, rising and stretching. "I wish something wonderful would happen! I feel like the Deluge. The waters of the deep have burst, and the windows of heaven are open. I must sit down and play the scales."

As Mab began opening the piano, the others laughed at her dramatic climax. Suddenly, a cab halted before the house, followed immediately by a sharp knock at the door.

"My goodness!" Mrs. Meyrick started, rising quickly. "It is past ten, and Phoebe is already asleep." She hurried out, leaving the parlor door ajar.

"Mr. Deronda!" The girls caught the exclamation from their mother. Mab clasped her hands, whispering loudly, "There now! Something *is* happening." Kate and Amy abandoned their tasks in stunned silence. But Deronda's voice was so low that his words remained unheard, and Mrs. Meyrick swiftly closed the parlor door.

"I know I am relying on your kindness in an extraordinary manner," Deronda continued once inside, after offering a brief, urgent summary. "You can imagine how helpless I feel with a young creature like this on my hands. I cannot leave her alone with strangers, and in her nervous state, I would dread bringing her into a house full of servants. I have placed my trust in your mercy. Please, do not consider my action unwarranted."

"On the contrary," Mrs. Meyrick responded warmly. "You have honored me by trusting me. I understand your difficulty. Please, bring her in. I will prepare the girls."

As Deronda returned to the cab, Mrs. Meyrick re-entered the parlor. "Here is someone to care for instead of your wounded conscripts, Mab," she announced. "A poor girl who was planning to drown herself in despair. Mr. Deronda found her only moments ago. He brought her along in his boat and didn't know what else to do safely, so he trusts us with her. She seems to be a Jewess, yet quite refined—he says she knows Italian and music."

The three girls, curious and expectant, moved forward, standing close in a silent unity of feeling. Mab looked awestruck, as if this fulfillment of her dreams were supernatural.

Meanwhile, Deronda approached the cab door where a pale face gazed out, alert and observant. "I have brought you to some of the kindest people in the world," he told her. "These are daughters like you. It is a happy home. May I take you to them?"

She stepped out obediently, slipping her hand into his and forgetting her hat. As Deronda led her into the warm light of the parlor, where the four little women awaited, she became a vision that would have moved even colder hearts. Initially dazed by the brightness, she focused her gaze, and instantly Deronda placed her hand in her mother's. He felt a quiet joy that the Meyricks were so small; her dark-curled head was the tallest among them. The wandering girl could not fear these gentle faces so close to hers. Now, she looked at each of them in turn as the mother said, "You must be weary, poor child."

"We will take care of you—we will comfort you—we will love you," Mab shouted, unable to hold back anymore, and took the small right hand tenderly between both of her own. This warmth penetrated the bewildered stranger. She hesitated just enough to scrutinize the four faces, whose goodwill was reflected back in her own eyes—not in smiles, but in that indefinable shift where anxiety melts into contentment.
