Only when the rival navy intercepted us at the mouth of the gulf did I fully grasp the weight of my position as a recruit to the Empress. Prior to that ambush, I had dismissed my own significance, but the ferocity with which those enemy ships lay in wait—fighting with a wild, singular intent to capture me—spoke volumes. It was an undeniable omen that I was destined to play a central, perhaps decisive, role in the nation's fortunes.

Yet, if our arrival was monitored by foes, it appeared Phorenice had her own vanguard. Scouts perched atop the surrounding mountains observed our approach and hastened news to the capital. The sea-arm leading to Atlantis, where the vast city stands, is notoriously variable. Where the mountains have collapsed, sending molten rock to solidify beneath, the channel narrows to a river's breadth. Beyond this choke point, the waters swell into a lake-like expanse for a half-day's sail, bordered by cliffs barely visible above the waves. Furthermore, the coastline is a labyrinth of winding paths, swamps, and smoking hills. A courier skilled in traversing this treacherous terrain, surviving fire-streams, flooding, and roaming beasts, could outrun any galley.

While the risks for a lone traveler were immense, Phorenice spared no expense. She dispatched a corps of twenty runners to the headland overlooking the strait. Each took a separate route, and their efficiency was remarkable: seven returned safely with tidings, and it was rumored that three others had survived. Though we were unaware of this at the time, pushing forward under the delusion that our return was unannounced, the reality of the situation weighed heavily on us.

The slaves powering our galley were largely savages from Europe, their stench so repulsive that it drained the voyage of any pleasure. Compounding this misery was the constant spray of volcanic grit from an erupting mountain nearby, coating everything in fine, abrasive dust. We were driven to press onward with minimal delay. Yet, beneath the stoicism taught by my priestly training—a faith that the future rests solely in the gods' hands—I harbored a deeply human, frantic curiosity regarding my reception. Would I be formally imprisoned and tried for the failure of the Yucatan colony? Would I step ashore unnoticed, summoned later by the Empress? The true nature of my welcome remained a mystery to me.

At dawn, the peak of the sacred mountain came into view, crowned with the eternal fires that guarded the city. The boatswains' whips cracked violently, driving the exhausted slaves to a final, desperate sprint. The wind was dead against us, leaving sails useless, but under the oars alone, the galley gained momentum. The sides of the mountain loomed larger, until the pyramids and high towers of Atlantis emerged from the mist, appearing to float upon the gleaming water. Twenty years had passed since I last beheld Atlantis, and my heart beat a frantic rhythm at the prospect of treading her paving stones again.

As we neared, the city seemed to spring from the sea itself. With every stroke of the oar, the shores leaped closer. I recognized the temple where I was first admitted to manhood, the pyramid containing the secrets of initiation, and finally, the modest house where I had been raised by parents long gone. Tears blurred my vision as the memories surged.

We anchored outside the white harbor walls, adhering strictly to the laws of procedure. The slaves slumped over their oars, panting and weeping in silence. Usually, ports are plagued by uncertainty, with captains proving their dignity by delaying arrivals indefinitely. However, a ten-oared skiff shot from behind the walls, racing alongside us faster than oars could drive her. The questions flew instantly:

"That must be Dason's galley?"
"It was," replied Tob.
"Oh, I saw Dason's head on your prow!" the port-captain exclaimed, his tone shifting to grim concern. "Were you Captain Tatho?"
"And remain so. Tatho's fleet was sunk to the seabed by Dason and his allies, forcing us to take this rotting vessel to finish the journey, as it was the only craft left afloat."

The captain's eyes roved over the deck. "Captain, I fear your reception will be perilous. I see no sign of Lord Deucalion. Has he arrived with another navy? By the Gods, if you allowed him to perish under your command, the Empress will flay you alive."

"With both Phorenice and Tatho so obsessed with his fate," Tob interjected smoothly, "Lord Deucalion appears to be a hazardous passenger. Rest assured, I shall save my skin this voyage." He jabbed his thumb toward me. "He is aboard to vouch for me personally."

The port-captain stared, disbelief warring with awe, before bowing low with practiced ceremony. "I trust His Lordship, in his infinite strength, will pardon my ignorance in failing to recognize him by his nobility. Truth be told, I expected attire far more befitting a lord."

"Pish," I said, shaking my head. "If I choose simplicity, I cannot begrudge being mistaken for a commoner. It is not my pleasure to advertise my status through gaudy finery. If you require restitution, I beg you to end this inquiry."

The captain melted into obsequious bows. "I am the humblest of your servants, my lord. It would be my supreme honor to pilot your galley to the appointed berth."

The skiff darted ahead, and the slaves resumed their labor. Tob watched me with a dry, knowing smile as he directed the helm.

"Well," I said, playing along, "what troubles you?"

"I suspect," Tob mused, "that when Lord Deucalion steps ashore among such refined gentility, he will recall me only as a coarse brute."

"You think nothing of the sort," I countered.

"Then I must prove my refinement," Tob declared, seizing my massive hand in his iron grip. "By the Gods, Deucalion, you may be a great prince, but I have known you only as a man. You are the finest warrior of beasts and men alive today. That spear-thrust of yours against the lizard will become a chant in every tavern singer's repertoire."

Rapidly, we entered the harbor. Soldiers in the entrance castle blew trumpets in welcome as we passed between them. The port-captain had hoisted my banner to the masthead of his skiff, presumably prepared for this exact announcement. Soon, the sounds of music drifted across the vast basin, accompanied by the pale, dancing glow of welcoming fires illuminated by the sun. I felt a surge of awe; surely no Empress of Atlantis would deign such intense interest in a mere returning subject.

Nothing was to be done by halves. The port-captain's boat led the way, compelling us to follow. Our galley was run alongside the royal quay and secured to posts and rings of gold—all sacred to the reigning house.

"If Dason had foreseen this honor," Tob joked with a ghastly grin, "he would have laid in silk rope to secure his vessel, rather than plebeian hemp. I'm certain a frown would be etched onto his skull this instant, were it not scorched stiff by the sun." Turning to me, he asked, "Will you, My Lord Deucalion, navigate carefully over this common ship and tread the genteel carpet spread yonder?"

The port-captain, hearing Tob's rough jest, looked up with horror. I sighed inwardly; colonial freedom had no place in Atlantis. Once more, I must prepare for the rigid dignity of rank and the formalities of grand ceremony.

Be that as it may, a man of self-respect must preserve his individuality. While I consented to enter a crimson pavilion, specially erected to shelter me until the Empress's arrival, my compliance ended there. Three gorgeously caparisoned chamberlains, who had ushered me inside, laid before me garments bedecked with every conceivable frippery, urging me to transform into a peacock to match their own fashion.

Curtly, I refused to alter my garb. When one of them stammered about the Empress's personal tastes, I asked plainly if he possessed any direct orders from Her Majesty regarding my clothing. Of course, he confessed that there were none.

"Therefore," I retorted, "Phorenice commanded *Deucalion*, the man, to attend her, and sent no word regarding his outer casing."
"This dress suits my temper perfectly," I continued, brushing aside the offered silks. "It shields me from the heat and wind, and it is clean. It seems to me, sirs, that your interference borders on impertinence."

With one accord, the chamberlains drew their swords, pushing the hilts toward me.
"It would be a favor," their spokesman pleaded, "if Great Lord Deucalion would take his vengeance now, sparing us the tormentors who await us hereafter."

"Nonsense," I dismissed. "You make too much of a triviality."

Nevertheless, their action offered me insight. They were deadly serious in offering me weapons, revealing that I had returned to a different Atlantis—one where a man feared torture for a mere disagreement over the cut of a coat.
