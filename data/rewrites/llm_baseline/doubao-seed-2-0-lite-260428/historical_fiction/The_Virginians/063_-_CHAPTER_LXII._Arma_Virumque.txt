If Harry Warrington harboured even a spark of passion for all things military, the roar of war echoing across 1750s Europe, and the endless din of campaign gossip in every London drawing room and coffee house he frequented, was more than enough to fan that spark into a roaring blaze. Our own gracious Hanoverian monarch might have met defeat on the continent, but that Protestant hero, the King of Prussia, was filling every corner of the known world with the thunder of his astonishing victories—for which I count myself singularly lucky that poor Harry played no part. Were he to have ridden to battle beside Frederick, this veracious little biography of mine would have been forced to describe great slaughters already claimed by far greater storytellers. I count it a mercy that Harry Warrington was not at Rossbach, that famous Gunpowder Fete-day on the 5th of November 1757, nor at the dreadful, blood-soaked field of Leuthen a month later. Those prodigious actions are destined for far grander volumes, which all the world—myself included—waits with bated breath to behold.

Would you have my humble story compete with that epic? Could my jaunty yellow park phaeton jostle for space against that grim, thundering war chariot? Could my meek little jog-trot Pegasus hope to stand against that foaming, fiery-eyed steed of battle? Dear, kind reader, with whom I love to step aside and chat from time to time, even as our characters go about their business in the dress and speech of a hundred years past—believe me, it is a blessing for both of us that Harry never followed the King of the Borussians as he once ached to do. I should hate for you to draw odious comparisons between my quiet little tale and the masterwork Carlyle is soon to paint of those wars.

Harry not only failed to join the Prussian ranks, he chafed and pined miserably at being kept from them. The truth is, he fell into a sullen, useless rut. He hung about every military coffee house in the city, and cared to read nothing but the daily papers. He was never a man of literary tastes; he thought popular novels ridiculous, and could not for the life of him understand what made fine ladies cry their eyes out over Mr. Richardson’s tales of sentiment. He laughed loud and hearty, to be sure, but always a beat or two after the joke had landed. And why should every gentleman be expected to turn a couplet, or pore over folios? Do we think less of our friends who never penned a line of verse in their lives? Ruined, forced into idleness, dependent on his brother for every shilling, falling asleep the second he opened any book not full of battle dispatches, with no worthy work for his great strong hands to do—what a miracle it is that he did not stumble into far worse mischief! Think of Achilles, spirited away by his mother to a faraway court to keep him out of harm’s way, left to idle his days among a house full of women spinning at their distaffs: look what came of that, when Pyrrhus was born. A hot-blooded, strong young Achilles is not meant to be led around by women’s apron strings, fawning over coffee cups and dawdling by the spinning wheel. When he cannot fight, depend on it, he will soon stumble into far greater trouble.

The two elder Misses Lambert, and their mother, with whom Harry spent most of his time, lavished on him that soft, tireless, tender kindness that only women—and very few of them at that—know how to give. Who cheers the grieving, consoles the troubled, soothes the wrathful? Who doubles your joy in times of happiness, rejoices unfeignedly in your prosperity, and stands by you against the whole world in your disgrace, dressing the rankling wounds Fortune dealt you with gentle unguents and warm compresses? Who but woman, I ask you? If you, worn and battered by Fate’s blows, count one or two of these sweet physicians among your friends, return thanks to the gods for that greatest of consolations. What gentleman is not a little bit Prometheus? Who has not his rock, his unbreakable chains, and his liver gnawed raw by trouble? But the sea nymphs come—gentle, sympathetic, unshaken: they kiss our writhing feet, they moisten our parched lips with their tears, they do their blessed utmost to console us Titans, and never turn their backs on us after our fall.

Mrs. Lambert and Theo were full of nothing but pity for Harry, but Hetty’s heart seemed curiously hard, almost savage, towards him. She chafed at his unremarkable, idle state. The whole world was in arms, and could he not carry a musket? It was harvest time for glory, with hundreds of thousands of reapers out swinging their flashing sickles—could he not lift his own, and cut down at least one small sheaf of renown?

“Whatever has gotten into that little thing, to be so sharp with him?” Mr. Lambert laughed one evening, after Hetty had spent an hour firing tiny stinging shots at Harry, who sat uncomplaining on their drawing room sofa.

“Her behaviour is perfectly abominable!” cried Mrs. Lambert. “She deserves to be sent straight to bed and whipped.”

“Perhaps, mother, she cares for him more than any of the rest of us do,” Theo said softly. “That’s why she’s angry. If I were fond of someone, I would want to admire and respect him always—think everything he did was right, and believe my gentleman better than every other man alive in the world.”

“The truth is, my dear,” Mrs. Lambert smiled, “that your father is so much better than all the rest of the world, he has spoiled us all. Did you ever see anyone to compare with him?”

“Very few indeed,” Theo admitted, blushing.

“Very few. Who is as even-tempered?”

“Nobody, mamma, I think.”

“Or as brave?”

“Well, I’m sure Mr. Wolfe, or Harry, or Mr. George are very brave.”

“Or as learned and witty?”

“Oh, Mr. George is wonderfully learned, and witty too, in his way,” Theo said eagerly. “And his manners are so fine—you must admit that. Madame de Bernstein, who has seen all the world, says so too. He has this lofty, noble way about him I never see in other men. And even though he is so fond of satire, he only ever directs it at mean, petty people and acts. No gentleman could have a tenderer heart. Just yesterday, after he’d been speaking so sharply about greedy people, I looked out the window and saw him stop to buy a whole crowd of ragged children apples from the corner stall. The day before that, he brought me a new copy of Moliere, and stopped on the steps to give a whole handful of coins to a beggar. And he reads French so beautifully! Though I do agree with him about Tartuffe: clever and lively as the play is, it seems such a waste to make a low hypocrite the main character of a great work. He said Iago is just as terrible a villain, but he is not the lead of *Othello*—that honour goes to the noble Othello, undone by his own great, trusting nature. And the verses he read us—oh, I daren’t repeat them properly!”

“But you know them by heart, my dear?” Mrs. Lambert teased.

Theo blushed brighter. “Oh yes, mamma! I know them by—nonsense!”

I do believe there were soft kisses, a few palpitations, and a very flustered Miss Theo fleeing the room then, cheeks flushed as a rose at midsummer. Why had she stumbled over her words? Because her mother was looking at her so very knowingly. And why did she always peer out the window looking for George when he was due to call? Why do cheeks blush? Why do roses bloom? Old Time still flies by, carrying spring and buds, summer and bloom, autumn and seed, and winter when the bare treetops are shivering under snow.

Whenever George knocked at the door, Theo would come tripping down the stairs minutes later, heart fluttering, cheeks flushed like a fresh-picked nosegay made just in his honour. And if Harry was the one at the door? She would stay in her own room, working at her embroidery or pretending to read, and send Hetty down to greet him, or one of her little brothers if they were home from school. Her eyes were sharp enough to tell the difference between Harry’s golden curls and George’s dark locks, even from halfway across the street—no one else could tell the twins apart half the time, but Theo never once mistook one for the other. And Hetty? She was never pert, never sharp, never rude, when the dark-haired Mr. Warrington walked into the drawing room.

The Warringtons could come and go to the Lamberts’ house as they pleased, and stay as long as they liked. One afternoon, Harry sprawled on the drawing room couch looking more idle and dejected than usual, when who but Hetty came tripping down the stairs to keep him company. It was a most curious little arrangement, that the sisters would never, ever admit to any collusion: Harry called, Hetty answered. George arrived, Theo came down. It was as natural as breathing, no scheming needed.

“Pray tell me, sir,” Hetty said, dropping into a chair opposite him, “what makes your honour look so very glum this morning?”

“Ah, Hetty!” he sighed. “I’ve nothing at all to do but look glum. I remember when we were boys, and I was the laziest scapegrace alive, I was always begging my tutor for a holiday. I’d spend the whole day swinging on a gate, or skipping stones across the pond, and those empty do-nothing days were always the most melancholy of all. Now what have I got to fill my time from dawn till dark?”

“Breakfast, a walk—dinner, another walk—tea, supper, and a pipe of your fine Virginia tobacco, I suppose,” Hetty said, tossing her head.

“Only the other night, I walked Charley back to his school at the Chartreux,” Harry went on, “and I almost turned to the master and said, ‘Sir, teach me. That boy at thirteen knows more Latin and Greek than I do, ten years his elder. I’ve nothing to do all day—why not let me go back to my books, make up for all that idleness when I was a lad?’ Why are you laughing at me, Hetty?”

“I can just picture you standing at the front of the class waiting for the master to call on you!” she giggled.

“I’d never be top of any class,” Harry said humbly. “George could be top of any class in the world, but I’m no scholar. I neglected all my studies when I was young, and I never had a head for books. Our tutors hardly ever got to cane us back home at Virginia, but if they’d laid the strap on me more, it might have done me a world of good.”

Hetty drummed her little foot on the floor, and looked at the big, strong, melancholy young man slouched across the couch. “Upon my word, it might do you a world of good even now!” she nearly said out loud. “Does Charley’s stories of school canings make you long to go back to the classroom, pray?” she asked instead.

“His stories just made me see how much time I wasted when I should have been working,” Harry said simply. “I’ve no gift for books. So what am I good for? To spend my patrimony the second I step off the boat in London? Lounge around coffee houses and racecourses? Gallop after fox hounds back home? I’m good for nothing at all.”

“What? A great brave strong fellow like you, good for nothing?” Hetty cried. “I’d never admit I was useless to any woman, not if I was twice as idle as you!”

“What am I supposed to do? I’ve written to ask my mother for leave to join the army, and Madam Esmond never writes back. It’s the only thing I’m fit for. I’ve no money left to buy a commission. I spent every penny of my own, and so much of George’s already— I can’t, I won’t, ask him for more. If only my mother would send me off to fight, you know I’d jump at the chance.”

“Pshaw! A gentleman of spirit doesn’t wait for a woman to buckle his sword on for him or clean his musket for him!” Hetty huffed. “Didn’t Papa tell us about that young courtier yesterday? Sir John Armytage—”

“Sir John Armytage? I knew him well at White’s, back when I was going to the clubs. Fine noble young man, has a great estate up north.”

“And he’s engaged to the famous beauty Miss Howe, Lord Howe’s sister! But I suppose that’s no obstacle to a man who wants glory?”

“An obstacle to what?” Harry blinked, confused.

“To glory!” Hetty declared. “No woman of spirit would say ‘stay’ to her lover, no matter how much she adored him, the second his country says ‘go.’ Sir John volunteered for that new expedition being readied at court, and when the King asked him when he would be ready to sail, he said ‘Tomorrow, please your Majesty.’ The King said that was a proper soldier’s answer. Even Papa is longing to go, though he has Mamma and all us brats at home. Oh, dear! Why wasn’t I born a man? Both my brothers are going into the Church, but I know I would have made a famous little soldier!” She strode around the room as she spoke, chin held high, looking as bold and fierce as Joan of Arc herself.

Harry watched her with soft, tender admiration. “I think I’d hate to see a musket slung over that tiny shoulder of yours, or a scar on that pretty face, Hetty.”

“Wounds? Who fears wounds?” the little maid cried. “Muskets? If I could carry one, I’d use it. You all think women are only good for making puddings and stitching samplers. Why wasn’t I a man, I say? George was reading us out of Tasso yesterday—look, here’s the book, we left a mark at the verse that made me think it was written just for me.”

“Marked in the book?” Harry said dutifully, leaning forward.

“Yes! It’s about a woman furious that her brother refuses to go to war, and she says:
‘Alas! why did not Heaven these members frail
With lively force and vigour strengthen, so
That I this silken gown and slender veil
Might for a breastplate and a helm forgo;
Then should not heat, nor cold, nor rain, nor hail,
Nor storms that fall, nor blust’ring winds that blow,
Withhold me; but I would, both day and night,
In pitched field or private combat, fight—’
Fight? Yes, that I would! Why are both Papa’s boys set to be parsons? One of his children should be a soldier!”

Harry laughed a gentle, kind laugh, looking down at her small frame. He could not imagine ever lifting a hand to hurt such a tiny, fierce little warrior. “Look here,” he said, holding out his own thick finger, “this finger is almost as big around as your whole arm. How do you think you’d stand against a great big soldier? But I’d love to see any scoundrel dare lay a hand on you. I should just like to catch some rogue Frenchman daring to be rude to Miss Hester Lambert!” He started pacing up and down the room himself, chafing at the very thought of anyone hurting her.

It was this quiet, unshakable courage of his that had won Hetty’s heart, the strength she had always dreamed of in a man. She was no virago, no fierce Amazon—else by the rules of nature and contrast she would have fallen for some soft, bookish young gentleman who could play the flute. Everyone knows the old truth: great strong men have an affinity for tiny tender women, and little sharp-witted women are drawn to big honest strong men, and your burliest war heroes are almost always the ones who end up happily henpecked. It was easy to see where this pair would end up, if they kept on this path.

For a while, Harry barely felt Hetty’s little stinging jabs, letting her shoot them off without bothering to pluck the tiny arrows out of his skin. She meant to rouse him to action, of course, but his simple, generous heart was too big to pick up on small hints. She meant to shame him by saying that even a weak woman would don a breastplate and fight—but Harry either melted at the thought of her in danger, or laughed soft and warm.

“Pray what is the use of that great strong hand of yours, if you only use it to hold a skein of silk for my mother?” Hetty went on. “What good is all that strength in a drawing room? No one’s asking you to throw a man out the window, Harry! A great strong man, indeed! You could find a stronger man than you wrestling at Bartholomew Fair any day. James Wolfe isn’t a strong man at all. He looks pale and sickly, was coughing the whole time he visited last month, as if he’d just seen a ghost.”

“I never could understand why anyone would be frightened of a ghost,” Harry said.

“Pray have you ever seen one, sir?” Hetty teased.

“No. Once back home when we were boys, I thought I did—but it was only old Nathan the butler wandering around in his nightgown. I wasn’t scared even when I thought he was a ghost. There’s no such things. Nurses just tell a pack of lies about them.” Harry said gravely. “George was a little frightened, but he’s different from me. Our mother is the boldest woman you ever met, but she screams at the sight of a mouse—can’t help it, it’s just her nature. So maybe George can’t bear ghosts, and I don’t mind them.”

“George always says you’d make a far better soldier than he ever would.”

“So I think I would, if anyone had ever let me try. But he can do a thousand things better than me, or any other man alive. Why didn’t he let me volunteer on Braddock’s expedition?
