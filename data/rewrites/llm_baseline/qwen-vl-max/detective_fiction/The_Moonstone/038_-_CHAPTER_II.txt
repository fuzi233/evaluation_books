“Betteredge!” I said, pointing to the well-worn book resting on his lap, “has *Robinson Crusoe* told you tonight that you might expect to see Franklin Blake?”

“By the lord Harry, Mr. Franklin!” the old man exclaimed, springing up with my help. He stood there for a moment, eyes darting between the book and me, as if trying to decide which of us had startled him more. In the end, it was the book that won. Holding it open with both hands, he stared at its pages with an expression of pure wonder—as though he expected Robinson Crusoe himself to step out and greet us.

“There’s the very passage, Mr. Franklin!” he announced once he’d found his voice again. “As sure as I live by bread, this is what I was reading just before you came in—page one hundred and fifty-six: ‘I stood like one thunderstruck, or as if I had seen an apparition.’ Now, if that doesn’t mean, ‘Expect the sudden appearance of Mr. Franklin Blake,’ then there’s no sense left in the English language!” With that, he snapped the book shut and finally freed one hand to grasp mine.

I had expected questions—perhaps even a barrage of them—given the circumstances. But Betteredge wasn’t interested in probing just yet. Hospitality came first. When a member of the family turned up unannounced, especially after so long away, the old servant’s instinct was to welcome, not interrogate.

“Walk in, Mr. Franklin,” he said, bowing deeply as he opened the door behind him. “I’ll ask what brings you here later—I must make you comfortable first. Things have changed since you left. The house is closed, the servants gone. But never mind that! I’ll cook your dinner, and the gardener’s wife will prepare your bed. And if there’s still a bottle of our famous Latour claret down in the cellar—down your throat it shall go, Mr. Franklin! I bid you welcome, sir! Heartily welcome!”

He spoke with such warmth, struggling bravely against the silence and emptiness of the deserted home. It pained me to disappoint him. But this house belonged to Rachel now. After everything that had happened in London, could I really eat here, sleep here? My sense of propriety forbade it. So I took Betteredge by the arm and led him into the garden.

There was no avoiding it. I had to tell him the truth.

Between his deep loyalty to Rachel and his affection for me, he was clearly torn. His opinion, when he finally voiced it, came with his usual bluntness—and carried the unmistakable stamp of Betteredge’s philosophy: direct, practical, and unyielding.

“Miss Rachel has her faults—I’ve never denied it,” he began. “And riding high, now and then, is one of them. She’s been riding over you, and you’ve let her. Lord, Mr. Franklin, don’t you know women better than that by now? You’ve heard me talk about the late Mrs. Betteredge?”

I had—he’d mentioned her often, always as proof of womanly weakness and stubbornness.

“Well, Mr. Franklin,” he continued, “different women ride their horses differently. The late Mrs. Betteredge would mount up whenever I refused her something she wanted. Every time I came home from work, she’d call from the kitchen stairs—‘After your brutal treatment of me, I can’t bear to cook your dinner!’ I put up with it—for a while, just as you’ve put up with Miss Rachel. But eventually, my patience ran out. One day, I went downstairs, picked her up—affectionately, mind you—and carried her straight into the best parlour. ‘That’s where you belong, my dear,’ I said. Then I locked myself in the kitchen, took off my coat, rolled up my sleeves, and cooked my own dinner. I served it properly, ate it heartily, had my pipe and grog afterward, cleaned the table, washed the dishes, polished the cutlery, swept the hearth—everything spotless again. Then I opened the door and let her in. ‘I’ve had my dinner, my dear,’ I said. ‘And I hope you’ll find the kitchen exactly as you wish it.’ From that day on, I never cooked another meal for her.”

He paused, then added, “Moral: You’ve let Miss Rachel walk all over you in London. Don’t let her do it in Yorkshire. Come back to the house.”

It was impossible to argue with that logic. All I could do was gently explain that even Betteredge’s wisdom couldn’t sway me this time.

“It’s a lovely evening,” I said. “I’ll walk to Frizinghall, stay at the hotel, and you can come tomorrow morning to breakfast with me. I need to talk to you.”

Betteredge shook his head slowly. “I’m sorry for this,” he said. “I hoped you’d come back with things settled between you and Miss Rachel. But if you must have your way, sir—there’s no need to go all the way to Frizinghall. There’s Hotherstone’s Farm—just two miles from here. You can hardly object to that, considering Miss Rachel.” He added slyly, “Hotherstone owns the land himself.”

The name brought it all back. The farm sat in a quiet valley beside the most beautiful stream in Yorkshire. The farmer regularly rented out a bedroom and parlour to artists, anglers, and travelers. It was the perfect place to stay.

“Are the rooms available?” I asked.

“Mrs. Hotherstone herself came to me yesterday,” Betteredge replied, “asking for my recommendation.”

“I’ll take them,” I said. “With pleasure.”

We returned to the yard, where I’d left my travelling bag. Betteredge slung it over his shoulder with a stick through the handle, then looked at the house, then at me—his face full of bewilderment.

“I’ve lived a long time,” he said, “but I never expected to see this. There stands the house. Here stands Mr. Franklin Blake. And—damme—if one of them isn’t turning its back on the other and going to sleep elsewhere!”

He led the way out, muttering under his breath. “Only one miracle left,” he muttered over his shoulder. “Next thing you’ll do, Mr. Franklin, is pay me back that seven-and-sixpence you borrowed when you were a boy.”

That little jab lightened his mood. We passed through the lodge gates. Once beyond the estate, hospitality ended and curiosity began. He fell back slightly, letting me catch up.

“Fine evening for a walk, Mr. Franklin,” he said, as if we’d met by chance.

“Suppose you’d gone to Frizinghall,” he said.

“Yes?”

“I’d have had the honor of breakfasting with you tomorrow.”

“Then come breakfast with me at Hotherstone’s instead.”

“Much obliged, sir. But that wasn’t quite what I meant. You said you had something to say to me. If it’s not a secret, I’m dying to know—what brings you here so suddenly?”

“What brought me here before?” I asked.

“The Moonstone,” he answered.

“And now?”

“The Moonstone again.”

He stopped abruptly, staring at me in the twilight, as if doubting his ears.

“If that’s a joke, sir,” he said, “I’m afraid I’m growing deaf in my old age. I don’t get it.”

“It’s no joke,” I said. “I’ve come to pick up where I left off. To finish what was abandoned when I left England. I’ve come to do what no one else has done—to find out who stole the Diamond.”

“Let the Diamond be, Mr. Franklin!” he insisted. “Take my advice. That cursed Indian jewel has ruined everyone who’s touched it. Don’t waste your time—or your temper—in the prime of your life—on the Moonstone. How can you possibly succeed when Sergeant Cuff himself failed? Sergeant Cuff!” he repeated, wagging a finger at me. “The greatest policeman in England!”

“My mind is made up,” I said. “Even Sergeant Cuff doesn’t scare me. By the way, I may need to speak with him soon. Have you heard anything of him lately?”

“The Sergeant won’t help you,” Betteredge said.

“Why not?”

“There’s been a change in the police world since you left. The great Cuff has retired. He’s got a cottage in Dorking and spends his days growing roses. I have it in his own handwriting. He’s grown the white moss rose without budding it on the dog-rose first. Even Mr. Begbie, the gardener, admits he’s beaten.”

“It doesn’t matter,” I said. “I’ll manage without him. But I’ll need your help—at the start.”

I may have sounded careless. At any rate, Betteredge seemed offended.

“You might trust worse than me,” he retorted sharply.

His tone, and the hesitation that followed, suggested he knew more than he was saying.

“I expect you to help me,” I said, “in gathering the clues Sergeant Cuff left behind. I know you can do that. But can you do more?”

“What more could I possibly do, sir?” he asked, pretending humility.

“I expect more—based on what you just said.”

“Just boasting,” he replied stubbornly. “Some people are born boasters and never stop. I’m one of them.”

There was only one way to reach him. I appealed to his loyalty—to Rachel, and to me.

“Betteredge,” I said, “would you be glad to hear that Rachel and I are friends again?”

“I’ve served your family faithfully, sir—if you doubt it!”

“Do you remember how she treated me before I left England?”

“As if it were yesterday! She wrote you a letter—showed it to me. Said she was mortally offended because of your part in trying to recover her jewel. And no one could guess why.”

“Quite right,” I said. “And now I return, and she’s still offended. I knew last year the Diamond was at the root of it. I know it’s still at the root. I’ve tried to speak to her—she refuses. I’ve written—no reply. How can I clear this up? The only path left is to investigate the loss of the Moonstone—the only clue Rachel has given me.”

Those words struck him. He hesitated, then asked:

“There’s no ill-feeling on your side, Mr. Franklin—is there?”

“A little anger when I left,” I admitted. “But that’s gone. I only want to reconcile with Rachel. Nothing more.”

“You don’t fear—supposing you uncover something—about Miss Rachel herself?”

I understood the protectiveness behind his question.

“I’m as certain of her as you are,” I said. “Whatever the truth, it won’t change how I see her—or how you do.”

At that, his final resistance crumbled.

“If I’m doing wrong, Mr. Franklin,” he said, “all I can say is—I’m as innocent as the babe unborn. But I can show you the way. You remember Rosanna Spearman?”

“Of course.”

“You always thought she had something to confess about the Moonstone—something she wanted to tell you?”

“I couldn’t explain her behavior any other way.”

“You can lay that doubt to rest whenever you like.”

Now it was my turn to stop. I strained to see his face in the dimming light.

“What do you mean?” I asked, a little impatiently.

“Steady, sir,” he said. “Rosanna left a sealed letter—a letter addressed to you.”

“Where is it?”

“In the hands of a friend of hers—Limping Lucy, at Cobb’s Hole. You must have heard of her—lame girl with a crutch.”

“The fisherman’s daughter?”

“The same.”

“Why wasn’t it sent to me?”

“Limping Lucy has a mind of her own. She wouldn’t give it to anyone but you. And you’d already left England before I could write.”

“We should go back and get it now!”

“Too late tonight, sir. They’re careful with candles along the coast. They go to bed early at Cobb’s Hole.”

“Nonsense! We could get there in half an hour.”

“You might, sir. But when you arrive, you’ll find the door locked.” He pointed to a faint light below us. “There’s the Farm, Mr. Franklin. Make yourself comfortable for the night. Come to me tomorrow morning—if you’re kind enough.”

“I’ll come with you to the fisherman’s cottage?”

“Yes, sir.”

“Early?”

“As early as you like, Mr. Franklin.”

We descended the path toward the farm.
