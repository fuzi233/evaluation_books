“With all my heart,” said the General; and though his voice held itself erect, it shook a little as a spoon trembles in a teacup when the hand is tired. He paused—one of those domestic pauses in which a man arranges not only his sentences, but his breath—and then began, as if he were opening a sealed jar whose contents had soured long ago.

“My dear child had been looking forward, with a pleasure that made the days brisker for her, to the visit you had been so good as to arrange—she spoke of your charming daughter as one speaks of a promised harvest.” Here he bent to me with a gallant inclination, but the bow had a weight in it, like a courtesy made at a graveside. “Before that happiness could be served to her, as it were, we received an invitation from my old friend, the Count Carlsfeld, whose schloss stands about six leagues beyond Karnstein. It was for the series of fêtes he gave in honour of his illustrious guest, the Grand Duke Charles.”

“Yes; and very splendid, I believe, they were,” said my father.

“Princely,” returned the General. “His hospitalities are regal; he might as well have had Aladdin’s lamp on his sideboard, so lavishly did he summon wonders. The night from which my sorrow dates was given to a magnificent masquerade. The grounds were thrown open; trees were hung with coloured lamps—globes and lanterns that made each branch look candied and unreal. And there was such a display of fireworks as Paris itself had never witnessed.

“But it is the sound I cannot forget. Music—music, you know, is my weakness—such ravishing music! The finest instrumental band, perhaps, in the world, and the finest singers that could be gathered from the great operas of Europe. In those fantastically lit gardens the chateau lay moonlit, the long rows of windows sending out a rosy sheen like heat from an oven-mouth. You would walk from a bright allee into a grove that seemed to swallow you, and then—without warning—voices would steal into the silence as if the leaves themselves had learned to sing; or you would hear them rise from boats upon the lake, the water taking the notes and carrying them under your ribs. The very nightingales, if there were any, must have been silenced, ashamed to compete. I felt, as I listened, carried back into the romance and poetry of my early youth; and yet that sweetness had in it a strain—like a flute-note stretched too thin—that made the hair stir on one’s neck.

“When the fireworks had died into smoke, and the ball began, we returned to the noble suite of rooms thrown open to the dancers. A masked ball is always a beautiful sight; but such brilliance, such dazzling multiplication of faces and fabrics, I never saw before. It was a very aristocratic assembly. I was myself almost the only ‘nobody’ present.

“My dear child looked—God help me—quite beautiful. She wore no mask. Her excitement and delight lent an unspeakable charm to features that were always lovely. I remember, with an exactness that is like a wound, the glitter of light on her hair when she turned, the little quick breath that came to her after dancing, as if the air itself were spiced.

“I remarked a young lady, magnificently dressed, but wearing a mask, who appeared to be observing my ward with extraordinary interest. I had seen her earlier in the evening in the great hall; and again, for a few minutes, walking near us on the terrace under the castle windows, similarly employed. A lady also masked, richly and gravely dressed, with a stately air—as of a person of rank—accompanied her as a chaperon. Had the young lady not worn a mask, I could have been more certain whether she truly watched my poor darling. I am now well assured she did.

“We were in one of the salons. My poor dear child had been dancing and rested a little in a chair near the door; I stood near her, like a man keeping watch over a dish that is too delicate to be trusted to strangers. The two ladies I have mentioned approached; the younger took the chair next my ward, while her companion stood beside me, and for a little time addressed herself in a low tone to her charge.

“Then, availing herself of the privilege of her mask, the elder turned to me; and in the tone of an old friend—calling me by my name—she opened a conversation that piqued my curiosity exceedingly. She referred to scenes where she had met me—at Court, and in distinguished houses—touching, with a light finger, little incidents I had long ceased to think of, but which had only lain in abeyance, for they sprang instantly into life at her touch, as scents do when a lid is lifted.

“Every moment I grew more curious to know who she was. She parried my attempts with an ease almost playful. The knowledge she showed of passages in my life seemed all but unaccountable; and she appeared to take a not unnatural pleasure in foiling my curiosity, in seeing me flounder from one conjecture to another, as if she were seasoning the talk with my perplexity.

“In the meantime the young lady—whom her mother, once or twice, called by the odd name of Millarca—had, with the same ease and grace, slipped into conversation with my ward. She introduced herself by saying her mother was a very old acquaintance of mine. She spoke of the agreeable audacity a mask makes practicable; she talked like a friend; she admired my child’s dress, and insinuated, very prettily, her admiration of her beauty. She amused her with laughing criticisms upon the people crowding the ballroom, and laughed at my poor child’s fun.

“She was very witty and lively when she pleased; and after a time they had grown very good friends. Then the young stranger lowered her mask, displaying a remarkably beautiful face. I had never seen it before; neither had my dear child. But though it was new to us, the features were so engaging, as well as lovely, that it was impossible not to feel the attraction powerfully. My poor girl did so. I never saw any one more taken with another at first sight—unless, indeed, it was the stranger herself, who seemed quite to have lost her heart to her.

“Meanwhile, taking the licence of a masquerade as one takes shelter under a cloak, I put not a few questions to the elder lady.

“‘You have puzzled me utterly,’ I said, laughing. ‘Is that not enough? Won’t you now consent to stand on equal terms, and do me the kindness to remove your mask?’

“‘Can any request be more unreasonable?’ she replied. ‘Ask a lady to yield an advantage! Besides, how do you know you should recognize me? Years make changes.’

“‘As you see,’ I said, with a bow, and, I suppose, a rather melancholy little laugh.

“‘As philosophers tell us,’ she said; ‘and how do you know that a sight of my face would help you?’

“‘I should take chance for that,’ I answered. ‘It is vain trying to make yourself out an old woman; your figure betrays you.’

“‘Years, nevertheless, have passed since I saw you—rather since you saw me, for that is what I am considering. Millarca there is my daughter; I cannot then be young, even in the opinion of people whom time has taught to be indulgent; and I may not like to be compared with what you remember me. You have no mask to remove. You can offer me nothing in exchange.’

“‘My petition is to your pity, to remove it.’

“‘And mine to yours, to let it stay where it is,’ she replied.

“‘Well, then, at least you will tell me whether you are French or German; you speak both languages so perfectly.’

“‘I don’t think I shall tell you that, General; you intend a surprise, and are meditating the particular point of attack.’

“‘At all events, you won’t deny this,’ I said, ‘that being honoured by your permission to converse, I ought to know how to address you. Shall I say Madame la Comtesse?’

“She laughed; and she would, no doubt, have met me with another evasion—if, indeed, I can treat any occurrence in an interview, every circumstance of which was prearranged, as I now believe, with profoundest cunning, as liable to be modified by accident.

“‘As to that,’ she began; but she was interrupted, almost as she opened her lips, by a gentleman dressed in black.

“He looked particularly elegant and distinguished; yet his face had that drawback—so deadly pale I never saw the like, except in death. He wore no masquerade: plain evening dress, as if he were too much in earnest for any borrowed face. He said, without a smile, but with a courtly, unusually low bow:

“‘Will Madame la Comtesse permit me to say a very few words which may interest her?’

“The lady turned quickly to him and touched her lip—silence. Then she said to me, lightly, as if we were only changing partners in a dance: ‘Keep my place for me, General; I shall return when I have said a few words.’

“With this injunction, playfully given, she walked a little aside with the gentleman in black and talked for some minutes—apparently very earnestly. I could not hear their words; the music and the continual rustle of the crowd made a kind of surf of sound, and in that bright room the very noise seemed to conceal rather than reveal, as though the night had learned to disguise itself not only with masks, but with melody. They then walked away slowly together into the press, and I lost them for some minutes.

“I spent the interval cudgelling my brains for a conjecture as to the identity of the lady who remembered me so kindly. I thought of turning to join the conversation between my pretty ward and the Countess’s daughter, and of trying whether, by the time her mother returned, I might not have a surprise in store for her—her name, title, château, and estates ready at my fingers’ ends, as neatly as a steward’s list.

“But at that moment she returned, accompanied by the pale man in black, who said:

“‘I shall return and inform Madame la Comtesse when her carriage is at the door.’

“He withdrew with a bow.”
