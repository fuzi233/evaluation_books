The Palmers departed for Cleveland the following day, leaving the households at Barton Park to their own devices. However, this solitude proved fleeting. Elinor had scarcely finished reflecting on Charlotte's inexplicable happiness, Mr. Palmer's surprisingly simple demeanor despite his evident talents, and the peculiar disconnects that often marred marital unions, before Sir John and Mrs. Jennings' fervent dedication to social engagement introduced her to fresh acquaintances. During a morning outing to Exeter, they encountered two young ladies whom Mrs. Jennings delightedly identified as relatives. This discovery prompted Sir John to immediately extend an invitation for them to visit the park once their business in Exeter concluded.

Lady Middleton received news of this impending visit with genuine alarm. She had never laid eyes on these strangers, possessed no evidence of their refinement or gentility, and found her husband's and mother's assurances utterly unpersuasive. The fact that they were her kin only compounded her dread. Mrs. Jennings attempted to soothe her daughter, advising her not to mind their lack of fashion, arguing that as cousins, they were bound to tolerate one another regardless. Yet, with no possibility of preventing their arrival, Lady Middleton resigned herself to the situation with the practiced stoicism of a well-bred woman, reserving her complaints for frequent, gentle reprimands directed daily at her husband.

When the sisters finally arrived, their appearance was neither ungenteel nor unfashionable. Dressed sharply and behaving with civility, they were enchanted by the house and rapturous over the furnishings. Furthermore, possessing an overwhelming affection for children, they won Lady Middleton's favor within the hour. Her ladyship declared them delightful girls—a sentiment of enthusiastic admiration in her household. Sir John's confidence swelled with this praise, and he hastened to the cottage to announce the Steeles' arrival to the Dashwood sisters, hailing them as the sweetest girls in existence.

Elinor, however, remained skeptical. She knew that "sweet" girls could be found everywhere in England, varying widely in form, face, temperament, and intellect. Sir John insisted that the entire family accompany him to the park to meet his guests. "Come now!" he urged. "Pray come—you must come! You'll adore them. Lucy is monstrously pretty, so good-humored, and agreeable! The children are already clinging to her as if she were an old friend. Both sisters are dying to see you; they've heard at Exeter that you are the most beautiful creatures in the world, and I've confirmed it for them, adding much more besides. You'll be delighted. They've filled the carriage with toys for the children. How can you be so cross? Besides, you're my cousins, and they are my wife's cousins, so you must be related." Despite his persistence, he failed to secure immediate company, managing only a promise of a visit within a day or two. Disappointed by their indifference, he returned home to boast anew to the Miss Steeles.

At the scheduled meeting, the older sister, nearing thirty, presented a plain and unsympathetic countenance, offering little to admire. The younger, however, aged barely twenty-three, displayed considerable beauty. Her features were pleasing, accompanied by a sharp, quick eye and a brisk air that lacked true elegance but granted her distinct presence. Their manners were notably civil, and Elinor quickly credited them with a measure of sense, observing how diligently and judiciously they endeavored to please Lady Middleton. With the children, they were in a state of perpetual ecstasy, praising their looks, seeking their attention, and accommodating their whims. Any spare moments were devoted to admiring Lady Middleton's activities or copying patterns from her latest dresses, which had captivated them previously.

Fortunately for those who court through such minor vices, a mother obsessed with praising her children is simultaneously the most demanding and the most gullible of humans. Her expectations are extreme, yet she accepts anything. Consequently, the Miss Steeles' excessive affection and patience toward Lady Middleton's offspring were viewed by the hostess without suspicion. She watched with maternal satisfaction as her cousins endured impertinent intrusions and mischievous pranks. She saw their ribbons loosened, hair tugged, bags rifled, and cutlery stolen, assuming it was mutual enjoyment. The only surprise she registered was that Elinor and Marianne sat so calmly by, seemingly uninterested in the commotion.

"John is in such high spirits today!" she exclaimed when Sir John tossed a pocket-handkerchief out the window. "Full of monkey tricks." Shortly after, upon seeing the second boy pinch one of the sisters' fingers, she fondly remarked, "How playful William is!" Then, tenderly embracing a three-year-old girl named Annamaria, who had been silent for two minutes, she proclaimed, "Here is my sweet little Annamaria... Never was there such a quiet little thing!" Unfortunately, the pin in her bonnet caught the child's neck during the embrace, eliciting screams so violent they rivaled any naturally noisy creature.

Lady Middleton's consternation was profound, but it paled compared to the panic of the Miss Steeles. All three acted swiftly to soothe the victim. The child was cradled in her mother's lap, kissed incessantly, treated with lavender water by one sister kneeling beside her, and fed sugar plums by the other. Yet, rewarded thus, the child continued to scream and kick her brothers, rendering all united efforts futile until Lady Middleton recalled a previous incident where apricot marmalade cured a bruised temple. Proposing the same remedy, they noted a brief cessation in the screaming, fostering hope. The child was carried out to seek the medicine, followed by the two eager boys, leaving the four young ladies alone in a silence unknown to the room for hours.

"Poor little creatures," sighed Miss Steele as the children departed. "It might have been a very sad accident."
"I hardly know how," cried Marianne, "unless it had been under totally different circumstances. But this is the usual method of heightening alarm where there is nothing truly alarming."
"What a sweet woman Lady Middleton is!" observed Lucy Steele.
Marianne remained silent; she could not feign feelings she did not possess, however trivial the occasion. Thus, the burden of lying for the sake of politeness fell entirely upon Elinor. She managed the situation by speaking of Lady Middleton with more warmth than she felt, though far less effusively than Lucy.

"And Sir John too!" cried the elder sister. "What a charming man he is!"
Again, Elinor's simple, just commendation lacked the éclat of her companion's enthusiasm. She merely noted that he was perfectly good-humored and friendly.
"And what a charming little family they have! I never saw such fine children in my life. I declare I quite doat upon them already, and indeed I am always distractedly fond of children."
"I should guess so," Elinor smiled, "given what I witnessed this morning."
"I have a notion," said Lucy, "that you think the little Middletons rather too indulged; perhaps they are spoiled enough. But it is so natural in Lady Middleton. For my part, I love to see children full of life and spirit; I cannot bear them if they are tame and quiet."
"I confess," replied Elinor, "that while I am at Barton Park, I never think of tame and quiet children with any abhorrence."

A short pause ensued, broken by Miss Steele, who seemed eager for conversation. "And how do you like Devonshire, Miss Dashwood?" she asked abruptly. "I suppose you were very sorry to leave Sussex."
Surprised by the familiarity, Elinor admitted she was.
"Norland is a prodigious beautiful place, isn't it?" added Miss Steele.
"We've heard Sir John admire it excessively," said Lucy, offering a mild apology for her sister's boldness.
"I think everyone *must* admire it," Elinor replied. "Though it is not to be supposed that anyone can estimate its beauties as we do."
"And had you a great many smart beaux there? I suppose you haven't so many here; for my part, I think they are a vast addition always."
"But why should you think," Lucy interjected, looking ashamed, "that there aren't as many genteel young men in Devonshire as Sussex?"
"Nay, my dear, I'm sure I don't pretend to say there aren't. There are plenty in Exeter. But I couldn't tell about Norland, and I was only afraid the Miss Dashwoods might find it dull without them. Perhaps you young ladies don't care about beaux and'd rather be without them. For my part, I think they're vastly agreeable, provided they dress smart and behave civil. But I can't bear them dirty and nasty. Now, take Mr. Rose at Exeter—a prodigiously smart young man, a clerk to Mr. Simpson, quite a beau. Yet if you meet him in the morning, he's not fit to be seen. Suppose your brother was quite a beau before he married, Miss Dashwood, since he was so rich?"
"Upon my word," Elinor responded, "I cannot tell you, for I do not perfectly comprehend the meaning of the word. But this I can say: if he ever was a beau before marriage, he is one still, for there is not the slightest alteration in him."
"Oh! dear! One never thinks of married men being beaux—they have something else to do."
