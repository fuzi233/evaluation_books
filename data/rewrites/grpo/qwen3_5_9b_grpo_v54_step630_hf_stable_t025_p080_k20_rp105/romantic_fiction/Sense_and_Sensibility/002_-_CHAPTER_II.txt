Mrs. John Dashwood settled into her new dominion at Norland, transforming the estate into a kingdom where she reigned supreme. Her mother and her sisters-in-law were relegated to the status of distinguished guests, a title that, while polite, served as a gentle reminder of their diminished standing. Yet, Mrs. Dashwood maintained a veneer of quiet civility toward them, and her husband, Mr. John Dashwood, offered them a kindness that was as genuine as it was limited, extending to everyone except himself, his wife, and their beloved infant son. With earnestness, he urged them to view Norland as their permanent home, a sentiment that Mrs. Dashwood eagerly embraced. For her, staying amidst the echoes of her past joys was the only logical course until she could secure a residence nearby.

Mrs. Dashwood possessed a heart that was a mirror to her circumstances. In times of mirth, her spirits soared with a buoyancy that seemed to generate its own happiness; conversely, in moments of sorrow, her imagination carried her so far beyond the reach of consolation that grief itself seemed impossible to sustain.

However, the prospect of aiding her sisters weighed heavily on her conscience. The idea of stripping three thousand pounds from the inheritance of their "dear little boy" struck her as a monstrous act of cruelty. She implored her husband to reconsider. How could he justify robbing his only child of such a substantial sum? Furthermore, she argued, what claim did the Miss Dashwoods hold against him? They were connected only by half-blood, a relationship she deemed virtually non-existent. It was common knowledge that affection rarely bridged the gap between children of different marriages. Why should he ruin his own future and that of his poor little Harry by squandering his fortune on distant relatives?

"It was my father's final request," Mr. Dashwood replied solemnly.

"My father was likely light-headed at the time," Mrs. Dashwood countered sharply. "Had he been in his right mind, he would never have asked you to give away half your fortune from your own child."

"He made no specific stipulation regarding the amount, my dear Fanny," he explained gently. "He merely requested that I assist them and improve their situation to the best of my ability. Perhaps he would have left it entirely to my discretion. But since he demanded a promise, I felt bound to give it. The vow stands, and it must be kept. We must do something for them once they depart Norland."

"Very well," Mrs. Dashwood conceded, though her tone remained skeptical. "Let us do something. But let it not be three thousand pounds. Remember, once money is parted with, it returns to us no more. Your sisters will marry, and it will vanish forever. Unless..."

"Unless what?"

"Unless it could be restored to our poor little boy."

Mr. Dashwood's face fell into a grave expression. "Indeed, that would alter matters significantly. There may come a time when Harry regrets such a large sum being dispersed. Should he one day have a numerous family, this capital would prove a most convenient addition."

"To be sure, it would."

"Then perhaps it would be wiser for all concerned if we reduced the sum by half. Five hundred pounds per sister would be a prodigious windfall!"

"Oh! Beyond anything great!" Mrs. Dashwood exclaimed, feigning surprise. "What brother in his right mind would offer half so much, even if they were truly his sisters? And considering they are only half-sisters—"

"But you possess such a generous spirit!"

"I would never wish to appear mean," he replied. "On such occasions, one would rather err on the side of excess than deficiency. No one could possibly think I have done enough; even they could hardly expect more."

"They may expect anything," she retorted. "But we must look to your capacity, not theirs. Certainly, I believe I can afford to give them five hundred pounds apiece. As it stands, upon their mother's death, they will each inherit about three thousand pounds—a very comfortable fortune for any young woman."

"To be sure," she agreed. "And indeed, it seems they require no further aid. Divided among them, they will hold ten thousand pounds. Whether they marry or remain single, they will live comfortably on the interest alone."

"That is true," Mr. Dashwood admitted. "Therefore, I wonder if it would be more prudent to assist their mother while she lives, rather than providing a lump sum for her daughters. Perhaps an annuity?"

Mrs. Dashwood paused, weighing the suggestion.

"Yes," she said slowly. "It is preferable to parting with fifteen hundred pounds at once. However, if Mrs. Dashwood lives fifteen years, we shall be utterly ruined."

"Fifteen years! My dear Fanny, her life is hardly worth half that purchase."

"Certainly not; yet, people seem to live forever when an annuity is involved. She is stout, healthy, and hardly forty. An annuity is a binding contract; it recurs annually and cannot be escaped. You are unaware of the trouble you invite. I have suffered greatly under similar obligations. My mother was burdened by three annuities to old servants left by my father. Twice a year, payments had to be made, accompanied by the tedious journey to deliver them. Then came the false reports of death, only to be corrected later. My mother grew weary of it all. She felt her income was no longer her own, shackled by perpetual claims. It bred such an abhorrence in me that I would not agree to pay an annuity for all the world."

"It is certainly an unpleasant drain on one's resources," Mr. Dashwood acknowledged. "As your mother noted, one's fortune ceases to be entirely one's own. Being tethered to regular payments strips away one's independence."

"Undoubtedly. And worse still, there is no gratitude to be gained. They assume it is their due; you perform no more than is expected, and no thanks are offered. If I were you, I would act entirely at my own discretion. I would not bind myself to a yearly allowance. It may become inconvenient to spare a hundred, or even fifty pounds from our own expenses in lean years."

"You are right, my love," Mr. Dashwood conceded. "It is better to avoid an annuity. Any occasional gift will be of far greater assistance than a fixed income, for they might simply elevate their standard of living without becoming truly richer. A sporadic present of fifty pounds will prevent distress and, I believe, amply fulfill my father's wish."

"To be sure," Mrs. Dashwood said, her voice softening into persuasion. "In truth, I am convinced your father never intended for you to give them a penny. The assistance he envisioned was merely practical: finding a modest house, helping with the move, sending seasonal gifts of fish and game. I wager my life he meant nothing more. It would be strange and unreasonable to expect financial ruin. Consider how comfortably they will live on the interest of seven thousand pounds, plus the fifty pounds a year each girl receives from her share. Altogether, they will have five hundred pounds annually. What more could four women possibly need? They will live frugally—no carriages, no horses, few servants, no social engagements. Five hundred a year is ample. I cannot imagine how they would spend half of it. To suggest you give more is absurd; they will be far more capable of giving *you* something."

"Upon my word," Mr. Dashwood said, his resolve crumbling under her logic, "you are perfectly right. My father certainly meant nothing more than you describe. I now clearly understand his intent, and I shall strictly fulfill my engagement through acts of kindness and neighborly assistance as you have outlined. When your mother moves, I shall gladly help accommodate her, and perhaps offer a small gift of furniture."

"Certainly," Mrs. Dashwood replied. "But there is one final matter. When your father and mother moved to Norland, although the furniture of Stanhill was sold, all the china, plate, and linen were preserved and left to your mother. Her new home will be almost fully furnished the moment she arrives."

"A valuable legacy, indeed," Mr. Dashwood murmured. "Though some of that plate would have been a pleasant addition to our own household."

"Yes; and the breakfast china is twice as exquisite as ours. Too handsome, in my opinion, for any home they could ever afford. But so it is. Your father thought only of them. And I must add, you owe him no particular gratitude, nor strict adherence to his wishes. We both know that if he had been able, he would have left almost everything in the world to them."

The argument was irrefutable. It supplied the final decision Mr. Dashwood lacked, and he resolved that it would be not only unnecessary but indecorous to provide the widow and her children with more than the mere gestures of neighborly kindness his wife had suggested.
