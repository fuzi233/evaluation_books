Thirty days after leaving Dawson, the Salt Water Mail train, with Buck and his companions leading the pack, stumbled into Skaguay. They were creatures of ruin, utterly exhausted and broken down. Buck's massive one hundred and forty pounds had shriveled to one hundred and fifteen. While his fellow dogs were naturally lighter, they had shed proportionately more weight than he. Pike, the habitual liar who had spent his life feigning injuries, was now limping with genuine agony. Sol-leks hobbled, and Dub suffered from a badly wrenched shoulder blade.

Every footstep was a tragedy. Their feet, raw and sore, struck the trail with a heavy thud that jarred their entire bodies, doubling the fatigue of the day's journey. Nothing was physically wrong with them save for a profound, soul-crushing exhaustion. This was not the weariness born of sudden, intense exertion, from which one might recover within hours. It was the deep, draining fatigue of months of relentless labor. There was no reserve energy left, no capacity for recuperation. Every muscle, fiber, and cell was depleted. The cause was clear: in less than five months, they had traversed two thousand five hundred miles, enduring only five days of rest during the final eighteen hundred. By the time they reached Skaguay, they were on their last legs, barely able to keep the traces taut, and on downhill slopes, they struggled merely to avoid being run over by the sled.

"Mush on, poor sore feets," the driver coaxed as they tottered down the main street. "Dis is de las'. Den we get one long res'. Eh? For sure. One bully long res'."

The drivers assumed a lengthy stop was imminent. Having covered twelve hundred miles with only two days of rest, they felt entitled to a period of leisure. However, the mail service was clogged with Alpine congestion due to the flood of prospectors rushing to the Klondike, leaving families and loved ones behind. Furthermore, official orders mandated that fresh batches of Hudson Bay dogs replace the current team, deemed worthless for the trail. Since dogs counted for little compared to dollars, the decision was made to sell them.

Three days passed, confirming how truly weak and drained Buck and his mates had become. Then, on the morning of the fourth day, two men from the United States approached and purchased the entire team, harness included, for a mere song. They introduced themselves as Hal and Charles. Charles was a middle-aged man with pale features, watery eyes, and a mustache that curled aggressively upward, mocking the drooping lip beneath it. Hal was a boy of nineteen or twenty, armed with a Colt revolver and a hunting knife, his belt bristling with cartridges. This belt was his defining feature, advertising a callowness that was sheer and terrifying. Both men were completely out of place in the North, an anomaly that defied explanation.

Buck observed the transaction, hearing the money change hands between the buyer and the government agent. He sensed that the Scotch half-breed and the mail drivers were departing his life forever. When driven to the new owners' camp, Buck found a scene of utter sloppiness: a tent half-stretched, dirty dishes piled up, and chaos everywhere. Most disturbingly, he saw a woman named Mercedes, Charles's wife and Hal's sister.

Buck watched them anxiously as they began dismantling the camp. Their movements lacked efficiency, filled instead with frantic effort. The tent was rolled into a clumsy bundle three times larger than necessary. Dirty dishes were stuffed away unwashed. Mercedes constantly interrupted, fluttering in their path with endless chatter and advice. She argued whether a clothes-sack belonged on the front or back of the sled; when they placed it on the back, she immediately discovered items that should have stayed inside it, forcing them to unload and restart.

Three men from a neighboring tent emerged, grinning and winking at one another.

"You've got a right smart load as it is," one remarked. "Though it's none of my business, I'd advise against towing that tent if I were you."

"Undreamed of!" cried Mercedes, throwing her hands up in dainty horror. "How could I possibly manage without a tent?"

"It's springtime; you won't encounter more cold weather," the man replied calmly.

She shook her head firmly. Charles and Hal piled the remaining odds and ends onto the already mountainous heap.

"Do you think it'll ride?" another man asked.

"Why shouldn't it?" Charles snapped curtly.

"Oh, that's fine, that's fine," the man hurried to assure him, looking meek. "I was just wondering... it seemed a bit top-heavy."

Charles turned his back and tightened the lashings poorly.

"And of course, the dogs can haul that contraption all day," affirmed the second observer.

"Certainly," Hal said with icy politeness, gripping the gee-pole with one hand and swinging his whip with the other. "Mush! Mush on there!"

The dogs lunged against their breastbands, straining hard for a moment before collapsing. They could not move the sled.

"The lazy brutes! I'll show them," Hal shouted, raising his whip.

Mercedes intervened, grabbing the whip and wrenching it from his grasp. "Oh, Hal, you mustn't!" she cried. "The poor dears! Promise me you won't be harsh with them for the rest of the trip, or I won't go a single step."

"Precious lot you know about dogs," her brother sneered. "Leave me alone. They're lazy; you have to whip them to get anything out of them. That's their nature. Ask anyone."

Mercedes looked at them with imploring eyes, her pretty face etched with revulsion at the thought of pain.

"They're weak as water," one of the bystanders noted. "Completely exhausted. They need a rest."

"Rest be blanked," Hal spat, his beardless lips curling. Mercedes gasped in pain and sorrow at the oath. Yet, being a protective sister, she immediately defended him. "Never mind that man," she insisted. "You're driving our dogs; do what you think best."

Once again, Hal's whip cracked against the dogs. They threw themselves against the breastbands, digging their claws into the packed snow, lowering their bodies, and pouring out every ounce of strength. The sled remained immovable, anchored by its weight. After two futile attempts, they stood panting. The whip whistled savagely until Mercedes dropped to her knees before Buck, tears streaming down her face, wrapping her arms around his neck.

"You poor, poor dears," she sobbed sympathetically. "Why don't you pull harder? Then you wouldn't be whipped." Buck disliked her, yet he felt too miserable to resist her touch, accepting it as part of the day's torment.

One of the onlookers, holding back angry words, finally spoke up. "It doesn't matter what happens to you, but for the dogs' sake, let me tell you: you can help them immensely by breaking out that sled. The runners are frozen solid. Lean your weight against the gee-pole, push right and left, and force it free."

Following this advice, Hal broke the frozen runners. The overloaded, unwieldy sled lurched forward, Buck and his mates struggling frantically under the storm of blows. A hundred yards ahead, the path curved sharply downward toward the main street. An experienced handler would have been needed to keep the top-heavy sled upright, but Hal was not such a man. As they swung around the turn, the sled tipped over, spilling half its contents through the loose lashings. The dogs did not stop. The lightened sled bounced on its side behind them. Fueled by anger at their mistreatment and the unfair burden, Buck erupted into a run, the team following his lead. Hal screamed "Whoa! Whoa!" but they ignored him. He tripped, was pulled off his feet, and the overturned sled crushed over him. The dogs dashed up the street, scattering the rest of the gear along the main thoroughfare.

Kind-hearted citizens caught the fleeing dogs and gathered the scattered belongings. They also offered counsel. "If you expect to reach Dawson," they advised, "you need half the load and twice the number of dogs." Reluctantly, Hal, his sister, and his brother-in-law listened, pitched the tent, and sorted through the equipment. Canned goods were produced, causing laughter among the men, for canned provisions on the Long Trail were a fantasy. "Blankets for a hotel," one joked. "Half as many is too much; get rid of them. Throw away that tent and all those dishes—who's going to wash them anyway? Do you think you're traveling on a Pullman car?"

Thus began the ruthless elimination of the superfluous. Mercedes wept as her clothing bags were dumped on the ground and item after item was discarded. She cried generally and specifically over each lost possession, clasping her hands around her knees and rocking back and forth in heartbreak. She declared she would not proceed an inch, not for a dozen Charleses. She appealed to everyone and everything, eventually wiping her eyes and casting out even essential articles of clothing. In her fervor, having finished with her own belongings, she turned on her men's possessions, sweeping through them like a tornado.
