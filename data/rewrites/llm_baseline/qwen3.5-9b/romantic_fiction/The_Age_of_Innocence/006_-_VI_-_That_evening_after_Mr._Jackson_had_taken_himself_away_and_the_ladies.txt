That evening, once Mr. Jackson had departed and the ladies had retreated to their chintz-draped bedrooms, Newland Archer climbed the stairs to his private study. As always, a watchful hand had tended the fire and trimmed the lamp. The room, lined with endless rows of books, adorned with bronze and steel figurines of "The Fencers" on the mantel, and filled with reproductions of masterpieces, seemed uniquely warm and inviting.

As he sank into his leather armchair before the hearth, his gaze landed on a large portrait of May Welland—a gift from her in the early days of their courtship. It now dominated the table, pushing aside all other images. With a fresh wave of reverence, he studied the open brow, the earnest eyes, and the cheerful, innocent smile of the young woman destined to be the guardian of his soul. Yet, staring back at him through May's familiar face was something terrifying: the product of the rigid social system he both inhabited and revered. This was the girl who knew nothing and expected everything. Suddenly, marriage ceased to appear as the safe harbor he had been taught to believe in; instead, it revealed itself as a voyage across uncharted waters.

The scandal surrounding the Countess Olenska had agitated his settled beliefs, setting them adrift in dangerous currents. His own earlier declaration—"Women should be free, as free as we are"—struck deep roots in a problem his world conveniently ignored. In polite society, "nice" women, regardless of their suffering, never claimed the liberty he envisioned; consequently, generous men like himself were all too eager to grant it verbally, masking their true adherence to convention with chivalric rhetoric. In truth, these words were merely a disguise for the iron laws binding everyone to the old patterns.

Here, however, he found himself pledged to defend, on behalf of his fiancée's cousin, behavior that, if committed by his own wife, would invite the full wrath of Church and State. Of course, the dilemma was theoretical; since he was not a reckless Polish nobleman, speculating on his wife's rights in such a scenario was absurd. Yet, Newland Archer was too imaginative to ignore the possibility that the bond between him and May might become a source of pain for reasons far more subtle than mere infidelity. How much did they truly know of one another? It was his duty as a "gentleman" to hide his past from her, and hers, as a marriageable maiden, to possess no past worth hiding. What if, driven by quieter, deeper forces, they grew weary of each other, misunderstood, or irritated?

He surveyed the marriages of his friends—the ones deemed happily united—and found none that resembled the passionate, tender companionship he dreamed of sharing with May. He realized that such an ideal required experience, adaptability, and independent judgment—qualities she had been meticulously trained *not* to possess. With a chill of dread, he foresaw his union devolving into what surrounded him: a dull alliance of material and social interests, cemented by ignorance on one side and hypocrisy on the other. Lawrence Lefferts came to mind as the husband who had perfected this enviable ideal. True to his role as the high priest of form, he had molded his wife entirely to his convenience. Even during his notorious affairs with other men's wives, she wandered about in a blissful unawareness, claiming, "Lawrence is so frightfully strict." She had even blushed indignantly when anyone hinted that Julius Beaufort, a "foreigner" of dubious lineage, maintained "another establishment."

Archer tried to comfort himself with the notion that he was not quite as foolish as Larry Lefferts, nor May as simple as poor Gertrude Lefferts. But the distinction was merely one of intellect, not of moral standard. In reality, they all existed within a kind of hieroglyphic world where the truth was never spoken, acted, or even thought, but only represented by arbitrary symbols. Just as Mrs. Welland, knowing precisely why Archer had urged her to announce May's engagement at the Beaufort ball (and expecting nothing less), felt compelled to feign reluctance and pretend she had been forced into compliance, so too did the savage bride in primitive tales scream as she was dragged from her parents' tent.

The result was that the young girl at the center of this elaborate charade became even more enigmatic because of her sheer openness. She was frank, poor darling, because she had nothing to hide; she was assured because she knew of nothing to fear. Armed with nothing more substantial than this, she was about to be plunged overnight into what people euphemistically termed "the facts of life."

Newland loved her sincerely, though placidly. He adored her radiant beauty, her health, her horsemanship, her grace, and her growing interest in literature and ideas under his tutelage. (She had progressed far enough to mock the *Idylls of the King*, though not yet to appreciate the depth of *Ulysses* or the *Lotus Eaters*.) She was straightforward, loyal, and brave, possessing a sense of humor proven chiefly by her laughter at *his* jokes. He sensed, in the depths of her innocently gazing soul, a warmth waiting to be awakened.

Yet, after circling her briefly, he returned discouraged. All this frankness and innocence appeared to him as an artificial construct. Untamed human nature was rarely so open; it was usually bristling with the twists and defenses of instinctive guile. He felt oppressed by this manufactured purity, so cunningly crafted by a conspiracy of mothers, aunts, grandmothers, and long-dead ancestors, simply because it was what he desired, what he felt entitled to demand, so that he might enjoy the lordly pleasure of shattering it like a statue made of snow.

These reflections carried a certain cliché, common to young men approaching their wedding day. Usually, they were accompanied by a sense of guilt and humility, feelings absent in Newland Archer. He could not lament, as Thackeray's heroes often did, that he lacked a blank slate to offer his bride in exchange for her unblemished one. He could not escape the realization that if he had been raised as she had, they would have been equally lost, no more capable of navigating life than the Babes in the Wood. Nor could he, despite his anxious rumination, find any honest reason—aside from his own fleeting pleasure and masculine vanity—why his bride should not have been granted the same freedom of experience as himself.

Such questions drifted through his mind inevitably at such an hour, but he recognized that their uncomfortable persistence stemmed from the untimely arrival of the Countess Olenska. Here he was, at the precise moment of his betrothal—a time for pure thoughts and cloudless hopes—suddenly thrust into a vortex of scandal that raised every issue he had hoped to avoid.

"Hang Ellen Olenska!" he muttered, covering the fire and beginning to undress. He could not fully grasp why her fate should concern him, yet he dimly sensed he had only just begun to measure the risks inherent in the championship his engagement demanded.

A few days later, the storm broke. The Lovell Mingotts issued cards for what was known as "a formal dinner"—complete with three extra footmen, double portions for every course, and Roman punch served mid-meal. They headed the invitations with the phrase "To meet the Countess Olenska," following the hospitable American custom of treating strangers as royalty or at least as their ambassadors.

The guest list displayed a boldness and discernment that only the initiated could recognize as the hand of Catherine the Great. Alongside eternal fixtures like the Selfridge Merrys, invited everywhere simply because they always were, and the Beauforts, linked by a tenuous claim of kinship, sat Mr. Sillerton Jackson and his sister Sophy (who followed her brother's lead unquestioningly). Also present were the Lawrence Leffertses, Mrs. Lefferts Rushworth (the lovely widow), the Harry Thorleys, the Reggie Chiverses, and young Morris Dagonet with his wife, a van der Luyden. Indeed, the assembly was perfectly curated, comprising the inner circle of individuals who, throughout the long New York season, amused themselves together day and night with seemingly undiminished enthusiasm.

Forty-eight hours later, the unthinkable occurred. Every single guest declined the Mingotts' invitation save for the Beauforts and the elder Mr. Jackson with his sister. The intended snub was sharpened by the fact that even the Reggie Chiverses, belonging to the Mingott clan, participated in the rejection. Furthermore, the notes were uniformly phrased: "We regret that we are unable to accept," omitting the customary mitigation of a "prior engagement" that ordinary courtesy dictated.

New York society, in those days, was far too small and resource-scarce for anyone—not even livery stable keepers, butlers, or cooks—to remain ignorant of exactly which evenings others were available. Thus, the recipients of Mrs. Lovell Mingott's invitations could cruelly demonstrate their resolve not to meet the Countess Olenska.
