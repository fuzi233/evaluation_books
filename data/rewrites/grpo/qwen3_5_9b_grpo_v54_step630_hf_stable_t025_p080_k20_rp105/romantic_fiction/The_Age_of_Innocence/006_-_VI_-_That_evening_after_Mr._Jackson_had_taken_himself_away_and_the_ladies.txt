That evening, once Mr. Jackson had retreated and the ladies had withdrawn to their chintz-draped sanctuary, Newland Archer climbed the stairs to his study with a thoughtful heaviness. A faithful hand had tended the fire and trimmed the lamp, transforming the room—lined with books, adorned with bronze fencers, and filled with reproductions of masterpieces—into a haven of singular comfort.

As he sank into his armchair before the blaze, his gaze settled upon a large portrait of May Welland. It was a gift from her early in their courtship, now the sole occupant of the table's center. With a sudden, reverent awe, he studied the frankness of her brow, the seriousness of her eyes, and the cheerful innocence of her mouth. The girl whose soul he was destined to guard stared back at him, yet through May's familiar face, he saw something terrifying: a stranger born of the very social system he revered. This young creature, knowing nothing and expecting everything, made him realize that marriage was not the safe harbor his upbringing had promised, but a voyage into uncharted waters.

The shadow of the Countess Olenska had already begun to unsettle his convictions. His own declaration—"Women should be free, as free as we are"—echoed in his mind, challenging a truth his world insisted did not exist. In polite society, "nice" women, no matter how wronged, never demanded such liberty; thus, men of his generation could feign chivalry without consequence. Yet here he stood, pledged to defend the conduct of his betrothed's cousin, behavior that, if committed by his own wife, would invite the full wrath of Church and State. While the dilemma seemed purely hypothetical, Newland knew his imagination well enough to fear that the bond between him and May might tighten painfully. What did they truly know of one another? His duty as a "decent" man required him to hide his past, and hers, as a marriageable girl, left her with no past to reveal. He worried that beneath the surface, they might tire of each other, misunderstand, or irritate. Reviewing the marriages of his peers, he found none that mirrored the passionate comradeship he envisioned. Instead, he saw dull alliances of material interest, held together by ignorance on one side and hypocrisy on the other. Lawrence Lefferts epitomized this ideal; a high priest of form who had molded his wife so perfectly to his convenience that she remained blissfully unaware of his affairs with other men's wives, blushing indignantly only when reminded of Julius Beaufort's "other establishment."

Archer tried to comfort himself with the notion that he was not quite so foolish as Larry Lefferts, nor May so simple as Gertrude, but the distinction lay merely in intelligence, not in moral standards. They all inhabited a world of hieroglyphs, where reality was never spoken or acted upon, but only represented by arbitrary signs. Just as Mrs. Welland, fully aware of why Archer had pushed for the engagement announcement, felt compelled to simulate reluctance and pretend she had been forced into it, so too did the young girl at the center of this elaborate charade remain inscrutable. She was frank because she had nothing to hide, and assured because she knew nothing to fear. With no preparation for the harsh realities of life, she was about to be plunged into them.

He was sincerely, placidly in love. He adored her radiant beauty, her health, her horsemanship, and the shy curiosity she was developing under his guidance. She was straightforward, loyal, and brave, possessing a sense of humor that manifested in her laughter at his jokes. He sensed, deep within her innocent gaze, a warmth waiting to be awakened. Yet, as he surveyed her, he felt discouraged. This frankness and innocence were artificial constructs, manufactured by a conspiracy of mothers, aunts, and grandmothers because society believed it was what he desired—a purity he could then shatter with the lordly pleasure of a conqueror.

These reflections were common to young men approaching their wedding day, usually accompanied by a sense of guilt. Newland felt none. He could not lament, as Thackeray's heroes often did, that he lacked a blank page to offer his bride. He knew that if he had been raised as she had, neither of them would have been capable of navigating the world. He could find no honest reason, aside from his own vanity and desire for pleasure, why she should not have enjoyed the same freedom of experience as he.

Yet, at this hour, these thoughts were driven by the inopportune arrival of the Countess Olenska. Here he was, at the precipice of his betrothal, thrust into a scandal that complicated every hope he held. "Hang Ellen Olenska!" he muttered as he extinguished the fire and began to undress. He could not understand why her fate should concern him, yet he sensed he had only just begun to measure the risks his engagement now imposed upon him.

A few days later, the storm broke.

The Lovell Mingotts issued cards for a "formal dinner"—complete with extra footmen, double portions, and Roman punch—headlined with the words "To meet the Countess Olenska," treating the stranger with the hospitality reserved for royalty. The guest list was curated with the boldness of a tsar: the Selfridge Merrys, the Beauforts, Mr. Sillerton Jackson and his sister Sophy, the Lawrence Leffertses, Mrs. Lefferts Rushworth, the Harry Thorleys, the Reggie Chiverses, and young Morris Dagonet and his wife, a van der Luyden. It was a gathering of the inner circle, united in their daily and nightly amusements.

Forty-eight hours later, the unthinkable occurred. Every single guest refused the invitation, save for the Beauforts and the Jacksons. The slight was sharpened by the absence of the Reggie Chiverses, despite their Mingott lineage, and by the uniform phrasing of the regrets, devoid of the customary excuse of a prior engagement. In a city as small and interconnected as New York, where everyone knew everyone's schedule, the refusal was a deliberate, cruel statement.

The Mingotts met the blow with characteristic gallantry. Mrs. Lovell Mingott confided the matter to Mrs. Welland, who passed it to Newland Archer. Blazing with righteous anger, he appealed passionately to his mother. After a period of painful internal resistance and external delay, she succumbed. Embracing his cause with renewed vigor, she donned her grey velvet bonnet and declared, "I'll go and see Louisa van der Luyden."

New York in Newland Archer's time was a slippery pyramid. At its base lay the "plain people," an honorable but obscure majority elevated by marriage into the ruling clans. Above them rose the compact, dominant group represented by the Mingotts, Newlands, Chiverses, and Mansons. While others assumed they were the apex, the older generation knew that true aristocracy belonged to a select few.

"Don't tell me," Mrs. Archer would chide her children, dismissing modern notions of New York aristocracy. "If there is one, neither the Mingotts nor the Mansons belong to it—not the Newlands or the Chiverses. Our ancestors were respectable merchants who stayed because they prospered. One signed the Declaration; another was a general under Washington. These are points of pride, but they mean nothing of rank. New York is a commercial community, and only three families claim true aristocratic origin."

Everyone knew these three: the Dagonets of Washington Square, allied with the Pitts and Foxes; the Lannings, intermarried with the descendants of Count de Grasse; and the van der Luydens, direct heirs of the first Dutch governor of Manhattan and linked by pre-revolutionary marriages to French and British nobility. The Lannings survived only in two elderly, lively spinsters; the Dagonets were a significant clan; but the van der Luydens stood above all, fading into a super-terrestrial twilight from which emerged only Mr. and Mrs. Henry van der Luyden.

Mrs. Henry van der Luyden was formerly Louisa Dagonet. Her mother was the granddaughter of Colonel du Lac, a Channel Islander who fought under Cornwallis and settled in Maryland with his bride, Lady Angelica Trevenna. The ties between the Dagonets, the du Lacs, and their Cornish kinsfolk, the Trevennas, remained unbroken. The van der Luydens maintained long visits to the Duke of St. Austrey in Cornwall and Gloucestershire, though the Duchess feared the Atlantic. They divided their time between Trevenna, their Maryland estate, and Skuytercliff, the great Hudson estate granted to the first Governor, where Mr. van der Luyden still held the title of Patroon. Their Madison Avenue mansion was rarely opened, reserved for their most intimate friends.

"I wish you would go with me, Newland," his mother said suddenly, pausing at the door of the carriage. "Louisa is fond of you; and of course, it is for May's sake that I take this step. Besides, if we do not all stand together, there will be no Society left."
