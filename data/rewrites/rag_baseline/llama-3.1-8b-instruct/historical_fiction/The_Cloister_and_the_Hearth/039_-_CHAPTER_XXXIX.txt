The scents of the Low Countries wafted through the air, carrying the promise of autumnal spices and the faint tang of salt from the North Sea. In the humble abode of the Van Groot family, a sense of unease settled over the dinner table. Jorian Ketel, a suitor for Margaret's hand, arrived at the doorstep, his intentions clear. However, Margaret lay abed, her health fragile, and Peter, her father, met Jorian with a stern warning to depart. The atmosphere was charged with tension, the entire village of Sevenbergen abuzz with the tale of Margaret and her would-be suitor. The air was heavy with the weight of popular sympathy, a volatile mixture of emotions that threatened to boil over at any moment.

As Jorian Ketel took his leave, his face twisted in a scowl, the villagers couldn't help but feel a twinge of satisfaction. Dierich Brower, a familiar figure in the tavern, was also feeling the sting of public opinion. His attempts to extract information from a tipsy informant had ended in failure, and he found himself facing the wrath of the burgomaster, who threatened to expel him from the burgh if he ever set foot in Sevenbergen again.

Ghysbrecht Van Swieten, the cunning burgomaster, had been watching the developments with growing unease. His emissaries had failed to uncover the whereabouts of Gerard, the Tergovian who had stolen the town records. The silence from the family at Tergou only added to Ghysbrecht's anxiety, fueling his darkest fears. He became increasingly withdrawn, his eyes darting about like a trapped animal, anticipating a blow that he knew was inevitable.

In the midst of this turmoil, the little family at Tergou struggled to maintain a façade of normalcy. Little Kate sensed the discord between her brother Cornelis and Sybrandt, the pair who had orchestrated Gerard's departure. Though she kept her observations to herself, the memory of their actions lingered, casting a shadow over her interactions with them. Her mother, Catherine, watched with a discerning eye, aware of the subtle undercurrents that flowed beneath the surface of their lives. The black sheep, Cornelis and Sybrandt, attempted to blend in with the general mood of sorrow, but their insincerity was not lost on Catherine, who observed with a quiet intensity, her eyes piercing the masks they wore.
