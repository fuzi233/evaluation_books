Contains Both Love and War

For a while, Cicero and Euripides ceased to occupy Mr. Pen's mind, leaving honest Mr. Smirke with an unusually leisurely schedule. However, the true victim of this mental shift was Rebecca. While Pen occasionally announced his intention to travel to Chatteris for fencing lessons—a trip undertaken openly with his mother whenever he had three clear hours—he frequently vanished into the city, making a dash for Prior's Lane. When Rebecca fell lame, his distress was as frantic as Richard III's at Bosworth when his horse was killed beneath him. He delved deeply into the books of the stable-keeper at Chatteris, seeking remedies for his own imaginary ailments and those of others.

Once a week, perhaps, under the guise of reading a Greek play with Smirke, this young reprobate timed his departure perfectly to catch the *Competitor* coach. He lingered in Chatteris for a couple of hours before returning on the *Rival*, which left for London at ten o'clock. On one occasion, his secret nearly slipped due to Mr. Smirke's simplicity. Mrs. Pendennis asked if they had read much the previous night. Smirke was on the verge of confessing he hadn't seen Mr. Pen at all when Pen's boot-heel ground down on the curate's toe under the table, a silent warning that saved him from betrayal.

Conversations on the tender subject naturally ensued. It is good sport, if you are not personally involved, to overhear two men in love. When informed under solemn vows of secrecy of Pen's condition, the curate trembled, asking if the object was "unworthy" or an "unlawful attachment." If so, he felt duty-bound to inform Mrs. Pendennis, fearing a quarrel that would cost him his chance of seeing the world's greatest beauty.

"Unlawful, unworthy!" Pen bounced out. "She is as pure as she is beautiful; I would give my heart to no other woman. I keep it secret because reasons of weighty nature forbid disclosure. But any man who breathes a word against her purity insults both her honor and mine, and dammy, I won't stand it."

Smirke, with a faint laugh, merely said, "Well, well, don't call me out, Arthur, for you know I can't fight," thereby placing the wretched curate entirely in his pupil's power, causing Greek and mathematics to suffer accordingly.

Had Mr. Smirke possessed discernment and checked the 'Poet's Corner' of the *County Chronicle*, he might have seen 'Mrs. Haller,' 'Passion and Genius,' and 'Lines to Miss Fotheringay' appearing weekly. But since these verses were no longer signed NEP but subscribed EROS, neither the tutor nor Helen knew that Nep was the same flaming Eros singing the praises of the new actress.

"Who is the lady," Mrs. Pendennis finally asked, "whom your rival is always singing in the Chronicle? He writes something like you, dear Pen, but yours is much the best. Have you seen Miss Fotheringay?"

Pen admitted he had; that night he saw "The Stranger," who played Mrs. Haller. She was preparing for a benefit in Ophelia. "Suppose we go?" he suggested. "Shakespeare, you know, Mother—we can get horses from the Clavering Arms." Little Laura sprang up with delight, longing for a play.

Pen introduced "Shakespeare" because the deceased Mr. Pendennis, a man of his character, professed an uncommon respect for the Bard of Avon, claiming his works contained more poetry than all 'Johnson's Poets' combined. Though Mr. Pendennis didn't read them much, he enjoined Pen to peruse them, often speaking of the pleasure he would take in taking his son and wife to see the immortal poet's plays.

Tears welled in the kind mother's eyes remembering these speeches. She kissed her son fondly and agreed to go. Laura jumped for joy. Was Pen happy? Ashamed? Holding his mother, he longed to tell her all but kept his counsel. He would test her reaction; the play should be the thing, and he would try his mother like Hamlet's.

Helen invited Mr. Smirke to join the party. The ecclesiastic, bred by a fond parent at Clapham who objected to drama, had never seen a play. But Shakespeare! To go with Mrs. Pendennis in her carriage and sit by her side all night! He couldn't resist the idea of such pleasure. He made a feeble speech about temptation and gratitude, accepting her offer. As he spoke, he gave her a look that made her exceedingly uncomfortable. She had seen that look more than once lately, pursuing her. He became positively odious in the widow's eyes daily.

We shall not dwell extensively on Pen's courtship of Miss Fotheringay, for the reader has already sampled her conversation. Pen sat with her hour after hour, pouring forth his honest boyish soul. Everything he knew, hoped, felt, or read, he told her. He never tired of talking and longing. Her role was not to talk, but to appear understanding (a difficult feat, as the young fellow blurted out nonsense) and to look exceedingly handsome and sympathetic. While Pen ranted for twenty minutes, clothed in words, the lovely Emily, who couldn't comprehend a tenth of his talk, thought about her own affairs: arranging the cold mutton, turning the black satin, or making a bonnet from her scarf. Pen spouted Byron and Moore; her business was to throw up her eyes or fix them on his face and cry, "Oh, 'tis beautiful! Ah, how exquisite! Repeat those lines again." Off the boy went, and she returned to her simple thoughts about the turned gown or hashed mutton.

Soon, Pen's passion was no secret to Emily or her father. On his second visit, his admiration was evident. As he departed, the old gentleman winked at his daughter over his glass of grog. "Faith, Milly darling, I think ye've hooked that chap."

"Pooh, 'tis only a boy, papa dear," Milly remarked. "Sure he's but a child." Pen would have been pleased by this phrase; he was galloping home wild with pleasure, shouting her name.

"Ye've hooked 'um any how," said the Captain. "Let me tell ye he's not a bad fish. I asked Tom at the George and Flint the grocer where his mother dwells—fine fortune, drives in a chariot, splendid park and grounds—Fairoaks Park—only son, property all his own at twenty-one. Ye might go further and not fare so well, Miss Fotheringay."

"Them boys are mostly talk," said Milly seriously. "Ye know at Dublin how ye went on about young Poldoody... but he went abroad, and his mother married him to an Englishwoman."

"Lord Poldoody was a young nobleman; and in them it's natural... But ye mustn't encourage this young chap too much, for, bedad, Jack Costigan won't have any thrifling with his daughter."

"No more will his daughter, papa, you may be sure of that," Milly said. "A little sip more of the punch,—sure, 'tis beautiful. Ye needn't be afraid about the young chap—I think I'm old enough to take care of myself, Captain Costigan."

So Pen came day after day, rushing in and galloping away, growing wilder with each visit. Sometimes the Captain was present, but having perfect confidence in his daughter, he often left the young couple to themselves, cocking his hat over his eye and strutting off on some errand. How delightful those interviews were! The Captain's drawing-room was a low wainscoted room with a large window looking into the Dean's garden. There Pen sat and talked—and talked—while Emily, looking beautiful as she worked, appeared calm and serene. Sunshine streamed in, lighting up her superb face and form. Mid-conversation, the great bell would boom, and he would pause smiling until the music died away, or the rooks in the cathedral elms made noise at sunset, or the organ and choristers hushed Pen's talking.

Miss Fotheringay, in a plain shawl and close bonnet, went to church every Sunday accompanied by her indefatigable father, who gave responses in a rich brogue, joined psalms, and behaved exemplarily.

Little Bows, the house-friend, was wroth at the notion of Miss Fotheringay marrying a stripling seven or eight years her junior. A cripple and deformed even more than Bingley the manager, Bows was a singular wild man of talent and humor. Attracted by her beauty, he began teaching her to act. He shrieked parts in his cracked voice; she learned them by rote, repeating them in full rich tones. He indicated attitudes, setting and moving those beautiful arms. Those who remember the grand actress on stage recall how she always used the same gestures, looks, and tones, standing on the same plank, rolling her eyes to the same degree, and weeping with heart-rending pathos over the same syllable. After coming out trembling and tearful, she would gather her hair behind the curtain, go home to a mutton-chop and brown stout, and sleep as resolutely as a porter.

Bows was indignant that his pupil should throw her chances away by bestowing her hand on a little country squire. London managers had seen her three years prior and failed from her utter stupidity. Since then, Bows had taken her in hand, working and screaming with indomitable patience while she followed him with dull obedience. She knew he made her; she let herself be made. She was not ungrateful or ill-humored; she was simply stupid. And Pen was madly in love with her.

The post-horses from the Clavering Arms arrived, carrying the party to the theatre. Pen was gratified to see a large audience. The young gentlemen from Baymouth occupied a front box with Mr. Foker and Mr. Spavin, splendidly attired. They saluted Pen cordially, examining his party with approval. Little Laura was a pretty red-cheeked girl with shining brown ringlets, and Mrs. Pendennis, dressed in black velvet with her diamond cross, looked majestic. Behind them sat Mr. Arthur and the gentle Smirke, who blushed to be there but was happy nonetheless. They brought books of 'Hamlet' to follow the tragedy, as honest countryfolk do. Samuel, the coachman, groom, and gardener took their place in the pit, alongside Mr. Foker's man. The pit was dotted with Dragoon non-commissioned officers, whose band played in the orchestra by permission of Colonel Swallowtail. The corpulent warrior himself, with his Waterloo medal and young men, made a handsome show in the boxes.

"Who is that odd-looking person bowing to you, Arthur?" Mrs. Pendennis asked.

Pen blushed. "His name is Captain Costigan, ma'am," he said—"a Peninsular officer." It was the Captain in new clothes, waving a large pair of white kid gloves, one sprawled over his heart and buttons. Pen said no more. How was Mrs. Pendennis to know Mr. Costigan was Miss Fotheringay's father?

Mr. Hornbull played Hamlet, Mr. Bingley modestly contenting himself with Horatio, reserving his strength for William in 'Black-Eyed Susan.' We need not discuss the play, except to say Ophelia looked lovely, performing with wild pathos, laughing, weeping, waving her white arms, and flinging flowers with charming madness. Her black hair tossed splendidly. She made the most charming corpse ever seen. While Hamlet and Laertes battled in her grave, she looked out from the back scenes with curiosity toward Pen's box.

There was but one voice in her praise. Mrs. Pendennis was in ecstasies with her beauty. Little Laura was bewildered but cried out great praises. Pen was charmed by the effect she produced on his mother, and the clergyman was enthusiastically enthusiastic.

When the curtain fell on the slaughtered personages, there was immense shouting and applause. The intrepid Smirke clapped his hands, crying "Bravo, Bravo" louder than the Dragoons. They were led cheering into action by the portly Swallowtail, who waved his cap. There was a roar of bravos; Pen bellowed the loudest, "Fotheringay! Fotheringay!" while Messrs. Spavin and Foker gave the view-halloo from their box. Even Mrs. Pendennis waved her pocket-handkerchief, and little Laura danced and looked up at Pen with wonder.

Hornbull led the beneficiaire forward. She looked handsome and radiant, hair still over her shoulders, and Pen hardly could contain himself for rapture. He leaned over his mother's chair, shouting and hurray-ing, waving his hat. It was all he could do to keep his secret from Helen and not say, "Look! That's the woman! Isn't she peerless? I tell you I love her." He disguised these feelings under enormous bellowing.

Miss Fotheringay surveyed the house with glances of gratitude, trembling over her favorite trap-door. She seized the flowers Foker discharged and even the shy rose Smirke offered, pressing them to her swelling heart. Twinkling in her breast, poor old Pen saw a locket he had bought with his last shilling and a sovereign borrowed from Smirke.

'Black-Eyed Susan' followed, charming our gentle-hearted friends. Susan, in a russet gown and pink ribbon, looked as lovely as Ophelia. Bingley was great as William; Goll, as the Admiral, looked like a figurehead; and Garbetts, as Captain Boldweather, a miscreant planning to carry off Susan, waved an immense cocked hat saying, "Come what may, he will be the ruin of her." All performed with accustomed talent, and it was with sincere regret that the curtain dropped.
