The air inside the tavern was thick with the scent of unwashed bodies and impending violence. In one corner, Colonel Washington paced, his movements a slow, grinding rhythm against the silence. His hands were clasped behind his back, his head bowed low enough to obscure the gravity settling on his handsome face. He was preparing for blood, not with the manic energy of a duelist, but with the grim resignation of a man accepting a sentence he had no power to alter. The law of honor demanded payment for the insult, and the country practiced cruelty as a routine.

Across the room, the atmosphere was different, charged with a frantic, childish desperation. Young George Warrington sat rigid, his face pale beneath the powder. Beside him, his brother Harry wept openly, his small hands clutching a sheet of paper until the ink blurred. The document was a testament to a death that might happen tomorrow—a list of bequests for a freed slave, a tutor, a silver knife, and a lock of hair for a sister who would never know her brother's end. George dictated the words with a majestic indifference, treating the finality of his potential demise as a bureaucratic formality. He spoke of debts paid and grievances settled, sealing the letter with a family crest that seemed suddenly heavy and obsolete.

"Two hours and a half," George noted, glancing at a gold watch inherited from a grandfather who was now a distant memory. "Time is running out."

Harry refused to let go of his brother, his voice cracking with a mixture of terror and loyalty. "If he kills you, I'll kill him. I swear it." The promise hung in the air, absurd and terrible, a vow made by a boy who understood the mechanics of a pistol but not the weight of a soul.

Outside, the world was already waking up to the noise. The tavern grounds, usually a chaotic arena for cockfights and brawls, fell silent except for the distant, rhythmic pounding of hooves. Sady, the enslaved boy tasked with delivering the pistols, burst into the courtyard like a storm front. He fired his weapon twice, the report echoing sharply against the wooden siding, silencing the cackling pigs and startled chickens. He grinned, a wide, unsettling expression, and pointed the barrel down the road, signaling the arrival of the inevitable.

Then came the clatter of hooves, breaking the tension. First, Dr. Dempster, spurring his pony with clumsy urgency. Behind him, Mrs. Mountain, astride a sturdy mare, galloped into the yard. She did not stop. She did not dismount gracefully. She crashed through the crowd of negro servants, elbowing past them, and ran straight for the garden where the two brothers stood frozen.

She threw herself around George's neck, sobbing. "It's a mistake! Oh, George, my darling, it's all my fault!"

George pulled away, his face hardening into a mask of aristocratic disdain. "What is a mistake?" he asked, his voice flat.

"It's the paper," Mrs. Mountain gasped, wiping tears from her cheeks. "The one you found in his portfolio. The one saying he intends to marry a widow with two children. I thought... I thought it was you, George. But it isn't. It's Miss Custis. The Colonel is marrying her after the campaign. Your mother is furious, boys. Furious."

The revelation hit the room like a physical blow. The justification for the duel evaporated, leaving only the raw, ugly residue of misunderstanding. George's anger, which had been building like pressure in a boiler, found a sudden vent. "I acted under a wrong impression," he admitted, the words tasting bitter. "But the behavior remains."

Harry, trembling, scrambled forward. He ignored the protocol of distance, ignoring the formalities that governed adult men. He ran toward the figure standing alone on the bowling green—the tall, stooped silhouette of Colonel Washington. With a hundred stumbling steps and a voice thrumming with genuine affection, Harry launched his story. He spoke of the error, of the misread letter, of the relief that washed over him.

Washington listened. When Harry finished, the Colonel did not offer a speech. He simply stepped forward and embraced the boy, holding him tight as if anchoring himself to something solid. "Thank God," Washington whispered, his voice rough. "Thank God for this."

He released Harry, turning to George. Their hands touched, a formal clasp between enemies who had just discovered they were allies in confusion. But the warmth did not spread. George's apology was stiff, a recitation of duty rather than forgiveness. "I find I have done you a wrong, Colonel Washington," George said. "I must apologize not for the error, but for the behavior that followed."

"The error was mine," Washington replied, his tone cool and detached. "I found the paper. I showed it to George. Jealousy is a disease, madam." He glanced at Mrs. Mountain, whose face was flushed with shame. "Mischief comes from secrets kept too long. Pain could have been spared if only private things remained private."

He offered no further comfort. No platitudes about friendship or future reconciliation. Instead, he issued a command: "Take a true friend's advice and try to be less ready to think evil of your friends. We shall meet again at the camp. Until then, keep our weapons for the enemy."

With a bow that was precise and empty, Washington turned his horse. He rode away, leaving the tavern, the weeping brothers, and the confused adults behind. The sun broke through the clouds, illuminating the dust motes dancing in the air, indifferent to the drama that had just unfolded. The threat of blood remained, suspended in the silence, waiting for the next order.
