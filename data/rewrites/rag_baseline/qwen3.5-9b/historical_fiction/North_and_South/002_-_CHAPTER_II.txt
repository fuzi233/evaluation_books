The rain lashed against the leaded glass of the library windows, a rhythmic drumming that felt, to Margaret Hale, like the persistent heartbeat of a city she had just fled—frantic, restless, and utterly indifferent to the quiet suffocations awaiting her in the countryside. She stood by the door, her morning dress crisp and unfamiliar, watching her father emerge from the carriage. He walked with a heaviness that seemed to anchor him to the earth, a stark contrast to the buoyant energy she had spent the last forty-eight hours projecting in Harley Street.

Margaret traced the line of his coat sleeve, her mind already dissecting the tragedy of her mother's absence. Mrs. Hale had been detained by a multitude of half-reasons, none of which anyone truly understood except Mr. Hale himself, who knew precisely how his arguments for a grey satin gown—a garment hovering precariously between antiquity and novelty—had failed to sway her. He lacked the funds to outfit his wife anew from head to toe, and thus, she would not appear at her only sister's only child's wedding. If Mrs. Shaw had suspected the real reason, she would have showered gowns upon her; but it was nearly twenty years since Mrs. Shaw was the poor, pretty Miss Beresford, and she had forgotten all grievances save the unhappiness born of age disparity in marriage, a topic she could descant upon for half an hour. *Dearest Maria* had married the man of her heart, only eight years older, possessing the sweetest temper and that rare blue-black hair. Mr. Hale was a preacher of delightful clarity, a perfect model of a parish priest. Yet, Mrs. Shaw's logic remained circular and cruel: *Married for love, what can dearest Maria have to wish for?* As if the answer were a list of silks and bonnets, rather than the silent erosion of a woman's spirit in a remote vicarage.

Margaret did not mourn her mother's absence; she welcomed the respite. Meeting at Helstone Parsonage spared her the chaotic theater of the Harley Street house, where she had played the role of Figaro, summoned simultaneously to a dozen rooms. Her mind and body ached with the recollection of her frantic performance, the hurried farewells to those she had lived with so long. Those times were gone, never to return, and the weight of them pressed down on her chest. Yet, as she watched her father lean back in the carriage seat, his blue-black hair now a thin, grey veil over his brow, she forced her thoughts toward the bright, serene contemplation of the future.

His face was a map of weariness. The bones were plainly visible, too plainly for beauty unless one possessed the grace of fine carving, which his features held in abundance. But this was not the serene calm of a contented life; it was the rest of a soldier returning from battle. The lines around his mouth spoke of habitual distress, a depression etched into his very skin. *Poor Frederick,* she thought, the name rising unbidden. *If he had been a clergyman instead of lost to the navy!* She knew only the fragments Aunt Shaw had whispered—that he could not return due to a terrible affair—but the full account remained buried in sad oblivion, a ghost haunting the edges of their conversation.

When her father stirred, waking with a faint, reluctant smile, Margaret offered hers, bright and devoid of fatigue. He smiled back, the effort seeming to drain him further. His face settled back into its familiar lines of anxiety, his mouth twitching as if to speak, settling into an expression of perpetual indecision. Yet, his eyes remained the same—large, soft, and moving slowly, almost grandly, in their orbits, veiled by transparent white lids. Margaret was more like him than her mother. While others marveled that such handsome parents could produce a daughter deemed "not beautiful at all"—her mouth too wide, lacking the demure rosebud of conventional charm—they missed the truth. Her wide mouth was a soft curve of rich red lips; her skin, though not porcelain white, possessed an ivory smoothness and delicacy. When she spoke to her father, her usual dignity melted into the morning light, filled with dimples and glances that spoke of childish gladness and boundless hope.

It was the latter part of July when she finally stepped onto the soil of Helstone. The forest trees stood in a dark, full, dusky green, while the ferns below caught the slanting sunbeams like embers. The air was sultry and broodingly still. Margaret loved this life. She tramped alongside her father, crushing the fern with a cruel glee, savoring the unique fragrance that rose to meet her light foot. Out on the broad commons, amidst the warm, scented light, she saw multitudes of wild, free creatures revelling in the sunshine. These walks realized all her anticipations. She took a fierce pride in her forest; its people were her people. She made hearty friends with them, learning their peculiar dialects, nursing their babies, reading to their elders, carrying dainties to the sick. She resolved to teach at the school her father visited daily, treating it as an appointed task, though she was continually tempted away to visit some individual friend in a cottage hidden in the green shade.

But her outdoor perfection masked an indoor decay. With the healthy shame of a child, she blamed herself for perceiving the cracks in their domestic foundation. Her mother, usually so kind and tender, seemed increasingly discontented. She viewed the Bishop's neglect of his episcopal duties with bitterness, feeling that Mr. Hale deserved a better living, and she almost reproached her husband for his inability to demand a transfer to a larger parish. Every time she urged him to seek preferment, Mr. Hale shrank further, sighing that if he could do his duty in little Helstone, he should be thankful. Yet, each day, the world became more bewildering to him, and the burden heavier. Margaret saw her father shrinking under the pressure of his wife's demands and strove to reconcile her mother to the reality of their situation. Mrs. Hale claimed the nearness of the trees affected her health; Margaret countered by tempting her onto the sun-streaked common, insisting her mother had simply grown too accustomed to an indoor existence. For a time, it worked. But as autumn drew on, bringing changeable weather, her mother's conviction of the place's unhealthiness hardened. She repined more frequently that her husband, more learned than Mr. Hume and a better priest than Mr. Houldsworth, had not secured the preferment those neighbors had achieved.

This marring of the peace of home by long hours of discontent was what Margaret was unprepared for. She had expected to surrender luxuries, viewing them as mere trammels to her freedom in Harley Street. Her keen enjoyment of sensuous pleasures was balanced by a conscious pride in her ability to do without them. But the cloud never arrives from the quarter of the horizon where we watch for it. There had been slight complaints and passing regrets over trifles connected with Helstone during her previous holidays, but in the general happiness of memory, she had forgotten the small, unpleasant details.

Now, in the latter half of September, the autumnal rains and storms descended, forcing Margaret to remain indoors more than she had ever done before. Helstone was isolated, distant from neighbors of their standard of cultivation.

"It is undoubtedly one of the most out-of-the-way places in England," Mrs. Hale declared in one of her plaintive moods. "I cannot help regretting constantly that papa has really no one to associate with here; he is so thrown away, seeing nothing but farmers and labourers from week's end to week's end. If we only lived on the other side of the parish, it would be something; there we should be almost within walking distance of the Stansfields; certainly the Gormons would be within a walk."

"Gormons?" Margaret asked, her tone sharpening. "Are those the Gormons who made their fortunes in trade at Southampton? Oh! I'm glad we don't visit them. I don't like shoppy people. I think we are far better off, knowing only cottagers and labourers, and people without pretence."

"You must not be so fastidious, Margaret, dear!" her mother replied, secretly thinking of a young, handsome Mr. Gormon she had once met at Mr. Hume's.

"No! I call mine a very comprehensive taste," Margaret insisted, her voice ringing clear in the gloomy room. "I like all people whose occupations have to do with land; I like soldiers and sailors, and the three learned professions. I'm sure you don't want me to admire butchers and bakers, and candlestick-makers, do you, mamma?"

"But the Gormons were neither butchers nor bakers, but very respectable coach-builders."

"Very well. Coach-building is a trade all the same, and I think a much more useless one than that of butchers or bakers. Oh! how tired I used to be of the drives every day in Aunt Shaw's carriage, and how I longed to walk!"

And walk Margaret did, in spite of the weather. She was so happy outdoors, at her father's side, that she almost danced. With the soft violence of the west wind behind her, crossing the heath, she seemed borne onwards, as lightly and easily as a fallen leaf wafted by the autumnal breeze. But the evenings were a different matter, difficult to fill agreeably. Immediately after tea, her father withdrew into his small library, leaving her and her mother alone.
