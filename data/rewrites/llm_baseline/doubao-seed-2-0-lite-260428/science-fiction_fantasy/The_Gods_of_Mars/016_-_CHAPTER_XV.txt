I could not have been unconscious more than a handful of seconds, but I know I was out: the next thing that pricked through the fuzz in my skull was the glow of warm, flaring torchlight spilling across the pit corridor’s stone flagging, and those baleful, paralyzing floating eyes were gone entirely. My only injury was a throbbing bruise on my forehead where it had cracked against the floor when I fell. I sprang to my feet at once, longsword half-drawn, and spotted the source of the light: four green Warhoon warriors, hurrying straight toward me down the main tunnel.

They had not yet rounded the bend that would put me in their line of sight, so I slipped noiselessly into the nearest side passage, pressing flat to the damp stone wall. I did not retreat too far this time — I well remembered the last time I’d wandered off too deep in these pits, and lost Tars Tarkas and his guard in the maze. The party drew near the mouth of my hiding spot, and as they tramped past, I breathed out slow in relief: this was the exact same squad I had followed down into the pits earlier, and at their head was Tars Tarkas, bound for his own cell with three of his captors.

I fell in behind them unseen, trailing them all the way to the cell where the great Thark had been chained. Two of the Warhoons paused outside the door to stand guard, while the man carrying the jailer’s keys — Tan Gama, the Warhoon chief — stepped inside with Tars Tarkas to refasten his irons. The two sentries soon began strolling slowly toward the spiral runway that led up to the floors above, vanishing around a corridor turn in seconds. The torch they’d carried was stuck in a wall socket beside the cell door, its golden light spilling both down the hall and across the room where Tars Tarkas was held.

As the last sentry disappeared, I stepped toward the cell entrance, my plan fully formed. I hated the necessity of what I was about to do, but there was no other path that would let both Tars Tarkas and I make it back to our hidden camp in the hills. I crept along the wall until I stood directly outside the cell door, both hands wrapped around the hilt of my longsword, held high over my head, ready to bring it down in one single, silent killing blow the second Tan Gama stepped across the threshold. I will not dwell on the seconds that followed. It is enough that two minutes later, Tars Tarkas was strapped in Tan Gama’s chief harness, the Warhoon’s torch in his hand, hurrying down the corridor toward the spiral runway, and I followed a dozen paces behind him, John Carter, Prince of Helium, free once more to make our escape.

The two sentries were just starting to climb the runway as Tars Tarkas rounded the corner. “Why so long, Tan Gama?” one called out.
“Trouble with a rusted lock,” Tars Tarkas called back, pitching his voice low to match the dead warrior’s. “And now I find I left my short-sword in the Thark’s cell. Go on ahead, I’ll head back to fetch it.”
“As you will,” the sentry laughed. “We’ll see you up top shortly.”
Tars Tarkas nodded, turned as if to trudge back toward the cells, and waited only until the two men had vanished up the next flight of steps before I joined him. We snuffed the torch out completely, and crept together toward the spiral incline leading up to the upper levels of the Warhoon stronghold.

The first floor hallway only ran halfway through the building, forcing anyone trying to reach the inner courtyard to cross a common room packed full of green Martian warriors, so we chose to climb straight up to the second floor, to the long corridor I had traversed earlier that stretched the full length of the fortress. We heard the rumble of conversation drifting up from rooms on the floor above, but the hall was dark, no guards in sight, and we threaded its full length without being spotted, emerging out onto the balcony that looked down over the courtyard.

To our right, an open window looked into the chamber where we had left Tan Gama’s remaining squad earlier. Their voices drifted out clear to us.
“What’s keeping Tan Gama?” one warrior groused. “He couldn’t possibly take this long just to fetch a short-sword from a Thark’s cell.”
“His short-sword?” a woman’s voice cut in. “What do you mean?”
“He said he left it in the Thark’s cell, sent us ahead to go back for it,” the first warrior explained.
The woman scoffed, and held up a blade we all recognized: Tan Gama’s shortsword. “He never carried a short-sword tonight. It broke in the battle with the Tharks earlier, and he gave it to me to repair. Look, I have it right here.”

The warriors all sprang to their feet, shouts of alarm breaking out. “Something’s gone very wrong!” one roared. “I thought his voice sounded strange when he spoke to us by the runway. Come — to the pits, now.”

We did not wait to hear the rest. I slung my harness out into one long, sturdy strap, lowered Tars Tarkas down ten feet to the courtyard stones below, and jumped right after him. We had barely spoken a dozen words since I’d struck down Tan Gama, and the only thing Tars Tarkas had said, staring at me in the torchlight with that faint, unamused, appreciative twitch of his lip, was “By now I should have learned to wonder at nothing John Carter can pull off.” He did not need to say he valued the friendship that made me risk my life to rescue him, nor that he was glad to see me. This fierce, implacable Thark Jeddak had been the very first being to greet me when I first set foot on Barsoom twenty years prior, charging me at full gallop on his mighty thoat, spear levelled and hatred hot in his heart — and now, across two whole worlds, there was no truer ally I had than him.

We huddled in the shadow under the balcony for a minute to lay our plans. “Our party is five strong,” I told him. “Thuvia, Xodar, Carthoris, you, and me. We will need five thoats to carry us all.”
“Carthoris?” he said, surprised. “Your son?”
“Aye. I found him in a First Born prison beneath the Sea of Omean. I will tell you the full, strange tale once we are clear of the Warhoons.”

We made our way safely across the courtyard to the great stables at the far end, where the green warriors kept their massive war mounts. Taming five wild Barsoomian thoats is no easy task: the beasts are as ferocious and untamed as their riders, broken only by brute force and cruelty. The second they caught our unfamiliar scent, they reared back, squealing in rage, circling us on their eight long, powerful legs, their ten-foot shoulders heaving, huge gaping mouths lifted high over our heads. Their skin is smooth, hairless, dark slate across their backs, fading to a bright vivid yellow on their padded, nailless feet, their bellies pure white, broad flat tails thicker at the tip than the root: perfect, terrifying war steeds for a race of born raiders. Guided only by unspoken telepathic commands, no reins or bridles needed, they charged us in a frenzy, the racket of their screams loud enough to rouse every guard in the fortress if we did not move fast.

I made a dash for the nearest great brute, swung myself up onto his glossy back before he knew what was happening, and sent him a sharp, unyielding telepathic command to stand still. A second later Tars Tarkas had mounted a second, and between us we herded three more loose mounts toward the great gates. Tars Tarkas leaned down from his thoat to throw the heavy latch, swinging the barriers open, and we galloped out onto the wide avenue beyond, not even pausing to close the gates behind us, heading straight for the southern edge of the dead city.

Our luck held: we slipped past the outer ruins completely, reached our hidden camp without hearing a single sound of pursuit. My low prearranged whistle brought Thuvia, Carthoris, and Xodar running to meet us, overjoyed to see Tars Tarkas returned safe. We wasted no time on long stories. Carthoris and Tars Tarkas exchanged the dignified, formal greetings common to Barsoomian nobility, but I could see the quiet, deep affection between the two warriors at once. Xodar, the former First Born black jeddak, was introduced to Tars Tarkas, and then we all mounted our thoats, setting out at a hard gallop east, then curving north, speeding noiselessly across the silver ochre dead sea bottom under the light of Barsoom’s two moons, putting miles between us and the Warhoon fortress, fleeing for the hills, toward unknown new dangers.

We halted at noon the next day to rest our mounts and ourselves, hobbling the thoats so they could wander and graze on the thin ochre moss that serves them for both food and water. Thuvia volunteered to take the first watch, while the rest of us slept. It felt like I had barely closed my eyes when I felt her soft hand on my shoulder, her voice low in my ear. “Wake, Prince. There is a great mass of pursuers behind us, far on the horizon.”

I sprang up, woke the others. Tars Tarkas, his giant frame towering over all of us, squinted out across the plain. “It is thousands of mounted green warriors,” he said. “They are riding hard, and gaining fast.”

There was no time to waste. We cut the hobbles off our thoats, mounted, and rode north at the top speed of our slowest beast. For the rest of the day, and all through the long cold Martian night, we raced across that barren ochre waste, the Warhoons behind us drawing ever closer. Just before sundown we could make out clearly they were all Warhoons, and through the dark hours we could hear the clink of their harness and their ferocious war cries drifting closer. When the sun rose on the second day of our flight, the horde was less than half a mile behind us. They spotted us, and a roar of triumphant, bloodthirsty howl went up from their ranks.

A range of hills lay a few miles ahead: the far shore of the old dead sea bed we were crossing. If we could reach the broken cover of those hills, we might have a fighting chance to escape — but Thuvia’s thoat, the lightest burdened of all our mounts, staggered suddenly, lurching against mine. I snatched her off its back right before it collapsed, swinging her to sit behind me on my own thoat, her arms wrapped tight around my waist. But the double weight dragged my beast’s speed down sharply, and none of my companions would ride on ahead and leave us. There was not one among our small party who would desert another, though we came from different worlds, different races, different colors, different faiths.

We were almost to the hills, but the Warhoons were so close we could see their bared fangs, and we thought we would never make it. I felt Thuvia’s soft warm lips press a quick kiss to the back of my shoulder. “For you, my Prince,” she whispered. Then her arms slipped loose, and she had deliberately slid off the back of my thoat, dropping straight into the path of the oncoming raiders, thinking that lightening my mount’s load would let me outrun them to the safety of the hills.

Foolish, brave girl. She should have known John Carter better than that. I wrenched my thoat around, galloping back for her, and Carthoris had seen the whole scene at once. He was already off his mount by the time I reached Thuvia’s side, lifting her up to throw her across the saddle of his own animal, slapping the beast hard across the rump with the flat of his sword to send it thundering for the hills with her. He would not climb on. He stayed at my side, drawing his longsword, as did Tars Tarkas and Xodar, who had turned back at the first sign we were missing, and now all four of us lined up on foot, our four thoats turned loose beside us, ready to meet the full charge of the thousands of Warhoon raiders.

I thought of Dejah Thoris then, and how I hated to die before I could hold my princess in my arms once more. But if fate would not grant me that, then I would sell my life as dearly as possible, and give the Warhoons a tale to tell for twenty generations. The raiders were less than a hundred yards from us when a deafening explosion boomed out from over the hills behind us, and a shell burst dead in the front ranks of the charging horde.

Chaos erupted instantly. A hundred warriors toppled dead. Riderless thoats reared and stampeded over the dead and wounded, trampling their own comrades underfoot. The Warhoons broke ranks completely, staring up over our heads to spot where the attack had come from, and their confusion turned to panic, then wild headlong flight back the way they had come. We turned to look, and saw a great, gleaming battleship sweeping majestically over the tops of the hills, its bow gun roaring once more to send another shell bursting after the fleeing raiders. I let out a wild shout of joy: blazoned clear across its bow was the crimson royal sigil of Helium.
