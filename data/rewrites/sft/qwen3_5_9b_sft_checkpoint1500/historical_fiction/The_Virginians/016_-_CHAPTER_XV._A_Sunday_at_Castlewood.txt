The second day following Harry's arrival at Castlewood proved to be a Sunday. The chapel attached to the castle served double duty as the village church. A private door from the mansion opened directly onto a grand state pew, reserved for the family, where they took their appointed places in strict order. Below them sat a rather large congregation drawn from the village. High above, a few ancient, dust-choked banners hung from the rafters; Harry indulged himself in the pleasant fantasy that these had been carried by retainers of his lineage during the Commonwealth Wars—a conflict in which his ancestors, as he knew well, had fought with loyalty and distinction.

Within the altar rails stood the effigy of the Esmond who lived in the time of King James the First, the common forefather of everyone gathered in the family pew. Madame de Bernstein, in her capacity as a Bishop's widow, never missed mass. She conducted her devotions with a gravity nearly equal to that of the ancestor seated nearby, who wore a square beard and a red gown, forever kneeling on his stone hassock before his great marble desk and book beneath his emblazoned coat of arms.

The clergyman, a tall, high-complexioned, handsome young man, read the service in a lively, agreeable voice, lending the biblical chapters a distinct dramatic flair. The music was excellent, performed by one of the young ladies of the family at the organ, though it might have been superior but for an interruption. Something akin to a burst of laughter erupted from the servants' pew, triggered by Mr. Warrington's lacqueman, Gumbo. Knowing the tune assigned for the Psalm, Gumbo launched into it with a voice so exceedingly loud and sweet that the entire congregation swiveled toward him. Even the parson covered his mouth with his handkerchief, and the liveried gentlemen from London were thrown into a state of profound impropriety. Perhaps pleased with the sensation he had generated, Gumbo continued his performance until it became virtually a solo, silencing the voice of the clerk himself. Indeed, though Gumbo held the book alongside the portress's daughter, Molly—who had been the first to welcome the strangers to Castlewood—he sang and recited entirely by ear, unable to read a single syllable of the verses printed before him.

Once this choral interlude concluded, a brief sermon duly followed, which Harry found rather too short. In a lively, familiar, and striking discourse, the clergyman recounted a scene he had witnessed the previous week: the execution of a horse-thief after the Assizes. He painted a picture of the man, detailing his formerly good character, his loving family, and his agony at separation. He depicted the execution itself in a manner that was startling, terrible, and picturesque. Unlike the somewhat Calvinistic preachers Harry's mother favored, this divine spoke not in Scripture phraseology but as a man of the world addressing other sinful folk who might profit from sound advice.

The unfortunate man, once a farmer with promising prospects, had succumbed to the vices of the age—drinking, card-playing, horse-racing, and cock-fighting. Against these, the young clergyman expressed generous indignation. The man eventually drifted into poaching and horse-stealing, crimes for which he now suffered. The divine rapidly sketched striking and fearful images of these rustic offenses. He startled his hearers by illustrating that the "Eye of the Law" watched the poacher at midnight and set traps to ensnare criminals. He galloped the stolen horse over highways and commons, from county to county, yet showed Retribution always hot on its heels, seizing the malefactor at a country fair, dragging him before the justice, and never unlocking his manacles until he laid them down at the foot of the gallows. "Heaven be pitiful to the sinner!" the clergyman intoned, acting out the scene. He whispered words in the criminal's ear at the cart; he dropped his handkerchief onto the clerk's head. Harry recoiled as the cloth fell. The clergyman had been speaking for over twenty minutes, yet Harry felt as though he had not been listening for five.

The gentlefolk in the great pew were thoroughly enlivened by the discourse. Occasionally, Harry, glancing toward the servants' pew, noted how attentively they listened. Especially Gumbo, his own man, sat in an attitude of intense consternation. Yet the smock frocks seemed to take no notice, clamping out of the church quite unconcerned. Gaffer Brown and Gammer Jones accepted matters as they came, while the rosy-cheeked, red-cloaked village lasses sat beneath their broad hats, entirely unmoved. From his pew, Lord Castlewood nodded slightly to the clergyman as the divine's head and wig surged up from the cushion.

"Sampson has been strong today," said his lordship. "He has assaulted the Philistines in great force."

"Beautiful, beautiful!" exclaimed Harry.

"Bet five to four it was his Assize sermon. He has been over to Winton to preach and to see those dogs," cried William.

Having played the small congregation out into the sunshine, the organist left the church. Only Sir Francis Esmond, *temp.* Jac. I., still knelt on his marble hassock before his stone prayer-book. Mr. Sampson emerged from his vestry in his cassock, nodding to the gentlemen still lingering in the great pew.

"Come up, and tell us about those dogs," said Mr. William, and the divine nodded with a laughing assent.

The gentlemen passed from the church into the gallery connecting it to the house. Mr. Sampson made his way through the courtyard and soon joined them. Introduced by Lord Castlewood to the Virginian cousin of the family, Mr. Warrington, the chaplain bowed profoundly, hoping Mr. Warrington would benefit from the virtuous example of his European kinsmen. Was he related to Sir Miles Warrington of Norfolk? Sir Miles was Mr. Warrington's father's elder brother. What a pity he had a son! It was a pretty estate, and Mr. Warrington looked as if he would secure a baronetcy and a magnificent holding in Norfolk.

"Tell me about my uncle," cried Virginian Harry.

"Tell us about those dogs!" said English Will, breathless.

"Two more jolly dogs, two more drunken dogs, saving your presence, Mr. Warrington, than Sir Miles and his son, I never saw. Sir Miles was a staunch friend and neighbor of Sir Robert's. He can drink down any man in the county, save his son and a few others. As for the other dogs regarding which Mr. William is so anxious—for Heaven hath made him a prey to dogs and all kinds of birds, like the Greeks in the *Iliad*—"

"I know that line in the *Iliad*," said Harry, blushing. "I only know five more, but I know that one." And his head fell. He was thinking, "Ah, my dear brother George knew all the *Iliad* and all the *Odyssey*, and almost every book that was ever written besides!"

"What on earth" (mentioning only a place under the earth) "are you talking about now?" asked Will of his reverence.

The chaplain returned to the dogs and their exploits. He opined that Mr. William's dogs were certainly a match for theirs. From dogs, the conversation drifted to horses. Mr. William was very eager regarding the Six Year Old Plate at Huntingdon. "Have you brought any news of it, Parson?"

"The odds are five to four on Brilliant against the field," said the parson, gravely. "But, mind you, Jason is a good horse."

"Whose horse?" asked my lord.

"Duke of Ancaster's. By Cartouche out of Miss Langley," said the divine. "Have you horse-races in Virginia, Mr. Warrington?"

"Haven't we!" cried Harry. "But oh! I long to see a good English race!"

"Do you... do you... bet a little?" continued his reverence.

"I have done such a thing," replied Harry with a smile.

"I'll take Brilliant even against the field, for ponies with you, cousin!" shouted Mr. William.

"I'll give or take three to one against Jason!" said the clergyman.

"I don't bet on horses I don't know," said Harry, wondering at hearing the chaplain speak thus, remembering his sermon half an hour prior.

"Hadn't you better write home and ask your mother?" suggested Mr. William, with a sneer.

"Will, Will!" called out my lord. "Our cousin Warrington is free to bet, or not, as he pleases. Take care how you venture on either of them, Harry Warrington. Will is an old file, in spite of his smooth face; and as for Parson Sampson, I defy our ghostly enemy to get the better of him."

"Him and all his works, my lord!" said Mr. Sampson, bowing.

Harry was highly indignant at this allusion to his mother. "I'll tell you what, cousin Will," he said. "I am in the habit of managing my own affairs in my own way, without asking any lady to arrange them for me. And I'm used to making my own bets upon my own judgment, and don't need any relations to select them for me, thank you.
