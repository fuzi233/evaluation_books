On the twenty-seventh of June last I had my orders from Sergeant Cuff: three men, described as Indians and suspected of murder, had been seen that morning on the Tower Wharf, taking their places on a steamer for Rotterdam. I was to follow.

London, with its iron-rimmed din—wheels fretting on stone, winches clanking on the river, calls and curses thrown across the water—made a man thankful for any honest sound that wasn’t commerce. I went down among the wharf-noises and coal-smell, and took passage by another company’s boat the next morning, Thursday the twenty-eighth. Steam hissed; ropes complained; gulls quarrelled. The river stank as usual. Still, there was the old comfort of routine in it: the kettle on the galley fire, the cook’s knife tapping, the steady pulse of the engines—domestic labours, even afloat, keeping time against the vast machinery.

At Rotterdam I found the commander of the Wednesday’s steamer. He told me the three men had certainly been on board—only as far as Gravesend. Off that place, one of them had asked when they would reach Calais. When the commander answered that the ship was bound for Rotterdam, their spokesman showed the greatest surprise and distress, declaring they would forfeit their passage-money if only they might be put ashore. The commander, seeing nothing but foreigners blundered into a wrong boat, and having no cause to detain them, signalled for a shore-boat. The three men went over the side and were taken off.

It had the look of a plan settled beforehand—the neatest kind of vanishing, like smoke slipping out under a door. I lost no time and came back. I left the steamer at Gravesend; learned the men had gone on to London; then traced them from London to Plymouth. At Plymouth, inquiries showed they had sailed forty-eight hours before, on the East Indiaman _Bewley Castle_, bound direct for Bombay.

Sergeant Cuff sent word overland to the authorities at Bombay, to board the ship as soon as she entered port. There my part ended. Since then I have heard nothing more.

I, Captain of the _Bewley Castle_, write at Sergeant Cuff’s request what I know of three men, believed to be Hindoos, who took passage with me last summer for Bombay.

They joined at Plymouth. On the outward run there was no complaint of them. They were berthed forward; I had few occasions to notice them closely. If I remember anything of their presence, it is as one remembers a scent in a ship’s close passages—spice and oil and some sharp resin on their clothes that cut through tar and bilge-water when they passed; and the quiet way they moved in a place where most men must stamp and shout to be heard above cordage and sea.

Near the end of the voyage we were becalmed for three days and nights off the coast of India. I have not my journal by me and cannot now recall our latitude and longitude; I can only say that currents drifted us landward, and when the wind returned we made our port within twenty-four hours.

In a long calm, discipline relaxes. Mine relaxed. The heat lay upon the decks like a lid; the sea, waveless, had a dead look; even the ship’s usual voice—creak, slap, clatter—seemed muffled, as if the world were holding its breath. Certain gentlemen passengers had the smaller boats lowered and amused themselves rowing and swimming in the evening when the sun abated. The boats should then have been slung up again. Instead, they were left moored alongside. Between vexation and weather, officers and men had not the heart for duty while the calm lasted.

On the third night nothing unusual was heard or seen by the watch. In the morning the smallest boat was missing—and the three Hindoos were reported missing too. If they stole the boat shortly after dark, as I make no doubt they did, we were near enough to the land that pursuit would have been vain when their absence was discovered. In that calm weather, allowing for fatigue and unskilful rowing, I have no doubt they got ashore before daybreak.

At Bombay I learned, for the first time, the reason they had for escaping. I could only state to the authorities what I have written here. They considered me blameworthy for letting discipline slacken; I expressed my regret to them and to my owners. Since then, to my knowledge, nothing has been heard of the three men. I have no more to add.

My dear Mr. Bruff—

Do you recall, in the autumn of ’forty-eight, meeting at dinner in London a semi-savage person called Murthwaite, with whom you held a long talk after the cloth was drawn? We spoke then of an Indian Diamond—the Moonstone—and of a conspiracy existing to regain it.

Since that time I have wandered in Central Asia, and have drifted back to the north and north-west of India, where certain old adventures of mine began. About a fortnight since, I found myself in Kattiawar, a district little known to Europeans. There an occurrence befell me in which, incredible as it may appear, you are personally concerned.

Kattiawar is wild enough that even husbandmen go to the plough armed to the teeth; yet they keep their old domestic order in the very midst of danger—seed sorted in the palm, grain winnowed on the threshing-floor, bread patted out and baked while a sword hangs within reach. The population is fanatically devoted to the ancient Hindoo worship of Bramah and Vishnu. A few Mahometan families, scattered thinly in inland villages, are afraid to taste meat. A Mahometan suspected of killing the sacred cow is put to death without mercy by his pious Hindoo neighbours.

Two of the most famous shrines of pilgrimage lie within Kattiawar: Dwarka, birthplace of Krishna; and Somnauth, the sacred city sacked and destroyed as far back as the eleventh century by Mahmoud of Ghizni. Finding myself for the second time in these regions, I resolved not to leave without seeing once more the magnificent desolation of Somnauth.

At the spot where I planned this, I was, as nearly as I could calculate, three days’ journey on foot from the city. I had not been long on the road before I noticed others—by twos and threes—travelling the same way. When spoken to, I gave myself out for a Hindoo-Boodhist from a distant province bound on pilgrimage. My dress supported the story. Add that I know the language as well as my own, and that I am lean and brown enough to make my European origin not readily detected, and you will understand I passed as a stranger from far parts of their own land.

On the second day their numbers increased to fifties and hundreds. On the third day the throng was thousands, all slowly converging upon Somnauth. A small service I rendered one fellow-pilgrim that day brought me into acquaintance with certain men of higher caste. From them I learned the multitude moved toward a great ceremony to be held at night on a hill a little distance from the city—an honour paid to the god of the Moon.

As we drew near, the press of bodies delayed us. The land itself seemed to listen: even the insects’ rasping in the grasses had a place in the hush between voices, and the careful tread of so many bare feet made a sound like the lightest rain. By the time we reached the hill the moon was high.

My companions possessed privileges that gained them entrance to the shrine; they allowed me to accompany them. We found the shrine hidden behind a curtain hung between two magnificent trees. Beneath the trees a flat projection of rock jutted out, forming a kind of natural platform. Below, I stood with my Hindoo friends.

When I looked back down the hill, I saw the grandest union of Nature and Man I have ever witnessed. The lower slopes melted into a grassy plain at the meeting of three rivers. On one side, the water wound away—now visible, now lost among trees—far as the eye could follow. On the other, the waveless ocean slept in the night’s calm. Tens of thousands of human creatures, all dressed in white, stretched down the hillside, overflowed the plain, and fringed the nearer banks. Wild red flames from cressets and torches sprang up at intervals from the innumerable crowd, as if the earth itself were breathing fire. Over all poured the moonlight of the East, unclouded, silvering every head and every fold of cloth.

Then a strain of plaintive music rose—stringed instruments and flutes—thin and searching as the cry of a night-bird. The hill answered it with silence; the crowd held itself as one creature. I turned toward the hidden shrine and saw, on the rocky platform, three figures.

In the central man I recognised him whom I had spoken with in England, when the Indians appeared on the terrace at Lady Verinder’s house. The other two, his companions then, were surely his companions now.

A spectator near me saw me start and, in a whisper, explained. They were Brahmins, he said, who had forfeited caste in the service of the god. The god had commanded their purification to be purification by pilgrimage. That night the three were to part, each setting forth in a separate direction to the shrines of India. Never again would they look on one another’s faces. Never again would they rest from wandering—from the day of their separation to the day of their death.

As the words reached me, the plaintive music ceased. The three men prostrated themselves upon the rock before the curtain. They rose. They looked at one another. They embraced. Then they descended separately into the people.

The multitude made way in dead silence; not a laugh, not a barter-cry, not the vulgar clink of coin—only the soft shifting of cloth and the faint ring of a bracelet now and then as the crowd divided and closed. In three directions at once the white mass parted, as if some unseen hand had cut three channels through it. Slowly the people flowed together again; the tracks of the doomed men were obliterated. We saw them no more.

A new music sounded—loud and jubilant—from within. The crowd around me shuddered and pressed closer, as though the very air had changed. The curtain between the trees was drawn aside, and the shrine stood revealed.

There, raised high on a throne—seated on his typical antelope, with four arms stretching toward the four corners of the earth—there, dark and awful in the mystic light of heaven, soared the god of the Moon. And in the deity’s forehead gleamed the yellow Diamond whose splendour I had last seen in England, resting against the bosom of a woman’s dress.

Yes: after the lapse of eight centuries, the Moonstone looks forth once more over the walls of the sacred city where its story first began. By what accident or what crime it found its way back to its wild native land—how those men regained possession of their gem—may be known to you; it is not known to me. You have lost sight of it in England, and, if I know anything of this people, you have lost sight of it for ever.

So years pass and repeat each other; so events revolve in the cycles of time. What will be the next adventures of the Moonstone? Who can tell?
