At sixteen, Arthur Pendennis, the young heir to Fairoaks, had begun to assert himself in the world. In stature, he was still somewhat stunted, his slight frame barely reaching five feet six inches in height, but his face, rosy and freckled, radiated an infectious warmth that put even the most hardened of souls at ease. His brown hair, flecked with hints of gold, framed a countenance that seemed almost too good-natured to belong to the son of the formidable Major Pendennis. Yet, as he grew in confidence, Arthur shot up to a respectable five feet eight inches, a phenomenon that left his mother, Helen, bewildered and delighted.

As he navigated the complexities of adolescence, Arthur's academic pursuits became increasingly lackluster. His mother, ever the advocate for her son's well-being, opted against sending him back to Greyfriars, where the discipline was rigorous and the company, according to Arthur, consisted of unsavory characters. His uncle, however, remained adamant that a classical education was essential for Arthur's future success. The Doctor's impassioned letters extolled the virtues of immersing oneself in the works of ancient Greece, but Arthur skillfully deflected his uncle's advice by regaling his mother with tales of the establishment's dubious reputation.

When his uncle proposed using his influence to secure a commission in the Foot Guards, Arthur's eyes widened with excitement. He had chanced upon the band at St. James's, mesmerized by the pomp and circumstance of the occasion. Tom Ricketts, a classmate from his previous school days, now donned the uniform of the regiment, and Arthur couldn't help but feel a pang of envy. The prospect of joining the ranks, of donning the scarlet coat and epaulettes, was tantalizing, but ultimately, he knew it was a path his mother would never condone.

Helen Pendennis, when confronted with the idea, displayed a countenance of utmost distress. A Christian, she argued, should eschew the profession of arms, and her late husband, Major Pendennis, would never have sanctioned such a career choice for their son. Arthur, sensitive to his mother's sentiments, promptly relinquished his dreams of military glory, presenting her with a gift of his own self-sacrifice. She, in turn, regarded him with an adoring gaze, convinced that her son was the epitome of nobility.
