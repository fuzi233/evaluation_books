The Blue Cross

Between the mist-shrouded hills of Essex and the glinting expanse of the North Sea, the ferry chugged into Harwich, releasing a tide of pilgrims like autumn leaves on a gusty day. Among them, the man we must follow moved unseen – nor did he wish to be seen. There was nothing remarkable about him, save for a subtle incongruity between the vibrant hues of his clothing and the measured gravity of his countenance. His outfit consisted of a soft grey jacket, a crisp white waistcoat, and a straw hat adorned with a delicate blue ribbon. His dark, lean face was a canvas of contrasts, framed by a dense, black beard that seemed to whisper stories of a distant past. He puffed on a cigarette with the air of a man indulging in a leisurely stroll, yet his gaze was intent, as if weighing the intricacies of a complex recipe. No discerning eye would suspect that beneath his unassuming attire lay a loaded revolver, a police badge, and one of the sharpest intellects in Europe – Valentin, the renowned head of the Paris police, and the world's greatest investigator.

Flambeau had arrived in England, and the authorities of three nations had been hot on his trail, tracking him from Ghent to Brussels, and from Brussels to the Dutch coast. It was believed he would seek to blend in with the throngs attending the Eucharistic Congress, then convening in London. Perhaps he would assume the guise of a low-ranking official or secretary, but even Valentin couldn't be certain – Flambeau's mastery of disguise was the stuff of legend. 

In his heyday, which many now regarded as a bygone era, Flambeau had been a colossus of crime, sending shockwaves across the globe with each daring heist. Every morning, the newspapers would announce that he had narrowly escaped punishment by committing another audacious crime. A Gascon of imposing stature and physical prowess, he was said to possess a wild, athletic sense of humor – tales abounded of how he would turn the tables on his interrogators, sending them tumbling to the ground, or how he would sprint down the Champs-Élysées with a pair of bobbies in tow. Though his feats of physical strength were often employed in such undignified yet bloodless escapades, his true crimes were those of cunning and large-scale deception.
