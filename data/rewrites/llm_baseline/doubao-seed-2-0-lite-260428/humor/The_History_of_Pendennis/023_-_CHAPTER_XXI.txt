Any halfwit who’s ever flipped through a peerage guide or a random weird heraldry meme page knows that the noble family Helen Pendennis hailed from has a very specific, very dramatic crest: a full-grown mama pelican, chest all pricked open and oozing, feeding a whole nest of squawking, greedy baby pelicans who peck at her like they’re ordering 2 a.m. Uber Eats. Turns out that wacky medieval legend isn’t nearly as made up as you’d think. Us men will never wrap our heads around it, but women absolutely love pouring every last drop of their energy, cash, and peace of mind on the chaos gremlins they love. Doesn’t matter if it’s a deadbeat husband, a son who can’t stop racking up bar tabs, or a brother who lost the rent money at the races: they’ll have the fatted calf on the table before you even finish texting that you’re on your way home, no questions asked. If we only had cold, unforgiving justice to look forward to, half of us would be sleeping in a ditch right now, let’s be real.

Towards the end of Pen’s time at Oxbridge University, his stuffy old bachelor uncle the Major had gone full stage mom over the lad. He was absurdly proud of Pen’s high spirits, easy charm, good looks, and posh little aristocratic mannerisms, and loved bragging that Pen ran with all the most promising young lords at the university. This was the same guy who was so notoriously stingy that his club friends made up a whole bunch of running jokes about him never buying a round, but he suddenly started hosting cozy little private dinners for Pen and his noble pals, plying them with top-shelf claret, and showing off all his very best (and some very not appropriate) old club stories that landed way better with his dapper old-guy delivery than they would have from anyone else’s. He schmoozed the young lords’ parents through the boys, popped down to Oxbridge half a dozen times to let the students host parties in his honour (half the time just to mess with him, half the time because he was actually kind of a fun old dude), and even showed up to cheer at Pen’s big Union debate, thumping his cane so loud and whooping so hard he nearly broke the thing, then came away 100% convinced his nephew was the next William Pitt, destined to run the country.

He wrote Pen playful little letters full of London gossip and cheeky advice, bragged about him nonstop at his clubs, and even finagled invites for Pen to the fancy Gaunt House fête, gushing so hard about the boy’s brilliant oratory skills and guaranteed top-tier exam results that he might as well have been a mom carting her 12-year-old to a child beauty pageant. He swore up and down Pen would get taken on for a fancy embassy post under Lord Runnymede, or get hand-picked by some duke to represent one of his pocket boroughs, and he sent every last one of these delusional hot takes straight to Helen, who ate every word up because her Arthur could never do any wrong.

All that pride? Wiped to ash, because Pen had been an idiot who spent all his time carousing instead of studying math. He flunked his exams. Properly plucked. The guy who was supposed to be a rising political star had blown his big shot before it even started, drained his mom’s bank account dry like a total chump, and now the shame hit him twice as hard: he knew he’d screwed over the sweetest, most forgiving person on the planet, and his overinflated ego was absolutely shattered at the thought everyone was laughing at him. He was so convinced every single person on campus — his angry tutor, his bill collectors, his bedmaker, even the freshmen he’d once looked down on — would point and whisper about his failure, he locked himself in his rooms, scribbled a dramatic note to his tutor full of remorse and over-the-top despair begging to have his name struck off the college books, implying death would probably come for him any day now, then snuck out the back of the college down the least used alleyways, rain soaking his ragged old academic gown, until he wandered down to the empty banks of the Cam, usually packed with cheering students at boat races, and kept walking until he was miles outside the city.

That’s when his old acquaintance Mr. Spavin — the guy they’d known back at Baymouth, who’d gotten plucked not once, not twice, but three whole times before finally scraping a degree — came rattling past in his gaudy bright yellow postchaise, puffing on a cigar and yelling at his postboy to go faster. Spavin spotted Pen’s wild, pale face bobbing up the hill, screamed for the driver to stop, leaned halfway out the window, and waved at Pen until he trundled over.

“Whoa, old boy!” Spavin yelled, grabbing his hand. “What’s the big crisis? Don’t mope just ‘cause you got plucked! It’s no big deal once you get used to it — I’ve been plucked three times, and after the first one I barely even cared. You’ll nail it next time, easy.”

Pen stared at him, dumbfounded. This guy who’d failed three times, who could barely read and write properly when he first showed up to uni, had passed, and he, the great would-be statesman, had not? It was almost too much for his ego to handle. Spavin wouldn’t let him wander off alone into the rain, dragged him into his yellow chaise, lent him a spare coat and hat, and dumped him at the Ram Inn in Mudford, 15 miles out of Oxbridge, just in time for Pen to grab a spot on the London mail coach.

Back at Boniface College, the drama got so thick you could spread it on toast. Some walkers found Pen’s beat-up old cap a few feet from a mill stream three miles down the Fenbury road, his name half-visible stitched on the inside, plus his family seal crest engraved on a little charm nearby, and for 24 whole hours everyone was convinced Pen had drowned himself in despair. His tutor was panicking, all the tradesmen he owed money to were seething that their deadbeat customer had supposedly bailed by dying, until a letter postmarked London showed up and everyone exhaled in relief.

Pen rolled into London at 5 a.m., bleary, unshaven, still wearing yesterday’s shirt, 100% convinced every random stranger he passed had seen the exam results and was laughing at him. Even the porter at the Covent Garden inn where he usually stayed, even the maid scrubbing steps outside his uncle’s Bury Street lodgings, even his uncle’s valet Morgan — Pen was sure every single one of them knew he’d flunked.

The Major popped out of his bedroom seconds later, freshly wigged, perfumed to the nines from Delcroix’s fancy shop, and chirped, “Well done, future Senior Wrangler! Double First Class, eh? I’ll be right out!” When he finally got a good look at Pen’s ghastly face, his cheery tone dropped so fast it was comical. “Good God, Pen — what’s the matter?”

Pen mumbled that his name wasn’t on the exam lists. The Major, who’d never heard of failing uni, assumed Pen had been a coward in a duel or something and squawked “Lost your honour? You didn’t show the white feather, did you?” Pen had to hold back a hysterical bitter laugh and explain he’d gotten plucked. The Major had a very weird very specific misunderstanding at first, thinking “plucking” was some barbaric public corporal punishment the college doled out to bad students, and it took Pen a minute of spluttering to clarify no, he’d just ignored his math textbooks for three years and flunked the exam.

Then Pen spilled about the 700 pounds in random stupid debts: tailor bills, horse hire, fancy shirts and gloves, prints, jewellery, stacks of unread books, absurdly expensive college desserts, hair products, hotel tabs, you name it. The Major blew a gasket. He ranted that Pen was going to ruin his poor mother before he turned 25, stormed off to his club without even finishing breakfast, and spent the whole day badgering every one of his old fogey friends at the club to ask what you do with a nephew who flunks uni and owes 700 quid. Every single one of them just shrugged and went “Dude, half the rich kids at Oxbridge owe way more than that. Just pay it.” Slightly comforted that he wasn’t the only relative stuck with a uni idiot, the Major decided to punt the hard part of breaking the news to Helen to Doctor Portman, because old Victorian guys love making other people deliver bad news instead of doing it themselves.

Pen spent the rest of the day skulking around London, hiding from groups of loud Oxbridge boys, wandering past Ackermann’s print shop in the drizzling rain, reading half a tattered novel at a secondhand stall near the Temple, and going to a theatre pit show that night he was so distracted he couldn’t even remember the name of the play the next day.

Two days later, a kind letter arrived from his tutor, telling him no one hated him, begging him not to drop out, and that all he needed was a month of cramming math to nail the resit — along with Pen’s full trunks, all packed, every single one of his unpaid bills neatly stacked on the very top.

On the third day, the letter from home arrived. Pen read it, burst into tears, prayed his heart out with his head buried in his bedclothes, ate a breakfast so huge he could barely move, then trundled off to the Bull and Mouth in Piccadilly to grab his spot on the evening Chatteris coach, finally ready to head home and face his very forgiving mom.
