To understand the architecture of this betrayal, one must first grasp the absolute fragility of the union it sought to sanctify. In the annals of Tergou, the banns of marriage were a ritual of three readings, a bureaucratic incantation designed to ensure no hidden kinship or prior vow obscured the path to the altar. Yet, in the fervor of youth and the novelty of the Cure's arrival, the young couple had manipulated the system with reckless brilliance, persuading the clergyman to compress the requisite triad of readings into a single, frenzied twenty-four-hour cycle. They were proclaimed on Monday at Matins and Vespers, a triumph of timing that ensured the church remained empty save for the eager witnesses of their own devotion. By Tuesday morning, however, the atmosphere had curdled into a suffocating dread. As Margaret and Gerard stood before the altar, their hearts hammering against their ribs like trapped birds, a stranger rose from the congregation—a shadow cast by the law itself. With a voice devoid of warmth, he declared the union void, citing the parties' minority and the absence of parental consent. The sanctuary, moments before a temple of joy, became a courtroom of ruin.

Outside, beneath the gray Dutch sky, Margaret and Gerard huddled in a trembling consultation, their despair bordering on paralysis. Before they could formulate a defense, the accuser approached, feigning a sorrowful reluctance to intervene. He spun a web of plausible deniability, claiming his interference was born of a desire to protect their happiness, yet admitting his livelihood depended entirely on the prohibition of such unions. "The young people give me a crown," he whispered, his eyes gleaming with the greed of a professional obstructionist, "and I shall undo my work handsomely. Tell the Cure I was misinformed, and all returns to smooth sailing."

"A crown!" Gerard exclaimed, his desperation momentarily eclipsing his pride. "I will give you a golden angel to do this!" The transaction was sealed with the alacrity of thieves meeting in the dark. The man departed with his payment, a golden angel purchased from the future misery of two lovers, and hastened to the Cure. With a flourish of contrition, he confessed a "ridiculous mistake," corrected by the mere sight of the aggrieved pair. The Cure, relieved of his burden, agreed to the wedding for the following day at ten. Thus, the professional assassin of bliss retired to a tavern in Tergou, where a green dedicated to archery served as a backdrop for his drunken boasting. There, amidst the raucous laughter of the common folk, he recounted his exploit to a notorious loiterer named Sybrandt. Sybrandt, a creature of idleness and malice, fled home to relay the news to his father, who was away in Rotterdam purchasing cloth from the merchants. Seizing the opportunity, Sybrandt signaled his elder brother, and together they became the vanguard of a conspiracy born of envy.

These two were the black sheep of the Van Swieten lineage, Gerard's darker reflections. Idleness is a slow poison, and the waiting for the death of loved ones a festering wound; these two one-dimensional curs were ready to tear anyone limb from limb who threatened their miserable inheritance, a phantom wealth that occupied their thoughts by day and haunted their dreams by night. While their parents viewed parsimony as a virtue rooted in industry and love, these twisted hearts perverted that same thrift into avarice, a fertile soil for crimes far more destructive than any natural vice. They plotted in secret, deciding not to inform their mother, whose sentiments were too volatile, but to bypass her entirely and seek the Burgomaster. They were cunning enough to sense his aversion to the match, though the reason remained obscure to them.

Ghysbrecht Van Swieten saw through their scheme instantly, yet he masked his insight behind a veil of magisterial indifference. Hearing their story, he adopted an air of cold authority, declaring, "Since the father of the family is not here, his duty falls upon me, who am the father of the town. I know your father's mind; leave all to me. Above all, tell not a woman a word of this, least of all the women in your own house, for chattering tongues mar the wisest counsels." With a dismissal laced with condescension, he sent them away, ashamed of his complicity with such foolish conspirators.

Upon returning home, they discovered a scene of profound transformation. Gerard sat on a low stool at his mother's knee, her hand caressing his hair, her voice filled with tender promises to support him against his father's wrath. The cause of this sudden capitulation was characteristic of the woman's capricious nature. In a fit of female irritation, she had previously torn Margaret's portrait to shreds, watching with misgiving as Gerard turned pale as death, sitting motionless like a bereaved ghost, tears blinding his vision. Terrified by her own cruelty, her heart had smote her bitterly, and she wept in secret, resolving to make amends without confession. Her maternal instincts, fierce and forgiving, had already transferred her fatal alliance to Gerard when the two black sheep entered the room. Unaware of the immediate catalyst, and inexperienced in the intricate machinations of the feminine heart, Gerard mistook her kindness for absolution. Ashamed of his suspicion that she was the destroyer of his love, he kissed her repeatedly, retreating to his bed a prince once more, believing his mother had reclaimed her rightful place beside him at the very crisis of his fate.

The next morning, at ten o'clock, Gerard and Margaret stood in the church of Sevenbergen. He was radiant with joy; she burned with blushes. Only Peter and Martin Wittenhaagen were present, for secrecy was paramount. Margaret had renounced Italy; she could not abandon her father, who was too learned and too helpless to travel. It was settled they would retire to Flanders for a few weeks until the storm in Tergou passed. The Cure did not keep them waiting long. Standing at the altar, he called them forward, and they walked hand in hand, the happiest souls in Holland. He opened his book, preparing to speak the sacred rites.

But ere a single syllable of the ceremony escaped his lips, a harsh voice shattered the silence: "Forbear!" Constables of Tergou surged up the aisle, seizing Gerard in the name of the law. Martin's long knife flashed out in an instant. "Forbear, man!" cried the priest, indignant. "What! Draw your weapon in a church? Ye who interrupt this holy sacrament, what manner of impiety is this?"

"There is no impiety, Father," replied the Burgomaster's servant respectfully. "This young man seeks to marry against his father's will, and his father has prayed our Burgomaster to deal with him according to the law. Let him deny it if he can."

"Is this so, young man?" the priest demanded.

Gerard hung his head, defeated. "We take him to Rotterdam to abide the sentence of the Duke," the servant announced.

At this, Margaret uttered a cry of despair. The young creatures, so happy a moment before, collapsed into each other's arms, sobbing pitifully. Even the instruments of oppression recoiled, ashamed of their cruelty, until one, of a better nature, stepped forward under the pretext of separating them. He whispered to Margaret, "Rotterdam? It is a lie. We merely take him to our Stadthouse."

They mounted him on horseback, riding the road to Rotterdam. After a dozen halts and sly detours, they arrived back at Tergou. Just outside the town gates, a rough vehicle covered in canvas awaited. Gerard was forced inside, and by five in the evening, he was secretly conveyed into the prison of the Stadthouse. He was hoisted up several flights of stairs and thrust into a small room illuminated only by a narrow window barred with vertical iron. The entire furniture consisted of a massive oak chest. Imprisonment in that age was a highway to death; even in its mildest form, it implied cold, unbroken solitude, torture, starvation, and often poison. Gerard felt he was in the hands of an implacable enemy.

"Oh, the look that man gave me on the road to Rotterdam," he murmured, kneeling to commend his soul to God. "There is more here than my father's wrath. I doubt I shall see the light of day again."

Presently, he rose and leaped at the iron bar of the window, clutching it tightly. This allowed him to press his knees against the wall and peer outward. For a fleeting minute, he witnessed a sight reserved only for the captive: Martin Wittenhaagen's back. Martin sat quietly fishing in the brook near the Stadthouse. Gerard sprang again at the window and whistled. Martin instantly ceased his pretense of fishing, turning hastily to see Gerard, making a signal before rising with his line and bow and departing quickly. Gerard realized his friends were not idle, yet he wished Martin had stayed; the sight of him was a comfort. Holding on, he gazed at the soldier's retreating form until he could no longer see, then fell back heavily. With a wrench, he pulled the rusty iron bar, held fast only by corroded nails, away from the stonework. At that precise moment, Ghysbrecht Van Swieten opened the door stealthily behind him. The Burgomaster's eye fell instantly on the dislodged iron, then glanced at the window, but he said nothing.
