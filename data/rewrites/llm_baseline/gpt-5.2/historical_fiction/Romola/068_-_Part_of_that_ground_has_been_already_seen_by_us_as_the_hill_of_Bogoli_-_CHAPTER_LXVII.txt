In the hour when the sky is neither night nor day, when the Arno seems to exhale its own cold breath and the stones of Florence hold a damp that seeps through soles, a man went out alone on the Oltrarno bank. He kept within the walls, yet chose a place no household watched—a reach of river where granaries and warehouses turned their backs like sullen beasts, leaving a sloping verge of long grass and rushes to drink the overflow of gutters. The gutters were his companions. They carried, now and then, the small mercies of a city that wastes as it feeds: a heel of bread that had fallen from a porter’s basket, carrot tops trimmed away in haste, apple-parings flung from some morning’s peeling, all sent down in thin bright ribbons of water that gurgled with a sound like a secret being told.

Baldassarre had learned those sounds as another man learns the crackle of a hearth. They were his kitchen now—those runlets, those rank stems, that sour smell of wet earth and grain-dust from the storehouses. He sat among the grass and bent over the swift channelled flow beside him, not praying—he had long since worn through that cloth—but watching with the same intentness a cook gives to a simmering pot when there is little fuel and one must not waste a bubble.

He had once been given a large piece of bread by that friendly current, and more than once a raw carrot, and parings still sweet at the rind. It was worth waiting where no eye could mock him. It might spare him for a day the worst hunger of all: the hunger that makes a man sit on a church-step beyond Porta San Frediano and beg without words, offering his very silence as alms-receipt.

He hated that. Not because his belly did not plead—his belly had become an animal he carried like any other burden—but because in him there lived, beneath all feebleness, one stubborn appetite that was not for food. He desired to live for the taste of vengeance, for the moment when the traitor’s name would be spoken true and not twisted by fine tongues. It was no longer hope, not even a plan: only that fixed possibility that can occupy a mind the way a forgotten dialect occupies an old man’s mouth—ready at any moment to leap forth whole, though no one around will understand it. Like a woman who watches a headland for a ship every dawn when all her neighbours have long agreed the ship went down, Baldassarre waited for something dim and formless, a sign without a shape, that made his heart pulse hard as if some hand had shaken him awake.

Since the monks of San Miniato had nursed him in their hospital until he could stand; since he had watched in vain for the Wife who was to help him and had begun to believe she lay among the pestilence-dead that filled the city like a stench in cloth; since his knife was gone and his body too broken for work and his mind too clouded for contrivance—since then he had moved like a man half translated into another language, his old speech still in him, but the city answering in sounds that did not fit. Yet he came, again and again, into the chill grey, and listened to the runlets, and watched the river that had a faint promise in it.

On the other side of Florence, far from that quiet reach, the morning was being kneaded into uproar.

The Compagnacci—those bands of armed men whom the Signoria, embarrassed if not secretly willing, let swagger and strike—had divided like a torrent meeting stones, parting into two violent streams. One pressed toward the Ponte Rubaconte, the larger surged for the Ponte Vecchio. In both, a word passed mouth to mouth—brief as a cook’s command in a hot kitchen, yet changing all hands at once—and almost every man knew his errand: to go into the Via de’ Bardi and sack a house there. If a man asked for reasons in such a crowd, he might as well ask why fire runs along dry straw. The armed leaders knew more; a brief order flies, and the meeting was set before a certain house toward the eastern end, where the master would likely be pulled from his morning sleep.

But that master had not slept.

Tito Melema’s desire to quit Florence had grown sharp with yesterday’s events: investigations would follow; appeals might be made; delay was a net, and in every mesh he felt danger. Falsehood had served him well and fattened like a hog at a rich trough, but it had nourished its twin beside it—Fear. He no longer wore armour; he no longer watched every shadow for Baldassarre. Yet from the corpse of that old fear something had risen: a habit, undying, that made his skin tighten at a footfall and his mind measure exits as naturally as it measured advantage.

He would not be safe, he told himself, until the fierce turbid air of Florence lay behind him. And now he was ready. Maso was to deliver the house to a new tenant. Horses and mules waited at San Gallo. Tessa and the children had been lodged for the night in the Borgo outside the gate—wrapped and placed like precious provisions to be carried out early before heat or thieves could spoil the journey—and would be dressed and ready to mount when he joined them.

He went down the stone steps into the courtyard and through the great doorway. In that cold light he was not the same Tito who had first entered that house with youth’s ease and had made, as he now named it, the mistake of falling in love with Romola. That life was cast off like a worn garment. He would leave it in Florence’s hands and go on with new silk on his shoulders.

He turned toward the Piazza dei Mozzi, intending the Ponte Rubaconte. Then sounds reached him—first distant, like the deep stir of a market before the stalls are set, then nearer: a tramp, a clatter of iron, voices thick with drink and anger. The noises of men are like the noises of mills: once they begin, they grind. He wheeled and walked quickly the other way.

Was the mob coming into Oltrarno? It was vexation as well as fear: he would have preferred the quieter road. He must go by the Ponte Vecchio. Unpleasant sensations tightened his throat; he drew his mantle close and hastened, the damp air smelling of river and closed-up houses.

No one saw him in the twilight. Yet before he had reached the end of the Via de’ Bardi, the same sounds fell on his ear again, louder and nearer. Had he been deceived before? No—this was the roll of a crowd. Coming over the Ponte Vecchio.

Again he turned—fear outrunning reflection—and found, too late, that the mob was also entering the street from the opposite end. He would not go back into his house. After all, they would not be hunting him—so he tried to tell himself. But he had valuables on him, and a mob is like a pot that has boiled over: it scalds whatever is nearest. Necessity did the work of courage. He went on toward the bridge.

The rush and trampling and confused voices grew so loud ahead that he ceased to hear what might be behind. He reached the end of the street. The crowd pouring from the bridge met him at the turning and closed round him. He had not time even to wonder at a sudden shout before he felt himself seized—not first by unarmed hands, but by men with weapons. His cap was knocked off; he was thrust violently forward among the rabble along the narrow passage of the bridge, where shops and woodwork narrowed the way and sound rebounded as if inside a barrel.

Then the words came distinct, flung like stones: “Piagnone! Medicean! Piagnone! Throw him over the bridge!”

His mantle was torn off him with such force that the pulls would have throttled him if the fibula had not given way. Fingers clawed at his scarsella, but the crowd’s own pressing and dragging foiled the snatch; it still hung at his side. Shouting and yelling—half without motive, mere relish of insult as of rancid wine—rang in his ears, spreading even to those who had not yet seen him and only knew there was a man to curse.

His horror fixed itself on one point: to reach the open arches at the centre. If they struck him down before then, he would be trampled and finished in the crush. There was one hope, black as the river itself—that they might throw him over before they had beaten the strength out of him. His whole soul narrowed to that hope and its reverse.

Yes—the arches. He saw the widening above the parapet, and in that moment, bloodless and with eyes stretched wide by terror, the mind that had lived on quick invention found one last resource. He fumbled at the clasp of his belt, mastered it with desperate speed, and flung belt and scarsella forward toward a yard of clear space against the parapet, crying out in a ringing voice that cut through the din like a knife through crust—

“There are diamonds! there is gold!”

In the instant, hands relaxed. Greed is a quicker hunger than hate. Men surged toward the thrown prize. Tito flung himself onto the parapet with a leap born of panic and strength, and the next moment he plunged—plunged with a great plash into the dark Arno far below.

It was his chance, and it was a good one. His life had once before been saved by fine swimming; as he came up after his long dive and the river closed cold around his skin, he tasted deliverance as sharply as if it were wine after thirst. He struck out with all the vigour still in his limbs. The current helped him, tugging him onward as if the river itself had taken sides.

If only he could pass beyond the Ponte alla Carrara, he might land where few watched, and even yet reach San Gallo. Life, he told himself, was still before him—another table laid, another city to feed on and be fed by.

On the bridge the mob shouted and bellowed, peering over, but they could not see far along the dark stream: they saw no more than a ripple and a vanishing blackness. They believed him drowned, as men often believe what pleases them.

Yet from another angle a face could be seen moving with the current—pale olive made white above the water, a face with a square forehead, long low arch of brows, and lustrous agate-like eyes that would not easily be forgotten. Onward it went with inflated quivering nostrils, the blue veins distended on the temples, hair floating like weed, sleeves heavy with water.

One bridge was passed—the Santa Trinita. Should he risk landing now rather than trust his strength? He fancied he heard cries pursuing him; terror pressed hardest from the side of men. Better the indefinite risk of mud and darkness than the certain hands of a crowd. He swam on, panting, straining. He had not slept; his muscles were not as fresh as his confidence pretended.

The next bridge—the last—was passed. He knew it, but in the tumult of blood in his ears he felt only vaguely that safety was possible, that he might land. But where? The current had its way with him. Exhaustion brought that dreamy slipping of mind that comes before unconsciousness, when sounds become muffled as if heard through cloth.

Now, at last, eyes discerned him—aged eyes trained by hunger to see chances at a distance.

Baldassarre had lifted his head from the runlet that brought him nothing. He had seen a pale object coming along the broader stream, and the sight struck him with that same start a cook has when a long-expected delivery appears at the kitchen door. Could this be fortune for him? He stared until the object gathered form; then he leaned forward, the blankness breaking, as if a lamp had been lit behind his eyes. Yet he did not move. He watched—motionless—because something was being brought to him.

The river cast a man’s body violently onto the grass two yards from him, as a wave casts a drowned branch. In the same instant Baldassarre sprang like a panther despite his years, clutching the velvet tunic, falling forward on the body, and flashing a look into the face.

Dead—was he dead? The eyes were rigid. The mouth slack. The skin cold with the river’s touch. For one beat of his heart despair iced him: if death had already taken this prey, what was vengeance now but an empty word?

But no—Justice had brought him. Men could look dead and yet have life creep back, as embers glow again when stirred. Baldassarre did not feel feeble then. His hands knew their work. He thrust his large fingers into the neck of the tunic and held them there, kneeling beside the body, watching the face with a fierce hope that trembled.

Rigid still. The half-fallen lids locked the eyes against him. Could it be that he was dead? There was no measure of time; it stretched and twisted like a bad dream. At last—surely—the eyelids quivered. The eyes were no longer stone. A vibrating light returned; they opened wide.

“Ah, yes! You see me—you know me!”

Tito knew him. But in that instant he did not know whether life or death had washed him into his injured father’s grasp. Perhaps death meant this chill gloom forever, with the hideous past leaning over him and no waking.

Baldassarre’s only dread was that those young limbs would escape. He pressed his knuckles against the round throat and knelt upon the chest with all the force his aged frame could summon. Let death come now, he thought—let it come while I hold him.

Again he watched the face. When the eyes grew rigid again, he dared not trust the seeming death. He would not loose his hold until a witness came. Justice, he believed, would send someone—an ear to hear, a tongue to speak what happened in plain words the city could not smooth into lies. Then he, Baldassarre, would say, “I killed this traitor to whom I was once a father.” They might believe him now; and he would be content with the struggle of justice on earth. Then he would desire to die with his hold on the body, and follow the traitor to hell, clutching still.

So he knelt, knuckles hard at the throat, hand hooked in the tunic, not trusting the stillness, holding fast with the obstinacy of a man who has had his true name taken from him and will not surrender the last syllable. The river beside them ran on with its ceaseless voice: slapping, whispering, sucking at the reeds—indifferent music, older than Florence, yet in that hour it seemed like a chorus under the act of a tragedy.

Light strengthened. His limbs gave way. He could kneel no longer, and he sank to sit upon the body, still clutching at the tunic’s neck as if it were the only rope left to him in a world of slipping ground.

Hours passed. No witness came. No eyes from the distant embankment detected two human bodies among tall grass by the riverside. Florence was busy with greater affairs and the preparation of a deeper tragedy. Not long after those two lay there, Savonarola was being tortured, crying out in his agony, “I will confess!” The city’s soundscape was filled with other cries—iron on stone, orders barked, moans stifled behind walls—so that this quiet reach of the Arno, with its whispering reeds and gurgling runlets, might have been another world.

It was not until the sun leaned westward that a waggon drawn by a mild grey ox came to the grassy margin. The man who led it bent to gather the round stones heaped there ready for carrying away; and as he straightened, something in the grass startled him—two bodies, one old and one young, locked together. The aged man had fallen forward, his dead clutch still fastening the other’s garment. It was not possible to separate them. Better to lift them as they were into the waggon and carry them into the great Piazza, so notice might be given to the Eight.

As the waggon entered the frequented streets, a crowd gathered and thickened around it, escorting the strange burden with that same appetite for spectacle that draws men to a cookshop window when the scent of roasting begins. People craned, jostled, listened for names. For a long while no one knew the bodies: the old face had fallen forward, half hiding the younger.

Then recognition spread like a spice thrown into warm wine.

“I know that old man,” Piero di Cosimo testified. “I painted his likeness once. He is the prisoner who clutched Melema on the steps of the Duomo.”

“He is perhaps the same old man who appeared at supper in my gardens,” said Bernardo Rucellai, one of the Eight. “I had forgotten him. I thought he had died in prison. But there is no knowing the truth now.”

And indeed, who shall put his finger on the work of justice and say, “It is there”? The city could name bodies and yet fail to name the meaning. Justice, like the Kingdom of God, is not outside us as a finished fact; it is within us as a great yearning—felt in the throat like hunger, heard in the ear like a remembered tongue, clung to with hands that will not let go even when the life has gone out of them.
