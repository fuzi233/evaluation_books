"You are easily frightened," Piero observed, his laughter cutting through the tension with a sharp, mocking edge. "My portrait lacks the ferocity of the original. Yet, that old man possessed a tiger's gaze. I intend to return to the Duomo and study him anew."

"It is unpleasant to be seized by a madman, provided he remains mad," Lorenzo Tornabuoni interjected, offering a polite defense for Tito's earlier panic. "Though perhaps he is merely a ruffian. We shall ascertain the truth. Furthermore, we must determine if we possess sufficient authority to halt this disturbance between our citizens and these foreigners," he added, turning his attention to the French officer.

They moved forward, swords drawn, flanked by a silent procession of onlookers who parted respectfully. Tito followed suit; the initial paralysis of terror had already yielded to the rapid calculation born of mortal peril. The mob, prone to jeering at the soldier rather than inflicting lethal harm, dispersed instantly upon seeing the nobles advance. The interrogation began. The Frenchman revealed that his party had entered the city solely to ransom captives: two Tuscan soldiers captured in Lunigiana and an elderly Genoese man, whose group had clashed with French foragers near Fivizzano. While the old man appeared deranged, he posed no threat. The soldier admitted ignorance of the elder's speech, unable to comprehend a single word.

Tito remained deaf to the proceedings until Tornabuoni addressed him directly. "Will you accompany us, Melema? Or, since Messere departs for Signa, will you wisely adopt the current custom and attend the Frate? The orator will be a torrent in full flood this morning—a necessity for anyone wishing to preserve their Medicean integrity. I would attend myself if leisure permitted."

Tito's complexion had regained its hue, and he managed a display of cheerful composure. "Naturally, I rank among the admirers of the inspired speaker," he smiled. "Unfortunately, I am detained by the Segretario until the procession commences."

"I shall enter the Duomo to observe that savage old man once more," Piero declared.

"Then, Piero mio, exercise the charity to present him to one of the hospitals for travelers," Tornabuoni commanded. "The monks may discern whether he requires confinement within a cage."

The group dispersed. Tito proceeded toward the Palazzo Vecchio to locate Bartolommeo Scala. Though the distance was short, the journey felt elongated, stretching into the agonizing duration of a waking nightmare. Every step across the street and piazza resurrected memories and precipitated anxieties that could have defined months of existence. A serpent seemed to coil tightly around his limbs.

Baldassarre alive and residing in Florence represented a living vendetta—an entity that would not rest, much like a coiling snake, until it had crushed its prey. Such a man did not allow injuries to pass unavenged; his love and hatred burned with a passionate fervor that subjugated all other faculties, driving a man to sacrifice himself to his obsession as if worshipping a destructive deity. Baldassarre had vanished, yet Tito understood the implication perfectly: the vengeance was being studied, refined to ensure absolute certainty.

If he had not uttered those decisive words—"He is a madman"—if he could have summoned the mental fortitude to acknowledge his recognition of Baldassarre openly, the risk would have been mitigated. He could have claimed positive evidence of the old man's death. The only witnesses capable of contradicting him were Fra Luca, who was deceased, and the crew of the companion galley that delivered news of the pirate encounter. The probability of Baldassarre having reunited with any member of that crew was infinitesimal. Tito harbored bitter resentment that a timely, well-constructed falsehood might have spared him fatal consequences. However, fabricating such a lie would have demanded perfect self-command during a moment of convulsive shock. He realized now that his words had erupted spontaneously, like a sudden birth nurtured in darkness, devoid of preconception.

This was the inexorable law governing human souls: we prepare for sudden acts through the reiterated choices of good or evil that gradually forge character. His only remaining recourse was Baldassarre's potential failure to execute his revenge. And then, a thought emerged, more actively cruel than anything Tito had previously entertained: might his own unpremeditated denial contain a kernel of truth? Sufficient truth, at minimum, to shield him against any declaration Baldassarre might attempt?

The old man appeared strange and wild; with his eager intellect and suffering heart, madness was a plausible outcome. If true, the vengeance seeking to inflict disgrace could be thwarted. Yet, there existed another form of vengeance that no amount of cunning deception could parry. Baldassarre belonged to a lineage where the thrust of the dagger was as instinctive as the tiger's leap. Tito recoiled with shuddering dread from disgrace, yet he shared a visceral terror inherent to a soft, pleasure-seeking nature—one that prevents a man from facing wounds and death as a welcome respite from shame.

His mind raced toward some hidden defensive armor capable of saving him from a vengeance no subtlety could deflect. He marveled at the potency of the passionate fear consuming him, transforming the joyful vitality of youth into acute pain. One resource remained theoretically open: he could have retreated, sought out Baldassarre again, and confessed everything—to Romola, to the world. But he never conceived of doing so. True repentance, severing all ties to evil, demands more than selfish fear. He possessed no conviction that truth offered strength or safety; he trusted only his ingenuity and his dissimulation.

Now that the initial shock, exposing the treacherous signs of fear, had subsided, he hoped to navigate all emergencies through cool deceit and defensive stratagems. It was a defining feature of Tito's experience at this critical juncture that no direct measure to eliminate Baldassarre ever crossed his mind. Every other possibility traversed his consciousness, including his own flight from Florence, yet no scheme arose to remove his enemy. His dread generated no active malignancy; he would still prefer not to cause pain to any mortal. He had simply chosen to make life easy for himself, carrying his human burden in a manner that pinched him nowhere. This choice had, at various intervals, deposited him in unforeseen predicaments.

The question now was not whether he should share the common pressure of destiny with his suffering fellow-men, but whether the entire arsenal of lying would suffice to prevent him from being crushed by the inevitable consequences of that habitual choice.
