I stand ready to confess a glaring inconsistency that would surely scandalize my Spaceland critics. Throughout the preceding chapters, I have insisted that every figure in Flatland presents itself merely as a straight line, implying that visual distinction between social strata is impossible. Yet, here I propose to demonstrate precisely how we perceive one another. If the attentive reader recalls the discussion on Recognition by Feeling, they will note the crucial caveat: that ability is universal *only* among the lower classes. Among the higher orders, and specifically within our temperate latitudes, recognition is achieved solely through the faculty of sight. This unique capacity arises not from clarity, but from the pervasive presence of Fog. While you in Spaceland regard this elemental veil as a malady that obscures vision and saps vitality, we revere it as a benefactor, a nurse of arts and parent of sciences. Without this atmospheric haze, all lines would appear with equal, blinding sharpness—a condition found only in those arid, miserable lands devoid of moisture. Here, however, the fog creates a gradient of visibility. Objects situated three feet away appear distinctly dimmer than those at two feet eleven inches. Through rigorous, lifelong calibration of these gradations in luminosity, we deduce the precise geometric configuration of our neighbors.

Consider two figures approaching: a Merchant, an equilateral triangle, and a Physician, a pentagon. How does the eye discern their difference? When I align my gaze to bisect the angle of an approaching stranger, my view falls evenly between the two sides nearest me. In the case of the Merchant, I observe a single line where the center glows brightly, flanked by edges that plunge rapidly into obscurity, vanishing quickly into the mist. His extremities are faint shadows. Conversely, the Physician presents a line where the central brightness fades gradually; his sides recede less swiftly into the gloom, rendering his outer points significantly brighter than the Merchant's. Thus, even a novice can distinguish the middle ranks from the lowest.

Yet, do not mistake this for simplicity. Life rarely offers static angles. If my father, a triangle, approaches presenting a side rather than an apex, I remain momentarily uncertain whether he is a triangle or a woman—a straight line—until he rotates or I shift my vantage point. Similarly, observing a hexagonal grandson head-on reveals a complex interplay: one dominant line of comparative brightness flanked by two smaller, dimmer lines fading toward the corners. To navigate a crowded ballroom, where elite polygons rotate and advance in chaotic vectors, requires a mastery of kinetic geometry that taxes the intellect to its limit. It is this complexity that justifies the existence of the Learned Professors of Static and Kinetic Geometry at the University of Wentbridge, institutions accessible only to the scions of our wealthiest houses.

For the common tradesman or serf, such a spectacle is incomprehensible chaos; to a Spaceland observer suddenly thrust into our midst, the crowd would appear as a shifting tapestry of irregular brightness, indistinguishable from noise. Even a student versed in the theory of the upper classes finds that practical application demands years of apprenticeship before they can move through society without jostling the elite, whom etiquette forbids one to touch. To comport oneself with propriety in Polygonal society, one must essentially be a polygon oneself.

This reliance on sight has profoundly shaped our education. Just as the deaf-mute in Spaceland fails to master lip-reading if denied the manual alphabet, so too do we in Flatland fail to achieve perfect sight-recognition if we rely on the tactile sense of Feeling. Consequently, among the higher classes, feeling is strictly proscribed. Children bypass elementary schools, where feeling is taught, and enter exclusive seminaries. At the university, to feel is a grave offense punishable by expulsion. Among the poor, however, sight remains an unattainable luxury; their sons are forced to labor, learning to feel from infancy. This grants them a precocious, mercurial energy that initially outpaces the lethargic, half-trained youth of the educated classes. But once the latter complete their university curriculum, a metamorphosis occurs. Suddenly, armed with theoretical precision, they surpass their triangular rivals in every pursuit, overtaking them with the speed of a new birth.

Only a small fraction of the nobility fails the final examination. Their fate is tragic. Cast down from the heights of the Polygonal aristocracy, they are simultaneously despised by the lower classes, lacking both the systematic rigor of the masters and the natural agility of the tradesmen. Professions and public service remain closed to them. Though legally permitted to marry, they struggle to find suitable partners, for experience dictates that the offspring of such mismatched unions tends toward misfortune and irregularity. It is from this rejected underclass—the refuse of our nobility—that the great seditions of history have drawn their leaders. Some progressive statesmen now argue that true mercy lies in their total suppression, advocating for imprisonment or painless extinction for those who fail the final test. I pause here, for the subject of irregularities demands a chapter unto itself.
