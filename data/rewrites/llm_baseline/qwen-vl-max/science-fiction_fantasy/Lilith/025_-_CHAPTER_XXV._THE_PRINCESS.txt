I circled the ancient fortress, passing through the open gates and crossing the deep, moat-like ravine that gaped beneath me like a wound in the earth. Beyond lay a vast courtyard paved with stone, lined at regular intervals by towering trees resembling poplars—tall, straight, and silent. At its center stood one tree taller than the rest, its upper branches fanning outward in a way that hinted at a palm’s grace. Between the trunks, I caught glimpses of the palace: long and low, with soaring towers at each corner and a massive dome rising from the roof, half as high as the towers themselves. The architecture was unlike anything I had seen—strange, yet unmistakably rooted in an Eastern tradition, perhaps Indian in origin.

The main entrance was a low arch shaped like a flattened oval. No guards stood watch. The doors hung wide, inviting. I stepped inside unchallenged into a great hall, elongated and elliptical, dimly lit. Near one wall sat a cage, within which rested a colossal leopardess. Her body was white, marked with dark oval spots, her head cradled on her paws. A steel collar encircled her neck, her mouth muzzled, her paws wrapped in thick cloth. Yet she stared at me with eyes wide open—pupils canoe-shaped, irises a deep green—unblinking, unmoving. Her tail lay rigid behind her like a bar of iron. I could not tell if she lived or was merely a statue carved from flesh and shadow.

From this chamber, two narrow corridors branched off. I chose one, only to find it splitting into a maze of twisting, uneven passages. In a tight space where barely two could pass, a young page collided with me. He recoiled in fear, but upon seeing my face, he stiffened, puffed out his chest, and demanded, “What is your business?”

“To see the princess,” I said.

“A likely thing!” he scoffed. “I haven up with her highness this morning myself!”

I seized him by the nape and shook him. “Take me to her now—or I’ll drag you until I find her. She shall know how her servants treat her guests.”

He glanced at me, then suddenly began pulling me forward like a blind dog guiding its master. We arrived at a large kitchen, where several servants moved sluggishly, their eyes half-lidded, barely awake. I expected them to attack, but instead they froze, staring—not at me, but at something behind me. Their faces grew pale, their breaths shallow.

I turned. There stood the white leopardess, watching them with stillness that seemed to hold the weight of a thousand deaths. Then, slowly, one servant recovered. I released the boy and turned to him. “Take me to the princess,” I said.

“She has not left her room, my lord,” he replied.

“Tell her I am here, waiting for audience.”

“Your name, sir?”

“Say only that one who knows the white leech seeks her.”

He hesitated. “She will kill me if I deliver such a message. I cannot—I dare not.”

“You refuse?” I asked.

He glanced at me, then fled toward the inner chambers.

The others remained transfixed, too afraid to look away from the beast.

I approached the leopardess, who now stood near me, muzzle lowered to my heel. Her fur gleamed like milk in the dim light, a warm radiance in the gloom. I knelt and gently stroked her. She looked up—just that movement sent the servants scattering like frightened birds. She rose on her hind legs, placed her paws on my shoulders, and I embraced her. But then she pricked her ears, broke free, and vanished into the shadows.

Moments later, the servant returned. “Please follow me, my lord,” he said.

My heart thudded. This was no ordinary encounter. I followed him through winding halls until we reached a vast, pitch-black chamber. The walls were invisible. Only a single patch of floor reflected faint light. Above, at great height, an oval opening pierced the ceiling, framed by joints of black marble. The floor itself was laid with tightly fitted slabs of the same dark stone. Later I would learn that the entire elliptical wall was also made of black marble, swallowing any light that touched it. The roof curved like the upper half of an ellipsoid, and the aperture above was positioned over one focus of the ellipse below.

For a moment, I thought I saw faint red lines across the darkness—but when I blinked, they were gone.

Then, suddenly, a figure appeared in the center of the void. Light erupted around her, flaring from her presence as if she carried the sun within her. She wore a soft white robe, and her hair fell in a waterfall of jet-black strands, cascading over the obsidian floor. Her eyes were pools of luminous black; her arms and feet glowed like polished ivory. She smiled—innocent, radiant—as though she had just stepped from childhood into womanhood.

“Alas,” I thought, “how wrong I was to judge her danger! Is this the woman who struck me, scorned me, abandoned me?”

I stood in the dark, watching her. She stood in the dark, searching for me.

Then she vanished.

“She does not recognize me,” I thought.

But in the next breath, her eyes flashed into mine—direct, piercing, alive.

“You have found me at last,” she said, placing her hand on my shoulder. “I knew you would.”

My body trembled—not from cold, but from a storm of emotions I could not name. I felt drawn to her, repelled by her—both at once.

“You shiver,” she said softly. “This place is cold for you. Come.”

I did not speak. She had silenced me with beauty. She held me with sweetness.

She took my hand and led me to the spot of light. Again, she blazed forth—radiant, undeniable.

“You’ve grown brown since I last saw you,” she said.

“This is almost the first roof I’ve been under since you left me,” I replied.

“And whose was the other?” she asked.

“I do not know the woman’s name.”

“I would gladly learn it. Hospitality is not strong in my people.”

She led me through the darkness to a curtain of black silk. Beyond it, a white staircase ascended. She guided me to a chamber of breathtaking beauty.

“How you must miss the hot flowing river,” she said. “But there is a bath in the corner—no white leeches here. At the foot of your couch, you’ll find a garment. When you come down, I’ll be in the room to your left at the stair.”

She left me standing, questioning my own judgment. How could I treat this woman as evil when she greeted me like a sister? What had changed her? She had struck me, mocked me, fled—but now welcomed me with warmth. She had reviled me, yet claimed she knew I would return.

Did she know my doubts? Did she know I needed answers?

Could she give them?

Could I believe her?

And yet—her hospitality was real. I had earned it. I could accept it—for now. Until I judged her truly.

Could such beauty and such darkness exist in one soul?

If so—how?

I stood before the bath, its water clear as crystal, sparkling in the corner where it sank into the marble. It looked celestial. I had not seen water since leaving home—except the stream, the cottage draughts, the pools near the wounded leopardess. This was pure, untouched.

I stepped in.

At once, a delicate scent filled my mind—strange, sweet, yet unsettling. I paused. Had she drugged it? Enchanted it? Was she manipulating me?

I remembered the crushed paw of the leopardess—the mother fleeing, the howl echoing, the limping beast.

But what mattered where it came from? Was the water not sweet? Was it not the same as the pitcher-plant’s dew, secreted for the weary traveler?

Water came from heaven. Why care about the well?

Still, I did not return to the bath.

I dressed in the white wool robe, embroidered at the neck and hem, and descended to the room she had named.

It was round, entirely of alabaster, windowless. Light seeped through the walls and dome, soft and pearlescent, like moonlight on mist. Shadowy forms drifted across the surfaces—like clouds over a sky of gray-blue.

She waited, draped in a silver-embroidered robe, stitched with rings, discs, rectangles, lozenges—close together, like armor of stars. It fell from her neck to her feet, but the sleeves were open, revealing arms like ivory.

On a table of ivory lay cakes, fruit, a jug of milk, a crystal carafe of pale rose wine, and a white loaf.

“We do not kill to eat here,” she said. “But I think you will like what I offer.”

I told her nothing could please me more.

She seated herself on a couch beside the table and gestured for me to sit beside her. She poured milk into a bowl, handed me the loaf, and filled two silver goblets from the wine jug—goblets of exquisite, twisted design.

“You’ve never drunk wine like this,” she said.

I tasted it—and was stunned. Every flower of Hybla and Hymettus had given its spirit to this elixir.

“And now,” she continued, “since you can listen, I must try to make myself understood. Our natures are different—so different that it may be hard. Men live to die. We—those like me—live to live. Old age terrifies you; to me, it is a longing. The older we grow, the closer we come to perfection. Yours is fleeting, fragile; ours is endless ripening.”

She paused. “I am not yet ripe. I have lived thousands of your years—how many, I do not count. Eternity cannot be measured.”

“Many lovers have sought me,” she went on. “None have I loved. They wanted to possess me—like men in my city seek gems. But when you found me… I found a man.”

She leaned forward. “I tested you. You passed. Your love was true—but not ideal. Pity is not love. No woman, in any world, returns love for pity. That kind of love… it repels me.”

She looked at me with sorrowful eyes. “I knew if you saw me as I am, you’d love me—to have and to8hold. I refused that. I wanted a love that endured beyond hope, beyond indifference, beyond hatred. So I wore cruelty, pride, ingratitude. When I left you, I made sure you could no longer follow from pity. I no longer needed you. But you must prove yourself—either priceless or worthless. Follow me without expecting anything—without gratitude, without even pity. Just be present. Be patient. And if you do not, then I have failed. I yield.”

She hid her face in her hands, trembling.

But I saw a flicker behind the sorrow—a spark of calculation. I did not believe her.

She rose, arms raised high, eyes blazing like lightning.

“What you have made me is yours!” she cried. “I will repay you as no woman ever has. My power, my beauty, my love—take them all!”

She dropped to her knees, arms across my knees, looking up at me.

Then I noticed—on her left hand, a clumsy glove. In my mind’s eye, I saw claws and hair beneath it. A hand bruised, clenched tight. I looked at the other—perfect, lovely, flawless. If I did not hate her, I might love her.

I turned my gaze away.

She rose abruptly.

“To me, she may be true,” whispered my vanity.

For a moment, I was tempted to love a lie.

A subtle scent brushed my skin—more than air, less than perfume.

I looked up.

She stood before me, arms waving in a gesture both graceful and strange.

Then—a roar, terrifying, shaking the very walls. The alabaster trembled. The princess flinched.

“My wine was too strong,” she said, voice quivering. “I should not have let you drink so much. Go sleep now. When you wake, ask me anything. I will answer.”

She led me up the stairs.

“I don’t wonder the roar startled you,” she said. “It startled me. For a moment, I feared she had escaped. But that is impossible.”

Yet I knew—somehow—that the roar had come from the white leopardess. And it was meant for *me*, not her.

She smiled as she left me at my door. But as she turned, I saw it—fear, deep in her beautiful eyes.
