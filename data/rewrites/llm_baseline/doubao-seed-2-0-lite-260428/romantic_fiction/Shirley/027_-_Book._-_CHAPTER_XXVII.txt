Shirley Keeldar and her uncle Mr. Sympson were two sparks that could never be hammered into the same anvil, not if you heated them to a white hot fury. He was short-tempered and tyrannical, convinced every person under his roof ought to bend to his will like wet willow; she was spirited, free to her bones, ready to snap back at the first whiff of control. He measured every man and every marriage in hard, cold pounds sterling, while Shirley walked through the world chasing the kind of bright, romantic fire most people had long forgotten existed.

He had not come down to Yorkshire on a casual visit. His mission was as clear as the ink on the legal papers he kept tucked in his coat pocket: marry his niece off to a "suitable" man, hand over all the tedious responsibility of managing her and her fortune to a proper husband, and wash his hands of her wild, willful presence for good.

The only flaw in his plan was that Shirley had never, not even when she was seven years old and he tried to make her wear scratchy woollen stockings that itched, agreed that his definition of "suitable" matched hers at all. No one could say if she would break that streak now, on the most important choice of her whole life.

The test arrived far sooner than he expected.
Mr. Wynne came calling, formally requesting Shirley's hand for his son Samuel Fawthrop Wynne.
"Decidedly suitable! The most proper match imaginable!" Mr. Sympson boomed, slamming his fist lightly on the oak parlour table, so sure of his victory he was already mentally tallying the combined estates. "A fine unencumbered property, solid wealth, impeccable family connections. This will happen."
He summoned Shirley to the panelled oak parlour, shut the door behind them, laid the offer out flat, stated his own unshakable approval, and demanded she give her consent immediately.

She refused outright.
"No. I will not marry Samuel Fawthrop Wynne."
"I must have a reason!" he sputtered. "In every conceivable way he is more than worthy of you."
She stood straight on the hearth, as pale as the white marble mantel slab behind her, her eyes wide and bright, no trace of a smile in them.
"And I ask in what universe that young man could ever be worthy of me?"
"He has twice your fortune! Twice your common sense! Equal standing, equal respectability!"
"Had he a hundred times my money, I would never vow to love him."
"Explain your objections at once."
"He has spent years wallowing in cheap, despicable, commonplace profligacy. That is the first reason I will not so much as look at him."
"Miss Keeldar, you shock me to the core!"
"That behaviour alone sinks him so far below me there is no way he could climb back up to my level. His intellect could not reach the lowest standard I respect – that is the second barrier. His views are narrow, his feelings are dull, his tastes are coarse, and his manners are vulgar, through and through."
"The man is a respected, wealthy gentleman! To turn him down is pure arrogance on your part."
"I refuse, point blank. Stop pestering me about this subject. I forbid you to bring it up again."
"Do you ever intend to marry, or do you intend to live out your days a lonely old maid?"
"I deny you have any right to ask me that question at all."
"Do you expect some titled peer of the realm to come begging for your hand?"
"I doubt there is a single peer alive on this green earth I would condescend to marry."
"If there was madness in our family line, I would swear you had lost your mind. Your eccentricity and conceit are one step away from full blown frenzy."
"Maybe before this conversation ends, you will see me leap right over that line."
"I have no doubt of it! Wild, impractical girl! Take warning! I will not let you sully our family name with a cheap, disgraceful misalliance!"
"Our name? Uncle, my name is Keeldar. Not Sympson."
"Thank the good Lord you are not! But be on your guard. I will not be made a fool of."
"What, under all the laws of common sense and the land, could you possibly do if I chose a husband you disapproved of?" she asked, tilting her chin.
"Take care! Take care!" he warned, his voice and his raised hand both trembling.
"Why? What power do you hold over me? What reason have I to fear you?"
"Take care, madam!"
"I will be very careful, Mr. Sympson. Before I marry anyone, I am resolved to esteem him, admire him, and love him – with my whole heart."
"Preposterous drivel! Unbecoming! Unwomanly!"
"I know I speak a language you have never heard before. I do not care if you understand me or not."
"And if this grand great love of yours lands on a beggar?"
"I would never fall for a beggar. Mendicancy is not worthy of respect."
"On a low clerk, a play actor, a hack writer, or – or –"
"Go on, Mr. Sympson. Or what?"
"Some grubby literary scrub, some shabby, whinging artist."
"I have no taste for grubby, whinging nobodies. But I do love literature and the arts. I wonder how your precious Fawthrop Wynne would fit that life? He cannot write a simple note without spelling half the words wrong, he only reads the sporting papers, and he was the dunce of Stilbro' Grammar School!"
"Unladylike language! Great God, what is to become of her?" he cried, throwing his hands up to the ceiling.
"I will never stand at the altar beside Sam Wynne, that is certain."
"What is to become of her? Why are the laws not stricter, that I could force her to listen to reason?"
"Calm yourself, uncle. Even if Britain was a serfdom and you were the Czar, you could not force me to marry that man. I will write to Mr. Wynne myself. Do not trouble yourself over this matter any further."

Fortune is said to be fickle, but often her whims run in the same familiar pattern. By this point, Shirley Keeldar and her vast fortune had made such a stir across the district that three more formal marriage offers landed at Fieldhead not long after Mr. Wynne's. Each was pressed on her in turn by her uncle, and each was turned down flat – even offers from men of unblemished character and great wealth. Everyone in the village gossiped about what the stuck-up heiress could possibly be waiting for, calling her insolently fastidious.

At last, the gossips thought they had cracked the secret, and her uncle was absolutely certain he had. The revelation changed how he saw his niece entirely, and he adjusted his whole behaviour around her overnight. Fieldhead had grown far too tense for both of them to share; Shirley's soft-spoken aunt could never smooth over their fights, and her two cousins Gertrude and Isabella would huddle whispering in their dressing room for hours, chilled to the bone with decorous fear if they were ever left alone with their bold, unapologetic cousin. But now, Mr. Sympson was appeased, and the whole household grew quiet and peaceful again.

Not far away lay the village of Nunnely, with its ancient church, its sprawling forest, and its crumbling monastic ruins. It also held Nunnely Priory, a far grander stately home than any in Briarfield or Whinbury – and it had a baronet, a title no neighbouring parish could claim. For years the title had only been a name on a deed; the young new Sir Philip Nunnely had been living far away in Scotland, completely unknown on his Yorkshire estate.

While Shirley and her friends were staying at the fashionable seaside watering place of Cliffbridge that summer, they had been introduced to him. They ran into him again and again on the sands, along the cliff paths, and even at the public dances in the assembly rooms. He seemed lonely, his manners so unpretending he came off as shy rather than proud. He did not condescend to join their company – he was relieved to find it.

Shirley, who could make fast friends with any unassuming person, struck up an easy acquaintance with him immediately. They walked and talked for hours, and once her aunt and cousins even joined him for a sail on his yacht. She liked him for his kindness and modesty, and was charmed by how easy it was to make him laugh.

There was just one small flaw – no friendship is without one. Sir Philip had a literary streak. He wrote poetry: sonnets, stanzas, long meandering ballads. Shirley thought he was far too fond of reciting his own work, that the rhymes were clunky, the meter off, the metaphors stale and utterly lacking fire. She always winced when he brought up his poems, and did her best to steer the conversation elsewhere.

He would beg her to walk the moonlit pier with him at night, just to read her his longest ballad. He would lead her to quiet, hidden benches where they could hear the soft crash of the surf on the shore, the scent of rose gardens drifting around them and the tall cliffs rising at their backs, then pull out his latest batch of sonnets and read them in a voice trembling with emotion. He did not seem to realise he was writing rhyme, not poetry. Shirley would sit with her eyes downcast, her face tight with quiet mortification, because she did not want to hurt the feelings of this sweet, good man.

She tried again and again to gently wean him off his all-consuming obsession with the Muses. It was his only monomania; on every other subject he was perfectly sensible, and she was happy to talk to him about anything else. He would ask her about his neglected Nunnely estate, and she would talk for hours describing the ancient priory, the wild wooded park, the old grey church and the tiny hamlet, urging him to come home to Yorkshire, to gather his tenants around him in his ancestral halls.

To her surprise, Sir Philip took her advice exactly. At the end of September, he moved back to Nunnely Priory for good.
He came to call at Fieldhead soon after, and his first visit was far from his last. After making the rounds of all the neighbouring estates, he declared that no house felt half so warm and welcoming as the grey old Briarfield manor, even if it was far smaller and plainer than his own grand Priory.

Soon, sitting in Shirley's panelled parlour with other visitors around them no longer felt enough. He wanted her alone, walking the pastures, beside the slow still streams. Shirley avoided those private tête-à-tête rambles, so he organised large parties for her to explore his forests, the remote woodlands split by the Wharfe river, the green valleys watered by the Aire.

His constant, obvious attention made Shirley the most talked about woman in the whole district. Her uncle's greedy soul dreamed up a glorious future for her. He was already imagining the day he could casually drop references to his "nephew the baronet" to all his acquaintances, one leg propped lazily over the other. His niece was no longer a mad, reckless girl to him – she was the most sensible, superior woman he knew. He treated her with absurd deference, jumping to open and shut doors for her, getting red in the face and giving himself headaches bending to pick up her dropped gloves, her handkerchief, any small thing she happened to leave behind. He made silly little jokes about how women's wit was sharper than men's wisdom, making vague apologies for all the stupid mistakes he had made in how he handled her. He was as giddy as a rooster strutting on top of a dung heap.

Shirley watched all his silly manoeuvres and ignored his hints completely, acting like she barely understood what he was hinting at. When he outright accused her of being Sir Philip's chosen bride, she said she believed he liked her, and that she liked him back. She had never imagined a man of rank, an only son adored by his mother and spoiled by his sisters, could be so kind, and on the whole, so sensible.

Time proved Sir Philip truly did care for her. He had found in her that strange, rare charm that Mr. Hall the curate had once commented on. He sought her company more and more, until being near her felt like an indispensable habit. Around that time, a strange restlessness settled over Fieldhead – there were unspoken hopes, haggard quiet anxieties drifting through some of the upstairs rooms. Some of the household wandered the quiet surrounding fields at dusk, their nerves stretched tight with waiting.

Sir Philip was no man to be dismissed. He was gentle, if not dazzlingly intellectual, he was kind and sensitive. Shirley could never say of him what she had said of Sam Wynne: that his feelings were blunt, his tastes coarse, his manners vulgar. He had a real, if unrefined, love of the arts, and every inch an English gentleman in his deportment. His title and fortune were far grander than her own.
At first Shirley had made a few gentle, good-natured jokes about his looks – he was boyish, his features plain, his hair sandy, his stature unremarkable. But she never let anyone else tease him about it. She would flare up if anyone else made a rude comment, insisting he had a lovely face, and a heart far more valuable than a handsome profile or flowing locks. She still made the occasional soft joke about his bad poetry, but she would never let anyone else mock him for it.

Things had reached such a point that Mr. Yorke pulled the tutor Louis Moore aside one afternoon to speak plainly.
"Your brother Robert, now – he's either a fool or he's mad, far as I can see. Two months ago I would have sworn he had his whole future with Shirley Keeldar in the palm of his hand. And what does he do? Runs off to London and holes up there for weeks on end. By the time he comes back, he'll find he's been checkmated. There's a tide in the affairs of men, Louis – if you take it at the flood it leads you right to fortune, but if you let it slip it never comes back. I'd write to Robert if I were you, remind him of that."
"Robert had feelings for Miss Keeldar?" Louis asked, acting like the idea was completely new to him.
"I put the idea in his head myself, and he could have made it work. She liked him. More than liked him. I've seen her face flush, change colour, just at the sound of his name. Write to the boy, tell him to come home. He's a finer gentleman than that little soft baronet, after all."
"Does it not strike you, Mr. Yorke, that for a penniless tutor, a man with nothing to his name, to aspire to marry a rich heiress is presumptuous? Contemptible?" Louis asked, his jaw tight.
"Oh, if you want to go in for those lofty, over-refined sentiments I have nothing more to say. I'm a plain practical man. If Robert is happy to hand that grand prize over to that soft little aristocrat, that's his choice. At his age, in his position, I never would have let a baronet, a duke, a prince, steal my sweetheart without a fight. But you tutors are always such solemn fellows – talking to you is almost as stuffy as talking to a parson."

Flattered and fussed over as Shirley was in those weeks, she had not been spoiled. Everyone in the village had long stopped linking her name with Robert Moore, and her own apparent indifference to the absent miller seemed to confirm they had forgotten each other. But Shirley had not quite forgotten him – if not with love, then at least with deep, lingering interest. That much was clear when Louis Moore, Robert's quiet, stern tutor brother, came down with a sudden fever, and Shirley began showing him far more attention than she ever had before. She had always treated him with a strange mix of cool, distant reserve and meek, almost schoolgirlish respect – one minute sweeping past him in all the dignity of a wealthy heiress and soon-to-be Lady Nunnely, the next flushing and lowering her eyes like a shy student caught breaking the rules when he met her gaze.

Louis had caught the fever visiting the poor cottagers of the district, which he did regularly alongside Mr. Hall and his young pupil Henry Sympson. He tried to fight the illness silently for a day or two, before he was forced to take to his bed.
One evening he was tossing restlessly on his thin mattress, little Henry limping faithfully beside his bed to watch over him, when there was a soft, light tap on the door – too gentle to be Mrs. Gill the housekeeper or the housemaid.
"How is Mr. Moore tonight?" a low voice whispered from the dark gallery outside.
"Come in and see for yourself," Henry called back.
"Is he asleep?"
"I wish he could sleep. Come in, Shirley."
"He won't like me disturbing him."
But she stepped across the threshold anyway, and Henry, seeing her hesitate, took her hand and pulled her over to the bedside.
The shaded lamp did not show her fully, but it revealed she was dressed for the party downstairs. The guests were gathered in the drawing room, including Sir Philip Nunnely, and Shirley had slipped away from the other ladies to visit her old tutor. Her white silk dress, her bare fair arms and neck, the thin gold chain around her throat that trembled as she breathed, glowed softly in the dim sickroom light. Her face was gentle, pensive, her voice soft as a sigh.
"Mr. Moore, how are you feeling tonight?"
"I was never very ill. I am better now."
"I heard you were thirsty. I brought you some grapes. Won't you try just one?"
"No, thank you. But thank you for remembering me."
"Just one." She plucked a single ripe berry from the full basket she held and held it to his lips. He shook his head, turning his flushed face away.
"What else can I bring you? You don't want fruit, but your lips are dry. What would you like to drink?"
"Mrs. Gill brings me toast and water. That is all I want."
Silence stretched for a few minutes.
"Do you have pain?"
"Very little."
"What made you sick?"
Silence.
"I wonder what caused this fever? What do you think brought it on?"
