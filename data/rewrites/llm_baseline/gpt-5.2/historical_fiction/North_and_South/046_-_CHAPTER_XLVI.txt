Margaret had been ready long before the hour, as if haste could keep sorrow from overtaking her. There was time enough, even with her cloak folded and her gloves drawn on, to let the tears come—small, quiet drops that she brushed away before any eye could witness them; and then, when footsteps sounded in the passage, to lift her face into brightness as one lifts a lamp against dark.

Her last fear was the old, nervous fear of being too late, of missing the train—as though the world might punish her by leaving her stranded between one life and another. But they were in time. When she found herself seated opposite Mr. Bell, the carriage already loosening its hold on the station, she breathed as if the tightest hand had been taken off her chest.

They flew southward past the stations she had once counted with impatience, and now watched as a kind of rosary. The country unrolled itself in warm light, the air clear as strained broth, and the villages lay as if steeped—tiled roofs ruddy as baked clay, not the northern slate that always seemed to drink up colour and give none back. Pigeons hovered above peaked gables and, settling, ruffled their neck-feathers until each glossy fibre caught the sun. Even the stations had a different temper: no sharp bustle, no clanging hurry, no pressed faces and elbows; only here and there a man lounging, hands in pockets, listening to the quiet with the patient absorption of someone for whom sound was an element, not an irritant. In the North, Margaret remembered, the very rails had seemed to speak—metal on metal, shriek and hammer and the hard staccato of industry; here the wheels ran as if on softer nerves, and the pauses between noises were long enough to be inhabited.

She let herself drift into that half-waking dream where the fields looked like old poems remembered—farms lying golden and still, heat trembling above them like a veil, each hedge-row a line of script. Then the voice in the carriage, the guard’s cry, the decisive slowing: it was the place where they must leave the train and take a fly to Helstone.

At once the sweetness sharpened. Every mile after that was laden, as a kitchen is laden when preserves are opened—scents that are pleasure and pain together, because they are the past made physical. She would not have spared herself one association; yet each one made her heart cry out for days that could not be called back. The last time she had gone along this road, the sky had been dim and she herself dimmer, but her father and mother had been beside her—human warmth in the cold season. Now the road was drenched in summer sunlight, and the familiar trees stood in their old glory, as if nature had never learned bereavement. It hurt her that the world could look so unchanged.

Mr. Bell, knowing enough not to thrust words where they would bruise, let the silence keep her company.

They drove up to the Lennard Arms, half farm-house, half inn, set a little apart from the road with the confident air of one who need not beg custom. The building faced the village green. Before it stood an immemorial lime-tree with a bench round its girth; somewhere within the thickness of its leaves hung the grim escutcheon of the Lennards, like an old family story that refused to be forgotten. The door stood open, but no one hurried to greet them. When the landlady appeared at last—so quietly that they might indeed have carried off half the house—she welcomed them as if they were expected guests, and made her apology at once.

“It’s hay-time,” she said, not at all ashamed, but full of that competent pride by which country women rule the seasons. “The men’s provisions had to be sent a-field. I’ve been packing the baskets and never heard your wheels, not on the turf road.”

Then a bar of sunshine slipped across Margaret’s face, and Mrs. Purkis’s eyes widened as if she had seen a familiar saint step down from a painted glass.

“Why, bless me! It’s Miss Hale, Jenny!” she cried, running to the door to call her daughter. “Come here, come directly—it’s Miss Hale!”

And she took Margaret’s hand with motherly fondness, as though three years were nothing beside village memory.

“And how are you all? How’s the Vicar and Miss Dixon? The Vicar above all! God bless him! We’ve never ceased to be sorry he left.”

Margaret’s throat closed. She touched the deep black of her mourning, and the one word she could get out was the word that held her whole childhood.

“Papa.”

Mrs. Purkis turned sharply to Mr. Bell, dread entering her face like a shadow crossing a threshold.

“Surely, sir—it’s never so? There was a gentleman here in the spring—maybe last winter—told us a deal of Mr. Hale and Miss Margaret; and he said Mrs. Hale was gone, poor lady. But never a word of the Vicar being ailing!”

“It is so,” Mr. Bell said gently. “He died quite suddenly, when on a visit to me at Oxford. He was a good man, Mrs. Purkis, and many of us might be thankful for as calm an end. Come, Margaret, my dear. He was my oldest friend, and she’s my god-daughter. We thought we’d come down and see the old place; and I know you can give us comfortable rooms and a capital dinner. You don’t remember me, I see, but my name is Bell. Once or twice, when the parsonage was full, I’ve slept here—and tasted your good ale.”

“To be sure!” Mrs. Purkis exclaimed, half laughing at herself through the shock. “I ask your pardon, sir, but you see I was taken up with Miss Hale. Let me show you to a room, Miss Margaret, where you can take off your bonnet and wash your face. It’s only this morning I plunged some fresh-gathered roses head downward in the water-jug—for thought I, perhaps someone will be coming, and there’s nothing so sweet as spring-water scented by a musk rose or two. To think of the Vicar being dead! Well—to be sure, we must all die; only that gentleman said he was quite picking up after his trouble about Mrs. Hale’s death.”

Mr. Bell nodded to her with the air of a man who had already begun to plan comfort as a kind of medicine.

“Come down to me, Mrs. Purkis, when you’ve seen to Miss Hale. I want a consultation about dinner.”

In the little bedchamber, the casement window was almost filled by rose and vine; the green pressed close as if eager to enter. Margaret pushed the branches aside, and leaning out could see, above the trees, the tops of the parsonage chimneys—faint lines she knew by heart, like a tune whose ending one can always anticipate.

“Aye,” Mrs. Purkis said, smoothing the bed and sending Jenny for towels that smelled of lavender and sun, “times is changed, miss. Our new Vicar has seven children, and he’s building a nursery ready for more, just where the arbour and tool-house used to be. He’s had new grates put in, and a plate-glass window in the drawing-room. He and his wife are stirring people, and have done a deal of good—at least they say it’s doing good; if it were not, I should call it turning things upside down for very little purpose.

“The new Vicar is a teetotaller, miss, and a magistrate; and his wife has a deal of receipts for economical cooking, and is for making bread without yeast; and they both talk so much, and both at a time, that they knock one down as it were. He’ll be after the men’s cans in the hay-field, and peeping in; and then there’ll be an ado because it’s not ginger-beer, but I can’t help it. My mother and my grandmother before me sent good malt liquor to hay-makers, and took salts and senna when anything ailed them; and I must e’en go on in their ways, though Mrs. Hepworth does want to give me comfits instead of medicine—which, as she says, is a deal pleasanter, only I’ve no faith in it. But I must go, miss—though I’m wanting to hear many a thing. I’ll come back to you before long.”

Downstairs, Mr. Bell had arranged a rustic luncheon with a scholar’s relish for honest provisions: strawberries—still warm at their hearts from the field—set beside a bowl of thick cream; a loaf of brown bread, its crust dark and faintly sweet; and a jug of milk. A Stilton stood by for his own private consolation, and a bottle of port that caught the light like old rubies. Margaret ate because she must, but the strawberries, bursting cool and fragrant, seemed to carry within them the very taste of Helstone summers; it was like swallowing a memory.

Afterwards they went out to walk, and for a moment stood uncertain, as people do when every path tempts with its own history.

“Shall we go past the vicarage?” Mr. Bell asked.

“No—not yet. We will go this way, and make a round so as to come back by it,” Margaret answered, as if delaying could soften the blow.

Here and there changes pricked her: old trees felled the previous autumn, a rough cottage disappeared, another built in its place, tidier and more respectable, but lacking that crooked, familiar look of endurance. She grieved over each absence like a friend not met at the usual door. They came to the spot where she and Mr. Lennox had once sketched. The white, lightning-scarred trunk of the venerable beech—where they had sat among its roots—was gone; the old man who had lived in the ruinous cottage was dead; the cottage itself had been pulled down. In its stead there was a small garden, primly fenced, on the very ground that had once been shaded.

“I did not think I had been so old,” Margaret said at last, and turned away with a sigh that seemed to come from deeper than her lungs.

“Yes,” Mr. Bell replied. “It’s the first changes among familiar things that make such a mystery of time to the young. Afterwards we lose the sense of the mysterious. I take changes as a matter of course. The instability of all human things is familiar to me; to you it is new and oppressive.”

“Let us go on to see little Susan,” Margaret said, drawing him up a grassy roadway that led under the shadow of a forest glade.

“With all my heart—though I’ve not an idea who little Susan may be. But I have a kindness for all Susans, for simple Susan’s sake.”

“My little Susan was disappointed when I left without wishing her good-bye,” Margaret said. “It has been on my conscience ever since. I gave her pain which a little more exertion might have prevented. But it is a long way. Are you sure you will not be tired?”

“Quite sure—that is, if you don’t walk so fast. Here there are no views that can give one an excuse for stopping to take breath. You would think it romantic to be walking with a person ‘fat and scant o’ breath’ if I were Hamlet, Prince of Denmark. Have compassion on my infirmities for his sake.”

“I will walk slower for your own sake. I like you twenty times better than Hamlet.”

“On the principle that a living ass is better than a dead lion?”

“Perhaps so. I don’t analyse my feelings.”

“I’m content to take your liking me without examining too curiously into the materials it’s made of. Only we need not walk at a snail’s pace.”

“Very well. Walk at your own pace, and I will follow. Or stop still and meditate, like the Hamlet you compare yourself to, if I go too fast.”

“Thank you. But as my mother has not murdered my father, and afterwards married my uncle, I shouldn’t know what to think about—unless it were balancing the chances of our having a well-cooked dinner or not. What do you think?”

“I am in good hopes. She used to be considered a famous cook, as far as Helstone opinion went.”

“But have you considered the distraction of mind produced by all this haymaking?”

Margaret felt the tenderness beneath his levity. He was trying to keep her from turning too fiercely back upon the past; yet she would have chosen silence, if she had not known it ungrateful to wish him away.

They reached the cottage where Susan’s widowed mother lived. Susan was not there; she had gone to the parochial school. Margaret’s face fell before she could prevent it, and the poor woman read it and began apologising, as if education were an offence.

“Oh, it is quite right,” Margaret said quickly. “I am very glad to hear it. I might have thought of it. Only she used to stop at home with you.”

“Yes, she did; and I miss her sadly,” the mother answered, hands busy with some small task as if motion could keep grief from settling. “I used to teach her what little I knew at nights. It were not much, to be sure. But she were getting such a handy girl, that I miss her sore. But she’s a deal above me in learning now.” And she sighed—part pride, part loneliness.

“I’m all wrong,” growled Mr. Bell. “Don’t mind what I say. I’m a hundred years behind the world. But I should say the child was getting a better and simpler and more natural education stopping at home, helping her mother, and learning to read a chapter in the New Testament every night by her side, than from all the schooling under the sun.”

Margaret did not wish the discussion to swell in front of the woman. She turned instead, seeking safer ground.

“How is old Betty Barnes?”

“I don’t know,” the woman replied shortly. “We’se not friends.”

“Why not?” Margaret asked, remembering how she had once been the village peacemaker.

“She stole my cat.”

“Did she know it was yours?”

“I don’t know. I reckon not.”

“Well—could not you get it back again when you told her it was yours?”

“No. For she’d burnt it.”

“Burnt it!” both Margaret and Mr. Bell cried.

“Roasted it,” the woman corrected, as if that made it less terrible.

It did not. By questioning—gentle at first, then urgent—Margaret drew out the story: Betty Barnes, led by a gipsy fortune-teller to lend her husband’s Sunday clothes on promise of their return before he should miss them, became frantic when they did not come. In her fear of her husband’s anger she resorted to an old savage charm—the belief that the cries of a cat, boiled or roasted alive, could compel the powers of darkness to bring about the executioner’s wishes. The woman telling it believed in it; her indignation lay not in the horror, but in the injustice that her own cat had been chosen.

Margaret tried to reason with her, tried to lead her through the chain of plain facts, so that the truth might emerge like a clear broth from muddied water; but though the woman would admit each separate circumstance, she could not—or would not—draw them together. In the end she only repeated, stubborn and bewildered, that it was cruel—very cruel indeed—but that there was nothing like it for getting what one wished; she had heard it all her life. Margaret left her, sick at heart, the sunlight in the trees suddenly seeming a mockery.

“You are a good girl not to triumph over me,” Mr. Bell said when they were out of earshot.

“How? What do you mean?”

“I own I am wrong about schooling. Anything rather than have that child brought up in such practical paganism.”

“Oh—I remember. Poor little Susan! I must go and see her. Would you mind calling at the school?”

“Not a bit. I am curious to see something of the teaching she is to receive.”

They went on with fewer words. The path led them through bosky dells where leaves stirred in soft harmonies, and birds called from depth to depth; the place had an old, balanced sound, as if it knew how to keep peace in its own acoustic boundaries. Yet even this could not charm away the shock. Margaret could not forget the flat manner in which cruelty had been recounted—the want of imagination that meant a want of sympathy; the deadening of feeling that came, not from loud factory din, but from a lexicon of superstition that made suffering into a tool.

As they emerged from the forest onto the village green where the school stood, the noise of children rose like a hive—warm, busy, human. The door stood wide open. They entered. A brisk lady in black seemed to be everywhere at once—here correcting, there arranging, there smiling—her steps and voice making quick, decisive punctuation in the murmur. She welcomed them with the air of a hostess, and Margaret remembered, with an inward pang, how her own mother had once received visitors at the school—more softly, more languidly, as if she too belonged to the quiet of the place.

Margaret knew at once this was the present Vicar’s wife. She would have drawn back had she been able, but she conquered the impulse and advanced. Bright glances of recognition met her; half-smothered whispers ran: “It’s Miss Hale.” The name reached Mrs. Hepworth, and her manner warmed—yet, to Margaret’s discomfort, warmed with something that felt like condescension.

The lady held out her hand to Mr. Bell.

“Your father, I presume, Miss Hale. I see it by the likeness. I am sure I am very glad to see you, sir, and so will the Vicar be.”

Margaret explained, stammering, and spoke of her father’s death. Even as she did so, she wondered how her father could ever have borne to revisit Helstone, had he come under such an assumption. She scarcely heard Mrs. Hepworth’s words in response; Mr. Bell answered for her, while Margaret’s eyes searched for old faces.

“Ah!” Mrs. Hepworth exclaimed suddenly, turning with energetic kindness. “I see you would like to take a class, Miss Hale. I know it by myself. First class—stand up for a parsing lesson with Miss Hale.”

Margaret, who had come with a sentimental hunger, not an inspecting mind, felt herself caught; but the sight of the eager faces—children her father had baptised—went through her like a touch. She sat down, took the book, and for a moment forgot herself in tracing the changed outlines of the girls’ faces. Susan’s hand found hers; Margaret held it for a minute or two, secretly, while the class searched for their places. Meanwhile Mrs. Hepworth, as near as a lady could, kept Mr. Bell by the button and poured out an explanation of the Phonetic system, including a conversation she had had with the Inspector, as though the Inspector’s words were a kind of scripture.

Margaret bent over the page. The hum of voices, the scrape of shoes, the little coughs and whispers, the rustle of paper—sounds that had once been the daily music of her life—brought old times up around her until her eyes filled and she could scarcely see the print.

Then there was a pause. A girl stumbled over the word “a,” uncertain what to call it.

“A—an indefinite article,” Margaret said gently, as one speaks from habit.

“I beg your pardon,” said Mrs. Hepworth, all attention at once. “But we are taught by Mr. Milsome to call ‘a’ an—who can remember?”

“An adjective absolute,” cried half-a-dozen voices together.

Margaret sat abashed, feeling suddenly ignorant in the very place that had once been her ground. The children knew more than she did. Mr. Bell turned away and smiled.

Margaret spoke no more through the lesson. When it ended, she went quietly among a few old favourites and talked to them. They were growing out of childhood into large, awkward girlhood; and just as their faces altered and their voices deepened, so she too was fading from their recollection with her three years’ absence. She was glad to have seen them; yet sadness threaded itself through the pleasure, as a darker spice sometimes runs through a sweet dish and changes its whole flavour.

School over, the summer afternoon was still young. Mrs. Hepworth proposed that Margaret and Mr. Bell should accompany her to the parsonage and see—she almost said “improvements,” but substituted “alterations,” with a momentary caution.

Margaret did not care to see alterations that jarred against her memory; yet she longed to stand once more in the old place, even though she trembled for the pain she knew would be there.

In truth the parsonage was so altered inside and out that the pain was less keen than she had feared. It was not the same place, and that very difference blunted the blow. The garden—once so trim that even a stray rose-leaf seemed a disorder—was strewn with children’s things: a bag of marbles, a hoop, a straw-hat jammed down upon a rose-tree as if the living branch were only a peg, crushing a long tender stem loaded with flowers that in former days would have been trained as carefully as one tends a prized herb. The little square matted hall was filled with the same signs—merry, healthy rough childhood, loud in presence even when silent.

“Ah!” Mrs. Hepworth said, smiling with brisk apology, “you must excuse this untidiness, Miss Hale. When the nursery is finished I shall insist upon a little order. We are building a nursery out of your room, I believe. How did you manage, Miss Hale, without a nursery?”

“We were but two,” Margaret answered. “You have many children, I presume?”

“Seven. Look here! we are throwing out a window to the road on this side. Mr. Hepworth is spending an immense deal of money on this house; but really it was scarcely habitable when we came—for so large a family as ours, I mean, of course.”

Every room had been changed. The greatest change of all was in what had once been Mr. Hale’s study, where green gloom and delicious quiet had helped his meditative habit—perhaps had made him, as Margaret sometimes thought, fitter for thought than action. Now a new window opened onto the road. Mrs. Hepworth pointed out its advantages with satisfaction: from it her husband could see the wandering sheep of his flock straggling toward the tempting beer-house, unobserved as they hoped, but not unobserved in reality. The active Vicar kept his eye on the road even while composing his most orthodox sermons; hat and stick hung ready to snatch up before he sallied forth after parishioners, who needed quick legs if they hoped to take refuge in the “Jolly Forester” before the teetotal magistrate caught them.

The household, Margaret saw, was brisk and loud-talking, kind-hearted, and not troubled with much delicacy of perception. Mr. Bell, piqued by Mrs. Hepworth’s exultation in the “superior sense” of each change, expressed admiration with such pointed politeness that Margaret feared the lady must perceive he was laughing at her. But no: she took it all in perfect good faith.

As they walked slowly back to the inn, Margaret could not help speaking.

“Don’t scold, Margaret,” Mr. Bell said at once, before she had done. “It was all because of you. If she had not shown you every change with such evident exultation, I could have behaved well. But if you must go on preaching, keep it till after dinner, when it will send me to sleep and help my digestion.”

They were both tired; Margaret more than she admitted even to herself. She had meant to ramble again among the woods and fields that lay so close to the home of her childhood, but her limbs were heavy and her spirit heavier. And somehow Helstone had not been all—had not been exactly—what she had expected. There was change everywhere: slight, but pervading, like a new seasoning that alters an old dish so that you can taste the difference even when you cannot name it.

Households had changed by absence, death, marriage, by the quiet, continual turning of days and months and years that carry us from childhood to youth, through manhood to age, until at last we drop like fruit—fully ripe—into the patient earth. Places had changed too: a tree gone here, a bough cut there, letting in a long ray of light where once there had been shade; a road trimmed and narrowed; the green straggling pathway beside it enclosed and cultivated. A great improvement, people said. Margaret only sighed for the old picturesqueness—the old gloom—the grassy wayside. She sat by the window on the little settle, watching the gathering shades of night soften the outlines of the green. The evening sounds came one by one—the far-off call across the fields, the nearer footfall, the hush that settled when work ceased—and the quiet seemed to belong to her childhood more faithfully than any wall did.

Mr. Bell slept soundly after his unusual exercise. At last he was roused by the tea-tray, brought in by a flushed country-girl who had plainly found variety from waiting by helping in the hay-field that day; and the scent of warm leaves and crushed grass clung to her as she moved, as if summer itself had entered with the crockery.
