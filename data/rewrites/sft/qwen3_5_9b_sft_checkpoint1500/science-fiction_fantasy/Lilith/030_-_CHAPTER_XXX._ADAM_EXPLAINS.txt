"We must stay vigilant," Adam declared, his voice tight with urgency, "or she will outmaneuver us once more. She would trick the Elect themselves!"

"And how exactly do we maintain vigilance?" I asked.

"By every possible means," he replied. "Her fear breeds hatred for her own creation, and she is within this house, intent on destruction. To her, the birth of offspring signifies the death of parents; every new generation is the adversary of the last. She views her daughter as a breach in her dam, a channel through which her inherent immortality is rapidly draining away. Since nearly the moment of birth, she has pursued the girl with unrelenting enmity. Yet, despite her schemes, a colony of children has emerged in the region she claims as her own—a group led by spirit and shielded by that very daughter."

Adam paused, his gaze distant. "My Eve yearned for the child. Had we been fit to guide her, she would have embraced the girl as a mother to her firstborn. Instead, we failed, sending her into the wilderness where we lost track of her for ages. But she was divinely nurtured, playing with young angels, untouched by care until she discovered a baby in the woods. That primal maternal instinct awoke within her. Since then, she has gathered children one by one, though her heart remains insatiable. Her family is her total obsession, raising them with a devotion never seen before. Her authority over them is absolute, yet she is unaware of it, revealing it only through constant watchfulness and service. She has forgotten the time before them, believing she herself emerged from the forest as the first of the clan."

"You have spared the lives of her and their enemies," Adam continued, turning back to me. "Consequently, your life now belongs to them and to her. The Princess was marching toward their annihilation, but as she crossed that stream, vengeance caught up with her. She would have perished had you not intervened. You did save her. Without your aid, she would now be ravaging the Little Ones, daring to cross the stream again. However, there remained a path to the blessed colony through the world of three dimensions. By slaying her former physical form, she had severed access to that realm, unable to re-enter it unless in direct contact with someone of the colony. You provided the opportunity. Never in her countless years had such a chance arisen. Her hand, with a feather-light touch, rested upon your muffled feet with every step you climbed. Within that small chamber, she watches, waiting for the slightest chance to depart."

"She knows nothing of the door!" I protested, though doubt gnawed at my confidence. "She cannot possibly know how to open it!"

"Hush!" Adam whispered, raising a hand to silence me. "She hears through everything. You must proceed immediately to my wife's cottage. I will remain here to guard her."

"Let me go to the Little Ones!" I cried.

"Beware, Mr. Vane. Go to my wife and heed her instructions."

His counsel offered no comfort. Why hasten to face delay? If not to protect the children, what purpose served going at all? Even now, I trusted him only enough to question, not to obey.

"First, Mr. Raven, why did you lock her in that specific place?" I demanded. "When I fled your home that night, I ran straight into that closet!"

"The closet is neither closer to our cottage nor farther from it than any other point."

"But how," I pressed, struggling with the illogical, "can you remove an entire book from that same door when I perceived only a fragment? The other portion you just described protruded into your library. When you return it to the shelf, will it not protrude again? Do not the two locations, where parts of the same volume exist simultaneously, lie adjacent? Can one part of the book occupy space, while the other exists nowhere?"

"I regret I cannot elucidate this for you," Adam answered gently. "There is no capacity within you to comprehend it. It is not merely inexplicable to you; its very nature is inapprehensible. I grasp it only partially myself. Yet, you constantly experience phenomena you do not, and indeed cannot, understand. You believe you do, but your familiarity blinds you to the surprise. You accept them not through understanding, but necessity: they are there, bound by unavoidable relations to you. Truthfully, no man understands anything. His first faltering step toward knowledge is admitting his ignorance. To matters like these, you are unused, so you feign comprehension. Neither I nor anyone else can help you understand, but perhaps I can assist you in believing."

He approached the closet door, emitted a low whistle, and stood listening. Moments later, I heard—or seemed to hear—the soft whir of wings. Looking up, I saw a white dove perch briefly atop the shelves above the portrait, descend to Mr. Raven's shoulder, and rest its head against his cheek. Their heads moved in silent conversation; I heard nothing. Before I could shift my gaze, the bird vanished, and Mr. Raven returned to his seat.

"Why did you whistle?" I asked. "Sound here cannot travel there!"

"You are correct," he replied. "I whistled so you would know I summoned her. It was not the sound, but the meaning behind it that reached her. There is not a minute to lose. You must go!"

"I will go at once!" I responded, moving toward the exit.

"You will spend tonight at my inn!" he stated, not as a query, but with gentle authority.

"My heart lies with the children," I countered. "But if you insist—"

"I do insist. Nothing else will achieve anything. I will accompany you as far as the mirror and ensure your departure."

He rose. Suddenly, a shock vibrated through the closet, sounding like the leopardess hurling herself against the heavy oak door. I glanced at my companion.

"Haste!" he urged.

Before we reached the library door, a blood-curdling yowl followed, mixed with the scraping of claws against hard oak. I hesitated, turning halfway.

"To let her lie there alone," I murmured, "with that terrible wound!"

"Nothing will heal that wound," he sighed. "It must consume her heart! Annihilation is no death for evil. Evil persists where it resides until goodness supplants it. Only then is evil truly slain."

I fell silent as a strange, incomprehensible sound overtook us.

"If she breaks free!" I shouted.

"Hurry!" he rejoined. "I will rush down the moment you leave. I have already disturbed the arrangement of the mirrors."

We sprinted, arriving at the wooden chamber gasping. Mr. Raven grabbed the chains, adjusted the hood, realigned the mirrors, and stepped beside me before the standing pane. Already, the mountain range began to emerge from the mist.

Between us, driving us apart, darted the massive, spotted form of the leopardess, yowling like a demon. She leaped through the mirror as easily as through an open window, instantly settling into a low, swift gallop.

A look of dread passed across my face, and I sprang through to pursue her. He followed at a leisurely pace.

"You need not run," he called back. "You cannot catch her. This is our path."

As he spoke, he turned in the opposite direction.

"She possesses more sorcery at her fingertips than I care to acknowledge!" he added quietly.

"We must do what we can!" I said, running forward, but sickness overwhelmed me as I watched her shrink in the distance. I halted and turned back.

"Doubtless we must," he agreed. "Yet my wife has warned Mara, and she will fulfill her duty. You must sleep first; you have given me your word!"

"I do not intend to break my promise," I insisted. "But surely sleep does not precede action! Action must come before repose!"

"A man accomplishes nothing for which he is unprepared," he said. "Behold—did I not tell you Mara would act?"

I looked where he pointed and saw a white speck moving at an acute angle relative to the leopardess's line.

"There she is!" he cried. "The spotted beast is mighty, but the white one is stronger!"

"I have witnessed their fights; the contest did not seem decisive regarding strength."

"How can eyes that have never slept judge such things? The Princess did not admit defeat—that she never does—but she fled! When she finally concedes that her last hope is lost, when kicking against the goad becomes too difficult, then her day will dawn! Come, come! He who cannot act must hasten to sleep!"
