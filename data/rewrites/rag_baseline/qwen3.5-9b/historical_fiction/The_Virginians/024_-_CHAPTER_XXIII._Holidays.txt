The illusion shattered quietly, not with a crash, but with the slow deflation of a balloon left in the wind. Mrs. Lambert's reverie ended. Miss Theo and her mother were forced, in the silent theater of their own minds, to admit the truth: the hero was merely a man. They spoke little of it, yet the understanding between them was complete, a shared language of affection that required no translation. To soothe the girl's quiet disappointment, the mother offered a tenderness that went beyond words, a physical warmth pressed against her daughter's cheek.

"Never mind, my dear," the whisper of the kiss suggested. "Our hero has turned out to be but an ordinary mortal. And none such is good enough for my Theo. Thou shalt have a real husband ere long, if there be one in England. Why, I was scarce fifteen when your father saw me at the Bury Assembly, and while I was yet at school, I used to vow that I never would have any other man. If Heaven gave me such a husband—the best man in the whole kingdom—sure it will bless my child equally, who deserves a king if she fancies him!"

Perhaps Mrs. Lambert, knowing the age of the Prince of Wales and the extent of his handsomeness, harbored a private fantasy that he too would ride past her gate, stumble from his horse, and be taken in to heal, thereby earning a regiment for Martin Lambert and winning the heart of Theo.

Colonel Lambert and his second daughter, Miss Hetty, stood on the opposite shore of this emotional river, laughing with a scornful disbelief. Match-making was the Colonel's trade; Mrs. Lambert was addicted to novels, weeping over them with such assiduity that her eyes seemed permanently swollen. No coach passed the gate without her expecting a husband to alight and ring the bell. Miss Hetty, however, wielded her tongue with a saucy frequency that bordered on the grotesque. She introduced Prussia and Persia into their conversation with an abominable pertness, asking if the current King of Prussia was called the Shaw or the Sophy, and calculating the distance from Ispahan to Saxony—a war her father was currently mapping. She produced the Persian Tales from her mother's closet and laid them on the parlor table where the family sat.

"I would not marry a Persian prince," Hetty declared, preferring a gentleman who possessed only one wife at a time. She dubbed the Virginian guest "Theo's prince" and asked her mother if she wished Hetty to claim the other visitor, the black prince, for herself. She rallied her sister and mother unceasingly on their sentimentalities, refusing to stop until she had provoked them into anger, at which point she would burst into tears, kiss them violently one after another, and coax them back into good humor.

Simple Harry Warrington knew nothing of these jokes, tears, quarrels, or hymeneal plans. He was the innocent occasion around which they spun, unaware that a hundred Parthian arrows were being shot at him. Did he hear the mention of Shaws or Sophys? Likely he thought a Sophy was a lady, and the notion that a man with a great black beard bore such a name would have seemed to him the height of absurdity.

We often drop like stones into a still pool, perfectly compact and cool, little realizing the flutter and excitement we create beneath the surface, disturbing the fish and frightening the ducks. How should Harry know the effect his sudden appearance produced in this quiet, sentimental family? He thought well enough of himself on many points but remained diffident regarding women, belonging to that age when young gentlemen require encouragement and introduction, raised in modest, primitive relations with the other sex. Thus, Hetty's jokes circled the lad like summer gnats; he minded them no more than insects buzzing near a wall. It was not stupidity, as Hetty believed, but simplicity—he was too occupied with his own honest affairs to consider others. Who among us does not remain ignorant, self-satisfied, and blind to the tragedies, comedies, and intrigues playing out in friends' drawing-rooms? While the sisters sat combing their flowing ringlets or talking in the great bed, according to the fashion of the day, how could Harry know he held such a large share in their thoughts?

Three days after his arrival, his new hosts walked with him through the fine park of my Lord Wrotham, wandering freely. On a sheet of water, they encountered swans, which the young ladies fed with bread. As the birds approached, Hetty cast a queer look at her mother and sister, then glanced at her father, standing nearby in a red waistcoat, honest and happy.

"Mamma's swans are something like these, papa," Hetty said.

"What swans, my dear?" the mother asked.

"Something like, but not quite. They have shorter necks than these, and are scores of them on our common," Hetty continued. "I saw Betty plucking one in the kitchen this morning. We shall have it for dinner, with apple-sauce and—"

"Don't be a little goose!" Miss Theo interrupted.

"And sage and onions. Do you love swan, Mr. Warrington?"

"I shot three last winter on our river," the Virginian replied. "Ours are not such white birds as these—they eat very well, though."

The simple youth had no idea that he himself was an allegory at that moment, and that Miss Hetty was narrating a fable concerning him. In some exceedingly obscure Latin work, I have read that long before Virginia was discovered, other peoples were equally dull of comprehension.

This was a premature sentiment on Miss Theo's part—that little tender flutter of the bosom she felt upon first beholding the Virginian, so handsome, pale, and bleeding. It was not the great passion she knew her heart could feel. Like the birds, it had awakened and begun to sing at a false dawn. *Hop back to thy perch, and cover thy head with thy wing, thou tremulous little fluttering creature! It is not yet light, and roosting is as yet better than singing.* Soon, anon, will come morning, and the whole sky will redden, and you shall soar up into it and salute the sun with your music.

One phrase, some thirty-three lines back, perhaps caught the eye of the fair and suspicious reader: "Three days after his arrival, Harry was walking with..." If he could walk—which he did perfectly well—what business had he to linger at Oakhurst instead of accompanying Lady Maria Esmond on the Pantiles at Tunbridge Wells? His shoulder was set; his health was entirely restored. He lacked even a change of coats, owing his raiment to the Colonel. Surely a young man in such a condition had no right to delay, bound by every tie of duty, convenience, love, and relationship, including a gentle heart waiting for him and, finally, a washerwoman, to depart for Tunbridge. Why did he stay, unless he loved one of the young ladies—and we assert he did not?

Could it be that he did not wish to go? Had the gracious reader understood the meaning of the mystic 'S' with which the last chapter commenced, depicting Sinbad the Sailor surmounted by that odious old man of the sea? What if Harry Warrington were that sailor, and his fate that choking, deadening, inevitable old man? What if, for two days past, he had felt those knees throttling him round the neck? If his fell aunt's purpose was answered, and if his late love was killed as dead by her poisonous communications as fair Rosamond was by her royal rival, then perhaps Hero was lighting the lamp and preparing supper while Leander sat comfortably with another party, never thinking of taking to the water. Since that coward's blow struck in Lady Maria's back by her own relative, surely kind hearts must pity her ladyship. I know she has faults—yes, and wears false hair—but a woman in distress commands pity. Are we to laugh at her because of her years? Between her old aunt and her unhappy delusion, Lady Maria Esmond was having no pleasant time at Tunbridge Wells. No one protected her; Madam Beatrix had her all to herself. Lady Maria was poor, hoping for money from her aunt, holding secrets the old woman knew and brandished over her. I am quite melted, growing soft-hearted as I think of her. Imagine her alone, a victim to that old woman! Paint to yourself that antique Andromeda, with her rich flowing head of hair falling over her shoulders, chained to a rock on Mount Ephraim and given up to that dragon of a Baroness! Succour, Perseus! Come quickly with thy winged feet and flashing falchion! Perseus is not in the least hurry. The dragon has her will of Andromeda for day after day.

Harry Warrington, who would not have allowed his dislocated and mended shoulder to keep him from hunting, remained day after day contentedly at Oakhurst, finding the kindly folks who welcomed him increasingly to his liking. Perhaps he had never, since his grandfather's death, been in such good company. His lot had lain amongst fox-hunting Virginian squires, with whose society he had put up contentedly, riding their horses, living their lives, and sharing their punch-bowls. The ladies of his own and mother's acquaintance were very well bred, decorous, and pious, no doubt, but somewhat narrow-minded.
