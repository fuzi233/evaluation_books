I wheeled and went after the spotted leopardess, catching only the streak of her flank as she skimmed up the hill and vanished through the palace gate like a thrown shadow.

When I reached the entrance-hall, the princess was just lifting the robe she had dropped in her haste and drawing it round her. The blood on her neck had stopped running; it had stiffened and dulled in the night wind, as if the air itself had baked it dry. She saw me, and anger flashed—quick as a knife catching moonlight—then she turned her face away. A moment later she looked back with a smile she tried to make easy, as if she were laying a cloth over a cracked table.

“I have met with a small accident!” she said lightly. “Happening to hear the cat-woman was again in the city, I went down to send her away. But she had one of her horrid creatures with her: it sprang upon me, and had its claws in my neck before I could strike it!”

She shivered, and the movement pulled the torn skin so that my own throat tightened in sympathy. I knew she lied—her voice had the smoothness of something over-stirred, all surface and no body—but the wounds were real. And the set of her mouth, the strained brightness of her eyes, dragged up the memory of her face as I had seen it in the cave: not merely frightened, but hungry in some inward way that made fear look like appetite.

My heart accused me that I had let her be alone with her enemy. Perhaps the pity showed in my face.

“Child of folly!” she said, with a little, broken laugh that tried to be mocking and almost became tender. “—not crying, surely! Wait for me here; I am going into the black hall for a moment. I want you to get me something for my scratches.”

But I followed her close. Out of my sight I feared her.

The instant she crossed the threshold, the air altered—as if I had stepped from an orchard into a mill-house. A buzzing began, not loud, but thick: a swarm of low voices with no single tongue I could catch. Then, one portion after another, the vast dimness inside started to kindle, as though a narrow beam travelled restlessly from place to place, touching now a cluster, now a corner, then slipping away.

Group after group would flare into clear sight for a breath, then sink back into the general dusk, while elsewhere something else brightened. The sound never resolved into words; it stayed at that uneasy pitch where listening becomes labour. It made me think of a kitchen full of talk that has been muffled by a closed door—only here the door was the skull.

In those wandering illuminations I saw actions I knew. I had been in them, or watched them. So had the princess. And now—present in every scene as it brightened—I saw her.

The skull-headed dancers stamped the grass in the forest-hall, their feet keeping time with a rhythm that was all bone and no warmth: there, at the door, was the princess looking in. The fight raged in the Evil Wood: there was the princess urging it, her posture eager as a cook leaning over a pot that has begun to boil over. Yet I was close behind her all the while, and she stood motionless, head bowed on her breast, as if she were only listening.

The confused murmur continued; colours and shapes churned and shifted; the ray kept hunting.

At last it settled on the hollow in the heath. There was the princess, walking up and down, gathering at the air with desperate hands, trying to wrap the vapour round her like a shawl, and failing, and trying again. The sight made the hair prickle along my arms—not because it was strange, but because it was familiar, as if a thing long unspoken had suddenly been named.

Then I saw what startled me into a colder kind of attention: the old librarian came up to her. He stood a moment looking at her as one who knows the true weight of a word in its own dying dialect. She fell.

Her limbs forsook her and fled, as if her body were only a story told badly and could no longer hold its parts together. Her shape collapsed into nothing. And with the fall of that likeness—a mere showing, an eidolon—there came a wild shriek that rang round the place, and the princess herself, who had stood like a statue before me, fell heavily and lay still.

I turned at once and went out. Not again would I seek to restore her.

I stood trembling beside the cage. The silence outside seemed to have its own sound after that buzzing chamber, as if the palace courtyards were an old landscape where the birds had been driven off by some distant industry. Then I knew what I had been inside: the black ellipsoid was in the brain of the princess. The thought sat in me like a foreign seed.

I saw the tail of the leopardess quiver once.

While I was still trying to steady my breathing, I heard the princess’s voice beside me, as if she had never fallen at all.

“Come now,” she said. “I will show you what I want you to do for me.”

She led the way into the court. I followed, dazed, in a compliance I could not account for—like a man who keeps walking because his feet remember the path even when his mind has forgotten why.

The moon stood near the zenith. Her silver seemed brighter than the gold of the absent sun, and it laid a clean glaze over stone, leaf, and skin, as if everything had been rinsed and set out to cool.

The princess brought me through the trees to the tallest—standing in the centre like a mast in the middle of a dark sea. It was not quite like the rest: its branches drew their ends together at the top, making a clump that from beneath looked like a fir-cone, tightly closed, keeping something hidden.

She stood close under it, gazing up, and spoke as if talking to herself, as one might mutter over a recipe that will not come right.

“On the summit of that tree grows a tiny blossom which would at once heal my scratches! I might be a dove for a moment and fetch it, but I see a little snake in the leaves whose bite would be worse to a dove than the bite of a tiger to me!—How I hate that cat-woman!”

The hatred in her voice sharpened the air. It had a hiss of its own, a sibilant edge that made me imagine the snake whether it was there or not.

She turned quickly to me, and for an instant her face wore one of her sweetest smiles, as if she were offering bread. “Can you climb?”

The smile vanished with the brief question, and her features fell into sadness and pain so practiced it might have been a garment she knew how to drape. I ought to have left her to suffer; yet the way her hand rose to her wounded neck—careful, as if seasoning a dish too hot to touch—went straight through my resolve.

I considered the tree. All the way up to the branches were projections on the stem like the remnants on a palm where fallen leaves have left their scars—steps for a barefoot man, if the bark did not bite.

“I can climb that tree,” I answered.

“Not with bare feet!” she returned at once, and for once her concern sounded like something more than strategy. “A fall from the top would kill you!”

“So would a bite from the snake,” I said—though I confess I did not truly believe there was any snake. It seemed too neat a story, too well placed, like a garnish meant to distract from a thin broth.

“It would not hurt YOU!” she replied. “—Wait a moment.”

In my haste to follow the leopardess I had left my sandals in my room. I was used to going barefoot; the soles of my feet had learned the language of stone and earth. Still, she would not allow it.

She tore from her garment the two wide borders that met in front—thick, embroidered strips that looked as if they had once been meant for ceremony, not for binding. She knelt on one knee, and made me put first my left foot, then my right, upon the cloth. She wrapped and tied them about my feet and ankles with the quick precision of someone who knows how to truss a bird so it will roast evenly.

“You have left the ends hanging, princess,” I said, watching the dangling lengths.

“I have nothing to cut them off with; but they are not long enough to get entangled,” she replied.

I turned to the tree and began to climb.

At first the bark felt warm from the day’s stored heat, as if the tree were a great oven cooling. But after sundown in Bulika, though the cold is gentler than in other parts—especially about the sexton’s cottage—the chill comes in layers, like thin sheets laid one after another. As I climbed I began to feel very cold; colder as I rose, and coldest of all when I got among the branches. My shivering made my grip uncertain, and my hands and feet seemed to lose their own sense, as if nerve and muscle had been numbed by some unseen frost.

There was hardly any wind; the branches did not sway. Yet as I neared the summit I became aware of a peculiar unsteadiness: every branch I stepped on, every limb I grasped, felt as if it were on the point of giving way—not bending like wood, but failing like an intention.

The moon laid patches of pale shine—silvery foam—on the rugged bole. High above, a little rush went through the top with a murmurous sound as of water falling softly into water. That sound drew me on with a thirst I had not expected. In a world where the ear is battered by false clamour and the gaudy selling of noises for music, the promise of honest water—water that does not advertise itself—has a power like bread on a fasting day.

When my head rose above the upper branches and I came into open moonlight, I began to look about for the tiny blossom—some pale star of a flower that could close flesh like a stitch.

At that instant I was drenched from head to foot.

The next, as if I had been plunged into stormy water, I was flung about wildly and felt myself sinking. There was no splash I could name, no clean roar of a river: only a smothered, swallowing rush, like being inside a great pot when the lid is clapped down and the boil turns violent.

Tossed up and down, hurled this way and that, rolled over and over, checked, rolled the other way and tossed up again, I sank lower and lower. I gasped, gurgled, choked—my mouth filling with cold, tasteless water that nonetheless carried a strange absence, as if it had forgotten the mineral words of earth.

At last I struck a solid bottom.

“I told you so!” croaked a voice in my ear.
