Those who have long observed the stately, clerical avian known as the rook may have noted a peculiar habit: when winging their way homeward toward twilight in a sedate flock, two rooks will suddenly detach themselves from the group. They retrace their flight for a considerable distance, hovering and lingering until they convey to mere mortals the fanciful notion that it is of some occult significance to the body politic that this artful pair should pretend to have severed ties with the collective.

Similarly, once service concluded in the ancient Cathedral with its square tower, and the choir shuffled out again, several venerable figures of rook-like appearance dispersed into the evening. Yet, two of these retracted their steps, walking together through the echoing Close. The day was waning, and so was the year. The low sun hung fiery yet cold behind the ruins of the monastery, while the Virginia creeper climbing the Cathedral wall had already showered half its deep-red leaves onto the pavement. Rain had fallen that afternoon, sending a wintry shudder through the small pools on the cracked, uneven flagstones and through the giant elm trees as they shed a gust of tears. Fallen leaves lay strewn thickly across the ground. Some, in a timid rush, sought sanctuary within the low arched Cathedral door; but two men emerging from the interior resisted them, kicking the intruders back outside. One of the pair locked the door with a substantial key, while the other departed swiftly, clutching a folio music-book.

"Was that Mr. Jasper?" asked Tope.
"Yes, Mr. Dean."
"He has stayed late."
"Yes, Mr. Dean. I remained for him, your Reverence. He seemed taken a little poorly."
"Say 'taken,' Tope—not 'took'—to the Dean," the younger rook interjected in a low tone, offering a subtle correction as if to imply: "One may offer bad grammar to the laity or the humble clergy, but certainly not to the Dean."

Mr. Tope, Chief Verger and Showman, accustomed to commanding respect from excursion parties, declined with silent loftiness to acknowledge that any suggestion had been tendered to him.

"And when, precisely, and how was Mr. Jasper taken? For, as Mr. Crisparkle has remarked, it is better to say 'taken'—taken—" repeated the Dean. "When and how was Mr. Jasper *Taken*?"
"Taken, sir," Tope murmured deferentially.
"Poorly, Tope?"
"Why, sir, Mr. Jasper was that *breathed*—"
"I wouldn't say 'that breathed,' Tope," Mr. Crisparkle interposed with the same precise touch. "Not English—to the Dean."
"Breathe to that extent," the Dean remarked condescendingly, not unflattered by this indirect homage, "would be preferable."

"Mr. Jasper's breathing was so remarkably short"—thus discreetly did Mr. Tope navigate around the sunken rock—"when he entered, that it distressed him mightily to retrieve his notes: which was perhaps the cause of his having a kind of fit shortly thereafter. His memory grew *DAZED*." Mr. Tope shot this word out, keeping his eyes fixed on the Reverend Mr. Crisparkle, daring him to improve upon it. "And a dimness and giddiness crept over him, as strange as ever I witnessed: though he didn't seem to mind it particularly himself. However, a little time and a little water brought him out of his *DAZE*." Mr. Tope repeated the word and its emphasis with the air of someone declaring, "As I *have* made a success, I shall make it again."

"And has Mr. Jasper gone home quite himself, has he?" asked the Dean.
"Your Reverence, he has gone home quite himself. And I am glad to see he has his fire kindled, for it is chilly after the wet. The Cathedral had both a damp feel and a damp touch this afternoon, and he was very shivery."

They all three looked toward an old stone gatehouse crossing the Close, with an arched thoroughfare passing beneath it. Through its latticed window, a fire shone out upon the fast-darkening scene, casting shadows over the pendent masses of ivy and creeper covering the building's front. As the deep Cathedral bell struck the hour, a ripple of wind passed through these vines at a distance, like a ripple of the solemn sound humming through tomb and tower, broken niche, and defaced statue in the pile close at hand.

"Is Mr. Jasper's nephew with him?" the Dean asked.
"No, sir," replied the Verger, "but he is expected. There is his own solitary shadow betwixt his two windows—the one looking this way, and the one looking down into the High Street—drawing his own curtains now."

"Well, well," said the Dean, with a sprightly air of breaking up the little conference. "I hope Mr. Jasper's heart is not too much set upon his nephew. Our affections, however laudable, in this transitory world, should never master us; we should guide them, guide them. I find I am not disagreeably reminded of my dinner by hearing my dinner-bell. Perhaps, Mr. Crisparkle, you will, before going home, look in on Jasper?"
"Certainly, Mr. Dean. And tell him that you had the kindness to inquire how he was?"
"Ay; do so, do so. Certainly. Wished to know how he was. By all means. Wished to know how he was."

With a pleasant air of patronage, the Dean cocked his quaint hat as far as a Dean in good spirits might allow and directed his comely gaiters toward the ruddy dining-room of the snug old red-brick house where he was currently residing with Mrs. Dean and Miss Dean. Mr. Crisparkle, Minor Canon—fair and rosy, perpetually pitching himself head-foremost into all the deep-running waters of the surrounding countryside; early riser, musical, classical, cheerful, kind, good-natured, social, contented, and boy-like; Mr. Crisparkle, Minor Canon and good man, lately "Coach" upon the chief Pagan high roads, but since promoted by a patron (grateful for a well-taught son) to his present Christian beat—betook himself to the gatehouse on his way home to his early tea.

"Sorry to hear from Tope that you have not been well, Jasper."
"Oh, it was nothing, nothing!"
"You look a little worn."
"Do I? Oh, I don't think so. What is better, I don't feel so. Tope has made too much of it, I suspect. It's his trade to make the most of everything appertaining to the Cathedral, you know."
"I may tell the Dean—I call expressly from the Dean—that you are all right again?"
The reply, accompanied by a slight smile, was: "Certainly; with my respects and thanks to the Dean."

"I'm glad to hear that you expect young Drood."
"I expect the dear fellow every moment."
"Ah! He will do you more good than a doctor."
"More good than a dozen doctors. For I love him dearly, and I don't love doctors, or doctors' stuff."

Mr. Jasper was a dark man of some twenty-six, with thick, lustrous, well-arranged black hair and whiskers. He looked older than he was, as dark men often do. His voice was deep and good, his face and figure were good, but his manner was a little sombre. His room was also a little sombre, and it may have had its influence in forming his manner. It was mostly in shadow. Even when the sun shone brilliantly, it seldom touched the grand piano in the recess, the folio music-books on the stand, the bookshelves on the wall, or the unfinished picture of a blooming schoolgirl hanging over the chimneypiece. Her flowing brown hair was tied with a blue ribbon, and her beauty was remarkable for a quite childish, almost babyish touch of saucy discontent, comically conscious of itself. (There was not the least artistic merit in this picture, which was merely a daub; but it was clear that the painter had made it humorously—one might almost say, revengefully—like the original.)

"We shall miss you, Jasper, at the 'Alternate Musical Wednesdays' tonight; but no doubt you are best at home. Good-night. God bless you! 'Tell me, shepherds, tell me; tell me-e-e, have you seen (have you seen, have you seen, have you seen) my-y-y Flo-o-ora-a pass this way!'"

Melodiously good Minor Canon the Reverend Septimus Crisparkle delivered himself in musical rhythm as he withdrew his amiable face from the doorway and conveyed it downstairs. Sounds of recognition and greeting passed between the Reverend Septimus and somebody else at the stair-foot. Mr. Jasper listened, started from his chair, and caught a young fellow in his arms, exclaiming: "My dear Edwin!"
"My dear Jack! So glad to see you!"
"Get off your greatcoat, bright boy, and sit down here in your own corner. Your feet are not wet? Pull your boots off. Do pull your boots off."
