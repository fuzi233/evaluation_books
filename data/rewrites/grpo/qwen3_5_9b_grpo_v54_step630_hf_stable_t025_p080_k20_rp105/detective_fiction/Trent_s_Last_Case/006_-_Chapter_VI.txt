Mr. Bunner on the Case

"Calvin C. Bunner, at your service," the newcomer corrected himself, his tone sharpening with a sudden, practiced deference as he withdrew an unlit cigar from his mouth. He was accustomed to the British tendency toward stiff formality with strangers, and Trent's brisk introduction clearly rattled him. "You must be Mr. Trent," he continued, recovering his composure. "Mrs. Manderson mentioned you earlier. Good morning, Captain." Mr. Murch returned the greeting with a curt nod. "I was ascending to my quarters when I detected an unfamiliar voice in here, prompting me to investigate. Perhaps you suspected I was eavesdropping?" Bunner chuckled lightly. "No, sir; I merely overheard a fragment regarding a pistol—likely this one—and that was sufficient cause for concern."

Mr. Bunner was a slender, somewhat diminutive figure with a shaven, pallid, and angular face that possessed a boyish quality, framed by large, dark, piercing eyes. His dark hair was parted strictly in the center. His lips, usually occupied by a cigar, remained perpetually slightly parted, betraying a look of restless eagerness. Yet, whenever he smoked or chewed tobacco, this tension vanished, revealing the consummate coolness and sagacity of the Yankee businessman he truly was.

A native of Connecticut, Bunner had entered a brokerage firm upon graduating college, quickly catching the eye of Manderson, whose transactions with the firm he frequently managed. The Colossus observed him for some time before offering him the position of private secretary. Bunner was the epitome of corporate efficiency: trustworthy, far-sighted, methodical, and precise. While Manderson could have secured many men with such virtues, he chose Bunner specifically for his speed, discretion, and a singular, almost supernatural instinct for the movements of the stock market.

Trent and the American regarded one another with a cool, assessing gaze. Both seemed satisfied with the impression gained. "I was informed," Trent said pleasantly, "that my discovery of a pistol capable of killing Manderson holds little significance. I am told it is a favored weapon among your people and has gained considerable popularity here."

Bunner extended a bony hand and retrieved the pistol from its case. "Indeed, sir," he remarked, handling the weapon with casual familiarity. "The captain is correct. Back home, we call this a 'Little Arthur,' and I wager there are duplicates in the pockets of a hundred thousand men right now. Personally, I find it too light," he continued, mechanically reaching beneath his jacket to produce a grim-looking firearm. "Feel this, Mr. Trent—it is loaded. Now, regarding the 'Little Arthur': Marlowe purchased it shortly before our arrival this year to please the old man. Manderson insisted it was absurd for a gentleman to be unarmed in the twentieth century. So, he procured whatever was offered, apparently without consulting me. It is a fine piece, I concede, squinting along the sights. Marlowe struggled with it initially, but I have instructed him over the past month, and he has practiced until he is competent. However, he never developed the habit of carrying it. For me, it is as natural as wearing trousers. I have carried one for years, anticipating threats against Manderson. And now," Bunner concluded with a sigh, "they have succeeded while I was absent. Gentlemen, I must depart. I am heading to Bishopsbridge. There is much to attend to, and I require dispatching a volume of cables sufficient to strangle a cow."

"I must leave as well," Trent replied. "I have an appointment at the 'Three Tuns' inn."

"Allow me to provide a lift in the automobile," Bunner offered warmly. "My route passes directly by that establishment. Captain, are you traveling in that direction? No? Then come, Mr. Trent, and assist me in retrieving the vehicle. The chauffeur is incapacitated, and we shall manage nearly everything ourselves, barring the removal of the accumulated grime."

With his characteristic measured drawl, Bunner guided Trent downstairs and through the residence to the garage situated at the rear. It stood at a slight distance from the house, offering a cool refuge from the midday sun's glare.

Bunner showed no haste in preparing the car. He offered Trent a cigar, which was accepted; for the first time, Bunner lit his own. He then settled onto the footboard of the vehicle, his thin hands clasped between his knees, and scrutinized Trent intently.

"Here is a matter of some importance, Mr. Trent," he said after a pause. "There are details I can share that may prove useful. I am familiar with your record. You are a sharp individual, and I enjoy conducting business with those of similar acumen. I am unsure if I have assessed your colleague correctly, but he strikes me as a mutt. I would answer any inquiries he possesses the intelligence to pose—I have done so, in fact—but I feel disinclined to impart my theories without his explicit request. Understood?"

Trent nodded. "That sentiment is common among those encountering our police force," he replied. "It is the official demeanor, I presume. However, let me assure you, Murch is far from what you perceive. He is one of the shrewdest officers in Europe. He lacks rapidity of thought, yet he possesses certainty. His experience is vast. My strength lies in imagination, but I guarantee you that in police work, experience vastly outweighs intuition."

"Outweigh nothing!" Bunner retorted crisply. "This is no ordinary case, Mr. Trent. Permit me to offer one reason. I believe the old man sensed an impending threat. Furthermore, I suspect it was something he believed he could not evade."

Trent pulled a crate opposite Bunner's position on the footboard and sat down. "This sounds like business," he said. "Lay out your thoughts."

"I speak thus due to the shift in the old man's demeanor over the last few weeks. You likely know, Mr. Trent, that he was a man of strict self-control. That was true. I have always regarded him as the coldest, hardest head in commerce. His calm was lethal—I have witnessed nothing comparable. I knew Manderson better than anyone else. I accompanied him in the endeavors he truly cherished. I suspect I understood him better than his wife, poor soul; better than Marlowe, who rarely saw Manderson in his office during critical matters; and better than any of his associates."

"Did he possess friends?" Trent interjected.

Bunner glanced at him sharply. "I perceive you are being positioned against me," he noted. "Properly speaking, I would say no. He had numerous acquaintances among the powerful, individuals he encountered daily, even engaging in yachting or hunting expeditions. Yet, I do not believe there was ever a single man to whom Manderson opened a corner of his heart. However, my point is this: several months ago, the old man began to resemble a version of himself I had never seen—gloomy and sullen, as if eternally brooding over some malady he could not resolve. This persisted without respite, whether in the city or at home; he acted as if a heavy burden weighed upon his mind. It was not until a few weeks prior to his death that his restraint began to falter. Let me tell you this, Mr. Trent"—the American placed his bony claw upon the other's knee—"I am the sole individual privy to this truth. With everyone else, he was merely morose and dull; but when alone with me in his office, or wherever we worked together, if the slightest error occurred, by George! he would explode with rage. In this very library, I have witnessed him opening a letter containing a slight displeasure, ripping it apart and behaving like a wild Indian, declaring he wished the writer were there to be dealt with, and so forth, until it became pitiful. I have never seen such a transformation. Moreover, for a week preceding his death, Manderson neglected his work, a first in my experience. He refused to answer letters or cables, despite affairs appearing to crumble overseas. I presumed this anxiety, whatever its nature, had exhausted his nerves. Once I advised him to consult a physician, to which he responded by sending me to hell. Fewer than I have seen this side of him. If he erupted in the library, for instance, and Mrs. Manderson entered, he would instantly revert to his usual cold composure."

"Do you attribute this to a secret anxiety, a fear that someone intended harm to his life?" Trent asked.

The American nodded.

"I suppose," Trent resumed, "you have considered the possibility of a mental breakdown due to overstrain. That is the initial inference your account suggests to me. Furthermore, is this not a common occurrence among your prominent businessmen in America? That is the prevailing impression from the newspapers."

"Do not allow them to deceive you with such nonsense," Bunner said earnestly. "It is only those who amassed wealth too rapidly and failed to sustain it who lose their minds. Consider all our truly great men—the individuals approaching Manderson's stature: have you ever heard of one losing their senses? They do not. Believe me. I acknowledge they say every man has a 'loco point,'" Bunner added reflectively, "but that does not signify genuine, confirmed insanity; it merely denotes a personal eccentricity... such as a hatred of cats... or my own inability to tolerate any fish food."

"Well, what was Manderson's?"

"He was replete with them, the old man. There was his disdain for the unnecessary fuss and luxury that the wealthy typically ignore. He held no use for expensive trifles or ornaments. He refused to have anyone perform trivial tasks for him; he detested having servants trailing behind him unless summoned. Although Manderson was as meticulous regarding his attire as any man I knew, and his shoes—well, sir, the sum he expended on footwear was sinful—in spite of this, I tell you, he never employed a valet. He never desired anyone to touch him. Throughout his life, no one ever shaved him."

"I have heard of that," Trent remarked. "What do you think was the reason?"

"Well," Bunner answered slowly, "it was the Manderson temperament, I suppose; a disposition of general suspicion and jealousy.

"They claim his father and grandfather were identical... Like a dog clutching a bone, acting as though the entire creation was plotting to steal it. He did not genuinely believe the barber would attempt to saw off his head; he simply felt there was a possibility, and thus took no risks. Furthermore, in business, he was always convinced others were after his bone—which was often true, though not always. The consequence was that the old man became the most cautious and secretive operator in the financial world; this contributed significantly to his success.... But this does not equate to lunacy, Mr. Trent; not by a long way. You ask if Manderson was losing his mind before his death. I say I believe he was merely worn out by worrying over something and losing his nerve."

Trent smoked thoughtfully. He wondered how much Bunner knew of the domestic strife within his chief's household and decided to probe gently. "I understood he had difficulties with his wife."

"Certainly," Bunner replied. "But do you suppose something of that magnitude would dismantle Sig Manderson? No, sir! He was far too substantial a man to be undone by such trivial worries."

Trent gazed at the young man with half-incredulous eyes. Yet, beneath their shrewdness and intensity, he perceived a massive innocence. Bunner genuinely believed a serious marital breach to be a minor inconvenience for a man of Manderson's stature.

"What exactly was the trouble between them?" Trent inquired.

"You cannot search me," Bunner replied briefly. He puffed on his cigar. "Marlowe and I have discussed it often, yet we could never arrive at a solution. Initially, I suspected," Bunner said in a lower voice, leaning forward, "that the old man was disappointed and vexed because he had anticipated a child; but Marlowe informed me that the disappointment likely ran the other way. His theory seems sound, I gather, derived from something said by Mrs. Manderson's French maid."

Trent looked up quickly. "Célestine!" he said, his thought flashing, "So that was her point!"

Bunner misinterpreted his glance. "Do you not think I am exposing a man, Mr. Trent?" he asked. "Marlowe is not of that nature. Célestine merely developed an affection for him because he speaks French like a native, and she would constantly solicit his gossip. French servants differ greatly from the English in this regard. And regardless of servant status," Bunner added with emphasis, "I do not see how a woman could introduce such a subject to a man. But the French confound me." He shook his head slowly.

"But returning to what you stated earlier," Trent said. "You believe Manderson was living in terror of his life for some period. Who should threaten it? I remain entirely in the dark."

"Terror—I am uncertain," Bunner replied meditatively. "Anxiety, if you prefer. Or suspense—that aligns more closely with my conception. The old man was difficult to terrify, anyway; moreover, he was not taking precautions—he was actively avoiding them. It appeared more as though he was inviting a swift conclusion, assuming there is truth to my hypothesis. Why, he would sit in that library window at night, gazing out into the darkness, his white shirt a perfect target for any assassin. As for who should threaten his life, well, sir," Bunner said with a faint smile, "it is certain you have not lived in the States. Consider the Pennsylvania coal hold-up alone; there were thirty thousand men, with women and children to support, who would have seized the opportunity to drill a hole through the man who forced them to starve or submit to his demands.
