To those who live in retreat, whose lives have settled into the quiet isolation of schools or secluded homes, there comes a sudden and prolonged erasure from the memories of friends living in the wider world. Without apparent cause, and often following a period of lively correspondence, a silent pause descends. Letters cease; visits stop; tokens of remembrance vanish. An unbroken, unexplained blank takes hold.

There are always excellent reasons for such lapses, if only the isolated one understood them. While the hermit stagnates in his cell, the world outside whirls in the vortex of life. For him, the intervening months drag endlessly, with clocks seeming frozen and hours moving like weary travelers resting at mile markers. Yet for his absent friends, that same interval teems with events, filled with urgency.

A sensible hermit learns to swallow his thoughts and lock away his emotions during these winters of solitude. He accepts that destiny may require him to mimic the dormouse: curling into a tight ball, retreating into a crevice of life's wall, and submitting quietly to the drifting snow that seals him in ice until spring returns. Perhaps the snow-cave opens, and the sun warms him again; perhaps not. The frost may take root in his heart forever, leaving only bones for crows to find when spring arrives. Even then, it is right. One must accept mortality and the eventual journey "as well soon as syne."

For me, the seven weeks following that eventful evening at the theatre were as blank as seven sheets of white paper. No words were written upon them; no visits, no tokens.

By the middle of this silence, I began to fancy that something terrible had happened to my friends at La Terrasse. The midpoint of a blank is always a clouded spot for the lonely; his nerves ache with the strain of long expectation. Doubts, previously repelled, gather into a heavy mass and roll back upon him with vindictive force. Night becomes unfriendly; sleep refuses him. Strange terrors and struggles harass his bed, led by a sinister band of nightmares promising calamity and the dread of total abandonment. Poor wretch! He tries his best to endure, but remains pale and wasting despite his efforts.

Towards the end of these seven weeks, I finally admitted what I had jealously denied for six: that these silences were inevitable. They were the result of circumstance, the decree of fate, a part of my lot. Above all, I resolved never to question their origin or utter a murmur against their painful sequence. Naturally, I did not blame myself for suffering; I thanked God for possessing a true sense of justice, avoiding the foolish extravagance of self-accusation. I knew logically that my friends were blameless, and my heart acknowledged it too. Still, it was a rough, heavy road to travel, and I longed for better days.

I tried various expedients to sustain existence: I began an elaborate piece of lacework, studied German diligently, and forced myself to read the driest, thickest books in the library. In all my efforts, I was as orthodox as I knew how to be. Was there error somewhere? Very likely. But the result was as if I had gnawed a file to satisfy hunger or drunk brine to quench thirst.

My hour of torment was the post-time. I knew it too well, trying vainly to cheat myself of that knowledge. I dreaded the rack of expectation and the sick collapse of disappointment that daily preceded and followed the well-recognized ring of the bell.

Animals kept in cages, starved to the verge of famine, await their food as I awaited a letter. Oh! To speak the truth, dropping the false calm I tried to maintain, I endured bitter fears and pains, strange inward trials, miserable defections of hope, and intolerable encroachments of despair in those seven weeks. Despair came so near sometimes that her breath passed right through me. I felt it like a baleful air or sigh penetrating deep, pausing motion at my heart or proceeding only under unspeakable oppression. The letter—the beloved letter—would not come; it was the only sweetness in life I had to look for.

In extremity, I resorted again and again to the little packet in my case—the five letters. How splendid that month seemed whose skies had witnessed the rising of those five stars! I visited them only at night, buying a wax taper and matches because I dared not ask for a candle in the kitchen. At study hour, I stole up to the dormitory and feasted on my crust from the Barmecide's loaf. It did not nourish me; I pined on it, growing as thin as a shadow, though otherwise not ill.

One evening, reading late, I felt the power of reading slipping away. The letters, from incessant perusal, were losing all sap and significance; my gold was withering to dust before my eyes, and I mourned the disillusionment. Suddenly, a quick, tripping foot ran up the stairs. I knew Ginevra Fanshawe's step. She had dined in town and was returning to replace her shawl and other items in the wardrobe.

Yes, she came in, dressed in bright silk, her shawl falling from her shoulders, her curls half-uncurled by the damp of night, drooping carelessly and heavily upon her neck. I had barely time to recasket my treasures and lock them away when she reached my side, her humor seemingly in a poor state.

"It has been a stupid evening: they are stupid people," she began.

"Who? Mrs. Cholmondeley? I thought you always found her house charming?"

"I have not been to Mrs. Cholmondeley's."

"Indeed! Have you made new acquaintances?"

"My uncle de Bassompierre is come."

"My uncle de Bassompierre! Are you not glad? I thought he was a favorite."

"You thought wrong: the man is odious; I hate him."

"Because he is a foreigner? Or for some other reason of equal weight?"

"He is not a foreigner. The man is English enough, goodness knows; and had an English name until three or four years ago. But his mother was a foreigner, a de Bassompierre, and some of her family are dead, leaving him estates, a title, and this name. He is quite a great man now."

"Do you hate him for that reason?"

"Don't I know what Mamma says about him? He is not my own uncle, but married Mamma's sister. Mamma detests him; she says he killed Aunt Ginevra with unkindness. He looks like a bear. Such a dismal evening!" she continued. "I'll go no more to his big hotel. Imagine walking into a room alone, and a great man fifty years old coming forward, and after a few minutes' conversation actually turning his back upon me, and then abruptly going out of the room. Such odd ways! I daresay his conscience smote him, for they all say at home I am the picture of Aunt Ginevra. Mamma often declares the likeness is quite ridiculous."

"Were you the only visitor?"

"The only visitor? Yes; then there was Missy, my cousin: a little spoiled, pampered thing."

"M. de Bassompierre has a daughter?"

"Yes, yes: don't tease one with questions. Oh, dear! I am so tired."

She yawned. Throwing herself without ceremony onto my bed, she added, "It seems Mademoiselle was nearly crushed to a jelly in a hubbub at the theatre some weeks ago."

"Ah! Indeed. And they live at a large hotel in the Rue Crècy?"

"Justement. How do *you* know?"

"I have been there."

"Oh, you have? Really! You go everywhere in these days. I suppose Mother Bretton took you. She and Esculapius have the *entrée* to the de Bassompierre apartments: it seems 'my son John' attended Missy on the occasion of her accident—Accident? Bah! All affectation! I don't think she was squeezed more than she richly deserved for her airs. And now there is quite an intimacy struck up: I heard something about 'auld lang syne,' and what not. Oh, how stupid they all were!"

"_All!_ You said you were the only visitor."

"Did I? You see, one forgets to particularize an old woman and her boy."

"Dr. and Mrs. Bretton were at M. de Bassompierre's this evening?"

"Ay, ay! as large as life; and Missy played the hostess. What a conceited doll it is!"

Soured and listless, Miss Fanshawe was beginning to disclose the causes of her prostrate condition. There had been a retrenchment of incense, a diversion or total withholding of homage; coquetry had failed of effect, and vanity had undergone mortification. She lay fuming in the vapors.

"Is Miss de Bassompierre quite well now?" I asked.

"As well as you or I, no doubt; but she is an affected little thing, and gave herself invalid airs to attract medical notice. And to see the old dowager making her recline on a couch, and 'my son John' prohibiting excitement, etcetera—faugh! the scene was quite sickening."
