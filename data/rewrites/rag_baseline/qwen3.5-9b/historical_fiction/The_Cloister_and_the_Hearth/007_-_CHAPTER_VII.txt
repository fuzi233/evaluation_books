To witness the architecture of joy in Tergou requires understanding the sheer vibrancy of that specific morning, a day where the ordinary fabric of civic life was torn open by spectacle. Velvet sunlight, unwonted in its intensity, washed the cobblestones, while horses' hooves galloped with a metallic gleam that echoed through the narrow streets. Windows and balconies, usually reserved for quiet domesticity, were now studded with faces straining to catch every movement. The French Ambassador rode through the center of this tableau, not for diplomacy, but for sport, venturing into the neighboring forest. He was accompanied by a retinue that included several servants of the Duke of Burgundy, lent specifically to honor the foreign dignitary and minister to his pleasures. Among them rode the Duke's tumbler, a figure of grave, sedate majesty whose very presence rendered his more noble companions—men of higher station—to seem light, frivolous, and almost clownish. Yet, whenever the atmosphere grew too thick with respect and awe, the tumbler would roll off his horse in a manner so ignoble and absurd that even the stoic Ambassador was forced to burst into laughter. With a graceless agility, he would climb back aboard by the tail, playing his part in a pantomime of servitude that provoked mirth rather than reverence.

Trailing behind this procession rode a creature that commanded far greater attention: the Duke's leopard. Mounted on a Flemish steed of giant, prodigious power, a huntsman carried a long box strapped securely to his loins by cunningly devised harnesses. Upon this platform sat a bright leopard, crouching in anticipation, chained tightly to the rider. The populace pressed forward, mesmerized by the animal's glossy hide and spotted coat, eager to touch her or pull at her tail. But the huntsman's voice cut through the crowd with a terrible warning. "Beware!" he shouted. "In Antwerp, one man dared but throw a handful of dust at her, and the Duke made dust of him."

"Gramercy!" someone cried out.

"I speak truth," the huntsman confirmed. "The good Duke imprisoned him in a cell beneath the earth, and the rats cleaned the flesh from his bones in a single night. He served him right for molesting the poor beast."

A murmur of fear rippled through the Tergovians, shrinking instinctively from the idea of tickling the sovereign's leopard. Yet, the tension broke with the arrival of the final element of the parade: the Duke's giant. Standing seven feet four inches tall, a Hungarian brute, he brought up the rear. Like others of his kind, he possessed a treble, fluty voice of negligible power, a flaw he was entirely unaware of due to his vanity. As he passed, he caught sight of Giles perched atop a balcony. Stopping abruptly, the giant began to mock the dwarf.

"Hallo! Brother!" he squeaked, his voice piercingly high despite his massive frame. "I nearly passed without seeing thee."

"You are plain enough to see," Giles bellowed back, his bass tones vibrating with a depth that contrasted violently with the giant's shrill complaint.

"Come on my shoulder, brother," the giant squealed again, holding out a shoulder shaped like a fist of mutton to assist the smaller man down.

"If I do, I'll cuff your ears," roared Giles.

The giant, sensing the dwarf's irascibility and emboldened by the surrounding laughter, played upon him. He failed to perceive that the crowd laughed not at his wit, but at the ridiculous incongruity of the two voices—the gigantic body emitting a feeble flute note, and the tiny form unleashing a deep, resonant drumbeat. It was a mountain delivering a squeak, a molehill belching thunder.

The singular duet ended as abruptly as it began. Giles, losing all patience and self-command, driven by a rage devoid of fear, dropped upon the giant's neck. Seizing the giant's hair with one hand, he punched his head with the other. The giant's initial impulse was to laugh, but the weight and rapidity of the blows quickly extinguished that inclination.

"He! he! Ah! ha! Hallo! Oh! Oh! Holy saints! Here! Help! Or I must throttle the imp! I can't! I'll split your skull against the—"

He made a wild run backward toward the balcony. Giles, sensing imminent danger, seized the railing with both hands and whipped over it just as the giant's head collided with the wood with a stunning crack. The people roared with laughter and exultation at the little champion's address. Indignant, the giant seized two of the laughers, knocking them together like dumbbells before shaking them until they lay flat. Catherine shrieked, throwing her apron over Giles, while the giant strode wrathfully away after the departing party.

No one present foresaw the consequences of this incident, yet its immediate effects were agreeable. The Tergovians turned proudly toward Giles, listening with newfound affability to his pleas for parchment. He conducted a regular trade with his brother Gerard in this commodity, wandering about begging sheets gratis while Gerard provided copper coins in exchange.

That same afternoon, Catherine and her daughter engaged in conversation about their favorite topic: Gerard, his goodness, his benefice, and the brightened prospects of the entire family. Their fortune had arrived in the exact shape they desired. Beyond the advantage of a benefice that the Countess Charolois would not disdain to grant, there lay the feminine delight of having a priest, a holy man, within their own household.

"He will marry Cornelis and Sybrandt," Catherine declared. "For they can wed, good housewives, now, if they will. Gerard will take care of you and Giles when we are gone."

"Yes, Mother, and we can confess to him instead of to a stranger," Kate replied.

"Ay, girl! And he can give the sacred oil to your father and me, and close our eyes when our time comes."

"Oh, Mother! Not for many, many years, I do pray Heaven. Pray speak not of that; it always makes me sad. I hope to go before you, Mother dear. No; let us be gay today. I am out of pain, Mother, quite out of all pain. It seems so strange; I feel so bright and happy that..."

She paused, her eyes lighting up. "Mother, can you keep a secret?"

"Nobody better, child. Why, you know I can."

"Then I will show you something so beautiful. You never saw the like, I trow. Only Gerard must never know; for surely he means to surprise us with it. He covers it up so, and sometimes he carries it away altogether."

Kate took her crutches and moved slowly away, leaving her mother in an exalted state of curiosity. Soon she returned, carrying something wrapped in cloth. Uncovering it revealed a lovely picture of the Virgin, adorned with all her insignias, wearing her tiara over a wealth of beautiful hair flowing loosely over her shoulders. Catherine, at first, was struck with awe.

"It is herself," she cried. "It is the Queen of Heaven. I never saw one like her to my mind before."

"And her eyes, Mother: lifted to the sky, as if they belonged there, and not to a mortal creature. And her beautiful hair of burning gold."

"And to think I have a son that can make the saints live again upon a piece of wood!"

"The reason is, he is a young saint himself, Mother. He is too good for this world; he is here to portray the blessed, and then to go away and be with them forever."

Before they had finished admiring the image, a strange voice was heard at the door. Driven by one of the furtive instincts common to their sex, they hastily hid the picture in the cloth, though there was no real need. The next moment, a man entered, casting his eyes furtively around the room. It had been ten years since Ghysbrecht Van Swieten had set foot in the house.

The two women were so taken aback that they merely stared at him and each other, murmuring, "The burgomaster!" in a tone so expressive that Ghysbrecht felt compelled to respond.

"Yes! I own the last time I came here was not on a friendly errand. Men love their own interests—Elias's and mine were contrary. Well, let this visit atone for the last. Today I come on your business and none of mine."

Catherine and her daughter exchanged a swift glance of contemptuous incredulity. They knew the man better than he thought.

"It is about your son Gerard."

"Ay! Ay! You want him to work for the town all for nothing. He told us."

"I come on no such errand. It is to let you know he has fallen into bad hands."

"Now Heaven and the saints forbid! Man, torture not a mother! Speak out, and quickly: speak ere you have time to coin falsehood. We know thee."

Ghysbrecht turned pale at this affront, spite mingling with the other motives that had brought him there. "Thus it is, then," he said, grinding his teeth and speaking very fast. "Your son Gerard is more likely to be the father of a family than a priest: he is forever with Margaret, Peter Brandt's red-haired girl, and loves her like a cow her calf."

Mother and daughter both burst out laughing. Ghysbrecht stared at them, bewildered.
