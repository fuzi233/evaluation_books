Margaret’s grief was not the kind that could be soothed with hollow comforts. The loss of her father struck deeper than any wound she had yet borne. Catherine, ever practical, tried to console her with reason—how death had been a mercy, how the burden of care was lighter now. But Margaret only turned hollow eyes upon her and whispered, *"Such cares were all the joy I had."*

The empty chair by the hearth seemed to mock her. She had loved the old man not as a duty, but as something tender and fragile—a scholar reduced to a child’s helplessness, dependent on her hands for bread, her voice for comfort. Now the silence where his voice had been was unbearable.

Catherine, chastened, fell quiet. At last, she admitted, *"I spoke from my head, not my heart. If I were you, I’d weep just the same."* That small honesty, that rare moment of true kinship, brought Margaret the first flicker of solace.

But Catherine’s sympathy was fleeting. Soon enough, she turned to her usual meddling. *"You were never meant to live alone,"* she declared. *"Gerard is gone—dead or faithless, who can say? But here’s Luke Peterson, honest as daylight, and sweet on you besides. Why waste your youth waiting for a ghost?"*

Margaret stared, bewildered. *"Luke? Our basket-boy?"*

*"Basket-boy? He’s near twenty, and a steady hand! Never a drunkard, unlike half the louts in Rotterdam. I’ve asked about."*

A faint, weary smile touched Margaret’s lips. *"He only comes for the puddings."*

Catherine scoffed. *"Aye, and for the cook who makes them. I’ve known his mind a year now."*

Margaret’s cheeks flushed. *"If he dares think of me while Gerard—"* She bit her lip. *"No. I’ll not believe him dead till I see his grave, nor faithless till I see him wed to another."*

Luke, poor fool, walked into the storm unwittingly. Where once he had been welcomed with warmth, he now met only frost. Confused, he stammered, *"Have I done something to displease you?"*

*"Nothing,"* Margaret said curtly. *"But keep away awhile."*

Catherine, ever the blunderer, confessed her interference. Luke blinked. *"You told her? But—she must’ve known! I’ve been courting her every Saturday night!"*

*"Courting?"* Margaret’s voice sharpened. *"Is that how Rotterdam men do it? Sitting at a woman’s table and calling it love?"*

*"I like your puddings,"* Luke admitted, *"but I like you more."*

*"Then you’ve supped your last here,"* she said coldly. *"I’ll carry my own basket."*

Luke slunk away, wounded. But by the next Saturday, he was back—huddled on the doorstep, red-eyed. *"I don’t know where else to go,"* he muttered.

Catherine relayed his misery. Margaret sighed. *"Let him in, then. But only if he remembers I’m another man’s wife."*

Luke hesitated at the terms. Catherine rolled her eyes. *"Promises are pie-crust, boy. Make them, break them—just get inside first. Humor her now, and if Gerard never comes… well, time wears down the loneliest heart."*
