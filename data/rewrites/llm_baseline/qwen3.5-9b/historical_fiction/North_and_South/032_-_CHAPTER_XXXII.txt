"What! Remain to be denounced—dragged, it may be, in chains!" Werner had cried.

All the next day, the three sat together. Mr. Hale spoke scarcely at all, answering only when his children pressed him with questions, forcing him, as it were, into the present moment. Frederick's grief had vanished from sight and sound; the initial paroxysm had passed, leaving behind a man ashamed of having been so utterly battered down by emotion. Yet, his sorrow for the loss of his mother remained a deep, real feeling that would endure his entire life, though it was never to be spoken of again. Margaret, though less passionate at first, suffered more acutely now. At times she wept profusely, and even when discussing other matters, her manner possessed a mournful tenderness that deepened whenever her gaze fell upon Frederick and she contemplated his rapidly approaching departure. She was glad he was leaving, primarily for her father's sake, despite her personal anguish over the separation.

The anxious terror in which Mr. Hale lived, fearing his son might be detected and captured, far outweighed any pleasure he derived from Frederick's presence. His nervousness had intensified since Mrs. Hale's death, likely because he dwelt upon it more exclusively. He started at every unusual sound and found comfort only when Frederick sat out of the immediate view of anyone entering the room.

Towards evening, Mr. Hale said, "Will you go with Frederick to the station, Margaret? I shall want to know he is safely off. Will you bring me word that he is clear of Milton, at any rate?"

"Certainly," said Margaret. "I shall like it, if you won't be lonely without me, papa."

"No, no! I should always be fancying someone had known him and that he had been stopped, unless you could tell me you had seen him off. Go to the Outwood station. It is quite as near, and there are fewer people about. Take a cab there; there is less risk of his being seen. What time is your train, Fred?"

"Ten minutes past six; very nearly dark. So, what will you do, Margaret?"

"Oh, I can manage. I am getting very brave and very hard. It is a well-lighted road all the way home, even if it should be dark. Besides, I was out last week much later."

Margaret felt thankful when the parting was finally over—the farewell to both the dead mother and the living father. She hurried Frederick into the cab to shorten a scene she saw was bitterly painful to her father, who intended to accompany his son for one last look at his mother. Partly due to this haste, and partly owing to a common mistake in the "Railway Guide" regarding arrival times at smaller stations, they found themselves with nearly twenty minutes to spare upon reaching Outwood. The booking office was closed, preventing them from purchasing tickets immediately. Consequently, they descended the flight of steps leading to the ground level beside the railway. There lay a broad cinder path cutting diagonally across a field adjacent to the carriage road, and they walked back and forth along it for the few remaining minutes.

Margaret's hand rested in Frederick's arm. He grasped it affectionately.

"Margaret! I am going to consult Mr. Lennox regarding the chance of exculpating myself, so that I may return to England whenever I choose. This is more for your sake than for anyone else's. I cannot bear to think of your lonely position if anything happens to my father. He looks sadly changed—terribly shaken. I wish you could persuade him to consider the Cadiz plan; there are many reasons why. What could you do if he were taken away? You have no friends nearby. We are curiously bereft of relations."

Margaret could hardly restrain her tears at the tender anxiety with which Frederick brought this probable disaster before her, given how severely the cares of the last few months had affected Mr. Hale. Yet, she tried to rally herself.

"There have been such strange, unexpected changes in my life during these last two years that I feel more than ever it is not worth while to calculate too closely what I should do if some future event takes place. I try to think only upon the present."

She paused. They stood still for a moment, close to the stile leading into the road, bathed in the light of the setting sun. Frederick held her hand, looking into her face with wistful anxiety, reading there more care and trouble than she betrayed in her words.

"We shall write often to one another," she continued. "And I will promise—for I see it will set your mind at ease—to tell you every worry I have. Papa is—" She started slightly, a movement hardly visible, but Frederick felt the sudden jerk of the hand he held and turned his full face toward the road. Along the path, a horseman rode slowly, just passing the very stile where they stood.

Margaret bowed stiffly. Her bow was met with a rigid return.

"Who is that?" Frederick asked, almost before the rider was out of earshot.

"Mr. Thornton; you saw him before, you know," Margaret replied, appearing a little drooping and flushed.

"Only his back. He is an unprepossessing-looking fellow. What a scowl he has!"

"Something has happened to vex him," Margaret said apologetically. "You would not have thought him unprepossessing if you had seen him with mamma."

"I fancy it must be time to go and take my ticket. If I had known how dark it would be, we wouldn't have sent the cab back, Margaret."

"Oh, don't fidget about that. I can take a cab here if I like, or go back by the railway when I should have shops and people and lamps all the way from the Milton station-house. Don't think of me; take care of yourself. I am sick with the thought that Leonards may be in the same train with you. Look well into the carriage before you get in."

They returned to the station. Margaret insisted on going into the bright glare of the flaring gas inside to purchase her ticket. Several idle-looking young men were lounging about with the station master. Margaret believed she had seen the face of one of them before and returned him a proud look of offended dignity for his somewhat impertinent stare of undisguised admiration. She hastened to her brother, who stood outside, taking hold of his arm.

"Have you got your bag? Let us walk about here on the platform," she said, a little flurried at the prospect of being left alone so soon, her bravado leaking out faster than she cared to admit even to herself.

She heard a step following them along the flagstones; it halted when they halted, as they looked out along the line and listened to the whistle of the approaching train. Neither spoke; their hearts were too full. Another moment, and the train would be here; a minute more, and he would be gone. Margaret almost repented the urgency with which she had urged him to go to London; it seemed to throw additional chances of detection in his way. If he had sailed for Spain via Liverpool, he might have been away within two or three hours.

Frederick turned around, facing directly the lamp, where the gas darted upward in vivid anticipation of the arriving train. Suddenly, a man dressed as a railway porter started forward—a bad-looking individual who appeared to have drunk himself into a state of brutality, though his senses seemed perfectly sharp.

"By your leave, miss!" he said, roughly shoving Margaret aside and seizing Frederick by the collar. "Your name is Hale, I believe?"

In an instant, Margaret did not see how it happened; everything seemed to dance before her eyes. But through some quick act of wrestling, Frederick had tripped him up, sending the man crashing from the height of three or four feet—the elevation of the platform above the soft ground beside the tracks. He lay there.

"Run, run!" gasped Margaret. "The train is here. Was it Leonards? Oh, run! I will carry your bag."

Taking him by the arm, she pushed him forward with all her feeble strength. A door opened in a carriage; he jumped in. As he leaned out to cry, "God bless you, Margaret!" the train rushed past her, leaving her standing alone.

She was so terribly sick and faint that she was grateful to turn into the ladies' waiting-room and sit down for a brief respite. Initially, she could do nothing but gasp for breath. It had been such a hurry, such a sickening alarm, such a narrow escape. Had the train not arrived at that precise moment, the man would have risen again and called for assistance to arrest him. She wondered if the man had gotten up; she tried to recall if she had seen him move; she feared he might have been seriously hurt.

Venturing out, she found the platform brightly lit yet completely deserted. She went to the end and looked over, somewhat fearfully. Seeing no one, she was relieved she had inspected the area, for otherwise, terrible thoughts would haunt her dreams. Even as it was, she was so trembling and affrighted that she felt unable to walk home along the road, which indeed seemed lonely and dark as she gazed down upon it from the station's blaze. She decided to wait until the down train passed and take her seat in it. But what if Leonards recognized her as Frederick's companion? She peered about before venturing into the booking office to buy her ticket. Only a few railway officials were standing about, talking loudly among themselves.
