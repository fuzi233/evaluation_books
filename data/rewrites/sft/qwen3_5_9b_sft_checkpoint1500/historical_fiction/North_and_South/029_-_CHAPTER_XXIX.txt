"Some wishes crossed my mind and dimly cheered it,
And one or two poor melancholy pleasures,
Each in the pale unwarming light of hope,
Silvering its flimsy wing, flew silent by—
Moths in the moonbeam!"

Morning broke with a letter for Margaret from Edith. The writing was as affectionate and scattered as its author, yet the warmth of it soothed Margaret's own tender heart, and her upbringing had taught her to overlook the lack of sequence. The message read: "Oh, Margaret, it is worth a journey from England to see my boy! He is a superb little fellow, especially in his caps, and most especially in the one you sent him, you good, dainty-fingered, persevering little lady! Having made all the mothers here envious, I want to show him to somebody new... perhaps, that's all the reason; perhaps it is not—nay, possibly, there is just a little cousinly love mixed with it; but I do want you so much to come here, Margaret! I'm sure it would be the very best thing for Aunt Hale's health; everybody here is young and well, and our skies are always blue, and our sun always shines..."

Edith rambled on about the band playing deliciously, the baby's constant smiles, and her husband Cosmo, whom she initially dismissed as stout and grumpy before retracting the insult with a laugh, declaring him a darling despite his "busy" nature. She urged Margaret to visit immediately, promising three months of sunshine and grapes to cure Aunt Hale of the "smoke of Milton," and joking that if Margaret didn't pack instantly, she might be descended from King Herod.

Margaret read the letter twice. First, she felt a profound longing for Edith's world—the freedom from care, the cheerful home, the endless sunlight. For the first time, she wished she could trade places, even if only for a single day, to feel young again amidst that brightness. Yet, beneath this envy lay a heavy realization: she was not yet twenty, but the crushing pressure of recent days had aged her spirit. Then, reading again, she laughed at the letter's resemblance to Edith herself, finding amusement in the chaos.

Her laughter ceased abruptly when Mrs. Hale entered the drawing-room, supported by Dixon's arm. Margaret rushed forward to arrange the pillows, her movements urgent as she settled her frail mother onto the sofa. "What were you laughing at, Margaret?" her mother asked, recovering her breath.

"A letter from Edith," Margaret replied softly, reading it aloud. For a moment, her mother engaged with the content, guessing names for Edith's son and speculating on the reasons behind them. Then Mr. Thornton entered, bringing a basket of peaches. He could not, or perhaps would not, deny himself the fleeting pleasure of seeing Margaret, though his motive was merely the gratification of the moment. His entrance was marked by a stiff, cold bow; thereafter, his gaze avoided hers entirely. He offered the fruit, exchanged polite, gentle words, and departed with a grave farewell that felt like an offense.

Margaret sat silent and pale. "Do you know, Margaret, I really begin to like Mr. Thornton," Mrs. Hale observed.

"Do you?" Margaret forced out, her voice icy.

"Yes! I think he is really getting quite polished in his manners." Margaret's tone softened. "He is very kind and attentive—there is no doubt of that."

"I wonder Mrs. Thornton never calls. She must know I am ill, because of the water-bed."

"I dare say, she hears how you are from her son."

"Still, I should like to see her. You have so few friends here, Margaret." A tender craving rose in Margaret's throat—the desperate wish to bespeak kindness toward a daughter who might soon be left motherless. But she remained mute. After a pause, her mother asked if Margaret could invite Mrs. Thornton to visit. "Only once—I don't want to be troublesome."

"I will do anything, if you wish it, mamma—but if—but when Frederick comes—"

"Oh, to be sure! We must keep our doors shut. I hardly know whether I dare wish him to come or not. Sometimes I think I would rather not. Sometimes I have such frightful dreams about him."

"Oh, mamma! We'll take good care. I will put my arm in the bolt sooner than he should come to the slightest harm. Trust the care of him to me, mamma. I will watch over him like a lioness over her young."

As they discussed arrangements for Frederick's expected arrival and potential househelp, Mrs. Hale chided Margaret for using "factory slang," specifically the word "slack." Margaret defended her usage, arguing that living in a factory town required its own language, and that she could surprise her mother with terms like "knobstick." Her mother dismissed it as vulgar, prompting Margaret to promise restraint.

Suddenly, Mrs. Hale turned the conversation back to the air in Milton, asserting that the smoke was indeed making her ill. Margaret sprang up, terrified that her father, who had just entered, might confirm her mother's suspicion. Anxious to protect his feelings from the faint shadow of injury he might have perceived, she began speaking rapidly of other topics, unaware that Mr. Thornton was following close behind.

"Mamma is accusing me of having picked up a great deal of vulgarity since we came to Milton," Margaret said, addressing both her mother and Mr. Thornton. The "vulgarity" concerned only local dialect, arising from their previous discussion, but Mr. Thornton's brow darkened. Margaret realized instantly how easily her words could be misinterpreted by him. Driven by a sweet, instinctive desire to avoid unnecessary pain, she pressed forward, turning directly to him.

"Now, Mr. Thornton, though 'knobstick' has not a very pretty sound, is it not expressive? Could I do without it, in speaking of the thing it represents? If using local words is vulgar, I was very vulgar in the Forest,—was I not, mamma?"

It was unusual for Margaret to dominate a conversation, but her anxiety to shield Mr. Thornton from annoyance caused her to speak with unexpected force. As she finished, she flushed crimson under the weight of conscious shame, particularly as Mr. Thornton seemed to misunderstand her intent, passing her by with a cold, ceremonial reserve to address Mrs. Hale.

His departure haunted her. The sight of him reignited her wish to see his mother and commend herself to her care. Sitting in burning silence, vexed and ashamed of her clumsiness, Margaret heard her mother's slow entreaty for Mrs. Thornton's visit repeated. Mr. Thornton promised that Mrs. Thornton would come, conversed briefly, and then left. Immediately, Margaret's movements and voice seemed released from invisible chains.

He never looked at her; yet his careful avoidance of her eyes betokened that he knew precisely where, by chance, his gaze might have rested. If she spoke, he gave no sign of attention, yet his subsequent speech to anyone else was subtly modified by what she had said. Sometimes he answered an unspoken remark, directing it to another person as though suggested by her. This was not the bad manners of ignorance, nor simple rudeness; it was a wilful, deep-seated offense, born of bitterness but tempered by repentance. No deep plan or cunning strategy served him here.

Margaret pondered him more deeply than ever before. There was no tinge of love in her thoughts, only a sharp regret for the wound she had inflicted, coupled with a gentle, patient striving to restore their former position of antagonistic friendship. She believed a friend's place was what he had held in her regard, as well as in the minds of the entire family. Her behavior toward him was humbled, almost a mute apology for the strong words spoken in reaction to the riots.

Yet he resented those words bitterly. They rang in his ears, and he prided himself on the sense of justice that compelled him to offer kindness to her parents. He exulted in the power he displayed by forcing himself to face her whenever he could devise an action to please her father or mother. He thought he disliked seeing someone who had mortified him so keenly, but he was mistaken. It was a stinging pleasure to be in the same room with her, acutely aware of her presence. Though he was no great analyst of his own motives, he was wrong to dismiss the complexity of his feelings.
