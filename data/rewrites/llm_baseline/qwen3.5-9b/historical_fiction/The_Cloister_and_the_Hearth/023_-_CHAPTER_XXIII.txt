It is a truth often overlooked until necessity strikes: life and liberty, when secure, are taken for granted, their value obscured by the very fact of their presence. It is only when threatened that we truly comprehend their worth. This sentiment mirrors the nature of sunlight; we rarely pause to admire its brilliance at high noon, when it floods the sky with undifferentiated light, but rather in the late afternoon, when it fractures into golden flakes beneath the shade of ancient elms. Though weaker then, the light is rendered more precious by the encroaching gloom, revealing a fire that contrasts sharply with the dark.

Thus, Gerard and Margaret, though trembling at every rustling leaf that sounded harsher than its neighbors, radiated with a profound joy and gratitude as they drifted through the friendly woods. Safety and deep, tranquil silence surrounded them, yet the memory of barking dogs and brutal voices still echoed in their minds. Soon, however, Gerard noticed something alarming: stains of blood on Margaret's ankles.

"Martin! Martin! Help!" he cried, panic rising in his throat. "They have wounded her! The crossbow!"

"No, no!" Margaret replied, offering a reassuring smile. "I am neither wounded nor hurt at all."

"But what is it, then?" Gerard demanded, agitated. "In Heaven's name?"

"Do not scold me!" Margaret blushed softly. "Have I ever scolded you?"

"No, dear Gerard."

"Well, then," she continued gently, "Martin told me the cruel dogs were following because of the scent of blood. So, thinking that if I could offer them just a little bit of my own blood on my shoes, they might follow me instead of letting my Gerard pass freely... I scratched my arm with Martin's knife. Forgive me! Whose else could I use? Not yours, Gerard? Ah, no. Will you forgive me?" she asked, her tone beseeching, loving, and fawning all at once.

"Let me see this scratch," Gerard choked out, overcome with emotion.

"There," she showed him. "I thought so. Just a scratch?"

Gerard shuddered. "A scratch? I call it a cut—a deep, terrible, cruel cut."

"She might have done it with her bodkin," the soldier muttered, stepping forward. "Milksop! That sickens at the sight of a mere scratch and a drop of blood."

"No, no," Gerard insisted fiercely. "I could look upon a sea of blood, but not on hers. Oh, Margaret! How could you be so cruel?"

Margaret smiled with an ineffable tenderness. "Foolish Gerard," she murmured, "to make such a fuss of nothing." She threw her guilty arm around his neck. "As if I would not give all the blood in my heart for you, let alone a few drops from my arm."

Under the weight of their recent danger, she began to weep against his neck, driven by pity and love; he wept with her.

"And I must part from her," he sobbed, the reality crashing down upon him. "We two who love so dearly—one must remain in Holland, the other in Italy. Ah me! Ah me! Ah me!"

At this, Margaret wept anew, yet her tears were patient and silent. Instinct never sleeps, and with her selflessness lay a sharp instinct. To voice her true thoughts would only deepen Gerard's agony at their separation, so she chose silence.

Suddenly, they emerged onto a well-worn path, and Martin halted.

"This is the bridle-road I mentioned," he said, hanging his head. "And there, lying ahead, is the inn."

Margaret and Gerard exchanged terrified glances.

"Come a step with me, Martin," Gerard whispered urgently.

Drawing the soldier aside, he spoke in a broken voice. "Good Martin, watch over her for me! She is my wife, yet I leave her. Look, Martin! Here is gold—it was meant for my journey. It serves no purpose for me to ask her to take it; she would refuse. But you will take it for her, will you not? Oh, Heaven! Is this all I can offer her? Money? But poverty is a curse. You will ensure she wants for nothing, dear Martin? The burgomaster's silver is sufficient for me."

"You are a good lad, Gerard," the soldier replied warmly. "Neither want nor harm shall come to her. I care for her little finger more than for all the world; and were she nothing to me, even for your sake, I would be a father to her. Go with a stout heart, and may God be with you going and returning."

The rough soldier squeezed Gerard's hand, turning his head away with an unusual display of feeling. After a brief silence, he prepared to return to Margaret, but Gerard stopped him.

"No, good Martin; pray, stay here behind this thicket and turn your head away from us, while I—Oh, Martin! Martin!"

By this ruse, Gerard avoided having a witness to his anguish at leaving the woman he loved, and Martin spared himself the sight of their pain. He saw nothing of the poor young couple kneeling to renew before Heaven those holy vows that cruel men had interrupted. He witnessed nothing of them clinging together as one entity, struggling to part, failing, returning, and embracing again like drowning, despairing creatures. He heard only Gerard's continuous sobbing and Margaret's mournful moans.

Finally, a hoarse cry pierced the air, followed by the patter of feet on the hard road. Martin sprang up to find Gerard running wildly, both hands clasped above his head in fervent prayer, while Margaret tottered backward toward him, palms extended in pitiful supplication, her cheeks ash-gray and her eyes vacant.

He caught her in his arms and offered words of comfort, but her mind could not process them. Only the sound of his voice caused her to moan and hold him tightly, trembling violently. He lifted her onto the mule, wrapping his arm around her. Supporting her frame—which had gone from the strength of a boy to a state of total relaxation and powerlessness—he carried her slowly and sadly home.

She shed not a single tear and spoke not a single word. At the edge of the wood, he helped her down from the mule and instructed her to walk across to her father's house. She obeyed immediately. Martin turned toward Rotterdam; Sevenbergen was too oppressive for him. Gerard, severed from the love of his life, moved forward like one in a dream.

He hired a horse and a guide at the small inn and rode swiftly toward the German frontier. Yet, everything felt mechanical; his senses seemed dulled. Trees, houses, and people passed him like objects viewed through a thick veil. His companions spoke to him twice, receiving no answer. Only once did he cry out savagely, "Shall we never escape this hateful country?"

After many hours of riding, they reached the crest of a steep hill, with a small brook flowing at the bottom.

"Halt!" the guide shouted, pointing across the valley. "Here is Germany."

"Where?" Gerard asked, bewildered.

"On the other side of the stream. No need to ride down the hill, I think."

Gerard dismounted without a word and drew the burgomaster's purse from his belt. As he opened it, the guide remarked, half-sulkily, "You will soon be out of this detestable country. Perhaps the one you are heading to won't treat you any better anyway. Still, even if it is a church you have robbed, they cannot touch you once you cross that boundary."

Such words, at another time, would have merited a stern rebuke or a slap. Now, they fell upon Gerard like idle wind. He paid the lad in silence and descended the hill alone.

The brook was silvery, murmuring over tiny pebbles that glittered, varnished by the clear water. Gerard sat down and stared stupidly at them. Then he drank from the stream and washed his hot feet and hands in the icy water; the shock woke him. Rising, he took a run and leaped across the stream into Germany.

Even as his foot touched the strange soil, he turned abruptly and looked back.

"Farewell, ungrateful country!" he cried. "But for her, it would cost me nothing to leave you forever, along with all my family and kin—and the mother who bore me, and my playmates, and my little native town. Farewell, fatherland—welcome, wide world! *Omne solum fortippatra*."

With these brave words on his lips, he suddenly collapsed, arms and legs weak, sitting down to sob bitterly upon the foreign earth.

When the young exile had sat thus for some time, bowed down in grief, he rose and wiped the tears from his eyes like a man steeling himself. Casting not a single glance behind him to weaken his resolve, he stepped out into the vast unknown. His love and heavy sorrow left no room for petty doubts. Compared with the tearing apart of his soul from Margaret, walking on foot to Italy in that rough age seemed a trivial matter. All nations meet within a convent. Thanks to his good friends the monks and his own insatiable thirst for knowledge, he could speak most of the languages required for that long journey.

"I will soon reach Rome," he told himself. "The sooner, the better."
