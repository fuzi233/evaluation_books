Our ancient ark was rudderless, oarless, stripped of all mechanism that might steer it, free to drift only where the high powers of the deep chose to carry it. Its interior was wrapped in total, unyielding darkness, thick with a resinous, centuries-old scent that clung to every breath, thrumming with the low groan of strained timber and distant, muffled echoes of the world we had lost. I fumbled blindly for the firebox of the fallen lantern for what felt like hours, but could not find it, and so we were forced to endure the chaos unfolding around us sightless, adrift in the black. The waves toyed with our hull in merciless, giddy sport, heaving it high before slamming it down into troughs that made our teeth rattle, and I clung tight to Nais, holding her pinned to the only solid berth in the dark belly of the craft. We spoke no more than a handful of words to each other those first days, but our shared grief at the loss of every person and every home we had ever known bound us closer than any conversation could.

When Atlantis tore free of the crust and plunged to form new ocean bed, it left roiling, ravenous whirlpools and scalding plumes of steam spouting from its drowned volcanic fires as a grim, fleeting gift to the sea gods. Trapped as we were in the ark’s pitch-black hull, we could not see any of that devastation, but we soon felt the next, far worse blow: a vast, screaming hurricane rolled in to scour the last traces of the broken continent from the face of the waters. That apocalyptic turmoil raged for seven nights and seven full days, counted later on the old water clock nailed to the ark’s inner wall, until at last the howl of the wind died away, leaving only the slow, heavy roll of an endless, oily swell.

Feeling the shift in the motion of the hull, I dared at last to climb the rough-hewn stair leading to the hatch set in the ark’s roof. When I pried that heavy panel open, sweet, sun-warmed salt air gushed down to chase the foul, stale air out of the hull. The ark rode high and dry over the unbroken swells, so I ducked back below to carry Nais up to bathe in the healing light of the sun. We knelt side by side on the warm deck planks, faces turned up to that golden light, and gave thanks for the mercy that had pulled us alive through the ruin of our world, before laying down to slip into the deep, bone-deep rest we had not dared to seek since the ground had split open beneath our feet.

Though I was exhausted past all words, sleep would not come for a long while. I stared out over the sun-dappled, glassy blue water, and not a single speck of land broke the unbroken ring of the horizon. The vault of the sky stretched endless overhead, and the bosom of the deep was littered with the broken wreckage of Atlantis: snapped great cedar trees, hewn house timbers, a caved-in upturned hide boat, and here and there the rounded forms of men and cattle drifting over the swells, keeping pace with our ark like a grim, silent funeral convoy to prove all that had happened was no nightmare. At last I slipped into unconsciousness, still holding Nais’s hand, and when I woke hours later I found her already watching me, her gaze soft and tender.

We were both fully rested, and that returned strength softened the edge of our sharp grief, left us ready to accept the fate the high gods had laid out for us without complaint. We climbed back down into the ark’s hull to eat and drink, to rebuild our strength for the unknown new life stretching ahead of us. It was only then, at leisure, that we discovered what a marvel this ancient craft truly was. Though this was the first time it had ever touched open water in all the centuries since its builders had finished it, it did not leak a single drop, not a single suncrack marred its timber, and its floor was bone dry. You could see the grain of the wood in the faint light that filtered down from the hatch, but there was not a single seam, joint, or nail to be found anywhere. The ancients had grown whole timbers into one seamless hull via a craft our generation has long lost, then treated the entire structure with a secret forgotten preservative that made the wood harder than metal, completely impervious to rot, storm, or salt.

Stowed in its cave-like inner hull were provisions enough for years. Great sealed tanks lined the central passage, one set holding a mountain of dry grain, another brimming with cool, fresh drinking water. Shelves lined other walls, stocked with herbal medicines, preserved essences, and extracts of hunted beasts, all the supplies the old builders had planned for a craft roaming at the sea’s mercy. Every wall, every tank partition, every surface of the seamless timber was carved in the rough, ancient art of a forgotten age, depicting every single beast that had ever lived in old Atlantis. As a hunter, I traced those carvings with fascination, many of them showing massive, long-extinct creatures I had only ever heard old storytellers whisper of, frozen in the wood before they vanished forever along with the rest of our world. A full store of sharp bronze weapons and fine carpentry tools sat stacked in a side nook too.

For weeks after that, our quiet life unfolded on the drifting ark, as the currents and the will of the gods carried us far across the empty expanse of ocean. We had no way to steer, no way to nudge the hull forward even an inch faster than the current allowed. The high gods who had plucked only the two of us from the tens of millions of lives that had died in Atlantis held full control over our fate, and we handed our future over to Them gladly. I will speak in time of the new shore we eventually reached, of the land we built our new home on, of the children born to Nais and me there. But this chronicle is written in the order of events as they unfolded, so first I must tell of how I came by the wax sheets I use to write it.

In a great iron-bound coffer set dead in the center of the ark’s floor, we found the sum of all Atlantis’s accumulated secret knowledge, written in precise, careful script: every hidden ritual, every forbidden power, every dark trick the scholars of ten generations had hoarded. I read through a portion of those scrolls in the long, quiet days of drifting, and the awful, world-breaking power they granted control over appalled me. I had seen a fraction of those powers unleashed on our homeland, watched it tear the continent apart. But these scrolls held secrets far greater: the lost mystery of life and death that the mad queen Phorenice had chased to her own destruction, and other, darker things that I will never, ever put pen to. The burden of being custodian to that terrible knowledge was too heavy to bear, the thought of it falling one day into greedy, reckless hands too horrible to face.

At last I took two hot iron pokers, and seared every wax sheet until every last character of that old forbidden writing melted away to nothing. If I did wrong, the high gods will mete out their justice in their infinite wisdom. If those great secrets were meant to endure on the earth, They can dictate them afresh to some worthy scribe of their choosing. But I burned every last trace of them there, as the ark swayed gently over the long, oily swells. Later, once we landed on new shores, I would overwrite these blank sheets only with the true story of the greed and folly that dragged great Atlantis down to its death. Nais, that I love so tenderly—
