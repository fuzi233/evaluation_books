Let us skip the history books regarding Deleglise's legendary Sunday suppers. Those tales—of the master chef, clad in spotless linen, conducting his symphony of steam and sizzle before a table glittering with cut glass and silver—are well-known enough. While the old Bonne fluttered her white cap and the guests whispered in anticipation, the air grew thick with the scent of triumph. When Deleglise finally presented his creation, the chatter would erupt like a sudden swell in the *Moonlight Sonata*, carried by artists, musicians, actors, and writers whose names still echo through the English-speaking world. Their wit and wisdom are etched into memoirs and biographies; if their jokes seem less sharp today, it is simply because fashion in humor shifts as surely as the seasons.

You, gentle reader, need not be burdened with second-hand accounts of that charmed circle. Instead, allow me to share my own brief glimpse into it. The true drama unfolded during the "Afternoon At Homes" hosted by Dan and myself on the second floor of our quiet Georgian townhouse in Queen Square. These were high-stakes affairs requiring a military precision in domestic management. Since we lacked professional staff, Dan and I adopted a system of mutual critique: I judged his cooking, and he audited my housekeeping.

"It's too much soda!" I would declare, tasting a cake with a critical frown.
"You silly Juggins!" Dan would retort. "It's supposed to taste of soda—it's a soda cake!"
"I know that," I'd insist. "But it shouldn't taste *only* of soda. Where is the cake flavor? This is just solidified Seidlitz powder. You can't serve medicinal powder for tea!"

Dan would fume, yet I remained immovable. His cookery was his Achilles' heel; he refused to admit failure, often devouring his own ghastly disasters with feigned delight. I recall him finishing a sponge cake with a hole in the center, insisting it was delicious, claiming that eating a dry sponge was akin to eating dust, and arguing that a sponge *must* be syrupy in the middle. Later, he would fall strangely silent, nursing a brandy in a wineglass.

Then it was my turn.
"Are these knives clean?" Dan would demand.
"Yes, I do."
Dan would drag his finger across a blade, creating a smear of *chiaro-scuro*.
"Not if you keep fingering them. Why don't you leave them alone and focus on your work?"
"You just wiped them."
"Well, there's no such thing as knife-powder."
"Yes, there is."
"Besides, over-cleaning ruins the edge. We'll need them razor-sharp for your lemon buns."
"Over-cleaning! You take no pride in this place!"
"Good Lord! Don't I work from dawn till dusk?"
"You lazy young devil!"
"Makes one lazy, your cooking. How can a man work when he suffers from indigestion all day?"

Eventually, Dan would be satisfied, and the house would be set in perfect order. Once lunch concluded, the maid and cook vanished, leaving two impeccably dressed gentlemen to receive our guests.

Our clientele was a eclectic mix drawn from Dan's journalistic circle and my theater friends. Occasionally, the eccentric Minikin and Jarman joined us, or even Mrs. Peedles, arriving breathless on our landing. Alone, I might have deemed them unsuitable company for our refined gatherings, but Dan, once introduced, argued that an ounce of originality outweighed a ton of convention.

"My dear Lord Chamberlain," Dan would charm his way through objections. "An ounce of originality is worth a ton of convention! Little tin ladies and gentlemen made to pattern? One finds them everywhere. Your friends would be an acquisition to any society."
"But are they quite good form?" I would hint anxiously.
"We'll forget that Mrs. Peedles runs a lodging-house in Blackfriars," Dan would counter smoothly. "We shall call her 'that dear, quaint old creature, Lady P.' A title that suits her wardrobe perfectly—a provincial actress's costume! My dear Paul, your society novelist would make a fortune with such a character. Her anecdotes feature Earl Blank and the Baroness de Dash. Editors will pay a shilling a line for them. Jarman? Yes, Jarman becomes the son of a South American millionaire. Vulgar? No, you mean *raconteur*. Minikin? He looks forty, not twenty—he shall be an eminent scientist. His glass eye is the result of a chemical experiment, a mark of distinction; his rudeness, a lovable trait. We'll have him wear a yard of red ribbon across his shirtfront and address him as 'Herr Professor.' It explains his grammar and accent quirks. They shall be our lions. You leave it to me. We'll invite common, middle-class folk to meet them."

To my terror, Dan persisted. Jarman embraced the role with gusto. As far as he knew, everyone was thoroughly deceived. The more he swaggered and boasted—the more he talked of himself, a habit he couldn't shake—the greater his success. While Dan's journalists tried to tempt him with visions of new journals and massive circulations (which amused me), my actress friends fought fiercely to capture his attention. Luring him into corners, gazing languidly, deploying every trick in the book, they quarreled amongst themselves over his favor. My conscience pricked me constantly, fearing our jest would turn into a scandal. Fortunately, Jarman possessed uncommon sense. He enjoyed the game but remained immune to the blandishments of young and old women showering him with affection that winter. Eventually, he married the eldest daughter of a small blacking factory. She was plain but pleasant, and later, she brought the factory into his possession. Now, stout and rubicund, he seems to have achieved his ideal.

Minikin required more explanation. Guests arrived with smatterings of science, insisting the Professor never discussed shop. Others flaunted unexpected German skills, unaware that Herr Professor had vowed to learn English and forbidden to speak his native tongue. When he revealed his unusual grasp of slang, or informed clamoring ladies he didn't like them "silly," or told a distinguished friend he bought his red ribbon for fourpence, we had to spin elaborate lies—that he was a scientist soured by love, or a foreigner learning the ropes. The ladies forgave him; the gentlemen grumbled it was lucky for the girls. Altogether, Minikin was a project of constant repair.

Lady Peedles, meanwhile, was unanimously declared the widow of some City knight. Behind her back, they mocked her; to her face, they were effusive. "My dear Lady Peedles" became a mantra in our rooms. At the theater, "my friend Lady Peedles" was shouted in loud tones. My social standing soared; rumors circulated that I was her presumptive heir, courting me as a gentleman of expectations.

The fishy-eyed young man became a regular. Dan won his heart by refusing to laugh at him.
"I like talking to you," the young man confessed one afternoon. "You don't go into fits of laughter when I remark it's been a fine day. Most people do. On stage, I'm a funny little devil, and I get my living that way. I saw a workman stop and stare at my photo in the lobby yesterday. He burst out laughing: 'Little—! He makes me laugh to look at him!' That's fine; I want the gallery to think I'm funny. But it annoys me when people laugh at me off-stage. If I ask someone to pass the mustard, they burst out laughing instead. I don't want people laughing at me while I eat. I want my dinner. It makes me very angry."

"I know," Dan agreed sympathetically. "The world fails to grasp that man is a collection, not a single exhibit. I recall a great Hebrew scholar at a party. During tea, a Miss Henman dropped butter. Our guest asked in a sepulchral voice, 'Why is Miss Henman like a caterpillar?' Silence followed. 'Because she makes the butter fly,' he announced. Nobody realized he was joking. The hostess, looking grave, asked, 'Oh, do you really think so?'"

"If I walked into a room and told them my mother was run over by an omnibus," the young man added, "they'd think it the funniest story they'd heard in years."

He was starring in the opera now, undeniably drawing the house, yet he was miserable.
"I am not a comic actor, really," he explained. "I could play Romeo with feeling. There is poetry in me. But with this face..."
"But aren't you sinning your mercies?" Dan countered. "Look at young Kelver. At school, his sole talent was making us laugh. His ambition was to kick a ball as well as anyone else. He could tell a good story if he wrote what the Almighty intended, instead of gloomy rigmaroles about suffering princesses in Welsh caves. I don't say it's bad, but a hundred others could write it better."

"Can't you understand?" the little man sighed. "No tragedian wishes to be a low comedian. The court fool had a great salary and likely two-thirds of the palace's brains, yet every wooden-headed man-at-arms looked down on him. Every gallery boy paying a shilling to laugh at me considers himself my intellectual superior, while a fourth-rate blank-verse spouter worships him."
