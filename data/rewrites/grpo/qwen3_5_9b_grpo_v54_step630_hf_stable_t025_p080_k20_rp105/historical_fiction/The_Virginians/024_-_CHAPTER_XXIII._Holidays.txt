The spell of Mrs. Lambert's reverie broke as swiftly as it had formed. Miss Theo and her mother were forced to admit, in the quiet corners of their hearts, that their hero was merely a man. They spoke little of it, yet the silence between them was thick with understanding; the mother, sensing her daughter's deflated spirit, offered a tenderness that bordered on adoration. "Never mind, my dear," she whispered, pressing a kiss to Theo's cheek. "Our hero has proven to be but an ordinary mortal, and no such man is worthy of my Theo. You shall find a true husband soon, if Providence has one waiting in England. I was scarcely fifteen when your father first saw me at the Bury Assembly, and I vowed then that no other man would suffice. Yet, if Heaven grants me a spouse—the finest man in the entire kingdom—surely He will bless my child equally, for she deserves a king if she dreams of one!" It was possible, Mrs. Lambert mused, that she harbored a secret fantasy: perhaps the Prince of Wales himself would ride past her gate, tumble from his steed, be ushered inside, be healed, and win the favor of her grandfather, securing a regiment for Martin Lambert and, inevitably, falling in love with Theo.

Colonel Lambert and his second daughter, Miss Hetty, stood firmly on the opposite shore of this emotional river, laughing and scoffing at the sentimentality. Mrs. Lambert was a woman perpetually engaged in matchmaking, addicted to novels that caused her to weep with excessive fervor. Not a carriage passed the gate without her expecting a suitor to alight and ring the bell. Miss Hetty, however, wielded her tongue with a saucy precision rarely seen. She peppered their conversations with references to Prussia and Persia, delivered with a pertness that bordered on the offensive. She queried whether the current King of Prussia was known as the Shaw or the Sophy, and calculated the distance from Ispahan to Saxony—a campaign her father was currently studying with maps and newspapers. She produced *The Persian Tales* from her mother's closet and slid them onto the parlor table where the family sat. "I would not marry a Persian prince," Hetty declared, "for I prefer a gentleman who does not require multiple wives simultaneously." She dubbed the young Virginian, Theo's companion, "Theo's prince," and jokingly asked her mother if she intended to claim the other visitor, the "black prince," for herself. She relentlessly teased her sister and mother on their sentimental inclinations, driving them to the brink of anger before retreating into tears, only to be coaxed back with violent kisses and affectionate coaxing.

Simple Harry Warrington remained oblivious to the whirlwind of jokes, tears, reconciliations, and matrimonial schemes that revolved around him. He was the innocent catalyst for a hundred allusions to Prussian kings and Persian sultans, yet these Parthian arrows failed to pierce his simple nature. Was a "Shaw" a title? Was a "Sophy" a lady? He likely found the notion of a man with a great black beard bearing such a name to be the height of absurdity. Like a stone dropped into a still pool, Harry entered this quiet domestic sphere, unaware of the flutter and excitement he generated among the fish and ducks of their social world. He possessed a sound opinion of himself but lacked confidence regarding women, being of an age that requires encouragement and having been raised in a modest, primitive environment concerning the opposite sex. Thus, Hetty's jests circled him like summer gnats, unnoticed and unheeded. He was not stupid, as Hetty assumed; he was simply consumed by his own honest concerns, too absorbed in his immediate reality to perceive the dramas unfolding in the drawing rooms of his hosts.

While the sisters combed their ringlets or conversed intimately in the great bed—a custom of the era where they lay side by side—Harry remained ignorant of the profound role he played in their thoughts. Three days after his arrival, the Colonel and his daughters walked with him through the magnificent park of Lord Wrotham, a space where they were free to roam. Upon reaching a sheet of water, they encountered swans, birds the young ladies were accustomed to feeding with bread. As the majestic creatures approached, Hetty cast a peculiar glance at her mother and sister, followed by a look at her father, who stood nearby, content and happy in his red waistcoat. "Mamma's swans are something like these, papa," Hetty remarked.

"What swans, my dear?" her mother asked.

"Something like, but not quite. They have shorter necks than these, and there are scores of them on our common," Hetty continued, her voice dropping slightly. "I saw Betty plucking one in the kitchen this morning. We shall have it for dinner, with apple-sauce and—"

"Don't be a little goose!" Theo interrupted.

"And sage and onions. Do you love swan, Mr. Warrington?"

"I shot three last winter on our river," the Virginian replied. "Ours are not such white birds as these—they eat very well, though."

The simple youth had no inkling that he was the allegory at that very moment, nor that Miss Hetty was weaving a fable about him. In some obscure Latin text, I have read that long before Virginia was discovered, other peoples were equally slow to comprehend the obvious.

It was a premature sentiment on Miss Theo's part—that tender flutter of the bosom she felt upon first beholding the handsome, pale, and bleeding Virginian. This was not the great passion her heart was capable of feeling. Like the birds, it had awakened and begun to sing at a false dawn. *Hop back to thy perch, and cover thy head with thy wing, thou tremulous little creature!* It was not yet light; roosting was preferable to singing. Soon, however, the morning would break, the sky would redden, and she would soar to salute the sun with her music.

A phrase from earlier in the narrative may have caught the eye of the fair and suspicious reader: "Three days after his arrival, Harry was walking with..." If he could walk, as he clearly could, what business did he have strolling with anyone but Lady Maria Esmond on the Pantiles at Tunbridge Wells? His shoulder was set, his health fully restored, and he lacked even a change of coats, owing his raiment to the Colonel. Surely, a young man in such a state had no right to linger at Oakhurst. He was bound by every tie of duty, convenience, love, and relationship—and by a washerwoman finally—to return to Tunbridge. Why did he stay, unless he was in love with one of the young ladies? Could it be that he did not wish to go? Had the reader grasped the meaning of the mystic 'S' that commenced the previous chapter, depicting Sinbad the Sailor surmounted by that odious old man of the sea? What if Harry Warrington was that sailor, and his fate that choking, deadening, inevitable old man? What if, for the past two days, he had felt those knees throttling him round the neck? If his aunt's purpose was answered, and if his former love was killed as dead by her poisonous communications as fair Rosamond was by her royal rival, then perhaps Hero was lighting the lamp and preparing supper while Leander sat comfortably with another party, never thinking of taking to the water. Since that coward's blow struck in Lady Maria's back by her own relative, surely kind hearts must pity her. I know she has faults—yes, and she wears false hair—but a woman in distress deserves pity, not laughter. Between her old aunt and her unhappy delusion, Lady Maria Esmond was having a most unpleasant time at Tunbridge Wells. No one protected her; Madam Beatrix held her entirely in thrall. Lady Maria was poor, hoping for money from her aunt, yet possessing secrets the old woman knew and brandished over her. I am quite melted thinking of her. Imagine her alone, a victim to that old woman! Picture that antique Andromeda, with her rich, flowing hair falling over her shoulders, chained to a rock on Mount Ephraim and surrendered to that dragon of a Baroness! Where is Perseus? He is not in the least hurry. The dragon has her will for day after day.

Harry Warrington, who would not have allowed his dislocated and mended shoulder to prevent him from hunting, remained day after day contentedly at Oakhurst, finding each passing day increased his affection for the kindly folk who welcomed him. Perhaps he had never, since his grandfather's death, been in such good company. His lot had previously fallen among fox-hunting Virginian squires, whose society he had endured contentedly, riding their horses, living their lives, and sharing their punch-bowls. The ladies of his home and mother's acquaintance were well-bred, decorous, and pious, yet somewhat narrow-minded. His home was a small place, defined by pompous ways, minute etiquettes, small flatteries, trivial conversations, and petty scandals. Only after leaving did he realize how confined his existence had been. He was personally free; he had dogs and horses, and could shoot and hunt for miles around. But the little lady-mother domineered at home, and within those walls, he had to submit to her influence and breathe her air.

Here, he found himself amidst a circle where everything was incomparably gayer, brighter, and freer. He lived with a man and woman who had seen the world, though they now lived retired from it, both enjoying from their earliest times not only good books but good company—those living books that are such pleasant and often profitable reading. Society possesses this great benefit: it lessens our conceit by teaching us our insignificance and introducing us to our betters. If you are a young person reading this, depend upon it, there is nothing more wholesome than to acknowledge and associate with your superiors. If I could, I would not have my son Thomas be the first Greek and Latin prize boy, the first oar, and the cock of the school. Better for his soul and body that he occupy a good place, not the first, surrounded by a fair set of competitors, receiving a good thrashing now and then, followed by a hearty shake of the hand that administered the beating. What honest man would choose to be a prince, with all society walking backwards before him, attended only by obsequious household gentlemen, and with all mankind silent except when your High Mightiness asks a question? One of the greatest benefits Harry Warrington received from this family, before whose gate Fate had shot him, was learning that he was a profoundly ignorant young fellow, and that there were many people in the world far superior to himself. Arrogant with some, in the company of his superiors, he became magnanimously docile. He had seen how faithfully he admired his brother at home and his friend, the gallant young Colonel of Mount Vernon; of the gentlemen, his kinsmen at Castlewood, he had felt himself at least their equal. But in his new acquaintances at Oakhurst, he found a man who had read far more books than Harry could judge, who had seen the world and emerged unwounded from its dangers and battles, possessing goodness and honesty written on his face and breathing from his lips—qualities for which our brave lad had always an instinctive sympathy.

As for the women, they were the kindest, merriest, and most agreeable he had yet known. They were pleasanter than Parson Broadbent's black-eyed daughter at home, whose laugh carried as far as a gunshot. They were as well-bred as the ladies of Castlewood, with the exception of Madam Beatrix, who was indeed as grand as an empress on certain occasions. Yet, after a talk with Madam Beatrix and vast amusement at her stories, the lad would depart with a bitter taste in his mouth, fancying all the world wicked. They were not squeamish; they laughed over pages of Mr. Fielding and cried over volumes of Mr. Richardson, containing jokes and incidents that would make Mrs. Grundy's hair stand on end, yet their merry prattle left no bitterness behind. Their tales of neighbors were droll, not malicious; their descriptions of the courteous salutations and cordial bounties received from the folks of the neighboring town were delightful. Truly, it is good to be with good people. How good Harry Warrington did not know at the time, perhaps, until subsequent experience revealed contrasts or caused him to feel remorse. Here was a tranquil, sunshiny day in a life destined to be agitated and stormy—a happy hour or two to remember. Little happened during these hours; it was only sweet sleep, pleasant waking, friendly welcome, and serene pastime. The gates of the old house seemed to shut the wicked world out, making the inhabitants within appear better, purer, and kinder than others. He was not in love—not the least, either with saucy Hetty or generous Theodosia. But when the time came for him to depart, he fastened on both their hands and felt an immense regard for them. He thought he should like to know their brothers, believing them to be fine fellows. As for Mrs. Lambert, I believe she was as sentimental at his departure as if he had been the last volume of *Clarissa Harlowe*.

"He is very kind and honest," Theo said gravely, looking from the terrace as they saw him, their father, and the servants riding away on the road to Westerham.

"I don't think him stupid at all now," little Hetty replied. "And, mamma, I think he is very like a swan indeed."

"It felt just like one of the boys going to school," their mother said.

"Just like it," Theo answered, sadly.

"Hush, Hetty!"
