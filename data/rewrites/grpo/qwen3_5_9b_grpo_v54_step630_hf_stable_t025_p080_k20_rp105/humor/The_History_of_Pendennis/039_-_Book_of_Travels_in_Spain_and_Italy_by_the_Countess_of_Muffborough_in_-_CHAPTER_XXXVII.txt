Let us, with a generous wave of the hand, skip over a few months of Mr. Arthur Pendennis's life. During this interval, events undoubtedly occurred that thrilled the protagonist to his marrow but would likely bore the reader of these memoirs to tears. We last saw him fully embarked on his profession as a literary hack—a title Mr. Warrington, his bosom friend, deems entirely appropriate. Now, we must confront the grim reality of the hack's existence, whether in a curacy, a marching regiment, or behind a merchant's desk. Life is a monotonous grind; one day's labor mirrors the next with tedious precision. A writer, much like any other daily toiler, often battles for his bread against time, against his own will, against failing health, or against a profound indolence. He writes in spite of his repugnance for the subject, just as a bricklayer lays stones he despises. When you must monetize Pegasus—the poetic muse—farewell to soaring flights and celestial inspiration. Pegasus now trots in harness over the stony pavement of the city, pulling a cart or a cab, rising only at advertised intervals when the spectator's money has already been paid. He pants, his sides heaving, his knees trembling, and frequently receives a sharp cut of the whip from his driver.

Yet, let us not be too prodigal of our pity for Pegasus. There is no divine exemption for the muse; he suffers from labor, illness, and decay just like any other creature of God's creation. If he gets the whip, he often deserves it. I am quite ready to protest against the sentimental doctrine that letters and genius are exempt from the prose duties of daily life, tax-paying, and bread-winning. Genius must work and pay like its neighbors.

Well, the *Pall Mall Gazette* was duly established, and Arthur Pendennis's merits as a flippant, witty, and amusing critic were recognized. He worked hard every week, preparing reviews with a certain flippancy, yet with honesty and to the best of his power. Occasionally, a historian of seventy, who had spent twenty-five years composing a tome that our young gentleman disposed of in two days' reading at the British Museum, felt unfairly treated. Or a poet, elaborating sublime sonnets for fame, was annoyed by two dozen pert lines in Mr. Pen's review, where the critic acted as a lord on the bench and the author a trembling suitor. The actors complained wofully, too; he was likely too hard on them. But little harm was done. It was a different era; there were so few great historians, poets, or actors in Pen's time that scarce any came up for judgment before his critical desk. Those who received a little whipping got what was good for them. Not that the judge was wiser than the sentenced, nor did he fancy himself so. Pen possessed a strong sense of humor and justice, lacking an overweening respect for his own works. Furthermore, he had his friend Warrington at his elbow—a terrible critic if Pen grew conceited, and more savage over Pen than over anyone else he tried at his literary assize.

Through these critical labors and occasional contributions to leading articles, Mr. Pendennis earned four pounds four shillings weekly, with no small pains. He also furnished magazines and reviews, and is believed to have been the London correspondent of the *Chatteris Champion*, which at the time featured brilliant letters from the metropolis. These labors enabled the fortunate youth to earn nearly four hundred pounds a year. On the second Christmas after arriving in London, he actually brought a hundred pounds to his mother as a dividend upon the debt he owed to Laura. Mrs. Pendennis read every word of her son's works, considering him the profoundest thinker and most elegant writer of the day. She viewed his retribution of the hundred pounds as an act of angelic virtue. She feared he was ruining his health and was delighted by his accounts of society and the great men he met. Readers who have witnessed son-worship among mothers understand this charming simplicity of love, where a mother in Somersetshire delights in hearing if John has held a brief, if Tom was invited to a ball, or if George met a famous man. In the second winter, Pen came for a very brief space, cheering the widow's heart and lightening the lonely house at Fairoaks. Helen had her son all to herself; Laura was visiting old Lady Rockminster; the folks of Clavering Park were absent. The few old friends, led by Doctor Portman, treated him with marked respect. Between mother and son, it was pure fondness, confidence, and affection. It was the happiest fortnight of the widow's life, perhaps of both theirs. The holiday vanished too quickly, and Pen returned to the busy world, leaving the gentle widow alone again. She sent Arthur's money to Laura; I do not know why this young lady chose to leave home when Pen was coming, nor whether he was more piqued or relieved by her absence.

By this time, through his own merits and his uncle's introductions, Pen was well-introduced into London, known in both literary and polite circles. His fashionable reputation stood him in good stead; he was considered a gentleman of good present means and better expectations, who wrote for his pleasure—the ultimate recommendation for a young aspirant. Bacon, Bungay, and Co. were proud to accept his articles; Mr. Wenham asked him to dinner; Mr. Wagg looked upon him favorably. They reported how they met him at fashionable houses, where he was welcome because his appearance and address were good, and because he had a character for being clever. Finally, he was asked to one house because he was seen at another. Thus, no small variety of London life presented itself to the young man. He became familiar with everyone from Paternoster Row to Pimlico, feeling as much at home at Mayfair dining-tables as at the tavern boards where his companions of the pen assembled.

Full of high spirits and curiosity, easily adapting himself to all he met, the young fellow pleased himself in this strange jumble of men. He would breakfast at Mr. Plover's in the morning alongside a Peer, a Bishop, a parliamentary orator, two blue ladies of fashion, a popular preacher, the author of the latest novel, and the newest lion imported from Egypt or America. Then, he would quit this distinguished society for the back room of the newspaper office, where pens, ink, and wet proofs awaited him. Here sat Finucane, the sub-editor, with the latest news from the Row. Soon Shandon would arrive, nodding to Pen, and begin scribbling his leading article at the other end of the table, flanked by a pint of sherry that the attendant boy silently brought whenever he appeared. Or Mr. Bludyer's roaring voice would be heard in the front room, where that truculent critic would impound books on the counter despite the timid remonstrances of Mr. Midge, the publisher. After looking through the volumes, Bludyer would sell them at his bookstall, drink and dine upon the produce in a tavern box, and then call for ink and paper to "smash" the author of his dinner and the novel. Towards evening, Mr. Pen would stroll toward his club to pick up Warrington for a constitutional walk. This exercise freed the lungs and created an appetite for dinner, after which Pen had the privilege of bowing at pleasant houses or exploring the town for amusement. There was the Opera, the Eagle Tavern, a ball in Mayfair, or a quiet night with a cigar, a book, and a long talk with Warrington. Or perhaps a wonderful new song at the Back Kitchen. At this stage of his life, Mr. Pen beheld all sorts of places and men, and very likely did not realize how much he enjoyed himself until long after, when balls gave him no pleasure, farces made him not laugh, tavern jokes produced no excitement, and the loveliest dancer never stirred him from his chair after dinner. At his present mature age, all these pleasures are over; the times have passed away. Bludyer will no more bully authors or cheat landlords. Shandon, the learned and thriftless, sleeps his last sleep. They buried honest Doolan the other day; never will he cringe or flatter, never pull a long-bow or empty a whisky-noggin again.

The London season was blooming in full vigor. Fashionable newspapers abounded with information regarding grand banquets, routs, and balls enlivening the polite world. Our gracious Sovereign held levees and drawing-rooms at St. James's. The bow-windows of the clubs were crowded with the heads of respectable, red-faced, newspaper-reading gentlemen. Thousands of carriages trailed along the Serpentine. Squadrons of dandy horsemen trampled over Rotten Row. Everybody was in town, and of course, Major Arthur Pendennis, who was somebody, was not absent.

With his head tied up in a smart bandana handkerchief and his meagre carcass enveloped in a brilliant Turkish dressing-gown, the worthy gentleman sat by his fireside on a certain morning, letting his feet gently simmer in a bath while he took his early cup of tea and perused the *Morning Post*. He could not have faced the day without his two hours' toilet, his early tea, or his *Morning Post*. I suppose nobody in the world, not even Morgan, knew how feeble and ancient the Major was growing, or what numberless little comforts he required.

If men sneer at the artifices of an old beauty—her paint, perfumes, ringlets, and stratagems to reconstruct the charms bereft by time—they should presume that ladies are not ignorant that men are vain as well. How does old Blushington keep that constant rose-tint on his cheeks? Where does old Blondel get the preparation that makes his silver hair pass for golden? Have you ever seen Lord Hotspur get off his horse when he thinks nobody is looking? Taken out of his stirrups, his shiny boots can hardly totter up the steps of Hotspur House. He is a dashing young nobleman on Rotten Row, but on foot, what an old, old fellow! Did you ever form an idea of Dick Lacy in a natural state, without his stays? All these men are objects the observer of human life may contemplate with profit, much like the most elderly Belgravian Venus or inveterate Mayfair Jezebel. An old reprobate daddy-longlegs who has never said his prayers (except perhaps in public) for fifty years; an old buck who still clings to the habits of youth as his feeble grasp of health allows; who has given up the bottle but sits with young fellows over it telling naughty stories upon toast-and-water; who has given up beauty but still talks about it wickedly as the youngest roue in company—such an old fellow, if any parson in Pimlico or St. James's were to order the beadles to bring him into the middle aisle and preach about him, could be turned to a wholesome use for once in his life. But we are wandering from our text: the honest Major sits with his feet cooling in the bath. Morgan takes them out of that place of purification, dries them daintily, and proceeds to set the old gentleman on his legs with waistband, wig, starched cravat, and spotless boots and gloves.

It was during these hours of the toilet that Morgan and his employer had their confidential conversations, for they did not meet much at other times—the Major abhorring the society of his own chairs and tables in his lodgings. Morgan, his master's toilet over and letters delivered, had his time very much on his own hands. He bestowed this spare time among the valets and butlers of the nobility, his acquaintances. Morgan Pendennis, as he was styled (for gentlemen's gentlemen are called by such compound names in private circles), was a frequent and welcome guest at some of the very highest tables in town. A member of two influential clubs in Mayfair and Pimlico, he knew the whole gossip of the town and could entertain his master agreeably during the two hours of toilet conversation. He knew a hundred tales and legends regarding persons of the very highest ton, whose valets canvass their august secrets just as our parlour-maids and dependants discuss our characters, stinginess, generosity, pecuniary means, and domestic tiffs. If I leave this manuscript open on my table, I have not the slightest doubt Betty will read it, and they will talk it over in the lower regions tonight. Tomorrow she will bring in my breakfast with a face of entire imperturbable innocence, that no mortal could suppose her guilty of playing the spy. If you and the Captain have high words, the circumstances of the quarrel will be discussed with impartial eloquence over the kitchen tea-table. Nothing is secret. Take it as a rule that John knows everything. As in our humble world so in the greatest: a duke is no more a hero to his valet-de-chambre than you or I. His Grace's Man at his club, in company with other Men of equal social rank, talks over his master's character and affairs with the ingenuous truthfulness which befits gentlemen met in confidence. Who is a niggard and screws up his money-boxes? Who is in the hands of money-lenders? Who is intimate with whose wife? Who wants whom to marry her daughter, and which he won't, no not at any price? All these facts gentlemen's confidential gentlemen discuss confidentially, known and examined by every person who claims rank in genteel society. In a word, if old Pendennis himself was said to know everything, being admirably scandalous and delightfully discreet, it is but justice to Morgan to say that a great deal of his master's information was supplied by his valet, who went out and foraged knowledge for him. Indeed, what more effectual plan is there to get a knowledge of London society than to begin at the foundation—that is, at the kitchen floor?

So Mr. Morgan and his employer conversed as the latter's toilet proceeded. There had been a drawing-room the previous day, and the Major read among the presentations that of Lady Clavering by Lady Rockminster, and of Miss Amory by her mother Lady Clavering.
