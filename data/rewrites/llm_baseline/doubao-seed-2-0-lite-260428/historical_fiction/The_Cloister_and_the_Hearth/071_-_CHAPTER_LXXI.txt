As goes the race of leaves, so too goes the race of men. That year, two fates played out on 15th century soil: one great life budded completely unnoticed in a tailor’s cramped, sunlit cottage in Rotterdam, while another grand, world-renowned figure fell to earth amid all the blare of court trumpets and public ceremony.

Philip, Duke of Burgundy, Earl of Holland, and half a dozen other titled honours besides, lay sick in Bruges. In that rough age, paupers sickened and recovered or died as Nature pleased, but woe betide the wealthy: for every one patient illness managed to kill, three were finished off by the bloodthirsty, untested tomfoolery of court physicians. The Duke’s complaint, nameless to 15th century healers, we now know as diphtheria. It was, and is, a terribly weakening malady, and the Duke was already well advanced in years. Taken together, the attending doctor saw fit to bleed him until the old man turned icy cold, his breath coming in shallow, faint gasps.

The physician then reached for the hidden, arcane secrets of his craft. “This is grave,” he announced. “Flay an ape incontinent, and press its warm pelt straight to the Duke’s breast to draw the sickness out!” Officers of state scattered seven different ways across the Bruges court and grounds, hunting high and low for an ape to counteract this reckless folly of their own species. To their utter fury, the duke’s menagerie held no apes at all: there were buffaloes, lizards, captured Turks, spotted leopards, every strange and unreasonable beast under the sun but the exact one they needed.
“Why, there used to be an ape about just last week,” one officer protested. “I saw him standing right where you are.” There had been, sure enough — but the castle mastiff had mangled the sprightly little creature for stealing his supper, and so perfectly fulfilled the unspoken human precept, “Be of your own century!”

In this emergency, the seneschal cast his despairing gaze around the crowd of panicking courtiers, and his eyes lit up. A hopeful gleam shot through them. “Here is this,” he murmured sotto voce, nodding at a figure strutting unawares across the tiles. “Surely this will serve: he is altogether apelike, doublet and hose apart.”
“Nay,” the Chancellor snapped peevishly. “Princess Marie would hang us all. She dotes on that fool.” That fool was none other than our old friend Giles, preening in a new suit of cloth of gold, entirely ignorant of the fate that had nearly befallen him.

The doctor grew impatient at the delay, and bade them flay a dog instead. “A dog is next best to an ape — only it must be a dog of one solid, uniform colour.” So they flayed a liver-coloured hound, and clapped its still palpitating warm skin to their sovereign’s chest. Philip the Good died beneath it.

Thus scientifically disposed of, the Duke left thirty-one children behind him: of that number, exactly one, somehow, was legitimate, and took the throne in his stead. The good Duke made provisions for nineteen of the other thirty; the remaining eleven were left to shift for themselves as best they could. Flemish chroniclers of the time claimed the deceased prince traced his line all the way back to the kings of Troy, through Thierry of Aquitaine, Chilperic, Pharamond, and all the old kings of Franconia. But that was no special distinction at all. Not a single prince of that era has the narrator ever been able to discover who did not claim descent from Troy. “Priam” was medieval shorthand for “Adam.”

The good Duke’s body was carried all the way to Burgundy, and laid in a grand black marble mausoleum at Dijon. All Holland rang with the news of his death, not one soul there guessing that a figure far more lasting in fame had been born on their own territory that very same year. That judgment has long since been reversed. Men now travel to gaze at the humble tailor’s cottage in Rotterdam, where the greatest birth of the 15th century took place. As the old song says, what house the good Duke died in “no one knows and no one cares.”

And why? Dukes Philip the Good come and go, and leave mankind not a halfpenny wiser, nor better, nor altered in the slightest than they found it. But when, once in three hundred years, such a child is born to the world as Margaret’s son, lo! a human torch lit by fire from heaven blazes forth, and *Fiat Lux* — “Let there be light” — thunders out from pole to pole.
