"The old fellow has vanished; he headed for Arezzo first thing the next morning. No doubt the stench of the French made him sick after being their prisoner. I stopped by the hospital to ask if those monkish cooks had managed to determine whether he was still in his right mind. They told me he showed no signs of madness—only a total refusal to engage, acting as though he were planting a vineyard fifty miles away. He was a mysterious old tiger. I would have liked to know more about him."

These words were spoken by Piero di Cosimo within the confines of Nello's shop on the twenty-fourth of November, a mere week since the French entered Florence. The air was thick with anticipation; a party of six or seven gathered at the unusual hour of three in the afternoon, driven by the city's feverish expectation of a decisive political turning point. Every public seat was occupied, and every shopkeeper without a wife or deputy stood at his threshold, thumbs hooked in his belt, watching the street. The thoroughfares were dotted with artisans pausing or drifting lazily like floating debris, poised to surge forward impetuously at the slightest provocation.

Nello had been strumming his lute while half-sitting on the shop counter, his gaze fixed on the piazza.

"Ah," he declared, setting down the instrument with emphasis, "I would not have missed that spectacle of the French soldiers waddling in their broad shoes after their fleeing prisoners for a gold florin! That is the price of leaving my shop to shave magnificent chins. It is always so: whenever I abandon this navel of the world, something dramatic happens in my piazza."

"Yes, you should have been there," Piero replied in his sharp, cutting tone, "just to witness your favorite Greek trembling as though Satanasso had seized him. I delight in seeing your ever-ready Messeri caught in a sudden gale, forced to reveal their linings despite themselves. What color do you suppose a man's liver turns when he resembles a bleached deer the moment a chance stranger grabs him?"

"Piero, keep your vinegar for your own eggs!" Nello retorted. "What is there against my *bel erudito* for looking startled when he felt claws upon him and saw an unchained madman at his side? Your scholar is not like those beastly Swiss and Germans, whose heads are built only for battering-rams and whose appetites are so voracious they consider eating a cannonball before breakfast acceptable. We Florentines value qualities in a man beyond that vulgar commodity called bravery, which is acquired by hiring dullards at a set price per dozen. Tell you what: once men realized they possessed more brains than oxen, they set the oxen to pull their plows; and when we Florentines discovered we had superior intellects to others, we set them to fight for us."

"Treason, Nello!" a voice cried from the inner room. "That is not the doctrine of the State. Florence is sharpening its weapons, and the last authenticated vision from the Frate depicted Mars standing atop the Palazzo Vecchio, his arm resting on the shoulder of San Giovanni Battista, who offered him a piece of honeycomb."

"It is well, Francesco," Nello said calmly. "Florence possesses a few thick skulls suitable for bombarding Pisa; there will remain the finer spirits at home to perform the thinking and the shaving. As for our Piero here, if he prizes valor so highly, let him take his largest brush as a weapon and his palette as a shield, and challenge the widest-mouthed Swiss he can find in the Prato to single combat."

"Go on, Nello," Piero grumbled. "Thy tongue runs on as usual, like a mill when the Arno is swollen—whether there is grain or not."

"Excellent grain, I tell thee. It would be as logical to expect a weathered painter like thee to enjoy having a javelin thrust into thy belly as to expect a man whose wits have been honed by the classics to relish having his handsome face clawed by a wild beast."

"There you go, assuming people will put their legs into a sack because you call it a pair of hose," Piero countered. "Who mentioned a wild beast, or an unarmed man charging into battle? Fighting is a trade, and it is not mine. I would be a fool to chase danger, but I could face it if it came to me."

"How is it you fear the thunder so, then, my Piero?" Nello pressed, determined to corner the accuser. "You ought to understand why one man is shaken by what seems trivial to another—you who hide with the rats the moment a storm breaks."

"That is because I possess a particular sensitivity to loud noises; it has nothing to do with my courage or my conscience."

"Well, and Tito Melema may have a peculiar sensitivity to being grabbed unexpectedly by prisoners who have fled from French soldiers. Men are born with antipathies; I myself cannot abide the smell of mint. Tito was born with an aversion to old prisoners who stumble and clutch. Ecco!"

A general laugh erupted at Nello's defense, revealing that Piero's dislike for Tito was not shared by the company. The painter, wearing an unreadable grimace, pulled tow from his *scarsella* and stuffed his ears in indignant contempt, while Nello continued triumphantly.

"No, my Piero, I cannot afford to have my *bel erudito* disparaged; nor can Florence, with her scholars molting off her at the tender age of forty. Our Phoenix Pico has gone straight to Paradise, as the Frate informs us; and the incomparable Poliziano, not two months ago, departed to—well, let us hope he is not among the eminent scholars in the Malebolge."

"By the way," interjected Francesco Cei, "have you heard that Camilla Rucellai has surpassed the Frate in prophecy? Two years ago she foretold that Pico would die in the time of lilies. He died in November. 'Not at all the time of lilies,' scoffed the critics. 'Go to!' replies Camilla; 'I meant the lilies of France, and they seem close enough under your nostrils.' I say, 'Euge, Camilla!' If the Frate can prove any of his visions fulfilled as accurately, I shall declare myself a Piagnone tomorrow."

"You are somewhat too flippant regarding the Frate, Francesco," observed Pietro Cennini, the scholar. "We are all indebted to him these past weeks for preaching peace and the cessation of party quarrels. Those of small discernment would gladly see the people slip the Frate's leash just now. And if the Most Christian King remains obstinate today and refuses to sign what is fair and honorable for Florence, Fra Girolamo is the man we must trust to bring him to reason."

"You speak truth, Messer Pietro," Nello agreed. "The Frate is one of the firmest nails Florence has to hang upon—at least, that is the opinion of the most respectable chins I have the honor of shaving. Yet young Messer Niccolò remarked the other morning—and doubtless Francesco means the same—there is as wonderful a power of stretching the meaning of visions as in Dido's bull's hide. It seems a dream may signify whatever follows it. As our Franco Sacchetti says, a woman dreams overnight of a serpent biting her, breaks a drinking cup the next day, and cries out, 'Look you, I thought something would happen—it is plain now what the serpent meant.'"

"But the Frate's visions are not of that sort," Cronaca insisted. "He does not merely predict what will happen—that the Church will be scourged and renovated, and the heathens converted—he declares it shall happen quickly. He is no slippery pretender who provides loopholes for himself; he is—"

"What is this? What is this?" Nello exclaimed, leaping from the counter and thrusting his head out the door. "People are streaming into the piazza, shouting. Something must have occurred in the Via Larga. Aha!" he burst forth with delighted astonishment, stepping out laughing and waving his cap.

The rest of the company hastened to the door. News from the Via Larga was precisely what they had awaited. Yet they were not little surprised by the manner of its arrival. Carried above the shoulders of the crowd on a bench apparently snatched from the street sat Tito Melema, smiling in amused resignation at the compulsion under which he labored. His cap had slipped from his head, hanging by the *becchetto* loosely wound around his neck. As he spotted the group at Nello's door, he raised his fingers in beckoning recognition. In the next instant, he leaped from the bench onto a cart laden with bales standing in the broad space between the Baptistery and the steps of the Duomo, while the people swarmed around him with the noisy eagerness of poultry awaiting feed.

Silence fell when he began to speak in his clear, mellow voice.

"Citizens of Florence! I have no warrant to tell the news except your will. But the news is good, and will harm no man in the telling. The Most Christian King is signing a treaty that is honorable to Florence. But you owe it to one of your citizens, who spoke a word worthy of the ancient Romans—you owe it to Piero Capponi!"

Immediately a roar of voices erupted. "Capponi! Capponi! What said our Piero?" "Ah! He would not stand being sent from Herod to Pilate!" "We knew Piero!" "Orsù! Tell us, what did he say?"

When the roar of insistence subsided slightly, Tito began again.

"The Most Christian King demanded a little too much—he was obstinate—and said at last, 'I shall order my trumpets to sound.' Then, Florentine citizens! Your Piero Capponi, speaking with the voice of a free city, replied, 'If you sound your trumpets, we will ring our bells!' He snatched the copy of the dishonorable conditions from the secretary's hands, tore it into pieces, and turned to leave the royal presence."

Again there were loud shouts, followed by impatient demands for more.

"Then, Florentines, the high majesty of France felt, perhaps for the first time, all the majesty of a free city. And the Most Christian King himself hastened from his place to call Piero Capponi back. The great spirit of your Florentine city accomplished its work by a great word, without need of the great actions that lay ready behind it. And the King has consented to sign the treaty, which preserves both the honor and the safety of Florence. The banner of France will float over every Florentine galley in sign of amity and common privilege, but above that banner will be written the word 'Liberty!'"

"That is all the news I have to tell; is it not enough?—since it is for the glory of every one of you, citizens of Florence, that you have a fellow-citizen who knows how to speak your will."

As the shouts rose again, Tito looked around with inward amusement at the various crowd, each elated by the notion that Piero Capponi had somehow represented them—that he was the mind whereof Capponi was the mouthpiece. He enjoyed the humor of the incident, which had suddenly transformed him, an alien and a friend of the Medici, into an orator tickling the ears of the people blatant for some unknown good they called liberty. He felt quite glad that he had been seized and hurried along by the crowd as he emerged from the palace in the Via Larga with a commission to the Signoria. It was very easy, very pleasant, this exercise of speaking to general satisfaction: a man who knew how to persuade need never be in danger from any party; he could convince each that he was feigning with all the others. The gestures and faces of weavers and dyers were certainly amusing when viewed from above in this manner.

Tito was beginning to feel at ease in his armor, and at this moment was entirely unconscious of it. He stood with one hand holding his recovered cap and the other at his belt, a light of complacent smile in his long, lustrous eyes, as he made a parting reverence to his audience before springing down from the bales—when suddenly his glance met that of a man who possessed none of the amusing aspect of the exulting weavers, dyers, and woolcarders. This man's face was clean-shaven, his hair close-clipped, and he wore a decent felt hat. A single glance would hardly have sufficed to assure anyone but Tito that this was the face of the escaped prisoner who had seized him on the steps. But to Tito, it came not simply as the face of the escaped prisoner, but as a face with which he had been familiar many years prior.

It seemed all compressed into a second—the sight of Baldassarre looking at him, the sensation shooting through him like a fiery arrow, and the act of leaping from the cart. He would have leaped down in the same instant, whether he had seen Baldassarre or not, for he was in a hurry to reach the Palazzo Vecchio; this time he had not betrayed himself by look or movement, and he said inwardly that he should not be taken by surprise again; he should be prepared to see this face rise up continually like the intermittent blotch that appears in diseased vision. But this reappearance of Baldassarre, so much more in his own likeness, tightened the pressure of dread: the idea of his madness lost its likelihood now that he was shaven and clad like a decent, though poor, citizen. Certainly, there was a great change in his face; but how could it be otherwise? And yet, if he were perfectly sane—in possession of all his powers and all his learning—why was he lingering in this way before making known his identity? It must be for the sake of making his scheme of vengeance more complete. But he did linger: that at least gave an opportunity for flight. And Tito began to think that flight was his only resource.
