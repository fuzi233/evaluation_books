"The daughter of debate,
That discord still doth sow,
Shall reap no gain where former rule
Hath taught still peace to grow.
No foreign banish'd wight
Shall anker in this port
Our realm it brooks no stranger's force;
Let them elsewhere resort."

Amyas had returned to the quietude of home, and for a year, the chronicles of his life remained largely unmarked by event. Yeo, the old sailor, had settled into the role of major domo, trailing Amyas like a grey wolfhound and spending his evenings at the hearth, his Bible resting on his knee while his hands crafted endless trifles for the household, particularly for Ayacanora. She received these offerings with a pout, chided by Mrs. Leigh, before accepting them to store away unseen. Her heart was set on becoming an English maid; she followed Mrs. Leigh through the kitchen and still-room, eager to learn the arts of sewing and housekeeping. Having grown accustomed to the savage's boundless hospitality, she now sought to understand the English concept of giving to those who could offer nothing in return. She watched Mrs. Leigh minister to the poor and saw Amyas distribute wealth to build almshouses for weary mariners, interpreting these acts as thank-offerings to God. Though her initial understanding of sacrifice leaned toward the bloody rituals of the Aztecs, Mrs. Leigh gently explained that giving to the poor was giving to the Great Spirit Himself, who dwelt within them all. Ayacanora absorbed this lesson, working tirelessly for the elderly women of Northam, her beauty radiant and beloved, though she often dressed in Spanish finery that dazzled the congregation. When the vicar protested the disturbance, she defiantly declared the garments belonged to the "Piache" (the conjuror) and insisted he wear them, eventually securing a magnificent mantle for the altar-cloth, a blaze of emerald and crimson that had once adorned an Aztec prince.

While Amyas watched her with a mixture of amusement and pride, Ayacanora's behavior toward the local ladies proved equally challenging. They came to congratulate Mrs. Leigh and glimpse the "fair savage," only to be snubbed by the girl, who refused to be a spectacle. Tears flowed when Mrs. Leigh questioned her conduct, for Ayacanora clung to her, declaring she would rather be a kitchen slave than return to the "bad people" of the forest. Yet, as months passed, the storms of her temper subsided into a rational, if unhappy, calm.

Clues to her origins began to surface. She recognized houses and red cattle, puzzled by the smallness of English sheep compared to the great camel-like llamas of Peru. Her greatest delight was a horse; after failing to steal one from Amyas, she successfully bartered her finery with the vicar for a pony, though the vicar's honesty thwarted the deal until Amyas purchased a jennet for her. One evening at Stow, after a day of riding, she awoke in a nightmare of the "Old ape of Panama," recounting a vision of being dragged into a courtyard, mounted on a horse, and transported by an Indian over mountains into a dark wood where she reverted to a little savage child. Mrs. Leigh accepted this as a divine revelation of her identity: a child exposed by a jealous stepfather, likely Don Xararte, somewhere in the Andes.

Further confirmation arrived through the story of an Indian named Raleigh, brought home by Sir Richard Grenville from Virginia. Baptized in Bideford as the first "Red man" on British soil, he was later listed in the parish registers as "Rawly, a Winganditoian" in 1589, though his entry noted he had died. The records suggested a tragic fate for the indigenous people, arriving at civilization too late to be saved, yet too late to prevent their decay.

While Amyas remained idle, England did not. The year 1588 approached, a climactic turning point foretold by astronomers. Tensions rose as Spain, emboldened by the Pope and Jesuit propaganda, prepared a massive armada to crush the "heretical" English and restore Catholic rule. The Jesuits preached the imminent doom of England, citing the sorrows of Mary, Queen of Scots, and the supposed wrongs of the Virgin Mary, urging a crusade against Elizabeth. Yet, the English people, driven by a fierce patriotism and a pragmatic refusal to sacrifice their ancestral lands, rejected the call for invasion. Instead, they resolved that Mary, Queen of Scots, must die, or the commonweal of England perish.

Drake wrote to Amyas, urging him to join the fleet to "singe the king of Spain's beard," promising hard blows and hard money. Amyas replied, bound by his mother's command to wait, but expressed his desire to hunt down Don Guzman, a Spanish commander rumored to be on one of the galleons. Though he obeyed his mother, his mood darkened with sullen malice against the Spaniard, a shadow that Salvation Yeo's fanaticism only deepened.

Finally, in January 1588, Sir Richard Grenville arrived in Bideford, bringing news of the Queen's fierce resolve. He revealed that the council had decided on a naval defense rather than land fortifications, arguing that England's true rampart was its fleet. Raleigh, now a key figure in the council, was to oversee the training of the militia. Amyas and Grenville spent the following months preparing seven ships in Bideford river, with Amyas serving as admiral. However, this fleet was destined not for Virginia, but for the defense of England itself.

On a long June evening in 1588, Mrs. Leigh sat by the window, stitching, while Ayacanora struggled with a book of heroes. Amyas paced the garden, his brow furrowed in calculation, ignoring the admiring glances of the children and Ayacanora. Mrs. Leigh spoke of heroism, pausing before a portrait of a lost loved one, and Ayacanora asked if she could ever be an English girl. Amyas entered, teasing her about sailing to the West Indies and seeing Indians again. His jests touched a nerve; Ayacanora burst into tears, hiding her face in Mrs. Leigh's lap. Amyas, realizing the depth of her pain, offered a gift, only for her to tear it apart, declaring she hated him and the memory of his past. Mrs. Leigh pleaded with her son to stop tormenting the child, revealing that Ayacanora loved him with a woman's devotion, a love born of gratitude and shared trauma. Amyas, blinded by prejudice and the taint of her Spanish blood, dismissed her feelings, fearing the inheritance of her family's cruelty. He confessed that he could not bear the thought of raising children with such bloodlines, recalling the look in her eyes as she stood over the captain of the galleon with a smoking knife.

Suddenly, a servant rushed in with a letter from Sir Richard. Amyas opened it and laughed aloud. "The Armada is coming!" he cried, his wish fulfilled. The letter confirmed the Spanish fleet had sailed from Lisbon. Amyas threw his arms around his mother, apologizing for his earlier despair. With renewed purpose, he dashed to Bideford, finding Sir Richard rallying the local gentry. The merchants and captains, inspired by Amyas's spirit, pledged their ships and cargo, replacing Virginian goods with powder and shot. Within a week, the seven ships were ready. On June 21st, the squadron sailed from Appledore, led by Amyas Leigh, to join the main fleet at the Sound. As Mrs. Leigh watched from the shore, Ayacanora stood beside her, tears streaming down her face. "And he never bade me good-bye," she sobbed. Mrs. Leigh comforted her, and in that shared sorrow, the savage faded, and the true woman rose anew, united in the sacred fire of grief and hope.
