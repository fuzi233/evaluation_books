The morning sun cast a silver ribbon across the water, meeting the green glitter of the sea just as the boat docked at Harwich. From the vessel poured a swarm of holidaymakers, indistinguishable from the throng, yet among them walked the man we must follow. He was unremarkable, save for a jarring contrast between the festive gaiety of his attire and the stern gravity of his face. He wore a pale grey jacket, a white waistcoat, and a silver straw hat trimmed with a grey-blue ribbon. His lean, dark face ended in a curt black beard that evoked a Spanish count or an Elizabethan ruff. He smoked a cigarette with the air of a man who had all the time in the world, showing no sign that the grey jacket concealed a loaded revolver, the waistcoat hid a police card, or the hat sheltered one of the most formidable intellects in Europe. This was Aristide Valentin, the head of the Paris police and the world's most celebrated investigator, arriving from Brussels to London to execute the arrest of the century.

His target was Flambeau, the colossus of crime. The police forces of three nations had finally tracked the fugitive from Ghent to Brussels, and then to the Hook of Holland. It was widely believed Flambeau intended to exploit the confusion of the Eucharistic Congress currently convening in London, perhaps disguising himself as a minor clerk or secretary. Yet, Valentin knew that with Flambeau, certainty was a luxury no one possessed.

Years had passed since the criminal ceased his reign of terror following the death of Roland, leaving a profound quietude over the earth. But in his prime, Flambeau was a figure as monumental and international as the Kaiser himself. Every morning, newspapers announced his latest escape, achieved only by committing another extraordinary crime. A Gascon of gigantic stature and daring, he was rumored to have overturned judges to "clear their minds" and run down the Rue de Rivoli with policemen under each arm. While his physical feats were often bloodless and undignified, his true crimes were ingenious robberies of staggering complexity. He had once run a dairy company in London with no cows, serving customers by simply moving milk cans from their doors to his own. He had maintained a secret correspondence with a young lady by photographing his messages onto microscope slides. He had repainted street numbers in the dead of night to lure travelers into traps and invented portable pillar-boxes to intercept postal orders. Furthermore, despite his massive size, he was a startling acrobat, capable of leaping like a grasshopper and climbing trees like a monkey. Valentin knew that finding Flambeau would be only the beginning of their adventure.

The problem remained: how to find him? Valentin's strategy was still evolving. There was one trait Flambeau could not disguise: his immense height. If Valentin's sharp eye had spotted a tall apple-woman, a grenadier, or a duchess, he would have arrested them instantly. However, throughout his journey, he had seen no one who could possibly be a disguised Flambeau. He had checked the passengers on the boat and the few people he had picked up at Harwich. There was a short railway official, three market gardeners, a very short widow, and finally, a diminutive Roman Catholic priest traveling from a small Essex village.

Valentin had initially scoffed at the priest, laughing at the absurdity of the man. He was the epitome of Eastern flatness, with a face as round and dull as a Norfolk dumpling and eyes as empty as the North Sea. He carried several brown paper parcels he seemed incapable of managing, a large shabby umbrella that constantly slipped from his grasp, and a return ticket he could not decipher. With a moon-calf simplicity, he explained to everyone in the carriage that he must be careful because he possessed something made of real silver "with blue stones" in his parcels. This quaint blend of Essex dullness and saintly simplicity amused the French detective endlessly until the priest miraculously arrived at Tottenham with all his parcels, only to return for his umbrella. Even then, Valentin, with good nature, warned him not to advertise his silver. Yet, amidst this amusement, Valentin kept his eyes peeled for someone else: anyone, rich or poor, male or female, standing well over six feet tall. Flambeau was four inches taller than that.

Arriving at Liverpool Street, Valentin felt secure that he had not missed the criminal. He visited Scotland Yard to organize assistance, then lit another cigarette and strolled through the streets of London. Pausing in a quiet, typical square beyond Victoria, he observed the tall, flat houses that looked simultaneously prosperous and uninhabited. In the center lay a shrubbery as deserted as a green Pacific islet. One side of the square rose higher like a dais, broken by a restaurant that seemed to have wandered from Soho. It was an unreasonably attractive object, featuring dwarf plants in pots and long, striped blinds of lemon yellow and white. It stood high above the street, accessed by a flight of steps that ran up to the front door like a fire-escape. Valentin stood before the yellow-white blinds, smoking and contemplating them.

To Valentin, the most incredible thing about miracles was that they happened. Clouds sometimes formed the shape of a human eye; trees occasionally stood in the exact shape of a question mark. Nelson died in the instant of victory; a man named Williams accidentally murdered a man named Williamson. Life contained an element of elfin coincidence that the prosaic often missed. Wisdom, as Poe had noted, must reckon on the unforeseen.

Aristide Valentin was unfathomably French. His intelligence was not that of a "thinking machine"—a phrase of modern fatalism—but of a thinking man. He was a plain man who achieved wonders through plodding logic and clear, commonplace French thought. The French electrify the world not by paradox, but by carrying out truisms to their extreme limits. Because Valentin understood reason, he understood its boundaries. Here, he had no strong first principles. Flambeau had been missed at Harwich; if he was in London, he could be anything from a tall tramp on Wimbledon Common to a toastmaster at the Hotel Metropole. In this state of uncertainty, Valentin had a unique method.

When the reasonable path was blocked, he followed the unreasonable. Instead of checking banks or police stations, he systematically went to the wrong places, knocking on empty houses and turning down dead ends. He defended this madness logically: if one had a clue, the direct route was best; but with no clue, the erratic path offered the chance that an oddity catching the pursuer's eye was the same one that had caught the fugitive's. Something about the flight of steps to the restaurant roused his romantic fancy, and he resolved to strike at random. He ascended the steps and ordered a cup of black coffee.

It was mid-morning, and he had not eaten. Adding a poached egg to his order, he musedly shook white sugar into his coffee, thinking of Flambeau. He recalled how the criminal had escaped via nail scissors, a burning house, unpaid postage, and a telescope aimed at a comet. Valentin believed his detective brain was equal to the criminal's, yet he acknowledged the disadvantage. "The criminal is the creative artist; the detective only the critic," he muttered with a sour smile, lifting his cup slowly and setting it down quickly. He had put salt in it.

Looking at the vessel from which the powder came, he noted it was unmistakably a sugar-basin. He wondered why they kept salt in it. Checking for orthodox vessels, he found two salt-cellars quite full. He tasted the contents; it was sugar. Refreshed by this discovery, he looked around the restaurant for other signs of this singular artistic taste—perhaps sugar in the salt-cellars and salt in the sugar-basin. Aside from a dark splash on a white-papered wall, the place was neat and ordinary. He rang the bell for the waiter.

When the fuzzy-haired, blear-eyed official hurried up, Valentin asked him to taste the sugar and judge its quality. The waiter yawned suddenly and woke up.

"Do you play this delicate joke on your customers every morning?" Valentin inquired. "Does changing the salt and sugar never pall on you as a jest?"

The waiter stammered, assuring him it was a curious mistake. He picked up the sugar-basin and the salt-cellar, his face growing bewildered. Finally, he excused himself and returned with the proprietor, who also examined the containers with confusion. Suddenly, the waiter became inarticulate with a rush of words.

"I zink," he stuttered eagerly, "I zink it is those two clergy-men."

"What two clergymen?" Valentin asked.

"The two clergymen," the waiter said, "that threw soup at the wall."

"Threw soup at the wall?" Valentin repeated, sensing an Italian metaphor.

"Yes, yes," the attendant pointed excitedly at the dark splash on the wall. "They threw it over there."

Valentin turned to the proprietor, who confirmed the story. Two quiet, respectable clergymen had arrived early. One paid and left; the other lingered. Just before stepping out, he deliberately picked up his half-empty soup cup and threw the contents slap onto the wall. The waiter had rushed out in time to find the wall splashed and the shop empty. It was cheeky, but the men had gone round the corner into Carstairs Street.

Valentin was on his feet, hat settled and stick in hand. In the universal darkness of his mind, he could only follow the first odd finger that pointed. Paying his bill, he clattered through the glass doors and swung into the adjacent street.

Fortunate that his eye remained cool, he noticed a shop-front flash by and went back to inspect it. It was a popular greengrocer's, displaying goods openly with ticketed prices. In the two most prominent compartments were heaps of oranges and nuts. On the nut heap lay a cardboard scrap reading, "Best tangerine oranges, two a penny." On the oranges was the equally clear description, "Finest Brazil nuts, 4d. a lb." Valentin felt he had seen this subtle humor recently. He drew the attention of the red-faced fruiterer to the inaccuracy. The shopman said nothing but sharply corrected the cards. Leaning on his cane, Valentin asked a question in experimental psychology.

"Why," he pursued gaily, "why are two tickets wrongly placed in a greengrocer's shop like a shovel hat that has come to London for a holiday? Or, to make myself clear, what is the mystical association connecting the idea of nuts marked as oranges with the idea of two clergymen, one tall and the other short?"

The shopman's eyes bulged like a snail's; he seemed ready to attack. Stammering angrily, he declared he would knock the heads of any parsons who upset his apples again.

"Indeed?" Valentin asked sympathetically. "Did they upset your apples?"

"One of 'em did," the heated shopman admitted. "Rolled 'em all over the street. I'd 'ave caught the fool but for havin' to pick 'em up."

"Which way did these parsons go?" Valentin asked.

"Up that second road on the left-hand side, and then across the square," the shopman replied promptly.

"Thanks," Valentin said, vanishing like a fairy. On the other side of the square, he found a policeman. "This is urgent, constable; have you seen two clergymen in shovel hats?"

The policeman chuckled heavily. "I 'ave, sir; and if you arst me, one of 'em was drunk. He stood in the middle of the road that bewildered that—"

"Which way did they go?" Valentin snapped.

"They took one of them yellow buses over there," the man answered, pointing to the vehicle heading to Hampstead.

Valentin produced his official card and demanded two men to accompany him. Crossing the road with contagious energy, he joined an inspector and a plainclothesman on the opposite pavement.

"Well, sir," the inspector began with smiling importance.

Valentin pointed suddenly with his cane. "I'll tell you on the top of that omnibus," he said, darting across the traffic. When they sank panting on the top seats, the inspector remarked they could go four times faster in a taxi.

"Quite true," Valentin replied placidly, "if we only had an idea of where we were going."

"Well, where are you going?" the inspector stared.

Valentin smoked frowningly for a few seconds, then removed his cigarette. "If you know what a man's doing, get in front of him; but if you want to guess what he's doing, keep behind him. Stray when he strays; stop when he stops; travel as slowly as he. Then you may see what he saw and may act as he acted. All we can do is to keep our eyes skinned for a queer thing."

"What sort of queer thing?" the inspector asked.

"Any sort of queer thing," Valentin answered, falling into obstinate silence.

The yellow omnibus crawled up the northern roads for what seemed like hours. Valentin refused to explain further, and his assistants grew silent doubts, perhaps mixed with a desire for lunch. The long roads of North London suburbs stretched out like an infernal telescope. London died away in draggled taverns and dreary scrubs, only to be reborn in blazing high streets and blatant hotels. It was like passing through thirteen separate vulgar cities. As winter twilight threatened, the Parisian detective sat silent and watchful. By the time they left Camden Town, the policemen were nearly asleep until Valentin leapt erect, struck a hand on each shoulder, and shouted for the driver to stop.

They tumbled down the steps, disoriented, only to find Valentin triumphantly pointing at a window on the left. It was a large window in a gilt and palatial public-house, labeled "Restaurant." Like the rest of the façade, it was frosted glass, but in the middle was a big, black smash, like a star in the ice.

"Our cue at last," Valentin cried, waving his stick. "The place with the broken window."

"What window? What cue?" his assistant asked. "Why, what proof is there that this has anything to do with them?"

The Secret Garden

Aristide Valentin, the Chief of the Paris Police, was late for his dinner, and his guests began to gather in the entrance hall before he arrived. They were reassured by his confidential servant, Ivan, an old man with a scar and a face as grey as his moustaches, who always sat at a table in the hall—a space hung with weapons. Valentin's house was peculiar, perhaps even celebrated, for its architecture held a peculiar police value: there was no ultimate exit from the house except through the front door, guarded by Ivan and the armoury. The garden behind was large and elaborate, with many exits from the house into it, but no exit from the garden to the world outside. A tall, smooth, unscalable wall with special spikes ran all around it, creating a perfect trap for a man in whom a hundred criminals had sworn to kill.

Ivan explained that their host was detained for ten minutes, making last arrangements about executions—duties repulsive to him yet performed with precision. Valentin was ruthless in pursuit but mild in punishment, a humanitarian freethinker who made mercy even colder than justice. When he finally arrived, dressed in black with a red rosette, he went straight to his study, which opened onto the grounds. He locked his official box and stood at the open door, regarding the garden with a wistfulness unusual for such a scientific nature. Recovering quickly from this mood, he entered the drawing-room to find his guests. He saw Lord Galloway, the choleric English Ambassador; his wife, slim and superior; their daughter, Lady Margaret, pale and elfish; the opulent Duchess of Mont St. Michel and her daughters; Dr. Simon, the French scientist with his characteristic wrinkles; and Father Brown of Cobhole. Most notably, he saw Commandant O'Brien, an Irish gentleman in the French Foreign Legion, a dashing yet melancholy figure who had known the Galloways in his youth.

Valentin, however, was not specially interested in them. He was expecting Julius K. Brayne, a multi-millionaire whose colossal endowments to various religions had amused the press. Brayne was a man ready to pour money into any intellectual vessel, particularly one he deemed "progressive." His solid appearance in the room was as decisive as a dinner bell. He was a huge fellow, clad in complete evening black, with white hair brushed back like a German's and a face red, fierce, and cherubic, marked by a dark tuft under his lip.

The Galloways were genial enough, provided Lady Margaret did not take the arm of the adventurer O'Brien. She had not, having gone in with Dr. Simon. Yet Lord Galloway was restless, stung by the thought that O'Brien might be signaling to his daughter. After dinner, when the younger men melted away to smoke in the conservatory, Galloway grew undiplomatic. He argued with Brayne and Valentin until boredom set in, then sought the drawing-room. He lost his way for several minutes, hearing the voices of the doctor and priest arguing about science and religion. When he finally opened the salon door, he saw only one thing: Commandant O'Brien and Lady Margaret were absent.

Driven by a senile and passionate suspicion, Galloway rushed to the back of the house, where he found a servants' entrance opening onto the garden. The moonlight, now clear after the storm, lit up the lawn. There, striding toward the study door, was a tall figure in blue: Commandant O'Brien. He vanished through the French windows, leaving Galloway in an indescribable temper. The moonlight maddened the diplomatist, who tripped over a stone and began running and bellowing. His shouts brought Dr. Simon to the study door.

"A corpse in the grass—a blood-stained corpse!" Galloway cried.

Valentin entered, his demeanor instantly transforming from concerned host to businesslike detective. "Strange," he said, "that I should hunt mysteries all over the earth and now one settles in my own back-yard." Guided by Galloway, they found the body sunken in deep grass: a very tall, broad-shouldered man lying face-down, his head severed from his body by a clean cut. A scarlet serpent of blood crawled from the wound.

"He is dead," Dr. Simon confirmed, lifting the head. It was a ponderous, yellow face, resembling a wicked Roman emperor. Underneath the body, they saw the white gleam of a shirt-front defaced with blood. Valentin examined the ground, finding only a few chopped twigs. "Twigs," he said gravely, "and a total stranger with his head cut off."

Suddenly, Galloway pointed to a small figure near the garden wall. It was Father Brown, who meekly remarked, "There are no gates to this garden, do you know." Valentin, though cross at the sight of the cassock, agreed. He proposed keeping the matter private for now, ordering everyone to stay in the house until noon while he investigated.

Ivan, the scarred detective, arrived with eager eagerness to examine the remains. He lifted the head and gasped, "Do you know this man, sir?" Valentin replied indifferently, "No." They carried the corpse to a sofa in the study and returned to the drawing-room. Valentin questioned the group, noting that Mr. Brayne and Commandant O'Brien were missing. He summoned them.

O'Brien entered defiantly, claiming he had been admiring the moon. When Valentin asked for his sword, Ivan returned with an empty scabbard. Lady Margaret then stepped forward, declaring that O'Brien had been asking her to marry him and that she swore he was innocent. Lord Galloway tried to intimidate her, but she retorted that if he had murdered someone, who else could have witnessed it?

Then Father Brown spoke, asking about the length of Mr. Brayne's cigar. Valentin, realizing the significance, ordered Ivan to fetch Brayne. Instead, Ivan reported that Brayne had fled the house, throwing his hat, coat, and a blood-streaked cavalry sabre fifty yards up the road to Paris. Valentin returned the sword to O'Brien, clearing him of suspicion. The atmosphere lightened, though the riddle of the death remained.

O'Brien and Dr. Simon discussed the impossibilities: why use a clumsy sabre when a knife would suffice, why no outcry, how the victim entered the sealed garden, and how Brayne escaped. Dr. Simon added a fifth difficulty: the cuts on the neck appeared to be made after death, suggesting the head was severed post-mortem.

Father Brown arrived and announced a second murder: another beheading, with the second head found bleeding into the river nearby. O'Brien felt sick at the thought of Brayne's monomania. In the library, they found the second head, battered and wet, alongside the first. Father Brown examined it closely, noting a specific chip in the left ear. "He had exactly that chip," he said. "It is Brayne."

Valentin scoffed, suggesting Brayne might be converting to Catholicism to gain the priest's favor. O'Brien intervened, warning Valentin against slander. Valentin dismissed them, promising to write to the authorities. Ivan then revealed that the body in the garden was identified as Arnold Becker, a German criminal recently executed, and his twin brother, Louis Becker, had also been guillotined.

Father Brown suddenly clutched his temples, overwhelmed by the realization. "I see half," he cried, "but I only see half." He composed himself and addressed the group, answering their five questions. He explained that the murderer needed a sabre to distract the victim with tricks before the silent slash. He claimed there was no strange man in the garden. When asked how Brayne entered and exited, Brown replied, "He didn't get out of the garden... Not completely."

Finally, Brown revealed the truth: the body in the garden was not Becker. The murderer had hacked off Becker's head, flung it over the wall, and attached the severed head of Louis Becker to the body of Arnold Becker, creating a false identity. The real murderer was Aristide Valentin. He had argued with Brayne, lured him into the sealed garden, and committed the murder using Becker's head to frame the situation. Valentin had taken Becker's head home in his official box.

Driven by this revelation, they rushed into Valentin's study. The great detective sat at his desk, appearing too occupied to hear their entrance. Dr. Simon noticed a small box of pills at Valentin's elbow and realized the truth: Valentin was dead in his chair, a suicide born of his fanaticism to break the superstition of the Cross.

The Twelve True Fishermen were a select club, and if you ever managed to gain entry to the Vernon Hotel for their annual dinner, you would notice a peculiar detail: the evening coats of the members were green, not black. Should you, with the audacity of a fool, ask why, the answer would likely be a defensive claim that they wished to avoid being mistaken for waiters. You would then retreat, crushed, leaving behind an unsolved mystery and a tale worth telling.

Imagine, instead, meeting a mild, hard-working little priest named Father Brown. If you asked him what the most singular stroke of luck in his life had been, he might reply that it occurred at the Vernon Hotel, where he averted a crime and perhaps saved a soul merely by listening to a few footsteps in a passage. He might be proud of this wild guess, but since it is highly unlikely you will ever rise high enough to join "The Twelve True Fishermen" or sink low enough to find Father Brown among the slums, you will never hear the story unless I tell it to you now.

The Vernon Hotel was an institution born of an oligarchical society gone mad on good manners. It was a topsy-turvy commercial enterprise that paid not by attracting people, but by turning them away. In the heart of a plutocracy, tradesmen had become cunning enough to be more fastidious than their customers, creating difficulties so that wealthy clients would spend money and diplomacy overcoming them. If there were a fashionable hotel in London where no man under six feet could enter, society would meekly assemble parties of tall men to dine there. The Vernon Hotel stood inconspicuously in Belgravia, a small and inconvenient place whose very inconveniences served as walls protecting a particular class. Its most vital inconvenience was that practically only twenty-four people could dine there at once. The only big table was the celebrated terrace table, open to the air on a veranda overlooking one of London's most exquisite old gardens. Thus, even the twenty-four seats could only be enjoyed in warm weather, making the experience both difficult and intensely desired.

The owner was a Jew named Lever, who made nearly a million by making it difficult to get in. He combined this exclusivity with the most careful polish in performance. The wines and cooking rivaled the best in Europe, and the attendants mirrored the fixed mood of the English upper class. There were only fifteen waiters in total, each known to the proprietor like the fingers on his hand. It was easier to become a Member of Parliament than a waiter there. Each was trained in terrible silence and smoothness, acting as a gentleman's servant. Indeed, there was generally at least one waiter to every gentleman who dined.

The Twelve True Fishermen would not have consented to dine anywhere else, insisting on luxurious privacy and being utterly upset by the thought of any other club dining in the same building. On the occasion of their annual dinner, they exposed all their treasures, including a celebrated set of fish knives and forks—the insignia of the society—exquisitely wrought in silver in the form of a fish, each hilt loaded with a large pearl. These were laid out for the fish course, which was always the most magnificent part of the repast. The society had vast ceremonies but no history or object; that was its aristocracy. You did not have to be anything to be one of the Twelve, unless you were already a certain sort of person, in which case you never even heard of them. It had existed for twelve years, with Mr. Audley as president and the Duke of Chester as vice-president.

How I came to know of this appalling hotel, or how Father Brown found himself there, is simple. There is an aged rioter and demagogue who breaks into refined retreats preaching that all men are brothers, and wherever this leveller went, Father Brown followed. One afternoon, an Italian waiter was struck down by a paralytic stroke. Lever, marveling at such superstitions, sent for the nearest Popish priest. Father Brown, with a meek impudence he would show equally in Buckingham Palace, asked for a room and writing materials. Lever was torn between kindness and the dislike of any difficulty or scene. The presence of an unusual stranger in his hotel that evening was like a speck of dirt on something just cleaned. There were fifteen waiters and twelve guests; finding a new guest would have been startling. Moreover, the priest's appearance was second-rate and his clothes muddy. Lever hit on a plan to cover the disgrace: he assigned Father Brown a small private room without other outlets, situated between the office and the cloak room, a space usually reserved for delicate matters like lending a duke a thousand pounds.

By the time Father Brown reached the final paragraphs of his document, his thoughts began to wander. The gathering gloom sharpened his sense of sound. Outside, he heard a recurrent noise, a patter of feet passing the door. In a hotel, this was unlikely, yet the footsteps were strangely odd. Taken as a whole, they defied classification as either regular or irregular. First came a long rush of rapid little steps, like a light man winning a race, followed by a slow, swinging stamp occupying about the same time. Then the run returned, then the thud. It was certainly the same pair of boots, distinguished by a small, unmistakable creak. Father Brown's head, which could not help asking questions, almost split. Why should a man run to walk? Or walk to run? His brain grew dark as his room.

Yet, in the blackness of his cell, his thoughts became vivid. He visualized the fantastic feet capering along the corridor in unnatural attitudes. Was it a heathen dance? A new scientific exercise? The slow step certainly wasn't the proprietor's; men of his type walked with a rapid waddle or sat still. Nor was it a servant. That heavy yet springy step, with a careless emphasis, belonged to only one animal on earth: a gentleman of western Europe who had never worked for his living. Just as he came to this certainty, the step changed to a quicker one, running past the door feverishly, almost as if walking on tiptoe. Yet it was not associated with secrecy, but with something he couldn't remember. Suddenly, he sprang to his feet with a new idea. He walked to the door, tried the lock on the office (it was locked), and looked at the window, now a square pane of purple cloud cleft by the livid sunset. For an instant, he smelled evil, as a dog smells rats.

Rationality regained supremacy. He remembered the proprietor's instruction to lock the door and that Lever would return later. He resolved to finish his work, catching the last stormy evening light. He had written for twenty minutes, bending closer to the paper, when suddenly he sat upright. He had heard the strange feet again. This time, they had a third oddity. Previously, the man had walked with levity and lightning quickness, but this time he ran. Swift, soft, bounding steps came along the corridor like the pads of a fleeing panther. Whoever was coming was a strong, active man in still yet tearing excitement. Yet, when the sound swept up to the office like a whispering whirlwind, it suddenly changed again to the old slow, swaggering stamp.

Father Brown flung down his paper and went into the cloak room on the other side. The attendant was absent. Groping through a grey forest of overcoats, he found the dim cloak room opened onto the lighted corridor in the form of a counter. A light above the semicircular arch threw theatrical illumination on the man standing outside. He was an elegant man in very plain evening dress, tall but with an air of not taking up much room, capable of sliding along like a shadow. His face was swarthy and vivacious, foreign. His figure was good, his manners confident, though his black coat was a shade below his dignity, bulging and bagging oddly. The moment he caught sight of Brown's black silhouette against the sunset, he tossed down a scrap of paper with a number and called out, "I want my hat and coat, please; I find I have to go away at once."

Father Brown took the paper and went to fetch the coat. Meanwhile, the stranger laughed, "I haven't got any silver; you can keep this," throwing down half a sovereign. In that instant, Father Brown lost his head. In such moments, he put two and two together and made four million. Often the Catholic Church did not approve of this, nor did he himself, but it was real inspiration.

"I think, sir," he said civilly, "that you have some silver in your pocket."

The tall gentleman stared. "Hang it," he cried, "if I choose to give you gold, why should you complain?"

"Because silver is sometimes more valuable than gold," said the priest mildly, "that is, in large quantities."

The stranger looked curiously up the passage, then back at Brown, then at the window. He seemed to make up his mind. He put one hand on the counter, vaulted over as easily as an acrobat, and towered above the priest, putting one tremendous hand upon his collar. "Stand still," he said in a hacking whisper. "I don't want to threaten you, but—"

"I do want to threaten you," said Father Brown in a voice like a rolling drum, "I want to threaten you with the worm that dieth not, and the fire that is not quenched."

"You're a rum sort of cloak-room clerk," said the other.

"I am a priest, Monsieur Flambeau," said Brown, "and I am ready to hear your confession."

The stranger stood gasping, then staggered back into a chair.

The soup course was light and unpretending, a simple vigil for the feast of fish to come. The talk was that strange, slight talk which governs the British Empire in secret. Cabinet ministers were alluded to by their Christian names with bored benignity. The Radical Chancellor was praised for his minor poetry; the Tory leader was discussed and praised as a Liberal. Politicians seemed very important, yet anything seemed important about them except their politics. Mr. Audley, the chairman, was an amiable, elderly man who still wore Gladstone collars, a symbol of phantasmal yet fixed society. He had never done anything, not even anything wrong. He was simply in the thing. The Duke of Chester, the vice-president, was a pleasant youth with flat, fair hair and enormous estates. In public, he was brilliant when he made a joke and able when he didn't. In private, he was frankly silly, like a schoolboy.

There were twenty-four seats at the terrace table, and only twelve members. The chairman sat in the center, the vice-president at the right-hand end. When the guests entered, it was custom for all fifteen waiters to stand lining the wall like troops presenting arms, while the fat proprietor bowed with radiant surprise. Along one such thoroughfare stands a house that seems to beckon the passerby with a silent, inexplicable allure. Quinton, a man who drank and bathed in color, had arranged his home to reflect his chaotic genius. The boat was designed for two souls and carried only the absolute essentials according to Flambeau's pragmatic philosophy: tins of salmon for sustenance, loaded revolvers for combat, a bottle of brandy for fainting spells, and a priest, presumably for the event of death. Like a true philosopher, Flambeau had no grand destination for his holiday, yet he possessed a valid excuse. He harbored a half-purpose, pursued with just enough seriousness to make its success a crown for the trip, but with just enough detachment that its failure would not ruin the experience. Years prior, during his reign as a king of thieves in Paris, he had received countless letters of approval, denunciation, or love. One, however, remained etched in his memory. The Thames, that singular smoky sparkle of confusion and transparency, shifted from its usual grey haze to a glittering brilliance as the sun climbed to its zenith over Westminster. The building itself was a marvel of modern engineering, a steel skeleton housing a labyrinth of telephones and elevators, yet it remained unfinished, its upper and lower floors echoing with emptiness. But it was the object affixed to the exterior of the floor above Flambeau's that arrested attention. It was a colossal, gilded effigy of a human eye, radiating golden beams, dominating the facade like a warning from the heavens.

"What on earth is that?" Father Brown asked, halting in his tracks.

"Oh, a new religion," Flambeau laughed, dismissing the spectacle. "One of those modern sects that absolve you of sin by claiming you never sinned in the first place. Reminds me of Christian Science. A fellow calling himself Kalon—though I doubt 'Kalon' is his real name—has taken the flat above me. He styles himself the New Priest of Apollo and worships the sun."

"Let him look out," Father Brown murmured. "The sun was the cruellest of all gods. But what does that monstrous eye signify?"

"It's their theory," Flambeau explained casually. "That a man can endure anything if his mind is perfectly steady. Their symbols are the sun and the open eye; they claim a truly healthy man could stare directly into the solar disc."

"If a man were truly healthy," Father Brown countered, "he wouldn't bother staring at it."

"Well, that's all I know," Flambeau shrugged. "They claim to cure all physical ailments."

"And what of the one spiritual disease?" Father Brown asked, his tone sharpening with curiosity.

"And what is that?"

"Oh, thinking oneself quite well," his friend replied.

Flambeau, however, was more captivated by the quiet intensity of the office below him than the flamboyant temple above. He was a man of clear logic, oscillating between Catholicism and atheism, skeptical of bright, pallid new faiths. The office downstairs was kept by two sisters, both slight and dark, dressed in severe black with masculine collars. The elder, Pauline Stacey, possessed a profile as sharp and clean-cut as the edge of a blade, her eyes blazing with the cold brilliancy of steel rather than diamonds. She was a woman who seemed to cleave her way through life, a modern heiress who had traded castles for a harsher, utilitarian existence. She held her vast fortune not for romantic indulgence, but for practical social engineering, funding a typewriting empire and various women's leagues. Her younger sister, Joan, followed her lead with a dog-like devotion, a tragic shadow to Pauline's rigid light.

Pauline's temper was notorious. On their first meeting, she had refused the lift-boy's assistance, blasting Flambeau with a tirade against those who sought to return to romance and reject mechanical science. She believed everyone should master machines as she mastered the elevator, viewing doctors' aids like glasses or crutches as badges of cowardice. "I was free-born!" she had declared, her voice ringing with abstract anger. "Doctors stick on legs and arms as if we were born cripples. I will open my eyes and stare at the sun whenever I choose."

Flambeau, charmed by her fiery independence, had noted the spiritual bond forming between the floors. Above him lived Kalon, a magnificent creature worthy of Apollo himself. With a golden beard, strong blue eyes, and a mane flung back like a lion's, he possessed an animal beauty heightened by genuine intellect. Despite the vulgarity of his surroundings—a brass plate on a street-facing wall, a clerk in a common suit—Kalon radiated an oppressive, inspiring presence. Three times daily, he stepped onto his balcony to address the sun, his litany piercing the busy streets of Westminster.

It was during one of these noon prayers that the disaster struck. "She was dead," Flambeau confirmed, his voice hollow. "Suicide? Impossible for such an optimist. Murder? Who could have done it?"

Kalon's clerk confirmed that the prophet had been on his balcony for fifteen minutes prior to the crash. Flambeau, turning to Father Brown with a desperate look, asked, "Then, if he was up there all along, who could have done it?"

"No," Brown answered softly. "It is the speech for the defense."

He spoke with the authority of an orator, admitting that Pauline was his love and bride, and that she had written a will leaving him half a million. "Penal servitude will only be like waiting for her at a wayside station," he mused. "The gallows will only be going to her in a headlong car."

"But," Kalon continued, his voice dropping to a conspiratorial whisper, "I believe I do know how my unhappy friend came by her death. Certain adepts have attained the power of levitation... Poor Pauline was of an impulsive temper. She thought herself deeper in the mysteries than she was. In some ecstasy of noble thoughts, she attempted the miracle. Her will, or faith, must have failed her at the crucial instant, and the lower law of matter had its horrible revenge. It is suicide. A heroic failure for the advance of science."

Flambeau, defeated by the sheer force of the argument, accepted the explanation. "If that is so, sir, you need do no more than take the testamentary paper..."

The forest stood as a thousand grey arms, its million fingers silvered by frost. Above, a sky of dark green-blue slate held stars bleak and brilliant, like splintered ice. The countryside, thick with wood and thin with life, was stiff with a bitter, brittle cold. The black hollows between the tree trunks resembled bottomless caverns of a Scandinavian hell, a place of incalculable chill. Even the square stone tower of the church appeared northern to the point of heathenry, a barbaric sentinel among the sea rocks of Iceland. It was a strange night for anyone to wander a churchyard, yet perhaps it was worth the exploration.

The church rose abruptly from the ashen wastes of the forest, a hump of grey-green turf. Most graves lay on a slant, and the path to the church climbed as steeply as a staircase. At the summit, in the one flat, prominent spot, stood the monument for which the place was famous. It contrasted sharply with the featureless graves surrounding it, crafted by one of modern Europe's greatest sculptors, yet his fame was eclipsed by the man he immortalized. Starlight traced the massive metal figure of a soldier lying down, his strong hands sealed in eternal worship, his great head pillowed upon a gun. The venerable face was whiskered in the old, heavy style of Colonel Newcome, while the uniform suggested the simplicity of modern war. Beside his right hip lay a sword, its tip snapped off; to his left rested a Bible. On glowing summer afternoons, wagonettes filled with Americans and cultured suburbans would visit this sepulchre, yet even then, the vast forest land with its dumpy dome felt oddly dumb and neglected. In this freezing mid-winter darkness, one might feel abandoned to the stars alone. Yet, in the stillness of the stiff woods, a wooden gate creaked, and two dim figures dressed in black climbed the path to the tomb.

The frigid starlight was so faint that nothing could be traced of them except that both wore black; one was enormously big, the other, by contrast, startlingly small. They approached the great graven tomb of the historic warrior and stood for minutes staring at it. Hundreds of grey, ghostly trees passed before the little priest answered. He bit his finger reflectively. "The mystery is one of psychology. Or rather, two psychologies. "Go on," said the large man impatiently as the other bit his finger again.

"Sir Arthur St. Clare was a soldier of the old religious type—the type that saved us during the Mutiny. He was always more for duty than for dash; with all his personal courage, he was a prudent commander, particularly indignant at needless waste of soldiers. Yet in this last battle, he attempted something a baby could see was absurd. One need not be a strategist to see it was as wild as wind; just as one need not be a strategist to keep out of the way of a motor-bus. Well, that is the first mystery: what had become of the English general's head? The second riddle is, what had become of the Brazilian general's heart? President Olivier might be called a visionary or a nuisance, but even his enemies admitted he was magnanimous to the point of knight-errantry. Almost every prisoner he had captured had been set free or loaded with benefits. Men who had wronged him came away touched by his simplicity. One of the wisest men in the world acted like an idiot for no reason. One of the best men in the world acted like a fiend for no reason. That's the long and the short of it; and I leave it to you, my boy."

"No, you don't," said the other with a snort. "I leave it to you; and you jolly well tell me all about it."

"It's not fair to say the public impression is just what I've said without adding that two things have happened since. They threw a new kind of darkness. First, the family physician of the St. Clares quarrelled with the family and published violent articles claiming the late general was a religious maniac. The story fizzled out; everyone knew St. Clare had eccentricities of puritan piety. The second incident was more arresting. In the regiment that made that rash attempt at the Black River was Captain Keith, engaged to St. Clare's daughter and later her husband. He was captured by Olivier, treated bounteously, and promptly set free. Twenty years later, as Lieutenant-Colonel Keith, he published an autobiography called 'A British Officer in Burmah and Brazil.' Where the reader looks for the mystery of St. Clare's disaster, he finds these words: 'Everywhere else in this book I have narrated things exactly as they occurred... The exception I shall make is in this matter of the defeat by the Black River; and my reasons, though private, are honourable and compelling. General St. Clare has been accused of incapacity on this occasion; I can at least testify that this action, properly understood, was one of the most brilliant and sagacious of his life. President Olivier by similar report is charged with savage injustice. I think it due to the honour of an enemy to say that he acted on this occasion with even more than his characteristic good feeling. To put the matter popularly, I can assure my countrymen that St. Clare was by no means such a fool nor Olivier such a brute as he looked. The tall Flambeau faced round and stared at him.

"Yours is a clean story," cried Father Brown, deeply moved. "A sweet, pure, honest story, as open and white as that moon. Madness and despair are innocent enough. There are worse things, Flambeau."

Flambeau looked up wildly at the moon, and from where he stood, one black tree-bough curved across it exactly like a devil's horn.

"Father—father," cried Flambeau, stepping more rapidly forward, "do you mean it was worse than that?"

"Worse than that," said Paul like a grave echo. And they plunged into the black cloister of the woodland, which ran by them in a dim tapestry of trunks, like one of the dark corridors in a dream.

"Well, well," cried Flambeau irritably. "What does he do?"

"He grows a forest to hide it in," said the priest in an obscure voice. "A fearful sin."

The Three Tools of Death

Father Brown possessed a peculiar wisdom, born of both vocation and experience, that every man stands dignified in death. Yet, even he felt a jarring dissonance when roused at dawn to learn that Sir Aaron Armstrong had been murdered. There was something absurd, almost grotesque, about secret violence inflicted upon a figure so thoroughly entertaining and popular that he bordered on the legendary. Sir Aaron was a man of such boundless mirth that his demise felt like hearing of Sunny Jim hanging himself or Mr. Pickwick dying in Hanwell. Though a philanthropist who tackled society's darker corners, Sir Aaron approached them with a style so bright it was nearly blinding. His speeches were cascades of anecdotes and raucous laughter; his health was robust to the point of bursting; his ethics were pure optimism; and his approach to the Drink Problem—his favorite topic—was marked by that immortal, monotonous gaiety typical of the prosperous total abstainer.

The story of his conversion was well-known on puritan platforms: how, as a boy, he had drifted from Scotch theology to Scotch whisky, only to rise above both and become, as he modestly claimed, what he was. Yet, seeing his wide white beard, cherubic face, and sparkling spectacles at countless dinners, it was difficult to believe he had ever been as morbid as a dram-drinker or a Calvinist. He was, one felt, the most seriously merry of mankind.

He resided on the rural fringe of Hampstead in a handsome, modern tower, narrow and high, whose steepest side overhung a railway embankment, shaken daily by passing trains. Sir Aaron boasted he had no nerves. But that morning, the tables had turned; it was the house that delivered a shock to the train.

The engine slowed and halted just beyond the angle where the house impinged upon the sharp turf slope. The arrest of mechanical things is usually slow, but the living cause here had been instantaneous. A man dressed entirely in black, down to the dreadful detail of black gloves, appeared on the ridge above the engine, waving his gloved hands like a sable windmill. This alone might not have stopped a lingering train, but from him came a cry described later as utterly unnatural. It was a shout so distinct in its horror that one could hear the terror even without catching the words. The word was "Murder!"

The engine driver swore he would have stopped regardless, but the sound confirmed the halt. Once arrested, the tragedy revealed itself to the casual observer. On the green bank stood Magnus, Sir Aaron's manservant, clad in black. The baronet had often laughed at the gloomy attendant's gloves, but no one laughed now. Below, rolled nearly to the bottom of the slope, lay the body of an old man in a yellow dressing gown with a vivid scarlet lining. A scrap of rope was caught around his leg, presumably from a struggle. There was a smear of blood, little of it, but the body was bent into a posture impossible for any living creature. It was Sir Aaron Armstrong.

Moments later, a large, fair-bearded man emerged, identified by travelers as the dead man's secretary, Patrick Royce, once famous in Bohemian circles. He echoed the agony of the servant with a vague but convincing intensity. Then came the third figure, Alice Armstrong, the daughter, tottering and waving into the garden as the engine-driver finally released the brakes, panting on to summon help from the next station.

Father Brown was summoned rapidly at the request of Patrick Royce, an Irishman of the casual Catholic variety who only remembers his faith when in deep trouble. The promptness of Father Brown's arrival was aided by the fact that one of the official detectives, Merton, was a friend and admirer of the unofficial "Flambeau," and one cannot be a friend of Flambeau without hearing endless tales of the priest. Thus, as Merton led the little priest across the fields, their conversation was more confidential than expected between strangers.

"As far as I can see," Merton said candidly, "there is no sense to be made of it. Nobody can be suspected. Magnus is a solemn old fool, far too foolish to be an assassin. Royce has been the baronet's best friend for years, and his daughter undoubtedly adored him. Besides, it's all too absurd. Who would kill such a cheery old chap? Who could dip their hands in the gore of an after-dinner speaker? It would be like killing Father Christmas."

"Yes, it was a cheery house," Father Brown agreed. "It was a cheery house while he was alive. Do you think it will be cheery now he is dead?"

Merton started, regarding his companion with an enlivened eye. "Now he is dead?" he repeated.

"Yes," the priest said stolidly. "He was cheerful. But did he communicate his cheerfulness? Frankly, was anyone else in the house cheerful but he?"

A window in Merton's mind opened to a strange light of surprise. He had often visited the Armstrongs on police business, and now, recalling it, he saw the house as it truly was: depressing. The rooms were high and cold, the decoration mean and provincial, the draughty corridors lit by electricity bleaker than moonlight. Though the old man's scarlet face and silver beard had blazed like a bonfire in each room, he left no warmth behind. His vitality was so exuberant he needed no stoves; he carried his own heat. But the other inmates were shadows of their lord. Magnus, with his monstrous black gloves, was almost a nightmare. Royce, the secretary, was a big bull of a man in tweeds, yet his straw-colored beard was startlingly salted with grey, and his broad forehead barred with premature wrinkles. He was good-natured, but it was a sad, heart-broken sort of nature, the air of a man who had failed in life. As for the daughter, it was almost incredible she was his child; she was so pallid and sensitive, graceful yet quivering like an aspen leaf. Merton had often wondered if she learned to tremble at the crash of the passing trains.

"You see," Father Brown said, blinking modestly, "I'm not sure that Armstrong cheerfulness is so very cheerful for others. You say nobody could kill such a happy old man, but I'm not sure; *ne nos inducas in tentationem*. If ever I murdered somebody," he added simply, "I dare say it might be an Optimist."

"Why?" cried Merton, amused. "Do you think people dislike cheerfulness?"

"People like frequent laughter," answered Father Brown, "but I don't think they like a permanent smile. Cheerfulness without humor is a very trying thing."

They walked in silence along the windy grassy bank until they came under the shadow of the tall Armstrong house. Father Brown said suddenly, as if discarding a troublesome thought: "Of course, drink is neither good nor bad in itself. But I can't help feeling that men like Armstrong want an occasional glass of wine to sadden them."

Their superior, Detective Gilder, stood on the bank waiting for the coroner, talking to Patrick Royce, whose towering shoulders and bristly beard dominated the smaller man. Royce walked with a powerful stoop, performing his clerical duties like a buffalo drawing a go-cart. He raised his head with unusual pleasure at the sight of the priest and took him aside. Meanwhile, Merton addressed Gilder with respectful but boyish impatience.

"Well, Mr. Gilder, have you got much farther with the mystery?"

"There is no mystery," Gilder replied, looking under dreamy eyelids at the rooks.

"Well, there is for me, at any rate," said Merton, smiling.

"It is simple enough, my boy," observed the senior investigator, stroking his grey, pointed beard. "Three minutes after you went for Mr. Royce's parson, the whole thing came out. You know that pasty-faced servant in the black gloves who stopped the train?"

"I should know him anywhere. Somehow he rather gave me the creeps."

"Rather a cool criminal, don't you think, to escape by the very train that went off for the police?" Gilder drawled.

"You're pretty sure he really did kill his master?"

"Yes, my son, I'm pretty sure," Gilder replied dryly, "for the trifling reason that he has gone off with twenty thousand pounds in papers from his master's desk. The only difficulty is how he killed him. The skull seems broken by some big weapon, but there is no weapon lying about. The murderer would have found it awkward to carry it away unless it was too small to be noticed."

"Perhaps the weapon was too big to be noticed," the priest said with an odd little giggle.

Gilder looked round sternly. "What do you mean?"

"Silly way of putting it," Father Brown apologized. "Sounds like a fairy tale. But poor Armstrong was killed with a giant's club, a great green club, too big to be seen, which we call the earth. He was broken against this green bank we are standing on."

"How do you mean?" the detective asked quickly.

Father Brown turned his moon face up to the narrow façade of the house. "Don't you see," he explained, pointing awkwardly like a child, "he was thrown down from there?"

Gilder frowned, scrutinizing the window at the top of the blind back quarter. "Well, it is certainly possible. But I don't see why you are so sure."

"Why," Brown said, opening his grey eyes wide, "there's a bit of rope round the dead man's leg. Don't you see that other bit of rope up there caught at the corner of the window?"

At that height, it looked like a faint particle of dust, but the shrewd investigator was satisfied. "You're quite right, sir," Gilder admitted. "That is certainly one to you."

Almost as he spoke, a special train took the curve on their left, disgorging another group of policemen, among whom was the hangdog visage of Magnus, the absconded servant.

"By Jove! they've got him," cried Gilder, stepping forward with new alertness. "Have you got the money!" he cried to the first policeman.

The man looked him in the face with a curious expression. "No. Then he added, 'At least, not here.'"

"Which is the inspector, please?" asked the man called Magnus.

When he spoke, everyone instantly understood how that voice had stopped a train. He was a dull-looking man with flat black hair, a colorless face, and a faint suggestion of the East in the level slits of his eyes and mouth. His blood and name had remained dubious since Sir Aaron had "rescued" him from a waitership in a London restaurant. But his voice was as vivid as his face was dead. Whether through exactitude in a foreign language or deference to his master, Magnus's tones had a peculiarly ringing quality, making the whole group jump.

"I always knew this would happen," he said aloud with brazen blandness. "My poor old master made game of me for wearing black; but I always said I should be ready for his funeral." He made a momentary movement with his two dark-gloved hands.

"Sergeant," Inspector Gilder said, eyeing the black hands with wrath, "aren't you putting the bracelets on this fellow; he looks pretty dangerous."

"Well, sir," the sergeant said with the same odd look of wonder, "I don't know that we can."

"What do you mean?" asked the other sharply. "Haven't you arrested him?"

A faint scorn widened the slit-like mouth, and the whistle of an approaching train seemed to echo the mockery. "We arrested him," the sergeant replied gravely, "just as he was coming out of the police station at Highgate, where he had deposited all his master's money in the care of Inspector Robinson."

Gilder looked at the manservant in utter amazement. "Why on earth did you do that?" he asked Magnus.

"To keep it safe from the criminal, of course," the servant replied placidly.

"Surely," said Gilder, "Sir Aaron's money might have been safely left with Sir Aaron's family."

The tail of his sentence was drowned in the roar of the train, but through the hell of noises to which that unhappy house was periodically subject, they heard the syllables of Magnus's answer in all their bell-like distinctness: "I have no reason to feel confidence in Sir Aaron's family."

All the motionless men had the ghostly sensation of a new person's presence. Merton was scarcely surprised when he looked up and saw the pale face of Armstrong's daughter over Father Brown's shoulder.
