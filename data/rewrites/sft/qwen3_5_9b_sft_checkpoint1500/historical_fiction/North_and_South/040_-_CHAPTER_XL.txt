"I have no wrong, where I can claim no right,
Naught ta'en me fro, where I have nothing had,
Yet of my woe I cannot so be quite;
Namely, since that another may be glad
With that, that thus in sorrow makes me sad."

Margaret Hale had not anticipated deriving much personal joy from Mr. Bell's visit; her anticipation was rooted solely in concern for her father. Yet, when her godfather arrived, the natural ease of their friendship unfolded instantly. He expressed that she possessed no merit in being what she was, a girl wholly aligned with his own nature; it was an inherited capacity within her to step into his affections and claim them as her own. In return, she offered him generous praise for maintaining a youthful spirit beneath his academic robes.

"Fresh and young in warmth and kindness, I mean," Mr. Bell remarked with a twinkle. "I must admit, however, that I find your opinions the most antiquated and dusty I have encountered in a long time."

"Hear this daughter of yours, Hale!" Mr. Bell exclaimed. "Her residence in Milton has utterly corrupted her. She is a democrat, a red republican, a member of the Peace Society, a socialist—"

"Papa," Margaret interjected gently, "it is simply because I advocate for the advancement of commerce. Mr. Bell would have preferred trade remain stagnant, trading wild-beast skins for acorns."

"No, no," Mr. Bell replied warmly. "I would rather dig the earth and cultivate potatoes. I would take a wild-beast skin, shave it, and weave it into broadcloth. Do not exaggerate, missy. But I am weary of this ceaseless bustle. Everyone rushing over one another, driven by a frantic desire to accumulate wealth."

"It is not everyone who can sit comfortably within university walls and watch their fortunes grow without exerting themselves," Mr. Hale observed. "There are surely many here who would be grateful if their properties increased as yours have, without any trouble on their part."

"I do not believe they would," Mr. Bell countered. "It is the struggle itself they crave. As for sitting still, learning from the past, or forging the future through faithful, prophetic labor—Pooh! I suspect there isn't a single soul in Milton who knows how to sit still. It is a profound art."

"The people of Milton, I suspect, assume Oxford men do not know how to move. It would be beneficial if they mixed a little more."

"It might benefit the Miltoners greatly. Many things could improve their lot, even if those changes would be disagreeable to others."

"Are you not a Milton man yourself?" Margaret asked, surprised. "I would have thought you would be proud of your town."

"To be honest, I see little cause for pride. If you would only come to Oxford, Margaret, I will show you a place worthy of glory."

"Well!" Mr. Hale smiled. "Mr. Thornton is coming to tea tonight, and he is as proud of Milton as you are of Oxford. The two of you must try to broaden each other's minds."

"I have no desire to become more 'liberal-minded,' thank you," Mr. Bell declared.

"Is Mr. Thornton coming to tea, Papa?" Margaret whispered softly.

"Either to tea or shortly thereafter. He was uncertain. He instructed us not to wait."

Meanwhile, Mr. Thornton had resolved against asking his mother how far she had progressed in her efforts to discuss the impropriety of Margaret's conduct with him. He was certain that if he learned the details, her account would only serve to annoy and chagrin him, inevitably colored by her perspective. He shrank from hearing Margaret's name uttered, even as he blamed her, envied her, and renounced her in his mind. Yet, he loved her intensely, despite himself. He dreamed of her approaching him, arms outstretched, with a lightness and gaiety that simultaneously repelled and allured him. This vision of Margaret, stripped of her true character as if by an evil spirit, was so deeply etched in his imagination that upon waking, he struggled to distinguish the idealized *Una* from the deceptive *Duessa*. His distaste for the latter seemed to mar the former. Nevertheless, his pride forbade him from acknowledging his vulnerability by avoiding her. He sought neither opportunity to be near her nor refuge from her presence. To prove his self-control, he lingered over every business matter that afternoon, forcing his movements into unnatural slowness and deliberation. Consequently, it was past eight o'clock before he reached Mr. Hale's.

In the study, business arrangements were conducted with Mr. Bell. The latter remained seated by the fire, talking wearily long after business was concluded, making it evident they might just as well have retired upstairs. Mr. Thornton refused to suggest moving, chafing at Mr. Bell's prolixity and considering him a tedious companion. Conversely, Mr. Bell secretly viewed Mr. Thornton as brusque, curt, and lacking in both intelligence and manner. Finally, a faint noise from above indicated it was time to ascend. They found Margaret with a letter open before her, eagerly discussing its contents with her father. Upon the gentlemen's entrance, she hastily closed it. However, Mr. Thornton's keen senses caught a fragment of Mr. Hale's words to Mr. Bell.

"A letter from Henry Lennox. It fills Margaret with hope."

Mr. Bell nodded. Margaret flushed crimson as Mr. Thornton fixed his gaze upon her. He felt an overwhelming urge to rise and depart the room instantly, never to return.

"We were reflecting," Mr. Hale said, "that you and Mr. Thornton have followed Margaret's counsel, attempting to convert one another, given your lengthy absence in the study."

"And you presumed we had reduced each other to mere opinions, like the Kilkenny cats' tails. Whose opinion do you imagine possesses the most obstinate vitality?"

Mr. Thornton had no concept of their discussion and disdained to ask. Mr. Hale politely clarified.

"Mr. Thornton, we accused Mr. Bell this morning of a certain Oxonian medieval bigotry regarding his native town. We, I believe with Margaret, suggested it would do him good to associate with the Milton manufacturers."

"My apologies. I thought it would do the Milton manufacturers good to associate more with Oxford men. Was that not the sentiment, Margaret?"

"I believe I thought it would do both sides good to see more of each other. I did not realize it was either of ours."

"So, Mr. Thornton, we ought to have been improving one another downstairs, rather than discussing vanished families of the Smiths and Harrisons. However, I am willing to fulfill my part now. I wonder when you Miltonians intend to settle down. Your entire lives seem devoted to gathering materials for existence."

"By living, do you mean enjoyment?"

"Yes, enjoyment. I do not specify the nature, for I trust we both consider mere pleasure a very poor substitute for enjoyment."

"I would prefer the nature of the enjoyment be defined."

"Well! Enjoyment or leisure—the enjoyment derived from the power and influence money affords. You are all striving for money. For what purpose?"

Mr. Thornton fell silent. Then he murmured, "I truly do not know. But money is not what I strive for."

"What, then?"

"It is a domestic question. I shall have to submit to such a catechism, and I am not certain I am prepared."

"No!" Mr. Hale interrupted. "Let us not make this catechism personal. Neither of you represents humanity; you are each too individual for that."

"I am unsure if I should consider that a compliment. I would like to be the representative of Oxford, with its beauty, learning, and proud history. What do you say, Margaret? Should I feel flattered?"

"I know Oxford poorly. But there is a distinction between representing a city and being the representative man of its inhabitants."

"True enough, Miss Margaret. Now I recall, you were opposed to me this morning, adopting a distinctly Miltonian and manufacturing stance."

Margaret perceived the swift, surprised glance Mr. Thornton directed at her and felt annoyance at the interpretation he might draw from Mr. Bell's remark. Mr. Bell continued:

"Oh! I wish I could show you our High Street, our Radcliffe Square. I am omitting our colleges, just as I grant Mr. Thornton leave to omit his factories when describing the charms of Milton. I have a right to critique my birthplace. Remember, I am a Milton man."

Mr. Thornton felt annoyed, more than warranted, by Mr. Bell's words. He was not in a humor for jest. Under different circumstances, he could have relished Mr. Bell's half-teasing condemnation of a town whose life clashed with every habit he had cultivated. But now, he was sufficiently irritated to attempt defending what was never seriously intended to be attacked.

"I do not set up Milton as a model town."

"Not in architecture?" Mr. Bell asked slyly.

"No! We have been too occupied to attend to mere outward appearances."

"Do not say *mere* outward appearances," Mr. Hale gently corrected. "They impress us all, from childhood onward—every day of our lives."

"Wait a moment," Mr. Thornton said. "Remember we are of a different stock from the Greeks, to whom beauty was everything.
