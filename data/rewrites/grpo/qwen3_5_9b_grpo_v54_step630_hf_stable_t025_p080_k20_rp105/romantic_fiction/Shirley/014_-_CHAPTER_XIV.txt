The morning light filtered through the window, illuminating the turmoil within Shirley's mind. As she rose, her first words were a declaration of inevitability: "Of course, I know he will marry Shirley." She spoke with a firm conviction that he ought to, for she believed Shirley possessed the strength to aid him. Yet, beneath this rational acceptance lay a cruel, gnawing thought: *I shall be forgotten.* The image of a future where Robert belonged entirely to another woman, where she would be erased from his life, sent a chill through her. *What shall I do?* she wondered, clutching the thought of her own Robert. She felt unworthy of such a title, viewing herself as poverty and incapacity compared to Shirley's wealth, power, and radiant beauty. There was no flaw in Shirley's claim; she loved him with a depth that commanded pride. Shirley was the rightful choice.

Consequently, Shirley resolved to accept this union. But she drew a hard line in the sand regarding her own future role. She despised the hollow pretense of being a sister or a distant friend. To a man like Robert, she demanded everything or nothing. She would not endure the degradation of feigning calm affection while her soul tore itself apart. Once the couple was united, she vowed to leave them, refusing to linger as a hypocrite or a nuisance. She could not stand between them, nor could she trample them. The alternative was clear: she must either cleave to him as part of his very being or be severed from him as widely as the poles of a sphere. "Sunder me then, Providence," she whispered to the empty room, praying for a swift separation.

These dark aspirations resurfaced late in the afternoon when Miss Keeldar appeared at the window, her usual blend of wistfulness and careless gaiety visible even in her slow saunter. Inside, she greeted Caroline with a sharp inquiry about her broken promise to visit.

"I was not in the humour," Caroline admitted truthfully.

Shirley's gaze was immediate and piercing. "No," she observed, her tone shifting from curiosity to a pitying intensity. "You are not in the humour for loving me. You are in one of your sunless moods, when a fellow creature's presence feels like a burden. You have such moods. Are you aware of it?"

"Do you mean to stay long, Shirley?" Caroline asked, sensing the edge in her friend's voice.

"Yes. I am here for tea, and I shall take the liberty of removing my bonnet." She did so, standing on the rug with her hands clasped behind her back, her expression unreadable yet heavy with unspoken sorrow.

"You look wonderfully self-supported," Shirley continued, her eyes scanning Caroline's face. "Like a wounded deer seeking solitude. Are you afraid I will worry if I discover you are hurt?"

"I never fear Shirley," Caroline replied.

"But sometimes you dislike her; often you avoid her. Shirley can feel when she is slighted. If you had not walked home with Moore last night, you would be a different girl today. What time did you reach the rectory?"

"By ten."

Shirley frowned. "Three-quarters of an hour to walk a mile? Was it you, or Moore, who lingered?"

"Shirley, you talk nonsense."

"He talked nonsense," Shirley countered, her irritation flaring. "Or he looked it, which is far worse. I see the reflection of his eyes on your forehead right now. I feel disposed to call him out, if only I had a trustworthy second. I am desperately irritated. I felt so last night, and I have carried it all day."

"You don't ask me why," Shirley pressed, her voice dropping. "You, the silent, over-modest thing, don't deserve my secrets poured into your lap without an invitation. Upon my word, I could have found it in my heart to dog Moore yesterday evening with dire intent. I have pistols, and I can use them."

"Stuff, Shirley! Which would you have shot—me or Robert?"

"Neither, perhaps. Perhaps myself. More likely a bat or a tree-bough. He is a puppy, your cousin—a quiet, serious, sensible, ambitious puppy. I see him standing before me, bearing me down with his fixity of purpose, and I have no patience with him!"

Miss Keeldar paced the room, venting her frustration against men in general and her tenant in particular.

"You are mistaken," Caroline urged, anxiety tightening her chest. "Robert is no puppy or male flirt. I can vouch for that."

"_You_ vouch for it! Do you think I'll take your word? There is no testimony I would credit sooner than yours. To advance Moore's fortune, you would cut off your right hand."

"But I didn't lie. He was just civil to me last night—that was all."

"I never asked what he was. I can guess. I saw him from the window take your hand in his long fingers just as he left my gate."

"That is nothing. I am an old acquaintance, his cousin."

"I feel indignant," Shirley declared, her voice rising. "All my comfort is broken up by his manoeuvres. He keeps intruding between you and me. Without him, we should be good friends; but that six feet of puppyhood creates a perpetual eclipse of our friendship. He crosses and obscures the disc I want to see clear; he renders me to you a mere bore and nuisance."

"No, Shirley, no."

"He does. You did not want my society this afternoon, and I feel it hard. I am a social personage who cannot live alone. If we were left unmolested, I could bear you in my presence forever. You cannot say as much respecting me."

"Shirley, I can say anything you wish. I like you."

"You will wish me at Jericho tomorrow, Lina."

"I shall not. I am growing fonder of you every day. You are too English to form a vehement friendship instantly, but you are so different from every-day young ladies. I esteem you, I value you. You are never a burden. Do you believe what I say?"

"Partly," Shirley replied, smiling with a touch of incredulity. "But you are a peculiar personage. Quiet as you look, there is force and depth within you that is not easily reached. Then you certainly are not happy."

"And unhappy people are rarely good. Is that what you mean?"

"Not at all. I mean that unhappy people are often preoccupied, unable to discourse with companions of my nature. Moreover, there is a sort of unhappiness that corrodes. That, I fear, is your portion. Will pity do you any good, Lina? If it will, take some from Shirley; she offers it largely, and warrants it genuine."

"Shirley, I never had a sister—you never had a sister—but it flashes on me now how sisters feel towards each other. Affection twined with their lives, which no shock can uproot, which little quarrels only trample momentarily before springing fresher. Affection that no passion can ultimately outrival, with which even love itself can only compete in force and truth. Love hurts us, Shirley. It is tormenting, racking, burning away our strength. In affection, there is no pain, no fire, only sustenance and balm. I am supported and soothed when you are near. Only you. Do you believe me now?"

"I am always easy of belief when the creed pleases me. We really are friends, then, Lina, in spite of the black eclipse?"

"We really are," Caroline returned, drawing Shirley down to sit beside her. "Chance what may."

"Then, let us talk of something else than the Troubler."

Just then, the rector entered, and the conversation shifted. When Miss Keeldar finally prepared to depart, she paused in the passage to confide a heavy secret. "Caroline, I have a great weight on my mind. My conscience is uneasy, as if I had committed a crime. Not my private conscience, but my conscience as a landed proprietor and lord of the manor. I have fallen under the clutch of an eagle with iron talons. I am under a stern influence I scarcely approve but cannot resist. Something terrible will happen ere long, and I fear it. To ease my mind and prevent harm, I intend to embark on a series of good works. Do not be surprised if I suddenly become outrageously charitable. I have no idea how to begin, but you must advise me. Tomorrow, ask Miss Ainley to come to Fieldhead. I intend to put myself under her tuition. Won't she have a precious pupil? Hint to her, Lina, that though well-meaning, I am a neglected character, and she will feel less scandalized by my ignorance regarding clothing societies."

The next morning, Caroline found Shirley sitting gravely at her desk, surrounded by an account book, a bundle of banknotes, and a well-filled purse. She looked serious, yet puzzled. "I have been casting an eye over the weekly expenditure at the hall," she explained, "trying to find where I could retrench. I also gave audience to Mrs. Gill, the cook, and sent her away convinced my brain was crazed. I lectured her on economy in a way quite new to her. I was so eloquent on the text of thrift that I surprised myself. It is a fresh idea; I never thought or spoke on it until lately. But it is all theory. When I came to the practical part, I could retrench nothing. I lacked the firmness to remove a single pound of butter or to investigate the destiny of dripping, lard, or bread. We do not wash for the parish, yet I viewed in silence items of soap and bleaching powder calculated to satisfy the most anxious inquirer. I am not carnivorous, nor is Mrs. Pryor, nor Mrs. Gill, yet I only hemmed and widened my eyes when I saw butcher's bills proving a falsehood. Caroline, you may laugh at me, but you cannot change me. I am a poltroon on certain points. I feel it. There is a base alloy of moral cowardice in my composition. I blushed and hung my head before Mrs. Gill when she ought to have been faltering confessions to me. I found it impossible to summon the spirit to hint, much less prove, that she was a cheat. I have no calm dignity, no true courage."

"Shirley, what fit of self-injustice is this?" Caroline protested. "My uncle, who is not given to speak well of women, says there are not ten thousand men in England as genuinely fearless as you."

"I am fearless, physically. I am never nervous about danger. I was not startled when Mr. Wynne's great red bull rose with a bellow before my face, stooping his begrimed head to charge me. But I was afraid of seeing Mrs. Gill brought to shame. You have twice, ten times, my strength of mind on certain subjects. You, whom no persuasion can induce to pass a bull, would have firmly shown my housekeeper she had done wrong, then gently admonished her, and sweetly forgiven her if she seemed penitent. Of this conduct, I am incapable."

"However," Shirley continued, "in spite of exaggerated imposition, I still find we live within our means. I have money in hand, and I must do some good with it. The Briarfield poor are badly off. They must be helped. What ought I to do, Lina? Should I not distribute the cash at once?"

"No, indeed, Shirley. You will not manage properly. I have noticed that your notion of charity is to give shillings and half-crowns in a careless, free-handed way, liable to abuse. You need a prime minister, or you will get into scrapes. You suggested Miss Ainley yourself; I will apply to her. Promise to keep quiet and stop throwing away your money. What a great deal you have! You must feel very rich?"

"Yes; I feel of consequence. It is not an immense sum, but I feel responsible for its disposal. This responsibility weighs on my mind more heavily than I expected. They say families are almost starving in Briarfield. Some of my own cottagers are in wretched circumstances. I must and will help them."

"Some people say we shouldn't give alms to the poor, Shirley."

"They are great fools for their pains. For those who are not hungry, it is easy to palaver about the degradation of charity. But they forget the brevity of life and its bitterness. We have none of us long to live. Let us help each other through seasons of want and woe without heeding the scruples of vain philosophy."

"But you do help others, Shirley. You give a great deal as it is."

"Not enough. I must give more, or my brother's blood will cry to Heaven against me. If political incendiaries come to kindle conflagration here and attack my property, I shall defend it like a tigress. I know I shall. Let me listen to Mercy as long as she is near me. Once her voice is drowned by the shout of ruffian defiance, I shall be full of impulses to resist. If the poor gather and rise as a mob, I shall turn against them as an aristocrat; if they bully me, I must defy; if they attack, I must resist."

"You talk like Robert."

"I feel like Robert, only more fiercely. Let them meddle with Robert, or his mill, or his interests, and I shall hate them. At present, I am no patrician, nor do I regard the poor as plebeians. But if they violently wrong me or mine and then presume to dictate to us, I shall forget pity for their wretchedness and respect for their poverty in scorn of their ignorance and wrath at their insolence."

"Shirley, how your eyes flash!"

"Because my soul burns. Would you, any more than me, let Robert be borne down by numbers?"

"If I had your power to aid Robert, I would use it as you mean to use it. If I could be such a friend to him as you can be, I would stand by him, as you mean to stand by him, till death."
