The Loggia de’ Cerchi stood tucked in the beating heart of old Florence, tangled in a maze of narrow lanes behind the Badia, paths modern strangers rarely tread unless they are hunting for one stark, unadorned doorway carved with the words: *Qui Nacque Il Divino Poeta* — Here the Divine Poet was Born. The same cobblestones would once have rung for Dante with the shouts and clashing steel of warring noble clans; but by the fifteenth century, the streets hummed only with the rough, unrecorded quarrels and raucous jests of wool-carders plying their trade in the clothmaking quarters of San Martino and Garbo.

Under the loggia’s stone arch at dawn on April 9th, 1492, two men stared each other down: one stooped slightly, leaning in with sharp, curious scrutiny, while the other lay splayed on the cold pavement, gazing up with the dazed, startled stare of a man woken mid-dream.

The standing man spoke first. Grey-haired, broad-shouldered, he was exactly the sort of fellow Tuscan folk liked to describe as moulded with a fist and polished with a pickaxe — coarse, unrefined, built for hard labor. But the deep, furrowed lines carved across his brow and around his mouth spoke of a pompous, self-important gravity, clearly meant to dissuade anyone from dismissing him out of hand for his rough exterior. A large, well-stuffed leather sack sat at his feet, and a pedlar’s basket hung slung over his shoulder, strung with pins and spools of thread for ladies’ small wares, and shards of colored glass he’d bartered for across half the city.

“Young man,” he said, pointing a gnarled finger at the gold ring on the other’s hand, “when you’ve got a tougher stubble on that chin, you’ll know better than to nap on street corners with a trinket like that on your forefinger. By the holy gospels! If it’d been any rascal but me standing over you two minutes ago, that ring’d be gone for good. But Bratti Ferravecchi isn’t the sort to steal. A cat can’t enjoy a mouse unless she catches it fair and alive, and Bratti can’t stomach gain that doesn’t come from a proper bargain. Why, three years ago on San Giovanni’s feast, the saint put a dead body right in my path — a blind beggar, his cap stuffed full of silver coins. But would you believe me? My stomach turned against that unearned money, till I convinced myself San Giovanni owed me every penny for what I spend every year at the festa. I buried the old man decent, even paid for a mass for his soul, and then I knew it was a fair trade. But how does a young lad with the face of Messer San Michele end up sleeping on a stone bed with the wind for a blanket?”

The stranger, still groggy from sleep, barely made out Bratti’s guttural Tuscan, but he understood the warning gesture well enough. Glancing down at his ring, he half-automatically slipped it off and tucked it deep inside his doublet, pushing himself upright to stretch.

“Your tunic and hose don’t half match that jewel, lad,” Bratti said slowly, nodding at his travel-stained clothes. “Anybody’d think the saints sent *you* a dead body to rob. If you took his jewels, I hope you buried him proper — can certainly afford to pay for a mass or two for the poor soul.”

A sharp, painful thrill shot through the stranger, freezing his half-raised arms mid-stretch. For a split second he turned on Bratti with a sharp, angry frown, but he schooled his face back to easy indifference fast. He pulled off his red Levantine cap, which hung like a plump purse over his left ear, pushed back his long dark brown curls, and smiled as he glanced down at his sea-spotted clothes.

“You speak truth, friend. My garments are stained as badly as an old sail, and they’re not even old — it’s just they’ve been splashed with sea water and rain alike. Fact is, I’m a stranger to Florence. I stumbled in last night footsore, and decided to curl up in this hospitable porch rather than trawl the streets any longer for a random inn that’d turn out to be a den of more than one kind of bloodsucker.”

“A stranger, right enough,” Bratti huffed. “Your words all melt soft out of your throat, so no proper Florentine can tell a hook from a hanger. You’re not from Genoa? Cut of your clothes says you’re more like to be from Venice.”

“Right now,” the stranger laughed, “it matters far less where I came from than where I can go to get a mouthful of breakfast. This city of yours is turning a pretty grim face on me right here — can you show me the way to a busier quarter where I can find a meal and a bed?”

“That I can,” Bratti said, hefting his leather sack onto his back. “You’re lucky, lad, that I was walking in from Rovezzano this morning, and detoured out of my way to Mercato Vecchio to say an Ave at the Badia. That’s your good fortune, sure. But I need my profit for the favor, mind. Nothing for nothing. If I lead you to the market, you swear by your patron saint to give me the first bid for that stained suit of yours, once you buy yourself something better — which you clearly will, with that pretty ring you’re hiding.”

“Agreed, by San Niccolò,” the stranger laughed. “Now let’s get moving to this market, because this doublet you’re so eager to buy could do with a hot meal inside it a lot more than new lining.”

“Coveting? Pfft,” Bratti snorted, setting off down the lane. He cut himself off mid-sentence, and began to shout in a loud, harsh tone that creaked like a ungreased cart wheel: *Chi abbaratta — baratta — b’ratta — chi abbaratta cenci e vetri — b’ratta ferri vecchi?* Who will barter for rags, broken glass, old iron?

“Your whole suit’s not worth much, truth be told,” he went on, dropping back to conversational tone after a moment. “Hose and all, it’s barely worth a handful of pennies. Still, if you want to buy yourself a lute that plays better than any new one, or a sword once worn by a Ridolfi, or the finest new prayer beads, I can cut you a grand bargain if I set the suit’s value against the price. Simple as I look, I’ve got the best stocked shop of any rag-and-bone man in the district, right near the market. Praise the Virgin, I’m not hauling a useless pumpkin on my shoulders. I don’t stay caged in the shop all day, neither — my wife and a pet raven stay home to mind the stock. *Chi abbaratta — baratta — b’ratta?*... Now, lad, tell me where you’re from, and what business you’ve got in Florence?”

“I thought you never liked getting anything without a proper bargain,” the stranger grinned. “You’ve offered me nothing in exchange for that information yet.”

“Well, well — a Florentine doesn’t mind paying a fair price for news. It fills the stomach a little even if you don’t walk away with a new pair of hose. I’ll take you to the prettiest lass in the market to get a cup of milk — that’s a fair trade, isn’t it?”

“Nay, I can find her myself if she’s that pretty,” the stranger said, shaking his head. “Pretty heads always lean out of doors and windows to see the passersby. No, no — besides, a sharp trader like you ought to know that any man who bids hard for nuts and news might well end up finding both are hollow.”

“Ah, lad,” Bratti said, glancing at him sideways with open admiration, “you weren’t born on a Sunday — the salt shops were wide open the day you came into the world. You’re not a Hebrew, eh? Come from Spain or Naples, right? Let me tell you, the Friars Minor are working hard to make Florence as hot as Spain for those hell’s dogs that want all the profit of usury for themselves and leave none for good Christians. If you wander the Calimara quarter with a scrap of yellow cloth sewn in your cap, it’ll spoil that smooth olive cheek of yours far worse than a sword cut would. — *Abbaratta, baratta — chi abbaratta?* Grey cloth is pitted against yellow cloth now, see? There’s enough grey cloth in Florence to make a gown and cowl for the whole Duomo, and not enough yellow cloth to make hose for Saint Christopher — bless his name, and let me catch sight of him today! — *Abbaratta, baratta, b’ratta — chi abbaratta?*”

“All that very entertaining information you’re giving away for nothing,” the stranger said, a little scornfully, “just happens not to concern me. I’m no Hebrew.”

“See there now!” Bratti crowed triumphantly. “I just made a fine bargain out of thin air. Got you to tell me something, even though you’re as slippery as a lamprey. Praise San Giovanni, a blind Florentine is a match for two one-eyed strangers. And here we are, in the Mercato.”

They stepped out of the narrow warren of lanes into the broad sunlit piazza known to every old Florentine writer as Mercato Vecchio, the Old Market. The space had been a provision market since time out of mind — the old tales liked to say it was the very spot the first Fiesole-dwelling ancestors of the Florentines had climbed down from their hill forts to trade with the valley villagers. Even wealthy noble families hadn’t shunned living there; in the early decades of the 15th century, the Medici and other powerful *popolani grassi* commercial nobility kept their palaces facing the square, never mind the roar of a dozen dialects and the sight of butcher stalls the poet Antonio Pucci had called the market’s chief glory, making it better than any other market on earth. But the butchers’ stalls were empty today: Lent was still in full swing, so the great butchers’ guild had suspended its work, leaving the greengrocers, cheesemongers, macaroni sellers, egg vendors, and dried fruit merchants to reign — a change that made women’s voices ring loudest over the square’s hum. Even in off-seasons, the air hummed with the clink of money changers’ coins, people tapping pots and pans to test their sturdiness, haggling over old clothes, dice players’ loud taunts, vendors shouting the praises of new linen, woodenware, iron kettles and frying pans. Narrow entrances choked with mules and carts echoed with the very same sharp, colorful insults modern women still hurl when they’re jostled and overheated in a crowd. Well-off ladies and gentlemen out to do their marketing would regularly stumble across impromptu street brawls, see rags and beggary and petty crime far worse than anything modern city dwellers could imagine. But there were kinder, sweeter sights too: plump brood rabbits just as innocent and wide-eyed as any today, doves and songbirds to buy as presents for children, even kittens for sale, here and there a fine handsome tomcat boasting a perfect mousing record. Best of all were the young women’s soft, round cheeks and bright eyes, fresh from the pre-dawn walk in from their home villages far outside the city walls, and older faces glowing with the steady warmth of honest goodwill, the sort of faces never absent from scenes of busy, ordinary human work. High on an ancient pillar in the square’s centre — a pillar hauled from the old church of San Giovanni — stood Donatello’s stone statue of Plenty, with a fountain at its foot. The old poet Pucci wrote that the market housewives would stop there to rinse out their utensils and take a sip of cool water, not because they couldn’t afford wine, but so they could save every penny of their wine money for their husbands.

But this particular morning, the whole market had fallen prey to a strange, shared distraction. The stalls were half set up with their wares, and early buyers were already hovering to snap up the freshest vegetables and finest butter — but almost no one was tending to their trade. Every vendor had turned their back on their goods, clustering in tight knots all across the piazza to talk. A secondhand clothes seller who’d been about to hang a pair of long hose out on display had distractedly slung them around his own neck so he could rush over to the nearest gossip group. A cheesemonger with a block of marzolino in one hand and a knife in the other was absentmindedly stabbing the soft cheese to emphasize his points. Elderly greengrocers stood holding their egg baskets tilted so far sideways they looked ready to spill, wailing invocations to the saints in a ragged, overlapping chorus.

Florence’s street boys — a particularly mischievous, sour-crab lot — saw their perfect chance. Some made a dash for piles of nuts and dried figs, others swarmed the stalls selling hot Lenten dishes, followed closely by stray dogs who’d learned to love fasting food too, who’d grab a mouthful and dart under the nearest stall to eat it. Mules jostled and kicked their way through scattered baskets, stretching their muzzles out to nibble bunches of fragrant leafy greens.

“Diavolo!” Bratti swore, as he and his companion wandered unnoticed through the chaos. “The whole market’s gone mad, like the Holy Father just excommunicated the lot of us. I’ve got to find out what’s going on. But don’t you worry — you might think it’s a thousand years till you get that cup of milk from the pretty lass, but stick close to me and I’ll keep my end of the bargain. And remember, I get first bid on that suit of yours — especially the hose. Spotted and stained as they are, that’s fine *panno di garbo* cloth, almost ruined by mud and sea spray, but still worth something.”

“Ola, Monna Trecca!” Bratti shouted, turning to the old greengrocer woman hovering just outside the nearest group, who’d paused her wailing to listen. “Your mules are upsetting all your bunches of parsley! Has the whole world come to an end?”

Monna Trecca — Dame Greengrocer — spun around at the unexpected yell right in her ear, half furious, half bewildered, staring first at Bratti, then at her ransacked stall, then back at him again.

“A bad Easter and a bad year to you, may you die by the sword!” she shrieked, rushing towards her produce stall, the whole of her anger landing first on Bratti. Bratti didn’t even flinch at the curse. He slipped neatly into the empty spot she’d left behind, close enough to hear the story everyone was crowding to listen to, and gestured for the young stranger to stay right next to him.

“I saw it myself,” a fat man said, waving a bunch of newly bought leeks in his hand. “I was at Santa Maria Novella, and I saw it all. The woman jumped up, threw her arms out, screaming that she saw a great bull with horns of fire charging down to crush the church. I saw it myself.”

“Saw what, Goro?” a slim, roguish man with a glint in his eye asked. He wore a tight jerkin, a skullcap slid carelessly over his left ear, a fine linen apron tucked up at one side, and a razor stuck in his belt. “Did you see the bull, or only the woman?”

“Well, the woman, of course!” Goro huffed. “But it’s all the same, isn’t it? Doesn’t change the meaning at all!”

“Meaning’s clear enough!” a dozen voices cried out at once, and then a storm of overlapping chatter broke out: “Lights shooting over San Lorenzo three nights in a row!” “Thunder heard when the stars were bright and the sky was clear!” “The lantern on top of the Duomo was struck by Saint Michael’s sword!” “The *Palle* — the Medici arms!” “All smashed to bits!” “Lions in the cage tearing each other apart!” “Ah, and you’d better believe they would!” “The wax votive offering fell down at Santissima Annunziata!” “Died like the best Christian there ever was!” “God will have pardoned his soul!” The phrases shot across each other like hail in a gale, every speaker desperate to be heard, not caring who was listening. The only silent members of the group were Bratti, who was busily piecing all the scattered fragments of gossip together; the barber; and a thin-lipped, sharp-looking man in spectacles, with a pen and ink case hung from his belt.

“Ebbene, Nello,” Bratti murmured, slipping around the edge of the group till he reached the barber. “So the Magnifico Lorenzo is dead, then? Rest his soul! Does that mean the price of wax is going up?”

“Just as you say,” Nello nodded, then added with mock gravity, speaking so fast his words tripped over each other: “And his little wax image in the Annunziata fell off its hook the exact moment he died, or so the Servite friars say, and they’d know better than anyone. Half a dozen cows had stillborn calves this Lent, and no one’s even counted all the bad eggs that got broken since Carnival. Ah, he was a great man — a great politician — a greater poet than Dante himself. And yet the whole cupola didn’t fall, only the lantern on top. Che miracolo! What a miracle!”

A sharp, long hissing *Pst!* cut through the gale of guttural voices. It came from the pale man in spectacles, and it worked instantly: the noise died down, every eye in the group fixed on him, waiting to hear what he had to say.
