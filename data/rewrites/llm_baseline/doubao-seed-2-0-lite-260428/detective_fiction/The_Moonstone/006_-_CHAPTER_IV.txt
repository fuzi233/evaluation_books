I know full well I risk boring you to tears rambling on about me and my rickety beehive armchair. A drowsy old man dozing in a sun-warmed back yard is hardly thrilling company, I grant you. But facts must fall in the order they actually happened, so bear with me just a little longer – we’re edging close to Mr. Franklin Blake’s long-awaited arrival later that same day.

My daughter Penelope had barely stepped out of sight before I was jolted out of my pre-nap haze by a racket of clattering plates and cutlery from the servants’ hall: dinner was served. I take all my meals in my own little parlour, so I had no stake in the staff’s dinner but to wish every last one of them a good appetite, before settling back to doze in my chair. I was just stretching my stiff old legs when a woman came barrelling straight into my path. It wasn’t Penelope this time – it was Nancy, the kitchen maid, face all set in a sullen pout. As head of the household staff, I never let a sulky face slip past unchallenged as a matter of principle, so I stopped her dead. She tried to push past without answering my questions, so I gave her ear a gentle tweak – my usual, friendly way of showing I approve of a plump, hardworking lass.

That’s how I learned Rosanna Spearman, our second housemaid, was late to dinner again. Nancy was sent to drag her back, and she was spitting mad about it, complaining all the heavy work fell to her shoulders while Rosanna lazed off. I felt a sharp twinge of pity for Rosanna, and I could see plain as day Nancy would march back to the sands yelling at the poor girl far harsher than she deserved. Since I had nothing better to do, I told Nancy to go back to her roast – I’d go fetch Rosanna myself, and give her the little warning about punctuality I knew she’d take kindly coming from me. Nancy, who has a famous appetite, lit up so fast I chucked her under the chin out of habit, grabbed my walking stick, and set off for the shore.

Wait – don’t let me rush ahead. I’d be doing you a disservice if I didn’t explain two things first, and both are tied tight to the great Moonstone mystery. You need to know Rosanna’s story, and you need to know the story of the strange stretch of sand she was so fond of. I promise we’ll be neck-deep in oddities and secrets soon enough.

To start with Rosanna: she was the only new hire in the house. Four months before this day, my lady visited a London reformatory, a home for women just released from prison who were trying to outrun their old bad choices. The matron, seeing how kind Lady Verinder was, pointed out Rosanna Spearman – a quiet girl who’d once stolen a small sum from a household, no grand city schemes to rob thousands, just the bad luck of being a nobody the law threw its full weight at. The matron swore she was one in a thousand, all she needed was one fair chance to prove she was worthy of trust. Lady Verinder, the most Christian and generous soul who ever walked this earth, gave her that chance. No one on the staff but me and Miss Rachel knew her past. She got full wages, all the same privileges as the other maids, quiet private words of encouragement from my lady when no one else was around, and she repaid that kindness ten times over. Even with her frequent faint spells, she did every task modestly, carefully, without a single complaint.

But she never made friends with the rest of the servants, save for my daughter Penelope who was kind but never close. She wasn’t pretty enough to make anyone jealous, anyway – the plainest woman in the whole house, with one shoulder lopsided and bigger than the other. The other maids resented her silence, her solitary ways. When they gossiped in their free time, she read or mended clothes. On her days off, she almost always slipped out alone. She never argued, never took offence, just kept a quiet, stubborn, polite distance from all of them. The other maids swore she was putting on airs, acting like she was too good for them – nothing but rank injustice, of course, no matter that there was some soft, quiet edge to her voice that didn’t sound quite like a common housemaid.

Now the sands. Our house perches high on the Yorkshire coast, almost right on the sea. Every path for miles around is pretty, scenic, well-trodden – except one. That quarter-mile trail winds through a sad, shadowy plantation of fir trees, and spits you out between two low, craggy cliffs onto the loneliest, ugliest little bay on the whole coast. Two jagged rocky spits, the North and the South, jutt out from the sand dunes into the water, and between them lies the worst patch of quicksand on the entire Yorkshire shore. At the turn of the tide, something deep in the unknown mud below stirs, and sets the whole surface of the sand heaving, rippling, shivering all over – that’s why the locals call it the Shivering Sand. A great sandbank half a mile out breaks the full force of the open ocean, so when the tide rolls in, it creeps over the quicksand dead silent, no waves, no roar, just smooth, rising water. No fishing boat dares sail into that bay. No kids from the nearby Cobb’s Hole village come here to play. Even the seagulls, it seems to me, fly wide of the spot.

And that horrid, lonesome bay was Rosanna Spearman’s favourite place on earth. She’d sit there alone, reading or stitching, hour after hour, when all the other maids would take the lovely paths into town together. That’s exactly where I was heading to fetch her to dinner.

I saw no sign of her in the fir plantation. When I climbed over the dunes onto the beach, there she was – in her little straw bonnet, that plain grey cloak she always wore to hide her deformed shoulder as best she could, all alone staring out at the quicksand and the open sea. She jumped when I came up behind her, and turned her face away so I couldn’t see her. As head of staff, I never let a maid refuse to meet my eye without asking what’s wrong, so I turned her back around, and saw she’d been crying. I pulled out one of my lady’s six fine bandanna handkerchiefs, told her to sit with me on the slope of the sandbank, I’d dry her eyes and hear her out. By the time I’d settled my old bones down, she’d already wiped her face on her own cheap, thin cambric handkerchief. She looked quiet, and wretched, but sat down right next to me like a good girl.

When I asked her what she was crying over, she said it was the old years of her past, the life she was running from, coming back to haunt her. I told her that part of her life was wiped clean, why couldn’t she forget it? She plucked at the lappet of my coat, the very spot she’d scrubbed a grease stain out of the day before with a new cleaning mixture. The grease was gone, but the nap of the cloth was still dull and marked right where the stain had been. “The stain is taken off, Mr. Betteredge,” she said, shaking her head. “But the place shows. The place still shows.”

That left me speechless. There was no easy answer to that, not a single one. Her soft brown eyes were fixed on me, full of a quiet respect for my happy, blameless old age – a life she knew she could never have, no matter how hard she tried, and my heart felt heavy for her. I couldn’t think of a single thing to say to comfort her, so I helped pull myself up, and told her it was time to go in to dinner. She refused to help me up, instead she slipped her hand into mine and squeezed it soft. She said she didn’t want dinner, begged me to let her stay a little longer. When I asked her what drew her to this horrid place, she drew little patterns in the sand with her finger, and said it felt like something was pulling her here, that sometimes she thought her own grave was waiting for her in that bay.

I was indignant, naturally, telling her at my time of life I wasn’t going to stand for a five-and-twenty year old talking about her grave when there was roast mutton and suet pudding waiting for her in the house. She didn’t even seem to hear me. She put her hand on my shoulder to hold me sitting next to her, said it was lonelier for her in the house, surrounded by all the honest maids who had no idea what she’d done, than it was here all alone. The tide was turning right then, and the whole brown surface of the quicksand was rippling, shivering, heaving. She pointed at it, wide-eyed, said it looked to her like hundreds of suffocating people were trapped under it, fighting to get to the surface, sinking lower and lower. She begged me to throw a stone in, watch the sand suck it down.

I had a sharp rebuke on my tongue, to knock that unwholesome fancy right out of her head, when a voice from the sand dunes yelled my name at the top of its lungs: “Betteredge!”

I shouted back before I even thought who it was. Rosanna jumped to her feet, staring towards the voice, and her face flushed a bright, beautiful red I’d never seen on her plain cheeks before. She looked stunned, breathless, like she couldn’t believe her ears. “Who is it?” I asked her. She whispered the same question right back, soft, like she was talking only to herself.

I twisted around on the sand, and there he was – coming out of the dunes, bright-eyed, dressed in a fine fawn-coloured suit, gloves and hat to match, a rose stuck in his buttonhole, a grin on his face bright enough to make the Shivering Sand itself want to smile back. Before I could scramble to my feet, he plopped down right next to me, threw his arms around my neck in that over-familiar foreign fashion he picked up on his travels across the Continent, and squeezed the breath clean out of me. “Dear old Betteredge!” he laughed. “I still owe you seven-and-sixpence. Now do you know who I am?”

Lord bless us – it was Mr. Franklin Blake, three full hours earlier than we’d ever expected him.

I didn’t even get a word out before he glanced past me at Rosanna, looking a little surprised at how flustered she was. I turned to look, and she was blushing even harder, bright as a cherry, when she caught his eye. She didn’t curtsy, didn’t say a single word to either of us, just turned and ran off, vanishing over the dunes, the hem of her grey cloak fluttering out of sight in seconds. That was so unlike her – she was always the most polite, well-mannered girl on the whole staff.

“Odd girl,” Franklin said, frowning a little confused. “I wonder what she sees in me to shock her so?”

I teased him a little, joking that it must be all that fancy foreign polish on him, the varnish he picked up on his travels, that spooked her. I write that silly exchange down here to comfort every plain, slow-witted soul reading this: even the sharpest, most well-educated young gentleman with all the Continental experience in the world, and me, an old man with decades of household sense, hadn’t the faintest ghost of an idea what Rosanna Spearman’s strange, flustered behaviour really meant. She was almost gone from our thoughts before her cloak vanished completely. But don’t you forget her. Read on, and you’ll end up feeling as sorry for that poor, lost girl as I did, when the whole truth of the Moonstone finally unspools.
