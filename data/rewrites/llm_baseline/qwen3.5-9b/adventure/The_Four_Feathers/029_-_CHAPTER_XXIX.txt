"Three more days." These were the final words spoken by both men before they succumbed to sleep. Yet, the following morning shattered the fragile peace. Trench awoke gasping, complaining of a fever that seized him with terrifying speed. By afternoon, his mind was clouded, and the roles reversed: the man who had once tended Feversham now required Feversham's care. The relentless torment of the House of Stone had finally claimed its toll. It was no accident that Trench collapsed precisely as the door to his prison cell swung open; the overwhelming surge of joy at the prospect of freedom had proven too much for his exhausted frame.

"In a few days he will recover," Feversham insisted, trying to maintain a facade of calm. "It is merely a slight ailment."

"It is *Umm Sabbah*," corrected Ibrahim, shaking his head grimly. "The terrible typhus that has swept through this infected jail, claiming victims on the seventh day."

Feversham refused to accept the diagnosis, clinging to his denial with stubborn passion. But deep within him, a different anxiety gnawed: *Will the men with the camels still be waiting?*

Every day, as he ventured toward the Nile, he spotted Abou Fatma in his blue robe standing guard at his usual spot. Every day, the Arab made his silent signal, and every day, Feversham offered no response. Meanwhile, alongside Ibrahim, he devoted himself to nursing Trench. A young boy arrived daily with provisions, fetching tamarinds, dates, and roots from which Ibrahim brewed cooling draughts. Together, they carried the suffering colonel from one patch of shade to another as the sun traversed the camp. Assistance was also extended to the starving family of Idris, allowing the heavy forty-pound chains binding Trench to be removed. He was fed vegetable marrow soaked in brine, his mouth stuffed with butter, and his body anointed and swaddled tightly in warm camel cloths.

On the seventh day, Ibrahim declared, "This is the end. Tonight he will die."

"No," Feversham retorted firmly. "That is impossible. 'In his own parish, beneath the trees he knew.' Not here, no." His voice rang with desperate obstinacy. For the first time, his thoughts drifted away from the man in the blue robe outside the walls or the slim chances of escape. The crushing fear that the third feather would never reach Ethne, or that she would never voluntarily return the fourth, vanished from his mind. Even the grand vision of "the afterwards" was temporarily banished. He focused solely on Trench and the brief, awkward conversation they had shared the first night under the open sky.

"No," he repeated. "He must not die here."

Throughout that agonizing day and night, Feversham stood vigil beside Trench, witnessing the brutal struggle between life and death. At moments, it seemed the three years of imprisonment would claim victory; at others, Trench's robust constitution and wiry frame appeared poised to triumph. That night, however, life held on, prolonging the fight. The perilous seventh day passed. Ibrahim's hope began to return, and by the thirteenth day, Trench slept peacefully without wandering delirium. Upon waking, his mind was clear.

He found himself alone, swaddled so tightly in camel cloths he could barely move. The heat of the day had broken, and the shadow of the House of Stone stretched black across the sand. Lacking energy to rise, he lay pondering idly how long he had been ill. Suddenly, the shouts of the jailers and the cries of fellow prisoners echoed from the riverbank. The gate burst open, and the inmates flooded in. Feversham was among them, walking straight to Trench's corner.

"Thank God!" Feversham cried, collapsing onto the sand beside him in exhaustion after a day of unloading boats. "I could not leave you, but I was forced. We have been busy all day."

"How long have I been ill?" Trench asked weakly.

"Thirteen days," Feversham replied. "It will take a month before you can travel. You must go, Feversham. You must leave me here while you still can. Perhaps when you reach Assouan, you can arrange something for me. I cannot move yet. Will you depart tomorrow?"

"No," Feversham answered resolutely. "I would not leave without you anyway. As it stands, it is too late."

"Too late?" Trench echoed slowly, reluctant to disturb the comfortable numbness of his recovery. He wanted simply to lie idle in the cooling sunset. Gradually, the weight of Feversham's words settled upon him. "Too late? Then the man in the blue gown has gone?"

"Yes," Feversham confirmed. "He spoke to me yesterday by the river. The camel drivers would wait no longer. Fearing detection, they intended to return regardless of whether we accompanied them or not."

"You should have gone with them," Trench murmured. For the moment, he did not care if he spent the rest of his life in prison, provided he could lie quietly where he was. Without a trace of despair, he added, "So our only chance is lost."

"Not lost," Feversham countered gently. "Deferred."

"The man who watched by the river in the blue robe brought me paper, a pen, and wood-soot mixed with water. He managed to drop them beside me as I lay on the ground. I hid them beneath my *jibbeh*. Last night—there was a full moon—I wrote to a Greek merchant who runs a café at Wadi Halfa. I delivered the letter this afternoon, and he has departed. He will carry it and collect payment. In six months, or at the very latest a year, he will return to Omdurman."

"Very likely," Trench agreed. "He will surely ask for another letter to earn more money, promising to return in six months or a year. I know these people."

"You do not know Abou Fatma," Feversham insisted. "He served as Gordon's servant before Khartum fell and has been mine ever since. He came with me to Obak and waited there while I traveled to Berber. He risked his very life to reach Omdurman. Within six months, he will certainly be back."

Trench ceased arguing. His gaze wandered until it fixed on a mound of freshly turned earth in a corner of the enclosure. "What are they digging?" he asked.

"A well," Feversham replied.

"A well?" Trench fretted. "And so close to the Nile! Why? What is the purpose?"

"I do not know," Feversham admitted, though a cold dread coiled in his stomach. He suspected the truth: the well was being dug within the prison grounds. He hesitated to voice his fear until Trench was strong enough to withstand the crushing blow of confirmation. But within days, his suspicion was validated. It was publicly announced that a high wall would encircle the House of Stone. Too many prisoners had previously escaped along the riverbank; henceforth, they were to remain imprisoned from New Year's Day to Christmas.

The prisoners constructed the wall themselves using sun-dried mud bricks. Feversham worked tirelessly, and Trench, as soon as he could stand, joined him.

"Here goes our last hope," Trench muttered. Though Feversham did not verbally agree, his heart reluctantly conceded. Brick by brick, mortar by mortar, they sealed their fate. When finished, the wall stood twelve feet high, smooth, solid, and impenetrable overnight. There was no ledge to climb, no gap to breach. Trench and Feversham stared at it in utter despair. The vibrant palm groves of Khartum were now invisible to them. Their entire world was reduced to a square of bright blue by day and dark blue by night, speckled with the silver and gold of distant stars.

Trench covered his face with his hands. "I dare not look at it," he whispered, his voice breaking. "We have been building our own coffins, Feversham. That is the truth."

Then, raising his arms, he screamed into the silence: "Will they never ascend the Nile with gunboats and soldiers? Have they forgotten us in England? Good God! Have they forgotten us?"

"Hush!" Feversham commanded softly. "We shall find a way of escape. Never fear. We must wait six months. Well, we have both waited years. Six months—what are they compared to that?"

Though he spoke bravely for his comrade's sake, Feversham's own heart sank. The details of their existence during those six months need not be recounted. In that pestilential enclosure, only the countless vermin thrived in comfort. No news from the outside world penetrated the walls. They subsisted on their own thoughts, so that spotting a lizard atop the wall became a cause for frantic excitement. Scorpions stung them at night; jailers flogged them by day. They lived entirely at the mercy of the whims of Idris-es-Saier and the peculiar spirit Nebbi Khiddr, who invariably reported against them to the Khalifa exactly when Idris needed funds for his starving family. Religious scholars were dispatched by the Khalifa to convert them to Islam, turning lengthy theological debates into eagerly anticipated events.
