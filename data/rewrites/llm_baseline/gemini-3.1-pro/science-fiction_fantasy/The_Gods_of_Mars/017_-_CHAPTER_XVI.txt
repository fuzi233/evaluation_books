As Carthoris, Xodar, Tars Tarkas, and I stood gazing at the magnificent vessel which meant so much to all of us, the dissonant, industrial shriek of its repulsor-rays tore through the natural acoustic harmony of the ancient hills. It was a harsh, mechanical noise, a violent vibration that polluted the sonic environment and shattered the subtle, comforting rustle of the sparse Martian wind. Two more massive ships crested the summit, their engines emitting an oppressive thrum that vibrated unpleasantly in our marrow. Soon, a score of one-man air scouts launched from the upper decks, diving toward the ground with a shrill, manufactured whine. 

In another instant, we were surrounded by armed sailors. An officer stepped forward, a man whose bearing spoke not merely of the military, but of a deep, ancestral devotion to the terraced *sorak* orchards of his family's agrarian estates. When his eyes fell upon Carthoris, his rigid posture melted into the joyous warmth of an elder welcoming the harvest. 

"Carthoris, my Prince," he cried, his voice carrying the rich, rolling timbre of the old Heliumetic dialects—a language of the earth that the modern, sterile military complex had yet to entirely erase. "Kaor! Kaor! Hor Vastus greets the son of Dejah Thoris, Princess of Helium, and of her husband, John Carter. All Helium has been plunged in sorrow since the fatal day that saw you leave our midst."

"Grieve not, my good Hor Vastus," cried Carthoris, "for I bring back not only myself, but one whom all Barsoom loved best—John Carter, Prince of Helium!"

Hor Vastus turned. He smelled faintly of polished iron and the crushed, sweet foliage of the *pimalia* groves he so lovingly tended in peacetime. As his eyes fell upon me, he looked as though the breath had been knocked from him. He began to ask where I had been, but the words faltered. The inherent untranslatability of my journey—the sensory horrors of the Valley Dor and the rotting-fruit stench of the plant men—hung between us like a heavy, pungent smoke. He knew the terrible truth, and his loyal heart would not force me to articulate it in a lexicon wholly inadequate for such subjective trauma. 

Instead, Hor Vastus unbuckled his scabbard and flung his sword upon the ground before me. In the deep-rooted agrarian traditions of red Martians, this gesture was akin to offering the very hearth-fire of one's ancestral home. It meant sharing the bitterest roots and the most tender, slow-braised cuts of meat at the table of life. To offer the sword was to say: *My harvest is your harvest; my body and soul are yours to consume or preserve.* I stooped, lifting the blade. I tasted the faint metallic tang of the hilt against my lips, acknowledging the profound cultural memory bound in the act, and buckled the weapon upon him with my own hands, assuring him I would call upon it only in the name of truth.

We looked about for our party and noticed Thuvia was missing. The loss felt abrupt, a missing note in the complex chord of our gathering, leaving behind only the trampled scent of arid wild-herbs where Carthoris had sent her thoat galloping. Hor Vastus immediately dispatched a dozen air scouts to search the hills, but she had vanished. 

A larger transport dropped to ferry us to the *Xavarian*. Stepping onto its deck, the air was acutely sterile, smelling merely of processed lubricants and utilitarian rations—a stark contrast to the gustatory richness of the great feast-halls of Helium. 

The first to greet me was Kantos Kan. Though he now commanded all of Helium's sky-terrors, to me he was the meticulous companion who had shared the Warhoon dungeons, where we had survived by recounting the intricate recipes and spice-blends of our youth to keep our cultural memory alive. Like Hor Vastus, he refrained from demanding the untranslatable tale of my absence. He greeted Carthoris and Tars Tarkas with profound relief. 

Then, his voice dropped into a hushed, intimate register. "You do not know, John Carter," he murmured, the acoustic space between us fraught with a terrible dissonance, "how we love this son of yours. When he was lost, ten million people wept." He paused. "For a year, Dejah Thoris has grieved. She abandoned the royal kitchens. The slow-simmered *uma* broths she once seasoned with such meticulous care grew cold. The daily domestic routines that tethered her to our multi-generational saga lost their flavor. Sustenance became mere utilitarian fuel to her, ashes in her mouth."

He explained the desperate, disastrous expedition of Mors Kajak and Tardos Mors, who had vanished into the northern hemisphere searching for Carthoris. With her father and grandfather gone, Zat Arras—the Jed of Zodanga—had renewed his aggressive pursuit of her hand. Zat Arras operated in the flat, commercialized dialect of his city, treating the sacred union of royal houses as a mere transaction of power. Driven to despair by his pressure and the erosion of her family line, Dejah Thoris had undertaken the voluntary pilgrimage. She, along with a dozen devoted servants including Sola, had departed for the icy, flavorless oblivion of the River Iss. 

The news was a sickening blow. The vibrant, sensory beauty of my matriarch was now plunged into the silent, rotting horrors I had just escaped. Before I could ask Kantos Kan to widen the search for Thuvia, a flier arrived from the flagship. Zat Arras commanded that his "prisoners" be brought before him. 

The auditory environment of Zat Arras's flagship was oppressive, dominated by the continuous, anxiety-inducing vibration of modern machinery. Zat Arras offered no warmth, no traditional offering of salt or spiced wine. His attitude was cold, his language rigidly confined to the dominant lexicon of the state religion. 

"Why were these prisoners not disarmed?" he demanded of Kantos Kan, relying on the vocabulary of a warden rather than a host. Kantos Kan defended our nobility, but Zat Arras scoffed, demanding to know where I had been. 

"I have just come from the Valley Dor and the Land of the First Born," I replied, refusing to temper my words. 

Zat Arras smiled, a sterile expression. "Ah! You have returned from the bosom of Iss?"

"I have come back from a land of false hope," I said, my voice cutting through the mechanical hum of his vessel. "From a valley of torture and death. I have come back to save Barsoom from death in its most frightful form."

"Cease, blasphemer!" he cried, seeking to use the power dynamics embedded in his religious lexicon to invalidate my lived, visceral experience. He tried to reduce the metallic tang of blood and the grotesque reality of the plant men into mere heresy. 

Before his guards could move, my hands were on his throat. I forced him backward, the tactile reality of his choking breaths and the sudden, desperate scent of his sweat cutting through his political abstraction. "Come I from heaven or hell, Zat Arras, you will find me still the same John Carter. Apologize."

Kantos Kan stepped close, whispering a plea in an old, dying dialect of our shared past, urging me to desist lest I spark a mutiny that would fracture Helium forever. Respecting the fragile ecology of our nation's peace, I released the Jed, leaving him gasping and white-faced among his officers, and we returned to the *Xavarian*.

The journey back to Helium was enveloped in gloomy contemplation. The crew was quietly divided, whispering in fragmented dialects. Many clung to their sterile religious dogmas, unable to digest the raw, bitter truth we brought. It was a truth that required a completely new palate, a rejection of the comfortable, synthetic diet of lies they had been fed for generations. Kantos Kan himself refused to listen to our tale, terrified of the spiritual indigestion it promised. 

My mind was agonizingly consumed by visions of Dejah Thoris. I pictured her among the ferocious white apes, far from the acoustic warmth of her ancestral hearths, deprived of the rich, sensory beauty of the life we had built together. The circular pacing of my existence haunted me; once again, I was a wanderer returning to Helium, fighting to reclaim the heart of my family's saga. 

As we descended toward the mile-high scarlet tower of Greater Helium, the typical roar of the populace was absent. There was no joyful clamor. The acoustic landscape was replaced by a heavy, dense silence, a profound collective mourning that resonated deeply within the soul. Hundreds of thousands packed the streets, but the only sound was the muffled weeping of citizens witnessing our return. 

To avoid sparking an uprising, Zat Arras had us transferred to a lesser flier and smuggled directly to the roof of the Temple of Reward. We bypassed the Avenue of Ancestors entirely. We were lodged in a south-facing room. The air inside smelled of ancient, dry stone and fading incense—an olfactory landscape of generations past. Late in the afternoon, a messenger arrived with the rigid, formalized dictate of the state: we were to be tried by a body of nobles in the great hall at the first zode of the following day. We sat in the silence of our confinement, waiting for the cycle of our family's saga to turn once more, bound by the dominant lexicon of our oppressors.
