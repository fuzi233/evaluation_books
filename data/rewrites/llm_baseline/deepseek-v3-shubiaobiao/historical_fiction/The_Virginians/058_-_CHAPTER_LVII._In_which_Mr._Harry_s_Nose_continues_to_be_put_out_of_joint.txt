Madame de Bernstein was nearly as delighted as her Virginian nephews by the resolution of Harry’s ill-fated entanglement with Lady Maria. That very evening, George dispatched a note to the Baroness, recounting the final interview. Soon after, Lord Castlewood—who seldom paid his aunt the compliment of a visit—presented himself at her house with remarkable candor.

The game was over, and my lord saw no reason for further dissimulation. "She is my sister, after all," he said with an air of fraternal tenderness. "Opportunities like this do not come often at her age. I may have pitied your young Virginian, but blood is blood. One must stand by one’s own."

"Such noble loyalty does you credit," remarked the Baroness dryly.

Castlewood bowed. "The stakes are decided. No bitterness remains for the loser—and surely you, as the victor, bear no grudge?"

The Baroness assured him she was in the finest of spirits. "Confess, Eugene—that tender scene at the spunging-house, where Maria returned all Harry’s gifts? That was your invention?"

"Pure devotion," Castlewood replied, feigning innocence. "What else could move her to such generosity toward a betrayed lover?"

"But it was *you* who advised it?"

With a shrug, he admitted as much. "We might still have won, had not the elder brother appeared. I’ve since consoled Maria by pointing out the mercy of our defeat. Imagine if she had married Harry, only for George to emerge afterward!"

"*Effectivement*," said the Baroness, taking snuff. "Better the grave should yield its dead before the vows are sworn. Your Mr. George seems a sensible man—no more selfish than most eldest sons. Poor Molly fancied him a greenhorn, like his brother. She dreamed he might divide his fortune, in which case she would have taken Harry even so."

"Harry with half a loaf might suffice, but Harry with none?" Castlewood smirked.

"And what does that mean?"

"Only that the elder made it clear Harry would dine at *his* table, not his own."

"What a hardened creature!" cried Madame de Bernstein.

"Come, aunt—*cartes sur table*! He did no more than any man would. Even Molly admitted it, once her weeping was done. The woman was absurd—so infatuated at her age!"

"Yet I think it was a true passion," mused the Baroness. "Her farewell—*c’était touchant, ma parole!* I wept myself. The boy, mud-spattered and wild-haired, burst in upon us at dinner, declared himself nobly, and she answered like a tragedy queen. It put me in mind of *Douglas*—the very dignity of despair! If she had stumbled, none could retreat more gracefully."

"She will not die of it," said Castlewood, baring his teeth.

"At her age, such maladies are seldom fatal," agreed the Baroness, sipping her chocolate.

The great world, as any observer knows, quarrels and reconciles without lingering resentment. The Baroness and the Castlewoods, having battled over Harry like wolves over a bone, now resumed their civilities as if no rupture had occurred. Madame de Bernstein’s carriage rolled up to Lady Castlewood’s rout; the Esmond ladies smiled at her assemblies—all affection restored.

When next the Baroness encountered George, she wagged her stick at him in mock reproach. "So, sir! You have played the stern elder brother to perfection!"

George reddened. "I only followed your ladyship’s advice—testing whether it was himself or his fortune they loved."

"Nay, Maria could hardly marry a pauper whose own brother denied him!"

"I acted for the best," George insisted.

"Hypocrite!" cried the old lady, laughing.

"Hypocrite? How so?"

"I know *all*, child!" she exclaimed in French. "You are your grandfather reborn! Come, let me embrace you. Harry has told me—you divided your patrimony with him!"

"It was no more than right," George said, eyes brightening. "We have shared all since birth. I feigned hardness only to unmask those who sought him."

"And will you divide Virginia as well?"

"That would not be just. The land must follow the elder’s right—Harry would not have it otherwise. But half of what is mine is his. His extravagance sprang only from error."

"A knight of old! A Bayard!" the Baroness declared that night to her maid. And when the brothers departed, it was Harry who received her fingers, while George was granted the rouged cheek—a favor that flushed him as deeply as the paint itself.

Yet for all his generosity, Harry could not escape a pang of humiliation. His fall from golden youth to dependent younger brother weighed upon him. At Kensington, George now reigned supreme, courted and flattered, while Harry was left to Maria’s mournful glances—reproachful eyes that accused him of accepting his freedom too readily.

Ah, the folly of love’s aftermath! What begins in poetry ends in farce. Damon and Phillis set out hand in hand, dreaming of eternal devotion—only to part at the first toll-gate. And when years later they meet again, he a worn-out rake, she a matron with a brood of children, how absurd the memory of past sighs!

So Harry found it at Kensington. Maria’s sighs, once enchanting, now rang hollow. The pipe in his chambers offered better company. He excused himself from George’s visits, pleading engagements.

Even in Hill Street, his uncle’s family grew cold. The ladies now denied him outright—those who had once fawned upon him! Once, calling upon Aunt Lambert after a rebuff at his aunt’s, he saw Lady Warrington’s chairmen arrive—and the lady herself, stepping in without a blush as he bowed from the window.

"My own father’s brother’s wife!" Harry lamented. "What have I done to earn this?"

"You will always find warmth here," said Aunt Lambert, with a glance toward her youngest.

"Your house is different," Harry replied. "I am always at peace within it."

The General, overhearing, quoted cheerfully:

"Where did I find that, scholar?" he challenged his son.

"Catullus," ventured the young man.

"Wrong! My old friend Burton—Democritus Junior—who has furnished many a lazy student with learning!" And the General chuckled, well pleased with his borrowed wisdom.
