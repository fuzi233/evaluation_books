As Archer stepped out into the lobby, the air shifted from the static hum of the club to the chaotic rhythm of the street. He collided with Ned Winsett, a figure whose shabby, rounded shoulders seemed to absorb the light rather than reflect it. Among the "clever people" Janey mentioned, Winsett was the only anomaly Archer trusted to pierce the surface of banal chatter. Archer had spotted him earlier, his eyes fixed on the Beaufort box, a silent observation that now demanded acknowledgment.

Their handshake was brief, a transaction of recognition. Winsett proposed a bock at a German restaurant around the corner, a place where the noise would drown out the weight of things. Archer declined. He needed to remain in the quiet architecture of his own duties. "Work to do at home," he lied, or perhaps stated as a simple fact. Winsett accepted the refusal with the easy indifference of a man who had long ceased to expect the world to accommodate his needs. "So have I," Winsett replied, adopting the persona of the Industrious Apprentice, a role he played with a weary sincerity.

They walked together, two figures moving through the crowd, until Winsett broke the silence. "Tell me the name of the dark lady in that swell box of yours—the one with the Beauforts. Wasn't it her?"

Archer felt a sudden, sharp irritation, though he could not name its source. Why did Winsett seek Ellen Olenska's name? And why link her to Lefferts? It was inconsistent with Winsett's usual aloofness. Yet, Archer recalled the journalist's nature; curiosity was his currency. "Not for an interview, I hope?" Archer asked, forcing a laugh to mask the tension.

"Not for the press," Winsett corrected, his voice dropping to a conspiratorial murmur. "Just for myself. She's a neighbor of mine—a queer quarter for such a beauty to inhabit. She saved my little boy. He fell in her garden chasing a kitten, cutting his knee badly. She rushed in, bareheaded, carrying him, his knee wrapped in bandages. She was so sympathetic, so radiant, that my wife was too dazzled to ask her name."

A warm, inexplicable glow expanded in Archer's chest. The story itself was ordinary; any woman might have acted so for a neighbor's child. But the image was specific: the bare head, the child in arms, the blinding kindness that rendered Mrs. Winsett speechless. It was precisely the kind of gesture Ellen would make.

"That is the Countess Olenska," Archer said softly. "A granddaughter of old Mrs. Mingott's."

"Whew—a Countess!" Winsett whistled, the sound thin and brittle. "I didn't know Countesses were so neighborly. The Mingotts aren't."

"They would be, if you'd let them," Archer countered, the old argument rising instinctively.

Winsett sighed, the sound of a man giving up a battle he knew was lost. "How does a Countess happen to live in our slum?"

"Because she doesn't care about where she lives—or about our little social sign-posts," Archer said, feeling a secret pride in his mental portrait of her.

"H'm. Been in bigger places, I suppose," Winsett murmured.

"Well, here's my corner," he added, turning away. He slouched across Broadway, disappearing into the flow of pedestrians. Archer stood watching him, musing on the journalist's final words. Winsett possessed flashes of piercing insight, moments that made Archer question why such a man accepted failure so stoically at an age when others were still clawing for position. Archer knew Winsett had a wife and child, yet he had never truly seen them. Their meetings were confined to the Century Club or the haunts of journalists and actors, places where the edges of life blurred. Winsett had hinted that his wife was an invalid—a phrase that could mean illness, or merely a lack of social grace, or the absence of evening gowns.

Winsett despised social rituals with a savage intensity. Archer, who dressed in the evening because he believed it cleaner and more comfortable, viewed Winsett's disdain as part of a bored Bohemian pose. To Archer, the fashionable elite, who changed clothes without comment and never counted their servants, seemed far less self-conscious than the "artists" who wore their poverty like a badge. Still, Winsett stimulated him. Whenever Archer caught sight of the journalist's lean, bearded face and melancholy eyes, he would drag him out of his corner for a long talk.

Winsett was not a journalist by choice. He was a man of letters, born too early for a world that no longer valued letters. After publishing a single volume of exquisite literary appreciations—one hundred and twenty copies sold, thirty given away, the rest destroyed by publishers to make room for marketable trash—he had abandoned his true vocation. He now worked as a sub-editor on a women's weekly, where fashion plates and New England love stories alternated with ads for temperance drinks. On the subject of "Hearth-fires," the paper's name, he was endlessly entertaining. But beneath the humor lay the sterile bitterness of a young man who had tried and failed.

His conversation forced Archer to measure his own life against Winsett's emptiness. Their exchanges, though intellectually stimulating, remained trapped within the limits of a pensive dilettantism. "Life isn't much a fit for either of us," Winsett had once said. "I'm down and out; nothing can be done. I have only one ware to produce, and there is no market for it. But you are free and well-off. Why don't you get into touch? Go into politics."

Archer threw his head back and laughed. There, instantly, lay the unbridgeable chasm between men like Winsett and Archer's class. Everyone in polite society knew that in America, a gentleman could not enter politics. Since he could not say this plainly, he answered evasively. "Look at the career of the honest man in American politics! They don't want us."

"Who is 'they'? Why don't you all get together and be 'they' yourselves?"

Archer's laughter faded into a slightly condescending smile. It was futile to continue. The country belonged to the bosses and the immigrants; decent people were relegated to sport or culture. "Culture! Yes—if we had it! But there are just a few little local patches, dying out here and there for lack of hoeing and cross-fertilizing: the last remnants of the old European tradition your forebears brought. But you are a pitiful minority. You have no center, no competition, no audience. You are like the pictures on the walls of a deserted house: 'The Portrait of a Gentleman.' You will never amount to anything unless you roll up your sleeves and get right down into the muck. Or emigrate... God! If I could emigrate..."

Archer mentally shrugged and turned the conversation back to books, the only terrain where Winsett remained interesting. Emigrate! As if a gentleman could abandon his own country! One could not do that any more than one could roll up one's sleeves and descend into the filth. A gentleman simply stayed home and abstained. But Winsett could not see this logic. That was why New York, with its literary clubs and exotic restaurants, ultimately revealed itself as a small box with a monotonous pattern, inferior even to the scattered atoms of Fifth Avenue.

The next morning, Archer searched the town in vain for yellow roses. Arriving late at the office, he realized his tardiness meant nothing to anyone, filling him with a sudden, sharp exasperation at the elaborate futility of his existence. Why not be, at that very moment, on the sands of St. Augustine with May Welland? No one was deceived by his pretense of professional activity. In old-fashioned legal firms like Mr. Letterblair's, where large estates and conservative investments were managed, there were always a few young men, fairly wealthy and devoid of ambition, who sat at desks performing trivial tasks or reading newspapers. Though propriety demanded an occupation, the crude act of money-making was considered derogatory; the law was deemed a more gentlemanly pursuit. Yet, none of these men hoped to advance, and the green mold of the perfunctory was already spreading over them.

The thought made Archer shiver. Could it spread over him too? He had other tastes, other interests. He spent his vacations traveling Europe, cultivated the "clever people" May spoke of, and generally tried to "keep up," as he had wistfully confessed to Madame Olenska. But once he was married, what would become of this narrow margin of life where his real experiences occurred? He had watched other young men dream his dream, only to sink gradually into the placid, luxurious routine of their elders.

From the office, he sent a note by messenger to Madame Olenska, asking if he might call that afternoon, begging her to leave a reply at his club. But the club held nothing. Nor did he receive a letter the following day. This unexpected silence mortified him beyond reason. The next morning, he saw a glorious cluster of yellow roses behind a florist's window, but he left them there.
