The sting of Fontenelle's critique—that one cannot claim humility for having learned the earth revolves around the sun—lingered in Gwendolen's mind, transforming into a fresh, peculiar ache. She refused to acknowledge the misfortune of lacking Miss Arrowpoint's musical tutelage, a deficit that robbed her of the authority to judge Herr Klesmer's taste with the confidence of true expertise. Even more intolerable was the admission, forbidden even to her own conscience, that every encounter with Miss Arrowpoint ignited a sudden, unwonted spark of jealousy within her. It was not the disparity of wealth that fueled this fire; rather, it was the infuriating precision of a girl whose physical presence—one might describe as slight, of medium height, with small features, tolerable eyes, and a sallow complexion—nevertheless radiated a mental superiority that defied explanation. Miss Arrowpoint possessed an exasperating thoroughness in her musical command and a fastidious discernment in her general tastes that rendered her admiration impossible to force, keeping others in a perpetual state of awestruck deference. This seemingly insignificant twenty-four-year-old, whom any observer would casually overlook were it not for her title, harbored a secret conviction that Miss Harleth's accomplishments belonged to the realm of the common. Such a thought was unbearable, especially when cloaked beneath Miss Arrowpoint's impenetrable veneer of perfect kindness.

Gwendolen shunned dwelling on facts that cast such an unfavorable shadow upon her own reflection. The Musical Magus, who had so abruptly expanded her horizons, was not a constant presence; his frequent shuttling between London and Quetcham soon appeared to offer convenient openings for converting him to a more admiring disposition. In the meantime, the evident pleasure her singing elicited at Brackenshaw Castle, the Firs, and elsewhere restored her equilibrium. She inclined toward believing approval more trustworthy than objection, certainly not among those rare souls who suffer from a parching thirst for a perfection their neighbors do not demand. Perhaps it would have been presumptuous to claim she was exceptionally unique in her interiority, beyond her rare grace of movement and bearing, and a certain daring that lent piquancy to a very common egoistic ambition—one that thrives beneath clumsy exteriors and remains unnoticed. After all, the tilt of the head does not truly dictate the inner self's hunger for supremacy; it merely alters the method by which that supremacy is pursued and the degree to which it can be achieved. This dynamic holds particularly true for a girl, whose passion for achieving the remarkable finds its ideal limit in consistency with the highest breeding and a complete freedom from the sordid necessity of income. Gwendolen was inwardly rebellious against the restraints of family conditions, ready to peer through obligations to her own fundamental lack of feeling for them, as if sustained by the boldest speculations. Yet, she possessed no such speculations whatsoever. She would instantly distinguish herself from any theoretically or practically reforming woman by satirizing them. She rejoiced in feeling exceptional, yet her horizon remained confined to the genteel romance wherein the heroine's soul pours out in journals filled with vague power, originality, and general rebellion, while her actual life moves strictly within the sphere of fashion. Should she wander into a swamp, the pathos lies partly, so to speak, in her wearing satin shoes. Nature and society provide this restraint on the pursuit of striking adventure; thus, a soul burning with a sense of what the universe is not, ready to consume all existence as fuel, remains captive to the ordinary wirework of social forms, accomplishing nothing particular.

This commonplace result was the threat Gwendolen sensed even amidst the novelty of the first winter at Offendene. She was clear that she did not wish to lead the same life as ordinary young ladies, yet she remained unclear on how to begin leading any other, or what specific acts she would assert her freedom through. Offendene remained a suitable backdrop if anything were to occur there, but overall, the neighborhood fell short. Beyond the initial impact of her beauty at a first presentation, there was little excitement to be gleaned from her earliest invitations. Returning home after brief sallies of satire and knowingness—which had previously offended Mrs. Arrowpoint—she filled the intervening days with the most girlish devices. Her strongest assertion of individual claim was to omit Alice's lessons, operating on the principle that Alice was more likely to excel in ignorance, and instead employ her with Miss Merry and the maid rumored to attend to all the ladies. Together, they arranged various dramatic costumes which Gwendolen delighted in preparing for future occasions of acting in charades or theatrical pieces, occasions she intended to summon forth by sheer force of will or contrivance. Though she had never acted professionally—having only posed in *tableaux vivants* at school—she felt assured of her ability to perform well. Having visited the Théâtre Français once or twice and listened to her mother speak of Rachel, her waking dreams and cogitations regarding her destiny often turned on the question of whether she should become an actress like Rachel, given that she was more beautiful than that thin Jewess.

The wet days preceding Christmas were spent pleasantly in the preparation of Greek, Oriental, and composite costumes. Gwendolen attitudined and declaimed before a domestic audience, which included even the housekeeper, who was once pressed into service to swell the notes of applause. However, having demonstrated her unworthiness by observing that Miss Harleth looked far more regal in her own attire than in that baggy garment exposing her arms, she was not invited to participate a second time.

"Do I look as well as Rachel, mamma?" Gwendolen asked one day, displaying herself in her Greek dress to Anna and enacting fragments of scenes with much tragic intent.

"You possess better arms than Rachel," Mrs. Davilow replied. "Your arms would suffice for anything, Gwen. But your voice lacks her tragedy; it is not so deep."

"I can deepen it, if I choose," Gwendolen said provisionally, then added with decision, "I believe a higher voice is more tragic. It is more feminine. And the more feminine a woman is, the more tragic she appears when performing desperate actions."

"There may be truth in that," Mrs. Davilow said languidly. "But I do not understand the utility of making one's blood creep. And if there is anything horrible to be done, I should prefer it left to the men."

"Oh, mamma, you are dreadfully prosaic!" Gwendolen exclaimed. "As if all the great poetic criminals were not women! I consider the men poor, cautious creatures."

"Well, dear, and you—who are afraid to be alone in the night—I do not think you would be very bold in crime, thank God."

"I am not speaking of reality, mamma," Gwendolen said impatiently. Once her mother was called out of the room, she turned quickly to her cousin, seizing the opportunity. "Anna, please ask my uncle to organize some charades at the rectory. Mr. Middleton and Warham could act with us—simply for practice. Mamma insists it is improper for Mr. Middleton to consult and rehearse here. He is a stick, but we could assign him suitable parts. Please ask, or else I will."

"Oh, not until Rex arrives. He is so clever, and such a dear old thing, and he will act as Napoleon surveying the sea. He looks exactly like Napoleon. Rex can do anything."

"I do not in the least believe in your Rex, Anna," Gwendolen laughed at her. "He will turn out to be like those wretched blue and yellow watercolors of his which you hang in your bedroom and worship."

"Very well, you will see," Anna retorted. "It is not that I know what constitutes cleverness, but he has secured a scholarship already, and Papa says he will obtain a fellowship. Nobody is better at games. He is cleverer than Mr. Middleton, and everyone but you considers Mr. Middleton clever."

"So he may be clever in a dark-lantern sort of way. But he _is_ a stick. If he were forced to say, 'Perdition catch my soul, but I do love her,' he would utter it in precisely the same tone as, 'Here endeth the second lesson.'"

"Oh, Gwendolen!" Anna cried, shocked by these promiscuous allusions. "And it is very unkind of you to speak so of him, for he admires you greatly. I heard Warham tell Mama one day, 'Middleton is regularly spooney upon Gwendolen.' She was furious with him; but I know what it means. It is the phrase used at college for being in love."

"How can I help it?" Gwendolen said, rather contemptuously. "Perdition catch my soul if I love _him_."

"No, of course; Papa, I think, would not wish it. And he is departing soon. But it saddens me when you ridicule him."

"What will you do to me when I ridicule Rex?" Gwendolen asked wickedly.

"Now, Gwendolen, dear, you _will not_?" Anna said, her eyes filling with tears. "I could not bear it. But there is truly nothing in him to ridicule. Only you may discover things. For no one ever thought of laughing at Mr. Middleton before you. Everyone said he was good-looking, with perfect manners. I am sure I have always been intimidated by him because of his learning, his square-cut coat, his status as the Bishop's nephew, and all that. But you will not ridicule Rex—promise me." Anna concluded with a beseeching look that touched Gwendolen.
