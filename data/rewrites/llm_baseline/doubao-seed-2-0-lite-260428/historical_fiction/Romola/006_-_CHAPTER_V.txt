The Via de’ Bardi, a street steeped in Florentine history, winds through the Oltrarno — the stretch of the city that clings to the southern bank of the Arno. It runs from the foot of the Ponte Vecchio all the way to the Piazza de’ Mozzi, where the Ponte alle Grazie begins. The houses and high walls on its right side back into the steep slopes of the old Bogoli hill, the famous 15th-century stone quarry that paved all of Florence, its soft rock notoriously prone to shifting and eroding after heavy rains. The buildings on its left side press right up to the river, their facades pocked with irregular, quaint little openings that cast soft, warm reflections on the water as the sun sinks low behind the western Tuscan hills. For all their charm, many of these surviving structures feel far too modern to anyone who remembers the fate of the original Bardi family palaces, burned to the ground by an enraged rioting crowd in the mid-1300s.

The Bardi were a proud, ferocious clan, first among the nobles who clashed in Florence’s earliest legendary civil wars, when narrow streets were shadowed by their high defensive towers and the old patron god Mars might well have grinned to see gutters running red with neighbours’ blood, after centuries of the city pretending to worship his rival the Baptist. But the Bardi were not only good with a sword: they had the true Florentine gift for spotting where real power lay beyond street brawls. By the 14th century, they had climbed from their roots as common popolani to buy feudal lands, strongholds, and the title of Counts of Vernio, rousing no small jealousy in their republican fellow citizens. Their sudden fortune is no mystery: a few years later, they were at the very peak of European commerce, the medieval equivalent of the Christian Rothschilds, advancing huge sums to King Edward III of England to fund his wars, and taking payment in coveted wool shipments carried by Florentine merchant galleys. But Edward defaulted spectacularly on his debts, and panicking Sicilian creditors demanded all their deposits back at once, sending the Bardi and their allied trading houses crashing in a commercial calamity whose shockwaves rippled across every Mediterranean coast.

For all that, the Bardi never slunk away in shame: they held their heads higher than ever, growing so arrogant in their remaining power that they drew the full fury of the armed citizen militia in the 1343 uprising, recorded in unflinching detail by the chronicler Giovanni Villani. Barricaded in their narrow street between the two bridges, they fought like cornered panthers to hold the militia back, only falling when rioters stormed down at them from the hill behind their homes. Twenty-two of their grand palaces along the river were sacked and burned, and most of the leading Bardi were exiled from Florence. But old Florentine families had deep, tangled roots: for generations afterwards, the Bardi kept bobbing back to the surface of public life, through fortunes of disgrace and renewal, of wealth and ruin, so common that Archbishop Antonine of San Marco founded the society of Buonuomini di San Martino explicitly to help the poveri vergognosi — “ashamed paupers,” fallen nobles of good family who would starve rather than beg publicly. By 1492, though, the Bardi no longer owned their old riverfront street, now mostly belonging to the wealthy Neri family, who held a long stretch of houses along the hill side of the road.

In one of those Neri homes lived a Bardi descendant, from the very line that had earned the Counts of Vernio title 150 years before. He carried all the old family pride, drive, and hunger to leave a lasting mark on the world — but under very different circumstances. This Bardo de’ Bardi was no street brawler, no feudal lord fortifying strongholds, no daring merchant plotting cross-continental trade schemes. His deep-veined hands were cramped and gnarled from decades of copying manuscripts, he ate meagre little dinners and wore threadbare clothes, first by choice and then by necessity. He spent all his days surrounded by books and fragments of ancient marble, seeing nothing of the present world, living entirely in the glowing memory of his long-past youth. He was penniless, blind, and it was to him that the barber Nello had promised to introduce the young Greek stranger Tito Melema.

Bardo’s home, on the hill side of the street, was one of those sombre, massive stone palaces dotted all over old Florence, pierced with tiny windows and topped with an open-air roof loggia. Its heavy oak doors, fitted with great curled iron hinges, led into a vaulted entrance courtyard, empty save for a single giant iron lamp hanging from the centre of the arched ceiling. A smaller side staircase door led up past the ground and first floors, both of which the merchant owner used as vast warehouses piled high with goods bound for ports as far away as the Scheldt river, the North African coast, the Aegean islands, and the shores of the Black Sea. Old Maso, Bardo’s slow-moving long-time servant, would trudge up all the way to the third floor after his trips to the market for cheap vegetables, to reach his master’s living quarters, where we arrive on a quiet morning not long after Nello made his promise to Tito.

Maso leads us through a small antechamber and opens the inner door, peeking in with a nod. A clear, soft young voice calls out, “Ah, you are back, Maso. All is well, we have wanted for nothing while you were gone.”

The voice comes from the far end of a long, spacious study lined with shelves, every book and antiquity arranged in scrupulous, exact order. Small stands tucked in front of the shelves hold a half-feminine marble torso, a headless Roman warrior statue with one muscular arm raised to swing a now-missing sword, rounded dimpled fragments of carved infant limbs that look soft enough to kiss, well-preserved imperial busts, and two or three elegant painted vases from Magna Grecia. A great heavy table in the centre of the room is heaped with antique bronze oil lamps and dark rough pottery vessels. Almost every surface is pale or muted: the deep ridged vellum bindings of the old manuscripts blend into the cool, livid marble that has spent centuries buried under Italian soil, the once-rich carpet at the far end of the room is worn almost completely dull, the dark bronzes have no sunlight to bring out their faint green patina — and the sun is not yet high enough to send bright slants of light through the narrow windows facing the Via de’ Bardi.

The only splash of vivid colour in the whole room is the hair of a tall seventeen or eighteen-year-old maiden, standing before a carved wooden reading desk of the kind usually found in church choirs. Her hair is a rippling reddish gold, like the colour of sunset wisping across vast autumn skies, held back from her small ears by a thin black fillet, the soft curls falling down to frame her neck above a plain square-cut gown of rough black serge. Her eyes are fixed on the great heavy volume open on the desk before her, one long white hand resting on its edge, the other curled gently around the back of her father’s chair.

The blind old Bardo sits with his head tilted up, turned a little towards his daughter as if he could see her. His fine pale face, framed by drooping white hair under a soft black velvet cap, shares every feature with the young girl beside him: neither of them has a trace of rose flush in their cheeks, both have the same refined high brow and delicate nostril, balanced by a full, firm mouth and strong, determined jaw that gives their faces an air of proud stubbornness and hidden, smouldering passion. It is a face that leaves you unsure if it would draw love, or only a kind of awed, reluctant admiration — the kind of question only the eyes can answer. But Bardo’s eyes have long been dark and silent, and Romola’s gaze is fixed on the Latin pages of Politian’s *Miscellanea*, reading aloud the eightieth chapter, telling the tale of the nymph Chariclo, beloved of Pallas Athene, mother of the prophet Tiresias. The story goes that Tiresias stumbled upon Athene bathing naked in the waters of the Hippocrene spring on Mount Helicon, and the goddess blinded him for seeing her against her will. But when Chariclo begged for mercy, Athene gave him the gifts of prophecy, extended life, a guiding staff to keep him from stumbling, and the ability to hold the perfect memory of her unveiled face in his mind forever, even in death.

At that line, Romola’s hand slips from the back of her father’s chair and meets his, as he lifts his own to find her. She does not look around, her voice trembling a little with unspoken feeling as she prepares to read the Greek verse quotation from Nonnus that follows, but Bardo stops her.

“Wait, Romola. Fetch my own copy of Nonnus. It is far more accurate than any Politian owns — I finished adding my own emendations to it in 1477, the very last year I could still see clearly, and I have never shown my notes to another soul.”

Romola crosses the room to the far shelf with a natural, unselfconscious, almost queenly grace, the easy bearing of her tall, well-built frame with no hint of affected posturing.

“Is it still where I left it, Romola?” Bardo asks, perpetual uncertainty pricking at him, always needing confirmation that the physical world still matches the perfect, minute map of it held in his memory.

“Yes, father. At the west end of the room, third shelf up from the floor, behind the bust of Hadrian, right between Apollonius Rhodius and Callimachus, below Lucan and Silius Italicus.”

A fine ear might catch a faint edge of quiet weariness under her soft, distinct tone, but when she sees Bardo stretch out his arms, trembling with nervous excitement to seize the book, her hazel eyes fill with pity. She hurries to lay the great volume in his lap, then kneels beside him, looking up at him as if the love shining out of her face could somehow pierce the dark wall of his blindness. For a moment, the ambiguous, proud tension in her features softens completely, transfigured by that warm, tender mingling of pity and affection that makes her face glow with the most gentle womanliness — the deepest fountain of her feelings has not yet etched itself into her steady features, and only pours out through her eyes.

Bardo, unaware of that soft radiance, is flushed and agitated, his fingers tracing the edges and spine of the great book. “The vellum has yellowed these past thirteen years, Romola.”

“Yes, father,” she says gently. “But your Roman ink lettering at the back is still dark and sharp, clear as can be — your Greek script is more beautiful than any of the handwritten manuscripts you ever bought from hired scribes.”

“Of course it is, child,” Bardo says, his finger brushing across the page as if he can still make out the lines. “What paid scribe could ever match a man who loves the words he copies, to whom a typo or smudge in a text feels worse than stumbling over a hidden obstacle in the dark? Even these new mechanical printing presses, that threaten to drag all great learning down into cheap vulgarity — they still have to copy their editions from the manuscripts we scholars have pored over, our insight into the poet’s meaning almost equal to the divine spark that made the poem in the first place. If they do not, they will flood the world with garbage, turn the very fountain of the Muses into a flood of poison mud. Now find that passage in Book Five, the one Politian cites — I know it by heart.”

Romola pulls a low stool close to his knee, takes the book in her lap, and reads the four lines of Nonnus where Actaeon cries out that Tiresias was the luckiest man alive: he did not have to die to see the gods unveiled, losing only his physical sight to carry the perfect memory of Athene in his soul forever.

“It is true, Romola,” Bardo says when she finishes. “That trivial, narrow sight that lets men see only the petty scenes right in front of their faces is nothing next to the wide, endless light of ancient wisdom, that stretches across centuries of thought, across the lives of entire nations, letting us walk and talk with the great immortal minds of the past. Even when I could see, I only ever truly lived with those dead great ones. The living always felt like faint, empty ghosts to me. Unlike those Florentines Politian compares to the Lamiae, who took off their eyes when they got home so they could pretend to have no knowledge of the world outside, I would walk home from the streets like I was waking up from a forgotten dream, sit down among my books, and quote Petrarch — the only modern man worthy to be named beside the ancients — ‘Books delight us to the marrow, they speak to us, they give us counsel, and wrap us in a bright, living friendship.’”

“And you are luckier than Petrarch, father,” Romola says, humouring his fond tangents with gentle affection. “He pored over his copy of Homer and grieved that he could never read Greek — he carried that inner blindness, far worse than your loss of sight.”

“True, child. I learned Greek under the great teachers Crisolora the younger, Filelfo, Argiropulo, and all that hard study bore fruit in my mind. But the great magnum opus I spent my whole life planning — the work that would weave every thread of my decades of research into a single definitive, masterful volume — was cut short by my failing sight, because I never had a fitting young assistant to help me. The sustained zeal and endless patience you need to blaze new paths in knowledge is even more impossible to reconcile with the wandering, flighty nature of the feminine mind than with their weaker physical bodies.”

Romola flushes bright red, stung and hurt. “Father! I read every page you ask me to, I hunt down every reference you name, I write every note you dictate. I will do anything for your work.”

Bardo shakes his head, smiling a bitter, pitying little smile. “You might as well try to compete in the five events of the pentathlon with the limbs of a delicate nymph. Have I forgotten how you fainted from exhaustion just searching for references to explain a single obscure line of Callimachus?”

“That was only because the great folios were too heavy,” Romola protests. “Maso could have carried them for me. It was not for lack of attention or patience.”

Bardo shakes his head again. “It is not just strong arms I need. I need the sharp, fresh edge of a young mind to cut new paths through the thickets of research, when my own faculties have gone blunt with age. Blindness acts like a dam, it sends all the streams of thought back down the channels I have already walked, and stops me moving forward. If only my son had never left me, lured away by debasing fanatical visions, the kind that only a madman who lives among tombs would chase. He was such a promising boy, I could have kept going, and my path would have broadened right up to the end of my days. Now the road has closed in around me. I have nothing left but a narrow little track to tread, alone in the dark.”

Romola leaps to her feet, stung too sharply by his words to stay seated and silent. She carries the great Nonnus folio back to its shelf, and stands a little way off, stretching her arms downwards and lacing her fingers tight, her young face heavy with quiet dreariness as she stares at the unmoving parchment spines, the mutilated marble fragments, the obsolete bronze and clay trinkets that line the room. Bardo, usually so attuned to every small movement she makes, is too lost in the bitter pain of old memories to notice she has moved away from his side.

“Yes,” he rants on, “if my son had stayed with me, the names of Bardi father and son would be spoken with reverence by scholars for centuries to come. Not for frivolous verses or presumptuous fake philosophy like all the vain men like Panhormita churn out, even Poggio was not free of that nonsense. No — we would have left a lamp for all future generations to read the greatest works of classical antiquity by. Why should a boy like Politian, who was not even born when I was already old enough to debate with Thomas of Sarzana, be remembered forever as a great commentator? Why should Ficino, whose Latin makes my flesh crawl, who stumbles blindly through the superstitious nonsense that marked the fall of art and literature and philosophy, be worshipped as the high priest of Platonism? Why, when I am far their equal? All because my son, whom I raised to bring my ripe old learning new young energy, abandoned me and all liberal pursuits to lash himself and howl at midnight alongside brainless friars, to wander off on stupid pilgrimages for men who can imagine no history older than the words in a missal. Left me just as the night began to fall over my eyes.”

His voice, which had risen high in indignant protest, drops into a tremulous, plaintive note of grief. Romola turns back to look at his blind, aged face, and her heart swells with forgiving pity. She sits back down beside him, laying her hand softly on his knee — too proud to blurt out empty consolations that would sound like she was trying to prove her own worth, but wanting him to feel she is there.

“Yes, Romola,” Bardo says, his heavy rings falling a little too hard on the back of her delicate blue-veined hand, making her bite her lip to keep from wincing. “If Florence remembers nothing else of me, it will remember me as they remember Niccolò Niccoli — a man who gave up chasing vulgar wealth in commerce to dedicate his life to collecting the precious remains of ancient art and wisdom, to leave them as an eternal gift to his fellow citizens, as the old munificent Romans did. Why stop at Florence? If Florence remembers me, the whole world will remember me... But Lorenzo de Medici’s untimely death has thrown everything into chaos. He promised me, he was going to give me a formal bond that my entire collection would keep my name forever, would never be sold off piecemeal to greedy harpies. But there is nothing to stop them now — they will take everything. And for you too, Romola, you will have nothing if we do not make plans. Bernardo del Nero keeps reproaching me for not finding you a suitable husband, a good family match. We will delay no longer. We will think of it now.”
