The Thames, usually a murky mystery of smoke and shadow, began to shimmer under the midday sun, transforming from a dull grey into a dazzling expanse of light. High above, the sun crested Westminster, casting its glare onto the bridge where two distinct figures crossed. One was towering and imposing; the other, diminutive and clad in clerical garb. They cut a silhouette reminiscent of the arrogant clock-tower of Parliament looming beside the humble, humped roofline of the Abbey.

The tall man was M. Hercule Flambeau, a private detective making his way to his new headquarters—a sleek, American-style skyscraper of flats facing the Abbey. Beside him walked the Reverend J. Brown, attached to St. Francis Xavier's in Camberwell, traveling from a local deathbed to visit his friend.

The building itself was a marvel of modern engineering, boasting elevators and telephone systems of oiled precision. Yet, it was unfinished, sparsely populated, and oddly vacant save for a few tenants. However, the most arresting feature was not the architecture, but a colossal installation erected outside the office directly above Flambeau's. It was a gigantic, gilded effigy of a human eye, radiating golden beams, dominating the facade as if challenging the sky.

"What on earth is that?" Father Brown asked, halting in his tracks.

"Oh, merely a new religion," Flambeau laughed dismissively. "One of those modern sects that absolve your sins by claiming you never committed them. Reminds me of Christian Science. A fellow named Kalon—though I'm unsure if 'Kalon' is his actual name—has rented the flat above me. I have two female typists beneath me, and this zealous old charlatan above. He styles himself the New Priest of Apollo and worships the sun."

"Let him beware," Father Brown warned. "The sun was the cruelest of all deities. But what purpose serves that monstrous eye?"

"It is their doctrine," Flambeau explained casually. "They believe a man can endure anything if his mind remains perfectly steady. Their twin symbols are the sun and the open eye; the theory posits that a truly healthy man could gaze directly into the sun."

"If a man were truly healthy," Father Brown countered, "he wouldn't bother staring at it."

"Well, that's all I know," Flambeau shrugged. "They claim to cure every physical ailment."

"And what of the one spiritual malady?" Father Brown pressed, his curiosity piqued.

"The spiritual sickness?" Flambeau smiled.

"Thinking oneself perfectly well," the priest replied.

Flambeau, however, found little interest in the flamboyant cult above. A lucid Southerner, he identified strictly as either Catholic or atheist; bright, pale new religions were not his forte. His attention was captured by the occupants of the office below. They were two sisters, slender and dark, dressed in severe black with sharp, masculine collars. The elder, Pauline Stacey, possessed a striking, aquiline profile and eyes of startling brilliance—not the soft glow of diamonds, but the cold flash of polished steel. She seemed to cleave through life with relentless determination. Her younger sister, Joan, was her diminished shadow: paler, quieter, yet bound to her leader by a dogged, tragic devotion.

Pauline Stacey was no ordinary secretary. She was theheiress to a vast fortune and a county estate, having traded castles for a "higher," harder existence. She retained her wealth not out of sentimentality, but for utilitarian purposes, funding a model typewriting empire and various women's leagues. While Joan followed her lead with blind loyalty, Pauline viewed tragedy with disdain, denying its very existence.

On their first meeting, Pauline had impressed Flambeau with her fierce independence. Refusing the lift-boy's assistance, she had ascended rapidly, lecturing him on the supremacy of mechanical science over romantic nonsense. She believed everyone should command machines as naturally as breathing, resented the chivalry of others holding doors, and possessed a temper that was both snappy and destructive. Once, in a fit of ethical fury regarding her sister's glasses, she had smashed them on the floor, declaring that relying on optical aids was a badge of cowardice.

"Batteries and motors are the force of man and woman!" she had declared earlier. "Science defies time! But these props and plasters sold by doctors are merely signs of poltroonery. I was free-born! People fear the sun because they were trained in fear, not power. Why should one star be forbidden? I will open my eyes and stare at the sun whenever I please."

"You will dazzle the sun," Flambeau had retorted with a bow, secretly amused by her defiance.

As he ascended to his own floor, Flambeau whistled low. "So, she has fallen into the hands of that conjurer upstairs with his golden eye." He recalled rumors of Kalon's ability to gaze unblinkingly at the noonday sun.

Indeed, the connection between the floors was tightening. Kalon was a magnificent figure, nearly as tall as Flambeau, with a golden beard, piercing blue eyes, and a mane swept back like a lion's. Despite the vulgar setting of a cramped office in Victoria Street, marked only by a brass plate and the giant eye outside, he commanded an aura of ancient royalty. Three times daily—at dawn, dusk, and noon—he stepped onto his balcony to chant a litany to his shining lord.

It was during the noon service that Father Brown first noticed the anomaly. While the crowds below blinked against the glare, the white-robed prophet stood erect, hands uplifted, eyes fixed on the blazing disk. He seemed to exist in a realm apart, oblivious to the world until a sudden, violent commotion shattered the scene.

A roar of chaos erupted as five people surged into the mansion gates while three fled outward. For a heartbeat, the street was filled with the sensation of impending disaster. Amidst the confusion, two figures remained frozen: the fair priest of Apollo on the balcony, and the dark priest of Christ below.

Moments later, Flambeau burst into the lobby, shouting orders for a surgeon, his voice booming like a foghorn. Father Brown slipped behind him, navigating the throng. Inside, they gathered around the elevator shaft. The lift hadn't moved; something else had plummeted.

Lying at the bottom was the body of Pauline Stacey. Blood stained her once-beautiful face. Flambeau, certain of her identity, assumed she was dead. Suicide seemed unlikely for such an insistent optimist; murder, however, raised questions about who could possibly have gained access to the isolated upper floors.

"We must find Kalon," Flambeau demanded, his voice cracking.

"He has been on his balcony for the last fifteen minutes," a calm voice replied. It was Father Brown speaking.

Flambeau turned, his face darkening. "If he was up there the whole time, who did it?"

"Perhaps we should check upstairs," suggested the priest.

Leaving the body to the surgeons, Flambeau raced to the offices below. Finding them empty, he dashed to his own floor, only to return with a look of stunned realization. "Her sister," he announced grimly. "Joan Stacey has gone for a walk."

"She may have gone to the sun-worshipper's office," Father Brown surmised. "Let us verify that, then discuss it in your office."

Together, they descended to the Stacey flat. Father Brown claimed a leather chair near the entrance, observing the stairwell intently. Soon, three solemn figures emerged: Joan Stacey, looking drawn and aged; Kalon, descending in utter magnificence in his white robes; and Flambeau, bewildered.

Joan immediately retreated to her desk, organizing papers with efficient composure. Father Brown watched her, then turned his gaze to the prophet.

"Prophet," he said, addressing Kalon. "Tell me about your religion."

"I would be honored," Kalon replied, bowing his crowned head.

"Why," Father Brown continued, "is it that you believe murder is permissible?"

"An accusation?" Kalon asked softly.

"No," Brown answered gently. "It is the defense's opening statement."

Silence stretched taut in the room. Then, Kalon rose, filling the space with a luminous intensity that seemed to expand the walls themselves.

"We meet at last, Caiaphas," the prophet declared. "Your church and mine are the only realities here. I adore the sun; you worship its decay. You are priests of the dying; I serve the living God. Your work is that of suspicion and slander; you seek confessions through treachery. You convict of sin; I proclaim innocence. You are a black police force; I am a beacon of virtue."

He paused, leaning against the mantle. "There is only one charge against me: I loved her. By a law purer than your tin chapels understand. This morning, she left me a will granting half a million pounds to my church. Where are the handcuffs? Do you imagine I fear prison? Penal servitude is merely waiting for her at a station; the gallows is a swift carriage ride to join her."

His words carried the weight of an orator, leaving Flambeau and Joan awestruck. Father Brown, however, looked deeply troubled.
