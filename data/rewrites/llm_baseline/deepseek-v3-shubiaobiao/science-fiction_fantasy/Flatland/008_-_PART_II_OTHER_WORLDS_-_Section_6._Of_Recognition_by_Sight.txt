At first glance, I may seem to contradict myself. Earlier, I asserted that all figures in Flatland appear as mere straight lines, making visual distinction between classes impossible. Yet now, I must explain to my Spaceland readers how we *do* recognize one another by sight. The key lies in a crucial qualification: Recognition by Feeling is universal only among the lower orders. Among the higher classes—and in our temperate climates—Sight Recognition is not only possible but refined into an art.

This ability exists thanks to an element your world considers a nuisance: **Fog**. Where you see it as a blight, obscuring landscapes and dampening spirits, we revere it as a gift nearly equal to air itself—the nurturer of arts and the foundation of sciences. Without Fog, all lines would appear equally sharp, rendering visual distinction impossible (as is tragically the case in arid, transparent regions). But in our world, Fog grants gradients of clarity. An object three feet away dims slightly compared to one at two feet eleven inches. Through meticulous observation of these subtle shifts in brightness, we deduce the shape of what we observe.

Suppose a Merchant (an Equilateral Triangle) and a Physician (a Pentagon) approach. How do I tell them apart?

If I position myself to bisect the angle of the Triangle, my gaze splits evenly between its two nearest sides (CA and AB). The midpoint (A) blazes bright, but the lines CA and AB fade swiftly into the Fog, their ends (D and E) nearly vanishing.

For the Pentagon, the same glance reveals a line (D'A'E') with a bright center (A'), but its sides (A'C', A'B') recede more gradually. The extremities (D' and E') remain more visible than the Triangle’s.

Thus, through long training, we learn to distinguish classes by the *rate* at which their edges dissolve into Fog.

Do not assume this skill is easily mastered. If my Father, a Triangle, approaches edge-first, I might mistake him for a Straight Line—a Woman—until he rotates. Similarly, observing my Hexagonal Grandson head-on, I see one bright line (AB) flanked by two dimmer, fading lines (CA and BD).

In motion, the challenge intensifies. Picture a ballroom: Polygons of high rank rotate, advance, retreat. To track them by sight demands immense mental agility—worthy of the Geometric Professors at Wentbridge University, where this art is taught to the elite. Few possess the time or wealth to master it fully. Even I, a seasoned Mathematician, find myself confounded amid a whirl of rotating nobility.

For the lower orders, Sight Recognition remains an unattainable luxury. Tradesmen’s children learn "Feeling" early, gaining quick wit but sacrificing the disciplined perception of the Polygonal class. Once the elite complete their education, however, they surpass their Triangular rivals in every field.

Only a fraction of Polygons fail the University’s Final Examination. These unfortunates are shunned by all—too refined for labor, yet too inept for intellectual society. History’s great rebellions often sprang from their ranks. Some progressive statesmen argue for their suppression: lifelong imprisonment or painless extinction.

But I digress. The plight of the Irregular demands its own discussion.
