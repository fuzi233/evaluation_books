“Old and young, boy, let ’em all eat, I have it;
Let ’em have ten tire of teeth a-piece, I care not.”

Margaret returned home burdened with the weight of what she had seen and heard in the narrow streets of Milton. Her thoughts were heavy, her spirits low, as she struggled to summon the cheerfulness required by her mother’s expectations. Mrs. Hale, confined to the house and eager for news from the outside world, looked to Margaret’s return as a source of daily gossip. The slightest excursion was expected to yield stories, laughter, and updates on the lives of others.

“And can your factory friend come on Thursday to see you dressed?” Mrs. Hale asked, her voice light but her eyes sharp with curiosity.

“She was so ill I never thought of asking her,” Margaret replied, her voice low and strained.

“Dear! Everyone seems to be unwell these days,” Mrs. Hale murmured, a flicker of resentment crossing her face—common among invalids who feel overlooked when others suffer more visibly. “But it must be dreadful to be sick in one of those cramped back lanes.” Her tone softened, touched by compassion, and the old Helstone sensibilities stirred within her.

“It’s bad enough here,” Margaret said gently. “What could we do for her? Mr. Thornton sent me some of his old port wine while I was out. Would that help?”

“No, mamma,” Margaret insisted. “I don’t think they’re very poor—at least, not by their own account. And Bessy has consumption. She won’t need wine. Perhaps I might take her some preserve—made from our Helstone fruit. But there’s another family I’d like to help—Oh, mamma, how can I go off to smart parties in my finery, after what I’ve seen today?” she burst out, breaking through the restraint she had imposed upon herself.

She told her mother everything—the squalor of the cottage, the feverish woman, the children huddled in silence, the desperate hopelessness that clung to the air like smoke.

Mrs. Hale was deeply disturbed. She could not sit still. She ordered Margaret to pack a basket immediately—right then—in the drawing-room. It must go without delay. When Margaret hesitated, explaining that Higgins had already provided for them and that money had been left with Bessy, Mrs. Hale turned sharply.

“You’re unfeeling!” she declared. “You don’t understand how much this means.”

She would not rest until the basket was dispatched. Then, a moment later, she paused, troubled.

“But perhaps we’ve done wrong,” she whispered. “Mr. Thornton said only last time he visited that helping the turn-outs only prolongs their struggle. And this Boucher man—he was one of them, wasn’t he?”

The question was passed to Mr. Hale when he came upstairs, fresh from tutoring Mr. Thornton. He had just finished a lesson, which, as usual, had led into a long conversation about philosophy, theology, and the nature of duty.

Margaret cared little about such consequences. In her present state, she could only feel sorrow and urgency. Mr. Hale listened carefully, trying to weigh both sides. He recalled Mr. Thornton’s arguments—clear, decisive, rooted in economic logic—and yet found himself unable to condemn his wife and daughter.

They had done right, he decided. Not just in this case, but in spirit. Yet, as a general rule, Mr. Thornton was correct: if the strike continued, the masters would eventually bring in laborers from afar—or invent machines that would render human hands obsolete. So, to aid the strikers now might only delay the inevitable, and prolong their suffering.

Still, he resolved to visit Boucher himself the next morning.

He did so. Boucher was not at home, but his wife spoke with him at length. He promised to secure an infirmary order for her. Seeing the provisions left by Mrs. Hale—plentiful, even generous—and watching the children, who seemed to run the household in their father’s absence, Mr. Hale returned with a surprisingly hopeful report.

“It’s better than I feared,” he said, though he admitted he still felt uncertain. “These homes are different from ours at Helstone. They have furniture our labourers wouldn’t dream of owning, food we’d consider luxuries. Yet now, with no wages, they have nothing but the pawnshop to turn to. One must learn a new standard here.”

Bessy, too, seemed slightly improved. Still weak, she appeared to have forgotten her earlier wish to see Margaret dressed—perhaps it had been a fever dream.

Margaret could not help comparing this evening’s preparation—her dressing for a party she didn’t want to attend—with the joyful, carefree rituals of the past. With Edith, under the sunlit skies of Helstone, they had laughed over ribbons and gowns, full of life and anticipation. Now, the act of dressing brought no joy, only a sense of obligation. The only comfort was knowing her mother would be pleased.

When Dixon opened the drawing-room door, she exclaimed, “Miss Hale looks well, ma’am—doesn’t she? Mrs. Shaw’s coral couldn’t have come in better. It gives just the right touch of colour. Otherwise, Miss Margaret, you’d have been too pale.”

Margaret’s thick black hair was wound tightly into massive coils around her head, like a crown, and fastened behind with two large coral pins shaped like arrows. Her white silk sleeves were looped up with coral strings, and across her throat lay a heavy strand of coral beads.

“Oh, Margaret!” Mrs. Hale sighed. “How I’d love to go with you to one of the old Barrington assemblies—just as Lady Beresford used to take me.”

Margaret kissed her mother, touched by the sentiment, though she could barely smile. “I’d rather stay home with you—much rather, mamma.”

“Nonsense, darling!” Mrs. Hale teased. “Be sure you notice the dinner well. I’ll enjoy hearing how they manage things in Milton. Especially the second course. Look what they have instead of game!”

She would have been astonished, had she seen the table—laden with silver, crystal, and dishes piled high with delicacies. To Margaret, accustomed to London refinement, it seemed excessive. Half the amount would have sufficed, and the effect would have been more elegant. But this was Mrs. Thornton’s way—generous, almost ostentatious. She believed every guest should have access to every dainty, whether they ate it or not.

Her son shared this belief. Though he lived frugally, denying himself even a sixpence, he took pride in the grandeur of his hospitality. This dinner, though reluctantly arranged, was to be magnificent. He had not known any society except one built on elaborate meals, and even now, he welcomed the spectacle.

Margaret and her father arrived first. Mr. Hale was punctual, as always. The drawing-room was empty save for Mrs. Thornton and Fanny. Every cover was removed. The room blazed with yellow damask and a brilliant floral carpet. Ornaments crowded every corner, creating a visual overload. Outside, the great mill loomed, casting its shadow early across the summer evening.

“My son was detained by business,” Mrs. Thornton said. “He will be here directly. May I ask you to take a seat?”

Mr. Hale stood by the window. “Doesn’t the proximity to the mill trouble you sometimes?” he asked.

“Never,” she replied, stiffening. “I am not so refined as to forget where my son’s wealth comes from. Besides, there is no other factory like it in Milton. One room alone measures two hundred and twenty square yards.”

“The noise and smoke—the constant comings and goings of workers—might be bothersome,” Mr. Hale suggested.

“I agree,” said Fanny. “There’s always steam and oil—and the noise is deafening.”

“I’ve heard music called worse,” Mr. Hale replied. “The engine-room is at the street end. We hardly hear it, except in summer. As for the hum of the workers—it’s like bees. It doesn’t disturb me. I associate it with my son. I know it belongs to him. His mind directs it.”

She paused. “Now, there are no sounds from the mill. The hands have turned out—ungrateful creatures. You may have heard. But my son’s business tonight concerns the steps he’s taking to teach them their place.”

Her face darkened with anger. It did not fade when Mr. Thornton entered. He carried the weight of worry, though he greeted his guests warmly. He shook hands with Margaret—though neither noticed it was their first meeting. He inquired after Mrs. Hale, heard Mr. Hale’s optimistic account, and glanced at Margaret, seeking signs of dissent. None appeared.

And then, as he looked, he was struck anew by her beauty. He had never seen her thus—so composed, so dignified in her attire. She seemed made for such elegance. Her presence was calm, her expression serene. She talked quietly with Fanny, whose restless fingers fidgeted with her dress, her eyes darting about aimlessly. Margaret sat still, her head slightly forward, her gaze steady, her lips parted in gentle interest. Light caught the raven hair atop her head, tracing a smooth line down to her ivory shoulder. Her arms rested softly, motionless, yet graceful.

Mr. Thornton sighed—deeply, silently. Then he turned away, forcing himself into conversation with Mr. Hale.

More guests arrived. Fanny left Margaret’s side to assist her mother. Mr. Thornton noticed that no one spoke to her. He felt uneasy—but he did not approach her. He did not look at her. Yet he knew exactly what she was doing, or not doing, better than anyone else in the room.

Margaret, unaware of her own presence, watched the others with quiet amusement. She didn’t care whether she was noticed.

Someone led her downstairs to dinner. She didn’t catch the name. He didn’t speak much.

At the table, the men debated fiercely. Mr. Horsfall, the visitor, asked questions about the trade. The Milton men answered with pride. A dispute arose. It was referred to Mr. Thornton, who had spoken little before. But when he spoke, his reasoning was clear, firm, undeniable. Even his opponents yielded.

Margaret listened, drawn in. She saw him differently now—not as the proud, impatient man who had once visited their home, but as a leader, confident and respected. Among his peers, he needed no defense. He was acknowledged. And that gave his manner a quiet strength she had never seen before.

He rarely spoke to the ladies. To Margaret, he said almost nothing.

Yet she found herself enjoying the dinner. She understood more now—technical terms, local interests. She followed the debate closely, silently aligning herself with the Milton men. Their conversation was earnest, alive, unlike the tired chatter of London soirées.

She wondered why no one mentioned the strike. Then she realized—these men saw it as inevitable. The workers were fools, playing into the hands of paid agitators. If they chose to destroy themselves, they must bear the consequences. Some noted that Thornton looked weary. Of course—he’d lose from the strike. But it could happen to anyone. And Thornton was strong—iron, they called him. The men had misjudged their man.

They chuckled inwardly at the idea of the workers’ defeat.

After dinner, the ladies chattered about dresses and dinners. Margaret found it dull. She was glad when the gentlemen returned. She liked their confidence, their belief in progress, their defiance of limits. They spoke of future triumphs—over matter, over time—grand visions that none would live to see.

Then Mr. Thornton stood beside her, speaking quietly.

“I could see you were on our side during the discussion—were you not, Miss Hale?”

“Certainly,” she replied. “Though I know so little. I was surprised to hear Mr. Horsfall speak of someone named Morison—someone who thinks quite differently. Is he a gentleman?”

“I’m not the one to judge,” Mr. Thornton said. “But I’d say he’s no true man. I don’t know him—only from Mr. Horsvalid’s description.”

“I suspect my ‘gentleman’ includes your ‘true man.’”

“And more,” he replied. “I differ. A man is higher, fuller, than a gentleman.”

“What do you mean?” she asked.

“I believe ‘gentleman’ describes a person in relation to others. But ‘man’—that speaks of one’s relationship to himself, to life, to eternity. A castaway on a desert island, a prisoner in a dungeon, even a saint on Patmos—they are best described as *men*. I grow weary of the word ‘gentlemanly.’ It’s often misused, exaggerated. While the simple words ‘man’ and ‘manly’ are ignored. I find it a kind of cant.”

Margaret thought. But before she could reply, he was pulled away by the manufacturers.

She heard their voices—short, firm answers, like distant cannon fire. They were discussing the strike.

“That has been done,” Mr. Thornton said.

A murmur rose. “All arrangements made.”

Mr. Slickson grasped his arm. “But there are risks—difficulties.”

“I take the risk,” Mr. Thornton replied. “You need not join unless you choose.”

More objections. “I’m not afraid of incendiarism. We’re open enemies. I can protect myself—and those who come to me for work. They know my resolve.”

Mr. Horsfall stepped aside, asking him about the strike—then, as Margaret guessed, about her.

“A Milton lady?” he asked.

“No,” came the cold reply. “From the south—Hampshire, I believe.”

Meanwhile, Mrs. Slickson questioned Fanny.

“Who is that beautiful girl? A sister of Mr. Horsfall’s?”

“Oh dear, no. That’s Mr. Hale—her father—talking to Mr. Stephens. He gives lessons. My brother John goes twice a week. That’s why mamma invited them—hoping to make connections. We have prospectuses, if you’d like one.”

“Mr. Thornton!” Mrs. Slickson exclaimed. “Does he really find time to study with a tutor—amidst all his business—and this terrible strike?”

Fanny hesitated. Was she proud? Ashamed? She wasn’t sure. She flushed, unsure of how to feel.

The guests began to disperse.
