It was the thirtieth of October, 1496. Morning lay clear over Florence, the air rinsed by an honest autumn wind that came down from the hills and through the narrow veins of street and loggia with a sound like a steady breathing. In other seasons the city would have listened for that wind—how it worried the plane leaves along the Arno, how it made the hanging shop-signs creak and the shutters clap like impatient hands. But now Florence listened past the roofs, past the bells, past the hawkers’ cracked cries, to the sea she could not see.

The sea had spoken in gales. It had flung back from the Leghorn coast the ships from Marseilles—ships that should have come heavy with corn and with soldiery. And Florence needed both with the same gnawing urgency: bread first, then men to defend the right to eat it. Hunger had a face in the streets—cheeks sharpened, lips greyed, eyes too bright—and where hunger went, pestilence hovered with its noiseless footfall, as if the very air were learning a new, poisonous music.

All Italy seemed tuned against Florence. The French king—hailed once as a new Charlemagne, sweeping into Italy with anticipatory triumph and taking Naples as easily as a man might lift a lid—had gone away again fifteen months before; and there were fears that grief for a new-born son lost had slackened what languid intention remained in him to return and set wrongs right. Against him a league had been formed—a Holy League, with Pope Borgia at its head—to “drive out the barbarians” still holding Naples. It sounded like piety, but it looked, to any clear sight, like wolves agreeing to chase off other wolves and then quarrel over the carcass. And Florence was not counted as fellow-hunter so much as meat.

Florence alone among the chief Italian states had refused to join that League, clinging still to the French alliance. The refusal had its price. Pisa, still fighting like a creature that will not be penned, was encouraged by strong forces from Venice and Milan; and the German Emperor Maximilian himself had come to help the Pisans with such troops as he had, attempting to seize Leghorn while Venetian and Genoese ships invested the coast. If Leghorn fell—if that single mouth to the sea were gagged—how were succours to reach Florence, hedged in by the Pope’s bitter ill-will and the jealous teeth of lesser states?

The government had shown a stubborn heart in this need, meeting loss and defeat with renewed effort: raising money, raising soldiers, and never forgetting the old Italian weapon of the conciliatory embassy. And, breaking with precedent, it had resolved not to shut out the starving country folk and the mendicants turned away from other gates—people who came flocking to Florence as birds come from a land of snow. The party of Savonarola held the strongest place in that government; and these acts—open gates, strained charity, endurance under isolation—were not spared criticism. The disaffected were many, and their judgments were ready: Florence ought to join the League, they said, and not draw down hostility by clinging to a foreign ally. Florence ought to feed her own citizens first, and not invite famine and pestilence within the walls by harbouring alien hunger.

Each day the want sharpened; each day the murmurs rose with it, like an undertone thickening beneath a chant. And for a month and more Fra Girolamo had been silent in the pulpit, obedient to a mandate from Rome. Silence, in a starving city, is not peace: it is the hush before outbreak. When the news came that the ships from Marseilles had been driven back and no corn was coming, the need for a voice that could pour patience into suffering became too urgent to resist. In defiance of Rome the Signoria asked Savonarola to preach; and two days before this morning he had mounted the pulpit again in the Duomo and told the people only to wait, to stand fast, and divine help would surely come. He had pledged his own body to that promise: let them strip the frock from him, he said, if Florence persevered in piety and citizenship and God did not rescue her.

But on this thirtieth there were no signs of rescue. The streets had their old sounds—carts creaking, sandals scuffing, the water murmuring under bridge-arches—yet beneath them ran the thin, perpetual note of emptiness: the hollow cough, the quarrel that began too quickly, the child’s cry that ended too soon.

Perhaps—so men whispered, with the same earnestness they put into measuring out the last handful of meal—perhaps if the precious Tabernacle of the Madonna dell’ Impruneta were brought into Florence and carried in solemn procession to the Duomo, the Mother rich in sorrows and therefore in mercy would plead for the suffering city. For a century and a half there were records: drought broken, floods stilled, pestilence scattered, famine turned away, wars averted, after the Florentines had fetched that hidden image within their walls. The high house of Buondelmonti, patrons of the church at L’Impruneta, guarded the veiled treasure with bare sword; wealth had been poured into chantings, chapels, ever-burning lights; and lands had been added until men quarrelled for the honour of serving her. The Florentines believed in her graciousness with the easy certainty with which they believed in bread when there was bread; and a proverb ran that Florence had a Madonna who would do what they pleased. When had they needed her pity more than now?

Already, the evening before, the tabernacle containing the miraculous image had been brought with high escort from L’Impruneta—six miles beyond the gate of San Piero that looks towards Rome—and had been deposited in the church of San Gaggio outside the gate, to be fetched thence by all the fraternities, trades, and authorities of Florence in ordered devotion. But the Pitying Mother had not yet crossed the threshold of the walls, and the morning rose on unchanged misery.

Not only the hospitals were full; courtyards of private houses had become refuges and infirmaries; still the want lay unsheltered. Early, as usual, members of the fraternities who made it their duty to bury the unfriended dead went searching and bearing away the bodies that had sunk by the wayside, the bier’s poles creaking to a rhythm the city had learned too well. As usual, too, women moved through the streets with the plain, swift step of accustomed mercy—women whose forms and bearing spoke of good birth even under homely cloth. They carried broth-pots that must be made to stretch, baskets of softened bread, little flasks of thin wine; their very garments were quiet, as if they would not add a harsh note to suffering.

One such figure was easily known as Romola de’ Bardi.

She wore the simplest black serge, and over her head a plain black drapery that hid all her hair save the bands of gold that rippled apart upon her brow. She was advancing from the Ponte Vecchio towards the Por’ Santa Maria—straight from the bridge into the narrow line of street—when she found her way checked by the pausing of a bier. The brethren of San Jacopo del Popolo, who went searching for unburied dead, had stopped; the men at the head stooped to examine something; and a cluster of idle workmen, their features sharpened by hunger, pressed in, all talking at once, their voices clattering against one another like tin pans.

“He’s dead, I tell you! Messer Domeneddio has loved him well enough to take him.”

“Ah, and it would be well for us all if we could have our legs stretched out and go with our heads two or three bracci foremost! It’s ill standing upright with hunger to prop you.”

“Well, well, he’s an old fellow. Death has got a poor bargain. Life’s had the best of him.”

“And no Florentine, ten to one! A beggar turned out of Siena. San Giovanni defend us! They’ve no need of soldiers to fight us. They send us an army of starving men.”

“No, no! This man is one of the prisoners turned out of the Stinche. I know by the grey patch where the prison badge was.”

“Keep quiet! Lend a hand! Don’t you see the brethren are going to lift him on the bier?”

“It’s likely he’s alive enough if he could only look it. The soul may be inside him if it had only a drop of vernaccia to warm it.”

“In truth, I think he is not dead,” said one of the brethren, when they had lifted him upon the bier. “He has perhaps only sunk down for want of food.”

“Let me try to give him some wine,” said Romola, coming forward.

At her belt she carried a small flask, and from long practice in the service of the sick she had learnt neatness where clumsiness can kill. She loosened the flask, and leaning over the prostrate body, she slipped a small ivory implement between the clenched teeth, as gently as one coaxes open a shell to save what is within. Then she let fall a few drops of wine—sharp, thin, with the scent of pressed grape-skin and cask-wood. The old man’s throat moved; the stimulus was answered: he swallowed.

She poured more, not in a flooding mercy but with the measure that respects a failing body: drop, pause, drop, listening as a cook listens to the first quiet simmer after a boil—watchful for the smallest sign that life is returning. Presently the head moved a little towards her; the eyelids lifted; and the eyes opened full upon her with the vague, wavering look of consciousness coming back as a lamp comes back when the wick is raised.

Then recognition—complete and involuntary—passed over Romola like a cold hand.

Those wild dark eyes in the sallow, deep-lined face, the white beard grown long again, were like an unmistakable signature to a remembered handwriting. Two summers had not blotted that image: the escaped prisoner she had seen in the Duomo on the day Tito first wore armour; the same terror-grasping figure in the strange sketch she had once seen in Piero’s studio. A wretched tremor took her—palpitation, faintness, as if the street beneath her had shifted. Here, perhaps, lay at last the knowledge she had been shut out from: some secret more bitter than all bitterness she had already swallowed.

For an instant she had the impulse to dart away as from a sight of horror. And then—stronger, more imperious—came the need to remain close beside this old man whom her heart, with a divination that was keener than any argument, told her had been injured by her husband.

Even in that conflict she did not cease her ministry. She still bent towards him, her right hand ready to administer more wine; her left passed under his neck to support him. Her fingers trembled, but the habit of soothing helpfulness guided them even when thought was shaken.

Baldassarre was looking at her for the first time. In the weeks before her flight and his arrest, Romola’s close seclusion in trouble had denied him the chance he had sought of seeing the wife who lived in the Via de’ Bardi; and now the descriptions he had heard of a fair, golden-haired woman fell away like foam. Hunger and exhaustion made his mind dim, but his gaze clung to her as a man clings to a sound in the dark.

“Will it not be well to carry him to the steps of San Stefano?” said Romola. “We shall cease then to stop up the street, and you can go on your way with your bier.”

They had only to move some thirty yards. By the time they reached the steps Baldassarre could make an effort to help himself down, and they propped him against the church doorway, his back to stone that held the morning’s chill. The charitable brethren lifted their poles again and passed on with their bier, the creak of wood and leather fading into the city’s restless murmur. But the circle of watchers did not fade: those who had nothing to do and much to say had increased, and now that it was certain the old man lived, the feeling grew less friendly. Respect for Romola kept voices lower, but could not sweeten them.

“Ah, they gave him his morsel every day in the Stinche—that’s why he can’t do so well without it. You and I, Cecco, know better what it is to go to bed fasting.”

“Gnaffè! that’s why the Magnificent Eight have turned out some of the prisoners, that they may shelter honest people instead. But if every thief is to be brought to life with good wine and wheaten bread, we Ciompi had better go and fill ourselves in Arno while the water’s plenty.”

Romola sat on the step beside Baldassarre. From her basket came the mild, clean smell of soft bread—bread no longer warm from the oven, but tender, meant for mouths without strength: a crumb that would not scrape the throat raw. She spoke to him quietly, as one speaks to the fevered, shaping her words so they might be taken in.

“Can you eat a little bread now? Perhaps by-and-by you will be able, if I leave it with you. I must go on, because I have promised to be at the hospital. But I will come back if you will wait here, and then I will take you to some shelter. Do you understand? Will you wait? I will come back.”

He looked dreamily, and his lips repeated only the last—like an echo caught in a vault.

“Come back.”

She opened her basket and put a piece of the soft bread into his hand, closing his fingers on it as if to teach them their duty.

“Do you keep your bread for those that can’t swallow, madonna?” said a rough-looking fellow in a red night-cap, elbowing his way nearer. The men pressed in, a hungry closeness that had its own smell—old sweat, sour wine, wool-dust, and the faint rancour that rises when bellies are empty.

“If anybody isn’t hungry,” said another, “I say, let him alone. He’s better off than people who’ve got craving stomachs and no breakfast.”

“Yes, indeed; if a man’s a mind to die, it’s a time to encourage him, instead of making him come back to life against his will. Dead men want no trencher.”

“Oh, you don’t understand the Frate’s charity,” said a young man in an excellent cloth tunic, his face unpinched by want. “The Frate has been preaching to the birds, like Saint Anthony, and he’s been telling the hawks they were made to feed the sparrows, as every good Florentine citizen was made to feed six starving beggar-men from Arezzo or Bologna. Madonna, there, is a pious Piagnone: she’s not going to throw away her good bread on honest citizens who’ve got all the Frate’s prophecies to swallow.”

“Come, madonna,” said the man in the red cap, “the old thief doesn’t eat the bread, you see: you’d better try us. We fast so much, we’re half saints already.”

The circle narrowed until there was hardly air between Romola and the coarse bodies that hemmed her. She had taken from her basket a small horn-cup; she crumbled the bread into it and moistened it with wine, not drowning it but making a sop—soft as mercy, easy for a failing throat. Through all the ugly talk she had seemed not to heed; her attention was on the old man’s mouth, the way his jaw slackened, the swallow that came too slowly.

But now she rose.

She looked round at the faces before her, and the men nearest drew back a little—instinctively, as if their nearness were a fault belonging to those behind them. Romola held out the basket of bread to the man in the night-cap. There was no reproach in her gaze, only a directness that made the words heavier than anger.

“Hunger is hard to bear, I know, and you have the power to take this bread if you will. It was saved for sick women and children. You are strong men; but if you do not choose to suffer because you are strong, you have the power to take everything from the weak. You can take the bread from this basket; but I shall watch by this old man; I shall resist your taking the bread from him.”

For a few moments there was perfect silence. The street seemed to hold its breath; even the small autumn wind sounded louder as it slid along the stone and set a shutter tapping somewhere like a nervous finger. Romola’s own face, pale and a little pinched by fasting, with the deepened eye-socket of one who has denied herself without complaint, had all the more force for its simplicity. Her hazel eyes were large and steady, and the men felt the weight of being seen—named, as it were, in the old language of conscience that hunger cannot quite kill.

The man in the night-cap looked foolish; he backed, thrusting his elbow into his neighbour’s ribs with an air of rebuke, as if he had been the one most offended by the crowd’s rudeness. The backing became general, each man eager to imply he had been pushed forward against his will; and the young man in the fine tunic had slipped away.

At that moment the armed servitors of the Signoria, beginning to patrol the line of streets through which the day’s procession would pass, came up to scatter the group that blocked the narrow way. A threatening mace made the retreat quicker. The man called Cecco sprang up the church steps out of reach and, turning, spoke to Romola with a rough respect.

“Madonna, if you want to go on your errands, I’ll take care of the old man.”

Cecco was wild-looking: a ragged tunic, shaggy and variegated with cloth-dust and clinging fragments of wool, fell away from bare bony arms and a long sinewy neck. His square jaw was shaded by bristly black beard; his bridgeless nose and low forehead made his face seem crushed down for packing; and a narrow strip of red rag tied over his ears appeared to assist the compression. Yet his voice held a note not wholly hardened.

Romola hesitated, looking at him—measuring with her eyes what she could not know.

“Don’t distrust me, madonna,” Cecco said at once, understanding her glance. “I am not so pretty as you, but I’ve got an old mother who eats my porridge for me. What! there’s a heart inside me, and I’ve bought a candle for the most Holy Virgin before now. Besides, see there, the old fellow is eating his sop. He’s hale enough: he’ll be on his legs as well as the best of us by-and-by.”

“Thank you for offering to take care of him, friend,” Romola said, with a touch of penitence for her doubt.

Then she leaned towards Baldassarre, her hand hovering a moment as if she would steady his shoulder with a promise.

“Pray wait for me till I come again.”

He assented with a slight movement of head and hand, and Romola went on, turning her steps towards the hospital of San Matteo in the Piazza di San Marco, where other hungers and other faint voices were waiting for what little comfort hands could make out of scarcity.
