XIV. The Meeting

"My dearest child," the General continued, his voice trembling with a mixture of grief and frustration, "was growing swiftly worse. The physician who had tended her had achieved nothing but delay, for I believed at the time that her ailment was merely physical. He perceived my mounting terror and proposed a second opinion. I summoned a far more distinguished colleague from Graz.

Days passed before his arrival. He was a man of profound piety and unimpeachable learning. After examining my poor ward, the two doctors retreated to my library to deliberate. From the adjacent chamber, where I paced in anxious anticipation, I overheard their voices rising above the bounds of polite discourse, erupting into a heated debate. I knocked and entered, finding the elder doctor from Graz holding firm to his theories, while his rival countered with derision, punctuated by loud, unseemly bursts of laughter. The discord ceased abruptly at my entrance.

"Sir," the younger physician said to the older, "my esteemed colleague suggests you require a conjurer, not a healer."

"Pardon me," the doctor from Graz replied, his brow furrowed with displeasure. "I shall articulate my perspective in due course. I grieve, Monsieur le General, that despite my science, I am powerless to aid her. Yet, before I depart, I must offer you counsel."

He appeared deeply thoughtful, sitting at a desk to compose a document. Disappointed, I bowed and prepared to leave. As I turned, the other doctor gestured toward his companion writing at the table, then shook his head and tapped his temple with a look of weary resignation.

This consultation served to confirm my despair. I stepped outside into the grounds, nearly undone by sorrow. Minutes later, the doctor from Graz overtook me. He apologized for following but confessed he could not leave without further words. He assured me that no natural malady presented such symptoms; death hovered imminently, perhaps within twenty-four hours. If the fatal seizure could be halted immediately, her strength might return, but everything now rested upon the razor's edge between life and oblivion. A single further assault could snuff out the final flicker of vitality.

"And what is the nature of this seizure?" I begged.

"I have detailed everything in this note," he said, pressing a folded paper into my hand. "Read it only in the presence of the nearest clergyman. You would otherwise dismiss it as madness, yet it concerns life and death. If the priest fails, then you may read it alone."

He departed, urging me to invite a scholar uniquely knowledgeable on the subject who would surely interest me once I understood his letter.

The priest was nowhere to be found, so I read the letter in solitude. In calmer times, it would have amused me; it was monstrous enough to warrant confinement. He declared the patient was being visited by a vampire! He identified the punctures near the throat as the work of two long, sharp fangs peculiar to these creatures, and the small livid mark around them as the imprint of the demon's lips. Every symptom described by the sufferer aligned perfectly with historical accounts of vampiric visitations.

Though I remained a skeptic regarding the supernatural, the desperation of losing a beloved soul drove me to act on his instructions. I hid in the darkened dressing room adjoining her chamber, watching from behind a sliver of light as a candle burned low. I lay in wait with my sword drawn, as directed, until shortly past midnight. Then, I saw a large, indistinct black shape crawl silently across the foot of the bed, swelling instantly into a great, throbbing mass against the girl's neck.

For a heartbeat, I stood frozen. Then, springing forward with my blade raised, I struck. The black form recoiled, gliding back toward the foot of the bed, only to stand upright on the floor a yard away. It faced me with a gaze of skulking ferocity and horror, revealing itself as Millarca. Confused, I struck again, but she vanished. My sword shattered against the doorframe, splintering into useless shards.

I cannot recount the terrors of that night. The entire household erupted into alarm. The specter of Millarca fled, but her victim succumbed rapidly, dying before dawn broke.

Later, amidst the somber silence of the cemetery, the old General was visibly shaken. My father wandered off, tracing the inscriptions on crumbling tombstones, eventually drifting into a side chapel. The General leaned against a broken pillar, drying his eyes with heavy sighs. Relief washed over me when I heard the approach of Carmilla and Madame. Their footsteps softened as they drew near.

Alone in the shadows, surrounded by the towering, silent foliage and the moldering dust of ancient graves, a deep, primal horror seized me. My heart sank, fearing that even my friends would soon be lost to this cursed place.

Near the base of a shattered monument, beneath a narrow archway carved with grotesque demons, I saw Carmilla emerge from the gloom. Her beauty was radiant, her smile engaging. Just as I prepared to greet her, a cry tore from the General beside me. He lunged forward, brandishing a woodcutter's axe.

Instantly, Carmilla's countenance transformed. The gentle warmth vanished, replaced by a brutal, animalistic fury. She recoiled, making a crouching step backward. Before I could scream, the General swung the axe with all his might. She dived beneath the blow, unharmed, and clamped her tiny, iron-hard fingers onto his wrist. He struggled frantically, veins bulging, but his hand went limp. The axe clattered to the earth, and she slipped away into the darkness like smoke.

The General staggered back, leaning against the cold stone. His grey hair stood erect, sweat pouring down his face as if he were on the brink of death.

The nightmare lasted but a moment. When I recovered consciousness, Madame stood before me, her voice frantic. "Where is Mademoiselle Carmilla?" she repeated, her eyes wide with panic.

"I... I do not know," I stammered, pointing toward the chapel door where she had vanished. "Only moments ago."

"But I stood right there in the passage!" she cried, rushing to the doors and calling out, "Carmilla! Where are you?" Her calls echoed through the corridors and windows, met only by silence.

"She called herself Carmilla?" the General asked, his voice hollow.

"Yes," I whispered.

"Aye," he murmured, his face pale. "That is Millarca. That is the same soul who was once known as Mircalla, Countess Karnstein. Depart from this accursed ground, my poor child, at once. Drive to the clergyman's house and remain there until we arrive. Flee! You shall never see Carmilla again. She is not here."
