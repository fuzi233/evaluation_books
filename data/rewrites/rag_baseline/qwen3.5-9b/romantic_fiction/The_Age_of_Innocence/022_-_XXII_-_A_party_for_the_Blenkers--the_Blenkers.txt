The silverware clicked against the china, a sharp, metallic rhythm that cut through the humid silence of the dining room. Mr. Welland lowered his knife, his brow furrowing as he stared across the table at his wife. She adjusted her gold-rimmed spectacles, the lenses catching the light, and read aloud in a tone that suggested a stage play rather than a social engagement.

"Professor and Mrs. Emerson Sillerton request the pleasure of Mr. and Mrs. Welland's company..." Her voice drifted, flat and detached, until she reached the crux of the matter. "...To meet Mrs. and the Misses Blenker. Red Gables, Catherine Street."

Mr. Welland gasped, a sound of genuine shock, as if the paper itself had betrayed him. "Good gracious," he muttered, needing a second reading to digest the sheer absurdity of the summons. "Poor Amy Sillerton—you never can tell what her husband will do next."

Mrs. Welland sighed, a practiced gesture of mild disapproval. "I suppose he's just discovered the Blenkers."

Emerson Sillerton was a thorn in the fabric of Newport society, a prickly growth rooted deep in a lineage too venerable to be pulled without tearing the soil. He possessed every advantage: a father who was Sillerton Jackson's uncle, a mother from the Pennilows of Boston, and a pedigree that demanded deference. Yet, he chose archaeology over commerce, lived in Newport only in winter, and surrounded himself with long-haired men and short-haired women. He had married Amy Dagonet, a woman entitled to better things and a carriage of her own, yet she submitted to his eccentricities with a passive resignation that baffled the Mingott set. Why did she allow him to drag her to the tombs of Yucatán instead of the salons of Paris? The answer lay somewhere in the quiet erosion of expectation, a slow surrender to a life that had become a series of small, unremarkable compromises.

"They gave a party for a black man on the day of Julia Mingott's *thé dansant* two years ago," Mrs. Welland noted, her voice tinged with the melancholy of someone recounting a distant tragedy. "Luckily, this time there is nothing else scheduled. Some of us will have to go."

"Some of us," Mr. Welland repeated, setting his fork down again. A flush of anxiety stained his wrinkled cheek. "More than one? Three o'clock is such an awkward hour. I must be here by half-past three for my drops. It is essential to follow Dr. Bencomb's regimen systematically; otherwise, the treatment fails. If I arrive late, I shall miss my drive."

"There is no reason for you to attend, my dear," his wife replied, her cheerfulness now a reflex, a mechanism to smooth over the friction of his needs. "I have cards to deliver at the other end of Bellevue Avenue. I shall stop by around half-past three, remain long enough to ensure poor Amy feels she has not been slighted."

She glanced hesitantly at their daughter. "Perhaps May can drive you out with the ponies and try their new russet harness."

In the Welland household, time was a commodity to be allocated, a resource to be managed. The concept of "killing time"—a phrase that haunted those who did not enjoy whist or solitaire—was akin to the specter of unemployment haunting a philanthropist. Parents were expected to respect the autonomy of their married children, yet the demands of Mr. Welland's health and Mrs. Welland's social obligations created a delicate equilibrium. Only through intricate maneuvering could every second of her day be accounted for, leaving no gap for idleness.

"I shall drive with Papa," May said softly, her tone gentle enough to remind her husband of his own lack of foresight. "I am certain Newland will find something to occupy him."

It was a source of constant frustration to Mrs. Welland that her son-in-law displayed such a paucity of planning. During the fortnight he had spent under her roof, whenever she asked how he intended to pass his afternoon, he offered paradoxical answers: "Oh, I think for a change I'll just save it instead of spending it." Once, when forced to accompany her and May on a postponed round of calls, he had confessed to lying beneath a rock on the beach below the house for the entire duration.

"Newland never seems to look ahead," Mrs. Welland murmured to May.

"No," May answered serenely. "But it does not matter. When there is nothing particular to do, he reads a book."

"Aha, yes—like his father!" Mrs. Welland conceded, treating it as an inherited quirk before the subject was quietly dropped.

Yet, as the day of the Sillerton reception neared, May's concern for her brother-in-law's welfare surfaced naturally. She suggested a tennis match at the Chiverses' or a sail on Julius Beaufort's cutter, attempts to atone for her brief absence.

"I shall be back by six, you know, dear. Papa never drives later than that."

She was not fully reassured until Archer spoke. "I intend to hire a runabout and drive up the island to a stud-farm to inspect a second horse for your brougham."

They had sought this horse for some time. The suggestion was so perfectly timed that May looked at her mother as if to say, *See? He understands how to plan.*

The seed of this excursion had been planted in Archer's mind the very day the Emerson Sillerton invitation was first mentioned. He had kept the plan to himself, treating it as a clandestine operation, fearing that disclosure might derail its execution. However, he had secured a runabout equipped with a pair of sturdy trotters from the livery stables, capable of covering eighteen miles on level ground. At two o'clock, he abandoned the luncheon table and sprang into the light carriage, driving off before the clock struck three.

The day was flawless. A breeze from the north scattered clouds of white foam across an ultramarine sky, while the sea ran bright and clear beneath it. Bellevue Avenue was empty at that hour. After dropping the stable lad at the corner of Mill Street, Archer turned down the Old Beach Road, crossing Eastman's Beach. He felt a peculiar, unexplained excitement, reminiscent of starting off into the unknown on half-holidays at school. Driving his pair at an easy pace, he calculated that he would reach the stud-farm, located just beyond Paradise Rocks, well before three o'clock. This would grant him four golden hours to dispose of after examining the horse.

As soon as the Sillertons' party had been announced, Archer had reasoned that the Marchioness Manson would undoubtedly arrive in Newport with the Blenkers. Consequently, Madame Olenska might once again seize the opportunity to spend the day with her grandmother. In any case, the Blenker estate would likely be deserted, offering him a chance to satisfy a vague curiosity without impropriety.

He was not certain he wished to see the Countess Olenska again. Yet, ever since he had glimpsed her from the path overlooking the bay, he had harbored an irrational, indefinable desire to witness the place where she resided. He wanted to trace the movements of her imagined figure, just as he had watched the real one in the summer-house. The longing persisted day and night, an incessant, undefined craving similar to a sick man's sudden whim for food or drink once tasted and long forgotten. He could not envision what this craving might lead to; he was not conscious of any desire to speak to Madame Olenska or hear her voice. He simply felt that if he could carry away the vision of the specific patch of earth she walked upon, and the way the sky and sea enclosed it, the rest of the world might seem less empty.

Upon arriving at the stud-farm, a quick glance revealed the horse was not what he desired. Nevertheless, he took a turn behind it to prove to himself that he was not in a hurry. But at three o'clock, he shook out the reins over the trotters and turned onto the by-roads leading to Portsmouth. The wind had died, and a faint haze on the horizon signaled that a fog was waiting to creep up the Saconnet River as the tide turned. Around him, however, fields and woods were steeped in golden light.

He drove past grey-shingled farmhouses nestled in orchards, past hayfields and groves of oak, past villages with white steeples piercing the fading sky. Finally, after stopping to ask directions from men working in a field, he turned down a lane bordered by high banks of goldenrod and brambles. At the end of the lane, the blue glimmer of the river appeared. To the left, standing before a cluster of oaks and maples, rose a long, dilapidated house, its white paint peeling from the clapboards. On the roadside, facing the gateway, stood one of the open sheds where New England farmers sheltered their tools and hitched their teams.

Archer jumped down, led his pair into the shed, tied them to a post, and turned toward the house. The patch of lawn before it had reverted to a hayfield, but to the left, an overgrown box-garden filled with dahlias and rusty rose bushes encircled a ghostly summer-house of trellis-work. Once white, it was now faded, surmounted by a wooden Cupid who had lost his bow and arrow but continued to aim ineffectually at the sky.
