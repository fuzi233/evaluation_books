Major Pendennis and Captain Costigan had both smelt powder in their time, and neither man’s nerves were likely to be undone by the mere sight of an unexpected enemy. The rest of the company, however—collected in Cos’s sitting-room like ingredients that hadn’t yet agreed to become a dish—were plainly put out when Pen’s uncle appeared in the doorway as if he’d been served up without warning.

Miss Fotheringay’s steady Irish blood ran a shade quicker; her cheek warmed to a good, wholesome flush, the kind you see rise when a hearth is newly stoked or when a young woman has climbed a stair too fast and pretends she hasn’t. Sir Derby Oaks, in the act of looking handsome, looked chiefly savage: he cast her a scowl that might have curdled milk at twenty paces.

In the window-seat, the little crooked old music-copyist—who had been trying to set down theatre airs until the stamping and lunging of the fencing lesson made the ink tremble on the paper—popped up his head like a startled wren. The room, small and low, had that particular lodging-house atmosphere of stale coal-smoke, boot-leather, cheap pomatum, and the faint ghost of yesterday’s onions. Into this domestic vapour came Major Pendennis, boots gleaming as if they’d been glazed with black treacle, distributing bows with the grace of a man ladling sauce: to each person a measured portion, no waste, no stinginess.

“Me daughter—me friend, Mr. Bows—me gallant young pupil and friend, I may call ’um, Sir Derby Oaks,” cried Costigan, sweeping his hand as if presenting courses at table. “In one moment, Meejor, I’m your humble servant,” and he darted into his little sleeping closet.

What happened there was as brisk as the whisking of eggs. With an ancient hair-brush—heroic relic of campaigns and pawnbrokers—he gave his lank hair a twist; tore off an old stock and replaced it with a new one stitched by Emily’s own fingers; clapped on a clean collar; and assumed the new coat ordered for the benefit night, as though by putting it on he also put on respectability. Sir Derby followed him into the same cupboard of a room and presently emerged cased in his tight shell-jacket, which made the large young man look as if he’d been packed for travel and nailed down.

Meanwhile, Miss Fotheringay and the Major made their first passes of conversation—weather, the inevitable seasoning—and had barely finished exchanging those polite, tasteless wafers before Costigan reappeared in his “Shoot,” as he called his new attire, radiating the satisfaction of a man who has found a clean napkin.

“I needn’t apologoise to ye, Meejor,” he said, rich as port, “for receiving ye in me shirt-sleeves.”

“An old soldier can’t be better employed than in teaching a young one the use of his sword,” the Major replied, as gallant as if the room had been a drawing-room and the foils silver. “I remember in old times hearing that you could use yours pretty well, Captain Costigan.”

“What, ye’ve heard of Jack Costigan, Major,” cried the Captain, swelling.

The Major said—very civilly, very firmly—that he perfectly recollected meeting Mr. Costigan and hearing him sing at Sir Richard Strachan’s table at Walcheren. Mr. Bows, who knew exactly which songs were sung at which tables when because he’d been paid to copy them, stared as if someone had claimed to remember a dinner they’d never attended. But the Major, never minding the puzzled look, turned his attention to the most important dish on the board.

“It is to Miss Fotheringay that I came to pay my respects to-day,” he said, and gave her a bow so finished it might have been polished like his boots.

He went on, with a seasoned tongue, to praise her performance: how his nephew raved; how nephews were boys and given to extravagant seasoning; how he himself, being older and less easily intoxicated, had wanted to judge. He declared himself delighted and astonished; he compared her to Siddons and O’Neill and Jordan with the easy generosity of a man handing out compliments like sugared almonds at a christening.

“Faith, I always said so,” Costigan put in, winking at his daughter. “Major, take a chair.”

Milly rose, lifted an unripped satin garment—some theatrical finery that had been draped over the only vacant seat like a sauce-cloth over a pot—and offered the chair with one of her finest curtseys.

“And your manner reminded me of Mars,” the Major continued, enjoying himself.

“There was two Mahers in Crow Street,” Miss Emily said briskly, proud of knowing a fact. “Fanny was well enough, but Biddy was no great things.”

“Sure, the Major means the god of war, Milly, my dear,” said her father, rescuing the compliment from the pit it was falling into.

“It is not that Mars I meant,” the Major replied, smiling, and sent the smile full at Sir Derby Oaks, who had re-entered like a sulky pudding forced back to table. Miss Fotheringay did not understand the words; Sir Derby likely understood even less, and in any case received them with stiffness, his brow knitted as if every syllable were an insult to his digestion.

Major Pendennis, who had lived long enough to relish the sour note in a room’s music, was not at all put out by Sir Derby’s ill-humour. It delighted him. A rival, then, was simmering in the same pot. He hoped—devoutly—that the rival would bubble up and boil over, and perhaps carry the whole foolish stew off the fire.

“I fear I interrupted your fencing lesson; but my stay in Chatteris is very short,” he said, “and I was anxious to make myself known to my old fellow-campaigner Captain Costigan, and to see a lady nearer who had charmed me so much from the stage.”

He spoke of last night’s theatre: a reverend friend who went home in raptures with Ophelia; Sir Derby’s bouquet; how he himself would have brought flowers had he known. Were not those the very flowers in a glass of water on the mantelpiece?

“I am very fond of flowers,” said Miss Fotheringay, with a languishing look at Sir Derby.

Sir Derby responded by looking as if flowers were a personal affront.

“Sweets to the sweet—isn’t that the expression of the play?” asked the Major, bent on amiability.

“’Pon my life, I don’t know,” Sir Derby answered. “Very likely it is. I ain’t much of a literary man.”

The Major widened his eyes—politely astonished. “You don’t inherit your father’s love of letters, then, Sir Derby? He was a remarkably fine scholar, and I had the honour of knowing him very well.”

“Indeed,” said Sir Derby, with a wag of his head which conveyed that learning was a family eccentricity he was glad to have avoided.

“He saved my life,” the Major continued.

“Did he now?” Miss Fotheringay cried, rolling her eyes first at the Major with surprise and then at Sir Derby with gratitude—an expression like a warmed-up compliment, offered again. Sir Derby, however, appeared less grateful than annoyed, as if the notion of his apothecary father’s having saved an officer’s life were an impertinent advertisement.

“My father, I believe, was a very good doctor,” he said. “I’m not in that line myself. I wish you good morning, sir. I’ve got an appointment—Cos, bye-bye—Miss Fotheringay, good morning.”

And out he went, stiff as a carving-knife, down the creaking stairs, his sabre clattering in the passage like a pan-lid in a scullery. Little Tom Creed, playing in the corridor, yelped as the Baronet kicked his peg-top—sent it spinning into the street with an oath, adding to the town’s noise as though Chatteris weren’t already plagued enough by horses, wheels, and men who thought loudness was a proof of breeding.

The Major didn’t smile—at least not visibly. His attention, in those moments, was very much like a skilled cook’s: absorbed in the state of the flame rather than the theatrics of the diners.

“Monstrous handsome young man that,” he remarked. “As fine a looking soldier as ever I saw.”

“A credit to the army and to human nature in general,” Costigan agreed, with the solemnity of a man praising a roast. “A young man of refoined manners, polite affabilitee, and princely fortune. His table is sumptuous: he’s adawr’d in the regiment: and he rides sixteen stone.”

“A perfect champion,” said the Major, laughing. “I have no doubt all the ladies admire him.”

“He’s very well, in spite of his weight, now he’s young,” said Milly, with the calm judgment of someone assessing a joint of meat, “but he’s no conversation.”

“He’s best on horseback,” Mr. Bows offered; and Milly, eager to prove her knowledge, replied that Sir Derby had ridden third in the steeple-chase on his horse Tareaways. The Major, listening to this dullest of menus delivered with confidence, began to comprehend that the young lady who could act tragedy so grandly could also talk like an empty pot.

Costigan, with the hospitality of a man who believes good feeling must be watered, pressed refreshment. The Major, who could no more have been hungry than a man after a Lord Mayor’s dinner, declared he should like a biscuit and a glass of wine above all things, as he felt quite faint from long fasting. He knew—like a food historian who understands the old laws of the hearth—that accepting small kindnesses makes the giver kinder still: a shared cup is a softening of the tongue.

“Some of the old Madara, Milly, love,” Costigan said, winking.

Milly understood at once with that domestic intelligence which often outruns her theatrical one. She slipped out, down the stair, and softly summoned her small emissary, Master Tommy Creed. Into his hand she put money and instructions: a pint of Madeira at the Grapes, and sixpennyworth of sorted biscuits at the baker’s, and hurry back—two biscuits for himself if he ran like a hare.

While Tommy was off, the house below filled with a different kind of murmur—Mrs. Creed’s talk, the scrape of crockery, the soft thud of feet, the little acoustic ecology of a lodging-house: noises that can soothe or fret a mind as much as any music. Milly sat with Mrs. Creed and told her, in confidence and excitement, that Mr. Arthur Pendennis’s uncle—the Major—was upstairs; such a nice, soft-spoken old gentleman, butter wouldn’t melt in his mouth; and how Sir Derby had stalked out in jealous rage. Milly, thinking of two men at once as one thinks of two sauces that cannot be poured into the same dish without curdling, wondered what must be done to pacify both.

Upstairs, Costigan watched the door with pride. “She keeps the keys of the cellar, Major,” he said, as if this were an office of state.

“Upon my word you have a very beautiful butler,” the Major answered, gallantly. “And I don’t wonder at the young fellows raving about her. When we were of their age, Captain Costigan, I think plainer women would have done our business.”

“Faith, and ye may say that,” said Costigan. “Ask me friend Bob Bows here whether Miss Fotheringay’s moind is not even shuparior to her person, and whether she does not possess a cultiveated intellect, a refoined understanding, and an emiable disposition?”

“O of course,” said Bows, drily—the tone of a man who has copied too many love-songs to believe in any of them.

Milly returned, cheeks bright from the cellar air and the stair’s exertion, and Mr. Bows, catching her eye, chose the moment to declare it was time for rehearsal, that she’d be fined if late. His look said plainly: leave the old men to their business; do not stand between them and their knives.

“Must you go?” the Major asked, and pressed her hand at the door with a paternal warmth that nevertheless had the unmistakable firmness of purpose in it, like a cook testing a joint and finding it ready.

Miss Fotheringay curtseyed; Bows hobbled away with his papers. The door closed. The room’s soundscape changed at once—less rustle, more silence; and in that silence the men’s real talk began to simmer.

“Now for it,” thought Major Pendennis.

Costigan, profiting instantaneously by his daughter’s absence, drank up what remained of the Madeira with the eager speed of a man afraid it might be taken from him. His hand shook slightly as he poured, as if the bottle were a promise and he meant to drain it before it vanished.

The Major went to the table and drained his own glass with a hearty smack. He made public-house Cape taste, by sheer performance, like Lord Steyne’s private bin.

“Capital Madeira, Captain Costigan,” he said. “Where do you get it? I drink the health of that charming creature in a bumper. Faith, Captain, I don’t wonder that the men are wild about her. I never saw such eyes in my life, or such a grand manner. I am sure she is as intellectual as she is beautiful; and I have no doubt she’s as good as she is clever.”

“A good girl, sir,—a good girl, sir,” Costigan cried, delighted. “And I pledge a toast to her with all my heart. Shall I send to the—to the cellar for another pint? It’s handy by. No? Well, indeed sir, ye may say she is a good girl, and the pride and glory of her father—honest old Jack Costigan. The man who gets her will have a jew’l to a wife, sir; and I drink his health, sir, and ye know who I mean, Major.”

“I am not surprised at young or old falling in love with her,” said the Major, “and frankly must tell you, that though I was very angry with my poor nephew Arthur, when I heard of the boy’s passion—now I have seen the lady I can pardon him any extent of it. By George, I should like to enter for the race myself, if I weren’t an old fellow and a poor one.”

“And no better man, Major, I’m sure,” Costigan burst out, transported. Tears gathered in his bloodshot eyes—those honest, drink-bright eyes—like moisture on a glass in a warm room. “Your friendship, sir, delights me. Your admiration for my girl brings tears to me eyes—tears, sir—manlee tears—and when she leaves me humble home for your own more splendid mansion, I hope she’ll keep a place for her poor old father, poor old Jack Costigan.”

“Your sentiments do you honour,” the Major said. “But, Captain Costigan, I can’t help smiling at one thing you have just said.”

“And what’s that, sir?” asked Costigan, still mounted on his high horse of emotion.

“You were speaking about our splendid mansion—my sister’s house, I mean.”

“I mane the park and mansion of Arthur Pendennis, Esquire, of Fairoaks Park,” Costigan cried, stiffening, “whom I hope to see a Mimber of Parliament for his native town of Clavering, when he is of ege to take that responsible stetion.”

The Major smiled again, recognising his own earlier talk—his own seasoning—coming back to him as brag. So the boy had been boasting here, had he? Or had the father been cooking castles out of rumour?

“Fairoaks Park, my dear sir,” said Major Pendennis. “Do you know our history? We are of excessively ancient family certainly, but I began life with scarce enough money to purchase my commission, and my eldest brother was a country apothecary: who made every shilling he died possessed of out of his pestle and mortar.”

“I have consented to waive that objection, sir,” Costigan replied, lofty, “in consideration of the known respectability of your family.”

The Major’s inward thought was unprintable; outwardly he smiled and bowed, as if the insult were merely an ill-chosen spice.

“The Costigans, too, have met with misfortunes; and our house of Castle Costigan is by no manes what it was. I have known very honest men apothecaries, sir, and there’s some in Dublin that has had the honour of dining at the Lord Leftenant’s teeble.”

“You are very kind to give us the benefit of your charity,” the Major said. “But permit me to say that is not the question. You spoke just now of my little nephew as heir of Fairoaks Park and I don’t know what besides.”

“Funded property, I’ve no doubt, Meejor, and something handsome eventually from yourself.”

“My good sir, I tell you the boy is the son of a country apothecary,” cried Major Pendennis, the temper beginning to rise like a pot neglected, “and that when he comes of age he won’t have a shilling.”

“Pooh, Major, you’re laughing at me,” said Costigan. “Me young friend, I make no doubt, is heir to two thousand pounds a year.”

“Two thousand fiddlesticks!—Has the boy been humbugging you? Upon my word and honour, as a gentleman and an executor to my brother’s will too, he left little more than five hundred a year behind him.”

“And with aconomy, a handsome sum of money too, sir,” Costigan answered, undaunted. “Faith, I’ve known a man drink his clar’t, and drive his coach-and-four on five hundred a year and strict aconomy, in Ireland, sir. We’ll manage on it, sir—trust Jack Costigan for that.”

“My dear Captain Costigan—I give you my word that my brother did not leave a shilling to his son Arthur.”

Then the Captain’s face changed as if the last sweetness had been burned. The Madeira, the praise, the imagined mansion—all turned suddenly bitter on his tongue.

“Are ye joking with me, Meejor Pendennis?” he cried. “Are ye thrifling with the feelings of a father and a gentleman?”

“I am telling you the honest truth,” said the Major. “Every shilling my brother had, he left to his widow: with a partial reversion, it is true, to the boy. But she is a young woman, and may marry if he offends her—or she may outlive him, for she comes of an uncommonly long-lived family. And I ask you, as a gentleman and a man of the world, what allowance can my sister, Mrs. Pendennis, make to her son out of five hundred a year— which is all her fortune,—that shall enable him to maintain himself and your daughter in the rank befitting such an accomplished young lady?”

The Captain’s indignation blew up like a sudden draught through a kitchen-door.

“Am I to understand, sir, that the young gentleman, your nephew, and whom I have fosthered and cherished as the son of me bosom, is an imposther who has been thrifling with the affections of me beloved child?” he shouted. “Have you yourself been working upon the feelings of the young man’s susceptible nature to injuice him to break off an engagement, and with it me adored Emily’s heart? Have a care, sir, how you thrifle with the honour of John Costigan. If I thought any mortal man meant to do so, be heavens I’d have his blood, sir—were he old or young.”

“Mr. Costigan!” the Major cried.

“Mr. Costigan can protect his own and his daughter’s honour, and will, sir,” Costigan thundered. He pointed to a chest of drawers, as if it were a larder full of evidence. “Look at that chest of dthrawers, it contains heaps of letthers that that viper has addressed to that innocent child. There’s promises there, sir, enough to fill a bandbox with; and when I have dragged the scoundthrel before the Courts of Law, and shown up his perjury and his dishonour, I have another remedy in yondther mahogany case, sir, which shall set me right, sir, with any individual—ye mark me words, Major Pendennis—with any individual who has counselled your nephew to insult a soldier and a gentleman.”

“I am to understand then,” said the Major, perfectly cool, “that you threaten in the first place to publish the letters of a boy of eighteen to a woman of eight-and-twenty: and afterwards to do me the honour of calling me out.”

“You have described my intentions with perfect accuracy, Meejor Pendennis,” Costigan answered, dragging his ragged whiskers over his chin, as though smoothing them could smooth his fury.

“Well, well; these shall be the subjects of future arrangements,” the Major replied, as calmly as if discussing tomorrow’s dinner. “But before we come to powder and ball, my good sir,—do have the kindness to think with yourself in what earthly way I have injured you? I have told you that my nephew is dependent upon his mother, who has scarcely more than five hundred a year.”

“I have my own opinion of the correctness of that assertion,” Costigan said, with a troubled flicker behind the bravado—like a man who suddenly hears, under the room’s words, another sound: the low, ominous hum of reality.

“Will you go to my sister’s lawyers, Messrs. Tatham here, and satisfy yourself?”

“I decline to meet those gentlemen,” Costigan said, disturbed now in earnest, as if lawyers were a louder, uglier machinery than any sabre-clatter. “If it be as you say, I have been athrociously deceived by some one, and on that person I’ll be revenged.”

“Is it my nephew?” cried the Major, starting up and putting on his hat. “Did he ever tell you that his property was two thousand a year? If he did, I’m mistaken in the boy. To tell lies has not been a habit in our family, Mr. Costigan, and I don’t think my brother’s son has learned it as yet. Try and consider whether you have not deceived yourself; or adopted extravagant reports from hearsay.

“As for me, sir, you are at liberty to understand that I am not afraid of all the Costigans in Ireland, and know quite well how to defend myself against any threats from any quarter. I come here as the boy’s guardian to protest against a marriage, most absurd and unequal, that cannot but bring poverty and misery with it: and in preventing it I conceive I am quite as much your daughter’s friend (who I have no doubt is an honourable young lady) as the friend of my own family: and prevent the marriage I will, sir, by every means in my power. There, I have said my say, sir.”

“But I have not said mine, Major Pendennis—and ye shall hear more from me,” Costigan answered, with a severity that tried to be magnificent though it wobbled on drink and wounded pride.

“’Sdeath, sir, what do you mean?” the Major asked, turning on the threshold, meeting him full in the face.

“Ye said, in the coorse of conversation, that ye were at the George Hotel, I think,” Costigan replied, stately as a man serving a challenge on a salver. “A friend shall wait upon ye there before ye leave town, sir.”

“Let him make haste, Mr. Costigan,” cried the Major, rage finally cracking through his polish. “I wish you a good morning, sir.”

And Captain Costigan, standing high on the landing as if it were a battlement, bowed a magnificent bow of defiance over the stairwell, while the Major went down into the noisy street below—where tops spun, sabres clattered, and men made threats in the common language of pride, which always sounds grand in the mouth and always tastes, afterward, like ashes.
