Sir Hugo Mallinger did what he had promised himself he would do: he went down for part of the autumn to Diplow, and his very arrival altered the temper of the country-side as surely as a new wind alters the sound of trees. There was more laughter in the lanes, more talk at shop-counters in Wanchester, more carriage-wheels at the gates of Brackenshaw and Quetcham; and, with it all, a steadier, pleasanter undertone—like a well-kept house where the clatter of plates is not a din but a practiced music.

He liked to be seen, to be felt as an easy friendliness moving among ranks without confusing them. There was art in his hospitality—an art as deliberate as a good cook’s management of courses and company: he would not throw every bird in the yard into one pen and call it kindness. Lord Brackenshaw could dine with an attorney and feel no hurt; but Robinson the attorney would have tasted humiliation in being asked to sit among people who treated him as one of their own sort. Sir Hugo understood these gradations as one understands the degrees of heat in an oven—how a little too much or too little spoils the intended rise. And he meant to have Diplow agreeable, not by accident, but by ordering.

So Diplow drew together old Wanchester solicitors and young village curates, gentlefolk and those who looked up at them, in combinations that flattered each without insulting the other. Even the rector of Pennicote, Mr. Gascoigne, found himself received now with a consideration he had never tasted in Grandcourt’s day. It was not that Sir Hugo had grown fonder of the man; it was that he meant to stand openly in friendliness toward him because of Mrs. Grandcourt. Sir Hugo’s chivalry had fastened on her with a protective firmness—quiet, steady, and, in one point, rare: he kept counsel. There were thoughts he would not even share with Lady Mallinger, not because he trusted her the less, but because he would not make one woman’s secret the food of another’s curiosity. It was a reticence that belonged to him like good breeding belonged to old silver.

Daniel Deronda, now bound to Mirah by an engagement that had changed the whole outline of his days, felt that he owed Sir Hugo the full truth of his position and intent. He chose to send it in a letter. Unwelcome tidings are less likely to draw out the first sharp syllable when they are read in solitude; and Daniel dreaded that moment of spoken collision when pain, quite pardonable at first, becomes memorable as cruelty.

Sir Hugo, reading, was not struck with thunder, but with a vexation that made his blood run impatiently. His first impulse was to carry the letter to Lady Mallinger—partly because her astonishment would give him something to argue with, a way of distributing the weight of his own discontent.

And she was, indeed, astonished—distressed that Daniel’s unusual gifts, and the comfort of having him always near, should end in this—this turning toward Judaism, this departure from what she understood and loved.

“Oh, nonsense, my dear,” Sir Hugo said, catching eagerly at the possibility of scolding her fears instead of nursing his own. “Depend upon it, Dan will not make a fool of himself. He has large notions about Judaism—political views which you can’t understand. No fear but Dan will keep himself head uppermost.”

But when the subject shifted to the marriage, Lady Mallinger gave him no relief. She spoke gently, without sourness, and yet her regret was unmistakable: she had never dreamed, when Mirah sang at her musical party and gave lessons to Amabel, that she would one day be Daniel’s chosen wife. And then, hesitating, she confessed that once—only once—it had crossed her mind that, after a proper time, Daniel might marry Mrs. Grandcourt. It seemed so remarkable, his being in Genoa at that hour, as though providence had laid a thread from his life straight into hers. Lady Mallinger, though she did not much like widows, could not help thinking that such a marriage would have been better than his “going altogether with the Jews.”

Sir Hugo could not even correct her as foolish; the thought struck his own vexation in the very place where it smarted most. But his ill-humor, having no decent vent, turned into a resolution.

“Say nothing of this till further notice,” he told her. And, privately, he told himself, If it’s an unkind cut to the poor thing—Gwendolen—the longer she is spared the knowledge of it the better, in her present state. And she must learn it from Dan himself. His conjectures, long at work, had made him feel as if he knew the whole set of circumstances—knew them with the confidence of a man who has built a story out of several true details.

Meanwhile, being at Diplow with Lady Mallinger and the girls, he could continue those fatherly attentions to Gwendolen which had become almost a duty to him. Lady Mallinger, despite her quiet prejudice against widows, was ready to aid him; she could be tender where she did not understand, and loyal where she had no taste.

Gwendolen, after her removal to Offendene, settled there with a calmness beyond her mother’s hope. It was not happiness; it was a peace that comes after renunciation—after the proud appetite has been forced to live on ordinary bread, and finds, with a kind of wonder, that even plain kindness can be sweet. There are people who, having once felt themselves slipping into a pit, no longer complain of the day’s mere light and air; they take the simplest return of morning and evening as a rescue. Gwendolen had begun to live thus. To be spoken to gently, to have the household go on without harshness, to receive fidelity even from a dog—these were no longer things she felt entitled to, but gifts above expectation.

And in that quieter air her memory worked with a dreadful industry. She lived through again the history of her temptation—from its first deceitful pleasure, when she struggled away from conscience as from an inconvenient voice, to its latest form, when hatred had dragged at her like a physical force, and she had cried and prayed for the help of the conscience she had once thrust aside. Now she dwelt on Deronda’s words—each phrase that had pointed to her deliverance from the worst evil in herself, and each that had seemed to lend strength against despair. She repeated them inwardly as one repeats a remembered cadence in a native dialect—meaning more than can ever be translated into another tongue, because it carries the whole breath and tone of the giver.

Yet she was upheld, too, by a prospect: she would see him again soon. Her need of him made her imagination egotistical—so that she could hardly picture his life as separate from her own. In her mind he was always within reach, as if his presence were part of the very ground she walked on. The future she dared to face was one in which she would continually shape herself toward whatever pattern he held before her. He had risen for her first as a corrective power she resented; then as a being she trusted utterly. And reliance, once it has become like the earth’s firmness, does not easily conceive an end.

Deronda came to Diplow not long after. He had wished to take Ezra and Mirah to some mild coast where the air would not bite, while he prepared a home that Mirah might enter as his wife and where, together, they might watch over her brother. But Ezra begged not to be moved—unless it were to go with them to the East. All outward change had grown burdensome to him; yet the possibility of that voyage shone in his mind with a visionary joy, as if it were more than travel—an approach to something he had waited for all his life.

Daniel, too, had practical reasons for his visit: in preparing for marriage—hoping it need not be delayed beyond a couple of months—he wished fuller consultation with Sir Hugo about his resources and affairs. But he was drawn by another reason quite as strongly: his promise to Gwendolen. The blessedness of his own lot held, strangely, an aching anxiety beside it. People talk as if the lover’s happiness must fatten the heart into indifference; but real experience is seldom so neat. It was no disloyalty to Mirah that his joy could bear, at the same time, a care for another soul. Love is not merely delight; it is an enfolding of immeasurable cares—and those cares are better, and more sacred, than any joys that lie outside love.

He came twice to Diplow and saw Gwendolen twice, and yet he returned to town without telling her anything of the change in his prospects. He reproached himself. But in the act of telling what must wound, one feels dependent on a right approach—some turn of talk, some agreement in the other’s mood with the shock that is coming.

At their first meeting Gwendolen was possessed by her own urgent questions—questions that clung to him like hands: how should she order her life, how make herself less ignorant, how be kindest, how make amends, how rid herself of the selfishness she feared as of an illness. Daniel shrank from pushing aside her immediate need in order to speak of himself; he shrank from inflicting pain while she leaned on him for strength.

At their second meeting he came with a fresh resolve to command the conversation into a path that would lead toward disclosure. But he found her drowned again in depression, overmastered by miserable memories that rose like a tide and seemed more real than any new material out of which she could build a future. She broke into hysterical crying and said he would always despise her. He could only soothe and encourage. And when she revived under his gentleness, and looked at him with that childlike, newly-awakened interest—lashes still wet with tears—it felt impossible to load another weight upon her.

Yet time moved, as time will, and his duty pressed harder. Gwendolen had never thought of him as having “affairs”; she had never even wondered why he had been in Genoa. That ignorance would make the revelation all the heavier. Worse—if she learned it through another voice, she would feel he had been cruelly inconsiderate. He could not write: he could not bear to picture her reading his virtual farewell alone, perhaps hearing in his words a hard gladness for himself and indifference to her. He went down again to Diplow, resolved that any peril was better than returning and leaving her still in ignorance.

On this third visit he found Hans Meyrick installed at Diplow with his easel, beginning his picture of the three daughters on a bank “in the Gainsborough style,” and varying his labor by wandering to Pennicote to sketch village children and enlarge his acquaintance with the Gascoignes. The place had its own soundscape now: the soft rasp of charcoal, the tap of a brush on a palette, the murmur of girls in the distance, the occasional hollow report of a horse’s hoof on the drive—quiet rural noises that steadied the mind, unlike the harsh, mechanical uproar of town where art is hawked and applauded amid a clanging that deadens the very nerves meant to feel.

Hans had recovered something of his vivacity; yet Deronda detected a falseness in it—as when color sits too fixedly on a cheek. With all his grateful friendship, Hans could not help a small irritation: the breaking of Deronda’s reserve had revealed not merely something unsuspected but the reverse of what Hans had hoped and cleverly guessed. There was also that soreness which open natures feel when they discover that the friend they trusted has held back a secret that matters. Hans had poured out his heart about Mirah; and now, looking back, he could not help feeling as if Deronda’s silence had hidden a rivalry. Still, Hans had done right by him in practice; he could tell himself, with a grudging self-respect, that his metal had rung better than he would have sworn.

Perhaps for that very reason he gave his tongue more liberty. He was so full of Deronda’s happiness that he could not imagine what he felt about Gwendolen; and he spoke of her without caution.

“When did you come down, Hans?” Deronda asked, joining him in the grounds where he was studying the proper bank and trees.

“Oh, ten days ago—before the time Sir Hugo fixed. I ran down with Rex Gascoigne and stayed at the rectory a day or two. I’m deep in the gossip of these parts; I know the state of the wheelwright’s interior and have assisted at an infant-school examination. Sister Anna—with the good upper lip—escorted me, else I should have been mobbed by three urchins and an idiot, because of my long hair and a general appearance which departs from the Pennicote type of the beautiful. Altogether, the village is idyllic. Its only fault is a dark curate with broad shoulders and broad trousers who ought to have gone into the heavy drapery line. The Gascoignes are perfect—besides being related to the Vandyke duchess. I caught a glimpse of her in her black robes at a distance, though she doesn’t show to visitors.”

“She was not staying at the rectory?” Deronda asked.

“No; but I was taken to Offendene to see the old house, and as a consequence I saw the duchess’s family. I suppose you have been there and know all about them?”

“Yes, I have been there,” Deronda said quietly.

“A fine old place. An excellent setting for a widow with romantic fortunes. And she seems to have had several romances. I think I’ve found out there was one between her and my friend Rex.”

“Not long before her marriage, then?” Deronda said, with a real stir of interest. “For they had only been a year at Offendene. How came you to know anything of it?”

“Oh—being not ignorant of what it is to be a miserable devil, I learn to gloat on the signs of misery in others. I found out that Rex never goes to Offendene, and has never seen the duchess since she came back; and Miss Gascoigne let fall something in our talk about charade-acting—for I went through some of my nonsense to please the young ones—something that proved to me Rex was once hovering about his fair cousin close enough to get singed. I don’t know what was her part in it. Perhaps the duke came in and carried her off. That is always the way when an exceptionally worthy young man forms an attachment. I understand now why Gascoigne talks of making the law his mistress and remaining a bachelor. But these are green resolves. Since the duke did not get himself drowned for your sake, it may turn out to be for my friend Rex’s sake. Who knows?”

“Is it absolutely necessary that Mrs. Grandcourt should marry again?” Deronda said, unable to keep the edge out of his tone.

“You monster!” Hans retorted. “Do you want her to wear weeds for you all her life—burn herself in perpetual suttee while you are alive and merry?”

Deronda said nothing; but his annoyance was plain enough that Hans changed the current of his talk. Later, alone, Hans shrugged a little at the idea that there had been some stronger feeling between Deronda and “the duchess” than Mirah would ever care to hear of. “Why didn’t she fall in love with me?” he thought, laughing at himself. “She would have had no rivals. No woman ever wanted to discuss theology with me.”

Deronda winced as if a thin lash had cut him. Even a light word, spoken by another, gave weight to what he dreaded, making the pain more real by being named aloud. And he had come down now determined not to evade again.

The next day he rode to Offendene. He had sent word that he meant to call and asked whether Gwendolen could receive him. She was waiting in the old drawing-room where the great crises of her life had unfolded. She looked less sad than she had looked since her husband’s death: there was no smile, but there was a composed stillness, a self-possession new in her. Yet she was keenly alive to the sadness in Deronda’s face.

They had scarcely seated themselves—he at a little distance opposite—when she spoke.

“You were afraid of coming to see me, because I was so full of grief and despair the last time. But I am not so to-day. I have been sorry ever since. I have been making it a reason why I should keep up my hope and be as cheerful as I can, because I would not give you any pain about me.”

There was an unwonted sweetness in her tone, an unguarded gentleness that seemed to Deronda to make his task almost cruel beyond bearing. But he felt that his answer must begin what he had come to do.

“I am in some trouble to-day,” he said, looking at her with a mournful tenderness; “but it is because I have things to tell you which you will almost think it a want of confidence on my part not to have spoken of before. They are things affecting my own life—my own future. I shall seem to have made an ill return to you for the trust you have placed in me—never to have given you an idea of events that make great changes for me. But when we have been together we have hardly had time to enter into subjects which at the moment were really less pressing to me than the trials you have been going through.”

He paused, with a look that pleaded as if he were the one who had received rather than given in those hours of confession and appeal.

A thrill of surprise ran through her, visible in the quick change of her face. But her surprise carried no fear; it ran at once toward some change in his position regarding Sir Hugo and property. Comfort even came with the humility of his asking pardon.

“You never thought of anything but what you could do to help me,” she said. “And I was so troublesome. How could you tell me things?”

“It will perhaps astonish you,” Deronda said, “that I have only quite lately known who were my parents.”

Gwendolen did not look astonished. Her mind, following its own track, felt more sure that she had guessed rightly about what was coming. Deronda continued.

“The reason why you found me in Italy was that I had gone there to learn that—in fact, to meet my mother. It was by her wish that I was brought up in ignorance of my parentage. She parted with me after my father’s death, when I was a little creature. But she is now very ill, and she felt that the secrecy ought not to be any longer maintained. Her chief reason had been that she did not wish me to know I was a Jew.”

“A Jew!” Gwendolen repeated, low and amazed; and her face had the look of one who has swallowed something bitter by mistake—some draught offered as comfort that turns strange on the tongue.

Deronda colored and was silent for a moment, while she stared down as if trying to find her footing in the dark. Memories came and went in her like half-heard phrases in a language she does not possess; she could not seize the meaning. At last she looked up, and the remonstrance in her voice seemed turned toward the mother rather than toward him.

“What difference need that have made?”

“It has made a great difference to me that I have known it,” Deronda said emphatically; yet he found it hard to go on. Between her ideas and his there was something like a difference of native speech; he could not be sure what force his words would carry in her mind.

She meditated again, and then said with a heartfelt intention of kindness, “I hope there is nothing to make you mind. You are just the same as if you were not a Jew.”

She meant: nothing external could alter her feeling toward him, or diminish his power over her. And Deronda, though he felt the misunderstanding, was helped by it; it made a bridge, however narrow.

“The discovery was far from being painful to me,” he said. “I had been gradually prepared for it, and I was glad of it. I had been prepared for it by becoming intimate with a very remarkable Jew, whose ideas have attracted me so much that I think of devoting the best part of my life to some effort at giving them effect.”

Again she was shaken. Again the look of frustration came—this time mixed with alarm. She gazed at him with lips a little parted, like a child listening for a sound it does not recognize. It was not that she connected his words with Mirah and her brother; it was that these particular “ideas,” shadowed and unfamiliar, seemed suddenly more formidable than his “great ideas” had ever been. The great, in general, had always sounded to her like noble music heard at a distance; but this was a nearer strain in an unknown mode, requiring her mind to travel where it had no maps.

He could not quite read what was passing in her; he could only try to choose the least abrupt path.

“That is an object,” he said after a moment, “which will by-and-by force me to leave England for some time—for some years. I have purposes which will take me to the East.”

This was clearer—and precisely because it was clear, it struck her with immediate agitation. Her lips trembled. Tears came and she tasted them before she thought of wiping them away.

“But you will come back?” she said.

Deronda could not sit still. He rose and leaned against the corner of the mantel-piece, turning his face a little aside. She pressed her handkerchief to her cheeks, and then looked up at him, waiting as if the air itself had paused.

“If I live,” he said—“some time.”

They were silent. He could not force himself to add more unless she drew it from him by a question; and she seemed gathering courage for something she must ask.

“What are you going to do?” she said at last, very mildly. “Can I understand the ideas, or am I too ignorant?”
