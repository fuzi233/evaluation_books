Before the housemaid had kindled the fire for the following morning, or until the pale sun of a bleak January day had managed to pierce the heavy gloom outside, Marianne knelt beside a window seat, half-dressed and trembling. She pressed her cheek against the cold glass, striving to capture even a sliver of light to illuminate her frantic handwriting. Tears flowed continuously, blurring her vision and slowing her pen, yet she wrote with a desperate urgency.

Elinor, awakened from sleep by the sharp, ragged sounds of her sister's sobbing, crept quietly toward the bedside. Watching Marianne for a moment with a heart full of silent dread, she spoke in a voice of gentle consideration, "Marianne, may I ask—"

"No, Elinor," Marianne interrupted, her voice strikingly steady despite the storm within. "Ask nothing; you will soon know everything."

That brief moment of composure vanished instantly, replaced by a fresh wave of overwhelming grief. Minutes passed before she could resume her letter, her hand frequently pausing as fresh bursts of sorrow forced her to lift it from the page. Each interruption confirmed what she feared most: that this correspondence might be the final one she would ever write to Willoughby.

Elinor offered her every ounce of quiet support, yet Marianne, driven by a nervous irritability, pleaded with all her might that Elinor say nothing to her at all. Under such circumstances, prolonged proximity was painful for both sisters. Marianne's agitated mind refused stillness; needing constant movement and isolation, she wandered through the house until breakfast, deliberately avoiding the gaze of everyone she encountered.

At the table, she ate nothing. Elinor, meanwhile, focused her entire attention on diverting Mrs. Jennings's curiosity, ensuring her sister remained unnoticed. The meal dragged on, a favorite pastime for the elderly hostess, until finally, a letter was delivered to Marianne. She snatched it eagerly from the servant's hand, her complexion draining to a deathly pallor, and fled the room.

Elinor recognized the sender immediately. Seeing Marianne's reaction, she felt a sickening lurch in her own chest, her limbs trembling so violently she feared Mrs. Jennings would notice her distress. However, the kindly old lady merely assumed Marianne had received a playful missive from Willoughby. Laughing cheerfully, she remarked on how delightfully absurd it was, asking when the couple might finally be wed.

Elinor, though reluctant to speak, felt compelled to correct this dangerous misunderstanding. Trying to force a smile, she replied, "Have you truly, Ma'am, convinced yourself that my sister is engaged to Mr. Willoughby? I thought it mere jest, but such a serious inquiry implies more. Please, do not deceive yourself further. Nothing would surprise me more than to hear they intend to marry."

"For shame!" Mrs. Jennings exclaimed, laughing again. "How can you speak so? Do we not all know it must be a match? They were head-over-heels in love from the very first meeting! Did I not see them together in Devonshire every single day? And did I not know your sister came to London specifically to purchase wedding attire? Come, come, this secrecy is unnecessary. Because you are so coy, you assume others lack sense, but I assure you, the news has circulated throughout the town for ages. I tell everyone, and so does Charlotte."

"Indeed, Ma'am," Elinor said gravely, "you are mistaken. You are doing a grave injustice by spreading this rumor, and I fear you will find yourself unable to believe me now."

Mrs. Jennings chuckled, dismissing Elinor's protest, while Elinor, drained of spirit, hurried upstairs to their chamber. Upon entering, she found Marianne collapsed upon the bed, nearly choking on her sobs, clutching one letter while two or three lay scattered nearby.

Elinor approached silently, sitting beside her sister. She took Marianne's hand, kissing it repeatedly, until her own restraint broke and she joined her in a flood of tears. After sharing this initial outpouring of shared grief, Marianne handed all the letters to Elinor. Covering her face with a handkerchief, she screamed with agony.

Knowing that such profound suffering required time to pass, Elinor waited until the intensity subsided slightly before turning to the letter from Willoughby. It read:

*I have just had the honor of receiving your letter, for which I beg to return my sincere acknowledgments. I am much concerned to find there was anything in my behavior last night that did not meet your approbation; and though I am quite at a loss to discover in what point I could be so unfortunate as to offend you, I entreat your forgiveness of what I can assure you to have been perfectly unintentional.*

*I shall never reflect on my former acquaintance with your family in Devonshire without the most grateful pleasure, and flatter myself it will not be broken by any mistake or misapprehension of my actions. My esteem for your whole family is very sincere; but if I have been so unfortunate as to give rise to a belief of more than I felt, or meant to express, I shall reproach myself for not having been more guarded in my professions of that esteem. That I should ever have meant more you will allow to be impossible, when you understand that my affections have been long engaged elsewhere, and it will not be many weeks, I believe, before this engagement is fulfilled.*

*It is with great regret that I obey your commands in returning the letters with which I have been honored from you, and the lock of hair, which you so obligingly bestowed on me.*

With what fury Elinor must have read such a letter can only be imagined. Although she suspected the truth before opening it—that it would confirm his infidelity and seal their eternal separation—she had not anticipated such language. She could not conceive that Willoughby possessed the audacity to depart so completely from the dignity of a gentleman, sending a message so brutally cruel. Instead of expressing regret or acknowledging any breach of faith, the letter denied all special affection, offering no defense, and serving only as an insult. Every line proclaimed a man steeped in hardened villainy.

She stared at it in stunned indignation, reading it over and over again, each passage deepening her loathing. So bitter was her hatred that she dared not speak aloud, fearing she might wound Marianne further by framing their breakup not as a tragedy, but as a liberation from a monstrous union. Lost in thoughts of the depravity required to write such words, and contrasting it with the pure heart of the person who held no part in this deceit, Elinor forgot her sister's immediate plight, ignored the three remaining letters on Marianne's lap, and lost track of time entirely.

Suddenly, the sound of a carriage approaching startled her. Rushing to the window, she was astonished to see Mrs. Jennings's chaise, which had not been scheduled to arrive until noon. Determined not to abandon Marianne, even though she felt powerless to help, Elinor excused herself from accompanying the visitor, citing her sister's illness. Mrs. Jennings accepted the excuse with her usual good-natured concern. Once the carriage departed, Elinor returned to find Marianne attempting to rise, weak and dizzy from days of starvation and sleeplessness. Now that the feverish suspense had faded, the physical toll became apparent: a throbbing headache, a hollow stomach, and profound weakness.

A glass of wine restored some color to Marianne's cheeks, allowing her to whisper, "Poor Elinor! How unhappy I make you!"

"I only wish," Elinor replied softly, "that there were anything I *could* do to comfort you."

These words struck Marianne like a blow. "Oh! Elinor, I am miserable, indeed," she cried, her voice dissolving into sobs.

Unable to watch this torrent of despair in silence, Elinor urged her, "Exert yourself, dear Marianne! If you would not destroy yourself and those who love you, try to think of your mother. Think of her misery while *you* suffer. For her sake, you must strive."

"I cannot, I cannot!" Marianne wailed. "Leave me, leave me if I distress you! Leave me, hate me, forget me! But do not torture me so. Oh! How easy for those who know no sorrow to speak of exertion! Happy, happy Elinor, *you* cannot possibly comprehend what I suffer."

"Do you call *me* happy?" Elinor asked, her voice trembling. "Ah! If you knew! And can you believe me so content while I see you in such agony?"

"Forgive me, forgive me," Marianne sobbed, throwing her arms around her sister's neck. "I know you feel for me; I know the depth of your heart. Yet you must be happy; Edward loves you—what, oh what, can erase such joy?"

"Many, many circumstances," Elinor said solemnly.

"No, no, no!" Marianne shrieked wildly. "He loves you, and only you. You *cannot* have any grief."

"I cannot find pleasure while witnessing your state."

"And you will never see me otherwise. Mine is a misery nothing can alleviate."
