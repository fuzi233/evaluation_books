The voyage from the Cape to Durban usually consumes four or five days, dictated by the vessel's speed and the whims of the weather. Occasionally, if the landing at East London proves treacherous—where they claim to be sinking fortunes into a harbor that remains little more than a dream—a ship may be stranded for a day while cargo boats struggle to navigate the breakers. But on this occasion, the Bar lay clear. The tugs surged forward immediately, trailing long lines of flat-bottomed barges that devoured the cargo with a chaotic crash. It mattered not whether the crates held delicate porcelain or heavy wool; they were tossed aboard with equal indifference. I watched a case containing four dozen bottles of champagne shatter instantly, the liquid fizzing and boiling in the filth of the barge. It was a wasteful spectacle, and the Kafirs on board seemed to share the sentiment. They scavenged two unbroken bottles, smashed the necks, and drank deeply. They had failed to account for the carbonation expanding within them; soon, they were rolling in the bilge, shouting that the "good liquor" was *tagati*—bewitched. I shouted down to them from the deck, warning that the white man's strongest medicine would kill them, but the terror in their eyes was sufficient punishment. They fled to the shore, and I suspect they will never touch champagne again.

While we steamed northward toward Natal, my mind was consumed by Sir Henry Curtis's proposal. We avoided the subject for a day or two, filling the silence with tales of the hunt, stories that required no embellishment for anyone who knows the wild.

Finally, on a brilliant January evening, our hottest month, we passed the Natal coast, intending to drop anchor at Durban Point before sunset. The shoreline was a tapestry of red sandhills and vivid green sweeps, dotted with Kafir kraals and laced with a ribbon of white surf crashing against the rocks. Yet, as we neared Durban, the landscape grew richer. Sheer kloofs carved by centuries of rain cut through the hills, feeding sparkling rivers, while the bush displayed its deepest greens alongside the cultivated patches of maize and sugar. Here and there, a white house smiled upon the placid sea, adding a touch of civilization to the scene. To me, a view is incomplete without the presence of man; perhaps it is because I have dwelt so long in the wilderness that I cherish the mark of society, even if it drives the game away. The Garden of Eden may have been fair before humanity, but I believe it was fairer when Eve adorned it.

We had miscalculated slightly; the sun dipped below the horizon before we anchored off the Point, hearing the signal gun that announced the arrival of the English Mail. Too late to cross the Bar that night, we retired to dinner, watching the mailbags secured in the lifeboat.

When we returned to the deck, the moon hung low, casting a silver pallor that nearly outshone the lighthouse beams. Sweet, spicy scents drifted from the shore, evoking hymns and missionaries, while a hundred lights twinkled in the windows of the Berea. Nearby, a brig groaned as sailors hauled up the anchor, their music mingling with the sea. It was a perfect night, one that draped a garment of peace over everyone, even the great bulldog of a sporting passenger. The beast, usually eager to confront the baboon in the forecastle cage, now snored contentedly at the cabin door, dreaming of victory.

Sir Henry Curtis, Captain Good, and I took our station by the wheel, falling into a quiet contemplation.

"Well, Mr. Quatermain," Sir Henry said eventually, "have you given thought to my proposals?"

"Ay," Captain Good echoed. "What is your decision, Mr. Quatermain? Will you join us for Solomon's Mines, or wherever this Mr. Neville has vanished?"

I rose, tapped out my pipe, and let the tobacco fall into the sea. I had needed that brief moment to decide.

"Yes, gentlemen," I said, resuming my seat. "I will go. But I must state my terms clearly.

"First, you shall bear all expenses. Any ivory or valuables we acquire shall be divided between Captain Good and myself.

"Second, you shall pay me £500 upfront for my services. I pledge my faithfulness until you abandon the enterprise, until we succeed, or until disaster claims us.

"Third, before we depart, you must execute a deed. In the event of my death or permanent disablement, you agree to provide my son, Harry—who studies medicine at Guy's Hospital in London—with £200 annually for five years. By then, he should be capable of supporting himself. That is my condition."

"No," Sir Henry replied promptly. "I accept them gladly. I am resolved on this project and would pay more for your unique knowledge."

"Pity I did not ask for more, but I shall not retract my word. Now, for my reasons. I have observed you both closely, and I find I like you; I believe we will work well together. That is a vital asset for such a journey.

"As for the journey itself, I must speak plainly: I do not believe we shall survive if we attempt to cross the Suliman Mountains. Consider the fate of Dom da Silvestra three centuries ago, his descendant twenty years later, and your own brother. Their ends were grim, and I fear ours will be similar."

I paused to gauge their reaction. Captain Good looked uneasy, but Sir Henry's expression remained stoic. "We must take our chance," he said.

"You may wonder," I continued, "why a man of my timidity undertakes a path I foresee as fatal. There are two reasons. First, I am a fatalist. I believe my time is appointed regardless of my will. If I am destined to die in the Suliman Mountains, I shall go and I shall die. God knows my fate, and I need not worry. Second, I am a poor man. For forty years I have hunted and traded, yet I have barely scraped a living. The average elephant hunter lives only four or five years. I have survived seven generations of my class; my time is near. If I were to die in the ordinary course of business, my debts would consume everything meant for my son Harry. But now, he is secured for five years. That is the whole matter."

"Mr. Quatermain," Sir Henry said, his gaze intense, "your motives for undertaking an enterprise you believe leads only to disaster reflect great credit upon you. Whether you are right or wrong, time will show. But I tell you now: I will see this through to the end, sweet or bitter. If we are to be knocked on the head, I hope we get a little shooting first, eh, Good?"

"Yes, yes," the captain agreed. "We are accustomed to holding our lives in our hands. There is no turning back now. Let us go below for a drink to toast our luck."

We descended through the bottom of a tumbler, and the next day we went ashore. I lodged Sir Henry and Captain Good in the small shanty I call home on the Berea—a three-room structure of green brick with a galvanized iron roof, surrounded by a garden of loquat and mango trees tended by Jack, an old Griqua hunter whose leg was shattered by a buffalo. Sir Henry and Good slept in a tent pitched in my grove of orange trees, enjoying the scent of bloom and the sight of fruit in all stages of ripeness.

With my decision made, preparations began. I secured the deed from Sir Henry, a process complicated by his status as a stranger and the overseas nature of the property, but eventually finalized with a lawyer's fee of £20. I pocketed my £500 cheque. On Sir Henry's behalf, I purchased a twenty-two-foot wagon with iron axles and seasoned stink wood, a "half-tented" design allowing space for necessities in the front and sleeping quarters in the rear. I bought a team of twenty Zulu oxen, smaller but hardier than Africander breeds, selected for their resistance to red water and inoculated against lung sickness—a cruel but necessary procedure involving a slit in the tail and a piece of diseased lung tissue.

Provisioning and medicine followed. Captain Good, possessing a surprising aptitude for surgery despite lacking formal qualifications, assembled a splendid traveling chest. He demonstrated his skills by amputating a Kafir's big toe with such precision that the patient, watching stolidly, requested a replacement toe, noting that a "white one" would suffice in a pinch.

Our armament was chosen with meticulous care from Sir Henry's English stores and my own collection. We selected three heavy breech-loading double-eight elephant guns, three double-500 Expresses for medium game and men, a double No. 12 central-fire shotgun for potting, three Winchester repeating rifles, and three Colt revolvers. Every weapon was of compatible caliber, ensuring interchangeable cartridges—a vital detail for survival.

For our crew, we limited ourselves to five: a driver, a leader, and three servants. We found two Zulus, Goza and Tom, easily. Recruiting servants, however, proved difficult. We required men of absolute trust and courage. Eventually, we secured Ventvögel, a Hottentot and a master tracker tough as whipcord, though prone to drink—a weakness irrelevant as we traveled beyond the reach of grog shops. We also hired Khiva, a small Zulu who spoke perfect English.

On the eve of our departure, Khiva informed me that a Kafir awaited me. A tall, handsome man of about thirty, lighter-skinned than most Zulus, entered and squatted silently, lifting his knob-stick in salute. He wore the black ring of a *Keshla*, a sign of dignity, and his face was strangely familiar.

"What is your name?" I asked.

"Umbopa," he replied in a deep, slow voice.

"I have seen your face before."

"Yes; the Inkoosi, my father, saw me at the Little Hand on the day before the battle."

The memory struck me. I had been a guide for Lord Chelmsford during the Zulu War, leaving the camp in charge of wagons the day before the disaster. I had spoken with this man, a commander of native auxiliaries, who had warned me of the camp's peril. I had dismissed his words then, but they had lingered in my mind.

"I remember," I said. "What do you want?"

"It is this, 'Macumazahn,'" he said. "I hear you embark on a great expedition far into the North with white chiefs. Is it true?"

"It is."

"I hear you go even to the Lukanga River, a moon's journey beyond Manica. Is this so?"

"Why do you ask? What is it to you?" I asked suspiciously, for our destination was a secret.

"It is this, O white men, that if you travel so far, I would travel with you."

There was an unusual dignity in his speech, particularly his use of "O white men" rather than "O Inkosis."

"You forget yourself," I said gently. "What is your name, and where is your kraal?"

"My name is Umbopa. I am of the Zulu people, yet not of them. My tribe's house lies in the far North, abandoned when the Zulus migrated south a thousand years ago, before Chaka reigned. I have no kraal; I have wandered for many years. I came from the North as a child to Zululand. I served in Cetewayo's Nkomabakosi Regiment under the great Captain, Umslopogaasi of the Axe, who taught my hands to fight. Later, I fled Zululand to see the white man's ways. I fought against Cetewayo in the war. Since then, I have worked in Natal. Now I am tired. This is not my place. I want no money, but I am a brave man, and I am worth my place and meat. I have spoken."

I was puzzled by his demeanor and his offer to serve without pay. Something about him felt distinct from the ordinary Zulu. Translating his words to Sir Henry and Captain Good, I sought their counsel.

Sir Henry instructed me to ask him to stand. Umbopa complied, shedding his long military greatcoat to reveal himself naked save for a *moocha* around his waist and a necklace of lion's claws. He was magnificent, standing over six feet three inches tall, broad and shapely, his skin appearing almost dark save for deep black scars marking old assegai wounds. Sir Henry approached him, studying his proud face.

"They make a good pair, don't they?" Captain Good remarked. "One as big as the other."

"I like your looks, Mr. Umbopa," Sir Henry said in English. "I will take you as my servant."

Umbopa understood, answering in Zulu, "It is well." He glanced at Sir Henry's great stature and breadth, adding, "We are men, thou and I."
