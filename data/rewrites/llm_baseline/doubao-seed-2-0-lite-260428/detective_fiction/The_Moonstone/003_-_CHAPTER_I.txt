If you flip to page 129 of the first volume of *Robinson Crusoe*, you will find the line written plain as day: “Now I saw, though too late, the Folly of beginning a Work before we count the Cost, and before we judge rightly of our own Strength to go through with it.”
Only yesterday, I chanced to open my own tattered, well-loved copy of Crusoe right on that exact page. And here I sit, May 21st, 1850, barely twelve hours later, fresh out of a short chat with my lady’s nephew, Mr. Franklin Blake, that has landed me in exactly the predicament that line warns against. The conversation went as follows:
“Betteredge,” says Mr. Franklin, just back from calling on the family lawyer Mr. Bruff, “I’ve been going over old family matters with him, and we got to talking about that missing Indian Diamond—the Moonstone, the one that vanished from my aunt’s Yorkshire house two years back. Both of us are agreed the whole tangled story ought to be set down in writing, in the interests of the truth, and the sooner the better.”
I did not see his drift at first, but I’ve long learned it’s always wisest to side with the lawyer if you want a quiet, peaceful household, so I nodded and said I thought that made perfect sense.
Mr. Franklin went on: “You know as well as I do that innocent folks have already fallen under unfair suspicion over that cursed stone. If we don’t get the facts down on paper now, future generations might go on smearing good names for no reason, with no official record to appeal to. There’s no question this strange, tangled family story of ours needs to be told. And Bruff and I think we’ve hit on exactly the right way to do it.”
I had no quarrel with that, of course—still, I could not for the life of me see what business any of that had to do with me, a humble house steward knocking on seventy years old.
“We’ve got two sets of facts to lay out,” he explained. “First, we need to show how the Moonstone first fell into my uncle Herncastle’s hands back when he was serving in India, fifty years ago. I already have that preface sorted, you see: an old eyewitness account from the family papers, so no extra work is needed there. The next part, the one no one else alive knows half as well as you, is how that diamond turned up at my aunt’s Yorkshire house two years ago, and how it vanished right under our noses in barely twelve hours. You were there the whole time. You saw every little thing that happened. So you’re the one who has to pick up the pen, and start the story off for us.”
That’s how I learned I’d been roped into the job. If you’re curious what I did next, I’ll be plain: I did exactly what you would do in my shoes. I made a big show of protesting that I was far too uneducated and unskilled to tackle such a weighty task—even while, deep down, I was certain I was clever enough to pull it off, if I just gave my wits a fair shot.
Mr. Franklin must’ve read that private little thought right off my face. He would not hear a word of my false modesty, refused to let me weasel out, and insisted I give those supposed wits the chance to prove themselves.
It has been two whole hours since he walked out my door. No sooner had his boots stopped clacking down the drive than I hauled out my writing desk, sharpened a pen, and settled in to start the tale. And I have sat here, useless as a wet match, ever since—staring at my open copy of Crusoe, seeing that line about counting the cost before you start your work stare right back at me.
You will mark that I stumbled on that exact line by pure accident the day before I rashly agreed to take this job on. If that is not a quiet, homemade kind of prophecy, I do not know what is.
Mind you, I am no superstitious fool. I have read my share of books in my time, a self-made scholar in my own humble way. At seventy, my memory is still sharp as a tack, and my legs keep up with it too. Do not go thinking I am some ignorant old man when I say there has never been a book written that holds a candle to *Robinson Crusoe*, and there never will be, not as long as I live. I have tested that book for decades, usually paired with a good pipe of tobacco, and it has never let me down, no matter what mess life throws at me. When my spirits are low? Crusoe. When I need a bit of plain common-sense advice? Crusoe. Back when my dear late wife was nagging my ears off? Crusoe. These days, when I have had a drop too much ale? Crusoe. I have worn out six stout, well-thumbed copies of the book over the years. Last year, for my lady’s birthday, she gave me my seventh—nice blue binding, price four shillings and sixpence, with a full-page illustration of Crusoe on his island for good measure. I celebrated by having a little extra ale, and sure enough, a few pages of Crusoe was all it took to set me right again.
Wait a minute, though—this is all just me rambling on about my favourite book, and I have not written a single word about the missing Moonstone, have I? I am wandering off chasing Lord knows what, Lord knows where. Let us set this rambling sheet aside, grab a fresh new page of paper, tip my cap to you, dear reader, and start the story properly, from the top.
