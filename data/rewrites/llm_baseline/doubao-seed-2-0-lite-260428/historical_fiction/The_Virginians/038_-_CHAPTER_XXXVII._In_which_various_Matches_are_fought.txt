At breakfast one morning, Mr. Harry Warrington flipped through his copy of the London Advertiser and spotted a public call to all lovers of manly British sport, inviting them to the Wooden House in Marybone Fields to witness a trial of skill between the legendary champion pugilists Sutton and Figg. The young Virginian, who had purchased a new matched pair of horses only the day before, resolved to attend the show immediately, hitching the team to his phaeton to drive himself out to the grounds.
He did not know the roads west of Covent Garden well, however, and veered and tacked far more than was strictly necessary on his journey, losing himself in the sun-dappled green lanes behind Mr. Whitfield’s round Tottenham Road Tabernacle, and the unfenced meadows that then stretched around the newly built Middlesex Hospital. He arrived at last, to find a vast crowd assembled to cheer on the two champions’ valorous feats.
A throng of ragged London roughs clustered around the doors of this temple of British sport, jostling for space alongside the gilded equipages of a handful of fashionable patrons who, like Warrington, had come to patronize the contest. Crippled beggars whined around the young gentleman’s heels, begging for alms; shoeblack boys tumbled over one another for the honour of polishing his boots; flower girls and itinerant fruiterers hounded Mr. Gumbo to buy their wares; piemen, pickpockets, tramps, and strollers of every sort hung about the perimeter of the battleground. A gaudy flag flapped atop the building, and a master of ceremonies repeatedly stepped out onto the front stage, accompanied by a drummer and a horn-blower, to announce to the restless crowd that the noble English sports were on the very brink of beginning.
Warrington paid his entry fee, and was shown to a gallery seat with an unobstructed perfect view of the combat stage. Gumbo settled himself in the cheap benches of the amphitheatre below, and whenever he grew bored, wandered out into the outer yard to drink a pot of ale or play cards with the other footmen and coachmen waiting by their masters’ carriages. The 18th century world was positively lousy with these liveried vassals, after all: no gentleman or lady could stir abroad without one, often two or three, trailing in their wake. Every theatre had a footman’s gallery of its own, an army of the uniformed throng clustered round every chapel door, they swarmed in anterooms, sprawled across landings and hallways, guzzled, cheated, bullied visitors for vails and lost whole fortunes at cards. That grand old race of footmen is all but gone now: only a handful linger still, tall, powdered, melancholy, in their plush livery and silver buckles, parading at royal levees, as rare a sight as the scattered camps of Red Indians you might still stumble across on the American frontier. The race is doomed: Uncas with his tomahawk and eagle plume, and Jeames the footman with his cocked hat and long cane, are both fading out of the world where they once walked in glory.
Before the headliners took the stage, a string of minor exhibition bouts were held. The first boxing match was a limp affair: neither man showed much spirit, neither landed a punishing blow, and the crowd grew quickly restless. Next came cudgel-play, but no competitor cracked a head memorable enough, no wound was severe enough to satisfy the bloodthirsty audience, who soon took to hissing, grumbling, and stamping their feet. “The masters! The masters!” they roared, until the two famous champions finally consented to make their entrance.
First up the stage steps strode the intrepid Sutton, sword in hand, saluting the crowd with his weapon and bowing very specifically to a private box where a stout, important-looking gentleman sat watching. Sutton was followed seconds later by the redoubtable Figg, to whom the stout man waved a hand of warm approval. Both fighters stripped to their shirts, their heads shaved clean to avoid any chance of an opponent grabbing their hair, their scalps crisscrossed with the old scars and cracks of dozens of former victorious bouts. Each wore a coloured silk ribbon, his “armiger,” tied around his burly sword arm. The two gladiators shook hands, and a roar went up from the crowd for the blunted combat swords.
The fight had barely commenced before Figg swung a blow so tremendous that, had it landed full on Sutton’s head, the comely young man’s skull would have been lopped off as clean as a carrot under a carving knife. But Sutton caught the full force of the blow on his own blade, and Figg’s sword snapped clean in his hand from the sheer weight of his strike. New blades were handed to the two men, and soon the first blood of the bout spouted from Figg’s heaving side, drawing a yell of triumph from Sutton’s supporters. The veteran called for a pause, however, appealing to the crowd and especially to the stout gentleman in the private box, to point out that the shard of his broken first sword had nicked him unfairly.
In the lull of the parley over the injury, Warrington spotted a gentleman in a riding frock and plain scratch-wig slip into the stout man’s private box, and recognised his old Tunbridge Wells acquaintance, the Earl of March and Ruglen. Lord March, who was notoriously sparing with politeness to most people, showed singular deference to the stout stranger, bowing very deeply as the other man pulled a handful of bank notes from his pocket-book and handed them over to him. As soon as their business was concluded, March spied Harry in his gallery, crossed over, and greeted his young friend warmly. They sat together to watch the rest of the fight, which raged with equal skill and valour on both sides for hours, until the two champions laid down their swords and took up quarter-staffs, whereupon the veteran Figg claimed the long-sought victory.
A heavy thunderstorm had broken over the building while the two men fought, and Warrington gladly accepted a seat in Lord March’s chariot for the ride back to Covent Garden, leaving his own phaeton to be driven home by his groom. Harry was utterly enraptured by the day’s entertainment, declaring it the finest sport he had witnessed since his arrival in England. True to his habit of wanting to share every pleasure he enjoyed with the companion of his boyhood, he sighed and began to say “I wish…” before he caught himself and fell silent.
“What do you wish, and what don’t you wish?” asked Lord March, lolling back against his carriage cushions and eyeing the young Virginian curiously.
“I was thinking of my elder brother, and wished he could have been here,” Harry explained. “We promised we’d share all sorts of sport back home, you see, talked of it a hundred times. Though he’d never have cared for this rough sort of fighting, even if he was the bravest lad alive. You should have seen him once, when he quarrelled with a gallant officer who was our friend – it was a foolish, trivial affair, but we could barely hold George back. I never saw a man so cool, or so savage and determined, God help me. Ah, for the credit of Virginia, I wish he could have come over here in my place, to show you what a real gentleman from the colony is like.”
“Nonsense, you’ll do very well on your own,” the amused nobleman replied. “What’s this I hear about Lady Yarmouth taking you into her favour, by the way?”
“I’ll do as well as any other man!” Harry laughed. “I can ride, and I daresay I shoot better than George, but my brother had the head, sir, the head!” he tapped his own honest skull for emphasis. “I give you my word, he’d read nearly every book ever printed, could play both fiddle and harpsichord, write the most elegant poetry and sermons. What can I do? I’m only good for riding, playing cards, and drinking Burgundy.” He hung his head for a moment in mock penitence, before grinning. “Though I’ll back myself to do all three of those better than most fellows you’ll meet.”
The jaded, world-weary Lord March found the young man’s unfeigned naivete as delicious as a perfectly cooked mutton chop to a man who has dined on every fancy dish in the world. “By Gad, Mr. Warrington,” he chuckled, “you ought to be taken straight to Exeter ‘Change and put on public display. A gentleman from Virginia who actually mourns the loss of his elder brother? The breed’s completely unknown in this country. Upon my honour, I do believe you’d actually like to have him back again.”
“Believe?” Harry cried, flushing bright red.
“Ah, you believe you believe you’d like him back,” March corrected, waving a careless hand. “But depend upon it you wouldn’t. It’s not in human nature, sir, not as I’ve ever read it. Look at these new houses we’re passing: that one on the corner is Sir Richard Littleton’s, that big empty one was Lord Bingley’s. It’s a crying shame they’ve left this great empty stretch of Cavendish Square fenced off with those ugly boards. By George, I don’t know where this town’s spreading to. Montagu House has been turned into a confounded Don Saltero’s museum, full of books and stuffed birds and rhinoceroses. They’ve cut that cursed New Road right behind Bedford House Gardens, spoiling the Duke’s privacy – though I suppose they’re lining his pockets for the trouble. Shall we go back via Tyburn Road and the Park, or cut through Swallow Street to the more habitable part of town? We can dine in Pall Mall, or go back to your rooms, and spend the evening however you like – with the Queen of Spades at the cards table, or…”
“With the Queen of Spades, if your lordship pleases,” Harry said, blushing.
The chariot accordingly pulled up at Warrington’s Covent Garden hotel, where the landlord hurried out to greet them, bowing so low at the sight of Lord March that his wig nearly brushed the nobleman’s shoes. The inn’s very best dinner was laid on for the two men, followed by more than enough wine, vintage good enough even for the famously epicurean earl. Over their glasses, they discussed heading to Vauxhall to watch the fireworks, or staying in to play cards. Harry had never seen any fireworks grander than a dozen squibs let off in Williamsburg on Guy Fawkes’ night, which he had thought a sublime display, but he yielded to his guest’s preference for piquet, and the two men were soon absorbed in the game.
Harry began winning, as was his usual luck, but after half an hour the tide turned, and favour ran entirely Lord March’s way. He was growing surly at an interruption when Mr. Draper, Warrington’s man of business, came bowing into the room. Harry, who was constitutionally generous to a fault, immediately invited him to sit and take a drink. If he had a crust, he shared it; a haunch of venison, he split it with his guest; a jug of water, he passed it around; a bottle of fine Burgundy, he drank it gaily with any thirsty friend who wandered by. It is a rare virtue, that unforced open-handedness, not the performative hospitality of the man who hosts six large dinners a year to show off to his acquaintances, but the genuine kind heart that does not wither away the moment it is transplanted to the stifling, selfish air of London.
Draper did not care much for wine, but he was utterly delighted to be in the company of a famous peer. He protested that nothing pleased him more than watching two consummate, fashionable players go at piquet, and he settled himself in a seat, unperturbed by Lord March’s sidelong scowls, to watch the game. Harry was nowhere near a match for the veteran London club player, and tonight March held far better cards to back up his skill. Their stakes were kept quiet, the two men agreed to play for shillings at the start, and counted their gains and losses in low undertones, perfectly polite and formal with one another, until a second interruption brought a vicious curse to Lord March’s lips.
First there was a scuffle outside the door, then whispering, then the sound of a woman sobbing, until at last a female figure burst headlong into the parlour, drawing that unseemly outburst from the earl.
“I wish your women would pick a better time to call, confound them!” March snapped, flinging his cards down on the table in a pet.
“What, Mrs. Betty?” Harry cried, astonished. It was indeed Lady Maria’s personal maid, and Gumbo stood right behind her, his face streaked with tears. “What’s the matter? Is the Baroness well?”
“Help! Help your honour!” Mrs. Betty shrieked, dropping straight down onto her knees.
Gumbo let out a loud wail from the doorway. “Gumbo, you scoundrel,” Harry said, half-amazed. “Have anything happened between you and Mrs. Betty?”
Gumbo stepped back with great dignity, laying his hand on his heart. “No, sir. Nothing hab happened 'twix dis lady and me.”
“It’s my mistress, sir!” Betty wailed. “Help, help! Here’s the letter she wrote me to give you, sir! They’ve taken her, sir!”
“Old Molly Esmond? Everyone knows she’s over head and ears in debt,” March said dismissively. “Dry your eyes in the next room, Mrs. Betty, and let’s get back to our game.”
“Help her! Mr. Harry, you won’t keep playing cards when my lady’s calling out to you for rescue!” Betty sobbed. “You used to come running fast enough when I fetched you over at Castlewood!”
“Confound you, hold your tongue!” March swore, more colourfully this time. But Betty would not stop weeping, and the fates had decreed that Lord March would not win another hand that night.
Harry pushed his chair back and reached for the bell pull. “My dear lord, the game is over for this evening. My relative writes to me in great distress, and I am bound to go to her aid.”
“Curse her! Couldn’t she wait until tomorrow?” March grumbled.
Harry ordered a postchaise brought round immediately, his own horses harnessed to take him as far as Bromley.
“Bet you don’t get there in an hour!” March crowed, already reaching for his pocket book. “Bet you don’t make it in five quarters of an hour! Four to one you get robbed on Blackheath! Bet you don’t reach Tunbridge Wells before midnight!”
“Done!” Harry laughed. March carefully scribbled all four wagers down in his notebook, before Harry unfolded the note the maid had brought him, scrawled in Lady Maria’s messy hand:
*MY DEAR COUSIN--I am fell into a trapp, the work of vile villains I can plainly see. I am a prisner. Betty will tell you all. Ah, my Henrico! come to the resque of your MOLLY.*
Half an hour after he received the note, Harry was settled in the postchaise, galloping across Westminster Bridge, the wheels kicking up spray from the rain-soaked road, on his way to succour his beleaguered kinswoman.
