Spring crept fast over Villette, and the weather burst into sudden, unseasonable warmth. For me, as for so many frail, overworked souls, that soft heat leached my strength thin: the smallest task left me drained, sleepless restless nights bled into heavy languid days where even lifting a pile of exercise books felt like a labour. One Sunday afternoon, I tramped half a league to the distant Protestant church, and by the time I stumbled back through the school gates, my legs were shaking. I fled straight to my quiet sanctuary, the first classe, and sank into my chair, resting my arms and head on the hard wooden desk, too exhausted to even light a candle.

For a while I listened to the bees droning low in the arbour outside, and watched through the glass door, past the tender new spring leaves, as Madame Beck paraded her dinner guests through the central orchard alley. The boughs were heavy with blossom that day, white and flushed the exact colour of mountain snow streaked with sunrise pink. My eyes kept drifting back to one figure in that party: a bright, handsome young girl I’d seen visiting before, Mademoiselle Sauveur, they said she was M. Emanuel’s goddaughter, that her mother had been a dear old friend of the professor’s. I’d seen her with him once before, laughing, looping her arm through his, leaning her weight on him like a spoiled adored child, and a strange sharp unwelcome little twinge had lanced through me then, some odd premonition I’d refused to name, refused to unpack. M. Paul was nowhere in that holiday party today, and I followed the flash of her rich silk gown winding through the flowers until my eyes grew heavy, the hum of bees and warm air lulling me fast asleep.

Two hours slipped by before I woke. The sun had sunk behind the tall city houses, the garden and the classroom draped in soft grey dusk, the bees all gone back to their hives, the flowers folded shut for the night, the last of Madame Beck’s guests vanished, every alley empty. But I was not cold, not stiff, not numb from pressing my cheek to the hard desk for two whole hours. Where the bare wood had been, there was a thick folded wool shawl tucked under my arms and face, and another soft shawl wrapped tight around my shoulders. Someone had crept in quiet as a shadow while I slept, and tucked me in.

I puzzled over it. Not Ginevra, that reckless bright thing would have shaken me awake laughing for a joke. Not Zélie St. Pierre, who had never hidden her dislike for me. Not the other teachers, none of them had that soft unthinking tenderness. It must be Madame Beck, I told myself pragmatically – she values her useful English teacher, would not have me fall ill and disrupt the classes. But the thought did not sit quite right.

I stepped out into the fresh cool evening air then, and wandered down my favourite alley. I had not forgotten the strange spectral illusion I’d seen there months before, the night I’d buried my little glass jar under the old patriarch tree, but a last slant of sunset still gilded the stone statue of Jean Baptiste at the far end, and the birds were still chattering in the ivy on the walls. I walked slow, turning over that old half-plan I’d nurtured for months: when I saved a thousand francs, I would rent a small flat, set up a little school of my own, start with day pupils, work my way up, just as Madame Beck had done. Independence, that steady safe prize. But then another thought crept in, soft and unruly: what then? Was that all? No true home, no one dearer to me than my own careful plans, no one at whose feet I could lay down all that hard-won self-reliance, and labour just to make them happy? I laughed at myself, quiet, in the dusk. Lucy Snowe, you ask too much. Most of us walk through life on the crescent, never the full moon. You will make do. I said my quiet goodnight to Dr. John then, out loud, the kind gentle man whose warm regard I’d mistaken for something meant only for me. “Goodnight, Dr. John. You are good, you are beautiful. But you are not mine. God bless you.”

A voice answered me, not three feet away, low and rough and familiar. “Good-night, Mademoiselle. Or rather, good evening – the sun is scarcely set. I hope you slept well?”

I started, my heart jumping, but I knew that voice instantly. M. Paul Emanuel stood half in the shadow of the arbour, his dark eyes glinting, his expression half teasing, half soft. He was the one who had left the shawls, he admitted when I told him I’d woken warm and coddled, and he brushed off my thanks like they were trivial. He told me he watched all of them from a little rented room in the boarding house across the way, with a telescope, his own little observation post for studying human nature. He laughed as he told me he’d seen through Zélie St. Pierre’s honeyed flattery months before, that he’d once almost married her, if he hadn’t spied her cruel petty rages through that window, seen her true face. He confessed gruff and half-embarrassed that for all his boldness in front of crowds and crowned heads, he could be shy, that he blushed like a schoolgirl at the thought of asking a woman to marry him. Then he told me he’d watched me, long before we’d ever exchanged more than a formal greeting – that he’d seen me wander this alley every evening, that he’d snuck up to me once when we were strangers, and pressed a little knot of white violets into my palm. I told him I still had those violets, pressed flat in the back of my prayer book, and his face lit up like I’d given him a gift.

The air turned cold then, clouds blotting out the last of the sunset, fat raindrops starting to patter through the leaves. He asked me if I believed in the supernatural, and I admitted I’d seen strange things in this very garden, months before. He said he’d seen them too – the ghost nun, the legend of the woman bricked up alive under the roots of the old patriarch tree hundreds of years before. He said he’d seen that nun’s black and white habit gliding through the halls at night, more than once.

And then, without warning, the great old tree behind us convulsed. There was no breeze, not a breath of wind to stir the feathery lilac bushes, but its great heavy boughs heaved and creaked, like something was struggling to be born out of its shadow, something dark and solid writhing free from the trunk. The prayer bell rang out sharp inside the house.

And out of the writhing foliage burst the nun herself, all stark black robes and stark white wimple, tall, fierce, rushing past us so close her habit brushed my sleeve, the wind screaming after her, the rain lashing wild and cold, the whole dark night seeming to shiver in her wake.
