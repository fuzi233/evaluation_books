There is a kind of villain whom plays delight to powder with flour and display upon the boards—your professional saint, your pulpit-thumper whose lips are forever honeyed with salvation while his heart keeps no more faith than a dry husk keeps sap. Yet in the common run of men and women, in the slow honest press of a Sunday street, hypocrisy is rarer than the satirist would have it. The chapel bell’s thin chink comes through the evening air; pattens clap on the stones; hobnailed shoes beat their blunt rhythm; umbrellas knock and open with a little gust of canvas and whalebone. The very sound of a congregation has its own weather: a sough of woollen gowns, the swish of ribbons, the stiff creak of prayer-books, the small coughs that try to be discreet and only advertise themselves the more.

Look at them filing in, and do not be too eager to call them false because their minds wander. The maid-servant’s thoughts fly, as naturally as a sparrow, to the lad who waits for her beyond the railings; the tradesman measures sugar and credit in his head even while he measures grace with his lips; the schoolboy mouths the responses and hears, under them, the Latin he must construe on Monday. Another thinks, not of the sermon, but of the pie to come after it—spiced, steaming, a comfort promised at the end of restraint. The parish clerk drones the psalm, his voice a weary pump drawing water from a deep place, and all the while his heart is at home with a daughter’s trouble. The parson himself, when he hides his face in his cushion, may be tasting in his mind the sourness of a bill due on Monday. They are not saints; they are not airy angels with feet lifted off the earth. But they are not stage hypocrites either. For most folk keep religion as they keep a cordial or a purge: a bottle in the cupboard, to be taken in the hour of sickness, and pressed upon a neighbour with that eager charity which loves to prescribe.

Of such household physic—bodily and spiritual—women are the most ardent dispensers. A century ago every great house had its still-room, where rosemary and lavender hung drying, where lemon-peel and angelica lay in sweet heaps, where syrups thickened in glazed pans and cordials brightened in glass, and where remedies stood labelled in a hand that believed its own authority. My Lady Warrington, at Sir Miles’s, took charge of consciences and digestions with the same sure air. She kept the servants’ hall as another woman might keep a dairy: everything must be ordered, wholesome, obedient; the dose measured, the doctrine measured; the swallow expected and praised. Whether her pill or her text was well chosen, Heaven can tell; but she administered each as one who never doubted that she was born to prescribe.

Sir Miles’s dinner, to do him justice, made a handsome show. The sideboard shone with plate enough to dazzle a poor man into thinking he had eaten his fill merely by seeing it. Liveries moved solemnly about the room as if the household had been raised, by broadcloth and buttons, into a sort of ceremony. Yet it required a steady mind to notice that the beer, brought in a tankard fit for a lord’s October, was small and thin upon the tongue; and that the grand silver dish carried but a single joint of mutton, carved with as much importance as if it were venison for a hunting party. Sir Miles smacked his lips over his wine, and looked upon the ruby in his glass as though it were nectar poured from Olympus. He asked Harry Warrington—our young Virginian, so lately the darling of fortune and the favourite at every table—whether they had port like that in Virginia; and vowed it was nothing to what Harry should taste in Norfolk.

Harry, who had known better wine, nearly believed by force of his uncle’s praising that this, too, must be excellent; and he held the glass up, squinting into it as if merit might be spied by close looking, like a bubble in amber. It is one of the small enchantments of society that a man may drink sour liquor and, if all around him swear it is ambrosia, find his own tongue wavering between truth and politeness.

As in many well-ordered families then and now, the Warringtons had their two paragons. Lady Warrington told Harry, with a pious pride that rose like leaven in all her speech, that of her grown daughters one was the greatest Beauty and the other the greatest Genius and Angel of any young lady alive. The Beauty—Flora—was, in her mother’s confidence, already engaged to dear Tom Claypool. The Genius and Angel—Dora—set herself, with unwearied zeal, to improve Harry’s wits and morals. She sang to him at the harpsichord; an angel, Harry thought, might have kept better time, but he did not say so. She was ready with advice, instruction, earnest conversation; and she gave it with such a plentiful hand that, though Harry was a good-natured young fellow, he often would have preferred the simpler company of the little cousin who reminded him of Fanny Mountain at home—a child’s voice, a child’s laugh, a child’s appetite for simple pleasures, like new bread.

That youngest maiden, however, after dinner commonly retired to her nursery. Beauty went off upon her own avocations. Mamma had her poor to visit, her voluminous letters to write, her conscience to inspect. Papa dozed in his great chair with a contented grunt, like a man who had eaten enough to forget that the world required effort. And Dora remained, the Angel, to keep her cousin company and to rescue him—so she imagined—from the world.

Yet there was a calm in the house that pleased Harry. He, who lived so much in riot and late hours, liked at times to take shelter under this roof where the evening sounded differently: not the clatter of dice and the shrill laughter that follows them, but the quiet tick of a clock, the low stir of servants clearing away, the faint jangle of the harpsichord when Dora tried a passage again, dissatisfied. Such domestic noises are a kind of music too, and they steadied him. The welcome he received was unfailing. The doors were open at all hours. If Flora was not at home, Dora was ready to receive him; if Dora was busy in her angelic labours, mamma herself would be motherly. In a few days he and his little cousin Miles had had a galloping-match in the Park, hoof-beats drumming on the softer ground, the boy’s laughter carried away on the wind like a lark’s. Harry, generous to everybody who came near him, even began to think of buying the lad a better horse than the poor little beast he rode—when events, with the suddenness of a snapped trace, brought all Harry’s coaches and horses to an abrupt standstill.

Sir Miles, who had once imagined Virginia to be an island, was not the best instructed in geography; but the ladies were more curious, and more knowing, and eager to hear of Harry’s home. Harry was not loth to talk of it. To speak of the rivers and woods of his birth was to feel, for a moment, his feet planted on something real. He described the length and breadth of his estate; the rivers that coasted it; the produce it bore. He had, as a boy, taken some pleasure in surveying with a friend, and now, in Lady Warrington’s drawing-room, he set to work with paper and pencil, making a map of his county. He placed towns here and there—fine towns upon his draught which, in truth, were little more than log-huts; but for the honour of his country he would have them look handsomer than they were. Here ran Potomac; here James River; here the wharves from which his mother’s ships carried tobacco to the sea. The estate, in truth, was as large as an English county. Harry did not brag; he spoke as a young man might speak of his mother’s domain with a natural pride, and because the telling itself warmed him.

To see him, handsome in his velvet and silver lace, bending over his draught, pointing to a hill, a forest, a water-course, you might have imagined him a travelling prince describing a realm. He almost fancied himself so at times. In Virginia he had miles where English gentlemen had acres. Dora listened, intent, as if maps could be moral lessons. Flora, too, bowed her fair head and heard him with a new attention. And why should she not? What was young Tom Claypool—Sir Miles’s nephew in Norfolk, with his great boots and great voice and heirship to a poor five thousand acres—compared to this American cousin with his wide rivers and woods and the air of a charming stranger? Dora, angel as she was proclaimed, began to lose her angelic temper and to twit Flora with being a flirt. Claypool, when he came, would sit dumb in his red waistcoat before splendid Harry, who talked of March and Chesterfield, Selwyn and Bolingbroke, and the whole glittering company of macaronis, as if London were his pantry and he knew where every sweetmeat lay.

Lady Warrington’s affection for Harry grew in proportion as she discovered his importance. She became anxious about the spiritual welfare of the poor Indians and poor negroes in Virginia, and asked what she might do to help dear Madam Esmond in her good work—Madam Esmond being, as Lady Warrington was sure, a precious woman. She had a serious butler and a serious housekeeper; and they were delighted with the spiritual behaviour and sweet musical gifts of Gumbo, Harry’s black servant, who could sing as if he had swallowed a little bell. “Ah, Harry, Harry!” she would cry. “You have been a sad wild boy! Why did you not come sooner to us, sir, and not lose your time amongst the spendthrifts and the vain world? But ’tis not yet too late. We must reclaim thee, dear Harry! Mustn’t we, Sir Miles? Mustn’t we, Dora? Mustn’t we, Flora?”

At this appeal, the three would look up to the ceiling—as if heaven were painted there and could be consulted at leisure—and agree, each in her own way, that the prodigal must be reclaimed. It became a little contest of virtue, which should rescue him most. Dora watched Flora; Flora smiled with that softness which is not an argument but often wins them. When the girls were away, mamma talked to Harry more tenderly, more seriously. She would be a mother to him, she said, in the absence of his admirable parent. She gave him a hymn-book, pressed it upon him as she might press a vial of cordial into a trembling hand. She kissed his forehead. She was moved, she believed, by the purest love and religious regard toward her dear, wayward, amiable nephew.

While these domestic sanctities were being kneaded and shaped in the Warrington parlour, Harry kept his own counsel about his affairs out-of-doors. For fortune, which had smiled upon him with a broad easy grin for weeks, had suddenly turned her face away; and in a few days she had kicked down, with a careless foot, all his heap of winnings. If my Lord Castlewood—his own kinsman—had dealt unfairly with the young man at the table, who shall say? We insinuate nothing so disrespectful. But the fact remained: Castlewood had won from Harry every shilling that properly belonged to him; and would have played him for his reversions too, had not Harry, seeing himself beaten past remedy, flung up his hands and declared the battle must end. One spar remained from the wreck, as it were—that portion he had set aside for poor Sampson. In an hour of rash hope he ventured that too at the table; and it went down with the rest, and fortune fluttered off into the storm, leaving him almost naked on the shore.

When a man is young and hearty, the loss of money does not at first bite so deep as it will later. Harry told himself he would sell his horses, dismiss his coach, lessen his train. If he wanted immediate supplies, were there not friends? Would not his Aunt Bernstein be his banker? Might not his kinsman, who had won so much from him, be appealed to? Or his kind Uncle Warrington and Lady Warrington, always preaching benevolence and calling him a son—would they not help? He could call on any of these at leisure.

But Sampson’s story of his landlord’s distress touched him more sharply than his own. The young gentleman, who could feel another man’s hunger more readily than his own ruin, determined to raise a hasty supply for the poor clergyman. He gathered his trinkets—rings, a repeater, little ornaments bought in prosperous days—and carried them to a pawnbroker in St. Martin’s Lane. The shop had its own smell: old metal, stale cloth, oiled wood, and that sour breath of coins handled by many hands. Behind the counter, under dim light, the broker weighed gold with a grave face, as though virtue itself were being measured in drachms. Harry left his finery there as a man might leave pieces of himself.

Now this broker was a relative or partner of that very Mr. Sparks in Tavistock Street from whom Harry had once purchased—purchased, did we say? No: taken—the trinkets meant for his Oakhurst friends. And it happened that Mr. Sparks came to visit his brother-tradesman soon after Harry had pledged his goods. Recognising at once the little enamelled, diamond-handled repeater he had sold to the Fortunate Youth, the jeweller broke into such expressions regarding Harry as need not be repeated. A man acquainted with a pawnbroker is likely to have acquaintances among bailiffs; and bailiffs have followers who, at the bidding of the impartial Law, will lay a hand with equal readiness on a captain’s epaulet or a macaroni’s shoulder.

The very gentlemen who had seized upon Lady Maria at Tunbridge were now set upon her cousin in London. From Gumbo—garrulous, pleased with his own importance, and at that moment engaged in courting Mrs. Lambert’s maid in the adjoining mansion—they learned that his honour was at Sir Miles Warrington’s house in Hill Street. So while the black fellow dallied and whispered and listened for the maid’s laugh over the area railings, Mr. Costigan and his assistant waited, patient as cats, opposite the Warrington door.

Within, poor Harry was enjoying the delights of a virtuous family circle. Never had Uncle Miles been more cordial; never had Aunt Warrington been more gracious and affectionate. Flora looked unusually lovely; Dora more than ordinarily amiable. At parting, my lady gave Harry both her hands and called benedictions down from the ceiling upon him. Papa, in his most jovial manner, cried, “Hang it, nephew! when I was thy age I should have kissed two such fine girls as Do and Flo ere this—and my own flesh and blood too! Don’t tell me! I should, my Lady Warrington! Odds-fish! ’tis the boy blushes, and not the girls! I think—I suppose they are used to it. He, he!”

“Papa!” cried the virgins.
“Sir Miles!” said the august mother at the same instant.

“There, there!” quoth papa. “A kiss won’t do no harm, and won’t tell no tales: will it, nephew Harry?”

And so, during those brief phrases, the harmless little ceremony was performed: blushing cousin Harry touched the damask cheek of cousin Flora and cousin Dora—two soft, perfumed touches, like pressing one’s lips to rose-petals. Then he went downstairs with his uncle. Lady Warrington, when the door had closed behind him, addressed the girls with her usual upward gaze, as if virtue itself sat in the cornice. “What precious qualities your poor dear cousin has! What shrewdness mingled with his simplicity, and what a fine genteel manner—though upon mere worldly elegance I set little store. What a dreadful pity to think that such a vessel should ever be lost! We must rescue him, my loves. We must take him away from those wicked companions, and those horrible Castlewoods—not that I would speak ill of my neighbours. But I shall hope, I shall pray, that he may be rescued from his evil courses!”

And again she looked at the ceiling with a most determined piety, while the girls, less celestial, looked wistfully toward the door through which their interesting cousin had vanished.

Sir Miles went with Harry into the hall. “God bless you, my boy!” he cried, pressing Harry’s hand, repeating his benediction at the very threshold with a hearty shake, as if blessing were a thing that could be kneaded into the palm like dough.

As the door closed and the light from within fell full upon Harry’s face and figure, two men who had been standing on the opposite side of the way advanced quickly. One took a strip of paper from his pocket; the other laid a hand upon Harry’s shoulder. The touch was not paternal like Sir Miles’s; it was the dry grip of business. The man declared Harry his prisoner.

A hackney-coach waited, its wheels standing in the gutter, its horse shifting with a tired jingle of harness. And thus—so swiftly that the warmth of Hill Street’s parlour seemed a dream already—poor Harry Warrington, Madam Esmond’s son, was carried away to sleep in Chancery Lane.

To think of it: a Virginian prince’s back slapped by a ragged follower of the law; that young man, who had talked of broad rivers and forests under English candles, lying now in a spunging-house in Cursitor Street. The night there had its own cruel soundscape: not the pleasant clink of glasses at Sir Miles’s, but the oaths and jollifications of hardened inmates boozing in the next room, their laughter striking the walls like knuckles on a coffin-lid; the scrape of a bench; the groan of a bedstead; the murmur of the street outside, where wheels went on turning for those who were free.

Let us, if we please, imagine him wakeful on his dingy pillow, the stings of remorse as sharp as vinegar on a raw tongue; shame burning in him hotter than any punch-bowl; rage beating time with his heart to the coarse chorus next door. There is no need to pity him overmuch—so the world says—for surely none of us have ever been extravagant or foolish, nor ever paid the reckoning of regret.
