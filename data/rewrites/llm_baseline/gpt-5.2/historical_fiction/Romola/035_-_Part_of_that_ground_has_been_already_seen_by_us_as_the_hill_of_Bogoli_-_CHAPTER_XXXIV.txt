Messer Naldo came again sooner than anyone in the Via de’ Bardi would have guessed. It was the evening of the twenty-eighth of November—only eleven days since his last ascent—and the haste of his return, like a man drawn back by a string he pretends not to feel, belonged to an impulse that had begun to tug him towards the hill of San Giorgio.

When Tito first found this lodging for Tessa—more than a year and a half ago, after his coming back from Rome—he had flattered himself that he acted merely out of kindness. The unlucky mummery of words and garlands had made the foolish little creature believe him her husband; and then, because she was a thing so pretty, so trustful, so ready to cling like a child to any hand that would not strike it, he had not had the heart to tear away her illusion. Or so he argued when he needed to stand well with his own conscience.

Yet he was not in love with Tessa. His first ideal passion had been stirred by another woman altogether—Romola—whose very presence seemed to take hold of him as the scent of warm bread takes hold of one passing a baker’s door: not a mere sweetness to be tasted and forgotten, but a nourishment that lodges itself in the blood. With Romola there was a reverence in his desire; with Tessa there was a softness, an ease, a yielding indulgence that asked nothing of him beyond smiles and caresses. His nature was too fine, his intellect too alive, to be tempted into coarseness; but he had spun a web about himself and Tessa, and once the first sticky threads were laid, the rest seemed to weave itself in spite of him—like that creeping vine that covers a wall not because the wall invites it, but because it can.

He had taken pains to keep the secret safe. Monna Lisa, who seldom stirred beyond her threshold save for two shops and a yearly confession, knew nothing of his true name or dwelling. She knew only that he paid well—well enough to make her old age comfortable—and she troubled herself little further. There was mystery, certainly: a contadina girl and a signor who came rarely and with caution. But to Monna Lisa, deaf as a post unless one shouted straight into her ear, it might well be a lawful marriage. Tessa, too, had been frightened into silence: Tito had made her believe that if she spoke of the manner of their wedding she would lose him forever. The old woman’s deafness—ironically—had served as a second lock on the door of the secret; it had saved Tessa from letting her words run away, as they had with Baldassarre.

At first Tito’s visits were rare enough to suggest he was forever travelling. They were prompted chiefly by a wish to see that Tessa was housed, fed, and harmlessly happy. And if he found his coming pleasanter than the anticipation of it—if he felt again, each time, the charm of her loving ignorance and her bright animal trust—still he had not needed her. He wanted to preserve the simplicity that made her so soothing: to keep her a true contadina, not dress her up into something she was not, not widen her world until her very talk lost its fresh piquancy and became like watered wine. He would have been shocked to see her aping another rank; he would have mourned the flower plucked and stuck into a vase among wilting exotics. So he provided little luxuries that suited her childish appetite: nuts enough to crack with squirrel joy, a bit of better bread, a small store of oil that shone green-gold in the flask, and cheese that smelt of the goats and the hillside—honest, pungent, domestic. It kept her pleased; it kept her unchanged; and, by a coincidence convenient to his own purse, it kept his expenses within bounds that left him money for his public life.

For a while, too, his conscience had been quieted by the belief that his “kindness” had simply overmastered him. On the day of his betrothal to Romola, when Tessa had pursued him with her innocent claim, he had felt real annoyance; when he set out to see her again, he had meant sincerely to tell her the truth. The intention served later as proof in his own mind that he had not planned this double life out of mere self-indulgence. In the first fervour of his devotion to Romola, he needed such proofs. Strong feeling makes its own piety. Even a man sinking in self-deceit will still shrink from offending the thing he most reveres. Tito could not yet commit, without some inward wince, a secret offence against his wedded love.

But he was careful—more careful, perhaps, because the offence was secret. And if once or twice, before Bardo’s death, the knowledge that Tessa was up the hill had tempted him to escape a little weariness of the old scholar, it had still been only an easy turning aside, like stepping out of a draughty passage into a warm kitchen where one can hear the fire crack and smell garlic rubbed on toast. The true hunger for Tessa—hunger not for her body only, but for her credulous adoration—had come much later, and it had a mark upon it as definite as an illness that alters the senses.

It was the day he first saw Baldassarre, and bought the armour.

Crossing the bridge that night with the coat of mail in his hands, he had felt a shrinking from going straight to Romola. Romola trusted him; she too knew little of the actual world’s brutal cunning. But behind her frank eyes lay a judging nature, and her trust was not the trust of a pretty creature unable to think; it sprang from nobleness—an alarming touchstone. After the shock of that day, after the living grasp of the Past on the steps of the Duomo, Tito had wanted ease—repose from self-control—somewhere he could let his mind settle itself for the morrow without caring what he looked like in the present hour. And there was Tessa: safe as a pen of kids, unconstraining as a hearth where no one asks questions. So while Romola waited and listened for him, he turned his steps up the hill.

And no wonder those steps took the same course again, eleven days later, on this November evening, when he had just recoiled from Romola’s first outburst of scorn. He could not wish Tessa in Romola’s place. Romola belonged to the world of his ambition, the world where a man with faculty and purpose seeks to plant his name. But he wanted refuge from a standard that stung him; he wanted to lie down, for an hour, where no voice of moral censure could reach him. Tessa’s little soul—so easily comforted, so quickly pleased—offered that refuge.

It was scarcely more than eight o’clock when he climbed the stone steps to Tessa’s door. Usually she heard his entrance and ran to meet him, her bare feet light, her chatter beginning before she crossed the threshold. Not tonight. When he opened the door, he saw why.

A single dim light burned above the dying fire, making the room smell of old smoke, warm straw, and the faint sweet-sour of goat’s milk spilled and dried on the earthen floor. By the bed where the baby lay, Tessa knelt, her body fallen forward, her cheek sunk on the pillow’s edge. Her brown rosary, usually hung above the picture of the Madonna with its little golden palm-branches, lay in the loose grasp of her right hand. She had gone asleep over her beads.

Tito stepped lightly across the little room and sat down close to her. Perhaps the creak of the door had slid into her dream; for within two moments she opened her eyes without any start, and remained motionless, looking at him as if the mere sight of him filled all her senses and made movement needless. When he slipped his hand under her chin and stooped to kiss her, she said, in that soft breathy wonder of hers—

“I dreamed it, and then I said it was dreaming—and then I awoke, and it was true.”

“Little sinner,” Tito said, pinching her chin, “you have not said half your prayers. I will punish you by not looking at your baby; it is ugly.”

The words, though smiling, hurt her. A child’s pride lives fiercely in a mother’s heart; and Tessa bent anxiously over the baby, pouting with distress.

“Ah, it is not true! He is prettier than anything. You do not think he is ugly. You will look at him. He is even prettier than when you saw him before—only he’s asleep, and you can’t see his eyes or his tongue, and I can’t show you his hair—and it grows—isn’t that wonderful? Look at him! It’s true his face is very much all alike when he’s asleep, there is not so much to see as when he’s awake. If you kiss him very gently, he won’t wake: you want to kiss him, is it not true?”

He satisfied her with a butterfly kiss on the warm little cheek of the bundled infant—a small mummy swaddled tight, breathing with a faint milky sweetness in the air—and then he set his hand on her shoulder, turned her face to his, and said, as if to draw her back to himself,

“You like looking at the baby better than looking at your husband, you false one!”

She was still kneeling. Now she rested her hands on his knee and looked up at him like one of Fra Lippo Lippi’s round-cheeked angels, adoring and unquestioning.

“No,” she said, shaking her head. “I love you always best, only I want you to look at the bambino and love him; I used only to want you to love me.”

“And did you expect me to come again so soon?” Tito asked, letting her prattle on because her simple sounds, like the clink of plates and the hush of a kitchen at night, soothed him. The jar of Romola’s scorn still throbbed in his nerves; he wanted noise that asked nothing—words that did not judge.

“Ah, no,” Tessa said. “I have counted the days—to-day I began at my right thumb again—since you put on the beautiful chain-coat, that Messer San Michele gave you to take care of you on your journey. And you have got it on now.” She peeped through the opening in the breast of his tunic, as if the hidden links were a charm. “Perhaps it made you come back sooner.”

“Perhaps it did, Tessa,” he said. “But don’t mind the coat now. Tell me what has happened since I was here. Did you see the tents in the Prato, and the soldiers and horsemen when they passed the bridges—did you hear the drums and trumpets?”

“Yes,” she said, and her voice took a small tremble, as if the noise had lodged itself unpleasantly in her. “And I was rather frightened, because I thought the soldiers might come up here. And Monna Lisa was a little afraid too, for she said they might carry our kids off; she said it was their business to do mischief. But the Holy Madonna took care of us, for we never saw one of them up here.” Then she drew nearer and lowered her voice, as if even walls might listen. “But something has happened—only I hardly dare tell you, and that is what I was saying more Aves for.”

“What do you mean, Tessa?” Tito said, anxiety pricking through the softness. “Make haste and tell me.”

“Yes—but will you let me sit on your knee? because then I think I shall not be so frightened.”

He lifted her onto his knee and put his arm around her; yet his face grew grave. It seemed that even here, where the fire was low and the baby slept, something unpleasant would pursue him.

“At first I didn’t mean to tell you,” she whispered, as if softness of sound could soften the deed. “Because we thought the old man would be gone away before you came again, and it would be as if it had not been. But now he is there, and you are come, and I never did anything you told me not to do before. And I want to tell you, and then you will perhaps forgive me, for it is a long while before I go to confession.”

“Yes,” Tito said, forcing gentleness. “Tell me everything, my Tessa.” He began to hope it was some little folly—some small mischief that could be laughed away.

“Oh, you will be sorry for him. I’m afraid he cries about something when I don’t see him. But that was not the reason I went to him first; it was because I wanted to talk to him and show him my baby, and he was a stranger that lived nowhere, and I thought you wouldn’t care so much about my talking to him. And I think he is not a bad old man, and he wanted to come and sleep on the straw next to the goats, and I made Monna Lisa say, ‘Yes, he might,’ and he’s away all the day almost, but when he comes back I talk to him, and take him something to eat.”

“Some beggar, I suppose.” Tito’s throat tightened. “It was naughty of you, Tessa, and I am angry with Monna Lisa. I must have him sent away.”

“No,” Tessa said quickly, eager to defend. “I think he is not a beggar, for he wanted to pay Monna Lisa, only she asked him to do work for her instead. And he gets himself shaved, and his clothes are tidy: Monna Lisa says he is a decent man.” She hesitated, her fingers picking at Tito’s sleeve as if she kneaded a little fear into crumbs. “But sometimes I think he is not in his right mind. Lupo, at Peretola, was not in his right mind, and he looks a little like Lupo sometimes, as if he didn’t know where he was.”

“What sort of face has he?” Tito asked, and his heart began a strange beating, as if it heard footsteps in the dark. Baldassarre had haunted him until the very air seemed full of his father’s breath and curses. Already, before Tessa finished, the image had leapt up in his mind—Baldassarre on straw not many yards away. “Fetch your stool, my Tessa, and sit on it.”

“Shall you not forgive me?” she said timidly, sliding from his knee.

“Yes. I will not be angry—only sit down, and tell me what sort of old man this is.”

“I can’t think how to tell you,” she said, troubled, searching for words as if they were herbs she had never gathered before. “He is not like my stepfather Nofri, or anybody. His face is yellow, and he has deep marks in it; and his hair is white, but there is none on the top of his head: and his eyebrows are black, and he looks from under them at me, and says, ‘Poor thing!’ to me, as if he thought I was beaten as I used to be; and that seems as if he couldn’t be in his right mind, doesn’t it? And I asked him his name once, but he couldn’t tell it me: yet everybody has a name—is it not true? And he has a book now, and keeps looking at it ever so long, as if he were a Padre. But I think he is not saying prayers, for his lips never move.” Her voice broke into a small frightened plaint. “Ah, you are angry with me, or is it because you are sorry for the old man?”

Tito’s eyes were fixed on Tessa, but he had ceased to see her. He saw only what her words conjured: the yellow face, the black brows, the bald crown, the book, the lost name—like a man whose native tongue has been rubbed away until his own past cannot answer when it is called. That absence in his gaze frightened Tessa more than anger would have done. She slipped down to kneel at his side again. She dared not touch him. She dared not speak.

Her fear made her fingers find the familiar comfort of the rosary as hands find bread in darkness. She lifted the beads from the floor and began telling them again, her lips moving silently, her wide blue eyes shining with anxious tears.

Tito was unconscious of her, unconscious of his own stillness. He was in that wrapt state in which the mind grips pain and presses it closer, not to soothe it but to master it. A new possibility rose before him—so sudden, so tempting, it seemed like a providence that had walked in unannounced.

Here—within a few steps—might be the Past itself, not in the public glare of the Duomo steps with witnesses and danger, but hidden behind a rough outhouse door. Here might be a chance to retrieve that moment when living hands had clutched him and he had denied them. He might be face to face with his father without any eyes to watch; he might ask forgiveness and reconciliation. He had money now from the sale of the library—money enough to carry Baldassarre away quietly, out of Florence, down to Southern Italy where, under the likely French rule, Tito had already laid foundations for patronage. Romola need never know the whole truth: she had no sure means of tying that prisoner in the Duomo to Baldassarre, nor of learning what had passed on the steps, except from Baldassarre himself. And if Baldassarre forgave, he might consent to bury the offence.

With that easy spring into relief from present misery, there rose the darker possibility: Baldassarre might refuse to be appeased, fierce-hearted as ever. Yet if he refused, things would be as before—only now there would be no witness. Tito was not preparing repentance with a white sheet and taper, for men to gape at; he was preparing a repentance that would make life pleasant again and keep all the ugliness hidden. His soft-heartedness—his craving never to stand in harsh relations with any living creature—stirred strongly now that his father was brought near. He longed to have the world cushioned again with goodwill, and he longed for it more fiercely because Romola’s scorn had left him bruised. He could smile pleadingly on those he had injured and offer kindness; his quick mind could not taste, truly, the honey of such offers on the lips of the wronged.

The opportunity hemmed in calculation. He started up and stepped toward the door.

Tessa gave a little cry as her beads slipped and dropped. The sound—small as it was—snapped Tito out of his trance. He turned quickly.

“My Tessa, get me a lantern,” he said. Then, as she rose in frightened obedience, he added, “and don’t cry, little pigeon. I am not angry.”

They went down the stairs. Tessa was about to shout the need of a lantern into Monna Lisa’s ear, as one must do with the deaf, when Tito—having opened the outer door—stopped her.

“Stay, Tessa—no. I want no lantern. Go upstairs again, and keep quiet, and say nothing to Monna Lisa.”

Before the half-minute was spent he stood alone before the closed door of the outhouse, moonlight whitening the old, paintless wood and throwing every crack into sharp relief. The night was quiet enough that he could hear, faintly, the goats’ shifting and a small scuffle of straw; the hill held the city’s distant murmurs as a shell holds sea-sound, dulled and far away.

In this last decisive moment, a tremor ran over him—a sudden shrinking from the possible tiger-glance, the possible tiger-leap. Yet why should he, young, armoured, fear an old man without a weapon? It was only a moment’s hesitation. He laid his hand on the door. Was his father asleep? Was there nothing but this plank and latch between him and the voice that could bless him—or curse him beyond bearing?

Baldassarre was not asleep.

High in the wall of the hovel was a square opening where the moonbeams fell in a pale stream. If Tito could have looked through it, he would have seen his father seated on straw, holding something that glimmered like a small white star. Baldassarre was feeling the edge of his poniard, taking refuge in sensation from a hopeless blank of thought—a gulf between his passion and its aim.

He was in one of those wretched moments when consciousness returns only to show helplessness. While there had been light he had pored over the book that lay open beside him, trying to force memory awake. He had tried to recall the names of his jewels, the symbols engraved on them—signs once vivid as dialect words in one’s mouth, now slipping away into a darkness that made him rage. At other times a name or symbol had come back, as if startled from hiding; but tonight all were gone. The effort at inward seeing ended in paralysis. He was reduced to a mad sense that he was a solitary pulse of just rage in a world full of defiant baseness. He had unsheathed his dagger, and for a long while his mind narrowed to one image and one dreamed sensation: the sensation of plunging steel into a base heart—of making the body confess what the tongue would not.

Tito pulled at the door. It dragged against the ground, as old doors do, scraping with a harsh complaint. Baldassarre started up from his dreamlike state in vague amazement, not knowing where he was. He had not yet risen fully; he was still on one knee when the door swung wide and he saw, dark against the moonlight, with the rays striking one bright mass of curls and one rounded olive cheek, the living image of his reverie—no longer shadow, but real, close, as water at the lips after thirst.

No thought crossed that thirst. In one instant, before Tito could spring back, the old man—given preternatural strength by rage—leapt forward and the dagger flashed.

In the next instant the blade snapped in two. Under the parrying force of Tito’s arm, Baldassarre fell backward onto the straw, clutching the hilt with its broken bit. The pointed end lay shining on the ground at Tito’s feet.

Tito had felt a great heart-leap of terror, a staggering under the weight of the thrust. Now he felt triumph—deliverance and safety. The armour had proved itself; vengeance lay helpless. But the triumph did not breed a devil’s impulse. Rather, seeing his father close and unable to harm him made reconciliation seem easier. Fear was gone; what remained was a naked need—stronger now for being unmixed—to be free from the knowledge that he was hated.

They looked at each other a little while: Baldassarre motionless in despairing rage, Tito bending slightly forward, listening with his whole body for some sign that the past might be turned into ease. Then Tito spoke in soft tones, as they had sounded long ago before the last parting on the shores of Greece.

“Padre mio!”

He paused. No movement. No sound but the faint restless stir of straw settling, and the far-off murmur of Florence like a city breathing in its sleep.

“I came to ask your forgiveness!”

Again he paused, letting the words hang as if they might be balm in the air. But there was no sign of change. Baldassarre lay as he had fallen, leaning on one arm, trembling—not with gentleness, but with shock and thwarted fury.

“I was taken by surprise that morning,” Tito went on, choosing what seemed to him the clearest, strongest words. “I wish now to be a son to you again. I wish to make the rest of your life happy, that you may forget what you have suffered.”

He stopped. It was useless to say more until he knew Baldassarre understood—until he saw, at least, that the mind behind those dark brows had caught the sense. Perhaps the mind was too distempered. Perhaps rage and the fall had stunned it.

Presently Baldassarre moved. He threw away the broken dagger and slowly, trembling still, began to raise himself. Tito put out his hand to help him. And so quick are men’s souls, that in the very instant when Tito felt his atonement might be accepted, a darting thought shot through him of the irksome efforts acceptance would require.

Baldassarre clutched the offered hand, raised himself, and still clutched it as he stepped close—so close that their faces were scarcely a foot apart. Then he spoke in a deep, shaking voice, each clause like a blow struck with remembered strength.

“I saved you—I nurtured you—I loved you. You forsook me—you robbed me—you denied me. What can you give me? You have made the world bitterness to me; but there is one draught of sweetness left—that you shall know agony.”

He let Tito’s hand fall. Going backward a little, he rested an arm on a projecting stone in the wall, then sank again to a sitting posture on the straw. The outleap of fury that had driven the dagger-thrust had exhausted him; but the words remained, sharp as iron on the tongue, and Tito stood in the moonlit doorway with the snapped blade at his feet as if the night itself had spoken judgment.
