"At the court of the Tuileries," Mr. Sillerton Jackson remarked, his smile curling with the nostalgia of a man recounting a distant, golden age, "such indiscretions were treated with a charming openness."

They sat in the van der Luydens' imposing black walnut dining room on Madison Avenue, the evening following Newland Archer's visit to the Museum. The hosts had arrived in town from Skuytercliff with hurried urgency, fleeing the scandal of Beaufort's financial collapse. Society, it was whispered, required their presence to stabilize; as Mrs. Archer had noted, they "owed it to society" to appear at the Opera and keep their own doors open.

"It would never do, my dear Louisa," Mrs. van der Luyden admonished, her voice sharp with the anxiety of a gatekeeper, "to allow women like Mrs. Lemuel Struthers to believe they can step into Regina's shoes. It is precisely during times of turmoil that newcomers push in and take root. Remember the chicken-pox epidemic last winter? That is how Mrs. Struthers first gained a foothold, allowing married men to slip away to her house while their wives tended the nursery. You and dear Henry, Louisa, must stand firm in the breach as you always have."

Bound by duty, the couple had reluctantly descended from their country estate, unveiling their home and issuing invitations for two dinners and a grand reception. On this specific night, they had enlisted Mr. Jackson, Mrs. Archer, and Newland with his wife to attend the premiere of *Faust*. Under the van der Luyden roof, nothing occurred without ceremony. Though only four guests graced the table, dinner commenced punctually at seven, ensuring the proper sequence of courses before the gentlemen retired to their cigars.

Newland had not seen his wife since the previous evening. He had buried himself in a mountain of trivial office work, interrupted by an unexpected call from a senior partner, arriving home so late that May had driven ahead to the van der Luydens' and sent the carriage back. Now, across the Skuytercliff carnations and heavy plates, she appeared pale and languid, yet her eyes burned with an exaggerated animation.

The topic that had sparked Mr. Jackson's favorite anecdote had been introduced, perhaps intentionally, by the hostess. The aftermath of the Beaufort failure remained a fertile ground for moralizing. After thoroughly dissecting and condemning the affair, Mrs. van der Luyden turned her scrutinizing gaze upon May Archer.

"Is it true, dear? I was told your grandmother Mingott's carriage was seen standing at Mrs. Beaufort's door." Notably, she no longer used the offending lady's first name.

May's cheeks flushed, and Mrs. Archer interjected hastily, "If it was, I am convinced it was there without Mrs. Mingott's knowledge."

"Ah, you think—?" Mrs. van der Luyden paused, sighing, and glanced at her husband.

"I fear," Mr. van der Luyden said, "that Madame Olenska's kind heart led her into the imprudence of calling on Mrs. Beaufort."

"Or her taste for peculiar individuals," Mrs. Archer added dryly, her eyes fixed innocently on her son.

"I am sorry to think it of Madame Olenska," Mrs. van der Luyden murmured. Mrs. Archer replied softly, "Ah, my dear—and after you had hosted her twice at Skuytercliff!"

It was then that Mr. Jackson seized the opportunity to deliver his allusion.

"At the Tuileries," he repeated, sensing the expectant gaze of the company, "the standards were excessively lax in certain respects. If you asked where Morny's fortune originated—or who settled the debts of certain Court beauties..."

"I hope, dear Sillerton," Mrs. Archer said, "you are not suggesting we should adopt such standards?"

"I never suggest," Mr. Jackson returned, imperturbable. "But Madame Olenska's foreign upbringing may render her less particular—"

"Ah," the two elder ladies sighed in unison.

"Still, to keep her grandmother's carriage at a defaulter's door!" Mr. van der Luyden protested, likely recalling and resenting the hampers of carnations he had sent to the small house on Twenty-third Street.

"Of course, I have always said she views things differently," Mrs. Archer summarized.

A flush rose to May's forehead. She looked across the table at her husband and said precipitously, "I am sure Ellen meant it kindly."

"Imprudent people are often kind," Mrs. Archer observed, as if the observation offered little comfort. Mrs. van der Luyden murmured, "If only she had consulted someone—"

"Ah, that she never did!" Mrs. Archer rejoined.

At this juncture, Mr. van der Luyden glanced at his wife, who bowed her head slightly toward Mrs. Archer. The shimmering trains of the three ladies swept out of the room as the gentlemen settled into their cigars. Mr. van der Luyden provided short ones for opera nights, yet they were so exquisite that his guests lamented his inexorable punctuality.

After the first act, Newland detached himself from the party and moved to the rear of the club box. From there, peering over the shoulders of various Chivers, Mingott, and Rushworth, he watched the same scene he had observed two years prior on the night of his first meeting with Ellen Olenska. He had half-expected her to appear in old Mrs. Mingott's box, but it remained empty. He sat motionless, his eyes fixed upon it, until suddenly Madame Nilsson's pure soprano broke out into "_M'ama, non m'ama..._"

Newland turned to the stage, where, amidst the familiar backdrop of giant roses and pen-wiper pansies, the same large blonde victim succumbed to the same small brown seducer. His gaze drifted to the point of the horseshoe where May sat between two older ladies, just as she had on that former evening, positioned between Mrs. Lovell Mingott and her newly arrived "foreign" cousin. As on that night, she was clad entirely in white. Newland, who had not noticed her attire earlier, recognized the blue-white satin and old lace of her wedding dress.

In old New York, it was customary for brides to wear this costly garment during the first year or two of marriage. His mother, he knew, kept hers in tissue paper, hoping Janey might one day wear it, though poor Janey was reaching an age when pearl grey poplin and no bridesmaids would be deemed more appropriate.

It struck Newland that May, since their return from Europe, had seldom worn her bridal satin. The sight of her in it prompted him to compare her appearance with the young girl he had watched with such blissful anticipation two years earlier. Though May's outline was slightly heavier, as her goddesslike build had predicted, her athletic erectness of carriage and the girlish transparency of her expression remained unchanged. Save for the slight languor he had recently noticed, she was the exact image of the girl playing with the bouquet of lilies-of-the-valley on her betrothal evening. This fact seemed an additional appeal to his pity; such innocence was as moving as the trustful clasp of a child. Then he remembered the passionate generosity latent beneath her incurious calm. He recalled her look of understanding when he had urged that their engagement be announced at the Beaufort ball; he heard the voice in which she had said in the Mission garden, "I couldn't have my happiness made out of a wrong—a wrong to some one else." An uncontrollable longing seized him to tell her the truth, to throw himself upon her generosity, and ask for the freedom he had once refused.

Newland Archer was a quiet, self-controlled young man. Conformity to the discipline of a small society had become almost his second nature. It was deeply distasteful to him to do anything melodramatic or conspicuous, anything Mr. van der Luyden would have deprecated and the club box condemned as bad form. Yet, he had suddenly become unconscious of the box, of Mr. van der Luyden, of everything that had so long enclosed him in the warm shelter of habit. He walked along the semi-circular passage at the back of the house and opened the door of Mrs. van der Luyden's box as if it were a gate into the unknown.

"_M'ama!_" thrilled out the triumphant Marguerite, and the occupants of the box looked up in surprise at Archer's entrance. He had already broken one of the rules of his world, which forbade entering a box during a solo.

Slipping between Mr. van der Luyden and Sillerton Jackson, he leaned over his wife.

"I've got a beastly headache; don't tell anyone, but come home, won't you?" he whispered.

May gave him a glance of comprehension. He saw her whisper to his mother, who nodded sympathetically; then she murmured an excuse to Mrs. van der Luyden and rose from her seat just as Marguerite fell into Faust's arms. Archer, while helping her on with her opera cloak, noticed the exchange of a significant smile between the older ladies.

As they drove away, May laid her hand shyly on his. "I'm so sorry you don't feel well. I'm afraid they've been overworking you again at the office."

"No—it's not that: do you mind if I open the window?" he returned confusedly, lowering the pane on his side. He sat staring out into the street, feeling his wife beside him as a silent, watchful interrogation, keeping his eyes steadily fixed on the passing houses. At their door, she caught her skirt in the step of the carriage and fell against him.

"Did you hurt yourself?" he asked, steadying her with his arm.

"No; but my poor dress—see how I've torn it!" she exclaimed. She bent to gather up a mud-stained breadth and followed him up the steps into the hall. The servants had not expected them so early, and there was only a glimmer of gas on the upper landing.

Archer mounted the stairs, turned up the light, and lit the brackets on each side of the library mantelpiece. The curtains were drawn, and the warm, friendly aspect of the room smote him like that of a familiar face met during an unavowable errand. He noticed that his wife was very pale and asked if he should get her some brandy.

"Oh, no," she exclaimed with a momentary flush as she removed her cloak. "But hadn't you better go to bed at once?" she added as he opened a silver box on the table and took out a cigarette.

Archer threw down the cigarette and walked to his usual place by the fire.

"No; my head is not as bad as that." He paused. "And there's something I want to say; something important—that I must tell you at once."

She had dropped into an armchair and raised her head as he spoke. "Yes, dear?" she rejoined, so gently that he wondered at the lack of wonder with which she received this preamble.

"May—" he began, standing a few feet from her chair and looking over at her as if the slight distance between them were an unbridgeable abyss. The sound of his voice echoed uncannily through the homelike hush, and he repeated, "There is something I've got to tell you... about myself..."

She sat silent, without a movement or a tremor of her lashes. She was still extremely pale, but her face possessed a curious tranquility of expression that seemed drawn from some secret inner source.

Archer checked the conventional phrases of self-accusal crowding to his lips. He was determined to state the case baldly, without vain recrimination or excuse.

"Madame Olenska—" he said; but at the name, his wife raised her hand as if to silence him. As she did so, the gaslight struck the gold of her wedding ring.

"Oh, why should we talk about Ellen tonight?" she asked, with a slight pout of impatience.

"Because I ought to have spoken before."

Her face remained calm. "Is it really worth while, dear? I know I've been unfair to her at times—perhaps we all have. You've understood her, no doubt, better than we did; you've always been kind to her. But what does it matter, now it's all over?"

Archer looked at her blankly. Could it be possible that the sense of unreality in which he felt imprisoned had communicated itself to his wife?

"All over—what do you mean?" he asked in an indistinct stammer.

May still looked at him with transparent eyes. "Why—since she's going back to Europe so soon; since Granny approves and understands, and has arranged to make her independent of her husband—"

She broke off, and Archer, grasping the corner of the mantelpiece in one convulsed hand and steadying himself against it, made a vain effort to extend the same control to his reeling thoughts.

"I supposed," he heard his wife's even voice continue, "that you had been kept at the office this evening about the business arrangements. It was settled this morning, I believe." She lowered her eyes under his unseeing stare, and another fugitive flush passed over her face.

He understood that his own eyes must be unbearable. Turning away, he rested his elbows on the mantel-shelf and covered his face. Something drummed and clanged furiously in his ears; he could not tell if it were the blood in his veins or the tick of the clock on the mantel.

May sat without moving or speaking while the clock slowly measured out five minutes. A lump of coal fell forward in the grate, and hearing her rise to push it back, Archer at length turned and faced her.

"It's impossible," he exclaimed.

"Impossible—?"

"How do you know—what you've just told me?"

"I saw Ellen yesterday—I told you I'd seen her at Granny's."

"It wasn't then that she told you?"

"No; I had a note from her this afternoon.—Do you want to see it?"

He could not find his voice, and she went out of the room and came back almost immediately.
