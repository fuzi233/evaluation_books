Tito’s carefully laid, clever arrangements had been unpleasantly derailed by trivial mishaps no sharp, calculating man could ever factor into his plans. It was rare that he walked with Romola after dark, yet by some cruel quirk of fortune, that exact evening—when her presence was the most inconvenient possible—was the one he had chosen to stroll at her side. Life was such a tangled, unpredictable game that the sharpest skill of a wily man could be defeated at any turn by wind-tossed chances, as weightless and incalculable as thistledown drifting down from the sky.

He did not grieve over Spini’s failed ambush itself. What gnawed at him was the tight, awkward corner he was backed into: he had no way to warn Savonarola on one hand and Spini on the other without rousing dangerous suspicion. Suspicion from Florence’s populist party could shatter his carefully curated reputation and public standing for good. Suspicion from Dolfo Spini, meanwhile, would be as unpleasant and lethal as the hatred of a feral hound no chain could restrain. If he hurried straight to San Marco that very night, before the friars retired to their cells, his warning would land so soon after he had handed over the forged luring letters that no one could miss the obvious, damning connection. He could not warn Spini immediately, either, without admitting the full truth that Savonarola had foiled the plot, and he knew Spini would leap straight to the conclusion that Tito had double-crossed him on purpose. On the other hand, if he delayed his warning to Savonarola until sunrise, he would almost certainly lose the chance to warn Spini that the Frate had changed his plans, leaving the gang of Compagnacci thugs to return seething with unspent rage. This last was the risk he chose, telling himself he could charm Spini into accepting the story that the fiasco owed nothing to Tito, and everything to the Frate’s overblown caution.

Tito was furious. Even summoning his usual easy, winning smile would have felt like an unnatural, heavy effort. He decided he would not return home to face Romola that night at all.

Romola never undressed a single stitch all through that long dark stretch, sitting awake by the window while the rain beat harder and harder against the panes. The sound calmed her, in a grim way: the storm-wracked sky felt like a safeguard against men’s schemes, forcing their hidden plans to a standstill. Her mind was torn raw, not just by the final, sinking doubt of her husband’s character, but by torturing doubt of her own conduct. What other lies had he told her? What other secret harm did he still plan, hidden from her sight? Everyone who trusted Tito walked in mortal danger—she could no longer lie to herself to pretend otherwise. Was she being selfish, guided by nothing but her own pride, when she shrank from warning all the people of Florence against the man who lived under her own roof? “If her husband was a malefactor, her place was in the prison by his side”—that much she was willing to do. But was that the full sum of her wifely duty? Could she stand idle and let him inflict the injuries that would make him a criminal, when a word from her might stop him? She could not bring herself to pray. Her thoughts raced too fast, too sharply, to sink into the quiet, waiting passivity that lay at the heart of all her old devotions.

As the night wore on, her restlessness only mounted. Her overwrought imagination spun a dozen scenarios where Tito had tricked her after all, evaded her threat and lured Savonarola out to his doom. Towards dawn, the rain softened, then stopped entirely. A fresh breeze rose to tear the clouds to shreds, and clear bright sunlight spilled over every terracotta rooftop in the city. That sharp, unobscured morning light made her unease almost unbearable. She threw her wool mantle around her shoulders and ran up to the loggia, staring out over the rolling Tuscan landscape as if somewhere out there the land might give her a sign of what to do—when all she could see was a maze of rooftops hiding the very streets where Savonarola might be walking straight into treachery.

She thought of going to her old godfather, of persuading him without naming Tito or revealing too much to post extra guards at the gates and stop Fra Girolamo from leaving. But by then, the plan felt far too late. It was long past seven o’clock, and she had gleaned no specific guiding details of the plot from Tito the night before. There was only one thing left to do: go to San Marco herself.

She flew down the stairs, barely pausing to greet the sick neighbours lingering at her door, and walked at a near-run down the Via de’ Bardi towards the Ponte Vecchio. Taking the straight route through the heart of the city was the fastest way, and more than that: in the main public piazza, there was a faint, desperate chance she might run into Tito, and he would assure her the Frate was safe, so she would have no need to make the journey to San Marco.

When she reached the Palazzo Vecchio, she craned her neck to scan every shadow of its pillared courtyard, then swept her eyes across the whole crowded Piazza della Signoria. But the figure she knew so well—once painted in her heart by the soft glow of young love, now seared there like a brand by bitter, eating pain—was nowhere to be seen. She hurried on straight to the Piazza del Duomo, which was already thrumming with early morning life: worshippers mounting and descending the cathedral’s white marble steps, neighbours pausing to chat in doorways, market porters hefting their heavy bundles through the crowd. Between all the moving bodies, she caught a glimpse of her husband. He had come from San Marco, turned in at Nello the bookseller’s shop, and was leaning lazily against the doorpost. As she drew closer, she saw he was chatting with perfect unruffled ease, cap in one hand, shaking back his freshly combed dark hair. The casual unburdened sight of him, when she had spent a whole night wrung dry by fear for the lives he endangered, sent a convulsion of raw indignation through her. His utter lack of guilt, the hard proof he felt no scrap of remorse, made her dread spike higher. She spotted Cronaca and two other regular followers of Savonarola standing close beside him. A sharp thought flashed through her mind: I will force him to speak the truth before these men.

Her light, fast step carried her right up to him before he could move away, as Cronaca said aloud, “Why, here comes Madonna Romola.” Tito felt a faint jolt run through his whole frame as he turned and saw his wife face to face. Her face was haggard from a full night without sleep, but there was a sharp, burning light in her eyes far sharper than mere anxiety, as she said, “Is the Frate gone out beyond the city gates?”

“No,” Tito said. He was completely helpless, caught entirely off guard, and had to summon every scrap of his famous self-command to keep his face from showing anything more than mild unconcerned surprise.

“And you are certain he will not go?” she pressed.

“I am certain he will not go.”

“That is enough,” Romola said, and turned to climb the cathedral steps, slipping into the Duomo to find a quiet corner where she could recover from the storm of her agitation.

Tito watched her retreat up the marble steps, and he felt a surge of feeling closer to pure hatred than he had ever experienced in his life. Not far off, there stood not just loyal followers of Savonarola, but Ser Ceccone the notary, a man who at that very moment, like Tito himself, was a secret paid agent of the exiled Medici party. Ser Francesco di Ser Barone, better known to infamy under his nickname Ser Ceccone, was no scholar, no beauty, no success in life, and the furthest thing from a generous spirit. He was a traitor without a single charming or redeeming quality. Which meant, of course, that he had never liked Tito Melema one little bit.
