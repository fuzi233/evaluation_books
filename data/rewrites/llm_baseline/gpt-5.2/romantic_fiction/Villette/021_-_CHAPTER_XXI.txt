Three days—only three—had been allotted me away from the Rue Fossette; and I counted them as a housewife counts out eggs for a cake, knowing each one gone lessens what may be made. I watched the clock as if my gaze could salt and preserve the minutes; but time is no pantry-thing. It slid away, smooth and cold, while I stood over it like one powerless beside a boiling pot.

At breakfast Mrs. Bretton leaned towards me with that coaxing softness she could use like warm milk—simple, nourishing, and meant for comfort.

“Lucy will not leave us to-day,” she said. “She knows we can procure a second respite.”

“I would not ask for one if I might have it for a word,” I answered. “I long to have the good-by done. My trunk is packed and corded. I must go this morning—directly.”

Yet it appeared my going hung not on my will, but on Graham’s. He had said he would accompany me; and the day, as if spiteful, swallowed him up in engagements till dusk. When he came in at last, there followed a small combat—Mrs. Bretton and her son pressing me to remain one night more. Their kindness fretted me as oil frets flame. I could have cried outright with impatience to be free of the pain; I wanted the pang finished, as one on a scaffold may desire the descending stroke—not from love of it, but because suspense is a torment with teeth.

It was dark when Dr. John handed me down from the carriage at Madame Beck’s door. A lamp burned above the threshold; rain had been falling all day, a thin November drizzle that did not cleanse but only damped and darkened, and the lamplight lay on the wet pavement like a smear of pale butter on cold stone.

Just such a night it had been, not a year ago, when I first stopped at this very door—solitary and suppliant—waiting for its unclosing with a heart beating thick and loud, as if it were a pestle in a mortar. The paving-stones, too, seemed the same; I remembered their shapes with the odd exactness of one who had stared down to keep courage from running out at the eyes. On that former night I had briefly met him who stood with me now. I had never reminded him of that meeting; I had stored it away like a small, sweet conserve in a private cupboard of my mind, and there it was best kept.

Graham rang. The door opened instantly—for it was the hour when the half-boarders departed, and Rosine was alert as a cat at the pantry-latch.

“Don’t come in,” I said; but he stepped for a moment into the lighted vestibule. I had not wished him to see that the water stood in my eyes. He was too kind a nature to be shown sorrow when there was no remedy to offer. A physician, he must always wish to heal—even when healing was not in his power.

“Keep up your courage, Lucy,” he said. “Think of my mother and myself as true friends. We will not forget you.”

“Nor will I forget you, Dr. John.”

My trunk was brought in. We had shaken hands; he had turned to go. But he was not satisfied: the generous impulse in him would not be dismissed with so scant a leave-taking.

“Lucy,” he added, stepping after me, “shall you feel very solitary here?”

“At first I shall.”

“Well—my mother will soon call to see you; and meantime I’ll tell you what I’ll do. I’ll write—just any cheerful nonsense that comes into my head—shall I?”

His goodness struck me with that sudden sharp sweetness one tastes when thirsty and handed a draught one has not dared to ask for.

I smiled, shook my head. “Never think of it. Impose on yourself no such task. You write to me? You’ll not have time.”

“Oh, I will find or make time. Good-by!”

He was gone. The heavy door crashed to. The stroke had fallen. The pain was taken.

Allowing myself no leisure to think—swallowing tears as if they had been wine, burning and bitter—I went at once to Madame Beck’s sitting-room and paid the needful visit of ceremony. She received me with cordiality perfectly acted; she was even demonstrative, though brief. In ten minutes I was dismissed. From the salle-à-manger I proceeded to the refectory, where pupils and teachers were assembled for evening study; there, too, I had a welcome, and not, I think, quite hollow. That over, I was free to the dormitory.

“And will Graham really write?” I asked myself as I sank tired on the edge of the bed.

Then Reason—thin, cold, and patient as a knife—came stealing through the twilight of the long dim room and spoke in my ear.

“He may write once. So kind is his nature, it may stimulate him for once to make the effort. But it cannot be continued; it may not be repeated. Great were the folly that should build on such a promise—insane the credulity that should mistake a rain-pool, holding one draught in its hollow, for a perennial spring.”

I bowed my head. I sat thinking an hour longer. Reason kept whispering, and I felt her withered hand on my shoulder—felt her chill old lips touch my ear.

“If he should write, what then?” she muttered. “Do you meditate pleasure in replying? Brief be your answer. Hope no delight of heart—no indulgence of intellect. Grant no expansion to feeling—give holiday to no single faculty. Dally with no friendly exchange; foster no genial intercommunion…”

“But I have talked to Graham and you did not chide,” I pleaded, weakly, as if arguing for a morsel.

“No,” said she. “I needed not. Talk for you is good discipline. You converse imperfectly. While you speak, there can be no oblivion of inferiority—no encouragement to delusion. Pain, privation, penury stamp your language.”

“But where the bodily presence is weak and the speech contemptible,” I urged, “surely there cannot be error in making written language the medium of better utterance than faltering lips can achieve?”

“At your peril,” returned Reason, “you cherish that idea, or suffer its influence to animate any writing of yours.”

“But if I feel, may I never express?”

“Never!” declared Reason.

The word struck like iron on teeth. Never—never. She would not let me look up or smile or hope. She could not rest unless I were crushed and cowed, broken-in and broken-down; born only to work for bread, and to wait for death, and through all life to despond. Perhaps she was right; yet no wonder we are glad at times to defy her—to slip from under her rod and give a truant hour to Imagination, her soft bright foe—our sweet Help, our divine Hope. We shall and must break bounds at intervals, despite the revenge awaiting our return.

For Reason is vindictive as a devil. To me she was ever envenomed as a step-mother. If I obeyed her, it was chiefly in fear, not love. Long ago I should have died of her ill-usage—her stint, her chill, her barren board, her icy bed, her savage ceaseless blows—but for that kinder Power who holds my secret and sworn allegiance. Often has Reason turned me out by night, in mid-winter, on cold snow, flinging for sustenance the gnawed bone dogs had forsaken; sternly has she vowed her stores held nothing more for me, harshly denied my right to ask better things. Then looking up have I seen in the sky a head amidst circling stars, of which the midmost and brightest lent a ray sympathetic and attent. A spirit, softer and better than Human Reason, has descended with quiet flight to the waste—bringing all round her a sphere of air borrowed of eternal summer; bringing perfume of flowers which cannot fade—fragrance of trees whose fruit is life; bringing breezes pure from a world whose day needs no sun to lighten it. My hunger has this good angel appeased with food sweet and strange; tenderly has she assuaged insufferable fears—kindly given rest to deadly weariness—generously lent hope and impulse to paralysed despair. When I bend the knee to other than God, it shall be at her white and winged feet.

She remembered me that night; she saw me weep, and she came with comfort. “Sleep,” she said. “Sleep sweetly—I gild thy dreams!”

She kept her word and watched me through a night’s rest; but at dawn Reason relieved the guard. I awoke with a start. Rain dashed against the panes; the wind uttered a peevish cry at intervals, as though even the air were fretted by confinement. The night-lamp was dying on the black circular stand in the middle of the dormitory; day had already broken, thin and raw.

The pang of waking snatched me out of bed as if a giant hand had gripped my shoulder. How quickly I dressed in the cold! How deeply I drank of the ice-cold water in my carafe—my sharp cordial, to which, like other dram-drinkers, I had recourse when chagrin shook me.

Soon the bell rang its réveillée. Being dressed, I descended alone to the refectory. The stove was lit; the air there was warm. Through the rest of the house it was cold with the nipping severity of a continental winter. Though it was but the beginning of November, a north wind had brought a blight early over Europe. I remembered that when first I came those black stoves pleased me little; but now I began to associate with them a sense of comfort, and liked them as in England we like a fireside—dark, steady, and faithful in a draughty place.

I sat down before this comforter and fell into a deep argument with myself on life and its chances, on destiny and her decrees. My mind, calmer and stronger than the night before, made for itself imperious rules: forbidding, under deadly penalties, all weak looking back on happiness past; commanding patient journeying through the wilderness of the present; enjoining reliance on faith—a watching of the cloud and pillar that subdue while they guide, and awe while they illumine—hushing the impulse to fond idolatry, checking the longing outlook for a far-off promised land whose rivers perhaps are never to be reached save in dying dreams, whose sweet pastures are to be viewed only from some desolate summit like Nebo.

By degrees, a composite feeling—strength braided with pain—wound itself wirily round my heart, held it, restrained its throbbings, and made me fit for the day’s work. I lifted my head.

Beside the stove, piercing the wall, was a small window looking into the carré. As I looked up, a cap-tassel, a brow, two eyes filled a pane. Those eyes struck right against mine: they had been watching me. I had not, till that moment, known tears lay on my cheek; but I felt them then, exposed by another’s gaze.

This was a strange house, where no corner was sacred from intrusion—where not a tear could be shed, nor a thought pondered, but a spy was at hand to note and divine. And this new spy—out-door, male—what business brought him across the premises at this hour? No other professor would have dared to cross the carré before the class-bell rang.

M. Emanuel took no account of hours nor claims. Some book of reference in the first-class library he had occasion to consult; he had come to seek it. On his way he passed the refectory; he was a man who wore eyes before, behind, and on each side. He had seen me through the little window; he now opened the refectory door, and there he stood.

“Mademoiselle, vous êtes triste.”

“Monsieur, j’en ai bien le droit.”

“Vous êtes malade de cœur et d’humeur,” he pursued, half in French, half in English, with that cutting briskness of his. “You are at once mournful and mutinous. I see on your cheek two tears which I know are hot as two sparks, and salt as two crystals of the sea. While I speak you eye me strangely. Shall I tell you of what I am reminded while watching you?”

“Monsieur,” I said, striving to keep the tremor out of my voice, “I shall be called away to prayers shortly; my time for conversation is very scant at this hour—excuse—”

“I excuse everything,” he interrupted. “My mood is so meek, neither rebuff nor, perhaps, insult could ruffle it. You remind me, then, of a young she wild creature, new caught, untamed, viewing with a mixture of fire and fear the first entrance of the breaker-in.”

Unwarrantable accost. Rash and rude to a pupil; to a teacher inadmissible. He meant to provoke a warm reply; I had seen him vex the passionate to explosion before. In me his malice should find no gratification. I sat silent.

“You look,” he said, “like one who would snatch at a draught of sweet poison, and spurn wholesome bitters with disgust.”

“Indeed, I never liked bitters,” I answered at last, because the sting of his words forced speech. “Nor do I believe them wholesome. And to whatever is sweet—be it poison or food—you cannot, at least, deny its own delicious quality. Better, perhaps, to die quickly a pleasant death than drag on long a charmless life.”

“Yet,” he returned, “you should take your bitter dose duly and daily, if I had the power to administer it; and as to the well-beloved poison, I would, perhaps, break the very cup which held it.”

I turned my head sharply away—partly because his presence utterly displeased me, partly because I wished to shun questions; in my present mood, the effort of answering might overmaster self-command.

“Come,” said he, and his voice softened into an insinuation I liked no better than his sharpness, “tell me the truth—you grieve at being parted from friends—is it not so?”

I was silent. He came in, sat down on the bench about two yards from me, and persevered long—patiently for him—in attempts to draw me into talk. They were necessarily vain; I could not talk. At last I entreated him to let me alone. In uttering the request my voice faltered; my head sank on my arms and the table. I wept bitterly, though quietly. He sat a while longer. I did not look up nor speak till the closing door and his retreating step told me he was gone. Those tears relieved me as a storm relieves the air.

I had time to bathe my eyes before breakfast, and I suppose I appeared at that meal as serene as any other person—though not so jocund as the young lady who placed herself opposite me, fixed on me a pair of small eyes twinkling with glee, and stretched frankly across the table a white hand to be shaken.

Miss Fanshawe’s travels, gaieties, and flirtations agreed with her mightily. She had become quite plump; her cheeks were as round as apples, ripe and shining. I had last seen her in elegant evening attire; yet I do not know that she looked less charming now in her school-dress—a careless peignoir of dark blue, dimly and dingily plaided with black. That dusky wrapper gave her fairness a triumph; by contrast it made her skin look whiter, her bloom fresher, her golden tresses more like a bright heap of spun sugar.

“I am glad you are come back, Timon,” said she. Timon was one of her dozen names for me. “You don’t know how often I have wanted you in this dismal hole.”

“Oh, have you? Then, of course, if you wanted me, you have something for me to do—stockings to mend, perhaps.” I never gave Ginevra a minute’s or a farthing’s credit for disinterestedness.

“Crabbed and crusty as ever!” she cried. “I expected as much. But now—come, grand-mother—I hope you like coffee as much and pistolets as little as ever: are you disposed to barter?”

“Take your own way.”

Her way was always to make me convenient. She did not like the morning coffee—its school brewage not strong or sweet enough for her palate—but she had an excellent appetite for the pistolets, new-baked and very good, served in a fixed allowance to each. As my portion was more than I needed, I gave half to Ginevra; and she, in return, would sometimes give me part of her coffee. This morning I was glad of the draught. Hunger I had none; thirst parched me.

I do not know why I chose to give my bread rather to Ginevra than to another, nor why—when two had to share the convenience of one drinking-vessel, as sometimes happened on long walks into the country, when we halted for refreshment at a farm—I always contrived that she should be my convive, and rather liked to let her take the lion’s share, whether of white beer, sweet wine, or new milk. So it was; she knew it; and therefore, while we wrangled daily, we were never alienated.

After breakfast my custom was to withdraw to the first classe and sit and read—or think (oftenest the latter)—there alone, till the nine o’clock bell threw open all doors, admitted the rush of externes and demi-pensionnaires, and gave the signal for that bustle and business which, till five, had no relax.

I was seated there this morning when a tap came.

“Pardon, Mademoiselle,” said a pensionnaire, entering gently; she took from her desk some needed book or paper and withdrew on tip-toe, murmuring as she passed, “Que mademoiselle est appliquée!”

Appliquée indeed! The means of application were spread before me, but I did nothing; had done nothing; meant to do nothing. Thus does the world give us credit for merits we have not. Madame Beck deemed me a regular bas-bleu and warned me solemnly not to study too much, lest “the blood should all go to my head.” Everybody in the Rue Fossette held a superstition that “Meess Lucie” was learned—with the notable exception of M. Emanuel, who by means peculiar to himself had obtained a not inaccurate inkling of my real qualifications, and took quiet opportunities of chuckling in my ear over their scant measure.

For my part, I never troubled about the penury. I dearly liked to think my own thoughts. I had pleasure in reading a few books, but not many, preferring those on whose style or sentiment the writer’s individual nature was plainly stamped; I flagged inevitably over characterless volumes, however clever. I perceived well that, as far as my own mind was concerned, God had limited its powers and its action; thankful, I trust, for the gift bestowed, but unambitious of higher endowments.

The polite pupil was scarcely gone when, without tap or ceremony, in burst a second intruder. Had I been blind I should have known who it was. A constitutional reserve of manner had by this time worked, for me, a wholesome convenience in the house; rarely did I suffer now from rude intrusions. When first I came, a blunt German would clap my shoulder and ask me to run a race; or a riotous Labassecourienne seize my arm and drag me toward the playground with urgent proposals for the “Pas de Géant,” or for a romping hide-and-seek called “Un, deux, trois.” These attentions had ceased, without my being driven to the trouble of point-blank refusals. I had now no familiar demonstration to dread or endure save from one quarter; and as that was English I could bear it.

Ginevra Fanshawe made no scruple, at times, of catching me as I crossed the carré, whirling me round in a compulsory waltz, and enjoying the mental and physical discomfiture she induced. It was Ginevra who now broke in upon my supposed “learned leisure,” carrying a huge music-book under her arm.

“Go to your practising,” I said at once. “Away with you to the little salon!”

“Not till I have had a talk with you, chère amie. I know where you have been spending your vacation, and how you have commenced sacrificing to the graces, and enjoying life like any other belle. I saw you at the concert the other night, dressed—actually—like anybody else. Who is your tailleuse?”

“Tittle-tattle. My tailleuse? A fiddlestick. Sheer off, Ginevra; I don’t want your company.”

“But when I want yours so much, ange farouche, what does a little reluctance on your part signify? Dieu merci! we know how to manœuvre with our gifted compatriote—the learned ourse Britannique. And so, Ourson, you know Isidore?”

“I know John Bretton.”

“Oh, hush!” she cried, stopping her ears. “You crack my tympanums with your rude Anglicisms. But—how is our well-beloved John? Tell me about him. The poor man must be in a sad way. What did he say to my behaviour the other night? Wasn’t I cruel?”

“Do you think I noticed you?”

“It was a delightful evening. Oh, that divine de Hamal! And then—to watch the other sulking and dying in the distance; and the old lady—my future mamma-in-law! But I am afraid I and Lady Sara were a little rude in quizzing her.”

“Lady Sara never quizzed her at all,” I returned. “And for what you did—don’t make yourself uneasy. Mrs. Bretton will survive your sneer.”

“She may; old ladies are tough. But that poor son of hers! Do tell me what he said: I saw he was terribly cut up.”

“He said you looked as if at heart you were already Madame de Hamal.”

“Did he?” she cried, delight lighting her whole face. “He noticed that? How charming! I thought he would be mad with jealousy.”

“Ginevra, have you seriously done with Dr. Bretton? Do you want him to give you up?”

“Oh! you know he can’t do that. But wasn’t he mad?”

“Quite mad,” I assented; “as mad as a March hare.”

“Well—and how ever did you get him home?”

“How ever, indeed! Have you no pity on his poor mother and me? Fancy us holding him tight down in the carriage, and he raving between us, fit to drive everybody delirious. The very coachman went wrong somehow, and we lost our way.”

“You don’t say so? You are laughing at me. Now, Lucy Snowe—”

“I assure you it is fact—and fact, also, that Dr. Bretton would not stay in the carriage: he broke from us, and would ride outside.”

“And afterwards?”

“Afterwards—when he did reach home—the scene transcends description.”

“Oh, but describe it—you know it is such fun!”

“Fun for you, Miss Fanshawe? but”—and I made my face stern with a gravity she could not imitate—“you know the proverb: ‘What is sport to one may be death to another.’”

“Go on—there’s a darling Timon.”

“Conscientiously, I cannot, unless you assure me you have some heart.”

“I have—such an immensity, you don’t know!”

“Good. In that case you will be able to conceive Dr. Graham Bretton rejecting his supper in the first instance—the chicken, the sweetbread prepared for his refreshment, left on the table untouched. Then—but it is of no use dwelling on harrowing details. Suffice it to say that never, in the most stormy fits and moments of his infancy, had his mother such work to tuck the sheets about him as she had that night.”

“He wouldn’t lie still?”

“He wouldn’t lie still: there it was. The sheets might be tucked in, but the thing was to keep them tucked in.”
