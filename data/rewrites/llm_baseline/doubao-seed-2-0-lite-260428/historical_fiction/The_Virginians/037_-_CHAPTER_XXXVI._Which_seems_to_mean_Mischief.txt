Though Lady Maria had clearly come off the losing end of their sharp, pitched battle the day prior, the Baroness Bernstein greeted her niece the next morning without a flicker of anger or lingering rancour.

“Of course, my dear Lady Maria,” she purred, sipping her chocolate, “you cannot possibly imagine that I, as Harry Warrington’s near kin, would ever be pleased to see him tie himself to a woman as old as his own mother, with not a penny of her own fortune to bring to the match. That said, if the boy is fool enough to do such a ridiculous thing, it is no business of mine. I doubt I would ever have been taken seriously, anyway, on that foolish offer of five thousand pounds I flung out in the heat of our argument last night. So this little entanglement of yours was settled back at Castlewood, was it? Had I known how far things had gone, I would have spared myself all that unnecessary opposition. What use is railing over a broken pitcher, after all?”

“Madam!” Maria cut in, cheeks flushing.

“Pardon me, I mean no slight to your honour and character, which I am sure are perfectly unassailable. Harry says so, you say so—what more is there for anyone to ask?”

“You have spoken with Mr. Warrington then, madam?” Maria pressed.

“And he confessed he made you a promise at Castlewood, in writing, no less?”

“Most certainly he did,” Lady Maria replied, lifting her chin.

The older lady did not so much as flinch. “Ah. I will own I at first misread the tone of your letters to him. They hint at scandals touching other members of the family—”

“Who have spoken wickedly against me, and worked night and day to turn my dear Mr. Warrington against me!” Maria snapped. “Of course I wrote against them to defend my own name.”

“Naturally. You would be horrified, I am sure, to think any stray wretch might get hold of those family secrets and spread them as public gossip, hence all your disquiet earlier, I take it?”

“Exactly so,” Lady Maria agreed. “I hide nothing from Mr. Warrington, of course, but that is a very different matter from letting all the world know the petty quarrels of a noble house.”

“Upon my word, Maria, I admire you. I have done you an injustice these past twenty years, no less.”

“I am only glad you choose to do me justice at last,” the niece replied, cool as marble. “When I saw you last night opening the ball with my nephew, do you know what I thought of, dear?” Lady Maria added haughtily. “I have no notion what the Baroness de Bernstein chooses to think of.”

“I remembered you danced that very same tune with your dancing master, back in Kensington, years ago!” the Baroness laughed.

“It is a vile, infamous calumny!” Maria cried.

“Which got that poor dancing master cudgelled half to death for no fault of his own, too!”

“It is cruel and unkind of you to drag that up,” Maria flared, “and I shall not stay under the same roof as anyone who utters such slander against me. I mean to leave at once.”

“Leave? I can hardly blame you. Tunbridge will grow very unpleasant for you indeed, if those letters of yours ever turn up.”

“There is not a single word written against you in them, madam—you may set your mind entirely at ease on that count.”

“So Harry said, and he did you perfect justice, I must own. Well, my dear, we have grown tired of each other’s company, and we will both fare far better apart for a while.”

“That is precisely my own opinion,” Lady Maria said, dropping a frigid curtsey.

“Mr. Sampson may escort you back to Castlewood, if you like. You and your maid may take a postchaise for the journey.”

“We will take a postchaise, and Mr. Sampson may escort me,” Maria echoed. “You see, madam, I play the dutiful niece still.”

“Tell me, my dear— I have a very strong suspicion that Sampson has those letters in his keeping,” the Baroness said, quite openly.

“I will confess the very same thought crossed my own mind not long ago,” Maria admitted.

“And you mean to ride home in that chaise, and coax them right out of him, you little Delilah!” the Baroness cackled. “Well, they are no use to me, and I wish you all luck getting them back. You want to leave as soon as possible, I assume? We are women of the world, you and I. We only call each other names when we are in a temper. We don’t want each other’s company, so we part on perfectly good terms. Shall we go along to Lady Yarmouth’s tonight? It’s her at-home night. Nothing drives away the sour thoughts after one of those little nervous fainting fits you get, like cards and company, far better than any physician’s draught.”

Lady Maria agreed readily enough, and dressed for the party first, waiting in the drawing-room for her aunt to come down. The Baroness, descending the stairs, noticed Maria’s bedchamber door stood ajar. “She has those letters tucked in her stays, no doubt,” she thought to herself. The pair set off for their respective chairs to the party, displaying to each other all that charming, polished cordiality that only two women can muster even at the height of a bitter, hidden quarrel.

When they returned home late that night, the Baroness’s own maid, Mrs. Brett, answered Lady Maria’s bell, when the lady called for her lady’s maid to help her undress. Mrs. Brett hung her head in shame as she explained that Betty, Maria’s maid, was in no fit state to appear before her ladyship. Betty had gone off carousing with Harry’s black servant Gumbo, alongside Lord Bamborough’s valet and half a dozen other household staff from the neighbourhood, and the rum punch they had drunk had proven far too strong for the girl. Mrs. Brett offered to undress Lady Maria herself, but the lady waved her off, saying she could manage perfectly well on her own.

“Letters are in her stays for certain,” the Baroness thought to herself, as the two bade each other a sweet, amicable good-night on the staircase.

The next morning, a contrite Betty came to wait on her mistress, blue eyes swollen, to take her scolding for the night before. She admitted she could not resist the fine rum punch Gumbo brewed, which everyone said was the most delicate in all three counties. She meekly finished dressing her lady, then withdrew to her own room.

Betty was one of the Castlewood housemaids who had long been charmed by the good-humoured Gumbo, which had stirred no small jealousy in the breast of the Baroness’s head man, Mr. Case, who had long set his sights on the pretty blue-eyed lass. He had sworn more than once he would break Gumbo’s bones if he kept flirting with Betty, and the peaceable Gumbo had been doing his best to avoid him ever since.

But the night of the rum punch, Case had been the very soul of good nature. He had found Gumbo and Betty whispering in the cool doorway air, and instead of flying into a rage, he insisted that they all make a bowl of Gumbo’s famous punch in Betty’s room, drinking glass after glass until poor Betty was thoroughly intoxicated, and Case himself was so ill the next day he stayed in his bed the whole morning, not appearing to wait on his ladies until supper time.

“Case, I could have sworn I saw you galloping along the London road on horseback first thing this morning!” Harry said, as he sat supping with his aunt and the rest of the party that night.

“Me, sir? Bless your soul, I was in bed all day, sure my head was going to split clean in two!” Case protested. “I ate a little bread at six, drank a whole flagon of small beer, and I’m almost myself again now. But I’ll never touch Gumbo’s punch again, saving your honour’s presence.”

As they sat after supper, the Baroness was all sweetness to Lady Maria. She prescribed all sorts of fortifying cordials for the lady’s frequent fainting fits, and suggested Harry ride up to London on her behalf, to carry a note to her lawyer Mr. Draper and see that he added Harry as a main beneficiary to her will. He was also to carry her heavy trinket-box to the banker’s strong room for safekeeping, as there had been a rash of highwayman robberies on the roads of late.

Lady Maria paled at the news—she had been counting on Harry to escort her back to Castlewood. But the Baroness waved her off. “The boy has been tied to our apron strings long enough! He’ll ride up tomorrow, do the errand, and be back to Tunbridge by Saturday, well in time to see you off.”

Harry could not have been more pleased. The ill-considered promise he had made Maria at Castlewood, to marry a woman old enough to be his mother, had hung around his neck like a heavy millstone for weeks, and the thought of a rattling jaunt to London sounded like the sweetest relief in the world. He set out at dawn the next day, his pistols primed, Gumbo riding beside him on the box of the postchaise. No highwaymen stopped them on Blackheath. He caught his first glimpse of the gleaming domes of Greenwich through the trees, the Thames crowded thick with merchant ships, the ancient stone walls of the Tower of London. He pointed them out to Gumbo, giddy as a schoolboy, remembering how he and his dead brother George had read all about the Tower in their storybooks back in Virginia, and played at being prisoners in the Tower’s dungeons when they were small boys. They rattled over London Bridge, then through Southwark, and pulled up to Mr. Draper’s chambers in Middle Temple Lane.

The old lawyer, whose family had served the Esmonds of Castlewood for generations, took the trinket box and the Baroness’s letter, and locked them away in his strong closet. As they reached for their hats to go out, a young clerk stuck his head into the hall. “Beg pardon sir, those papers the Baroness asked for were given to Mr. Case two days ago!”

“Mind your own business, Mr. Brown,” Draper snapped at him, then steered Harry down the dark Temple stairs. Harry smiled to himself. So Case was the man he had seen galloping down the London road a couple days prior. Mischief was afoot, that was plain—but it was no business of his to pry.

He tipped his hat to the mouldering rebel heads nailed to Temple Bar as they walked past, startling the staid lawyer. “They were gentlemen who died for their rightful king,” Harry said, simply. “My brother George and I always swore we’d pay our respects when we saw them.” The rest of the day was pure delight to the young Virginian: he wandered the Strand, visited Leicester Field where his grandfather had fought a fatal duel decades before, caught a glimpse of the rough, shabby great author Mr. Johnson strolling in the gardens, bought a fine matched pair of bay geldings at the horse market in Hatton Garden, tramped through Westminster Abbey and the Tower and Kensington Palace, and ate and drank like a prince at his inn in Covent Garden.

The next morning, he read the Virginia dispatches in the newspaper, and his throat tightened. The leading gentlemen of the colony had formed a volunteer regiment to march against the French and their native allies, and he ached to be back in his home country, riding with them, instead of tangled up in all the silly, scheming mischief brewing back in Tunbridge Wells. The church bells of St. Paul’s, Covent Garden, began to ring for morning prayers, and Harry smiled to himself. He had not forgotten that the oily chaplain Mr. Sampson was scheduled to preach his great sermon for Lady Yarmouth that day. He had a shrewd suspicion that none of the tangled schemes the ladies back home were weaving would end quite as any of them hoped. The mischief was only just getting started.
