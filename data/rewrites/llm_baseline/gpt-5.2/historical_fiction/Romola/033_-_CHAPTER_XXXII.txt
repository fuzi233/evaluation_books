The next day, Romola—like every Florentine who had listened for eleven days to the foreign tramp of boots and the scrape of alien harness—lived in a fever of expectancy. The French were going at last. The thought ran through her like warm wine after a chill fast: not merely relief for the city, but a dim, half-superstitious hope that the dread which had thickened round Tito since their coming might loosen and fall away with the last file of soldiers.

All day the streets had been full of sound—the high metallic clatter where lance-shafts struck stone, the hoarse calls of men driving carts, the restless stir of hooves, and above it the impatient undertone of the Arno, muffled under bridges. Romola, wrapped close in her mantle, went up beneath the loggia at the top of the house and waited where she could command the bridges and the road towards the Porta San Piero. She watched for the passing gleam of steel and the ripple of banners, and as each division moved on, the noise altered like a change of wind in a pinewood: nearer, harsher; then thinning, breaking, and at last leaving behind that peculiar hollow after-silence which makes one aware of every domestic sound—the far clink of a pail, a child’s cry, the soft scuffle of sandals on stair.

When the gates were shut she went back again, as if she must take the final vibration into her body. Then the bells burst out—great peals that rolled through dusk like waves of bronze. She let them buffet her head and heart; she wanted to be shaken, deafened, made other than herself for a moment. In those strokes she fancied deliverance, as if the city were being rung clean.

It was almost dark when she went down to the library. The room had that wide, solemn gloom which seemed to drink in light rather than reflect it; her single oil-lamp made a little yellow island at the far end of the table, and the fire, neglected, had sunk to a dull red, smelling faintly of cold ash. She had forced herself to come there with a resolution—she would not waste another day in trembling fancies; she would copy the catalogue as she had promised, line after line, name after name, each title like a grain stored against future hunger.

Tito had gone out early; she did not look for him yet. Before he came she meant to leave this chill room and take her place in the pretty saloon with the dancing nymphs and birds—she had made that change every evening since Tito had spoken of the library as gloomy, as if she could warm his temper by warm walls.

She had scarcely dipped her pen twice when she heard the step she knew, quick and light upon the stones. Tito came in.

Her first impulse was tenderness at once: how lonely he must feel entering this cavern of shadow with only one thin lamp and the fire dying. She rose and almost ran to him.

“Tito, dearest—I did not know you would come so soon,” she said, nervously, lifting her white arms to unwind his becchetto.

He smiled—one of those bright, well-practised smiles that had often been like sunshine for her—and clasped her, but with a playful backward motion of the head.

“I am not welcome then?”

“Tito!” The reproach in her voice was the old one—loving, half-laughing—and he answered it with a fond kiss, stroking her hair as he used to do, lingering as if he had no concern to rid himself of cloak or mantle. It was so like the manner she had missed that her heart leaped.

It will come back, she said to herself, the old happiness will perhaps come back. He is like himself again.

And indeed he was taking pains to be like himself, while beneath the smoothness his heart beat hard with a fear that no armour could wholly keep off.

“If I had expected you,” Romola said, helping at last to loosen his wrappings, “I would have had a little festival prepared for this joyful ringing of the bells. I did not mean to be here in the library when you came home.”

“Never mind, sweet,” he answered carelessly. “Do not think about the fire. Come—come and sit down.”

There was a low stool against his chair, and she took it by habit, as once she had sat at her father’s knee. She rested her arm upon Tito’s knee and looked up, waiting for him to speak, noticing—because she could never help noticing—that he still had not once looked towards the portrait propped near Bardo’s chair.

“I have been enjoying the clang of the bells for the first time, Tito,” she began, trying to keep her voice light. “I liked being shaken and deafened by them: I fancied I was something like a Bacchante, possessed by a divine rage. Are not the people looking very joyful to-night?”

“Joyful after a sour and pious fashion,” Tito said, shrugging. “But in truth, those who are left behind have little cause for joy. The most reasonable ground of gladness would be to have got out of Florence.”

His tone had been careless, almost conversational, and yet he made his face grave enough to draw her into anxious question.

“Why, Tito? Are there fresh troubles?”

“No need of fresh ones, my Romola. There are three strong parties in the city, all ready to fly at each other’s throats. If the Frate’s party is strong enough to frighten the other two into silence—as seems most likely—life will be as pleasant as a funeral. They have their plan of a Great Council simmering already; and if they get it, the man who sings sacred lauds the loudest will be the most eligible for office. Besides, the city will be drained—first by this great subsidy to the French king, and then by the war to get back Pisa. The prospect would be dismal enough without the rule of fanatics. Florence will be delightful for worthies who amuse themselves by going into crypts and lashing their own backs; but for everything else, the exiles have the best of it.”

He paused only long enough to let his conclusion seem the natural end of his list.

“For my own part, I have been thinking seriously that we should be wise to quit Florence, my Romola.”

She started as if the bells had struck her in a new place. Cold ran up her arms; the pressure in her throat made speech difficult. Of course he knew what such words touched.

“Tito—how could we leave Florence? Surely you do not think I could leave it—not yet—not for a long while.”

“That,” he said, with the same easy gentleness, “is all a fabric of your own imagination, my sweet one. Your secluded life has made you lay false stress on a few things. You remember I used to tell you, before we were married, that I wished we were somewhere else. If you had seen more places and more people you would know what I mean. There is something in the Florentines that reminds me of their cutting spring winds—always keen, always prying. I like people who take life less eagerly. It would be good for my Romola to see a new life. I should like to dip her a little in the soft waters of forgetfulness.”

He kissed her brow, laid his hand on her hair with that caress which had once been enough to dissolve every resistance in her. But now she felt it no more than if a cool mouth had touched plaster. Her mind was filling with the sick sense of distance—of some widening gulf between their meanings.

“Tito,” she said, gathering her breath, “it is not because I suppose Florence is the pleasantest place in the world that I desire not to quit it. It is because I—because we have to see my father’s wish fulfilled. My godfather is old—seventy-one. We could not leave it to him.”

“It is precisely those superstitions,” Tito said, still gently, “that hang about your mind like bedimming clouds, that make one reason the more why I would have us two hundred leagues from Florence. I am obliged to take care of you in opposition to your own will sometimes. If those dear eyes—so tender—see falsely, I must see for them, and save my wife from wasting her life in disappointing herself by impracticable dreams.”

Romola sat still. She heard in his words, as one hears a knife on a whetstone, the direction they must go: he wanted to be rid of the task, the tie, the vow made at a death-bed; and he expected her to let his cleverness decide the shape of duty. Inside her, something set itself hard as a kneaded dough that has taken its salt: she would resist—she would bear pain rather than yield on this trust. But the first pain was the confession that they were divided, that his will moved where hers could not follow.

Her silence pleased him. Like many men who have learnt to talk fluently, he overvalued the power of his arguments and did not imagine how they looked when they touched another’s deepest tenderness. He continued in a remonstrating tone, as if soothing a child from needless fear.

“You know, dearest—your own clear judgment always showed you—that the notion of isolating a collection of books and antiquities, and attaching a single name to them for ever, has no valid good for its object; and, more, it may be defeated in a thousand ways. See what has become of the Medici collections! For my part, I consider it blameworthy to entertain petty views of appropriation. Why should Florence possess the benefits of learned research and taste more than any other city? I understand your feeling about the wishes of the dead; but wisdom must put limits to such sentiments, else lives may be wasted in futile devotion—like praising deaf gods for ever. You gave your life to your father while he lived; why demand more of yourself?”

“Because it was a trust,” Romola said, low but distinct. “He trusted me. He trusted you, Tito. I did not expect you to feel as I do—but I did expect you to feel that.”

“Yes, dearest—of course,” he replied with smooth patience. “If your father’s real welfare or happiness were concerned. But there is no question of that now. If we believed in purgatory, I should be as anxious as you to have masses said; and if I believed it could now pain your father to see his library preserved and used in a rather different way from what he had set his mind on, I should share your strictness. But a little philosophy should teach us to rid ourselves of those air-woven fetters mortals hang round themselves—spending their lives miserable under mere imagination of weight. Your mind, which seizes ideas so readily, can discriminate between substantial good and these brain-wrought fantasies. Ask yourself—what possible good can these books and antiquities do, stowed under your father’s name in Florence, more than if they were divided, or carried elsewhere? Is not dispersion into hands that know how to value them one means of extending usefulness? This rivalry of Italian cities is petty. The loss of Constantinople was the gain of the whole civilised world.”

As he spoke, Romola felt a contempt rising within her like bile—contempt that did not quench love, but made love bruised and despairing. His large phrases had no warmth for what lay nearest; his wide sympathies seemed like painted fruit—fair to look at, without scent, without juice. She drew her arm from his knee and clasped her hands before her, sitting cold and motionless as water that has locked itself under frost.

“You talk of substantial good, Tito,” she said, and her voice began to rise in spite of her restraint. “Are faithfulness and love and sweet grateful memories no good? Is it no good that we should keep our silent promises—promises on which others build because they believe in our truth? Is it no good that a just life should be justly honoured? Or is it good that we should harden our hearts against the wants and hopes of those who have depended on us? What good can belong to men who have such souls? To talk cleverly, perhaps, and find soft couches for themselves, and live and die with their base selves as their best companions.”

There was a ring of scorn in the last words, a sharpness as of metal struck. She paused, but other words trembled at her lips, and he let them come, watching.

“I know of no good for cities or the world if they are to be made up of such beings. But I am not thinking of other Italian cities and the whole civilised world—I am thinking of my father, and my love and sorrow for him, and his just claims on us. I would give up anything else, Tito—I would leave Florence—what else did I live for but for him and you? But I will not give up that duty. What have I to do with your arguments? It was a yearning of his heart, and therefore it is a yearning of mine.”

Her voice had steadied into firmness; she believed she had met the hardest part—she had spoken the truth and would stand by it.

But Tito had seen, with a quick inward calculation, that persuasion would not bend her. He must end the struggle by making resistance useless. If he did not speak now, the disclosure would come in another form to-morrow. Necessity braced him. He kept his tone quiet.

“I am sorry to hear you speak in that spirit of blind persistence, my Romola,” he said. “Because it obliges me to give you pain. But I partly foresaw your opposition; and as a prompt decision was necessary, I avoided that obstacle, and decided without consulting you. The care of a husband for his wife’s interest compels him to separate action sometimes—even when he has such a wife as you, my Romola.”

She turned her eyes on him, breathless.

“I mean,” he said, answering the look, “that I have arranged for the transfer—both of the books and of the antiquities—where they will find the highest use and value. The books have been bought for the Duke of Milan. The marbles and bronzes and the rest are going to France. Both will be protected by the stability of a great Power instead of remaining in a city exposed to ruin.”

Before the last words were out, Romola had sprung up. She stood over him, hands tightened and hanging rigid, and in her face there was, for the first time in her life, a fierceness that made her beauty terrible.

“You have sold them?” she said, as if her ears were betraying her.

“I have,” Tito answered, and his boldness shrank a little before her look.

“You are a treacherous man!” The words grated as they left her throat.

For a moment she was silent. Tito sat still; he knew that now no ingenuity could sweeten what had been done. Then she turned away abruptly, her breath coming fast.

“It may be hindered—I am going to my godfather.”

In an instant Tito started up, went to the door, locked it, and put the key in his scarsella. Something hard—an authority that had lain latent in him—came to the surface. He was not angry; he only felt the scene as something exceedingly unpleasant, something after which he would wish to be away. But he must first reduce her to passiveness.

“Try to calm yourself a little, Romola,” he said, taking up the easiest attitude he could manage, leaning against a pedestal under the bust of a grim old Roman. His face had paled; his heart beat with a moral dread that made his cleverness feverishly alert. He had shut her scorn in—but had shut himself in with it.

Romola had halted as she saw the key vanish into his pouch. Her eyes flashed; her whole frame seemed possessed by a force that sought outlet in action. The pain of disappointed love, which a little before had been crushing her, was annihilated now in pure indignation. She could not care, in this moment, that the man she despised was her husband. The pride and fierceness of her father’s blood woke fully at last.

“Try at least to understand the fact,” Tito went on. “Do not seek to take futile steps that may be fatal. It is of no use for you to go to your godfather. Messer Bernardo cannot reverse what I have done. Only sit down. You would hardly wish—if you were quite yourself—to make known to any third person what passes between us in private.”

He saw that he had touched the fibre of her delicacy. But she did not sit; she was scarcely conscious of her body.

“Why can it not be reversed?” she said after a pause. “Nothing is moved yet.”

“Simply because the sale has been concluded by written agreement. The purchasers have left Florence, and I hold the bonds for the purchase-money.”

“If my father had suspected you of being a faithless man,” Romola said, and her scorn seemed to insist on darting out before any other thought, “he would have placed the library safely out of your power. But death overtook him too soon; and when you were sure his ear was deaf and his hand stiff, you robbed him.”

She drew breath, and the next words came with gathered passion.

“Have you robbed somebody else—who is not dead? Is that the reason you wear armour?”

It was as if she had struck him. At first Tito felt horribly cowed; the disgrace he dreaded seemed to loom larger. Then resistance rose in him—such dislike as he had, such instinct to master what threatened him. Her, at least, his quick mind told him, he could master.

“It is useless,” he said coolly, “to answer the words of madness. Your peculiar feeling about your father has made you mad at this moment. Any rational person—looking at the case from due distance—will see I have taken the wisest course. Apart from the influence of your exaggerated feelings on him, I am convinced Messer Bernardo would be of that opinion.”

“He would not!” Romola cried. “He lives in the hope of seeing my father’s wish exactly fulfilled. We spoke of it together only yesterday. He will help me yet. Who are these men to whom you have sold my father’s property?”

“There is no reason why you should not be told, except that it signifies little. The Count di San Severino and the Seneschal de Beaucaire are now on their way with the king to Siena.”

“They may be overtaken,” Romola said eagerly, the first blind fury giving way to anxious thought, “and persuaded to give up their purchase.”

“No, they may not,” Tito answered with cool decision.

“Why?”

“Because I do not choose that they should.”

“But if you were paid the money?—we will pay you the money,” Romola said. The words showed more nakedly than reproach how far he had become to her a stranger—an adversary to be bought off. Yet she spoke them with less bitterness than pleading. Tito felt stronger; her first storm had spent itself.

“No, my Romola. Understand that such thoughts are impracticable. You would not, in a reasonable moment, ask your godfather to bury three thousand florins in addition to what he has already paid on the library. I think your pride and delicacy would shrink from that.”

Her courage chilled; she trembled and, as if her limbs had suddenly remembered their weight, sank down on a carved chest near her.

Tito’s voice continued, clear and narrow, and it made her shudder as if cold water were being poured over a heated cheek.

“Moreover, it is not my will that Messer Bernardo should advance the money, even if the project were not utterly wild. And I beg you to consider, before you take any step or utter any word, what will be the consequences of placing yourself in opposition to me—of trying to exhibit your husband in the odious light which your own distempered feelings cast over him. What object will you serve by injuring me with Messer Bernardo? The event is irrevocable. The library is sold. And you are my wife.”

He spoke for effect, measuring each phrase. He waited and watched.

Romola kept her eyes on the ground. Several minutes passed. When she answered, her voice was changed—quiet, cold, as if something in her had drawn back and shut a door.

“I have one thing to ask.”

“Ask anything I can do without injuring us both, Romola.”

“That you will give me that portion of the money which belongs to my godfather, and let me pay him.”

“I must have some assurance from you first,” Tito said, “of the attitude you intend to take towards me.”

“Do you believe in assurances, Tito?” The bitterness came back like a taste on the tongue.

“From you, I do.”

“I will do you no harm. I shall disclose nothing. I will say nothing to pain him or you. You say truly—the event is irrevocable.”

“Then I will do what you desire to-morrow morning.”

“To-night, if possible,” Romola said, “that we may not speak of it again.”

“It is possible,” he answered, moving towards the lamp. She sat still, looking away with absent eyes, as if she no longer inhabited the room but only waited in it.

Presently he returned and bent over her, putting a paper into her hand.

“You will receive something in return—you are aware, my Romola?” he said gently; now that he felt secure, his manner sought to be propitiating, as if the matter were already passing into a thing to be forgiven.

“Yes,” she answered, taking the paper without looking at him. “I understand.”

“And you will forgive me, my Romola, when you have had time to reflect.”

He touched her brow with his lips; she made no sign—seemed unaware, as if her skin were numb. She heard him unlock the door and go out. She listened. The great door of the court opened and shut again, and the sound had in it a finality like the drop of a bar.

At that, she sprang up—as if some sudden liberty, terrible and empty, had been flung upon her. She went straight to her father’s chair, where his picture was propped, and fell on her knees before it. The strong control that had held her voice so cold broke utterly; sobs burst from her as if her very breath had been dammed and was rushing out at last.
