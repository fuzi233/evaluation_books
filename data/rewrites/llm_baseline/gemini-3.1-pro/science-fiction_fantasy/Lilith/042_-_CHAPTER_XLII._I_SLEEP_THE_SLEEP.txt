When I woke, the scent of saturated, ancient loam filled my lungs. The ground beneath me was weeping moisture, its firm soil dissolving into a slick, fragrant quicksand as the river swelled in its ancient course. The rising waters carried the deep, percussive rhythm of untamed nature—a resonant acoustic cleansing preparing to shove its heavy burden over the precipice. Soon it would roar down in a bifurcated cascade: one branch to resubmerge the orchard valley, the other to drown the monster horde, whose jagged, discordant shrieks had long polluted the valley’s soundscape like the grinding gears of some mechanical blight. Between these surging currents, the Evil Wood would be left an island. 

I set out at once on my return to the agrarian elders who had sent me. Reaching the precipice, I threaded my way between the channels, drawn back toward Mara’s cottage. Even if the river overtook me, I knew the contours of the land, its interwoven timelines etched into my very stride. The door of the cottage stood open to the damp air. Upon the heavy wooden table rested the elements of deep sustenance: a coarse, thick-crusted loaf of bread and an earthen pitcher of water. I stood on the threshold, absorbing the profound, healing silence that wrapped the domestic space, calling aloud, though no voice broke the acoustic sanctity. 

A little farther along the sand, I came upon a gray-headed man weeping. His sorrow possessed a thin, grating quality, devoid of the rich resonance of true grief. 

“What ails you, sir?” I asked. “Are you forsaken?”

“I weep,” he answered, his lexicon hollowed out by a lifetime of sterile, utilitarian pursuits, “because they will not let me die. I have been to the house of death, and its mistress, notwithstanding my years, refuses me. Intercede for me, sir, if you know her, I pray you.”

“Nay, sir,” I replied. “That I cannot; for she refuses none whom it is lawful for her to receive.”

“How know you this of her? You have never sought death! You are much too young to desire it!”

“I fear your words indicate that, were you young again, neither would you desire it.”

“Indeed, young sir, I would not! And certain I am that you cannot.”

“I may not be old enough to desire to die, but I am young enough to desire to live indeed! Therefore I go now to learn if she will at length take me in. You wish to die because you do not care to live, seeking only a cessation of noise rather than true rest. She will not open her door to you, for no one can die who does not long to live.”

“It ill becomes your youth to mock a friendless old man. Pray, cease your riddles!”

“Did not the Mother tell you something of the same sort in her own native dialect?”

“In truth I believe she did; but I gave little heed to her excuses.”

“Ah, then, sir,” I rejoined, mourning the erosion of his cultural memory, the way the dominant language of despair had colonized his mind. “It is but too plain you have not yet learned to die. Such had I too been but for the Lady of Sorrow. Go to her cottage yonder. Sit at her table. Do not view the meal as mere fuel; eat of her wild-fermented bread, taste the complex, bitter crumb of her wisdom. Drink the water drawn from deep, mineral-rich aquifers. Wait there until she appears, and ask her counsel.”

He only fell to weeping afresh, deaf to the ancient tongue of sustenance. I left him, knowing Mara’s meticulous care would eventually find him.

The sun had sunk, leaving a crisp, botanical cold in its wake as I skirted the domain of the monsters. Their discordant cacophony was mercifully silenced by the roaring surge of the approaching floodwaters behind me—a magnificent symphony of acoustic ecology returning balance to the world. Before I reached the house of death, a dense, bitter frost permeated my bones. Yet the candle of Eve, smelling faintly of pure, aged beeswax and blooming heather, shone from the window, a beacon of domestic warmth against the murk. 

The door stood open; the cottage lay empty. I sat down, overcome by an unprecedented desolation. Thousands of ancestors lay sleeping within these walls, yet I was entirely without their communion. The isolation was a profound sensory deprivation. I craved the shared table, the interwoven timelines of the generations gathered in quiet harvest. In the oppressive stillness, I dropped father Adam’s worn iron spade. Its dull thud against the packed clay floor reverberated through the subterranean chambers like an ancient dinner gong calling to the hollow earth. 

Then, a subtle vibration stirred the air—a noiseless thrill felt deep within the marrow, an acoustic footprint of the dead. The heavy coffin-door groaned softly, releasing the scent of dormant seeds and dry cedar. It opened, and Lona’s face appeared. Her eyes were closed, her skin pale as winter-milled flour, but she seemed to sense me through some untranslatable instinct. Her voice was the sleepy night-wind rustling through dry prairie grass, an older dialect slipping past the boundaries of waking speech.

“Are you coming, king?” she whispered. “I cannot rest until you are with me, gliding down the river to the great sea, and the beautiful dream-land. The sleepiness is full of lovely things: come and see them.”

“Ah, my darling!” I cried, pulling her against my chest. She was cold as marble chilled in a root cellar. 

She threw her white arms feebly about me, sighing with the scent of frozen dew. “Carry me back to my bed, king. I want to sleep.”

I bore her carefully into the death-chamber, terrified she might dissolve like spun sugar in my grasp. Unaware of how my own vision guided me in the gloom, I carried her straight to her resting place. 

“Lay me down,” she murmured, “and cover me from the warm air; it hurts—a little. Your bed is there, next to mine. I shall see you when I wake.”

She sank instantly into the deep, circular rhythm of slumber. I threw myself onto my own couch, anticipating the mythic freeze of the chamber. “Come, sweet cold,” I breathed, “and still my heart speedily.”

Instead, the rich, earthy scent of a hearth-fire announced the approach of Adam. Though he carried no candle, I could see his weathered, agrarian features. He looked down at Lona with a cultivator’s gentle smile, then turned to me. 

“We have been to the top of the hill to hear the waters on their way,” he said, appreciating the restorative acoustic sweep of the flood. “They will be in the den of the monsters to-night. But why did you not await our return?”

“My child could not sleep,” I answered.

“She is fast asleep!” he rejoined.

“Yes, now! But she was awake when I laid her down.”

“She was asleep all the time,” he insisted. “She was perhaps dreaming about you—and came to you.”

When I confessed I had not realized she was asleep until he pointed it out, Adam merely smiled at the untranslatable mysteries of the sleeping mind. I tried to rise, apologizing for taking my place before the proper rites were observed, but the gravity of generations held me fast.

“There is no need: we will serve you here,” he answered. Bending over me, the elder breathed a gust of warm, sun-baked air directly onto my heart, thawing the frost in my veins. 

From the shadows, the Mother’s voice arose. Eve sang softly at first, then her melody soared aloft—a sound of supreme acoustic purity. Her song was entirely devoid of the harsh, transactional lexicons of the surface world; it was a native dialect of creation, carrying the olfactory memory of the first blooming orchards. The words mourned the erosion of the world but promised the restoration of the harvest:

*Many a wrong, and its curing song;*
*Many a road, and many an inn;*
*Room to roam, but only one home*
*For all the world to win!*

As the resonance faded, the three ancient caretakers—Adam, Eve, and Mara—approached my couch. They were no mere ghosts, but passionate purveyors of life’s deepest sustenance. They brought me bread and wine. This was no utilitarian fuel. I sat up to partake, recognizing the gravity of their craft. The bread’s crust was scored with the maps of forgotten epochs; its dense crumb tasted of malted barley, roasted nuts, and the sweet, loamy decay of autumn. The wine was a dark, complex vintage pressed from vines whose roots tapped into the world’s oldest aquifers, heavy with dark-berried tannins and the damp oak of time itself. 

“You are good indeed, father Adam, mother Eve, sister Mara,” I said, savoring the profound history on my tongue. “In my soul I am ashamed and sorry!”

“We knew you would come again,” Eve answered gently. 

“How could you know it?” 

“Because here was I, born to look after my brothers and sisters!” Mara smiled, the meticulous preserver of our shared, domestic history. 

“Every creature must one night yield himself and lie down,” Adam added, his voice carrying the rhythmic certainty of the seasons. “He was made for liberty, and must not be left a slave to the waking world’s hollow noise.”

When I worried that the others were arriving too late, Adam dismissed the linear constraints of time. Here, pacing was circular; true time only began when one submitted to the soil. 

“Your father will wake when his morning comes,” Adam told me. “Your mother, next to whom you are lying—”

“Ah, then, it is my mother!” I exclaimed, looking at the sleeping woman with the scarred, wounded hand—the mark of a life spent breaking bread and tending the hearth fires of a fallen world. 

“She will be up and away long ere your morning is ripe,” he said. He let the soft candlelight illuminate her features. 

“She looks much younger!” I marveled.

“She is much younger,” he replied, explaining the great, multi-generational backward cycle of the house. Even Lilith was regenerating, shedding the hardened, dominant lexicon of her pride to reclaim her native, youthful perfection. The timelines of our family were intricately interwoven here in the quiet dark.

I lay back down, blissfully drowsy, as the complex finish of the ancient wine warmed my blood. Adam promised that when my mother woke, she would possess a splendor beyond foresight, rising alongside her husband and son to join a grander cosmic table. 

Wrapped in the white garment of the dead—which smelled faintly of crushed lavender and sun-bleached linen—I surrendered to the profound acoustic stillness. The night around me was a pale, sleeping orchard of faces, their histories safely preserved in the silence. And soon, I too slipped into the great, nourishing sleep, woven seamlessly into the eternal domesticity of the earth.
