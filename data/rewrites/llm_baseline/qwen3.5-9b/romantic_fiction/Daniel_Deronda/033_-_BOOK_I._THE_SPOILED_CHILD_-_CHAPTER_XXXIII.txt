"No man," a Rabbi once declared as an undeniable example, "shall turn the bones of his father and mother into spoons"—certainly, his listeners felt a moral recoil against such economic reductionism. Yet, the market for spoons has never expanded sufficiently for anyone to ask, "Why not?" and argue that human progress demands such a utilitarian application of material. The only barrier is sentiment, a force that coerces only those who believe feelings constitute the greater portion of the world's wealth.

Deronda, meanwhile, abandoned the fashionable exercise of riding in Rotten Row. Instead, he frequently wandered through those districts of London most densely populated by common Jews. He visited synagogues during services, peered into shop windows, and studied faces—a pursuit offering little promise of specific revelation. Why did he not seek out an influential Rabbi or a prominent member of the Jewish community to inquire about the odds of finding a mother named Cohen, a son named Ezra, and a lost daughter named Mirah? He considered doing so after Christmas. The truth was, despite his appreciation for the poetry inherent in everyday things, Deronda, whenever a keen personal interest was stirred, could not, any more than the rest of humanity, consistently escape the crushing weight of the hard, unyielding Reality. This reality never consults our tastes and admits no selection. We know that enthusiasm finds comfort among abstract ideas; it tolerates the scent of garlic in medieval times and sees no shabbiness in the ceremonial trappings of classic processions. However, it grows squeamish when ideals press upon it as something warmly incarnate, and struggles to face them without fainting.

Lying dreamily in a boat, imagining oneself seeking a beautiful maiden's relatives in Cordoba amidst Jews in the era of Ibn-Gebirol, one bears all physical inconveniences without shock. Similarly, if the grim scenery of St. Mary Axe and Whitechapel were imaginatively transplanted to the banks of the Rhine at the end of the eleventh century—where the cries of the Crusaders sounded like the bay of bloodhounds, and the cowering figure of the reviled Jew stood erect, heroic, and flashing with sublime constancy in the face of torture and death before devilish missionaries with swords and torches—what significance would the dingy shops and unlovely faces hold against the thrill of contemplative emotion? Yet, the fervor of sympathy we feel for a grandiose martyrdom pales in comparison to the enthusiasm that remains unslaked where there is no danger, no challenge—only the impartial midday sun falling upon commonplace, perhaps half-repulsive, objects which are truly the beloved ideas made flesh. Here lies the true poetic energy: in the power of imagination that pierces or exalts solid fact, rather than floating among cloud-like fantasies. To glory in a prophetic vision of knowledge covering the earth is an easier exercise for the believing mind than to witness its beginning in newspaper placards staring from a bridge beyond the cornfields. Indeed, it might well happen to most of us, the delicate few, that we stand in the thick of the Battle of Armageddon without realizing anything more than the annoyance of a puff of explosive smoke and the struggle of bodies on the ground immediately around us.

It was contrary to Deronda's nature to despise the weak, fastidious sympathy that shrinks from the broad life of mankind; but now, with Mirah standing before him as a living reality, whose fate he was compelled to care for, he viewed every common Jew and Jewess through the lens of comparison with her. He harbored a presentiment of the collision between her concept of an unknown mother and brother and the discovered fact—a foreboding sharpened by a suppressed awareness that a similar possibility of collision might lie hidden within his own destiny. He would not have looked with greater complacency at wealthy Jews who outdid the lords of the Philistines in their sports; but since there was no likelihood of Mirah's friends being found among that class, their habits did not immediately concern him. In this mood, he wandered without expecting a result more fruitful than a preparation of his own mind, perhaps for future theorizing as well as practice—much as if Mirah were related to Welsh miners, and he had gone to study their ways, hoping simultaneously to gather details regarding the history of strikes.

He did not truly wish to find any particular individual. When, as was his habit, he glanced at the sign above a shop door, he was perfectly content that it did not read "Ezra Cohen." I confess, he particularly desired that Ezra Cohen should not keep a shop. Wishes are often deemed ominous; according to this belief, the order of the world is arranged such that if one holds an impious objection to a squint, one's offspring is more likely to be born with one; conversely, if one desires a squint, one will not receive it. This despondent view of probability is entirely rejected by the hopeful, who take their wishes as sufficient security for fulfillment. Who is absolutely neutral? One morning, Deronda happened to turn into a quiet side street to escape the noise and obstructions of Holborn, and he felt the scales tip toward the despondent side.

He was growing weary of the streets and had paused to hail a hansom cab when his attention was caught by some exquisite old clasps in chased silver displayed in the window to his right. His initial thought was that Lady Mallinger, who possessed a strictly Protestant taste for such Catholic relics, might appreciate having these missal-clasps transformed into a bracelet. Then his eyes traveled over the other contents of the window, revealing that this was a pawnbroker's establishment where lead is given for jewelry, lace, and all equivocal objects introduced as *bric-à-brac*. A placard in the corner announced: *Watches and Jewelry exchanged and repaired*. However, his scrutiny had been noticed from within. A figure appeared at the door, looking around at him with a tone of cordial encouragement, "Good day, sir." That instant was sufficient for Deronda to recognize the face, unmistakably Jewish, belonging to a young man of about thirty. Wincing from the shopkeeper's persuasive tactics that surely followed, he returned the greeting and immediately crossed to the other side of the street, beckoning the cabman to pull up there. From that vantage point, he read the name above the shop window: **Ezra Cohen**.

There might be a hundred Ezra Cohens lettered above shop windows, but Deronda had not encountered them before. Probably the young man, interested in a potential customer, was Ezra himself; he was of the expected age to be Mirah's brother, having grown up while she remained a child. Yet, Deronda's first effort as he drove home was to convince himself that there was no warrantable presumption that this Ezra was Mirah's brother. Next, he reasoned that even if, despite sound logic, he turned out to be that brother, and inquiries revealed the mother to be dead, it was not his duty to disclose the discovery to Mirah. This conclusion was disturbed by his recently acquired knowledge that Mirah would possess a religious desire to know of her mother's death and to learn whether her brother lived. How far was he justified in determining another life by his own notions? Was it not his secret grievance against the way others had ordered his own life that he lacked open daylight on all its relations, preventing him, like other men, from following the full guidance of primary duties?

Immediate relief from this internal debate came from the reflection that he had made no real discovery yet, and that by investigating the facts more closely, he would certify that no demand existed for any decision whatsoever. He intended to return to the shop as soon as convenient to purchase the clasps for Lady Mallinger. However, he was hindered for several days by Sir Hugo, who, preparing to deliver an after-dinner speech on a burning topic, required Deronda to research the legal aspects of the question, alongside wasting daily hours in arguments that invariably ended in a stalemate. On many issues, they held opposing views, yet Sir Hugo cared little for this. When Deronda presented his point effectively, Sir Hugo replied with a mixture of satisfaction and regret:

"Confound it, Dan! Why don't you make an opportunity of saying these things in public? You're wrong, you know. You won't succeed. You've got the massive sentiment—the heavy artillery of the country against you. But it's all the better ground for a young man to display himself on. When I was your age, I should have seized it. And it would be quite as well for you to be in opposition to me here and there. It would throw you more into relief. If you would seize an occasion of this sort to make an impression, you might be in Parliament in no time. And you know that would gratify me."

"I am sorry not to do what would gratify you, sir," Deronda replied. "But I cannot persuade myself to look at politics as a profession."

"Why not? If a man is not born into public life by his position in the country, there is no other way but to embrace it through his own efforts. The business of the country must be conducted—Her Majesty's Government carried on, as the old Duke said. And it never could be, my boy, if everyone regarded politics as prophecy, demanding an inspired vocation. If you are to get into Parliament, it won't do to sit still waiting for a call either from heaven or constituents."
