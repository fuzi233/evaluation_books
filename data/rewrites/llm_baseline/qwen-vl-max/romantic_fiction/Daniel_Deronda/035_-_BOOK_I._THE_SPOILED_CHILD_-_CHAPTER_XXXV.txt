If guilt were measured by the scale of one’s sins, human history might have taken a far different turn. We would likely find the architects of greed-driven wars and the ruthless predators of finance standing shoulder to shoulder with petty thieves and common murderers—each lacerating their souls in equal penitence. Yet wickedness does not go unrewarded; those who win in this devil’s game must be more base, more cruel, more hardened than most of us can bear to imagine. For while many are born with conscience—a flicker of justice, a whisper of love—that prevents them from embracing untroubled evil, others rise above such scruples, leaving behind the fragile moral compass that binds ordinary humanity.

On December twenty-ninth, Deronda learned that the Grandcourts had arrived at the Abbey, though he had seen nothing of them before dressing for dinner. A heavy snowfall had transformed the grounds into a winter wonderland, delighting the children with snowball fights and snowmen. During the Christmas holidays, the Mallinger girls found no joy unless “cousin”—as they always called Deronda—was part of the fun. After playing outdoors, he had spent some time at billiards, letting the hours slip by without thought of meeting Gwendolen again. Still, the prospect stirred something within him. When he finally retired to his room, still warm from exertion, his mind turned to her—not with longing, but with quiet speculation.

What kind of woman would she become now that she was married? Would the weight of her new life show in her eyes, her voice, her bearing? He remembered her at Diplow, then at Leubronn—how vividly she had been shaped by experience. "Some people," he mused, "you could watch grow or decay day by day. I believe she is one of them—someone who carries every impression like a mark upon her soul. That necklace incident, the idea that gambling was wrong—those things cut deep. But sensitivity can lead either way: it may drive a person to despair as easily as to redemption."

And what of Grandcourt? Could anyone truly feel affection for him? His face seemed carved from stone—emotionless, detached. "One might almost wish to strike him," Deronda thought, "just to see if any passion might stir beneath that cold exterior." He feared Gwendolen had married not for love, but for escape—from poverty, perhaps, or from the crushing pressure of expectation. Why had she once fled from him? The poverty came later. Poor thing—had she been pressured? Was she trapped?

He felt only pity for her—this young woman, full of life yet so recklessly blind, believing herself entitled to rule the world simply because she was beautiful and admired. How swiftly her dreams might curdle into bitter regret! And yet, after all he’d seen of her, how could he not look forward to seeing her again?

Still, three weeks had passed since the wedding. She had already lived at Ryelands, at Diplow—enough time to mask her true feelings. She was not one to expose herself carelessly to curious eyes.

A gathering had been arranged to welcome the newlyweds. Among the guests were Lord and Lady Pentreath, representatives of old aristocracy; young Mr. and Mrs. Fitzadam, of the Worcestershire branch of an ancient gentry family; Mr. Fenn, Member of Parliament for West Orchards, accompanied by his two daughters—his presence symbolizing civic duty through cider interests; Mr. Raymond and his wife, cousins to Lady Mallinger; the distinguished lawyer Mr. Sinker; and Mr. Vandernoodt, a man of Dutch descent whom Sir Hugo had met at Leubronn and deemed worth keeping in England.

The drawing-room buzzed with polite conversation before the couple appeared. Children—little Raymonds, nephews and nieces of Lady Mallinger, along with her own three daughters—were allowed to roam freely, adding warmth to the scene. Portraits lined the cedar-paneled walls, their dark backgrounds framing noble faces. Above, the ceiling glowed with heraldic colors between candle sconces. The fire crackled with oak boughs, casting a golden light across the deep-piled carpet, while voices remained hushed, subdued by the dignity of the setting. Ages mingled—from the white-haired Pentreaths to four-year-old Edgar Raymond—creating a tapestry of life.

Lady Mallinger moved gracefully in black velvet, cradling a tiny white dog like a finishing touch to her elegance. The children clung to the ladies; the men stood apart, speaking softly, awaiting dinner. Deronda stood slightly apart, engaged in conversation with Mr. Vandernoodt—a man of bland charm, impeccably connected, but utterly unremarkable in himself. He was the sort who knew everything about everyone except what truly mattered.

They spoke of the bride and groom. Vandernoodt prided himself on collecting personal details. He hinted that he knew much about Grandcourt since their meeting at Leubronn.

“Men who’ve seen much of life don’t always choose wisely,” he said. “He’s had a colorful past—indulged deeply in pleasures, though lazy at heart. But you know all this, I’m sure.”

“No,” replied Deronda lightly. “I know little more than that he’s Sir Hugo’s nephew.”

At that moment, the door opened.

The room was made for grand entrances—and indeed, when Mr. and Mrs. Grandcourt stepped in, no one could deny their presence commanded attention. Grandcourt wore the same flawless suit, the same impassive expression as before his marriage. One sensed he would tolerate nothing less than perfection—in dress, in manner, in wife. And Gwendolen—she was exactly what he might have chosen. “By George,” murmured Vandernoodt, “I think she’s even handsomer now!”

Deronda agreed, though he said nothing. Her white silk gown, adorned with diamonds—on her neck, in her ears, woven into her hair—added to her regal beauty. It struck him with greater certainty than ever before: this was a woman whose grace had deepened, though not necessarily softened. Some beauties are like rare works of art—first met with skepticism, then recognized as masterpieces. At Leubronn, he had glimpsed a tenderness beneath her boldness. Now, at Diplow, he had seen a deeper, more complex woman. Was there change now?

She greeted guests with a proud calm, a smile that touched only her lips. But beneath it, he sensed the same fierce will—the same force that had once held him under her gaze and sent him away defeated at the roulette table. There was no time to reflect further—the dinner bell rang.

Seated near her, Deronda caught fragments of her conversation with Sir Hugo, who was in high spirits. He glanced toward her, intending to bow, but she offered no sign. Finally, Sir Hugo said, “Deronda, you’ll be pleased to hear what Mrs. Grandcourt tells me about your favorite Klesmer.”

Gwendolen’s eyelids lowered. Deronda watched her—saw a brief tremor as she raised them, returned his nod and smile, her own smile stiff, mechanical. It lasted only a second. Sir Hugo continued:

“The Arrowpoints have accepted the marriage. Klesmer is spending Christmas with his bride at Quetcham.”

“I suppose he’s glad for his wife’s sake,” said Deronda. “Otherwise, he might not mind staying away.”

“It’s like a troubadour tale,” said Lady Pentreath, a stately, deep-voiced elder. “Good to see a bit of romance left in our world. Our youth today are too worldly-wise.”

“True,” said Sir Hugo. “But it shows good sense on the Arrowpoints’ part to accept it after the scandal. Disowning your child over a _mésalliance_ is like losing your only eye—everyone knows it belongs to you, and you have no other to show.”

“As for _mésalliance_,” added Lady Pentreath, “there’s no bloodline to speak of. Admiral Arrowpoint was one of Nelson’s men—a doctor’s son. And we all know where the mother’s fortune came from.”

“If anything is a _mésalliance_,” said Deronda, “it’s on Klesmer’s side.”

“Oh?” said Sir Hugo, turning to Gwendolen. “You think it’s the immortal marrying the mortal? What do you say?”

“I have no doubt Herr Klesmer believes himself immortal,” she replied, regaining composure. “But I suspect his wife will burn enough incense to satisfy him.”

“Don’t you approve of a wife honoring her husband?” asked Sir Hugo, grinning.

“Oh, yes,” she said, “if only to make others believe in him.” She paused, then added with a flicker of humor, “When Herr Klesmer admires his own genius, it helps if his wife says Amen.”

“Klesmer isn’t your favorite, then,” said Sir Hugo.

“I hold him in high regard,” she replied, suddenly serious. “His genius is beyond my judgment. And I know him to be generous.”

Her tone carried a hidden edge—bitterness she couldn’t explain, even to herself. Deronda wondered: what would he have thought of her if he hadn’t known her past? Perhaps he’d have seen only a woman masking pain with defiance. But why didn’t she acknowledge him with kindness?

Sir Hugo changed the subject. “Isn’t this room magnificent? It was once the abbey’s refectory. Those pillars and arches divided the space—later built up. Originally, it was twice as large. Benedictine monks sat here. Imagine if the lights dimmed and the ghosts rose behind every chair!”

“Please don’t!” Gwendolen laughed, shivering playfully. “It’s lovely to follow in ancestors’ footsteps—but they should stay underground. I’d be afraid to walk alone in this house. I suppose the old generations are angry we’ve changed so much.”

“Oh, the ghosts are of all political stripes,” said Sir Hugo. “Those who wanted reform in life but failed—they’re on our side now. But if you don’t like walking alone, you’ll enjoy it with company. You and Grandcourt should see the whole place. Let’s ask Deronda to guide us—he knows more about it than I do.”

Gwendolen glanced at Deronda—her eyes fleeting, awkward. He had been serving himself, his face calm, unreadable. The thought of him showing her and Grandcourt the house—this place that might have been his, had things been different—stirred memories she couldn’t control. She felt exposed, ashamed. With her usual quick wit, she said, “You don’t know how afraid I am of Mr. Deronda.”

“Why?” asked Sir Hugo, intrigued. “Because he’s too learned?”

“No,” she said. “Since I first saw him at Leubronn. When he looked at the roulette table, I began to lose. He cast an evil eye on my play. He disapproved. He told me so. Now, whatever I do in front of him, I fear he’ll judge it.”

“Gad!” said Sir Hugo, glancing at Deronda. “I’m afraid of him too when he disapproves.” Then, lowering his voice, he added, “I don’t think ladies usually mind having his eyes upon them.”

“I object to critical eyes,” she said coolly, turning her head. “Are there many old rooms left in the Abbey?”

“Not many,” said Sir Hugo. “There’s a cloistered court with a gallery above. But the finest part—part of the old church—is now stables. I improved the rest, but couldn’t touch the stables. So the horses enjoy the choir.”

“I’d like to see the horses as well as the building,” said Gwendolen.

“Oh, I don’t keep many,” said Sir Hugo. “Grandcourt will scoff at my horses. I’ve given up hunting. I jog along, as becomes an old gentleman with daughters. Truth is, I did too much here. We lived at Diplow for two years during renovations. Do you like Diplow?”

“Not particularly,” she said, indifferent.

“Ah,” said Sir Hugo, pleased. “It won’t do after Ryelands. Grandcourt chose it for the hunting—but found something better.” He lowered his voice. “Something so much better he’d prefer it to any other place in the world.”

“It has one attraction for me,” she said, smiling coldly. “It’s near Offendene.”

“I understand,” said Sir Hugo, and let it drop.

No baronet is immune to desire. Sir Hugo wished Grandcourt disliked Diplow—for reasons of his own. But he also hoped that this preference wouldn’t stem from his marriage to such a charming woman. Gwendolen suited him perfectly, yet, as he later confided to Lady Mallinger, he would never have believed her capable of marrying beyond her expectations.

Deronda had paid little attention to the talk, but the glimpses he caught of Gwendolen confirmed his growing sense: her manner had grown colder, more artificial.

Later, in the drawing-room, Deronda sang at the piano, requested by someone. Afterward, Mrs. Raymond took his place. As he rose, he noticed Gwendolen had moved to the far end of the room, standing with her back to the others, gazing at a finely carved ivory head on a small table. He longed to speak to her—why not? He would have approached any other lady. Yet he hesitated, drawn instead to the graceful curve of her back.

Staring at a woman’s back is dangerous when you wish to speak to her. It only makes you want to see what lies beyond. There may be a smile waiting—soft, inviting. Deronda finally stepped forward, positioning himself at right angles to her. But before he could speak, she turned—not with a smile, but with a look of profound sadness, so different from her earlier chillness that he froze.

For a moment—long enough to feel the depth of understanding between them—they stared. She seemed to offer confession; he responded with silent empathy, absorbing all else.

“Will you join in the music?” he asked, needing to break the silence.

Her expression shifted—just a flicker—as she composed herself. “I join in by listening. I love music.”

“Are you a musician?”

“I’ve spent years studying it. But I lack talent. I shall never sing again.”

“But if you love music, it’s always worth it—private joy. I make it a virtue to accept my mediocrity,” he smiled. “It’s forgivable, as long as I don’t pretend it’s excellence.”

“I can’t imitate you,” she said, returning to her bright tone. “To me, being middling means being dull. And the worst fault I find in the world is that it’s dull. In fact,” she added, “I’m going to defend gambling—despite you. It’s a refuge from boredom.”

“I don’t accept that justification,” he said. “I think what we call ‘dullness’ is really a sickness in ourselves. Otherwise, how could anyone find life intensely meaningful? Many do.”

“Ah,” she smiled, “so the fault I find in the world is actually my own.”

Then, looking up at the ivory carving, she asked, “Do you never find fault with the world—or with others?”

“Oh, yes,” he said. “When I’m in a grumbling mood.”

“And hate people? Admit it—when they stand in your way, when their gain is your loss? That’s your own phrase.”

“We often stand in each other’s way without meaning to,” he replied. “Hating them for that seems foolish.”

“But if they harm you—and could have avoided it?” she pressed, her voice sharp, uncharacteristic.

Deronda was startled. Why this sudden intensity? A pang of unease silenced him for a moment. Then, quietly, he said, “Then I prefer my place to theirs.”

“There,” she said, laughing softly, “I believe you’re right.” And with that, she turned away, joining the group at the piano.
