"I question things but do not find One that will answer to my mind: And all the world appears unkind." — WORDSWORTH.

Gwendolen breathed a sigh of relief that her audience with Klesmer was concluded before she faced her uncle and aunt. She had resolved that whatever lay ahead consisted solely of unpleasantness, and she felt a grim determination to remain impassively calm in the face of any humiliation they might propose.

The confrontation did not occur until Monday. Gwentolen accompanied her mother to the rectory. On their way, they had stopped at Sawyer's Cottage, inspecting every inch of the cramped quarters bathed in the harsh, unfiltered light of noon. Blinds and curtains were nonexistent; the furnishings intended to soften the space had not yet arrived, gathered piecemeal from the rectory's stores.

"How *shall* you endure it, mamma?" Gwendolen asked as they walked back, her voice tight. During the tour of the bare walls, the scrubbed floors, the tiny garden choked with cabbage stalks, and the yew arbor thick with dust and cobwebs, she had kept her mouth shut. Now, the reality settled on her. "You and the four girls squeezed into that closet of a room, staring at green and yellow wallpaper that presses right against your eyes? And without me?"

"It will be some comfort to you, dear, that you do not have to share it."

"If it weren't for the need of money, I'd sooner be there than go to serve as a governess."

"Do not set your heart against it beforehand, Gwendolen. If you secure a post at the palace, every luxury will surround you. You know how deeply you have always cherished that. You won't find it nearly as difficult as climbing those steep, narrow stairs, listening to crockery clatter through the house, and overhearing the girls' conversations."

"It feels like a nightmare," Gwendolen exclaimed impulsively. "I cannot believe Uncle will allow you to go to such a place. He should have taken other measures."

"Don't be unreasonable, dear child. What else could he have done?"

"That was for him to figure out. It seems to me a remarkably strange world if people in our position must sink so abruptly," Gwendolen remarked, her mental landscape shifting instantly to accommodate only futures that suited her own desires.

It was her temperament that shaped her words under this crushing weight of adversity. She might have spoken more wisely regarding the hardships of others, though expressing virtue was never her ambition; she aimed for cleverness. This distinction serves to excuse her words, which were often sharper than her intentions warranted.

Despite the acute pain of her own situation, Gwendolen felt a pang of guilt when her uncle and aunt greeted her with a warmth they had rarely shown before. She was struck by the dignified cheerfulness with which they discussed the necessary cuts in their lifestyle and the education of the boys. Mr. Gascoigne's inherent worth, slightly clouded by worldly distractions—much as a woman's poetic beauty is obscured by the demands of fashion—shone brightly under this sudden reduction of fortune. Prompt and methodical, he had not only ordered the carriage withdrawn but also planned to mend his worn suits, skip meat at breakfast, abandon periodicals, remove Edwy from school to tutor the younger boys himself, and organize the entire household on the starkest possible budget. For healthy minds, economy holds its own pleasures; the rector's spirit had permeated the home. Mrs. Gascoigne and Anna, who always modeled themselves after their father, genuinely missed nothing they truly valued. In sincere sorrow, they agreed that the most tragic aspect of their losses was the fate awaiting Mrs. Davilow and her children.

For the first time, Anna could merge her resentment over Rex into her sympathy for Gwendolen. Mrs. Gascoigne hoped that trouble might prove beneficial for her niece, carefully avoiding any additional bitterness that might compound the lesson. Both women had been busy planning how to procure blinds and curtains from the household stores, yet with delicate tact, they pushed these practicalities aside to discuss Gwendolen's upcoming journey and the comfort her return brought to her mother.

There was nothing for Gwendolen to justify extending her discontent toward those immediately around her. Instead, she felt shaken into a state of hyper-alertness, as if summoned to a drill everyone else was obediently attending, when her uncle began, in a voice of firm kindness, to outline the efforts he had made to secure her a suitable position. Mr. Gascoigne had not forgotten Grandcourt, but the vagueness of any potential advances from that quarter was insufficient to distract a man of his sound judgment from acting decisively for his niece's present needs.

"I felt there was no time to lose, Gwendolen," he said. "A position in a respectable family, offering proper consideration, does not appear at a moment's notice. However long we wait, we could hardly find one where you would be better situated than at Bishop Mompert's. I am known to both him and Mrs. Mompert, which is an asset for you. Our correspondence has been favorable, but I am not surprised that Mrs. Mompert wishes to meet you before finalizing an agreement. She intends to arrange for you to meet her at Wanchester on her way to London. I daresay you will find the interview somewhat trying, my dear; but you will have time to prepare your mind."

"Do you know *why* she wants to see me, uncle?" Gwendolen asked, her mind rapidly cataloging the various, disagreeable reasons a hypothetical Mrs. Mompert with three daughters might possess for interviewing a prospective governess.

The rector smiled gently. "Do not be alarmed, my dear. She simply wishes to form a clearer impression of you than my report allows. A mother is naturally meticulous about choosing a companion for her daughters. I have mentioned that you are very young. However, she personally supervises her daughters' education closely, which makes her less concerned about age. She is a woman of taste and strict principle, and she objects to having a French person in the house. I am certain she will deem your manners and accomplishments adequate; furthermore, regarding the religious and moral tone of your education, she—and indeed the Bishop himself—will preside."

Gwendolen dared not reply. The suppression of her profound dislike for the entire prospect sent a deep flush racing over her face and neck, fading as quickly as it arrived. Anna, filled with tender fear, slipped her small hand into her cousin's. Mr. Gascoigne, a kindly man, could not fail to perceive the trial this sudden reversal posed for a girl like Gwendolen. Determined to offer a cheerful perspective, he continued in an easy, conversational tone, as if addressing no objections:

"I hold this position in such high regard that I would have been tempted to try to secure it for Anna, had she been likely to meet Mrs. Mompert's requirements. It is truly a home, offering a continuation of education in the highest sense; 'governess' is a misnomer. The Bishop's views lean decidedly Low Church compared to mine—he is a close friend of Lord Grampian's. Yet, though privately strict, he is not narrow in public matters. Indeed, he has aroused as little dislike in his diocese as any bishop currently on the bench. He has always remained friendly to me, although prior to his promotion, when he served as an incumbent in this diocese, we had a minor disagreement concerning the Bible Society."

The rector's words were laden with meanings that satisfied his own conscience, leading him to assume similar effects would resonate in his niece's mind. "Continuation of education"—"Bishop's views"—"Privately strict"—"Bible Society"—to Gwendolen, already recoiling from the opening prospect, these phrases felt like the rising heat of a burning glass, not the comforting links of logical persuasion they formed for her good uncle. Desperate, she began searching for an alternative.

"There was another situation, I think, Mamma mentioned?" she said, forcing a display of self-control.

"Yes," the rector replied in a slightly apologetic tone. "But that is in a school. I would derive less satisfaction from you taking that role. It would involve much harder work, as you are aware, and offer fewer benefits in other respects. Moreover, you do not have an equal chance of securing it."

"Oh, dear no," interjected Mrs. Gascoigne. "It would be much harder for you, my dear—and much less appropriate. You might not even have a private bedroom." Memories of school flooded Gwendolen's mind, suggesting other indignities that forced her to admit this alternative offered no relief. Turning back to her uncle, she feigned acceptance.

"When is Mrs. Mompert likely to send for me?"

"That is somewhat uncertain, but she has promised not to entertain any other proposals until she has seen you. She has entered deeply into your plight. It will likely be within the next fortnight. But I must depart now; I am going to lease part of my glebe property."

The rector concluded with cheerful assurance, leaving the room convinced that Gwendolen would adapt to her circumstances with the sense of a sensible girl. Having spoken appropriately, he naturally assumed the results would follow suit; accustomed, as a head of household and parish authority, to "speaking to" refractory individuals with the expectation of moral correction, he believed his words carried sufficient weight.

"What a stay Henry is to us all!" Mrs. Gascoigne remarked once her husband had left.
