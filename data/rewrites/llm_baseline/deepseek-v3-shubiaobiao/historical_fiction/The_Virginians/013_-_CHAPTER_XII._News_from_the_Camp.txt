The parting between the brothers had come and gone. George had taken his place among Mr. Braddock’s retinue, while Harry returned to the quiet halls of Castlewood, where duty now bound him. Yet his heart remained with the army, and the familiar comforts of home brought him no joy. He would not confess the depth of his disappointment, but the absence of his brother weighed upon him like a leaden cloak.

Harry walked past George’s empty chamber with averted eyes. He sat in his brother’s place at the head of the table, raising the silver tankard to his lips with a sigh. Each day, Madam Warrington lifted her voice in the loyal toast to the King, unwavering. On Sundays, when Harry read the service and prayed for travelers upon land and sea, she echoed the words with solemn emphasis. She spoke of George often—cheerfully, as though his return were assured. She entered his vacant room without flinching, saw that his books and linen were kept in order, and addressed the servants with quiet authority, speaking of tasks to be done "when Mr. George comes home."

Mrs. Mountain’s eyes welled at the mention of his name, and Harry’s face darkened with dread, but their mother remained composed. Her hands faltered more than usual at cards, and the servants found her awake at all hours, as if sleep had forsaken her. She had urged Mr. Dempster to resume his residence at Castlewood, and her manner, once so imperious, had softened into something gentler. She spoke often of her father’s campaigns, how he had emerged with little harm, and she clung to the hope that her son would do the same.

Letters from George arrived with welcome frequency, and as the army marched, he sent rough journals for Harry to read aloud in the evenings. The family gathered in the fading light—Madam Esmond erect at her tea-table, little Fanny bent over her needlework, Mr. Dempster and Mrs. Mountain at their cards, the servants hovering in silence—while Harry’s voice filled the room with his brother’s words.

*"The provinces betray His Majesty shamefully,"* George wrote. *"Virginia is remiss, Maryland worse, but Pennsylvania is the most perfidious of all. We beg for troops, promise to sustain them, then cheat the very men who come to defend us. No wonder the General curses and the men grow sullen. The delays have been endless. Had we moved sooner, the French would not now be strengthening their forts.

Colonel Washington, though no friend of mine, is brave and able. The General values his counsel, for he alone has faced the Indians in war-paint. I confess he was right to fire upon Jumonville.

As for the quarrel at Benson’s Tavern—let it rest. Captain Waring wished to revive it, but wiser heads intervened. The affair began in drunken folly, and the 44th’s officers were made to swallow their pride. Washington played the diplomat, and the matter ended over a dull dinner. He is too faultless—that is why I cannot like him. If he marries that widow, Heaven help her."*

“I marvel at the taste of some men—and the boldness of some women,” remarked Madam Esmond, setting down her cup. “That a widow should forget herself so far as to marry again!”

“Monstrous,” murmured Mrs. Mountain, with a peculiar glance.

Dempster studied his punch. Harry stifled a laugh—or some other emotion—but his mother bade him continue.

*"Discipline in camp is strict,"* George wrote. *"The men are flogged for drunkenness, and the drummers are ever at work. It sickened me to see the lash bite into a man’s back and hear his cries.

The officers, however, are less restrained. The Indians quit us in disgust after the young fools drank with their women—but I say no more of that."*

“Spare us the details,” said Madam Esmond. Harry skipped ahead.

*"Each Sunday, Divine Service is held at the colors. The General does his utmost to prevent plunder, offering his own money to secure provisions. He is a strange man—swears like a trooper yet never misses prayers. He idolizes the Duke, and speaks of him constantly.

Colonel Washington has been ill with fever. Perhaps his widow should nurse him. When one of us is ailing, we forget our quarrels. Yet I cannot forgive him entirely. We should have settled matters with powder and ball that day at the tavern. Still, though I do not love him as you do, I respect him as a soldier and an honest man—and I’ll not begrudge him for refusing to be our stepfather."*

“A stepfather!” Madam Esmond’s voice was sharp. “Does the boy think the Marquis of Esmond’s daughter could find no better match than a provincial surveyor? Skip such folly, Harry.”

*"The march is a grand sight,"* Harry read on. *"The redcoats winding through the forest, ever wary of ambush. Our scouts have taken scalps, for the French and their savages show no mercy. We found a scalped child, still alive, in a ruined homestead—his parents butchered. The General now offers five pounds per Indian scalp.

Our camp is orderly, with the baggage at its heart and sentries posted thick. I march on foot now, my horses pressed into service. Poor Roxana bears packs instead of me, but she knows my step and whinnies when I pass."*

The letter closed with tender duty to his mother and love to Harry.

But as July wore on, unease crept over Virginia. Rumors flew—whispered by servants, debated in taverns. The army had met disaster. An ambush. Slaughter. The General dead, his scalp taken.

When the news reached Castlewood, Mrs. Mountain let out a cry. Little Fanny clung to her mother. Madam Esmond sank to her knees, hands raised to Heaven. “Lord God Almighty, defend my boy!”

Harry and Dempster armed themselves and rode north, following the army’s trail. Each mile brought darker tidings. A thousand men lost. Officers cut down. The General slain.

And George?

Harry’s heart clenched like a fist. Memories of their boyhood—vows made in youthful earnestness—rose like ghosts. Wounded men pitied his grief; women softened at his stricken face. Even the stern tutor wept—not only for the pupil lost, but for the brother who rode through the gathering dark, desperate for word of the dead.
