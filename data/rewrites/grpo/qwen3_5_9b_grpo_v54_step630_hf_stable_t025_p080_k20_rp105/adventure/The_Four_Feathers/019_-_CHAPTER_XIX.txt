Ethne intended to slip away unseen, but Mrs. Adair was already waiting. Seated upon the terrace in the cool shadow of the house, near the open window of the drawing-room, she watched Ethne cross the stone path and sprint down the steps into the garden. The suddenness of her departure struck Mrs. Adair as peculiar, bordering on desperation. From her vantage point, commanding the view of the window where moonlight spilled onto the floor, Mrs. Adair had witnessed the entire sequence: Ethne extinguishing the lamp, plunging the room into darkness, and the swift, secretive shift in the atmosphere that suggested a private intimacy. It had been a torture to witness, yet Mrs. Adair had not fled. Instead, she had remained, listening to the music that drifted out into the garden—a sound filled with happiness and yearning—followed by hushed conversation. These sounds had ignited a jealous fire within her.

Then Ethne had fled. Mrs. Adair quickly dismissed the idea of a quarrel; she knew Ethne was not the type to fight, nor to run if caught in a dispute. Yet, something more bizarre occurred. Durrance continued to speak inside the darkened room, his voice carrying clearly to Mrs. Adair's ears though the words remained indistinct. It was evident he believed Ethne was still there with him. Rising silently on the tips of her toes, Mrs. Adair approached the open window. She heard Durrance laughing lightly, then speaking with distinct clarity. He sat in the shadows, unaware of her presence.

"I began to find out," he said, "even on that first afternoon at Hill Street two months ago, that there was only friendship on your side. My blindness helped me. With your face and your eyes in view, I should have believed without question just what you wished me to believe. But you had no longer those defences. I, on my side, had grown quicker. I began in a word to see. For the first time in my life, I began to see."

Mrs. Adair held her breath. Durrance spoke with the easy voice of a man recounting difficulties that had long since unraveled, expecting no answer.

"I should have definitely broken off our engagement at once," he continued. "For I still believed, as firmly as ever, that there must be more than friendship on both sides. But I had grown selfish. I warned you, Ethne, that selfishness was the blind man's particular fault. I waited, I deferred the marriage, I made excuses. I led you to believe there was a chance of recovery when I knew there was none. For I hoped, as a man will, that with time your friendship might grow into more. So long as there was a chance, I could not let you go. I listened for some new softness in your voice, some new buoyancy in your laughter, some new deep thrill in the music you played, longing for it—how much! Well, tonight I have burnt my boats. I have admitted to you that I knew friendship limited your thoughts of me. I have owned that there is no hope my sight will be restored. I have even dared to tell you what I kept secret for so long: my meeting with Harry Feversham and the peril he has run. And why? Because for the first time tonight I heard just those signs for which I waited. The new softness, the new pride in your voice, the buoyancy in your laughter—they have been audible to me all this evening. The restraint and tension were gone from your manner. And when you played, it was as though someone with just your skill and knowledge played, but someone who let her heart speak resonantly through the music as until tonight you have never done. Ethne, Ethne!"

At that precise moment, Ethne was in the small, enclosed garden where she had led Captain Willoughby that morning. Here she was private, accompanied only by her collie dog. She had finally reached the solitude and silence that had become necessities for her soul. A few more words from Durrance, and her carefully constructed pretense of affection—the wall she had built around him which he was never to breach—would have crumbled. Indeed, Durrance had already looked over the wall, staring at her with amazed eyes, but Ethne did not know this. To prevent him from seeing the truth, she had fled.

Moonlight slept in silver upon the creek; tall trees stood dreaming under the stars; the lapping of the tide against the bank was softer than the music of a river. Ethne sat upon a bench, striving to gather the quietude of the summer night into her heart, to learn from the growing things around her something of their patience and extraordinary perseverance.

But the day's events had overtaxed her, and she could not find peace. Only that morning, in this very garden, had good news arrived, allowing her to regain Harry Feversham. That was how she interpreted Willoughby's message. She had regained him, only to lose him again this evening, likely forever. Harry Feversham meant to pay for his fault to the uttermost scruple, and Ethne cried out against his thoroughness, a trait he had learned from no one else but herself. "Surely," she thought, "he might have been content. In redeeming his honour in the eyes of one of the three, he has redeemed it in the eyes of all."

Yet he had gone south to join Colonel Trench at Omdurman. Of that squalid, shadowless town, of its hideous barbarities and the horrors of its prison-house, Ethne knew nothing. But Captain Willoughby had hinted enough to fill her imagination with terrors. He had offered to explain what captivity in Omdurman implied, and she remembered wringing her hands as she recalled refusing to listen. What cruelties might not be practiced? Even now, at this very hour, perhaps on this night of summer—but she dared not let her thoughts wander that way.

The lapping of the tide sounded like a river's song. It reminded Ethne of a particular river that had sung in her ears five years ago when she had watched out another summer night until dawn. Never had she so hungered for her own country and the companionship of its brown hills and streams. Not even this afternoon, when she had sat at her window watching the lights change upon the creek. Donegal held a sanctity for her; dwelling there seemed to set her apart from earthly taints. As her heart went out in great longing toward it, a sudden, fierce loathing sprang up within her for the concealments, shifts, and maneuvers she had practiced and still must practice. A great weariness came upon her, yet she did not waver from her fixed resolve. Two lives were not to be spoiled simply because she lived in the world. Tomorrow she could gather her strength and begin again. For Durrance must never know that there was another whom she placed before him in her thoughts.

Meanwhile, inside the drawing-room, Durrance brought his confession to an end.

"So you see," he said, "I could not speak of Harry Feversham until tonight. For I was afraid that what I had to tell you would hurt you very much. I was afraid that you still remembered him, in spite of those five years. I knew, of course, that you were my friend. But I doubted whether in your heart you were not more than that to him. Tonight, however, I could tell you without fear."

Now, at all events, he expected an answer. Mrs. Adair, still standing by the window, heard him move in the shadows.

"Ethne!" he said, with some surprise in his voice. Since no answer came, he rose and walked toward the chair where Ethne had sat. Mrs. Adair could see him now. His hands felt for and grasped the back of the chair. He bent over it, as though he thought Ethne was leaning forward with her hands upon her knees.

"Ethne," he said again, and there was more trouble and doubt in this repetition than surprise. It seemed to Mrs. Adair that he dreaded finding her silently weeping. He began to speculate whether, after all, he had been right in his inference from Ethne's recapture of her youth that night, whether the shadow of Feversham did not after all fall between them. He leaned farther forward, feeling with his hand, and suddenly a string of Ethne's violin twanged loudly. She had left it lying on the chair, and his fingers had touched it.

Durrance drew himself up straight and stood motionless and silent, like a man who had suffered a shock and was bewildered. He passed his hand across his forehead once or twice, and then, without calling upon Ethne again, he advanced to the open window.

Mrs. Adair did not move, holding her breath. There was just the width of the sill between them. Moonlight struck full upon Durrance, and she saw a comprehension gradually dawn in his face that someone was standing close to him.

"Ethne," he said a third time, now appealing.

He stretched out a hand timidly and touched her dress.

"It is not Ethne," he said with a start.

"No, it is not Ethne," Mrs. Adair answered quickly. Durrance drew back a step from the window and remained silent for a little while.

"Where has she gone?" he asked at length.

"Into the garden. She ran across the terrace and down the steps very quickly and silently. I saw her from my chair. Then I heard you speaking alone."

"Can you see her now in the garden?"

"No; she went across the lawn towards the trees and their great shadows. There is only the moonlight in the garden now."

Durrance stepped across the window sill and stood beside Mrs. Adair. The last slip Ethne had made had betrayed her inevitably to the man who had grown quick. There could be only one reason for her sudden, unexplained, secret flight. He had told her that Feversham had wandered south from Wadi Halfa into savage country; he had spoken out his fears regarding Feversham's fate without reserve, thinking that she had forgotten him, and indeed rather inclined to blame her for the callous indifference with which she received the news. That callousness was a mere mask, and she had fled because she no longer had the strength to hold it up before her face. His first suspicions had been right. Feversham still stood between Ethne and himself, holding them at arm's length.

"She ran as though she was in great trouble and hardly knew what she was doing," Mrs. Adair continued. "Did you cause that trouble?"

"Yes."

"I thought so, from what I heard you say."

Mrs. Adair wanted to hurt, and in spite of Durrance's impenetrable face, she felt that she had succeeded. It was a small sort of compensation for the weeks of mortification she had endured. There was something to be said for Mrs. Adair; extenuations might be pleaded, even if no defense was made. For she, like Ethne, was overtaxed that night. That calm, pale face of hers hid the quick passions of the South, and she had been racked by them to the limits of endurance. There had been something grotesque, something rather horrible, in that outbreak and confession by Durrance after Ethne had fled from the room. He was speaking his heart to an empty chair. She herself had stood without the window with a bitter longing that he had spoken so to her and a bitter knowledge that he never would. She was sunk deep in humiliation. The irony of the position tortured her; it was like a jest of grim, selfish gods played off upon ineffectual mortals to their hurt. And at the bottom of all her thoughts rankled the memory of the extinguished lamp and the low, hushed voices speaking one to another in darkness. Therefore she spoke to give pain and was glad that she gave it, even though it was to the man whom she coveted.

"There's one thing which I don't understand," said Durrance. "I mean the change which we both noticed in Ethne tonight. I mistook the cause of it, that's evident. I was a fool. But there must have been a cause. The gift of laughter had been restored to her. Her gravity, her air of calculation, had vanished. She became just what she was five years ago."

"Exactly," Mrs. Adair answered. "Just what she was before Mr. Feversham disappeared from Ramelton. You are so quick, Colonel Durrance. Ethne had good news of Mr. Feversham this morning."

Durrance turned quickly toward her, and Mrs. Adair felt a pleasure at his abrupt movement. She had provoked the display of some emotion, and the display of emotion was preferable to his composure.

"Are you quite sure?" he asked.

"As sure as that you gave her the worst of news tonight," she replied.

But Durrance did not need the answer. Ethne had made another slip that evening, and though unnoticed at the time, it came back to Durrance's memory now. She had declared that Feversham still drew an allowance from his father. "I heard it only today," she had said.

"Yes, Ethne heard news of Feversham today," he said slowly. "Did she make a mistake five years ago? There was some wrong thing Harry Feversham was supposed to have done. But was there really more misunderstanding than wrong? Did she misjudge him? Has she today learnt that she misjudged him?"

"I will tell you what I know. It is not very much. But I think it is fair that you should know it."

"Wait a moment, please, Mrs. Adair," said Durrance, sharply. He had put his questions rather to himself than to his companion, and he was not sure that he wished her to answer them. He walked abruptly away from her and leaned upon the balustrade with his face toward the garden.

It seemed to him rather treacherous to allow Mrs. Adair to disclose what Ethne herself evidently intended to conceal. But he knew why Ethne wished to conceal it.
