The Sergeant stood silent, his mind working in the shadows of the plantation of firs that bordered the treacherous quicksand. When we finally stepped onto the shifting sands, he rallied, his demeanor sharpening into the resolve of a man who had reached a conclusion.

"Mr. Betteredge," he addressed me, "since you have shared the burden of rowing my boat and proven yourself a capable assistant, there is no further need for our mutual games of pretense. I propose to speak plainly. You are withholding information that would implicate Rosanna Spearman, driven by the belief that she is a good girl to you and that you pity her plight. These are noble sentiments, Mr. Betteredge, but in this specific instance, they are a luxury we cannot afford. Rosanna Spearman faces no danger whatsoever—not if I pin her to the crime of the Diamond's disappearance with evidence as undeniable as the nose on your face!"

"Are you suggesting my lady will not prosecute?" I asked, my voice tight.

"I mean that your lady *cannot* prosecute," Cuff corrected sharply. "Rosanna is merely an instrument in the hands of another. She will be held harmless for the sake of that other person."

He spoke with a gravity that brooked no denial, yet a cold knot tightened in my stomach. "Can you name that other person?" I pressed.

"Can *you*, Mr. Betteredge?" he countered.

"No."

Cuff froze, surveying me with a look of melancholy curiosity. "It is my nature to be tender toward human frailty," he mused. "I feel particularly tender toward you, Mr. Betteredge, and I suspect you feel the same toward Rosanna. Do you happen to know if she has acquired a new outfit of linen recently?"

The question was abrupt, yet I saw no harm in answering truthfully. "She was formerly scantily provided," I admitted, emphasizing her past diligence. "However, my lady, in reward for her good conduct, gifted her a fresh outfit not two weeks ago."

"A miserable world," Cuff sighed, his tone shifting to something akin to grim amusement. "Life is a target, Mr. Betteredge, and misfortune is the archer. Had it not been for that new outfit, we would have found a nightgown or petticoat among her effects and pinned her down immediately. You have examined the servants yourself; you know what the others discovered outside her door. Do you recall what she was doing yesterday after falling ill? You cannot guess? It is as plain as the strip of light at the end of the trees. At eleven on Thursday, Superintendent Seegrave pointed out a smear on the door to the women. Rosanna, suspecting foul play regarding her own clothes, seized the first chance to retreat to her room. She found the paint stain on her nightgown or petticoat, feigned illness, and slipped away to town. There, alone in her room on Thursday night, she lit a fire—not to destroy the evidence, for she knew two servants were watching her door—but to dry and iron a substitute garment she had made. She kept the stained dress hidden, perhaps wearing it, and is currently engaged in disposing of it on that lonely stretch of beach ahead of us. I have traced her to your fishing village and a specific cottage. She lingered there, emerging with something concealed beneath her cloak. A cloak is an emblem of charity, covering a multitude of sins. I saw her depart northward along the coast. Is your shoreline here considered a fine specimen of marine scenery, Mr. Betteredge?"

"Yes," I replied briefly.

"Tastes differ," Cuff observed, his gaze fixed on the desolate water. "From my perspective, I have rarely admired a seascape less. If you are following someone along this coast, there is no cover to hide you. I chose between arresting Rosanna on suspicion or allowing her to finish her current gambit. For reasons I shall not trouble you with, I decided to delay alarming a certain individual until later tonight. I returned to the house to request that you guide me to the northern end of the beach via an alternate route. Sand is one of my finest detective tools; it prints footsteps with ruthless accuracy. If we do not intercept Rosanna, the sand may reveal her secrets if the light holds. Here is the sand. If you will excuse my suggestion, perhaps you should remain silent and let me lead?"

A peculiar fever, akin to a contagion, had seized me. Cuff moved between the sand dunes toward the beach, and I followed, my heart hammering against my ribs, waiting anxiously for the outcome.

As fate would have it, I found myself standing nearly where Rosanna and I had conversed previously when Mr. Franklin first arrived from London. While my eyes tracked the Sergeant, my mind drifted involuntarily to that past encounter. I felt a phantom sensation of Rosanna slipping her hand into mine, offering a grateful squeeze of thanks for my kindness. I almost heard her voice again, speaking of the Shivering Sand's magnetic pull, and saw her face brighten as she imagined Mr. Franklin approaching. My spirits sank with these memories, and the view of the lonely bay only deepened my unease.

The last vestiges of daylight were fading, casting a still, awful calm over the desolation. The ocean heaved silently against the great sandbank; the inner sea lay dim and motionless. Patches of yellow-white ooze floated on the dead surface, while scum and slime glimmered faintly near the rocky spits jutting north and south. It was the turn of the tide, and as I waited, the broad brown face of the quicksand began to dimple and quiver—the only movement in that horrific place.

The Sergeant started as the sand's shiver caught his eye. After a moment's observation, he turned back to me. "A treacherous location, Mr. Betteredge," he noted. "No sign of Rosanna Spearman anywhere on the beach."

He guided me lower to the shore, confirming that our own footprints were the only marks visible.

"How does the fishing village bear, standing where we are now?" Cuff asked.

"Cobb's Hole," I answered, "lies due south."

"I saw the girl this evening walking northward from Cobb's Hole," Cuff stated. "Therefore, she must have been heading toward this place. Is Cobb's Hole on the other side of that point of land? Can we reach it now, at low tide, via the beach?"

"Yes," I confirmed.

"We shall move briskly," Cuff urged. "I wish to locate where she left the shore before darkness falls."

We walked perhaps two hundred yards toward Cobb's Hole when Cuff suddenly dropped to his knees on the beach, appearing seized by a sudden fervor for prayer.

"There is merit in your marine landscape after all," Cuff remarked, rising. "Here are a woman's footsteps, Mr. Betteredge! Let us assume they belong to Rosanna until proven otherwise. They are confused—deliberately confused. Ah, poor soul, she understands the detective virtues of sand as well as I do! Yet, she seems to have been in too great a hurry to tread them thoroughly. Here is a footprint moving *from* Cobb's Hole, and another returning. Note the toe pointing straight to the water's edge, and two heel-marks further down, close to the water. I fear Rosanna is sly. It appears she intended to reach the place we just came from without leaving a trail. Did she walk through the water to that ledge of rocks behind us and return, leaving only those two heel marks? That fits my theory that she carried something under her cloak when she left the cottage. Not something to destroy, for then why the precautions? Something to hide, I believe. Perhaps visiting the cottage will reveal what that something is?"

At this proposal, my detective-fever cooled instantly. "You do not require me," I protested. "What good can I do?"

"The longer I know you, Mr. Betteredge, the more virtues I discover," Cuff replied. "Modesty is rare in this world, and you possess it in abundance. If I go alone, the villagers will be tongue-tied. If I go with you, I arrive as a neighbor's guest, and conversation flows naturally. How does that strike you?"

Lacking a witty retort, I asked which cottage he sought. Upon his description, I recognized it as the home of a fisherman named Yolland, his wife, and their two children, a son and a daughter. As I had noted before, Rosanna occasionally visited friends at Cobb's Hole; these were the Yollands, respectable and worthy. Her acquaintance began with the daughter, Limping Lucy, who suffered from a deformed foot. The two deformed girls shared a bond of sympathy. The fact that Cuff had traced Rosanna to *their* cottage changed the nature of my involvement. She was merely visiting familiar ground; proving her association with the Yollands would demonstrate her innocence. Convinced by Cuff's logic, I agreed to assist.

We proceeded to Cobb's Hole, following the tracks on the sand as long as the light permitted.

Upon reaching the cottage, the fisherman and his son were out at sea. Limping Lucy, weak and weary, rested upstairs. Good Mrs. Yolland received us alone in her kitchen. Hearing that Cuff was a celebrated figure in London, she produced a bottle of Dutch gin and two pipes, staring at him with wide-eyed admiration.

I sat quietly in the corner, waiting for Cuff to steer the conversation toward Rosanna. His usual roundabout method proved even more circuitous this time. He began with the Royal Family, the Primitive Methodists, and the price of fish, eventually winding his way, in his dismal, subterranean style, to the loss of the Moonstone, the malice of the first housemaid, and the general harsh treatment of Rosanna by the other servants. Having reached his topic, he claimed his inquiries aimed both to recover the Diamond and clear Rosanna from unjust suspicion. Within twenty minutes, Mrs. Yolland was convinced she was speaking to Rosanna's best friend, pressing Cuff to drink from the Dutch bottle to revive his spirits.

Persuaded that Cuff was wasting his efforts on Mrs. Yolland, I sat observing the interaction like an audience member at a play. The great Cuff displayed wonderful patience, firing shot after shot at random, aiming only at Rosanna's credit and avoiding any hint of prejudice. Despite his efforts, Mrs. Yolland talked incessantly, placing absolute trust in him. His final attempt came as we checked our watches and prepared to depart.

"I shall now wish you good-night, ma'am," Cuff said. "Only at parting, I must say that Rosanna Spearman has a sincere well-wisher in myself. However, she will never succeed in her present position; my advice is to leave it."

"Bless your heart! She is *going* to leave it!" cried Mrs. Yolland.

Rosanna intending to leave! I pricked up my ears. It seemed strange that she had given no prior warning to my lady or me. A doubt crept into my mind: had Cuff's final random shot hit the mark? Was I complicit in mystifying an honest woman with a web of lies? Remembering that the father of lies is the Devil, and that mischief often follows close behind, I sensed trouble in the air. I attempted to extract Cuff, but he sat down immediately, requesting another sip from the Dutch bottle. Mrs. Yolland obliged, sitting opposite him. I moved to the door, feeling excessively uncomfortable, intending to bid them good-night yet unable to leave.

"So she means to leave?" Cuff asked. "What will she do when she does? Sad, sad! The poor creature has no friends in the world except you and me."

"Ah, but she has!" Mrs. Yolland interjected. "She came in this evening, spoke with my girl Lucy and me, then asked to go upstairs to Lucy's room—the only room with pen and ink. 'I want to write a letter to a friend,' she said, 'but I cannot do it with the servants prying.' I cannot tell you who the letter was for, but it must have been very long, judging by the time she spent upstairs. I offered her a postage stamp when she returned; she did not have the letter in her hand and declined the stamp. She is a little close about her affairs, poor soul, but she has a friend somewhere, and she will go to that friend."

"Soon?" Cuff asked.

"As soon as she can," Mrs. Yolland replied.

Stepping back from the door, I could not allow such loose talk about a servant's departure to continue unchecked. "You must be mistaken about Rosanna Spearman," I declared. "If she intended to leave, she would have informed me first."

"Mistaken?" Mrs. Yolland exclaimed. "Only an hour ago she purchased items for traveling from me, right here in this room. And that reminds me," she added, suddenly feeling in her pocket, "of something concerning Rosanna and her money. Are you likely to see her when you return?"

"I would be delighted to deliver a message," Cuff offered before I could speak.

Mrs. Yolland produced several shillings and sixpences, counting them out with meticulous, exasperating care. She offered the money to Cuff, looking reluctant to part with it.

"Might I ask you to return this to Rosanna with my love?" she said. "She insisted on paying for the few things she bought, and money is welcome in our house. Still, I am uneasy taking a poor girl's savings. To tell the truth, I do not think my husband would like to hear I took Rosanna's money when he returns tomorrow. Please tell her she is welcome to the items as a gift. And do not leave the money on the table," she added, placing it down abruptly as if it burned her fingers—"times are hard, and flesh is weak; I might be tempted to put it back in my pocket."

"Come along!" I urged. "I cannot wait; I must return to the house."

"I will follow you directly," Cuff said.

For the second time, I moved to the door, yet for the second time, I could not cross the threshold.
