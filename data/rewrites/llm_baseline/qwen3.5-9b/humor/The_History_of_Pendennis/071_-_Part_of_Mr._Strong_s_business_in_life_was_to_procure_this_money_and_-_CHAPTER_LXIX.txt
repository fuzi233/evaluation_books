Early the next morning, the shutters of Bury Street were raised by none other than Mr. Morgan. He presented himself with his customary air of grave respect, carrying the old gentleman's clothes, cans of water, and an elaborate array of toilet requisites.

"It's you, is it?" grunted the Major from his bed. "I shan't take you back again, you understand."

"I have not the least wish to be taken back again, Major Pendennis," Mr. Morgan replied with profound dignity. "Nor do I wish to serve you or any other man. However, seeing that I desire you to remain comfortable while you stay in my house, I have come to perform what is necessary."

Once more, and for the last time, Mr. James Morgan laid out the silver dressing-case and strapped the shining razor. With an indescribable solemnity, he turned to the Major and announced:

"Thinking that you would most likely require a respectable person to attend to you until you settle your affairs myself, I spoke to a young man last night who is here."

"Indeed," said the warrior in his tent-bed. "Has he lived in the first families? Can you vouch for his respectability?"

"You are monstrously polite," grinned the old Major.

The truth was, following the events of the previous evening, Morgan had retired to his own club, *The Wheel of Fortune*. There, he had encountered Frosch, a courier and valet recently returned from a foreign tour with young Lord Cubley and currently available. Representing to Frosch that he had "had a devil of a blow up with his own Governor" and was retiring from the business altogether, Morgan had suggested that if Frosch sought temporary employment, he might apply at Bury Street.

"You are very polite," said the Major, "and your recommendation, I am sure, will carry every weight."

Morgan blushed slightly; he sensed his former master was chaffing him.

"The man has waited on you before, sir," he stated with great dignity. "Lord De la Pole gave him to his nephew, young Lord Cubley. He accompanied him on his foreign tour. Not wishing to return to Fitzurse Castle—which Frosch finds difficult due to his delicate chest and inability to bear the Scottish cold—he is free to serve you or not, as you choose."

"I repeat, sir, that you are exceedingly polite," said the Major. "Come in, Frosch—you will do very well. Mr. Morgan, will you have the great kindness to—"

"I shall show him what is necessary, sir, and what is customary for you to wish done. Will you please take breakfast here or at the Club, Major Pendennis?"

"With your kind permission, I will breakfast here, and afterwards we will make our little arrangements."

"If you please, sir."

"Will you now oblige me by leaving the room?"

Morgan withdrew. The excessive politeness of his ex-employer made him nearly as angry as the Major's bitterest words. As the old gentleman commenced his mysterious toilet, we shall also modestly retire.

After breakfast, Major Pendennis and his new aide-de-camp prepared for departure. The establishment of the old bachelor was remarkably uncomplicated. He possessed no useless wardrobe. His collection consisted of a Bible (his mother's), a road book, *Pen's* novel bound in calf-skin, the Duke of Wellington's Despatches, and a few prints, maps, and portraits of the illustrious General, various sovereigns, and the Commander-in-Chief under whom the Major had served in India. Always ready to march at a few hours' notice, the cases in which he had brought his property into his lodgings fifteen years prior remained in the loft, ample enough to hold all his goods.

These items were brought down by the young woman who performed the household duties, known as "Betty" to her mistress and "Slavey" to Mr. Morgan. Under the watchful, terrifying gaze of Morgan, she diligently dusted and cleaned everything. His demeanor was guarded and solemn; he had spoken no word to Mrs. Brixham regarding his threats of the previous night, yet he looked as though he intended to execute them, causing the poor widow to tremble in anticipation of her fate.

Old Pendennis, armed with his cane, supervised the packing of his goods under Frosch's hands, while Slavey burned the papers the Major chose not to keep. Doors and closets were flung open until entirely empty. Now, all boxes and chests were sealed, save for his desk, which stood ready to receive the final accounts of Mr. Morgan.

That individual soon made his appearance, bringing his books.

"As I wish to speak to you in private, perhaps you will have the kindness to request Frosch to step downstairs," he said upon entering.

"Bring a couple of cabs, Frosch, if you please—and wait downstairs until I ring for you," commanded the Major.

Morgan sent Frosch away, watched him depart down the street, and then produced his books and accounts, which were simple and easily settled.

"And now, sir," he said, pocketing the cheque handed to him by his ex-employer and signing his ledger with a flourish, "now that accounts are closed between us, sir, I propose to speak to you as one man to another." (Morgan loved the sound of his own voice; whenever possible, he indulged in public speaking at the Club or in the housekeeper's room.) "I must inform you that I am in possession of certain information."

"And may I inquire of what nature, pray?" asked the Major.

"It is valuable information, Major Pendennis, as you know very well. I know of a marriage that is no marriage—of an honorable Baronet who is no more married than I am, whose wife is married to someone else, as you also know, sir."

Pendennis immediately grasped the situation.

"Hah! This explains your behavior. You have been listening at the door, I suppose," said the Major, looking haughty. "I forgot to check the keyhole when I went to that public house, or I might have suspected what sort of person was hiding behind it."

"I may have my schemes as you may have yours, I suppose," answered Morgan. "I may gather my information, act upon it, and find it valuable as anyone else. A poor servant may have a bit of luck as well as a gentleman, mayn't he? Do not put on your arrogant looks and pretend to be an aristocrat over me. That is all gammon. I am an Englishman, and as good as you."

"To what the devil does this tend, sir? And how does the secret you have discovered concern me? I should like to know," demanded Major Pendennis with majestic indignation.

"How does it concern me, indeed! How grand we are! How does it concern my nephew? How does it concern my nephew's seat in Parliament? And what of subornation of bigamy? How does that concern me? What, are you to be the only man to possess a secret and trade upon it? Why shouldn't I go halves, Major Pendennis? I've found it out too. Look here! I'm not going to be unreasonable. Make it worth my while, and I'll keep the matter close. Let Mr. Arthur take his seat and his wealthy wife, if you prefer; I have no intention of marrying her. But I will have my share, as sure as my name is James Morgan. And if I don't—"

"And if you don't, sir—what?" Pendennis asked.

"If I don't, I split and tell all. I'll smash Clavering and have him and his wife prosecuted for bigamy—so help me, I will! I'll expose young Hopeful's marriage and reveal that you and he are using this secret merely to squeeze a parliamentary seat out of Sir Francis and a fortune out of his wife."

"Mr. Pendennis knows no more of this business than the babe unborn, sir!" cried the Major, aghast. "Not more than Lady Clavering, nor Miss Amory."

"Tell that to the marines, Major," retorted the valet. "That cock won't fight with me."

"Do you doubt my word, you villain?"

"No bad language. I don't care one penny whether your word is true or not. I tell you, I intend this to be a nice little annuity for me, Major. For I have everyone of you. I'm not such a fool as to let you escape. I'd say you might make it five hundred a year among you, easy. Pay me down the first quarter now, and I'll be as mute as a mouse. Just give a note for one thousand two hundred and fifty. Here's your cheque-book on your desk."

"And there's this too, you villain!" shouted the old gentleman.

Pointing to the desk, Morgan indicated a small double-barreled pistol that had belonged to Pendennis's old patron, the Indian Commander-in-Chief, and which had accompanied him through countless campaigns.

"One more word, you scoundrel, and I'll shoot you like a mad dog. Stop—by Jove, I'll do it now. You'll assault me, will you? You'll strike at an old man, will you, you lying coward? Kneel down and say your prayers, sir, for by the Lord you shall die."
