To execute the grand design of his stratagem, Martin required a specific alibi: he must remain invisible to the outside world. Consequently, the morning sun found no welcome in his stomach; appetite fled him entirely. Just as the bell for the grammar school began to toll, a sharp, searing agony seized his heart—a physical manifestation of his internal plotting. It was medically advisable, he decided, to abandon the journey to school alongside Mark and instead inherit his father's armchair by the fireside, surrendering himself to the comforting weight of the morning paper. With this tactical retreat secured, and Mark dispatched to Mr. Summer's class while Matthew and Mr. Yorke retreated to the counting-house, Martin faced a quartet of remaining objectives.

The primary mission was immediate: to consume the breakfast he had denied himself, a caloric necessity for a boy of fifteen whose energy reserves were dangerously depleted. The secondary, tertiary, and quaternary missions involved orchestrating the removal of his mother, Miss Moore, and Mrs. Horsfall from the domestic sphere before the clock struck four.

The hunger warred with the plot, but the plot ultimately prevailed. Martin navigated the familiar corridors to the larder, finding the kitchen staff locked in solemn communion behind closed doors, while his mother and Miss Moore strolled the lawn, oblivious to the conspiracy unfolding within. Inside the pantry, Martin exercised the fastidious palate of a gourmet. His usual diet of bland bread and milk was deemed insufficient; he sought the *recherché*. From shelves lined with straw, he plucked three rosy apples. From a dish of pastry, he claimed an apricot puff and a damson tart. His gaze lingered disdainfully on the common loaf but descended upon currant tea-cakes, selecting one with condescending grace. Armed with his clasp-knife, he carved a wing of fowl and a slice of ham, rounding out his spoils with a cantle of cold custard pudding. Satiated and armed, he emerged into the hall.

He was halfway across the threshold—three steps from the sanctuary of the back parlour—when the front door swung open, revealing Matthew. Had the Old Gentleman arrived in full regalia of horns, hooves, and tail, the outcome might have differed, but Matthew, the skeptic and scoffler, presented a different challenge. Already, Matthew had refused to subscribe to the narrative of cardiac distress, muttering phrases like "shamming Abraham" with distinct clarity. The sight of Martin seated in the armchair, reading the paper, had induced mental spasms in the younger man. Now, confronted with the visual evidence of Martin's feast—the apples, the tarts, the poultry, the ham, the pudding—Matthew's confidence in his own sagacity swelled to dangerous proportions.

Martin paused, momentarily *interdit*, paralyzed by the unexpected variable. Then, in an instant, he regained his footing. Possessing the true perspicacity of elite souls, he recognized the opportunity hidden within this collision. How could this untoward event be transmuted into an asset? He envisioned a scenario where the confrontation would serve his second objective: the disposal of his mother. He understood the psychological levers at play; a clash between brother and brother invariably triggered Mrs. Yorke's propensity for hysteria. And on the principle that calm follows the storm, a morning of hysterical fits guaranteed an afternoon of somnolent repose. This was the perfect accommodation.

The collision occurred precisely as predicted in the hall. A dry laugh, an insulting sneer, a contemptuous taunt met with a nonchalant yet cutting retort served as the signal. They rushed together. Martin, usually a ghost of silence during such skirmishes, erupted into a cacophony. Servants poured in, followed by Mrs. Yorke and Miss Moore; no female hand could sever the combatants. Mr. Yorke was summoned.

"Sons," he declared, "one of you must vacate my roof if this recurrence continues. I will tolerate no Cain and Abel strife beneath my eaves."

Martin allowed himself to be led away. He feigned injury, playing the role of the youngest and slightest victim. Yet, internally, he was cool, devoid of passion, even smiling with contentment that the most arduous phase of his labor was complete.

For a brief interval, the morning seemed to falter.
"It is not worth bothering myself for that Caroline," he remarked dismissively. Yet, merely a quarter-hour later, he returned to the dining room, staring at the head with disheveled tresses and eyes clouded with despair.
"Yes," he murmured, "I made her sob, shudder, nearly faint. I shall see her smile before I finish with her; besides, I intend to outwit all these womenites."

True to his calculations, directly after dinner, Mrs. Yorke retired to her chamber. Now, the hunt turned to Hortense.

Hortense was comfortably ensconced in the back parlour, repairing stockings, when Martin approached. Laying aside a book he had been perusing with the voluptuous ease of a callow pasha, stretched languidly on the sofa despite his claimed indisposition, he lazily initiated a discourse concerning Sarah, the maid at the Hollow. Through much verbal meandering, he insinuated intelligence regarding Sarah's three suitors—Frederic Murgatroyd, Jeremiah Pighills, and John-of-Mally's-of-Hannah's-of-Deb's—and revealed that Miss Mann had confirmed the girl, now sole keeper of the cottage, frequently entertained these swains with the finest provisions the house could offer.

It required no further provocation. Hortense could not endure another hour without hastening to the scene of these nefarious transactions to inspect the state of affairs personally. Mrs. Horsfall remained behind.

Now master of the field, Martin extracted a bunch of keys from his mother's work-basket. With these, he unlocked the sideboard cupboard, retrieving a black bottle and a small glass, placing them upon the table. Nimbly mounting the stairs, he approached Mr. Moore's door and tapped. The nurse answered.
"If you please, ma'am, you are invited to step into the back parlour and take some refreshment. You will not be disturbed; the family is out."
He watched her descend, watched her enter, and then sealed the door behind her. She was secure.

The arduous labor concluded; now came the pleasure. Snatching his cap, he departed for the wood.

It was barely half-past three. The morning had begun fair, but the sky now wore a darkened hue. Snow began to fall; the wind bit with cold ferocity. The wood appeared dismal, the ancient tree grim. Yet, Martin approved of the shadows stretching across his path. He found a peculiar charm in the spectral aspect of the doddered oak.

He was forced to wait. Walking to and fro as the flakes intensified, he listened to the wind shift from a mournful moan to a pitiful howl.
"She is tardy," he muttered, glancing along the narrow track. "I wonder," he added, "what compels me to desire her presence so acutely? She is not coming for me. But I hold power over her, and I require her to arrive so that I may wield that power."
He continued his pacing.
"Now," he resumed after a further lapse of time, "should she fail to appear, I shall hate and scorn her."

Four o'clock struck. The distant chime of the church clock resonated through the air. A step, so swift and light that, save for the rustling of leaves, it would have been imperceptible on the woodland path, checked his impatience. The wind blew fiercely now, and the thickening white storm grew bewildering; yet she advanced, undismayed.
"Well, Martin," she said eagerly, "how is he?"
"It is curious how she thinks of *him*," Martin reflected internally. "The blinding snow and bitter cold are nothing to her, I believe; yet she is but a 'chitty-faced creature,' as my mother would say. I could find in my heart to wish I had a cloak to wrap her in."
Thus meditating, he neglected to answer Miss Helstone.
"You have seen him?"
"No."
"Oh! You promised you would."
"I intend to surpass that promise. Did I not say *I* do not care to see him?"
"But now, it will be so long before I learn anything certain about him, and I am weary of waiting. Martin, *please* see him. Give him Caroline Helstone's regards, tell him she wishes to know how he fares, and if anything can be done for his comfort."
"I refuse."
"You are changed. You were so friendly last night."
"Come, we cannot stand in this wood; it is too cold."
"But before I depart, promise me to return tomorrow with news."
"No such thing. I am far too delicate to make and keep such appointments in the winter season. If you knew the pain in my chest this morning, how I skipped breakfast, and how I was knocked down besides, you would feel the impropriety of dragging me here into the snow. Come, I say."
"Are you truly delicate, Martin?"
"Don't I look it?"
"You have rosy cheeks."
"That is hectic. Will you come—or will you not?"
"Where?"
"With me. I was a fool not to bring a cloak. I would have made you cozy."
"You are heading home; my nearest road lies in the opposite direction."
