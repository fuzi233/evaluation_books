As Pen stood outside Warrington's chambers, dressed in his morning attire, he made his way upstairs to share the news of the previous evening's encounter with his uncle. Upon entering, he found the laundress, Mrs. Flanagan, the sole occupant of the room. George had departed, leaving behind a carpet-bag and a trail of scattered papers. "I chanced upon him sitting at his desk," Mrs. Flanagan said, "engrossed in his writing, the flame of one candle reduced to a mere ember." Indeed, George had spent the night at his desk, driven by an insatiable passion to complete his latest work. The night wore on, and as the first light of dawn crept into the room, George's fervor remained unabated, fueled by the weight of his thoughts.

The articles and reviews awaiting delivery on the table bore witness to his diligence. As a writer, George was accustomed to pouring his heart and soul onto the page, often veiling his true emotions beneath a veneer of wit and satire. Few were privy to the turmoil that accompanied him as he worked, the shadows of melancholy and bitterness that occasionally crept into his words. It was as if the writer's mask hid a deeper, more somber reality, one that only those closest to him could discern. Like a harlequin behind his mask, each writer must confront the solitary struggles that accompany their craft – the weight of responsibility, the ache of loss, and the relentless pursuit of creation.

As Pen surveyed the room, his gaze fell upon the remnants of George's departure: books, torn manuscript fragments, and the scattered detritus of his writing process. He settled into George's chair, taking in the familiar scene. A gap in the bookcase, once occupied by Helen's Bible, now stood as a poignant reminder of George's absence. Pen's thoughts turned to his friend's character, and he couldn't help but feel a sense of awe and admiration. Where could one find a companion as noble, as kind, and as selfless as George? Where could one discover a voice so frank, a laughter so warm, and a heart so true? Pen's reverie was interrupted by the realization that he was but a pale reflection of his friend, and that he could only hope to aspire to George's level of greatness.
