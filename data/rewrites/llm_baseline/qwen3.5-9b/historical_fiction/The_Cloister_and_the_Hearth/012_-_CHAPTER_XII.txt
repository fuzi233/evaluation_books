Ghysbrecht Van Swieten clutched the key to Gerard's prison within the folds of his tunic, waiting until the clock struck ten before making his descent. "A touch of hunger serves a purpose," he muttered to himself, "it softens their resolve." At the appointed hour, he ascended the stairwell, carrying a loaf of bread and a water pitcher, trailed by his loyal servant, Dierich, who moved with a weapon drawn tight against his hip. Ghysbrecht paused at the heavy oak door, listening intently. Silence answered him. A cold, grim smile twisted his features. "By now, he must be as despondent as poor Albert Koestein," he reflected.

With a slow turn of the lock, he pushed the door open. The cell was vacant. Ghysbrecht froze, his posture stiffening until he appeared carved from stone. Then, slowly, the rigidity broke; he collapsed to his knees, trembling violently. Behind him, Dierich sensed the shift in his master's demeanor and stepped forward, peering over the old man's shoulder. Upon seeing the empty cot, the discarded rope, and the removed iron bar, the servant let out a gasp of astonishment. Yet, his awe doubled when he witnessed his master, ignoring everything else, hurling himself before the hollow chest. With quivering fingers, Ghysbrecht frantically patted every inch of the wooden surface, refusing to believe his own eyes.

"Master?" Dierich ventured, bewildered. "What troubles you?"

Ghysbrecht's pale lips moved, forming words that never escaped his throat. His hands dropped limply to his sides, and he stared vacantly into the void of the chest.

"Why stare at that empty box?" Dierich pressed, frustration rising. "The boy is gone. Look! Note the cleverness of the rascal; he has removed the bar—"

"GONE!" Ghysbrecht shrieked, the word tearing from his throat. "GONE! GONE!"

"Gone? What is gone, holy saints? Has he been struck blind?"

"THIEF!" Ghysbrecht roared, spinning around to seize Dierich by the collar and shaking him with furious energy. "Do you stand there, knave, while your master is robbed? Run! Fly! Offer a hundred crowns to anyone who brings him back. No, it is futile. Oh, fool! Foolishness to leave that in the same room with him! But none ever discovered the hidden latch before. Only he could have done it. It was meant to happen. It is happening. Lost! Lost!"

As the old man's brief frenzy gave way to the weight of his age and infirmity, he slumped onto the chest, whispering brokenly, "Lost! Lost!"

"What is lost, master?" Dierich asked gently.

"My house, my lands, and my reputation," Ghysbrecht groaned, wringing his hands weakly.

"WHAT?" Dierich cried, his voice cracking with shock.

The intensity of the question, laced with genuine concern, pierced through Ghysbrecht's despair and reignited his natural cunning. "I have lost the town records," he stammered, casting a furtive glance at the servant like a fox cornered near a henhouse.

"Oh, is that all?"

"Is it not enough? How will the burghers react? What will the council do?" Suddenly, his composure returned. "A hundred crowns to whoever recovers them! Every single item contained in this box. If even one piece is missing, I pay nothing."

"'Tis a bargain, master," Dierich replied smoothly, reaching into his pouch. "The hundred crowns are right here. Do you not see those sheets of sheepskin you hold in such high regard?"

"That is true, that is true, good Dierich. My faithful Dierich. All of it, mind you, all that was in the chest."

"Master, I shall take the constables straight to Gerard's dwelling and arrest him for the theft."

"Theft? Yes, excellent. Very good. It is theft. I had forgotten. Since he is now a thief, we shall cast him into the dungeons below, among the toads and the rats. Dierich, that man must never see the light of day again. It is his own doing; he was prying. Haste, haste! Before he has time to speak, you understand—time to talk."

Within half an hour, Dierich Brower led four constables to the hosier's residence, demanding the presence of young Gerard from the terrified Catherine.

"Alas, what has he done now?" she wailed. "That boy will break my heart."

"Nay, dame, merely a youthful prank," Dierich assured her, smoothing his coat. "He has simply absconded with some parchment skins, likely in a fit of merriment. However, the Burgomaster is accountable to the city for their safekeeping, hence his anxiety. As for the youth, he will surely receive a mere reprimand."

These polished words lulled Catherine into relief, though her daughter remained wary. Her suspicions hardened further upon learning that Gerard was not at home and had not returned that evening.

"Come along," Dierich commanded roughly. "We waste precious time." He added, with vehemence, "I shall find him if he breathes air anywhere."

Affinity sharpens the intellect, often enabling the innocent to outwit the scheming. Just as Dierich prepared to depart, Kate signaled him to wait. He dismissed his men and stepped outside. She approached him quietly.

"Hush!" she whispered. "My mother knows nothing. Gerard has fled Tergou."

"How?"

"I saw him last night."

"Ay! Where?" Dierich asked, his interest piqued.

"At the base of the haunted tower."

"How did he manage to get the rope?"

"I do not know; however, I know this: my brother Gerard bid me farewell there, and he is leagues away from Tergou by now. You know the town was never worthy of him. When they imprisoned him, he swore never to return. Let the Burgomaster rest easy; he has imprisoned him, and in doing so, banished him from his birthplace and homeland. Why rob him further, and stain our own good name?"

Had this occurred at another time, Dierich might have acknowledged the logic. Yet, mortified by Gerard's escape and the potential loss of a hundred crowns, he snapped.

"Why did he need to steal?" he retorted bitterly. "Gerard did not steal the trash; he took it solely to spite the Burgomaster, who stole his freedom. But he shall answer to the Duke for it. As for these parchment skins you fuss over so much, check the nearest stream or pigsty; chances are you will find them there."

"Do you truly think so, mistress? Think you so?" Dierich's eyes flashed with sudden suspicion. "Perhaps you know the truth."

"This I know," Kate insisted, "that Gerard is too noble to steal, and too wise to burden himself with worthless rubbish on a journey."

"Then good day to you," Dierich said sharply. "Those sheepskins you despise, I value more highly than the hide of any beast in Tergou." With that, he hurried off, chasing a phantom trail.

Kate retreated indoors and pulled Giles aside. "Giles, my heart forebodes ill. Speak to no soul of what I tell you. I lied to Dirk Brower, saying Gerard has left Holland, yet I strongly suspect he is not a league from Tergou."

"Why, where is he, then?"

"Where else could he be, but with the woman he loves? But if so, he must not linger. These are deep, dark, and wicked men hunting him. Giles, I see the fear in Dirk Brower's eyes, and it makes me tremble. Oh, why cannot I flee to Sevenbergen and summon him away? Why am I not robust and agile like other maidens? God forgive me for fretting against His will; but I have never felt until now the agony of being lame, weak, and useless. But you are strong, dear Giles," she coaxed, "very strong."

"Yes, I am strong," Perpusillus thundered, then, noticing her implication, grumbled, "but I detest traveling on foot."

"Alas, alas! Who will aid me if you refuse?" she pleaded. "Dear Giles, do you not love Gerard?"

"Yes, I favor him most of all. I will ride to Sevenbergen on Peter Buyskens's mule. I shall ask him, for he will not lend it to me otherwise."

Kate protested. The entire town would follow his tracks. It would become known where he had gone, placing Gerard in greater peril than before. Giles deflected her worry by promising to ride out of the town in the opposite direction, turning the mule's head away from Sevenbergen only once he had shed the curious gaze of the populace.

Kate agreed, borrowing the mule. She entrusted Giles with a brief, significant message, drilling him to repeat it word for word until he mastered it perfectly. As Giles mounted the animal, little Kate withdrew to perform the final act in her power for her beloved brother: she knelt and prayed long and fervently for his safety.
