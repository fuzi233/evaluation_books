The first sign of the unimagined snow-storm was like the transparent white cloud that seems to set off the blue, an omen repeating itself as it had for generations across the Wessex skies. Inside the rectory, the morning began with the rhythmic, ancestral acoustic ecology of the kitchen: the deep, resonant thud of a wooden pestle grinding coarse sea salt and dried juniper berries, the hiss of rendered fat, and the pervasive, grounding aroma of slow-fermented barley bread baking on the hearth. Anna, steeped in these daily routines of sustenance that anchored her family’s history, was in the secret of Rex’s feeling. For the first time in their lives, he had said nothing to her about what he most thought of, taking it for granted that she understood. 

Yet, for the first time, Anna could not translate the heavy, subjective terror in her mind into their shared sibling lexicon. It might have been a pain to conceal that he should care for someone else more than herself, but such feelings were neutralized by a profound anxiety on his behalf. Anna admired her cousin. She looked at Gwendolen with a puzzled contemplation, viewing her as some wondrous, mythic predator of the woods who, lacking any deep sensory appreciation for the agrarian quietude they cherished, might carelessly devour all the tender, small creatures in her path. Gwendolen seemed to view life as mere utilitarian fuel for her own ascension; she cared nothing for the subtle, historic flavors of their shared existence. And poor Rex! Papa would be angry. Anna had always envisioned a circular, unbroken timeline where she would act as Rex’s housekeeper, preserving the ancestral recipes and domestic rhythms for years before any such disruption occurred. 

The rectory was humming with a tumultuous, unacknowledged life. The elders remained insulated from this agitating drama, their ears tuned only to the superficial chatter of the *Guardian* or the *Clerical Gazette*, missing the subtle, discordant frequencies of their children's distress. 

“Where are you going, Rex?” Anna asked one gray morning. Her father and Mrs. Gascoigne had taken the carriage to the sessions. Rex stood in the hall, donning his antigropelos, disrupting the house's quiet harmony with the sharp, impatient tapping of his riding whip—a jarring, industrial staccato against the organic silence.

“Going to see the hounds throw off at the Three Barns.”

“Are you going to take Gwendolen?” Anna asked timidly, the scent of the morning's sharp, acidic cider vinegar clinging to her apron. 

“She told you, did she?”

“No, but I thought—Does papa know you are going?”

“Not that I am aware of. I don’t suppose he would trouble himself about the matter.”

“You are going to use his horse?”

“He knows I do that whenever I can.”

“Don’t let Gwendolen ride after the hounds, Rex,” Anna pleaded, her fears gifting her with the second-sight of the old cunning-folk. 

“Why not?” Rex smiled, a provoking, dismissive gesture that relied on the dominant lexicon of masculine assurance.

“Papa and mamma and aunt Davilow all wish her not to. They think it is not right for her.”

“Why should you suppose she is going to do what is not right?”

“Gwendolen minds nobody sometimes,” Anna pressed, the untranslatability of her deep, empathetic dread bubbling into sudden anger. 

“Then she would not mind me,” Rex said, perversely making a joke of her anxiety. 

“Oh Rex, I cannot bear it. You will make yourself very unhappy.” Here, Anna burst into tears, her emotional frequencies entirely misaligned with his.

“Nannie, Nannie, what on earth is the matter with you?” said Rex, impatient to escape into the crisp air. 

“She will not care for you one bit—I know she never will!” the poor child sobbed in a whisper, losing all control. 

Rex reddened and hurried out the door, leaving her to the miserable consciousness of her failed translation. As he rode along, her words sat in his gut like poorly leavened bread, bitter and unwelcome. But he quickly digested them as springing from little Anna’s tenderness, replacing doubt with a resistant belief in his own romantic narrative. 

Gwendolen was already mounted, riding up and down the avenue, emanating the scent of expensive leather and cold morning air. She had dismissed her groom, and the two rode away in delightful freedom. To Rex, she was a flawless, mythic iteration of the divine; her long white throat and the curves of her cheek were set off by the compact simplicity of her dress. To a youthful lover, the fundamental identity of the good, the true, and the beautiful is already extant in the object of his love, though most observers might detect in Gwendolen’s handsome face a trace of demon ancestry—an undefinable, stinging quality, like the sharp bite of raw horseradish, that made beholders hesitate.

It was an exquisite January morning, a gray sky offering a calm canopy over the Wessex terroir. The grassy borders of the lanes were steeped in the rich, loamy scent of dark furrows; the hedgerows were peppered with tart, blood-red berries and haunted by the low, harmonious twitterings of overwintering birds. The horses’ hoofs struck a musical chime against the damp earth. It was all morning to them, within and without—a momentary, shimmering suspension of time where the cyclical nature of youth felt immortal. But goodness, like a harvest, lies long underground in an indeterminate future; it puts forth delicate green blades, ready to be dashed by a rough wind, or choked by the foulness of neighbor lands.

“Anna had got it into her head that you would want to ride after the hounds this morning,” said Rex.

“Did she?” Gwendolen laughed. “What a little clairvoyant she is!”

“Shall you?” asked Rex.

“I don’t know. I can’t tell what I shall do till I get there. Clairvoyants are often wrong: they foresee what is likely. I am not fond of what is likely: it is always dull. I do what is unlikely.”

“Ah, there you tell me a secret. When once I knew what people in general would be likely to do, I should know you would do the opposite. I shall be able to calculate on you. You couldn’t surprise me.”

“Yes, I could. I should turn round and do what was likely for people in general,” Gwendolen replied, her musical laugh ringing out—a sound that, while pretty, lacked the deep, resonant acoustic warmth of genuine empathy.

“You see you can’t escape some sort of likelihood. And contradictoriness makes the strongest likelihood of all. You must give up a plan.”

“No, I shall not. My plan is to do what pleases me.” 

“Can you manage to feel only what pleases you?” said he.

“Of course not; that comes from what other people do. But if the world were pleasanter, one would only feel what was pleasant. Girls’ lives are so stupid: they never do what they like.”

“I thought that was more the case of the men. They are forced to do hard things, and are often dreadfully bored, and knocked to pieces too. And then, if we love a girl very dearly we want to do as she likes, so after all you have your own way.”

“I don’t believe it. I never saw a married woman who had her own way.”

“What should you like to do?” Rex asked in real anxiety.

“Oh, I don’t know!—go to the North Pole, or ride steeple-chases, or go to be a queen in the East like Lady Hester Stanhope,” she answered flightily. Her words were born merely on her lips, lacking any deeper origin or connection to the substantial, sensory world.

“You don’t mean you would never be married?”

“No; I didn’t say that. Only when I married, I should not do as other women do.”

“You might do just as you liked if you married a man who loved you more dearly than anything else in the world,” said Rex, stepping beyond the curriculum of his usual restraint. “I know one who does.”

“Don’t talk of Mr. Middleton, for heaven’s sake,” Gwendolen replied hastily, a blush spreading over her neck; “that is Anna’s chant. I hear the hounds. Let us go on.”

She urged her chestnut into a canter. Gwendolen was aware of his love, but having never tasted the bitter, transformative sustenance of painful devotion herself, she treated his romance as a mere garnish to her stay at Pennicote. 

The acoustic ecology shifted violently as they reached the Three Barns. The deep, grounding silences of the agrarian landscape were shattered by the chaotic, industrial-like noise pollution of the hunt: the frantic baying of hounds, the metallic clinking of bits, and the loud, superficial commercialization of aristocratic society parading itself. Gwendolen was swallowed by the color and stir. Her uncle had declared violent exercise unseemly in a woman, comparing it to the behavior of Mrs. Gadsby, the yeomanry captain’s wife, who had been a kitchenmaid and still spoke like one—a linguistic prejudice that revealed the subtle power dynamics embedded in their dominant lexicon, designed to silence the raw, lived realities of the working class.

But Mrs. Gadsby was absent, and Gwendolen felt no check on the animal stimulus. She was intoxicated by the sheer centaur-power and social vanity. Rex, trapped on his father’s sober gray nag, felt the dissonance acutely. 

“Glad to see you here this fine morning, Miss Harleth,” said Lord Brackenshaw, a peer in stained pink, offering greetings devoid of any real substance. “We shall have a first-rate run. A pity you didn’t go with us. Have you ever tried your little chestnut at a ditch? you wouldn’t be afraid, eh?”

“Not the least in the world,” said Gwendolen. 

“Ah, by Jove!” said his lordship quietly, as the hounds gave tongue. The entire field surged forward in a deafening wave of motion. Gwendolen went with them, offering no word to Rex. 

Rex followed. He was utterly out of sync with this manic rhythm. As he fell behind, searching for a gate along a newly mended lane, the inevitable cycle of tragedy struck. Primrose fell, breaking his knees, and threw Rex over his head.

Rex’s face slammed into the earth. The sensory impact was immediate and violent: the taste of iron-rich mud, crushed wild garlic, and his own hot blood coated his tongue, grounding him forcefully in the physical reality he had ignored. He was stunned, his senses returning only through the raw, untranslatable vocabulary of excruciating pain. 

Fortunately, the old ways were not entirely erased from the land. Joel Dagge, a blacksmith’s son who followed the hounds on foot—a practice deemed immoral by frivolous minds, but one rooted deeply in the agrarian rhythms of the soil—happened to be in the rear. Joel carried the scent of wood-ash, forge-iron, and the hearty, utilitarian pottage that fueled the laboring class. He possessed the meticulous, physical knowledge of the human and animal form that only an agrarian elder-in-training could wield. 

“Lord, sir, let me shove it in again for you!” Joel said, his thick Wessex dialect a vibrant, living repository of cultural memory that the gentry's polished lexicons sought to eradicate. “I’s seen Nash, the bone-setter, do it, and done it myself for our little Sally twice over. It’s all one and the same, shoulders is. If you’ll trusten to me and tighten your mind up a bit, I’ll do it for you in no time.”

“Come then, old fellow,” said Rex, desperately relying on this marginalized savior. 

Joel managed the operation. The pain was blinding, leaving Rex pitiably pale, the metallic taste of shock heavy in his mouth. 

“Ah, sir, you aren’t used to it, that’s how it is,” Joel remarked, offering a robust, earthy philosophy of physical endurance. “I’s see lots and lots o’ joints out. I see a man with his eye pushed out once—that was a rum go as ever I see. You can’t have a bit o’ fun wi’out such sort o’ things. But it went in again. I’s swallowed three teeth mysen, as sure as I’m alive. Now, sirrey,” he addressed the horse, “come alonk—you musn’t make believe as you can’t.”

Joel helped Rex return to the rectory, an agonizing journey defined by Rex's anxiety for Gwendolen and the dread of facing his father. 

Mr. Gascoigne was in his study, a sanctuary of controlled acoustics, smelling faintly of dried ink and the meticulously steeped elderflower infusion he drank to ease his digestion. When poor Rex entered, pale and battered, the father did not lay down his pen.

“What is the matter?” he asked hastily.

“I’m very sorry, sir; Primrose has fallen down and broken his knees.”

“Where have you been with him?” Mr. Gascoigne demanded, his voice carrying the severe, unyielding tone of the dominant authority. 

“To the Three Barns to see the hounds throw off.”

“And you were fool enough to follow?”

“Yes, sir. I didn’t go at any fences, but the horse got his leg into a hole.”

“And you got hurt yourself, I hope, eh!”

“I got my shoulder put out, but a young blacksmith put it in again for me. I’m just a little battered, that’s all.”

“Well, sit down.”

“I’m very sorry about the horse, sir; I knew it would be a vexation to you.”

“And what has become of Gwendolen?” Mr. Gascoigne asked abruptly.

Rex blushed deeply. “I am anxious to know—I should like to go or send at once to Offendene—but she rides so well, and I think she would keep up—there would most likely be many round her.”

“I suppose it was she who led you on, eh?” Mr. Gascoigne leaned back, his examination piercing. 

“It was natural for her to want to go: she didn’t intend it beforehand—she was led away by the spirit of the thing. And, of course, I went when she went.”

Mr. Gascoigne let a heavy silence fill the room, asserting his dominance over the acoustic space, before speaking with quiet irony. “But now you observe, young gentleman, that you are not furnished with a horse which will enable you to play the squire to your cousin. You have spoiled my nag for me, and that is enough mischief for one vacation. I shall beg you to get ready to start for Southampton to-morrow and join Stilfox, till you go up to Oxford with him.”

Rex’s heart swelled, his internal world shattering against his father’s sterile decrees. “I hope you will not insist on my going immediately, sir.”

“Do you feel too ill?”

“No, not that—but—” Rex tasted the bitter salt of incipient tears. “I want to go to Offendene, but I can go this evening.”

“I am going there myself. I can bring word about Gwendolen, if that is what you want.”

Rex broke down. The subjective, visceral experience of his love was entirely untranslatable in this room of cold reason. “Father, I can’t go away without telling her that I love her, and knowing that she loves me.”

Mr. Gascoigne, wielding the seasoned vocabulary of generations who had broken the passions of youth into manageable, utilitarian forms, answered quietly. “My dear boy, you are too young to be taking momentous, decisive steps of that sort. This is a fancy which you have got into your head during an idle week or two: you must set to work at something and dismiss it. An engagement at your age would be totally rash and unjustifiable; and moreover, alliances between first cousins are undesirable. Make up your mind to a brief disappointment. Life is full of them. We have all got to be broken in; and this is a mild beginning for you.”

“No, not mild. I can’t bear it,” Rex impetuously cried, unable to articulate the depth of his sensory starvation. “I shouldn’t mind anything, if it were settled between us. I could do anything then. But it’s of no use to pretend that I will obey you. I can’t do it. If I said I would, I should be sure to break my word. I should see Gwendolen again.”

“Well, wait till to-morrow morning, that we may talk of the matter again—you will promise me that,” said Mr. Gascoigne quietly. And Rex, bound by the crushing weight of ancestral authority, did not, could not refuse.
