To comprehend the geopolitical gravity of the moment depicted in Chapter XVI, one must first analyze the macroeconomic and strategic imperatives driving the English Crown during the late sixteenth century. The scene unfolds aboard the *Pelican*, a vessel preserved not merely as a maritime artifact but as a monument to the tangible results of state-sponsored exploration—a physical manifestation of the transition from feudal staticism to dynamic global expansion. The ship, currently moored at Deptford Creek, serves as a microcosm of the era's tension between imperial ambition and domestic stability. Its transformation into a venue for a Lord Mayor's feast illustrates the commodification of discovery; the "savory steams" issuing from former portholes represent the attempt to integrate the risks of overseas trade into the safe, regulated sphere of urban commerce.

At the head of this tableau sits Sir Edward Osborne, a figure embodying the synthesis of traditional chivalric virtue and emerging mercantile pragmatism. His presence anchors the narrative in the reality of London's commercial elite, whose wealth was increasingly derived from the very ventures discussed around the table. The conversation among the assembled dignitaries—Walter Raleigh, Anthony Jenkinson, and the scholars of Oxford—is not idle chatter but a rigorous exchange of intelligence regarding the "North-West Passage," a hypothetical route representing the ultimate solution to England's balance-of-trade deficits. Their discourse reflects a sophisticated understanding of cartography and geopolitics, grounded in the empirical observations of travelers like Jenkinson and the theoretical frameworks provided by figures such as Doctor Dee.

However, the idyllic atmosphere of this intellectual gathering is abruptly disrupted by the arrival of Amyas Leigh, signaling a shift from abstract planning to immediate crisis management. The revelation of Sir Humphrey Gilbert's death introduces a profound variable into the equation: the collapse of a key state asset. Gilbert's failure represents a significant sunk cost for the crown and its investors, challenging the optimistic projections of the "Westward-ho" ideology. The reaction of the attendees—their silence, the cessation of the feast, and the somber departure—demonstrates the psychological impact of such losses on the collective psyche of the ruling class. The metaphor of the Egyptian corpse at banquets serves as a didactic reminder of human mortality, grounding the lofty ambitions of empire in the harsh reality of biological limitation.

As the narrative transitions to the private quarters of the Leigh brothers, the focus shifts from national strategy to individual agency and familial obligation. The dialogue between Amyas and Frank reveals the internal conflict inherent in the pursuit of colonial expansion. Frank's physical deterioration—pale, translucent, and frail—stands in stark contrast to Amyas's robust, battle-hardened appearance, illustrating the physiological toll exacted by the climate and lifestyle of the court versus the rigors of active service. This dichotomy underscores the argument that survival in the New World requires a specific type of resilience, one that Frank lacks but feels compelled to acquire.

The philosophical debate regarding the "West" as a destination for peace and renewal is not merely poetic license but reflects contemporary eschatological beliefs. The reference to Elysium lying beneath the setting sun aligns with the period's fascination with the transformative potential of the Americas, viewed as a space where societal norms could be reset. Frank's invocation of Thales' hydrological philosophy—that the ocean is the mother of all life—provides a scientific justification for his willingness to risk his life, framing his journey not as suicide but as a necessary return to the source of vitality.

Ultimately, the resolution of this chapter hinges on the interplay between personal desire and external validation. The mother's intervention, informed by the astrological prognostications of Doctor Dee, acts as the final catalyst. Her acceptance of Frank's departure validates the family's participation in the broader project of English expansion, transforming a potentially tragic sacrifice into a divinely sanctioned mission. The narrative concludes with a reaffirmation of the "Westward-ho" trajectory, suggesting that despite individual costs, the movement toward the unknown remains the defining characteristic of the era's progress. The story thus serves as a case study in how historical actors navigated the intersection of faith, science, economics, and duty in the face of uncertainty.
