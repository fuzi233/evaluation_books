Late afternoon had thickened the air in the Via de’ Bardi until even the street seemed to breathe more slowly. The day’s brightness had sunk into that Florentine dusk which does not so much fall as steep itself—like saffron threads left too long in warm broth—turning edges and corners to blur, and making the lamps that were being lit below sound louder than they looked: flint on steel, the short flare, the tiny hungry hiss.

Romola sat in her narrow room opposite the cabinet, copying documents as she had done so often that the scratch of her pen had become a kind of household clock. The ink smelled faintly metallic; the parchment held a dry animal tang. Beside her was the small economy of any studious room—sand for drying, wax, twine—and the whole place carried the subdued hush of a convent cell, broken only by the low cough of the Arno in the distance and the occasional cry of a vendor turning a corner. That voice, that dialect sung out over stones and water, always pierced her with a strange tenderness; it was Florence speaking itself into being, syllable by syllable, as if memory depended on sound.

She was about to desist, because the light had grown too dim for accurate strokes, when Tito entered.

He came straight to that room as if he had aimed at it. He did not pause to call, nor did he lay aside cap and mantle. He leaned an elbow on the cabinet and stood directly before her, close enough that the chill damp of the street clung to him—wet wool, leather, and rain that had been in contact with old walls. His silence pressed on her like a weight set down without ceremony.

All day Romola had held fast to the assurance that the Frate was safe. With that certainty came reaction: penitence for the distrust and indignation that had driven her, in the morning, to speak publicly to her husband of what he wished to keep private. She told herself she had likely been wrong. It was easy—too easy—to imagine that the scheming duplicity of parties, which even her godfather had spoken of as inseparable from Florentine tactics, might account for Tito’s connection with Spini without proving that Tito meant to further a plot. She had been rehearsing words of atonement, tasting them in her mind like bread one offers after bitterness, hoping they might break the two years’ silence between them.

That silence had been complete. Tito did not even know she had once fled and come back again. They had never approached an avowal of that past which, in its young love and in the shock that shattered love, lay shut away like a banquet-room after death has broken the feast: tables still set in memory, but the air spoiled.

Romola lifted her eyes with the submissive look born of self-reproach; but something in Tito’s face arrested her speech. The blandness she remembered had drawn itself tight. There was a hidden tension in him, as when a man’s arm appears calm but the muscles under the skin are working at some silent violence.

For some moments they only looked at each other. Romola heard, with a kind of cruel distinctness, the house’s small sounds: a servant moving far below, the soft knock of a pot-lid, and then—thin and fragrant through the corridor—the ghost of supper beginning: oil warming in a pan, garlic bruised, the first sharp sweetness of onion meeting heat. Domestic routine went on, faithful as the bells; and yet in this room the air felt as if it had been cleared for judgment.

Tito, too, felt a crisis had come.

“Romola,” he began, in that cool liquid tone which made her shiver, “it is time that we should understand each other.”

“That is what I most desire, Tito,” she said, faintly.

Her face, pale and sweet with all its anger gone and only the timidity of self-doubt remaining, gave a marked predominance to his dark strength.

“You took a step this morning,” Tito continued, “which you must now yourself perceive to have been useless—which exposed you to remark and may involve me in serious practical difficulties.”

“I acknowledge that I was too hasty,” Romola said. “I am sorry for any injustice I may have done you.”

She spoke more firmly, as if the very act of confession ought to soften him. She watched for it the way one watches the surface of wine for a change of light—an answering sign.

None came.

“I wish you once for all to understand,” he said, without any change of voice, “that such collisions are incompatible with our position as husband and wife. I wish you to reflect on the mode in which you were led to that step, that the process may not be repeated.”

“That depends chiefly on you, Tito,” Romola answered, taking fire in spite of herself.

It was not what she had meant to say. But speech between two souls long divided does not follow the path one has laid out; it trips, it jolts, it strikes sparks from flint.

“You would say, I suppose,” Tito replied, “that nothing is to occur in future which can excite your unreasonable suspicions. You were frank enough to say last night that you have no belief in me. I am not surprised at any exaggerated conclusion you may draw from slight premises, but I wish to point out to you what is likely to be the fruit of your making such exaggerated conclusions a ground for interfering in affairs of which you are ignorant. Your attention is thoroughly awake to what I am saying?”

“Yes,” Romola said, flushing—resentment rising in her throat at this cold superiority that treated her mind as if it were a child’s hand snatching at knives.

“Well, then,” he went on, “it may possibly not be very long before some other chance words or incidents set your imagination at work devising crimes for me, and you may perhaps rush to the Palazzo Vecchio to alarm the Signoria and set the city in an uproar. Shall I tell you what may be the result? Not simply the disgrace of your husband, to which you look forward with so much courage, but the arrest and ruin of many among the chief men in Florence, including Messer Bernardo del Nero.”

The name struck her like a slap. The flush died; her lips went pale, which was unusual with her—she was not made for fear. But Tito’s words had brought up consequences with the terrible clearness of a map unrolled: corridors of power, doors broken, men dragged, families left to hunger, the city’s great machine of rumor and punishment grinding on. Florence could be tender as a mother at table and cruel as a butcher in the square; it depended on who spoke first and in what language—who set the lexicon of guilt.

Tito saw his success and pressed it.

“You would perhaps flatter yourself,” he said, “that you were performing a heroic deed of deliverance. You might as well try to turn locks with fine words as apply such notions to the politics of Florence. The question now is, not whether you can have any belief in me, but whether, now you have been warned, you will dare to rush, like a blind man with a torch in his hand, amongst intricate affairs of which you know nothing.”

Romola felt as if her mind were held in a vice. She could almost hear the vice tightening—not in imagination only, but in the actual soundscape of the city outside: the steady hammering somewhere down the street, iron against iron; a cart’s wheel complaining; a boy’s voice rehearsing an oath he did not understand. A whole world trained to noise that drowned the delicate distinctions by which a conscience tries to live.

“I am too rash,” she said. “I will try not to be rash.”

“Remember,” Tito said, with unsparing insistence, “that your act of distrust towards me this morning might, for aught you knew, have had more fatal effects than that sacrifice of your husband which you have learned to contemplate without flinching.”

“Tito, it is not so,” Romola burst out, pleading, rising and going nearer, driven to speak by something desperate. “It is false that I would willingly sacrifice you. It has been the greatest effort of my life to cling to you. I went away in my anger two years ago, and I came back again because I was more bound to you than to anything else on earth. But it is useless. You shut me out from your mind. You affect to think of me as a being too unreasonable to share in the knowledge of your affairs. You will be open with me about nothing.”

She bent towards him with dilated eyes and laid her hand on his arm—an appeal made with the whole body, as if warmth and touch could translate what words had failed to carry. Her fingers met the texture of his sleeve, dampened at the edge, and beneath it the firm pressure of his forearm. In another life, that contact would have drawn tenderness like the scent drawn out of crushed herbs.

But Tito’s face did not soften. Romola’s touch and glance no longer stirred any fiber of tenderness in him. The good-humored, tolerant Tito who was gentle toward the world felt himself becoming strangely hard toward the wife whose presence had once been his strongest influence. He was not a man made for hatred—hardly even for impatience; yet there was in him a masculine effectiveness of intellect and purpose, an edge that could work its way quietly. Romola’s energy thwarted his. And no man, unless exceptionally feeble, endures being thwarted by his wife. Marriage, for most, must become either sympathy or conquest.

No emotion darted across his face at her first mention of having gone away. Only his lips set a little harder, and he smiled slightly, not with mirth but with the thinness of decision.

“My Romola,” he said, “when certain conditions are ascertained, we must make up our minds to them. No amount of wishing will fill the Arno, as your people say, or turn a plum into an orange. I have not observed even that prayers have much efficacy that way. You are so constituted as to have certain strong impressions inaccessible to reason. I cannot share those impressions, and you have withdrawn all trust from me in consequence. You have changed towards me; it has followed that I have changed towards you. It is useless to take any retrospect. We have simply to adapt ourselves to altered conditions.”

His tone made the words feel like a physician’s pronouncement over a body: nothing to be done but accommodate to loss.

“Tito, it would not be useless for us to speak openly,” Romola said, exasperation rising from the effort of pushing living feeling against a lifeless resistance. “It was the sense of deception in you that changed me, and that has kept us apart. And it is not true that I changed first. You changed towards me the night you first wore that chain-armour. You had some secret from me—it was about that old man—and I saw him again yesterday. Tito”—her voice tightened with entreaty—“if you would once tell me everything, let it be what it may—I would not mind pain—that there might be no wall between us! Is it not possible that we could begin a new life?”

This time a flash crossed Tito’s face. He stood perfectly still, but the flash seemed to whiten him, as if a sudden cold had touched his blood. For a moment the whole room felt sharpened: the pen on the table, the grit of the drying sand between fingers, the faint smell of warmed oil drifting up from below as if supper were insisting on itself, indifferent to sin.

He did not answer her plea. After a pause, he said quietly, “Your impetuosity about trifles, Romola, has a freezing influence that would cool the baths of Nero.”

The phrase cut. Romola shrank, then drew herself up into her usual self-sustained posture, as if she must recover dignity the way one steadies a dish one has almost dropped.

Tito went on, his paleness governed now by will. “If by ‘that old man’ you mean the mad Jacopo di Nola who attempted my life and made a strange accusation against me—of which I told you nothing because it would have alarmed you to no purpose—he, poor wretch, has died in prison. I saw his name in the list of dead.”

“I know nothing about his accusation,” Romola said. “But I know he is the man whom I saw with the rope round his neck in the Duomo—the man whose portrait Piero di Cosimo painted, grasping your arm as he saw him grasp it the day the French entered, the day you first wore the armour.”

“And where is he now, pray?” Tito asked, still pale, but holding himself.

“He was lying lifeless in the street from starvation,” Romola said. The words brought back the sensation as if it were present: the stench of unwashed flesh and cold stone, the hollow sound of breath that was hardly sound at all, the way hunger makes even the air smell empty. “I revived him with bread and wine. I brought him to our door, but he refused to come in. Then I gave him some money, and he went away without telling me anything. But he had found out that I was your wife. Who is he?”

“A man,” Tito said, “half mad, half imbecile, who was once my father’s servant in Greece, and who has a rancorous hatred towards me because I got him dismissed for theft. Now you have the whole mystery—and the further satisfaction of knowing that I am again in danger of assassination. The fact of my wearing the armour, about which you seem to have thought so much, must have led you to infer that I was in danger from this man. Was that the reason you chose to cultivate his acquaintance and invite him into the house?”

Romola was mute. To speak was like rushing with bare breast against a shield; the shield did not even ring, it merely stopped you.

Tito moved from his leaning posture, slowly took off cap and mantle, and pushed back his hair. The gesture had the composure of someone who believes the matter is ended. Romola stood upright, looking at him as she might at an oncoming deadly force to be met only with silent endurance.

“We need not refer to these matters again, Romola,” he said, in the same tone with which he had first entered—precise, cool, and final. “It is enough if you will remember that the next time your generous ardour leads you to interfere in political affairs, you are likely, not to save any one from danger, but to be raising scaffolds and setting houses on fire. You are not yet a sufficiently ardent Piagnone to believe that Messer Bernardo del Nero is the prince of darkness, and Messer Francesco Valori the archangel Michael. I think I need demand no promise from you?”

“I have understood you too well, Tito,” she said.

“It is enough,” he answered, and left the room.

The door’s closing sounded louder than it should have—wood meeting wood, latch catching—like a sentence sealed. The quiet that followed was not peace but absence, as after a choir stops and the ear keeps listening for the missing harmony. From below came the ordinary music of the household: the clink of earthenware, the soft pour of liquid, the low exchange of servants in Florentine speech—words thick with local turns that could never be carried whole into any other tongue. Romola felt the cruelty of it: in the kitchen, things were still translatable—salt, oil, bread; in her marriage, nothing was.

She turned and sank into her seat, despair changing her limbs to heaviness.

“O God, I have tried—I cannot help it. We shall always be divided.”

The words passed silently through her mind. Then, as if startled by a sudden vision that forced itself into voice, she said aloud, “Unless—unless misery should come and join us!”

Tito, too, carried away a new thought when he had closed the door behind him. His project of leaving Florence—as soon as his life there had become a high enough stepping-stone to a life elsewhere, perhaps at Rome or Milan—now for the first time had attached itself to a desire to be free from Romola, to leave her behind. She had ceased to belong to the desirable furniture of his life. There was no possibility of an easy relation between them without genuineness on his part, and genuineness implied confession; confession meant a change of purpose. Tito had as little bent that way as a leopard has to lap milk when its teeth are grown.

From all relations that were not easy and agreeable, he shrank: why cling to them? And Romola had made his relations difficult with others besides herself. There had been a troublesome interview with Dolfo Spini, who had returned in a rage after an ineffectual soaking with rain and long waiting in ambush. That morning’s scene at Nello’s door, once carried into Spini’s ear, might sprout into something more unmanageable than suspicion—rumor, accusation, a vocabulary not of truth but of advantage.

Yet Tito believed, at least, that he had mastered Romola by a terror that appealed to the strongest forces of her nature. He had alarmed her affection and conscience with shadowy consequences; he had arrested her intellect by hanging before it the idea of hopeless complexity, as if moral judgment were a child’s game unsuited to the labyrinth of Florence.

Still, he was not at ease. The world was not yet quite cushioned with velvet; and even if it had been, he could not have abandoned himself to softness with thorough enjoyment.

For before he went out again that evening, he put on his coat of chain-armour.
