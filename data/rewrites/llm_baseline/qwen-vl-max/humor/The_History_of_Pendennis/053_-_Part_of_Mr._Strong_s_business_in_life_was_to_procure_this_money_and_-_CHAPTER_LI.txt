CHAPTER LI. The Happy Village Again—Or, How a Little Gossip Ruined a Gentleman

Early in this history, we’ve mentioned the little town of Clavering—nestled near Fairoaks like a well-behaved sheepdog beside a noble hound—and its inhabitants, whose lives were so uneventful that even their misfortunes seemed to happen in slow motion. We didn’t linger long over them before, because what’s there to say about people who live by the rhythms of the harvest and the church bell? But now, dear reader, we must return—because sometimes the most harmless villages become the stage for tragedies more absurd than any play at Drury Lane.

Take, for instance, Mr. Samuel Huxter—the same gentleman we met at Vauxhall, where he was trying to impress a lady with his knowledge of opera and his ability to name every wine on the menu. When not in London, Sam was a man of taste and wit in Clavering, where he’d descend upon friends with the air of a visiting philosopher, regaling them with stories from Bartholomew’s and the latest scandals from Mayfair. He’d been known to quote Byron while sipping tea and then immediately ask if anyone had seen the new issue of *The Times*.

Then there was Mr. Hobnell—the young man whom Arthur Pendennis once thrashed after a quarrel involving a certain Fotheringay affair (a story best left buried, like bad cheese). At the time, Hobnell was a grammar school boy, but already showing signs of becoming a gentleman: he knew where the tamarind pots were kept in Mrs. Huxter’s surgery, could scent his handkerchief with rosewater without spilling it on the carpet, and once saved a cat from a tree using only a walking stick and a dramatic flourish.

He also fell in love with Miss Sophy Huxter—Sam’s sister—and married her after his father passed away. That father had been a man of ambition: he tore down the old farmhouse, built a flashy whitewashed mansion with a piano in the drawing room (a scandal!), kept a pack of harriers, and insisted on being called “Squire” as though he’d inherited the title from King George himself. When the son took over, the family officially became “county gentry”—which meant they now owned a dog named “Buck,” wore waistcoats with embroidery, and believed they were above the local butcher.

Sam Huxter, naturally, liked to brag about his brother-in-law’s grandeur. “Hobnell’s got horses!” he’d announce at dinner parties. “And hounds! And a house with a veranda! He’s practically a duke.” His comrades would nod sagely, pretending to care, while secretly wondering if the piano really worked.

Every year, when Mrs. Hobnell was busy feeding babies or changing diapers (which she did with the grace of a queen), Squire Hobnell would come up to London for a week-long spree. He’d stay at the Tavistock Hotel, wear a cravat with a knot so tight it looked like it might strangle him, and spend evenings at Ascot, the theatres, and Vauxhall, always accompanied by Sam, who would narrate the events like a Greek historian covering a battle.

“Did you see the way he danced?” Sam would whisper. “It was like a bear trying to waltz—graceful, yet terrifying.”

When Hobnell returned to Clavering, he’d bring back a new bonnet for his wife and a shawl made of something suspiciously like goat hair. Then he’d vanish into the countryside, ready to hunt, drink brandy, and forget the city—except for the occasional letter to Sam, which usually began: “Dear Sam, I have just shot three pheasants and one rabbit. Also, the weather is frightfully cold. What news from London?”

Sam, meanwhile, sent baskets of game, cream, and gossip—especially gossip about Pen.

Ah, yes—Arthur Pendennis.

After their meeting at Vauxhall, Pen had vowed never to see little Fanny again. Not out of cruelty, mind you, but because he felt guilty. So he threw himself into work—writing scathing reviews for the *Pall Mall Gazette*, tearing apart poems and novels with such ferocity that one author wept into his soup. He dined alone at the Polyanthus Club, where the silence was so thick it could be cut with a knife. He went to the theatre, but found the audience laughing at jokes that seemed ridiculous to him. One scene, in particular—a man falling into a tub of water—made him think: “Why do they find this funny? It’s tragic. It’s pathetic. It’s… damp.”

Still, he needed distraction. So he headed to the Back Kitchen, his old haunt, where Bows played the piano and the air smelled of stale beer and regret. He sat down, ordered a whisky-and-water, and began to reflect on life, love, and how badly he’d handled things.

And then—of course—Mr. Huxter walked in.

Huxter, fresh from the theatre, greeted everyone with a wink and a grin. He saw Pen, nodded familiarly, and extended a hand covered in crumbs from a previous meal. Pen shook it, feeling awkward. He’d been harsh at Vauxhall, and now he regretted it. But Huxter, blissfully unaware of anything beyond his own charm, began chatting cheerfully.

“Jolly night at Vauxhall—wasn’t it?” he said, winking again. “I’m glad you enjoyed it. You were dancing with a lovely girl—what was her name? She had such nice muslin!”

Pen froze.

“That was *not* my fault,” he muttered.

“You’re quite the ladies’ man,” Huxter continued, oblivious. “I heard you were seen at a party with some young thing—must be hard to keep track of all of them.”

Pen stood up.

“Have I asked you any questions about yourself, Mr. Huxter?” he said coldly.

“No, no—I didn’t mean offence,” Huxter stammered. “Hang it, you’re acting like a grumpy old owl!”

“I beg your pardon,” Pen said, voice rising. “But I asked you not to speak to me. I asked you not to recognize me. And I’ll thank you to remember that.”

He turned and left, leaving behind a room full of laughter—because Huxter’s friends thought the whole thing was hilarious. The president of the club had to shout: “Silence, gentlemen! Silence for *The Body Snatcher!*”

Pen walked home, heart pounding. He wanted to fight someone. Or scream. Or throw something. But instead, he went to bed—and slept worse than ever.

A few days later, Sam Huxter wrote a letter to Hobnell in the country. It was full of outrage.

“Arthur Pendennis is a disgrace!” he wrote. “A Don Juan! A rogue! I saw him at Vauxhall, dancing with a poor innocent girl—probably stealing her virtue! I’ve learned from an Irish fellow at my club that she’s from a good family. I think I should warn her father. This is terrible. Terrible.”

He signed off with thanks for the rabbits and a request for more clouted cream.

Now, once a year, there was a christening at the Warren. On this occasion, it was a little girl named Myra Lucretia—named after two godmothers, Miss Portman and Mrs. Pybus. After reading Sam’s letter, Hobnell told his wife, who told her friends, and soon the whole village was buzzing.

Mrs. Pybus, ever the moral compass, declared that men were born liars and quoted poetry about “deceivers.” Mrs. Portman blamed Pen’s upbringing: “His mother spoiled him! He read too many novels! He forgot Doctor Portman’s teachings!”

Captain Glanders, the local dragoon, whistled during dinner. “Ah, young lovers,” he said. “Always getting into trouble.” Mrs. Glanders called him a brute and sent the girls out of the room.

Mr. Simcoe remained calm—but only because he’d already decided Pen was a villain. He hadn’t read any of Pen’s books—he wouldn’t dream of it—but he knew that young men who wrote novels were doomed.

Even the chapel-of-ease held a special sermon on the dangers of London and the sin of reading fiction. The congregation didn’t wait to hear the truth—they assumed Pen was guilty. Who would fling the first stone? Everyone.

Meanwhile, Mrs. Pendennis received an anonymous letter—written in scriptural tones, accusing her son of great wickedness. She fainted. Her daughter Laura read it and blushed—not with shame, but rage.

“It isn’t true,” she said.

“It *is* true,” cried Helen, furious. “You refused him! You broke my heart! You led him into sin! Now he’s with that woman—don’t speak to me!”

She ran to Dr. Portman’s house, half-crazed.

Doctor Portman, seeing the letter, recognized the handwriting—though he pretended otherwise. He tried to comfort her: “It’s anonymous. It could be false. We must hear Arthur’s side.”

But Helen saw through him.

“You think he’s guilty,” she said. “You know he is. Oh, why did I leave him? Why did I let him go?”

She collapsed.

Dr. Portman sent a letter to Pen: “Dear Arthur, I fear rumours are spreading about you. Please repent. Break off this fatal connection. Come home. Your mother is broken-hearted.”

And what of Laura?

Her heart was torn. She loved Arthur. She knew he wasn’t a monster. But she also knew that if the rumors were true—if he had truly done such a thing—then she would lose both him and her mother forever.

So she sat in silence, thinking: *What if it’s true? What if I am the reason for all this pain?*

And thus, the happy village of Clavering—where nothing ever happened—became the epicenter of a drama so absurd, so tragic, so perfectly English, that even the cows paused to listen.
