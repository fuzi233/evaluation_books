A hundred years past, Kensington Square sat a long, lonesome carriage ride out from London’s bustling heart—far enough that footpads lurked in the hedgerows along the lane, far enough that George Selwyn swore he dared not travel there after dark, for fear of being robbed twice over: once by masked highwaymen on the road, and once by rouged ladies at the quadrille table within the house of his neighbour, Lord Castlewood. The nobleman’s Kensington dwelling was spacious enough to house his entire family, and perfectly convenient for their frequent attendances at the royal palace just a stone’s throw away, whenever the King was in residence there. His lady wife held regular evening card-parties for all the court circle who cared to make the journey, though attendance had always been thin for the reason Selwyn joked of.

On the noon the day after Harry Warrington had reappeared at White’s, the whole of the virtuous Castlewood clan gathered for breakfast around the table, even Will, who was due to present himself for court duties that afternoon. The ladies came first, sipping their chocolate, then Will trundled in in his full formal court suit, and last of all the lord of the house wandered languidly in, still wrapped in his bedgown and nightcap, not yet having donned his periwig for the day. It was Will who brought the gossip, fresh from the Star and Garter tavern the night before, where he’d supped with men who’d just returned from Ranelagh and heard the story straight from White’s:
“Heard what? Seen what?” Lord Castlewood asked, flipping open his *Daily Advertiser*.
“Ask Maria,” Lady Fanny snickered. My lord turned to his elder sister, who sat with a face of utter, portentous woe, pale as a starched linen tablecloth.
“Tis nothing but one of Will’s usual coarse, silly fabrications,” Maria said sharply.
“Fabrication my arse,” swore Will, with his favourite string of oaths. “Tom Claypool from Norfolk saw them both at Ranelagh last night, plain as day. Jack Morris rode straight back from there to White’s, where Harry Warrington told the whole story himself. Curse the fellow, I’m bloody glad of it!” He slapped the table hard enough to rattle the chocolate cups. “What d’you make of your precious Fortunate Youth, your Virginian prince that you all fawned over? Turns out he’s nothing but a second son!”
“The elder brother isn’t dead?” my lord asked, sitting up a little.
“Never was. Not a day. I’ll bet they swapped places or pulled some cross trick from the very start.”
“Mr. Warrington is far too honourable for such duplicity!” Maria cried, her voice shaking.
“I’m sure no one can say I ever encouraged the fellow,” Lady Castlewood put in quickly. “Nor Fanny, either. We paid him no special attention at all, indeed!”
“Nor we, indeed!” Lady Fanny echoed at once.
“The man’s nothing but a beggar now, I’ll wager he hasn’t a penny to pay for the coat on his back,” Will went on sneering. “I’m glad of it. Hang him, I’ve always hated him.”
“You’ve never liked him, not since he blacked your eye at the stables, Will,” my lord grinned. “So the poor lad found his brother alive, and lost his whole estate in one go?” He glanced over at Maria, who looked the very picture of misery, and the sight struck his warped sense of humour so sharply he burst into a shrill, high laugh. The poor lady’s face flamed bright red, and the next moment she was sobbing into her handkerchief. “It’s a shame! A cruel shame!” she gasped.
Lady Castlewood and her daughter exchanged a look. “We never do quite follow your lordship’s particular brand of humour,” the former said, stiff and grave.
“I don’t see the least reason you should,” my lord replied coolly. “Maria, my dear, forgive me if I said or did anything to hurt your feelings.”
“Anything? You fleeced him for every penny he had when he was prosperous, and now you laugh at him when he’s ruined!” Maria shot to her feet, glaring at every face around the table.
“Excuse me, my dear sister, I was not laughing at him,” my lord said gently.
“Oh, never mind who or what you laughed at!” she cried. “You took every last thing he had to lose. All the world points at you, says you feed on your own flesh and blood. Now you’ve stripped him bare, you make merry over his misfortune!” And she rustled out of the room, flinging looks of open defiance at every member of the family left behind her.
“Tell us the whole story, Will, at length. My sister’s grief won’t interrupt us now,” my lord said. Will recounted every juicy detail of the gossip buzzing through London that day, cackling with unbridled glee at Harry’s downfall, while Lord Castlewood listened with a faint frown. Harry was a good, brave lad, he thought—he liked him well enough, as much as any worldly peer likes his handsome, rich young cousin. Of course he’d played piquet with Harry, and taken every advantage he could to win his money—but why shouldn’t he? Any other man would have plucked that ripe peach just the same, so why shouldn’t he eat it himself? If that was the worst sin on his conscience, he’d never lie awake a night uneasy over it, he thought. “Where is Mr. George Warrington lodging these days?”
Will grumbled that he’d rather be damned than know or care where the man stayed.
“Then we shall invite both of them here, and treat them with every possible mark of respect,” my lord declared.
“Full of piquet tables, I suppose,” Will growled.
“Or do you plan to drag him out to the stables and sell him one of your overpriced broken nags, Will?” my lord retorted. “You’d have won all Harry’s money fast enough if you hadn’t cheated so clumsily he thrashed you with a cudgel. I tell you, I want every attention paid to our cousin Warrington.”
“Meaning no one is to disturb you when you sit down to the card table with him, of course, my lord,” Lady Castlewood said.
“Madam, I desire fair play—for Mr. Warrington, for myself, and for every member of this amiable family,” my lord snapped back.
“Then Heaven help the poor gentleman if your lordship intends to be so very kind to him,” his stepmother replied, dropping a sarcastic curtsy. The quarrel would no doubt have dragged on far longer, if a phaeton had not just that moment driven up to the front door, with both of the young Virginian cousins seated inside.

It was the same carriage Harry had bought in the height of his fortune, and he still drove it—George sat at his side, their two negro servants perched on the seat behind them. Harry had begged to hand the reins and full ownership of the whole equipage over to his brother the moment they’d met. “What business has a poor devil like me with horses and carriages, Georgy?” he’d said humbly. “I’ve nothing in the world but the coat on my back and the purse my aunt gave me. You take the driving seat, brother. It’ll ease my mind to see you behind the horses.” George had laughed him off, saying he did not know the London roads half as well as Harry did, and that as for the carriage, he would only claim half of it, just as he’d already claimed half of Harry’s fine new wardrobe. “A bargain’s a bargain,” he’d grinned. “If I share your coats, you must share my breeches pocket, Harry. That’s only fair!” Harry swore a dozen times over there was no better brother alive in all the world. He rattled his horses down the Kensington road at a fine pace, glowing with pride to have his older brother beside him, and their servant Gumbo thundered on the Castlewood front door with a knock loud enough to rival the grandest footman in all St. James’s.

Only Lady Castlewood and Lady Fanny were in the drawing-room to greet them. Will had no wish to face Harry after their old quarrel, my lord was still not dressed, and Maria had her own reasons for staying out of sight until the swell of tears in her eyes had dried. It is a strange, universal little comedy of society, is it not? How often we walk in on our friends unannounced, grinning and simpering, stepping over the still-smouldering ashes of a domestic row that raged only minutes before we arrived? The very second a servant calls out your name, the entire family rearranges themselves into a pretty tableau: the daughter pretending to arrange flowers in a vase, the son leaning over a devotional book, mamma shoving the unpaid butcher’s bill under the sofa cushion and picking up a piece of fancy work, even the grumpy pater sticking out a warm hand and exclaiming over how delighted he is you’ve dropped by for lunch. We are all of us the victims of these little deceptions, and we ought to be grateful for them. That polite hypocrisy is not vice—it is the kindest self-denial imaginable. What a dreadful, unbearable world it would be if every man said exactly what was on his mind the moment you walked through the door!

Lady Castlewood advanced to greet the young men with perfect ease and good humour, no trace of the earlier quarrel on her face. “Harry, we have heard this most extraordinary news!” she said, laying a soft hand on his arm. “Lord Castlewood said at breakfast he meant to call on you both this very day. Cousin Harry, we promise you we shall not love you one bit the less now you are poor.”
“Now we can prove we never liked you for your acres at all!” Lady Fanny chimed in, following her lead.
“And what of the acres that have fallen to me, then?” George asked, with a smile and a polite bow.
“Oh cousin, we shall like you for being just as good and kind as Harry!” the lively Lady Fanny replied.
The two ladies turned almost all their attention to Harry from that moment, barely more than civil to the new heir George. They were soft and familiar with Harry, gently affectionate, playfully reproachful for not coming to call on them for days at a time. “It would have done you far more good to sit and drink tea and play a quiet game of piquet with us than to gamble with all those unsavoury characters at White’s,” Lady Castlewood told him. “If we had won enough off you to buy a paper of pins we would have been perfectly content. Young gentlemen never know what is good for them.”
“Now you have no more money to play away, you can come and visit us every day, cousin!” Lady Fanny added, lifting a playful finger. “Your misfortune has turned to good luck for us!”
George was puzzled. The welcome given to his penniless younger brother was far warmer than any he had expected, even from this family of sharp fortune-hunters. Perhaps, he thought, they were not quite so bad as Harry had painted them?

After twenty minutes of this gushing affectionate chatter, Lord Castlewood made his entrance, fully wigged, his sword buckled at his side. He greeted both brothers with equal, easy politeness. “If only you had come to us a few months earlier, Mr. George,” he sighed, shaking George’s hand. “That fateful evening of piquet would never have happened then. A younger son would surely have been more prudent.”
“Or an older kinsman more compassionate,” Harry put in.
“Alas, the love of play runs in all our blood, I fear,” my lord said, spreading his hands. “I inherited it from my father, and it has made me the poorest peer in all England. Those two ladies before you are not immune to it, my brother Will is a martyr to it, and what I win one night I lose the very next day. It is a shocking plague, this rage for gambling that has seized all of England. And as for your banknotes, Harry—every last one of them was gambled away by me within twenty-four hours of winning them. Not a penny of it left.”
“I play like any other gentleman, but I never cared overmuch for the sport,” George remarked.
“We scolded him so thoroughly when we heard he’d played against you, Harry!” Lady Castlewood cried.
“And even if I had not won that money, you know very well some other stranger would have fleeced you of every penny,” my lord went on. “It is a poor consolation, but better that your own friends, who wish you well, take your money than some random crook who would not care if you starved in the gutter afterwards.”
“An aching tooth still hurts the same, even if your own brother is the one pulling it out, my lord,” George laughed. “Harry must bear the penalty of his faults, and pay his debts like any other man.”
“I am not the sort of Englishman who sulks when he’s fairly beaten,” Harry said, holding out his hand to my lord.
“Spoken like a true man!” my lord exclaimed, shaking it warmly. The ladies exchanged pleased little smiles.
“Our cousin in Virginia has raised her sons perfectly, like proper gentlemen!” Lady Castlewood gushed.
“You must not turn so very amiable now you are poor, cousin Harry!” Lady Fanny teased. “We never noticed half your good qualities back when you were just the Fortunate Youth, Prince of Virginia! You look exactly like him, cousin George, but I vow you cannot be half as amiable as your brother.”
“I may be the Prince of Virginia now, but I fear I am not the Fortunate Youth,” George said gravely.
Harry was about to blurt out that George was the best man alive on earth, when the jangle of a harpsichord drifted down from the upstairs drawing-room. Harry blushed bright red, and the two ladies smiled knowingly.
“That is Maria, up there,” Lady Castlewood said. “Shall we all go up and join her?”
The ladies rose and made for the door, Harry trailing after them, his face still flushed. George was about to follow too, but Lord Castlewood held him back.
“Nay, if all the ladies are going after your brother, let me have the pleasure of your company to myself for a little while,” he said. “I have been dying to hear the full story of your time in captivity and your rescue, cousin George!”
“Oh, we all want to hear that story too!” one of the ladies called back, lingering by the door.
“I am greedy, and I wish to hear it first hand, all to myself,” my lord said, staring at her so sternly she scurried out after the rest of the party. He bowed them out, and closed the drawing-room door softly behind them.
“Your brother has no doubt told you everything that has befallen him in this house, cousin George?” he asked, turning back to their guest.
“Yes, including his quarrel with Mr. Will, and his engagement to my Lady Maria,” George replied, bowing politely. “I think I may be forgiven for saying he has met with nothing but ill fortune here, my lord.”
“No one on earth deplores that more than I do,” my lord said earnestly. “My brother Will keeps company with jockeys, boxers, and the wildest young rakes in town, and we have barely a scrap of sympathy between us. We would never all live under the same roof if we were not all so dreadfully poor. This very house belonged to our grandmother, before she left for America to marry Colonel Esmond. Half the wainscoting and furniture here was hers.” George glanced around the panelled parlour with faint interest. “Our family has not thrived these last twenty years, though we did manage to secure a promotion in our rank at court some years back, thanks to a bit of influence we had there. But the mania for gambling has ruined us all. I am its most miserable victim. I am far too proud to sell my title and marry a common rich tradesman’s daughter, as so many less scrupulous noblemen have done. Pride is my great fault, cousin. I never forget the blood that runs in my veins!” He laid his hand on his ruffled shirt front, stuck out his toe, and looked George nobly in the face.
George was naturally inclined to trust everyone he met— but once he saw through a man’s pretences, he was completely, dryly sceptical. He hid his sardonic amusement well, keeping his face perfectly grave.
“We all have our faults, my lord. A love of cards is widely forgiven amongst gentlemen of our rank. Having already forgiven my brother for all his follies, I am hardly fit to sit in judgement over you. Instead of both of you playing and losing, I only wish both of you had played and won!”
“So do I, with all my heart!” my lord sighed. “I can tell you are a good man, with far more sense and knowledge of the world than most young men of your age. Poor Harry has the kindest heart in the world, but I fear his wits are not the strongest.”
“They are not, indeed. But he has a knack for making friends everywhere he goes, and most people love him in spite of all his imprudences.”
“We all love him, as if he were our own brother!” my lord cried.
“He wrote to our mother constantly, describing what a warm welcome he received here. She has kept every one of his letters. Harry’s style is not very learned, but his heart is so good, reading his notes is better than any witty book.”
“I think you have a kind heart and plenty of wit too, cousin,” my lord said, still forcing graciousness.
“I am what Heaven made me, with a little more experience and sorrow than most young men my age,” George said. “This great good fortune of mine, my sudden reappearance—has it left Harry with no resources at all?” Lord Castlewood asked, very gently.
“Nothing but whatever small sums our mother chooses to leave him, or whatever little I can spare him after her death. What is the usual portion for a younger brother here in England, my lord?”
“Ah, a younger brother is… well, everyone knows what a younger brother’s lot is,” my lord said, shrugging his shoulders, and meeting George’s eye.
George went on smoothly: “We are the best of brothers, but we are flesh and blood, and I have no intention of spoiling him by giving him large sums of money he will only squander away at cards or on horse races.
