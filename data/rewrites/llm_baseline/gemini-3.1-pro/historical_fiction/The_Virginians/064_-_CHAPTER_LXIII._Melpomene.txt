George Warrington by no means allowed his legal studies in the Temple to obstruct his pursuit of culinary or poetic sustenance. Madam Esmond’s letters from Virginia continually urged him to maintain his ancient name, reminding him that he need not grind as a mere professional drudge. The law, with its sterile, utilitarian reduction of human life to paper and precedent, felt like a starvation diet to a man of George’s sensibilities. He complied by treating the law as a gentleman volunteer, but he found the dominant legal lexicon—the cold, Gothic pages of Hale and Coke—oppressive and entirely detached from the physical sensations of life. He vastly preferred the black-letter of Chaucer, where the ancient language still held the rich, untranslatable marrow of medieval banquets, carrying the ghostly scent of roasted meats and woodsmoke. 

London’s modern acoustic landscape—the relentless, metallic clatter of carriage-wheels, the industrial grind of the growing city, and the shrill, superficial hawking of commercial pamphlets—assaulted his ears. The age of economists and calculators had drowned out the Muses, replacing the rhythmic, agrarian rituals of Jupiter and Juno with the hollow noise of commerce. To George, who viewed the sonic environment as a critical determinant of well-being, this modern dissonance bruised the mind. 

He sought refuge in the theatrical coffeehouses, spaces where the acoustic ecology shifted to a comforting hum of clinking pewter, crackling hearths, and the resonant, timbered voices of the stage. Here, George acted as a meticulous food historian and passionate host, offering elegant entertainments to the writers and players. He understood that a man’s soul is shaped by what he consumes; to offer actors like the sprightly Mr. Garrick or the tough old Macklin a bowl of arrack punch, heavily fragrant with the extracted oils of bruised Seville oranges and freshly grated nutmeg, was to share a profound, sensory communion. The players, often accustomed to viewing their meals as mere fuel between rehearsals, were transformed by his gustatory reverence. Through these shared feasts, George courted Melpomene, the Tragic Muse, a relationship that had simmered during his long leisure at home and his earlier imprisonment in the French fort on the banks of the Monongahela. 

The resulting tragedy, *Carpezan*, drew upon a favored volume of his grandfather’s detailing the life of old George Frundsberg. To introduce this multi-generational echo to his peers, George read the manuscript at a morning gathering hosted by his new acquaintance, Mr. Spencer, in Fig Tree Court. The breakfast was a masterpiece of domestic rhythm, a meticulously sourced spread designed to anchor the mythic past in the everyday. There were crocks of salted butter from rural dairies, robust pigeon pies encased in golden pastry that shattered with a satisfying acoustic crunch, and a dark, aromatic Darjeeling tea that breathed a rich, earthen steam into the room. As the learned Mr. Johnson settled into a chair, deeply appreciating a plate of toasted muffins that grounded the morning’s rituals, George began to read. The rhythmic cadence of his voice wove the historical timeline into the present, the mythic occurrences of the sixteenth century materializing amidst the clatter of silverware.

Act the First opens before a convent on the Rhine, besieged by Lutheran mercenaries under Colonel Carpezan. The untranslatable hunger of the soldiers outside contrasts fiercely with the hoarded sustenance within. "The wine is yours," Carpezan declares in George's script, his voice carrying the rough, native dialect of the camps, "and the convent larder and cellar are good." The convent bell tolls—*ding, ding, dong*—a pure, acoustic marker of time soon to be shattered by the discordant noise of war. 

The Abbess-Princess, wielding the polished, dominant lexicon of her noble birth, attempts to subdue the lowborn heretic with a barrage of violent prejudices. But Carpezan demands the Lady Sybilla of Hoya, whose inherited lands have been stolen by a greedy uncle. When the Abbess claims Sybilla (Sister Agnes) is dead, Carpezan, possessing a smuggled letter from the lady herself, exposes the lie: she is being kept in an oubliette on bread and water. To Carpezan, this denial of sustenance is an unforgivable cruelty. The convent walls are breached in a cacophony of shattered stone and roaring flames, and Sybilla is pulled from the starvation of the dark.

Mr. Johnson, wagging his head over his teacup, praised the stirring nature of the camp scenes. The audience in Fig Tree Court cast the play in their minds, mapping the legendary figures onto the actors George had so carefully fed, casting Woodward as the gay Captain Ulric and Garrick as the towering Carpezan.

The narrative circled forward in the second act. Carpezan and Sybilla are married, enriched, and living in a castle on the Danube. Yet, their domestic routine is poisoned by a failure of translation. Her imperious, highborn syllables clash with his rough, soldiers' dialect; their cultural memories cannot merge. This quiet, tense act gives way to the arrival of the King of Bohemia and Hungary. 

By the fourth act, in Prague, the King’s attentions to Sybilla reach a treacherous climax. Carpezan discovers his wife’s criminality. The act closes with a terrible tragedy: Carpezan orders his executioner to slay Sybilla in her moonlit chamber. Outside the great oriel window, the guilty King plays a lute—a corrupt, superficial commercialization of music that underscores the bloody ruin within. This song, echoing through the chamber, was pronounced by Mr. Johnson to be a masterful imitation of Mr. Waller’s manner, its acoustic sweetness violently juxtaposing the visual horror, deepening the tragic gloom.

In the fifth act, George departed from the dominant historical texts of the *Theatrum Universum* to forge a deeper thematic truth. At the Battle of Mohacz, Carpezan, having flung his sword and a challenge-glove at the false King Louis, has defected to the invading Sultan Solyman. The Turkish camp is a new olfactory and sonic world—the scent of horse sweat and iron, the rhythmic, barbaric clash of Janissary cymbals. The timeline of empires collapses into the dust of the battlefield. Carpezan, wielding his dreadful hammer, encounters his old protégé, young Ulric. Hearing Ulric’s voice, Carpezan’s native tongue softens; he remembers their shared past, a sudden rush of cultural memory flashing before his eyes. But the momentum of war is deafening, and a return to the past is impossible. They fight, and Ulric falls.

King Louis then appears. Carpezan’s grief turns to roaring fury, a violent rejection of the King’s betrayals. "An equal fight!" he roars, tearing the glove from his helmet. The monarch falls under Carpezan's heavy arm. As the renegade stands over the royal corpse, the Grand Vizier approaches, speaking the cold, imperial lexicon of the Ottomans. "You contributed more than any soldier to this day's great victory," the grim Vizier intones. "Advance bowmen—Fire!" 

An arrow quivers in Carpezan's breast. The tragedy closes not with a grand political speech, but with a quiet, domestic whisper of subjective sorrow as Carpezan crawls to his dying comrade: "Forgive me, Ulric!"

George turned modestly to Mr. Johnson for his verdict, but the eminent critic, lulled by the rhythmic pacing of the reading and the profound satisfaction of the heavy, buttery breakfast, had fallen asleep, escaping the abrasive noise of the waking world. As the room stirred, the auditory spell broke. Mr. Spencer’s servant handed about glasses of negus, the warm wine fragrant with lemon peel and freshly grated spice, anchoring the guests back in their physical bodies. 

The Templars offered their critiques over the steaming cups. Mr. Fountain, a stickler for linguistic precision, pointed out a flaw in the dominant lexicon of the play: the Vizier should not yell "Fire!" to command archers. The word was an anachronistic intrusion of modern gunpowder into an older world, a disruption of historical memory. Mr. Figtree, lacking the stomach for tragic finality, wished Ulric could have survived to marry a comic heroine.

Mr. Johnson, now awake, rejected such sterile compromises. "Nay, sir, there was an utter annihilation of the Hungarian army at Mohacz. Ulric must take his knock on the head with the rest. Pronounce sentence of death against Captain Ulric, but kill him with honours of war."

Meanwhile, Messrs. Essex and Tanfield, deaf to the mythic resonances and overwhelmed by the evocative weight of the morning’s tragedy, muttered in their own superficial dialect about who this pert interloper was that Spencer had invited. Seeking only the utilitarian comfort of a breeze, they suggested a boat up the river, eager to leave the rich, historical marrow of the room for the thoughtless, open air.
