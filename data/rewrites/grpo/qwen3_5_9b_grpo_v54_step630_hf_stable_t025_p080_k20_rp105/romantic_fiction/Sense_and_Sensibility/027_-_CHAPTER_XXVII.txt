"If this fine weather persists," remarked Mrs. Jennings as they gathered for breakfast the following morning, "Sir John will be miserable to depart from Barton next week. It is a tragedy for men of sport to lose a single day's pleasure. Poor fellows! I always feel a pang for them; they seem to carry the loss so deeply."

"That is precisely true," cried Marianne, her voice brightening as she hurried to the window to inspect the sky. "I hadn't considered that angle. This weather will keep countless sportsmen rooted to the countryside."

It was a fortunate thought; her spirits, previously dim, were instantly restored. "It is delightful weather for *them* indeed," she continued, settling into her chair with a radiant countenance. "They must be enjoying it immensely! But" (a flicker of anxiety crossing her face) "it cannot be expected to endure. At this season, following such relentless rain, we shall surely see little more. The frosts will arrive soon, likely with severity. Perhaps within a day or two; this extreme mildness cannot last—nay, perhaps tonight itself will freeze!"

"At any rate," interjected Elinor, hoping to veil her sister's anxious calculations from her mother's gaze, "I am certain Sir John and Lady Middleton will be back in town by the end of next week."

"Ay, my dear, I warrant it. Mary always gets her own way."

"And now," Elinor thought silently, "she will dispatch a letter to Combe by today's post."

Whether she did or not, the letter was written and sent with a discretion that evaded all of Elinor's watchful scrutiny. Regardless of the truth, and though Elinor felt no profound contentment regarding the matter, she could not be entirely uncomfortable while Marianne was in high spirits. Marianne was buoyant, delighted by the mild air, and even more so by her anticipation of the coming frost.

The morning was largely devoted to calling on Mrs. Jennings's acquaintances to announce their presence in town. Throughout the visits, Marianne remained engrossed in tracking the wind's direction, scrutinizing the shifting clouds, and envisioning atmospheric changes.

"Do you not find it colder than this morning, Elinor? There seems a marked difference. I can barely keep my hands warm in my muff. It wasn't so yesterday, I believe. The clouds appear to be parting; the sun will break through momentarily, and we shall have a clear afternoon."

Elinor felt alternately amused and pained, yet Marianne persisted, interpreting every flicker of the firelight and every nuance of the morning sky as a sure sign of impending frost.

The Dashwood sisters found no greater dissatisfaction with Mrs. Jennings's lifestyle or circle of friends than with her treatment of them, which was invariably kind. Her household arrangements were conducted on the most generous scale, and aside from a few old city friends whom, to Lady Middleton's regret, she had never abandoned, she visited no one whose acquaintance might disturb the feelings of her young cousins. Delighted to find herself more comfortably situated than anticipated, Elinor was willing to trade the lack of genuine enjoyment in their evening gatherings—which, whether at home or elsewhere, devolved solely on cards—for the comfort of their surroundings.

Colonel Brandon, holding a general invitation, visited almost daily. He came to admire Marianne and converse with Elinor, who often found greater satisfaction in his company than in any other daily occurrence, yet she observed with deep concern his unrelenting attachment to her sister. She feared it was a strengthening bond. It grieved her to witness the earnestness with which he watched Marianne, and his spirits were noticeably lower than they had been at Barton.

About a week after their arrival, it became certain that Willoughby had also arrived in London. His calling card lay on the table when they returned from their morning drive.

"Good heavens!" exclaimed Marianne. "He has been here while we were out!"

Elinor, rejoiced to confirm his presence in London, ventured to say, "Be assured, he will call again tomorrow." But Marianne seemed hardly to hear her, and upon Mrs. Jennings's entrance, she snatched up the precious card and fled the room.

This event, while lifting Elinor's spirits, restored to her sister all her former agitation and more. From that moment, her mind knew no peace; the expectation of seeing him every hour rendered her unfit for anything else. She insisted on remaining behind the next morning while the others went out.

Elinor's thoughts were filled with speculations on what might be transpiring in Berkeley Street during their absence, but a fleeting glance at her sister upon their return sufficed to inform her that Willoughby had paid no second visit. Just then, a note was delivered and placed on the table.

"For me!" cried Marianne, stepping forward hastily.

"No, ma'am, for my mistress."

But Marianne, unconvinced, seized it immediately.

"It is indeed for Mrs. Jennings; how provoking!"

"You are expecting a letter, then?" said Elinor, unable to remain silent any longer.

"Yes, a little—not much."

After a brief pause, "You have no confidence in me, Marianne."

"Nay, Elinor, this reproach from *you*—you who have confidence in no one!"

"Me!" returned Elinor, somewhat confused; "indeed, Marianne, I have nothing to tell."

"Nor I," answered Marianne with energy, "our situations are then alike. We have neither of us anything to tell; you, because you do not communicate, and I, because I conceal nothing."

Distressed by this accusation of reserve against herself, which she was not at liberty to dismiss, Elinor did not know how to press for greater openness from Marianne under such circumstances.

Mrs. Jennings soon appeared, and the note being handed to her, she read it aloud. It was from Lady Middleton, announcing their arrival in Conduit Street the previous night and requesting the company of her mother and cousins the following evening. Business on Sir John's part and a violent cold on Lady Middleton's prevented their calling at Berkeley Street. The invitation was accepted; yet when the appointed hour approached, and it became necessary, out of common civility to Mrs. Jennings, that they both attend her on such a visit, Elinor found it difficult to persuade her sister to go, for she had still seen nothing of Willoughby and was therefore no less disinclined to seek amusement abroad than unwilling to risk his calling again in her absence.

Elinor discovered, upon the evening's conclusion, that disposition is not materially altered by a change of residence, for although scarcely settled in town, Sir John had managed to gather nearly twenty young people around him and amuse them with a ball. This was an affair, however, of which Lady Middleton disapproved. In the country, an unpremeditated dance was perfectly acceptable; but in London, where the reputation for elegance was more vital and harder to attain, it was risking too much for the gratification of a few girls to have it known that Lady Middleton had hosted a small dance of eight or nine couples, accompanied by two violins and a mere side-board collation.

Mr. and Mrs. Palmer were among the guests. From the former, whom they had not seen since their arrival in town, as he carefully avoided any appearance of attention to his mother-in-law and thus never approached her, they received no mark of recognition upon entering. He glanced at them slightly, seemingly unaware of their identity, and merely nodded to Mrs. Jennings from across the room. Marianne cast one glance around the apartment as she entered; it was sufficient—he was not there—and she sat down, equally ill-disposed to receive or communicate pleasure. After they had been assembled for about an hour, Mr. Palmer sauntered toward the Miss Dashwoods to express his surprise at seeing them in town, though Colonel Brandon had been first informed of their arrival at his house, and he himself had remarked something very droll upon hearing they were coming.

"I thought you were both in Devonshire," he said.

"Did you?" replied Elinor.

"When do you go back again?"

"I do not know." And thus ended their discourse.

Never had Marianne been so unwilling to dance in her life as she was that evening, nor so fatigued by the exercise. She complained of it as they returned to Berkeley Street.

"Aye, aye," said Mrs. Jennings, "we know the reason of all that very well; if a certain person who shall be nameless had been there, you would not have been a bit tired: and to say the truth, it was not very pretty of him not to give you the meeting when he was invited."

"Invited!" cried Marianne.

"So my daughter Middleton told me, for it seems Sir John met him somewhere in the street this morning." Marianne said no more, but looked exceedingly hurt. Impatient in this situation to do something that might lead to her sister's relief, Elinor resolved to write the next morning to her mother, hoping that by awakening her fears for Marianne's health, she would procure those inquiries which had been so long delayed; and she was still more eagerly bent on this measure by perceiving, after breakfast on the morrow, that Marianne was again writing to Willoughby, for she could not suppose it to be addressed to any other person.

About midday, Mrs. Jennings went out alone on business, and Elinor began her letter immediately, while Marianne, too restless for employment and too anxious for conversation, walked from one window to another or sat by the fire in melancholy meditation. Elinor was very earnest in her appeal to her mother, relating everything that had passed, her suspicions of Willoughby's inconstancy, and urging her by every plea of duty and affection to demand from Marianne an account of her real situation regarding him.

Her letter was scarcely finished when a knock foretold a visitor, and Colonel Brandon was announced. Marianne, who had seen him from the window and who hated company of any kind, left the room before he entered. He looked more than usually grave, and though expressing satisfaction at finding Miss Dashwood alone, as if he had something particular to tell her, he sat for some time without speaking. Elinor, persuaded that he had some communication to make concerning her sister, impatiently awaited its opening. It was not the first time she had felt such a conviction; more than once before, beginning with observations like "your sister looks unwell today" or "your sister seems out of spirits," he had appeared on the verge of disclosing or inquiring about something specific. After a pause of several minutes, their silence was broken by his asking her, in a voice of some agitation, when she was to congratulate her on the acquisition of a brother? Elinor was unprepared for such a question and, having no answer ready, was obliged to adopt the simple and common expedient of asking what he meant.

He tried to smile as he replied, "Your sister's engagement to Mr. Willoughby is very generally known."

"It cannot be generally known," returned Elinor, "for her own family do not know it."

He looked surprised and said, "I beg your pardon; I am afraid my inquiry has been impertinent; but I had not supposed any secrecy intended, as they openly correspond, and their marriage is universally talked of."

"How can that be? By whom can you have heard it mentioned?"

"By many—by some of whom you know nothing, by others with whom you are most intimate, Mrs. Jennings, Mrs. Palmer, and the Middletons. But still I might not have believed it, for where the mind is perhaps rather unwilling to be convinced, it will always find something to support its doubts, if I had not, when the servant let me in today, accidentally seen a letter in his hand, directed to Mr. Willoughby in your sister's writing. I came to inquire, but I was convinced before I could ask the question. Is everything finally settled? Is it impossible to—? But I have no right, and I could have no chance of succeeding. Excuse me, Miss Dashwood. I believe I have been wrong in saying so much, but I hardly know what to do, and on your prudence I have the strongest dependence. Tell me that it is all absolutely resolved on, that any attempt, that in short concealment, if concealment be possible, is all that remains."

These words, which conveyed to Elinor a direct avowal of his love for her sister, affected her profoundly. She was not immediately able to speak, and even when her spirits were recovered, she debated for a short time on the answer it would be most proper to give. The real state of things between Willoughby and her sister was so little known to herself that, in endeavoring to explain it, she might be as liable to say too much as too little. Yet, as she was convinced that Marianne's affection for Willoughby left no hope for Colonel Brandon's success, whatever the event of that affection might be, and at the same time wished to shield her conduct from censure, she thought it most prudent and kind, after some consideration, to say more than she really knew or believed. She acknowledged, therefore, that though she had never been informed by themselves of the terms on which they stood with each other, of their mutual affection she had no doubt, and of their correspondence she was not astonished to hear.

He listened to her with silent attention, and upon her ceasing to speak, rose directly from his seat. After saying in a voice of emotion, "To your sister I wish all imaginable happiness; to Willoughby that he may endeavour to deserve her," he took his leave and departed.

Elinor derived no comforting feelings from this conversation to lessen the uneasiness of her mind on other points; she was left, on the contrary, with a melancholy impression of Colonel Brandon's unhappiness, and was prevented even from wishing it removed by her anxiety for the very event that must confirm it.
