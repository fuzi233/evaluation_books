"Full up. No room."

The door slammed with the brutal finality of a garrison lock, sealing out the last shred of dignity.

I stood in the biting chill of the morning mist, calculating the caloric deficit that had turned my legs to lead. Scouring the coastlines of Italy for passage was a known hazard; seeking refuge in a hospice was a tactical necessity. To be rejected by both was a failure of logistics, a cruel reminder that institutions collapse under the weight of their own inefficiency.

A figure detached itself from the gloom of the stone wall.

"No space?" the man asked, his voice raspy, betraying a cynicism honed by years of navigating the margins of society.

"The keeper insists on capacity," I replied, keeping my voice level despite the tremor seizing my fingers.

"That's the standard defense mechanism of the coastal towns," he muttered, adjusting the ragged collar of his tunic. "They claim fullness to filter the weak. Keep the numbers down, keep the trouble away."

"You suggest it's a fabrication?"

"I'm telling you the gatekeeper is bluffing. If there is a cot, he is bound to provide it. If I were you, I'd press the issue until the bureaucracy breaks."

There was a pragmatic logic to challenging the status quo. I stepped forward, forcing the door open with renewed vigor. The same gaunt sentinel emerged, his expression shifting from smug authority to confused irritation. Even a parish priest, draped in the vestments of his office, could not wear a heavier cloak of incompetence.

"Again?" he sneered. "What is your strategic objective? Do you expect me to serve as a permanent concierge for the destitute?"

Inside the boat, the transition from dream to wakefulness was abrupt, yet the landscape outside remained suspended in a state of serene suspension.

My eyes opened to the pale gold of dawn. The vessel drifted motionless in a secluded cove, anchored by the sheer geometry of the coastline. To starboard stretched the unblemished azure of the Mediterranean; to port, the terrain unfolded in a rhythmic repetition of nature's design—a deep curve of mountains framing a verdant expanse, draped in the dappled shade of ancient oaks.

Sunlight, sharp and oblique, struck the western rim of this crescent-shaped bay, illuminating a cluster of buildings that seemed to have grown organically from the rock face, crowned by a solitary belfry reaching toward the sky. Below, the village slept in clusters of terracotta roofs. The air was still, the water glass-smooth, holding its breath in anticipation of the day.

The warmth of the sun penetrated my skin, a physical caress that dissolved the tension of the previous night. In the early hours of consciousness, mere existence becomes a form of anesthesia; the exquisite, undefined sensation of peace creates a bliss devoid of memory or desire. As the heat settled into my limbs, the chaotic turbulence of the past receded like the tide, leaving behind the tranquil aftermath of childhood reveries. For a fleeting moment, the oblivion was absolute. I did not calculate the duration of my stay, nor the implications of my arrival; I simply *was*.

Then, the illusion fractured.

Reality returned with the jarring clarity of a ship hitting a reef. I was not in a sanctuary of eternal rest, but in a drifting skiff, adrift on the edge of the known world. The boat had not delivered me to death, as I had feared, but served as a cradle for a new, uncertain existence. Despite the despair that had gripped me at dusk, the morning brought a grim relief: I was alive, bathed in familiar sunlight, rather than consigned to the void.

*Could* I remain here?

The silence of Florence, miles away, was a comforting fiction. Already, the golden haze of the morning gave way to the silhouette of domes and towers, bisected by the Arno and hemmed in by green hills. The isolation was temporary.

I rose, pushing myself upright in the hull. The urge to analyze my position was overwhelming, but I suppressed it. This was a sheltered inlet, inhabited by simple folk unlikely to harbor malice. For now, I would conserve energy, allowing my mind to settle before addressing the immediate needs of sustenance. Bread and milk awaited; thereafter, I would nestle into this green quietude and accept the pause in my journey.

I turned my gaze back to the valley, seeking to reclaim the serenity of my awakening.

Less than a minute passed before the tranquility was shattered by a sound that cut through the stillness like a knife. It was not a cry of joy or warning, but a continuous, rising wail of pure distress.

Instinct took command. I assumed the posture of a rescuer, planting one foot on the gunwale, ready to launch onto the shore. But I hesitated, listening. A mother must be nearby; the cry should subside. It did not. Instead, the sound intensified, drawing me with an irresistible magnetic pull, amplified by the profound contrast with the preceding peace.

I leapt onto the beach, moving blindly until the source of the noise revealed itself. It emanated from a dilapidated hovel nestled among rough vegetation, yards to my right. I scrambled over a crumbling stone fence, traversing patches of weedy greens and neglected grain stalks. The cry grew distinct, a piercing shriek that vibrated in my chest.

As I approached the hovel, I sensed a shift in the atmosphere—a subtle corruption hanging over the lush greenery, a taint that promised danger. Through the gap in the roof, I saw the interior. The cry was a physical lance, driving me forward.

I crossed the threshold.

The dim light revealed a tableau of horror. Three bodies lay strewn across the straw-strewn floor. One was a tall man, rigid and pale; another, a girl of approximately eight years, equally desiccated. The third, a young woman, lay clutching a living child. The infant, the source of the scream, writhed violently, its tiny hands tearing at the long black hair of the dying mother.

My training in the theaters of plague and death activated instantly. Thought and action merged into a single reflex. I scooped the child from the chaos, pressing it to my breast to soothe its terror, while simultaneously scanning the corpses to confirm their status.

The features were unmistakable: the high cheekbones, the specific cast of the eyes, the garb. These were Sephardic Jews, victims of the Inquisition's relentless purge. Greed had driven them from their ships; superstition had cast them back to the rocks. Their property stripped, their lives forfeited, they had been abandoned to die.

*"Surely,"* I reasoned, *"there is a woman in this village whose maternal instinct will compel her to aid this orphan."*

Yet, doubt lingered. The man and the girl appeared emaciated, long since departed. The woman, however, retained a stubborn vitality, her frame still robust. I knelt, preparing to check for a pulse, lifting the coarse yellow woolen cloth covering her chest. Beneath it, the skin was marred by purplish blotches—the signature of the pestilence.

If the villagers knew of this contagion, my mission would be fraught with peril. They might recoil from me, terrified of the child I carried. But I possessed coin. Gold was a universal language, and they would not refuse goat's milk in exchange for it.

I moved toward the settlement, my mind focused entirely on calming the dark-skinned infant and devising a strategy to recruit a guardian. I hoped that the aura of sanctity radiating from my religious habit would disarm suspicion. As I crossed a field of cultivation, I noted with alarm that sections of the harvest had been left to rot, golden apples and figs decaying on the soil. No livestock grazed the visible pastures. The stillness had transformed from peaceful to ominous.

I hurried toward the densest cluster of dwellings, seeking the pulse of life. Clutching two figs, I tore off small pieces of the sweet pulp to quiet the child's cries, using the distraction to mask my own growing anxiety.

Between two rows of houses, I found the village. By all rights, the inhabitants should have been stirring, but the streets were deserted. The air grew increasingly oppressive, thick with an invisible, foul impurity. I peered into an open door, finding only grim emptiness. Another door yielded a similar view: a man collapsed mid-stride, his head resting awkwardly across the handle of a spade, an earthenware jug clutched in a stiffened hand.

Horror seized me. Was this a graveyard of the unburied? I sought to listen for any sign of life, but the child's fresh burst of crying drowned out my efforts.

Finally, a figure crawled from a doorway, collapsing against the wall in a fetal position. I rushed to her side. She was a young woman, racked by fever, holding a pitcher. As I approached, she did not flinch; her focus was singular, consumed by agony.

"Water!" she gasped, her voice a broken whisper.

I lowered myself beside her, placing the pitcher in her grasp. "You shall have water. Can you indicate the location of the well?"

Her hand pointed vaguely toward the far end of the narrow street. I adjusted my load, balancing the pitcher against the child, and set off. The infant, fed intermittently with the fig pulp, grew quieter, allowing me to observe my surroundings with greater clarity.
