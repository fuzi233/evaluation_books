Pen, in that loose, domestic costume which makes even a philosopher look like a culprit—his dressing-gown—went up, as regularly as if it were morning prayers, to Warrington’s chambers, meaning to pour out the whole history of last night’s campaign with the Major, and to get from George, as usual, counsel, consolation, contradiction, or laughter.

Instead of George he found Mrs. Flanagan, laundress, empress, and sole surviving inhabitant of those beloved rooms; and she stood there with her arms folded, as if to say that linen, not literature, was now the reigning power.

“Gone, is it?” Pen cried.

“Gone, sir,” said Mrs. Flanagan; and there was in her voice that particular melancholy with which a good woman speaks of a gentleman who has not only paid his washing, but also behaved like one. “I found him at the table, when I came, the dear gentleman—writing at his papers, and one of the candles burned out; and hard as his bed is, he wasn’t in it all night, sir.”

On the table lay the evidence of his trade—packets directed to the newspaper and the review; proofs and scribbled sheets; books tugged from the shelves and left open like mouths caught mid-remark; a litter of torn manuscript, like peelings from some harsh fruit he had been obliged to pare in haste; pens, ink, a blotting-paper scored with half-visible lines; and a bit of sealing-wax twisted, bitten, and broken, as if it had been some tough piece of toffee gnawed in angry thought. If you could have heard the room you might have known him too: no talk, no fiddle, no companionable tread—only the faint after-rustle of paper, the imaginary scratch of a pen, and that city hush which is never truly quiet, but merely waiting to clang again.

George had come home from the Club when the brawl there grew too coarse even for a satirist to endure; and instead of drinking and shouting with the rest, he had set himself down to his work with that savage steadiness by which men try to master what they cannot otherwise bear. He bent all his strength upon the labour; and the night wore away—one candle consumed, the other guttering—until the tardy November dawn, grey as dishwater, looked in at the windows and found him still there, solitary at his desk, finishing what the public would presently read over breakfast as though it had been baked for them in the night like a batch of rolls.

In the next day’s paper, or the quarter’s review, many a comfortable gentleman would admire the fierce vigour of his satire, the depth of his reason, the variety of his illustration—taking it, as people do take their meals, without guessing the kitchen’s heat. There was no hint in the writing of the other thoughts that sat always beside him, like a silent second at the table. Those who knew his name and flavour might have detected, perhaps, an extra bitterness; a melancholy note behind the laughter, as if a bell had been muffled. For the face behind the mask is often grave; and the man who lives by the pen knows nights of labour when the room is full of invisible company—care, sickness, dread, grief—while the hand goes on, because ink must be earned, and the world’s appetite does not wait.

Pen flung himself into George’s empty chair and stared about him, noting things according to his habit, or in spite of it. In the bookcase there was a gap, next to the old College Plato with the Boniface Arms; and that gap hit him like a hand on the heart.

Helen’s Bible had been there.

“He has taken that with him,” Pen thought; and he knew—without any need of Mrs. Flanagan’s commentary—why his friend was gone. The room seemed suddenly to have lost not merely a tenant but a tone, as when a familiar instrument is removed from an orchestra and the air misses its particular string.

Dear, dear old George! Pen rubbed his hand over his eyes, and the thought went through him with a humility that was almost rage. How much wiser, better, nobler he is than I! Where shall I ever hear that frank voice and kind laughter again? Where shall I see such a true gentleman? No wonder she loved him. What was I compared to him? What could she do else but love him? And then, with that queer loyal imagination by which men console themselves when they have been beaten fairly, he vowed that to the end of their days they would be her brothers—knights even—waiting on her, and when old, telling one another how they had loved her.

Coming down to his own chambers, he saw, at last, the little letter that had been slipped into his letter-box—addressed to A. P., Esq., in George’s unmistakable hand. He broke it with an impatience that was almost fear.

“Dear Pen,—I shall be half-way home when you breakfast, and intend to stay over Christmas, in Norfolk, or elsewhere.

“I have my own opinion of the issue of matters about which we talked in J——— St. yesterday; and think my presence de trop.

“Vale. G. W.”

“Give my very best regards and adieux to your cousin.”

So George was gone; and Mrs. Flanagan reigned over the empty chambers as over an abandoned province.

Pen, of course, had to go dutifully and present himself at his uncle’s door the day after their colloquy. Not being admitted—Major Pendennis, when he was offended, could barricade himself as grandly as a fortress—Arthur did what he always did when turned out of one room: he went to another where he was sure of kindness, and presented himself in Lady Rockminster’s apartments.

The old lady instantly asked for Bluebeard and insisted he should come to dinner.

“Bluebeard is gone,” Pen said; and he took out George’s scrap of paper and handed it to Laura.

Laura looked at it—did not look at Pen in return—gave it back, and walked away, as if the small square of writing had been a coal and her fingers scorched.

Pen, who could not bear a silence, rushed into an eloquent eulogium of his dear old George for Lady Rockminster’s benefit; the dowager stared at him in honest amazement. She had never heard him so warm in praise of anybody, and told him, with her usual frankness, that she didn’t think it had been in his nature to care so much about any other person.

Not long after, as Arthur was passing through Waterloo Place in one of his many walks to the hotel where Laura lived—and whither his daily duty to his uncle carried him—he saw issuing from Messrs. Gimcrack’s celebrated shop an old acquaintance, followed to a brougham by an obsequious shopman bearing parcels.

The gentleman was in the deepest mourning: the brougham, the driver, and the horse were in mourning. Grief, when it can afford the comfortablest springs and cushions, has a way of looking properly upholstered; and the whole equipage seemed to say, “Affliction is very distressing, but please observe we have ordered it with the best appointments.”

“What, Foker! Hail, Foker!” cried Pen; and indeed it was his old schoolfellow, the heir of the late John Henry Foker, Esq., master of Logwood and other houses, principal partner in the great brewery of Foker and Co.—the greater portion of Foker’s Entire, now solemnly transferred into the care of a son who looked as if he had been bred to sip rather than to govern vats.

A little hand, gloved in the blackest possible ebony and set off by three inches of snowy wristband, came forth to meet Arthur’s. The other little hand held a morocco case, which, no doubt, contained something precious newly acquired under Gimcrack’s persuasive lights.

Pen’s sharp eyes and satiric instinct took in the errand at once, and thought of Horace and heirs, of gathered wine from a father’s vats; and how human nature keeps its recipes unchanged whether it be in the Via Sacra or Regent Street.

“Le Roi est mort. Vive le Roi!” said Arthur.

“Ah!” said the other, and that “Ah!” contained condolence, embarrassment, and a desire to get away before any moral observation could be served with the handshake. “Yes. Thank you—very much obliged. How do you do, Pen?—very busy—good-bye!” And he jumped into the black brougham and sate like a little black Care behind the black coachman.

He had blushed at seeing Pen—signs of guilt and perturbation which Arthur, in his sardonic way, set down partly to the novelty of being a monarch in mourning, partly to the knowledge that Gimcrack’s is not the natural retreat of a broken heart.

“Yes: so wags the world,” Pen thought as he walked on. “The stone closes over Harry the Fourth, and Harry the Fifth reigns in his stead. The old ministers at the brewery come and kneel before him with their books; the draymen fling up their red caps and shout. What grave deference and sympathy the bankers and lawyers show!” And then, because his mind would always knead bitterness into whatever loaf of thought was rising, he reflected that when one man keeps another out of twenty thousand a year, the younger is apt to hanker after the crown; and the wish is father to the thought of possession. “Thank Heaven,” he concluded, “there was no thought of money between me and our dear mother, Laura.”

“There never could have been. You would have spurned it!” cried Laura when he said as much to her later. “Why make yourself more selfish than you are, Pen?—and allow your mind to own for an instant that it would have entertained such—such dreadful meanness? You make me blush for you, Arthur: you make me—” Her eyes finished the sentence, and her handkerchief followed.

“There are some truths women will never acknowledge,” Pen said, “and from which your modesty always turns away. I do not say I ever knew the feeling—only that I am glad I had not the temptation. Is there any harm in that confession of weakness?”

“We are all taught to ask to be delivered from evil, Arthur,” said Laura in a low voice. “I am glad if you were spared from that great crime; and only sorry to think you could by any possibility have been led into it. But you never could; and you don’t think you could. Your acts are generous and kind: you disdain mean actions. You take Blanche without money, and without a bribe. Yes, thanks be to Heaven, dear brother. You could not have sold yourself away; I knew you could not when it came to the day, and you did not. Praise be—be where praise is due. Why does this horrid scepticism pursue you, my Arthur? Why doubt and sneer at your own heart—at every one’s? Oh, if you knew the pain you give me—how I lie awake and think of those hard sentences, dear brother, and wish them unspoken, unthought!”

“Do I cause you many thoughts and many tears, Laura?” asked Arthur.

The fulness of innocent love answered him more plainly than words: a smile pure as fresh milk, a glance of unutterable tenderness and sympathy shone in her face—those indications of love and goodness which he could only worship, as one watches them in a child, as one fancies one might regard them in an angel.

“I—I don’t know what I have done,” he said simply, “to have merited such regard from two such women. It is like undeserved praise—or too much good fortune, which frightens one—or a great post, when a man feels he is not fit for it. Ah, sister, how weak and wicked we are; how spotless, and full of love and truth, Heaven made you! I think for some of you there has been no fall,” he added, looking at her with an almost paternal admiration. “You can’t help having sweet thoughts, and doing good actions. Dear creature! they are the flowers which you bear.”

“And what else, sir?” asked Laura, quick as a bird to hear the change in his breathing. “I see a sneer coming over your face. What is it? Why does it come to drive all the good thoughts away?”

“A sneer, is there? I was thinking, my dear, that nature in making you so good and loving did very well: but—”

“But what? What is that wicked but? and why are you always calling it up?”

“But will come in spite of us. But is reflection. But is the sceptic’s familiar, with whom he has made a compact; and if he forgets it and indulges in happy day-dreams—or listens to sweet music, let us say, or to the bells ringing to church—But taps at the door and says, Master, I am here. You are my master; but I am yours. Go where you will, you can’t travel without me. I will whisper to you when you are on your knees at church. I will be at your marriage pillow. I will sit down at your table with your children. I will be behind your deathbed curtain. That is what But is.”

“Pen, you frighten me,” cried Laura.

“Do you know what But came and said to me just now, when I was looking at you? But said, If that girl had reason as well as love, she would love you no more. If she knew you as you are—the sullied, selfish being which you know—she must part from you, and could give you no love and no sympathy.” And then, softening, as he always did when he saw her pained: “Didn’t I say some of you seem exempt from the fall? Love you know; but the knowledge of evil is kept from you.”

“What is this you young folks are talking about?” asked Lady Rockminster, sweeping in at this moment, having performed, in the mystic retirement of her apartments and under the hands of her attendant, those elaborate rites without which she never presented herself to the public. “Mr. Pendennis, you are always coming here.”

“It is very pleasant to be here,” Arthur said; “and we were talking, when you came in, about my friend Foker, whom I met just now; and who, as your ladyship knows, has succeeded to his father’s kingdom.”

“He has a very fine property, he has fifteen thousand a year. He is my cousin. He is a very worthy young man. He must come and see me,” said Lady Rockminster, with a look at Laura that was as plain as a command.

“He has been engaged for many years past to his cousin, Lady—”

“Lady Ann is a foolish little chit,” Lady Rockminster cut in, with immense dignity; “and I have no patience with her. She has outraged every feeling of society. She has broken her father’s heart, and thrown away fifteen thousand a year.”

“Thrown away? What has happened?” asked Pen.

“It will be the talk of the town in a day or two; and there is no need I should keep the secret any longer,” said her ladyship, who had already written and received a dozen letters on the subject, and enjoyed the catastrophe as some people enjoy a new sauce. “I had a letter yesterday from my daughter, who was staying at Drummington until all the world was obliged to go away on account of the frightful catastrophe which happened there. When Mr. Foker came home from Nice, and after the funeral, Lady Ann went down on her knees to her father, said she never could marry her cousin, that she had contracted another attachment, and that she must die rather than fulfil her contract. Poor Lord Rosherville, who is dreadfully embarrassed, showed his daughter what the state of his affairs was, and that it was necessary the arrangements should take place; and in fine we all supposed she had listened to reason, and intended to comply with the desires of her family. But what has happened? Last Thursday she went out after breakfast with her maid, and was married in the very church in Drummington Park to Mr. Hobson, her father’s own chaplain and her brother’s tutor; a red-haired widower with two children.

“Poor dear Rosherville is in a dreadful way: he wishes Henry Foker should marry Alice or Barbara; but Alice is marked with the small-pox, and Barbara is ten years older than he is. And of course now the young man is his own master, he will think of choosing for himself. The blow on Lady Agnes is very cruel. She is inconsolable. She has the house in Grosvenor Street for her life, and her settlement, which was very handsome. Have you not met her? Yes, she dined one day at Lady Clavering’s—the first day I saw you, and a very disagreeable young man I thought you were. But I have formed you. We have formed him, haven’t we, Laura? Where is Bluebeard? Let him come. That horrid Grindley, the dentist, will keep me in town another week.”

To the latter part of this speech Arthur gave no ear. His mind—perverse in its curiosities—had run back to Gimcrack’s parcels. For whom could Foker be buying those trinkets? Why had Harry looked so eager to escape? Could he be still faithful to that attachment which had once agitated him so much and sent him abroad eighteen months back?

Psha! The bracelets and fine things were for some old acquaintance of the Opera or the French theatre. Rumours from Naples and Paris—those airy, greasy whispers borne into club smoking-rooms—had announced that the young man had found distractions; or, precluded from his virtuous attachment, had flung himself back upon old companions and amusements: not the only man (or woman) whom society forces into evil, or debars from good; not the only victim of the world’s selfish laws. Even the city seemed complicit: it clattered, hissed, and rolled—wheels, hoofs, voices—like a huge indifferent kitchen, forever turning out noise, and caring little what hearts were being chopped beneath it.

Meanwhile Laura, with the zeal of a person who thinks happiness must be served piping hot or it will spoil, pressed Pen’s marriage on with a feverish anxiety. Pen, who could afford to let the matter simmer, would have waited; but Laura would hear of no delay. She wrote, implored, urged, hurried. It seemed as if she could have no rest until Arthur’s happiness was complete, as if she were trying by speed to outrun some sound she dreaded hearing behind her.

She offered herself to Blanche—dearest Blanche—to come and stay at Tunbridge when Lady Rockminster should go on her intended visit to the reigning house of Rockminster; and though the old dowager scolded, ordered, commanded, Laura was deaf and disobedient. The dowager might nurse her rheumatism, might read herself to sleep if she would not hear her maid (whose voice croaked and made sad work of sentimental passages); but Laura must go—would go—and be with her new sister. She who ordinarily had no will of her own, and complied smilingly with anybody’s whim, showed, in this one instance, what the world calls selfishness and what love calls necessity.

Thus began a correspondence—No. 1, No. 2, No. 3—so full of “dearests” that the very paper might have turned sugary.

Laura wrote, proposing, with many loves and regards to Lady Clavering, to pass some time with dearest Blanche.

Blanche answered instantly to dearest Laura’s No. 1: with what extreme delight she should welcome her sister; how charming it would be to practise their old duets together, to wander o’er the grassy sward and amidst the yellowing woods of Penshurst and Southborough. Blanche counted the hours till she should embrace her dearest friend.

Laura’s No. 2 expressed delight at this affectionate reply. She hoped their friendship would never diminish; that their confidence would grow in after years; that they should have no secrets from each other; that the aim of the life of each would be to make one person happy.

Blanche’s No. 2 came two days later with a grievance: how provoking! Their house was very small, the two spare bedrooms were occupied by that horrid Mrs. Planter and her daughter, who had thought proper to fall ill (she always fell ill in country-houses), and she could not or would not be moved for some days.

Laura’s No. 3: it was indeed provoking. She had hoped to hear one of dearest Blanche’s dear songs on Friday; but she was consoled to wait, because Lady Rockminster was not very well and liked to be nursed by her. Poor Major Pendennis was very unwell too, in the same hotel—too unwell even to see Arthur, who was constant in his calls on his uncle. Arthur’s heart was full of tenderness and affection. She had known Arthur all her life. She would answer—yes, even in italics she would answer—for his kindness, his goodness, and his gentleness.

And then Blanche’s No. 3 arrived like a plate dashed suddenly down in the middle of a gentle meal:

“What is this most surprising, most extraordinary letter from A. P.? What does dearest Laura know about it? What has happened? What, what mystery is enveloped under his frightful reserve?”

Blanche required an explanation; and it could not be better given than in the surprising and mysterious letter of Arthur Pendennis.
