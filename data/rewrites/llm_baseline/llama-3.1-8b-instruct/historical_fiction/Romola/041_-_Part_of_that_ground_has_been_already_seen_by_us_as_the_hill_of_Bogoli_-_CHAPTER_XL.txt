As Romola settled onto the weathered stone beneath the towering cypress, the landscape conspired to envelop her in an atmosphere of serene solitude. The distant hum of the city receded, replaced by the gentle rustle of leaves and the soft hush of the morning breeze. With each passing moment, she felt the weight of her husband's departure grow further removed, like the ebbing tide of a lake. Tito Melema rode towards Siena, leaving Romola to follow the winding road eastward, further and further into the unknown. For the first time in her life, she felt a deep connection to the land and sky, unencumbered by the constraints of human expectation.

A low, resonant voice cut through the stillness, sending a shiver down Romola's spine. "You are Romola de' Bardi, the wife of Tito Melema." The words were spoken with a quiet authority, one that echoed within her memory. She recognized the voice – it was one that had resonated through her before – and yet, she chose not to turn or look up. Instead, she remained seated, her heart pounding with a mix of awe and indignation. A monk, clad in the dark habit of his order, stood before her, his presence a jarring intrusion into her newfound solitude.

"You are fleeing Florence in disguise," he continued, his tone unwavering. "I have been commanded by a higher power to prevent you from leaving." Romola's anger flared, fueled by a sense of injustice. Who was this monk to dictate her actions? She refused to turn and confront him, instead sitting rigidly still, her resentment simmering just below the surface. "What right do you have to speak to me, or to stand in my way?" she asked, her voice steady despite the turmoil within.

The monk's response was measured, his words laced with a sense of duty. "I am a messenger, sent to convey a message from above. You have donned the robes of a pilgrim, but your intentions are not pure. You seek to escape the path laid out for you, to forge your own way and answer to no one but yourself. But I have been instructed to call you back. You must return to your place, Romola."
