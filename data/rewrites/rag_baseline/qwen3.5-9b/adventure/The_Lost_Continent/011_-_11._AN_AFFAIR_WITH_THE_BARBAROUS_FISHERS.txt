The Empress's jealousy was petty, a trivial reaction to a prisoner's existence. My mind, conditioned for military efficiency during crises, processed the insult instantly. I was already marked for sacrifice by the High Council of Priests; Nais, too, faced the altar unless I could engineer a rescue in the span of a heartbeat.

I bowed low before Phorenice. "In all matters where human hands suffice," I said, "your will, Empress, shall be executed." Her touch on my arm was warm, her eyes flooding with a depth of emotion that suggested the gods had crafted her specifically to win half the empire's schemes and half its battles.

Merchants and harbor dwellers converged, offering clean clothes and safe lodging. Phorenice accepted graciously. Though dampening my coat mattered little, the section of the harbor where the mammoth had dumped us reeked of brine and decay. I followed her lead. To Nais, I offered no final glance, no whispered comfort. Such gestures were insufficient currency for Phorenice anyway. Before departing, she issued strict orders to the port-captain regarding the custody of her prisoner.

Inside the merchant's house, I washed away the salt and exchanged my travel-worn garb for the simplest available attire. The merchant, relieved to avoid a deeper debt, smiled. However, an hour lingered on the pavement, listening to the distant thunder of bombardment, before Phorenice emerged. I observed the merchant trailing us with a grim amusement; he was ruined. His stock of jewels and gauds, purchased in partnership, had vanished from his shelves to adorn Phorenice.

"Pretty?" she asked, displaying the stolen treasures. "They flash beautifully under the sun. I am grateful, Deucalion, that the mammoth's fury delivered this gift. Tell him that when he earns more, I shall return as his guest." She gestured to approaching litters. "Send one away. Share mine with me, sir. Let us pretend to be lovers today, even if certainty awaits us tomorrow." She refused additional bearers, preferring her own slaves. "I am slender, Deucalion. Do not burden me with the weight of your overfed men."

"To the royal pyramid?" I asked.

"No. Nor to the walls. I seek neither combat nor the throne today. I wish to be Phorenice, the maiden, for a few hours. If someone worthy woos me, I shall consider it a luxury." She commanded the litter to circle the harbor's edge, warning any followers that she would summon fire upon them as she had upon the sacrificial victims.

I knew the streets were unruly, having heard hints from the pyramid's gatekeeper regarding the Empress's previous banquet. Since this behavior was common knowledge to her, I held my tongue. I dismissed the guard and shooed away the gaping crowd of sightseers with sharp words. Seated in the litter, poles raised by the bearers, we moved along the wide pavement separating merchant homes from the dimpling waters.

My thoughts drifted to the few ships remaining in the basin. Once, vessels packed the estuary so tightly that overflow waited outside the walls, prompting petitions to double the port's capacity. Those were the days of oars and sails, powered by the sun, when voyages to Egypt took a year. Today, with faster machines, the journey shrunk to two months, yet three ships still perished annually to the wide ocean's beasts. Trade, once roaring with lusty growth, had grown vulgar. Merchants of substance abandoned commerce for courtiership, curling their beards and directing seamstresses instead of tallying cargoes. The poor, ground down by taxes and war, starved. Markets vanished. A massive cargo of slaves, recently unloaded, was cast adrift across the estuary to die or flee into the wilds. No one in Atlantis wished to own another slave.

Grief at this decline gnawed at me, but speaking of remedies in the litter would only sour the mood. I learned Phorenice's moods; she, in turn, seemed to study mine, interspersing serious observation with trifling chatter. We stopped at the westernmost harbor wall, where a stone house stood alone on a tidal promontory. After unlocking the door, she revealed her new invention: gold collectors.

Textbooks from the Sacred Mountain taught that gold existed dissolved in seawater, but no plan existed to extract it. This field-born Empress had stumbled upon the method as if reading from a divine script.

"I tell Atlantis only the gods possess this secret," she laughed, patting my arm. "They gifted it to me. But I am no goddess to you, Deucalion. Perhaps I dreamt it." She indicated the mechanism: seawater sluiced through a culvert, flowed over rough metal plates set in the floor, and exited downstream. Gold particles trapped in the plate ridges were melted into bars by her smiths. Tidal variations dictated the yield. Initially, her workers attempted theft, but she towed two behind a galley until cannibal fish consumed them. Since then, they credited her with divine inspiration.

"You will be a god among these fools if you apply your wit," she urged.

Did she mock me? Or was she sincere? Her success oscillated between rational brilliance and impossible mystery, leaving me increasingly despaired that Atlantis could be restored to peace and the ancient gods through my agency. Even after fulfilling the High Council's commands and marrying her, the path seemed blocked.

The only viable solution appeared brutally simple: end her mischief with a dagger stroke and let the nation heal naturally. The concept revolted me. Yet, whenever the desperation surfaced, I clung to the hope that the High Gods, in their infinite wisdom, would issue a command to justify such an act.

Fascination with Phorenice outweighed caution. When we left the gold collectors and entered a zone of peril, where holding back my hand might have saved her soul, I fought harder than I ever had before. Part of this stemmed from the primal thrill of battle, but mostly it was the urge to protect her from the crude, uncouth fishers.

Street violence and desperate poverty drove the populace to risk everything for a meal. We resumed our journey in the litter. Phorenice spoke of new sumptuary laws. Previously, gold was scarce, reserved for the elite. Now, abundant and cheap, a common smith wore a gold collar. Phorenice had ordered his neck severed as punishment, a threat that silenced similar presumptions. Governing these people, she noted, was exasperating.

Suddenly, two clumsy fishing boats paddled toward us, while savage-faced men ran from the narrow lanes behind, forming threatening clusters.

"With permission," I said, stepping out of the litter, "I will disperse this rabble."

"Oh, the people always cluster around me," she replied, seemingly amused by their ugliness. "Scatter them. I do not wish to be followed. I am on holiday, Deucalion, learning to be wooed."

I descended onto the ground. The fishermen shouted warnings to their accomplices hiding in the houses. The crowd on the shore tightened its grip. We had crossed the merchants' quarter into the fishing district—a landscape of mud and grass huts, historically prone to riots even in the old King's peaceful era.

The fisherman's life was the hardest toil humanity endured. Wind, waves, hail, and predatory sea beasts claimed them daily. Their method involved training sea-birds with bronze rings around their necks to retrieve fish, feeding the birds the catch before removing the ring. While the birds were loyal, their lives were precarious; predators hunted them relentlessly. If a fisherman lost his trained birds, he was stripped of livelihood and ostracized by his guild, left to starve or steal.

These circumstances forged men who were rude, desperate, and devoid of fear. Rejected by all other trades, they spent their scant earnings on immediate debauchery, fearing the morrow would rob them of life. They possessed a singular code of honor: to feel no restraint from the fear of consequences. As the situation unfolded, they would prove this trait.
