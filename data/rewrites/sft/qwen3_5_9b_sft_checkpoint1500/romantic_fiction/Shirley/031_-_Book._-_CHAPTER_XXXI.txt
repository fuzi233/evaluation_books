Sir Philip Nunnely felt the weight of it; Shirley felt it; and Mr. Sympson, though he wore a smile, felt it keenly. That evening, as the Fieldhead family gathered at Nunnely Priory for dinner, the future hung suspended, waiting for the fork to drop.

Three forces drove Sir Philip toward a decision. First, he noted Miss Keeldar's demeanor—she appeared pensive, delicate, a fragility that struck a chord in his own poetic, perhaps slightly weak, heart. A spontaneous sonnet began to brew in his mind, unfinished and swirling. Then, at his sister's prompting, his beloved was seated at the piano to sing a ballad—one of Sir Philip's own compositions. It was his least elaborate, his least affected, yet undoubtedly the finest among his many efforts.

Just before this, Shirley had gazed from a window down onto the park. She had witnessed the stormy moonlight, perhaps the same beams Professor Louis was contemplating from his oak parlour. She saw the domain's isolated giants—the broad, strong oaks and the high-towering heroic beeches—wrestling with the gale. Her ears caught the forest's full roar, the swift rushing of clouds, and the moon itself seemed to hasten across her vision. Turning from sight and sound, she was touched, if not rapt; awakened, if not inspired.

When called upon, she sang. The ballad spoke of faithful love, undeterred by disaster, growing fonder in calamity, clinging tighter in poverty. Set to a fine old air, the words were simple and sweet. Perhaps, on paper, they lacked force, but when sung, they needed nothing. Shirley sang them with mastery. She breathed softness into the feeling, pouring force around the passion. Her voice was fine, its expression dramatically vivid. She impressed everyone, and charmed one in particular.

Leaving the instrument, she moved to the fire and sat upon a semi-stool, semi-cushion. The ladies circled her, silent. The Misses Sympson and the Misses Nunnely regarded her with the wary curiosity of poultry observing an egret or an ibis—a strange bird among familiar ones. How could she sing so? Why did she possess such originality, such expression, so unlike a schoolgirl? To them, it was simply not proper. What was strange was wrong; what was unusual was improper. Shirley was judged.

Old Lady Nunnely eyed her stonily from her great chair by the fireside. Her gaze declared, "This woman is not of my kind, nor of my daughters'. I object to her as my son's wife."

Her son caught the look and read its meaning instantly. Alarm seized him. What he wished to win, he feared he might lose. He must make haste.

The room, once a picture gallery, retained a shadowy, withdrawing quality. Sir Philip's father, Sir Monckton, had converted it into a saloon, yet it remained a space within a space—a deep recess holding a single couch, a table, and a fairy cabinet. Standing there, two persons might exchange a dialogue unheard by others.

Sir Philip induced two sisters to perform a duet, occupied the Misses Sympson, and noted with pleasure that Shirley rose to inspect the pictures. He approached her, beginning a tale of an ancestress whose dark beauty resembled a southern flower. As Shirley stooped to examine the missal and rosary on the inlaid shelf of the cabinet, while the Misses Nunnely indulged in prolonged, guiltless screeches devoid of expression or meaning, Sir Philip leaned down and whispered hurried sentences. At first, Miss Keeldar stood so still she might have been transformed into a statue by the whisper's charm, but she presently looked up and answered. They parted. She returned to the fire and resumed her seat. The baronet gazed after her, then retreated to stand behind his sisters. Only Mr. Sympson had marked the pantomime.

That gentleman drew his own conclusions. Had he been as acute as he was meddling, as profound as he was prying, he might have seen the truth in Sir Philip's face. But ever shallow, hasty, and positive, he departed quite cock-a-hoop.

He was not a man of secrets. Elated, he could not refrain from speaking. The next morning, employing his son's tutor as secretary, he announced with mouthing accents and flimsy pomp that he must prepare for a return to the south at an early day. The important business detaining him in Yorkshire was nearing fortunate completion. His anxious labors were about to be crowned with success. A truly eligible addition was about to join the family connections.

"In Sir Philip Nunnely?" Louis Moore conjectured.

Mr. Sympson treated himself to a pinch of snuff and a chuckling laugh, choked only by a sudden surge of dignity, before ordering the tutor to proceed with business.

For a day or two, Mr. Sympson remained as bland as oil, though he sat as if on pins, his gait emulating a hen treading a hot girdle. He watched the windows constantly, listening for chariot wheels. Bluebeard's wife and Sisera's mother were nothing to him compared to the impending drama. He waited for the formal opening, for consultation, for lawyers, for settlements, and for all the delicious worldly fuss.

Finally, a letter arrived. Mr. Sympson handed it to Miss Keeldar from the bag, recognizing the handwriting and the crest. He did not witness it being opened, for Shirley took it to her own room; nor did he see the reply, for she wrote it shut up, taking nearly a day. He questioned her if it was answered; she responded, "Yes."

He waited again, in silence, absolutely daring not to speak, mute by something in Shirley's face—an awful, inscrutable expression, as baffling as the writing on the wall to Belshazzar. Twice he was moved to summon Daniel, in the person of Louis Moore, to interpret, but his dignity forbade familiarity. Even Daniel, perhaps, had his own difficulties with that baffling bit of translation; he looked like a student for whom grammars are blank and dictionaries dumb.

* * * * * * *

After visiting De Walden Hall to while away an anxious hour, Mr. Sympson returned sooner than expected. His family and Miss Keeldar were assembled in the oak parlour. Addressing her, he requested she step with him into another room for a "strictly private interview."

She rose, asking no questions, professing no surprise.

"Very well, sir," she said, in the tone of a determined person informed that the dentist has arrived to extract a troublesome tooth. She left her sewing and thimble on the window-seat and followed him.

Shut into the drawing-room, they sat opposite, a few yards apart.

"I have been to De Walden Hall," said Mr. Sympson. He paused. Miss Keeldar's eyes were on the pretty white-and-green carpet. That information required no response. She gave none.

"I have learned," he went on slowly, "a circumstance which surprises me."

Resting her cheek on her forefinger, she waited to be told what.

"It seems that Nunnely Priory is shut up—that the family has returned to their estate in ----shire. It seems that the baronet, that Sir Philip himself, has accompanied his mother and sisters."

"Indeed!" said Shirley.

"May I ask if you share the amazement with which I received this news?"

"No, sir."

"_Is_ it news to you?"

"Yes, sir."

"I mean—I mean," Mr. Sympson pursued, fidgeting, returning to his customary wordy, confused, irritable style—"I intend to have a _thorough_ explanation. I will _not_ be put off. I shall _insist_ on being heard. My questions _must_ be answered. I require clear, satisfactory replies. I am not to be trifled with."

Silence fell.

"It is a strange and extraordinary thing! A very singular, most odd thing! I thought all was right, knew no other; and there—the family is gone!"

"I suppose, sir, they had a right to go."

"_Sir Philip is gone!_" (with emphasis).

Shirley raised her brows. "_Bon voyage!_" said she.

"This will not do; this must be altered, ma'am."

He drew his chair forward, pushed it back, looking incensed and helpless.

"Come, come now, uncle," expostulated Shirley, "do not begin to fret and fume, or we shall make no sense. Ask me what you want to know. I am as willing to explain as you. I promise truthful replies."

"I want—I demand to know, Miss Keeldar, whether Sir Philip has made you an offer?"

"He has."

"You avow it?"

"I avow it. But now, go on. Consider that point settled."

"He made you an offer that night we dined at the priory?"

"It is enough to say that he made it. Go on."

"He proposed in the recess—in the former picture gallery?"

No answer.

"You were both examining a cabinet. I saw it all. My sagacity was not at fault—it never is. Subsequently, you received a letter from him. On what subject? Of what nature were the contents?"

"No matter."

"Ma'am, is that the way you speak to me?"
