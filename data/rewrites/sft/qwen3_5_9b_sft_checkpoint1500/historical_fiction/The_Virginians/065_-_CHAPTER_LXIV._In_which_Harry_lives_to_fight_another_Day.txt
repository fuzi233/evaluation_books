Even the faithful Gumbo could not find solace for the loss of his beloved master. To judge by his tears and wailing upon hearing the news of Mr. Harry's enlistment, one might have believed the negro's heart shattered by the separation. Indeed, he sought sympathy wherever he turned, drifting to the maid-servants at Mr. Lambert's lodgings. There, amidst the dusky youth, he found comfort in the society of women. Their fair and tender bosoms possessed a genuine capacity for pity toward the poor African, rendering the darkness of Gumbo's complexion no barrier to affection, any more than Othello's blackness repelled Desdemona. I venture to say that Europe has never been so prudish regarding Africa as in that certain respectable quarter. Nay, some Africans—take the Chevalier de St. Georges, for instance—have become notorious favorites of the fair sex.

Thus moved Mr. Gumbo in his humbler walks. The Lambert servants wept freely in his company, and the maids kindly regarded him not merely as Mr. Harry's man, but as their brother. Hetty could not suppress a laugh when she discovered Gumbo roaring because his master had gone off as a volunteer—and notably, had not taken him along. "He would save Master Harry's life any day," Gumbo declared, "and would rather be cut into twenty thousand pieces than let harm come to him!" Yet, nature required support, and he condescended to fortify his weary frame with generous supplies of beer and cold meat from the kitchen. It was true that he was greedy, idle, and prone to lying, yet Hetty bestowed upon him half a crown and treated him with exceptional kindness. Her tongue, usually so pert and quick-witted, fell strangely silent now, as if it had never known a jest. She moved through the house with a humility and meekness that seemed transformative. She was submissive to her mother, grateful to John and Betty when serving dinner, patient with Polly when the latter pulled her hair during combing, and enduring toward Charley from school when he trampled on her toes or disrupted her workbox. Silent in Papa's presence, she was utterly transmogrified. If Papa had commanded her to roast the leg of mutton or walk to church arm-in-arm with Gumbo, she would have curtsied gently and whispered, "Yes, if you please, dear Papa!" But what sort of meal was some poor volunteer consuming, with cannonballs raining around their heads? And what of church? When the prayers of war-time were intoned, how her knees struck together as she knelt, hiding her face in the pew! She lowered her gaze when the parson read, "Thou shalt do no murder," from the communion rail, fearing his eyes were fixed upon her. Her mind wandered constantly to travelers by land and sea, sickenng as she scanned newspapers for news of the Expedition. She watched Papa closely upon his return from the Ordnance Office, searching his countenance for signs of fortune or misfortune. Was he safe? Had he been promoted to General? Was he wounded and imprisoned? Ah, me! Or worse, had both his legs been severed by a single shot, like that pensioner they had seen in Chelsea Gardens the day before? She would have walked upon wooden legs all her life if his sacrifice brought him home safely; indeed, she felt she ought never rise from her knees until he was returned. "Haven't you heard of people, Theo?" she asked, "whose hair turns gray in a single night? I should not wonder if mine did—I wouldn't wonder in the least." Peering into the glass, she scrutinized her reflection for evidence of this phenomenon.

"Hetty dear, you used not to be so nervous when Papa was away in Minorca," remarked Theo.

"Ah, Theo! One can plainly see that George is not with the army, but safe at home," rejoined Hetty. Upon this, the elder sister blushed deeply, looking profoundly pensive. *Au fait*, if Mr. George had actually been in the army, that would have constituted another pair of boots. Meanwhile, we shall spare anyone further harrowing of their kind feelings and simply state that Harry, for the present, was as secure as any officer of the Life Guards stationed at Regent's Park Barracks.

The first expedition in which our gallant volunteer was engaged may be termed successful, though certainly not glorious. The British Lion, or any other lion, cannot always encounter a worthy adversary to combat, nor a battle-royal to deliver. Suppose he ventures forth seeking a tiger that refuses to emerge, and instead lays his paws upon a goose, gobbling it up? Lions, we know, must subsist like any other animal. But suppose, advancing into the forest in search of that aforementioned tiger and bellowing his challenge of war, he spies not one, but six tigers rushing toward him? This manifestly is not his game. He tucks his tail between his royal legs and retreats into his own snug den as swiftly as possible. Were he to attempt fighting six tigers, you might fairly write that Lion down an Ass.

Now, Harry Warrington's first feat of war unfolded thus. He and approximately 13,000 other fighting men embarked in various ships and transports on the 1st of June from the Isle of Wight. At daybreak on the 5th, the fleet steamed into the Bay of Cancale in Brittany. For a while, Harry and the gentlemen-volunteers enjoyed the pleasure of surveying the French coast from their vessels, while the Commander-in-Chief and the Commodore reconnoitered the bay in a cutter. Cattle were spotted, and some dragoons trotted off into the distance; moreover, a small fort armed with a couple of guns displayed sufficient audacity to fire upon His Grace the Duke of Marlborough and the Commodore within the cutter. By two o'clock, the entire British fleet was anchored, and signals were given for all the grenadier companies of eleven regiments to board flat-bottomed boats and assemble around the Commodore's ship, the *Essex*. Simultaneously, Mr. Howe, hoisting his broad pennant aboard the *Success* frigate, drew near the shore as close as possible, followed by other frigates, to protect the landing of the troops. Now, under the command of Lord George Sackville and General Dury, the gentlemen-volunteers, the grenadier companies, and three battalions of guards pulled ashore.

On this occasion, the gentlemen-volunteers could perform no heroic deeds, for the French, who ought to have remained to fight, fled. Having silenced the fire of the small fort that had disturbed the Commander-in-Chief's reconnaissance, the frigates enabled the army to assault it immediately, capturing the entire garrison prisoner and shooting the commander in the leg. Indeed, he was but an elderly gentleman who had gallantly fired his two guns, telling his conquerors, "If every Frenchman had acted like me, you would not have landed at Cancale at all."

The advanced detachment of invaders took possession of the village of Cancale, where they lay upon their arms throughout the night. Our volunteer was jested at by his comrades regarding his eagerness to tread the war-path and bring in two or three scalps of Frenchmen. None such, however, fell beneath his tomahawk; the only person slain on the whole day was a French gentleman riding with his servant, who was surprised by Volunteer Lord Downe, marching forward with a company of Kingsley's. My Lord Downe offered the gentleman quarter, which he foolishly refused. Whereupon the gentleman, his servant, and the two horses were straightway shot.

The following day, the whole force was landed and advanced from Cancale to St. Malo. All villages through which the troops passed were emptied, and the roads were so narrow in many places that the men were forced to march single file, vulnerable to being shot down from behind the tall, leafy hedges had there been any enemy to disturb them.

At nightfall, the army arrived before St. Malo and were greeted by a discharge of artillery from the town, which caused little damage in the darkness. Taking cover under this barrage, the British set fire to ships, wooden buildings, pitch and tar magazines within the harbor, creating a prodigious conflagration that raged all night.

This feat was accomplished without any attempt by the French to molest the British force. However, as it was confidently asserted that a considerable French force remained within the town of St. Malo, despite their refusal to engage, His Grace the Duke of Marlborough and Lord George Sackville resolved not to disturb the garrison. Consequently, they marched back to Cancale again—and thus, eventually, boarded their ships.

If this were not a veracious history, would it not have been easier to send our Virginian on a more glorious campaign? Exactly four weeks after his departure from England, Mr. Warrington found himself once again at Portsmouth. He addressed a letter to his brother George, which the latter hurried to Dean Street to receive as soon as it arrived.

"Glorious news, ladies!" cried George, finding the Lambert family gathered at breakfast. "Our champion has returned. He has undergone all sorts of dangers, but has survived them all. He has seen dragons—upon my word, he says so."

"Dragons! What do you mean, Mr. Warrington?"
