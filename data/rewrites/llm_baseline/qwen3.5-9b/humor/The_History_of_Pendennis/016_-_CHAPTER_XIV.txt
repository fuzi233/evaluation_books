Within a fortnight of the events previously recounted, Mr. Manager Bingley was delivering his signature role of 'Rolla' in *Pizarro* to an audience so sparse it threatened to collapse under the weight of its emptiness. It became evident that while the part of Rolla was a favorite with the accomplished actor himself, the citizens of Chatteris held little affection for it. Scarcely anyone filled the theater. Poor Pen occupied the boxes almost alone, sitting there with bloodshot eyes, leaning precariously over the ledge and staring haggardly toward the stage.

When Cora was not center-stage, he saw nothing. Spaniards, Peruvians, processions, battles, priests, and virgins of the sun marched in and out, engaging in their theatrical dialogues, yet Arthur paid them no heed. His entire world narrowed down to Cora, the object of his soul's longing. Later, he confessed that he marveled he hadn't grabbed a pistol to shoot her; he was consumed by a volatile mix of love, rage, and despair. Had it not been for his mother at home—who remained silent regarding his misfortune but offered a comforting, watchful sympathy—he might have succumbed to desperation, ending his days prematurely in front of Chatteris gaol.

There he sat, miserable, fixed on her image. Yet, she took no more notice of him than he did of the rest of the house. Miss Fotheringay was strikingly handsome, dressed in white raiment and leopard skin, with a golden sun pinned to her breast and fine, glittering bracelets adorning her beautiful arms. She delivered her few lines to perfection and embodied the role even better. The eyes that had once shattered Pen's soul rolled and gleamed with their usual luster, but tonight they were not directed at him. He did not know to whom they belonged, nor did he notice the two gentlemen seated in the box adjacent to his.

Nor had Pen observed the dramatic shift that occurred shortly after these men entered. With the house nearly empty, the first act dragged on interminably, leading to whispers of refunding tickets, reminiscent of another disastrous night when Pen had been driven out. The actors were indifferent, yawning through their lines and chatting loudly during pauses. Even Bingley was listless, and Mrs. Bingley, playing Elvira, muttered her lines under her breath.

Then, suddenly, Mrs. Bingley raised her voice and bellowed like a bull of Bashan. Bingley, shedding his apathy, darted across the stage yelling like Dean. Garretts, Rowkins, and Miss Rouncy abandoned all subtlety, strutting, scowling, and shouting their lines with maximum volume, all aimed squarely at the two gentlemen in Box Number Three.

One was a quiet, diminutive man in black with a grey head and a shrewd, jovial expression. The other was a magnificent figure: tall, portly, sporting a hooked nose and a profusion of curling brown hair and whiskers. His coat was encrusted with rich frog-braiding and velvet, layered with waistcoats, splendid rings, jeweled pins, and neck chains. When he unfolded his yellow pocket-handkerchief with a hand gloved in white kid, a delightful cloud of musk and bergamot drifted through the theater. He was clearly a personage of high rank, and the entire Chatteris troupe was performing solely for him.

In a word, he was Mr. Dolphin, the great impresario from London, accompanied by his inseparable secretary, Mr. William Minns, who never traveled without him. Within ten minutes of his arrival, his august presence transformed the theater. Bingley and the rest instantly switched gears, trying desperately to capture his attention. Even Miss Fotheringay's dull heart, usually undisturbed, fluttered slightly at the sight of the famous London manager. Her role required little more than looking handsome and standing in picturesque poses around her child, which she executed to admiration.

Despite the actors' frantic efforts to win the "Great Stage Sultan's" favor, *Pizarro* received no handshakes. Bingley yelled, Mrs. Bingley bellowed, and Mr. Dolphin merely took a pinch of snuff from his massive gold box. It was only in the final scene, when Rolla (played by Bingley, who was losing strength; his fourth son, Master Talma Bingley, being monstrously large for his age) staggered onto the stage holding the infant, rushing toward Cora who screamed, "Oh God, there's blood upon him!" that the London manager finally clapped his hands and erupted in an enthusiastic bravo.

Concluding his applause, Mr. Dolphin slapped his secretary on the shoulder and declared, "By Jove, Billy, she'll do!"
Old Billy, a sardonic gentleman, replied, "Who taught her that dodge?"
"I remember her at the Olympic," Billy mused. "Hang me if she could say 'Bo' to a goose."
It was Little Mr. Bows in the orchestra who had instructed her. As the curtain fell, the entire company swarmed around her, congratulating and hating Miss Fotheringay in equal measure.

Mr. Dolphin's sudden appearance in the remote Chatteris theater could be explained thus: despite his relentless advertising of triumphs, coruscations of talent, and victories of good old English comedy, his theater (the Museum Theatre) was failing. The great Hubbard had played legitimate drama for twenty nights without paying a single soul but himself. The celebrated Mr. and Mrs. Cawdor had toured in Mr. Rawhead's tragedies to no avail. Herr Garbe's lions and tigers had drawn crowds briefly until one animal bit a chunk out of the Herr's shoulder, prompting the Lord Chamberlain to ban the act. Even the grand Lyrical Drama, produced with unprecedented splendor featuring Monsieur Poumons as lead tenor, had nearly crushed poor Dolphin under its weight. Dragging on with half-salaries, small operas, feeble comedies, and a struggling ballet company, everyone awaited his obituary in the Gazette.

Among his illustrious patrons was the Most Noble the Marquis of Steyne, a refined lover of the arts who rarely attended plays due to state affairs. However, he occasionally arrived just in time for the ballet, receiving respectful nods from the manager. Secluded behind a curtain, invisible to the audience, Lord Steyne watched intently. His presence was known by the way the corps-de-ballet and principal dancers constantly glanced toward his box. I have witnessed dozens of pairs of eyes—like the hundred-and-twenty lovely female savages in the Palm Dance of *Cook at Otaheite*—ogling that specific box. I marvelled at the composure of Mademoiselle Sauterelle or Mademoiselle de Bondi ("la petite Caoutchoue"), who, while suspended in mid-air quivering like shuttlecocks, managed to wink lovingly at the box where the great Steyne sat. Occasionally, a harsh voice cried "Brava!" from behind the curtain, or white gloves waved in approval. Bondi or Sauterelle would land, curtsey, and smile specifically at those hands before resuming their panting ascent.

One evening, surrounded by friends, Lord Steyne's box was raucous enough to scandalize the pit, with indignant shouts demanding silence. Mr. Wenham amused the party with excerpts from a private letter from Major Pendennis, whose absence during the London season was noted with regret.

"The secret is out," Mr. Wenham announced. "There's a woman in the case."
"Why, d—— it, Wenham, he's your age," the gentleman behind the curtain retorted.
"Pour les âmes bien nées, l'amour ne compte pas le nombre des années," Mr. Wenham replied gallantly. "For my part, I hope to be a victim till I die, breaking my heart every year of my life."
The meaning was clear: *My lord, you need not worry; I'm three years younger and twice as resilient.*
"Wenham, you affect me," the great man laughed. "By ——— you do. I like seeing a fellow preserve the illusions of youth... Who's that gal in the second row with blue ribbons? Fine girl. Yes, you and I are sentimentalists. Wagg, I suspect you care less—it's the stomach rather than the heart with you, isn't it?"
"I like everything that's good," Mr. Wagg agreed generously. "Beauty and Burgundy, Venus and Venison. Though I admit Venus's turtles aren't cooked at the London Tavern..." He trailed off, sensing his patron's disinterest. Lord Steyne adjusted his glasses, examining the stage.
"Yes, I've heard that joke about Venus's turtle before—you're beginning to fail, my poor Wagg. If you don't mind, I shall require a new Jester." He turned back to Wenham. "Go on, about old Pendennis."
