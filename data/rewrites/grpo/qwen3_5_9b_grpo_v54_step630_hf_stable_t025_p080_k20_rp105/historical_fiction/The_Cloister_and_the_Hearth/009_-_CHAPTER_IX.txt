Margaret severed a massive chunk of venison and hurled it through the window, where it landed before the emerald eyes of the fire. The beast lunged forward with a savage snarl, tearing and crunching the meat. Suddenly, a hound bayed so loudly and close that the sound echoed through the very rafters, causing the three animals at the window to recoil in terror. The leopard, fearing her supper was stolen, glided swiftly and silently away toward the woods. Before she had vanished, a chaotic throng of horses, men, and dogs thundered past the window, pursuing her with a full cry. Martin and his companions exhaled, relieved; the leopard was too swift to be caught within a league of their house. They clasped hands in relief. Seizing the moment, Margaret let out a small sob, and Gerard tenderly kissed the tears from her cheeks.

They returned to the table, and Gerard raised a toast to woman's wit, declaring it stronger than man's physical force. Margaret agreed, adding that this strength was reserved solely for moments when those she loved were in peril. That night, Gerard stayed with her longer than usual, returning home feeling prouder than ever and as happy as a prince. A short distance from home, beneath the shadow of ancient trees, he encountered two figures blocking his path. It was his father and mother.

"Why are you out so late?" Gerard asked, a chill settling over him. "What could be the cause?"

His father stopped him with a grim silence. "Why ask? You know why we are here."

"Oh, Gerard!" his mother interjected, her voice thick with reproach yet underlined with affection.

Gerard's heart hammered against his ribs; he fell silent. Seeing his son's confusion, his father softened slightly. "Do not hang your head. You are not the first young fool caught by a red cheek and a pair of blue eyes."

"Nay, nay!" Catherine added, "It was witchcraft. Peter the Magician is well known for such tricks."

"Come, Sir Priest," the father resumed, "you know you must not meddle with women folk. But give us your promise to go no more to Sevenbergen, and here all ends. We will not be hard on you for one fault."

"I cannot promise that, father."

"Not promise it, you young hypocrite!"

"Nay, father, do not miscall me. I lacked the courage to tell you what I knew would vex you. I am right grateful to that good friend, whoever he be, who let you know. It is a load off my mind. Yes, father, I love Margaret. And call me not a priest, for a priest I will never be. I would die sooner."

"That we shall see, young man. Come, gainsay me no more; you will learn what it is to disrespect a father."

Gerard held his peace, and the three walked home in a gloomy silence, broken only by deep sighs from Catherine.

From that hour, the little house at Tergou was no longer a sanctuary of peace. The next day, Gerard was summoned before the entire family, and every voice rose against him, save for little Kate's and the dwarf's, who instinctively sided with him. Cornelis and Sybrandt were bitterer than their father. Gerard was dismayed to find so many enemies and looked wistfully at his little sister. Her eyes brimmed with tears as harsh words were showered upon one who had been the universal pet just yesterday. Yet she offered no encouragement, turning her head away to say, "Dear, dear Gerard, pray to Heaven to cure you of this folly!"

"What, are you against me too?" Gerard asked sadly. He rose with a heavy sigh and left the house, walking toward Sevenbergen.

The beginning of a quarrel between parties bound by affection but opposed in interest is often innocent; both may be right at the start. It is only as the dissension endures, widening and deepening through human fallibility and irritability, that both sides inevitably become wrong. The combatants were unequally matched: Elias was angry, Cornelis and Sybrandt spiteful. But Gerard, possessing a larger and more cultivated mind, saw both sides where they saw only one. He suffered from fits of irresolution, feeling not wrath but profound unhappiness. He was lonely in this struggle, unable to open his heart to anyone. Margaret was a high-spirited girl; he dared not tell her of his torment at home, fearing she might side with his relations by resigning him, even at the cost of her own happiness. Though Margaret Van Eyck had been a great comfort to him on another occasion, he dared not make her his confidant now. Her history was well known: in her youth, she had refused many offers of marriage for the sake of her art, duties that a wife's and mother's life would render fatal. Thus, she remained single, painting with her brothers. How could he tell her that he had declined the benefice she had secured for him, doing so for the sake of something she had once despised and sacrificed lightly?

Gerard seemed poised to succumb. But the other side had a terrible ally in Catherine the elder. This good-hearted but uneducated woman could not act quietly or firmly like her daughter, nor could she execute a plan. She irritated Gerard at times, which paradoxically helped him, for anger sustains courage; at others, she turned round and launched attacks on her own forces. On one occasion, when they were all at home, Cornelis declared, "Our Gerard wed Margaret Brandt? Why, it is hunger marrying thirst."

"And what will it be when you marry?" Catherine cried. "Gerard can paint, Gerard can write, but what can you do to keep a woman, ye lazy loon? Nought but wait for your father's shoes. Oh, we can see why you and Sybrandt would not have the poor boy to marry. You are afraid he will come to us for a share of our substance. And say that he does, and say that we give it him, it isn't yourn we part from, and mayhap never will be."

On such occasions, Gerard smiled slyly, picked up his heart, and temporary confusion fell on Catherine's unfortunate allies. But finally, after more than six months of irritation, the climax arrived. The father announced to the son, before the whole family, that he had ordered the burgomaster to imprison him in the Stadthouse rather than let him marry Margaret. Gerard turned pale with anger but held his peace through a great effort. His father continued, "And a priest you shall be before the year is out, nilly-willy."

"Is it so?" Gerard cried. "Then, hear me, all. By God and St. Bavon I swear I will never be a priest while Margaret lives. Since force is to decide it, and not love and duty, try force, father; but force shall not serve you. For the day I see the burgomaster come for me, I leave Tergou forever, and Holland too, and my father's house, where it seems I have been valued all these years not for myself, but for what is to be got out of me."

He flung himself out of the room, white with anger and desperation.

"There!" cried Catherine. "That comes of driving young folks too hard. But men are crueller than tigers, even to their own flesh and blood. Now, Heaven forbid he should ever leave us, married or single."

As Gerard emerged from the house, cheeks pale and heart panting, he met Reicht Heynes, who bore a message: Margaret Van Eyck desired to see him. He found the old lady seated grim as a judge. She wasted no time on preliminaries, inquiring coldly why he had not visited her of late. Before he could answer, she said in a sarcastic tone, "I thought we had been friends, young sir."

At this, Gerard looked the picture of doubt and consternation.

"It is because you never told her you were in love," Reicht Heynes said, pitying his confusion.

"Silence, wench! Why should he tell us his affairs? We are not his friends; we have not deserved his confidence."

"Alas! my second mother," Gerard replied, "I did not dare to tell you my folly."

"What folly? Is it folly to love?"

"I am told so every day of my life."

"You need not have been afraid to tell my mistress; she is always kind to true lovers."

"Madam—Reicht, I was afraid because I was told..."

"Well, you were told—?"

"That in your youth you scorned love, preferring art."

"I did, boy; and what is the end of it? Behold me here a barren stock, while the women of my youth have a troop of children at their side and grandchildren at their knee. I gave up the sweet joys of wifehood and motherhood for what? For my dear brothers. They have gone and left me long ago. For my art. It has all but left me too. I have the knowledge still, but what avails that when the hand trembles? No, Gerard; I look on you as my son. You are good, you are handsome, you are a painter, though not like some I have known. I will not let you throw your youth away as I did mine: you shall marry this Margaret. I have inquired, and she is a good daughter. Reicht here is a gossip; she has told me all about it. But that need not hinder you from telling me."

Poor Gerard was overjoyed to be permitted to praise Margaret aloud to one who understood what he loved in her. Soon, there were two pairs of wet eyes over his story; and when the poor boy saw that, there were three.

Women are creatures brimful of courage. Theirs is not exactly the same quality as manly courage; that would never do, hang it all; we should have to give up trampling on them. No; it is a vicarious courage. They never take part in a bull-fight by any chance; but it is remarked that they sit at one unshaken by those tremors and apprehensions for the combatants to which the male spectator—a feeble-minded wretch—is subject. Nothing can exceed the resolution with which they have been known to send forth men to battle. As some witty dog says, "Les femmes sont très braves avec le peur d'autrui."

By this trait, Gerard now profited. Margaret and Reicht were agreed that a man should always take the bull by the horns. Gerard's only course was to marry Margaret Brandt off-hand; the old people would come to after a while, the deed once done. Whereas, the longer this misunderstanding continued on its present footing, the worse it would be for all parties, especially for Gerard.

"See how pale and thin they have made him amongst them."

"Indeed you are, Master Gerard," said Reicht. "It makes a body sad to see a young man so wasted and worn. Mistress, when I met him in the street today, I had liked to have burst out crying: he was so changed."

"And I'll be bound the others keep their colour; ah, Reicht? Such as it is."

"Oh, I see no odds in them."

"Of course not. We painters are no match for boors. We are glass, they are stone. We can't stand the worry, worry, worry of little minds; and it is not for the good of mankind we should be exposed to it. It is hard enough, Heaven knows, to design and paint a masterpiece, without having gnats and flies stinging us to death into the bargain."

Though exasperated by his father's threat of violence, Gerard listened to these friendly voices telling him the prudent course was rebellion. But though he listened, he was not convinced.

"I do not fear my father's violence," he said, "but I do fear his anger. When it came to the point, he would not imprison me. I would marry Margaret tomorrow if that was my only fear. No; he would disown me. I should take Margaret from her father and give her a poor husband, who would never thrive, weighed down by his parent's curse. Madam! I sometimes think if I could marry her secretly, and then take her away to some country where my craft is better paid than in this; and after a year or two, when the storm had blown over, you know, could come back with money in my purse, and say, 'My dear parents, we do not seek your substance, we but ask you to love us once more as you used, and as we have never ceased to love you'—but, alas! I shall be told these are the dreams of an inexperienced young man."

The old lady's eyes sparkled.

"It is no dream, but a piece of wonderful common-sense in a boy; it remains to be seen whether you have spirit to carry out your own thought. There is a country, Gerard, where certain fortune awaits you at this moment. Here the arts freeze, but there they flourish, as they never yet flourished in any age or land."

"It is Italy!" cried Gerard. "It is Italy!"

"Ay, Italy! Where painters are honored like princes, and scribes are paid three hundred crowns for copying a single manuscript. Know you not that His Holiness the Pope has written to every land for skilful scribes to copy the hundreds of precious manuscripts pouring into that favored land from Constantinople, whence learning and learned men are driven by the barbarian Turks?"

"Nay, I know not that; but it has been the dream and hope of my life to visit Italy, the queen of all the arts; oh, madam! But the journey, and we are all so poor."

"Find you the heart to go, I'll find the means. I know where to lay my hand on ten golden angels: they will take you to Rome: and the girl with you, if she loves you as she ought."

They sat till midnight over this theme. And after that day, Gerard recovered his spirits, seeming to carry a secret talisman against all the gibes and harsh words that flew about his ears at home.
