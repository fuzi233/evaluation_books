Before we turn our attention further to the adventures of Miss Lydia, it becomes necessary to unravel the cryptic remark in Mr. Warrington's correspondence regarding Lady Maria Esmond—a sentence that may still puzzle the unsophisticated reader. How, indeed, could a person of steady morals foresee such a destiny? How could virtuous youth imagine that a lady of noble lineage, ancient pedigree, and mature judgment—who had been passionately devoted to her cousin merely a dozen months prior—could so entirely lose her senses as to fall in love with a man of humble origin, many years her junior, and wed him in defiance of the world? Or rather, not openly against the world, but secretly behind its back, for it was Parson Sampson who privately bound them in matrimony within the confines of his Mayfair chapel.

Pause, however, before you render your final condemnation. Did Lady Maria possess a fleeting affection for her youthful cousin, thereby forfeiting the right to love anyone else? Must men enjoy the exclusive liberty of changing hearts, while women are censured for seizing occasional solace? There exists nothing more crude and unphilosophical than Hamlet's scathing reproach to his mother concerning her second union. The reality, likely, is that this tender, clinging creature sought something to grasp onto; with her father removed from the scene, she coiled herself around Claudius. Indeed, we have witnessed females so determined in attachment that they entwine themselves around two gentlemen simultaneously. Why, pray, must there not exist wedding tables following funeral feasts? If you offered grace for yesterday's banquet, does that preclude hunger today? Your innate, refined appetite for tonight's repast proves that at eight o'clock tomorrow evening, you will undoubtedly crave your supper. As for me, when Flirtilla or Jiltissa once favored me—with minds conjuring images of breathtaking beauty and exalted rank—I invariably recalled that a season would arrive when their affections would shift elsewhere. Life serves us *à la russe*, dishes devoured one by one, much like the inhabitants of Polyphemus's cavern. *Hodie mihi, cras tibi*: there are anthropophagites who consume dozens of us—the aged, the young, the soft, the sturdy, the plump, the lean, the plain, the fair—and there is no escape; eventually, one by one, according to fate, we vanish down their insatiable throats. Recall Lady Ogresham! All remember last year how she seized poor Tom Kydd, consumed him, gnawed his bones, and cast them aside. Now, Ned Suckling lies beneath her gaze, trembling and entranced. Behold the poor, timid creature, panting and helpless under those great eyes! She drags herself nearer, step by step; he retreats, inch by inch. Soon, a few faint squeals of pity will rise, followed by—"hobblegobble"—and he will disappear! Alas, it is pity as well. I knew, long before Harry Warrington claimed it, that Maria Esmond had lost her heart countless times; yet I liked to dream that he would retain it, and that, lamenting misfortune and broken order, she would preserve her love in decorous singleness. If, in some fit of senile madness, I fell in love tomorrow, I would still strive to believe I held the freehold of my beloved's heart—not merely a tenancy on a brief lease of a dilapidated apartment, where dusty wineglasses bore the stains of countless lips and tattered sofas smelled of the previous occupant's boots. Dear, dear nymph! To be cherished and beautiful! Should I harbor a passing infatuation for Glycera (whose complexion truly rivaled splendid Parian marble), and you a fancy for Telephus, with his low collars and ridiculous neck—those follies are surely ended, are they not? We love each other forever now, do we not? Yes, eternally; and Glycera may depart for Bath, while Telephus takes his pink throat to Jack Ketch, *n'est-ce pas*?

No. We never conceive of alteration, my dear. Whichever way the winds blow, or time flies, or spoons stir, our potage, now boiling hot, shall never grow cold. Passing whims we may have indulged in days past; truly, your obsession with Telephus (do not frown so, my darling creature, nor deepen the wrinkles on your brow)—indeed, it was the town's gossip—and regarding Glycera, she behaved dreadfully ill to me. Well, well, now that we understand one another, our hearts are united forever, and we can regard Sir Cresswell Cresswell and wave his wig away. Yet this Maria of the last century possessed an unruly mind. You, my love, who know the world, understand that considerable matters must have transpired in her career that neither endure the light nor edify the telling. You know (not, my dear, that I imply you possess worldly wisdom, but you have heard others say—you have heard your mother declare)—that an elderly flirt, having played the fool with one passion, will inevitably play it with another; that flirting resembles drinking, and when the brandy is exhausted, Glycera—who has adopted the habit—will turn to gin. Thus, if Maria Esmond has found a successor to Harry Warrington and installed a new Sultan in the precious empire of her heart, what, after all, could you reasonably expect? That realm was akin to the Low Countries, accustomed to conquest and perpetually open to invasion.

And Maria's current master was none other than Mr. Geoghegan or Hagan, the young actor who had starred in George's tragedy. His voice was so moving, his eyes so radiant, his bearing so noble, appearing so handsome in his gilded leather armor and voluminous buckled periwig as he recited the poet's fiery verses, that the lady yielded her heart to him, just as Ariadne surrendered hers to Bacchus when her tryst with Theseus concluded. The young Irishman was deeply touched and elated by the high-born damsel's favor. Though he might have preferred a Lady Maria Hagan younger in years, it would have been difficult to find one more tender in disposition. She clung to him tightly. Indeed, when it became necessary to reveal their marriage and her furious relatives disowned her, she retreated to his modest lodging in Westminster with him.

General Lambert brought the news home from his office in Whitehall one day and merrily shared it with his household. In those simpler times, a joke was often improved by being slightly broad; a fine lady might laugh at a jovial page of Fielding and weep over a letter of Clarissa, causing your present ladyship's eyes to pop out in horror. The merry General uttered every manner of wag upon this marriage, speculating on George's role in facilitating it, mocking Barry's jealousy upon hearing the news. He vowed it was cruel that Cousin Hagan had not selected George as his groomsman, suggesting the first child be named Carpezan or Sybilla after the tragedy, and so forth. They would scarcely afford a carriage, but they might acquire a chariot adorned with pasteboard dragons from Mr. Rich's theater. The babe might be baptized in Macbeth's cauldron; Harry and Harlequin ought certainly to serve as godfathers.

"Why shouldn't she marry him if she loves him?" asked little Hetty. "Why shouldn't he love her because she is somewhat older? Mamma is a little old, and you love her none the worse. When you married my mamma, sir, I heard you say you were very poor; yet you were very happy, and no one laughed at you!" Thus did this impudent little soul speak, guided by her tender age and unaware of Lady Maria Esmond's former follies.

"So her family has abandoned her?" George described their wrath, noting how Lady Castlewood had entered mourning, how Mr. Will swore to have the rascal's ears cut off, and how furious Madame de Bernstein was, the angriest of all. "It is an affront to the family," declared haughty little Miss Hett, "and I can easily imagine how ladies of such standing must be indignant at their relative's union with someone of Mr. Hagan's station; but to desert her is a far graver matter."

"Indeed, my dear child," cried Mamma, "you speak of things you do not comprehend. After Lady Maria's conduct, no respectable person can visit her."

"What conduct, Mamma?"

"Never mind," cried Mamma. "Little girls cannot be expected to know, nor ought they to be too curious to inquire, what Lady Maria's conduct has been! Suffice it, miss, that I am shocked her ladyship should ever have been here; and I repeat, no honest person should associate with her!"

"Then, Aunt Lambert, I must be whipped and sent to bed," said George, with mock solemnity. "I own to you (though I did not confess sooner, seeing the affair was not mine) that I visited my cousin the player and his ladyship, his wife. I found them in very dismal lodgings in Westminster, where the wretch has the shame to keep not only his wife but his old mother and a little brother, whom he sends to school. I found Mr. Hagan and departed with a liking, nay, almost a respect for him, though I admit he has made a very imprudent marriage. But how imprudent some folks are about marriage, are they not, Theo?"
