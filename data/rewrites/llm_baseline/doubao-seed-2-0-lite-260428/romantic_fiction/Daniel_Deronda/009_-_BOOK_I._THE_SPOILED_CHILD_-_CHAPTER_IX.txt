I’ll tell thee, Berthold, what men’s hopes are like:
A silly child that, quivering with joy,
Would cast its little mimic fishing-line
Baited with loadstone for a bowl of toys
In the salt ocean.

Eight months after the Davilow and Harleth household settled into Offendene, as the sweet warm haze of the following June settled over the Wessex countryside, an electric little rumor spread through every village, market town, and country estate within driving distance of Wanchester. It had nothing to do with the distant American war, but it sent ripples of bright, greedy excitement through every class: corn factors, brewers, horse dealers, and saddlers all praised it as proof of how valuable a landed aristocracy was to English prosperity, the blacksmith in little Diplow hamlet rubbed his hands over the promise of steady work for years to come, laborers’ wives daydreamed of their quick ten- and twelve-year-old boys getting steady, well-paid jobs as footmen in livery, and local farmers, even with a hint of gruff, bitter reserve, admitted they might finally get a fair price for a rick of old hay or a wagon of straw at last.

If the working classes had such small, bright hopes tied to the news, the gentry’s excitement ran even deeper, tangled up in all the soft, glittering pleasures of social life—and the rumor centered on marriage, that grand intersection of business and joy that makes even the most respectable household hold quiet, secret dreams. When news of a royal visit circulates, every city man tucks a quiet fantasy of a knighthood under his nightcap; and this news set off half a dozen floating, half-formed visions of a perfect, prestigious match across half a dozen well-bred drawing rooms.

The news was this: Diplow Hall, Sir Hugo Mallinger’s grand estate that had stood shuttered for two whole years, its rows of white window shutters half-closed like the squinting eye of a slumbering giant turning its back on its ancient elms, lily-fringed pool, and deer-dappled lawns, was being prepped for a new tenant. All summer, and through the entire hunting season, the house and its stables would be occupied in proper, lavish style. Not by Sir Hugo himself, but by his nephew, Mallinger Grandcourt, the presumptive heir to the baronetcy—since Sir Hugo and his wife had only had daughters. Fortune had stacked every possible advantage in young Grandcourt’s favor: the baronetcy would come through his father’s line, and his mother’s noble blood meant that if a handful of distant, little-known relatives happened to pass, he would inherit a barony and a seat in the House of Lords itself.

Fate is uneven in its gifts, of course, but even the most strict moralist could not argue that a man with that kind of fortune and peerage potential would not have a line of eager young women waiting to accept his hand—no one could blame those around him for quietly daydreaming that their daughter or ward might be the one to claim that title. Some readers may protest that no respectable, well-bred people would ever build entire marriage prospects on nothing more than the news that a wealthy unmarried man was moving within ten miles of their home, that such scheming is not real human nature. But we speak only of a small, tight-knit corner of Wessex, where every household was on visiting terms with the peerage, and every family had their own quiet stakes in the game.

Take the Arrowpoints, for example, who owned the sprawling, beautiful estate at Quetcham. No one could accuse them of being sordid about their daughter Catherine’s prospects, not when they would leave her a fortune of at least half a million pounds. But they were affectionate, anxious for her to have the perfect position in life—she had already turned down Lord Slogan, an otherwise perfectly respectable Irish peer whose estate was only lacking proper drainage and enough residents to pay its rents—and they found themselves quietly wondering, with more than casual curiosity, if Mr Grandcourt was handsome, of sound constitution, virtuous, or at least reformed from any wild youthful habits, and if his liberal-conservative politics were not too wildly extreme. They never wished anyone dead, of course, but they found themselves thinking his ascension to the peerage would be a most welcome event.

If the wealthy Arrowpoints ruminated this way, it is no surprise that the same thoughts crept into the mind of Reverend Gascoigne, the local rector. For all his clerical good sense, he was still a parent and guardian, already overburdened with the task of reining in his wild, willful niece Gwendolen, who would not be guided by bit, bridle, or any amount of judicious, gentle advice.

Of course, no one spoke these thoughts out loud. No one asked a neighbor if they had reliable intel on Grandcourt as a possible husband for their charming daughter, no one admitted out loud that they dared to hope for such a glittering match. Mrs Gascoigne only let one quiet remark slip to her sister Mrs Davilow: “It would be a true mercy, Fanny, if that girl Gwendolen were settled well and properly.” Soft, mild Mrs Davilow caught the faint hint of criticism of her darling daughter in her sister’s tone, and said nothing out loud, though she thought privately, No one will get her to marry just to suit you—her gentle temper turned sharp the moment anyone dared question Gwendolen’s perfection. Even to his own wife, Mr Gascoigne never admitted he had thought for a second that Grandcourt might meet Gwendolen at the upcoming annual Archery Meeting and be immediately captivated, though Mrs Gascoigne’s own mind was running along exactly that same track.

When she casually asked her husband what kind of man Grandcourt was, the rector brushed off the question: he thought it foolish, even improper, to pry into the wild youth of a man of such high birth and vast wealth, whose position excused a dozen small vices that would have ruined a man of lesser station. Any rich man who gambles and stops before he loses his own fortune, he reasoned, is practically a model of reformed virtue—no one would subject a future baronet to the same suspicious vetting one might give a new applicant for the position of footman.

Mrs Davilow, for all her quiet pride in Gwendolen, could not help but daydream a little too. She pictured Grandcourt first as a handsome, accomplished, perfect gentleman who would make any girl a wonderful husband—then stopped to wonder if that would be enough for Gwendolen, whose tastes were so wild and unreadable no one could guess what might catch her fancy, let alone win her love. She even found herself thinking that it would not matter if Gwendolen was not head over heels in love, if only she would accept the right, good husband, spared all the hardship and grief that had marked Mrs Davilow’s own married life. But she dared never whisper any of these daydreams to Gwendolen herself, not after the heartbreak of Rex’s farewell: she knew the moment Gwendolen guessed her family was pushing her toward a man, she would immediately detest him on principle.

The only safe topic was the dress Gwendolen would wear to the Archery Meeting. They had settled on soft white cashmere, accented with a touch of pale green that perfectly set off her fair complexion, and a matching green feather for her hat. Gwendolen was striking a pose before the mirror, pretending to draw her bow, when she suddenly turned to her mother with a glint of wicked, playful glee in her eyes. “I pity every other girl at that archery gathering,” she laughed. “All of them already mooning over Mr Grandcourt. Not a single one of them stands a chance.”

Mrs Davilow froze, caught completely off guard. Gwendolen spun around to face her, grinning wider. “You know they don’t, mamma. You, and uncle, and aunt—all three of you are already planning for him to fall madly in love with me.”

Piqued into a quick, playful defense, Mrs Davilow said, “Oh, my dear, that is not certain at all. Miss Arrowpoint has charms you do not.”

“I know, but her charms demand effort and thought from a man,” Gwendolen laughed. “My arrow will pierce him right through the heart before he has time to think. He will declare himself my absolute slave. I shall send him round the whole world to bring me back the wedding ring of a perfectly happy woman. While he’s gone, all the distant relatives standing between him and his title will die of convenient, separate diseases. He will come back as Lord Grandcourt, but without that ring I asked for. He will throw himself at my feet, and I shall laugh at him. He will rise, stung by my mockery, and I shall laugh harder. He will call for his horse and ride straight to Quetcham, only to find Miss Arrowpoint just eloped with a penniless musician, Mrs Arrowpoint tearing her cap off in a rage, and Mr Arrowpoint standing there gaping. Exit Lord Grandcourt, defeated, who rides back to Diplow and changes his clothes, like that character in the French play, and never bothers me again.”

No young witch ever had half of Gwendolen’s sharp, unreadable intuition. You could sit on your secret and pretend you were innocent, and she would see right away from the tiny twitch of your eye exactly what you were hiding. It was as useless as locking your front door to keep out the rain. Mrs Davilow, half amused, half unsettled, asked, “What sort of man do you imagine him to be, then, Gwendolen?”

Gwendolen pressed one finger to her lip, frowning playfully in thought, then held the finger out sharply. “Short,” she declared. “Just a little taller than my shoulder. He tries to look taller by twirling his mustache and growing his beard long. He wears a monocle in his right eye to look distinguished. He has very strong opinions about the cut of his waistcoat, but is vague and wishy-washy about the weather, which he will try to draw me out on. He will stare at me nonstop the entire time we talk, and that monocle will make him pull terrible, cross-eyed faces every time he tries to smile at me in a flattering way. I shall cast my eyes down demurely, and he will think I am flattered by his attention. That night, I will dream I am looking at the grossly magnified face of some giant insect, and the next morning he will show up at our door to propose to me. And then the rest of my silly little play will follow.”

“That is a portrait of some man you have already met,” Mrs Davilow protested. “Mr Grandcourt may be a perfectly delightful young man, for all you know.”

“Oh, of course he will,” Gwendolen agreed airily, setting down her best hat and turning it thoughtfully in her hands. “I already know he has a stable full of hunters and racehorses, a grand house in London, and two country seats—one with battlements, and one with a wide veranda. I am quite sure, with a little bit of well-timed murder of those inconvenient relatives, he will get his peerage easily enough.”

There was a thread of sharp, genuine irony tangled in that playful speech, and it made poor literal, gentle Mrs Davilow very uncomfortable. “Do not talk like that, child, for heaven’s sake,” she pleaded, her brow creased with distress. “All those novels you read fill your head with such terrible ideas. When your sister and I were your age, we never knew a thing about wickedness. It was far better that way.”

“Why did you not bring me up to be that innocent, then, mamma?” Gwendolen teased—then saw the crushed look on her mother’s face, and the sob rising in her throat, and immediately felt terrible. She tossed her hat aside, knelt at her mother’s feet, and clung to her knees. “Mamma, mamma, I was only joking. I did not mean a single word of it.”

“How could I?” Mrs Davilow sobbed, the tears spilling over as she tried to speak. “Your will was always far too strong for me—even if everything else in our lives had been different.”

Gwendolen understood perfectly. “Dear mamma, I am not complaining about you,” she said, wiping her mother’s tears away gently with her handkerchief. “I love you. Besides, I am far more charming than your generation of girls. You all must have been so dreadfully bored.” That soft, cajoling tone always soothed her mother, as it had a dozen times after similar little clashes. Both of them learned to avoid the topic of Grandcourt entirely after that, for Gwendolen hated that prick of guilt she felt whenever she hurt her mother, and Mrs Davilow hated even the faintest hint that she was pushing Gwendolen down a path her daughter did not want.

Gwendolen had grown more defiant lately, anyway. Anna Gascoigne still could not hide her quiet hurt and resentment over what had happened to Rex, even though neither of them dared speak his name, and that cold distance only made Gwendolen more determined to keep her own freedom. “I see now why all girls are so glad to get married,” she told her mother one day. “It is the only way to escape the endless expectation that you will spend every moment of your life pleasing everyone but yourself.”

Even though thirty square miles of Wessex was full of handsome, eligible young men who loved to sit and chat with lively, easy Gwendolen, not a single one of them had actually proposed to her in the eight months since she arrived at Offendene. For all the local gossip about how charming she was, no man had worked up the courage to formally ask for her hand. But that did not stop the whole neighborhood from assuming that the new, far more eligible Mr Grandcourt would do exactly what all the local young men had not. Likelihood in this world, after all, is nothing more than the reflection of what people badly want to be true. And the Arrowpoints, for their part, did not care at all whether Gwendolen made a brilliant match—they were completely convinced Grandcourt would see no one but their own Catherine the moment he arrived.
