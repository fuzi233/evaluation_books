Leaving Vienna behind, and long before the city of Budapest greets your gaze, the Danube surrenders its disciplined course. It flows into a realm of profound solitude and desolation, where the water abandons any notion of a single channel, spreading outward in a chaotic embrace. For miles, the countryside dissolves into a vast, sucking swamp, blanketed by an endless ocean of low willow-bushes. On grand maps, this forgotten expanse is rendered in a fading, fluffy blue, labeled in sprawling, careless letters simply as *Sümpfe*—marshes.

During times of high flood, this immense tract of sand, gravel beds, and willow-choked islands is nearly submerged. Yet, in ordinary seasons, the bushes bow and rustle in the open breezes, displaying their silver leaves to the sun in a shifting plain of dizzying beauty. These willows never achieve the stature of true trees; they lack rigid trunks, remaining humble, rounded shrubs with soft outlines. They sway on slender stems that tremble at the slightest touch of the wind, supple as grasses. Their constant motion creates the illusion that the entire plain is alive and breathing. The wind rides over them, sending up rolling waves of foliage instead of water—green swells akin to the sea, until the branches flip upward, revealing undersides of blinding silver-white against the sun.

Joyful in escaping the tyranny of the river's strict banks, the Danube wanders freely here. It navigates an intricate labyrinth of channels cutting through the islands, carving broad avenues where waters pour forth with a deafening shout. It churns whirlpools, eddies, and foaming rapids; it gnaws at sandy banks, dragging away chunks of earth and clusters of willows, only to forge new islands innumerable. These phantom lands shift daily in size and shape, possessing at best a fleeting existence, destined to be erased entirely by the next flood.

Technically, this wild phase of the river's life begins shortly after departing Pressburg. We arrived here in our Canadian canoe, accompanied by a Gypsy tent and a frying pan, riding the crest of a rising flood in mid-July. That very morning, as the sky reddened before dawn, we had slipped swiftly through the sleeping city of Vienna. By noon, it was merely a smudge of smoke against the blue hills of the Wienerwald. We ate breakfast below Fischeramend beneath a grove of birch trees roaring in the wind, then swept onward on the tearing current past Orth, Hainburg, and Petronell—the ancient Roman Carnuntum of Marcus Aurelius. Finally, we passed under the frowning heights of Thelsen, a spur of the Carpathians, where the March River stole in quietly from the left, marking the border between Austria and Hungary.

Racing along at twelve kilometers per hour, we soon entered Hungary. The muddy waters, a sure sign of the flood, grounded us repeatedly on shingle beds and twisted our canoe like a cork in sudden, belching whirlpools. Soon, the towers of Pressburg (Pozsóny) rose against the sky. Our canoe leaped like a spirited horse, flying at top speed beneath the grey stone walls, negotiating the sunken chain of the *Fliegende Brücke* ferry, turning sharply left, and plunging into yellow foam toward the wilderness of islands, sandbanks, and swamp-land beyond—the domain of the willows.

The transition was abrupt, like a film reel snapping from a bustling street scene to a silent forest. Within less than half an hour, there was no boat, no fishing hut, no red roof, and no sign of human civilization. The overwhelming sense of remoteness, the absolute isolation, and the magnetic pull of this strange world of willows, winds, and waters cast an instant spell upon us. Laughingly, we admitted to one another that we should have possessed a special passport to enter this separate kingdom of wonder and magic—a realm reserved for those with a rightful claim, guarded by unwritten warnings for the imaginative trespasser.

Though still early afternoon, a tempestuous wind battered us relentlessly, forcing us to seek immediate shelter. Landing proved a nightmare; the swirling flood dragged us ashore only to sweep us back out. Willowing branches tore at our hands as we grabbed them to halt the canoe, and we pulled yards of sandy bank into the water before finally catching a backwater. With a great sideways lunge driven by the wind, we beached the bows in a cloud of spray. Exhausted and laughing, we lay on the hot yellow sand, sheltered from the gale, basking in the full blaze of a scorching sun under a cloudless blue sky. Around us, an immense army of dancing, shouting willow bushes closed in from every side, shining with spray and clapping their thousand tiny hands as if applauding our survival.

"What a river!" I remarked to my companion, recalling our journey from the source in the Black Forest and the arduous wading required in the upper shallows months prior.

"Willn't stand much nonsense now, will it?" he replied, hauling the canoe further up the sand and settling himself for a nap.

Lying beside him, I felt a profound peace amidst the elements—water, wind, sand, and the great fire of the sun. I reflected on the long road behind us and the vast stretch ahead to the Black Sea, grateful for the company of my friend, the Swede.

We had undertaken many journeys together, but the Danube impressed us more than any other river with its undeniable *aliveness*. From its tiny, bubbling birth among the pine gardens of Donaueschingen to this moment where it played the great river-game of losing itself in deserted swamps, it felt like witnessing the growth of a living creature. Initially sleepy, it later developed violent desires as it became conscious of its deep soul. It rolled through the countries we traversed like a massive fluid entity, carrying our small craft on its mighty shoulders. Sometimes rough, always friendly, it eventually earned our status as a "Great Personage."

How else could it be, given the secrets it revealed? At night, while we slept in our tent, we heard it sing to the moon—an odd, sibilant note caused by the rapid tearing of pebbles along its bed. We knew the voice of its gurgling whirlpools, the roar of its shallows, the constant thunder beneath the surface, and the ceaseless tearing of its icy waters against the banks. We witnessed it stand up and shout when rain fell flat upon its face, and hear its laughter roar when the wind blew upstream, fighting to slow its momentum. We knew all its sounds: its tumblings, its foamings, its self-conscious chatter near hills, its affected dignity in small towns, and its faint, sweet whispers when the sun poured down upon its curves.

It was also full of tricks in its youthful days. In the Swabian forests, before the weight of the world pressed upon it, it would vanish through holes in the ground, emerging miles later with a new name, leaving our canoe stranded in shallow pools requiring miles of wading. Its favorite amusement was lying low, like Br'er Fox, just before the turbulent tributaries from the Alps joined it. It would pretend not to acknowledge them, running side-by-side for miles with a clearly defined boundary, refusing to merge. Only below Passau did it abandon this charade; there, the Inn arrived with thundering power, pushing the parent river so hard that they squeezed through a twisting gorge in a frantic struggle. During that fight, our canoe slipped from the river's shoulder to its breast, enjoying a lifetime of chaos among the battling waves. But the Inn taught the old river a lesson; after Passau, it accepted new arrivals without pretense.

Those days were many weeks ago. Since then, we had learned other facets of this great creature. Across the Bavarian wheat plains of Straubing, she had wandered so slowly under the blazing June sun that we imagined only the surface inches were water, while beneath a silken mantle, an entire army of Undines moved silently and unseen toward the sea, proceeding with deliberate leisure lest they be discovered.

We also forgave her for her kindness to the wildlife haunting the shores. Cormorants lined the banks like black pickets; grey crows crowded the gravel; storks stood fishing in the shallows between islands; and hawks, swans, and myriad marsh birds filled the air with glinting wings and petulant cries. One could not resent the river's whims after seeing a deer leap into the water at sunrise to swim past our bows, or spotting fawns peering from the brush, or locking eyes with a stag as we rounded a corner. Foxes, too, haunted the banks, tripping daintily among driftwood and vanishing so suddenly that their movements seemed magical.

But now, after leaving Pressburg, everything shifted. The Danube grew serious. It ceased its trifling. Halfway to the Black Sea, nearing stranger lands where such games were forbidden, it suddenly matured, demanding our respect and even our awe. It split into three arms that reunited only a hundred kilometers downstream, offering no clue to a canoeist which path to follow.

"If you take a side channel," warned a Hungarian officer we met in Pressburg while purchasing supplies, "you may find yourself, when the flood recedes, forty miles from anywhere, high and dry. You may easily starve. There are no people, no farms, no fishermen. Do not continue. The river is still rising, and this wind will strengthen."

Suddenly, I found myself lying awake, peering through the flap of my tent at the pale, sandy floor where my mattress rested. I glanced at my watch, which I'd pinned haphazardly to the canvas wall, and saw by the harsh glare of the full moon that the hands had long since passed midnight. We had slept only a few hours. Beside me, the Swede breathed heavily in his sleep, oblivious to the chaos outside. The wind continued its relentless howling, a sound that seemed to scrape against the inside of my skull, stirring a primal terror in my chest. There was a distinct, heavy wrongness hanging over our campsite, a disturbance in the air that made my skin crawl.

I sat up abruptly and squinted into the darkness. The trees were thrashing violently, their branches whipping like mad snakes as the gusts hammered them, yet our small patch of green canvas seemed miraculously secure, tucked safely away in the hollow of the clearing. The wind roared over us without finding purchase strong enough to tear us apart. But the unease didn't fade. Instead, it gnawed at me until I couldn't stand it anymore. I crawled out of the tent, moving with deliberate slowness so as not to wake my sleeping companion. A strange, electric excitement buzzed through my veins.

Halfway out, kneeling on the damp sand, my gaze caught the tops of the bushes directly opposite. Against the black sky, the moving tracery of their leaves formed shifting patterns. I froze, sitting back on my heels, staring in disbelief. It was impossible, yet there, hovering just above the brushline and slightly ahead of me, were shapes. Indeterminate forms, yes, but undeniably present. As the willow branches swayed in the gale, they seemed to cluster around these shapes, creating a series of monstrous, writhing silhouettes that danced rapidly beneath the moon's light.

Bracing myself, I stepped closer, closing the distance to within fifty feet. My first impulse was to rouse the Swede, to show him what I saw, but a sudden hesitation gripped me. Perhaps it was the dawning realization that I wouldn't want his confirmation—that seeing it together would strip away whatever fragile sanity held me together. So, I crouched there, staring with burning eyes, wide awake and terrified.

*I am not dreaming,* I told myself, the words echoing in my mind. *I am not dreaming.*

Then, the figures resolved. Emerging clearly from the foliage, they were immense, colored in a sickly, metallic bronze, moving with a fluidity entirely independent of the swaying branches. They were far larger than any human being, and something in their posture screamed that they were *not* human. They were certainly not mere shadows cast by the wind. They shifted on their own accord, rising in a continuous, unbroken stream from the earth straight up into the sky, vanishing completely the moment they touched the deepest darkness of the heavens.

They were interlocked, a great, twisting column of flesh and shadow. I watched their limbs and massive bodies melt into one another, forming a serpentine line that bent and swayed and coiled in spirals, mimicking the contortions of the wind-tossed trees. They were naked, fluid entities pouring upward through the bushes, weaving *within* the leaves themselves, ascending in a living pillar toward the stars. I could never see their faces; they remained featureless masks of bronze.

Unceasingly, they flowed upward, curving in great, graceful arcs, their dull bronze skin glowing faintly in the moonlight. I strained my eyes, forcing every ounce of focus onto them, hoping desperately that they would dissolve into the movement of the branches and reveal themselves as an optical trick. I scanned the horizon for any sign of reality, searching for a logical explanation, even as I knew deep down that the very definition of "reality" had shifted beneath my feet.

The longer I watched, the more certain I became: these figures were real and alive, though perhaps not in the way a scientist or a photographer would define life. Far from feeling fear, I was seized by a profound awe and wonder unlike anything I had ever experienced. It felt as though I was witnessing the personification of the raw, elemental forces of this haunted, ancient land. Our intrusion here had awakened the dormant powers of the place. We were the catalyst for this disturbance. My mind overflowed with tales and legends of spirits and deities revered by humanity throughout history, stories that suddenly felt terrifyingly relevant.

But before I could formulate any coherent theory, an irresistible urge drove me further out. I crept forward across the sand, standing upright. The ground beneath my bare feet was still warm from the sun; the wind whipped fiercely at my hair and face, stinging my cheeks; and the roar of the river crashed against my ears with sudden violence. These sensations confirmed it: these things were real, proving my senses were functioning correctly. Yet, the figures continued to rise from the earth to the heavens, silent and majestic, spiraling upward in a display of grace and power that overwhelmed me with a deep, visceral need to worship.

*I must kneel,* I thought. *I must absolutely worship.*

Perhaps I would have fallen to my knees in that instant if a sudden, violent gust hadn't slammed into me, knocking me sideways and nearly sending me sprawling. It shook the spell violently, breaking the trance. At least, it offered me a different perspective. The figures remained, still ascending from the heart of the night, but my reason finally began to claw its way back to the surface. *It must be subjective,* I argued with myself. *None the less real, but still subjective. Just the moonlight and the branches working together on the mirror of my imagination, projecting outward until I mistook them for objective truth.*

I knew this had to be the case, logically speaking. Gathering my courage, I started to move forward across the open patches of sand. *By Jove,* I thought, panic rising in my throat, *is this all a hallucination? Is it merely a product of my own fractured mind?* Does my reason not argue in the old, futile way, clinging to the tiny, narrow standards of the known world?

All I know is that for what felt like an eternity, that great column of figures ascended darkly into the sky, possessing a completeness of reality that matched everything most people accept as true. And then, suddenly, they were gone.

As soon as their presence vanished and the immediate shock of their existence faded, a cold wave of fear washed over me. The esoteric, terrible nature of this lonely, haunted region flared up within my consciousness, and I began to tremble uncontrollably. I cast a frantic, horrified glance around, calculating vainly for any route of escape, desperate to flee. Realizing how utterly helpless I was to achieve anything meaningful, I turned and crept back silently into the tent. I lay down again upon my sandy mattress, pulling the door-curtain down hard to blot out the sight of the willows in the moonlight, and then buried my face as deeply as possible beneath the thick blankets, trying to drown out the terrifying roar of the wind.

As though further to convince me that I had not been dreaming, I recall that it was a long time before I succumbed again to a troubled, restless slumber. Even then, only the superficial layer of my being drifted off; beneath it, a core of consciousness remained perpetually alert, lying in wait. But this second time, I jolted upward with a genuine start of pure terror. It was neither the wind nor the river that roused me, but the slow, inexorable approach of something that caused the sleeping part of me to shrink until it vanished entirely. I found myself sitting bolt upright, listening intently.

Outside, there was a sound of countless tiny patters. I realized they had been approaching for some time, becoming audible only as I slipped into sleep. I sat there, nervously wide-awake, as if I had never closed my eyes. My breathing felt labored, weighed down by a heavy burden upon my chest. Despite the heat of the night, I was clammy with cold, shivering uncontrollably. Something was surely pressing steadily against the sides of the tent, weighing it down from above. Was it the body of the wind? Could this be the pattering rain, the dripping leaves, or the spray driven from the river by the gale, gathering into massive drops? My mind raced through a dozen possibilities. Then, suddenly, the explanation leapt into my mind: a branch from the poplar—the only large tree on the island—had fallen with the wind. Still tangled in other limbs, it would crash down with the next gust and crush us. Meanwhile, its leaves brushed and tapped against the tight canvas of our shelter.

I raised a loose flap and rushed outside, shouting for the Swede to follow. But when I emerged and stood tall, I saw that the tent was free. There was no hanging bough; there was no rain or spray; nothing approached. A cold, grey light filtered down through the bushes, resting on the faintly gleaming sand. Stars still crowded the sky directly overhead, and the wind howled magnificently, yet the fire offered no glow anymore. Through the trees, I saw the east reddening in streaks. Several hours must have passed since I had stood there watching the ascending figures, and the memory of it now returned to me horribly, like a nightmare. Oh, how exhausting that ceaseless, raging wind made me feel! Yet, despite the deep lassitude of a sleepless night, my nerves tingled with the activity of an equally tireless apprehension. All idea of rest was impossible.

The river had risen further. Its thunder filled the air, and a fine mist made itself felt through my thin sleeping shirt. Yet, nowhere did I discover the slightest evidence of anything to cause alarm. This deep, prolonged disturbance in my heart remained wholly unaccounted for. My companion had not stirred when I called him, and there was no need to wake him now. I looked about me carefully, noting every detail: the overturned canoe, the yellow paddles—two of them, I am certain; the provision sack and the extra lantern hanging together from the tree; and, crowding everywhere around me, enveloping all, the willows, those endless, shaking willows.

A bird uttered its morning cry, and a string of ducks passed with whirring flight overhead in the twilight. The sand whirled, dry and stinging, about my bare feet in the wind. I walked around the tent and then ventured a little way into the bush, so that I could see across the river to the far landscape. The same profound yet indefinable emotion of distress seized me again as I beheld the interminable sea of bushes stretching to the horizon, looking ghostly and unreal in the wan light of dawn. I walked softly here and there, still puzzling over that odd sound of infinite pattering and that pressure upon the tent that had woken me. It *must* have been the wind, I reflected—the wind bearing upon the loose, hot sand, driving dry particles sharply against the taut canvas, dropping heavily upon our fragile roof. Yet, all the while, my nervousness and malaise increased appreciably.

I crossed over to the farther shore and noted how the coastline had altered in the night, what masses of sand the river had torn away. I dipped my hands and feet into the cool current, bathing my forehead. Already there was a glow of sunrise in the sky, bringing the exquisite freshness of the coming day. On my way back, I passed purposely beneath the very bushes where I had seen the column of figures rising into the air. Midway among the clumps, I suddenly found myself overtaken by a sense of vast terror. From the shadows, a large figure went swiftly by. Someone passed me, just as surely as one man passes another.... It was a great, staggering blow from the wind that propelled me forward again. Once out in the more open space, the sense of terror diminished strangely. "The winds are about and walking," I remembered telling myself, for the winds often move like great presences under the trees.

And altogether, the fear hovering about me was such an unknown, immense kind of fear, so unlike anything I had ever felt before, that it awakened a sense of awe and wonder within me that did much to counteract its worst effects. When I reached a high point in the middle of the island from which I could see the wide stretch of river, crimson in the sunrise, the whole magical beauty of it all was so overpowering that a sort of wild yearning woke in me, almost bringing a cry up into my throat. But this cry found no expression. For as my eyes wandered from the plain beyond to the island surrounding us, noting our little tent half-hidden among the willows, a dreadful discovery leaped out at me—a revelation compared to which my terror of the walking winds seemed insignificant.

For a change, I thought, had somehow occurred in the arrangement of the landscape. It was not that my vantage point gave me a different view, but that an alteration had apparently been effected in the relationship of the tent to the willows, and of the willows to the tent. Surely the bushes now crowded much closer—unnecessarily, unpleasantly close. *They had moved nearer.* Creeping with silent feet over the shifting sands, drawing imperceptibly nearer by soft, unhurried movements, the willows had advanced during the night. But had the wind moved them, or had they moved of their own accord? I recalled the sound of infinite small patters and the pressure upon the tent and upon my own heart that had caused me to wake in terror.

I swayed for a moment in the wind like a tree, finding it hard to maintain my upright position on the sandy hillock. There was a suggestion here of personal agency, of deliberate intention, of aggressive hostility, terrifying me into a sort of rigidity. Then the reaction followed quickly. The idea was so bizarre, so absurd, that I felt inclined to laugh. But the laughter came no more readily than the cry, for the knowledge that my mind was so receptive to such dangerous imaginings brought the additional terror that the attack would come through our minds, not our physical bodies, and was already coming.

The wind buffeted me about, and very quickly, it seemed, the sun rose over the horizon, for it was after four o'clock. I must have stood on that little pinnacle of sand longer than I knew, afraid to descend to close quarters with the willows. I returned quietly, creepily, to the tent, first taking another exhaustive look around—and yes, I confess it—making a few measurements. I paced out on the warm sand the distances between the willows and the tent, making a particular note of the shortest gap. I crawled stealthily into my blankets. My companion, to all appearances, still slept soundly, and I was glad that this was so. Provided my experiences were not corroborated, I could find strength somehow to deny them, perhaps. With the daylight, I could persuade myself that it was all a subjective hallucination, a fantasy of the night, a projection of an excited imagination.

Nothing further disturbed me, and I fell asleep almost at once, utterly exhausted, yet still in dread of hearing again that weird sound of multitudinous patters, or of feeling the pressure upon my heart that had made breathing difficult.

The sun hung high and indifferent in the heavens when my companion roused me from a heavy, dreamless sleep. He announced that the porridge was ready and that we had just enough time to bathe before the river claimed us. Immediately, the savory scent of frizzling bacon drifted through the flap of the tent, cutting through the damp air.

"The river is still rising," he said, his voice carrying a strange edge. "Several islands in the mid-stream have vanished entirely. Our own island has shrunk considerably."

"Is there any wood left?" I asked, my voice thick with sleep.

"The wood and the island will finish each other off in a dead heat tomorrow," he laughed, though the sound lacked its usual warmth. "But there's enough to last us until then."

I plunged into the water at the point of the island, which had indeed shifted dramatically in size and shape during the night. I was swept downstream in moments toward the landing place opposite the tent. The water was ice-cold, and the rushing banks blurred past like scenery flying by from an express train. Bathing under such violent conditions was exhilarating; the terror of the previous night seemed to evaporate from my brain, washed away by the shock of the current. Above, the sun blazed with relentless intensity; not a cloud marred the azure sky. Yet, despite the heat, the wind had not diminished—not one jot.

Suddenly, the implication of the Swede's earlier words struck me with force. "Enough to last till tomorrow"—he meant we would remain on the island for another night. It was a startling reversal. The night before, he had been adamant about leaving immediately. How had his resolve shifted so completely?

Breakfast was marked by great crumbling of the banks, accompanied by heavy splashes and clouds of spray driven into our frying pan by the gale. While my fellow traveler talked incessantly about the impossibility of the Vienna-Pesth steamers finding a channel in such a flood, his mental state fascinated and unsettled me far more than the raging river. He had changed since the evening prior. His manner was different—a trifle excited, a trifle shy, laced with a suspicion that colored his voice and gestures. I struggled to articulate it now, in the calm of hindsight, but at the time, I was certain of one thing: he was frightened.

He ate almost nothing and, for the first time, omitted his pipe. Instead, he spread the map open beside him, studying its markings with intense focus.

"We'd better get off sharp in an hour," I said eventually, probing for an opening that might lead to a partial confession. His response puzzled me deeply. "Rather! If they'll let us."

"Who'll let us? The elements?" I asked quickly, masking my anxiety with affected indifference.

"The powers of this awful place, whoever they are," he replied, refusing to lift his gaze from the map. "The gods are here, if they are anywhere at all in the world."

"The elements are always the true immortals," I retorted, forcing a laugh that felt brittle. Yet I knew my face betrayed my true feelings when he looked up gravely, speaking across the smoke.

"We shall be fortunate if we get away without further disaster."

These were precisely the words I had dreaded. I steel-ed myself for the inevitable confrontation. It was like consenting to a tooth extraction; the pain was unavoidable, and the rest was mere pretense.

"Further disaster! What has happened?"

"For one thing—the steering paddle is gone," he said quietly.

"The steering paddle!" I repeated, my excitement spiking. Without a rudder, the Danube in flood was suicide. "But what—"

"And there is a tear in the bottom of the canoe," he added, a genuine tremor shaking his voice.

I stared at him, unable to form coherent speech, merely echoing the horror in his face. There, under the scorching sun on the burning sand, a freezing atmosphere descended around us. He nodded gravely and led the way toward the tent, a few yards away from the fire. The canoe lay there as I had seen her the night before, ribs exposed, with the single paddle resting on the sand beside her.

"There is only one," he said, stooping to retrieve it. "And here is the rent in the base-board."

It was on the tip of my tongue to correct him, noting that I had clearly seen *two* paddles just hours ago, but a second impulse silenced me. I stepped closer to inspect the damage.

There was a long, cleanly made tear in the bottom of the canoe, as if a sharp rock or snag had gnawed through her length. Investigation confirmed the hole went all the way through. Had we launched without noticing, the initial swelling of the wet wood might have sealed it temporarily, but once in mid-stream, the water would have poured in instantly. The canoe, barely two inches above the surface, would have filled and sunk with terrifying speed.

"There," he murmured, more to himself than to me, running his fingers along the slit. "An attempt to prepare a victim for sacrifice. Two victims, rather."

I began to whistle—a reflexive habit when utterly nonplussed—and deliberately chose to ignore his words. I resolved to dismiss them as foolishness.

"It wasn't there last night," he stated, straightening up and avoiding my gaze.

"We must have scratched her during landing," I interjected, stopping my whistle. "The stones are very sharp."

I halted abruptly. He turned then, meeting my eye squarely. We both knew the lie was transparent. There were no stones.

"And then there is this to explain," he added softly, handing me the paddle and pointing to the blade.

A new, chilling emotion spread over me as I examined it. The blade was scraped down uniformly, smoothed with meticulous care, as if sanded by invisible hands until it was so thin that the first vigorous stroke would snap it off at the elbow.

"One of us walked in his sleep and did this," I said weakly. "Or... perhaps filed by the constant stream of sand particles blown against it by the wind."

"Ah," the Swede said, turning away with a slight, mocking laugh. "You can explain everything."

"The same wind that caught the steering paddle and flung it so near the bank that it fell into the crumbling earth," I called after him, desperate to construct a rational narrative.

"I see," he shouted back, turning his head briefly before vanishing into the willow bushes.

Left alone with these perplexing evidences of deliberate malice, my first thought was, "One of us must have done this, and it certainly wasn't me." But my second thought dismissed the possibility entirely. Could my companion, the trusted friend of a dozen expeditions, knowingly sabotage us? Absurd. Was this impervious, practical man suddenly insane? Equally ridiculous.

Yet, the disturbing reality remained: a profound alteration in his mind. He was nervous, timid, suspicious, aware of unseen movements, waiting for a climax he expected imminently. This intuition grew within me, unbidden and undeniable.

I conducted a hurried inspection of the tent and surroundings. The measurements of the night remained unchanged, save for deep, basin-shaped hollows in the sand, ranging from the size of a teacup to a large bowl. The wind, undoubtedly, created these miniature craters, just as it had tossed the paddle. The rent in the canoe remained the sole inexplicable anomaly. Clinging to the theory that a sharp point had caught it during landing, I relied on that diminishing fragment of my intelligence I called "reason." An explanation was an absolute necessity, a psychological requirement for facing the horrors of existence.

I melted the pitch, and shortly the Swede joined me. Under ideal conditions, the canoe could not travel safely until the following day. I casually drew his attention to the hollows in the sand.

"Yes," he replied. "They are all over the island. But *you* can explain them, no doubt!"

"Wind, of course," I answered without hesitation. "Have you never watched those little whirlwinds in the street that twist debris into circles? This sand is loose enough to yield."

He offered no reply. We worked in silence for a while. I watched him surreptitiously, sensing he was watching me in return. He appeared constantly attentive to sounds I could not hear, or perhaps to something he awaited. He turned frequently, staring into the bushes, up at the sky, and out across the water visible through the willow gaps. Occasionally, he pressed his hand to his ear, holding it there for minutes at a stretch. He said nothing, and I asked no questions. Meanwhile, as he mended the torn canoe with the skill of a red Indian, I was relieved by his absorption in the task, fearing he might speak of the changing aspect of the willows. If he had noticed *that*, my imagination would no longer suffice to explain the situation.

After a long pause, he began to speak.

"Queer thing," he said in a hurried tone, eager to get it over. "Queer thing. About that otter last night."

I braced myself for something entirely different, catching him off guard. I looked up sharply.

My immediate assumption was that my companion had rolled off his mattress onto mine during his slumber. I called out to him and sat up, and in that same instant, a chilling realization struck me: the tent was *surrounded*. The sound of countless soft patters resumed outside, filling the night with pure terror. I shouted for him again, louder this time. There was no answer. The comforting rhythm of his snoring was gone, and I noticed the tent flap hung open—a fatal error. Crawling out into the darkness to secure it, I realized with absolute certainty that the Swede was missing. He had fled.

Seized by a dreadful panic, I dashed out in a frantic run. The moment I emerged, I was engulfed by a torrent of humming that surrounded me completely, emanating from every direction of the sky. It was that same familiar hum, now utterly mad. It was as if a swarm of giant, invisible bees hovered in the air around me. The sound seemed to thicken the very atmosphere, making my labored breathing difficult. But my friend was in peril, and hesitation was impossible. Dawn was breaking, casting a faint, whitish light upward from a thin strip of clear horizon. There was no wind. I could barely distinguish the bushes and the river beyond, along with the pale sandy patches. Driven by excitement, I ran frantically back and forth across the island, screaming his name, shouting the first words that came to my mind. However, the willows smothered my cries, and the humming muffled them, limiting the sound's travel to mere feet around me.

I plunged blindly into the bushes, tripping over roots, tumbling headlong, and scraping my face as I tore through the obstructing branches. Suddenly, I emerged onto the island's point and saw a dark silhouette outlined between the water and the sky. It was the Swede. Already, one of his feet was in the river! Another moment, and he would have jumped. I threw myself upon him, wrapping my arms around his waist and dragging him toward the shore with all my strength. Naturally, he struggled furiously, emitting noises identical to that cursed humming while spouting bizarre phrases in his rage about "going inside to Them" and "taking the way of the water and the wind"—words I tried in vain to recall later, which left me sick with horror and awe as I listened. Eventually, I managed to haul him into the relative safety of the tent and flung him, breathless and cursing, onto the mattress. I held him there until the fit subsided.

I believe the suddenness of the event, coupled with the abrupt cessation of the humming and pattering outside, was nearly the strangest aspect of the entire ordeal. Just as the silence fell, he opened his eyes, lifting his weary face to me so the dawn cast a pale light upon it through the doorway. Speaking with the innocence of a frightened child, he said, "My life, old man—it's my life I owe you. But it's all over now anyhow. They've found a victim in our place!" Then he collapsed back onto his blankets, falling asleep literally beneath my gaze. He simply gave up, beginning to snore as healthily as if nothing had occurred and he had never attempted to sacrifice his own life by drowning.

When the sunlight roused him three hours later—hours of unceasing vigil for me—it became evident that he remembered absolutely nothing of his attempted suicide. Deeming it wise to avoid dangerous inquiries, I let him wake naturally and easily, as I mentioned, when the sun was already high in a windless, hot sky. He immediately rose to prepare the fire for breakfast. I followed him anxiously to bathe, but he made no attempt to plunge in, merely dipping his head and commenting on the unusual coldness of the water. "The river is falling at last," he remarked, "and I'm glad of it."

"The humming has stopped too," I said.

He looked up at me calmly, his expression entirely normal. Evidently, he retained memories of everything except his own act of self-destruction. "Everything has stopped," he replied, pausing briefly. "Because—" He hesitated. I sensed a reference to the remark he made right before fainting lingered in his mind, and I was determined to hear it. "Because 'They've found another victim'?" I asked, forcing a weak laugh.

"Exactly," he answered without hesitation. "Exactly! I feel as positive of it as though... as though... I feel quite safe again, I mean," he concluded. He began looking curiously around him. Hot patches of sunlight lay on the sand. There was no wind. The willows stood motionless. Slowly, he rose to his feet. "Come," he said; "I think if we look, we shall find it."

He broke into a run, and I followed closely. He stayed near the banks, probing with a stick among the sandy bays, caves, and small backwaters, while I trailed behind him. "Ah!" he exclaimed suddenly, "ah!" The tone of his voice instantly revived a vivid sense of the horror from the past twenty-four hours, and I hurried to join him. He pointed his stick at a large black object lying half in the water and half on the sand, trapped by twisted willow roots so the river could not carry it away. Only a few hours prior, this spot must have been submerged.

"See," he said quietly, "the victim that made our escape possible!" As I peered over his shoulder, I saw his stick resting on the body of a man. He flipped it over. It was the corpse of a peasant, his face buried in the sand. Clearly, the man had been drowned recently, likely swept down upon our island sometime around dawn—at the precise moment the fit had ended.

"We must give it a decent burial, you know," I suggested.

"I suppose so," I replied, shuddering involuntarily. Something about the appearance of that poor drowned man chilled me to the bone. The Swede glanced up sharply, an unreadable expression on his face, and began climbing down the bank. I followed more leisurely. Noticing that the current had stripped much of the clothing from the body, leaving the neck and part of the chest bare, my companion suddenly halted halfway down, holding up a hand in warning. Either my footing slipped or I had built too much momentum to stop quickly; I bumped into him, sending him forward in a leap to save himself. We tumbled together onto the hard sand, our feet splashing into the water. Before anyone could react, we collided heavily against the corpse.

The Swede let out a sharp cry. I sprang back as if shot. The exact moment we touched the body, a loud sound of humming erupted from its surface—the sound of multiple hummers passing with a vast commotion of winged things in the air above us. The noise disappeared upward into the sky, fading until it ceased in the distance. It was precisely as though we had disturbed some living, yet invisible, creatures at work. My companion clutched me, and I think I clutched him, but before either of us could fully recover from the shock, we saw the current turn the corpse, releasing it from the willow roots. A moment later, it flipped completely over, presenting the dead face upward, staring blankly at the sky. Lying on the edge of the main stream, it would soon be swept away.

The Swede started to save it, shouting something unintelligible about a "proper burial," before abruptly dropping to his knees on the sand and covering his eyes with his hands. I was beside him instantly. I saw what he saw. Just as the body swung toward the current, the face and exposed chest turned fully toward us, revealing clearly how the skin and flesh were indented with small hollows, perfectly formed and exactly resembling the sand-funnels we had discovered all over the island.

"Their mark!" I heard my companion mutter under his breath. "Their awful mark!" When I turned my eyes away from his ghastly face back to the river, the current had done its work. The body had been swept into mid-stream, already beyond our reach and almost out of sight, rolling over and over on the waves like an otter.
