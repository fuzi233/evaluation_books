“Well, Miss Dashwood,” Mrs. Jennings said, her eyes twinkling with knowing amusement as soon as the gentleman had left the room, “I won’t ask you what Colonel Brandon has been saying to you—though, by my word, I _did_ try to stay out of earshot, I couldn’t help catching enough to understand the whole affair. And I assure you, I’ve never been more delighted in all my life! I wish you joy from the bottom of my heart.”

“Thank you, ma’am,” Elinor replied, her cheeks warm and her voice soft with emotion. “It truly is a great joy to me—and I feel so deeply the kindness of Colonel Brandon. There are few men who would act with such generosity. Few hearts so full of compassion! I must confess, I was utterly astonished.”

“Oh, nonsense, my dear!” Mrs. Jennings laughed. “You’re far too modest. I wasn’t surprised at all—I’ve thought for weeks now that this was bound to happen.”

“You judged rightly by his character,” Elinor said gently, “but surely you couldn’t have foreseen that the moment would come so quickly?”

“Opportunity?” Mrs. Jennings repeated, waving her hand airily. “Why, when a man’s mind is made up, opportunity finds him—don’t you worry about that. Well, my dear, I wish you joy again and again. If there’s ever been a pair meant for each other, I think I’ll soon know where to find them.”

“You mean to go to Delaford after them, I suppose?” Elinor asked, a faint smile touching her lips.

“Aye, indeed I do,” Mrs. Jennings declared. “And as for the house being in poor repair—well, I don’t see what he’s complaining about. It’s as fine a place as any I’ve seen!”

“He mentioned it was in need of some attention,” Elinor said.

“And whose fault is that?” Mrs. Jennings retorted sharply. “Why doesn’t he fix it? Who else should?”

Their conversation was interrupted by the servant announcing the carriage was ready. Mrs. Jennings immediately rose, bustling toward the door.

“Well, my dear,” she said, pausing only briefly, “I must be off before I’ve half finished my talk. But we’ll have it all over tonight—we’ll be quite alone then. I won’t ask you to come with me; I can tell your mind’s too full to care for company. Besides, you’ll want to tell Marianne everything.”

Marianne had already left the room.

“I shall certainly tell Marianne,” Elinor replied, “but I won’t mention it to anyone else just yet.”

“Oh, very well,” Mrs. Jennings said, visibly disappointed. “Then you wouldn’t have me tell Lucy? I’m thinking of going as far as Holborn today.”

“No, ma'am,” Elinor answered firmly, “not even Lucy, if you please. A day’s delay won’t matter much. And until I’ve written to Mr. Ferrars, I think it ought not to be spoken of to anyone. I’ll write to him straight away—it’s important no time is lost. He has much to attend to regarding his ordination.”

Mrs. Jennings stared, puzzled. Why such haste to inform Mr. Ferrars? She pondered for a moment, then her face lit up with sudden understanding.

“Oh, ho!” she exclaimed. “I see! Mr. Ferrars is to be the one! Well, all the better for him. Of course he must be ordained in time. I’m glad things are moving along so nicely between you. But, my dear—isn’t this rather odd? Shouldn’t the Colonel write himself? Surely he’s the one best suited.”

Elinor didn’t fully grasp the beginning of her remark, nor did she feel inclined to question it. She simply responded to the end.

“Colonel Brandon is so sensitive,” she explained, “that he’d rather someone else deliver the news to Mr. Ferrars than speak of it himself.”

“And so you’re the one who has to do it,” Mrs. Jennings observed, shaking her head. “Well, that’s an unusual kind of delicacy! But I won’t keep you—seeing you’re preparing to write. You know your own affairs best. So good-bye, my dear. I haven’t heard such happy news since Charlotte gave birth.”

And off she went—but returned almost instantly.

“I just thought of Betty’s sister,” she said. “She’d make a splendid mistress for her. Though whether she’d suit as a lady’s maid, I can’t say. She’s excellent at housework and needlework. You’ll consider it later, I suppose?”

“Certainly, ma'am,” Elinor replied, barely listening, her thoughts already turning to the letter she must write. How to begin? What to say? The situation between her and Edward made even the simplest words difficult. She feared both saying too much and too little, and sat frozen, pen poised, until Edward himself entered the room.

He had met Mrs. Jennings at the door on his way to leave a farewell card. She, after apologizing for not returning herself, had insisted he come in, saying Miss Dashwood was upstairs and wished to speak with him on urgent business.

Elinor had just told herself that, however hard it might be to write, at least it would be easier than speaking aloud—when he appeared, forcing her into the most daunting task of all. Her surprise and embarrassment were overwhelming. She hadn’t seen him since his engagement became known—since he knew she was aware of it. And now, with the weight of what she had been thinking, and what she had to tell him, she felt utterly unsteady.

Edward, too, seemed troubled. They sat down together, both silent, both awkward, both caught in a quiet tension. He could not remember if he had apologized for intruding upon her first—but to be safe, he offered a formal one as soon as he could speak.

“Mrs. Jennings told me you wanted to speak with me,” he said. “At least, I understood her to say so—or I certainly wouldn’t have come in like this. Though I’d have been very sorry to leave London without seeing you and your sister—especially since it may be some time before I meet you again. I’m leaving for Oxford tomorrow.”

“You wouldn’t have gone,” Elinor said, gathering herself and determined to get through it quickly, “without our best wishes—even if we couldn’t give them in person. Mrs. Jennings was right. I have something important to tell you—one I was about to write. I’ve been entrusted with a pleasant duty.” Her voice trembled slightly. “Colonel Brandon—just here ten minutes ago—asked me to tell you that, knowing you intend to take holy orders, he’s pleased to offer you the living at Delaford, which has just become vacant. He regrets it isn’t more substantial, but he hopes it will serve you well.”

She paused, breathless. “Allow me to congratulate you on having such a noble and discerning friend—and to share his hope that the living—about two hundred pounds a year—were more generous, so it might support you fully, not merely provide temporary comfort. In short, so it might fulfill all your hopes for happiness.”

Edward sat stunned. He could not speak. His face showed every trace of astonishment, disbelief, and gratitude—all at once. Yet all he managed was, “Colonel Brandon?”

“Yes,” Elinor said, gaining strength as the worst passed. “Colonel Brandon means it as a mark of his sympathy for what you’ve endured—for the unjust treatment by your family, which has placed you in such distress—a feeling I’m sure Marianne, myself, and all your friends share. And also as a sign of his high regard for your character, and his approval of your conduct in this matter.”

“Colonel Brandon offering _me_ a living!” Edward whispered. “Is it possible?”

“The cruelty of your own family has made you doubt kindness anywhere,” Elinor said gently.

“No,” he replied, suddenly aware, “not kindness from _you_. I cannot forget that to you—to your goodness—I owe everything. I feel it—I would express it if I could—but as you know, I am no speaker.”

“You’re mistaken,” Elinor said firmly. “You owe it entirely—almost entirely—to your own merit and Colonel Brandon’s recognition of it. I had no part in it. I didn’t even know the living was vacant until I learned of his plan. Nor had it ever occurred to me that he might hold such power. As a friend of mine, perhaps he takes greater pleasure in giving it to you—but I swear, you owe nothing to my influence.”

She hesitated, truth compelling her to admit a small role, yet unwilling to appear as the giver of his fortune. That hesitation likely confirmed in Edward’s mind a suspicion already forming.

For a moment, he sat deep in thought. Then, as if with effort, he said, “Colonel Brandon seems a man of great worth. I’ve always heard him praised, and I know your brother holds him in high esteem. He’s undoubtedly sensible—and perfectly gentlemanly in manner.”

“Indeed,” Elinor said, “I believe you’ll find him all that and more. And since you’ll be such close neighbours—the parsonage is nearly beside the mansion house—it’s especially important that he _should_ be all these things.”

Edward said nothing. But when she turned her head, he gave her a look—serious, intense, shadowed with unease—as though he wished the distance between their homes were far greater.

“Colonel Brandon lodges in St. James Street,” he said shortly, rising.

Elinor told him the address.

“I must go at once,” he said, “to thank him—thank him for something you won’t let me thank _you_ for. To tell him he has made me—very, very happy.”

Elinor made no attempt to stop him. They parted with her offering earnest wishes for his future happiness, and him attempting—though failing—to return the sentiment with equal warmth.

As the door closed behind him, Elinor whispered to herself, “When I see him again, I shall see him married to Lucy.”

And with that comforting thought, she sat down to reflect—on the past, on Edward’s words, on his feelings, and on her own, which she found deeply troubling.

When Mrs. Jennings returned, her mind was so consumed by the secret she carried that she couldn’t wait to share it. As soon as Elinor appeared, she burst out:

“Well, my dear! I sent you up the young man—didn’t I do right? And I suppose you had no trouble convincing him—he didn’t resist your proposal, did he?”

“No, ma'am,” Elinor said quietly. “That was unlikely.”

“And how soon will he be ready?” Mrs. Jennings pressed. “Because everything depends on that.”

“Honestly,” Elinor replied, “I know little about such matters. I can’t guess the time or preparation needed. But I suppose two or three months will complete his ordination.”

“Two or three months!” Mrs. Jennings gasped. “Lord, my dear, how calmly you speak! Can the Colonel wait that long? Lord bless me—I’d be driven mad! And though I’m glad to help poor Mr. Ferrars, I don’t see why we should wait months for him. Surely someone else could be found—someone already ordained!”

“My dear ma'am,” Elinor said, “what are you thinking? Colonel Brandon’s only goal is to help Mr. Ferrars.”

“Lord bless you!” Mrs. Jennings cried. “Do you really think the Colonel marries you just to give ten guineas to Mr. Ferrars?”

The pretense ended. A mutual understanding sparked, bringing laughter and relief. Neither lost any joy—Mrs. Jennings simply exchanged one delight for another, still holding fast to her original hope.

“Aye, aye,” she said, once the shock passed, “the parsonage is small, and likely in need of repair. But to hear a man apologize for a house that has five sitting rooms downstairs—and I’m told the housekeeper says it could hold fifteen beds! And to _you_, who’ve lived in Barton cottage! It’s ridiculous. But, my dear, we must urge the Colonel to fix it up—make it comfortable before Lucy moves in.”

“But Colonel Brandon doesn’t seem to think the living is enough to marry on,” Elinor said.

“The Colonel’s a fool,” Mrs. Jennings declared. “Just because he has two thousand a year, he thinks no one else can live on less. Take my word for it—if I’m alive, I’ll be visiting Delaford Parsonage before Michaelmas. And I shan’t go unless Lucy is there.”

Elinor agreed wholeheartedly. She saw no reason they would wait for anything more.
