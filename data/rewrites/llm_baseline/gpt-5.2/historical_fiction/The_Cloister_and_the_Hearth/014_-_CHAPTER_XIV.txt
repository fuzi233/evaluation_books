The first blow upon the back door came so sudden and so brutish that the very hearth seemed to start. A second followed, and a third, and with them the wet roar of the storm that had been battering Sevenbergen all night—rain worrying the thatch, wind whining down the eaves, and the thin iron note of a latch-plate struck with knuckles that had no patience left in them.

They were ringed.

At that sound Margaret’s terror did a strange thing: it did not vanish, but it gathered itself into shape, as dough gathers under a practiced hand when the baker will have it rise true. She leaned close to Gerard; her breath smelled faintly of warm milk and the brown crust of the bread she had eaten earlier, as if the house, even in peril, insisted on being a house.

“Say he was here,” she breathed, “but is gone.”

And before he could answer, she had seized him—no weak clutching, but a grip with sinew in it—and almost dragged him up the rude steps to her father’s sleeping-room. Hers lay beyond; the narrow stair creaked like an old fiddle-string tuned too tight. Below, the pounding resumed, and the whole dwelling listened, every board and beam, as if sound itself were the law that governed men.

“Who knocks at this hour?” Martin Wittenhaagen called, voice roughened by sleep and by the peat-smoke that lived in his beard.

“Open, and you will see!”

“I open not to thieves—honest men are all abed now.”

“Open to the law, Martin Wittenhaagen, or you shall rue it.”

Martin’s answer came slow, and, in it, recognition: “Why, that is Dirk Brower’s voice, I trow. What make you so far from Tergou?”

“Open, and you will know.”

Martin drew the bolt as if the act were a kindness done to the door rather than to the men outside. The wind snatched at the gap the instant it opened; rain-spatter and cold rushed in, with Dierich Brower and four more behind him, their boots dragging mud and ditch-water onto the clean-swept boards. A sixth slipped in at the back, as if the night itself had posted him there.

The kitchen—its soot-dark rafters, its hanging pots, the smell of yesterday’s onions clinging to the stone—was suddenly full of wet wool, steel, and authority.

“Now, Martin,” said Dierich, shaking off rain like a dog, “where is Gerard Eliassoen?”

Martin blinked at him with a sleepy insolence that was almost artistry. “Gerard Eliassoen? Why, he was here but now!”

“Was here?” Dierich’s face fell, as if a purse had been cut from it. “And where is he now?”

“They say he has gone to Italy. Why, what is to do?”

“No matter. When did he go? Tell me not he went in such a storm as this!”

“Here is a coil about Gerard Eliassoen,” Martin said, contempt in every syllable. He went to the fire and, with the unhurried air of one doing a homely task, lighted the candle. Then he sat himself down as if this were some neighborly visit and began whipping fine silk round his bow-string where the nick fretted it—winding and smoothing, winding and smoothing, with fingers as exact as a cook trussing a bird.

“I’ll tell you,” he said carelessly. “Know you his brother Giles?—a little misbegotten imp, all head and arms? Well, he came tearing over here on a mule, and bawled out something. I was too far off to hear the creature’s words, but only its noise. Any way, he started Gerard. For as soon as he was gone, there was such crying and kissing, and then Gerard went away. They do tell me he has gone to Italy—mayhap you know where that is, for I don’t.”

Dierich listened, and the more Martin kept to one plain thread, the more it held. A subtler man might have embroidered, and the embroidery would have betrayed him; Martin lied once, and let the rest be honest.

“Mates,” said Dierich at last, sourly, “I doubt he speaks sooth. I told the burgomaster how ’twould be. He met the dwarf galloping Peter Buyskens’s mule from Sevenbergen. ‘They have sent that imp to Gerard,’ says he, ‘so, then, Gerard is at Sevenbergen.’ ‘Ah, master!’ says I, ‘’tis too late now. We should have thought of Sevenbergen before, instead of wasting our time hunting all the odd corners of Tergou for those cursed parchments that we shall never find till we find the man that took ’em. If he was at Sevenbergen,’ quoth I, ‘and they sent the dwarf to him, it must have been to warn him we are after him. He is leagues away by now,’ quoth I. Confound that chalk-faced girl! she has outwitted us bearded men; and so I told the burgomaster, but he would not hear reason. A wet jerkin apiece, that is all we shall get, mates, by this job.”

Martin grinned in his face as if to say, You have come far for nothing and brought your clatter with you. Yet when Dierich added, “However, to content the burgomaster, we will search the house,” Martin’s grin died as quick as a candle snuffed.

That shift did not pass Dierich unnoticed. His eyes narrowed, and he weighed the room as a man weighs a loaf by the hand, knowing by feel if it is hollow.

“Watch outside, two of you,” he ordered. “One on each side of the house, that no one jump from the upper windows. The rest come with me.”

He took the candle and mounted the stairs, his men following, their heavy tread making the whole dwelling ring. The sound was worse than speech; it was the noise of men who do not need to ask permission. Martin was left below with the fire’s low crackle and the wet hiss of his own thoughts.

For a moment he stood very still, hearing the house like a living creature hears: the wind’s bass in the chimney, the rain’s fingers on the panes, the men’s boots thudding overhead. If Gerard meant to escape, it must be from Peter’s room or Margaret’s; neither was too high to leap. Dierich had left a watcher below—but then, what of that? For half a minute Gerard and the watcher would be two to one, and in half a minute much can be done: a man can break a wrist, crack a skull, vanish into a hedge-row, or into the night that swallows sound.

Martin went to the back door and held it ajar, peering out. The storm breathed in like a cold animal.

A light shone above—in Peter’s room. Martin’s mouth tightened. “Curse the fool!” he muttered. “Is he going to let them take him like a girl?”

The light moved, passed along, slid into Margaret’s chamber. Still no window opened. If Gerard had meant to jump, he would have done it before the men were in the room. Martin saw that at once, and closed the door softly, the bolt sliding home with a small final click that sounded too loud in his ear.

He crossed to the foot of the stair and listened. The men’s voices were muffled above, but their boots spoke plainly: prying, rummaging, stamping from board to board. Then a lull—so long a lull that it began to persuade Martin. Perhaps Gerard had slipped away while all were in the house. Perhaps he was already out in the storm, soaked to the bone, but free.

Then the silence was ripped by faint cries from the inner bedroom—Margaret’s.

“They have taken him,” groaned Martin. “They have got him.”

In that instant he saw, as if the fire flared and showed it, what taking Gerard away would mean. A man hauled off for parchments and accusations in such times did not come back with his skin whole. And if Gerard were broken—or hanged—Margaret’s heart would be broken with him, and the house would be emptied of its best sound: a girl’s laugh in the kitchen, a song under breath while kneading, the clink of a spoon against a pot. There are silences that last longer than storms.

Martin’s eyes ranged the room, wild as a trapped beast’s, and then steadied. A resolve came over him, not gentle, not modern—made for iron times and for a soldier driven into a corner.

He stepped to each door in turn—the front and the back—and, mimicking Dierich Brower’s voice, snapped out, “Watch the window!”

Then, very quietly, he closed and bolted both doors. The house was now a sealed oven, heat within, men within, and no easy way out.

He took up his bow and six arrows. One he fitted to the string; the others he dropped into his quiver with a soft, dry tap, like beans into a wooden bowl. His knife he placed upon a chair behind him, the hilt turned so that his hand could find it without looking—like a cook laying ready a blade before carving.

And there he waited at the foot of the stair.

He counted it coldly. Two men he could take with arrows before they closed; then Gerard and he must go hand-to-hand with the remaining pair—if Gerard were not bound, if Gerard could move. And panic—panic was always a spice in men’s blood; if Brower and his fellows hesitated a breath, Martin might loose three shafts instead of two, and the odds would shift.

He did not wait long. Heavy steps sounded overhead, then nearer, then the scrape of a boot on the top stair. The candlelight approached, throwing long trembling bars of brightness down the stairwell. Voices came with it, low and exasperated.

Martin’s heart beat hard—not from fear of dying, but from the knowledge of what he was about to do: to make this narrow space, ten feet square, into a slaughter-pen. Four against one, maybe two; and exile if he lived, death if he failed. Yet the man who had been seen shaking at a foolish lantern in a marsh did not waver now that the danger was honest and made of flesh and steel. His body braced itself; his eye burned. He was ready to take life, ready to lose it.

Above, Dierich Brower and his men had found Peter Buyskens in his first sleep. They flung open cupboards, upsetting the quiet order of a room that smelled of dried herbs and old paper. They ran their knives into an alligator Peter had nailed to the wall—a foolish trophy pierced for no reason but suspicion. They stooped and looked beneath the bed. The chamber was large, full of corners that might have hidden a man, and yet it hid nothing of what they wanted.

Then they went on to Margaret’s room. At the threshold, the sight of it discouraged them: small, bare, no cupboards, no deep shadows to tuck a fugitive into. Only a large fireplace and chimney, black-mouthed and honest. Dierich’s eye went straight there, for men hunting men learn to read stone and soot.

In that room they found the beauty of Sevenbergen asleep on an old chest scarcely a foot high. There was no careful attempt at hiding, no bed-curtains drawn close in guilt. The sheets were snowy white, and so was Margaret’s own linen—cleanliness like a silent witness. She lay as if fallen there, pale and still, a lily dropped into a rut of the world.

Presently she woke and sat up, staring like one pulled from a dream into a nightmare. Seeing the men, she began to scream—faintly at first, from shock—and then to pray for mercy. The sound of her fear, thin as it was, made Dierich Brower oddly ashamed, as if a sour note had crept into his own music.

“Here is a to-do,” he said, a little confused, trying for a jest to cover the ugliness of his errand. “We are not going to hurt you, my pretty maid. Lie you still, and shut your eyes, and think of your wedding-night, while I look up this chimney to see if Master Gerard is there.”

“Gerard! in my room?” Her voice gathered strength; indignation warmed it, and the warmth gave it flavor, like pepper shaken over bland broth. “Why not? They say that you and he—”

“Cruel!” she cried. “You know they have driven him away from me—driven him from his native place. This is a blind. You are thieves; you are wicked men; you are not men of Sevenbergen, or you would know Margaret Brandt better than to look for her lover in this room of all others in the world. Oh, brave! Four great hulking men to come, armed to the teeth, to insult one poor honest girl! The women that live in your own houses must be naught, or you would respect them too much to insult a girl of good character.”

Dierich, who had meant to be hard, found himself flinching beneath words sharper than steel. He glanced at his comrades, as if fearing the contagion of shame.

“There! come away, before we hear worse,” he said hastily. “He is not in the chimney. Plaster will mend what a cudgel breaks; but a woman’s tongue is a double-edged dagger, and a girl is a woman with her mother’s milk still in her.”

And with that, he beat a hasty retreat, the candle bobbing as he went, his men stumbling after him.

“I told the burgomaster how ’twould be,” he muttered, as if the storm outside could be blamed for everything that did not go as planned.
