The wind cut sharp and cold from the north, carrying with it the scent of distant sand and ancient stone. The camels, refreshed by the breeze, picked up speed, their hooves thudding rhythmically against the earth.

"Quicker," Trench muttered through clenched teeth. "Idris might already have missed us."

"Even if he has," Feversham replied, voice steady despite the tension coiling in his chest, "it will take time to gather men, to bring camels—by now it’s dark. They won’t catch us tonight."

Yet even as he spoke, he turned again toward Omdurman, where the sky still glowed faintly above the city. The drums were silent—thank God for that—but silence was no comfort. In this land, a man could vanish into the night without a sound. No thundering hooves would warn them of pursuit. Perhaps at that very moment, Ansar soldiers were riding within thirty paces behind them. Feversham strained his eyes into the blackness, searching for the glint of a white turban.

Trench did not look back. His jaw was set, his gaze fixed ahead. But fear gnawed at him just as fiercely, perhaps more so—because he dared not turn. His mind replayed the narrow streets of Omdurman, the scattered fires flickering at street corners, the shadows darting between houses, hunting two fugitives who had slipped from the House of Stone.

A word from Feversham broke his trance.

"What is it?"

"I can't see the fires of Omdurman anymore."

"The golden blot," Trench murmured, half to himself, "very low down."

Feversham didn’t press for meaning. He knew better than to ask. The words had come unbidden, stirred by memory—by the image that had been the last thing Feversham saw when he left Omdurman on his mission, and now, strangely, the first thing returning to him as he neared its end.

They rode on in silence until two figures emerged from the darkness, rising like phantoms at the feet of their camels. Abou Fatma whispered, “Instanna.”

They halted, guiding the beasts to kneel.

"The new camels are here?" Abou Fatma asked. Two men vanished briefly and returned with four fresh animals. Saddles were removed from the tired camels, replaced upon the new ones.

"Good camels?" Feversham asked, helping to secure the gear.

"Anafi breed," Abou Fatma said. "Fast. Strong." He glanced eastward, listening.

"The arms?" Trench demanded, voice urgent. "You’ve got them? Where?" He leaned forward, scanning the ground.

"In a moment," Abou Fatma said. But Trench could barely wait. His hands trembled with need.

"There’s ammunition?" he asked, breathless.

"Yes. Rifles. Revolvers. Ammunition."

Abou Fatma led them twenty yards away, to a patch of desert grass swaying gently in the wind. He knelt, digging into the soft sand with his bare hands.

"Here," he said.

Trench dropped to his knees beside him, clawing at the earth with frantic speed. A low whimper escaped his lips—a guttural sound, almost animal. To Feversham, it was chilling: the man reduced to instinct, driven by survival.

Then fingers brushed metal—the lock of a rifle. Instantly, Trench straightened, calm returning. He lifted the weapon, cradling it like a child.

"Now," he said, laughing—a sudden, wild joy breaking through the strain. "Now I don’t care. Let them chase us from Omdurman! One thing is certain: I’ll never go back. Not even if they catch us." He caressed the rifle as though it were alive, speaking to it in quiet reverence.

Two Arabs mounted the old camels and rode slowly back toward Omdurman. Abou Fatma and one other stayed with the fugitives. They mounted and began moving northeast.

Less than fifteen minutes had passed since their halt. That night, they rode through halfa grass and mimosa trees, progress slow and cautious. At sunrise, they reached flat, barren land dotted with small hills.

"Are the Effendi tired?" Abou Fatma asked. "Would you rest and eat? There is food on each camel's saddle."

"No," Feversham said. "We can eat as we ride."

Dates, bread, water from a zamsheyeh—this was their meal, consumed while seated atop their camels. Now freed from the thick grass, the beasts moved swiftly. By sunset, they stopped for an hour’s rest.

That night and the next day, they pushed onward—over rocky ridges, through valleys, across honey-colored sands. The pace felt interminable, like a funeral procession. A mountain rose at dawn, standing motionless on the horizon, unchanged all day.

At times, farmers tending sorghum fields sent their hearts racing. They veered wide. Caravans spotted far off by Abou Fatma’s sharp eyes forced them to kneel behind rocks, rifles ready.

Ten miles from Abu Klea, fresh camels awaited them. They switched mounts and traveled west of the Nile, keeping clear of known routes. Through the Ababdeh desert, they caught sight of a broad gray road stretching across their path.

"The road from Berber to Merowi," Abou Fatma said. "We cross it tonight. If God wills, tomorrow evening we cross the Nile."

"If God wills," Trench echoed, voice heavy with dread. "If only He wills."

They were now in caravan country—no longer safe by daylight. They dismounted and hid behind shrubs on high ground, watching the road like hawks. People moved along it like specks. When night fell, they crossed quietly, then pressed hard toward the river.

At dawn, Abou Fatma called a halt. They were in open, desolate terrain—no shelter, no cover.

"We must build a circle of stones," he said. "Lie close within it. I’ll go to the river. I’ll check the boat, ensure our friends are ready. I’ll return after dark."

They gathered stones quickly, forming a low wall about a foot high. Inside, Feversham and Trench lay flat, rifles and water-skins at hand.

"You have dates?" Abou Fatma asked.

"Yes."

"Then do not move until I return. I’ll take your camels and bring fresh ones tonight."

He rode off with his companion, leaving the two men alone in the sun.

They dug out the sand beneath the stones, lying still, staring through gaps in the wall. For both, this was perhaps the longest day of their lives. So near to safety—and yet not safe.

To Trench, it felt longer than any night in the House of Stone. To Feversham, longer than those days six years ago, sitting in his rooms above St. James’s Park, waiting for darkness before stepping outside.

They had no shade. The sun beat down mercilessly. Within the stone ring, there was no room to shift. They spoke little.

But sunset came at last. Darkness crept over the desert, cool winds rustling through the dunes.

"Listen!" Trench whispered.

Both men strained their ears. Soft padding—camels approaching. A low whistle.

"We’re here," Feversham said calmly.

"God be thanked!" Abou Fatma exclaimed. "Good news and bad. The boat is ready. Friends await. Fresh camels are prepared by the riverbank near Abu Hamed. But your escape is known. Roads and ferries are watched. Before sunrise, we must strike inland from the eastern bank."

They crossed the Nile cautiously around one o’clock. The boat was sunk on the far side. Camels waited. They traveled inland, slower than they wished—boulders littered the ground, forcing the beasts to walk.

The next day, they hid again in a stone ring while the camels grazed on higher ground. But that night, they made good progress. After two days, they reached the groves of Abu Hamed, rested twelve hours, and took fresh camels.

From there, their path led across the great Nubian Desert. Today, travelers pass through 240 miles of coal-black rock and yellow sand aboard trains, stopping at stations marked by signs and telegraph poles stretching endlessly into the sky. But in those days, movement was harder.

They kept east of the modern railway, following the line of wells among the hills.

On the second night of this leg, Trench shook Feversham awake.

"Look," he said, pointing south. "There’s no Southern Cross tonight."

His voice cracked with emotion. "For six years—every night for six years—I’ve seen it. How many times have I lain awake, watching those four slanting stars, wondering if this night would be the last? I tell you, Feversham, this is the first time I’ve truly dared believe we’d make it."

They sat up, gazing at the southern sky, hearts full of gratitude. When sleep came, it was restless—each waking to fear the constellation might still blaze low on the horizon, only to fall back into dreams of freedom.

After seven days, they reached Shof-el-Ain—a tiny well in a barren valley between featureless ridges. Here, they camped.

"We are safe," Abou Fatma declared. "God is good. North to Assouan, west to Wadi Halfa—we are free!"

He spread a cloth before the kneeling camels and poured dhurra for them. In his joy, he patted one on the neck. The camel turned and snarled.

Trench reached out to Feversham. "Thank you," he said simply.

"No thanks needed," Feversham replied, not taking the hand. "I served myself from the start."

"You’ve learned the churlishness of a camel," Trench laughed. "It carries you wherever you want, till it drops. Yet if you show gratitude, it bites. Hang it all, Feversham—there’s my hand."

Feversham untied a knot in his jibbeh and pulled out three white feathers—two small, heron feathers; one large, an ostrich feather broken from a fan.

"Will you take yours back?"

"Yes."

"You know what to do with it."

"Yes. There shall be no delay."

Feversham carefully wrapped the remaining feathers and tucked them deep into his ragged jibbeh, tying them securely.

"We shake hands, then," he said. And as their hands met, he added, "Tomorrow morning, we part company."

"Part company?" Trench protested. "After a year in Omdurman, weeks of flight? Why?"

"There’s nothing left to do. Castleton’s dead. You keep the feather he sent—but he’s gone. You can’t use it. You must go home."

"Yes," Feversham said. "But not with you. You go to Assouan, then Cairo. Friends await you. I won’t go with you."

Trench was silent. He understood. Feversham wanted to tell Ethne Eustace first—alone. Without him.

"I ought to tell you—no one knows why you resigned your commission, or about the feathers. We never spoke of it. Agreed never to speak—for the honor of the regiment. I’m glad we kept that vow."

"Perhaps you’ll see Durrance," Feversham said. "If you do, give him a message. Tell him the next time he invites me to visit—whether in England or Wadi Halfa—I’ll accept."

"And which way will you go?"

"To Wadi Halfa," Feversham said, pointing west. "I’ll take Abou Fatma. We’ll travel slowly down the Nile. The other Arab will guide you to Assouan."

They slept that night in peace beside the well.

At dawn, they parted.

Trench rode off first. As his camel rose, he leaned down. Feversham handed him the nose rein.

"Ramelton—that was the name? I won’t forget."

"Yes," Feversham said. "Ramelton. There’s a ferry across Lough Swilly to Rathmullen. Drive twelve miles to Ramelton. But she may not be there."

"If not there," Trench said, "I’ll find her somewhere else. Make no mistake, Feversham—I’ll find her."

And he rode forward, alone with his guide.

More than once, he looked back—Feversham still standing by the well. He longed to turn back, to ride to him, but only waved instead. The wave went unanswered.

Feversham had no eyes for his companion. His six years of trial, of purpose, had ended. And with it, a hollow emptiness settled in.

For six years, his mission had given him strength. Now, it was over.

Ethne? She was likely married by now. A wave of bitter despair washed over him—the folly of that night six years ago, the mistaken choice that had shattered everything.

He saw again the room in London, the quiet park beyond the window, the knock on the door, the telegram in his hand.

Then he remembered—his father. Probably reading the *Times* now, on the terrace at Broad Place, among the pine trees of Surrey. He must visit him. He must return the fourth feather to Ramelton.

And there was a telegram to send—to Lieutenant Sutch at Suakin.

He mounted his camel and rode west with Abou Fatma, slowly toward Wadi Halfa.

But the weight of loss did not lift. Nor did the anger at the foolish act that had brought him down.

Across the shimmering heat, the wooded slopes of Ramelton were visible.

And for the first time in his life, Harry Feversham doubted the truth of the "afterwards."
