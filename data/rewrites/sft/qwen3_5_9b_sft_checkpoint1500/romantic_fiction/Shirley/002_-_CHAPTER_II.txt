Night had fallen with a heavy, suffocating weight, the sky a bruised canvas of gray rain-clouds that swamped both stars and moon. Even Malone, usually indifferent to the shifting moods of nature, felt the damp chill seep through his clothes. He walked with a dogged persistence, his hat pushed back on the crown, a distinctively Irish habit, his boots sinking rhythmically into the mud of the cart tracks. *"Tramp, splash, tramp, splash"* echoed his progress along the causeway, where the road itself seemed swallowed by the earth. His destination was Hollow's Mill, but his path first led him past the glowing warmth of the Redhouse Inn. Inside, a fire crackled, glasses clinked, and laughter bubbled over an oak settle. For a fleeting second, Malone, a curate with a taste for the comforts of a tumbler of whisky-and-water, hesitated. But looking around, he saw familiar faces—Mr. Helstone's parishioners, neighbors who knew his business. With a resigned sigh, he turned away from the warmth, stepping back into the cold, wet world.

Leaving the high road, Malone cut across the flat, monotonous fields, jumping over hedges and walls with practiced ease. Beyond them stood a large, irregular building, a mill dominated by a towering stack of chimneys. It was silent, save for the rhythmic dripping of rain from the eaves and the low, wild whistle of the wind tearing through the boughs. Further down, the land dropped sharply into a valley, where a single light glimmered like a beacon through the gloom. Steered by this faint glow, Malone reached a small, white-washed house. A fresh-faced servant admitted him, revealing a narrow staircase carpeted in crimson, flanked by doors of matching velvet.

"Is Mr. Moore at home?" Malone asked.

"Yes, sir, but he is not in," the girl replied. "At the mill—in the counting-house."

A crimson door swung open, and a woman appeared. Her hair was dressed with elaborate curls, a style that somehow managed to look both artificial and imposing. To Malone, she seemed less a goddess and more a formidable obstacle. He shrank back slightly, muttering, "I'll go to him," and hurried down a short lane toward the massive black silhouette of the mill.

Inside the counting-house, the atmosphere shifted from the gloom outside to a stark, utilitarian brightness. The room was simple, furnished with sturdy green chairs and a solid desk cluttered with blueprints and machinery designs. An excellent fire burned in the grate, casting a warm glow that did little to soften the sharp edges of the room's function. Mr. Moore entered, a man who looked as though he had been carved from stone and shadow: thin, dark, with a sallow complexion and hair streaked with premature gray. He moved with a quiet intensity, his features marked by a perpetual anxiety that betrayed a mind constantly working ahead of the present moment.

"Welcome, Mr. Malone," Moore said, his voice carrying a faint, foreign accent. "I regret deeply that Mr. Helstone sent for you on such a night. The new machinery requires careful supervision."

Malone sat by the fire, warming his chilled limbs, and the two men fell into conversation. Moore spoke passionately about his business, the "Orders in Council," and the Luddites—the "frame-breakers" who destroyed his machines. He described the devastation caused by the war and the economic blockade, which had cut off America, his primary market. "These fellows have done to others what they may do to me," Moore declared, his eyes flashing with a mixture of fear and defiance. "But I stand by my trade. I am not like Sykes, who gave up when his shop burned. I will fight."

As the conversation waned, Moore noticed Malone growing restless. The curate kicked his heels together, yawning, clearly bored by the grim details of industrial strife. Sensing his guest's need for comfort, Moore produced a gridiron and a kettle. "Can you cook a mutton chop?" he asked, turning to the task with vigorous enthusiasm. Malone, eager to prove his worth, accepted the challenge. Together, they prepared a simple meal, the smell of cooking meat filling the room, breaking the tension of the evening. Moore poured a glass of punch, offering it to Malone with a gesture of genuine hospitality, though his eyes remained fixed on the door, listening intently for any sign of disturbance.

For some time, Moore watched Malone with a strange, unreadable expression, his gaze piercing and meditative. Suddenly, his demeanor shifted. He raised a hand, signaling silence. *"Chut,"* he whispered in a hushed tone. Rising abruptly, he extinguished the lamp and slipped out into the night, leaving Malone alone in the counting-house.

Outside, the air was still and stagnant, the river running swiftly beneath the bridge. Moore's keen ear caught a sound that cut through the silence: the crunch of heavy wheels on stones, distant but unmistakable. He returned to the millyard, lighting a lantern, and waited. Soon, the great wagons emerged, drawn by enormous horses, their hooves splashing through the mud. Moore hailed them, expecting his workers, Joe Scott and the others.

"Ay, ay, devil! All's raight! We've smashed 'em!" a voice cried out.

Before Moore could react, the drivers vanished. The wagons stood empty, abandoned in the yard. Moore rushed to inspect the vehicles, his heart pounding. The frames, the costly machinery he had risked his entire fortune to acquire, were gone. In their place, pinned to the harness of one of the lead horses, was a crumpled piece of paper—a challenge.

Moore read the scrawled message, his face pale under the lantern light. *"Your hellish machinery is shivered... Take this as a warning..."* Anger surged through him, hot and violent, mixed with a terrifying resolve. He would not let them win. He would hunt them down.

Hurrying back inside, Moore gave urgent instructions to Sarah, the servant. Ring the bell, he commanded, until the whole neighborhood heard. Then, he fetched his saddlebags and mounted his horse. Moments later, the heavy tolling of the mill bell shattered the silence, echoing across the hollow. Neighbors from the Redhouse arrived in a panic, lanterns blazing, drawn by the sound. Among them rode Mr. Helstone, the local rector, accompanied by a young man on a shaggy pony.

Moore met them in the yard, his voice steady despite the chaos. "The wagons have been attacked," he announced, holding up the note. "Joe Scott and his men are waylaid on Stilbro' Moor. The frames are destroyed."

A murmur of outrage rippled through the crowd. The situation demanded immediate action. Moore proposed a rescue mission, urging a group of men to accompany him to Stilbro' Moor to find the trapped workers and recover whatever remained of the equipment. Helstone and the young man on the pony volunteered eagerly, their spirits lifted by the prospect of adventure and justice.

As Moore mounted his horse, Helstone leaned close, asking quietly if he had secured the mutton chops, lest anyone else try to steal them. Moore nodded, a grim smile playing on his lips. "Reserve them for those who earn them," he muttered, though the thought lingered uneasily in his mind. The rescue party set off, riding into the dark, wet night, driven by the urgency of the moment and the fierce determination to protect what was theirs. Behind them, the empty wagons stood as a testament to the violence that had erupted, a stark reminder of the fragile balance between progress and tradition, industry and survival.
