The wind cut sharp and cold from the north, driving the camels into a frantic trot.

"Quicker," Trench gritted out, his teeth clenched against the strain. "Idris may already be gone."

Feversham offered a thin hope. "Even if he has, gathering a pursuit takes time. Men must fetch their mounts. Look at the sky—it is already dark."

He spoke with false optimism, yet his gaze betrayed him, darting repeatedly toward the distant, blinding glow of Omdurman. The rhythmic drumming that had once marked the city's heartbeat was silenced, a small mercy in the vast, suffocating quiet of the desert. Here, speed meant silence; there was no thunder of hooves to announce a pursuer. Even now, Ansar riders could be mere paces behind, invisible in the gloom. Feversham squinted backward, expecting to catch the flash of a white turban, but saw only void.

Trench, however, refused to look back. He rode with a rigid posture, eyes locked ahead, though terror burned hotter in him than in his friend. He did not dare turn his head. Instead, his mind traveled backward, visualizing the long, narrow streets of the town, the scattered fires at street corners, and the frantic shadows of men scouring the houses for the two prisoners who had fled the House of Stone.

When Feversham finally spoke, breaking Trench's trance, the younger man answered without shifting his gaze.

"What is it?"

"I no longer see the fires of Omdurman."

"The golden blot, eh? Very low down?" Trench murmured, his voice detached.

Feversham said nothing, understanding the weight of the words. Trench could not explain why he had spoken; the phrase had surfaced suddenly, fitting perfectly as the last image of the city faded from Feversham's memory before this final mission began. Now that the mission was done, the vision returned.

Silence stretched between them until two figures materialized from the darkness at the camels' feet.

"Instanna!" cried Abou Fatma softly.

The camels halted and knelt.

"The new camels?" Abou Fatma asked. Two men vanished briefly, returning with four fresh beasts. Simultaneously, the saddles were stripped from the exhausted animals Trench and Feversham had ridden from Omdurman.

"They are good camels?" Feversham asked, helping secure the tack.

"Anafi breed," Abou Fatma confirmed. "Quick! Listen to the east!"

"The arms?" Trench demanded instantly. "Do you have them? Where are they?"

He dropped to his knees, frantically searching the ground.

"In a moment," Abou Fatma assured him, though Trench seemed unable to wait. The young officer displayed more urgency in handling the weapons than in fearing capture.

"Ammunition?" he whispered, feverish.

"Yes, rifles, revolvers, and plenty of powder."

Abou Fatma led them twenty yards away, where tall desert grass rustled around their ankles. With swift, digging motions, he tore into the soft sand.

"Here."

Trench threw himself onto the ground beside him, scooping furiously with both hands. A guttural, inhuman whine escaped his throat—a sound like a hound at a kill. To Feversham, it was horrifying to watch his comrade sink into such primal behavior, his hands moving with the frantic rhythm of a dog's paws. For a moment, Trench was not a man, but an animal driven by instinct.

Seconds later, Trench's fingers closed around the cold metal of a rifle lock. He straightened up, calm once more, clutching the weapon. The other arms were unearthed, the ammunition distributed.

"Now," Trench breathed, a wild thrill of joy cracking his voice. "Now I don't care. Let them chase us from Omdurman! One thing is certain: I shall never return there. Not even if they catch us."

He cradled the rifle as if it were a living child.

Two Arabs mounted the old camels and rode slowly back toward the city. Abou Fatma stayed with the fugitives. They spurred their mounts northeast, covering little ground in the first fifteen minutes, but soon they pushed through fields of halfa grass and mimosa trees. By sunrise, they emerged onto flat, barren land dotted with small hills.

"Are the Effendis tired?" Abou Fatma asked. "There is food on the saddles."

"No," Feversham replied. "Eat as we ride."

Dates, bread, and water from a leather skin formed their meal, consumed while seated atop their moving mounts. Freed from the dense vegetation, the camels broke into a rapid trot. That evening, they paused for an hour's rest, but the journey resumed under the cover of darkness.

For days, they pushed their bodies and beasts to the limit, climbing rocky heights, crossing valleys, and tearing across plains of honey-colored sand. Yet, to each man, the pace felt agonizingly slow, dragging like a funeral procession. Mountains loomed on the horizon at dawn, towering and unmoving throughout the day, refusing to shrink or retreat. Occasionally, a farmer tending a patch of sorghum would make their hearts leap, forcing a dangerous detour; or a distant caravan spotted by Abou Fatma's keen eyes would cause them to drop flat behind a rock, rifles raised, holding their breath.

Ten miles from Abu Klea, a relay of fresh camels awaited them. They pressed westward, keeping a day's march clear of the Nile. Passing through the territory of the Ababdeh tribe, they finally caught sight of a broad gray expanse cutting across their path.

"The road from Berber to Merowi," Abou Fatma noted. "We turn east toward the river north of it. Tonight we cross that road. If God wills, tomorrow evening we cross the Nile."

"If God wills," Trench echoed. "If *only* He wills."

His glance swept the landscape, fear tightening in his chest as safety drew nearer. This was caravan country; daylight travel was suicide. They dismounted and hid behind a belt of shrubs on high ground, watching the road like specks moving below. They descended into the darkness to cross it, pushing hard toward the river through the night.

At dawn, Abou Fatma ordered another halt. They stood in a desolate openness where the flatness offered no concealment. Feversham and Trench scanned the horizon to the right. The Nile flowed somewhere in that direction, visible only as a vague promise.

"We must build a stone circle," Abou Fatma instructed. "Lie close to the ground inside. I will go to the river to check the boat and our friends. I return after dark."

They gathered stones quickly, raising a low wall a foot high. Inside, Feversham and Trench lay prone, water skins and rifles at their sides.

"You have dates?" Abou Fatma asked.

"Yes."

"Do not stir until I return. I will take your camels and bring fresh ones tonight."

With his companion, Abou Fatma rode off toward the river. Trench and Feversham dug out the sand beneath the stones, lying motionless and staring at the gaps between the rocks. For both men, this was perhaps the longest day of their lives. So close to safety, yet so dangerously exposed. To Trench, it felt longer than his imprisonment in the House of Stone; to Feversham, longer than any single day spent waiting in his London rooms above St. James's Park six years prior.

They were nearing Berber, meaning the pursuit must be hot on their heels. Feversham wondered how he had ever summoned the courage to face Berber. There was no shade; the sun beat down mercilessly on their backs, baking them within the narrow stone ring. They spoke scarcely at all.

As twilight approached, bringing a cool breeze and softening the heat, Trench leaned forward.

"Listen!"

Both men strained their ears, hearing the soft, rhythmic padding of camels approaching rapidly. A low whistle signaled their arrival.

"We are here," Feversham whispered.

"God be thanked!" Abou Fatma replied. "Good news and bad. The boat is ready, our friends await, and fresh camels stand by the bank at Abu Hamed. But your escape is known. Roads and ferries are heavily guarded. Before sunrise, we must strike inland from the eastern bank."

They crossed the river cautiously around one in the morning, sinking the boat on the far shore. Waiting camels carried them inland, moving slower than the fugitives wished due to the heavy boulder-strewn terrain. Throughout the following day, they hid again within a stone ring while the camels grazed on higher ground.

That night, they made excellent progress. Two days later, they reached the groves of Abu Hamed, resting for twelve hours before mounting a fresh relay. Their route now led across the great Nubian Desert. Today, travelers traverse these two hundred and forty miles of black rock and yellow sand in comfort aboard trains, passing stations marked by telegraph poles stretching endlessly into the sky. Then, in those days, progress was a grueling struggle. They kept east of the current railway line, following ancient tracks of wells among the hills.
