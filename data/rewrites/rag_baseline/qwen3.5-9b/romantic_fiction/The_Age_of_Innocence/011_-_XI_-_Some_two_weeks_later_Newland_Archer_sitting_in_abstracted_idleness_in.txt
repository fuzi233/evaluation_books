The air in the private compartment of Letterblair, Lamson, and Low was thick with the scent of old paper and the dust of unspoken histories. Newland Archer sat in abstracted idleness, a posture of polite vacancy that served as armor against the encroaching demands of the world outside. The summons from the head of the firm interrupted this suspended animation.

Old Mr. Letterblair loomed behind his mahogany desk, a figure carved from the very wood of the establishment. He stroked his close-clipped white whiskers, his hand moving through the rumpled grey locks atop his jutting brows with a rhythm that suggested a man trying to soothe a fever that refused to break. To Archer, the junior partner, the gesture evoked a specific, unsettling image: the Family Physician, annoyed by a patient whose symptoms defied classification, whose illness existed in a realm beyond the reach of standard remedies.

"My dear sir—" Letterblair's voice was low, carrying the weight of three generations of New York gentility. "I have sent for you regarding a little matter. A matter which, for the moment, I prefer not to mention either to Mr. Skipworth or Mr. Redwood."

These names referred to the other senior partners, ghosts on the letterhead. In the ancient legal associations of New York, the living were merely custodians of the dead; Mr. Letterblair, professionally speaking, was his own grandson. He leaned back, the leather creaking, his brow furrowing into a landscape of worry.

"For family reasons," he continued, the words dropping like stones into a still pond.

Archer looked up, expecting the usual trivialities of estate disputes or inheritance squabbles. Instead, Letterblair offered an explanatory smile, a bow that acknowledged the gravity of the name he was about to speak.

"The Mingott family," he said. "Mrs. Manson Mingott sent for me yesterday. Her granddaughter, the Countess Olenska, wishes to sue her husband for divorce. Certain papers have been placed in my hands."

He paused, drumming his fingers on the polished surface of the desk—a nervous tic that betrayed the magnitude of the breach. "In view of your prospective alliance with the family, I should like to consult you—to consider the case with you—before taking any further steps."

The sensation hit Archer instantly, a hot flush rising in his temples. He had seen the Countess Olenska only once since his visit to her, a fleeting encounter at the Opera within the gilded confines of the Mingott box. During the intervening weeks, her image had receded, fading from the foreground of his consciousness as May Welland resumed her rightful, luminous position. He had not heard the word divorce spoken aloud since Janey's first casual allusion, dismissing the rumor as the kind of unfounded gossip that circulated in drawing rooms.

Theoretically, the concept of divorce was almost as distasteful to him as it was to his mother. Yet, he was annoyed that Mr. Letterblair, seemingly prompted by the iron will of old Catherine Mingott, should be so evidently planning to drag him into the fray. After all, there were plenty of Mingott men already entrenched in such affairs, and as yet, he was not even a Mingott by marriage.

He waited for the senior partner to elaborate, but Letterblair simply unlocked a drawer and produced a packet of documents.

"If you will run your eye over these papers..."

Archer frowned, the movement sharp and defensive. "I beg your pardon, sir; but just because of the prospective relationship, I should prefer your consulting Mr. Skipworth or Mr. Redwood."

Letterblair looked surprised, a flicker of offense crossing his face. It was unusual for a junior to reject such an opening, a refusal of the privilege that defined their hierarchy. He bowed, a gesture of forced politeness.

"I respect your scruple, sir; but in this case, I believe true delicacy requires you to do as I ask. Indeed, the suggestion is not mine, but Mrs. Manson Mingott's and her son's. I have seen Lovell Mingott; and also Mr. Welland. They all named you."

Archer felt his temper rising, a slow burn beneath the surface of his composure. He had been drifting languidly with events for the last fortnight, allowing May's fair looks and radiant nature to obliterate the importunate pressure of the Mingott claims. But this behest from old Mrs. Mingott roused him to a sudden, painful awareness of what the clan believed they had the right to exact from a prospective son-in-law. He chafed at the role, feeling the constriction of expectations tightening around his chest.

"Her uncles ought to deal with this," he said, his voice tight. "They have. The matter has been gone into by the family. They are opposed to the Countess's idea; but she is firm, and insists on a legal opinion."

The young man fell silent, refusing to open the packet in his hand.

"Does she want to marry again?" Letterblair asked.

"I believe it is suggested; but she denies it."

"Then—"

"Will you oblige me, Mr. Archer, by first looking through these papers? Afterward, when we have talked the case over, I will give you my opinion."

Archer withdrew reluctantly, clutching the unwelcome documents. Since their last meeting, he had half-unconsciously collaborated with events in ridding himself of the burden of Madame Olenska. His solitary hour by the firelight had drawn them into a momentary intimacy, fragile and bright, until the Duke of St. Austrey's intrusion with Mrs. Lemuel Struthers, and the Countess's joyous greeting of them, had providentially shattered the spell. Two days later, he had assisted at the comedy of her reinstatement in the van der Luydens' favor, telling himself, with a touch of tartness, that a lady who knew how to thank all-powerful elderly gentlemen so effectively for a bunch of flowers did not require the private consolations or public championship of a young man of his small compass.

To view the matter in this light simplified his own case and surprisingly restored the luster to his domestic virtues. He could not picture May Welland, in whatever conceivable emergency, hawking about her private difficulties and lavishing her confidences on strange men. She had never seemed to him finer or fairer than in the week that followed. He had even yielded to her wish for a long engagement, since she had found the one disarming answer to his plea for haste.

"You know, when it comes to the point, your parents have always let you have your way ever since you were a little girl," he had argued.

And she had answered, with her clearest look: "Yes; and that's what makes it so hard to refuse the very last thing they'll ever ask of me as a little girl."

That was the old New York note; that was the kind of certainty he wished to anchor his life to. If one had habitually breathed the New York air, there were times when anything less crystalline seemed stifling.

The papers he had retired to read did not tell him much in fact; they plunged him instead into an atmosphere where he choked and spluttered. They consisted mainly of an exchange of letters between Count Olenski's solicitors and a French legal firm to whom the Countess had applied for the settlement of her financial situation. There was also a short letter from the Count to his wife. After reading it, Newland Archer rose, jammed the papers back into their envelope, and reentered Mr. Letterblair's office.

"Here are the letters, sir. If you wish, I'll see Madame Olenska," he said in a constrained voice.

"Thank you—thank you, Mr. Archer. Come and dine with me tonight if you're free, and we'll go into the matter afterward: in case you wish to call on our client tomorrow."

Newland Archer walked straight home that afternoon. It was a winter evening of transparent clearness, with an innocent young moon hovering above the rooftops. He wanted to fill his soul's lungs with the pure radiance of the night, to avoid exchanging a word with anyone until he and Mr. Letterblair were closeted together after dinner. It was impossible to decide otherwise than he had done: he must see Madame Olenska himself rather than let her secrets be bared to other eyes.

A great wave of compassion had swept away his indifference and impatience. She stood before him as an exposed and pitiful figure, to be saved at all costs from further wounding herself in her mad plunges against fate. He remembered what she had told him of Mrs. Welland's request to be spared whatever was "unpleasant" in her history, and he winced at the thought that it was perhaps this attitude of mind which kept the New York air so pure.

"Are we only Pharisees after all?" he wondered, puzzled by the effort to reconcile his instinctive disgust at human vileness with his equally instinctive pity for human frailty.

For the first time, he perceived how elementary his own principles had always been. He passed for a young man who had not been afraid of risks, knowing that his secret love-affair with poor, silly Mrs. Thorley Rushworth had not been too secret to invest him with a becoming air of adventure. But Mrs. Rushworth was "that kind of woman"—foolish, vain, clandestine by nature, and far more attracted by the secrecy and peril of the affair than by such charms and qualities as he possessed.
