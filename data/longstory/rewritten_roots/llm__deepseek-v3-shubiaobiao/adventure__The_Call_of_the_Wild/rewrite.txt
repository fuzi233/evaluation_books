*"Ancient instincts stir,
Breaking the chains of the tame;
From winter’s slumber, the wild awakens once more."*

Buck knew nothing of the fever sweeping the North—the rush of men hungry for gold, their desperate need for dogs like him: strong, thick-furred, and built for toil. While prospectors clawed through frozen darkness, he ruled over sunlit acres in California’s Santa Clara Valley. Judge Miller’s estate sprawled behind poplar groves, a kingdom of orchards, stables, and vine-draped cottages. Here, Buck was sovereign.

He was no pampered lapdog like the yapping terriers or the frail Mexican hairless that cowered indoors. Buck hunted with the Judge’s sons, escorted his daughters through twilight fields, and sprawled before the library fire on winter nights. At a hundred and forty pounds, with the blood of a St. Bernard and a Scotch shepherd, he carried himself like royalty—confident, even prideful.

But destiny cares little for kings.

Manuel, the gardener’s assistant, had a weakness for gambling and debts that outran his wages. One evening, while the household was distracted, he slipped a rope around Buck’s neck.

Trust turned to betrayal in an instant.

Buck fought—jaws snapping, fury blazing—but the rope crushed his breath away. Darkness swallowed him. He woke to the lurch of a train car, his throat raw, his pride in tatters.

Men came and went, prodding him with sticks, laughing at his rage. For two days, he refused food and water, his defiance hardening like ice. By the time he reached Seattle, his eyes were blood-red, his spirit a storm barely contained.

Then came the man in the red sweater.

Hatchet in hand, he split the crate open. Buck erupted in a whirl of teeth and fury—only to meet the brutal lesson of the club. Blow after blow hammered him down, until pain drowned even his anger.

"Enough," the man said, wiping sweat from his brow. Buck lay panting, beaten but unbroken. He had learned the first law of the wild: *strength alone does not rule*. The club was power. And power must be respected.

Days passed. Other dogs arrived, some defiant, some broken. Buck watched, silent and calculating. Then came Perrault, a wiry French-Canadian with government money and a sharp eye for dogs.

"That one’s worth a thousand," he muttered, counting bills into the red sweater’s hand.

Buck’s last glimpse of the South faded as the *Narwhal* carried him northward. Below deck, he faced new trials: the sly aggression of Spitz, a white-furred gladiator; the indifference of Dave, a morose veteran of the trails. And then—cold.

Snow.

It burned his paws, dusted his coat, melted on his tongue like ghostly fire. The men laughed. Buck bristled with shame, though he did not yet understand: this was his baptism into the wild.

And the wild would not be gentle.

Buck’s first day on the Dyea shore was a plunge into savagery. Gone was the sunlit idleness of Judge Miller’s estate—here, survival demanded claws and cunning. The air hummed with violence, every snarl a promise of blood. These weren’t the tamed curs of civilization; these were wolves in harness, governed by a single brutal law: *Kill or be killed.*

The lesson came swift and brutal. Curly, ever friendly, approached a husky with a wagging tail. In a flash, teeth gleamed—a slash, a recoil—and her face split open. Before Buck could react, the pack swarmed, a writhing mass of fur and fury, burying Curly’s shrieks beneath their weight. Spitz, the white-furred demon, laughed with a lolling tongue. The men clubbed the dogs back, but too late. Curly lay in crimson snow, her body a ruin.

*So this was the way of it.* No second chances. No mercy. Buck’s muscles coiled tight. He’d never fall.

Then came the harness, rough straps biting into his fur. A sled dog now, hauling like a common mule. François’ whip taught obedience; Dave’s teeth corrected missteps. Spitz, the lead dog, snarled commands, yanking the traces to steer him. Yet Buck learned fast—*ho* meant halt, *mush* meant run—and the sled became an extension of his will.

New dogs joined—Billee, a whimpering fool; Joe, a snapping storm of teeth; Sol-leks, a one-eyed veteran who tore Buck’s shoulder for approaching his blind side. That night, the cold gnawed his bones. The tent spat him out, and the snow hid no warmth—until he stumbled upon Billee curled in a hollow. A stolen trick: Buck dug his own den, and survival grew easier.

Dawn revealed a world remade. The team lunged into the wilderness, glaciers yawning beneath their paws. Buck’s hunger sharpened. A meager ration of fish vanished too quick; soon, he stole like the others, swift and silent. Pike’s thievery taught him—bacon disappeared, and clumsy Dub took the blame. Morality was a luxury; hunger was law.

His body hardened. Muscles turned to iron, paws cracked ice without flinching. His senses sharpened—wind spoke of storms, the faintest snap of twigs betrayed danger. By night, his howl echoed ancestors long dust, their wildness singing in his blood.

Men called it progress. Buck knew better. It was an awakening—a shedding of softness. The North had whispered, and he answered with tooth and claw.

For gold had lured men north. And men, in turn, had unleashed the beast in Buck.

The wild spirit within Buck grew stronger with every mile, forged by the brutal demands of the trail. Yet he wore this newfound ferocity like a hidden blade—controlled, calculating. Survival had sharpened his instincts; he avoided conflict where he could, biding his time. But between him and Spitz, the lead dog, there simmered a hatred too deep for words.

Spitz, sensing a rival, bared his teeth at every chance. He goaded Buck, hungry for the fight that would end with one of them dead. It nearly came to blows one bitter night on the shores of Lake Le Barge, where the wind carved through them like a knife. Exhausted, they camped on the frozen lake itself, backs to a sheer cliff.

Buck had burrowed a warm nest beneath an overhang—until he returned from his meager meal to find Spitz curled in his spot. A snarl ripped from his throat. No more retreating. He lunged, the force of his attack shocking even himself. Spitz reeled, unprepared for the fury of a dog he'd dismissed as soft.

François whooped as the two clashed, but before the fight could be decided, chaos erupted. Starving huskies—dozens of them—swarmed the camp, drawn by the scent of food. Gaunt, desperate, they were more wolf than dog. The team scattered under their assault. Buck found himself flanked by three at once, teeth tearing into his shoulders. Blood slicked the snow.

Pandemonium ruled. Dave and Sol-leks fought back-to-back; Joe snapped like a demon, crushing bone. Buck wrenched free from one attacker, only to feel Spitz’s fangs sink into his throat—treacherous, cowardly. A club swung, yelps filled the air, and the invaders fled—but not before half their supplies were gone, the sled’s harnesses chewed to ruin.

The team limped away, battered. Dolly’s throat was torn; Joe lost an eye. Yet the trail spared no one. They pushed on through the Thirty Mile River’s treacherous ice, where every step risked drowning. Perrault, unshaken, led them through the frozen jaws of death.

Buck’s paws, unhardened by generations of wilderness, left him hobbling. François carved his own moccasins into boots for him, and Buck repaid the kindness with stubborn charm—refusing to move one morning until they were laced on.

Then madness struck. Dolly, quiet until then, howled like a wolf and charged Buck in a frenzy. He fled, her frothing jaws a breath behind, until François’s axe ended her. Spitz seized the moment, ripping into Buck’s weakened body—until the lash sent him sprawling.

**"Spitz is a devil,"** Perrault muttered. **"One day, he’ll kill Buck."**

François grinned. **"No. Buck is two devils. Watch—when he snaps, he’ll eat Spitz whole."**

The rebellion had begun. Buck undermined Spitz at every turn—protecting shirkers, stealing his authority. The team unraveled, defiance spreading like a sickness. Even gentle Billee grew bold.

Then came the chase. A snowshoe hare sent the dogs into a frenzy, Buck at the lead, the ancient thrill of the hunt singing in his blood. This was life at its peak—the raw joy of pursuit, of teeth meeting warm flesh. But Spitz, ever cunning, cut the hare off at a bend. His killing strike drew a shriek, and the pack howled in savage triumph.

The message was clear: only one of them would lead. The final fight loomed, inevitable as the frozen dawn.

“Ha! What did I tell you? I said Buck was two devils in one—and I was right!” François exclaimed the next morning when he found Spitz missing and Buck battered but victorious. He dragged Buck closer to the fire, inspecting the fresh wounds that marked his triumph.

Perrault whistled low. “Spitz fought like a demon.”

“And Buck fought like two,” François shot back. “Now we make good time. No more Spitz, no more trouble.”

As Perrault packed camp, François harnessed the team. Buck trotted straight to the lead position—Spitz’s old place—but François ignored him, pulling Sol-leks forward instead. Buck’s lips curled. In a flash, he lunged, forcing Sol-leks back and planting himself at the front.

François laughed. “Look at him! Kills Spitz and thinks he can take the lead now!” He shoved Buck aside, but the dog refused to yield. Even when François grabbed him by the scruff, Buck growled, defiant. Sol-leks, though reinstated, trembled—he knew who truly commanded respect.

François snatched a club. Buck remembered the man in the red sweater and retreated—but not far. He circled just beyond reach, snarling, eyes locked on the weapon. He was no fool; he had learned the dance of clubs.

Perrault joined in. For an hour, they chased Buck, hurling curses and clubs. He dodged, weaving through the camp, never fleeing—only demanding what he had earned.

Finally, François groaned. “We’re wasting time!”

Perrault threw up his hands. “Fine. Let him lead.”

François unhooked Sol-leks. At once, Buck trotted forward, victorious. The traces were fastened, and the team surged onto the trail.

---

Buck exceeded all expectations. Where Spitz had ruled with teeth, Buck ruled with cunning and raw power. He enforced order swiftly. Pike, the shirker, learned to pull his weight after a few sharp corrections. Joe, the troublemaker, was pinned and throttled into submission. Even Sol-leks and Dave, seasoned workers, respected the new regime.

By the time they reached the Rink Rapids, François could only marvel. “Never seen a dog like Buck! Worth a thousand dollars, I swear!”

The team flew across the ice, smashing records. They covered in a single day what had once taken ten. From Lake Le Barge to White Horse Rapids—sixty miles in a single run. Men clung to sled ropes, dragged in their wake.

But triumph was fleeting. In Skaguay, François and Perrault vanished from Buck’s life, replaced by a Scotch half-breed and a grueling mail run to Dawson. The work was brutal, the loads heavy. The dogs wore thin, their paws cracked, their breath labored.

Dave suffered most. Something inside him had broken. He faltered in the traces, crying out in pain. When they finally pulled him from the team, he refused to accept it. He attacked Sol-leks, gnawing through the traces to reclaim his place.

The drivers understood. A dog’s pride was in his work—even if it killed him.

They harnessed Dave again. He ran until his legs gave out, until the sled rolled over him. At camp, he crawled toward the traces, inch by inch, before collapsing.

The next morning, a single gunshot echoed behind the trees.

The team marched on, but every dog knew.

Buck knew.

The law of the wild was mercy.

The Salt Water Mail team, with Buck at its lead, stumbled into Skaguay after thirty brutal days on the trail. They were hollow-eyed specters of their former selves—bones barely held together by exhaustion. Buck’s powerful frame had withered from one hundred forty pounds to one hundred fifteen. The others fared worse: Pike, the sly shirker, now limped in earnest; Sol-leks dragged his injured leg; Dub’s shoulder-blade screamed with every step.

Their paws were raw, their muscles liquid lead. This wasn’t the weariness of a single hard day’s labor—this was the slow, grinding depletion of months. Twenty-five hundred miles in less than five months, with only five days’ rest in the last eighteen hundred. They were spent, down to the marrow.

"Keep movin’, you poor beat-up devils," the driver coaxed as they swayed down Skaguay’s muddy thoroughfare. "Almost there. Then—long rest. A *real* one."

But rest never came.

Three days later, two greenhorns bought the team for pocket change. Hal, a brash boy with a revolver strapped to his hip like a toy, and Charles, a watery-eyed man whose mustache seemed the only firm thing about him, fumbled through the transaction. Their camp was a disaster: half-pitched tent, filthy dishes, chaos. And then there was Mercedes—Charles’s wife, Hal’s sister—a woman who fluttered like a trapped bird, offering useless advice while the men botched packing the sled.

Neighboring trappers watched, smirking.

"That load’s top-heavy as a drunk on stilts," one remarked. "Ditch the tent."

Mercedes gasped. "*No tent?* How *dare* you suggest—"

Charles and Hal ignored them, lashing the wobbling monstrosity together. When the dogs strained, the sled didn’t budge. Hal raised his whip—

"*Don’t!*" Mercedes wrenched it from him. "They’re *trying*!"

A bystander spat. "Runners are frozen. Break ’em loose or you’ll kill your team."

Hal heaved against the sled. It lurched forward—then capsized on the first turn, scattering supplies across Skaguay. The dogs, furious and starved, bolted. Buck led the charge, the sled bouncing behind like a dying beast.

---

The trio repacked, drowning in advice: *Half the load. Double the dogs.* They scoffed. Instead, they bought six mangy Outsiders—pointers, a Newfoundland, mongrels—useless creatures who cowered under Buck’s glare. Fourteen dogs now, a ludicrous parade. Hal beamed. Mercedes nodded sagely. None understood: one sled couldn’t carry enough food for fourteen mouths.

The journey was a slow-motion collapse.

They bungled every step—slack ropes, misplaced loads, camps pitched in darkness. The dogs starved as Hal overfed then underfed, his arithmetic crumbling. The Outsiders died first, their stomachs rejecting frozen horse-hide traded for Hal’s revolver. Dub was shot. Billee’s skull met the axe. Koona vanished into the snow.

Misery stripped the humans bare. Mercedes, who’d never lifted a finger in her life, now rode the sled, her 120 pounds crushing the team. When they dragged her off, she went limp, forcing them to carry her. Arguments raged—over firewood, over ancestors, over art. The trail’s cruelty had peeled them down to raw, snapping nerves.

---

By the end, only five dogs remained: Joe, hollow-eyed; Pike, limping; Sol-leks, blind and loyal; Teek, beaten for being fresher; and Buck, a ghost of himself, moving by instinct alone.

Spring bloomed around them—willows greening, ice cracking on the Yukon—but they were corpses in motion. The world sang with life; they staggered toward death.

And still, the whip fell.

The brutal winter had nearly claimed John Thornton—his partners had left him behind to recover while they pushed on toward Dawson to haul a raft of logs. By spring, the last of his limp faded, and he spent the long, sun-drenched days by the riverbank with Buck, the great dog slowly mending beside him.

Rest was a luxury after three thousand miles of torment. Buck’s wounds closed, his muscles thickened, and his ribs vanished beneath renewed flesh. They were all idle—Thornton, Buck, and the two other dogs, Skeet and Nig—waiting for the raft that would carry them downstream. Skeet, a gentle Irish setter, had nursed Buck as tenderly as a mother cat cleans her kittens. Nig, quieter but just as kind, was a massive black crossbreed with laughing eyes.

To Buck’s surprise, neither resented him. They shared Thornton’s generosity. As Buck regained strength, they lured him into wild games, and even Thornton would abandon dignity to join. Through laughter and roughhousing, Buck discovered something new: love. Not the dutiful bond of Judge Miller’s household, but something fierce, consuming. Thornton had saved him, yes—but more than that, he was a master unlike any other. Other men cared for their dogs out of obligation; Thornton did it as if they were his own blood.

Buck adored him. He didn’t nudge for attention like Skeet or lean heavily like Nig. Instead, he watched, studied, memorized every flicker of Thornton’s expression. He would lie for hours, gaze fixed, heart pounding, until Thornton turned—and then their eyes would meet, wordless, brimming with understanding.

Yet beneath this devotion, the wildness in Buck still stirred. He fought with the same ruthless cunning as before, dominating any challenger. Mercy was a foreign concept—kill or be killed, the law of the wild. At night, whispers from ancient ancestors coiled in his dreams, urging him toward the forest’s depths. But always, Thornton’s love pulled him back.

Others meant nothing. Travelers might praise him; Buck remained indifferent. Even Thornton’s partners, Hans and Pete, earned only tolerance. But for Thornton? There was nothing Buck wouldn’t do.

One afternoon, perched on a cliff’s edge, Thornton—reckless with affection—pointed to the abyss below. *“Jump, Buck!”* The dog lunged without hesitation, and Thornton barely caught him in time.

*“It’s uncanny,”* Pete muttered afterward.

*“No,”* Thornton said, shaken. *“It’s terrible. And magnificent.”*

Months later, in a Circle City saloon, a brute named Burton struck Thornton without warning. Buck moved like lightning, his jaws closing on the man’s throat before the crowd could react. The attack earned him a reputation—and Thornton’s life, again, when their boat capsized in violent rapids. Buck fought the current, dragging Thornton to safety despite broken ribs.

But his greatest trial came in Dawson, where a boast turned wager: could Buck pull a sled laden with a thousand pounds? The stakes were high—Thornton’s pride, their fortune. Men laughed, doubting. Yet when Thornton knelt, whispering *“As you love me,”* Buck answered with a pressure of teeth against his hand—not in warning, but promise.

Harnessed, he strained. The sled groaned, ice cracking beneath the runners. The crowd fell silent. Then, with a surge, Buck moved it—not just breaking free, but hauling it a hundred yards. Cheers erupted. Thornton, breathless, stumbled forward as Buck returned to him, panting, triumphant.

In that moment, Buck was more than a dog. He was force, loyalty, love incarnate. And though the wild still called to him, its voice grew dim beside the one thing that bound him to the world of men—John Thornton.

Buck had earned Thornton his fortune—sixteen hundred dollars in five wild minutes—and with it came freedom from debt and the chance to chase a legend. The Lost Cabin mine whispered its secrets through the wind, a tale of gold and graves. Many had sought it; few returned. Now Thornton, with Pete and Hans, turned their sleds eastward, where the map ended and the wilderness began.

Seventy miles up the Yukon, then left into the Stewart River, they pushed until the land shrugged them into silence. Thornton asked little of the world: a rifle, salt, and time. Like the wolves, he hunted as he traveled, patient as the slow turn of seasons. Buck reveled in it—the endless wandering, the scent of game thick in the air, the crunch of snow giving way to the softness of spring earth.

They crossed divides where blizzards howled in July, climbed peaks that scraped the midnight sun, and waded through valleys humming with gnats. Once, they found a blazed trail, ancient and purposeless. Another time, a rotted lodge, its only relic a rusted flintlock. The land gave no answers, only echoes.

Then came the strike—not the Lost Cabin, but a placer vein glowing like butter in the pan. Gold piled high, sack after sack, while Buck lounged by the fire, dreaming of the hairy man from his visions. Fear haunted those dreams: the man slept restless, ears pricked for danger, fingers clenched around his spear. Together, they had crept through primordial forests, noses to the wind.

Now the call came louder. Not memory, not dream—a howl, slicing through the dark. Buck lunged toward it, past the camp, into the trees. There, on a moonlit rise, stood a timber wolf, lean and wild. They circled, sniffed, and then ran shoulder-to-shoulder, the wolf leading him deeper into the unknown. For hours they raced, until Buck hesitated. *Thornton.* He turned back. The wolf’s mournful cry followed him home.

Two days later, the call dragged him out again. This time, he did not return for weeks. He hunted like a shadow—stalked ptarmigan, stole salmon from bears, fought a moose bull for four grueling days until the beast faltered. As the kill cooled, Buck felt the land shift. Birds chattered warnings; the wind carried a metallic tang. Something was wrong.

He ran for camp, faster than he’d ever run before. The forest blurred. His paws knew the way.

Then—smoke. Gunpowder. Blood.

The call had been a warning.
