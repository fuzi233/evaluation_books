Sally surveyed the long table with a look of pure contentment. Finally, happiness had arrived. The room buzzed with chatter and laughter; her party, which had stumbled toward existence with shaky legs, was clearly blossoming into the triumphant event she had envisioned. The initial tension—the stiff, uncomfortable silence born of her brother Fillmore's dazzling white evening waistcoat—had evaporated. The transient and permanent residents of Mrs. Meecher's select boarding house were finally relaxing into their natural selves.

At the head of the table, the conversation had inevitably drifted back to the Great Vital Topic: Sally's inheritance and what she intended to do with it. Nothing quite satisfies the soul like possessing one's own money, but the next best thing is dictating how someone else spends theirs. Consequently, Sally's guests were deriving immense pleasure from drafting a budget for her. Since rumors suggested the sum was staggering, their proposals were correspondingly grandiose.

"Let me tell you," declared Augustus Bartlett, speaking with brisk efficiency, "exactly what I'd do if I were you." Augustus, occupying a deeply subordinate role within the prestigious firm of Kahn, Morris & Brown, always adopted a clipped, authoritative tone suitable for a man constantly rubbing shoulders with financial titans. "I'd invest two hundred thousand in a solid, safe bond issue—we just released one you should seriously consider—and use the remainder for speculative fun. By 'fun,' I mean placing bets on promising ventures. Multiple Steel is looking excellent; insiders claim shares will hit one hundred and fifty by next Saturday."

Elsa Doland, the lovely brunette with large, expressive eyes seated to Augustus's left, disagreed entirely.

"Buy a theater, Sally, and mount a truly magnificent production," she urged.

"And lose every bean you possess," countered a mild young man with a resonant baritone across the table. "If I held a few hundred thousand," the mild young man continued, "I'd wager every cent on Benny Whistler for the heavyweight title. I have confidential intelligence that Battling Tuke has been approached and intends to retire in the seventh round..."

"Listen here," interjected another voice, "let me explain what I'd do with four hundred thousand..."

"If I possessed four hundred thousand," Elsa Doland chimed in, "there is precisely one thing I would do first."

"What is that?" Sally asked.

"Settle my bill from last week, which was due this morning."

Sally sprang up instantly. Flitting down the length of the table, she draped an arm around her friend's shoulder and whispered urgently, "Elsa darling, are you actually broke? If you are, you know I'll—"

Elsa laughed brightly. "You're an angel, Sally. No one compares to you. You'd give your last penny to anyone. Of course, I'm not broke. I just returned from the road and have saved a fortune. I merely said that to test you."

Relieved, Sally returned to her seat. She noticed the company had split into two distinct factions. The conservative wing, led by Augustus Bartlett, had unanimously voted for three hundred thousand in Liberty Bonds and the rest in secure real estate. Meanwhile, the more adventurous group, swayed by the mild young man's insider tips, had already allocated Sally's funds to Benny Whistler, distributing the money cautiously in small increments to avoid inflating the odds. Given Mr. Tuke's formidable reputation among the initiated, the mild young man was confident that a cunning approach could yield three-to-one returns. It struck Sally that it was time to correct these misconceptions.

"I don't know where you're getting your figures," she announced firmly, "but I'm afraid they're incorrect. I only have twenty-five thousand dollars."

The announcement sent a chill through the room. To these masters of millions, the figure seemed laughably small, akin to an allowance for a carriage ride. Yet, they adjusted their mental gears. After all, even a pittance like twenty-five thousand could be invested wisely.

"If I had twenty-five thousand," Augustus Bartlett rallied first, "I'd buy Amalgamated..."

"If I had twenty-five thousand..." Elsa Doland began.

"If I'd had twenty-five thousand in the year eighteen hundred," observed a gloomy man with spectacles, "I could have sparked a revolution in Paraguay."

He brooded somberly on alternate histories.

"Well, I'll tell you exactly what I intend to do," Sally declared. "I'm starting with a trip to Europe—France, specifically. I've heard wonderful things about France; I'll get my passport as soon as possible, loaf around for a few weeks, then return to find a cozy little business to invest in and sustain my luxury. Any objections?"

"Even a couple of thousand on Benny Whistler..." the mild young man persisted.

"I don't want your Benny Whistler," Sally replied sharply. "I wouldn't have him even if you handed him to me. If I wish to lose money, I'll travel to Monte Carlo and do it properly."

"Monte Carlo!" the gloomy man exclaimed, lighting up at the mention of the famous casino. "I was there in '97, and if I'd had another fifty dollars... just fifty... I would have—"

At the far end of the table, a cough echoed, followed by the screech of a chair leg. Slowly, with the effortless grace characteristic of actors of the old school, Mr. Maxwell Faucitt, the boarding house's longest-resident patron, rose to his feet.

"Ladies," Mr. Faucitt bowed politely, "...and gentlemen." He paused, lifting his gaze beneath his white, venerable brows to cast a silencing glare at several restless young males, "...I feel unable to let this occasion pass without offering a few words."

His audience showed no surprise. Life in a metropolis like New York was always prone to dramatic incidents, and perhaps someday Mr. Faucitt might feel capable of remaining silent, but not today. From the moment the meal began, everyone knew expecting the old gentleman to refrain from speaking at Sally Nicholas's farewell dinner was sheer madness. Having braced themselves and buoyed by the generosity of Miss Nicholas's hospitality, they settled in with equanimity. An attempt by the Marvellous Murphys—a new arrival duo performing acrobatics—to heckle the speaker collapsed instantly under Sally's icy stare. Though their acquaintance was brief, both lissome young men admired Sally immensely.

Their admiration was well-placed. Hard to find anyone who wouldn't be drawn to Sally. She was a petite, trim wisp of a girl with tiny hands and feet, a smile as warm as the sun, and a dimple that danced across her rounded chin. When she laughed—which was frequently—her eyes vanished, replaced by a bright hazel glow. Her hair was a soft cascade of brown. Above all, she possessed an air of distinction absent in many of Mrs. Meecher's guests, carrying youth like a banner. The Marvellous Murphys, in approving of her, had certainly not failed their high critical standards.

"I have been asked," Mr. Faucitt continued, "though I acknowledge others here are far more worthy of such a task—Brutus-like figures against whom I, like Marc Antony, am no orator—I have been requested to propose a toast to the health of our charming hostess (applause), alongside the name of her brother, our old friend, Fillmore Nicholas."

The man addressed, sitting at the speaker's end, acknowledged the tribute with a brief, condescending nod. It was the nod of someone surrounded by social inferiors yet striving to remain benevolent. Seeing this, Sally momentarily debated hurling an orange at her brother. One lay conveniently near her hand, and his glistening shirt-front presented a perfect target. However, she restrained herself. If a hostess succumbs to primitive impulses, chaos ensues. She had just subdued the rebellious Murphys; if she began tossing fruit, her moral authority would crumble.

She leaned back with a sigh. The temptation had been fierce. Disliking pomposity as a democratic girl, she couldn't hide the fact that since inheriting his fortune months prior, her brother Fillmore had become unbearable. Wealth hadn't improved him; if anything, it had made him insufferably arrogant. He viewed himself as a Man of Destiny. Conversing with him felt like an audience with a standoffish monarch. It had taken Sally an hour to convince him to leave his Riverside Drive apartment and return to the boarding house. Upon arrival, clad in flawless evening wear, he made the rest of the party resemble a gathering of ragged cyclists. His white waistcoat alone served as a silent indictment of honest poverty, causing awkward stiffness throughout the soup and fish courses. Most guests remembered Fillmore Nicholas as an impoverished youth who stretched a tweed suit to its absolute limit; they had called him "Fill" and lent him money during lean times. Tonight, however, they eyed the waistcoat in stunned silence, shrinking back in embarrassment.

"Speaking," Mr. Faucitt intoned, "as an Englishman—for though I have long since acquired my papers, I first visited this great nation as a subject of the island kingdom—I may say that the two aspects of American life that have left the deepest impression on me are the lavishness of American hospitality and the charm of the American girl. Tonight, we witnessed the American girl as hostess, and I assert, solemnly, that this has been a night none of us present will ever forget. Miss Nicholas has bestowed upon us a banquet.
