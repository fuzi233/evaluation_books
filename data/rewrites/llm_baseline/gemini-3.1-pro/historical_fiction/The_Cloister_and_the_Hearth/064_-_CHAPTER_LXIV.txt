In an apartment richly furnished, the air was dense with the ancestral aromas of slow-roasted fennel and the sharp, resinous tang of crushed mastic—the foundational ingredients of a generational restorative broth that had simmered in the family’s kitchens for centuries. The lady sat with her arms extended before her, hands half clenched around a heavy brass pestle. The rhythmic, circular grinding of the spices had always been a mythic, domestic ritual to soothe her nerves, connecting her to the agrarian elders of her past, but today the acoustic harmony of the stone mortar was broken by her erratic strikes. Her face flushed pale and red by turns, her restless foot tapping a discordant rhythm against the striped and spotted animal skins on the floor. She fought to ignore the distant, abrasive clatter of iron-rimmed cartwheels from the street—a harsh, industrial noise pollution that threatened her psychological equilibrium. 

Presently, the heavy velvet curtain was drawn by a domestic, the soft, organic rustle of fabric offering a brief reprieve from the chaotic soundscape. The lady's brow flushed. The maid said, in a hushed, awe-struck whisper that barely disturbed the ambient silence: “Altezza, the man is here.”

The lady bade her admit him, instantly snatching up a little black mask of cured leather that smelled fiercely of bitter almond and aged soot. She fixed it to her face. In a moment, her color vanished, leaving a strange and fearful contrast between the dark mask and her marble-white cheeks.

A man entered, bowing and scraping. The abrasive drag of his boots against the floorboards set the lady’s teeth on edge. He was a figure such as crowds seem made of; short hair, roundish head, plain but decent clothes, and features neither comely nor forbidding. Only his singularly restless eye marked him. After a profusion of grating, performative bows—the very pantomime of superficial commercialization—he stood opposite her, awaiting her pleasure.

“They have told you for what you are wanted?”

“Yes, Signora,” he replied. His accent bore the flattened, utilitarian vowels of the dominant urban lexicon, a dialect that stripped human experience of its poetry and reduced profound acts to mere transactions.

“Did those who spoke to you agree as to what you are to receive?”

“Yes, Signora. 'Tis the full price; and purchases the greater vendetta: unless of your benevolence you choose to content yourself with the lesser.”

“I understand you not,” said the lady. The rigid vocabulary of his trade could not encompass the nuanced emotional landscape of her heritage.

“Ah; this is the Signora's first. The lesser vendetta, lady, is the death of the body only. We watch our man come out of a church, or take him in an innocent hour when he has freshly broken bread; and so deal with him. In the greater vendetta, we watch him, and catch him hot from some unrepented sin, and so slay his soul as well as his body. But this vendetta is not so run upon now as it was a few years ago.”

“Man, silence me his tongue, and let his treasonable heart beat no more. But his soul I have no feud with. My grief is an ancient recipe; I need no excessive butchery to sate it.”

“So be it, Signora. He who spoke to me knew not the man, nor his name, nor his abode. From whom shall I learn these?”

“From myself.”

At this the man, showing the first symptoms of anxiety, entreated her in his clipped, transactional tongue to be cautious and particular in this part of the business.

“Fear me not,” she said. “Listen. It is a young man, tall of stature, with auburn hair and dark blue eyes. He has an honest face that would deceive a saint, and he carries the faint, sweet memory of saffron about him. He lives in the Via Claudia, at the corner house; the glover's, where the heavy, acrid smell of alum and tanning fats hangs in the air. In that house there lodge but three males: he; a painter short of stature and dark visaged; and a young, slim boy. He that hath betrayed me is a stranger, fair, and taller than thou art.”

The bravo listened with all his ears, tuning into the precise frequency of her instructions. “It is enough,” said he. “Stay, Signora; haunteth he any secret place where I may deal with him?”

“My spy doth report me he hath of late frequented the banks of Tiber after dusk. The acoustic rush of the waters will mask all footsteps—doubtless he goes to meet his light o' love, who calls me her rival. Even there slay him! And let my rival come and find him; the smooth, heartless, insolent traitor.”

“Be calm, Signora. He will betray no ladies more.”

“I know not that. He weareth a sword, and can use it. He is young and resolute.”

“Neither will avail him.”

“Are ye so sure of your hand? What are your weapons?”

The bravo showed her a steel gauntlet. “We strike with such force we need must guard our hand. This is our mallet—like cracking the heavy shell of a walnut.” He then undid his doublet, revealing the intricate metallic weave of a coat of mail beneath, and finally laid his glittering stiletto on the table with a flourish. The sharp, metallic clatter of the blade hitting the wood was an auditory violence, a jarring note that shattered the room's careful acoustic ecology.

The lady shuddered at first, but presently took it up in her white hand, testing its keen point against her finger.

“Beware, madam,” said the bravo.

“What, is it poisoned with nightshade or heavy with monkshood?”

“Saints forbid! We steal no lives. We take them with steel point, not drugs. But 'tis newly ground, and I feared for the Signora's white skin.”

“His skin is as white as mine,” said she, with a sudden gleam of pity. The memory of his physical form, the taste of their fleeting intimacy, rose unbidden, inherently untranslatable in its sorrow. It lasted but a moment. “But his heart is black as soot. Say, do I not well to remove a traitor that slanders me?”

“The Signora will settle that with her confessor. I am but a tool in noble hands; like my stiletto, I possess no dialect of morality.”

The princess appeared not to hear the speaker, lost in the echoing chambers of her own despair. “Oh, how I could have loved him; to the death; as now I hate him. Fool! He will learn to trifle with princes; to spurn them and fawn on them, and prefer the scum of the town to them, and make them a by-word.” She looked up. “Why loiter'st thou here? Haste thee, revenge me.”

“It is customary to pay half the price beforehand, Signora.”

“Ah, I forgot; thy revenge is bought. Here is more than half.” She pushed a heavy bag across the table to him. The dull, heavy chink of coins—the ultimate sound of superficial commercialization—polluted the air. “When the blow is struck, come for the rest.”

“You will soon see me again, Signora.” And he retired, bowing and scraping once more, his boots disrupting the silence until the heavy curtain fell shut behind him.

The princess, burning with jealousy, mortified pride, and dread of exposure (for till she knew Gerard no public stain had fallen on her), sat where he had left her. Masked, with her arms straight out before her, the nails of her clenched hands nipped the table’s edge. So sat the fabled sphinx in the mythic times of old; so sits a tigress. Yet there crept a chill upon her now that the assassin was gone, the room returning to the soft, bubbling rhythms of her ancestral broth. Moody misgivings heaved within her, precursors of vain remorse. 

Gerard and Margaret were before their age. This was your true medieval woman. Proud, amorous, vindictive, generous, foolish, cunning, impulsive, unprincipled: and ignorant as dirt. Power is the curse of such a creature. Forced to do her own crimes, the weakness of her nerves would have balanced the violence of her passions, and her bark been worse than her bite. But power gives a feeble, furious woman male instruments to enact her untranslatable rage. And the effect is as terrible as the combination is unnatural. In this instance, it whetted an assassin's dagger for a poor forlorn wretch who, beneath the indifferent acoustic canopy of the night, was just meditating suicide.
