The scent of saltwater and seaweed filled the air as the villagers of a small coastal town near Naples and Rome gathered on the beach, their eyes fixed on a ship struggling against the relentless wind. At times, it seemed as if the vessel would ride out the storm, and the crowd erupted in cheers, only to be silenced by the next wave crashing against the hull, sending the ship perilously close to the shore. The onlookers exchanged knowing glances, a mix of concern and morbid fascination etched on their faces.

As the Latin proverb went: "Non quia vexari quemquam est jucunda voluptas / Sed quibus ipse malis careas quia cernere suave est." The poor ship, though not designed for the open sea, was surprisingly well-suited for running aground, its high stern catching the wind and its lines resembling a cocked hat turned upside down. To those on the beach, the ship seemed alive, its wooden frame straining against the elements with a pitiful cry.

If the spectators had been able to board the vessel, they would have discovered a different reality – a cacophony of prayers and curses, the sailors stumbling about the deck as they clung to the ropes for dear life. The passengers huddled together around the mast, some sitting, some kneeling, others lying prostrate, their hands grasping the bulwarks as the ship pitched and rolled in the turbulent waves. Among them was a young man, Gerard, his pale cheeks and clenched lips betraying the internal struggle between fear and dignity as he clung to a shroud, wincing with each jolt.

Prayers and vows rose from the terrified crowd, each person invoking a different deity in a desperate bid for salvation. The sailors, however, called upon a single goddess, addressing her as "Queen of Heaven," "Star of the Sea," or "Mistress of the World," their faith unwavering. The landsmen, on the other hand, were polytheistic, each vowing to a different patron saint or goddess, their offerings ranging from gold to wax candles. A Genoese merchant vowed a silver collar to Our Lady of Loretto, while an English merchant promised a heap of gold to Our Lady of Walsingham. A Tuscan noble pledged ten pounds of wax lights to Our Lady of Ravenna, each person convinced that their chosen deity would intercede on their behalf.
