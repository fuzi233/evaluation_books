On a charming common near Tunbridge Wells, Lady Clavering had installed herself in a delightful villa—a retreat necessitated by certain *disagreements* with her husband at the close of an unfortunate London season. Naturally, Miss Amory accompanied her mother, and Master Clavering, home for the holidays, provided Blanche with her chief occupation: quarreling. Not that the boy minded much—home amusements were dull compared to cricket, horses, and the society of other thirteen-year-old gentlemen, who descended upon the Begum’s villa in alarming numbers. They devoured tarts, overindulged in champagne, raced imaginary steeds across the lawn (to the horror of their hostess), and occasionally smoked themselves into queasy disgrace, rendering the dining-room uninhabitable for Blanche.

Now, Blanche, being a creature of refined sensibilities, found the company of young gentlemen who still considered belching an art form rather tiresome. Change, however, was her delight—poverty, a cottage, even a night in a dungeon with only bread and water would have charmed her for a week, provided it was *novel*. Tunbridge, therefore, suited her well enough. She wandered the woods, sketching trees with more enthusiasm than accuracy. She devoured French novels. She attended every play, ball, or traveling conjurer Tunbridge Wells offered. She slept excessively. She quarreled with her mother and Frank each morning before seeking fresh diversion—sometimes the village school, where she alternated between smothering the girls with affection and undermining the poor schoolmistress, then scolding both for their deficiencies.

And, of course, she went to church—a darling little Anglo-Norman *bijou*, built approximately yesterday and lavishly adorned with painted windows, gilded scripture, and saints’ heads that looked suspiciously like the local squire’s relatives. Here, Blanche set to work on an exquisitely high-church altar cover, securing for herself a fleeting reputation as a saint. The clergyman, poor man, was thoroughly taken in—at first.

This clergyman was none other than our old friend Smirke, once tutor to Pen and hopeless admirer of Helen. Since those days, he had consoled himself with a bride from Clapham (selected by his late mother) and developed ecclesiastical tendencies that would have horrified her. Collars were shorn, hair grew apostolically long, neckties abandoned. He fasted on Fridays. He read Roman Hours. He *hinted* at hearing confessions. In short, he was the most harmless of men, yet Muffin of the Dissenting chapel declared him a Jesuit in disguise, while Mr. Simeon Knight at the old church denounced him as a Papist infiltrator.

Smirke had built his chapel-of-ease with his mother’s legacy. One shudders to imagine her ghostly outrage at the candlesticks now gracing the *altar* (formerly a table), or the letters he signed on the Feast of Saint Whoever. Yet none of this was done with malice—merely earnest enthusiasm.

Then came Blanche.

For two blissful weeks, Smirke was convinced she was an angel. Her singing! Her piety! Her *soul*! His wife, Belinda, paced the churchyard, eyeing the two vacant plots beside their children’s graves and contemplating a third—or perhaps a nunnery. The parishioners whispered of Romish plots. Blanche, meanwhile, poured on the charm like syrup—then, abruptly, lost interest. She rapped a child’s knuckles. She yawned during sermons. Smirke, bewildered, concluded she was perhaps *not* a celestial being after all. Belinda rejoiced. Blanche forgot them both entirely.

Enter Arthur Pendennis.

Smirke, still nursing bruised ideals, gushed about Blanche’s *precious soul* and *heavenly music*. Arthur, amused, observed his old tutor’s transformation—the long hair, the ecclesial airs, the genuine kindness beneath the absurdity. And Blanche? Blanche was delighted to have a visitor who brought London gossip and didn’t reek of tarts. She laughed at his jokes. She sang his verses. She sighed over moonlit gardens.

They walked. They reminisced. They discussed Laura (*dearest Laura!*). Blanche was all sentiment and sisterly affection—until, of course, she wasn’t.

One evening, under a particularly poetic moon, Arthur smoked at his window. From a nearby casement, Blanche’s voice sighed, *“Oh, heavenly!”*

Pen laughed. “Don’t report me for smoking.”

“I adore it!” she declared, then—remembering *les moeurs*—retreated with a flutter.

The next day, they rambled through Penshurst’s park, Blanche’s laughter ringing like chapel bells. Smirke, watching her instruct village children, marveled at her patience. Arthur marveled too.

“Do you truly love the country?” he asked.

“I never wish to see London again,” she sighed. “Here, one’s thoughts bloom like wildflowers—you know, the kind that *die* in London.”

“Then why sigh?”

“Because now that you’re here, I shall miss you when you leave.”

Cue *Mes Larmes*.

Ah, the rural idyll! Two jaded London souls, playing at Phyllis and Corydon among the roses, each convinced—for a moment—that the other was the romance they’d been missing. How many marriages, one wonders, begin in such fleeting folly?
