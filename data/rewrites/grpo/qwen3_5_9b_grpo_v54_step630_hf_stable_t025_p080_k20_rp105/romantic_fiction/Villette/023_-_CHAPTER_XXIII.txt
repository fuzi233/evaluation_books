The sadness that had settled over me was lifted, not by a gradual fading, but by a sudden, violent intrusion of a new influence. Imagine a secluded dell, deep and hidden in the forest's secrecy, lying perpetually in dimness and mist. Its turf is damp, its grass pale and humid, a place where light dares not tread. But imagine a storm tearing through the oak trees, or an axe felling them, creating a wide gap. Suddenly, the breeze sweeps in; the sun looks down. That sad, cold hollow transforms into a deep cup of lustre, pouring out the blue glory and golden light of high summer from a sky it had never known.

So, a new creed became mine: a belief in happiness.

It had been three weeks since the adventure in the garret. In that cramped room, within a box, drawer, and casket upstairs, I now possessed four companions like the first letter, traced by the same firm pen and sealed with the same clear wax. They were full of the same vital comfort. To me, then, they seemed the very juice of a divine vintage, a draught that the gods themselves would approve. I read them in later years, and time had mellowed them into a mild beverage, but when I first tasted that elixir, fresh from the honored fount, it was the nectar of Hebe.

Did I answer these letters under the dry check of Reason or the full impulse of Feeling? Truth be told, I compromised. I served two masters. I bowed in the houses of Rimmon, yet lifted my heart at another shrine. I wrote two answers to each letter: one for my own relief, the other for Graham's perusal.

First, Feeling and I turned Reason out of doors, barred her in, and sat down to pour out our hearts with deep enjoyment. We filled two sheets with the language of a strongly adherent affection, a rooted gratitude. Once, for the record, I disclaim with scorn any "warmer feelings" of a romantic nature. Women do not entertain such illusions where, from the start, they know that to do so would be a mortal absurdity. Nobody launches into love unless they have seen the rising of Hope's star. When I had expressed this closely clinging, deeply honouring attachment—one that wanted to absorb all the storms and lightnings from its object's destiny—just then, the doors of my heart shook. The bolts yielded. Reason leaped in, vigorous and revengeful. She snatched the sheets, sneered, erased, tore, rewrote, folded, sealed, and directed a terse, curt missive of a single page. She did right.

I did not live on letters alone. I was visited, looked after. Once a week I was taken to La Terrasse; always I was made much of. Dr. Bretton failed not to tell me why: "To keep away the nun," he said. He was determined to dispute with her for her prey. He had taken a thorough dislike to her, chiefly on account of that white face-cloth and those cold grey eyes. The moment he heard of those odious particulars, consummate disgust had incited him to oppose her. He wished she would look in upon him when he was present, but she never did. In short, he regarded me scientifically as a patient, exercising his professional skill and natural benevolence with a course of cordial treatment.

One evening, the first in December, I walked alone in the carré. It was six o'clock; the class doors were closed, but within, the pupils were counterfeiting a miniature chaos. The carré was dark, save for a red light under the stove. The wide glass doors and long windows were frosted over, a blanched winter veil spangled with a crystal sparkle of starlight. To dare remain alone in such darkness showed my nerves were regaining a healthy tone. I thought of the nun, but hardly feared her, though the staircase was behind me, leading up through blind, black night to the haunted grenier. Yet, my heart quaked, my pulse leaped, when I suddenly heard breathing and rustling. Turning, I saw in the deep shadow of the steps a deeper shadow—a shape moving and descending. It paused at the class door, then glided before me. Simultaneously came the clangor of the distant door-bell. Life-like sounds bring life-like feelings; this shape was too round and low for my gaunt nun. It was only Madame Beck on duty.

"Mademoiselle Lucy!" cried Rosine, bursting in with a lamp from the corridor, "on est là pour vous au salon."

Madame saw me, I saw Madame, Rosine saw us both; there was no mutual recognition. I made straight for the salon. There I found what I anticipated: Dr. Bretton, but in evening dress.

"The carriage is at the door," he said. "My mother has sent it to take you to the theatre. She was going herself, but an arrival prevented her. She immediately said, 'Take Lucy in my place.' Will you go?"

"Just now? I am not dressed," I cried, glancing despairingly at my dark merino.

"You have half an hour to dress. I should have given you notice, but I only determined on going since five o'clock, when I heard there was to be a genuine regale in the presence of a great actress."

He mentioned a name that thrilled me—a name that, in those days, could thrill Europe. It is hushed now; its once restless echoes are still. She who bore it went years ago to her rest, but *then* her day stood at its full height, light and fervour.

"I'll go; I will be ready in ten minutes," I vowed. Away I flew, never checked by the thought that perhaps at this moment checks you: namely, that to go anywhere with Graham and without Mrs. Bretton could be objectionable. I could not have conceived, much less expressed to Graham, such a scruple without risking a tyrannous self-contempt, an inward fire of shame so devouring it would have licked up the life in my veins. Besides, my godmother, knowing her son and knowing me, would as soon have thought of chaperoning a sister with a brother as of keeping anxious guard over our movements.

The present required no showy array; my dun mist crape would suffice. I sought it in the great oak wardrobe in the dormitory, where hung no less than forty dresses. But there had been changes; an innovating hand had pruned this crowded wardrobe, carrying divers garments to the grenier—my crape amongst the rest. I must fetch it. I got the key and went aloft fearless, almost thoughtless. I unlocked the door, plunged in. The reader may believe it or not, but when I thus suddenly entered, that garret was not wholly dark: from one point there shone a solemn light, like a star but broader. So plainly it shone that it revealed the deep alcove with a portion of the tarnished scarlet curtain drawn over it. Instantly, silently, before my eyes, it vanished; so did the curtain and alcove. All that end of the garret became black as night. I ventured no research; I had not time nor will. Snatching my dress, which hung on the wall near the door, I rushed out, relocked the door with convulsed haste, and darted downwards.

But I trembled too much to dress myself. Impossible to arrange hair or fasten hooks-and-eyes with such fingers, so I called Rosine and bribed her. Rosine liked a bribe, so she did her best, smoothing and plaiting my hair as well as a coiffeur, placing the lace collar mathematically straight, tying the neck-ribbon accurately. Having given me my handkerchief and gloves, she took the candle and lighted me down-stairs. After all, I had forgotten my shawl; she ran back to fetch it. I stood with Dr. John in the vestibule, waiting.

"What is this, Lucy?" he said, looking down at me narrowly. "Here is the old excitement. Ha! the nun again?"

I utterly denied the charge, vexed to be suspected of a second illusion. He was sceptical.

"She has been, as sure as I live," he said. "Her figure crossing your eyes leaves on them a peculiar gleam and expression not to be mistaken."

"She has *not* been," I persisted. Indeed, I could deny her apparition with truth.

"The old symptoms are there," he affirmed. "A particular pale, and what the Scotch call a 'raised' look."

He was so obstinate I thought it better to tell him what I really *had* seen. Of course, with him, it was held to be another effect of the same cause: optical illusion, nervous malady. Not one bit did I believe him, but I dared not contradict; doctors are so self-opinionated, so immovable in their dry, materialist views.

Rosine brought the shawl, and I was bundled into the carriage.

The theatre was full—crammed to its roof. Royal and noble were there; palace and hotel had emptied their inmates into those tiers so thronged and hushed. Deeply did I feel myself privileged in having a place before that stage. I longed to see a being of whose powers I had heard reports making peculiar anticipations. I wondered if she would justify her renown. With strange curiosity, with feelings severe and austere yet of riveted interest, I waited.

She rose at nine that December night. Above the horizon I saw her come. She could shine yet with pale grandeur and steady might, but that star verged already on its judgment-day. Seen near, it was a chaos—hollow, half-consumed: an orb perished or perishing—half lava, half glow.

I had heard this woman termed "plain," and I expected bony harshness and grimness—something large, angular, sallow. What I saw was the shadow of a royal Vashti: a queen, fair as the day once, turned pale now like twilight, and wasted like wax in flame.

For a while, I thought it was only a unique woman who moved in might and grace before this multitude. By-and-by I recognised my mistake. Behold! I found upon her something neither of woman nor of man: in each of her eyes sat a devil. These evil forces bore her through the tragedy, kept up her feeble strength—for she was but a frail creature. As the action rose and the stir deepened, how wildly they shook her with their passions of the pit! They wrote HELL on her straight, haughty brow. They tuned her voice to the note of torment. They writhed her regal face to a demoniac mask. Hate and Murder and Madness incarnate she stood.

It was a marvellous sight: a mighty revelation. A spectacle low, horrible, immoral. Swordsmen thrust through, dying in their blood on the arena sand; bulls goring horses disembowelled made a meeker vision for the public—a milder condiment for a people's palate—than Vashti torn by seven devils: devils which cried sore and rent the tenement they haunted, but still refused to be exorcised.

Suffering had struck that stage empress. She stood before her audience neither yielding to, nor enduring, nor, in finite measure, resenting it. She stood locked in struggle, rigid in resistance. She stood, not dressed, but draped in pale antique folds, long and regular like sculpture. A background and entourage of deepest crimson threw her out, white like alabaster—like silver: rather, be it said, like Death.

Where was the artist of the Cleopatra? Let him come and study this different vision. Let him seek here the mighty brawn, the muscle, the abounding blood, the full-fed flesh he worshipped.

I have said that she does not *resent* her grief. No; the weakness of that word would make it a lie. To her, what hurts becomes immediately embodied: she looks on it as a thing that can be attacked, worried down, torn in shreds. Scarcely a substance herself, she grapples to conflict with abstractions. Before calamity she is a tigress; she rends her woes, shivers them in convulsed abhorrence. Pain, for her, has no result in good: tears water no harvest of wisdom. On sickness, on death itself, she looks with the eye of a rebel. Wicked, perhaps, she is, but also she is strong; and her strength has conquered Beauty, has overcome Grace, and bound both at her side, captives peerlessly fair and docile. Even in the uttermost frenzy of energy, each maenad movement is royally, imperially borne. Her hair, flying loose in revel or war, is still an angel's hair, glorious under a halo. Fallen, insurgent, banished, she remembers the heaven where she rebelled. Heaven's light, following her exile, pierces its confines, disclosing their forlorn remoteness.

Place now the Cleopatra, or any other slug, before her as an obstacle, and see her cut through the pulpy mass as the scimitar of Saladin clove the down cushion. Let Paul Peter Rubens wake from the dead, let him rise out of his cerements, and bring into this presence all the army of his fat women; the magian power gifting that slight rod of Moses could, at one waft, release and re-mingle a sea spell-parted, whelming the heavy host with the down-rush of overthrown sea-ramparts.

Vashti was not good, I was told; and I have said she did not look good: though a spirit, she was a spirit out of Tophet. Well, if so much unholy force can arise from below, may not an equal efflux of sacred essence descend one day from above?

What thought Dr. Graham of this being?

For long intervals I forgot to look how he demeaned himself, or to question what he thought. The strong magnetism of genius drew my heart out of its wonted orbit; the sunflower turned from the south to a fierce light, not solar—a rushing, red, cometary light—hot on vision and to sensation.
