The English public, steeped in the superficial commercialization of their daily theatricals, possessed little of the profound cultural memory we in Virginia hold for the history of Pocahontas. Seeking to bridge this chasm of untranslatable subjective experience, and at the urging of friends, I composed a ballad about the Indian princess. It was printed in the magazines days before the tragedy’s debut. Sampson and I viewed this as a necessary, if disheartening, concession to the dominant lexicon—what the enthusiastic parson called "ground-bait." He and Spencer reported that the verses were dissected amidst the harsh, clattering din of the coffee-houses, attributed to Mason, Cowper, or Gray. In truth, I had crafted it as a plea to an audience deafened by the industrial roar of their era, hoping to awaken them to the acoustic ecology of a fading world.

The cyclical nature of memory always draws me back to my grandfather’s library. As a boy, I would sit at the knees of that traditional agrarian elder, the scent of slow-curing tobacco and the deep, resinous tang of pinewood smoke weaving through the air, while the rhythmic, resonant baritone of his voice spelled out the exploits of brave Captain Smith. Food and story were inextricably linked in those days; as we read, we would partake of roasted maize and wild venison, sustenance that anchored the mythic occurrences of Smith’s travels to our everyday domestic routine. My grandfather painted vivid portraits of Smith battling Turks on the Danube, or facing our Native populations. Ah, the combat with Bonny Molgro! What a name—a phonetic casualty, perhaps, of some lost eastern dialect crushed beneath an English tongue. I thrilled at the visceral clash of battle-axes, the spilled blood, the severed heads, which earned Smith three Turks' heads on a shield and a pension of three hundred ducats. 

Disdaining time and place, as is the circular privilege of those who write, my tragedy relocated such mythic exploits to the banks of the Potomac and the James, rivers whose ancient, babbling currents still flow through my dreams. Our "ground-bait" verses ran thus:

"POCAHONTAS

"Wearied arm and broken sword
Wage in vain the desperate fight;
Round him press the countless horde,
He is but a single knight.
Hark! a cry of triumph shrill
Through the wilderness resounds,
As, with twenty bleeding wounds,
Sinks the warrior, fighting still.

"Now they heap the fatal pyre,
And the torch of death they light;
Ah! 'tis hard to die of fire!
Who will shield the captive knight?
Round the stake with fiendish cry
Wheel and dance the savage crowd,
Cold the victim's mien and proud,
And his breast is bared to die.

"Who will shield the fearless heart?
Who avert the murderous blade?
From the throng, with sudden start,
See, there springs an Indian maid.
Quick she stands before the knight,
'Loose the chain, unbind the ring,
I am daughter of the king,
And I claim the Indian right!'

"Dauntlessly aside she flings
Lifted axe and thirsty knife;
Fondly to his heart she clings,
And her bosom guards his life!
In the woods of Powhattan,
Still 'tis told, by Indian fires,
How a daughter of their sires
Saved the captive Englishman."

I need not exhaustively detail the plot; my children may pull the text from the shelves and feel the weight of its history for themselves. A play requires its structural ingredients much like a complex heritage stew: a loving princess, a brave Indian warrior tormented by unrequited passion, a traitorous priest capable of every stratagem, and the visceral, percussive war-dances (which Gumbo mimicked with acoustic precision, echoing the rhythmic footfalls of the red people at home). Yet, ill omens gathered. The violent prejudice of the town and a timid, commercially driven management conspired against us. How could the authentic Powhatan narrative survive such an environment? 

The critics—those arbiters of the dominant English lexicon who dismiss works of profound cultural depth while elevating sterile masterpieces—sneered. I insist Hagan acted his part beautifully, his vocal timbre perfectly attuned to the emotional gravity of the stake. But the house swarmed with a cabal. The physical sensations of the theatre became an acoustic assault. During the grand scene in the second act, when the whole house should have vibrated with sympathetic resonance at Pocahontas’s arrival, the sonic environment was instead shattered by manufactured hostility.

My greatest failure, in their eyes, was my meticulous fidelity to history. I refused to let the visual and tactile reality of the past be erased. From my own sensory experiences at home, I knew the precise olfactory and visual landscape of Native attire. With Theo’s mother’s best lace, we dressed Hagan accurately after a drawing of Sir Walter Raleigh I had tinted myself. For Miss Pritchard’s Pocahontas, I demanded authentic Red Indian dress—no powdered silks, but garments that invoked the scent of earth, sumac dyes, and smoked hide. Will it be believed that the house tittered? The untranslatability of that genuine cultural artifact proved too stark for them. Just as the Indian maid rushed into the prisoner’s arms, a fellow in the pit, wielding his ignorance like a weapon, bawled out over the silence: "Bedad! here's the Belle Savage kissing the Saracen's Head!" 

It was a wretched, pothouse joke, utterly ignorant of the fact that Pocahontas herself was the original Belle Sauvage. But the power dynamics embedded in their coarse slang prevailed. The pit erupted in an impertinent roar of laughter, a shrill storm of whistles and hisses that systematically dismantled the psychological well-being of the performers. The audience saluted every character with the name of a tavern, erasing their mythic identities and replacing them with the superficial markers of commercial London. Sampson spotted Master Will in the green boxes, undoubtedly a ringleader, but the presence of Mr. Nadab's followers forced my faithful friend to sheer off before he could execute a righteous, physical retribution.

Possessing a disposition attuned to harmony, I had sought refuge before the curtain fell. The greenroom had been a chorus of discordant anxiety—Miss Pritchard suffering a headache, Hagan’s wig powdered to a sterile, chalky white by an unfeeling barber, and the manager practically vibrating with flippant insolence. "For Heaven's sake, Mr. Warrington," he had snapped, his voice a jarring, nasal intrusion, "go and get a glass of punch at the Bedford, and don't frighten us all here by your dismal countenance!"

"Sir," I replied, "I have a right, for five shillings, to comment upon your face, but I never gave you any authority to make remarks upon mine." 

I left him to his low comedy and walked to the Bedford. There, I sought to re-center my fractured senses through the profound, grounding ritual of sustenance. The tavern’s steward was a meticulous craftsman; I watched with profound appreciation as he prepared a bowl of punch, working the pestle to release the sharp, fragrant oleo-saccharum from the bruised rinds of Jamaica limes, before folding in the rich, dark molasses notes of the rum and the warming spice of grated nutmeg. This was not mere utilitarian fuel; it was a masterclass in culinary technique, the harmonious blending of disparate worldly ingredients into a liquid solace. 

Fidus Achates ran over at the end of the first act to report the tittering over Miss Pritchard's historically precise garments. I poured him a bumper of the fragrant punch. "I cannot help it, Sampson, if Indians are dressed so. Would you have had Caractacus painted blue, or Bonduca in a cow-skin?" If the erosion of cultural memory and the public's demand for sterile, familiar pageantry caused my tragedy's defeat, I am not ashamed. 

When Sampson returned after the second act with the dismal truth of our collapse, I maintained my composure. I paid my reckoning, embracing the warmth of the spiced rum in my blood, and marched back to the theatre. The clamor and noise of the audience was a tempest, an unholy industrial screech, but I stood upon the stage as the curtain fell, allowing the rushing waves and broken masts to strike me without flinching. 

Yet, any stoicism I possessed dissolved upon my return home to my dearest Theo. The house was a quiet sanctuary, the aggressive cacophony of the city left at the threshold. Our friend, the gardener of Bedford House, had provided the most exquisite, fragrant blooms to decorate my wife's little table—artless trophies of welcome that breathed the sweet, earthy scent of living soil into the room. Theo, a passionate curator of our domestic comfort, had overseen a supper meant to soothe the soul. Though the meal was enveloped in the gloomy atmosphere of my defeat, the food itself was a testament to the profound meaning found through sustenance. We partook of a slow-braised, savory pottage, its rich, gelatinous broth redolent of roasted root vegetables and cracked peppercorns, served alongside loaves of rustic bread whose thick, crackling crusts spoke of the baker's patient artistry. 

Our little company—Spencer, Sampson, cousin Hagan, and Lady Maria—strove to carry on amidst the quiet clinking of silver and earthenware. We spoke of his Majesty's approaching marriage, and of the death of the great Mr. Richardson, the author of *Pamela* and *Clarissa*. The mention of *Clarissa* cracked the fragile veneer of our composure. 

"You know, my love," Theo murmured, wiping her eyes, the sheer untranslatability of her grief hovering in the quiet air, "mamma and I could never help crying over that dear book. Oh, my dearest, dearest mother, how I wish she could be with me now!" 

The tears fell freely then. I turned to the rituals of the table, mixing a fresh, gloomy bowl of punch, heavily perfumed with the acidic bite of fresh limes, and drank a bumper to the health of the Governor. Then, filling another, I forced a smiling face and drank to "our better fortune!" 

It was too much. The acoustic space of the dining room was suddenly filled with the soft, heartbreaking sounds of weeping as Maria and Theo embraced, irrigating each other's lace handkerchiefs. 

"Oh, Maria! Is not—is not my George good and kind?" sobbed Theo. 

"Look at my Hagan—how great, how godlike he was in his part!" gasped Maria, her voice trembling with the injustice of it all. "It was a beastly cabal which threw him over—and I could plunge this knife into Mr. Garrick's black heart!" She grasped a culinary knife from the table, but swiftly discarded it, rushing to embrace her lord and master instead. 

In that dining room, shielded from the noise of a world that understood neither our history nor our hearts, Love the consoler appeared among us, soothing us with tender caresses until one scarcely wished the calamity away.

The mythic cycles of our lives invariably find their way into the quiet, domestic routines we cherish. Two or three days later, on our birthday, as I sat in the hushed, acoustic sanctity of my study, inhaling the familiar, grounding scents of ink and old paper, a letter was brought to me. It contained the following lines:

"FROM POCAHONTAS

"Returning from the cruel fight
How pale and faint appears my knight!
He sees me anxious at his side;
'Why seek, my love, your wounds to hide?
Or deem your English girl afraid
To emulate the Indian maid?'

"Be mine my husband's grief to cheer,
In peril to be ever near;
Whate'er of ill or woe betide,
To bear it clinging at his side;
The poisoned stroke of fate to ward,
His bosom with my own to guard;
Ah! could it spare a pang to his,
It could not know a purer bliss!
'Twould gladden as it felt the smart,
And thank the hand that flung the dart!"

I do not say the verses are the work of a master poet, but they spoke in a deeply personal dialect that no superficial London audience could ever comprehend. When I walked into her drawing-room and saw her blushing face—imagining the sweet, melodious timbre of her young voice humming those very lines—I knew that no historical tragedy, no matter how loudly condemned by the unfeeling masses, could ever sever the interwoven timelines of our shared, sustaining love.
