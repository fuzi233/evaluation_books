The second Fanny laid eyes on the two ladies at the door, the younger one’s face set like a block of carved marble and the elder’s eyes wild with a look of someone who’s just found a rogue mouse eating her prized jam tarts, she knew exactly who she was looking at: Pen’s mother. The widow’s haggard, harried eyes were the spitting image of Arthur’s, the same glassy, fever-bright look he’d had thrashing around in his sickbed, yelling nonsensical college ditties at the ceiling. Fanny blinked back tears, darting a desperate pleading look past them to the Major hovering behind their skirts. Old Pendennis peeked out from under lowered lids like a mischievous old sparrow sizing up a stray crumb, not about to interfere with the maternal freight train barrelling through the hallway.

“I— I wrote you yesterday, ma’am,” Fanny stammered, shaking so hard the tray of lemonade in her hands rattled, pale as the stone-faced Laura peering over Mrs. Pendennis’s shoulder.

“Did you, madam?” Mrs. Pendennis’s voice was sharp as a pin. “You may stand down from nursing my son now. I am his mother, you understand.”

“Yes, ma’am, only— wait a second!” Fanny yelped, skittering to block her path for half a second. “I have to warn you first, he’s—”

The widow, who’d marched in looking ready for a showdown, gasped so loud she nearly choked, stumbling back a step. A full-throated shriek of delirious laughter boomed out of Pen’s open bedroom door, followed by a yell, then a slurred, off-key rendition of the worst drinking song he’d learned at Camford, him thumping the wainscot so hard the laundress’s teacup rattled in the kitchen below. The man was three sheets to the wind on fever fumes, had no idea where he was half the time.

“He doesn’t recognize anyone right now, ma’am,” Fanny whispered.

“Then he may recognize his own mother. Step aside, please.” Mrs. Pendennis shoved past her, determined as a steam engine, Laura sailing right after her without so much as a “thank you for staying up three nights to save my love” kind word, the Major trailing in their wake like a stray pup. Fanny sank onto the hard bench in the passageway and cried, so hard her bonnet lopsided, praying no one would throw her out entirely. She’d have happily died for that daft boy, and these fine ladies treated her like a stray cat that’d wandered in off the street. She sat there for what felt like three whole lifetimes, no one coming out to check on her, until Dr. Goodenough turned up for his second visit of the day, nearly tripping over her huddled on the doormat.

“Why, little nurse! How’s our patient? Got any rest into him?” the kind man asked, patting her shoulder.

“Go ask the people who stole my job,” Fanny mumbled, nodding at the closed bedroom door.

“Off to bed with you, my poor girl,” he said. “You’ll end up in a fever yourself next, mark my words.”

“Oh no, please, don’t send me away! I love him so,” Fanny wailed, dropping to her knees and clinging to his hand so tight her knuckles went white. The doctor’s spectacles fogged up entirely, he was that moved, he nearly stepped on her own boot lace.

“Nonsense, you silly thing,” he said, swiping a tear off his cheek under the pretense of adjusting his glasses. “Of course you can see him. But first, I’ve got jobs for you.”

Mrs. Pendennis meanwhile was settled like a sentry in the biggest armchair next to Pen’s bed, her watch sat on the table next to his medicine bottles, her Bible open in her lap (she never travelled an inch without it). The very first thing she’d done when she walked in was grab Fanny’s bonnet and shawl off the dresser, fling both out into the study like they were lumps of toxic waste, lock the bedroom door behind her, and stake a claim on her sick son that no one was getting past. She’d been terrified he wouldn’t know her, but the delirious Pen spotted her and beamed, waved, and rambled on like they were both back home at Fairoaks having a picnic on the lawn. Laura could hear him laughing out in the hall, and every cackle felt like a little stab to the heart— so it was true, then, he’d gotten tangled up with this servant girl, the man she’d half planned to marry was raving through his fever and probably going to die any minute, unrepentant as could be.

Everyone in that little flat was so tense the air could’ve been sliced with a butter knife, until Dr. Goodenough walked in like a ray of very practical, lancet-carrying sunshine. There’s no one in the world people cling to harder than the family doctor when someone’s deathly ill. We’ve all held our breath waiting for his carriage wheels to clatter down the street, hung on every single one of his words, nearly kissed his boots if he dared smile and say the patient might pull through. A good doctor gets treated like a temporary god of life and death, and no one dares argue with him when he says it’s time for a bleed.

After quick pleasantries with the new arrivals, he examined Pen, confirmed the fever was spiking dangerously high, told the ashen-faced mother there was no reason to panic just yet, the lad was young and strong enough to pull through, then dragged the Major off into Warrington’s empty bedroom for a private chat. The case was critical: if they didn’t bring the fever down fast, Pen would be dead before sundown. “And what about that other young woman loitering in the passage?” the Major asked, winking. “The little nurse girl. Got a glimpse of her on the way in, begad, she was uncommonly pretty.” The doctor snickered, even in the shadow of death the silliest little jokes still pop up to lighten the gloom for two seconds. He had a plan.

He headed back out to the study, scribbled two quick notes, grabbed Fanny’s abandoned bonnet and shawl, and found her still on that bench in the passage. “Quick, run this note to the surgeon on the Strand, tell him to come at once with his lancet, then pop round to my Hanover Square house, give the other note to my man Harbottle, wait there for the prescription to be made up. It’ll take him a while to mix, don’t rush.”

Poor Fanny trundled off, none the wiser, and Dr. Goodenough made sure no one would ever let her set foot back in Pen’s chambers as a nurse, what with Mrs. Pendennis ready to chase her down the street with a broom. For three days after that, you could spot her little sad face hovering around the staircase, buttonholing the apothecary’s boy, the laundress, even the doctor himself every time he left Pen’s rooms to beg for updates. On the third day, Goodenough’s fancy carriage pulled up right outside Shepherd’s Inn, and he trundled straight up to Costigan’s little garret where Fanny was lying sick in bed from all her worry, and told her the fever had broken, Pen was going to live.

Captain Costigan spotted the fancy green-liveried carriage from his window, tipped his hat so hard he nearly knocked it off his head. “Foine pair of horses that is, bedad!” he yelled at old Mr. Bows next to him. “That’s the good docthor that saved the little girl’s life, he is! I’ll salute that carriage every time I see it from now on like he’s the Lord Lieutenant himself, I will.”

Mrs. Pendennis’s gratitude to Dr. Goodenough knew no bounds. He laughed off every attempt to pay him a fee, he wouldn’t take a penny from the widow of a fellow medical man, so she decided she’d send him the pride of the Fairoaks household: a silly massive silver-gilt vase, covered in carvings of Hippocrates, Hygeia, King Bladud and a whole wreath of snakes, presented to her late husband back in Bath by Lady Elizabeth Firebrace when her son recovered from scarlet fever. It was the ugliest, most over-the-top piece of Victorian art ever made, and she thought it was worth more than the crown jewels. The only favour he asked she refused to grant: to think a single kind, charitable thought about poor Fanny. The second Goodenough even mentioned the girl’s name, Mrs. Pendennis’s sweet soft face went so cold you could chill a whole bottle of claret on it. There’s no medicine on earth, no poppy syrup, no mesmerism, no fancy new hydropathy cure, that can fix that particular streak of maternal rivalry in a lady once it’s set in, and Goodenough gave up asking before long.

People who nitpick novels and ask how on earth a tiny three-room Temple bachelor flat could fit Pen, his mom, Laura, the country maid Martha, a hospital nurse, the laundress, the Major, his valet, Pen’s errand boy and about six other hangers on need not worry their pretty little heads. Everyone in the whole of the Temple was out of town for the summer, and the second floor chambers belonging to Percy Sibwright, that foppish Camford graduate who collected shiny boots and French romance novels, were entirely empty. The laundress who worked for Sibwright was pals with Pen’s laundress, so they broke in and claimed Sibwright’s little brass chintz-curtained bedroom for Laura, who would have been the most honoured guest Sibwright could ever have dreamed of. That silly dandy had a whole museum of pomade and bear’s grease pots, a wall covered in sad pictures of fancy fictional ladies pining away, and his shelves were full of unread law books and very very read French novels. Years later, Laura would blush and laugh and admit to her husband that while she was staying there, she’d dug Percy’s legal wig out of its japanned box, plopped it on her head, and preened at his mirror for a full minute. If Percy had walked in then? He’d have written twelve sonnets on the spot, proposed that very evening, and had the wig framed as a holy relic in his drawing room. You don’t do that sort of silly prank if the person you’re caring for is at death’s door, after all— which means Pen was well on the mend.

Sure enough, the fever broke for good a day or two later. The blisters, the lancet, the bitter potions all did their work, and Pen woke up after a sixteen hour long sleep and yelled for a slice of bread and butter, so hungry he’d have fought a street urchin for a crumb. The joys of convalescent hunger are the best joys there are, no one over thirty gets that feral ravenous feeling ever again, alas. No sooner had the happy news spread than Warrington showed up, tramping all the way back from Norfolk after getting Bows’s letter about his friend’s illness, carpet bag in one hand, cigar in the other, smoke billowing out behind him like a little steam train. He let himself in with his old key, and found the Major half dozing in an armchair, Laura reading him Shakespeare in that soft sweet voice of hers. Warrington was so flustered he dropped his cigar on the doormat (it singed the edge, no one noticed), dropped his hat too, pumped the Major’s hand until his arm ached, then took Laura’s hand and stared at her like he’d just spotted an angel in the middle of his messy bachelor flat.

Just then, Pen’s bedroom door creaked open very quietly, Mrs. Pendennis poked her head out, put one finger to her lips, and shushed them all, like they were a bunch of rowdy schoolboys disturbing her convalescent prince.
