The night passed without so much as a moth’s flutter at the window; and in the morning Rosa rose as if sleep had been a cool cloth laid over a fevered brow. London, already awake, murmured beyond the panes—wheels worrying the stones, hawkers pitching their cries to the height of the houses, a hammer somewhere striking too early and too often. It was the kind of noise that did not belong to any one life, but to a thousand lives pressed together; and yet, in the rooms where she waited, there was a pocket of hush, like a cloistered garden set behind a market.

At ten, precisely as the clock gave out its strokes with a dry little authority, Mr. Grewgious arrived—and with him Mr. Crisparkle, fresh from his morning plunge in the river at Cloisterham, as if he had carried the sound of running water in his very voice.

“Miss Twinkleton was so uneasy, Miss Rosa,” he explained, warmth and apology in the same breath, “and came round to Ma and me with your note, in such a state of wonder, that, to quiet her, I volunteered on this service by the very first train to be caught in the morning. I wished at the time that you had come to me; but now I think it best that you did as you did, and came to your guardian.”

“I did think of you,” Rosa answered; “but Minor Canon Corner was so near him—”

“I understand. It was quite natural.”

“I have told Mr. Crisparkle,” said Mr. Grewgious, “all that you told me last night, my dear. Of course I should have written it to him immediately; but his coming was most opportune. And it was particularly kind of him to come, for he had but just gone.”

Rosa, who could not keep her thoughts from Helena even in the safe company of these two, brought the question back as one brings a kettle back to the fire.

“Have you settled what is to be done for Helena and her brother?”

Mr. Crisparkle’s open face clouded with honest perplexity.

“Why really, I am in great perplexity. If even Mr. Grewgious, whose head is much longer than mine, and who is a whole night’s cogitation in advance of me, is undecided, what must I be!”

At that moment, the door gave a cautious rap, and the Unlimited—having been authorised to present herself—put in her head with the air of one announcing weather.

“A gentleman wishes for a word with another gentleman named Crisparkle, if any such gentleman be here. If no such gentleman be here, he begs pardon for being mistaken.”

“Such a gentleman is here,” said Mr. Crisparkle, “but is engaged just now.”

Rosa, whose nerves had learned to start at the smallest change in air, retreated instinctively upon her guardian.

“Is it a dark gentleman?” she asked.

“No, Miss, more of a brown gentleman.”

“You are sure not with black hair?” Rosa persisted, taking a little courage from the answer.

“Quite sure of that, Miss. Brown hair and blue eyes.”

“Perhaps,” hinted Mr. Grewgious, with his habitual caution, “it might be well to see him, reverend sir, if you don’t object. When one is in a difficulty or at a loss, one never knows in what direction a way out may chance to open. It is a business principle of mine, in such a case, not to close up any direction, but to keep an eye on every direction that may present itself. I could relate an anecdote in point, but that it would be premature.”

“If Miss Rosa will allow me, then? Let the gentleman come in,” said Mr. Crisparkle.

The gentleman entered with a frank modesty, apologised for not finding Mr. Crisparkle alone, and then—turning on him with a smile that belonged to sunshine and out-of-doors—asked, without preface, the oddest question:

“Who am I?”

“You are the gentleman I saw smoking under the trees in Staple Inn, a few minutes ago,” returned Mr. Crisparkle, blinking as if to focus his memory as he would focus his sight.

“True. There I saw you. Who else am I?”

Mr. Crisparkle fixed his attention upon the sunburnt, handsome face. Something rose in him—some faded schoolroom, some echo of boys’ voices, some smell of old books and damp playing-fields—like a ghost of a departed boy that would not quite come clear in the daylight.

The stranger watched the struggle, and, smiling again, struck the flint that made the memory blaze.

“What will you have for breakfast this morning? You are out of jam.”

“Wait a moment!” cried Mr. Crisparkle, lifting his right hand as if to command the past to stand still. “Give me another instant! Tartar!”

They shook hands with a heartiness that made the room warmer. Then—going the wonderful length, for Englishmen—each laid a hand upon the other’s shoulder, and they looked, joyfully and squarely, face to face, as if time had merely stepped out for a moment and stepped in again.

“My old fag!” said Mr. Crisparkle.

“My old master!” said Mr. Tartar.

“You saved me from drowning!” cried Mr. Crisparkle.

“After which you took to swimming, you know!” returned Mr. Tartar.

“God bless my soul!”

“Amen!”

And again they fell to shaking hands, as if there were no other language that could bear the press of what they felt.

“Imagine,” exclaimed Mr. Crisparkle, his eyes bright, “Miss Rosa Bud and Mr. Grewgious—imagine Mr. Tartar, when he was the smallest of juniors, diving for me, catching me, a big heavy senior, by the hair of the head, and striking out for the shore with me like a water-giant!”

“Imagine my not letting him sink, as I was his fag!” said Mr. Tartar. “But the truth being that he was my best protector and friend, and did me more good than all the masters put together, an irrational impulse seized me to pick him up, or go down with him.”

“Hem! Permit me, sir, to have the honour,” said Mr. Grewgious, advancing with extended hand, “for an honour I truly esteem it. I am proud to make your acquaintance. I hope you didn’t take cold. I hope you were not inconvenienced by swallowing too much water. How have you been since?”

It was not at all clear that Mr. Grewgious knew exactly what he said, but it was perfectly clear he meant friendship in every syllable. Rosa, watching Mr. Tartar’s steady, capable figure, had one of those sharp thoughts that cut without warning: if Heaven had sent such courage and such handiness to her poor mother’s aid!—and he had been so slight and young then.

Mr. Grewgious, after a pause, began to walk across the room with sudden jog-trots so abrupt that they all stared, half thinking he was choking or seized with cramp. He trotted twice, stopped, and announced, with an air of discovery:

“I don’t wish to be complimented upon it, I thank you; but I think I have an idea. I think I have an idea. I believe I have had the pleasure of seeing Mr. Tartar’s name as tenant of the top set in the house next the top set in the corner?”

“Yes, sir,” returned Mr. Tartar. “You are right so far.”

“I am right so far,” repeated Mr. Grewgious. “Tick that off;” which he did, gravely, with his right thumb upon his left.

“Might you happen to know the name of your neighbour in the top set on the other side of the party-wall?” He came very close to Mr. Tartar, to lose nothing of his face, his short sight making nearness a kind of scrutiny.

“Landless.”

“Tick that off,” said Mr. Grewgious, and trotted again, and came back. “No personal knowledge, I suppose, sir?”

“Slight, but some.”

“Tick that off,” said Mr. Grewgious, and took another trot, and returned. “Nature of knowledge, Mr. Tartar?”

“I thought he seemed to be a young fellow in a poor way, and I asked his leave—only within a day or so—to share my flowers up there with him; that is to say, to extend my flower-garden to his windows.”

Mr. Grewgious’s face took on the composed look of a man laying papers in order.

“Would you have the kindness to take seats?” said he. “I have an idea!”

They sat: Mr. Crisparkle attentive as ever, Mr. Tartar good-humouredly at sea, and Rosa poised between dread and relief. Mr. Grewgious, in the centre, hands upon knees, delivered his “idea” as if he had learnt it by heart overnight and would not vary a word.

“I cannot as yet make up my mind whether it is prudent to hold open communication under present circumstances, and on the part of the fair member of the present company, with Mr. Neville or Miss Helena. I have reason to know that a local friend of ours”—here his thin mouth tightened—“(on whom I beg to bestow a passing but a hearty malediction, with the kind permission of my reverend friend) sneaks to and fro, and dodges up and down. When not doing so himself, he may have some informant skulking about, in the person of a watchman, porter, or such-like hanger-on of Staple. On the other hand, Miss Rosa very naturally wishes to see her friend Miss Helena, and it would seem important that at least Miss Helena (if not her brother too, through her) should privately know from Miss Rosa’s lips what has occurred, and what has been threatened. Am I agreed with generally in the views I take?”

“I entirely coincide with them,” said Mr. Crisparkle.

“As I have no doubt I should,” added Mr. Tartar, smiling, “if I understood them.”

“Fair and softly, sir,” returned Mr. Grewgious; “we shall fully confide in you directly, if you will favour us with your permission. Now, if our local friend should have any informant on the spot, it is tolerably clear that such informant can only be set to watch the chambers in the occupation of Mr. Neville. He reporting, to our local friend, who comes and goes there, our local friend would supply for himself, from his own previous knowledge, the identity of the parties. Nobody can be set to watch all Staple, or to concern himself with comers and goers to other sets of chambers: unless, indeed, mine.”

“I begin to understand to what you tend,” said Mr. Crisparkle, and the approving note in his voice sounded like a steadying hand laid upon a door that might otherwise slam.

“I needn’t repeat that I know nothing yet of the why and wherefore,” said Mr. Tartar; “but I also understand to what you tend, so let me say at once that my chambers are freely at your disposal.”

“There!” cried Mr. Grewgious, smoothing his head with triumph. “Now we have all got the idea. You have it, my dear?”

“I think I have,” said Rosa, blushing a little as Mr. Tartar looked quickly towards her, his blue eyes attentive in a way that made her feel both seen and safe.

“You see, you go over to Staple with Mr. Crisparkle and Mr. Tartar,” said Mr. Grewgious; “I going in and out, and out and in alone, in my usual way; you go up with those gentlemen to Mr. Tartar’s rooms; you look into Mr. Tartar’s flower-garden; you wait for Miss Helena’s appearance there, or you signify to Miss Helena that you are close by; and you communicate with her freely, and no spy can be the wiser.”

Rosa hesitated, and the hesitation was not fear exactly—more a scruple, like feeling one has entered a kitchen and touched what ought to be handled by the cook alone.

“I am very much afraid I shall be—”

“Be what, my dear?” asked Mr. Grewgious.

“Not frightened?” Mr. Crisparkle suggested.

“No, not that,” said Rosa, shyly; “in Mr. Tartar’s way. We seem to be appropriating Mr. Tartar’s residence so very coolly.”

“I protest to you,” Mr. Tartar returned, with a warmth that had nothing theatrical in it, “that I shall think the better of it for evermore, if your voice sounds in it only once.”

Rosa did not quite know what to say to that, and so did what she could do: she cast down her eyes, and turned to her guardian with dutiful practicality.

“Should I put my hat on?”

Mr. Grewgious, being of opinion she could not do better, nodded her away. While she withdrew, Mr. Crisparkle gave Mr. Tartar, in brief, the distress of Neville and his sister—how suspicion and solitude had closed about them like a net, and how necessity now demanded not merely kindness, but cautious contrivance. The summary took some minutes; Rosa’s hat, requiring a little extra fitting, took as many.

They went out together into London’s great restless mouth of sound. Mr. Tartar offered his arm; Rosa took it, and Mr. Crisparkle walked detached in front, as if to meet the stream and break it for them. Wheels rasped, voices clashed and overlapped, iron rang on iron; and through it all, Rosa’s mind kept returning, like a tongue to a sore place.

Poor, poor Eddy!

Mr. Tartar talked in an animated way, and as he did so he waved his right hand—broad and browned, a hand that looked made for rope and oar, for holding fast in wet weather. Rosa glanced at it.

It was not so powerful or so sun-browned when it saved Mr. Crisparkle; but it must have been very steady and determined even then.

He told her he had been a sailor, roving everywhere for years and years.

“When are you going to sea again?” she asked.

“Never!”

She wondered, with a sudden childish fancy, what the girls would say if they could see her crossing the wide street on a sailor’s arm. And she fancied the passers-by must think her very little and very helpless beside that strong figure—a figure that could have caught her up and carried her out of danger, miles and miles without resting. His far-seeing blue eyes looked as if they had been trained to watch peril come on from a long way off, and to watch it without flinching as it drew nearer and nearer.

Thinking that, she happened to raise her own eyes and found that he seemed—confusingly—to be thinking something about them.

After that, Rosebud never afterwards quite knew how she ascended (with his help) to his garden in the air; how the stair’s city echoes fell away; how the harsher sounds of London were screened and softened; and how, at the top, there opened a little miracle of green—a sudden bloom, as if a marvellous country had sprung up there like the country on the summit of a magic bean-stalk.

May it flourish for ever.
