Spring of 1849 found me drifting across the sun-scorched wastes of the Near East, my travel plans tossed over after a last-minute whim that altered every stop I’d mapped out months prior, every detail I’d forwarded to my lawyer and banker back in London. That sudden shift left me no choice but to dispatch a trusted servant to the city I’d cut from my itinerary, to collect my piled-up letters and waiting remittances from the English consul there, with orders to rejoin my encampment at a fixed time and place.
A mishap entirely out of his control delayed the man a full week. For seven long days my small party and I waited, camped on the ragged edge of the desert, the sand piling higher at our tent flaps every windless noon. He finally stumbled to the tent opening at the end of that stretch, purse full of my money and a stack of letters in his hand, his face drawn.
“I’m afraid I bring bad news, sir,” he said, and pointed to one envelope edged all around with black mourning crepe, its address scrawled in the familiar handwriting of Mr. Bruff, my lawyer.
There is no torment sharper than the sick suspense of that moment. I ripped the black-edged letter open first. It told me my father was dead, and I was heir to his entire vast fortune. The wealth that had landed so unexpectedly in my lap came heavy with responsibility, and Mr. Bruff begged me to lose not a single hour hurrying back to England. By daybreak the next morning, I was already on the road toward the nearest port bound for home.
My old friend Gabriel Betteredge’s portrait of me the day I left England, all that time ago, is a little overdrawn, if I am honest. He took one of Rachel Verinder’s sharp, playful jabs at my patchwork foreign education completely seriously, and convinced himself he actually saw the fussy French, brooding German, and dramatic Italian sides of my character my cousin had only ever mocked for a joke — traits that never existed anywhere but in the old steward’s warm, whimsical imagination. Setting that silly exaggeration aside, though, he told the unvarnished truth when he wrote that I left England raw to the bone, gutted by the worst disappointment of my entire life, cut deep by Rachel’s rejection of me.
I’d gone abroad determined that if change and distance could help me forget her, I would. I still believe it is a lie to say absence and new sights do nothing to dull that kind of grief: they force your attention away from staring fixed, unblinking, at your own sorrow. I never forgot Rachel — not for a single day — but the sharp, cutting edge of the memory softened little by little, as time, miles, and a hundred strange new scenes slipped between her and me.
But the second I turned my ship’s prow toward England, all that slow-won healing unspooled just as steadily. The closer I drew to the country she lived in, the closer I got to the chance of seeing her again, the tighter her old hold on me coiled around my chest. When I left England, her name was the last I would have let cross my lips. When I stepped off the boat and met Mr. Bruff in his office, she was the very first person I asked about.
He told me every last thing that had happened while I was gone, every event that picks up where Betteredge’s earlier narrative left off — save for one secret. At that point, Mr. Bruff was not at liberty to tell me the private, unspoken reasons that had led Rachel and Godfrey Ablewhite to call off their engagement. I did not press him on the delicate subject. It was relief enough, after the sick jolt of learning she had once nearly married that man, to know she had thought better of it, and broken the engagement of her own free will.
Once I had the full story of the past, my next questions all circled right back to Rachel, naturally. Who was caring for her after she left Mr. Bruff’s house? Where was she living now?
She was under the guardianship of Mrs. Merridew, a widowed sister of the late Sir John Verinder, whom her mother’s executors had asked to take charge of her. The two got on famously, I was told, and were settled for the season at Mrs. Merridew’s house in Portland Place.
Half an hour after hearing that news, I was hailing a cab for Portland Place — I didn’t even work up the courage to admit where I was going to Mr. Bruff. The footman who answered the door could not say for certain if Miss Verinder was at home, so I sent my card upstairs to settle the question straight away. He came back down a minute later, his face perfectly blank, to tell me Miss Verinder was out.
I might have suspected almost anyone else of deliberately hiding from me. But I could never suspect Rachel. I left word I would call again at six that evening. At six o’clock, I was told a second time that Miss Verinder was not at home. No message had been left for me. The footman politely confirmed she had received my card.
There was no way to miss the plain meaning: Rachel was refusing to see me. I had no intention of accepting that treatment without at least trying to find out why. I sent my name in to Mrs. Merridew, asking for a private interview at whatever hour was most convenient for her.
She received me immediately, a soft, comfortable little elderly lady in a cozy sitting room, full of polite surprise and regret — for my sake, of course. But she could not offer me a single explanation, she said, nor could she press Rachel on a matter that was entirely a question of private feeling. She repeated that line over and over, with unshakable gentle patience, and that was all I got for my trouble.
My last remaining option was to write to Rachel. The next day I sent my servant round with the note, strict orders to wait there until he got a reply. The note that came back was exactly one sentence, no more: “Miss Verinder begs to decline entering into any correspondence with Mr. Franklin Blake.”
I was so stung by that cold, deliberate insult I could barely think straight. Mr. Bruff walked in to speak to me on business before I had calmed down, and I brushed the business aside entirely to lay the whole mess out for him. He was just as clueless as Mrs. Merridew. No slander about me had ever reached Rachel’s ears, he swore. She had never once spoken my name while she stayed under his roof. She had not even once asked, through all my long years away, if I was alive or dead.
I pulled Lady Verinder’s old letter out of my pocketbook — the one she had written to me from Frizinghall the very day I left her Yorkshire house, back when the Moonstone first vanished — and pointed Bruff to two lines I had never been able to forget:
“The valuable assistance which you rendered to the inquiry after the lost jewel is still an unpardoned offence, in the present dreadful state of Rachel’s mind. Moving blindfold in this matter, you have added to the burden of anxiety which she has had to bear, by innocently threatening her secret with discovery through your exertions.”
“Is it possible,” I asked him, “that the anger she felt toward me back then is still just as bitter, a year later?”
Mr. Bruff looked genuinely distressed. “If you force me to answer straight,” he said, “I can read no other meaning into her behavior.”
I rang the bell at once, told my servant to pack my portmanteau and send out for a railway guide. Bruff stared at me, bewildered, and asked where I was going.
“To Yorkshire,” I said. “By the very next train.”
“Whatever for?”
“Mr. Bruff,” I told him, “the help I gave that investigation into the Moonstone, entirely by accident, was an unpardonable offense to Rachel nearly a year ago — and it still is. I will not accept that. I am going to dig up the secret behind why she stayed silent to her own mother, and why she hates me. If time, work, and money can do it, I will track down the thief who stole the Moonstone.”
The good old lawyer tried to remonstrate with me, to talk me down, to do his duty and talk me into sense. I did not hear a word of it. No power on earth could have shaken my resolution that moment.
“I am picking up the investigation exactly where I left it,” I went on. “I will follow it step by step, all the way to the present day. There are gaps in the evidence I could never fill on my own — gaps Gabriel Betteredge can supply. And that is exactly where I am going.”
By sunset that same evening, I was standing on the familiar old terrace of the Verinder country house, staring at the peaceful, ivy-draped old home I had not seen in so long. The gardener was the first person I spotted in the quiet grounds. He told me he had left Betteredge an hour earlier, sunning himself in his usual corner of the back yard. I knew that spot like the back of my own hand, so I set off to find him myself.
I wandered down the familiar old paths, rounded the corner, and peered through the open gate of the back yard. There he was — my dear old friend, from all the happy days that felt long gone — sat in his old spot in his creaky beehive chair, pipe clamped between his teeth, his dog-eared copy of *Robinson Crusoe* open across his lap, two of the house dogs dozing on either side of his feet.
The last slanting rays of the setting sun threw my long shadow across the yard. The dogs caught sight of it, or caught my scent on the breeze, and jolted up with a low growl. Betteredge jumped right after them, quieted them with a single sharp word, then shaded his failing old eyes with one hand, squinting curiously at the figure standing in the gate.
My own eyes were full of tears. I had to pause for a long, quiet moment before I could trust myself to speak to him.
