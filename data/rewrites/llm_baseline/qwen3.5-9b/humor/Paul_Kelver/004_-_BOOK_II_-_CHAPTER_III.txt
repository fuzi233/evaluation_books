"Louisa!" my father bellowed down the kitchen stairs, his voice booming like a cannon shot. "Are you all asleep? I've actually had to answer the front door myself!" With that, he marched into his study and slammed the door shut. My father possessed a terrifying capacity for anger when the household was silent.

Fifteen minutes later, the hallway bell rang—a sharp, demanding jingle. My mother, struggling to peel potatoes inside thick wash-leather gloves, glanced nervously at my Aunt Fan, who was shelling peas. The bell rang again, louder and more insistent.

"Once for Louisa, twice for James, isn't it?" Aunt Fan guessed.

"You go, Paul," my mother instructed. "Tell Louisa—" But suddenly, a deep crimson flushed her cheeks. Before I could even put down my slate, she had peeled off her gloves and rushed toward me. "No, don't stop your lessons! I'll go myself," she declared, and bolted out the door.

Moments later, the kitchen door creaked open. My mother's hand appeared through the crack, beckoning me with mysterious urgency.

"Walk on your toes," she whispered, demonstrating as she led me upstairs. The stairs, seemingly offended by our deceit, protested loudly with every step, groaning and creaking far worse than usual. We slipped silently into my parents' bedroom. Inside the old-fashioned wardrobe—a relic of happier times—lay my finest suit. Or, to be grammatically precise, my *better*.

I had never worn this ensemble on a weekday morning. All chatter irrelevant to the urgent task of dressing quickly was swept aside by my mother. Once fully attired, down to the brand-new Blucher shoes (the fashion of the day), she took my hand. Together, we crept down the stairs as we had ascended them: silent, stealthy, and alert.

At the street door, my mother opened it.

"Shan't I want my cap?" I whispered anxiously.

My mother merely shook her head and slammed the door shut behind us. Only then did the true purpose of this pantomime click into place. Was it comedy or tragedy? I was becoming increasingly familiar with such dramatic performances. With my mother's hand resting reassuringly on my shoulder, we entered my father's study.

There was a reason why this room served double duty. Perhaps because evenings were spent in the drawing-room—which remained perpetually staged as a showroom for unexpected guests, complete with Cowper's poems face-down on the wobbly table and half-finished crochet work draped elegantly over an armchair—or perhaps simply because my father was prone to switching games instantly from cricket in the garden to ninepins in the hallway, retreating to the study whenever a knock sounded at the front door. Either way, as a child, I never viewed my father's profession seriously. To me, he was merely playing dress-up amidst towering books and bundles of documents labeled impressively but containing mostly blank paper. Japanned tin boxes bore grand titles like "Sutton Hampden, Esq.," which functioned essentially as my mother's sewing box. "Drayton Estates" produced nothing but apples, a fruit my father adored. As for "Mortgages," I didn't discover until adulthood that they had nothing to do with the manuscripts of poetry lying around, some corrected, others finished.

As the door swung open, my father rose and approached us. His hair stood on end; it was his habit to ruffle it while talking, adding to his earnest attempt to project businesslike gravity, though it ultimately emphasized the look of an overgrown schoolboy pretending to be grown-up.

"This is the youngster," my father announced, taking me from my mother and presenting me. "Tall for his age, isn't he?"

With a twist of his thick lips, he rolled the foul-smelling cigar from the corner of his mouth to the other side. He extended a large, slightly unclean hand that gripped mine firmly, bringing to mind the walrus illustration in my natural history book. With his other hand, he patted my head affectionately.

"He's like his mother," Mr. Hasluck observed, still holding my grip. "Wonderfully like his mother. And that," he added, winking one small eye at my father, "is probably the highest compliment I can offer, eh?"

His eyes were remarkably small yet piercingly bright. When he fixed them on me, I scrambled desperately to recall something complimentary, convinced he could see straight through my soul.

"And where are you planning to send him?" he asked. "Eton or Harrow?"

"We haven't quite decided yet," my father replied smoothly. "For now, we are educating him at home."

"You take my advice," the portly gentleman urged. "Learn everything you can. Look at me! Had I the chance to be a scholar, I certainly wouldn't be here offering your father an outrageous sum to do my work; I'd be doing it myself."

"You seem to have managed quite well without it," my father laughed, his air of prosperity suggesting a healthy dose of self-satisfaction. Rings glittered on his blunt fingers, and a massive gold chain rested heavily against the folds of his waistcoat.

"I'd have done better with it," Hasluck grunted.

"But you look very clever," I blurted out. Though I sensed with childish intuition that I was supposed to charm him, I genuinely hoped my praise would please him.

He roared with laughter, his entire body trembling like a giant gelatin dessert.

"Well, old Noel Hasluck isn't exactly a fool," he admitted. "But I'd prefer to talk about something other than business, and I wish I didn't have to drop my 'h's. Neither does my little girl."

"You have a daughter?" my mother asked. As a mother, a child instantly replaced the role usually filled by respectful servants or competent help; she sought a bond of sympathy with strangers.

"I won't tell you about her. But I'll bring her to visit you occasionally, ma'am, if you don't mind," Mr. Hasluck replied. "She doesn't often meet genteel folk, and it would do her good."

My mother glanced hopefully at my father, but he intercepted her gaze and answered for himself.

"Don't worry, ma'am, she isn't anything like me," he assured her cheerfully. "Nobody believes she's my daughter except me and the old woman. She's a proper little lady. A freak of nature, I call it."

"We shall be delighted," my mother explained.

"You will be once you see her," Mr. Hasluck confirmed, sounding entirely satisfied.

He pressed half a crown into my palm, dismissing my parents' modesty with the casual confidence of a man used to getting his way.

"No squandering it on the heathen," he warned as I turned to leave. "Spend that on a Christian tradesman."

It was the first money I distinctly remembered receiving. Visions of new Sunday gloves, a personal Latin grammar, and other luxuries had previously clouded my thoughts regarding stray coins. But this time, I was left to choose.

The pressure was immense. I tossed sleeplessly in bed, wandered bewildered through the streets, and received no guidance.

"You must learn to think for yourself," my father advised eloquently, preaching the necessity of early judgment and what he termed "commercial aptitude."

"No, dear," my mother countered gently. "Mr. Hasluck wanted you to spend it however you like. If I told you what to do, that would be spending it according to my wishes. Your father and I want to see what you decide."

The virtuous boys in stories bought gifts for the needy. I despised them with a fierce, primal hatred. I turned to Aunt Fan.

"If someone gave you half a crown," I asked, "what would you buy?"

"Side-combs," she answered immediately. She was constantly losing or breaking hers.

"But I mean if you were me," I clarified.

"Drat the child!" Aunt Fan snapped. "How can I know what he wants if he doesn't know himself? Idiot!"

I stared at shop windows, my nose pressed against the glass. I asked prices, made decisions, then changed my mind. Even my patient mother began to show signs of irritation. Old Hasluck's half-crown was rapidly becoming a family curse.

Finally, I resolved the dilemma. In a small plumber's shop tucked away in a backstreet, among brass taps and lead pipes, sat various squares of colored glass—typically used for bathroom doors or stairwell windows. Some featured stars; others bore intricate, severe designs. I bought a dozen. The friendly plumber, eager to please, threw in two extras out of pure generosity.

I couldn't explain why I bought them then, nor can I now. My mother wept upon seeing them. My father could only mutter, "But what are you going to do with them?" leaving me speechless. Aunt Fan offered the only comfort: "If a person likes colored glass, they're a fool not to buy it when they can. We don't all share the same tastes."

Eventually, I cut myself badly with the shards and agreed to throw them in the bin. Looking back, I consider myself less a victim of folly and more a casualty of fate. Since then, I've met countless recipients of Hasluck's half-crowns—men who slapped their pockets and thanked heaven for meeting the "Napoleon of Finance"—yet it always ended the same way: colored glass and bleeding fingers.
