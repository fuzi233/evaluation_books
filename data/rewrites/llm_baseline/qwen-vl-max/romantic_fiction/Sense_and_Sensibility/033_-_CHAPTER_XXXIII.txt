CHAPTER XXXIII. After some hesitation, Marianne finally agreed to accompany her sister and Mrs. Jennings on a short outing one morning, though she insisted they would not call upon anyone—only go to Gray’s in Sackville Street, where Elinor was negotiating the sale of a few old-fashioned jewels left by their mother. As they reached the shop, Mrs. Jennings remembered she had a friend to visit at the other end of the street and, having no business there herself, decided to go alone while the sisters attended to theirs. She would return for them shortly.

Inside, the shop was crowded, and so many customers were ahead of them that the Miss Dashwoods had no choice but to wait. They took seats at the far end of the counter, where only one gentleman stood, seemingly indifferent to the delay. Elinor, perhaps hoping his attention might be drawn to their presence, watched him closely. But his focus was entirely absorbed by a peculiar task: selecting a toothpick-case. He examined every option with meticulous care, debating size, shape, and ornamentation as if it were a matter of great consequence. For nearly fifteen minutes, he scrutinized each piece, rejecting them all until he finally settled on a design of his own invention—one combining ivory, gold, and pearls. Only then did he turn his gaze toward the two sisters, offering them a glance so broad and unfeeling it seemed less like admiration than an idle curiosity. The man was impeccably dressed, yet his manner revealed a soul devoid of warmth or charm. Elinor noted him well—a figure of fashion without grace, polish without character.

Marianne, meanwhile, remained untouched by the scene. Lost in her thoughts, she sat quietly, unaware of the gentleman’s rudeness or the absurdity of his choices. Her mind was elsewhere, drifting through memories and emotions too deep for such trivial surroundings to disturb.

At last, the transaction was complete. The gentleman carefully slipped on his gloves, cast another glance at the sisters—this time more suggestive of superiority than interest—and departed with an air of self-satisfaction. Elinor quickly resumed her own business, nearly finishing when another man stepped forward beside her. She turned—and with surprise recognized her brother, John Dashwood.

Their meeting brought a moment of genuine affection, which made a pleasant contrast to the otherwise dull atmosphere of the shop. John was clearly glad to see them again, and his inquiries about their mother were courteous and thoughtful. He told them he and Fanny had arrived in town just two days before. “I truly meant to call yesterday,” he said, “but we had to take Harry to see the wild beasts at Exeter Exchange, and afterward spent the day with Mrs. Ferrars. Harry was delighted. This morning I intended to come, if I could find half an hour, but one always has so much to do upon first arriving.” He added that he was now there to order a seal for Fanny, but hoped to visit them the next day and meet Mrs. Jennings. “I understand she is quite well-off,” he remarked. “And the Middletons too—you must introduce me to them. As my mother-in-law’s relations, I should be pleased to show them every respect. I’ve heard they’re excellent neighbors.”

“Indeed they are,” Elinor replied. “Their kindness and thoughtfulness have been beyond words.”

“I’m very glad to hear it,” he said warmly. “It should be so—they’re wealthy, related to you, and naturally ought to offer every comfort. And you’re happily settled in your little cottage, wanting for nothing? Edward gave us a wonderful description—said it was the most perfect dwelling of its kind, and you all seemed utterly content. That pleased us greatly.”

Elinor felt a quiet embarrassment at his tone, but was spared further conversation by the arrival of Mrs. Jennings’s servant, who announced that her mistress waited at the door. John Dashwood accompanied them downstairs, was introduced to Mrs. Jennings, and promised to call again soon. His manners were calm yet polite, especially toward Mrs. Jennings, whom he treated with particular deference. When Colonel Brandon entered shortly after, John studied him with a curious eye—his expression suggesting that he found the colonel worth knowing precisely because he appeared to be wealthy.

After staying half an hour, John asked Elinor if she would walk with him to Conduit Street to meet Sir John and Lady Middleton. The weather was bright and inviting, and she consented readily.

Once outside, he began with questions. “Who is Colonel Brandon? Is he well-off?”

“Yes,” Elinor answered. “He has good property in Dorsetshire.”

“That’s good,” John said. “He seems a gentlemanly sort. And I think, Elinor, I may congratulate you on the prospect of a respectable future.”

“Me?” she asked, startled. “What do you mean?”

“He likes you,” John declared. “I observed him closely and am certain of it. What is the extent of his fortune?”

“About two thousand a year,” she replied.

“Two thousand!” John exclaimed, then paused, gathering himself into a burst of generous enthusiasm. “Elinor, I wish with all my heart it were twice that—for your sake.”

“I believe you,” she said, though she doubted his sincerity. “But I assure you, Colonel Brandon has no intention of marrying me.”

“You’re mistaken,” he insisted. “A little encouragement from you would secure him. Perhaps he hesitates now—your modest fortune may give pause, or his friends may advise against it. But a few gentle attentions, a word here and there—those small things women can so easily offer—would settle him, even against his will. There’s no reason why you shouldn’t try. You know, attachments of that nature are out of the question—objections are insurmountable. You have too much sense not to see that. So Colonel Brandon must be the one. And I’ll do everything in my power to make him pleased with you and your family. It’s a match that will bring universal satisfaction. In fact”—he lowered his voice conspiratorially—“it will be exceedingly welcome to all parties.”

Then, as if remembering himself, he added, “That is to say—your friends are deeply concerned for your welfare. Fanny especially; she cares for you very much. And Mrs. Ferrars too—she’s a kind woman, and I’m sure she’d be delighted. She mentioned it the other day.”

Elinor offered no reply.

“It would be rather amusing,” John continued, “if Fanny married a brother and I a sister at the same time. Not unlikely, really.”

“Is Mr. Edward Ferrars going to marry?” Elinor asked firmly.

“It’s not settled,” he said, “but it’s being discussed. He has an excellent mother. Mrs. Ferrars, with great generosity, will settle a thousand pounds a year on him if the match goes through. The lady is the Honorable Miss Morton, daughter of the late Lord Morton, with thirty thousand pounds. A splendid connection on both sides—I have no doubt it will happen. A thousand a year is a great deal for a mother to give up, but Mrs. Ferrars has a noble spirit. Let me tell you another example of her generosity: as soon as we came to town, realizing our finances might be tight, she handed Fanny two hundred pounds in banknotes. Very welcome indeed—we must live extravagantly while we’re here.”

He paused, expecting sympathy. Elinor forced a response: “Your expenses in town and country must certainly be high, but your income is substantial.”

“Not as large as people think,” he replied. “I don’t complain—it’s comfortable enough, and I hope it will improve. The enclosure of Norland Common is draining us heavily. And I’ve made a recent purchase—East Kingham Farm, you remember it? Old Gibson used to live there. The land was ideal—adjacent to my own property, so I felt compelled to buy it. I couldn’t let it fall into someone else’s hands. A man must pay for convenience. It cost me a fortune.”

“More than it’s truly worth,” Elinor murmured.

“Perhaps not,” he said. “I could have sold it back the next day for more. But I might have been ruined—the stocks were so low that if I hadn’t already had the money in my banker’s hands, I’d have had to sell out at a terrible loss.”

Elinor smiled faintly.

“There are other unavoidable costs too,” he went on. “Our dear father left all the Stanhill effects at Norland to your mother—very valuable, of course. I wouldn’t dream of complaining—he had the right to dispose of his estate as he wished. But because of that, we’ve had to replace everything—linens, china, and so on. You can imagine how much that’s cost. After all this, we’re far from rich. Mrs. Ferrars’s kindness is invaluable.”

“Certainly,” Elinor said. “With her help, I hope you’ll soon be comfortably established.”

“Another year or two may do much,” he replied gravely. “But still, there’s much to be done. Fanny’s greenhouse hasn’t begun, and the flower garden is only sketched out.”

“Where will it be?” she asked.

“On the knoll behind the house. We’ve felled all the old walnut trees to clear space. It will be visible from many parts of the park, and the garden will slope down before it—very pleasing. We’ve cleared away the old thorns too.”

Elinor kept her disapproval silent, grateful that Marianne wasn’t present to witness the extravagance.

Having laid bare his financial constraints, John shifted to lighter matters. He praised Mrs. Jennings, calling her “a most valuable woman,” noting her fine home and elegant lifestyle. “Her invitation to you to come to town is a great advantage,” he said. “And her kindness suggests such regard that when she dies, you may not be forgotten. She must have considerable wealth to leave.”

“She likely won’t leave anything,” Elinor countered. “She only has her jointure, which passes to her children.”

“But surely she doesn’t spend everything,” John insisted. “Few sensible people do. Whatever she saves, she can dispose of freely.”

“And do you suppose she’d leave it to us instead of her daughters?” Elinor asked.

“Her daughters are well married,” he replied. “So there’s no need to provide further for them. But by treating you so kindly, you’ve earned a claim on her consideration—one a conscientious woman wouldn’t ignore. Her behavior is remarkably kind. She can’t possibly do all this without creating expectations.”

“But she raises none in those most involved,” Elinor said gently. “Brother, your concern for our welfare is touching—but it goes too far.”

“Oh,” he said, as if recollecting himself, “people have little control over such things. But tell me—what’s wrong with Marianne? She looks poorly, pale, thin. Is she ill?”

“She’s not well,” Elinor admitted. “She’s had a nervous complaint for weeks.”

“I’m sorry to hear it,” John said. “At her age, illness can ruin a woman’s bloom forever! Hers was so brief—last September she was the most beautiful girl I ever saw, and clearly destined to attract a fine match. There was something about her beauty that men particularly admired. Fanny once said she’d marry sooner and better than you. Not that she doesn’t love you—she does—but that was her impression. She’ll be mistaken, though. I doubt Marianne will marry anyone worth more than five or six hundred a year. But you—well, I have no doubt you’ll do better. Dorsetshire! I know little of it, but I’d be happy to learn more. And I promise you, Fanny and I will be among your earliest visitors.”

Elinor tried earnestly to dissuade him from believing Colonel Brandon would propose, but the idea brought him such pleasure he refused to abandon it. He was determined to cultivate a friendship with the colonel and encourage the match with every gesture. He carried enough guilt over neglecting his sisters to feel compelled to ensure others did much for them—an inheritance from Mrs. Jennings or a proposal from Colonel Brandon would serve as ample atonement.

They found Lady Middleton at home, and Sir John joined them before the visit ended. Warm greetings passed between them all. Sir John was eager to like everyone, and though John Dashwood knew little about horses, he quickly judged him a pleasant fellow. Lady Middleton admired his fashionable appearance and welcomed the acquaintance. John returned home delighted.

“I shall have a wonderful story for Fanny,” he said as they walked back. “Lady Middleton is truly elegant—exactly the sort Fanny would enjoy knowing. And Mrs. Jennings—most respectable, though not quite so refined as her daughter. Your sister needn’t hesitate to visit her, which is fortunate, since we’ve had reservations. We knew only that Mrs. Jennings was the widow of a man who made his fortune in trade, and Fanny and Mrs. Ferrars both assumed she and her daughters weren’t suitable company. But now I can report back with full confidence—both are admirable.”
