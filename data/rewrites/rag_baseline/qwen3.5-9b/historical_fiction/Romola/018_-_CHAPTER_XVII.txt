The loggia atop Bardo's house rose like a crown above the surrounding structures, forming a gallery that encircled the quadrangular walls. On the street-facing side, the roof was upheld by sturdy columns; on the others, a solid wall was pierced by arched openings. Through these arches, Romola could survey the chaotic sprawl of irregular, poorly built dwellings stretching toward the hill of Bogoli, enjoying a walk entirely shielded from prying eyes.

Near one of these arches, close to the entry point, Tito waited. Sunlight slanted before him, mingling with the ruin of his hopes, casting a sickening pallor over his anticipation. He had never truly relied on Romola's passion to withstand the repulsion born of his secret; he lacked the presumptuous vanity that might have blinded him to the fact that her love grew from the same soil as her belief in him. Yet, as he imagined her approaching in her radiant beauty, softened by the mortal tenderness of her soft hazel eyes, a desperate wish seized him: that she were something lower, merely flesh and blood, so he might clasp her and kiss her before they parted. He had received no real caress from her save for occasional glances, brief touches, and fleeting pressures of the hand. He had longed endlessly for solitude with her. Now, they were finally alone, yet she stood inexorably aloof.

His heart hammered a frantic rhythm as he sensed movement behind the door. Romola appeared. It struck him with the sudden, blinding intensity of lightning—not visually, but viscerally—he felt the glory haloing her head, the tearful appeal in her gaze. He felt, rather than heard, the cry of love erupting from her lips: "Tito!" In the same instant, she was in his arms, sobbing, her face pressed against his chest.

How desperately Romola had yearned through the long watches of the night to behold that bright face! The new specter of death, the bewildering doubt injected into her psyche by the story of a life alien to her understanding, the haunting vision she seemed not only to hear in the low, gasping voice of her brother but to inhabit as her own dream—it had rendered her more conscious than ever that Tito was the source of the warm stream of hope and gladness that had flooded her existence, turning away the keen edge of pain regarding her brother. She would confide in Tito; there was no other ear capable of hearing it. She had restrained herself in her father's presence all morning, but now, the dam of pent-up grief burst. Proud and self-possessed before the world, Romola became as simple and unreserved as a child in her devotion to Tito. Content with days spent merely gazing at one another, she now felt the urgent need to cling, with no inhibitions hindering her.

"My Romola! My goddess!" Tito murmured, clutching her with passionate fondness, kissing the thick golden ripples of her neck. He was in paradise; disgrace, shame, separation—none of it mattered anymore. This happiness was too potent to be tarnished by the knowledge that Romola was deceived; indeed, he rejoiced in her delusion. After all, concealment had been wisdom. The only regret he harbored was his needless dread, though perhaps that fear had been worth enduring for the sake of this sudden rapture.

The sob subsided, and Romola lifted her head. Silence stretched between them, filled only with the sweet wonder characteristic of young love. Her long white hands rested on his dark-brown curls; his dark fingers were bathed in the streaming gold of her hair. Each was the epitome of beauty to the other, experiencing that undisturbed mutual consciousness for the first time. A cold pressure of fresh sadness settled on Romola's heart, making her linger in that silent, soothing proximity. Tito dared not press his lips to hers, for that would alter the sacred nature of the moment.

"Tito," she said finally, her voice trembling slightly, "it has been altogether painful, but I must tell you everything. Your strength will help me resist the impressions that reason cannot shake off."

"I know, Romola—I know he is dead," Tito replied. The long, lustrous eyes betrayed nothing of the myriad wishes that would have summoned that death long ago if mere desire held such potency. Romola read her own pure thoughts reflected in their dark depths, much as one reads letters in a lucid dream.

"So changed, Tito! It pierced me to think it was Dino. And so strangely hard: not a word to my father, nothing but a vision he wanted to convey. Yet it was so pitiful—the struggling breath, the eyes that seemed to look toward the crucifix, yet failed to see it. I shall never forget it; it feels as though it stands between me and everything I shall ever look at."

Romola's heart swelled again, forcing her to pause. Yet the imperative need to unload her mind onto Tito urged her to suppress the rising anguish. When she resumed speaking, her thoughts had drifted.

"It was strange, Tito. The vision concerned our marriage, yet he knew nothing of you."

"What was it, my Romola? Sit down and tell me," Tito said, guiding her to the nearby bench. A fear crossed him lest the vision somehow implicated Baldassarre; this sudden shift prompted him to change positions.

Romola recounted every detail from her entrance into San Marco, omitting not a single word of her brother's speech, which had burned itself into her memory. But as she reached the climax of the vision, she faltered. The remainder remained too vivid to utter. She sat staring into the distance, momentarily unaware of Tito's presence.

*His* mind, however, was at ease. The vague vision had passed over him like white mist, leaving no trace. He remained silent, awaiting her continuation.

"I took it," she continued, as if Tito had read her thoughts aloud. "I took the crucifix; it is down below in my bedroom."

"And now, my Romola," Tito entreated, "banish these ghastly thoughts. The vision was an ordinary monkish hallucination, bred of fasting and fanatical ideology. Surely it holds no weight for you."

"No, Tito; no. But poor Dino... *he* believed it was a divine message. It is strange," she mused, "this life of men possessed by fervid beliefs that appear madness to their peers. Dino was not a vulgar fanatic; and that Fra Girolamo—his very voice seems to penetrate me with a sense that there is some truth in what moves them: some truth of which I know nothing."

"It was only because your feelings were highly wrought, my Romola. Your brother's state of mind was no more than a form of that theosophy, the common disease of excitable, dreamy minds throughout all ages; the same ideas your father's old antagonist, Marsilio Ficino, pores over in the New Platonists. Only your brother's passionate nature drove him to act out what others merely write and discuss. As for Fra Girolamo, he is simply a narrow-minded monk with a gift for preaching and instilling terror in the multitude. Any words, any voice, would have shaken you at that moment. When your mind has found repose, you will judge such matters as you have always done."

"Not about poor Dino," Romola insisted. "I was angry with him; my heart seemed to close against him while he spoke. But since then, I have thought less of my own mind and more of his. Oh, Tito! It was terribly pitiful to witness his young life ending thus. That yearning look at the crucifix as he gasped for breath—I can never forget it. Last night, I stared at the crucifix for a long time, trying to imagine how it could help him, until, by the lamplight, it seemed to me as if the suffering face shed pity."

"My Romola, promise me to resist such thoughts. They are fit for sickly nuns, not for my golden-tressed Aurora, who appears made to scatter all such twilight fantasies. Try not to dwell on them now; we shall not remain alone for long."

The final words were delivered in a tone of tender beseeching. He turned her face toward him with a gentle touch of his right hand. Romola had been gazing absently at the arched opening, failing to notice the distant hill. She had been trapped within the chapter house, observing the pale images of sorrow and death. Tito's touch and pleading voice recalled her to the present. Now, bathed in warm sunlight, she perceived that rich, dark beauty that seemed to gather around it images of joy: purple vines festooned between elms, robust corn maturing under the vibrating heat, bright-winged creatures hurrying and resting among the flowers, round limbs beating the earth in gladness with cymbals held aloft, light melodies chanted to the thrilling rhythm of strings—all objects and sounds testifying to Nature reveling in her force.

A strange, bewildering transition occurred in her mind, shifting from those pale specters of sorrow and death to this vibrant youthfulness, akin to a sun-god ignorant of night. What thought could reconcile the worn anguish on her brother's face, that straining reach for the invisible, with this satisfied strength and beauty, rendering them intelligible parts of the same world? Or was there no reconciliation, only a blind worship of clashing deities, first in mad joy, then in wailing?
