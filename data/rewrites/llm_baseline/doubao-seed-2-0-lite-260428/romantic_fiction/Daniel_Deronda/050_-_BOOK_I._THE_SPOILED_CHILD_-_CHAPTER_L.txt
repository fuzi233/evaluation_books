“If some mortal, born too soon,
Were laid away in some great trance—the ages
Coming and going all the while—till dawned
His true time’s advent; and could then record
The words they spoke who kept watch by his bed,
Then I might tell more of the breath so light
Upon my eyelids, and the fingers warm
Among my hair. Youth is confused; yet never
So dull was I but, when that spirit passed,
I turned to him, scarce consciously, as turns
A water-snake when fairies cross his sleep.”
—BROWNING: *Paracelsus*

That was the verse humming soft and unplaceable under Deronda’s skin the day Sir Hugo Mallinger slid a sealed letter across the oak table to him. The handwriting on the envelope was unfamiliar, sharp and pale, and the greeting sent a jolt straight to his ribs: *TO MY SON, DANIEL DERONDA.*

My good friend and yours, Sir Hugo Mallinger, will have told you that I wish to see you. My health is shaken, and I desire there should be no time lost before I deliver to you what I have long withheld. Let nothing hinder you from being at the *Albergo dell’ Italia* in Genoa by the fourteenth of this month. Wait for me there. I am uncertain when I shall be able to make the journey from Spezia, where I shall be staying. That will depend on several things. Wait for me—the Princess Halm-Eberstein. Bring with you the diamond ring that Sir Hugo gave you. I shall like to see it again.—Your unknown mother, LEONORA HALM-EBERSTEIN.

Its plain, colorless lines gave him no hint of what lay waiting at the end of the Italian road. He did not press Sir Hugo for details, could see in the old baronet’s carefully averted gaze that he had been bound by a long-ago pledge not to steal the moment of revelation from the woman who had been absent from his life for twenty-odd years. The realization that every quiet, years-long hunch he had nursed about his parentage—all the soft, confused guesses that Sir Hugo must be his true father—shattered cleanly, and he refused to let his imagination run wild with new possibilities. He would not stoke any one hope or dread into a consuming fire, would not let himself crave a birth story more than another, only steeled himself to meet whatever truth came, head on.

He could not speak of the errand to anyone, not even those he loved most. Least of all Mordecai. If he told his dear, ragged, glowing friend that he was going to unlock the secret of his own birth, Mordecai’s fragile, long-nursed hope that Deronda was the heir to his own lost people’s legacy would spark into a wild, dangerous excitement that might break him if the truth did not fit. So he framed the journey as a trivial errand at Sir Hugo’s behest, put all the casual nonchalance he could muster into his voice as he said he did not know how long he would be gone, that it might end in less than a week.

Mordecai’s face softened after the first flash of sad, surprised disappointment. “I will ask if little Jacob can come stay with me while you are gone,” he said, as if the boy’s bright chatter would stitch the gap Deronda’s absence left behind.

“I’ll drive round to the Cohens’ right now and ask,” Mirah said immediately, her dark eyes lighting. “The grandmother will not say no to anything I ask these days.”

“Thank goodness you two were both wrong about her,” Deronda said, smiling at Mordecai. “You swore old Mrs. Cohen would never bear to lay eyes on Mirah again.”
“I underestimated her heart,” Mordecai said gently. “She is the sort of woman who can rejoice that another’s plant blooms, even when her own has withered in the pot.”

“Oh, they are all such dear, good people,” Mirah laughed, a soft, merry lilt in her tone. “I feel as if we are all tangled up in the same family now.”

Deronda’s tone turned playful, a little mischievous, teasing her for warming so quickly to the family that had once caused him such worry on her behalf. “How would you have felt if that Ezra Cohen had turned out to be your long-lost brother, instead of the Ezra you found?”

Mirah blinked at him, surprised for a heartbeat, then her cheeks flooded scarlet. “He is not a bad man,” she said softly, staring down at her hands. “I do not think he would ever forsake anyone who needed him.” She darted a timid glance at Mordecai before turning away to busy herself folding a stack of linen, and Deronda knew instantly what memory had made her flush: her own missing, wandering, dangerous father, the shadow that hung over her and her brother both, the fear that he might turn up around any street corner, that the bright daylight of London might turn as haunted and dark as a forest where every tree hid an unseen apparition.

He understood. How could he not, when that same kind of hidden, painful filial longing was thrumming loud in his own bones now? The soft, tender image of a mother who had never gotten to hold him, who he had spent years imagining as a woman who must have grieved to be parted from him, had dissolved the second he held her cold, distant letter in his hands. A veiled, enigmatic stranger had shoved aside the gentle, loving ghost his own heart had built for her.

All the thoughts that crowded his mind as he packed his bags had less to do with the mother he had never met, and everything to do with Mirah and Mordecai. When Sir Hugo shook his hand at the door of the Abbey, his gruff voice thick with unspoken feeling, he clapped Deronda’s shoulder hard enough to make him stagger a little. “God bless you, Dan. No matter what changes for you out there, nothing’s ever going to change the fact that I’m the oldest friend you ever had, the one who’s cared for you more than anyone else all these years. I couldn’t have loved you better if you’d been my own flesh and blood. I’d have liked it better, truth be told, if you were going to be the master of this Abbey after me instead of my fool of a nephew, and you’d have gone into politics like you ought to. But—things go how they go, I suppose.” He rambled off on a trivial, offhand story to hide the fact his eyes were glistening, the way gruff men always do when their feelings are too big to say out loud.

When Deronda arrived at the *Albergo dell’ Italia* in Genoa, the Princess was nowhere to be found. A note waited for him the second morning, saying her arrival might be a week away, or might be delayed a fortnight or more—circumstances out of her control kept her from naming a day, and she begged him to wait as patiently as he could.

Faced with weeks of suspended waiting, with the most important truth of his life hanging over him, Deronda set out to wander the sun-drenched city to still the restlessness in his bones. He had only passed through Genoa once before, in a rush, and now he spent every cool hour wandering its winding streets, the bustling quay, the quiet lanes on the city’s edges, often hiring a small boat to row out on the bay to gaze back at the grand, sunlit stretch of palaces rising from the water. Every sight he saw wove itself back to Mirah and Mordecai: he would sit in the boat staring at the harbor, and imagine the thousands of Spanish Jews, driven destitute from their homes centuries before, crowding these very docks, dying of famine and plague on the stone quays, mothers clutching their dead babies to their chests, haggard fathers staring at their sons as if they had been turned to ghosts. No matter how hard he tried to push the fanciful thought away, he could not stop weaving those old histories into daydreams about his own unknown ancestry. He would not let himself admit he hoped those daydreams were true, that he was one of them. To wish for it felt like a betrayal of the fact that he did not get to choose where he came from, that he had no right to disown whatever kin fate had given him. But still, he could not deny that the sacrifice lay heavy on one side, if he turned out to be no part of the legacy Mordecai had dreamed of sharing with him.

And over all that, another soft, persistent weight settled in his chest: the thought of Gwendolen. There was a kind of fondness for her that was not friendship, not the burning passion of great love, but something softer, sharper, something that felt like “I would have loved her, if only”—if only his heart had not already been claimed by the quiet purpose he had found with Mirah, if only the two of them had not met when she was already tangled in the cruel chains of her marriage. He could still hear her voice ringing in his ears, begging him not to leave her, not to forsake her, the words piercing him like little arrows tipped with pity, as Dante had written so long ago. Her lot was small, sharp, personal, while his own was swelling with wide, far-reaching purposes he could not yet name, but he could not shake the feeling that if he let her fall, a piece of his own heart would fall with her.

The days blurred into one another. Italy hummed with the news that war had been declared against Austria, the whole world rushing headlong toward the earth-shaking battle of Sadowa. The Genoa noons grew hotter, dust thick and white coating the roads leading out of the city, the oleanders in their tubs along garden edges drooping like tired holiday-makers. At dusk, the whole city poured out of its shaded houses: mule bells tinkled down the cobblestones, stringed instruments thrummmed from open windows, footsteps pattered light and happy along the quays, the heights crowned with forts and draped in villas coming alive after their long midday siesta. When the moon rose, it washed the streets silver, turning the steps of the cathedrals and the faces of the great stone palaces into something dreamlike, before the moon sank and the only lights left were the glow of the great Lanterna lighthouse on the dark water, and the faint glimmer of stars overhead. Deronda watched all of it unfold like the slow turning of a great magic clock, waiting for the signal that would announce his mother had come.

He began to chafe at the waiting. His books sat unread on his table, every line of them feeling distant, irrelevant, when his whole future hung balanced on the edge of decision. He would stay up half the night leaning out his open window, staring at the dark, vast stretch of sea and sky, swinging between moods: sometimes bitterly angry that fate had kept his parentage hidden from him his whole life, sometimes soft with a quiet devotion that made even sacrifice feel like a gift, sometimes glowing with a sweet, unshakable hope that he might get everything he ever wanted—love that wrapped around his whole heart, and a purpose big enough to hold all of it.

Then the knock came. It was his third week of waiting, early one morning. A servant in the fine livery of a chasseur stepped across the threshold, and delivered his message in crisp French: Her Highness the Princess Halm-Eberstein had arrived. She would rest through the day, and requested that Monsieur dine early that evening, so that he might present himself to her at seven o’clock.

Deronda’s fingers curled tight around the diamond ring he had carried in his pocket since he’d packed, and his heart began to thud so loud he could hear it over the distant clink of carriages on the street. The waiting was over, at last.
