Four days hence, Romola set forth for the dwelling of Piero di Cosimo in the Via Gualfonda. The streets she traversed were thick with a tense, mutual scrutiny: Frenchmen gazing upon Florence with an air of arrogant discovery, and Florentines staring back with a mixture of resentment and wary observation. The atmosphere was far from cordial. Having initially played the gracious host, the city now found itself weary of its "too superior" guests. The French, having tasted the fruits of conquest elsewhere, now viewed Florence through the lens of imperial entitlement. They spoke of leaving a viceroy behind and restoring the Medici, treating the republic as a conquered prize despite the singular logic of their claim—that the only crime of Florence was the policy of a disowned man. The Florentines, however, were resolved not to bow. This defiance rippled through the Old Palace in heated councils and manifested in the defiant banners fluttering in the piazzas, turning the thoroughfares into a perilous promenade for ladies of birth. Yet, Romola, cloaked in her heavy black veil and mantle, accompanied by the steady presence of old Maso, felt a shield against the prying eyes of the crowd.

Her haste to reach the painter's studio was born of a specific, gnawing anxiety. A copy of her father's likeness, painted by Piero in the guise of Oedipus, remained unfinished. Piero was a man of fickle devotion; when the demand was not absolute, he would abandon a canvas for months, tucking it away in a corner or coffer until it risked total oblivion. Though Romola was his favorite and he inclined to grant her wishes, no general goodwill could guard against his sudden whims. He had promised completion within the week, and Romola's nerves frayed with the thought of losing the only existing image of her father in his days of blindness. To lose that image was to lose a vital thread of memory, and the guilt of such potential neglect drove her to vigilance. Love, she knew, demanded more than mere affection; it required perfect loyalty of heart, aiming for its own completeness through the preservation of the beloved's true form.

By special favor, she was permitted to enter without prior warning. She lifted the iron slide and called out in a flute-like tone, mimicking the little maiden with the eggs who once visited Titus. Piero answered quickly, though his explanation upon opening the door offered no compliment.

"Ah, Madonna Romola, is it you?" he exclaimed. "I thought my eggs had arrived; I wanted them."

"I have brought you something superior to hard eggs, Piero," Romola replied, smiling as she drew back her veil. She took the basket from Maso and stepped inside. "I know you appreciate these treats when they come without trouble. Confess, do you?"

"Yes, when they arrive as effortlessly as the light," Piero admitted, folding his arms and gazing down at the sweetmeats as Romola uncovered them. He then lifted his eyes to her face and hair, his gaze sharpening with a painter's admiration as her hood, weighed down by the veil, fell backward. "But I suspect you bring these sweets to stop my mouth while you scold me. Very well, proceed to the next room; you will find I have made progress on the picture, though it remains incomplete. I take care never to promise what I cannot guarantee:"

The door leading to the wild garden stood shut, and the painter was already at work. However, he was not attending to Romola's commission. That portrait stood propped against the wall, and Piero stooped to lift it, intending to move it into the proper light. In shifting the canvas, he inadvertently revealed another—a small oil sketch of Tito, upon which he had made significant additions only days prior. It was diminutive compared to the other works, standing far within the frame, and Piero, prone to forgetfulness regarding his own arrangements, was unaware of what he had exposed. As he peered at a detail in the painting he held, preparing to place it on an easel, Romola gasped, her face flushing with astonishment.

"That is Tito!"

Piero turned, offering a silent shrug, vexed by his own lapse in memory.

Still staring at the sketch in bewilderment, Romola turned to the painter, her voice tinged with puzzled alarm. "What a strange picture! When did you paint this? What does it signify?"

"A mere fancy," Piero said, removing his skull-cap and scratching his head, adopting the familiar grimace used to mask deeper emotions. "I required a handsome young face for it, and your husband's was the ideal choice."

He moved forward, stooping to the picture. With his back to Romola, he pretended to conduct a final inspection before setting it aside as unworthy of display. But Romola, recalling the fact of the chain-armour and struck by the eerie coincidence linking Tito to the imagery of fear, stepped to his elbow.

"Do not put it away; let me look again. That man with the rope around his neck—I saw him. I saw you approach him in the Duomo. What compelled you to place him in a picture with Tito?"

Seeing no better recourse than partial truth, Piero hesitated. "It was an accident. The man was fleeing—running up the steps—and caught hold of your husband; I suppose he stumbled. I happened to be there and saw it. I thought the savage-looking old fellow made a fitting subject. But it is worthless, merely a freakish daub of mine." He spoke with contempt, moving the sketch away with decisive air and placing it on a high shelf. "Come and view the Oedipus."

He had displayed too much anxiety in concealing the sketch, inadvertently confirming the very unease he sought to suppress—that there existed something unpleasant, something disadvantageous to Tito, in the circumstances surrounding the painting. This impression silenced Romola; her pride and delicacy recoiled from further questioning, fearing that inquiries might imply even a whisper of suspicion against her husband. In a quiet tone, she merely asked, "He was a strange, pitiful-looking prisoner. Do you know anything more of him?"

"No more," Piero replied. "I showed him the way to the hospital, that is all. Now, observe the face of Oedipus; it is nearly finished. Tell me your thoughts."

Romola gave her full attention to the portrait of her father, standing in long silence before it.

"Ah," she finally said, "you have achieved what I desired. You have captured the listening look." Turning to him with bright, moist eyes, she added, "My good Piero, I am deeply grateful."

"Now that is what I cannot bear in you women," Piero retorted impatiently, kicking aside the clutter on the floor. "You pour out feelings where there is no cause. Why should you be grateful for a picture you paid for, especially when I make you wait? If I paint, it is for my own pleasure and credit to execute it well. Are you to thank a man for not being a rogue or a fool? It suffices if he thanks Messer Domeneddio for granting him such a nature. But women imagine walls are held together with honey."

"You crusty Piero! I forgot how snappish you are. Here, take this crisp sweetmeat," Romola said, smiling through her tears and offering a treat from the basket.

Piero accepted it with the grace of a proverbial bear dreaming of pears—truly enjoying the gift, yet accustomed to concealing his pleasures and pains beneath a shaggy exterior. "It is good, Madonna Antigone," he remarked, reaching for another. He had subsisted on hard eggs for two weeks. Romola stood opposite, her fresh anxiety momentarily suspended by the sight of this naive enjoyment.

"Good-bye, Piero," she said eventually, setting down the basket. "I promise not to thank you if you finish the portrait soon and well; I shall simply tell you that you were bound to do it for your own credit."

"Good," Piero replied curtly, assisting her with deftness to fold her mantle and veil.

Once the door closed behind her, Piero thought, *I am glad she asked no more questions about that sketch. I should be sorry for her to guess that I considered her fine husband a good model for a coward. But I made light of it; she will not think of it again.*

Piero was too sanguine, a common failing of open-hearted men attempting clever simulation. Yet, the thought of the picture pressed increasingly upon Romola as she walked homeward. She could not help weaving together the two threads of the chain-armour and the encounter Piero described between her husband and the prisoner, both occurring on the morning the armor was adopted. Had Tito perceived that look of terror in the painter's eyes? What could it all signify?

"It means nothing," she tried to assure herself. "It was a mere coincidence. Shall I ask Tito about it?" Her mind concluded, *No: I will not question him about anything he did not reveal spontaneously. It is an offense against the trust I owe him.* Yet her heart whispered, *I dare not ask him.*

There was a terrible flaw in that trust; she feared any hasty movement, just as men who hold something precious and desperately wish to believe it remains unbroken often hesitate to test its integrity.
