Delivered into the keeping of the maîtresse, I was guided down a long, narrow corridor that smelt of soap and scoured stone, and so into a kitchen foreign to my English sense of kitchens—spotless, chill, and oddly contrived. At first glance there seemed neither hearth nor oven; no red mouth of fire, no familiar grate with its homely hiss and crackle. In one corner, instead, squatted a great black furnace—mute as a closed organ, yet potent; its iron mass held heat as a chest holds treasure. I had not yet learned to read such machinery; to me it looked like an engine that might warm, but could not cook.

Perhaps it was mere fatigue; perhaps the first faint whisper of pride (I did not like to name it) stirred when I felt relief that I was not left amongst the pots and pails, as I half expected, but led onward into a small inner room called a cabinet. A cook—jacketed, short-skirted, her feet in sabots that clapped the flags with a blunt wooden report—brought my supper and set it down with brisk exactness.

It was plain fare, yet it spoke to hunger in a language no dialect can quite mistranslate. There was meat, of what creature I could not tell, bathed in a sauce sharp with some acid grace—vinegar, perhaps, tempered by sugar; a taste that bit and then softened, like a reprimand followed by pardon. There were potatoes chopped small, made savoury by that same mingling of sweet and sour and something I could not name—mustard? a pinch of spice?—and there was a tartine, bread spread with butter thin enough to show thrift yet thick enough to console. For dessert, a baked pear, its skin puckered, its flesh yielding and perfumed, the syrup clinging to the spoon like a last, gentle promise. I ate because I was hungry; I was grateful because the world, all day, had been a door shutting and opening without my consent, and here at least was food placed within reach.

After the prière du soir, Madame herself came to look at me again. She desired I should follow her upstairs. We went by a warren of little dormitories—later I heard they had once been nuns’ cells—each narrow room holding its bed like an afterthought, each wall breathing old restraint. We crossed an oratory, long and low and dim, where a pale crucifix hung against the wall and two tapers kept their small, steady vigils. The house had its own soundscape: not the free racket of English corridors, but a measured hush, broken by the faint creak of boards and the remote murmur of girls settling like birds in roosts. Even my own steps seemed too loud, as though the building listened.

Madame conducted me at last to a chamber where three children slept in three little beds. A heated stove made the air oppressive; and, as if heat were not enough, the room was thick with a scent strong rather than delicate—surprising, almost insolent under such a nursery roof. It was perfume, yes, but fouled: smoke married to some spirituous essence; the unmistakable breath of whisky, clinging to curtains and bedclothes as a foreign tongue clings to the mouth that has tried to speak another.

By a table where the stump of a candle guttered in its socket sat a coarse woman, asleep in a chair. Her dress was broad-striped, showy silk, but over it she wore a stuff apron—finery and drudgery stitched together without shame. Beside her elbow stood a bottle and an empty glass. The bottle did not look like a mere object; it seemed an uninvited guest that had been allowed to sit at the nursery table, and now claimed precedence over duty.

Madame Beck contemplated this tableau with a calm so complete it was like a rule of nature. She neither smiled nor frowned; no anger wrinkled her brow, no disgust tightened her mouth. She did not even wake the woman. With serene precision she pointed out a fourth bed, intimating it was to be mine; then she extinguished the candle, substituted a night-lamp, and glided through an inner door which she left ajar—the entrance to her own chamber, a large, well-furnished apartment discernible through the slit of darkness.

My devotions that night were all thanksgiving. Strangely had I been led since morning; unexpectedly had I been provided for. It was scarcely credible that not forty-eight hours had elapsed since I had left London under no other guardianship than that which protects the passenger-bird—no prospect but the dubious cloud-tracery of hope.

I am a light sleeper. In the dead of night I woke suddenly. All was hushed, and yet a presence altered the air: a white figure stood in the room—Madame in her night-dress. She moved without perceptible sound, as though she belonged to the house’s own breathing. She visited the three children; she approached my bed. I feigned sleep and felt her gaze, steady as lamplight on a page.

A small pantomime followed, curious enough. She sat, I believe, a quarter of an hour on the edge of my bed, studying my face as one studies a specimen—quiet, intent, not unkind, and not indulgent. Then she bent nearer, raised my cap slightly, and turned back the border to expose my hair; she looked at my hand lying on the bedclothes, as if the shape of fingers could tell its own history. After that she went to the chair where my clothes lay at the foot of the bed.

I heard the faint rustle of fabric under careful hands. Curiosity overcame prudence: I opened my eyes a little, and watched, cautious as a thief watching another thief. Her taste for research led her far. She inspected each article; I divined her motive—to judge the wearer by the garments: station, means, neatness, habits. The end might be reasonable; the means were not fair. In my dress was a pocket; she turned it inside out. She counted the money in my purse. She opened a little memorandum-book and coolly perused its contents, as though my private notes were a school register. Between the leaves she found a small plaited lock of Miss Marchmont’s grey hair and drew it forth, holding it a moment as if to weigh even memory.

My three keys—those of trunk, desk, and work-box—received special attention. With these she withdrew to her own room. I raised myself slightly and followed her with my eye through the half-open door: the keys were not brought back until they had left, upon her toilet within, the impress of their wards in wax. It was done decently and in order, like any other domestic arrangement—bread weighed, coffee measured, linen tallied. When all was finished, she replaced everything; my clothes were refolded with care, my property returned to its place.

What conclusions did she draw? Were they favourable? Her face—stone in its night aspect—betrayed nothing. Human, and even motherly, it had seemed earlier in the salon; now it was an instrument, not a countenance.

Her duty done—for in her eyes it was duty—she rose, noiseless as a shadow, and moved towards her own chamber. At the door she turned and fixed her eye upon the sleeper in the chair, who still snored with the loud abandon of those who have drowned shame. Mrs. Svini, I presumed—Anglicé or Hibernicé, Sweeny. In Madame Beck’s eye lay her doom: an immutable purpose, unhurried but sure. Madame’s visitations for shortcomings might be slow; they were certain. All this was very un-English; truly, I was in a foreign land.

The morrow made me further acquainted with Mrs. Sweeny. She had introduced herself as an English lady in reduced circumstances, a native, she said, of Middlesex, speaking the tongue with “the purest metropolitan accent.” Madame—reliant upon her own infallible expedients for finding the truth in time—had a singular intrepidity in hiring service off-hand, as was abundantly proved in my own case. Mrs. Sweeny had been received as nursery-governess to the three children.

She was, in effect, Irish; her exact station I do not pretend to fix. She boldly declared she had “had the bringing-up of the son and daughter of a marquis.” I thought she might have been hanger-on, nurse, fosterer, washerwoman—anything that brings one near the nursery without granting entry to gentility. She spoke a smothered tongue, curiously overlaid with mincing cockney inflections; it was as if her native idiom had been pressed down, half-suffocated beneath a purchased accent, the way a strong smell is masked with cheap scent—never truly gone, only more offensive for the disguise.

By some means she had acquired and maintained a wardrobe of suspicious splendour: stiff, costly silks that fitted her indifferently, as though made for other proportions; caps with real lace borders; and, chief of all, the spell by which she struck a certain awe through the household—quelling teachers and servants who would otherwise have scorned her, and even influencing Madame herself so long as those broad shoulders wore its folds—an actual Indian shawl, “un véritable cachemire,” as Madame Beck said with reverence and amaze. I am sure that without this cachemire she would not have kept her footing in the pensionnat two days; by virtue of it, and it only, she maintained herself a month.

But when Mrs. Sweeny learned I had come to fill her shoes, then she declared herself. Then she rose on Madame Beck in her full power; then she came down on me with her concentrated weight. Madame bore this revelation and visitation so well—so stonily—that for very shame I could not support it otherwise than with composure. For one little moment Madame Beck absented herself from the room; ten minutes after, an agent of the police stood among us. Mrs. Sweeny and her effects were removed. Madame’s brow had not been ruffled during the scene; her lips had not dropped one sharply accented word.

This brisk affair of dismissal was all settled before breakfast: order to march given, policeman called, mutineer expelled; chambre d’enfans fumigated and cleansed, windows thrown open; every trace of the accomplished Mrs. Sweeny—down to the fine essence and spiritual fragrance which gave token so subtle and so fatal of her head and front offending—annihilated from the Rue Fossette. All was done between the moment Madame Beck issued, Aurora-like, from her chamber, and that in which she coolly sat down to pour out her first cup of coffee.

The coffee itself—dark, bitter, and aromatic—rose in a thin steam that carried the room’s morning hush upon it. I noticed, as one does when newly placed, the small acoustics of the meal: porcelain clink, spoon’s soft tap, the restrained murmur of household movement. It was a disciplined soundscape, and, after the night’s snores and the bottle’s silent boasting, it felt like cleanliness rendered audible.

About noon, I was summoned to dress Madame. It appeared my place was to be a hybrid between gouvernante and lady’s-maid. Till noon she haunted the house in wrapping-gown, shawl, and soundless slippers—souliers de silence. How would the lady-chief of an English school have approved this custom? In England, even ease likes to be noisy with self-importance; here, authority padded like a cat.

The dressing of her hair puzzled me. She had plenty of it—auburn, unmixed with grey, though she was forty years old. Seeing my embarrassment, she asked, “You have not been a femme-de-chambre in your own country?” And taking the brush from my hand, setting me aside—not ungently, not disrespectfully—she arranged it herself. In other offices of the toilet she half-directed, half-aided me, without the least display of temper or impatience. That was the first and last time I was required to dress her; henceforth Rosine, the portress, performed that duty.

When attired, Madame Beck appeared a personage: rather short and stout, yet graceful in her own way—the grace that comes of proportion, of parts that suit each other and have been trained to obey. Her complexion was fresh and sanguine, not too red; her eye blue and serene; her dark silk dress fitted her as only a French sempstress can make a dress fit. She looked well, though a little bourgeoise, as bourgeoise indeed she was. Harmony pervaded her whole person; and yet her face offered contrast: the outlines were stern, the forehead high but narrow, expressing capacity and some benevolence, but no expanse. Her peaceful, watchful eye never knew the fire kindled in the heart, nor the softness that flows thence. Her mouth was hard; it could be grim; her lips were thin. For sensibility and genius, with their tenderness and temerity, I felt somehow she would be the right sort of Minos in petticoats.

In the long run I found she was something else in petticoats too. Her name was Modeste Maria Beck, née Kint: it ought to have been Ignacia. She was charitable and did much good. There never was a mistress whose rule was milder. She had never once remonstrated, I was told, with the intolerable Mrs. Sweeny, despite tipsiness, disorder, neglect; yet Mrs. Sweeny went the moment her departure became convenient. I was told, too, that neither masters nor teachers were found fault with; yet masters and teachers were often changed. They vanished and others filled their places; none could well explain how.

The establishment was both pensionnat and externat: the day-pupils exceeded one hundred; the boarders were about a score. Madame must have possessed high administrative powers. She ruled the pupils, four teachers, eight masters, six servants, and her three children; she managed, at the same time, parents and friends—without apparent effort, without bustle, fatigue, fever, or any symptom of undue excitement. Occupied she always was—busy, rarely. She had a system for managing this mass of machinery, a pretty system: the reader has already seen a specimen in that nocturnal business of my pocket and my book. “Surveillance,” “espionage”—these were her watchwords.

Still, Madame knew what honesty was, and liked it—when it did not obtrude clumsy scruples in the way of her will and interest. She respected Angleterre; and as to les Anglaises, she would have the women of no other country about her children, if she could help it. Often in the evening, after plotting and counter-plotting, spying and receiving the reports of spies all day, she would come up to my room with a trace of real weariness on her brow, and sit down to listen while the children said their little prayers to me in English. The Lord’s Prayer, and the hymn beginning “Gentle Jesus,” these little Catholics were permitted to repeat at my knee. When I had put them to bed, she would talk to me—my French soon enough serving for understanding and reply—about England and Englishwomen, and the reasons for what she termed their superior intelligence and more reliable probity.

Very sound sense she often showed. She seemed to know that keeping girls in distrustful restraint, in blind ignorance, under a surveillance that left them no corner for retirement, was not the best way to make them honest and modest women. Yet she averred that ruinous consequences would ensue if any other method were tried with continental children: they were so accustomed to restraint that relaxation, however guarded, would be misunderstood and fatally presumed on. She declared herself sick of the means she had to use, but use them she must. After discoursing—often with dignity and delicacy—she would move away on her souliers de silence and glide, ghost-like, through the house, watching and spying everywhere: peering through every keyhole, listening behind every door.

And all the while the house kept its own murmur—doors breathing on hinges, skirts brushing stair-edges, the soft stamp of many young feet in unison. In such a place, sound was authority’s ally; silence, its net. I could not help thinking how health and happiness depend on the noises one is allowed, and the noises one is denied—how the mind withers when every honest exclamation is made suspect, and every whisper rewarded.

After all, Madame’s system was not bad—justice requires it. Nothing could be better than her arrangements for physical well-being. No minds were overtasked; lessons were well distributed and made easy; there was liberty of amusement and provision for exercise that kept the girls healthy. The food was abundant and good; neither pale nor puny faces were seen in the Rue Fossette. She never grudged a holiday; she allowed plenty of time for sleeping, dressing, washing, eating. Her method in these matters was easy, liberal, salutary, rational—many an austere English school-mistress would do well to imitate it, and many would gladly do so if exacting parents would let them.

As she ruled by espionage, she had her staff of spies. She knew the quality of the tools she used: she would not scruple to handle the dirtiest for a dirty occasion, flinging them aside like squeezed rind when the juice was got; yet she could be fastidious in seeking pure metal for clean uses, and when once a bloodless, rustless instrument was found, she was careful of it, keeping it in silk and cotton-wool. Still—woe to the man or woman who relied on her one inch beyond the point where it was her interest to be trustworthy. Interest was the master-key of her nature—the mainspring of her motives—the alpha and omega of her life. I have seen her feelings appealed to, and have smiled, half in pity and half in scorn. None ever gained her ear through that channel. To attempt to touch her heart was the surest way to rouse her antipathy and make of her a secret foe: it proved to her she had no heart to be touched; it reminded her where she was impotent and dead.

Never was the distinction between charity and mercy better exemplified. While devoid of sympathy, she had rational benevolence: she would give readily to people she had never seen—rather to classes than individuals. Pour les pauvres she opened her purse freely; against the poor man, as a rule, she kept it closed. In schemes for society at large she took cheerful part; no private sorrow touched her. No concentrated suffering in one heart had power to pierce hers. Not the agony in Gethsemane, not the death on Calvary, could have wrung from her eyes one tear.

Madame was a very great and capable woman. The school offered too limited a sphere; she ought to have swayed a nation, led a turbulent legislative assembly. Nobody could have browbeaten her, irritated her nerves, exhausted her patience, over-reached her astuteness. In her single person she could have comprised the duties of first minister and superintendent of police. Wise, firm, faithless; secret, crafty, passionless; watchful and inscrutable; acute and insensate—perfectly decorous—what more could be desired?

The sensible reader will not suppose I gained all this knowledge in one month, or half-year. No. At first I saw only the thriving outside of a flourishing educational establishment: a great house full of healthy, lively girls, all well dressed, many handsome, gaining knowledge by a marvellously easy method without painful exertion or useless waste of spirits; not making very rapid progress perhaps, but always employed, never oppressed. I saw a corps of teachers and masters more stringently tasked—real head-labour being theirs, to spare the pupils—yet arranged to relieve each other. I saw, in short, a foreign school, whose life, movement, variety made it a charming contrast to many English institutions.

Behind the house lay a large garden, and in summer the pupils almost lived outdoors among rose-bushes and fruit-trees. Under a vast berceau draped with vine, Madame would take her seat on summer afternoons and send for classes in turn to sit round her and sew and read. Masters came and went, delivering short lively lectures rather than lessons; the pupils made notes—or did not—secure that, if neglect occurred, they could copy a companion’s page. Besides regular monthly jours de sortie, Catholic fête-days brought holidays all year; and sometimes on a bright morning or soft evening the boarders were taken for a long walk into the country, regaled with gaufres and vin blanc, or new milk and pain bis, or pistolets au beurre and coffee.

These treats had their own aromas and little rites: warm waffles cracking under sugar; rolls split and buttered so the heat made them sweat; coffee poured in haste but with practised hand. Even I, who stood apart, felt that sustenance here was not merely fuel but a ceremony—an old, domestic myth that repeats itself and makes a household feel like a people. Yet beneath the pleasant surface ran the other current: language taught and language lost, privacy thinned to nothing, the mind disciplined not only by rules but by the very words permitted it. What cannot be said cannot be wholly remembered.

Thus the view appeared, seen through distance. But a time came when distance melted for me—when I was called down from my watch-tower of the nursery, where I had hitherto observed, and compelled into closer intercourse with this little world of the Rue Fossette.

One day I sat upstairs, as usual, hearing the children their English lessons while, at the same time, turning a silk dress for Madame. The fabric slid under my fingers with that peculiar whisper silk makes—like dry leaves rubbing together—and the sound soothed me more than it ought. Madame came sauntering into the room with the absorbed air and hard brow she sometimes wore, which made her look so little genial. Dropping into a seat opposite mine, she remained some minutes silent. Désirée, the eldest, was reading to me some little essay of Mrs. Barbauld’s, and I made her translate currently from English to French as she proceeded, to ascertain comprehension. Madame listened.

Presently, without preface, almost in the tone of accusation, she said, “Meess, in England you were a governess?”

“No, Madame,” I answered, smiling; “you are mistaken.”

“Is this your first essay at teaching—this attempt with my children?”

I assured her it was. Again she became silent; but looking up as I took a pin from the cushion, I found myself an object of study. She held me under her eye; she seemed turning me round in her thoughts—measuring my fitness for a purpose, weighing my value in a plan. She had scrutinized all I had, and believed herself cognizant of much that I was; but from that day, for about a fortnight, she tried me by new tests. She listened at the nursery door when I was shut in with the children; she followed at cautious distance when I walked out with them, stealing within earshot whenever trees of park or boulevard afforded a screen. A strict preliminary process observed, she made a move forward.

One morning she came upon me abruptly with the semblance of hurry and said she found herself in a little dilemma. Mr. Wilson, the English master, had failed to come at his hour; she feared he was ill. The pupils were waiting in classe; there was no one to give a lesson. Would I, for once, object to giving a short dictation exercise, so that the pupils might not say they had missed their English lesson?

“In classe, Madame?” I asked.

“Yes, in classe: in the second division.”

“Where there are sixty pupils,” said I; for I knew the number, and with my usual base habit of cowardice, I shrank into my sloth like a snail into its shell, alleging incapacity and impracticability as a pretext to escape action. If left to myself, I should infallibly have let the chance slip. Inadventurous, unstirred by practical ambition, I was capable of sitting twenty years teaching infants the hornbook, turning silk dresses, making children’s frocks. Not that contentment dignified this resignation: my work had neither charm for my taste nor hold on my interest; but it seemed a great thing to be without heavy anxiety, relieved from intimate trial. The negation of severe suffering was the nearest approach to happiness I expected. Besides, I seemed to hold two lives—the life of thought and that of reality; and provided the former was nourished with a sufficiency of strange necromantic joys of fancy, the privileges of the latter might remain limited to daily bread, hourly work, and a roof of shelter.

“Come,” said Madame, as I stooped more busily than ever over the cutting-out of a child’s pinafore, “leave that work.”

“But Fifine wants it, Madame.”

“Fifine must want it, then, for I want you.”
