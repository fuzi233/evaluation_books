For a handful of breaths after that awful laughter died away, Tars Tarkas and I held the silence as though it were a weapon—tight-gripped, ready to be turned. Nothing else stirred. The air lay still in the rock-hewn chamber, tasting of old dust and the faint, sharp tang that radium light always seems to leave upon the tongue, like metal kissed too long.

At last the great Thark gave one of those low, rumbling laughs his people loose in the face of terror—not hysteria, but a hard-edged relish, as though horror were a spice and they had been bred to crave it. I had seen them laugh so, green giants rolling in mirth while the Great Games wrung screams from women and children. It was the same sound now, though quieter, as if even he measured his breath in this place.

I forced a smile, because there are moments when a steady mouth is a better shield than a trembling jaw.

“What do you make of it?” I asked. “Where in the deuce are we?”

He stared as though I had said the sky was underground.

“Where are we?” he repeated. “Do you tell me, John Carter, that you know not where you be?”

“That I am upon Barsoom is all I can guess,” I said. “But for you—and the great white apes—I should scarcely even guess that. The sights of this day are as strange to me as though I had walked out of my own grave and found another planet in Mars’s place. No, Tars Tarkas, I know not where we be.”

He drew a deep breath, and in it I heard the dry rasp of the chamber’s silence answering him.

“Where have you been,” he demanded, “since you opened the mighty portals of the atmosphere plant—years ago—after the keeper had died and the engines stopped and all Barsoom was dying, that had not already died, of asphyxiation? Your body even was never found, though a whole world sought it. Though the Jeddak of Helium and his granddaughter—your princess—offered such rewards that even princes hunted for you.

“There was but one conclusion when all failed: that you had taken the long, last pilgrimage down the mysterious River Iss, to await in the Valley Dor upon the shores of the Lost Sea of Korus the beautiful Dejah Thoris.

“Why you had gone none could guess, for your princess still lived—”

“Thank God,” I broke in, and the words came up out of me like water from a struck spring. “I did not dare ask you. I feared I might have been too late. She was very low when I left her in the royal gardens of Tardos Mors that night—so low I scarcely hoped even then to reach the atmosphere plant before her dear spirit fled from me forever. And she lives yet?”

“She lives, John Carter.”

The relief ran through me so keenly it was almost pain. For a heartbeat the radium-lit gold of the walls seemed warmer, as though the chamber itself had softened.

“You have not told me where we are,” I said, clinging to the one practical thread left to me.

“We are where I expected to find you,” he answered, and his voice turned heavy, like a blade held point-down. “And another. Long ago you heard the story of the woman who taught me the thing green Martians are reared to hate—the woman who taught me to love. You know the tortures and the death her love won at the hands of Tal Hajus.

“She, I thought, awaited me by the Lost Sea of Korus.

“You know it was left for a man from another world—you—to teach this Thark what friendship is; and you, I thought, also roamed the care-free Valley Dor.

“Thus were the two I most longed for at the end of the pilgrimage I must take some day. And so, as the time elapsed which Dejah Thoris had hoped might bring you once more to her side—since she has always tried to believe you had but temporarily returned to your own planet—I at last gave way to my yearning. A month since I started upon the journey the end of which you have this day witnessed. Do you understand now where you be, John Carter?”

“And that was the River Iss,” I said slowly, “emptying into the Lost Sea of Korus in the Valley Dor?”

“This is the valley of love and peace and rest to which every Barsoomian since time immemorial has longed to pilgrimage at the end of a life of hate and strife and bloodshed,” he replied. Then, with a cold, iron twist that tasted like ash: “This, John Carter, is Heaven.”

The bitterness in it was not loud. It did not need to be. It was the bitterness of a man whose cradle-songs had promised sweetness and whose mouth had met only poison.

I laid my hand upon his shoulder, the tough hide of his harness warm under my palm.

“I am sorry,” I said, because there was nothing else that did not sound like mockery.

“Think,” he said, “of the countless billions who have taken the voluntary pilgrimage down that cruel river since the beginning of time, only to fall into the ferocious clutches of the creatures that assailed us to-day.

“There is an ancient legend that once a red man returned from the banks of Korus—returned from Dor—back through the River Iss. The legend says he told a blasphemy: that horrid brutes lived in a valley of wondrous loveliness, brutes that pounced upon each Barsoomian as he ended his pilgrimage and devoured him upon the banks where he had looked to find love and peace. But the ancients killed the blasphemer, as tradition ordains any shall be killed who return from the bosom of the River of Mystery.

“But now we know it was no blasphemy. The man told only what he saw. And what does it profit us? Even should we escape, we would be treated as blasphemers. We are between the wild thoat of certainty and the mad zitidar of fact—we can escape neither.”

“As Earth men say,” I replied, and could not help the thin smile the phrase pulled from me, “we are between the devil and the deep sea, Tars Tarkas.”

“There is naught to do,” he said, “but take things as they come. At least we shall have the satisfaction of knowing that whoever slays us will count far more of their own dead than they gain in return. White ape or plant man, green Barsoomian or red man—whosoever takes the last toll from us will know it is costly in lives to wipe out John Carter, Prince of the House of Tardos Mors, and Tars Tarkas, Jeddak of Thark, at the same time.”

The grim boast had a sturdy comfort in it; I laughed, and he joined me—one of those rare, clean laughs of his that sounded almost human.

“But about yourself,” he said at length, the merriment dropping away like a cloak. “If you have not been here all these years—where have you been? How is it that I find you here to-day?”

“I have been back to Earth,” I answered. “For ten long Earth years I have prayed and hoped for the day that would carry me once more to this grim old planet of yours—for which, with all its cruel customs, I feel a bond of sympathy and love even greater than for the world that gave me birth.

“For ten years I have endured a living death of doubt as to whether Dejah Thoris lived. And now, the first time my doubt is relieved, fate hurls me into the one tiny spot of all Barsoom from which there is apparently no escape—or if there be escape, at a price that would snuff out forever the last flickering hope of seeing my princess again in this life. And you have seen to-day with what pitiful futility man yearns toward a material hereafter.

“Only a bare half-hour before I saw you battling with the plant men I stood in moonlight upon the banks of a broad river that taps the eastern shore of Earth’s most blessed land. I have answered you. Do you believe?”

“I believe,” said Tars Tarkas, “though I cannot understand.”

While we spoke, my eyes had been ranging over the chamber. It ran perhaps two hundred feet in length and half as broad. In the center of the wall opposite our entrance stood what appeared to be another doorway.

The room had been hewn from cliff material that dulled to a muted gold under the dim, steady glow of a single radium illuminator set in the roof’s center. Here and there, polished patches of ruby, emerald, and diamond caught and threw back the light like hard, cold candies in a dull loaf—bright flashes embedded in something otherwise lifeless. The floor was of a different substance, worn by long use until it had the smoothness and slick gleam of glass underfoot.

No other openings showed. Since the door behind us was locked, I went to the one ahead.

As I stretched my hand toward the controlling button, that cruel, mocking laugh rang out again—so close this time that my body drew back before my mind could order it, and my fingers tightened on my sword hilt.

Then from the far corner of the chamber a hollow voice began to chant, each phrase falling with the same dull insistence as a pestle striking mortar:

“There is no hope, there is no hope; the dead return not, the dead return not; nor is there any resurrection. Hope not, for there is no hope.”

We snapped our gazes toward the sound. No figure waited there. The emptiness itself seemed to speak. A cold ripple ran down my spine; the short hairs at my neck lifted as a hound’s will when it scents what it cannot see.

I crossed quickly to the spot, but the voice ceased before I reached the wall, and then from the opposite end of the chamber a second voice burst out, shrill and piercing, like a knife drawn across glass:

“Fools! Fools! Thinkest thou to defeat the eternal laws of life and death? Wouldst cheat the mysterious Issus, Goddess of Death, of her just dues? Did not her mighty messenger, the ancient Iss, bear you upon her leaden bosom at your own behest to the Valley Dor?

“Thinkest thou, O fools, that Issus wilt give up her own? Thinkest thou to escape from whence in all the countless ages but a single soul has fled?

“Go back the way thou camest, to the merciful maws of the children of the Tree of Life or the gleaming fangs of the great white apes, for there lies speedy surcease from suffering; but insist in your rash purpose to thread the mazes of the Golden Cliffs of the Mountains of Otz, past the ramparts of the impregnable fortresses of the Holy Therns, and upon your way Death in its most frightful form will overtake you—a death so horrible that even the Holy Therns themselves, who conceived both Life and Death, avert their eyes from its fiendishness and close their ears against the hideous shrieks of its victims.

“Go back, O fools, the way thou camest.”

And again the laughter—now here, now there—skittered through the chamber like something alive.

“Most uncanny,” I said.

“What shall we do?” Tars Tarkas asked. “We cannot fight empty air. I would almost sooner return and face foes into whose flesh I may feel my blade bite—know I am selling my carcass dearly—before I go down to that eternal oblivion which is evidently the fairest eternity mortal man may hope for.”

“If, as you say, we cannot fight empty air,” I replied, “neither can empty air fight us. I, who have faced and conquered thousands of warriors and tempered blades, shall not be turned back by wind; nor no more shall you, Thark.”

“But unseen voices may come from unseen creatures who wield invisible blades,” he insisted.

“Rot,” I snapped. “Those voices come from beings as real as you or I. In their veins flows blood that may be let. The fact they remain invisible is proof enough they are mortal—and not over-courageous mortals at that. Think you John Carter will fly at the first shriek of a coward who dares not come out and face a good blade?”

I spoke loudly on purpose, so our tormentors might hear. I was weary of being played upon like a stringed instrument—twitch here, tremble there, dance to a disembodied laugh. And too, it had occurred to me that the whole performance was meant to drive us back to the valley of death we had escaped, where the plant men and apes might finish us more conveniently.

A long silence followed.

Then a soft, stealthy sound behind me—more felt than heard, like the faintest scrape of a claw on stone—made me whirl.

A banth was creeping upon me, sinuous and low.

The banth is a great beast of prey from the low hills around the dead seas of ancient Mars. Like most Barsoomian beasts it is nearly hairless save for a bristly mane about its thick neck. Its long lithe body runs on ten powerful legs. Its enormous jaws bear several rows of needle fangs, and the mouth splits back almost to its tiny ears. Its eyes—bulging, green, coldly bright—give the final touch of nightmare.

It lashed its tail against its yellow sides; and when it saw itself discovered it loosed that roar which freezes lesser creatures for the instant it springs.

It sprang.

But the voice that would have paralyzed prey had no hold on me. It met steel. My long-sword took it full, and a heartbeat later I drew the blade free from its still heart.

Turning, I saw Tars Tarkas facing another such monster. He dispatched it with one heavy, sure stroke, and before the carcass finished shuddering, instinct—some guardian something that has served me more than once—pulled my head around again.

Another beast was already leaping across the chamber.

From then on, for the better part of an hour, one hideous creature after another was loosed upon us—banths and other savage denizens of the Martian wilds—springing as though from empty air.

Tars Tarkas grew almost content. Here at last was an enemy he could feel, a throat to cut, a bone to cleave. For my part, the diversion was a marked improvement over ghost-voices and laughter. These things bled. Their rage and pain were honest in sound. Their death stank as real death does—hot blood and torn meat—filling the golden chamber with the harsh animal musk of slaughter.

And yet the manner of their coming was too neatly staged. I noticed that they appeared only when our backs were turned. We never saw one truly materialize. I never once allowed myself to believe in any supernatural birth from nothing. There had to be a concealed entrance—well contrived, well fitted to the golden stone.

Among the ornaments of Tars Tarkas’ leather harness—his only clothing besides capes and robes for the cold—hung a small mirror midway down his back, about the size of a lady’s hand glass.

Once, as he stood looking down at a newly fallen brute, my gaze caught that mirror’s shine. And in it I saw something that made my breath hitch and my voice drop to a thread.

“Move not, Tars Tarkas! Move not a muscle!”

He did not ask why. He simply froze like a carved idol while I stared into the mirror as though it were a window.

In its bright surface I saw a section of wall behind me shift—a precise turn upon pivots. With it, a section of floor in front of it turned as well, moving together so cleanly that no seam had shown in the dim light. It was as though one set a visiting-card upon its edge on a silver dollar: the card the wall, the coin the floor, both turning in one smooth motion.

Halfway through the turn, a great beast was revealed sitting upon its haunches on the moving floor—waiting like a weapon in a hand. When the section stopped, the beast faced toward me, now on our side of the partition.

Simple—once you saw it.

But what struck deeper than the mechanism was what the half-open partition revealed beyond: a larger chamber, well lighted, where several men and women were chained to the wall. In front of them, close to the pivoting device, stood a wicked-faced man—not red like the red men, nor green like the green men, but white like myself, with a great mass of flowing yellow hair.

Behind him, chained prisoners—red Martians. With them, chained beasts: the very sort that had been turned upon us, and others as savage.

When I turned back to meet the next foe, my heart was lighter than it had been since the laughter began.

“Watch the wall at your end,” I whispered to Tars Tarkas when we were close enough. “It is through secret doorways that the brutes are loosed.”

I spoke low so the watchers would not learn we knew; yet I was certain they did watch. No further attacks came as long as we stood facing opposite ends of the chamber. It was clear enough: there were peepholes or slits somewhere, and they dared not swing the door while their trick would be seen in the open.

A plan came to me.

Backing close to Tars Tarkas, I breathed it into his ear while keeping my eyes fixed on my end of the room. He grunted assent—quick, decisive.

He began backing toward the wall I faced while I advanced before him. We moved slowly, like men inching toward a fire they mean to seize, not to flee. When we were within ten feet of the secret doorway, I halted him.

“Stay motionless until my signal,” I murmured, and then—hard though it was—I turned my back upon the wall I knew could open, as though I were weary and careless.

My eyes snapped to the mirror on his back.

A moment. Two.

Then the golden surface began to move—rapidly this time, confident.

Before the turn was complete I gave the signal.

In the same instant I sprang for the receding half; Tars Tarkas wheeled and leaped for the opening made by the in-swinging section. One bound carried me clean through into the adjoining room.

I came up face to face with the yellow-haired man.

He was about my height, well muscled, shaped in every outward detail like an Earth man. At his side hung a long-sword, a short-sword, a dagger—and one of the destructive radium revolvers common on Mars.

Though I held only my long-sword, and by Barsoomian law he should have met me with the same or lesser weapon, his ethics were as warped as his grin. He whipped out the revolver before my feet had fully found the floor.

I met it with an upstroke; my blade slapped the weapon from his grasp before it could speak.

Then he drew his long-sword, and we were evenly armed.

We went at it.

He was a marvelous swordsman—trained and practiced. I had not gripped a sword for ten years before that morning. At first my arm felt the old motions like a forgotten language, familiar in the bones but rusty on the tongue. Yet the body remembers what the mind mislays. Within minutes I had found the rhythm again—the measure of his reach, the timing of his shifts. And then I saw it dawn on him: he had met his match.

His face went livid with rage as my guard held. Blood began to bead and run from a dozen small cuts on his cheek, his brow, his forearm—little red proofs that steel had found him.

“Who are you, white man?” he hissed through clenched teeth. “That you are no Barsoomian from the outer world is evident from your colour. And you are not of us.”

The last came almost as a question.

“What if I were from the Temple of Issus?” I threw it out on a wild guess, the words tasting like bait on my tongue.

“Fate forfend!” he breathed, and his face blanched beneath the blood.

I stored that reaction away like a cook noting which spice makes a man flinch—useful later, perhaps, when I might need fear as a seasoning in another’s will.

But the present matter required no guessing. It required only the simple, final thrust.

Within seconds I got my sword between his ribs. He stiffened, his eyes widening in disbelief, and then he sagged to the floor in a heavy, inert heap.

All through our duel the chained prisoners had watched in tense silence. No sound in the room but steel’s clang, the soft shuffle of our bare feet, and the few tight words we had forced out between breaths.

Now, as the body slumped down, a cry broke from one of the chained women.

“Turn! Turn! Behind you!” she shrieked.

I wheeled at the first note and found myself facing a second man of the same white race. He had crept out of a dark corridor and was almost upon me, sword raised.

Tars Tarkas was nowhere in sight. The secret panel through which I had come was closed.

How I wished the great green warrior stood at my shoulder then. I had fought almost continuously for hours; I had been wrung through adventure as though fate meant to see what strength could be drained from a man. I had eaten nothing for nearly twenty-four hours, and slept not at all. Weariness clung to me like a soaked cloak.

For the first time in years I felt the faint, ugly question: Can I do this?

There was no choice. My salvation lay only in speed and ferocity—ending him before the long strain could finish me.

I rushed him.

But he would not be rushed into death. He backed and parried, parried and sidestepped, cool as a man stirring a pot he knows will boil in time. If possible he was even more adroit than the first. He led me—step by step—until my limbs began to feel distant from my will, until the room’s edges softened, until the radium light smeared in my sight.

I grew weaker, and he knew it. He played me, drawing me around until I stood before the corpse of his fellow.

Then he rushed suddenly—forcing me back.

My heel struck the dead body. The momentum of my own wearied weight pitched me backward across it. My head hit the hard pavement with a crack that rang in my ears.

And to that pain alone I owe my life, for it snapped my brain clear as cold water in the face. Temper flared up through exhaustion—hot, bright, and clean—and as I flung a hand down to drive myself up, my fingers closed upon something cold and metallic on the floor.
