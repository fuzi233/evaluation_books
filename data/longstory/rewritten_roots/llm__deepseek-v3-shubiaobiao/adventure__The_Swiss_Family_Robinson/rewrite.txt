For days, the storm had raged around us, relentless and furious. Six times, darkness swallowed the ship, and six times, dawn brought only worse tempests. By the seventh day, hope had all but vanished.

Our vessel was battered beyond recognition—masts shattered, hull splintered, water rushing in from every crack. The crew, exhausted and broken, no longer cursed the wind but cried out to heaven for mercy, making wild vows in their desperation. Every soul aboard prepared for the end, some praying, others scrambling for a way to survive.

My heart ached as I turned to my family—my wife and our four young sons, their faces pale with terror.

*"Children,"* I said, forcing calm into my voice, *"if it is God’s will, He can save us even now. If not, then we must trust Him still. Death is only bitter when it divides those who love one another, but we shall never be parted in eternity."*

On deck, the scene was dire. The ship was wedged between jagged rocks, the bow shattered, but the stern—where our cabin stood—was miraculously above water. The crew had already fled in the lifeboats, heedless of our cries. We were abandoned.

As night fell, the storm raged on. The ship creaked ominously, but we had done what we could—fastening makeshift floats for the younger boys, gathering knives, cord, and tinder. Fritz, too old to be comforted by false hope, kept watch with us.

At dawn, the winds eased. The sky lightened, and for the first time in days, we saw the sun.

The boys, realizing the crew had deserted us, were dismayed. *"What will we do alone?"*

*"We are not alone,"* I said firmly. *"God is with us. Now—let us think. How do we reach shore?"*

Working swiftly, we sawed barrels into buoyant compartments, lashed them to planks, and fitted outriggers for balance. It was clumsy, but it floated. We loaded it with provisions—guns, tools, food—and, at the last moment, a protesting collection of poultry.

My wife, now clad in a midshipman’s borrowed trousers (to the boys’ great amusement), carried a sack of seeds—her quiet contribution to our survival.

Then we cast off.

The sea was calmer now, but the current pulled us toward the rocks. Yet as we neared, I spotted an inlet—a narrow creek where the water was still. We steered toward it, the dogs swimming desperately behind us.

At last, we reached solid ground.

The shore was barren at first glance, but beyond the cliffs lay green slopes and trees. We knelt in gratitude, then set to work—unloading, raising a tent, kindling a fire.

As the boys scattered to gather moss for bedding, my wife stirred a pot of portable soup. Little Franz, peering in, frowned.

*"Is that glue?"*

She laughed. *"No, my dear. This is supper."*

*"I’d rather have mutton,"* he muttered, and we smiled despite ourselves.

Night fell, but for the first time in days, we faced it together—not as victims of the storm, but as survivors.

My wife greeted me with weary relief when we returned. "I won’t describe our first day’s struggles," she said. Ernest scoffed. "Not every large bird is an eagle, Franz."

Ernest flushed, but Jack swept off his cap and bowed. "Farewell, noble bird! Until we meet again!"

We ate our midday meal there, the boys sprawling in contentment. Even the dogs, after scavenging crabs along the shore, curled up without begging—a miracle in itself.

The longer we lingered, the more certain I became: *This* was our new home. If we could build a dwelling among these branches, we would want for nothing.

Back at the shore, we salvaged what we could—rolling casks above the tide line, noting how the dogs feasted on turtle eggs buried in the sand. Then, spotting a distant sail, we hurried back, hearts pounding until we recognized my husband’s raft.

That evening, as we rested by the fire, I pleaded my case. "Let us move to the grove. We could build a platform in the trees—safe from jackals, cool in the heat. In Switzerland, I’ve seen arbors high in the lindens, reached by stairs. Why not here?"

My husband chuckled. "So we’re to live like birds now?"

But I saw the spark of consideration in his eyes.

The next morning, plans took shape. We would fortify the rocky outpost as a storehouse but make the grove our home. A bridge was needed first—no simple task.

As the boys clamored to start, my husband and Fritz sailed back to the wreck, returning with planks and an odd prize: strips of shark skin, which Fritz nailed to dry in the sun. "For polishing wood," my husband explained. "Rough as shagreen."

Ernest, ever the scholar, mused, "I thought shagreen came from donkeys."

"In Turkey, yes," my husband replied. "But angel-fish skin serves the same purpose."

Fritz wrinkled his nose. "An *angel*-fish? That sounds beautiful."

Ernest smirked. "Not all angels are kind."

The morning sun rose bright and clear, casting golden light through the branches of our towering treehouse. The children, restless with energy, bounded about like young monkeys, eager for the day’s tasks.

*"Father, what shall we do today?"* they clamored.

*"Rest?"* echoed Jack, incredulous. *"But why?"*

*"Remember the words of Scripture,"* I said. *"Six days you shall labor, but the seventh is a day of rest. Today, we honor that command."*

Jack wrinkled his nose. *"But there’s no church here! No hymns, no sermons—what are we to do?"*

*"The whole earth is God’s temple,"* I answered. *"And beneath this great tree, we shall worship as faithfully as in any cathedral."*

The children listened intently, their young minds turning over the lesson.

The lime worked perfectly. Birds alighted, only to find themselves trapped. The more they struggled, the more entangled they became, until at last they tumbled to the earth in a pitiable state. Gently, I cleaned its oil-smeared feathers and caged it, instructing him to save any others like it. By evening, we had several pairs secured, promising future pies and a thriving coop.

As dusk fell, we armed ourselves with torches and sacks, venturing into the acorn wood. The moment flame touched pitch, chaos erupted. Birds, startled by the blaze, floundered blindly, many dropping before we even struck the branches. My wife and Franz scrambled to collect them, sacks bulging by the time we retreated home, triumphant.

The next day was spent plucking and roasting our bounty, but our rest was short-lived. The monkey menace at Woodlands demanded action. With a stock of reinforced bird-lime, stakes, and bait, we set out, the buffalo laden with supplies. Concealing ourselves near the cottage, we staked a labyrinthine fence, smearing every surface with lime and scattering tempting treats. Then, we waited.

Dawn brought a cacophony—screeching, rustling, the thunder of dozens of simian feet. The horde descended, swarming over the cottage, some scaling the roof, others lunging for the bait. Soon, the lime took effect. They scratched at their gummed paws, tripped over cords, and in one hilarious case, a gourd of palm wine glued itself to an elder’s face, sending him blundering about blind. Panic spread. Dogs were loosed, clubs swung, and when silence finally fell, the aftermath was grim. Forty monkeys lay dead. The boys, sobered, helped me bury them and burn the fouled traps.

Amid the cleanup, we discovered three splendid pigeons caught in our leftover lime-sticks—two Moluccan blues and a resplendent Nicobar, its plumage gleaming like emerald and steel. These became the first tenants of our new dovecot, carved into the cliffside at Tentholm.

My true scheme, however, was subtler. Using clay, aniseed, and salt, I crafted a scented lure, coating the entrance holes to attract wild birds. The boys watched, baffled, as I "conjured" our pigeons free, only for them to return days later—each with a mate in tow. The Nicobar, most regal of all, brought a dazzling partner, and soon nests dotted the cote.

Yet mischief was never far off. Jack, ever adventurous, returned one day caked in swamp muck, having nearly sunk chasing reeds. Only Fangs’ heroics—and a hastily woven reed raft—saved him. His misadventure, though, gifted me the perfect canes for a secret project: a loom for my wife. The children, convinced it was a peculiar instrument, roared with laughter when she "played" it, weaving cloth with deft movements.

As storms began brewing on the horizon, we fortified our stores, stockpiling grains, fruits, and tubers. The cavern transformed—lanterns hung from bamboo poles, workshops and libraries took shape, and even the animals found shelter with us. By the time the first true rains lashed the cliffs, we were ready, nestled in our well-lit, orderly home, the fruits of our labor secure against the tempest.

The morning sun rose over our bustling camp as we prepared to finish the gruesome task of rendering whale oil. "Now for the final stretch of this dirty work!" I called cheerfully. After our usual chores, we set up a scaffold to press the blubber-filled tubs, allowing the purest oil to drain into containers below.

The blubber was then boiled in a cauldron over a fire safely distanced from our home. Through skimming and straining, we collected a substantial store of fine train oil, carefully stored in casks and waterproof bags made from the whale’s intestines. The stench was oppressive, especially for my wife, who bore it with admirable patience.

She suggested establishing a secondary colony on the nearby island, where such messy work could be conducted without disturbing our home. The boys eagerly supported the idea, though I reminded them of the rotting carcass still present there. "Let nature cleanse the air first," I advised. "Wind, rain, and scavengers will reduce it to bones in time."

Meanwhile, my mind dwelled on improving our boat’s efficiency. I fashioned a crude rowing mechanism: an iron bar fitted with paddles, turned by a hand-cranked jack. Though imperfect, it worked well enough that Fritz and I glided swiftly across the bay, earning cheers from the family. Excited, they clamored for a voyage the next day to Cape Disappointment and Prospect Hill.

The following morning, we set out well-provisioned. Approaching the farm, the crowing of roosters stirred bittersweet memories of home—our arrival here, unnoticed by the animals, stood in stark contrast to the warm welcomes we once knew. The creatures fled in terror, forcing the boys to lasso goats for milk and tempt fowls with grain.

Dinner was a disappointment when the prized whale tongue proved inedible, tainted by oil. Thankfully, fresh coconuts and goat’s milk salvaged the meal. Later, exploring the coast, we dodged hidden reefs before landing on Whale Island to plant sugarcane.

While working, Jack shouted, "Father! A mammoth skeleton!" It was, of course, the whale’s remains—misidentified until I pointed out our footprints nearby. Ernest teased Jack, sparking a quarrel, but I intervened, stressing harmony.

Returning home, we experimented with a palanquin—a basket slung between Storm and Grumble. Ernest’s ride turned comically rough as the beasts trotted faster, but laughter soon mended frayed tempers.

Then, disaster struck. Fritz spotted a monstrous serpent approaching—a boa constrictor, thirty feet long. We barricaded ourselves in the cave, firing futile shots as it slithered into the reeds. For days, we lived in dread, until our donkey, Grizzle, bolted into the marsh. The boa struck, coiling around him in a horrifying embrace.

"Shoot it!" the boys begged, but I knew interference was futile. "It swallows prey whole," I explained grimly. As Grizzle’s brays faded, we watched helplessly, knowing the beast would now be sluggish—vulnerable.

The chapter closed with a grim resolution: we would strike when the serpent gorged, ending its threat once and for all.

Though we had survived the serpent’s attack, unease settled over me like a shadow. Had it been the only one? Could others lurk unseen in the swamp or slither through the Gap into our sanctuary?

Determined to secure our territory, I planned two expeditions: first, a sweep of the marshlands; second, a journey to the Gap—the only breach where danger might enter.

When I summoned my sons to explore the swamp, Ernest hesitated, and Jack shuddered. "That serpent’s tail could’ve crushed my ribs!" he protested. But I insisted, and we set out, laying planks and woven hurdles to cross the sodden ground.

We found only trampled reeds where the beast had lain. Pressing onward, we emerged near the cliffs, where a sparkling stream gushed from a gaping cave mouth. Inside, the grotto yawned wide, its ceiling hung with glistening stalactites. The floor was dusted with fine white earth—fuller’s earth, I realized, perfect for washing.

Fritz ventured deeper, his voice echoing as he called me forward. The cave expanded into a cavern so vast our candlelight barely pierced the gloom. The walls shimmered.

"Salt crystals?" Fritz wondered.

"Rock crystal," I corrected, running a hand over the cool, glassy surfaces. Though dazzling, it was useless to us now—like Crusoe’s lump of gold. Still, Fritz pried loose a specimen before we retreated, his farewell shot ringing through the cavern like thunder.

Outside, Jack rushed to me, tear-streaked. "I thought the cave collapsed on you!" he cried. Ernest, meanwhile, had been weaving a fish trap—and proudly showed off a "serpent" he’d slain. It was an eel, thick as his arm. "Courage over fear," I praised, though his trembling hands betrayed his nerves.

At home, my wife rejoiced at the fuller’s earth and the eel supper. But the next task loomed: scouring the land beyond the river for serpents. We packed the cart with supplies and set off, Storm and Grumble hauling our gear while the dogs patrolled our flanks.

At Woodlands, all was peaceful—no apes, no serpents. The next day, Franz bagged a capybara, its musk pungent but its meat edible. Ernest, meanwhile, stumbled upon a colony of beaver rats, their lodges like miniature dams. Their pelts would make fine caps.

Days passed in a blur of hunting and curing meat. Fritz roasted a peccary in an earth oven, its flesh infused with fragrant leaves. "Ravensara," I mused, recalling Madagascar’s spice-scented trees.

Yet mischief awaited at Prospect Hill—the apes had ransacked it again. We vowed revenge but pressed on to the Gap, where cliffs and torrent framed the narrow pass. Camping in a sheltered grove, we prepared for the morrow’s expedition beyond the defile.

That night, beneath a sky pricked with stars, we rested—ready for whatever lurked in the unknown.

Evening had settled over our camp, and the rich aroma of stewing bear paws filled the air as we gathered around the fire. The boys had not yet returned from their expedition, and though we kept our conversation light, our ears were alert for the sound of hoofbeats.

At last, the rhythmic thud of galloping horses reached us. Across the open ground, our young hunters came into view, their faces flushed with excitement. They dismounted in a flurry, their saddlebags bulging with spoils from the hunt.

Franz and Jack each carried a young kid slung across their backs, legs bound together, while Fritz’s game bag wriggled with unseen creatures.

"Father!" Jack called, breathless. "You should have seen us! Storm and Grumble outpaced the antelopes—we ran them down like true hunters!"

Fritz grinned. "And we’ve driven a whole herd through the Gap into our valley. They’re ours now—trapped!"

I praised their cleverness, though I questioned how they would keep the antelopes from escaping. Fritz, ever resourceful, explained how they had strung a line of feathers and rags across the Gap—a trick he had read of in his books.

Then came the tale of the honeyguide bird, which had led them to a bees’ nest—though Jack’s attempt to smoke out the insects had ended in disaster, leaving him covered in stings. Amid laughter, he dashed off to seek his mother’s remedy.

As night fell, we feasted on the savory bear paws, their rich flavor a reward for our patience. The next morning, we set out early, eager to explore further.

Our journey took us past Turtle Marsh and up to the rocky slopes where Fritz had once spotted distant riders. There, we collected the red gum from the euphorbia trees—a deadly poison I intended to use against any future ape invasions.

Then, an unexpected sight: four ostriches burst from the sand, their powerful legs carrying them at astonishing speed. Fritz’s eagle, its beak safely bound, swooped upon the lead male, slowing him just enough for Jack to lasso the great bird.

The ostrich fought fiercely, lashing out with dangerous kicks—until I threw my coat over its head. Darkness calmed it instantly, and we bound its legs, leading it home between Storm and Grumble like a prisoner of war.

My wife was less than thrilled at our latest acquisition. "Must you tame every creature in this wilderness?" she sighed.

But Jack was already dreaming of riding the ostrich like a steed. With stubborn determination, I set about devising a solution—a hood with blinkers to control its movements, and a saddle to keep the rider secure.

After days of trial, the bird finally submitted. Fed on maize and pebbles (much to Franz’s amusement), it grew tame, and soon trotted obediently at our command.

Our return to Rockburg was triumphant. The antelopes roamed freely near Woodlands, the ostrich was staked before our home, and the eggs we had collected were nestled in warm cotton, awaiting their hatching.

As we settled back into our routines—plowing fields, curing hides, and reinforcing our defenses—I marveled at how much we had accomplished. The wilderness was no longer just our refuge; it was becoming our kingdom.

And with each passing day, we grew more certain: whatever challenges lay ahead, we would face them together.

Winter returned with brooding clouds and relentless storms, confining us once more to the shelter of our home. Though our dwelling was spacious and our occupations varied, the days grew wearisome. Even the occasional ride during a lull in the rain did little to lift our spirits.

Fritz, ever restless, approached me one evening with an idea. "Father," he said, "why not build a swifter boat? Something lighter than the pinnace, easier to handle—a true skiff for skimming the waves."

The suggestion ignited excitement in the boys, but my wife paled at the thought. "Must we tempt the sea further?" she protested. "Between the pinnace and the canoe, we court danger enough!"

I reassured her. "This will be no flimsy craft. I mean to model it after the Greenlanders' *cajack*—sturdy, reliable, and swift."

With the boys' eager help, construction began. We shaped a skeleton of whalebone, reinforced the sides with split bamboo, and fashioned a narrow deck with only a small opening for the rider. The work consumed us, and by the time we finished, the rains had ceased, leaving the sun triumphant.

The *cajack* was a curious sight—light as a feather, yet tough. When we launched her, she danced upon the water like a rubber ball. Fritz was declared her rightful captain, but his mother, still uneasy, insisted on fashioning a swimming-dress for him.

The garment was peculiar: a double-layered waistcoat, sealed with rubber solution, inflated by a small air hole. When Fritz tested it, paddling across the bay to Shark Island, the boys howled with laughter—until they saw how well it worked. Soon, even Ernest and Jack clamored for their own.

While on Shark Island, we checked on our earlier plantings. The antelopes had found the shelter we built, and the crops flourished. My wife, ever resourceful, gathered strange notched leaves from the shoreline, refusing to explain their purpose. Days later, she presented us with shimmering, quivering jelly—made from the very seaweed we had mocked. It was delicious, a taste of the tropics, and the boys vowed to gather more.

Meanwhile, the whale's bones, bleached white by the elements, gave me an idea. I collected vertebrae to construct a crushing machine, a project that occupied us for days. Hunting, too, remained a passion. The boys returned one evening with a bounty: kangaroos, musk-rats, even a monkey. Their excitement was infectious, though I reminded them to warn us before such long expeditions.

The harvest then demanded our attention. The grain stood ripe, and we reaped it swiftly, Italian-style—cutting handfuls and tossing them into baskets without stooping. My wife fretted over the "wasted" straw, but I assured her it would feed our livestock. Threshing was a merry affair, the boys riding our animals in circles to trample the grain, while the breeze carried away the chaff.

By season's end, our stores overflowed. We prepared the fields anew, sowing rye and barley, while the herring and seal migrations provided fresh bounty. The seals, especially, were vital—their skins would perfect Fritz’s *cajack*, waterproofing its deck storage for future voyages.

Thus ended a season of invention and plenty, each challenge met with ingenuity, and each day proving anew that perseverance and unity could turn even a wild shore into home.

The day had finally arrived for Fritz to test his newly crafted cajack. Dressed in his waterproof attire—trousers, jacket, and cap—he squeezed into the narrow vessel, inflating the bladders until he resembled nothing so much as a puffing frog ready to burst. His absurd appearance sent the family into fits of laughter, though his mother managed only a restrained smile, proud of her handiwork despite the comical sight.

I readied the larger boat, ensuring we could assist him if needed. With a gentle push, the cajack glided onto the calm, glassy surface of the bay. Fritz paddled with growing confidence, darting forward, spinning in swift circles, and even tipping precariously to one side—much to his mother’s alarm—only to right himself with ease.

Encouraged by our cheers, he ventured boldly into the current of Jackal River, and before I could protest, the swift waters carried him out to sea. Heart pounding, I signaled Ernest and Jack to join me in pursuit, while my wife fretted behind us, calling the cajack a "horrid soap-bubble" sure to bring disaster.

We rowed beyond the bay, scanning the horizon until a distant pistol shot cracked through the air. Answering with a signal of our own, we followed the sound and soon spotted Fritz waving excitedly from behind a rocky outcrop.

"Come quickly!" he cried. "You must see this!"

There, sprawled on the shore, lay a massive young walrus, its life ended by Fritz’s harpoon.

"Did you truly slay this beast alone?" I asked, half-expecting some hidden hunter to emerge.

"Of course!" Fritz grinned, gesturing to the embedded harpoon. "Though I confess, the current swept me farther than I intended. Once I spotted the herd, I couldn’t resist the chase."

Pride warred with concern as I examined the creature—nearly fourteen feet long, with formidable tusks. "You took a great risk," I admitted. "A wounded walrus can crush a boat with those tusks. But well done, my boy!"

Fritz’s eyes gleamed. "Let me keep the head! I’ll mount it on the cajack’s prow—it shall be the *Sea-Horse*!"

Jack clamored in agreement, while Ernest wrinkled his nose. "And how splendid it will smell in a week’s time!"

Laughing, we set to work, hacking through blubber and bone to claim the tusks and hide. The air grew thick, the sky darkening ominously. "A storm comes," I warned. "We must hurry."

Fritz, ever eager, insisted on returning ahead of us in the cajack, while we followed at a steadier pace.

No sooner had we lost sight of Fritz than the tempest struck. Black clouds swallowed the sun, wind screamed, and waves heaved like mountains. We lashed ourselves to the boat, bracing as seawater crashed over us. Ernest and Jack held fast, faces pale but resolute. My heart ached for Fritz—surely the fragile cajack could not survive such fury.

Yet when the storm finally relented and we limped into Safety Bay, there stood my wife and Fritz, kneeling in prayer. Their joyous cries at our safe return brought tears to my eyes. Together, we gave thanks for our deliverance.

Back at Rockburg, we dried off and feasted, recounting our adventures. The walrus head, cleaned and preserved, soon adorned the cajack, a trophy of Fritz’s daring. Meanwhile, the storm’s damage demanded repair—flooded fields, a broken bridge, and damaged pipes kept us busy for days.

A discovery near the waterfall—clove berries!—sparked new culinary experiments, much to my wife’s delight. With clay from Falconhurst, I planned irrigation channels to nourish our crops, while the boys’ fishing expeditions replenished our stores.

Restlessness soon stirred the boys. "We need more bamboo," Fritz declared. "And clay from the Gap." And so, with pemmican (Fritz’s dubious experiment) and supplies, they set off for Woodlands.

Their adventures unfolded in terse pigeon-post missives:

Laughter aside, the real trials came at Prospect Hill, where monkeys had wreaked havoc. Fritz’s poisoned traps eradicated the pests, but not without nights of eerie howls and shrieks that left them sleepless.

Then, the final warning:

I rode out at once under the moonlight, finding the boys unharmed but uneasy. The culprit? Elephants. Their massive footprints and destruction pointed to a passing herd. We fortified the barricades, vowing to deter future invasions.

As we sat by the fire that night, exchanging tales, I marveled at their courage. Whatever dangers lay ahead, we would face them together.

Time, as King David once said, passes like a tale told—swiftly, silently. As I looked back on ten years in our island home, I marveled at all we had built, endured, and cherished.

Our little dominion, New Switzerland, flourished. Rockburg and Falconhurst stood as our strongholds, winter and summer, softened now by flowering vines and groves of transplanted trees. The barren cliffs that once dismayed my wife were now draped in greenery, alive with birdsong. Shark Island, crowned with palms, kept watch over Safety Bay, while the drained swamp had become a lake, glittering with waterfowl—black swans, crimson flamingos, and the absurdly giant frogs Jack adored.

Our sons had grown into men: Fritz, strong and spirited; Ernest, thoughtful and studious; Jack, agile and quick-witted; and Franz, the bright-eyed youngest, blending the best of his brothers. They were fearless, kind, and bound by loyalty—traits nurtured in the heart of our family.

Yet, amid this contentment, a restlessness stirred. Fritz, now twenty-four, often ventured beyond our mapped shores. One evening, he returned late, his cajack laden with strange treasures—a sack of peculiar, sponge-like nests and a box of iridescent pearls.

"Edible birds' nests," I explained, "prized in distant lands. And these pearls—" I held one to the light, "—could be riches beyond measure, if ever we return to the world."

But Fritz's true discovery came in hushed tones the next morning. Wrapped around an albatross's leg was a scrap of cloth, its message stark: *"Save an unfortunate Englishwoman from the smoking rock!"*

The words struck like lightning. A fellow castaway? A survivor? Fritz, aflame with purpose, vowed to find her. We agreed—he would prepare, modify his canoe, and go quietly, lest hope turn to heartbreak.

Days later, we sailed for Pearl Bay, where the oyster beds gleamed. The boys, thrilled by their haul, dreamed aloud of pearl fisheries. But Fritz's mind was elsewhere, his gaze often drifting westward—toward the unknown, toward a voice crying out in the wilderness.

As we anchored that night, the firelight flickered on determined faces. Tomorrow, we would gather pearls. But soon after, Fritz would embark on a journey far greater—one that might change our lives forever.

My wife and Franz were overjoyed at our safe return, though their relief was tinged with concern at Fritz’s absence. As we recounted our battle with the lions and Jack’s narrow escape, my wife grew pale, her hands trembling. Franz, his eyes brimming with tears, asked if we had brought back Juno’s remains. The next day, we buried her near the house, and Ernest, ever the scholar, carved an epitaph upon a stone:

The wild boar’s meat and truffles were handed to my wife, who promised savory feasts—though I had to deny her the head, as it was destined for Ernest’s museum. The lions’ hides, along with the boar’s trophy, were taken to Whale Island for tanning.

Five days passed with no sign of Fritz. Anxiety gnawed at me, and I resolved to set sail in search of him. To my surprise, my wife agreed to join us, and soon we were aboard the pinnace, skimming toward Cape Minster.

The sea was calm, the breeze steady—until a dark shape loomed beneath the waves. I wrenched the helm, bracing for impact—only for the "rock" to vanish. Before we could recover, Jack shouted, *"Another! To starboard!"* The mass surged upward, a jet of water erupting from its blowhole.

The beast, enraged by our near-collision, thrashed toward us. Jack and I fired in unison. The shots struck true; the whale retreated, only to reappear in the shallows, staining the water crimson. A second volley silenced its struggles forever.

Just as we cheered, Franz gasped. *"A canoe!"* A dark figure—a savage, we thought—paddled toward us in what looked like Fritz’s kayak. Terror seized me—had Fritz been captured?

Ernest, ever composed, raised the speaking trumpet. *"Fritz, ahoy!"*

The figure hesitated—then waved. In moments, Fritz was aboard, his face smeared with charcoal, grinning at our stunned faces. *"I thought you were pirates!"* he laughed, explaining his disguise. After sharing our whale tale, he offered to guide us to an island with *"something worth seeing."*

Now certain of his secret, I revealed Fritz’s mission to my wife. Her hands flew to her mouth. *"You mean—another person? A woman?"*

The boys, baffled by our hushed excitement, followed Fritz ashore. Through the trees, we spotted a hut, smoke curling from its fire. Fritz vanished inside—then emerged with a slender figure in naval attire.

*"Meet Edward Montrose,"* he announced.

We embraced the "young officer" warmly—though my wife and I alone knew the truth. The boys, oblivious, were enchanted by their lively new companion, who regaled them with tales over supper. Only when Fritz began his story—of the albatross, the tiger, and the castaway’s signal fire—did the truth dawn.

*"Miss Montrose?!"* Jack exploded.

Laughter erupted as Fritz confessed. The boys cheered, toasting their new sister with mead. Then, with the fire crackling, Fritz resumed his tale—of Jenny Montrose, the colonel’s daughter, shipwrecked and surviving alone for years with nothing but wit and a trained cormorant for company.

As the night deepened, we marveled at the twist of fate that had brought us together—and the new chapter beginning for us all.

The winter months had passed in warmth and contentment within our rocky refuge. Evenings were spent by the fire, the boys and Jenny sharing stories as we worked—weaving, sketching, or mending tools. But when spring arrived, breathing life back into the island, we emerged like creatures shaking off hibernation, eager to return to our labors.

Fields, orchards, and gardens demanded attention. Falconhurst, Woodlands, and even distant Shark Island required inspection. Jack and Franz, ever the eager sentinels, took charge of the island battery, polishing the guns and raising the flag anew.

One evening, as the sun dipped low, they fired a test shot at a barrel set adrift in the bay. The crack of the cannons echoed—and then, astonishingly, an answer came. Three distant booms rolled across the water from the west.

Silence gripped us. Then came the frantic splash of paddles as the boys raced back to shore, their faces alight with excitement.

"Did you hear?" Jack gasped. "A ship! A real ship!"

Franz’s eyes shone. "It must be European! We’ll see home again!"

Joy and dread warred within me. Who were these strangers? Friends in need? Or predators eyeing our haven? The night was too far advanced to act, so we waited, restless, watching the horizon until a storm swallowed the sea in fury.

For two days, the tempest raged. When calm returned, we fired the guns once more—and again, an answer came.

Fritz and I set out in the cajack, hugging the cliffs until we rounded Cape Pug-Nose. There, anchored in the sheltered cove, lay a brig flying the English flag. Tents dotted the shore, smoke curling from campfires.

Relief washed over me—until suspicion crept in. What brought a naval vessel so far from trade routes? Mutineers, perhaps? But the ship’s orderly decks and disciplined crew soon eased my fears.

We returned to Rockburg, debating how to present ourselves—not as castaways, but as masters of this land. The next morning, we sailed our refurbished yacht to meet them, dressed in our finest.

Captain Littlestone welcomed us warmly. To our astonishment, he knew of Jenny—his mission was to trace survivors of the *Dorcas*. Overcome, Fritz clasped his hand as the captain praised our rescue of her.

Jenny, my wife, and the boys soon joined us aboard the brig. Laughter and stories flowed, but beneath the cheer lay unspoken questions. That night, my wife and I whispered of the future. Did we truly wish to return to Europe? Or had this island become home?

By morning, the decision was clear. New Switzerland was ours. Fritz would sail to England with Jenny, to reunite her with her father. Ernest chose to stay, dedicating himself to study. Jack, grinning, declared he’d remain the finest rider on the island. Only Franz hesitated—then chose to seek education abroad, promising to return one day.

Captain Littlestone, ever gracious, agreed to carry them. Preparations began in earnest—gifts packed, letters written, farewells murmured. When the *Unicorn* sailed at last, our hearts were full. Some departed, some stayed, but all knew this:

Wherever fate led, we were bound by more than blood. We were pioneers of a new world.

And as the ship vanished beyond the horizon, the flag of New Switzerland fluttered proudly in the breeze.
