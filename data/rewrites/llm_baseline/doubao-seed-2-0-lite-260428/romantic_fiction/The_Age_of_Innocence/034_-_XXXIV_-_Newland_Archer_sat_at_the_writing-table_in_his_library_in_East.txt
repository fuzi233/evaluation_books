Newland Archer sat at his well-loved writing table in his East Thirty-ninth Street library, still dust-spattered from the glittering official inauguration of the Metropolitan Museum’s brand new gallery wing. The great, high-ceilinged spaces, crammed full of curated treasures from across centuries, thrumming with New York’s fashionable elite drifting past labeled vases and statues, had suddenly clicked open a rusted, long-forgotten lock in his memory.

“Wait a minute — this used to be one of the old Cesnola rooms,” he’d heard a stranger murmur beside him, and in an instant the whole crowded reception vanished. He was twenty years old again, perched on a hard leather divan pressed up against a clanking radiator, watching a slim figure swathed in a long sealskin cloak drift further and further away down the bare, underfurnished hallway of the old, unrenovated museum.

That faint, soft vision had roused a whole flood of half-buried memories, and he looked around the library at last with fresh eyes — the room that had held every quiet, meaningful moment of his life for thirty years. It was here, twenty-six years prior, that May had stumbled out her news of their first pregnancy with such pink, awkward circumlocution that any modern young woman would have laughed out loud at her shyness. It was here that their eldest boy Dallas, too fragile to be carted to church in the depths of winter, had been christened by the beloved, larger-than-life Bishop of New York. It was here Dallas had tottered across the unrolled rug, yelling “Dad!” for the very first time, while May and the nursemaid giggled hidden behind the door. It was here their second child Mary — the spitting image of her mother — had stammered out her engagement to the sturdiest, most reliable of Reggie Chivers’s half-dozen sons, and where Archer had kissed her through her wedding veil before they climbed into the motor to drive to Grace Church, the one unshakable, unchanged New York ritual even the shifting times had not dared to touch.

Every big family conversation had unfolded here: they’d mapped out Dallas’s school years, his little brother Bill’s messy love of wild adventures, Mary’s complete disinterest in “ladylike accomplishments” and all-consuming passion for sports and charity work. The restless, curious Dallas had spurned the expected paths of law or business to join a rising New York architect’s firm, one of dozens of the younger generation chasing brand new dreams — Central American archaeology, landscape design, restoring forgotten pre-Revolutionary houses, sneering at the tacky “Colonial” houses only newly rich suburban grocers seemed to want to build these days.

But the memory that still made his chest glow brightest, even now, was the night the Governor of New York had come down from Albany to dine, slammed his fist down on this very table, and declared “Hang the professional politicians! You’re exactly the kind of man this country needs, Archer. If we’re ever going to clean out the mess, men like you have to roll up their sleeves and help.” Archer had jumped at the call, exactly as Ned Winsett had begged him to do decades earlier — but after a single term in the State Assembly, he’d not been re-elected, and had settled gratefully into quiet, unglamorous municipal reform work, writing occasional pieces for the small weekly papers that were trying to jolt the city out of its lazy apathy. It was no grand legacy, but it was something, far more than the narrow, money-focused, society-bound lives his old peers had all settled for. He was the kind of man everyone called “a good citizen” — the first name on every new charity drive, every push to open schools for crippled children, every effort to reorganize the museum, found the Grolier Club, or launch a chamber music society. His days were full, and decent, and he’d long thought that was all a man could reasonably ask for.

He knew he’d missed the bright, wild, fleeting flower of life — but he’d come to see it as a lottery prize so unlikely, the grief of never winning it would be as silly as crying that you hadn’t drawn the one grand prize out of a hundred million tickets. When he thought of Ellen Olenska now, it was gently, abstractly, as you’d think of the heroine of a favorite old novel, or the face in a painting you’d loved your whole life. That faint, tender vision had kept him faithful to May, all their long years together. When May had died suddenly of infectious pneumonia, after nursing their youngest boy Bill back from the very edge of death, he’d mourned her honestly. Their decades together had taught him that even a quiet, unremarkable marriage rooted in shared duty was worth more than the messy, ugly battle of selfish appetites he saw in so many other couples around him. There was goodness in the old ways, he’d always thought.

His eyes drifted around the room, newly decorated by Dallas with soft English mezzotints, Chippendale cabinets, bits of hand-painted blue and white porcelain, and gentle shaded electric lamps, before landing back on the scuffed old Eastlake writing table he’d never had the heart to replace. Beside his inkstand still sat the very first photograph he’d ever taken of May: tall, willowy, round-chested, in a starched muslin dress and floppy Leghorn hat, the way he’d first seen her under the orange trees in a California mission garden. She’d stayed that generous, faithful, unchanging woman her whole life, so completely lacking in imagination that the whole world had shifted and rebuilt itself around her without her ever noticing. That bright, willful innocence had led their whole family to fall into a gentle, unspoken pretense that nothing ever changed, no one ever wanted anything outside the soft boundaries of May’s small happy world. She’d died convinced the world was full of loving, harmonious households just like theirs, certain Archer would pass all their old familiar values on to Dallas and Bill, that Mary would live just as safe and happy a life. She’d gone to her vault in St. Mark’s content, never even noticing the terrifying “trend” of the new century her mother-in-law had always ranted about.

Opposite May’s portrait hung one of Mary, loose-waisted, flat-chested, slouching a little as the new fashion required, proof that the new world had its own goodness too — kinder, more tolerant, less tightly laced than the one her mother had grown up in.

The telephone on his desk clicked, and Archer leaned over to answer it. “Long distance from Chicago for you,” the operator said. It was Dallas, of course — sent out to Illinois by his firm to hash out plans for a grand, palatial lakeside house they were building for a young millionaire with endless ideas.

“Hey Dad!” his son’s bright laugh crackled down the line, clear as if he was leaning in the armchair by their library fire. “How do you feel about sailing Wednesday on the Mauretania? Our client wants me to pop over to Italy to look at old garden designs before we finalize the plans, and he’s covering the whole trip. I’ve got to be back June first, so it’ll be a quick run — but I want you to come with me. No excuses, alright? Book the tickets tomorrow, get us a return out of Marseilles. It’ll be our last little quiet trip just the two of us, before — well, you know.”

Archer hung up the phone, and paced the room slowly. Dallas was right. They’d have a hundred happy gatherings after he married Fanny Beaufort, Archer was sure, but this last little unplanned adventure, no duties, no family, just him and his boy? He’d never get that chance again. He thought back to the only other time the whole family had traveled abroad, years before, when May had arranged for them to do the standard tour of England, Switzerland, and Italy, deliberately skipping France to make time for the boys’ mountain climbing. She’d even offered to let him slip off to Paris alone for two weeks to meet them later at the Italian lakes — but he’d refused, saying he’d stick with the family, and May’s face had lit up like he’d done the most noble thing imaginable.

Two years after her death, he’d been too stuck in his old familiar routine, too scared of new things, to ever strike out on his own. But now, as he looked back over the winding path of his life, he realized how deep a rut he’d sunk into. The strict black-and-white moral codes of his youth had left almost no room for surprise, for the wild, unplanned joy he’d once nearly reached for. He laughed to himself, remembering old Lawrence Lefferts’s sneering prophecy he’d spat out right in this room, decades before: “If things keep going the way they are, our children will be marrying Beaufort’s bastards.”

That was exactly what Dallas was doing. No one gossiped, no one judged. Even his elderly Aunt Janey had dug her mother’s old emeralds and seed pearls out of their pink cotton wool wrapping and brought them to Fanny Beaufort herself, who’d cooed over their old-fashioned beauty and said she felt like an Isabey miniature just holding them. Fanny, the beautiful, funny, accomplished orphaned daughter of old Julius Beaufort and his scandalous second wife, who’d grown up in Buenos Ayres, had walked into New York society at eighteen and charmed everyone instantly, the way Ellen Olenska had thirty years prior — but this time, no one was suspicious of her, no one dug up old half-forgotten scandals. The world had changed, for good.

Three days later, Archer leaned out the open window of their suite at the Hotel Bristol in Paris, staring out at the bright, stately gaiety of the Place Vendôme below, and felt his heart hammering hard in his chest, fast and wild, the way it hadn’t since he was a boy. All the decades of quiet duty had fallen away, and for a second he felt shy, old, grey, nothing like the bold, reckless young man he’d once dreamed of being, who would have run off to Paris with Ellen thirty years before.

Dallas clapped a cheerful hand on his shoulder. “Say, Dad, isn’t this perfect? Oh, by the way — I ran into an old acquaintance this morning. Countess Olenska is in Paris, and I told her we’d stop by to see her at half past five this afternoon.”

He said it so casually, like he was just mentioning what time their train left for Florence the next day. Archer stared at him, and saw a glint of old Grandmother Mingott’s familiar mischief in his son’s eyes.

“Wait — you told her I was here?”

“Of course! Why wouldn’t I?” Dallas laughed, slipping his arm through his father’s. “Fanny made me promise to look her up. Madame Olenska was so kind to her when Fanny first went to Paris from Buenos Ayres, no friends at all, she’d take her out for holidays and show her around. Dad — come on. You don’t have to be all mysterious about it. Weren’t you two great friends back in the day? Wasn’t she the most alive, different thing you ever met? Mom told me, the day before she died, when she sent for me alone — she said she knew we’d all be safe with you, because once, when she’d asked you to, you’d given up the one thing you wanted most in the whole world.”

Archer went very still. His eyes stayed fixed on the sunlit crowds drifting across the square below. “She never asked me,” he said softly, at last.

Dallas grinned. “Oh, right! Your generation never asked each other for anything, never said a word. All quiet secret understandings. C’mon, Dad, don’t be mad. I’m heading off to Versailles for the afternoon — I’ll meet you back here at five, alright?”

Archer didn’t go to Versailles. He wandered the streets alone instead, all the decades of unspoken regrets, all the stifled memories he’d buried deep, rising soft to the surface. It didn’t feel like a tragedy, now, to know May had guessed his secret, had known all along. He sat on a bench in the Champs Élysées for an hour, watching the stream of Parisians drift past, thinking that Ellen Olenska was just a few streets away, waiting for him. She’d never gone back to her brutal husband, and when he’d died years before, she’d stayed right here in Paris, living her quiet, independent life. There was nothing left keeping them apart, after all this time.

He got up, crossed the Place de la Concorde, and wandered through the Tuileries to the Louvre. Ellen had told him once that she loved to walk the galleries there, and he wanted to spend the next hour in the space where she’d so often been, surrounded by the paintings he’d spent thirty years daydreaming about. The soft afternoon light spilled over the canvases, their long-forgotten splendour wrapping around him, and for the first time in his whole quiet, dutiful life, Newland Archer felt like he was finally, finally walking toward something he’d wanted, all the way back from the days he’d sat on that hard museum divan, watching a woman in a sealskin cloak walk away.
