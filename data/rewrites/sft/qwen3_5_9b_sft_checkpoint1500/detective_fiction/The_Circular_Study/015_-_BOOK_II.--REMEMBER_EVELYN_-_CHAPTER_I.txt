Thomas Cadwalader proposed his narrative rather than dictating it. We refuse to emulate this reticence, nor would it serve the listener's interest to recount these facts with the stark brevity and lack of detail that plagued this unhappy man due to the urgency and anxiety of his situation. Great tragedies are born of remarkable facts, and since those facts are merely the offspring of human passion, we must delve into those passions if we wish to comprehend either the facts themselves or their terrifying consequences. In this instance, the first link in the chain that culminated in Felix Adams's violent demise was forged before the birth of the woman who eventually struck him down. We must therefore begin with nearly forgotten days, narrating the story, as her advocate did, from the dual perspective of Felix and Thomas Cadwalader.

Thomas Cadwalader—known to the world as Adams—never knew his mother, who perished in his early infancy. Nor can it be said that he truly knew his father; he was raised in France by an elderly Scottish lawyer, a relative of his mother's. This guardian occasionally spoke of the mother but remained silent regarding the father until Thomas reached his fifteenth year. Then, placing several volumes into the boy's hands, he issued a striking injunction:

"Here are romances, Thomas. Read them; but remember that none of them, regardless of how thrilling their content or effect, will ever surpass the story of your father's life, which was grievously wronged and marked by suffering."

"My father!" Thomas cried. "Tell me about him; I have never heard."

Yet his guardian, content with an allusion that he knew would yield fruit in the highly susceptible nature of this isolated boy, said no more that day. Thomas turned to the books, but nothing thereafter could divert his mind from thoughts of his father. Having scarcely thought of him for years, the sudden illumination of his father as a wronged man caused Thomas to constantly scour the deepest recesses of his memory for any long-forgotten recollection of his features capable of restoring his image. Sometimes he succeeded, or so he believed; yet this image, if it were an image, was swiftly lost to a sensation of something strange and awe-inspiring that enveloped it. Consequently, he found himself more consumed by the intangible impressions associated with this memory than by the memory itself. What were these impressions, and whence did they originate? He vainly attempted to determine. They were as vague as they were persistent. A stretch of darkness—two bars of orange light, shining eternally and identically, black lines against them resembling the tops of distant gables—an inner thrill—a vague, creeping dread—a rushing about him like a swooping wind—all arrived with his father's image, only to fade away with it, leaving him troubled, uneasy, and perplexed. Finding these impressions so tenacious, and receiving no explanation from his own mind, he finally asked his guardian what they signified. That guardian was as ignorant as himself on the subject; satisfied with having roused the boy's imagination, he confined himself to hints, dropping them now and then with a judiciousness that revealed a deliberate purpose. Some duty awaited him on the other side of the water, a duty explaining his long exile from his sole parent, a mission for which Thomas must prepare himself through study and the acquisition of accomplishments rendering a young man a positive force in society, whether of the Old World or the New. He displayed his shrewdness in managing this pliable and deeply affectionate nature. From that time forward, Thomas felt himself leading a life of mystery and intrigue.

To feel himself appointed for a work whose unknown character only heightened its importance gave point to every effort now made by this young man and lent to his studies that vague touch of romance that made them a delight, turning him into an adept in many fields where he might otherwise have cared little. At eighteen, he was a graduate of the Sorbonne and a musical virtuoso. He could fence, ride, and win prizes in games demanding both physical prowess and mental acuity. He was, in fact, a prodigy in many respects and was considered such by his fellow students. However, he was not perfect; he lacked social charm and, in that regard, failed to be the complete gentleman. This deficiency was made painfully clear to him in the following manner:

One morning his guardian presented him with a letter from his father. Alongside words of commendation for his current attainments, the father expressed a specific dissatisfaction with Thomas's general manner, noting it was too abrupt and self-satisfied toward members of his own sex, and far too timid and deprecating toward the other. Thomas felt the sting of the criticism and recognized its justice; yet how had his father, proven by the letter to be no longer a myth, become acquainted with defects that Thomas instinctively felt could never have attracted the notice of his far-from-polished guardian?

His questions on this point elicited a response that confounded him. He was not the only son of his father; he had a brother living, older by some twenty years or more, who had recently been in Paris, where, in all probability, he had met Thomas, spoken with him, and perhaps pressed his hand.

It was a discovery calculated to deepen the impression already made upon Thomas's mind. Only a purpose of the utmost importance could account for so much mystery. What could it be? What was he destined to do, say, or be? He was not told, but while awaiting enlightenment, he resolved not to disappoint the two anxious souls who watched his career so eagerly and demanded such perfection from him. Consequently, he moderated his manner, and during the following year, through constant association with the gilded youth about him, he acquired that indescribable charm of the perfect gentleman which he was led to believe would alone meet with the approval of those he now felt bound to please. By the end of the year, he found himself a finished man of the world. How truly so, he began to realize when he noted the blush with which his presence was hailed by women and the respect shown him by men of his own station. In the midst of the satisfaction thus experienced, his guardian paid him a final visit.

"You are now ready," he said, "for your father's summons. It will arrive in a few weeks. Be careful, then. Form no ties you cannot readily break; for, once recalled from France, you are unlikely to return here. What your father's purpose concerning you may be, I do not know, but it is no ordinary one. You will have money, a well-appointed home, family affection—all that you have hitherto craved in vain—and in return, you will carry solace to a heart which has awaited your healing touch for twenty years. So much I am ordered to say; the rest you will hear from your father's own lips."

Aroused, encouraged, and animated by the wildest hopes and the most extravagant anticipations, Thomas awaited his father's call with feverish impatience. When it came, he hastened to respond by taking an immediate voyage to America. This occurred some six months prior to the tragedy in ---- Street. Upon his arrival at the wharf in New York, he was not met by his brother, as he had every reason to expect, but by a messenger whose face bore evident signs of evil tidings before a word was spoken. Thomas was soon made acquainted with them. His father—who he now learned was named Cadwalader, whereas he himself had always been called Adams—was ill, possibly dying. He must therefore hasten, and, being provided with minute instructions as to his route, he took the train immediately for a small village in northern Pennsylvania.

All that followed was a dream to him. He was hurried through the night, the motion of the ship still fresh in his blood, to meet... what? He dared not think. He swam in a veritable nightmare. Then came a stop, a hurrying from the train, a halt on a platform reeking with rain (for the night was stormy), a call from someone to hurry, the sight of a panting horse steaming under a lamp whose flickering flame he often woke in after nights to see, a push from a persuasive hand, then a ride over a country road whose darkness seemed impenetrable, and finally, the startling vision of an open door, with a Meg Merrilies of a woman standing in it, holding a flaming candle. The candle went out as he looked at it, leaving only a voice to guide him—a voice which, in tones shaken by chill or emotion, he could not distinguish, cried eagerly:

"Is that you, laddie? Come awa in. Come awa in. Dinna heed the rain. The maister's been crying on you a' day. I'm glad you're no ower late."

He dismounted, followed the voice, and, stumbling up a step or two, entered a narrow door, which was with difficulty held open behind him, and which swung to with a loud noise the instant he crossed the threshold. This, or the dreariness of the place in which he found himself, disturbed him greatly. Bare floors, stained walls, meagre doorways, and a common pine staircase, lit only by the miserable candle which the old woman had relit—were these the appointments of the palatial home he had been led to expect? Were these the surroundings, this the abode of him who had exacted such perfection on his part, and for whose standard he had devoted years of hourly, daily effort in every department of art and science?
