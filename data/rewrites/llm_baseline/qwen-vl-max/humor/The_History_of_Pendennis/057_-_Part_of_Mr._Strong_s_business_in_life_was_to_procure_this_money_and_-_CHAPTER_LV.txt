CHAPTER LV. Fanny’s Occupation’s Gone

Ever since her son’s illness, good Mrs. Pendennis—Helen, as we’ve come to know her—had taken over Arthur’s entire life like a well-meaning but slightly overzealous housekeeper. His drawers, his closets, even the very socks and shirts that needed mending (and there were many) had all fallen under her watchful eye. And then, of course, there were the letters—those mysterious, paper-wrapped confessions that had piled up in his chambers while he lay weak and unconscious. Letters from bootmakers demanding payment, hatters with urgent bills, and, more troublingly, one or two from a certain Miss Fanny Bolton—whose name, though never spoken aloud, hung over the household like a cloud.

Mrs. Pendennis, bless her, was not cruel; she simply couldn’t bear the thought of her boy being led astray by such a girl. She’d removed the brass knocker from the inner door of Arthur’s room so that the postman’s knock wouldn’t disturb him—though it also meant no one could deliver any mail without her permission. It was a small act of maternal control, but one that carried great weight. She read every letter herself, answered them when necessary, and kept the whole correspondence under lock and key—especially those from Fanny Bolton.

And why not? Pen, as Arthur was affectionately known, had always been a free spirit—a man who gave generously, laughed loudly, and paid little attention to bills or consequences. But this made his mother all the more anxious. She had saved money—carefully, frugally, almost stingily—and now she used it to settle his debts. Not because he asked, but because she believed in honor, in duty, in the idea that a gentleman should never be burdened by shame. At least, that’s what she told herself.

She didn’t tell him about Fanny. Not yet. But she did ask her brother-in-law, Major Pendennis, whether he knew anything about “that dreadful affair.” The Major, a man whose morals were shaped more by experience than by doctrine, sat on a bench at Richmond with a look of profound amusement, as if he’d just heard a joke too good to spoil.

“Ah,” said the Major, squinting through his spectacles. “You want to know about Fanny Bolton?”

“I do,” Helen replied, clutching her handkerchief. “I must know. My son—my dear Arthur—is involved in some terrible scandal.”

The Major sighed, leaned back, and said: “My dear, I don’t know anything about it. And I don’t wish to know. And if you’re asking for my advice, I suggest you don’t know either.”

“You don’t think it serious?” Helen asked, trembling.

“Serious?” said the Major, raising an eyebrow. “Is it worse than flirting with a grisette? Because if so, I’d say you’re overreacting. Young men will be young men. If every mother broke her heart over every little flirtation—why, half England would be mourning.”

Helen gasped. “But it’s not just flirting! It’s a sin! A crime!”

“Oh, come now,” said the Major, gently. “What proof have you? Has Arthur confessed? Has anyone accused him? Or is it just a letter?”

“A letter,” she whispered. “Only one. From her. I found it. I opened it.”

“Did you?” said the Major, pretending surprise. “And what did it say?”

“It was written in bad spelling,” Helen said, “and full of sorrow. She said she was unhappy. That her father was cruel. That she loved him. That she prayed for his health. And—oh, Major, I can’t bear it—she signed it ‘Your poor little Fanny.’”

The Major chuckled. “Well, I’ll be damned. That sounds like a love letter. Not a blackmail note. Not a declaration of war. Just a girl writing to the boy who once took her to Vauxhall Gardens and paid for her ice cream.”

“But she was in his rooms!” Helen insisted. “While he was ill!”

“Yes,” said the Major, “but only after he was delirious. He didn’t know who she was. And Flanagan, the laundress, says she came with Mr. Bows, that old Irish gentleman who helped me get away from Stillbrook—by the way, I never paid the cab fare. Quite forgettable, really.”

“And the girl’s parents?” Helen pressed.

“They’re divided,” said the Major. “Her father beats her mother, who insists the girl is innocent. But the mother also told Goodenough—our dear doctor—that Arthur treated her daughter like a brute. So, you see, the story is murky. But here’s the thing: if Arthur had done something truly awful, wouldn’t they have come forward? Wouldn’t the girl’s family have demanded justice? Instead, they’re keeping quiet. And Arthur hasn’t said a word. So perhaps—just perhaps—he’s innocent.”

“Innocent?” Helen repeated, tears glistening. “Oh, thank God! Thank Heaven!”

The Major patted her hand. “Now, listen. If your son has done nothing wrong, why punish him with suspicion? If he has done something… well, then he’ll confess. But until then, let’s not assume guilt. Let’s not make him feel like a criminal before he’s even committed a crime.”

Helen wiped her eyes and stood up. “I’ll go to him now. I’ll show him the letter. I’ll ask him plainly. He’s innocent. I know he is.”

“Don’t,” said the Major, stopping her. “If you confront him, and he denies it, you’ll break his trust. If he admits it, you’ll break his heart. And if he’s guilty—well, then you’ll have to marry him off to Fanny Bolton. Which, by Jove, would be quite a scene.”

Helen laughed, despite herself. “Marry Fanny Bolton?”

“Or worse—marry Mrs. Flanagan,” the Major added, grinning. “She’s a fine woman, but she drinks gin. And she’s already offered to take care of Arthur. I wouldn’t mind. But I suspect she’d expect a ring.”

Helen giggled. “You’re terrible, brother.”

“I’m practical,” said the Major. “And I’ve seen enough of life to know that sometimes the best way to avoid a scandal is to ignore it. Like a dog ignoring a bone it doesn’t want.”

So, instead of confrontation, they decided on a plan. They would travel abroad. To the Rhine. To a watering-place where no one knew Arthur’s name, where the air was fresh, and the memories faded. Doctor Goodenough agreed—it was the best cure for both mother and son. And Warrington, Arthur’s closest friend, was invited along.

But Warrington hesitated. “I can’t go,” he said. “I’ve got responsibilities. And no money.”

Arthur looked crushed. “But you must come! You’re part of the family now.”

“No,” said Warrington. “I’ve got to stay. I’ve got to earn a living.”

And so the party dwindled. But Arthur, ever hopeful, turned to his mother. “We’ll go anyway. We’ll leave tomorrow. I’ll write to Warrington later. He’ll understand.”

And indeed, as the sun set over Richmond, the mood lightened. The fear of Fanny Bolton faded into the distance. The letter was tucked away. The past was buried. And for the first time in weeks, Arthur felt like himself again.

Later, as he walked with Warrington on the terrace, he confessed: “You know, I was tempted. Very tempted. But I escaped. Thanks to my illness.”

Warrington nodded gravely. “It’s easier to run than to fight. Especially when the enemy is a girl with pretty eyes and a broken heart.”

Arthur smiled. “Exactly. And the best thing about running? You don’t have to explain yourself afterward.”

They laughed. And somewhere, far away, Fanny Bolton probably wondered what had become of the man who once promised her the moon.

But that, of course, was another story.
