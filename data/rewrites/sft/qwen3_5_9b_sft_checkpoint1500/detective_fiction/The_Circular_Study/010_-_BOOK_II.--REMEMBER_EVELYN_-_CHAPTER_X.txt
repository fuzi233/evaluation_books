"A blonde, you say, sir?"

"Yes, Sweetwater; not of the ordinary sort, but one of those frail, ethereal beings with whom it is so difficult to connect criminal intent. He, conversely, matches Miss Butterworth's description perfectly—those gentlemanly athletes whose towering presence commands immediate notice. Seen together, they would stand out instantly. Yet, what evidence leads us to believe they are together?"

"How were they attired?"

"Dressed in the height of fashion and respectability. He wore a brown-checked suit, seemingly freshly tailored; she, a dove-gray gown trimmed in white. Her parasol matched the hue of her hat and plumes. Both were youthful, and, judging by Miss Butterworth's account, possessed sensitive tempaments unused to the rigors of crime. She was overcome by faintness upon exiting the house, and he, despite ample opportunity for restraint, succumbed to overwhelming emotion. He set her down in the vestibule and immediately merged into the throng of passersby. Can you locate them, Sweetwater?"

"Do you possess any clues to their identity beyond this parasol?"

"None, Sweetwater, save for these few faded rose petals recovered from the floor of Mr. Adams's study."

"That gives me a puzzle, Mr. Gryce," the young detective observed, peering skeptically at the parasol offered to him before letting the petals slip casually through his fingers. "I lack the same confidence in success that I felt previously. Now that I see how heavily fortune weighs on such inquiries, I realize the daunting nature of the task. Had Miss Butterworth not been so precise, I surely would have failed in my previous endeavor, and I suspect I shall fail here as well. Nevertheless, I will make another attempt to identify the owner of this parasol, if only to learn the value of failure. And now, sir, where do you propose I begin? With a florist, using these faded rose petals. While every other officer on the force would start with the parasol, I intend to derive a clue from these petals. I may be an egotist, but I cannot help it. The parasol offers me no assistance."

"And what hope do you harbor from the rose petals? How can a florist aid in locating this young woman through them?"

"He may identify the species of rose from which they fell. Once I know that, I may trace the specific shop where the bouquet was purchased by this somewhat conspicuous pair."

"You may. I am not the man to dampen anyone's spirits. Every individual possesses their own method, and until proven worthless, I remain silent."

Young Sweetwater, brimming now with nerve, enthusiasm, and hope, bowed. Satisfied to operate independently, he nodded.

"I may return within an hour, and you may not see me for a week," he noted as he prepared to depart.

"Luck to your search!" was the brief reply, concluding the interview. Moments later, Sweetwater was gone.

An hour elapsed; he did not return. The day passed, and still no Sweetwater. Another day followed, marked only by the exchange of notes between Mr. Gryce and Miss Butterworth. Hers was read by the veteran detective with a smile, perhaps due to its terseness or its distinct character.

*I do not presume to dictate or even suggest to the New York police, but have you inquired of the postman in a certain district regarding whether he recalls the postmark on any letters delivered to Mr. Adams?*

His response, however, was scrutinized with a frown by his demanding colleague in Gramercy Park. The rationale was evident.

*Suggestions are always welcome, and even dictation can be tolerated from you. However, the postman delivers countless letters on that block; asking him to recall specific postmarks is futile. Regrettably, I must close this avenue of inquiry.*

In the interim, the anxiety of both parties grew intense, Mr. Gryce's exceeding so. Consequently, he felt a profound relief when, on the third morning, he discovered Sweetwater waiting at the office, a satisfied smile illuminating his plain countenance. Sweetwater had withheld his findings for his special patron, and upon entering the private room, he turned with beaming eyes toward the old detective, exclaiming:

"News, sir! Good news! I have found them; I have located both, and by what a fortuitous stroke! It was a blind trail, but when the florist suggested those petals might have fallen from a bridal rose—and well, sir, I know any woman can carry bridal roses—but recalling that her companion's attire seemed fresh from the tailor, clad in gray and white—it sparked an idea. I initiated my search at the Bureau of Vital Statistics."

"Brilliant!" ejaculated the old detective. "Provided, of course, that it works."

"And it did, sir! I may have been born under a lucky star, certainly, but once embarked on this line of investigation, I proceeded straight to the conclusion. Shall I explain? Scouring the registry of marriages within the city limits over the past fortnight, I encountered the name Eva Poindexter. 'Eva!' a name familiar to the household on ---- Street. I resolved to pursue this Eva."

"A prudent deduction! How did you proceed?"

"I went directly to the officiating clergyman. He was a kind, affable minister, sir, and I encountered no difficulty in engaging him in conversation."

"And you described the bride?"

"No, I steered the dialogue so thoroughly that he described her."

"Excellent. And what impression did he convey? Delicate? Pale?"

"Sir, he had not witnessed a wedding of such beauty in years. She was slight, almost fragile, yet radiant, possessing a delicate complexion suggesting better health than her dainty frame implied. It was a private ceremony, held in a hotel parlor, but her father was present—"

"Her father?" Mr. Gryce's hypothesis received its first jolt. The elderly man who had departed laughing from Mr. Adams's residence was not the father to whom those cryptic lines in Mr. Adams's handwriting were addressed. Or perhaps this young woman was not the subject of those lines.

"Is there something amiss?" Sweetwater inquired.

Mr. Gryce resumed his impassivity.

"No; I had not anticipated his attendance at the wedding; that is all."

"Apologies, sir, but there is no denying his presence. The groom—"

"Yes, tell me of the groom."

"He was precisely the man you described as leaving Mr. Adams's house with her. Tall, finely developed, with a majestic demeanor and gentlemanly bearing. Even his attire aligns with your expectations: a checked suit, brown in color, of the latest cut. Oh, he is the man!"

Mr. Gryce, suddenly fixated on the lid of his inkstand, recalled the lines penned by Mr. Adams moments before his demise. He found himself utterly bewildered. How could these facts be reconciled? What allusion could there possibly be in these words to the newlywed bride of another? They read, rather, as if she were his own bride, as evidenced by:

Something must be wrong. Sweetwater must have been misled by a series of extraordinary coincidences. Dropping the lid of the inkstand with a motion meant to provoke a smile, he looked up.

"I fear it has been a foolish chase, Sweetwater. The facts you recount regarding this couple, the fact of their marriage itself, tally so poorly with the evidence leading us to expect otherwise..."

"Pardon me, sir, but allow me to finish? At the Imperial Hotel, where they wed, I learned that the father and daughter registered as hailing from a small town in Pennsylvania, yet I could glean nothing regarding the groom. He did not appear until the ceremony commenced, and afterward, he was seen escorting his bride away in one carriage while the older gentleman departed in another. The latter concerned me little; it was the young couple I was tasked to find. Using standard investigative techniques, I traced them to the Waldorf, where I confirmed my pursuit of the correct pair. On the afternoon of the very day Mr. Adams died, this young husband and wife left the hotel on foot and did not return. Their clothes, which had been abandoned, were retrieved two days later by an elderly gentleman claiming to be her father, whose appearance matched the registrant at the Imperial. All of this supports my theory, does it not, particularly considering the groom's name—"

"You have not revealed it."

"It is Adams, Thomas Adams. Same surname as the deceased man, you see. At least, it is the same name."

Mr. Gryce surveyed the young man with admiration, though he hesitated to grant full credence.
