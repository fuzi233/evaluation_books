Luckily for us, Infadoos and the elder chiefs carried the map of Loo in their bodies as surely as a cook carries the memory of a hearth: every alley, every cattle-gap, every narrow run between hut-walls where the smell of last night’s ash still clung. In the unnatural dusk men’s voices had turned low and startled, as if even speech might break. We slipped along side-ways, unchallenged, while above us the sky held its breath.

For more than an hour we went on so, feet scuffing in dust that felt suddenly cold. Then the eclipse began to loosen. The bitten edge of the moon showed again—first a thin, pale rim, like the bright blade of a knife drawn from a sheath. A silver streak burst out, and with it a ruddy glow, as though some unseen fire had been coaxed to life behind a shutter. The stars, those watchful pin-pricks, began to fade. In five minutes there was enough light to see our own hands and the sheen of spear-points.

We found ourselves clear of Loo and nearing a flat-topped hill, a great table of rock and grass, horseshoe-shaped, its sides strewn with boulders like pots knocked from a shelf and left where they fell. It was not high—no more than a couple of hundred feet—but it rose with a stubborn, compact steepness that made the lungs work and the thighs burn. On the summit, the table-land offered wide camping-ground; it had been used like a sanatorium for regiments returned from fever-country, a place to sleep and sweat the sickness out. Tonight it was something else. As we climbed in returning moonlight, we saw more than one regiment’s fires; there were several camps, their rings and ranks spread across the grass.

When we gained the top, we walked into a human tumult: men roused from sleep, shivering, huddled in knots, fear moving through them like wind through dry mealie leaves. They had seen the moon go out. They did not know whether the world had been swallowed or merely punished. We pushed through without a word, and came to a hut near the centre.

There, to our astonishment, stood two men waiting, bent under loads—our few goods and chattels. In the rush of flight we had left them behind as one leaves a pot on the fire when the roof catches. Infadoos nodded toward the bundles as calmly as if he had merely ordered extra water for a stew.

“I sent for them,” he said. “And also for these.”

He lifted, with a flourish, Good’s long-lost trousers.

Good gave a little cry that was half delight, half relief, the sound of a man reunited with an old certainty. He pounced on them, and, without shame or ceremony, began to put them on at once.

“Surely my lord will not hide his beautiful white legs!” Infadoos exclaimed, sincerely regretful, as though a prized dish had been taken off the table before the guests had tasted it.

But Good persisted. It was, I believe, the last time the Kukuanas ever had a fair chance of admiring those celebrated limbs. Good is modest to the marrow. Henceforward they had to satisfy their aesthetic hungers with his single whisker, his transparent eye, and the extraordinary trick of his movable teeth, which gave them more wonder than any song.

Infadoos’s face still held a sort of fond amazement over the trousers when he turned to business. He told us he had ordered the regiments to muster at daybreak. The chiefs had agreed: the rebellion must be explained while men’s minds were still soft with fear of the darkened moon, and Ignosi must be shown plainly—named, placed, claimed—as rightful heir.

So, when the sun was up, the troops—some twenty thousand, the flower of the Kukuana army—were drawn together on an open space. We went out with Infadoos and Ignosi. The men stood in three sides of a dense square, shields overlapping in disciplined blocks, the fourth side left open to us. The sight was magnificent: dark bodies, iron glints, the steady breathing of many thousands making a low, living hush. Around us pressed chiefs and captains; silence was proclaimed.

Then Infadoos spoke.

Like most Kukuanas of high rank, he was a born orator. His words came with a cadence that made even plain facts sound like a chant remembered from grandfathers. He told them of Ignosi’s father—how he had been murdered by Twala, and how the wife and child had been driven out to starve. He reminded them what they all knew in their bones: that Twala’s rule was a famine of mercy, a long gnawing hunger in the heart of the land. He pointed to the previous night’s horrors, when noble men had been dragged forth under the name of evil-doers and done to death with wicked haste.

Then he turned their fear into a tool.

The white lords from the Stars, he said, had looked down and seen the country’s trouble, and had come—at great personal inconvenience, which pleased them mightily as proof of greatness—to relieve it. They had taken Ignosi, the true king, by the hand and led him over the mountains. They had seen Twala’s wickedness, and to save the girl Foulata and steady the wavering, they had, by high magic, put out the moon and slain Scragga, the young fiend. And they would stand by the Kukuanas to overthrow Twala and set Ignosi upon the seat of his fathers.

A murmur rose—approval, awe, the shifting sound of a people choosing a story to live inside.

Ignosi stepped forward. He reiterated what his uncle had said, but in his own manner—less flowing, more like the ring of iron on stone. He ended with power:

“O chiefs, captains, soldiers, and people, ye have heard my words. Now must ye make choice between me and him who sits upon my throne, the uncle who killed his brother and hunted his brother’s child forth to die in the cold and the night. That I am indeed the king these”—he pointed to the chiefs—“can tell you, for they have seen the snake about my middle. If I were not the king, would these white men be on my side with all their magic? Tremble, chiefs, captains, soldiers, and people! Is not the darkness they have brought upon the land to confound Twala and cover our flight, darkness even in the hour of the full moon, yet before your eyes?”

“It is,” answered the soldiers.

“I am the king; I say to you, I am the king,” he went on, drawing himself up and lifting his broad-bladed battle-axe till it flashed. “If there be any man among you who says that it is not so, let him stand forth and I will fight him now, and his blood shall be a red token that I tell you true. Let him stand forth, I say.”

He shook the axe; sunlight ran along it like water.

Nobody accepted this heroic invitation to be slaughtered for argument’s sake, so Ignosi went on.

He promised rewards—oxen, wives, precedence of regiments. And he promised something rarer than cattle: an end to fear used as law. When he sat on the seat of his fathers, bloodshed would cease in the land. No longer would men cry for justice and find slaughter. No longer would the witch-finder hunt a man out so that he might be slain without cause. The “eating up” of kraals would cease; each would sleep secure in his own hut; justice would walk blindfold through the land.

“Have ye chosen, chiefs, captains, soldiers, and people?”

“We have chosen, O king,” they answered, and their voices held that relieved steadiness men have when they finally stop wavering.

Ignosi pointed beyond the hill to Loo. Twala’s messengers, he said, were even then running east and west and north and south to gather an army to kill them all. To-morrow, or the next day, Twala would come in force. Then Ignosi would see who was truly his man, who feared not to die; and such a one would not be forgotten when spoil was divided. He sent them to their huts to make ready for war.

There was a pause, like the moment before a drum is struck. Then a chief lifted his hand and out rolled the royal salute: “Koom.” It was the sound of acceptance, as final as a seal. The battalions marched away in order.

Half an hour later we held a council of war with all the commanders. From our height we could see Loo like a disturbed anthill: troops mustering, runners darting out in every direction to summon more. We had about twenty thousand men—seven of the best regiments. Twala, Infadoos calculated, had thirty to thirty-five thousand already in Loo, with perhaps another five thousand by midday on the morrow. Some might desert to us, but one could not season a pot with salt not yet fetched.

Active preparations were clear enough. Armed bodies patrolled the hill’s foot in slow circles, a tightening ring. Yet Infadoos and the chiefs judged there would be no assault that day. Twala would use the daylight to steady his men’s minds, to scrape away the moral terror left by the moon’s darkening. The onslaught would come tomorrow. They were right.

So we set to work to strengthen the position. Nearly every man laboured; the day, though long in the sky, felt far too short in the hands. The paths up the hill were blocked with masses of stone; every approach was made as hard as time allowed. Boulders were piled where they could be loosed and rolled down on climbing ranks. Stations were allotted to regiments, orders passed, messengers ran like ants between knots of authority. The hill that had nursed regiments back from fever now braced itself to break an army’s teeth.

Just before sundown we rested, sweating and dusty, and saw a small company approaching from Loo. One man held a palm leaf as sign of a herald. Ignosi, Infadoos, a couple of chiefs, and we went down to meet him at the foot of the mountain.

He was a gallant-looking fellow, wrapped in the regulation leopard-skin cloak. His voice carried with the assurance of one who believes the world belongs to his master.

“Greeting!” he cried. “The king’s greeting to those who make unholy war against the king; the lion’s greeting to the jackals that snarl around his heels.”

“Speak,” I said.

“These are the king’s words. Surrender to the king’s mercy ere a worse thing befall you. Already the shoulder has been torn from the black bull, and the king drives him bleeding about the camp.”

The phrase turned my stomach. There are cruelties that announce war more plainly than any drum.

“What are Twala’s terms?” I asked, partly from curiosity and partly because sometimes even foul offers reveal the measure of the man.

The herald swelled, like a singer reciting a long list of patrons.

“His terms are merciful, worthy of a great king. These are the words of Twala, the one-eyed, the mighty, the husband of a thousand wives, lord of the Kukuanas, keeper of the Great Road, beloved of the Strange Ones who sit in silence at the mountains yonder, Calf of the Black Cow, Elephant whose tread shakes the earth, Terror of the evil-doer, Ostrich whose feet devour the desert, huge One, black One, wise One, king from generation to generation! These are the words of Twala: ‘I will have mercy and be satisfied with a little blood. One in every ten shall die, the rest shall go free; but the white man Incubu, who slew Scragga my son, and the black man his servant, who pretends to my throne, and Infadoos my brother, who brews rebellion against me, these shall die by torture as an offering to the Silent Ones.’ Such are the merciful words of Twala.”

We spoke together quickly. Then I answered him loudly so that those above might hear and carry it like smoke into the camp:

“Go back, thou dog, to Twala who sent thee, and say that we—Ignosi, veritable king of the Kukuanas; Incubu, Bougwan, and Macumazahn, the wise ones from the Stars who make dark the moon; Infadoos of the royal house; and the chiefs, captains, and people here gathered—make answer and say: ‘We will not surrender. Before the sun has gone down twice, Twala’s corpse shall stiffen at Twala’s gate, and Ignosi, whose father Twala slew, shall reign in his stead.’ Now go, ere we whip thee away, and beware how thou dost lift a hand against such as we are.”

The herald laughed, loudly and contemptuously, like a man snapping a twig.

“Ye frighten not men with such swelling words,” he cried. “Show yourselves as bold to-morrow, O ye who darken the moon. Be bold, fight, and be merry, before the crows pick your bones till they are whiter than your faces. Farewell; perhaps we may meet in the fight. Fly not to the Stars, but wait for me, I pray, white men.”

With that he turned back toward Loo, his palm leaf bobbing like a pennant.

Almost at once the sun sank. Night came down, and with it work. Exhausted as we were, preparations went on by moonlight—stones shifted, posts reinforced, sentries placed, messages carried in whispers so as not to rouse panic. The camp’s soundscape changed: fewer voices, more footfalls, the scrape of rock on rock, the occasional metallic click of a spear-head, and the steady night wind combing the grass. In those sounds men tried to find courage. There is a kind of health in quiet; the hill’s natural hush held us together better than any shouting could have done.

At last, about an hour after midnight, all that could be done was done. The camp sank into silence, broken only by the challenge of a sentry now and then—two words flung out and answered, like the call and response of nocturnal birds.

Sir Henry and I, with Ignosi and a chief, went down and made a round of the pickets. As we walked, spears gleamed suddenly from unexpected shadows and vanished again when we spoke the password. No one slept at his post.

We climbed back, picking our way through thousands of sleeping warriors. Many were taking their last earthly rest. Moonlight flickered along spear-shafts and played over faces, turning them solemn, almost carved. The chilly wind tugged at tall plumes until they looked like hearse-feathers. Men lay in confusion, arms flung out, legs bent at odd angles, shields tucked close as if even in sleep they expected the shove of an enemy’s body.

“How many of these do you suppose will be alive at this time to-morrow?” Sir Henry asked.

I could only shake my head. I looked again at the sleepers, and to my worn, excited mind it seemed Death had already marked them. It is a strange thing, the way the mind reaches for patterns when the future becomes a blade: it singled out men as if it could see the ones sealed to slaughter, and pity came on me like a flood. Tonight these thousands slept their healthy sleep. Tomorrow, they and many others—ourselves, perhaps—would be stiff in the cold. Wives would wake as widows. Children would become fatherless. The world would not pause for them.

Yet above, the old moon shone on serenely, untroubled as a domestic lamp kept burning while the house argues. The wind moved through grass the way it must have moved ages before men named it, and will move ages after our names are forgotten. And still—though names vanish—the breath a man breathes does not entirely leave the earth. The sound of words spoken once does not wholly die; it becomes part of the great, unownable murmur that lives in places. I felt, standing there in that lunar hush, that the universe keeps our voices the way a valley keeps echoes: changed, thinned, mingled, but not nothing. Not sheeted ghosts, but elements of lives—stubborn remnants that blend and change and change again.

All these reflections crowded in—for as I grow older, I regret to say, a detestable habit of thinking seems to be getting hold of me—while I stared at those grim, fantastic lines.

“Curtis,” I said, “I am in a condition of pitiable fear.”

Sir Henry stroked his yellow beard and laughed softly.

“I have heard you make that sort of remark before, Quatermain.”

“Well, I mean it now. I very much doubt if one of us will be alive to-morrow night. We shall be attacked in overwhelming force, and it is quite a chance if we can hold this place.”

“We’ll give a good account of some of them,” he answered. “Look here, Quatermain, this business is nasty, and one with which, properly speaking, we ought not to be mixed up. But we are in for it, so we must make the best of our job. Speaking personally, I’d rather be killed fighting than any other way, and now that there seems little chance of finding my poor brother, it makes the idea easier to me. Fortune favours the brave, and we may succeed. Anyway, the battle will be awful, and having a reputation to keep up, we shall need to be in the thick of it.”

He said the last in a mournful voice, but his eye held a gleam that contradicted him. I have long suspected Sir Henry Curtis actually likes fighting.

We slept a couple of hours.

Just about dawn Infadoos woke us. Great activity, he said, was to be seen in Loo; skirmishers were driving in our outposts. We rose and dressed for the fray, each of us putting on his chain armour shirt—never had iron felt so welcome on my skin.

Sir Henry went the whole length and dressed like a native warrior.

“When you are in Kukuanaland, do as the Kukuanas do,” he remarked, drawing the shining steel over his broad breast; it fitted him like a glove.

At his request Infadoos had provided full war gear. He fastened round his throat the leopard-skin cloak of a commanding officer. He bound on his brow the plume of black ostrich feathers worn only by generals. Around his middle he tied a magnificent moocha of white ox-tails. Sandals, a leglet of goat’s hair, a heavy battle-axe with a rhinoceros-horn handle, a round iron shield covered with white ox-hide, and the regulation number of tollas completed him—save that he added his revolver.

Savage it was, no doubt; but I seldom saw a finer sight. The barbaric finery seemed designed to announce what his body already declared: strength. When Ignosi arrived presently in similar dress, I found myself thinking I had never seen two such splendid men.

As for Good and myself, the mail did not suit us nearly so well. Good insisted on keeping his trousers; and a stout, short gentleman with an eye-glass, one half of his face shaved, arrayed in a mail shirt tucked carefully into a seedy pair of corduroys, looks more remarkable than imposing. My own chain shirt was too big, so I wore it over all my clothes; it bulged in an ungainly fashion. I discarded my trousers, however, keeping only my veldtschoons, meaning to fight bare-legged to run the lighter if retreat became necessary. The mail coat, a spear, a shield I hardly knew how to use, a couple of tollas, a revolver, and a huge plume pinned into the top of my shooting hat to give a bloodthirsty finish—this was my modest equipment.

Of course we had our rifles too, but ammunition was scarce; and in a charge they would be useless. We arranged they should be carried behind us by bearers.

When we were equipped we swallowed food hastily—dry, hurried mouthfuls, eaten not for pleasure but to keep the limbs steady. Even so, the smell of it—smoke and grain—seemed to anchor the mind for a moment in the ordinary world, as though somewhere, in some hut, a pot still simmered as it always had, indifferent to kings.

Then we went out to see how matters stood.

On the table-land there was a little brown-stone koppie that served as head-quarters and conning tower. There we found Infadoos among his own regiment, the Greys—the finest in the Kukuana army, three thousand five hundred strong now—held in reserve. The men lay on the grass in companies, watching.

Below, the king’s forces crept out of Loo in long ant-like columns. There seemed no end to them—three columns, each, by our reckoning, eleven or twelve thousand men. As soon as they were clear of the town they formed up. Then one body marched to the right, one to the left, and the third came on slowly toward us.

“Ah,” said Infadoos, quietly, “they are going to attack us on three sides at once.”

It was serious news. Our hill-top position, a mile and a half in circumference, was too wide for comfort; we needed concentration, yet could not dictate the shape of the storm. So we made the best of it. Orders went out to the regiments to prepare to receive the separate onslaughts, and the great waiting began, the air full of distant footfalls and the thin, metallic whisper of arms being readied—sounds that, once heard, are hard to forget, as though the land itself has a language for war that no man can truly translate.
