“Dearest Blanche,” Arthur Pen penned, mid a very chaotic post-catastrophe hangover, “you’re forever spinning grand dramatic romances out of real life, aren’t you? Well, congrats! You’ve just landed a starring role in one, and it’s not the fun part where the heroine sashays into her father’s royal palace, shows her new husband all the gold and crown jewels, and coos ‘all this is ours.’ It’s the part where you wake up to find the prince you married is actually penniless Claude Melnotte, or you walk in just as your husband Alnaschar has kicked his entire fortune of porcelain bowls all over the cobblestones. Quick side note: that Alnaschar bloke wasn’t even married, for the record—he hadn’t even popped the question to the Vizier’s daughter before he smashed his whole investment to smithereens.

“So here’s your choice: laugh me off, dump the broke guy, and go chase the fancy rich husband you were daydreaming about, or be the Lady of Lyons and stick around with the penniless fool? I’ll do my absolute best to make it worth your while, I promise. But let’s be real, there’s no secret fortune waiting for us. Our Cinderella carriage vanished at midnight, our Belgravia townhouse was spirited away by a very unimpressed evil genie, and I’m no more a Member of Parliament than I am a bishop in the House of Lords or a duke prancing around with a fancy garter on his knee. We’ll scrape by, writing silly stories no one remembers after a month, me dragging my feet through law school, if I’m very very lucky (and work very very hard, which we both know I’m bad at) maybe we’ll score a random colonial post one day and you can be an Indian judge’s lady. I’m gonna buy back the *Pall Mall Gazette* too, Shandon’s old partners are sick of running it and will sell it for pennies. Warrington will run the show for me, we’ll get Finucane the sub-editor on board, and the three of us will churn out respectable journalism and pay our rent on time. We can get four nice rooms above the newspaper offices in Catherine Street, Strand, plus a kitchen and a little garret for Laura—our very own live-in saint, basically. Or if you hate the Strand, we could get a tiny house in Waterloo Road! It’s very charming, you just have to pay a halfpenny toll every time you cross the bridge. Our hypothetical future kids can go to King’s College, that’s nice, right? Does all this sound like a joke?

“Blanche, it’s not a joke. I’m as sober as I’ve ever been, and I’m laying all my cards on the table. We’ve already won enough to afford a cab ride when we’re too tired to walk, and never have to skip the omnibus on a rainy day. That’s it. Is that enough for you, my dainty little fancy-lady? I genuinely don’t know if you can hack the boring, humdrum life I’m offering. If you say ‘yes Arthur, I’ll stick with you through thick and thin and cheer you on no matter what,’ I’ll be the luckiest guy alive, and I’ll do everything I can to make you happy. If you’d rather chase the grander life you deserve? I won’t stand in your way. I’ll just wave from the crowd when you ride past in your fancy chariot to your first court presentation, like I saw Lady Mirabel do last season—her husband was dripping with medals and ribbons, the coachman had more roses pinned to his coat than the entire royal garden. Which do you want? The gilded chariot, or a quiet walk home where you have to mend your husband’s holes in his socks?

“I can’t tell you exactly what blew up all my prospects these last 24 hours, not yet anyway, but it’s all irreversible, like Alnaschar’s shattered porcelain. I already told Sir Francis Clavering I can’t take that empty Parliament seat anymore, and I won’t touch a penny of your fortune beyond the tiny sum you’ve had since your grandpa died. None of this is anyone’s fault, but it’s all done, no do-overs. I’m not moping about it—no point crying over smashed teacups. I’ll be happy enough if you are. Warrington and Laura both know the whole messy secret, and they’re 100% in my corner, so we’ve got two of the best people alive on our side. Who cares if some random lord doesn’t invite us to his fancy ball, right?”

Blanche wrote back two letters: one for Laura, one for Pen himself, and her reply was so flowery and dramatic Pen stared at it for ten full minutes trying to work out if she’d said yes or no. It was covered in her fancy scented ink, gilt-edged, every line oozing over-the-top romantic wailing. “You don’t love me right!” she raved. “You treat my whole heart like it’s common dross, not the precious diamonds of the deepest caverns of my soul! I’ve cried buckets over this letter! I prayed, I stayed up all night, my whole dream of being your everything is fading like a wilted flower! You’re so cold! You’d let me go without even a fight! Is love just disappointment for everyone, and I was a fool to think I’d be the exception? I’ll write more when my brain stops spinning. Tell Laura to come visit as soon as we get back from the country. Bless you forever, Arthur! B.”

Pen cackled sardonically, twiddling the musky letter in his hand like it was a very silly riddle. Fortune had well and truly dumped him, and he kind of deserved it, honestly. He was still grinning to himself, turning the letter over and over, when his servant popped in to say a Mr. Samuel Huxter was here on very urgent business. If Pen had peeked out into the hall he’d have seen his old med school acquaintance sucking his walking stick like it owed him money, rolling his eyes so hard they almost got stuck, looking like he was about to confess to arson.

“Send him in!” Pen said, highly amused, and Sam shuffled in, looking more hangdog than ever.

“Take a seat, Mr. Huxter,” Pen said, putting on his fanciest posh voice. “What can I do for you?”

“I don’t wanna talk in front of the flunkey—before the man,” Sam yelped, so the servant ducked out. “I’m in a right old mess.”

“Oh dear?”

“Fanny sent me to you,” the young surgeon mumbled.

“Fanny? Is she all right? I’ve been meaning to visit, I’ve just been drowning in work since I got back to London.”

“Heard all about you from my dad and Jack Hobnell,” Sam cut in, blushing. “Congrats on the Parliament seat, and the posh lady you’re gonna marry. Fanny says congrats too.”

“Ah, well, there’s many a slip between cup and lip, who even knows who’ll end up MP for Clavering next session?”

“You’ve got my dad wrapped around your little finger,” Sam pushed on. “You got him that fancy gig tending to Clavering Park, he was over the moon when you called him in. Hobnell wrote me all about it. You think you could talk him round for me, Mr. Pendennis?”

“Talk him round about what?”

“I done it,” Sam said, making a very weird guilty face.

“Wait a second—you didn’t hurt that sweet little Fanny, did you?” Pen shot up, furious.

“Nah, nothing like that—we eloped! We got married five weeks ago last Thursday. And my dad’s gonna have a conniption fit, he was supposed to take me into the family surgery as partner once I passed my exams, we were gonna be ‘Huxter and Son’ for goodness sake. But I had to go and do it, and he’s already writing to say he’s coming up to London tomorrow to pick up some medical supplies, and then the whole thing’s gonna blow up in my face.”

“When did all this happen?” Pen asked, not exactly thrilled that a girl who’d once had a little crush on him had moved on, but not that mad about it either.

“Two days after Miss Amory showed up at Shepherd’s Inn,” Sam said. “I was there tending to old Cos’s bad leg, ran into Strong who said some lady had a fit of hysterics in his rooms. Got up there, and it was Blanche’s old housekeeper, kicking and screaming like a banshee. Strong, Altamont, Blanche all there too, Blanche as pale as a sheet, Altamont stomping around furious. Whole lot of drama, we got the old lady calmed down and sent her home in a cab. Next day I popped round to their place in Grosvenor Place to check everyone was okay, but they’d already bolted without saying thank you. Then two days later… well, me and Fanny got hitched.”

Oh! Pen thought, a cold little jolt hitting him. Blanche knew that secret about her dad a whole month ago. That’s why she wrote that waffly letter today. She doesn’t want to spill her father’s secret, so she’s making up a fake excuse to break off our engagement so no one gets hurt. She’s the most generous girl alive!

“You know who Altamont is, right?” Sam asked, breaking the silence. “Me and Fanny have been gossiping about it for ages, we swear he’s Mrs. Lightfoot’s dead first husband come back from the grave, just as she’s remarried. And—uh,” Sam scowled, jealousy still gnawing at him even though he was married now, “Fanny still talks about you sometimes, y’know.”

“Enough about that,” Pen said, cutting him off. “Let’s sort your mess out. Congratulations on the marriage, honestly. Fanny’s the sweetest, kindest girl alive, she deserves a good husband, and you seem like a solid bloke. What do you need me to do?”

“She said you’d talk my dad and my mum round,” Sam said, shaking Pen’s hand gratefully. “Mum’s dead proud of being a clergyman’s daughter, she’s gonna throw a fit when she finds out Fanny’s not posh. But she’s a Huxter now, right? She’ll fit right in once she’s had a bit of practice in society. You should hear her sing and play piano! Old Bows taught her. And she’s a total natural flirt, no way around it. Even my best man Jack Linton from med school, he’ll stop by and sit there staring at her and she’ll bat her eyes at him without even trying. Bows says if you put 20 men in a room with her, she won’t rest till every single one of them is hanging off her every word.”

“Get her mum to stay with her, that’ll settle her down,” Pen laughed.

“Nah, her mum’s gotta keep working the porter’s lodge at Clavering, she can’t come live with us. Can’t have the neighbours thinking I’m related to the lodge keeper, it’d ruin my professional standing,” Sam said, stroking his chin like he was a very important dignitary. Pen was trying not to snort when there was another knock at the door, and the servant announced Mr. Bows.

The old grumpy music teacher shuffled in, his bald head glowing in the sun, face pink, hands shaking a little as he shook Pen’s hand. He coughed, wiped his face on his ragged cotton handkerchief, and plopped down with his hands on his knees. Pen looked at him, and felt a sharp little twinge of sympathy. This old guy had poured all his heart and his talent out at some woman’s feet, and she’d trampled all over it, same as half the people he knew. Sam winked at Bows, then winked at Pen like ‘ain’t this daft old sod a fool, head over heels for my wife?’

“Well, we lost, Bows,” Pen said, nodding at Sam. “Here’s the lucky winner.”

“Lucky winner’s right,” Bows said dryly.

“You just came from my place, didn’t you?” Sam said.

“Yep, Fanny sent me after you. She said you’d be an idiot and try to barge in here and offend Mr. Pendennis, and you’d forget to ask him to come over and visit her. Knew you’d never deliver that message, but I would.”

Sam went bright red and swore under his breath. Pen cackled, this whole absurd scene was exactly the kind of chaotic fun he needed. “Of course I’ll come pay Mrs. Huxter a visit,” he said, grinning.

“Her place is over the baker’s in Charterhouse Lane, right hand side coming from St. John’s Street, you know Smithfield?” Bows said, no mercy for Sam’s pride. “Old Doctor Johnson used to walk that street with holes in his shoes, carrying bundles of penny-a-line articles for the magazines. You fancy literary types have it easy now, riding around in cabs, wearing yellow kid gloves.”

“Old friend, I’ve watched a hundred brilliant, good guys flop, and a hundred total frauds get all the prizes, I’m not the least bit puffed up about my own luck,” Pen said, serious for a second. “You’re every bit as good as me, you know that. You’re just mad at fortune for messing with you, that’s all.”

“Fair,” Bows mumbled, wiping his bald forehead. “Most of us get grumpy when we’re talking about our own bad luck. Hey, Sam? You wanna run home right now? Fanny’s got Jack Linton and Bob Blades, two of your med school mates, over visiting. They turned up right after you left, so she sent you off on this errand to get you out of the house for a bit. They’ll stay there gawking at her for another two hours easy.”

Sam shot up so fast he almost knocked his chair over, stuffed his stick in his coat pocket and grabbed his hat. “You’ll talk my dad round, right, Pen? You’ll come visit us soon?”

“Only if you patch me up for free the next time I get sick at Fairoaks, you silly sod,” Pen laughed. “I’ll help you, no problem.”

As soon as Sam had bolted out the door, Bows plopped back down in the chair. “There he goes. Every word I said was true, that little flirt. She’s got that whole crowd of lads wrapped around her finger already. If no men are around she’ll even cozen the old German baker downstairs into giving her free buns, or chat up the crossing sweep. You asking if she’s actually fond of Sam? Yeah, weirdly enough, she is. She decided she wanted him, and wouldn’t rest till she married him. Slipped out of the lodge one day, got the banns read, ran off to Gravesend with him, left a note for me to break the news to her mum. Her mum knew the whole time, of course, just pretended she didn’t. Now I’m an old fool hanging around their tiny flat, watching her mend Sam’s shirts and sing while she cooks his dinner. I even lent ’em three quid to get by till his dad forgives him. I can’t stand to see her sad, even when she’s making a total fool of me.”

After Bows left, Pen grabbed Blanche’s confusing letter and trotted off to find his favourite person to dump all his problems on: Laura Bell. It was absurd how much Pen, who usually thought he was the cleverest man alive, now ran to Laura for every decision, even what colour waistcoat to wear. The old lady Laura lived with was highly amused by it, of course.

He handed Blanche’s letter over to Laura, and asked her to decode it. Laura read it over, her face going all serious.

“Arthur, it seems to me like Blanche is being very artful here,” she said quietly.

“Making sure she can dump me if she wants to, no hard feelings?” Pen asked.

“I’m afraid so. That’s a bad sign for your future happiness, Arthur, after you were so honest and open with her. Wait—wait a second, I almost hate to say this,” Laura said, blushing bright red, “I think she knows the secret about her father. She didn’t even ask you why you gave up the Parliament seat that would have made you so rich and successful. She’s not confused about why things changed, not at all.”

“And if that means she’s making up an excuse to break off the engagement, and I’m free?” Pen burst out, grinning. “Then will the best, sweetest woman in the world—”

“You are not free yet, Arthur,” Laura said calmly, smiling a little. “You still belong to someone else. And I think we both know that for now, that’s how it has to be.”
