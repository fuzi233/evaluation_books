If you’d wandered into Chatteris’s little theatre not long after our last round of chaos, you’d have found Manager Bingley hamming up his signature role of Rolla in *Pizarro* to an audience so sparse it looked like half the ticket holders had gotten lost on the walk over, and the other half had bailed to get fish and chips instead. Poor Arthur “Pen” Pendennis had the entire box section pretty much to himself, slouched over the rail with bloodshot, red-rimmed eyes, staring so hard at the stage he’d have bore a hole in the scenery if he could. None of the Spanish soldiers, sun priest virgins, or messy battle scenes registered for him—he only saw Cora, the second she stepped onto the boards. He’d admit later he was so hopped up on unrequited love, rage, and despair he’d half considered bringing a pistol to the theatre, and the only reason he didn’t do something daft enough to land him in a cell outside Chatteris gaol for the rest of his youth was his mum at home, who never pressed him on his moping but left warm tea and silent sympathy out for him every night he trundled home miserable.

Miss Fotheringay made for a very fine Cora, all in her flowing white gown, leopard-skin drapery, sun brooch on her chest, and cheap sparkly bracelets glinting on her bare arms. She delivered her handful of lines perfectly, and looked even better, but that night those eyes that had completely rotted Pen’s brain weren’t cast his way at all. He was too wrapped up in his own drama to notice her glances were fixed firmly on two guys in the box right next to his.

The whole theatre had been half-asleep up to that point. The cast yawned through their lines, chatted to each other mid-dialogue, and everyone was already halfway to agreeing to issue full refunds like they’d done the night Pen got chased out of the theatre earlier. Bingley was phoning in his performance, his wife was muttering Elvira’s lines under her breath like she was apologising to the empty seats for being there.

Then suddenly, Mrs. Bingley started bellowing her lines so loud she sounded like a bull escaped from the local fair. Bingley tossed off his apathy and started sprinting across the stage yelling like a street preacher. Garbetts, Rowkins, and Miss Rouncy all pulled out every over-the-top acting trick they knew, hamming so hard their faces were cramping. They were all performing for the two new arrivals in Box 3: one was a quiet, sharp little old man in black, the other was the most absurdly overdressed dandy you’d ever seen in Chatteris.

That was Mr. Dolphin, London’s biggest, fanciest theatre impresario, complete with a hooked nose, piles of curling brown hair and whiskers, a coat covered in more gold braid and velvet trim than a royal parade, three under-waistcoats, rings so heavy they weighed his fingers down, and a white kid-gloved hand that pulled out a yellow handkerchief smelling so strongly of musk and bergamot the fumes wafted all the way to the back of the gallery. No wonder every actor on stage was trying to suck up to him.

He barely noticed any of their efforts. Bingley yelled, Mrs. Bingley bellowed, Dolphin just took snuff out of a giant solid gold box and nodded vaguely. It was only the final scene, when Rolla (Bingley, huffing and puffing, hauling around his extremely overgrown 5-year-old son who was playing the “baby” of the play) staggered in with the kid, and Cora screamed “O God, there’s blood on him!” that Dolphin finally clapped hard and yelled “Bravo!” at the top of his lungs. He smacked his secretary Billy on the back so hard the poor old guy nearly spilled his snuff. “By Jove, Billy, she’ll do!” he crowed.

Old Billy, the sardonic secretary, squinted. “Who the hell taught her that trick? I saw her at the Olympic Theatre three years back, she couldn’t even say boo to a goose.” That was grumpy old Mr. Bows, the guy tucked away in the orchestra pit plucking at his fiddle, who’d coached her through every beat. The whole cast heard the cheer, and swarmed around Fotheringay the second the curtain fell to offer gushing congratulations, all while silently hating her guts.

Dolphin hadn’t popped up in tiny nowhere Chatteris for no reason, of course. His London Museum Theatre was this close to collapsing. The big A-list legitimate drama star had run 20 nights, and no one made a penny except him. The celebrated Mr. and Mrs. Cawdor had flopped so hard no one turned up to their run of tragedies. The lion tamer’s big animal act had gotten mauled mid-show, the Lord Chamberlain shut the whole thing down, and his grand fancy opera production had left him so deep in debt everyone in London was just waiting for his bankruptcy notice to turn up in the Gazette.

His fancy rich patron was none other than the Marquis of Steyne, who hung out in his private curtained box so the audience couldn’t see him, but every single ballet girl knew exactly when he was there—they’d all be mid-leap 3 feet off the ground, twirling like a top, and still manage to cast adoring little glances at his hidden box. One night he was in there with his two toadies Wenham and Wagg, cackling so loud the pit was yelling for silence, when Wenham pulled out a private letter from Major Pendennis. The Major was begging them to get Dolphin to travel out to Chatteris, see Miss Fotheringay, sign her to a London contract, because his young nephew was head over heels obsessed with her and swore she was better than every leading actress on the West End stage. Steyne saw right through the story, remembered his own daft teenage fling with a market gardener’s daughter back when he was at Eton, and waved a hand. “Send Dolphin down. If she’s half that good, sign her.”

That’s how Dolphin ended up in Chatteris, and before he left he sent Steyne a gushing note saying he’d signed Miss Fotheringay on the spot, and she’d be making her London debut very soon.

Pen saw the announcement in the Chatteris local paper, the same paper he’d spent months writing gushing, star-struck reviews of her in. The editor ranted about her talent, predicted she’d take London by storm, and the theatre started slapping up posters announcing “Miss Fotheringay’s Final Nights in Chatteris!” Pen and Sir Derby Oaks showed up to every single performance: Sir Derby hung out in the stage box throwing bouquets and grinning when Fotheringay tossed him a little smirk, Pen moped all alone in his empty back box, pale, haggard, and miserable.

One night, old Mr. Bows the fiddler climbed out of the orchestra pit, trundled into Pen’s box, and held out a hand. “C’mon. Let’s go walk.” They trundled down the moonlit street together, and parked themselves on Chatteris bridge, dangling their legs over the edge, talking about *her*.

“We’re both fools for the same woman,” Bows said, puffing on a crummy cigar. “You’re not the first, you won’t be the last. And I’ve got less excuse than you, I’m old enough to know better. She hasn’t got a heart bigger than the stone you’re leaning on. If you or I fell off this bridge and drowned tonight, she wouldn’t bat an eye. She only puts up with me ‘cause she knows she can’t act for shit without me coaching her. She’ll send for me the second she gets settled in London, guaranteed. Not ‘cause she likes me. ‘Cause she needs me. No deep feelings, no big dramas, no soft spots. She likes her dinner, and she likes people clapping for her. That’s it.”

“…And you still stick around?” Pen asked, shocked out of his own self-pity.

“Habits, lad. Like snuff, or cheap whisky. I’ve put five years of work into making her what she is. Can’t quit now. One day she’ll get married, though, and dump me like I’m dumping this cigar end.” He flicked the little glowing spark into the river, and it vanished in the dark water. Pen rode home that night, and for the first time in months, he stopped thinking only about his own broken heart.
