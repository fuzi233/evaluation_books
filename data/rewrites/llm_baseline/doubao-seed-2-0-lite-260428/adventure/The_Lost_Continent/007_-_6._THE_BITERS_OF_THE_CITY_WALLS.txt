This was how I was welcomed back to Atlantis’ capital, and my first look at the new order Phorenice had carved out for herself. I make no excuses for my deliberate stillness in those first hours. All the old, simple lines of duty had frayed to nothing. Once, to serve the king was to serve the ruling Priest Clan that had anointed him, whose very head he was. But Phorenice had clawed her way to power alone, answering to no one but herself. Zaemon, the priests’ mouthpiece, had defied her openly. What was a plain, honest soldier supposed to do, pick a side in that mess on the spot?

Cold logic settled the question for me. Governments exist for the good of all the realm, don’t they? I’d find out which faction actually promised Atlantis a future, and throw my full weight behind it. That line of thought led me straight to the other half of the puzzle: the rebels I’d heard mutterings about, the group strong enough to have the whole capital under siege. Before I pledged loyalty to anyone, I owed it to the country to weigh their claims too. So that very same night, hours after I’d set foot on Atlantean soil again, I slipped away from the royal pyramid to see the state of things with my own eyes.

No one inside that gilded tomb dared stop me. The banquet had shattered into chaos the second Tarca collapsed on the stone floor, his leprosy scars bulging and glistening for every feaster to see. Even the greediest glutton there lost his appetite fast. Phorenice glowered at the crowd in silent fury for a long minute, said not a word, then turned her gaze on me, her hard face softening just a little.
“You may be honest, Deucalion,” she said at last, with a sharp, amused smile, “but you are the coldest man I’ve ever met. I wonder when you’ll thaw? Soon, I think. For now, farewell. At dawn we’ll take this whole country by the shoulders and wrench it into something new.”
She swept out with Ylga at her heels, and I went next, stepping ahead of my rank, while every lord in the hall stood and bowed. I stopped first at Tarca’s side, laid my hand on his diseased flesh. “You’re an unlucky man,” I told him, “but I know a brave soldier when I see one. If there’s any cure for this plague to be found, I’ll pull every string I have to get it for you.”
He mumbled his thanks through his wrecked, rotting mouth, and the nobles around him shuddered in fake disgust. I rounded on them, my face dark. “Your charity runs as thin as your courage. You all put on a grand show of scoffing at Zaemon and his priests, but the second you have to stand up to anything ugly, you hide like sniveling children. I’d rather be Tarca, all unclean, than any one of you right now.”
I waited cold as ice until they’d all given me the proper salute due my rank, then turned and walked out of the banqueting hall without a second glance. I headed straight for the pyramid’s grand main gate, called for the guard officer, and demanded to leave. The man was obsequious, but he hesitated.
“My lord’s attendants haven’t arrived yet.”
“I have none.”
“Does my lord know what the streets are like after dark?”
“I knew them well twenty years ago. I’ll find my way.”
“The city is under siege, my lord,” he pressed on. “The people are starving. They prowl in packs once the sun goes down, and I don’t doubt you could win any fight, no matter the odds, but—”
“Correct. I’ve no desire to brawl in the gutters tonight. Lend me enough men to make sure no one tries. You’ll know how many we need. I’m used to city streets that don’t try to eat you.”
He assigned twenty broad, armed guards to my escort, and with them flanking me two deep, I marched out of the thick, cloying perfume of the pyramid into the cool, silver glow of Atlantean moonlight. My plan was to circuit the city walls and get a clear sense of the besiegers’ strength.

The Gods, though, had a far rougher lesson waiting for me first. The city I walked that night was nothing like the capital I’d remembered: the one built slow, over centuries, balanced with comfortable homes for the many and grand monuments for the few. The grandeur was still there, bigger than ever. Entire working-class wards had been bulldozed flat to make space for new palaces and new pyramids for the empress’s inner circle. I couldn’t help but admire the skill and raw ambition that built those soaring, moon-glowing structures. Gazing up at them, I almost understood why those barbarian Europeans worship giant buildings as gods, convinced no ordinary human could raise something so majestic.
For all their beauty, though, the cost of those monuments blared at you from every corner. For every grand new palace rising where a hundred small homes had stood, a hundred families had been thrown out into the streets with nowhere to go. Now every alley niche slept three or four homeless wretches, every shadow hid a pack of feral, hungry men ready to rob or kill anyone who wandered past without enough guards. I’ve never been soft on the common folk: a man who chooses to starve instead of work deserves what he gets, and the state is better off without him. But what I saw that night was blind, pointless cruelty. Starved corpses, their bones poking through paper-thin skin, sprawled in the gutters. It was plain as day: outside the tiny circle of the empress’s favorites, every last soul in the capital was being ground under a boot.
Even that, though, I couldn’t complain too hard about. By old Atlantean law, every person, high or low, was the property of the king, to be put to whatever use he saw fit. Phorenice sat on the throne now, after all. I tried to push the anger down, not let treason creep into my thoughts, sighed, and kept walking, staring up at the purple night sky and the stars, hoping the High Gods would send me some guidance for what to do next.
It wasn’t long before the winding streets brought us to the great outer walls. We marched beside them, answering the sentries’ drum calls as we passed, until we reached the main western gate, where the garrison captain kept post.

Something was very wrong there. Laughter boomed up into the quiet night, mixed with the snarl and roar of some enormous beast, and the sharp, high shriek of a man in agony. The old builders had designed the city’s main gates to be as unbreakable as the walls themselves: four sets of stone valves, each two men tall, one man thick, set in the doubled thickness of the wall around an open, circular stone circus. Hidden gears linked the valves so one could never open until the other was fully shut. Without siege engines, you’d have better luck trying to push over the royal pyramid with your bare hands than forcing those gates.
My escort blew the horn hung on the wall for summoning, and a warder peered out through an arrow slit, going through the long, tedious old forms of the gate check. Half the time we couldn’t hear each other over the racket coming from the circus inside. At last he’d finished all the formalities, shot back the massive stone bolts, and cracked open one heavy valve to let me and my men crowd into the antechamber between the two gate sets. He pulled the door shut behind us, bowed low to the ground at my feet.
“I’ve no coin for you,” I said bluntly. “Get on with it. Open the other outer gate.”
“With all respect, my lord, it would be far wiser if I announced you first. There’s a baiting going on in the circus, and the tigers there are untamed beasts, no respecters of rank.”
“The what?”
“Tigers, for lack of a better name. They’re tearing through prisoners the Empress (blessed be her name) sent us to guard the gate. But if you’d like, the ward rooms lead to old archer galleries that overlook the whole circus. You can watch the sport from there, perfectly safe.”
I’ve never gotten any thrill out of mindless killing, but I was curious what Phorenice had dreamed up for her new gate defenses. So I followed him through the lodge to the galleries, built by the old architects as a second line of defense: if invaders ever forced their way into the circus, the archers could pick them off from the slits without ever being struck back, turning the whole gate complex into a perfect death trap.
The moon lit the circus bright, and the air drifting in reeked thick of blood. There was no “sport” to what was happening – just cold, cruel slaughter. I’m no prude: give a condemned man a sharp blade, toss him in a pit with a beast of equal strength, let him fight to his last breath, and I’ll cheer every good strike. A war prisoner earns his death, and he’d far rather go out fighting hot blood than under a headsman’s axe. But this baiting was no fair fight.
The “tigers” weren’t ordinary big cats. They were cave tigers: the biggest, most terrifying land predators alive, so huge and strong they’ll drag down a full mammoth alone if they catch it away from its herd. I’ve hunted beasts my whole life, and I’ll not be ashamed to say I always stalked cave tigers with every trick and bit of caution I could muster. To trap one alive, chain it, and drag it all the way to the city? That was a feat I’d never even dared imagine. Phorenice was more of a genius than I’d given her credit for.
Their purpose was obvious. Loose in the circus, no living soul could cross through the gates. Each wore a thick bronze collar attached to a massive iron chain that ran back to a windlass hidden in the galleries. On normal days, the tigers would be hauled tight to either end of the circus, leaving a clear safe path between the gates. But right now, the chains were slack, and the two striped brutes prowled the paving, links clinking behind them. Eight dead men already lay sprawled on the stone, and the tigers nuzzled them now and then, swatting their bodies with heavy paws, but made no move to eat. They knew the game. They had more victims coming.
Sure enough, the outer gate valve cracked open a crack, and a fighting prisoner was shoved out into the circus. He fell flat on his face, then lay perfectly still, eyes on the ground, waiting for death. The stone gate clanged shut. The onlookers behind the arrow slits chattered and yelled, betting on how long he’d last.
The windlass clanked, the chains were pulled in twenty links. One tiger crouched, lashed its tail, and launched itself in a terrifying spring. The chain went taut, singing with strain, and the beast roared so loud the walls shook. It missed the prone man by a hand’s breadth. The watchers shrieked with delight. The second tiger sprung, missed by even less, and the crowd howled louder. The prisoner never moved an inch.
The tigers soon grew bored of that. They lay down, purred, stretched, and slowly crept their chains out, reaching for the man with their huge forepaws. The male couldn’t quite reach. The female’s claw tip just barely grazed his bare side, raking bright red scratches across his skin. Still he didn’t stir. Then the male tiger executed a brutal trick: he turned his back, backed against the chain, and kicked out with his hind paw as hard as he could.
I heard the crunch of the prisoner’s ribs. His body went flying like a tossed fruit. The tigress knew the move. She caught him mid-fall, tossed him 10 feet in the air, and leaped up after him, catching him on her paws. The crowd screamed their approval, but my gorge rose. Toying with a man’s dead body like he was a cheap doll was an insult to every human’s pride. I held my peace, though. I was there to learn the shape of Atlantis’s new order, not throw a scene.
The tigers got bored of the bodies, started prowling again, waiting for more fun. The windlass clanked once more, the beasts were hauled clear of the outer gate, and another prisoner was shoved out. My stomach turned. It was a woman.
I’ve seen my share of death in battle, I’ve fought women who picked up blades and fought as hard as any man, earned their fatal blows fair. But this was wanton, cruel, unnecessary. I had to clamp down hard on the urge to leap down into the circus and end the game myself. I was supposed to be thinking of the good of the whole country, not one single life. But my heart was hammering. This woman was no sullen, resigned victim. She was going to fight.
The guards had stripped her naked, to make sure she had no hidden weapons. She planted her back to the stone gate she’d been pushed out of, eyes glowing bright, facing the tigers. She snatched up a sharp bone from the litter of bodies on the paving, broke its thick end against the wall, then bit the tip to a razor point with her teeth. The tigers paced closer, noiseless, playing their usual torment game, and the woman didn’t wait for them to strike. She lunged first, driving that sharpened bone straight for the tigress’s eye. The crowd roared. She didn’t blind it, but she cut a deep, stinging gash across its eyelid, and the huge beast bellowed in surprise and pain, scampering back to get a running start to charge her.
She didn’t wait for the charge. She ran full tilt across the moonlit circus straight for the wall where I stood, threw every muscle in her body into a leap I’d have thought impossible, flying so high her feet dangled a man and a half above the paving. Her fingers curled tight around the sill of my arrow slit. She would have slipped right back down if I hadn’t reached out, grabbed her wrist, and hauled her halfway through the slit.
I expected her to beg for mercy. Instead, she panted, savage and wild, tightened her grip on that sharp bone, and lunged it straight for my throat, using the hand she had wrapped around my wrist to pull me closer. I barely dodged the first blow. I grappled her as gently as I could, the thick stone wall between us, pried the weapon out of her hand, and held her still until she stopped fighting.
“That was an ungrateful strike,” I said. “Without my hand on you, you’d be down there getting torn apart by the tigers right now.”
“I have to kill someone,” she panted, “before they kill me.”
“Then you’ll have plenty of chances another day. You’re safe now.”
“You’re lying. You’ll throw me to the beasts the second I let go. I know your kind. You won’t give up your cruel sport.”
“Then I’ll prove it to you.” I called for the warder. “Wind those tigers’ chains in short, then go down into the circus and help this lady down to the ground gently.”
The order was carried out. I climbed down to the circus too, and joined the woman standing there in the moonlight. But the men behind the arrow slits who’d been watching the sport were furious. One of the outer gate valves was thrown open hard, and a red-faced armed captain stormed out, sword drawn.
“By all the Gods!” he shouted. “Who dares get between me and my pastime?”
