The atmosphere in the rectory office shifted instantly when Mr. Moore emerged from his private consultation. Messrs. Helstone and Sykes, emboldened by the dismissal of the workmen's deputation, greeted him with a chorus of jovial congratulations, praising his steely resolve. Yet, Mr. Moore remained unnervingly still. His face was a mask of somber calm, devoid of the warmth or animation expected of a man receiving such accolades. It was the visage of a stormless, cloud-choked sky—devoid of sun or wind. Mr. Helstone, perceiving the dissonance between his words and Moore's demeanor, wisely checked his flowery praise. He buttoned his coat, signaling the end of the performance, and suggested to Sykes that they depart, leaving Moore to his solitary reverie.

As they reached the door, Moore paused, his gaze fixed. "Where is Sugden?" he asked, his voice flat.

Helstone grinned, revealing a secret he had kept while Moore was occupied. "Ah, ha! I confess to a little unauthorized assistance. While you parleyed with that downcast gentleman, Farren, I slipped through the back window, summoned Murgatroyd, and smuggled Sugden and his brother Moses—wooden leg and all—into Sykes's gig. Sugden drove like a demon, and within fifteen minutes, Barraclough was safely en route to Stilbro' jail."

"Very good," Moore replied simply, offering a polite nod before ushering them out.

For the remainder of the day, Mr. Moore was a man of few words. Even Joe Scott, the miller's foreman, found him reticent. Joe, usually chatty, offered only the bare necessities of business, casting sidelong glances at his master. As the mill slowed for the evening, Joe ventured a suggestion: "It's a grand evening, sir. A walk up th' Hollow might do you good."

Moore burst into a short, sharp laugh. "Do you take me for a woman or a child, Joe?" he demanded, seizing the keys from the foreman's hand and shoving him toward the gate. He called Joe back, however, before he reached the yard.

"Do you know those Farrens?" Moore asked. "Are they well off?"

"They cannot be, sir," Joe replied, his voice dropping. "No work for three months. William is ruined, fair paired. They've sold most of their goods."

"He was a good workman?"

"The best you've had since you started."

"And decent folk?"

"Niver dacenter. The wife is a canting body, but clean enough to eat off the floor. They are down and out. William knows gardening; he learned it from a Scot. He could manage a garden."

"Then you may go."

Joe departed, leaving Moore alone in the silence of the counting-house.

Outside, the spring evening had turned raw. Though the day had been warm, the air had chilled at sunset, and a hoar frost was silently creeping over the grass and buds. The pavement in front of Briarmains, Mr. Yorke's residence, was already white. The great oak guarding the gable and the walnut grove behind the house stood defiant against the cold, their bare branches stark against the twilight.

Inside, however, life was vibrant. Lights burned brightly in the windows. Briarmains stood near the highway, close enough to hear the hum of Briarfield and see the glare of the new Wesleyan chapel, Briar Chapel, just a hundred yards away. Within its walls, a prayer meeting was in full swing. The hymns were raucous and ecstatic, sung with a fervor that seemed to defy the quiet countryside.

*"Oh! who can explain / This struggle for life... / Plague, earthquake, and famine... / The wonderful coming / Of Jesus declare!"*

The congregation moved from hymn to hymn with buoyant energy, interspersed with periods of clamorous prayer and fearful groans. Shouts of "I've found liberty!" rang out, followed by anthems of unspeakable bliss and the fathomless love of God. The climax arrived with a terrifying shout: *"Here we raise our voices higher, / Shout in the refiner's fire, / Clap our hands amidst the flame, / Glory give to Jesus' name!"* The roof remained intact, a testament to its sturdy slating.

Yet, if the chapel was alive with noise, Briarmains possessed a different kind of vitality. The lower windows opened onto the lawn, curtains drawn but not fully obscuring the candlelight within. Voices and laughter drifted out, hinting at a gathering of the family.

The scene inside the back parlour was intimate and charged with atmosphere. The room was decorated with stained glass windows featuring the heads of Shakespeare and Milton, alongside Canadian landscapes and a vivid painting of Vesuvius erupting. A fierce coal fire burned in the ample chimney, a luxury Mr. Yorke insisted upon regardless of the season.

Mr. Yorke sat beside the fire, not reading, but watching his children. Opposite him sat his wife, a large woman of grave aspect. Her face bore the weight of a perpetual, voluntary cloud of care. To her, mirth was profanity and cheerfulness frivolity. She was a stern, democratic woman with a cynical view of human nature, suspicious of all men and creeds. She was a devoted wife and mother, yet her suspicion extended to her husband's friendships, keeping his relations at arm's length.

Their children were a study in contrasts. The youngest, a baby, clung to her mother's knee, the only one she did not yet suspect or condemn. Next were the girls, Rose and Jessy. Rose, twelve, sat at her father's knee, resembling him in spirit but softer in feature. Her eyes held a serious, mature intelligence that frightened her father, who feared she might not live long. Jessy, the younger, was a chattering, arch creature, a pet to her father and a rival to her mother. She was destined for a short life, a fact foreshadowed by the narrator's vision of her grave in a foreign land, while Rose was envisioned as a lonely emigrant in the southern hemisphere.

The three eldest sons—Matthew, Mark, and Martin—sat in a corner playing a game. Matthew, the eldest, possessed a sinister intensity, his face reminiscent of the erupting volcano on the wall. He was the favored son, treated with a protective coddling that bred resentment in his brothers. Mark was calm and shrewd, his soul already middle-aged despite his youth. Martin, the youngest, was plain and cynical, a boy who vowed to hate women and wear only blue cloth, promising a future transformation into something handsome and brilliant.

A tap at the door announced the arrival of Robert Moore. Mrs. Yorke greeted him with her characteristic solemnity, questioning his nocturnal wandering. Moore, a man of gravity and abstemious habits, exchanged pleasantries, though Mrs. Yorke remained wary of his bachelor status.

"Well, is it you?" she asked as he approached. "You should be at home."

"Can a single man be said to have a home, madam?" Moore countered.

"Pooh!" she dismissed, her plain speaking often startling. "Pray, does not your sister make a home for you?"

"Not she," Mr. Yorke added dryly. "Hortense is an honest lass, but I looked out for a wife."

Mrs. Yorke cracked a dry joke about her own repentance of marriage, pointing to her brood of rough lads as evidence of her punishment. She warned Moore against the temptations of matrimony, citing their four sons and two daughters as a deterrent.

"I am not tempted now," Moore replied. "These are not times for marrying."

This lugubrious sentiment pleased Mrs. Yorke, who nodded in acquiescence. Soon, little Jessy jumped from her father's lap to run into Moore's arms.

"You talk of marrying him," she declared indignantly to her mother. "He promised that I should be his wife last summer! Didn't he, father?"

"Ay, my little lassie," Mr. Yorke confirmed. "But make him say it over again. Such as he are only false loons."

"He is not false. He is too bonny to be false," Jessy insisted, looking up at Moore with absolute trust.

"Bonny!" Mr. Yorke scoffed. "That's the reason he should be, and proof that he is, a scoundrel."

"But he looks too sorrowful to be false," Rose interjected quietly from behind her father.

"Your sentimental buck is the greatest cheat of all," Mr. Yorke remarked.

"He's not sentimental," Rose corrected.

Moore turned to her, surprised. "How do you know I am not sentimental, Rose?"

"Because I heard a lady say you were not."

"Voilà, qui devient intéressant!" Mr. Yorke exclaimed, drawing his chair closer to the fire. "A lady! We must guess who it is."

Rose refused to name her, describing the encounter instead. She recalled a party at Mrs. Pearson's where several ladies had discussed Moore. Some called him a misanthrope and a "solemn puppy." Others noted an affectation in his dark hair and pale face, comparing him to a "sentimental noodle."

"And who contradicted them?" Moore asked.

"A lady whom I don't know," Rose said. "She never visits here, though I see her every Sunday at church. She sits near the pulpit. She is like a picture in our dining-room, with a dove in her hand. She has eyes like it, and a straight nose that makes her face look clear."

Jessy, impatient with her sister's vagueness, revealed the identity. "She means Caroline Helstone, the rector's niece. Miss Helstone was quite angry with Anne Pearson. She said, 'Robert Moore is neither affected nor sentimental; you mistake his character utterly.'"

Jessy proceeded to describe Miss Helstone in detail: fair, with a slender white throat, long loose brown curls, and a quiet, clear tone. She often wore a gray silk dress and was neat in every way. "She is what I call a lady," Jessy declared. "When I am as tall as she is, I mean to be like her. Shall I suit you if I am? Will you really marry me?"

Moore stroked Jessy's hair but gently pushed her away. "Oh! you won't have me? You push me away."

"Why, Jessy, you care nothing about me," Moore said, feigning hurt. "You never come to see me now at the Hollow."

"Because you don't ask me," Jessy retorted.

Moore, seeing the opportunity, invited both girls to visit him the next day at the Hollow, promising presents. Just as Jessy was about to respond, Martin interjected cynically.

"I know that Miss Helstone you have all been palavering about. She's an ugly girl. I hate her. I hate all womenites. I wonder what they were made for."

"Martin!" Mr. Yorke warned.

Martin merely turned his truculent face toward his father. "I'll say the same then. I mean always to hate women. They're such dolls... I'll never marry. I'll be a bachelor."

Mr. Yorke chuckled, recalling his own youthful misogamy before he had traveled to France and Italy and become a lover of women. Martin, however, vowed to wear only blue cloth, rejecting the colorful attire of others as beneath human dignity.

Mark, observing the tension, stepped in with a slow, ironic voice. "Mr. Moore, you think perhaps it was a compliment on Miss Helstone's part to say you were not sentimental. I thought you appeared confused... as if you felt flattered."

Mark explained his definition: a sentimental man is one with thoughts and ideas; an unsentimental man is destitute of them.

"There are different kinds of thoughts, ideas, and notions," Rose interjected gently. "Sentimental must refer to the bad, or Miss Helstone must have taken it in that sense, for she was not blaming Mr. Moore; she was defending him."

"That's my kind little advocate!" Moore said, taking Rose's hand.

"She was defending him," Rose repeated, noting the spiteful tone of the other ladies.

"Ladies always do speak spitefully," Martin observed. "It is the nature of womenites to be spiteful."

Matthew, the eldest, finally spoke up. "What a fool Martin is, to be always gabbling about what he does not understand!"

"It is my privilege, as a freeman, to gabble on whatever subject I like," Martin retorted.

"You use it, or rather abuse it, to such an extent," Matthew rejoined, "that you prove you ought to have been a slave."

"A slave! a slave! That to a Yorke, and from a Yorke!" Martin stood up, pointing at Matthew. "This fellow forgets, what every cottier in Briarfield knows, that all born of our house have that arched instep under which water can flow—proof that there has not been a slave of the blood for three hundred years."

"Mountebank!" Matthew sneered.

"Lads, be silent!" Mr. Yorke commanded. "Martin, you are a mischief-maker."

"Indeed! Is that correct? Did I begin, or did Matthew?" Martin challenged.

"A presumptuous fool!" Matthew repeated.

Mrs. Yorke began to rock herself, a movement that often preceded hysterics when Matthew was defeated. "I don't see why I should bear insolence from Matthew Yorke," Martin protested.

"He has no right, my lad; but forgive your brother until seventy-and-seven times," Mr. Yorke soothed.

"Always alike, and theory and practice always adverse!" Martin murmured as he turned to leave.

"Where art thou going, my son?" his father asked.

"Somewhere where I shall be safe from insult, if in this house I can find any such place."

Matthew laughed insolently. Martin threw a strange look at him, trembling slightly, but restrained himself.
