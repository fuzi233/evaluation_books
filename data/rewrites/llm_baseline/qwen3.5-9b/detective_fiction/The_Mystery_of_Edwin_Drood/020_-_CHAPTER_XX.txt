Rosa hardly regained consciousness before the entirety of her recent encounter flooded back into her mind. It seemed the terrifying interview had chased her even into her faintness; she had possessed not a single moment of oblivion regarding it. Panic seized her. What was she to do? The only clear thought piercing her fear was absolute flight. She must escape this terrible man. But where could she hide, and how could she move?

She had confessed her terror to no living soul save Helena. To approach Helena now, to recount what had transpired, risked unleashing the very catastrophe Jasper threatened—a disaster he claimed the power to inflict and, she feared, the will to execute. The more monstrous he appeared in her agitated memory, the heavier her burden felt. One slip, one hesitation, could unleash his malice upon Helena's brother.

Rosa's mind, storm-tossed for the past six months, churned with a half-formed, unspoken suspicion. It rose and sank, gaining and losing substance. Jasper's obsession with his nephew—whether alive or dead—had saturated the town. Everyone assumed foul play was impossible; no one suspected the nephew's own hand. Had she asked herself, "Am I so wicked as to conceive a wickedness others cannot?" She had pondered if her prior revulsion proved the suspicion baseless. Then, the motive struck her: *himself*. The thought of building murder on such petty vanity filled her with shame, covering her face as if the idea were a crime greater than the deed itself.

She replayed his words by the sundial. He insisted the disappearance was murder, consistent with his public stance since finding the watch and shirt-pin. If he feared the truth coming out, surely he would encourage the theory of voluntary departure? He had even hinted that weaker ties might allow him to sweep "even him" away. Was that like a man who actually committed the crime? He spoke of laying six months of labor at her feet for justice. Would he speak so passionately of sacrifice if it were a lie? His first offering was his fidelity to the dead boy. These facts argued strongly against her fanciful fears.

Yet, he was a terrible man. The poor girl, unable to comprehend the criminal intellect—which scholars fail to read because they try to force it into the mold of average minds—concluded only one thing: he was a monster, and she must flee.

She had been Helena's comfort, assuring her of Neville's innocence and sharing her grief. Yet, she had never seen Neville since his vanishing, nor had Helena mentioned his confession to Mr. Crisparkle, despite its notoriety. To Helena, he was merely an unfortunate brother. Rosa's promise to her suitor was technically true, though now she regretted voicing it. Though terrified, her spirit swelled at the thought of him knowing it directly from her lips.

*Where?* Anywhere beyond his reach was the only answer. She decided to seek her guardian, Miss Twinkleton, immediately. The feeling of insecurity that had gripped her since their first night together—the conviction that the convent walls could not stop his spectral pursuit—could not be calmed by reason. Repulsion had fascinated her for so long, culminating in darkness, that she felt bound by a spell. Even glancing at the sundial as she dressed sent a chill through her; it seemed invested with his awful nature.

She scribbled a hurried note to Miss Twinkleton, stating she needed to see her guardian urgently but urging the lady not to worry, for all was well. Packing a few trivial items into a tiny bag, she left the note in plain sight and slipped out, softly closing the gate behind her. It was the first time she had ever walked alone on Cloisterham High Street. Knowing the winding paths intimately, she rushed straight to the corner where the omnibus waited.

Just as it pulled away, she hailed it. "Stop and take me, if you please, Joe. I am obliged to go to London."

Within a minute, she was on her way to the railway station, protected by Joe. He waited until she reached the platform, guided her safely into the carriage, and carried her small bag as if it were a hundredweight trunk she could not possibly lift.

"Can you return and tell Miss Twinkleton you saw me off safely, Joe?"
"It shall be done, Miss."
"With my love, please, Joe."
"Yes, Miss—and I wouldn't mind having it myself!" Joe thought, though he kept the sentiment silent.

As the train whisked her toward London, Rosa allowed her racing thoughts to settle. Initially, indignation fueled her resolve: his declaration of love defiled her; only appeal to honesty could cleanse the stain. But as dusk deepened and the great city loomed closer, doubt crept in. Was this rash? How would Mr. Grewgious react? What if he wasn't there? Alone in a strange, crowded metropolis, what awaited her? Regret gnawed at her. Should she have waited? Would she thank herself if she could turn back now?

The train finally crossed the rooftops into London. Below lay gritty streets, their lamps glowing on a hot summer night. "Hiram Grewgious, Esquire, Staple Inn, London." That was her destination. Enough to send her rattling through desolate, dusty thoroughfares where crowds gathered for air and others shuffled with monotonous weariness. Music played here and there, but it offered no cheer; barrel-organs failed to mend matters, drums beat out no care, and wind instruments seemed broken-hearted, longing for the country.

Her cab halted before a heavily barred gateway, belonging to someone who retired early and feared burglars. Disembarking, Rosa timidly knocked. A watchman admitted her, carrying her small bag.

"Does Mr. Grewgious live here?"
"He does, Miss," the watchman indicated deeper within.

Rosa proceeded further in. By ten o'clock, standing before P.J.T.'s door, she wondered where the street-door had vanished. Following the painted sign, she ascended the stairs and tapped softly on Mr. Grewgious's door. Unanswered, yet the handle yielded easily. Inside, the room was dim, lit by a shaded lamp far in the corner. Mr. Grewgious sat on a window seat.

Rosa approached in the twilight. He saw her and whispered, "Good Heaven!"

She threw herself upon his neck, weeping. He embraced her, soothingly asking, "My child... what has happened? Who brought you here?"

"No one. I came alone."
"Came alone! Why didn't you write for me to fetch you?"
"I had no time. A sudden resolution. Poor, poor Eddy!"
"Poor fellow!"
"His uncle has made love to me. I cannot bear it," she burst out, stamping her foot. "I shudder with horror. Come to you to protect me and all of us from him, if you will?"

"I will!" Mr. Grewgious cried, surging with unexpected energy. "Damn him! 'Confound his politics! Frustrate his knavish tricks! On Thee his hopes to fix? Damn him again!'"

After this explosive outburst, seemingly torn between loyal fervor and combative rage, he wiped his face. "Forgive me, my dear. I feel better. Tell me no more just now, or I might lose control. Refresh yourself. What did you eat? Breakfast, lunch, dinner, tea, or supper? What will you take next? Shall it be breakfast, lunch..."

With chivalrous tenderness, kneeling before her, he removed her hat and untangled her hair. Who, judging him by the surface, would expect such genuine gallantry?

"Rest must be arranged," he continued. "You shall have the finest chamber in Furnival's. Your needs attended to by an unlimited head chambermaid—one not restricted by budget. Is that a bag?" He scrutinized the tiny bundle. "Admirably sized for a canary-bird's provisions. Did you bring a bird?"

Rosa smiled and shook her head.
"If you had, he'd be welcome," Grewgious mused. "Though I suspect he'd prefer hanging on a nail outside to fight our Staple sparrows. Whose executions aren't quite up to their intentions. Many of us are like that! You didn't specify a meal. Have a jumble of all."

Rosa declined, requesting only tea. Grewgious scurried in and out, ordering marmalade, eggs, watercress, salted fish, and frizzled ham. Soon, the board was set.

"Lord bless my soul," Grewgious exclaimed, placing the lamp on the table and sitting opposite Rosa. "A new sensation for a poor old angular bachelor!"

"What do you mean?" Rosa's eyebrows queried.

"The sensation of a sweet young presence here," he declared. "It whitewashes, paints, papers, gilds, and glorifies the place!" He sighed mournfully.

Touching him gently with her teacup, then her small hand, Rosa offered comfort. "Thank you, my dear."

"Ahem! Let's talk!"
"Do you always live here, sir?"
"Yes, my dear. Always alone, except for my clerk, Mr. Bazzard."
"He doesn't live here?"
"No. Off duty entirely right now. A firm downstairs lends a substitute. Hard to replace Mr. Bazzard."
