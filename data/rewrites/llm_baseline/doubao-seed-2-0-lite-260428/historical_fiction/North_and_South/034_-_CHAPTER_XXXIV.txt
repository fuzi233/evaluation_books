“Truth will fail thee never, never!
Though thy bark be tempest-driven,
Though each plank be rent and riven,
Truth will bear thee on for ever!”

Everyone had praised Margaret for bearing her mother’s loss far better than anyone could have expected, but that constant, rigid self-restraint was tearing her to pieces from the inside. Mid-laugh while making soft, gentle conversation with her father, a sudden sharp memory would cut through her: her mother was gone, forever, and it was all she could do not to cry out from the raw, unnameable pain of it.

Worse still was the gnawing, unspoken anxiety over Frederick. The Sunday post had delayed their expected letters from London, and when Tuesday rolled around, Margaret opened the post bag to find no word from him at all. She had no idea what plans he had made, no way of knowing if he was safe, and the uncertainty was eating her father alive. He could no longer stick to his new, quiet habit of sitting still in his favorite easy chair for half a day at a time. He paced restlessly from room to room, wandered out onto the landing to open and shut empty bedroom doors for no clear purpose, and could not even follow along when Margaret tried to read aloud to soothe him. She thanked God every minute that she had never told him of their run-in with the disreputable Leonards at the railway station— that extra weight of fear was hers, and hers alone to carry.

Her relief was immense when the maid announced Mr. Thornton. His visit would distract her father, pull his jumbled, anxious thoughts onto something steady. He walked straight across the room to Mr. Hale, clasped both his hands in silence, and held them for a long minute. The grief and quiet sympathy on his face spoke volumes more than any formal condolence could have managed. Then he turned to Margaret. Her usual stately, luminous beauty was dimmed from weeks of late nights watching over her ailing mother and all the hidden tears she had cried when no one was looking. Her face bore the soft mark of patient, unhidden suffering, sharp with quiet current pain.

He had fully intended to keep up the cold, distant demeanor he had practiced around her since the misunderstanding in Outwood Lane, but he could not stop himself from stepping toward her where she stood half-hidden at the edge of the room, and speaking the expected polite greetings in a voice so soft and tender that her eyes filled with tears at once, and she turned quickly away to hide her emotion. She picked up her sewing and sat very still, silent in the corner. Mr. Thornton’s heart thudded fast in his chest, and for a little while he forgot all about that bitter quarrel entirely. He talked with Mr. Hale, and his steady, decisive presence was such a comforting, safe anchor for the frazzled, unsettled older man that Margaret could see the lines of tension in her father’s face softening just a little.

Then Dixon appeared at the drawing room door, her face flushed and flurried. “Miss Hale, might I have a word?” Margaret’s stomach dropped like a stone. She knew at once something had gone wrong with Frederick. She was grateful her father and Mr. Thornton were so caught up in their conversation that neither noticed her pale face as she stepped out into the hall.

“What is it, Dixon?” she whispered the second the drawing room door clicked shut behind her. “Come this way, miss,” Dixon said, leading her to the bedchamber that had once belonged to Mrs. Hale— now Margaret’s, for Mr. Hale could not bear to step foot in it after his wife’s death. “It’s nothing, probably nothing at all, miss— just a police inspector asking to see you. I tried speaking to him first, but he says he will only speak to you. Martha showed him into your father’s study.”

Margaret did not speak again until her hand was on the study door lock. She turned back to Dixon, her voice tight. “Make sure my father does not come down here. Mr. Thornton is with him, he can keep him occupied.”

The inspector was almost taken aback by the haughty, unyielding air she carried into the room. There was sharp, carefully contained indignation on her face, no surprise, no fidgety fear, no eagerness to explain herself. She simply stood, perfectly still, waiting for him to state his business.

“Begging your pardon, ma’am, but duty compels me to ask a few plain questions. A man died this morning at the town Infirmary, from injuries sustained in a fall at Outwood railway station last Thursday, between five and six in the evening. Doctors say the fall itself would not have killed him, but a long-standing internal ailment and his heavy drinking turned the injury fatal. We are expecting to hold an inquest. A witness on the platform saw a scuffle break out, when a half-intoxicated man made impertinent advances to a young lady walking with a gentleman, and that gentleman pushed the deceased back over the edge of the platform. The witness thought nothing of it at the time, as the fall looked trivial. We have reason to believe that young lady is you, Miss Hale.”

Margaret’s large dark eyes dilated just a fraction. Otherwise, she did not flinch, did not tremble, did not look away. Her lips tensed into a firm, unyielding curve, but the inspector, though sharp, did not know her face well enough to spot the defiance in the set of her jaw. “Well?” she said, steady as a rock. “Go on.”

“I was not there,” Margaret said, her voice even, unwavering, her gaze fixed on his face like that of a sleepwalker, glassy and unblinking.

The inspector ran over the facts he knew in his head as he studied her: the porter who saw the scuffle had only caught a glimpse of the pair from the far end of the platform, the station master remembered a remarkably handsome young lady and gentleman on the platform at that exact hour, and a local grocer’s assistant who was waiting for a train had recognized the lady as Miss Hale of Crampton. The dead man, Leonards, had stumbled away from the station drunk and enraged, raving that a “Cornish trick” had cost him a hundred pounds, before collapsing unconscious in the road. He had never woken up enough to give a clear statement, only rambling about being at sea and shouting names of naval lieutenants and captains alongside those of his fellow porters.

“So, madam, you deny that you were the lady accompanying the gentleman who gave the push that caused this poor man’s death?” he asked directly.

A searing, sharp pain lanced through Margaret’s brain. *Oh God—if only I knew Frederick was safely across the water.* A master observer of human emotion might have caught that flash of raw, cornered agony shoot across her features, but the inspector missed it. Her reply was slow, heavy, almost a dull echo of her first denial: “I was not there.”

His suspicions pricked. It sounded as if she had forced herself to speak one untruth so hard, she had become incapable of varying it. He deliberately closed his notebook. “It is very unlikely, ma’am, but I may be forced to call on you again, and summon you to appear at the inquest to provide an alibi, if our witness persists in identifying you as the lady present.” He had expected to see her wince, to see a flicker of guilt cross her proud face. She did not move a muscle, as still and unyielding as a carved Egyptian statue. Abashed a little by her total composure, he apologized for the intrusion, noting he had only been doing his duty, and turned to leave.

Margaret walked before him, eyes still fixed on his back in that glazed, unblinking stare, until she had seen him out the front door. She shut the door behind him, half-stepped into the study, then turned back on a sudden fierce impulse to lock the front bolt. She stumbled a few more steps forward, swayed once where she stood, and fell prone to the floor in a dead faint, all the iron self-control she had held for weeks finally snapping clean in two.
