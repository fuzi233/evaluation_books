The final farewell to London was marked by a brief, cheerful stop at Harley Street, where Henry Dashwood congratulated his sisters on their progress toward Barton without financial strain, and announced that Colonel Brandon would join them for Cleveland within a day or two. This concluded their urban interactions, leaving only a tentative invitation from Fanny to visit Norland—a prospect so unlikely it was barely worth mentioning—and a warm, private promise from John to Elinor that he would soon seek her out at Delaford.

It was amusing to Elinor how every friend seemed intent on sending her to Delaford, a destination she now dreaded above all others. To her brother and Mrs. Jennings, it was her future home; even Lucy, upon their parting, pressed an invitation to visit her there.

By early April, the two parties departed from their respective homes in Hanover Square and Berkeley Street, meeting on the road as planned. Given the need to accommodate Charlotte and her child, the journey would take more than two days, while Mr. Palmer, traveling with greater speed alongside Colonel Brandon, intended to catch up to them at Cleveland shortly after their arrival.

Marianne, though she had spent few comfortable hours in London and had longed desperately to escape it, felt a profound pang of sorrow as she finally bid adieu to the house where she had last harbored her hopes and confidence in Willoughby—hopes now extinguished forever. Leaving the place where Willoughby remained, busy with new engagements and schemes from which she was excluded, brought forth many tears.

Elinor's relief, however, was absolute and positive. She held no lingering attachments that would cause regret, parted from no creature she loved, and welcomed the freedom from the persecution of Lucy's friendship. Grateful to have removed her sister unseen by Willoughby since his marriage, she looked forward with hope to the tranquility of Barton, confident it would restore Marianne's peace and confirm her own.

Their journey proceeded safely. On the second day, they crossed into Somerset, a county that alternated between being cherished and prohibited in Marianne's imagination. By the forenoon of the third day, they drove up to Cleveland.

Cleveland was a spacious, modern residence situated on a sloping lawn. Lacking a park, it possessed extensive pleasure grounds, complete with an open shrubbery and a closer wood walk. A smooth gravel road wound around a plantation to the front, while the lawn was dotted with timber. The house itself stood under the protective canopy of firs, mountain-ashes, and acacias, screened by a thick wall of these trees interspersed with tall Lombardy poplars that hid the offices from view.

Marianne entered the house with a heart swelling with emotion, conscious of being merely eighty miles from Barton and not thirty from Combe Magna. Within five minutes of entering, while the others assisted Charlotte in showing the housekeeper her child, she slipped away again. She wandered through the winding shrubberies, which were just beginning to display their beauty, seeking a distant eminence. From a Grecian temple there, her gaze wandered over a vast tract of country to the southeast, resting fondly on the farthest ridge of hills in the horizon, imagining that from their summits, Combe Magna might be visible.

In these moments of precious, invaluable misery, she rejoiced in tears of agony to be at Cleveland. Returning by a different path, she savored the happy privilege of country liberty—the freedom to wander in luxurious solitude. She resolved to spend nearly every hour of her stay indulging in such solitary rambles.

She returned just in time to join the others as they prepared to explore the immediate premises. The remainder of the morning passed easily as they lounged around the kitchen garden, admiring the bloom on its walls and listening to the gardener's lamentations about blights. They dawdled through the green-house, where Charlotte laughed at the loss of favorite plants nipped by lingering frost, and visited the poultry-yard, where the dairy-maid's disappointed hopes regarding forsaken nests, foxes, or dwindling broods provided fresh sources of merriment.

The morning had been fine and dry, and Marianne had not anticipated a change in weather. With great surprise, she found herself confined indoors after dinner by a settled rain. She had planned a twilight walk to the Grecian temple and perhaps all over the grounds; while a merely cold or damp evening might not have deterred her, a heavy, persistent rain was unbearable to her.

The party was small, and the hours passed quietly. Mrs. Palmer attended to her child, and Mrs. Jennings worked on her carpet-work. They discussed the friends they had left, arranged Lady Middleton's engagements, and wondered if Mr. Palmer and Colonel Brandon would travel farther than Reading that night. Elinor, though little concerned, joined their discourse. Marianne, possessing the knack of finding her way to the library regardless of the family's avoidance of it, soon procured a book.

Mrs. Palmer displayed constant and friendly good humor, making them feel welcome. The openness and heartiness of her manner more than compensated for her lack of recollection and elegance, which often rendered her deficient in polite forms. Her kindness, recommended by a pretty face, was engaging; her folly, though evident, was not disgusting because it lacked conceit. Elinor could have forgiven everything but her laugh.

The two gentlemen arrived the next day for a late dinner, providing a pleasant expansion of the party and a welcome variety to the conversation, which had been reduced to a low ebb by a long morning of continuous rain.

Elinor had seen so little of Mr. Palmer and observed such variety in his address to her sister and herself that she knew not what to expect in his own family. She found him perfectly gentlemanly in his behavior to all visitors, though occasionally rude to his wife and her mother. He proved capable of being a pleasant companion, hindered only by an excessive tendency to fancy himself superior to everyone else, particularly to Mrs. Jennings and Charlotte. Otherwise, his character and habits showed no unusual traits for his sex and time of life. He was fastidious in his eating, uncertain in his hours, fond of his child yet affecting to slight it, and idled away mornings at billiards instead of business. Nevertheless, she liked him much better than expected and was not sorry she could like him no more—not sorry to be driven by the observation of his Epicureanism, selfishness, and conceit to rest complacently on the remembrance of Edward's generous temper, simple taste, and diffident feelings.

Regarding Edward, or at least some of his concerns, Elinor received intelligence from Colonel Brandon, who had recently visited Dorsetshire. Treating her as both the disinterested friend of Mr. Ferrars and the kind confidante of himself, he spoke extensively of the parsonage at Delaford, describing its deficiencies and detailing his plans to remedy them. His behavior in this matter, as well as in every other, including his open pleasure in meeting her after only ten days' absence, his readiness to converse, and his deference for her opinion, justified Mrs. Jennings's persuasion of his attachment. It would have been sufficient proof had not Elinor, from the first, believed Marianne to be his true favorite, causing her to suspect it herself. Yet, such a notion had scarcely entered her head except through Mrs. Jennings's suggestion. She prided herself as the nicer observer of the two; she watched his eyes while Mrs. Jennings focused only on his general behavior. While his looks of anxious solicitude regarding Marianne's unexpressed head and throat ailments—the beginnings of a heavy cold—entirely escaped the latter lady's notice, Elinor discovered in them the quick feelings and needless alarm of a lover.

Two delightful twilight walks on the third and fourth evenings, traversing not merely the dry gravel of the shrubbery but all over the grounds, especially the most distant parts where the trees were oldest and the grass longest and wettest, combined with the greater imprudence of sitting in wet shoes and stockings, gave Marianne a violent cold. Though initially trifled with or denied, it forced itself upon the concern of everyone and her own attention through increasing ailments. Prescriptions poured in from all quarters, as usual declined. Though heavy and feverish, with pain in her limbs, a cough, and a sore throat, she was told a good night's rest would cure her entirely. It was with difficulty that Elinor prevailed upon her, when she went to bed, to try one or two of the simplest remedies.
