Before dawn broke over the vast, verdant plains, we began our march. Our procession cut a striking figure against the azure sky and emerald earth—a column of beasts and children moving with purpose. Our plan was simple yet demanding: travel through the morning, halt for the heat, resume under cover of darkness, and rest again the following day. By dividing the final leg of our journey carefully, we hoped to slip silently within the city gates before the first light of morning revealed us.

It seemed the entire forest ecosystem had decided to accompany us. A vanguard of birds soared ahead, convinced they led the charge; swarms of butterflies, wasps, and dragonflies danced around our heads; and a throng of four-legged companions trailed behind. As night fell, most of the larger beasts departed, seeking shelter, but the avian and insect life remained faithful, fluttering alongside us all the way to the city's threshold.

We paused for a deep, undisturbed slumber during the afternoon. Though this marked our first true long-distance trek, fatigue had not claimed any of us. That night, the cold air spurred us onward, and many drifted off to sleep atop their mounts, awakening refreshed. Not a single rider tumbled from their beast. Some rode shaggy, lumbering bears that somehow managed surprising speeds, matching the pace of the elephants. Others were astride various species of deer, racing with reckless abandon were it not for my constant intervention. Perched high on the haystacks of the elephants, unseen by the massive animals beneath them, the children chattered incessantly. One little voice, as he reached out to pull hay for his mount, cooed warmly: "Nosy dear, I'm digging you out of the mountain, and soon I'll be down to you! Be patient, I'm coming! Very soon you'll pop your trunk up to sniff me, and then we'll kiss like proper elephants!"

That same night, a cacophony erupted—elephant trumpets, horse neighs, and imitations by the children—that echoed far across the silent plains. Uncertain of our proximity to the city, I quickly hushed the uproar to prevent giving away our location.

Then, without warning, the sun and the city appeared to rise simultaneously. To the children, the towering walls looked merely like a colossal mound of rock. Yet, when I explained that the interior teemed with stone nests, a wave of apprehension washed over them. For the first time in their brief, fragile lives, genuine fear took root. The place seemed ominous, and questions of where they might find mothers arose. Nevertheless, they pressed forward with bravery, trusting implicitly in Lona—and in me, though I knew I hardly deserved their faith.

Passing through the echoing archway, the ground vibrated with a drumming of hooves and the padding of countless paws. The horses, startled by the resonance of their own footsteps, skittered and panicked briefly before settling into a steady trot. The children shivered, falling into a silence as profound as death. Only the bears and butterflies showed no sign of alarm; everyone else, including the three girls clutching infants, froze in the grip of terror.

Upon the woman's face sat a dark, heavy anxiety. Nor was I immune to the collective dread, for the entire army rested on my shoulders and my conscience. I had led them straight into the shadow of this peril! Yet, I clung to the vision of the kingdom awaiting us—a realm for the Little Ones, freed from slavery to the giants, bound instead to their gentle, loyal animal friends. Alas, the dreamer who envisioned this utopia was himself but a slave, a prisoner of untrustworthy obstinacy, much like any tyrant I had left behind. Beside me rode Lona, a child indeed, yet a free woman—calm, silent, watchful, utterly unafraid.

We approached the heart of the city before any inhabitant noticed our arrival. Then, windows began to pry open, and sleepy faces peered out. At first, their expressions were blank, stunned by sheer astonishment. But as soon as they discerned the animals accompanying us, consternation replaced their wonder. Despite their fear, seeing that our invaders were almost exclusively children, the women rushed into the streets, followed closely by the men. Yet, they dared not venture near the beasts, keeping to the periphery of the buildings and leaving a wide, empty corridor in the center of the road.

Finally, a boy of about five, consumed by thoughts of his mother, spotted a woman in the crowd whose features caught his eye. He leaped from his antelope, throwing his arms around her neck. She embraced him instantly, returning his kisses. But a man's hand snaked over her shoulder, seizing the child by the throat. Instinctively, a girl thrust her sharp spear into the stranger's arm. He let out a savage howl, was stabbed repeatedly by others, and fled screaming.

"They are just bad giants!" Lona cried, her eyes blazing as she drove her horse against a towering man who, goaded by the boy's spirit, stood blocking her path with a club. Daring not to withstand the impact, the giant slunk aside and collapsed moments later, struck down by flying stones. Another hulking brute, ignoring my charger, stepped abruptly between me and the boy riding behind. The boy ordered him to address the king; the giant responded by smashing his little horse with a hammer, sending it crashing to the ground. Before the monster could deliver another blow, an elephant from behind crushed him flat, trampling him until hundreds of feet had passed over him, leaving the army long gone by the time he attempted to rise.

Yet, at the sight of the women, a cloud of dismay settled over Lona's face. Scarcely one of them possessed a kind expression. Could her charges ever hope to find mothers among such horrors?

Hardly had we halted in the central square when two girls galloped up, breathless with news that two boys had been whisked away by women. Turning immediately, we realized the woman we had befriended had vanished, taking her baby with her.

At that very instant, a white leopardess bounded down a narrow lane leading to the palace. The Little Ones, remembering the fierce battle with the two leopardesses in the forest, trembled; their ranks began to waver. But they recalled my command and stood firm.

We paused to witness the outcome. Suddenly, a swift boy named Odu, renowned for his courage and reminded of the leopardess's kindness, leaped from his shambling bear. Running to meet her, he caused the beast to reel backward, rolling over in surprise. When she regained her footing, the child sat securely on her back. Who could deny the subjugation of a people that witnessed an urchin of their enemy straddling an animal they lived in daily terror of? Confident this display would ripple through the army, we rode on.

Stopping at the house designated by our guides, we heard a scream. I sprang down, pounding on the door. My horse pushed me away, turning to batter the entrance with his heels. Just then, little Odu returned, riding the leopardess. Seeing her, he froze, trembling violently. She, too, had heard the cry; forgetting the child on her back, she threw herself at the door, hurling the boy against it and rendering him senseless. Before I could reach him, Lona scooped him into her arms. As consciousness returned, she placed him back onto his bear, which continued to follow.

When the leopardess hurled herself at the door a third time, it gave way. She darted inside. We followed, but she had already vanished. Sprinting back up the stairs and scouring the house, we found no trace of anyone. Darting down again, we located a secret door beneath the stairs, opening into a labyrinth of excavations. We had not ventured far before encountering the leopardess once more, carrying the missing child across her back.

He revealed that the woman he believed to be his mother had thrown him into a hole, intending to give him to the leopardess. However, the leopardess proved benevolent and retrieved him.

Searching for the second missing boy, we entered the adjacent house more easily, only to discover, with heavy hearts, that we arrived too late. One of the savages had just slain the little captive. Lona found solace, however, in learning his identity; she had feared he would grow into a terrible giant, and death had spared him that fate. The leopardess sprang upon the murderer, gripping him by the throat and dragging him into the street. She followed Lona, clutching the man like a cat with a great rat in its jaws.

"Let us depart from this horrific place," Lona declared. "There are no mothers here! This people is not worth saving."

The leopardess released her burden and charged into the densest part of the crowd, scattering the slaves. They screamed and ran, tumbling over one another in chaotic heaps.

Returning to the army, we found them exactly as we had left them: standing in perfect order, ready for departure. Yet, peace eluded me. The princess offered no sign of intent, and her hidden plots remained unknown. Vigilance would have to be maintained throughout the night.

The Little Ones were remarkably resilient, capable of resting anywhere. We instructed them to lie down with their animals where they stood and sleep until summoned. In a heartbeat, they were down; in another, they were lost in the sweet music of their slumber, a sound like water flowing over grass or a soft breeze through leaves.
