The rain lashed against the leaded glass of the Hale household windows, a rhythmic drumming that felt, to Margaret, like the frantic, indifferent heartbeat of the industrial city itself. She stood in the hallway, the heavy scent of coal smoke and damp wool clinging to her clothes, her gaze fixed upon the staircase leading upward. Her visitors had departed, leaving behind a silence that pressed against her ears, and now she ascended, driven by a sudden, fierce need to inquire after Betsy Higgins. It was a journey she undertook with a purpose that felt both urgent and sacred; she intended to sit with the dying girl as long as possible before the summons of dinner.

As she navigated the crowded, narrow streets of the lower district, Margaret felt a profound shift within her. The mere act of caring for a resident of this grim quarter had imbued the alleyways with a new significance, transforming them from mere thoroughfares into spaces of moral weight. In the Higgins home, the atmosphere was stifling. Mary Higgins, the slatternly younger sister, had endeavored to tidy the space for the expected visit, but the effort was a desperate patchwork against decay. Rough-stoning marred the center of the floor, while the flags beneath the chairs and tables retained their dark, unwashed appearance, absorbing the gloom of the room. Though the day outside was sultry, a large fire burned aggressively in the grate, turning the interior into an oven. Margaret, steeped in the logic of the North, failed to interpret the lavish consumption of coal as a gesture of hospitable welcome; instead, she perceived the oppressive heat as a necessity for Bessy's fragile constitution, a cruel irony given the lack of ventilation.

Bessy lay upon a squab, a short sofa positioned precariously under the window. She appeared far more feeble than the previous day, exhausted by the exertion of raising herself at every passing moment to peer through the glass, searching for Margaret's arrival. Now that the visitor had entered and claimed a chair beside her, Bessy collapsed back into silence, her eyes fixed on Margaret's face with a childlike, starving admiration. She reached out to trace the textures of Margaret's garments, marveling at their fineness.

"I never knew why folk in the Bible cared for soft raiment afore," Bessy whispered, her voice thin and reedy. "But it must be nice to go dressed as yo' do. It's different fro' common. Most fine folk tire my eyes out wi' their colours; but some how yours rest me. Where did ye get this frock?"

"In London," Margaret replied, a flicker of amusement touching her lips despite the gravity of the scene.

"London!" Bessy gasped. "Have yo' been in London?"

"Yes! I lived there for some years. But my home was in a forest; in the country."

"Tell me about it," Bessy urged, leaning back and shutting her eyes, crossing her hands over her breast as if to create a barrier against the world. "I like to hear speak of the country, and trees, and such like things."

Margaret spoke, her voice carrying the echo of memories she had tried to suppress. She described Helstone not as a lost place, but as a living dream, more vivid than reality. She painted pictures of great trees with branches stretching level to create deep shades of rest, of turfs soft as velvet or lush with the moisture of hidden brooks, and of billowy ferns catching streaks of golden sunlight like the sea. She spoke of wide commons rising above the tree line, places of silence broken only by the distant, sharp voices of farmers or the solitary song of a lark.

"I am glad of that," Bessy murmured, her breathing shallow. "I felt smothered like down below. When I have gone for an out, I've always wanted to get high up and see far away... I get smothered enough in Milton, and I think the sound yo' speak of among the trees, going on for ever and ever, would send me dazed; it's that made my head ache so in the mill."

The conversation drifted inevitably to the source of her suffering. Bessy confessed that she feared the end of all things, terrified of a life spent solely in toil and sickness without the promise of divine reward. Her fear curdled into a violent outburst when she realized the finality of her condition. Sitting up abruptly, she clutched Margaret's hand with a ferocity that bordered on madness.

"If this life is th' end, and there's no God to wipe away all tears from all eyes—yo' wench, yo'!" she cried, her voice cracking. "I could go mad, and kill yo, I could."

She fell back, completely worn out by the intensity of her passion. Margaret knelt immediately, placing a steadying hand on the girl's shoulder.

"Bessy—we have a Father in Heaven."

"I know it! I know it," Bessy moaned, turning her head uneasily. "I'm very wicked. I've spoken very wickedly. Oh! don't be frightened by me and never come again. I would not harm a hair of your head." Opening her eyes, she looked at Margaret with a terrifying clarity. "And I believe, perhaps, more than yo' do o' what's to come. I read the book o' Revelations until I know it off by heart, and I never doubt when I'm waking, and in my senses, of all the glory I'm to come to."

"Do not let us talk of these fancies that arise in your feverish state," Margaret pleaded gently. "I would rather hear of what you used to do when you were well."

Bessy recounted her early employment in a carding-room, describing the "fluff"—tiny bits of cotton that filled the air like white dust, winding around the lungs and tightening them. She explained the economic paralysis of the industry: the expensive wheels designed to remove the dust brought no profit, and workers, accustomed to the toxic environment, refused to accept lower wages required to operate them. Thus, the machines remained unbuilt, and the poison continued.

"How old are you?" Margaret asked softly.

"Nineteen, come July."

"And I too am nineteen," Margaret answered, a wave of sorrow washing over her as she considered the stark contrast between their lives. She struggled to maintain composure, the emotion threatening to break her resolve.

Turning to the topic of Mary, Bessy implored Margaret to be a friend to her sister, unsure of what future Mary was fit for. Margaret hesitated, glancing at the uncleaned corners of the room, acknowledging the impossibility of taking Mary in as a servant given her current state. Yet, she promised friendship nonetheless, a bond forged in the shared tragedy of their circumstances.

"I'll know yo' won't forget me again," Bessy insisted, her grip tightening. "I'll not mistrust yo' no more. But remember, in a week or a fortnight I may be dead and buried!"

"I'll come again as soon as I can, Bessy," Margaret said, squeezing her hand tightly. "But you'll let me know if you get worse."

"Ay, that will I," Bessy returned, her strength fading.

From that day forward, Mrs. Hale descended further into the role of a suffering invalid. As the anniversary of Edith's marriage approached, Margaret reflected on the year's accumulated heap of troubles, wondering how they had been endured. If she could have foreseen them, she would have fled, hiding from the inevitable. Yet, day by day, small, keen, bright spots of positive enjoyment had pierced the darkness, rendering the burden bearable. A year prior, the prospect of a long illness in a strange, noisy, desolate place would have caused her to groan bitterly. But now, amidst the increasing grounds for complaint, a new, stoic patience had taken root in her mother's mind.

Mr. Hale, however, remained trapped in a stage of apprehensive denial. Irritated by his daughter's anxiety, he dismissed her concerns as fanciful delusions. He pointed to his wife's rosy cheeks, insisting they were signs of health rather than the flush of pain. When Margaret persisted, suggesting the color was symptomatic, he grew agitated, pushing her away gently but firmly, as if her words were an intrusion he wished to expel. He paced the room, muttering to himself about the impossibility of doing right without sacrificing others, expressing a hatred for the town and himself alike.

When Margaret finally questioned him about his wife talking of Helstone, he seized upon the silence as proof of her mental stability. "Then, you see, she can't be fretting after them, eh?" he reasoned, seeking comfort in the simplicity of his wife's ignorance. He dismissed her fears as "foolish morbid ideas," urging her to kiss him and retire to bed. But as Margaret lay in her own bed later that night, listening to the rhythmic, anxious pacing of her husband's footsteps—a sound she and Edith had once called *racooning*—she knew better. The truth was not in the silence of the sickroom, nor in the flushed cheeks of the invalid, but in the relentless, grinding march of the city outside, and the quiet, terrible cost of survival within it.
