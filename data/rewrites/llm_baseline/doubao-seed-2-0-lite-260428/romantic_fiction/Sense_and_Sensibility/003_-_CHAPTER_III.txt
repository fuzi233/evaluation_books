For months after her husband’s passing, Mrs. Dashwood lingered at Norland Park—though not out of any fondness for the sharp new edge of Fanny’s household, or any reluctance to leave once the first ragged, searing grief of seeing every well-loved path, every old apple orchard, every window where she’d watched sunsets with her husband softened into something she could bear. When her spirits began to lift, and she found she could think of something other than wrapping herself in melancholic memories to feed her sorrow, she grew restless to move, chasing every lead on a small, cozy home within a few miles of Norland. She could not bear the thought of fleeing the land that had been the heart of her marriage, the only home her daughters had known for years. But no house seemed to strike the perfect balance between the warm, easy comfort she craved, and the modest prudence her eldest daughter Elinor insisted on; Elinor’s steady, level head turned down half the places her mother loved, pointing out they were far too large for their limited income.

Mr. Dashwood had made his wife swear he would not be forgotten on his deathbed, telling her of the solemn, earnest promise he’d wrung from their son John to care for his stepmother and half-sisters. That promise had been the last comfort of his dying hours, and Mrs. Dashwood trusted John’s sincerity no less than her husband had. She thought of the seven thousand pounds he’d pledged to set aside for the girls with quiet satisfaction, certain she herself could live in perfect ease on a far smaller sum—but the money would give her daughters the security they deserved. She even scolded herself for ever doubting John’s generosity, for thinking him too selfish to care for his half-sisters; his gentle attentions, his constant little gestures of care for them all, had convinced her that their welfare was dear to his heart, and for a long while she never once questioned he would keep his word.

The quiet contempt she’d felt for her daughter-in-law Fanny from their first meeting only deepened over their six months living under the same roof. It would have been impossible to stay in the same house a day longer, even for the sake of politeness, if a new, soft, sweet circumstance had not arisen that made Mrs. Dashwood glad her girls were still at Norland: Elinor had grown deeply attached to Fanny’s younger brother, Edward Ferrars.

Edward was a gentle, charming young gentleman who had come to stay at Norland not long after Fanny moved in as its new mistress, and he’d spent nearly every day there ever since. Some mothers would have chased the pair apart at once, for all Edward owned in his own right was a tiny, trifling legacy—every other part of his fortune depended entirely on the whims of his very wealthy, very headstrong mother. Other mothers would have pushed the match relentlessly, for he was the eldest son of one of the richest men in the county. But Mrs. Dashwood cared nothing for either consideration. It was enough for her that Edward was kind, that he looked at Elinor as if she outshone every other person in the room, and that Elinor returned his quiet fondness tenfold. She thought it a cruel wrong that difference in fortune should ever keep two people apart whose hearts and temperaments fit so perfectly together, and she could not fathom how anyone who knew Elinor could fail to recognize her quiet, shining merit.

Edward was not the sort of young man to win over strangers at first glance. He was not strikingly handsome, and his manners were so shy that new acquaintances often mistook him for dull. Too diffident to show off his own strengths, he had to be drawn out of his shell before the warmth of his open, affectionate heart shone through. His mind was sharp, honed by years of solid, careful education—but he had neither the ambition nor the disposition to live up to the grand plans his mother and sister had laid out for him. They ached to see him rise to fame, to make a grand splash in high society, though they could never quite agree on what sort of fame that ought to be. His mother wanted to nudge him into politics, get him a seat in Parliament, or tie him to some great, powerful lord of the day. Fanny wanted that too—but until such a grand position could be secured, she would have been perfectly happy just to see him driving a fancy, grand barouche through the streets of London. Edward wanted none of those things. All his heart desired was a quiet home, the comfort of his family, and the soft peace of private life. Fortunately for his family’s ambitions, he had a younger brother Robert who was far more eager for glitter, status, and fine carriages.

Edward had been staying at Norland for weeks before he caught Mrs. Dashwood’s notice; in those early months of grief, she was too wrapped up in her own sorrow to pay much mind to the new guest. She only saw that he was quiet, unobtrusive, and she liked him for it—he never pestered her with ill-timed small talk that would have cut through the fragile quiet she needed to grieve. It was a passing remark Elinor made one day about how very different Edward was from his sister Fanny that made Mrs. Dashwood sit up and take full notice of him. The contrast was all the recommendation he needed.
“To say he is not like Fanny is more than enough,” she said, smiling. “That implies every amiable quality in the world. I love him already.”
“I think you will grow very fond of him, once you know him better,” Elinor said softly.
“Fond of him?” her mother laughed. “I do not feel mere approval. I already hold him in my heart as someone I love.”
“You may at least esteem him,” Elinor teased.
“I have never in my life been able to separate esteem from love,” her mother replied.

After that, Mrs. Dashwood made a point of getting to know Edward. Her warm, easy, open manner melted away his shyness in no time, and she soon saw every bit of his merit. The quiet certainty that he loved Elinor no doubt helped her see his goodness all the faster, but she was truly convinced of his worth: even that quiet, reserved demeanor that once felt odd, against her usual preference for lively, sparking young men, felt perfectly charming once she knew his heart was warm and his temper was gentle. The moment she saw the first soft signs of his love for Elinor in the way he looked at her, the way he hovered near her side at dinner, she was certain their attachment was serious, and began to daydream of their fast-approaching wedding.

“In just a few months, my dear Marianne,” she told her second daughter one afternoon, “Elinor will almost certainly be settled for life. We will miss her dearly—but she will be so very happy.”
“Oh Mama, how will we ever get on without her?” Marianne cried.
“My love, it will hardly feel like a separation at all,” Mrs. Dashwood said, stroking her hair. “We will live only a few miles apart, and we will see each other every single day. You will gain a brother, a true, affectionate brother. I have the highest possible opinion of Edward’s heart.” She paused, noticing Marianne’s pensive, grave face. “You look so serious, my dear. Do you disapprove of your sister’s choice?”

Marianne hesitated. “Edward is very amiable. I love him tenderly. But there is something missing. He is not the kind of young man I would expect to win Elinor’s whole heart. His bearing is not striking, he has none of that easy grace that ought to shine in the man she loves. His eyes have none of that spark, that fire, that announces a soul full of virtue and deep, bright intelligence. And besides, I fear he has no real taste for the things that matter most to us. Music hardly seems to interest him at all, and though he praises Elinor’s drawings over and over, his admiration is not that of a connoisseur who can see every stroke of her skill. It is obvious, even when he sits and watches her draw for hours, that he knows nothing of art at all. He admires the drawings only because he loves her, not because he feels their beauty. For me, those two things must go hand in hand. I could never be happy with a man whose tastes did not match mine in every single way. He must feel all my feelings. The same books, the same music, the same wild rapture at a sunset must charm us both. Oh Mama—how flat, how tame his voice was when he read poetry to us last night! I felt so sorry for Elinor, I could hardly sit still in my chair. To hear those beautiful lines of Cowper that have so often made my own heart beat wild with feeling, spoken with such calm, such dreadful, unfeeling indifference!”

“He would have done far better with simple, elegant prose,” Mrs. Dashwood said, laughing gently. “I thought that at the time, but you insisted on handing him Cowper.”
“No, Mama—if a man cannot feel Cowper, what is there to say? We must allow for difference of taste, I suppose. Elinor does not feel things as deeply as I do, so she can overlook that flatness and be happy. But if I loved a man, hearing him read my favorite poetry with no passion at all would break my heart. The more I see of the world, the more I am convinced I will never meet a man I can truly love. I require so much! He must have all Edward’s kindness and goodness, and his bearing and spirit must drape that goodness in every possible grace and fire.”

“Remember, my love, you are not quite seventeen yet,” Mrs. Dashwood said, pulling her close. “It is far too early to despair of that happiness. Why should you be less fortunate than your own mother? In only one thing, my Marianne, I hope your destiny will be different from mine: I hope you get to keep the man you love for far longer than I did.”
