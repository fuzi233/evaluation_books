Supper was being served. Eli's family gathered around the long table, save for one: Margaret was absent. To Catherine's astonishment, Eli declared he would wait for her return. "But I told her you wouldn't wait for the Duke," someone remarked.

"She is no longer the Duke," Eli replied firmly. "She is a poor, good girl who has waited not mere minutes, but years, for a graceless son of mine. Set the food down anyway; once she arrives, we shall eat without further delay."

The aroma of the smoking dishes was irresistible, and Eli relented. "She will come if we begin," he insisted. "They always do." He motioned for Mistress Joan to take her seat. "You are not here as a servant, I believe, but as a guest." Just then, he heard a hurried pace approaching the threshold. "Come, sit down!" he called out. "And fall to!"

The tablecloths were drawn back, and the knives were raised. Instead of the anticipated Margaret, however, a Dominican friar burst into the room, his face drained of all color by sheer fury. In a single bound, he crossed the space between the door and the table, throwing his tall frame across the narrow surface. With both hands hovering menacingly above the shrinking heads of Cornelis and Sybrandt, like eagles poised over trembling prey, he unleashed a torrent of invective, cursing them by name, soul, and body—here and in the life to come.

That era was eloquent in its curses, and this particular diatribe was so dense, so precise, so utterly blighting and terrible that I dare not transcribe every syllable.

"Cursed be the lips," he shrieked, "that spoke the lie declaring Margaret dead! May they rot before the grave and kiss white-hot iron in hell thereafter! Doubly cursed be the hands that altered those letters, may they be severed by the hangman's blade and forced to handle hellfire forever! Thrice accursed be the cruel hearts that conceived that damnable falsehood to tear true lovers apart! May they sicken and wither on earth, joyless, loveless, and hopeless; may they turn to dust before their time and burn in eternal flames!"

He cursed the meat before their mouths and every atom of their existence, from the top of their heads to the soles of their feet. Turning then to the cowering, shuddering pair who had nearly hidden beneath the table, he tore a letter from his bosom and hurled it onto the floor before his father.

"Read this, you hard-hearted old man who imprisoned your own son! Read it and behold the monsters you have spawned! The memory of my wrongs and hers shall haunt you all eternally! I shall meet you again at Judgment Day; on this earth, you shall never see me once more."

With the speed of lightning, he vanished as he had arrived, leaving behind a tableau of horror: Eli and his sons stood stiff, cold, and white as statues around the untouched, steaming meal.

This was the scene that greeted Margaret and Jorian. Pale figures of men and women seemed petrified around the uneaten food, much as Eastern poets might imagine. Margaret scanned the room, her breath catching in her throat. "Oh, joy! All are here! No blood has been spilled!" she gasped. "Oh, you cruel, cruel men! I thank God he has not slain you."

At the sight of her, Catherine emitted an eloquent scream before turning her head away in distress. But Eli, having just glanced at the forged letter and beginning to grasp the full weight of the deception, saw the other victim enter at that very moment, her sorrows mirrored in her sweet, pale face. A surge of righteous rage lifted him from his chair.

"Stand clear!" he roared. "Let me reach these traitors! I will hang myself for them!"

In an instant, he drew his short sword and lunged at them.

"Flee!" screamed Margaret. "Flee!"

They scrambled howling under the table and crawled out the opposite side. Before they could reach the door, the furious old man circled around and blocked their path. Catherine merely screamed and wrung her hands; such notable figures are often useless in moments of crisis. Blood surely would have flowed, but Margaret and Jorian seized the elderly man's arms, holding him fast with all their strength until they escaped the house. Only then did they release him, and he went forth, raging vainly after them into the street.

They were a furlong away, fleeing like hares, when he hacked down the board bearing their names, dragged it inside, and flung it into the fireplace.

Catherine sat rocking herself, her apron pulled over her head. Joan had rushed to her husband. Margaret embraced Catherine tightly; though pale and panting, she strove to comfort her. Yet, it proved impossible.

"Oh, my poor children!" Catherine wailed. "Oh, miserable mother! It is a mercy Kate was ill upstairs. There, I live to thank God for that!" She broke into fresh sobs. "It would have killed her. He had better have remained in Italy than returned home to curse his own flesh and blood and drive us all mad."

"Hold your tongue, woman!" Eli snapped angrily. "You remain on the side of the wrongdoer. You are poorly served; your weakness made these rogues what they are. I intended to correct them in their youth; for sore ills, sharp remedies are needed. But you sided with their faults, undermined me, and thwarted wise severity. And you, Margaret, cease comforting her when you ought to comfort yourself. What is her injury compared to yours? Besides, she never possessed a grain of justice within her, nor will she ever. Come to me now, for I am your father henceforth."

It was a command. Margaret kissed Catherine goodbye and tottered toward him. He placed her on a chair beside him, and she rested her feeble head against his honest chest, though not a tear fell—it was sorrow too profound for tears.

"Poor lamb," he murmured.

After a pause, true Eli spoke in a broken voice to Jorian and Joan. "Come, good folk, we are in some trouble, as you see. But that is no reason to starve. For our Lady's sake, eat; do not add to my grief by earning the reputation of a miser. What the devil!" he added, attempting a sudden, ghostly display of stout-heartedness, "the more knaves I manage to shake off, the greater my need for true men and women to help clear the dish and cheer my eyes with honest faces where gaps otherwise exist. Eat, I implore you."

Catherine, sobbing, encouraged his request. Poor, simple, antique, and hospitable souls! Jorian, whose appetite, particularly since his illness, was keen, was ready to accept this generous invitation. But Joan whispered something in his ear, and he immediately recoiled.

"Nay, I will touch no meat that the Holy Church has cursed."

"In truth, I forgot," Eli apologized humbly. "My son, who was raised at my table, has cursed my provisions. That seems strange indeed. Well, whatever God wills, man must submit."

The supper was thrown out into the yard. Heavy sadness settled over Eli's house that night.

Meanwhile, where was Clement? He lay stretched full-length upon the floor of the convent church, his lips pressed against the lowest step of the altar. He existed in an indescribable state of terror, misery, penitence, and self-abasement, through which struggled faint glimmers of joy that Margaret was alive.

Night fell, finding him there weeping and praying. Morning would likely have found him in the same posture; but suddenly, he recalled that, absorbed in his own grievances and Margaret's plight, he had committed another sin besides his intemperate rage. He had neglected a dying man.

He rose instantly, groaning under the weight of his accumulated wickedness, determined to make amends for his omission. The weather had shifted; it was raining heavily. As he cleared the town, he heard wolves bay—they were close at hand. Yet Clement was himself again, or nearly so. He cared little for danger or discomfort, driven solely by the shameful neglect of his religious duty. He moved stoutly forward through the rain and darkness.

As he walked, he frequently beat his breast and cried out, "Mea culpa! Mea culpa!"
