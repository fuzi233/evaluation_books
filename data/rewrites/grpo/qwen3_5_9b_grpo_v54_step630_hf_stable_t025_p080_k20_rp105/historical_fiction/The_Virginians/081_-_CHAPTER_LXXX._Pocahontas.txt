The English public, unfamiliar with the true history of Pocahontas, knew her only through the lens of our Virginia affection for that simple, kindly soul. Seizing the opportunity, Mr. Warrington, urged on by his friends, composed a ballad about the Indian princess. Printed in the magazines just prior to the tragedy's premiere, this piece served as "ground-bait," a strategy the enthusiastic parson and Sampson hailed with great enthusiasm. They claimed the poem was already being debated and admired in coffee-houses, with some attributing it to Mr. Mason, Mr. Cowper, or even the renowned Mr. Gray. I suspect poor Sam himself spread these rumors; had Shakespeare been named the author of the tragedy, he would have declared *Pocahontas* among the Bard's finest achievements.

My connection to the story began in childhood, sitting at my grandfather's knee in his library, tracing the exploits of Captain Smith with my finger. I loved the tales of his travels, sufferings, and escapes across both America and Europe. The old man would paint vivid pictures for me: Smith battling Turks on the Danube, or being led by savages to his death. I particularly delighted in the account of his combat with Bonny Molgro, the last and most dreadful of the three Turkish champions. With his prodigious turban, scimitar, and whiskers, the Turk seemed a figure of terror. According to my favorite volume, after slaying his first two foes, Smith faced Bonny Molgro. Their battle-axes clashed with such force that neither rider could keep his seat, yet Smith, displaying great judgment and dexterity, pierced the Turk under the cutlets, through back and body. Though the Turk dismounted, he fell swiftly, losing his head as the others had. In reward, Duke Segismundus granted Smith a shield bearing three Turkish heads and a pension of three hundred ducats annually.

In my tragedy, I dared to transport these exploits to the banks of the Potomac and James River, disregarding time and place—a liberty poets alone possess. The "ground-bait" verses I wrote ran thus:

Wearied arm and broken sword
Wage in vain the desperate fight;
Round him press the countless horde,
He is but a single knight.
Hark! a cry of triumph shrill
Through the wilderness resounds,
As, with twenty bleeding wounds,
Sinks the warrior, fighting still.

Now they heap the fatal pyre,
And the torch of death they light;
Ah! 'tis hard to die of fire!
Who will shield the captive knight?
Round the stake with fiendish cry
Wheel and dance the savage crowd,
Cold the victim's mien and proud,
And his breast is bared to die.

Who will shield the fearless heart?
Who avert the murderous blade?
From the throng, with sudden start,
See, there springs an Indian maid.
Quick she stands before the knight,
'Loose the chain, unbind the ring,
I am daughter of the king,
And I claim the Indian right!'

Dauntlessly aside she flings
Lifted axe and thirsty knife;
Fondly to his heart she clings,
And her bosom guards his life!
In the woods of Powhattan,
Still 'tis told, by Indian fires,
How a daughter of their sires
Saved the captive Englishman."

I need not detail the full plot of my tragedy, for my children could peruse it at any time from the shelves. Nor shall I rush to read it again to my young folks, recalling how Captain Miles and the parson fell asleep last Christmas during a reading of two acts at my mother's request. However, anyone with a moderate acquaintance with plays and novels could easily construct a picture from the sketch above. The elements included an Indian king, a loving princess, her attendant enamored of the British captain's servant, a traitor within the English fort, a brave Indian warrior harboring an unhappy passion for Pocahontas, and a medicine-man and priest capable of every treason and crime, bent on the torture and death of the English prisoner. These characters, combined with the accidents of the wilderness, war-dances mimicked by Gumbo, and references to recent victories in Canada, seemed promising.

Yet, ill omens preceded the day. Peevish, jealous, and timid management threw obstacles in the piece's path, and violent prejudice existed in certain high quarters. Was it surprising, then, that *Pocahontas* failed to be a victory? I laughed at the malignity of critics who faulted the performance, calling Carpezan a masterpiece while sneering at my superior work. I insisted that Hagan acted so admirably that a certain actor-manager might well have been jealous, and that but for the cabal outside, the piece would have succeeded. Sampson declared the order had been given for failure. "The house swarmed with Macs, by George," the honest fellow swore, vowing that Mr. Garrick himself would not have allowed the piece to succeed. He was particularly enraged during the grand scene in the second act, where Smith, bound to the stake, was saved by Pocahontas, causing the entire house to thrill with applause and sympathy.

Those curious enough may refer to the published tragedy in the octavo or the subsequent quarto edition of my Collected Works. Can they say the scene lacks merit, or that the verses are not elegant, rich, and noble? One cause of failure was my fidelity to history. I had copied a figure of Sir Walter Raleigh from the Museum, complete with frill and beard, and with Theo providing some of her mother's finest lace for the ruff, we dressed Hagan accurately. No man could look better. Similarly, I dressed Miss Pritchard as a Red Indian, having seen enough of that costume in my own experience. Would it be believed that the house tittered when she first appeared? They eventually grew accustomed to her, but just as she rushed into the prisoner's arms, a fellow in the pit bawled, "Bedad! here's the Belle Savage kissing the Saracen's Head!" An impertinent roar of laughter erupted, breaking out in fitful explosions throughout the performance.

As the wag in Mr. Sheridan's *Critic* noted regarding morning guns, playwrights were not content with one joke but must fire two or three. Thus, this wretched pothouse joke of the Belle Savage struck—ignorant audiences unaware that Pocahontas herself was the very Belle Sauvage from whom the tavern took its name. My friend in the pit repeated it ad nauseam, saluting each new character with a tavern name: the English governor became the Goat and Boots, his lieutenant Barker the Bull and Mouth. The curtain descended amidst a shrill storm of whistles and hisses, especially assailing poor Hagan. Sampson spotted Master Will in the green boxes with pretty acquaintances, convinced he was a ringleader in the conspiracy. "I would have flung him over into the pit," the faithful fellow said, though he was forced to sheer off due to Mr. Nadab's followers prowling the lobby. Our hoped-for profits were broken, our poor hopes shattered.

Before the curtain rose, I looked in from the stage and saw the house fairly filled, notably Mr. Johnson in the front boxes with Mr. Reynolds, the latter unable to hear and the former unable to see, forming a duo to judge my tragedy. I spotted Lady Maria in the lower gallery, once more watching her beloved actor perform a principal role. As for Theo, she frankly admitted she preferred not to attend unless ordered, and I, knowing that witnessing her suffering would be intolerable pain, acquiesced to her desire to stay away.

Of a pretty equanimous disposition, I took a light dinner at the Bedford and arrived at the theatre shortly before the play began, intending to remain until victory or defeat was decided. I could not help sensing the gloomy, disastrous aspect of everything around. Miss Pritchard suffered from a headache; the barber who brought Hagan's wig had powdered it like a wretch; among the gentlemen and ladies in the greenroom, I saw only doubtful faces. The manager, a flippant and impertinent gentleman who looked as dismal as a mute at a funeral, insolently suggested, "For Heaven's sake, Mr. Warrington, go and get a glass of punch at the Bedford, and don't frighten us all here by your dismal countenance!"

"Sir," I replied, "I have a right, for five shillings, to comment upon your face, but I never gave you authority to make remarks upon mine." He retorted in a pet, "Sir, I most heartily wish I had never seen your face at all!" "Yours, sir!" I said, "has often amused me greatly; and when painted for Abel Drugger is exceedingly comic"—indeed, I have always thought Mr. G. unrivalled in low comedy. I bowed and walked off to the coffee-house, never speaking to the gentleman again for five years until he apologized at a nobleman's house. I feigned forgetfulness, noting that on a play's first night, both author and manager are flurried alike, adding, "After all, there is no shame in not being made for the theatre. Mr. Garrick—you were." He appeared pleased with the compliment.

Fidus Achates ran to me at the end of the first act to report things going pretty well, though he confessed to the titter upon Miss Pritchard's first appearance in her Indian princess attire. "I cannot help it, Sampson," I said, filling him a bumper of punch, "if Indians are dressed so." "Why," he asked, "would you have had Caractacus painted blue like an ancient Briton, or Bonduca with nothing but a cow-skin?" Indeed, fidelity to history may have caused the ridicule, in which case I am not ashamed of the defeat.

After the second act, my aide-de-camp returned with dismal news. Nervous before action, I became surprisingly cool and cheerful in disaster. "Are things going ill?" I asked. I called for my reckoning, put on my hat, and marched to the theatre as calmly as if dining at the Temple, with Fidus Achates pressing my elbow, kicking link-boys aside, and crying, "By George, Mr. Warrington, you are a man of spirit—a Trojan, sir!" So there were men of spirit in Troy, but alas, fate was too strong for them.

No man can say I did not bear my misfortune with calmness; I could no more help the audience's clamor than a captain can help a ship's howling storm. Yet I was determined that the rushing waves and broken masts should impavidum ferient. Sampson told my wife, "Not Regulus, my dear madam, could step into his barrel more coolly." It is unjust to say men of parasitic nature are unfaithful in misfortune; whether prosperous or poor, the wild parson remained equally true and friendly, sharing our crust as eagerly as our better fortune.

Taking my place on the stage, I watched the actors of my poor piece and the portion of the audience condemning me. The performers likely gave me a wide berth out of pity. I felt as little moved as any spectator; no one would judge from my mien that I was the unlucky hero of the night.

But my dearest Theo, when I returned home, looked so pale and white that I knew the knowledge of my disaster had preceded my arrival. Spencer, Sampson, cousin Hagan, and Lady Maria were to come after the play to congratulate the author, God wot! Poor Miss Pritchard was engaged but sent word she was too unwell to sup that night. My friend, the gardener of Bedford House, had given my wife his best flowers to decorate her little table. There they were—the poor little painted standards—and the battle lost! I had borne the defeat well enough, but as I looked at the sweet pale face of my wife across the table and those artless trophies of welcome she had set up for her hero, my courage gave way, and my heart felt a pang almost as keen as any that ever smitten it.

Our meal was dismal, rendered no gayer by our strained conversation. Fortunately, Old Mrs. Hagan was very ill, a blessing in disguise. We discussed his Majesty's approaching marriage, remembering futile incidents of the day, including a tune a carpenter whistled by my side just before the dreary curtain fell. We talked of the death of good Mr. Richardson, author of *Pamela* and *Clarissa*, whose works we all admired. As we spoke of *Clarissa*, my wife wiped her eyes, saying faintly, "You know, my love, mamma and I could never help crying over that dear book. Oh, my dearest, dearest mother, how I wish she could be with me now!" This occasioned open tears, for a young lady may naturally weep for her absent mother. We mixed a gloomy bowl with Jamaica limes and toasted his Excellency the Governor. Then, for a second toast, I filled a bumper, smiling, and drank to "our better fortune!"

This was too much. The two women flung themselves into each other's arms, irrigating each other's neck-handkerchiefs with tears. "Oh, Maria! Is not—is not my George good and kind?" sobbed Theo. "Look at my Hagan—how great, how godlike he was in his part!" gasped Maria. "It was a beastly cabal which threw him over—and I could plunge this knife into Mr. Garrick's black heart—the odious little wretch!" Grasping a weapon at her side, she threw it down and rushed to her lord and master, flinging her arms around him and embracing him in the presence of the little company.

I am not sure if someone else did likewise. We were all in a state of extreme excitement and enthusiasm. In the midst of grief, Love the consoler appeared, soothing us with fond blandishments and tender caresses until one scarcely wished the calamity away. Two or three days later, on our birthday, a letter brought to my study contained these lines:

How pale and faint appears my knight!
