What use was there in being a Colonel without a regiment? The Governor and Council who had paraded so much pomp and ceremony to thank me for my commission, to endow me with that empty honour, were long gone from sight: skulking aboard their ships offshore, making occasional forays to commit piracy and arson on the undefended shore. My Lord Dunmore’s black allies had turned the very people he claimed to protect against him, and worse still, we had word he had sent envoys south to court the Indigenous nations of the interior, promising them plunder if they swept down on our settlements and tomahawked us all back into “good behaviour.” “And these are the allies we choose to fight our own kinsmen!” I said to my mother one evening, our faces grim as we exchanged looks, and my mind darted back to that ghastly, sharp memory from Braddock’s Field: the painted warrior whose glare had been inches from my own, his knife pressed to my throat before Florac struck him down mid-strike.
We fortified Castlewood as best we could, stacking furniture against lower windows, posting nightly watches, but the truth was we feared the raiding parties of Natives and freed enslaved fighters far more than the local rebel militia. I never saw my mother lose her courage but once before that night: she had broken down recounting the details of my father’s death in an Indigenous raid more than forty years prior. So when a group of travelers stumbled past the house after dark one evening, no amount of reasoning could convince her they were not raiders. She sank straight to her knees, hands clasped, crying out “Lord have mercy on us! The Indians— the Indians!”
In the end, Lord Dunmore’s Black allies scattered back to his ships or vanished to find pay and plunder elsewhere, and the fearsome painted war parties from the South never materialized. Even so, I could not look at my mother’s grey head, my wife’s soft brown hair, or our little boy’s bright golden ringlets without a cold, sharp pang of dread, thinking they might fall victim to this brutal, unregulated ruffian’s war. And we of the Royal cause were the ones who had unleashed these horrors, who had enlisted these cruel allies! If I did not hate the silliness of men who claim to read the direct finger of Providence in every small event, I would say our choice to hire German mercenaries and rouse Indigenous nations to fight for us brought its own retribution. The Provincials cut the mercenaries down easily whenever they met them, and the triumph of beating those brutal foreign troops lifted the Continental army’s spirit higher than any victory over regular British soldiers could have. Worse still, the murder of a single young woman, Miss McCrea, by half a dozen drunken Natives did more to turn public sympathy against the Crown than the loss of a dozen pitched battles or the destruction of whole regiments.
Once the Native panic passed, Madam Esmond’s courage returned in full, and she grew justly, seriously uneasy at the danger my presence at Castlewood put my family in.
“What harm can they do me, a poor old woman living alone?” she would argue. “I have one son a colonel with no regiment, and another with two hundred Continentals at his back in Mr. Washington’s camp. If the Royalists ride through, they will spare me for your sake. If the rebels come, Harry’s name will be my passport. I will not have your delicate wife and this sweet little baby here, adding risk to all of us. You must send them away to Boston or New York. Do not talk of staying to defend me! No one would dream of hurting a harmless old widow. If the rebels break in, I will hide behind Fanny’s petticoats, and I will be far safer with no able-bodied Loyalist gentlemen loitering about the house making trouble.”
She spoke partly out of practical good sense, but far more out of a quiet determination to send me and my family out of harm’s way. For all the danger, she would never leave the land where her father was buried, the ground she herself had been born on. She lived, you might say, looking backwards. She had welcomed the new generation, blessed them, and bid them safe farewell. She belonged entirely to the past, to old days and half-forgotten memories no one else could touch.
We were still debating the journey to Boston when the news arrived: the British had evacuated that unlucky city entirely. They had never once dared to storm Mr. Washington’s camp at Cambridge, even when he lay there for months with almost no gunpowder, completely at their mercy. They had waited, instead, until he procured enough ammunition to seize and fortify Dorchester Heights, the high ground that overlooked the entire town, forcing the whole British army and every loyalist resident to beat a hasty retreat to their ships. That the King’s troops won the battle of Bunker’s Hill is as certain as the fact that we beat the French at Blenheim—but after that day, our commanders seemed perpetually terrified of storming entrenched Continentals. Why else would they hesitate for six months, from July to March, to strike an enemy almost completely defenceless? Why the dithering at Long Island, when the whole Continental army was well within our grasp? Why that absurd timorousness of General Howe’s before Valley Forge, where the ragged, starving, sick remnants of the Continental force could barely man their lines, while a great, fresh, fully provisioned British army sat just out of striking distance?
As the hopes and fears of both sides rose and fell, the shifting tone of the partisans around us was impossible to miss. When news of the Boston evacuation reached the Virginia countryside, every small local Whig stopped by Madam Esmond’s door to bow politely and advise her to submit quickly to the new government. She did not flaunt her Loyalist flag in public faces as openly as she once had, but she never wavered from her loyalty. Poor brave Parson Hagan prayed for the Royal Family every morning and night at our private household services, and on Sundays any neighbor was welcome to attend, where my mother acted as a boisterous, emphatic clerk, and the prayer for the High Court of Parliament under our most religious and gracious King was delivered with ringing, unmistakeable defiance.
Hagan was a parson without a living, just as I was a Militia Colonel without a regiment. He had kept praying loudly for King George in Williamsburg long after Governor Dunmore had run away to his ships—until one Sunday he arrived at his church to find a corporal’s guard posted at the door. They told him the Committee of Safety had installed a new minister in his place, and he would do well to keep his tongue quiet. Hagan, ever fond of grand tragic speeches, told them to lead him before their chiefs, and so he was marched all the way through the streets to the Capitol, a jeering mob of white and Black idlers tugging at the hem of his gown the whole way. He got his audience with Patrick Henry and the new state officers, who he called “the robbers in their den.” He was all ready to deliver a grand heroic martyr’s speech, the sort of speech that would sound far better to a crowd watching him be dragged off to jail, but Mr. Henry had no intention of giving him that satisfaction. He kept Hagan waiting in an antechamber for three hours with a crowd of enslaved servants, and when Hagan finally marched into his office with his chin held high and began “Sir, by what authority am I, a minister of the—” Henry cut him off short. “Mr. Hagan, I am too busy to listen to speeches. And as for King George, he has no more authority in this country than King Nebuchadnezzar. Mind that, and hold your tongue if you please! Stick to King John and King Macbeth, sir, and if you send round benefit tickets, the whole Assembly will come and hear you. Did you ever see Mr. Hagan perform on the London stage when you were there, General?” He turned to General Lee, Washington’s second in command, who had come to Virginia on state business, and before Hagan could protest, he was bustled out of the room, red-faced and near tears with humiliation. After that, we thought it best to move the worthy parson out of the city and keep his ministrations confined to our small private congregation at Castlewood.
The new revolutionary government had flattered and bribed many of our leading Virginia gentry with the highest civil and military posts, men who might well have stayed loyal to the Crown if the British government had not behaved so outrageously foolishly, and made a real stand against the rising rebellion. Even so, though we Loyalists were gagged and muzzled, though the Capitol was in Whig hands, and most of our vaunted loyalist recruit regiments were no more real than Falstaff’s ragtag band, the faithful kept in secret communication across the whole colony. We did not rise up, many of us did run away, and many more of our people, when dragged before Committees of Safety or local justices, put on a great show of being Republican, vowing perdition to kings and nobles if it would keep them safe. But we all knew each other, perfectly well. As the tides of war shifted, we hid our loyalties, showed them, half-showed them, or even apostatised for a little while and shouted “Down with King George!” when we had to. Our enslaved servants carried messages and tokens from house to house, and endless quiet underhand plots were hatched in the dark where no prying Whig eyes could see. When the fighting is done, the neutrals all rush to join the winning side, shouting their support as loud as the most ardent patriots. No one counts the runaways. Will any man tell me that the signers and ardent supporters of the Declaration of Independence were not a minority of the nation, and that that minority did not win?
We had word that a part of the British army defeated at Boston was about to launch a major southern expedition, and the Loyalists of Virginia rested all their greatest hopes on its success. I, for one, was eager to move as soon as there was a chance for real action, so I made contact with ex-Governor Martin of North Carolina, and arranged to meet him with three or four other Virginia gentlemen—all officers of that fine Loyalist corps we were still waiting to recruit privates for. We made no particular secret of our departure from Castlewood. The new Congress government was still not strong enough to tyrannize over gentry of doubtful loyalties, and in our southern fashion, it was perfectly normal for a gentleman and his family to visit friends for months at a time. My wife, our infant, and a small retinue of servants took our leave of Madam Esmond, ostensibly to pay visits to neighboring plantations. We moved from friend’s house to friend’s house, leisurely, until we reached Wilmington, North Carolina, the port where we were supposed to meet the coming British relief force.
Before we arrived, the Loyalist Carolinians had already risen in arms, but their clashes with the Whig militia had gone badly. The poor Highlanders who fought for King George now were no luckier than the Highlanders who had drawn their swords against him on their own native soil, decades prior. We reached Wilmington at the end of May, to find Admiral Parker’s whole squadron anchored there, with General Clinton and five full British regiments aboard, bound for a landing at Charleston.
I presented myself to the General immediately, and when he saw that my “regiment” consisted of Lady Warrington, our nursing infant, and three Black servants, he gave me a very grim welcome indeed. But Captain Horner of the frigate *Sphinx*, who had served on the Jamaica station and had received many kindnesses from my father-in-law General Lambert, heard that my wife was the General’s daughter, and welcomed her aboard at once, giving up his own best cabin for our use. So we were refugees, just like my Lord Dunmore—we had waved our flag proudly, then tucked it away in our pockets and slipped out the back door. We sailed quickly from Wilmington for Charleston, and during the voyage and our long wait in the harbor before the assault, I got to know Mr. Clinton far better, and the acquaintance grew into a lasting friendship. It was the King’s birthday when we sailed into the river, and we all agreed it was a glorious day to launch our expedition.
The attack did not commence for several days after, and I will spare you all the overwrought scenes of parting between my Penelope and her Hector, before I climbed down the ship’s side into the landing boats. For one thing, Hector was perfectly well, a little gouty but unharmed, and no rascal of a Pyrrhus was there to carry his wife off. And in times of war and chaos, scenes of grief, terror, and parting play out every single hour. I can still see my wife’s gentle face peeking over the bulwark as we pulled away, and the little smile of our baby in her arms. Old memories, yes! Captain Miles, my boy, you have no taste for poetry, so you have forgotten the verses in Mr. Pope’s Homer that describe a young son waving his father off to battle, but your mother read them to you a hundred times as a boy, and she still keeps the steel gorget I wore that day tucked in the back of her dressing box.
My second attempt at fighting was no luckier than my first. We came back to our ships that evening thoroughly beaten. That madcap Charles Lee, the same man who had faced Clinton at Boston, now met him at Charleston, and he and his gallant garrison put up a brilliant, completely successful defence. The fort on Sullivan’s Island we attacked was a nut we could not crack. The fire from all our frigates could not pound through its soft palmetto walls, and the shallow creek we had planned to wade through to storm the fort was far deeper than our maps had told us, as Sir Henry and his leading officers found out when they waded in, chest-deep in the cold water, the first to charge. I had braved death by gunshot, by drowning, by the risk of catching my death of cold, many times before I stumbled back to the boats and returned to my wife. Our frigate ran aground for a time, and while we struggled to free it, the enemy cannonaded us mercilessly until we floated free of the bank.
A small incident in the middle of that disastrous battle sparked the close friendship that bound me to Sir Harry Clinton for all the rest of the war I spent with the Royal army. Men may argue all they like about his abilities as a commanding officer, and I will not speak ill of a man I respect and admire with all my heart. But of his personal courage and bearing there can be no doubt. He was always the first to charge, whether at Bunker’s Hill leading the rallied troops to carry the Continental lines, here outside Sullivan’s Fort, or a year later at Fort Washington, where he charged up the hillside with the regimental standard in his hand, the first man through the fort’s gates.
We were pushing off in our boats to force a path to the fort, hoping our own gunfire had deepened the channel enough to let us wade through, when a well-aimed shot struck the boat right beside ours. It knocked three quarters of its crew out of action instantly, killing the officer steering and tearing the flag right out of his hands. I could not help crying out “Bravo! Well aimed!” for no set of ninepins ever went down as helplessly as those poor fellows did before that round shot. The General turned to me, his face grim. “Sir! The behaviour of the enemy seems to please you!” “I am pleased, sir,” I answered, “that my countrymen over there fight as well as any Englishman should.” We floundered on towards the fort under constant fire from small arms and cannon, until we found the water rising higher and higher, up to our breasts, and had no choice but to turn our boats back and pull out of harm’s way. Afterwards, Sir Henry came aboard the *Sphinx* to call on my Lady Warrington, and was most gracious to her, joking endlessly about the author of this memoir, whom he teasingly described as a rebel at heart.
I pray my children will never live to see, or take part in, a great revolution like the one currently tearing apart our poor neighbors in France. Save for a very few people, the actors in these grand tragedies do not bear close scrutiny. Their great leaders are often no better than ranting quacks, their heroes no more than ignoble puppets, their heroines far from pure. The prize does not always go to the bravest man. In our revolution, for once and by some miracle, it fell to the most deserving. His great triumphs were never the rare small battles where he won a trifling upper hand—they were his triumph over Congress, over hunger and disease, over lukewarm friends and smiling enemies in his own camp, whose petty jealousies he had to meet and master with his great, steady spirit. When the war ended, our own Royal commanders spent all their time squabbling and making excuses before Parliament, pointing fingers, claiming their fleets arrived too late, their regiments misread their orders, their cannonballs would not fit their guns. But Washington beat us all, often with no shot, no powder, no money at all. He never once considered surrender. Through all the doubt and darkness, the danger and long tempest of the war, I think only his indomitable soul stayed entirely steady.
Of course, the enemy made the most of our failed Charleston expedition, declaring it a prodigious, history-making victory. Mr. Lee rushed back to New York, trumpeting himself as the conqueror of conquerors, and no man received him with more eager delight than the Commander-in-Chief of the American Army. It was “my dear Lee” and “my dear General” between them, and it always touches me to read that simple, unfeigned confidence Washington had for officers under him who had once served in the King’s army. The old Mexicans looked on in the same awed wonder when they first saw an armed Spanish horseman galloping towards them. That vain, flashy braggart Lee, and other Continental generals whose names and luck became notorious later, took shameless advantage of Washington’s modesty, blustering, sneering, and disobeying his orders, piling up new obstacles in his path every single day, obstacles only his astonishing endurance could have allowed him to overcome.
While we were away on the South Carolina expedition, the famous Fourth of July had come and gone, and we and the thirteen United States were parted forever. My own native state of Virginia had also made its grand declaration: that all men are born equally free, that all power rests with the people, who have the unalienable right to alter, reform, or abolish their government whenever they please, and that the idea of a hereditary ruler is unnatural and absurd.
