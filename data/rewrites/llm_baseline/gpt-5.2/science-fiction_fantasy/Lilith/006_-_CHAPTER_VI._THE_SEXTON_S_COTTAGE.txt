We had gone long across a moor of broken stone and stiff, dead stalks, the kind of ground that gives back nothing but a dry rasp beneath the boot and a smell of old peat long since burnt out. Moss lay in patches like faded cloth. The light had not yet failed, yet the sun was already muffled—wrapped in a grey lid of cloud that kept the day from ever quite warming. The wind came with a peculiar chill, not the honest cold of height or season, but as if it had slipped through a door left ajar on a room where night never ended.

Far off, a single cottage showed itself, small and low against the heath—so distant it seemed at first a darker knot in the pallor of the horizon.

“Here we are at last!” said the raven at my side. His voice had that dry, amused edge it always carried, like a twig snapping between clever fingers. “What a long way it is! In half the time I could have gone to Paradise and seen my cousin—him, you remember, who never came back to Noah! Dear! dear! it is almost winter!”

“Winter!” I cried. The word tasted wrong against what I remembered. “It seems but half a day since we left home!”

“That is because we have travelled so fast,” he returned. “In your world you cannot pull up the plumb-line you call gravitation, and let the world spin round under your feet! But here is my wife’s house! She is very good to let me live with her, and call it the sexton’s cottage!”

The thought of a sexton set my mind searching for its usual furniture: church, stones, gate, the hush of clipped grass and the particular quiet that belongs to the dead when the living have agreed to be gentle.

“But where is your churchyard—your cemetery—where you make your graves, I mean?” I asked, looking round. There was nothing but heath, flat as a board, and wind.

He stretched his neck and held his beak straight out, then slowly swung it, point by point, through every quarter of the compass, saying nothing.

My eyes followed that beak as if it were a needle drawing a circle, and the circle widened and widened until it had no rim. Without wall, without gate, without cross or stone, the whole moor became a burial-ground. Wherever the dreary wind ran its cold fingers, there lay his cemetery. He was sexton of all he surveyed—keeper of what had been laid aside. The horizon itself, low and starless, seemed a boundary not built but endured.

I stood in the burial-ground of the universe: its compass the unenclosed heath, its wall the grey edge of seeing. It was as if I had walked out of spring and summer, out of autumn and sunshine, and into the winter waiting for me—had set out in the prime of my youth and found myself already at the last season.

But the place corrected me even as the thought formed. The day there was not a day as I had known it; it was a turning wheel that carried all seasons in its slow revolution. Winter slept there through the night in a winding-sheet of ice; with a childlike smile, Spring woke at dawn; at noon Summer blazed abroad; and with the long change of afternoon, old Autumn crept in and died at the first breath of the vapourous, ghostly night.

As we neared the cottage, the clouded sun rushed down the steepest slope of the west as if eager to be rid of the sky. He sank while we were still a few yards from the door. At the instant his light went out, cold struck me—almost with weight, as if a pale hand had closed round my chest. I staggered over the threshold like a man escaping an icy death that had already caught his sleeve. Behind me the wind swelled and flung itself at the door; I fought it shut, feeling its insistence like an animal’s shoulder. Then, abruptly, all was still.

The cottage held a silence that was not emptiness but shelter, as if thick walls had caught the outside world’s hiss and worrying moan and pressed it into harmlessness. The change was so sudden that I noticed the sound of my own breath, and the small tick of something cooling.

A candle burned on a deal table in the middle of the room. Its flame made a little golden pond of light with edges of brown shadow. The air carried the clean scent of wax and dried wood, and beneath it a faint tang as of earth that has been turned and left to settle. Against the wall stood what I took at first for the lid of a coffin set upright. Even that seemed fitting, and made my skin tighten.

But it opened.

It was a door, and a woman entered.

She was all in white—as white as new-fallen snow. Yet her face, though as pale as her dress, did not suggest cold. At once it suggested warmth: the warmth of milk just drawn, of bread before it crusts, of linen folded from sun and wind. Her features might have been perfect; but her eyes made me forget to measure them. All life in her face and person was gathered and concentrated there, and what had gathered became light.

If death was coming for her, it had made her luminous; but in her eyes there was life enough for a nation. They were large and dark—dark not like soot but like depth, ever deepening as I gazed. A whole night-heaven seemed condensed in each pupil; within that blackness stars flashed; and round it, as a horizon, lay an iris like eternal twilight coiled and held.

What any eye is, God only knows. Hers looked as if they had come straight out of his own hand—fresh made, not inherited. The still face might have been primeval perfection; the living eyes were continuous creation.

“Here is Mr. Vane, wife!” said the raven.

“He is welcome,” she answered, in a low, rich, gentle voice. It was the kind of voice that carries not merely words but their native country: its vowels had valleys, its consonants stones warmed in the mouth. I felt in it buried treasures of immortal sound—tones too old to be fashionable and too true to be sold. After the outer wind’s harshness, the sound of her speech was like stepping from a street of iron into a kitchen where the only noises are knife on board, kettle beginning to murmur, and the soft thud of dough being worked.

I stared, and could not speak.

“I knew you would be glad to see him!” added the raven.

She stood in front of the door by which she had entered, and did not come nearer, as if distance were part of her courtesy.

“Will he sleep?” she asked.

“I fear not,” he replied; “he is neither weary nor heavy laden.”

“Why then have you brought him?”

“I have my fears it may prove precipitate.”

“I do not quite understand you,” I said. Uneasy foreboding pressed against my ribs like that earlier cold, yet with it came a thin hope of escape—as if misunderstanding might be a door.

“Surely a man must do a day’s work first!” I protested.

I looked into the white face of the woman and my heart fluttered, like a bird caught between wanting to flee and wanting to trust the hand that would hold it. She returned my gaze in silence, and that silence had a weight of its own, like a loaf resting on a board, proving its readiness without argument.

“Let me first go home,” I resumed, scrambling for the habits of my own world, “and come again after I have found or made, invented, or at least discovered something!”

“He has not yet learned that the day begins with sleep!” said the woman, turning to her husband. “Tell him he must rest before he can do anything!”

“Men,” he answered, “think so much of having done, that they fall asleep upon it. They cannot empty an egg but they turn into the shell, and lie down!”

At his words my eyes shifted from the woman to him—and the shift broke something.

I saw no raven.

In his place stood the librarian—the same slender elderly man I had known, in a rusty black coat, large in the body and long in the tails. Before, I had seen only his back; now I saw his face for the first time. It was so thin the bones shaped it plainly, suggesting the skulls his last-claimed profession must have made him familiar with. Yet I had never seen a face so alive, or a look so keen and friendly as that in his pale blue eyes—eyes that wore a haze, as if they had done much weeping and could not entirely forget the salt.

“You knew I was not a raven!” he said, smiling.

“I knew you were Mr. Raven,” I replied; “but somehow I thought you a bird too!”

“What made you think me a bird?”

“You looked a raven, and I saw you dig worms out of the earth with your beak.”

“And then?”

“Toss them in the air.”

“And then?”

“They grew butterflies, and flew away.”

“Did you ever see a raven do that? I told you I was a sexton!”

“Does a sexton toss worms in the air, and turn them into butterflies?”

“Yes.”

“I never saw one do it!”

“You saw me do it!—But I am still librarian in your house, for I never was dismissed, and never gave up the office. Now I am librarian here as well.”

“But you have just told me you were sexton here!”

“So I am. It is much the same profession. Except you are a true sexton, books are but dead bodies to you, and a library nothing but a catacomb!”

“You bewilder me!”

“That’s all right!”

He stood silent for a few moments. The woman stood silent too, motionless as a statue, by the coffin-door. In the stillness I became aware of the candle’s small, steady sound—yes, sound, a faint thread of burning—and beyond it, through the thick walls, the moor-wind trying the world with its long, low breath. The cottage seemed to choose what it would admit and what it would refuse.

“Upon occasion,” said the sexton at length, “it is more convenient to put one’s bird-self in front. Every one, as you ought to know, has a beast-self—and a bird-self, and a stupid fish-self, ay, and a creeping serpent-self too—which it takes a deal of crushing to kill! In truth he has also a tree-self and a crystal-self, and I don’t know how many selves more—all to get into harmony. You can tell what sort a man is by his creature that comes oftenest to the front.”

He turned to his wife. I watched him more closely now. He was above the ordinary height, and stood more erect than when last I saw him. His face was very pale—pale like her own. The nose handsomely encased the beak that had withdrawn within it. His lips were very thin, without colour, but beautifully curved; about them quivered a shadowy smile that held humour and love and pity together, as if none could be true without the others.

“We are in want of something to eat and drink, wife,” he said; “we have come a long way!”

“You know, husband,” she answered, “we can give only to him that asks.”

She turned her unchanging face and radiant eyes upon me, and it came to me with a sudden shame how often I had taken without asking—how often I had used the very words of my tongue as if they were mine by right, not gifts that require humility. Her gaze asked for no coin but speech.

“Please give me something to eat, Mrs. Raven,” I said, “and something—what you will—to quench my thirst.”

“Your thirst must be greater before you can have what will quench it,” she replied; “but what I can give you, I will gladly.”

She went to a cupboard set into the wall. Its door opened with a quiet wooden sigh, and the sound seemed old, as if that cupboard had been opening on the same errand for generations. She brought out bread and wine and set them on the table with a care that made the simple act ceremonial. The bread’s crust was browned, not hard but decisive; it carried the smell of grain that has known sun and wind and the patient turning of soil. The wine held a dark light in it, as if it remembered summer even here.

We sat down to the perfect meal.

I ate, and the bread and wine went deeper than hunger and thirst. The bread had a sweetness that was not sugar but field; the wine warmed like a small hearth laid inside the breast. With each mouthful I felt something in me loosen—anxiety and discomfort slid away as if unclenched. In their place came expectation, a quiet that listened. I became sharply aware of the sonic cleanliness of the cottage: no clatter of needless machines, no manufactured music insisting on itself, no loudness pretending to be joy. Only the soft dealings of life—swallowing, breathing, the candle’s whisper, and far off the wind, now more like surf than threat.

I grew very sleepy. Only then did I first feel weary, as if the body, granted permission, remembered it was mortal.

“I have earned neither food nor sleep, Mrs. Raven,” I said, “but you have given me the one freely, and now I hope you will give me the other, for I sorely need it.”

“Sleep is too fine a thing ever to be earned,” said the sexton; “it must be given and accepted, for it is a necessity. But it would be perilous to use this house as a half-way hostelry—for the repose of a night, that is, merely.”

As he spoke, a wild-looking little black cat sprang up on his knee. Its fur had the dull shine of soot, and its eyes were like small green lamps. He patted it as one pats a child to make it go to sleep; and yet the motion struck me strangely—as if he were patting down sod upon a grave, smoothing it lovingly, humming an inward lullaby that needed no tune.

“Here is one of Mara’s kittens!” he said to his wife. “Will you give it something and put it out? she may want it!”

The woman took it gently from him. The cat did not struggle; it yielded as if it understood her hands. She gave it a little piece of bread—small, but offered with the same gravity as the whole loaf—and went out with it, closing the door behind her with a sound as soft as a page turned.

“How then am I to make use of your hospitality?” I asked.

“By accepting it to the full,” he answered.

“I do not understand.”

“In this house no one wakes of himself.”

“Why?”

“Because no one anywhere ever wakes of himself. You can wake yourself no more than you can make yourself.”

The words struck at something deep and stubborn in me—my old pride in doing, in beginning, in claiming the first motion as mine.

“Then perhaps you or Mrs. Raven would kindly call me!” I said, still not understanding, but feeling again that vague foreboding—stronger now, because sleep was drawing near and with it the surrender of control.

“We cannot.”

“How dare I then go to sleep?” I cried. The question came out like a child’s, and I hated that it was true.

“If you would have the rest of this house, you must not trouble yourself about waking. You must go to sleep heartily, altogether and outright.”

My soul sank within me. He sat looking me in the face. His eyes seemed to say without words, Will you not trust me? And I, staring back, heard in the quiet between us the whole moor’s wind held at bay, and the steadier, kinder rhythm of this room—a place where sound itself had been disciplined into mercy.

“I will,” I answered.

“Then come,” he said. “I will show you your couch.”

As we rose, the woman came in again. She took up the candle. The flame leaned toward her as if it loved her. She turned to the inner door and led the way. I went close behind her, and the sexton followed.
