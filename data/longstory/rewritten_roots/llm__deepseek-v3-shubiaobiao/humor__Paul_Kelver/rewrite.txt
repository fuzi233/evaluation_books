At the far end of a London street so monotonous it could make a brick weep—a street where the windows and doorsteps line up with military precision, as if auditioning for a geometry textbook—there’s a house. Not just any house, mind you. This one has *character*, which is more than can be said for its neighbors, whose idea of architectural flair is "let’s all look exactly the same forever."

This house, you see, was built back when the area still had delusions of being countryside, before the marshland gave up and accepted its fate as a soot-stained urban wasteland. Now it sulks behind a patch of dirt that might’ve been a garden once, if optimism were a fertilizer. Clothes hang there like spectral flags of surrender, perpetually drying in the smog. The porch, stripped of its stucco vanity, stands naked and unashamed—much like my dignity after three pints.

Occasionally, I wander by in daylight, when the street is alive with the melodious cries of coal vendors (who’ve long since given up hope) and the clatter of gossip at every gate. But the house, embarrassed by its shabbiness under the sun’s harsh glare, pretends not to know me. Only at night, when the shadows are kind enough to hide its flaws, does it welcome me like an old nanny with too many stories and not enough filters.

Then the street falls silent, the drunks at the "King of Prussia" across the way pass out, and the house and I get to chatting. It’s like flipping through a scrapbook filled with faces you half-remember—some fondly, some with a shudder.

First comes my father, hobbling along with his fancy cane, pausing every fifty yards like a wind-up toy running low on steam.

*"Handsome chap, wasn’t he?"* I muse.

*"Oh, sure,"* snorts the house. *"Before life chewed him up and spat him out. Though honestly, what did he expect, setting up shop as a solicitor in Poplar? ‘A rising neighborhood,’ he said. Rising straight into the Thames, more like."*

I bristle. *"It was a calculated risk!"*

*"Calculated by a toddler, maybe,"* retorts the house. *"The man starved himself on five shillings a week just to keep his name clean, and you think he’d have thrived among dockyard hustlers? Please."*

Then my mother’s face flickers by, soft and sad. *"And her! Bless her heart, she couldn’t spot a viper in a nursery. Trusted the wrong people, loved the wrong ones, cried over spilt milk and then cried harder when there was no milk left to spill."*

*"Children,"* I mutter.

*"Exactly!"* crows the house. *"The pair of them, bumbling through life like kittens in a dog park."*

A tiny ghost of a girl darts past the railings—her feet pattering like raindrops. *"Whatever happened to her?"* I ask.

*"Oh, she’s around,"* says the house, grinning. *"Fourteen stone and nine kids later. Runs a shop. You’d walk right past her. Probably for the best."*

Other faces swirl in the gloom: a jolly hypocrite I never quite trusted, a rogue with a mouth like sin and eyes like salvation, a sweet old lady who’d skin you alive if you looked at her sideways. And at the center of it all, a quiet boy with curls too pretty for his own good, watching the world like it’s a puzzle he’ll never solve.

*"Would he even know me now?"* I wonder.

*"Doubt it,"* says the house. *"You’re not the ghost he’d expect."*

Then, as always, it needles me: *"You’re a writer. Why not tell his story?"*

*"Because nothing happened!"* I protest. *"No dragons, no princesses, just… life."*

*"And what’s more interesting than that?"* snaps the house. *"You think people only want tales of heroes and villains? Half of ’em are just trying to get through the day without setting their trousers on fire."*

So here it is—a story without much glory, where the good guys fumble, the bad guys aren’t all bad, and the magic thread leads straight into a ditch. If you’re the sort who likes your endings neat and your morals clearer than gin, move along. But if you’ve ever looked in the mirror and thought, *"Well, that could’ve gone better,"* then pull up a chair.

Just don’t blame me if it’s a bit of a mess. Blame the house. It started this.

Fate had grand designs for me—or so I assumed, until I discovered I’d been born in *May*, the unluckiest month of the year. Had I possessed the decency to arrive in June, as all sensible infants do, my life might have unfolded with considerably less chaos. But no, I burst into the world two hours after my father’s mines were flooded (because misfortunes, as I was frequently reminded, *never* travel alone). To add insult to injury, my mother, in her distress, shattered a cheval-glass—an act which, according to Welsh superstition, doomed my first seven years to spectacular disaster.

“And I must say,” mused my bony nurse, Mrs. Fursey, with a sigh, “it *does* look like there’s something to the superstition after all.”

“So I’m *not* a lucky little boy?” I asked, perplexed. Up until then, Mrs. Fursey had insisted I was the most fortunate child alive. I was *privileged* to go to bed at six while lesser children were cruelly kept awake until eight. I was *blessed* to dine on brimstone and treacle (a revolting concoction masquerading as food) whenever I sniffled, while other children were left to suffer in neglect. And, of course, I had *her*—Mrs. Fursey herself—as my personal guardian, a privilege so immense it surely outweighed all other hardships.

But now, it seemed, my luck was in question.

Mrs. Fursey hastily corrected herself. “Oh, *you’re* lucky enough,” she said. “I was thinking of your poor mother.”

“Isn’t *she* lucky?”

“Well, she hasn’t had much luck since *you* arrived.”

“Wasn’t it lucky for her to have me?”

“Not at that particular time, no.”

“Didn’t she *want* me?”

Mrs. Fursey was of the old school that believed children should apologize for existing. “Well,” she said, “I daresay she could have done without you.”

I sat there, staring into the fire, grappling with the existential horror of my own superfluousness. If neither Heaven nor Earth had actively *wanted* me, why was I here?

Then, in a rare moment of clarity, it struck me: *Somewhere, something must need me.* The thought was comforting, though I couldn’t explain it. (Years later, a Portuguese Jew’s son would put it into words for me. At the time, all I knew was that I *was*, and that was enough.)

But my philosophical musings were soon interrupted by more pressing concerns.

“Nurse,” I asked, “why haven’t we got a husband?”

Mrs. Fursey’s needle froze mid-stitch. “What maggot’s in your head *now*?”

“Mamma doesn’t have one.”

“Don’t talk nonsense! Your mother’s husband is your father, who lives in London.”

“What’s the good of *him*?”

Mrs. Fursey gasped as though I’d blasphemed. “You wicked child! Your father works hard to keep you in idleness, and *this* is your gratitude?”

I hadn’t meant to be ungrateful. I was merely quoting my mother, who had recently lamented, *“I want a husband I can* get at *!”* Evidently, a faraway father who sent money was no substitute for a real, present one—preferably one who carried you piggyback or took you sailing.

Mrs. Fursey, missing the nuance, launched into a lecture on filial duty, then dropped a bombshell: Soon, we would leave for London, never to return.

*Never again* the garden. *Never again* the pebble-ridge, or Jacob’s Tower, or Anna, or Pincher the dog. The permanence of loss settled over me like a fog.

That night, as my mother kissed me goodnight, I turned away, pretending to sleep—until I blurted out my fear:

She laughed, then grew serious. “Who told you such nonsense?”

“Mrs. Fursey said you could have done without me.”

My mother knelt beside the bed, her eyes level with mine. “I was unhappy, and you came to comfort me. Every time you’re good, I’m the luckiest mother alive. Every time you fail—well, that’s *my* bad luck.”

It was a heavy responsibility for a small boy, but I promised to remember.

After that, Mrs. Fursey faded into the background, and my mother became my sun and stars. We read together in the garden, spun tales of knights and ogres, and climbed to Jacob’s Tower, where she wove legends into the wind.

(“Of course,” she’d add, “there are no such things *nowadays*.” Yet we still hurried home at dusk, hand in hand, just in case.)

When the letter came announcing our move to London, my mother danced through the house like a girl. My aunt, ever the realist, muttered over her bread and butter:

“Mad with joy. Why? Trading a decent house for a hole in Plaistow.”

But my mother was undeterred. “It’s near the river!”

“The *marshes*,” corrected my aunt.

“It has a *garden*!”

“Drat the woman,” said my aunt. “Why can’t she sit down and let us have our tea in peace?”

For three days, my mother packed and sang, unpacked and laughed, until at last—on the eve of our departure—I found her weeping under the lime tree.

“I thought you were *glad* to go,” I said.

“I am,” she sniffed.

“Then why are you crying?”

“Because I’m sorry to leave.”

Grown-ups, I decided, were *very* confusing.

My parents’ marriage was not what you might call conventional. You see, they had been separated for years, and though by most standards they weren’t exactly young—my father pushing forty, my mother past thirty—in spirit, they were practically children. Had anyone asked me, a wide-eyed observer of their antics, for marital advice, I might have painted a rather misleading picture. I’d have described a husband as a man who couldn’t bear to be more than three feet from his wife, constantly interrupting her with cries of, *"Maggie, are you busy? I’ve got a pressing matter to discuss!"*—only for the "pressing matter" to be whether he preferred peas or carrots with supper. A wife, in my youthful estimation, was a woman whose eyes sparkled like a lovesick heroine’s whenever her husband entered the room—though in reality, that sparkle might just as easily have been suppressed irritation.

I’d also have warned prospective spouses about the perils of domestic espionage. Never enter a room unannounced unless you enjoy the sight of two adults springing apart like guilty cats, followed by a frantic scramble to assume perfectly innocent positions—my father suddenly engrossed in a ledger, my mother stitching a button with unnatural intensity. And under no circumstances should you leave your feet unattended beneath the table, lest they be mistaken for someone else’s and subjected to enthusiastic toe-crushing.

Personally, I was immune to such hazards. My parents had long since dismissed my interruptions with a resigned, *"Oh, it’s only Spud,"* usually followed by a sofa cushion to the head. But my aunt was another matter. Out of respect (or terror), they maintained some decorum in her presence. Not that she ever *said* anything—no, her methods were subtler. She moved through the house like a one-woman tuberculosis ward, coughing loudly enough to wake the dead before entering any room—*backwards*, mind you, as if to prove she hadn’t seen anything scandalous. Dark hallways were navigated via tuneless humming. The woman was a walking fire alarm.

You’d think they’d be grateful for her discretion. You’d be wrong.

*"Fanny, I swear you take pleasure in sabotaging our happiness,"* my mother snapped one day, catching my aunt mid-spoon-drop outside the drawing room—a delicate operation involving the strategic clattering of cutlery to announce her arrival.

*"Sabotage?"* My aunt’s voice was syrup laced with arsenic. *"Why, I’m simply enchanted by the sight of two turtledoves, their combined ages barely scraping seventy, billing and cooing like spring chickens."*

My father, meanwhile, had reached the stage of outright hostility. One evening by the canal, he stopped dead, seized by a sudden, violent thought. *"Your aunt,"* he began (with the usual unspoken accusation that *I* was somehow responsible for her existence), *"is the most cantankerous, meddling—"* He broke off, shaking his fist at the sunset. *"I swear to God, Spud, if she had a cottage and a modest income, I’d buy her a one-way ticket to the countryside myself!"* Then, remembering himself: *"Not that she isn’t… occasionally tolerable. Don’t tell your mother I said that."*

The other keen observer of our household melodrama was Susan, our maid-of-all-work—a woman whose heart was, by her own proud declaration, *always in the right place*, even if her body was usually slumped in a chair, fast asleep. She wept at the sight of marital affection, claiming it reminded her of her own tragically complicated past.

*"I thought your husband was dead?"* I once ventured, after she’d spent Monday weeping over his unwatered grave and Tuesday vowing to strangle him.

*"So did I,"* Susan replied darkly. *"That was his cunning."*

*"Not yet,"* she said, cracking her knuckles. *"But he will be."*

Such philosophical riddles were beyond me. I retreated to the safer confines of arithmetic.

Susan’s reign ended abruptly one Saturday night when my mother found her sprawled on the kitchen floor, breathing like a broken harmonium. The doctor—a bear of a man named Washburn, locally known as *Dr. Fighting Hal*—arrived, took one look at her, and promptly upended a pail of water over her head.

*"Feeling better?"* he boomed, as she spluttered to life. *"Fancy another round?"*

What followed was a duel of insults so vivid I’m certain the wallpaper blushed. When Susan finally flounced out, the doctor stayed for supper, filling the house with laughter so loud I’m surprised the neighbors didn’t complain.

That was the magic of the man. He turned my timid parents into brighter versions of themselves—my mother sparkling, my father cracking jokes, even my aunt thawing slightly. Under his influence, our shabby little world seemed to crack open, revealing a brighter, bolder place where life was a grand, rowdy adventure.

Of course, whether that was a good thing or not remained to be seen.

“Louisa!” bellowed my father down the kitchen stairs, his voice rattling the pots. “Have the lot of you gone into hibernation? I’ve just had to answer my own front door like a common peddler!” Then, with the dramatic flourish of a thwarted king, he stormed into his office and slammed the door hard enough to shake the windows. My father was a virtuoso of temper—when there was no audience to witness it.

Fifteen minutes later, his bell jangled like an alarm. My mother, wrestling with potatoes while encased in wash-leather gloves, exchanged a glance with my aunt, who was shelling peas with the precision of a clockmaker. The bell rang again, louder, more insistent.

“Once for Louisa, twice for James, isn’t it?” mused my aunt, as if consulting an invisible rulebook.

“Paul, you go,” said my mother, then abruptly flushed and yanked off her gloves. “No, never mind—stay with your sums. I’ll handle this.” And off she dashed, leaving me to ponder the mysteries of adult urgency.

Moments later, the kitchen door creaked open just enough for my mother’s hand to snake through, waggling fingers summoning me like a conspirator.

“Walk like a thief,” she whispered, tiptoeing up the stairs with exaggerated caution—a futile effort, given that the stairs chose that moment to announce our ascent with all the subtlety of a brass band. We reached my parents’ bedroom, where my best suit (or, more accurately, my *less-worst* suit) lay in state in the wardrobe like a relic of a grander era.

Never before had I been forced into these garments on a weekday. My mother swatted away all protests and questions, and before I knew it, I was trussed up like a Sunday roast, down to the stiff new Bluchers pinching my feet. Then, with the same stealth we’d employed on the way up, we crept back down—only for my mother to fling the front door open with a theatrical bang.

“Won’t I need my cap?” I whispered.

She ignored me, steering me instead into my father’s office, where the performance unfolded.

The office had always been a place of mystery—part study, part stage set. By night, it transformed into our sitting room, the books and papers shoved aside like embarrassing relatives. By day, it was my father’s domain, though one he abandoned at the slightest provocation—cricket in the garden, ninepins in the hall—only to come charging back at the sound of a knock, as if he’d been hard at work all along.

Today, he rose like a barrister preparing his closing argument, his hair standing on end from his habitual rumpling. His face struggled to settle into professional solemnity, but he still looked like an overgrown schoolboy playing at being a grown-up.

“This is the boy,” he announced, nudging me forward. “Tall for his age, wouldn’t you say?”

Our visitor—a walrus of a man with small, glittering eyes—rolled a cigar from one corner of his mouth to the other and extended a hand like a slab of ham. His grip was warm, slightly damp, and brought to mind the illustrations of sea mammals in my natural history book.

“Spittin’ image of ’is mother,” he declared, ruffling my hair with his free hand. “And that,” he added with a wink at my father, “is about the ’ighest compliment I can pay ’im, eh?”

His eyes were tiny but unnervingly sharp, the kind that made you feel he could see the lies you hadn’t even thought of yet.

“Where you plannin’ to school ’im? Eton? ’Arrow?”

“We’re still weighing our options,” my father said smoothly. “For now, he’s educated at home.”

The man—Mr. Hasluck, as I would learn—chuckled, his whole body quivering like a bowl of jelly. “Take it from me, learn all you can. If I’d ’ad book-learnin’, I wouldn’t be ’ere payin’ your father fancy prices to do my work—I’d do it meself.”

“You seem to have managed well enough without it,” my father remarked. And indeed, Mr. Hasluck was a walking advertisement for prosperity: rings like knuckledusters, a waistcoat straining over his belly, and a gold watch chain thick enough to moor a ship.

“I’d ’ave done better with it,” he grunted. Then, to my surprise, he turned those gimlet eyes on me. “But you look clever enough. What d’you think?”

“You *seem* very clever,” I offered, hoping to flatter but also meaning it.

He roared with laughter, his bulk shaking the room. “Well, old Noel Hasluck ain’t exactly a fool,” he admitted. “But I’d like myself better if I could talk about somethin’ besides business and didn’t drop my aitches. And so would my little gel.”

“You have a daughter?” my mother asked, brightening at once—children being her preferred currency of conversation.

“I won’t describe ’er,” he said. “But I’ll bring ’er round one day, if you don’t mind. She don’t meet gentlefolk often—do ’er good.”

My mother glanced at my father, but Mr. Hasluck answered the unspoken question himself.

“Don’t fret, ma’am—she ain’t a bit like me. Nobody believes she’s mine, ’cept me and the missus. She’s a proper little lady. Freak o’ nature, I call it.”

“We’d be delighted,” my mother said warmly.

“You will be when you see ’er,” he replied, utterly confident.

Then, to my astonishment, he pressed half a crown into my hand, overriding my parents’ polite protests with the ease of a man used to getting his way.

“Don’t go wastin’ it on foreigners,” he advised as I was ushered out. “Spend it on a proper English shop.”

Thus began the Great Half-Crown Dilemma. Never before had I been granted such autonomy over money. Previous windfalls had been swiftly diverted toward gloves, grammars, or other grim necessities. But this was mine—*mine*—to squander as I pleased.

The responsibility was crushing. I lay awake, agonizing. I paced the streets, paralyzed by indecision. My parents, for once, refused to advise me.

“You must learn to think for yourself,” my father intoned, as if I were a junior stockbroker.

“No, dear,” said my mother. “Mr. Hasluck wanted *you* to choose. If I tell you, it’s *my* choice, not yours.”

Even the good boys in storybooks were no help—always buying gifts for the poor or saving kittens. *Humbugs*, the lot of them. I turned in desperation to my aunt Fan.

“If someone gave *you* half a crown, what would you buy?”

“Side-combs,” she said promptly. (She was always losing hers.)

“But if you were *me*?”

“Drat the child! How should I know? Use your noggin, idiot!”

The shop windows tormented me. I priced everything. I made decisions and unmade them. My mother’s patience frayed. The half-crown became a family curse.

Then, one day, inspiration struck—or perhaps madness. In a grubby little plumber’s shop, nestled among pipes and washers, were squares of coloured glass—the sort used for lavatory windows and staircase panes. Some had stars, others austere geometric patterns. I bought twelve. The plumber, delighted by my patronage, threw in two extra for free.

Why? I couldn’t say then. I still can’t.

My mother nearly wept. My father stared. “But *what are they for*?”

Only my aunt offered solace. “If a lad wants coloured glass, who’s to stop him? Not all tastes are the same.”

Eventually, I cut myself on the edges, and the lot were tossed into the dustbin. But looking back, I see it as Fate’s joke. Many have pocketed Hasluck’s half-crowns since—men who’ve slapped their pockets and blessed his name—yet it always ends the same: coloured glass and bleeding fingers. Fairy gold, perhaps.

Soon after, change swept through our house. A maid answered the door. New oilcloth gleamed in the hall. Lace curtains—the mark of *real* money—advertised our rising fortunes. And, most shocking of all, my father’s Sunday best became his *weekday* wear, new clothes filling the wardrobe that had long been the last bastion of our respectability.

One evening, after I’d been put to bed, I woke from a nightmare—one of those where you fall forever, then find yourself in the street with nothing on. Needing comfort, I crept downstairs.

The office was dark, but light spilled from under the drawing-room door. I pushed it open.

There, in state, sat my parents—my father in his buckled shoes and frilled shirt, my mother in her Indian shawl and ceremonial cap. They started like guilty children.

The last I’d seen them, they’d been cleaning windows in the dark. Now, they sipped sherry, spoke of Byron and the Great Exhibition, and for all the world looked like gentry at leisure.

I wonder if this makes them seem foolish? It is not my intent. Their little pretenses are dear to me. Virtues we admire, faults we forgive—but follies? Ah, follies we treasure.

For me, Hasluck’s arrival marked a shift. My father was now always busy—in his office or lost in the city’s labyrinth. My mother had calls to make, shopping to oversee. I was left to my own devices.

I took to wandering—Victoria Park, Clapton’s leafy lanes—but more often, the squalid streets. Not the bustling thoroughfares, but the dim, silent ones, where life lurked behind grimy windows. Fear and fascination drew me.

One day, a cry pierced the stillness—long, shrill, rising to a shriek. I ran, stumbling into a desolate lane that ended in black mudflats by the river. There, I met a boy—a hopping, twitching creature with a mouth that worked like a puppet’s.

“Wot ’appens when yer dead?” he asked.

We talked of Heaven and Hell, of corpses and pins. He gave me a knife, swore me to secrecy, then danced away across the mud—sinking, stumbling, vanishing beneath the sludge.

I told no one. I had sworn.

But the streets lost their charm after that. Now, the danger was human. Boys—always boys—who’d set upon me like sparrows on a crumb. The shame of running, the burn of their laughter. The knowledge that I, who fancied myself a hero in the making, fled from ruffians half my size.

Oh, to have turned and fought! But terror froze me. I’d skulk home, climb to my room, and weep into my pillow, hating myself.

Only once did I nearly stand my ground. But then I’d see them—the grins, the gathering crowd—and run again, heart pounding, their jeers ringing in my ears.

Robin Hood would have scorned me. Ivanhoe disowned me. Even Jack Harkaway, mere schoolboy though he was, would have thrashed the lot.

But I? I ran. Always ran.

The East India Dock Road today is a teeming artery of commerce, alive with the clatter of trams, the rumble of carts, and the ceaseless stampede of feet. But in those distant days of my youth, it was a lonesome stretch, flanked by the echoing wall of the docks on one side and a scattering of shabby houses amid patches of cabbage plots and rubbish heaps on the other. The sole survivor of that bygone era—at least, the last time I wandered down it—was the humble brick toll-house at the bridge’s mouth. And it was there, in that unassuming little building, that my childhood slipped from me like a discarded coat, leaving me staring, wide-eyed and uneasy, at the vast, bewildering world beyond.

I can’t explain it better than that. I’d been to Plaistow that afternoon, paying a visit to our family dentist—an odd location for such a professional, but one that came with certain *advantages*.

"Keep the half-crown in your hand," my mother would instruct, after ensuring the coin was securely tucked into my knickerbocker pocket. "But if he refuses it, naturally, you must bring it home again."

I suspect he was some distant relation—or, at the very least, a kindly old soul. Seated in his plush chair of state, he’d unroll his gleaming instruments before me like a magician revealing his tricks and invite me to pick my poison, lavishing praise on the most fearsome-looking tools.

But the moment I opened my mouth to protest—*snap!*—a pair of pincers would dart from his sleeve, and before I could so much as yelp, the ordeal was over. Then came tea. He was a bachelor, living in a house surrounded by a sprawling garden (for Plaistow was still a village then), and his housekeeper conjured the most marvelous jams and jellies from its bounty. Oh, those teas! Our conversation usually revolved around my mother, who, it transpired, had once been a mischievous little girl—nothing like the stern figure I knew. According to him, she’d been a whirlwind of pranks and defiance, though excellent company if his tales were to be believed.

And I *did* believe them, despite my mother’s protests when I repeated them to her.

"What else did he say?" she’d demand, feigning indifference, though her tapping foot betrayed her.

After tea, he’d fetch out his magnificent microscope, and I’d lose myself in a world of fantastical creatures—dragons, monsters, wonders unseen by ordinary eyes. It was his passion, and looking back, I wonder if his fascination with the tiny and intricate wasn’t a rebellion against his own diminutive stature—for he was a very small man, but with a heart as vast as the universe.

On leaving, I’d solemnly present my half-crown "with Mamma’s compliments," and he’d solemnly accept it. Yet without fail, I’d find it nestled in my pocket the moment I stepped outside. The first time, I tried returning it, but he feigned ignorance with a straight face.

"Must be another half-crown," he’d muse. "Such things happen. Coins hide in pockets, you know. Slippery things, half-crowns."

On this particular day—the day that marked the end of my childhood—I paused on the bridge, watching sluggish barges navigate the piers. It was one of those hushed summer evenings when even a city hums with secrets. And as I passed through the white toll-gate, I felt an odd sensation—as though I’d left *myself* behind on the bridge. So strong was the impression that I turned, half-expecting to see my smaller self still leaning against the iron railing, gazing down at the sunlit water.

Foolish, perhaps, but there it is. That little boy never returned to me. He slipped away like a shadow at dusk, leaving only memories and a vague, persistent ache. For a while, I tried to play his games, to dream his dreams, but the magic was gone. I was a ghost of myself, merely going through the motions.

The realization that I was being marched inexorably down the road of growing up troubled me deeply—enough, at times, to bring me to tears. I didn’t *want* to grow up. Couldn’t it be stopped? I longed to stay as I had been—forever playing, forever dreaming. The future loomed dark and frightening. Must I go forward?

But slowly, over months and years, a new self emerged—a creature of fresh desires, fresh fears, fresh fascinations. And little Paul, the boy I’d been, faded into the past.

Before I let him go entirely, though, let me dredge up what else I can remember of him.

Chief among my memories is my Aunt Fan, crouched before the kitchen fire, her skirt and crinoline hitched up around her waist (decency preserved only by her petticoat), rocking rhythmically while stroking her own knees. Opposite her, I’d brandish a paper-knife sword or straddle a broomstick horse, declaiming grandly. Sometimes I was a knight, and she a wicked ogre. After a suitably dramatic death scene (complete with growls and curses), she’d transform into the beautiful princess I’d rescue.

Realism faltered at the "bearing away" part, however, since Aunt Fan refused to budge from her chair.

Other times, armed with the meat chopper, I became an Indian brave, and she, Laughing Water or Singing Sunshine, would join me on scalping expeditions. Or, in gentler moods, I’d play the Fairy Prince to her Sleeping Beauty—though she was never very convincing in the role.

Her true talent lay in villainy.

"I’ll skin him and hang him in the larder till Sunday week," she’d muse, smacking her lips. "By then he’ll be just right—not too tough, not too high."

(My aunt had a *gift* for culinary detail.)

I don’t mean to imply her participation was purely selfless. Often, she’d suggest the game herself—always casting herself as the villain, the dragon, the wicked sorcerer. Her imagination was a thing of terror. She could turn a prince into a prawn, a queen into a Brussels sprout, and devise elaborate slaughter scenes with meticulous attention to sharpened knives and cleanup logistics.

Her death scenes were masterpieces. Indeed, her groans and convulsions sometimes frightened *me*. Only after the final, dramatic gurgle would she let down her skirt—our curtain call—and set about making tea.

Another recurring figure in my memories is a small, curly-haired girl, trotting beside me through grimy streets, her eyes wide with admiration. She had attached herself to me after hearing tales of my exploits, and I tolerated her company for more reasons than I cared to admit. For one, she acted as a shield against my own cowardice. With her beside me, I couldn’t flee from imaginary foes. Instead, we courted adventure, for danger, once you grow accustomed to it, adds a thrill to walks that safer streets lack.

(Perhaps this is how my dog feels when we stroll together—while I see only pavement, he navigates a world of ambushes and sudden threats.)

A second benefit of her company was that we were less likely to be trampled underfoot. She had a sweet face but a *vocabulary*, and when some oblivious fool tried to walk *through* us instead of around, she’d unleash it.

"Oi! Where d’you think you’re going, four-eyes? We ain’t ghosts!"

And if they dared retort, her voice would rise to a shriek.

"Garn! They ran out of brains when they made you—had to stuff your head with sawdust!"

(These are *mild* samples.)

Attempts at retaliation were useless. As well might a hippopotamus scold a terrier. The only result was street-wide entertainment.

On these occasions, our roles reversed—I became the admiring audience to her ferocity. Yet with me, she was meek to the point of exasperation. She’d wait for me for hours, her face pressed against the railings, until I either emerged or shook my head from my window. Then she’d scamper off, her footsteps fading into silence, leaving me oddly melancholy.

I suppose I cared for her, in a way—but she never entered my daydreams, which meant she existed only in the periphery of my life.

She was unwise to introduce me to her father’s shop, though. Children, like dogs, are snobs. Had he been a woodcutter, I could have consoled myself with fairy-tale logic—mistaken identity, royal blood hidden beneath humble trappings. Even a pork butcher might have been spun into a swineherd with romantic potential.

But a *fishmonger*? From six p.m., a *fried* fishmonger? History offered no precedent for such a figure ascending to nobility.

So our meetings dwindled, though I knew she waited for me in the afternoons, her cheeks often streaked with dust and tears. And when Barbara entered my life (of whom more later), they ceased altogether.

Thus began and ended my first romance.

One day, perhaps, when nostalgia softens even Pigott Street’s harsh edges, I’ll step into that little grocer’s shop and ask to see her. I’ve traced her already—she’s now the mother of many, grown stout, her tongue likely sharper than ever. Yet beneath it all, I’ll see the little girl with the adoring eyes.

I didn’t realize how much I needed those eyes until they were gone.

Then there were the "generals"—the procession of maids who passed through our kitchen. Few lingered long enough to leave an impression, but those who did were memorable. Ours was not a neighborhood where domestic service was a career. The ambitious went west; ours were the discontented, the lovelorn, the disillusioned—girls who took to cap and apron as others might have taken the veil.

Most were kind, if unimaginative playmates. They couldn’t "make believe," but they’d spare ten minutes to romp, which was probably healthier for me anyway. Less healthy was their awe at my precocious chatter, which they mistook for wisdom. They’d take me home to show me off, and I, basking in admiration, would hold forth like a miniature orator.

"As good as a book, ain’t he?" was the usual verdict.

"As good as a *play*," one enthusiastic greengrocer declared.

I began to see myself among the Immortals.

One girl, Janet, stayed for months—a cheerful, foul-mouthed creature, the daughter of a barge captain. Her language was a thing of horror and delight.

"Janet!" my mother would cry, hands clapped over her ears. "How *can* you use such words?"

"What words, mum?"

"What you just called the gas man!"

"*Him*? Did you see what he did? Marched straight into my clean kitchen, didn’t wipe his boots, the—" And before my mother could stop her, she’d unleash another volley, entirely unaware that she’d done anything remarkable.

We were friends, Janet and I, so I took it upon myself to reform her. I told her bluntly that if she kept swearing, she’d go to hell.

"Then where’s *my father* going?" she demanded.

"Does *he* swear?"

Janet assured me that compared to her father, her own language was milk-and-water.

"I’m afraid," I said gravely, "if he doesn’t stop—"

"But it’s the only way he *can* talk!" she protested. "He don’t mean anything by it."

I sighed but held firm. "People who swear go to hell, Janet."

She scoffed.

"God send my father to hell just ’cause he don’t talk posh? Don’t be daft. *He’s got more sense.*"

I hope no one is pained by Janet’s theology. Her words stayed with me, a lifeline in later years when faced with the pompous pronouncements of fools. More than once, when drowning in solemn absurdity, I’ve whispered to myself:

And I needed that comfort then, for I was deep in mortal terror of hellfire. The literature of the time spared no detail—eternal flames, relentless torment, devils with pitchforks. I’d been burned once by live coals; the thought of that pain, *everywhere*, *forever*, haunted me.

"Then be good," advised the well-meaning voices around me.

But doing wrong was *easy*.

"Then repent."

But how? Did I *truly* hate my sin, or just fear punishment?

Worst of all was the specter of the *Unforgivable Sin*. No one would tell me what it was—only that it existed. I lived in dread of discovering I’d already committed it.

"Believe," they said. "Only belief will save you."

But *how* did one believe? How could one be *sure*?

Hours I’d spend on my knees in the dark, whispering, "*I believe, I believe, oh, I do believe!*"—then rise, trembling, wondering if it had worked.

Thus passed the days of my trembling childhood.

Doctor Florret was the very model of correctness—not too much, not too little, just the sort of man you’d expect to preside over a respectable middle-class school. He was solid, but not excessively so; his voice carried the weight of authority without ever tipping into pomposity. Even his furniture seemed to whisper, *I am here for usefulness, not admiration.* His opinions were the right ones, naturally. To disagree with him was to brand oneself a radical.

For four long years, I labored under his system—a system that prized Latin declensions over practicality, Greek verbs over common sense. I *wanted* to learn, to become something—a statesman, a general, a great author. My ambitions shifted like the wind, but one thing remained constant: the grinding, soul-crushing weight of useless knowledge.

Why must we, the sons of shopkeepers and clerks, waste half our days wrestling with Homer and Horace? What possible good would *amo, amas, amat* do us in the real world? Doctor Florret, ever the parrot of tradition, would intone, *"Latin and Greek are the finest mental gymnastics."* Gymnastics! As if twisting a boy’s brain into knots over dead languages were the same as strengthening it.

Then came salvation—delivered, as all great mercies are, by a friend.

Dan.

Dan was everything I was not—strong, easygoing, possessed of a practical mind that cut through nonsense like a hot knife through butter. He arrived a term after me in the Lower Fourth and, within a week, was parsing Latin verses with the ease of a scholar.

One day, in a quiet corner of Regent’s Park, he revealed his secret.

*"Don’t tell the others,"* he warned, glancing around. *"But if you want to actually* understand *this rubbish instead of just suffering through it, get yourself a Key."*

A *Key*—one of those magical little books that translated the ancient tongues into plain English. Suddenly, the *Frogs* of Aristophanes became amusing, Ulysses’ wanderings an adventure rather than a torment. Doctor Florret, oblivious, praised my sudden progress.

Dan was more than just a cheat sheet for Latin. He was the anchor to my daydreaming, the voice of reason when my head was in the clouds. He was also, I realized with admiration, entirely ungovernable.

The school hierarchy meant nothing to him. He *could* have been in the Upper Fourth, but he preferred the Lower Third—specifically, the seat nearest the door, where he could slip in and out unnoticed. Teachers scolded, friends cajoled, but Dan remained unmoved.

*"The Lower Third suits me,"* he’d say with a shrug.

He rarely fought, though he could have flattened most of the school without breaking a sweat. Once, when a bully struck him lightly on the cheek—a challenge no boy of honor could ignore—Dan merely walked away.

*"Glad he didn’t insist,"* he remarked cheerfully. *"I’ve got a party tonight."*

Yet another time, when a street ruffian knocked his hat into the mud *and* kicked it, Dan thrashed him soundly.

*"I wouldn’t have minded the knock,"* he explained afterward, dusting off his bruised hat, *"but the kick was unnecessary."*

His moral compass was entirely his own. Once, on a scorching afternoon, he produced a stolen pear from his pocket and offered me a slice.

*"You* stole *it?"* I gasped.

*"Yes,"* he said, utterly unbothered. *"And so did you, last week, when you robbed that orchard at Mill Hill."*

No argument could convince him there was a distinction. To Dan, theft was theft, whether from a basket or a tree.

When exam season arrived, I confided in him my hopes—and my prayers—for the History prize.

*"You* prayed *for it?"* He frowned. *"That’s not fair."*

*"Because if you win, it won’t be because you knew the most history—it’ll be because you prayed the hardest. That’s not how prizes are supposed to work."*

I didn’t win. Dan, to everyone’s shock, *did*—for arithmetic, the only subject that held his interest. *"I like seeing things come out right,"* he explained.

My father, examining me afterward, sighed. *"You seem to know a good deal,"* he admitted, puzzled.

*"They only asked me the things I* didn’t *know,"* I muttered, near tears.

He ruffled my hair. *"Perhaps you’re like me, Paul—one of the unlucky ones."*

At the time, our fortunes seemed bright. My father spoke of moving to Guilford Street, closer to the city’s pulse, where clients would flock to him. But beneath his optimism, I sensed doubt.

*"When I married your mother,"* he confessed once, *"I was a rich man. She had everything. Now… I watch her. She’s lost faith in me."*

Yet when times were darkest, he was at his boldest—planning, scheming, dreaming. It was only in moments of ease that fear crept in.

*"Education helps a man bear adversity,"* he told me. *"With a well-stored mind, even bread and tea can be interesting."*

A lesson Dan, in his own way, had already taught me—that the world could be mastered, not by its rules, but by your own.

“Nothing’s missing,” my mother declared, scanning the room with a housekeeper’s precision. “Mark my words, she took fright and bolted.”

“But what on earth was there to frighten her?” My father paused mid-pour, decanter in one hand, bottle in the other, his brow furrowed in bewilderment.

“The sheer horror of the idea,” my mother sighed. “She’d never waited at table in her life. Last night, she was near tears just thinking about it.”

“Then what’s to be done?” My father’s voice rose in alarm. “They’ll be here within the hour!”

“They’ll have no dinner,” my mother said grimly, “unless I don an apron and serve it myself.”

“Where does she live?”

“Ilford.”

“Then we must laugh it off,” my father decided.

But my mother, sinking into a chair, burst into tears. The week had been merciless—a whirlwind of scrubbing, rearranging, and last-minute purchases. The kitchen range, as if unionized, had staged a mutiny, refusing to bake unless granted “permanently improved conditions.” Cookbooks, long neglected, had been dusted off, consulted, and promptly cursed. Experiments had ended in disaster, triumphs in anxiety. And now, on the brink of victory, the reward had been snatched away.

Downstairs, the dinner waited. Upstairs, the guests would soon assemble. And between them? A yawning chasm, bridged only by a parlour-maid who had fled to Ilford.

A knock at the door.

“Surely not already?” My father lunged for his coat.

“Barbara, likely,” my mother sniffed. “She promised to help me dress. Not that it matters now.”

But it was Barbara, swift as sunlight, kissing them all in one fluid motion. And with her came laughter, as shadows do when light enters.

“There’ll be no dinner,” my father announced cheerfully. “We’ll pretend it was yesterday. Imagine their faces!”

“There *will* be dinner,” my mother corrected, “but it’s stranded in the kitchen with no way up.”

Barbara’s face lit with inspiration. “I’ll find someone.”

Before protest could form, she had snatched the latchkey and vanished.

Silence fell, heavy as regret.

“If only there were a hotel nearby,” my father muttered.

“You’ll entertain them alone,” my mother decided. “I’ll serve.”

“Absurd!”

“Cook can’t cook *and* serve.”

“Fan?”

A look silenced him.

“Paul?”

“I’ll not turn my son into a footman.”

“Then dress, at least.”

“An apron will suffice.”

“I’d hoped to see you in that new frock.”

The words, clumsy as they were, struck true. My mother softened. “I’d looked forward to it too.”

Then—the key in the lock.

“She’s failed,” my mother sighed.

The door swung open.

“Will I do?”

There she stood, framed in the doorway, golden hair crowned with the daintiest of caps, apron crisp as new snow. Every objection withered before her.

“*You* shall be footman,” she commanded me—and this time, my mother laughed. “Wait here.” Then, to my mother: “Ready?”

It was my first glimpse of my mother in evening dress, and I confess—I was scandalized. The gleaming whiteness of her shoulders seemed improper, and I must have shown it, for she flushed and reached for a shawl.

But Barbara cried out, “A sin to hide such beauty!” And my father, with a conjurer’s deftness, swept the shawl aside and knelt, pressing my mother’s hand to his lips as though she were royalty. Barbara, behind her chair, insisted I follow suit. “The Queen is receiving,” she whispered.

And so I knelt, shyly gazing up at this radiant stranger who was my mother—and glimpsed, for the first time, the philosophy of clothes.

---

Soon, my parents’ voices would clash like cymbals—hers sharp as shattered glass, his low and sullen. Doors would slam; silence would fester. I lay awake, listening through thin walls to whispers that cut deeper than shouts.

Some cruel, unseen thing had crept into our home, slipping between them, whispering malice. I fancied I could see it—a grinning shadow, laughing as it turned love to venom.

To this day, I see it still—lurking behind every whispered sweetheart, mocking every love tale. Does it spare any of us? Or must we all pass through this valley where love’s light dims?

---

My duties as footman left little time for peeking. The guest of honor, one Mr. Teidelmann (or Tiedelmann—memory falters), was a snuffling, deaf old titan of industry, his name emblazoned on Limehouse warehouses. My mother shouted pleasantries at him; he cupped his ear and demanded repetition.

Hasluck, ever the salesman, bellowed praise for Teidelmann’s disinfectant. “My wife swears by it!”

“Our drains!” chimed Mrs. Hasluck. “You’d hardly know they existed.”

“Eh? What’s that?” Teidelmann squinted.

“Your disinfectant,” my mother translated.

“Delighted,” he mumbled, bored.

Hasluck persisted. “Reminds me of coconuts!”

“Does it?”

“Doesn’t it you?”

“Can’t say. Never use the stuff.”

A pause. Then, sotto voce: “Silly fool.”

The table froze.

Hasluck, unruffled, laughed. “He’s right. I *am* a fool—talking shop like a tradesman.”

But soon, he had Teidelmann’s ear again—this time on art. “Pearsall says you’re the best judge in Europe.”

Teidelmann chuckled. “He’d know. Tried to palm off rubbish on me often enough.”

“That last purchase made young —’s career.”

“Paid two thousand. They’ll sell for twenty.”

“But you’d never sell?” my father asked.

“No. My widow will.”

A soft laugh floated from the corner—the second Mrs. Teidelmann, a languid beauty. “Anderson’s despair is my indifference to art. He’ll pay thousands for a ragged child in a painting. Such waste.”

“Yet you’d pay thousands for a diamond,” my father countered.

“To enhance my neck.”

“An even greater waste.”

Again, that musical laugh.

“Who is she?” I whispered to Barbara as we shared a jelly on the stairs.

“The second Mrs. Teidelmann. Married him for his money. Beautiful, but I don’t like her.”

“Not as beautiful as you.”

She scoffed. “I’m just a child. No one notices me—except other children.” A rare note of discontent.

“Dr. Hal thinks you beautiful.”

Her voice brightened. “What did he say?”

“I forget exactly—but he admires you.”

“Ask him again. Properly. I’m curious.”

For no one at that dinner shone brighter than the parlour-maid.

“Wherever did you find her?” Mrs. Florret marveled.

“A neat-handed Phillis,” Dr. Florret approved.

“She’d never wait at *my* table,” laughed Mrs. Cottle, our minister’s wife.

“She’s temporary,” my mother confessed.

“Thank Mrs. Hasluck,” my father added.

“And me!” Hasluck grinned.

Later, my father absentmindedly called Barbara “my dear,” drawing a sharp glance from Mrs. Cottle. Then Hasluck pinched her elbow, eliciting a yelp. For the house’s honor, my mother explained the ruse—earning Barbara even greater admiration.

By dessert, cap still perched atop her golden head, Barbara sat among the guests, the evening’s darling.

“Kind hearts,” Dr. Florret intoned, “are more than coronets.”

Only my aunt struck a discordant note. “When I was a girl, I told myself, ‘Fanny, you must be amiable.’ And I was. For *years*.”

“Did it pay?” Hasluck asked.

“It attracted,” she said dryly, “no attention whatever.”

“What do you respectable folk know of life?” Washburn flung out. “You’re marionettes, dancing to etiquette’s strings. You weep, sin, and die by rule. Only in death do you face life—leaving others to don black and pretend grief.”

“Assume a virtue,” Dr. Florret murmured.

“Like the ass in the lion’s skin,” Washburn retorted. “Here, at least, men live—kill what they hate, die for what they love. Savages, perhaps, but not bloodless dolls.”

“I prefer the dolls,” Dr. Florret said.

“They *are* pretty,” Washburn conceded.

“Like my first masked ball,” my father mused. “Everyone identical. I fled to the street just to see faces again.”

“But didn’t they unmask at midnight?” purred Mrs. Teidelmann.

“I didn’t stay.”

“Pity. I’d have liked to see what lay beneath.”

“You might,” my father said, “have been disappointed.”

Only a truly great man would dare to utter something so blindingly obvious. Once upon a time, a modest Sunday roast was a luxury. Now, joints of meat appeared daily, and pudding became so frequent it lost all charm—another joy of life reduced to mere routine. We ate without restraint, no longer scheming how to stretch yesterday’s scraps into tomorrow’s meal. No more breaking bread with careful hands, mentally tallying crumbs for Saturday’s pudding.

Yet the old proverb failed us, for now, despite full bellies, laughter had fled the table. An unseen guest sat among us, stifling our chatter. No more teasing over half a pound of sausages or the scent of fresh herrings. Instead, we spoke in stiff, hollow voices of things that meant nothing.

Moving to Guilford Street would have been simple. Occasionally, in the lifeless tones they now used for everything but *the* subject, my parents discussed it—until some stray word would ignite the buried anger, and the months dragged on, the shadow between them deepening.

Then came the news: old Teidelmann had dropped dead in his counting house.

*"You’re going to her?"* asked my mother.

*"I’ve been sent for,"* said my father. *"It may be business."*

My mother laughed—a bitter sound I didn’t understand then—and my father stormed out. For hours, she locked herself away, and if I pressed my ear to the door, I was sure I heard crying. That she grieved so for old Teidelmann puzzled me.

After that, Mrs. Teidelmann visited more often. Mourning softened her—or seemed to. Her eyes lost some of their hardness. She was always sweet to my mother, who, beside her, seemed awkward and graceless. To me, she was unfailingly kind, arriving with books that *actually* interested me, not the dreary moral tales my mother favored, or planning treats that felt like treats, not dutiful lessons in virtue.

*"Has he ever seen a pantomime?"* she asked one morning, quirking her mouth in a way that made my heart leap.

My father raised his brows. *"What would your mother say?"*

*"She thinks theatres are wicked,"* I muttered—the first doubt I’d ever voiced about my mother’s judgments.

Mrs. Teidelmann’s smile deepened it. *"Dear me, I must be terribly wicked then. I’ve always found pantomimes quite moral. All the villains get their comeuppance so neatly. And we’d leave before the Clown stole the sausages, wouldn’t we, Paul?"*

My mother was summoned. She looked small and plain beside Mrs. Teidelmann’s rustling elegance.

*"Let him come,"* the lady coaxed. *"It’s Dick Whittington—such an uplifting tale."*

My mother stood silent, fingers twisting. *"No,"* she said at last, her voice grating. *"When he’s older, others may decide. For now, he follows me—alone."*

*"Surely there’s no harm,"* my father protested. *"Times change."*

*"Do they?"* said my mother. *"I wouldn’t know. I seem too old for this world."*

Mrs. Teidelmann yielded gracefully. *"I only thought to please him. He’s worked so hard."* Her hand lingered on my shoulder, warm and approving.

*"You’re very kind,"* said my mother. *"I would do anything for him—anything I could."* Her hand groped for mine, but I, nursing my grudge, refused to take it.

She didn’t mention it again. Yet the very next afternoon, she took me herself to a magic-lantern show and a conjurer in a drafty hall. She wore her prettiest dress, laughed brightly—but I, sulking, barely noticed the open-mouthed sleeper swallowing rats or the rabbit in the old man’s hat. All I saw was the glittering pantomime I’d been denied.

We walked home in silence through the greasy twilight. That night, half-waking, I thought I saw a white figure crouched at my bed’s foot. I cried out—and my mother rose from her knees.

*"A silly dream,"* she said, her laugh broken. *"A wicked magician stole you. I couldn’t find you. I was alone."*

Her arms tightened around me until I slept again.

Near the holidays’ end, we visited Mrs. Teidelmann at her Clapton mansion—a dull house outside, but inside, a treasure-cave. Old Teidelmann’s hoard spilled over every surface: paintings, statues, gem-bound books. Now he lay in his coffin under a famous tomb, and his daughter-in-law reclined in lace on a sofa, asleep.

We waited. The French clock ticked softly. Then—clear as a struck bell—she murmured in her sleep: *"Ah no, we must not. Luke, my darling."*

My mother rose, pale but composed. *"Go, Paul. Wait downstairs."*

Half an hour later, we left. All the way home, she walked like a ghost. At our gate, she woke. *"Never speak of this,"* she said. *"Delirium means nothing."*

From that day, she changed. No more anger, only quiet smiles. When Mrs. Teidelmann returned months later, my mother was the first to call. They became inseparable—or so it seemed. Peace returned; my father softened, my mother faded.

*"She needs air,"* said Dr. Hal. *"This city is killing her."*

So we returned to the village, climbed again to Jacob’s tower. One afternoon, my father came striding through the pines, his eyes alight—the old Viking look he wore when trouble loomed.

*"I’ve broken with Hasluck,"* he said. *"The man’s a thief."*

*"I thought you would,"* said my mother.

*"He laughed,"* said my father. *"Said that’s better than some."*

Noel Hasluck—kind to me, ruthless to the world. *"Business,"* he’d say, *"is a game of cheats. The sharpest win."* Once, I asked about the widows and orphans.

*"My dear boy,"* he chuckled, *"the last ‘widow’ who wept over her losses sold out at a profit—to her brother-in-law. Besides, I’m an orphan too."* His laugh boomed, rich with honesty. It was worth thousands to him.

But that was Hasluck’s way. And now, my father stood beside my mother on the tower, gazing at the hills where their youth had been.

*"We planned our future here,"* he said.

*"The future plans for us,"* she replied.

*"I’ve been unlucky,"* he admitted. *"Lost your money too."*

*"Fate,"* she said. *"Not you."*

*"If only that damned pump hadn’t jammed,"* he muttered.

She smiled. *"Remember Mrs. Tharand? ‘Of all troubles, money’s easiest to bear.’"*

*"I wish I believed that,"* he said.

The silence stretched. Then he laughed. *"Well, we’ll manage."*

But my mother was already far away, listening to echoes only she could hear.

The eighteen months that followed—though shorter than anticipated—were perhaps the most tenderly joyful my father and mother had ever known. Not "happy" in the careless sense, but rich with a quiet, deepening love, as though Death had whispered, *Not yet. A little longer together.*

In those final days, pretense fell away. Petty concerns—keeping up appearances, fretting over neighbors’ opinions—vanished. What remained was pure and simple: their love for one another, their comfort in each other’s presence. We were poor, yes, but no longer straining under the weight of false pride.

Their hearts grew larger, their kindness wider. Even Aunt Fan, that bastion of prickliness, made a second attempt at amiability—an experiment she announced with characteristic bluntness.

"I'm a beast of an old woman," she declared one afternoon.

"Don’t say that, Fan," my mother protested.

"Why not? It’s true," Aunt Fan snapped. "People think I’m disagreeable."

"It’s your manner," my mother explained gently. "You don’t *mean* to be."

"Stuff and nonsense. I *like* being disagreeable. Watching people squirm—now *that’s* entertainment."

Yet, to everyone’s astonishment, Aunt Fan persisted in her newfound pleasantness—though her efforts often landed closer to sarcasm than sincerity.

"Your father’s stories!" she’d exclaim. "What wit! Such effortless humor!"

Or to my mother, admiring a retrimmed bonnet: "A *girl* in that! No, a saucy young thing—not a married lady with a son nearly grown!"

Her enthusiasm was so relentless it bordered on parody.

Meanwhile, a new figure entered our lives: Mr. James Wellington Gadley (or rather, Wellington James Gadley, though he’d long since reversed the order to spare himself Napoleonic comparisons). A portly, earnest solicitor’s clerk, he carried himself with the gravity of a man who’d witnessed the clandestine dramas of England’s elite—though he spoke only in veiled allusions to "a certain Lady X."

Aunt Fan, to everyone’s surprise, adored his stories. Where others nodded politely, she interjected with dark theories of hidden motives.

"Why did the husband return after eighteen years?" mused Gadley.

"To murder his wife—slow poison," Aunt Fan declared.

"Murder her? But why?"

"To marry the other woman, of course."

Gadley blinked. "How extraordinary! Though, in fact, the wife *did* die shortly after, and he *did* remarry..."

"Told you," said Aunt Fan.

Their debates became legendary. Gadley, enthralled by her macabre imagination, would lean closer—until Aunt Fan, wielding her darning needles like a knight’s lance, kept him at a safe distance.

Meanwhile, Amy, our cheerfully pragmatic maid, breezed through engagements with the ease of one changing gloves.

"Married in the spring? Oh, *that* one!" she’d say, waving away suitors like yesterday’s news. "Changed my mind. We can’t help our feelings, can we?"

And Barbara—bright, laughing Barbara—brought lightness where there might have been shadow. She coaxed my father into investments with the same ease she coaxed him into theater seats.

"Argentinos are rising!" she’d say.

"Are they?" my father would murmur. "I had a feeling about Argentinos..."

And so, on a close summer evening, we found ourselves waiting for the omnibus—my father, my mother, and I—bound for *A Voice from the Grave, or The Power of Love* (a drama in five acts and thirteen tableaux).

"Let’s ride on top," my father said, grinning.

My mother hesitated only a moment. "Very well. If we’re to be wicked, we may as well do it properly."

And with that, we climbed aboard—ready, for once, to let joy lead the way.

Where have you gone, loves of my youth? Tubby, with his golden mop of hair; Langley, whose nose bore the dents of many playground battles; Shamus, stubborn as a mule, easy to knock down but impossible to make surrender; scrawny Neal and roly-poly Dicky; Ballett, whose eyes always seemed on the verge of tears; Beau Bunnie, the dandy of our ranks, who insisted on fighting in black kid gloves—where do your ghosts linger now? If someone had told me then that a day would come when I’d never see your grins again, never hear your raucous shouts, never feel the sticky grip of your ink-stained fingers—never fight you, never love you—could I have borne it?

Once, not long ago, I think I saw you, Tubby. You, with whom I’d braved the Arctic wastes of Regent’s Park and traced the Nile to its secret source. (Do you remember our hidden camp by the canal, where we feasted on roasted elephant tongue—or was it just jam tarts?—while the distant roars of lions and growls of polar bears filled the air until precisely half-past four? Then we’d grab our rifles—wooden sticks, really—and stalk through the jungle until the zoo’s spiked fence stopped us dead.) It must have been you, despite the sideburns and the thinning, greying hair. You were hurrying down Throgmorton Street, chained to a little black bag. I would have stopped you, but I had a train to catch and a shave to squeeze in. Did you recognize me? You looked at me a little too long.

And Shamus, the tender-hearted warrior who once fought for half an hour to save a frog from dissection—they tell me you’re an Income Tax assessor now, a man famed for his skepticism, even among the taxmen. May Providence guide you to audit other districts, not mine.

Time, Nature’s tidy housemaid, sweeps through the rooms of life, smoothing over the scars, turning wreckage into compost, patching up the bark where lovers carved their names.

At first, I was the outcast of the school, and the sting of it was sharper than any grown man’s loneliness. Later, we learn to lie to ourselves, to believe that we’ve always been liked, more or less. But a child has no such comfort—the truth is naked and cruel. I was shunned, and I knew it.

"Can you run?" The question came from the Lower Fourth’s unofficial king, a lanky boy with a nose like a parrot’s beak and the air of a born dictator. (History would forget him as a mere clerk, but to us, he was Napoleon in short trousers.)

"Yes," I said. Running was my one talent.

"Prove it. Twice around the playground, full speed."

I clenched my fists and bolted. The mere fact that he’d spoken to me—me, the class pariah—was enough to make me sprint like a hare. When I skidded to a halt before him, gasping, he looked satisfied.

"Why don’t the others like you?"

If only I could have stepped out of my shyness, spoken the truth! *Oh, mighty chieftain of the Lower Fourth, you who command loyalty without effort, tell me your secret! How do I win their laughter, their arms around my shoulders? Why do they fall silent when I approach? Why am I the butt of their jokes, never the joker?*

But all I muttered was, "Some of them do."

"Liar," he said flatly. "None of them do."

He was right. I hung my head.

"Here’s your chance," he announced, like a lord bestowing a favor. "We’re starting hare and hounds next Saturday. You can be a hare. Don’t tell anyone—just show up. But you’ll have to run like the devil."

He strode off, leaving me stunned. This was my moment—the politician’s first applause, the actor’s first callback, the soldier’s first medal.

I kept my mouth shut at school but exploded the news at home. "I’m to be a hare! Only two are chosen, and they picked me! I need a jersey—blue and white stripes—and proper running shoes!"

We bought them that very day, lest the last jersey in London vanish. That evening, I paraded before the mirror, leaped over chairs, and practiced perilous stair-descending techniques. "The honor of the Lower Fourth is at stake!" I declared.

Saturday arrived. The others gathered at the Spaniards Inn, barely glancing at me. I stood apart, hugging my secret. Then the Duke—our leader—arrived.

"But we’ve only got one hare," someone protested.

"We’ve got two," the Duke said. "Kelver’s the other."

Silence. Then the mutters began.

"We don’t want him. He’s a muff."

"Let him run home."

Laughter. My hands, halfway through unbuttoning my coat, fell limp.

"I don’t want to come," I mumbled, "if they don’t want me."

"Fine. Stay," snapped the Duke, impatient as ever with hesitation.

And just like that, my moment crumbled. I slunk off, tears burning, while another boy snatched my glory.

For weeks after, I ran alone, pretending. I scattered paper like a real hare, dodged imaginary hounds, and returned home spinning tales of triumph.

Popularity came later—not as I’d dreamed, earned by heroic deeds, but as a jester’s cap. "You’re funny," they said. And so I became the clown, trading dignity for laughter, spinning jokes like a wind-up toy.

Even Wakeham, the boy with the ear-splitting whistle, envied me. "Teach me to be funny," he begged. In exchange, I tried to learn his vulgar art. Neither of us succeeded.

"I suppose humor’s something you’re born with," I sighed.

"Or maybe your mouth’s the wrong shape for whistling," he offered kindly.

And so we gave up, each returning to his own small fame—he to his noise, I to my laughter, neither quite what we’d hoped for.

Ah, the dreams of youth! How grand they seem, how comical in hindsight. Poor little Paul, racing after shadows, never quite catching them.

My father died, rather ironically, on the morning of his birthday. We hadn’t expected the end so soon, and at first, we didn’t even realize it had come.

“He’s still asleep,” my mother whispered, tiptoeing out in her dressing gown. “Washburn gave him something last night. Best not disturb him.”

So we sat around the breakfast table, murmuring like conspirators in a flimsy house where every whisper carried through the walls. Later, my mother crept upstairs, and I followed, peering cautiously into the darkened room.

The blinds were drawn, the air heavy. My mother stood listening, ear pressed to the door, as if waiting for some sign. Outside, a costermonger’s shrill cry pierced the quiet—“Watercresses, fresh watercresses!”—while farther off, a hoarse youth warbled the dismal anthem of the milkman.

Slowly, the door creaked wider, and we slipped inside. He lay still, eyes closed, lips slightly parted. I had never seen death before, and it didn’t strike me at first. All I saw was how young he looked—younger than I’d ever seen him. Only gradually did it dawn on me that he was gone. For weeks afterward, I’d catch myself turning at the sound of footsteps in the street, half-expecting to see him. But at first, I felt no grief.

To my mother, it was merely a brief separation. Her faith was unshaken, a placid certainty. He was waiting for her. In God’s time, they’d meet again. No need for sorrow. Without him, the days dragged, the house a little duller—as any home would be without its good man. That was all. She spoke of him always with a smile, recalling his quirks, his little follies we loved, never as a memory, but as someone just ahead, beckoning.

We stayed on in the old house, though we often talked of moving. The great brick monster of London had crept closer, devouring fields and cottages, swallowing every patch of green. Highgate, Hampstead, Hornsey—we’d wander out to these leafy suburbs, now themselves vanishing into the sprawl of streets. But somehow, we never left.

“We must think it over,” my mother would say. “No hurry. I’d be sorry to leave Poplar, in a way.”

“Why?” I’d ask.

“Oh, no reason. Only we’ve lived here so long.”

The clinging vine wraps itself around the nearest support, be it castle wall or pigsty. We root ourselves in the familiar, even when it’s dreary. Dying Sir Walter Scott raced home to Scotland, craving its damp mists. An old woman I knew, hauled from her slum attic to the seaside, pined for the reek and clamor she called home. Who understands us but the One who made us?

So the old house held us—until, one by one, my aunt and mother passed, and I stood in the empty shell, saying goodbye to Amy amid the echoes.

My aunt died as she lived: grumbling.

“You’ll be glad to be rid of me,” she announced, dropping all pretense. “Can’t say I’ll miss it much either. Life’s not been what I’d call entertaining.”

Poor soul. That was two weeks before the end. Had she known, she might have softened.

“Don’t talk nonsense,” my mother scolded.

“What’s the use of lying?” snapped my aunt. “Got to die sometime. May as well be now, when the house is in order. It’s not as if I’m enjoying myself.”

“We do our best for you,” my mother said.

“I know you do,” my aunt conceded. “I’m a burden. Always have been.”

“Not a burden,” my mother corrected.

“What else d’you call it?” my aunt shot back. “Been a trial to everyone. That’s my purpose in life.”

My mother kissed her. “We’d miss you.”

“Hope so!” cackled my aunt. “Only legacy I’ve got worth leaving.”

We didn’t mourn her deeply, though we missed her—her sharp tongue, her corners that kept life from being too smooth. The world needs all sorts, even the prickly.

As for Amy, she genuinely grieved.

“Never know what’s good for you till it’s gone,” she sighed.

“You liked her?” my mother asked, surprised.

“Course,” said Amy. “I’m one of a big family. A bit of a row now and then keeps things lively. I’ll lose my edge if things stay too quiet.”

“You’ll be married soon,” my mother reminded her.

Amy shook her head. “Men are poor substitutes for a good argument. Just when you’re warming up, they put their hat on and slope off.”

After my aunt’s death, it was just my mother and me. Barbara was abroad, “finishing” her education (or, as old Hasluck put it, “learning the tricks of the nobs”). I’d left school and started at Stillwood & Co., studying law without pay—a privilege, according to old Gadley.

“You’re in luck, my boy,” he said. “Starting at Stillwood, Waterhead & Royal stamps you. Opens every door.”

Mr. Stillwood himself was ancient, frail, and mysteriously still working.

“I mean to retire,” he confided once. “When your father joined us, he told me frankly he hadn’t long. ‘Mr. Kelver,’ I said, ‘stay as long as you like. There are matters here I’d like cleared up. Once they’re settled, I’ll retire.’ Yet here I linger. Like an old hackney horse—if you took the shafts away, I’d collapse.”

He’d married late—a coarse woman much younger, trailing needy relatives. No wonder he preferred the office. We saw her occasionally: a blustering “manager” who mistook noise for efficiency. Ashamed of her roots, she aped aristocracy, succeeding only in resembling a barmaid on her best behavior.

Old Gadley alone dared mock her. He’d known her when.

“It’s not fair to the girls,” she once complained. “What gentleman marries an attorney’s daughter? He should’ve retired, gone into Parliament.”

“Like your father planned,” Gadley shot back, “when the coal business was rocky.”

She called him a “low beast” in her grandest tones and swept out.

Mr. Stillwood himself had no great love for the law.

“I’m not sure you’ve chosen wisely, Kelver,” he mused once. “The law makes you see everyone as a rogue. When people behave, we hear nothing. All we get is trickery, hypocrisy. It sours you. Sometimes I wonder if it’s contagious.”

“Not in your case, sir,” I said. The firm’s reputation was spotless.

“I hope not,” he sighed. “Yet sometimes I suspect myself—wonder if I’m a scamp without knowing it. A rogue never thinks himself one.”

The words briefly alarmed me—my mother had entrusted our small savings to him. But seeing his twinkling eyes, my fear vanished.

He laughed. “Suspect yourself, Kelver. No one can harm you as much as you.”

Washburn became a fixture. “Hal,” we called him now. He’d slipped past my mother’s shyness, becoming family—she treating him like a wayward boy, I like an elder brother.

“You rest me, Mrs. Kelver,” he’d say, sinking into his chair. “Your voice, your quiet eyes—they soothe.”

“A man should test his wisdom,” he’d muse, pipe smoke curling, “by two things: a good woman’s face and a child’s ear—sorry, a young man’s. A woman’s face is sunlight. Under gaslight, who can tell diamond from glass? Bring it to the sun. The children—they’re the soil. Is what we give them chaff or seed? Wait and see. I shower thoughts on Paul. Some he forgets—rubbish. Others take root. Ah, those were alive.”

“You should marry, Hal,” my mother urged.

“I would,” he grinned, “if I could wed six women. I’d neglect one for five days, smother her the sixth. I’m not one man, Mrs. Kelver—I’m six. One worships a queen, groveling; another wants a comrade; a third—”

“You’ll find one woman who’s all of them,” she interrupted.

“A man is many, a woman one,” he countered.

“Nonsense,” said my mother. “Every true woman contains all women. To know her, love her with all loves.”

Sometimes we talked religion. My mother’s faith was alive, unafraid of air.

“What are we?” Hal once burst out. “Spiders are kinder. Pigs cleaner. Tigers less cruel. We’re savages in coats.”

“But Sodom would’ve been spared for ten just men,” my mother reminded.

“Should’ve whisked the ten out and buried the rest,” Hal grumbled.

“We’ll be purified,” she said. “The evil washed away.”

“Why make us ill to mend us? If He wanted us decent, why not start that way?” Hal was fresh from a Poor Law meeting, disgusted with humanity.

“It’s His way,” she said. “The gem’s hidden in clay. You see heroism every day—self-sacrifice, love stronger than death. Will He waste that?”

Often, I’d join Hal on his rounds. The slums saw him as both priest and tyrant—serving them day and night, bullying them just as hard. He earned little but wielded power, and that kept him there.

“Nature forgot me for two thousand years,” he joked. “Born earlier, I’d have been a warlord. Here, I’m king by brain and fist.”

Justice was his passion. Oppressors feared him. If the law failed, he didn’t hesitate to arrange his own.

One night, he insisted a sick child go to the Infirmary at once.

“They’ll just shout at me,” the mother protested.

“Tally-ho!” cried Hal, scooping up the child. Off we marched, Hal singing raucously, the baby wide-eyed.

At the Workhouse, he made her ring the bell—modestly.

“What d’ye mean, this hour?” snarled the porter.

Hal erupted. The gate flew open. Inside, he turned the place upside down, bullying attendants, rearranging wards, thoroughly enjoying himself.

Two hours later, he emerged, grinning. “Sport!”

Justice, Hal-style.

Sometimes, in a fit of nostalgia or sheer absurdity, I find myself knocking on the door of a certain lodging house—the kind that announces, with a touch of tragic optimism, *“Room to let for a single gentleman.”* The door is invariably opened by the “slavey,” a creature seemingly preserved in the amber of perpetual bewilderment. In the morning, she is a vision of disheveled domesticity, arms tucked beneath her apron like a reluctant martyr. By afternoon, she has metamorphosed into something resembling a half-hearted maid, her cap and apron serving as a flimsy disguise for the chaos beneath.

I know her well—the round, vacant eyes, the ever-parted lips, the hair that appears to have been styled by a gust of wind. I must resist the urge to mutter, *“Terribly sorry, forgot my key,”* and stride past her as if I belonged. Instead, I ask, with exaggerated politeness, *“What rooms have you available?”* She scuttles off like a startled beetle to summon the landlady, a woman of faded gentility, clad in respectable black, who eyes me with the weary skepticism of one who has seen too many hopeful young men come and go.

We perform the usual ballet of squeezing past the hat stand—a feat requiring the precision of a contortionist—before inspecting the offerings. The first-floor room is an exercise in delusion: a marble-topped sideboard (three feet by two, if you’re generous), a loo-table draped in green to match the curtains (though *match* is a generous term), and a lamp that must never be moved lest it revolt and set the place ablaze. The pièce de résistance is the paper fire-stove ornament, which lends the room an air of tragic respectability, as if to say, *“Yes, we are poor, but we are moral.”*

The bed, tucked behind the door, is the room’s dirty secret. If you face the window, you can almost forget it exists—until visitors arrive, at which point it becomes the most popular seat in the house. No one wants the “easy” chair, a fiendish contraption of coiled steel and horsehair that rejects all attempts at comfort, ejecting sitters with the disdain of a bouncer at a high-class club.

*“And the rent?”* I inquire.

She stiffens. *“The usual size, I believe.”*

Ah, yes. The *lodging-house* scuttle—a unit of measurement known only to landladies.

The third-floor back is a masterpiece of minimalist despair. Here, the bed is not so much a piece of furniture as a domineering presence, refusing to be ignored. Its rival is the washstand, which glares from its corner like a disapproving aunt. The remaining furnishings consist of two chairs (which make no pretenses about comfort), a chest of drawers that might collapse under the weight of a thought, and a table so spindly it seems to be apologizing for existing. The sole decoration is a framed funeral card, suspended by beer corks—a cheerful reminder of life’s fleeting nature.

The rent here is a modest three-and-six a week, though one must question why the kitchen fire is half the price of the first floor’s. *“Well, the first floor tends to require more cooking,”* the landlady explains.

Of course. The third-floor gentleman’s culinary ambitions likely extend to a breakfast of toast so thin it’s translucent, a lunch consumed furtively from a paper bag in a deserted square (saveloys at twopence, meat pies likewise), and a tea featuring an egg or a haddock fried in the same pan as yesterday’s chop—because grilled is a luxury, and variety is merely the art of closing one’s eyes and pretending.

I promise to discuss the matter with my *“young friend”* (a fiction she accepts with the resignation of one accustomed to the “wandering Christian”), slip a shilling into the slavey’s limp hand, and escape into the street, where the cry of *“Muffins!”* sounds like a lament for lost dreams.

On evenings when the lamp is lit and the fire casts flickering illusions of coziness over the dingy walls, I pay visits—not in person, but in spirit. Through the thin partitions of memory, I see my fellow lodgers of yore: the clerks, the shopmen, the hopefuls in paper collars that began the week starched and proud but by Tuesday bore the stains of struggle. We wore bell-bottomed trousers, tied our cravats with horseshoe pins, and fancied ourselves dashing.

Ah, the dreams of those days! The Saturday nights at the theater, squeezing into the gallery with the fervor of devotees. The extravagant dinners in Soho—one-and-six, *wine included*—where we banqueted like kings and imagined ourselves connoisseurs. The photographs of actresses on our mantels, transforming our grim little rooms into shrines of adoration.

And the toil—the endless, hopeful toil. The Birkbeck lectures, the dog-eared copies of *Self-Help*, the dogged belief that one day, somehow, we would rise. Some of us did. Some of us didn’t.

But in those rooms, for a time, we were young, and the world was still a thing to be conquered.

My first real foray into employment was answering an advertisement from *Lott & Co., Indian Commission Agents*—a firm whose office resembled a poorly stocked general store more than a place of business. Jam, pickles, boots, soap, guns, and stationery fought for space in a room that smelled of damp and defeat.

The clerk, one Sylvanus Minikin, was a creature of startling proportions: a head so large it seemed a miracle his spindly body could support it. He regarded me with a glass eye that never blinked.

*“You’re here for the job?”* he asked.

*“I have an appointment,”* I said, producing my letter.

But I stayed, because I had to. And when Mr. Lott arrived—a man whose temper was as stable as gunpowder—I learned that employment, like lodging, was often a choice between bad and worse.

Yet in those days, even the worst had its humor. And humor, when you’re young and hungry, is sometimes the only thing that keeps you going.

Time, that relentless healer, even mends the self-inflicted wounds of shy young men doomed by nature to solitude. Winter evenings arrived, and with them, work—a fine drowning pool for private sorrows. My "apartment," now warmed by a handful of fire and lit by two candles, became almost inviting. I stocked up on paper, pens, and ink. What more can a writer do, after all? He’s merely the would-be vessel—will the spirits of inspiration use him or toss him aside?

London sprawled before me, a city of a million characters, each with their own dramas, mysteries, tragedies, and jokes. The raw material was everywhere, begging only for observation and a halfway truthful report. Yet the notion of writing about what I actually *knew* never crossed my mind. No, my tales were of rosy-cheeked milkmaids and sturdy yokels. My scenes played out in windmills, on misty moors, or in crumbling granges. I suspect this phase of artistic delusion is common among young scribblers.

A gentle melancholy draped my stories. Sentiment was all the rage back then, and like any novice, I followed fashion slavishly. To be a heroine in one of my tales was, more often than not, a death sentence. No matter how vigorously her curls bounced, how merrily her laughter rang, or how fawn-like her agility, she was doomed to an early grave. Snowdrops and primroses (botany hastily skimmed from *Our Garden Favourites, Illustrated*) sprouted around her tombstone as if in a hothouse. If the setting was coastal, the sea breeze would, with suspicious enthusiasm, make her churchyard its favorite playground. Years later, a white-haired man would visit, leading small children by the hand, and regale them with the dismal tale—a thoroughly depressing bedtime story.

Occasionally, for variety, I’d kill off the gentleman instead. His final soliloquy would murmur something about it being "better this way." Snowdrops were beyond his reach, of course—his grave usually lay in some godforsaken patch of African veldt or scorching desert. Describing such exotic locales required trips to the British Museum reading room, where I’d squint at maps and hope no one noticed my frantic note-taking.

Pitiful little fledglings, these stories of mine! And yet, no matter how often I shoved them from the nest, they fluttered back to me—creased, coffee-stained, and smelling of rejection. Only one person ever admired them, wept over them: myself.

I tried every strategy. Sometimes I’d send them off with a curt note: *Take it or leave it.* Other times, I’d attach a groveling plea for consideration. Occasionally, I’d name a price—five guineas, cheque crossed—or dangle the bait of a free sample (*first contribution gratis!*). Once, in a fit of sarcasm, I preemptively included my own rejection letter, penned in brutal mock-editorial scrawl. Another time, I played the humble novice, begging for criticism.

Not a single one found a home. A month, a week, sometimes mere days later, they’d return. I never lost a manuscript. I *wished* I had—just for variety.

I despised the little housemaid who’d burst in, joyfully waving my returned envelopes between thumb and apron-covered finger. Her chronic sniff struck me as contemptuous. If I intercepted the postman myself, I swore he smirked. Tearing open the envelopes, I’d curse the stories, call them trash, sometimes fling them into the fire—only to snatch them out half-scorched, smooth their pages, and reread them. The editors couldn’t *possibly* have seen them. No, some jealous underling must have intercepted them. Or they’d arrived at the wrong moment. Their triumph was coming. Rewriting the first and last pages, I’d send them out again, hope rekindled.

Meanwhile, recognizing that a would-be literary warrior must also shine in society, I set about polishing my social graces. Smoking and drinking proved the hardest hurdles. Not out of moral superiority—my body simply rebelled. At school, I’d occasionally attempted a cigarette, only to turn green and abandon the effort, cloaking my failure in sanctimonious mutterings about *bad for growing boys*. Deep down, I knew the truth: I was a coward hiding behind virtue’s skirts.

Likewise, I’d sipped my mother’s claret and wondered why anyone voluntarily consumed such stuff. Watching grown men wince at it had been reassuring—clearly, a taste for liquor wasn’t innate. But now, a gentleman adrift in the world, my education could no longer be postponed.

Fashion then demanded that artists—especially literary ones—smoke and drink to excess. A teetotal genius would’ve been dismissed as a fraud. I might not yet be sure of the *genius* part, but the smoking and drinking? Those, at least, I could fake.

I approached the task methodically. Smoking was best practiced on Saturday nights, leaving Sunday to walk off the effects. Persistence paid off: I learned to puff a cigarette convincingly. But mediocrity wasn’t enough. The cigarette conquered, I advanced to cigars. My first remains etched in memory—as does every man’s. It was at a smoking concert in the Islington Drill Hall, courtesy of Minikin. Dizziness struck. Unsure whether the cigar or the stifling room was to blame, I slipped outside into a courtyard. A low wall divided it from a neighboring garden. The cigar was clearly the culprit. My instinct was to fling it away, but duty demanded perseverance.

Climbing onto the wall, I resolved to walk its length. Success would prove my mastery; failure would excuse me from further torment. Two yards in, I found myself draped over the wall like a discarded coat, legs dangling one side, head lolling the other. Inclination whispered vows of eternal abstinence. Pride hissed back: *What others can do, so can you. Victory comes through suffering.*

Liquor proved even harder. Whiskey tasted like hair oil (which, once accidentally swallowed, I’d found *less* revolting). Twice a week, I forced down a glass of beer, standing over myself like a stern parent until the last bitter drop was gone. As reward, I’d suck on chocolates, assuring myself that one day I’d *enjoy* this ordeal and thank my past self for the discipline.

In saner pursuits, I made better progress. Shyness ebbed—slowly. The trick, I found, was not to fight it but to laugh at it. The world’s indifference sanded down my sharper edges. People became less terrifying; I became less terrified of them.

Fate, in guiding me to this particular house in Nelson Square, had done me a favor. We were a curious menagerie under its leaky roof. Mrs. Peedles, our landlady (who shared the basement with the slavey), had once trod the boards with Charles Keane’s company at the old Princess’s Theatre. True, London had never fully appreciated her—actor-managers with wives were *complicated*, she hinted. But in the provinces? Ah, there she’d been Juliet, Amy Robsart, Mrs. Haller in *The Stranger*—the whole grand parade of *legitimate* heroines.

Doubters were silenced with yellowed handbills: *"On Tuesday Evening!!! By Special Desire!!! Blessington’s Theatre!!!"* If pressed, she’d burst into Lady Macbeth’s sleepwalking scene or Ophelia’s mad ravings.

Now, she was a faded, wheezing little woman, prone to sudden descents onto the nearest chair for tearful recitals of her woes. Unaware of this habit, she fancied herself unusually cheerful.

*"But there, fretting’s no good. We must grin and bear things in this world,"* she’d declare, wiping her eyes. *"Better to laugh than cry, I always say."* And then, to prove it, she’d laugh—a sound like a door hinge in need of oil.

Years of stair-climbing had left her perpetually breathless. *"You don’t know ’ow to breathe,"* our second-floor front—a kindly soul—once told her. *"You gargle it. Swallow it proper, like it’s medicine."* He’d pressed a handkerchief to her mouth, but to no avail. *"There ain’t room inside me,"* she’d wheezed.

Bells ruled her life. Conversation was filler between their rings.

One Christmas, a whiskey bottle fell into my hands—a gift from a commercial traveler. Not liking the stuff myself, I reserved it for Mrs. Peedles’ moments of collapse. Her face would brighten. *"Ah, well, I don’t mind if I do. It’s a poor heart that never rejoices."* Thus fortified, she’d recount tales of my predecessors—young men who’d occupied my room before meeting grim fates. One, a poet, now languished in Portland for forgery. Another, a brilliant scholar, had ended in Hanwell Lunatic Asylum.

*"Always the deserving ones,"* she’d sigh. *"I don’t know why."*

I was relieved when that whiskey bottle emptied. A second might’ve driven me to suicide.

Mr. Peedles existed—just not for Mrs. Peedles. He’d been "general utility" at the Princess’s—a fine figure of a man, easily led astray (especially by minxes). Mrs. Peedles spoke of "general utilities" with bitterness.

On weekdays, she wore a single black dress and cap. But Sundays transformed her. She’d preserved her stage wardrobe—Hermia’s flowing robes, Lady Teazle’s brocade, even paste shoes and tinsel jewels. Beneath a mackintosh, she’d sneak to visit fellow has-beens, her cheeks flushed with excitement, her eyebrows (ordinarily faint) suddenly bold. Only her thin grey hair and work-worn hands remained for identification.

Next door lived a law-writer and his wife—old, poor, and worn down by relentless bad luck. Illness, debt, a friend’s betrayal—their history was a dull recital of misfortune without moral or meaning. Now they worked listlessly, indifferent to fate, their sole luxury an occasional glass of beer. Nights, I’d hear his pen scratching through the thin wall.

He became my nightmare vision of the future. I’d tiptoe past his door, dreading encounters. Hadn’t *he* once dreamed? Might I, too, end a shabby clerk in a four-room Brixton house, with a fading wife and squalling children?

Our second-floor front was Jarman—Young London incarnate. Noisy, brash, generous, cunning, and utterly shameless. He woke like an explosion, bathed like a whale, and whistled through breakfast. His comings and goings were a symphony of slammed doors and shouted meal instructions. His absence left the house eerily silent.

Sometimes he’d march into my room with a supper tray, ignoring hints that I was busy. *"You fire away, Shakespeare Redivivus,"* he’d say. *"I’ll be quiet."* His idea of quiet was muttering melodramas in the corner: *"Ah, I ’ave killed ’er father! Stolen ’er fortune! Yet she loves me not!"*

Finding work impossible, I’d listen instead. His chief topic? Love affairs—numerous and disastrous. *"Girls,"* he’d declare, *"are like shop goods. Bright and shiny till you buy ’em, then the paint peels. Not that there ain’t good ’uns—but you’ve got to look past the advertising."*

What he sought was *"a first-class article,"* not something that would crumble at the first wear. *"They’re out there,"* he’d say cheerfully. *"Just not the ones shouting about it."*

Sunlight stabbed through my window like an overeager houseguest, announcing that the day had begun without consulting me. I sat up, squinting at the empty space where my watch usually resided on the wobbly dressing table. My clothes lay scattered across the floor like casualties of war. One boot had claimed the chair by the fireplace as its throne; the other had vanished entirely, possibly staging a rebellion.

My head, meanwhile, had apparently expanded overnight to twice its usual size. For a delirious moment, I wondered if I’d accidentally swapped skulls with Minikin. If so, I wanted mine back. This borrowed model ached like it had been used as a bowling ball.

Then last night’s memories crashed into me like a drunken uncle at a wedding. Church bells chimed nearby. I counted carefully. Eleven. I leapt from bed—or rather, I attempted to. My legs had other plans. I sat down hard on the floor.

The room, which I dimly recalled waltzing with me in a spirited but uncoordinated manner last night, was still swaying gently, as if recovering from its own hangover. I crawled to the washstand, missed the basin twice, and finally dunked my head in like a man trying to drown his regrets.

Clutching the easy-chair like a lifeline, I took stock of my situation, working backward from the present like a detective who already knows he’s the culprit.

**Fact One:** I felt like death warmed over.
**Fact Two:** Something had disagreed with me violently.

*Ah yes,* I thought, *that cigar. Clearly the culprit. And the stuffy room. And overwork. Definitely not the three bottles of champagne. No, no—it was the cigar.*

Then my own brain, unsympathetic and relentless, cut through the delusion: *You were drunk, you idiot.*

I winced. *Not drunk. That’s such a crude word. Some people can’t tolerate sweet champagne. It’s a liver thing.*

*Hopelessly, vulgarly drunk,* my conscience shot back. *Like an ‘Arry on Bank Holiday.*

*It was my first time!* I protested weakly.

*And your first opportunity,* came the merciless reply. *How old are you?*

*Old enough to know better. What if this is the start of a downward spiral? What if you end up a confirmed sot, staggering home to frighten small children?*

Temperance pamphlets I’d skimmed on dull Sundays flashed before my eyes. I saw my future self—haggard, red-nosed, begging coins outside pubs. My father’s disappointment, my mother’s tears. Barbara’s scorn. Hal’s withering contempt. Shame and terror gripped me.

*Never again!* I vowed aloud, with the dramatic fervor only a repentant teenager can muster. *I’ll leave this place! Wipe the slate clean! Start anew! I’ll—*

Then, like a specter at the feast, Miss Rosina Sellars materialized in my mind’s eye.

*What’s she doing here?* I demanded of myself.

*Your affianced bride,* my brain reminded me with a smirk.

*Exactly. And now you’re stuck with her. Two cramped rooms. Her family. A lifetime of listening to her drop every ‘h’ this side of the Thames. How’s that for a bright future, Mr. Ambition?*

I groaned. *What do I do?*

*No idea,* my inner voice admitted cheerfully.

---

The day passed in a haze of self-loathing. Too ashamed to face Mrs. Peedles, I barricaded myself in my room. At dusk, I slunk out for a penitent meal of unsweetened tea and a kippered herring at a nearby coffee-house. When another patron ordered buttered toast, the smell alone sent me fleeing.

At eight o’clock, Minikin arrived, took one look at me, and said, *Thought as much. Looks like you had an A1 night of it.*

*I’m glad it happened,* I lied. *A lesson I’ll never forget.*

*Nothing like a proper drunk for making you feel virtuous,* Minikin agreed. *Better than a sermon.*

Desperate for advice, I confessed the full extent of my folly.

*Engaged?* Minikin blinked. *You quiet ones go hard when you do go. Nice girl?*

Minikin polished his glass eye thoughtfully. *Artful type?*

Minikin glanced around. *Must be fond of you.*

I nodded miserably.

*Let her wait,* Minikin advised. *Plenty of time for disaster later.*

Just then, Jarman barged in without knocking. He shook my hand solemnly. *Friend of yours?* he asked, eyeing Minikin.

After introductions:

*Proud to meet you,* said Jarman.

*Glad to hear it,* said Minikin. *Don’t look like you’ve got much else to be proud about.*

Jarman, instead of taking offense, grinned. *Wonderful gift, that. Ever think of hiring yourself out to ruin parties?*

Thus began a lengthy debate between the two on how to extract me from my predicament, treating me as though I were an inanimate object with poor judgment.

*She’s a hundred and sixty pounds of determination,* Jarman explained. *Bit much for our Paul here.*

*Some chaps have all the luck,* Minikin deadpanned.

After much deliberation, Jarman slapped his knee. *The stage!*

*Can do anything that doesn’t require sense,* Jarman declared. *No questions, no character needed. Sanctuary!*

*Sing something,* he demanded.

I croaked out *Sally in Our Alley,* every mention of the name conjuring Miss Sellars’ formidable silhouette.

*Not grand opera material,* Jarman mused, *but you’ll do for a chorus where cheap is the main requirement.*

Their plan: I would appeal to the O’Kelly for help breaking into theater. *Lose the girl in the footlights,* Jarman advised.

---

The next evening, alone in my room, gloom set in. The empty parlor below yawned like a tomb. Even the scratch of my neighbor’s pen sounded accusatory.

Then—a knock. A pink, scented note.

*Dr mr. Paul—I herd you was took hill. You must not work so hard or I shall be cros. Come for a wark if you are good. With much love. ROSIE.*

Against all reason, my pulse quickened. My first love letter. For a fleeting moment, Miss Sellars’ face softened in my mind’s eye, her arms outstretched.

I dressed in a fever, then hesitated at her door.

*Come in, dear!* she trilled.

Inside, the illusion shattered. Powder dusted her cheeks like flour on a loaf. Her dress crinkled like gift wrap. A wart on her hand held my horrified gaze.

*You’re a naughty boy,* she scolded. *Why didn’t you visit me?*

She offered her cheek. I pecked it, tasting stale pastry.

As we walked, she narrated her entire life story—her *refined* instincts, her *shameful* lineage (a waiter father!), her determination to marry *a gentleman* (me, apparently).

*I could never abide anything common,* she declared, while I prayed for a tram to intervene.

By the time we reached Newington Butts, I knew two things:

1. I was trapped.
2. The stage was my only hope.

“Don’t be nervous,” advised the O’Kelly, patting my shoulder. “And for heaven’s sake, don’t try to drown out the orchestra. You’ve got a decent voice, but it’s not a foghorn. Keep cool, open your mouth—but not *too* wide—and let it happen naturally.”

It was a crisp morning, and we loitered outside the stage door like a pair of conspirators. For the past fortnight, the O’Kelly had been coaching me in secret, a task that had strained both his patience and his ingenuity. The obstacle? Mrs. O’Kelly. A formidable woman of rigid piety, she viewed all non-sacred music as a direct path to perdition. In her crusade to save her husband’s soul, she had banished the piano from their home and replaced it with an American organ—an instrument seemingly designed to suck all joy from the universe.

Our rehearsals of *Sally in Our Alley* had thus taken on a funereal quality. The O’Kelly, a man of true musical genius, wrestled heroically with the organ’s mournful wheeze, but even he couldn’t entirely mask its inherent gloom. My rendition of the cheerful ballad had, as a result, acquired the emotional depth of a gravedigger’s soliloquy.

“Lighter, lighter! Not so much like a man contemplating his own mortality,” the O’Kelly would implore as the organ groaned beneath his fingers.

Once, disaster nearly struck. Mrs. O’Kelly returned early from a meeting of the District Visitors’ Purity League and caught us mid-performance.

“William,” she said, in a tone that could freeze a volcano, “that was *not* a hymn.”

“Ah, my dear,” the O’Kelly countered smoothly, “it’s all in the timing. Allow me to demonstrate—”

“William, do *not* pervert sacred melodies in my presence. If you lack respect for religion, at least remember *I* do not. And I distinctly heard the word ‘Sally’ as I entered.”

“‘Salvation,’ my love,” the O’Kelly corrected.

“Your diction is not usually so atrocious.”

“A touch of hoarseness, my dear.”

“You sounded perfectly clear to me. We shall discuss this later.”

With that ominous promise, she swept out, leaving the O’Kelly to exhale like a man reprieved from the gallows.

“A most excellent woman,” he confided to me later, “provided you are at death’s door. When I had typhoid, her devotion was *touching*. But in robust health? Well, let’s just say she keeps me on the straight and narrow—whether I like it or not.”

I murmured something about the proverbial vinegar and honey.

“Mrs. O’Kelly to a T,” he agreed. “I sometimes wonder if Lady Macbeth started out the same way.”

---

At last, the day arrived. The O’Kelly clapped me on the back. “Meet me at the Cheshire Cheese at one. If Hodgson doesn’t take you, we’ll try elsewhere. But I’ve put in a word—you’ll do fine.”

With that vote of confidence, I faced my first trial: the stage door.

Behind a glass partition sat a man who resembled a disgruntled owl. He eyed me with the enthusiasm of a tax collector.

“Business?” he snapped.

I presented my letter.

“Passage, stage, corridor, first floor, second right,” he rattled off in one breath and slammed the window shut.

Navigating the labyrinthine corridors, I emerged onto the stage—and felt my theatrical illusions crumble in an instant. The auditorium was a gaudy relic; the stage, a cluttered playground of papier-mâché and painted canvas. The Theatre, I realized, was a dollhouse for grown-ups—a charming delusion, but a delusion nonetheless.

The greenroom was already packed with hopefuls, all radiating that indefinable *theatrical* aura. The men were clean-shaven but lavishly coiffed; the women uniformly pink-cheeked and bright-eyed. Conversation buzzed with affectionate insults and Christian-name camaraderie.

“Does anyone know what this opera is *about*?” asked a chirpy soprano.

“Does anyone *ever* know?” sighed a veteran chorister.

“I once asked the author,” drawled a world-weary baritone. “He said, ‘At the start of rehearsals, maybe. Now? Not a clue.’”

A dapper fellow in a velvet coat mused, “I’d wager there’s a drinking chorus for the men.”

“And the heroine will confide her love troubles to us,” added a willowy blonde. “Thrilling, no doubt.”

One by one, we were summoned. When my turn came, I faced a tribunal: Hodgson (absorbed in his mail), a despondent pianist, a harried stage manager, and the company’s comedian, who never looked at anyone directly.

“Moncrieff?” Hodgson muttered, still reading.

“Yes.”

“What’s your piece?”

“‘Sally in Our Alley.’”

“Voice?”

“Tenor. Not a *full* tenor,” I added hastily.

The comedian smirked. “Can’t fill a tenor. Ever tried?”

The room erupted in obligatory laughter—except the pianist, who looked as if he’d rather be anywhere else.

I sang. Hodgson barely glanced up.

“Audible. Just.” He flipped a page. “Thirty shillings a week, half for rehearsals. Take it or leave it.”

I took it.

---

Monday’s rehearsal was a whirlwind. The principals lounged, gossiping, while we choristers were marshaled into couples.

The stage manager, a whirlwind of Gallic energy, paired me with the willowy blonde.

“You’ll need to put your arm around my waist,” she informed me cheerfully. “Shocking, I know, but it’s in the contract. Think you can manage?”

I did.

The fishy-eyed young man, meanwhile, had been partnered with a Valkyrie of a woman. His earnest attempts at stage romance—all soulful gazes and stiff gestures—sent the entire chorus into suppressed giggles.

“He’s not *playing* the fool,” Hodgson remarked dryly. “He *is* one.”

“Widowed mother,” the stage manager muttered. “Five starving siblings.”

The comedian groaned. “We’re staging an opera, not a charity.”

Act Two was chaos—soldiers, conspirators, a mob, and a climactic brawl. The fishy-eyed gentleman stole every scene, dying with the gravitas of a Shakespearean tragedian.

By the final act, I had to kiss the willowy blonde.

“Duty calls,” she sighed. “Try not to enjoy it *too* much.”

I complied. She pronounced it “adequate.”

And so began my theatrical career.

Over supper, Dan and I traded life stories like a pair of sailors swapping tall tales in a dockside tavern. His, as it turned out, had more than a few echoes of my own. After leaving school—considerably earlier than I had—Dan had ventured to Cambridge, only to have his academic career cut short by the death of his father, a casualty of the Indian Mutiny whose wounds had never quite healed.

“You’d think I’d be relieved,” Dan admitted with a wry grin. “But truthfully, it gutted me. Cambridge was where I discovered I was, at heart, a scholar. School had made learning about as thrilling as watching paint dry—they rushed us through like tourists on a guided tour of a museum, more concerned with bragging rights than understanding. But at university? I chose my subjects, studied them my way. I *devoured* knowledge instead of being force-fed it like a Strasbourg goose.”

Back in London, he’d taken a position at a bank, thanks to an old friend of his father’s. The job offered a modest income and, more importantly, time to keep studying. But the crushing monotony of ledgers and ink stains had driven him to rebellion. After his mother’s death, he abandoned the City’s dust and drudgery for the footlights, joining a ragtag theatrical troupe. Eighteen months later, he’d played everything from Romeo to Paul Pry, painted scenery, and mastered the perilous art of bill-posting—a skill he assured me was more hazardous than it sounded.

“The paste has a vendetta against the uninitiated,” he explained. “It *wants* to leap back onto your head and cling there like a vindictive octopus.”

From the stage, he’d tumbled into the circus—first as Signor Ricardo, the Daring Bareback Rider, then as half of the Brothers Roscius, Trapeze Artists Extraordinaire. A brief stint as a waiter in Ostend followed, then a detour into footmanhood.

“One never knows,” Dan mused. “If I ever turn to writing society novels, my inside knowledge of the aristocracy will give me an edge over the competition.”

He’d sampled professions like a gourmand at a buffet—tramped through Ireland with a fiddle, lectured on Palestine in Scotland (accompanied by dissolving views that dissolved rather too enthusiastically), marked billiards, taught schoolboys, and, for the last three months, critiqued drama and music for a Sunday paper.

“How on earth did you land *that*?” I asked, green with envy.

“Sheer luck,” he said. “I was strolling down the Strand one evening, penniless and pondering my next move, when I wandered into the first newspaper office I saw, marched upstairs, and blundered into the editor’s lair. The poor man was in a state—ink spilled, matches lost, bell-pull broken—and in the gloom, I mistook him for the office boy. Turned out he *was* the editor, a slip of a fellow who owed his position to his aunt owning the paper. He was so harried, I took pity on him. Lit his gas, found his ink, and before I knew it, I’d appointed myself the new dramatic critic.”

He leaned back, triumphant. “They still don’t know I’m an imposter. The sub-editor thinks I’m the proprietor’s nephew, and the proprietor thinks I’m the sub-editor’s protégé. Journalism suits me. I might stick with it.”

“You’ve been a rolling stone,” I observed.

Dan laughed. “And gathered no moss—because moss is *dull*. Stones should roll, see the world, not get buried under greenery. But enough about me—what’s *your* plan? Your opera’s set to run, thanks to our friend ‘Goggles.’ Are you drawing a salary?”

“Thirty shillings a week,” I said. “Two pounds with matinees.”

“Then between us, we’ve five pounds a week,” he declared. “Enough to set up our own establishment. I’ve found rooms in Queen’s Square—four of them, charmingly furnished, going for a song because the landlord’s an eccentric who loathes petticoats. No landladies, no charwomen, just us and our wits. I can cook; you can manage the housekeeping. What do you say?”

I said yes before he’d finished speaking.

A week later, we were installed in our new domain—a Georgian relic with Adams mantelpieces, oak-panelled walls, and a stone staircase worn smooth by generations of feet. Our landlord, Deleglise, was an engraver with a temper and a terror of female domestics. His sole servant was an ancient Frenchwoman with whom he waged perpetual housekeeping warfare. The place was ours on one condition: we did *everything* ourselves.

And so, for nearly two years, we did.

Let it be known—despite the protests of an offended sex convinced that men left alone would starve amidst their own filth—that we not only survived but thrived. Dan’s cooking was, for the most part, superb. His coffee was ambrosia, his omelettes flawless, his devilled kidneys worthy of a clubhouse. But when ambition struck—usually prompted by a supper party or Deleglise’s culinary bravado—disaster loomed.

Pastry was his nemesis. His hand was heavy, his temperament too earnest. Lemon sponge, a delicate vision in the cookbook, emerged from his efforts resembling a geological upheaval. Manna-cake—a biblical horror—could have explained the Israelites’ complaints in the desert. And his pancakes? Early attempts involved more tossing than catching, with casualties including his scalp, the fire, and the cat.

For twenty-three hours a day, Dan was the most even-tempered man alive. But during that twenty-fourth, when custards curdled or sauces congealed, he became a tyrant. Suggestions were met with spoilt tomatoes hurled at my head. The oven, he declared, was a malevolent entity bent on his ruin.

As for housekeeping, I excelled—largely by avoiding it whenever possible. Dust, I argued, was best left undisturbed. Why rouse it into unhealthy activity? Crumbs, match-ends, and cigarette stubs found sanctuary under rugs, where they could be dealt with *later*—a time that never quite arrived. Needlework defeated me; my second finger rebelled at thimbles, preferring to guide the needle via doorframes.

Meanwhile, the opera flourished, and my salary swelled to princely sums. Visions of theatrical glory danced in my head—six hundred a week, adoring crowds, perhaps even a knighthood. But the O’Kelly, ever the realist, punctured my dreams.

“Ye’d be wasting your time,” he said bluntly. “Your voice is—well, let’s call it *unique*, but not in a way that fills theatres.”

Undeterred, I turned to literature. My first story—“The Witch of Moel Sarbod”—was accepted, and the day I saw my name in print was the sweetest of my life. It was a maudlin, overwrought thing, dripping with faux-medieval Welsh and sacrificial heroines, but to me, it was perfection. Candid friends called it drivel. I called it immortality.

Because that’s the thing about first loves—whether they’re stories or dreams or foolish ambitions. They may be flawed, even laughable in hindsight. But when you hold them in your hands for the first time, they’re not just words on a page. They’re the future, bright and boundless, and nothing else in the world matters quite so much.

During my days of noble poverty (which is to say, I was starving artistically rather than practically), I had carefully avoided old Hasluck. Not out of pride—though I flattered myself it was pride—but because I knew the man well enough to foresee his reaction to my suffering. He would have laughed, clapped me on the back, and offered me a position at twice the going wage, all while cheerfully misunderstanding every principle I was attempting to starve for. Why I preferred the dignified destitution of Lott & Co. to honest employment under a man who, after all, had been kind to me, I cannot explain. It wasn’t ethics; Lott & Co. were hardly more scrupulous than Hasluck. Nor was it because of Barbara—she existed in a realm entirely separate from her parents, who might as well have been innkeepers for all the relation they bore to her divinity.

No, the truth was, I simply had one of those inconvenient temperaments that would rather navigate by a faulty map than ask for directions. Some ancestral throwback, no doubt—some solitary cave-dwelling forebear who preferred chipping his own arrowheads to joining the communal flint-knapping circle.

But now that I had clawed my way to self-sufficiency, I could afford to be gracious. I wrote to old Hasluck, and by return post received an invitation so effusive it bordered on the suspicious. He informed me Barbara had just returned from abroad, added—quite without authority—that she would be *delighted* to see me, and flung his doors open as though I were a prodigal son rather than a former employee who had avoided him for years.

My boyish passion for Barbara had never been confessed to a soul. Not even my mother, fond as she was of the girl, had guessed. To her, Barbara was merely a charming but flawed creature, and she spoke of her as such—unwittingly lacerating me, as one might casually discuss theology in the presence of a mystic mid-vision. Often, under cover of darkness, I would wander the perimeter of the Haslucks’ vast red-brick fortress on Stamford Hill, circling like a pilgrim at a shrine before descending again into the grime of London, purified, resolved to greatness, all unworthy impulses burned away in the fire of my devotion.

Four years had passed since I’d seen her. In that time, my adoration had not waned; it had crystallized. The real Barbara—the girl I had played with, argued with, touched—had receded into memory. In her place stood the vision of my dreams, clearer and more radiant with each passing day. When we met again, I knew there would be a gulf between us, and I had no desire to bridge it. To worship from afar was sweeter than possession. The thought of sharing a roof with her, of witnessing the mundane mechanics of her existence—hair in curl-papers, complaints of chilblains—would have been sacrilege. Fortunately, such desecration was unthinkable. She was older now, a woman grown, while I remained, in some essential way, still a boy. She would marry. The idea caused me no pain, for my love was utterly without appetite. No one could desecrate the temple I had built for her in my mind; no priest could bar me from its inner sanctum.

A week after receiving Hasluck’s letter, I presented myself at Stamford Hill. It was a golden summer evening. Hasluck was still in the City, Mrs. Hasluck was out making social calls, and Miss Hasluck, I was informed, was in the garden. I waved away the footman’s offer of escort—I knew where to find her. Her favorite spot had always been a sunlit corner of the garden, a bower of flowers enclosed by a thick yew hedge clipped into the shapes of absurd animals. She was there, as I’d known she would be, strolling with a book in hand.

And just as when I’d first seen her in my father’s office, the world reshaped itself around her. All the fairy tales were true again—only now, she was no princess, but a queen. Taller, statelier, with a dignity that had been the only grace her girlhood lacked. She didn’t hear me approach, and for a moment I stood in the shadow of the yews, drinking in the sight of her: the clean line of her profile, the curve of her neck, the way her hand—so white against the lilac of her dress—turned a page with regal indolence.

I didn’t speak. I would have been content to watch forever. But at the end of the path, she turned and saw me.

I knelt and kissed her hand.

I don’t recall deciding to do it. The gesture was as natural as breathing. When I looked up, her lips were curved in a smile that held both amusement and something sharper—contempt, perhaps. Yet she seemed pleased. Even the contempt, if that’s what it was, couldn’t wound me.

“So you’re still in love with me,” she said. “I wondered if you would be.”

“You knew?”

“I’d have been blind not to.”

“But I was only a boy.”

“You were never the usual sort of boy. You won’t be the usual sort of man.”

“You don’t mind my loving you?”

“I can’t prevent it, can I? Nor can you.”

She seated herself on a stone bench beside a sun-dial, leaned back with her hands clasped behind her head, and laughed.

“I’ll always love you,” I said. “But it’s not the ordinary kind of love. I don’t understand it myself.”

“Describe it,” she commanded, still smiling.

I rested an arm against the sun-dial’s column. The light was fading, gilding her face, stretching the shadows across the lawn.

“I wish you were some great queen of antiquity,” I said, “and I could spend my life in your service, never asking for anything but the right to kneel at your feet. I don’t want your love in return—that would spoil it. To glimpse you from afar, to press my lips to a glove you’d dropped, to kiss the ground where your shadow had fallen—that would be enough. Or better still, if you were a priestess in some forgotten temple, and I could steal in at dawn or dusk to watch you move about the altar, to lie with my lips against the stones your robes had brushed.”

She laughed—a light, mocking sound. “I’d prefer the queen. Priestesses have such dreary duties. Temples are so *cold*.” A shiver passed through her, and she beckoned me closer. “That’s how you shall love me, Paul—blindly. I’ll be your queen, and you shall adore me as it pleases me. You’ll hear all my thoughts, all my deeds, and you’ll tell me they are right. The queen can do no wrong.”

She took my face between her hands, bent close, and stared into my eyes. “Do you understand, Paul? The queen can do no wrong—*never*.” There was a sudden fierceness in her voice, almost a plea.

“My queen can do no wrong,” I repeated.

She laughed again and released me. “Now you may sit beside me. This honor, Paul, will have to last you a long time. First, tell me everything you’ve been doing—it may amuse me. Then you shall hear what *I’ve* done, and you will say it was right and good.”

I obeyed, sketching my struggles, leaving nothing out—not even the regrettable episode of the Lady ’Ortensia. At that, her expression darkened.

“You must never shame me again, Paul,” she said. “Or I’ll banish you forever. I must be proud of you. Dishonor yourself, and you dishonor me. I’m angry with you. Don’t make me angry again.”

And so the matter was closed. Though my love for her—as she intended—did not always save me from folly, the desire to be worthy of her, to honor her with my whole being, shaped my life as only love can.

When my tale was done, she said, “Now I’ll tell you mine. I’m to be married soon. I’ll be the Countess Huescar—I’ll teach you to pronounce it—and I shall have a castle in Spain. Don’t look so alarmed, Paul; we shan’t live there. It’s a ruin among the mountains, and he loathes it even more than I do. Paris and London will be my courts. You’ll see me often. You’ll enter the great world, Paul—the world I mean to conquer.”

“Is he very rich?” I asked.

“As poor as a Spanish nobleman can be,” she laughed. “The money will come from dear Dad. My fiancé provides the title. Of course I don’t love him, handsome though he is. Don’t look so grave, Paul. We’ll deal well enough together. Queens don’t make love matches—they make alliances. I’ve done well. Congratulate me. Say I’ve acted rightly.”

“Does he love you?”

“He says so,” she answered with a laugh. “How un-courtier-like you are, Paul! Do you imply any man could see me and *not* love me?”

She sprang to her feet. “I don’t *want* his love—it would bore me. Women hate love they can’t return. Not love like yours, devout little Paul,” she added, smiling. “That is sweet incense. I want that. But the love of a husband one doesn’t care for—that would be hideous!”

For the first time, I saw her not as a goddess, but as a girl in need of guidance.

“But have you thought—” I began.

“Yes, yes,” she cut in, “I’ve thought till I can’t think anymore. There must be sacrifice—but let it be as little as possible. He doesn’t love me; he marries me for my money, and I’m glad of it. You don’t know me, Paul. I must have rank, position. What am I now? The daughter of old Hasluck, who began life as a butcher in the Mile End Road. As the Countess Huescar, society will forget. As plain Mrs. Smith, it would remember, no matter how rich I was. I’m vain, Paul. I crave power—ambition. I have my father’s blood. He spent his life gaining wealth; I must spend mine gaining place.”

“But you needn’t marry the first title that offers,” I argued. “Wait. You’ll meet someone who can give you position *and* your heart’s desire. Wouldn’t that be better?”

“The man I could love?” She shook her head. “He came already, Paul. Hush—the queen can do no wrong.”

“Who is he?” I asked. “May I know?”

“Yes,” she said. “I want you to know. Then you’ll tell me I’ve acted rightly—that you still respect me. He couldn’t help me. As his wife, I’d be less than I am now—a rich nobody, giving dinner parties to other rich nobodies, envied only by vulgar women, courted by Bohemians for my cook’s sake. Is that the court for a queen, Paul?”

“Is he so ordinary?” I asked. “I can’t believe it.”

“He isn’t ordinary,” she said. “*I* am. The things I want are beneath him. He’d never trouble himself to win them.”

“Not even for love of you?”

“I wouldn’t have him, even if he would. He’s great, Paul—greater than these times can understand. In another age, I’d have married him, knowing he’d rise by sheer force of will. But today, men don’t rise—they crawl. He couldn’t do that. I’ve done right.”

“What does *he* say?”

“Shall I tell you?” She laughed bitterly. “His exact words: ‘You’re half a woman and half a fool, so woman-like you’ll follow your folly. But let your folly see your woman doesn’t make a fool of herself.’”

I knew the voice at once—the ring of it, even through her mimicry.

“Hal!” I exclaimed. “It’s *him*.”

“You never guessed?” She smiled sadly. “I thought sometimes I’d like to shout it—but it wouldn’t have changed anything. Anyone who knew me must have seen it in my eyes.”

“But he never seemed to notice you,” I said.

She laughed. “Need you be so cruel, Paul? What did I ever do for you but snub you? We’re all alike, boys and girls—we love our masters. But don’t think too poorly of me. I was a child to him then. But later—we were locked in Paris together during the siege. Haven’t you heard? He noticed me then, Paul, I assure you.”

Would it have been better if she’d followed the woman, not the fool? It seems an easy question—until you think of years later, of a winter’s night in the Julier Pass, when I found Hal a dying man in a gloomy inn, coughing his life out in a room that had once been a chapel.

“What is she doing?” he asked me then. “Do you ever see her?”

When I told him, he laid his wasted hand on mine and said, “What a pity you and I couldn’t have rolled into one, Paul—you the saint, I the satyr. Together, we’d have made her perfect lover.”

And I thought of the voices of my parents, quarreling through the thin walls, and wondered if it was always my fate to stand between those I loved, powerless to help.

“Tell me,” I asked him, “whose fault was it? Hers or yours?”

He laughed. “Whose fault, Paul? God made us.”

For a moment, hating him, I thought of her fair face. Then I saw the pain in his eyes, and pitied him instead.

“Ah, well,” he said, “the curtain must come down, however badly things are going. It’s only a play, Paul. We don’t choose our parts.”

And perhaps that is the last word on love—that we are all of us, in the end, only players in a drama not of our own writing.

Deleglise’s Sunday suppers were legendary—a spectacle of culinary devotion and social mayhem. Clad in immaculate linen, he ruled his gleaming kitchen like a maestro, orchestrating sauces and soufflés while his guests, an eclectic mix of London’s artistic elite, loitered around the massive oak table, whispering like conspirators until the grand reveal. Then, as Deleglise emerged, flushed with triumph, bearing his latest masterpiece, the room would erupt—a cacophony of wit, laughter, and the occasional misplaced epiphany.

But this tale is not about Deleglise’s illustrious gatherings. No, this is about the far more chaotic, infinitely less dignified affairs hosted by Dan and myself in our second-floor lodgings in Queen Square—affairs where the only thing more unpredictable than the guests was the food.

Our preparations were rigorous. The cook (Dan) and the housemaid (me) would labor all morning under a system of mutual criticism.

*“Too much soda,”* I’d declare, poking at a suspiciously foaming cake.

*“You absolute philistine! It’s a soda cake,”* Dan would retort. *“It’s supposed to taste like a science experiment.”*

Dan would glower, but the cake would be demoted to doorstop status, and another culinary adventure would commence. His cooking was his pride, his Everest—and like many mountaineers, he often found himself buried under an avalanche of his own making. I once watched him finish an entire sponge cake that had collapsed into a sticky crater, insisting between gulps of brandy that *“it’s supposed to be moist in the middle.”*

Housekeeping was no less contentious.

*“Call these knives clean?”* Dan would demand, dragging a finger across one and producing a grimy streak worthy of a charcoal sketch.

*“Not if you go smearing them like a toddler,”* I’d snap. *“We’re out of knife powder.”*

Yet somehow, by some miracle, the rooms would be presentable by afternoon, and our guests would arrive—journalists, actors, and the occasional bewildered stranger who’d wandered in under false pretenses.

Dan had a knack for elevating the most unlikely individuals to social stardom. Take Mrs. Peedles, our landlady from Blackfriars, a woman whose fashion sense suggested a theatrical wardrobe exploded near her. Dan rebranded her as *“the delightfully eccentric Lady P.”* and insisted she was an aristocrat slumming it for amusement. Our guests, desperate to appear in the know, played along. *“My dear Lady Peedles!”* became the refrain of the season.

Then there was Jarman, whom Dan introduced as *“the South American millionaire.”* Jarman, to his credit, leaned into the role with gusto, spinning tales of diamond mines and yacht races while our actress friends fluttered around him like moths to a very dubious flame. I lived in terror that one of them would actually marry him, but Jarman, ever practical, eventually wed the daughter of a blacking factory and retired to a life of stout contentment.

Minikin, our *“eminent German scientist,”* was harder work. His habit of informing ladies they were *“silly”* and his glass eye (explained as the result of *“a daring experiment”*) required constant damage control. When a guest asked about the red ribbon across his chest, Minikin deadpanned, *“I got it for fourpence.”* We hastily clarified that he was *“a genius tragically soured by love.”* The ladies forgave him; the gentlemen muttered about lucky escapes.

The fishy-eyed comedian—now a rising star—was another regular.

*“I’m not a comic,”* he’d lament. *“I could play Hamlet if it weren’t for this face. But no, I’m doomed to be the man who makes butter fly.”*

*“You’re sinning your mercies,”* Dan told him. *“Laughter’s harder than tears. Paul here could write comedies, but he insists on gloomy Welsh princesses.”*

*“Doesn’t matter,”* the little man sighed. *“The gallery boy pays a shilling to laugh at me and thinks himself my superior.”*

The O’Kelly, when he visited, was a study in marital tension. His wife, a paragon of virtue, had banished tobacco from their home.

*“A saint,”* he’d groan. *“If I could be half the man she is—”*

*“You’d die of boredom,”* I suggested.

Yet he kept a pipe in our rooms, and after sufficient lubrication, his resolve would crumble. One evening, staring at a poster of the Signora—resplendent in tights—he sighed.

He crossed the road, bought an alarm clock loud enough to *“wake an Egyptian mummy,”* and vanished into the night, presumably to reform.

Hal, meanwhile, had returned, setting up as a doctor in the City.

*“I’d last a month in the West End,”* he admitted. *“I can’t lie to patients. Sooner or later, I tell them the truth: ‘You’re not ill, you’re just lazy. Get up and do something useful.’”* He grinned. *“The poor don’t mind honesty. The rich call it impertinence.”*

Once, he picked up a photograph of Barbara in her bridal gown, studied it, and set it down without a word.

Some quests end quietly.

I left London with drums beating in my heart and flags of triumph waving in my brain. A little over a year later, I crept back to it on a damp, fog-choked December evening—defeated, disgraced, a whipped cur slinking to its kennel. I had meant to be a great man, or at the very least a decent one. Instead, I returned as something best hidden in the crowd until I could muster the courage to end the sorry farce of my existence.

Ambition? Oh, I’d had that in spades. I was going to carve my name into the world with a chisel of genius and integrity. But ambition without backbone is just vanity in a waistcoat. I’d fancied myself a Galahad, pure and untouchable. Turned out I was more of a Launcelot—if Launcelot had tripped over his own scruples and landed face-first in the mud.

Urban Vane, my partner in crime (literal and metaphorical), was a swindler of the first order. The play we’d been touring wasn’t his. It wasn’t even mine. He’d lifted it straight from the desk of a half-dead dramatist he’d charmed in some literary scullery. When the poor devil recovered enough to crawl back from Italy, Vane broke the news to me over a bottle of wine, grinning around his cigar.

“Your language, my dear Kelver,” he said, when I ran out of indignant spluttering, “might wound me if I thought you had any standing to lecture on morality. You’ve been perfectly happy to take credit for work that wasn’t yours. ‘Drama, in five acts, by Mr. Horace Moncrieff’—how many of those lines were actually yours? Two hundred? And none of them the good ones.”

The worst of it was, he wasn’t wrong. I’d been eager to slap my name on anything that might float me into fame. Now the tide was going out, and I was left stranded in the stinking shallows.

What followed was a masterclass in moral gymnastics. Vane, ever the pragmatist, proposed we rewrite the play just enough to dodge outright theft. “He’ll think he mislaid the manuscript,” he assured me. “Or maybe he’ll assume he only dreamed of writing it. Artists are terribly forgetful.”

So I became a literary counterfeiter, stitching together a Frankenstein’s monster of stolen scraps and my own feeble embellishments. The irony wasn’t lost on me—here I was, the would-be knight of artistic purity, sweating to make a crime look like inspiration.

Vane was a peculiar sort of villain. Not the mustache-twirling kind, but the sort who genuinely believed villainy was just advanced thinking. He lectured me on the absurdity of morality, the pettiness of honesty. “The world’s only rule,” he’d say, “is *don’t get caught*. Everything else is sentimental drivel.”

I wish I could say I resisted. Instead, I let him sand down my conscience till it fit neatly into his philosophy. Worse, I started to *like* him. The man was a walking moral vacuum, yet he had a way of making depravity sound like higher wisdom.

Our leading lady was a Juno-esque creature with the charm of a sledgehammer and the subtlety of a music-hall ballad. She’d married a sensitive novelist, ruined him, and now regarded our tour as a stepping stone to better things. Vane, ever the matchmaker, saw in her the perfect instrument for my “artistic education.”

“All great men are libertines,” he declared. “Suppression is for shopkeepers. You need *experience*.”

I tried very hard to want her. Not out of passion—God knows there was none—but because it seemed the literary thing to do. If I was to be a proper artist, surely I ought to be dissolute? I rehearsed my debauchery like a bad actor stumbling through a part.

Fate, in her blunt way, saved me from myself. Our plagiarized play was recognized. Rumors spread. Vane, ever the strategist, vanished one evening with the week’s takings and a breezy note: *Life’s a comedy—laugh it off.*

I stood in the theater, surrounded by a chorus of outraged actors, all suddenly very concerned with artistic integrity. Their salaries were gone. Their trust—what little they’d had—was gone. And I was left holding the bag.

That night, our leading lady paid me a visit. She’d come, she cheerfully informed me, to accept my drunken proposal of elopement. “Damned lucky I took a day to think it over!” she laughed on her way out.

I walked thirty miles to Belfast that night. By the time I reached London, I was a ghost in my own life. I took a room in Deptford and gave singing lessons for sixpence a half-hour. I taught French and German to ambitious shopboys (may they never discover the crimes I committed against their subjunctives). I existed in a fog, moving through streets that felt like a dream I couldn’t wake from.

The oddest thing? I remember every detail of those months—the grime, the chatter, the thousand little tragedies of the streets—and yet I have no memory of *seeing* any of it. I was a spectator in my own ruin, watching from somewhere outside myself.

Defeat is one thing. Disgrace is another. But the real tragedy is realizing you’ve become the villain of your own story—and not even an interesting one.

It took me three years to earn that handshake—three years of scraping by in Deptford, where the streets were as rich in absurdity as they were in grime. The place teemed with material for humorous sketches, tragic vignettes, and essays on human folly. But my debts loomed larger than my ambitions—two hundred pounds, give or take a shilling—so I stuck to comedy. Tears were a luxury I couldn’t afford. Laughter paid the bills.

At first, I funneled every manuscript through Norah. She acted as my agent, mercilessly haggling with editors and sending me whatever scraps of profit remained after deducting her own cut. Dan, ever the patron saint of struggling writers, feigned outrage at her demands.

*"The work is passable,"* he’d grouse in letters, *"but hardly worth the ransom you’re demanding. Business is business, Miss Deleglise!"*

Norah, unimpressed, would reply in the crisp third-person style of a Victorian solicitor: *"Miss Deleglise is in perfect agreement with Mr. Brian regarding the nature of business. Should his publication find the price excessive, others will not."*

Dan, recognizing a fellow predator, grumbled but paid. Whether he ever guessed that "Jack Homer" was me, I never learned—but it wouldn’t have mattered. He’d have played the game regardless. To keep him honest, Norah sprinkled my work among rival editors, and soon "Jack Homer" became a name in demand.

Still, humor is a fickle mistress. She refuses to be rushed, scoffs at deadlines, and vanishes for weeks without warning. To fill the gaps, I turned to journalism—not the grand, doorstep-barging kind Dan excelled at, but the penny-a-liner’s trade. I joined the ranks of the shabby scavengers haunting inquests and police courts, feeding on society’s misfortunes like vultures on roadkill.

Our methods were simple. A corpse’s brother-in-law might murmur, *"I’d rather this didn’t make the papers."*

We’d nod gravely, scribble furiously, then adjourn to the nearest pub. Five minutes later, our spokesman would return, wiping his lips.

Sometimes we held out for ten.

My knack for finding comedy in catastrophe gave me an edge. When a plumber’s haste sent him tumbling onto a Lascar sailor—killing the sailor and barely bruising the plumber—my rivals headlined *"Sad Accident."* I went with *"Plumber in a Hurry—Fatal Result,"* sparing readers the sailor’s tragedy to instead lecture the trade on the perils of impatience.

The sub-editor of an evening paper even deigned to shake my hand. *"That’s the stuff,"* he said. *"A light touch. Bit of humor."*

So I mined mirth from fires (hoping the insurance was paid), spun wit from brawls, and turned calamities into copy.

***

All this time, I avoided Poplar like the plague. I was two people now: the old Paul, dreamy and sentimental, and the new Paul, brisk and pragmatic. The old Paul would whisper, *"Let’s go back. Just for a moment."* But I refused. I knew his tricks. He’d lead me to that dingy room, push open the sash, and drown me in nostalgia.

*"Later,"* I told him. *"When I’m rich and famous. Then we’ll dream again."*

Only once did I indulge him. When my fortunes turned, I moved westward—not just anywhere, but to the very room in Guildford Street my father had once considered for me.

*"This shall be yours, Paul,"* he’d said.

Now it was. I shut out the bed with a screen, as he’d suggested, and sometimes, in the quiet of evening, I’d almost hear the door creak open.

***

Months passed. My byline replaced "Jack Homer," and one day, an envelope arrived—a wedding invitation from the maternal Sellars. Her daughter, the erstwhile Lady ’Ortensia, was marrying a Mr. Reginald Clapper.

I bought a silver teapot (extravagant, but symbolic) and arrived at Kennington Church in frock coat and gray trousers, just in time to squeeze into a pew between the weeping Signora and Mrs. Peedles, who was dressed as a discount Queen Elizabeth.

*"Such a beautiful service,"* sighed the Signora. *"I should so love to be married."*

The O’Kelly, seizing her hand, whispered, *"Perhaps ye will be, one day."*

Mrs. Peedles sobbed into her ruff. *"Weddings always remind me how fleeting love is."*

In the vestry, I congratulated the happy couple. Mr. Clapper, a small but enthusiastic man, shook my hand with alarming vigor. *"Come and see us whenever you like!"* he bellowed three times, as though I might otherwise forget.

The Lady ’Ortensia, now Mrs. Clapper, assured me she’d always regard me as a friend—though her mother’s calculating glance suggested she’d hoped for more.

*"Real silver,"* the maternal Sellars muttered, inspecting my gift. *"Must’ve cost ten pounds."*

At breakfast, Uncle Gutton dominated the table, holding forth on marriage while the disillusioned Joseph muttered prophecies from behind a floral centerpiece.

*"When me and the old girl tied the knot,"* Gutton announced, *"I told her straight: 'I’m no duke.'"*

*"Had she mistaken you for one?"* asked Minikin.

Mr. Clapper roared with laughter. Gutton’s face purpled.

The Lady ’Ortensia, glowing, squeezed her bridegroom’s arm. *"We’ll show them how it’s done."*

Clapper gurgled in delight.

And I, watching them, sipped champagne and thought: *Perhaps happiness is just a matter of finding someone who laughs at your jokes—even the bad ones.*

Slowly, steadily, and with all the grace of a man trying to balance a teacup on his nose, I climbed the ladder of literary success—mostly by avoiding the rungs labeled "Dignity" and "Profundity." My other self, that melancholy little specter named Paul-with-the-sad-eyes, occasionally popped up like a disapproving ghost, whispering about artistic integrity and the soul’s true calling. But I had debts to pay, Norah’s hand to dream of grasping again, and—most pressingly—a landlord who did not accept "artistic fulfillment" as legal tender.

So, I wrote. And wrote. And occasionally, when the mood struck (or when I had a week to kill between deadlines), I scribbled stories purely for my own amusement. These made no impression on the world whatsoever, which was fine, because purposeful success, like fine wine, takes time to mature. Or so I told myself while depositing checks marked "humorous filler."

"Madness!" declared my practical friends, shaking their heads like disappointed schoolmasters. "You’re throwing away a fortune for the dubious privilege of calling yourself a Serious Artist. Stick to what you know—people *like* you being funny."

"Impossible now," sighed the wise old owls of the literary world. "Once a clown, always a clown. You might as well try to play Hamlet while juggling teacups. The audience would either laugh or walk out."

In moments of mutual despair, Goggles (a fellow artist tragically miscast by fate as a comic actor) and I would huddle in the Club’s quietest corner, drowning our sorrows in lukewarm tea and grandiose plans.

"*My* Romeo," Goggles would begin, striking a pose that suggested a man attempting to swat a fly with tragic dignity, "is a revelation of tortured passion—"

I nodded sympathetically while mentally calculating how much pathos one could feasibly inject into a comic opera before the audience revolted.

One evening, as the clock struck seven (the hour when all serious artists traditionally collapse into existential crises), Goggles fixed me with his famously fishy gaze. "Marry some nice girl," he advised solemnly. "Nothing clarifies life like small humans demanding food and explanations for the universe."

I stared into the fire, imagining a domestic idyll: a sensible wife, a cozy home, children who would one day ask profound questions like, "Papa, why *do* you write jokes for a living?" It was a soothing vision. Also, it conveniently ignored the fact that my current romantic prospects included a landlady who thought "artistic temperament" was a polite term for "rent arrears."

Then Hodgson arrived with an offer I *should* have refused.

"I want a comic opera," he announced, waving a letter like a man brandishing a dare. "Something fresh! Something English! Something—" he squinted at the page, "—‘new and original.’"

"The 'English' part is easy," I said. "The 'new and original'? That’s where things get dangerous."

He laughed, undeterred. "You’ll manage. Throw in some comic business for Atherton, a waltz for Gleeson, and let Duncan change dresses every ten minutes. The public will lap it up."

A week later, I found myself on a coach to Richmond, surrounded by the full theatrical brigade: Atherton (our resident clown, currently attempting to climb the coach roof via the tenor’s lap), the leading lady (who emitted a shriek of horror upon spotting a small crowd and demanded immediate evacuation), and an elegant divorcee who regarded the whole affair with the weary disdain of a cat at a dog show.

Atherton spent the journey honing his craft—which, in his case, meant convincing an elderly gentleman at the hotel that they were long-lost relatives. ("Aunt Martha! *Dear* Aunt Martha! Surely you remember me?") The gentleman did not, and after a brief altercation involving stolen Vermouth, we retreated, leaving behind a man who now understood why some species eat their young.

Over lunch, Atherton outlined his vision for the opera:

"I play a young man in love. The father’s a pompous fool—‘Damme, sir! By gad!’—that sort of thing. I disguise myself as an organ-grinder, then a policeman, then a swell. And—here’s the twist—*pathos*. Just a *hint* of it. Make ’em laugh, make ’em cry, make ’em say, ‘Good Lord, is that *Atherton* making me feel things?’"

The leading lady cornered me next. "I want to *flirt*," she declared, batting her eyelashes with the precision of a metronome. "None of this simpering village maiden nonsense. Give me a role with *depth*—or at least a lot of costume changes."

The tenor, sipping tea like a man preparing for his own execution, murmured about "human interest" and "less clowning."

By the time Marmaduke Trevor (a man whose entire career hinged on a single soda-water-syphon gag) begged for his moment of fizzy glory, I was ready to flee to the Yorkshire Wolds and write the thing in blessed solitude.

I returned with a finished script—only to watch, horrified, as the cast "improved" it.

"Cut this scene," Atherton declared, slashing entire pages. "It’s unnecessary."

"It explains the plot!" I protested.

"*I’ll* explain it. ‘Hullo, Roderick! Escaped from prison? Jolly good. And how’s Miriam?’ Done."

The leading lady had notes too. "What if I fall in love with *John* instead?"

"But John’s in love with Arabella."

"Who’s *also* been cut. I’ll sing her songs."

The tenor, meanwhile, envisioned a moonlit balcony scene—despite the first act being set at dawn. ("Hunting by moonlight! A *novelty*!")

After a week of this, I stormed into Hodgson’s office and slammed down two manuscripts: the original and the butchered "improved" version.

"Pick one," I said. "But if it’s the second, find another writer."

Hodgson sighed, tossed the mangled draft into the wastebasket, and informed the cast that, against all reason, they would perform the opera *as written*.

The rehearsals that followed were a study in silent suffering.

"Is this line *meant* to be funny?" Atherton would ask, reciting it like a man reading a grocery list.

"Would a *real* woman behave this way?" the leading lady mused, as if pondering the habits of an alien species.

The tenor, ever hopeful, murmured, "Perhaps the audience will understand it…"

And Hodgson, watching from the shadows like a man at a funeral, whispered, "I never make mistakes."

Which, as it turned out, was his first one.
