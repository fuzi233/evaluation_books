The next morning, Fritz and I set out early to retrieve the sledge we had left in Calabash Wood, laden with the dishes, bowls, and baskets we had crafted. The rest of the boys stayed behind with their mother, as I intended to explore beyond the rocky hills and preferred a smaller party for the task.

As we passed through the grove of evergreen oaks, we spotted our sow feasting on acorns, seemingly unbothered by yesterday’s chaos. She regarded us with mild interest, perhaps recognizing us as her unlikely protectors from the wild dogs.

The woods were alive with birdsong, their melodies undisturbed until Fritz fired his gun, bringing down a vibrant blue jay and two parakeets—one scarlet, the other green and gold. He was reloading when a strange, rhythmic drumming echoed through the trees. Cautiously, we crept toward the sound, parting the undergrowth until we glimpsed a magnificent bird perched on a fallen log.

It was a ruffed grouse, its chestnut plumage mottled with dark brown and grey. Tufts of velvety black feathers adorned its shoulders, glinting emerald in the sunlight. The bird strutted proudly, fanning its tail and beating its wings with increasing speed, producing a deep, resonant rumble like distant thunder.

I watched, mesmerized—until Fritz, impulsive as ever, fired. The grouse fell lifeless to the ground, and the spell was broken. I scolded him for his haste; such a rare spectacle should not have ended so needlessly. Regretfully, we loaded the bird onto the patient donkey and pressed onward.

The sledge lay undisturbed where we had left it. With time to spare, I decided to survey the cliffs and hills bordering the shore, searching for any passage inland. The terrain was lush, dotted with manioc, potatoes, and unfamiliar plants, nourished by clear, trickling streams.

Among the foliage, I spotted clusters of waxy white berries—Myrica cerifera. Excited, I explained to Fritz how we could render them into candles, a prospect sure to please his mother, who disliked retiring at sunset. We gathered a sackful before continuing.

Further on, we encountered a marvel of avian architecture: a communal nest, a sprawling mass of grass and twigs enveloping a tree like a great, spongy fortress. Hundreds of sociable grosbeaks flitted in and out, their chirping filling the air. Fritz, ever curious, climbed to inspect the nests but recoiled when a sharp beak clamped onto his finger. Undeterred, he snatched the offender—a small green parrot—and tucked it into his pocket, enduring a furious assault from its kin as he descended.

The bird’s antics amused us as we walked, sparking a discussion on communal creatures—beavers, bees, and ants. Fritz recounted watching ants laboring in intricate colonies, and I shared tales of monstrous foreign ants whose towering nests could withstand storms. Their greatest foe, I explained, was the ant-eater, a peculiar beast with a whip-like tongue and claws designed for tearing through fortified walls.

Our path soon led us to a grove of towering palms, one of which had toppled in the wind. Its pith bore a mealy texture, and nestled within were plump, sago-eating grubs. On impulse, I roasted a handful over a fire. To our surprise, they were rich and savory, a welcome addition to our meal.

By afternoon, we reached the coconut grove near Cape Disappointment. Amid the palms, I noticed a smaller variety—the sago palm—its trunk teeming with edible grubs. Nearby, rubber trees oozed sticky sap, which Fritz eagerly collected. To our delight, it stretched and rebounded like true caoutchouc, a discovery that promised endless utility.

We returned to Calabash Wood, loaded the sledge, and hitched it to the donkey. The journey home was uneventful, and by evening, we were back at Falconhurst, greeted by eager faces and a warm supper. The boys clamored to hear of our adventures, marveling at the parrot and the sack of wax berries.

That night, as we settled into our treehouse, I reflected on the day’s discoveries—candles, rubber, and the bounty of the land. Yet beneath the satisfaction lingered a quiet thrill: the island still held secrets, and tomorrow would bring another chance to uncover them.
