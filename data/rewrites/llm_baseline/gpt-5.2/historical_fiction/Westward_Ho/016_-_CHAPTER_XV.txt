Six weeks, give or take, had hardly turned since the duel, when trouble came padding up the drive to the great house at Stow on a pair of frightened legs.

The miller from Stow, cap in his hands and his face as pale as flour, begged for the bloodhounds.

Rose Salterne had vanished in the night.

There are disappearances that leave the house tidy, as if a woman had merely stepped out to gather herbs and been delayed by gossip; and there are disappearances that leave a taste in the mouth like burnt fat, the sense that something has been spoiled past mending. This was the latter. Her little fineries—those bright trifles a girl will treasure even when her pockets are empty—lay where they always lay. Nothing seemed stolen. Only some linen was gone; and old Anthony the steward, who could smell a lie as readily as he could smell sour ale, hinted broadly that missing linen had a way of turning up in other people’s boxes.

They found, under her chamber window, a slight mark in the earth where a foot had pressed—light as a hare’s, and gone as quickly. That was enough. The keepers were fetched; the serving-men ran down, and behind them trailed all the idle lads of the parish, eager as boys at a fair—because a maiden-hunt was sport to such as had never been hunted.

They spoke, as men will, with their hearts already made up. Rose must be the wronged one; the miller and his wife the hard old heathens who had driven a tender creature to fling herself from cliff or drown in the sea. Meanwhile the women of Stow, with a sterner relish for scandal, agreed to a voice that the hussy had “gone off” with some bad fellow, and that pride had met its fall.

The bloodhound was brought—leashed, of course; for even the simplest countryman knows what a true hound will do if you loose him on a scent and let him reason it out with teeth. They laid him to the mark. He gave a little premonitory whimper, like a kettle beginning to sing before the boil; then lifted his great bell-mouth, and the sound rolled through garden and lane and down into the hollow places like a church note made flesh.

He started.

The keeper, towed and panting, stumbled after him; and the whole rabble streamed behind, boots scuffing, breath sawing, voices cracking jokes that sounded thin against the steady music of the hound. Past the garden gate, up the lane, over the rise and out upon the downs, where the wind carried every noise farther than it had any right to go—the baying, the men’s curses, the clatter of borrowed staves. In a countryside without factories and stamp-mills, a sound could rule a man’s mood: a calm stream could soothe; a hunting-cry could turn even decent fellows cruel with excitement. So it did now.

Straight away the hound drew them towards Marslandmouth. There, at the door of Lucy Passmore’s cottage, the whole posse comitatus pulled up, lungs aflame.

Lucy’s door was fast.

Lucy herself—where was she?

Round the threshold her goats stood crying to be milked, their thin bleats fretting the air with hunger and habit; and from the slope above came answer from her donkeys, those shaggy grey philosophers of the down, who had wandered to their own sweet will and now brayed back at the bloodhound in a burst of discordant harmony, as if making sport of human urgency.

“They’m laughing at us, keper,” one of the lads said, winded and red. “They neddies. Sure enough, we’m lost our labor here.”

But the bloodhound did not agree. He worked about the door awhile, nose low, then turned away down the glen with the certainty of one who reads what men cannot. He never checked till he reached the margin of the sea.

“They’m taken water,” a voice declared, with that grim satisfaction men feel when a mystery resolves into something they can blame. “Let’s go back, and rout out the old witch’s house.”

“’Tis just like that old Lucy,” another cried, “to lock a poor maid into shame.”

Indignation makes a fine cloak for greed. They went back and attacked the cottage—not with any warrant but the loud consent of the crowd—and ransacked it from hearth to rafters, half in wrath and half in the hope of plunder. Yet plunder there was none. Lucy had decamped with all her movable wealth; only the huge black cat remained, crouched among the embers. At the sight of the bloodhound it vanished up the chimney; some swore they smelt brimstone in its wake. Seen again outside, it was chased into the woods, and there, no doubt, lived long and happily as a scourge to the rabbits of the glen.

The goats and donkeys, bewildered and bawling, were driven up to Stow. The crowd straggled home, their brief fury cooling into a shamefaced silence—and with it a smaller fear of what Sir Richard Grenville might say.

Sir Richard, when he returned, sold the donkeys and goats and gave the money to the poor, promising to refund the same if Lucy came back and yielded herself to justice. Lucy did not return. Her cottage, which the neighbours thereafter passed wide as they would a plague-pit, stood empty and began to crumble. Fern and damp worked their slow cookery on the stones; the roof sank; the walls leaned. Only the little stream went on murmuring from pool to pool—the lone voice that broke the silence of that glen for many a year, steady as a lullaby, as if it remembered a language the people had forgotten.

A few days later Sir Richard, riding from Bideford to Stow, looked in at Clovelly Court and dropped, with a careless “by the by,” a piece of news that made Will Cary spring from his chair as if the floor had turned to flame.

“And there is no clue?” asked old Cary; for Will had no words.

“Only this,” said Sir Richard. “Some fellow prowling about the cliffs that night saw a pinnace running for Lundy.”

Will went out without excuse. Within half an hour he had three or four armed servants in a trawling-skiff and was away for Lundy, the oars thumping and the tide hissing past the bow like a whisper that would not be stilled. He did not return for three days. When he came back, the salt had dried white on his clothes and his eyes were gritty with sleeplessness; but he brought news.

An elderly man, seemingly a foreigner, had for months been lodging in a part of ruined Moresco Castle on Lundy, where one John Braund lived—farmer, fisherman, smuggler, and whatever else fed seven hungry children and paid arrears of rent. Some weeks back a younger man, a foreigner too, had joined him from a ship. The ship came and went—Flushinger, or Easterling, the fishermen thought—and the young man in her. Then, a few days ago, a lady and her maid, a stout woman, had come with him up to the castle and spoken long in secret with the elder man. They had slept there; and in the morning all three sailed away. The fishermen on the beach had heard the young man call the other “father.” The old one was still and priest-like; more they did not know, or did not choose to know.

Old Cary and Sir Richard sent Will again, this time with the parish constable of Hartland—within whose huge parish, for its sins, lay Lundy ten miles out at sea. They returned not with Rose, nor even with the foreign priests, but with the body of poor John Braund dragged into the coils of law. He was examined, and the examiners found him afflicted with extreme deafness and loss of memory; which was convenient, perhaps, for a man who had lived by keeping his ears open and his mouth shut. Fruitless as the questioning was, it ended as such things do: he was dispatched to Exeter gaol on a charge of “harboring priests, Jesuits, gipsies, and other suspect and traitorous persons.”

There are prisons that punish by iron; Exeter punished by air. The place was a stew of human breath, damp straw, and sickness. In it, the soundscape itself was misery: coughing that never ended, moans that rose and fell like a foul tide, chains that clinked time into men’s bones. John Braund, taken from the clean wind of Lundy into that pestiferous den, lasted a week before the gaol-fever took him. He died raving. His secret—if he had one—perished with him; and nothing remained but suspicion, like a bad smell that lingers after the pot is scoured.

That Rose had gone off with the Spaniard few doubted. But whither, and in what character? On the last point, mercy was in short supply. Many a Bideford dame, who had hated Rose simply for being beautiful, found a full cupboard of wickedness to feed her tongue; and many a country lady, who had always declared that “the girl would be brought to ruin by the absurd notice, beyond what her station had a right to,” now felt herself proved prophetic. Every ambitious young maiden aspired to step into the place Rose had left.

So Bideford, busy as a kitchen at fair-time, went on as well without Rose as it had done with her—perhaps better, in its own opinion. A few still kept her in their minds as one keeps a scent of summer apples in winter; but the bustle of each day swept such tenderness away, and her place knew her no more.

Will Cary did not go on so easily. For a while he was like a man out of his wits. He loaded himself with superfluous reproaches—first, he said, he had brought Rose into disgrace; then he had driven her into the Spaniard’s arms. St. Leger, being sensible enough, tried in vain to comfort him: the attachment must have been long before the quarrel; it would have ended so sooner or later; old Salterne’s harshness had hastened the catastrophe more than Cary’s wrath; and, lastly, that a girl who had eloped with a Spaniard was not worth troubling their heads about.

Will would not be so comforted. He wrote to Frank at Whitehall, telling the whole truth, calling himself all fools and villains, and begging forgiveness. Frank replied with a letter that Will read as if it were scripture: Will had no reason to accuse himself; such strange attachments were due to a synastria, a sympathy of the stars ruling each person’s destiny, and to fight it was to fight the heavens. Frank, as Rose’s brother, was bound to believe—nay, to assert at sword’s point if need were—that the incomparable Rose of Torridge could make none but a worthy and virtuous choice; and to the man she had honoured by her affection was due, Spaniard and Papist though he might be, all friendship, worship, and loyal faith for evermore.

Honest Will took it for gospel, never guessing what it had cost Frank to write those sentences—what agony of despair, what fearful suspicions, what bitter prayers must have been kneaded into those smooth words, as a cook kneads salt into dough that will not rise.

Will showed the letter triumphantly to St. Leger. St. Leger, wise enough, gainsaid no word aloud; but he was wise enough too to believe, in secret, that Frank looked on the matter in quite another light. He only said, “The man is an angel as his mother is!” and for a few days the matter might have dropped—if one had not stepped forward with no mind to let it drop.

That was Jack Brimblecombe, now curate of Hartland town, passing rich on forty pounds a year.

He came in upon Will Cary with his small pig’s eyes earnest, his face set in that obstinate honesty which makes a fool of fear.

“I hope no offence, Mr. William,” said he; “but when are you and the rest going after—after her?”

The name stuck in his throat.

Cary stared. “What’s that to thee, Catiline the blood-drinker?” he asked, trying to laugh it away.

“What? Don’t laugh at me, sir. It’s no laughing matter. I drank that night naught worse, I expect, than red wine. Whatever ’twas, we swore our oaths, Mr. Cary; and oaths are oaths, say I.”

“Of course, Jack, of course. But to go to look for her—and when we’ve found her, cut her lover’s throat. Absurd, Jack. Even if she were worth looking for, or his throat worth cutting. Tut, tut—”

Jack did not blink. The room was quiet; Cary could hear, beyond the shutters, the soft domestic noises that make a house feel safe—some pan being set down, a distant murmur of servants, a crackle of wood. The ordinary world went on, indifferent to vows.

After a silence Jack asked, very simply, “How far is it to the Caracas, then, sir?”

“What is that to thee, man?”

“Why, he was made governor thereof, I hear; so that would be the place to find her?”

“You don’t mean to go thither to seek her?” Cary forced a laugh, as if laughter could bar a door.

“That depends on whether I can go, sir; but if I can scrape the money together, or get a berth aboard some ship—why, God’s will must be done.”

Cary looked hard at him, to see whether he had been drinking or had gone mad; but those little eyes were sane and sober. It is a terrible thing, sometimes, to find conscience in another man’s face; it makes one’s own prudence taste like stale bread.

“Well,” Jack went on, in his stupid steadfast way, “it’s a very bad look-out. But mother’s pretty well off, if father dies, and the maidens are stout wenches enough, and will make tidy servants, please the Lord. And you’ll see they come to no harm, Mr. William, for old acquaintance’ sake, if I never come back.”

Cary could find no answer. Heroism in a fat, vulgar curate shamed him more than any knight-errant could have done, because it offered no romance to hide behind.

“And, Mr. William,” Jack added, “you know me for an honest man, I hope. Will you lend me five pound, and take my books in pawn for them, just to help me out?”

“Are you mad, or in a dream? You will never find her.”

“That’s no reason why I shouldn’t do my duty in looking for her, Mr. William.”

“But, my good fellow, even if you get to the Indies, you will be clapped into the Inquisition and burnt alive, as sure as your name is Jack.”

“I know that,” said Jack, and his voice went dull with dread. “And a sore struggle of the flesh I have had about it; for I am a great coward, Mr. William—a dirty coward—and always was, as you know. But maybe the Lord will take care of me, as He does of little children and drunken men. And if not, Mr. Will—I'd sooner burn, and have it over, than go on this way any longer, I would!”

And with that the poor fellow burst out blubbering.

“What way, my dear old lad?” Cary asked, softened in spite of himself.

“Why—not—not to know whether—whether she’s married to him or not—her that I looked up to as an angel of God, as pure as the light of day; and knew she was too good for a poor pot-head like me; and prayed for her every night, God knows, that she might marry a king, if there was one fit for her—and I not to know whether she’s living in sin or not, Mr. William. It’s more than I can bear, and there’s an end of it. And if she is married to him—they keep no faith with heretics; they can dissolve the marriage, or make away with her into the Inquisition—burn her, Mr. Cary, as soon as burn me, the devils incarnate!”

A shudder ran through Cary. That plain, ugly possibility—Rose not merely lost, but trapped—had never struck him with such force. Jack’s fear had given it a voice, and once spoken aloud it seemed to fill the room.

“Yes,” Jack hurried on, “or make her deny her God by torments, if she hasn’t done it already for love to that— I know how love will make a body sell his soul, for I’ve been in love. Don’t you laugh at me, Mr. Will, or I shall go mad!”

“God knows,” said Cary, “I was never less inclined to laugh at you in my life, my brave old Jack.”

Jack’s face broke. “Is it so, then? Bless you for that word!” He held out his hand.

“But what will become of my soul, after my oath, if I don’t seek her out—just to speak to her, to warn her, for God’s sake, even if it did no good; just to set before her the Lord’s curse on idolatry and Antichrist, and those who deny Him for the sake of any creature—though I can’t think He would be hard on her—for who could? But I must speak all the same. The Lord has laid the burden on me, and done it must be. God help me!”

“Jack,” Cary said slowly, “if this is your duty, it is others’.”

“No, sir, I don’t say that. You’re a layman; but I am a deacon, and the chaplain of you all, and sworn to seek out Christ’s sheep scattered up and down this naughty world—and that innocent lamb first of all.”

“You have sheep at Hartland, Jack, already.”

“There’s plenty better than I will tend them, when I am gone; but none that will tend her, because none love her like me, and they won’t venture. Who will? It can’t be expected, and no shame to them.”

“I wonder what Amyas Leigh would say to all this, if he were at home?”

“Say? He’d do. He isn’t one for talking. He’d go through fire and water for her—you trust him, Will Cary—and call me an ass if he won’t.”

“Will you wait, then, till he comes back, and ask him?”

“He may not be back for a year and more.”

“Hear reason, Jack. If you will wait like a rational and patient man, instead of rushing blindfold on your ruin, something may be done.”

“You think so!”

“I cannot promise; but—”

“But promise me one thing. Do you tell Mr. Frank what I say—or rather, I’ll warrant, if I knew the truth, he has said the very same thing himself already.”

“You are out there, old man; for here is his own handwriting.”

Jack read Frank’s letter and sighed bitterly. “Well, I did take him for another guess sort of fine gentleman. Still, if my duty isn’t his, it’s mine all the same. I judge no man; but I go, Mr. Cary.”

“But go you shall not till Amyas returns. As I live, I will tell your father, Jack, unless you promise; and you dare not disobey him.”

“I don’t know even that, for conscience’ sake,” Jack muttered, uncertain.

“At least you stay and dine here, old fellow,” said Cary, seizing the only leash he could find, “and we will settle whether you are to break the fifth commandment or not, over good brewed sack.”

Now a good dinner was what Jack loved, and loved too often in vain. He submitted for the nonce. Cary thought, when Jack went, that he had talked him round. Jack kept away for a week.

Then he returned, brisk and bright as a man with a plan. “I have settled all, Mr. Will. The parson of Welcombe will serve my church for two Sundays, and I am away for London town, to speak to Mr. Frank.”

“To London? How wilt get there?”

“On Shanks his mare,” said Jack, pointing to his bandy legs. “But I expect I can get a lift aboard a coaster so far as Bristol, and it’s no way on to signify, I hear.”

Cary tried in vain to dissuade him, and then, half in pity and half in shame, forced on him a small loan. Away went Jack, with his poor bundle and his great vow; and Cary heard nothing for three weeks.

At last Jack walked into Clovelly Court again just before supper-time, thin and leg-weary. He sat down among the serving-men till Will appeared. Will brought him up above the salt, made much of him—as a man will who sees another wasted by roads and purpose—and after supper asked in private how he had sped.

“I have learnt a lesson, Mr. William,” Jack said. “I’ve learnt there is one on earth loves her better than I—if she had but had the wit to have taken him.”

“But what says he of going to seek her?”

“He says what I say—Go! and he says what you say—Wait.”

“Go? Impossible! How can that agree with his letter?”

“That’s no concern of mine. Of course, being nearer heaven than I am, he sees clearer what he should say and do than I can see for him. Oh, Mr. Will, that’s not a man—he’s an angel of God; but he’s dying, Mr. Will.”

“Dying?”

“Yes, faith, of love for her. I can see it in his eyes, and hear it in his voice. But I am of tougher hide and stiffer clay, and so you see I can’t die even if I tried. But I’ll obey my betters, and wait.”

And Jack went home that very evening, weary as he was, despite all entreaties to spend the night. Yet he left behind him in Cary’s mind a restlessness that would not be quieted. It lay there like yeast, working in the dark, swelling thought into thought until a small touch would make the whole rise sudden and irresistible.

That touch came, as such touches often do, by what men call chance.

One murky day Cary was lounging on Bideford quay, listening to the harbour noises—the slap of water against piled timber, the creak of rope, the cry of gulls that cut the air clean as a knife—when up came Mr. Salterne.

Cary had shunned him of late, partly from delicacy, partly from dislike of his supposed hard-heartedness; but now they met full, and Cary could not pass without speaking.

“Well, Mr. Salterne, and how goes on the shipping trade?”

“Well enough, sir,” Salterne answered, “if some of you young gentlemen would but follow Mr. Leigh’s example, and go forth to find us stay-at-homes new markets for our ware.”

“What? You want to be rid of us, eh?”

“I don’t know why I should, sir. We sha’n’t cross each other now, sir, whatever might have been once. But if I were you, I should be in the Indies about now, if I were not fighting the queen’s battles nearer home.”

“In the Indies? I should make but a poor hand of Drake’s trade.”

The talk dropped there, as talk does when two men skirt the edge of what matters. But Cary did not forget the hint; it rang in him like a remembered refrain.

So, later, when he told Amyas Leigh—making an end, as he said, of a long story—his decision had already been simmering to a boil.

“If you are minded to take the old man’s offer, so am I,” said Cary. “Westward-ho with you, come foul come fair.”

“It will be but a wild-goose chase, Will.”

“If she is with him, we shall find her at La Guayra. If she is not, and the villain has cast her off down the wind, that will be only an additional reason for making an example of him.”

“And if neither of them are there, Will, the Plate-fleets will be; so it will be our own shame if we come home empty-handed. But will your father let you run such a risk?”

“My father?” Cary laughed, and the laugh had more bitterness than mirth. “He has just now so good hope of a long string of little Carys to fill my place, that he will be in no lack of an heir, come what will.”

“Little Carys?”

“I tell you truth. I think he must have had a sly sup of that fountain of perpetual youth which our friend Don Guzman’s grandfather went to seek in Florida; for some twelvemonth since he must needs marry a tenant’s buxom daughter. Mistress Abishag Jewell has brought him one fat baby already. So I shall go—back to Ireland, or with you—but somewhere. I can’t abide the thing’s squalling, any more than I can abide seeing Mistress Abishag sitting in my poor dear mother’s place and informing me every other day that she is come of an illustrious house, because she is—or is not—third cousin seven times removed to my father’s old friend, Bishop Jewell of glorious memory. I had three-parts of a quarrel with the dear old man t’other day; for after one of her peacock-bouts, I couldn’t for the life of me help saying that as the Bishop had written an Apology for the people of England, my father had better conjure up his ghost to write an apology for him, and head it, ‘Why green heads should grow on gray shoulders.’”

“You impudent villain! And what did he say?”

“Laughed till he cried again—and told me if I did not like it I might leave it; which is just what I intend to do. Only mind—if we go, we must needs take Jack Brimblecombe with us, or he will surely heave himself over Harty Point, and his ghost will haunt us to our dying day.”

“Jack shall go. None deserves it better.”

There followed a long consultation on practical matters—ships, money, men, and the slow patience of waiting for the right wind. They concluded that Amyas should go up to London and sound Frank and his mother before any further steps were taken. The other brethren of the Rose were scattered far and wide, each at his post; St. Leger had returned to his uncle; and it would be unfair, as well as a delay, to demand from them all the fulfilment of the vow.

“And besides,” Amyas said, with the homely wisdom of a man who had seen too many gentlemen spoil good work, “too many cooks spoil the broth, and half-a-dozen gentlemen aboard one ship are as bad as two kings of Brentford.”

With which maxim he departed next morning for London, leaving Yeo with Cary, and leaving behind him, in the quiet house by the sea, a sense of things set moving—like a pot newly put upon the fire, whose first small whisper foretells the boil.
