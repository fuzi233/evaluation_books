*“I know that Deformed; he has been a vile thief this seven years; he goes up and down like a gentleman: I remember his name.”* — *Much Ado About Nothing*

Amyas slept that night heavy-limbed from the day’s triumph, but his rest was far from easy. His mother and brother Frank, hovering over his pillow, could see his brow furrowed and his lips moving, as if his mind was tangled in a dozen half-spoken dreams.

No wonder. Even above the roar of the harbor crowds and the thrill of being home after years at sea, the memory of lost adventurer John Oxenham had sunk its hooks deep into his brain. All that evening, as he sat in the bay window where he had first met the man, he replayed every look, every gesture of Oxenham’s, turning the memory over and over till he crawled to bed, only for the vision to follow him straight into his sleep. One moment he found himself sailing due west, chasing the wake of the setting sun, straining to catch up to a tiny distant sail that he knew belonged to Oxenham’s lost sloop. A cold dread gnawed at him—if he did not overtake it in time, some unnameable horror would unfold. But his ship would not move. The great tangles of sargasso weed stretched for miles all around, wrapping their snaky fronds around his bows and dragging him to a standstill. He strained at the rigging, he willed the ship forward, till the sun dipped below the horizon and the world dropped to utter blackness. Then the moon rose.

Oxenham’s wreck was suddenly right alongside him. Her sails were tattered rags flapping in the gale, pitch streamed in black rivulets down her rotting sides, and her bulwarks crumbled at a touch. A line of dark shapes dangled along her mainyard: hanged men, swaying gently in the moonlight. Worst of all, slung from the yardarm directly over Amyas’s head, hung John Oxenham himself. His corpse’s eyes glowed with the pale cold light of the moon, and he beckoned, pointed, strove to speak but could not, not ahead toward home, but back the way they had come. When Amyas turned to look, the snow-capped peaks of the Andes glittered at his back, and he knew he was adrift in the South Seas, with the whole breadth of the Americas standing between him and Devon. The corpse kept pointing back, its face twisted with a yearning agony, as if its lips ached to spill some terrible secret, till Amyas jolted upright with a shout of raw terror, wide awake in his little curtained chamber at Burrough, the soft gray light of autumn morning seeping around the edges of his window.

Feverish and restless, he tossed for an hour before he gave up on sleep, pulled on his clothes, and tramped down to the shingle ridge he had swum in since he was a small boy. As he passed his mother’s door, he glanced in. The bed was untouched, its pillow never pressed that night. His mother stood kneeling at her prie-dieu in the thin dawn light, clad in her long white nightgown, lost in her prayers. He slipped in soft as a shadow and knelt beside her. She turned, smiled, slipped an arm around his shoulders, and went on praying silently. He knew those prayers were for him, and he prayed too: for her, for lost John Oxenham, for every last man of his vanished crew.

When she finally rose, she bent over him, brushed the stray yellow curls back from his forehead, and looked at him for a long, soft moment. No words were needed—there was no secret between these two, their souls as open and clear as glass. She kissed his brow, and as she turned away, a single tear dropped onto his forehead. He spied her small bare feet peeking out from under the hem of her gown, knelt, and kissed them again and again. When he looked up, grinning a little to excuse himself, he said, “You have such pretty feet, mother!”

She hid her feet behind her skirts with a soft, laughing huff, the instinct of a woman who had once been one of the greatest beauties of Devon, and still was, to anyone who could see deeper than rose-pink cheeks and glossy hair. “Your dear father said exactly that, thirty years back.”

“And I say it still. You were always beautiful. You are beautiful now.”

“What a silly boy you are, playing the lover to your old mother?” she chided gently. “Go on, take your swim, and think of younger ladies, if you can find any worthy of you.”

So he tramped off to the sea, and she went back to her prayers.

The great pebble ridge rose before him, a rampart of smooth gray boulders two miles long, heaped up over centuries by the bay’s own surges, curved and fitted and worn as neatly as if a mason had laid it by hand, holding back the high spring tides to protect the stretch of rich flat alluvial turf that ran behind it. Sniffing the sharp salt air like a young hound, he stripped off and plunged headfirst into the breakers, diving and rolling and tossing through the foam, till a hail rang out from the shore. He looked up, and saw his cousin Eustace Leigh standing tall on the crest of the ridge.

Amyas was half disappointed to see him. He had been daydreaming of Rose Salterne all the walk down, and had no use for a companion who would break that reverie on the way back. Still, it had been three years since they had seen each other, so he clambered out of the water, dressed quickly, while Eustace paced up and down on the turf inside the ridge.

Eustace was the son of the younger brother of the Leigh of Burrough, who had cut himself off from most of his family and his neighbors by remaining a stubborn Roman Catholic. He had converted to Protestantism under Edward VI, then back to Catholicism under Mary, and refused to flip a second time when Elizabeth took the throne—an uncharacteristic bit of honesty for the era, when hundreds of gentry shifted their faith as easily as they shifted their hats to keep their lands. He lived far out of the way, in a great rambling dark old house called Chapel, perched on the Atlantic cliffs in the parish of Moorwinstow, not far from Sir Richard Grenville’s home at Stow. The penal laws against recusants never troubled him; the crown had no interest in harassing quiet Catholic landowners who stayed out of conspiracy, and even the ones who courted martyrdom were mostly left alone till they went out of their way to provoke Elizabeth’s council. Hidden away at the far western edge of Devon, Chapel House was a perfect refuge; Jesuits and seminary priests slipped in and out all year round, unsuspected by most, hiding in tiny secret turret rooms and celebrating mass behind a trap door in the roof, playing at persecuted saints to their hearts’ content. Once, the local Puritan parson of Moorwinstow complained loudly to Sir Richard Grenville that Chapel House harbored “mass priests and idolatry,” demanding he enforce the new laws—but Grenville only berated him for being a meddlesome fool, threatened to run him out of the parish if he didn’t mind his own business, and the parson wisely settled for accepting Mr. Leigh’s dinner invitations and politely pretending not to notice the soft-spoken “tutor” who sat across the table from him at meals was actually a wandering Jesuit priest.

But those priests held a heavy weight over Eustace’s father Thomas Leigh’s conscience: he owned broad lands that had once belonged to Hartland Abbey. He was half-convinced he would be damned for holding stolen church property, but not quite convinced enough to give the lands back—just like the vast majority of Mary’s Catholic subjects, who were perfectly happy to return to the Church as long as they got to keep all the abbey plunder they had looted. Most compromised their guilty consciences by petted foreign priests, sending one of their sons off to Rheims or Douay as a sort of sin-offering, to be trained as a seminary priest. That was exactly what had happened to Eustace Leigh. His father had given his son over to the Jesuits, paying for the sin of his greed by sacrificing the fruit of his body to be turned into a loyal agent of their order.

And Eustace had made a very fine, polished dissembler. He was not cruel or bad at heart, not exactly—but the priests had spotted in him a talent for hazy, hysterical fear of the unseen, that vague devoutness that so easily curdles into double-dealing when it is twisted to their ends. Now he was tall and handsome, fair-skinned, with a high, broad forehead and a tiny tight mouth, his face set in a permanent, forced attempt at a smile: he knew a good Christian man should show cheer and charity, so he forced his lips up into dimples whenever he could, but his pre-planned pious smiles looked no more warm or natural than a skeleton’s rictus. He had returned to England only a month before, obeying a royal proclamation ordering all families to recall their children and wards studying overseas within four months—though he still carried out secret, unspoken missions for the Jesuit order that had raised him, the very business that had brought him into Bideford the night before.

He sat down beside Amyas on the shingle, glancing at him gently out of the corners of his eyes, as if he would not hurt even a fly on his cousin’s back. Amyas turned full to face him, grinning the broadest, heartiest grin, and held out a great paw of a hand. Eustace grabbed it rapturously, and they shook hard: Amyas’s grip was firm, steady, warm, as if to say plainly *I am so glad to see you*. Eustace’s thin white fingers pinched hard, sawing their joined hands violently up and down in a great show of warmth, as if to cry *Can’t you see how delighted I am?* It was the very opposite of a natural, unforced greeting.

“Easy there, old man,” Amyas laughed. “You’ll snap my elbow. Where’ve you wandered from?”

“From going to and fro in the earth, and walking up and down in it,” Eustace replied, with a little smug, mysterious nod.

“Like the devil, eh?” Amyas said. “Well, every man’s got his own pattern. How’s my uncle?”

Eustace was terrified of Amyas, more than any other man on earth. For one thing, he knew Amyas could kill him with a single blow. For another, he was perfectly certain Amyas despised him. Before Amyas had left for sea, Eustace could never win an argument with him, simply because Amyas wrote off all his pious Jesuit talk as “wind and moonshine” and treated him like a harmless, half-baked lunatic, too silly to waste a proper debate on. Eustace resented it bitterly, and swore to himself he would prove he was more than a match for his big, brute-force cousin—only to immediately twist himself into a spasm of self-reproach for prioritizing his own pride over “the glory of God,” as the priests had taught him.

Eustace began to murmur piously that he had prayed nightly for Amyas’s safe return, that the saints had interceded for him, especially the Blessed Virgin, the star of the sea that guided all mariners. Amyas cut him off. “Halt there, coz. If those saints are half the good fellows you say they are, they’d help me out even if I never asked.”

Just then Frank wandered over, greeted them both, and sat down on the shingle beside them. Amyas laughed and told his brother that Eustace was already trying to convert him, claiming the Virgin was responsible for all his safe voyages.

Frank smiled, and replied that Amyas owed his safety far more to the prayers of another peerless, spotless virgin: Queen Elizabeth herself, whose loyal subjects prayed for her seamen daily, and for whose glory they sailed to spread English power across the seas.

Eustace’s smile turned thin and venomous. “At least I know the patroness I name is an undefiled virgin.”

Both brothers’ faces darkened instantly. Amyas, lying back on the warm pebbles, called out to the gulls wheeling overhead, “I wonder what that Frenchman I beheaded in the Azores is thinking about that, right now.”

Frank stared. “You cut off a Frenchman’s head?”

“Aye, I did,” Amyas laughed, and told the tale: he and George Drake had stepped into a tavern, and found a loudmouthed French bully ranting against Queen Elizabeth, spouting the foulest, lewdest slanders about her honor, the sort of lies spread by the corrupt hangers-on of the French court. Amyas grabbed the man by the ears to haul him out into the street, the man drew his sword, and Amyas drew his. The Frenchman fought with that fancy new unchristian point-fencing trick, pricking and stabbing and dancing about, and got his blade straight through Amyas’s left arm before Amyas could close with him. Then Amyas lost his temper, leapt in, wrenched the man’s wrist out of the way, and swung one clean side-stroke that sent the Frenchman’s head tumbling onto the tavern table, slandering no more.

“So perish all her enemies!” Frank said warmly.

Eustace, who had been pretending not to listen, rose stiffly to his feet. “I trust you do not number me among them?”

“As far as that speech goes, I half do,” Frank said. “For your own sake, I beg you to trust the word of the people who see our queen’s virtue every day, the same way we see the sun rise and the earth grow crops, instead of the tattle of cowardly back-stair rogues who curry favor with the Guises. Come, walk back with us to Appledore, and we’ll go home for breakfast.”

But Eustace declined, saying he had urgent business in Northam, then back to Bideford. He left the two brothers to laze on the beach for half an hour more, before they set off walking across the turf toward the little white fishing village of Appledore, two miles up the coast, at the meeting of the Torridge and Taw rivers.

But Eustace had lied. He had no business in Northam. His true destination was Appledore, same as theirs. He walked just far enough to the first house on the edge of Northam to salve his conscience, then struck sharp left across the fields, muttering prayers to the Virgin all the way, going miles out of his way—just as all crooked, Jesuit-trained souls tend to do, he only outwitted himself. For Amyas and Frank, walking straight down the open turf road as honest men do, arrived at the door of the *Mariner’s Rest* inn in Appledore just in time to see four of Thomas Leigh’s horses standing outside, saddled and packed, three men mounting them: Eustace, and two complete strangers.

“Now there’s one lie already this morning,” Amyas growled. “He told us he was going to Northam.”

“We don’t know he didn’t stop there first,” Frank said mildly.

“Bless your innocent heart,” Amyas laughed, clapping his big hand on Frank’s head and mimicking their mother’s tone. “Come on, coz, let’s pop into that little shop there and buy a pennyworth of whipcord.”

“What in the world do you want whipcord for?”

“To spin my top, of course. And so I have an excuse for lingering, so I can see exactly what our friend Eustace is hiding from us.”

They slipped into the tiny shop unspotted by the group at the inn door, and Amyas snickered at the sight of the third stranger, a tall meek-looking man draped in gaudy fancy clothes, a great plumed hat on his head, and a sword so long and heavy it swayed uselessly between his thin stick-like legs. He was hopping awkwardly, trying to clamber up the wrong side of his horse, his thin legs clawing at the horse’s ribs like a cat scratching an elder tree, so clumsy he looked like he had three legs.

“David wearing Saul’s armor,” Frank chuckled. “He never proved he can wield that sword.”

“Look,” Amyas said, snorting, “his third leg is about to turn into a tail. Someone ought to yank that belt of his in half a yard, for charity’s sake.”
