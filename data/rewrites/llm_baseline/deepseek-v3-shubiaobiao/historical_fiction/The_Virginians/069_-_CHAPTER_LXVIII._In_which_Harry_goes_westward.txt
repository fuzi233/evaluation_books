The heart aches at farewells, and so I shall spare you the details of Harry Warrington’s parting from his brother and friends. Were there not thousands of men in those days who faced the same wrench? Did not General Wolfe himself kiss his grieving mother goodbye—his father having died while the son was away winning glory at Louisbourg—and embrace his sweetheart one last time? Even Admiral Holmes, that stout old sea-dog, had his tearful farewells with Mrs. Holmes and their daughters before hoisting his flag aboard *The Dublin* and leading his squadron westward: *The Somerset, The Terrible, The Northumberland, The Royal William, The Trident, The Diana, The Seahorse*. And Admiral Saunders, who sailed a day later—was he not human too?

So the ships press on, cutting through the waves, while those left behind stand on the shore, watching the sails shrink into the horizon. Night falls, the ocean darkens, and though the fleet is gone, the hearts aboard still linger with those they have left. Prayers rise—silent, desperate—from the decks and the empty homes where wives kneel by cold beds, where children huddle close to their mothers, where sailors gaze at the stars through the salt spray. Tomorrow, the sun will rise on duty once more, and life will march forward.

George stays with Harry in Portsmouth, waiting for the wind to turn. He clasps Wolfe’s hand one last time, studying the man’s pale, determined face before the cannons roar and the bells clamor as the ships depart. The next day, George returns to London, to the selfish bustle of life—that relentless business which, by thirty, claims every man. He pauses at Harry’s old lodgings, staring at the half-burnt papers in the grate, then shakes off the melancholy and hurries to Dean Street, where a certain pair of bright eyes and whispering lips await him.

She is happy—too happy, she thinks, when poor Hetty mopes in the corner. *It’s wicked of me to be so glad when she’s so low!* Theo scolds herself, though the firelight hides her blushes. Then supper arrives, the lamps are lit, and the family gathers. The fleet’s destination is common talk now: enough force to crush the French in Canada, led by Wolfe—a man too frail in body but mighty in spirit. The ministers have staked everything on him.

After supper, as is his custom, Mr. Lambert calls his household to prayer—George included—and as they kneel, Theo and Hetty side by side, their thoughts chase the ships into the west. Night passes. Dawn comes. Life resumes.

For George, it means burying himself in work. *Carpezan* has had its run; the theater has moved on to fresher spectacles. Some grumble at the fickleness of audiences, but George, ever practical, counts his earnings—a tidy sum from Dodsley the bookseller, another from the theater manager—and sets to work on a new play, determined to outshine his first.

Months slip by in a haze of ink and whispered words. Love, in its first bloom, makes every hour radiant. The joy of reunion each morning, the lingering *goodnight*, the certainty that the world is kind and fortune smiles—who would trade such days for thrones or glory? Even the elders, watching from the sidelines, soften at the sight of youth’s sweet comedy.

In letters to Harry, George recounts the world they’ve left behind: the bungled charge at Minden, where Lord Sackville’s deaf ear (or was it cowardice?) cost them a decisive victory; the dying Duke, his body failing but his pride unbroken; the grand speeches in Parliament, all pomp and no substance. He writes of *Pocahontas*, his new play, and Hagan’s outrage at being cast as an Irish buffoon. (*As if he needs to imitate brogue when his own tongue is musical enough!*)

And then—should he tell? Should he stir the pot? Maria Esmond, the widow who once set her cap at Harry, now haunts the theater, eyes fixed on Hagan. Let her console herself; George has no interest in playing informer. Let the Esmonds chart their own ruin.

*O beati agricolae!* he muses. Dull as Virginia was, it spared them the corruption of London’s gaming hells and false friendships. Harry’s misfortunes began only when he left home. But now—now he has a chance to reclaim his honor, to rise as a soldier, while George contents himself with quieter dreams: a snug house, a well-stocked library, and a certain sweet voice murmuring across the hearth.

As for Miss Van den Bosch, polished to perfection at Kensington School—she is a vision of brown beauty, all grace and wit, though her pretensions amuse Theo and infuriate Hetty. She will marry a lord, no doubt, and leave Monument Yard far behind.

But George’s heart, full as it is, has no room for her. His letters trail into verses—extravagant, tender, foolish—the kind that youth alone can write without shame.

(And if you, dear reader, are too young to understand such raptures, wait. Your spring will come.)
