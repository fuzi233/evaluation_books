Since that fatal but delightful night in Grosvenor Place, Mr. Harry Foker's heart had been in a state of agitation so profound that you would hardly believe a man of his supposed philosophical stature could endure it. Consider his history: a boy of early wisdom, a cynic by trade, a gentleman of means who, by all rights of nature and finance, should have grown harder and colder with every passing year. Yet here he stood, a victim to Love, the All-Conqueror, having fallen for a woman.

That night, after bidding farewell to Arthur Pendennis at Lamb Court, the Back Kitchen held no charms for him. The gin-twists and deviled turkeys tasted like ash; the jokes of his cronies fell flat on his ears. Even when Mr. Hodgen, the singer of 'The Body Snatcher,' launched into a new chant even more dreadful and hilarious than the original, Foker offered the requisite "Bravo" as a polite gentleman must, yet he heard not a syllable of the song titled 'The Cat in the Cupboard.' Exhausted, he fled to his private apartments, but the downy pillow offered no respite. Before his eyelids could close, the fever of his soul erupted, and the image of Miss Amory materialized, declaring, "Here I am, I am your princess and beauty; you have discovered me, and shall care for nothing else hereafter."

Suddenly, his former pursuits seemed stale and distasteful. For years, he had barely tolerated the company of women of his own rank, dismissing them as "modest women"—a virtue he found tedious compared to the lively, theatrical charm of the *Mesdemoiselles*. His mother, though tender, failed to amuse him; his cousins, the daughters of the Earl of Rosherville, bored him beyond endurance. One was a blue geologist; another, a cigar-smoking horsewoman; the third, a High Church fanatic who turned her room into an oratory and fasted Fridays. Their father, the Earl of Gravesend, regarded Harry as a vulgar blasphemer, recalling the infamous incident where young Harry poked his uncle in the side with a billiard cue and called him an "old cock." Though the Earl played on with angelic sweetness, the insult lingered. To save his sister Agnes's heart and maintain a profitable financial exchange of IOUs, the Earl kept quiet, but the shame remained.

Furthermore, Harry was technically engaged. From infancy, Lady Ann Milton had been destined for him, a union ordained by her parents and his own father, who, despite being a man of few words, trusted the arrangement implicitly. "She won't have any money, but she's good blood," the old gentleman had reasoned, allowing Harry a final "fling" before the wedding. Consequently, Harry hung a tiny portrait of his cousin amidst his gallery of French prints, racing posters, and actresses, a modest token of a future that now felt like a cage. While Lady Ann stayed mostly at home in Gaunt Square, avoiding the whirlwind social scene her sisters enjoyed, her engagement to Harry was the only story about her that mattered.

When Foker awoke the next morning, Blanche's image glared at him from the bedclothes, her tune echoing in his ears. The room, usually a sanctuary of French art and the charming signatures of opera divas like Miss Calverley and Miss Rougemont, now seemed garish. A letter from Miss Calverley, written in a degage style of spelling and calling him "Hokey-pokey-fokey," lay beside a pile of tickets for Ben Budgeon's night at the Three-cornered Hat. These trivialities, once sources of joy, now filled him with dreary disgust. The prospect of standing dinner with the seventy-seven-year-old Mrs. Clavering at the Elephant and Castle repelled him utterly.

His mother, Lady Agnes, noted the pallor of his cheeks and the gloom of his countenance. She worried about the wicked Spratt's billiard hall and the Back Kitchen, suggesting he limit his wine intake. Harry, however, poured his heart out. He admitted it wasn't the billiards or the Back Kitchen—it was the fact that he was in love. He spoke of the Claverings' magnificent furniture and Sir Francis's agreeable nature, arranging an invitation for them to Grosvenor Street. But as he handed the card to his mother, he noticed a glaring omission.

"You have not asked Miss Whatdyecallem—Miss Emery, Lady Clavering's daughter."
"Oh, that little creature!" Lady Agnes cried. "No! I think not, Harry."
"We must ask Miss Amory," Foker insisted, stammering slightly. "I—I want to ask Pendennis; and—and he's very sweet upon her. Don't you think she sings very well, ma'am?"
Lady Agnes, trusting her son's judgment completely, added Miss Amory's name to the card.

Triumphantly, Harry embraced his mother and retreated to his chambers. There, he lay on his ottoman, brooding silently, devising wild schemes to woo the fair Miss Amory. His valet, Anatole, a polyglot of dubious origin who spoke all languages poorly, observed the disturbance in his master's spirits. As Harry prepared for the day, he became notoriously difficult to please, cursing pantaloons of every stripe and vowing his shirts were too loud. In a moment of sudden vanity, he asked Anatole if he could dress his hair, leading to a comical scramble for tongs that resulted in Harry sporting a head of curls as curly as a negro's.

Dressed in his finest, Harry set off, intending to cancel his appointment with Miss Calverley. However, upon reaching Pelham Corner, he realized he couldn't abandon the gathering of Poyntz and Rougemont. Instead, he commandeered his own carriage, driving straight to Lamb Court to spy on Miss Amory. Peering upward at her pink window curtains in Grosvenor Place, he felt a surge of satisfaction before rushing to Pen's chambers. But Pen was gone, attending to proofs at the printing office. Desperate, Harry raced to the *Pall Mall Gazette*, only to find Pen had left there as well.

Disconsolate, Harry decided to visit Lady Clavering at her own house, delivering two engraved cards to her footman, Jeames, in a speechless agony. The doors closed, shutting him out from the beloved object who was likely within earshot. Defeated, he rode off to Tattersall's, mounting his pony. Just as he was about to depart, Lady Clavering's splendid barouche arrived. Dodging around the Green Park, Harry followed the carriage, hoping to catch a glimpse of his enchantress. He spotted her emerging, clad in gossamer with a pink bonnet and light-blue parasol, accompanied by Miss Amory.

They visited Madame Rigodon's lace shop, Mrs. Wolsey's worsted emporium, and eventually stopped for ices at Hunter's. Harry, riding his pony alongside the carriage, watched them eat large pink ices in the sunshine, enduring the heat and the exertion until they finally departed for the Park. Following them, Harry dashed forward, desperate for a nod of recognition, a mere glance across the ditch where the horsemen assembled. Yet, despite the proximity, he exchanged not a single word with his charmer. Mute, he watched her return to the carriage, driven away amid ironic salutes from other gentlemen.

Back at the club, surrounded by friends like Mr. Eales and Mr. Poyntz, Harry indulged in gossip about Lady Clavering's finances and the scandalous rumors surrounding Sir Francis. When the topic turned to Miss Amory's singing, comparing her to a mermaid, Harry grew flushed and tearful, admitting his lack of scholarly knowledge in Homer and feeling inferior to the poets and musicians who surely surrounded her. "What am I," he thought, "compared to her? She's all soul... I am a poor, ignorant little beggar, good for nothing but Foker's Entire."

Just as his soliloquy reached its nadir, a cheery voice cried out, "Harry, Harry!" Looking up, he beheld his aunt, Lady Rosherville, and two of her daughters. Sitting in the back seat of a large carriage was Lady Ann, his betrothed. A terrible truth struck him: his fate, his arranged future, sat literally behind him. Pale and scared, he muttered greetings about a pressing engagement, waving goodbye as the great carriage rolled away, leaving the little man and his pony to vanish instantly. Inside, the Countess attended to her spaniel, Lady Lucy read sermons, and Lady Ann focused on a new novel, none aware of the storm raging in the hearts of the departing young gentleman.
