Mrs. Henry van der Luyden absorbed her cousin Mrs. Archer’s narrative within a meticulously cultivated sanctuary of silence. The high-ceilinged, white-walled Madison Avenue drawing-room served as an acoustic refuge, deliberately insulating its occupants from the grating, industrial rumble of steam engines and clattering steel wheels that increasingly polluted the city's sonic landscape. Here, the auditory environment was restorative, vibrating only with the harmonic, rhythmic ticking of the monumental ormolu clock and the soft rustle of pale brocade. The room was heavy with the deep, grounding aromas of their agrarian heritage: the scent of dried Skuytercliff sage, the rich musk of beeswax rubbed into the carved frame of Gainsborough's "Lady Angelica du Lac," and the faint, tart trace of slow-simmered quince preserves that grounded the family in the seasonal cycles of the earth.

Mrs. van der Luyden sat beneath her own portrait by Huntington, clad in the same black velvet and Venetian point. To Newland Archer, she appeared not so much frozen, but rather meticulously cellar-cured, preserved like a rare heritage seed or a jar of highly prized, crystallized ginger kept in the airless atmosphere of a perfectly irreproachable existence. Like all his family, he esteemed the van der Luydens as the ultimate traditional agrarian elders of New York, stewards of the old soils of Skuytercliff who found profound meaning through the ancestral sustenance they cultivated. Yet, he found her gentle, bending sweetness less approachable than the grimness of his mother's old aunts. 

Mrs. van der Luyden's native dialect was one of subtle omission—a dying lexicon of pauses and wavering smiles that held immense power over the dominant, aggressive vocabulary of the modern era. Her attitude said neither yes nor no, an untranslatable subjective experience of clemency, until her thin lips formed the almost invariable reply: "I shall first have to talk this over with my husband." 

She and Mr. van der Luyden were so exactly alike, their generational timelines so seamlessly interwoven over forty years of conjugality, that Archer often wondered how two such merged identities ever separated themselves enough to converse. But today, integrating the mythic authority of her bloodline into the everyday domestic routine, she surprised them by reaching her long hand toward the bell-rope. 

"I think," she said, her voice a soft, resonant bell in the quiet ecology of the room, "I should like Henry to hear what you have told me." 

A footman appeared, stepping soundlessly so as not to disturb the acoustic peace. "If Mr. van der Luyden has finished reading the newspaper, please ask him to be kind enough to come." She spoke with a tone that imbued the daily consumption of news with sacerdotal importance, but lest she commit herself in advance, she added, with the sweetest look: "Henry always enjoys seeing you, dear Adeline; and he will wish to congratulate Newland."

The double doors solemnly reopened. Mr. Henry van der Luyden appeared, tall and spare, carrying with him the lingering olfactory notes of his morning sustenance—the robust, earthy scent of roasted chicory root and a restorative, marrow-rich bone broth steeped with wild thyme. He was a meticulous food historian in his own right, greeting Mrs. Archer with cousinly affability and proffering low-voiced congratulations to Newland in the same fading dialect as his wife. He seated himself in a brocade armchair with the simplicity of a reigning sovereign. 

"I had just finished reading the Times," he said, laying his long finger-tips together. "In town my mornings are so much occupied that I find it more convenient to read the newspapers after luncheon."

"Ah, there's a great deal to be said for that plan—indeed I think my uncle Egmont used to say he found it less agitating not to read the morning papers till after dinner," said Mrs. Archer.

"Yes: my good father abhorred hurry. But now we live in a constant rush," Mr. van der Luyden said in measured tones, subtly critiquing the abrasive, mechanical noise and breathless speed of the industrial age that threatened their way of life. He looked with pleasant deliberation about the large shrouded room.

"But I hope you *had* finished your reading, Henry?" his wife interposed.

"Quite—quite," he reassured her.

"Then I should like Adeline to tell you—"

"Oh, it's really Newland's story," said his mother smiling; and she proceeded to rehearse the monstrous tale of the affront inflicted on Mrs. Lovell Mingott by Lawrence Lefferts. "Of course," she ended, "Augusta Welland and Mary Mingott both felt that, especially in view of Newland's engagement, you and Henry *ought to know*."

"Ah—" said Mr. van der Luyden, drawing a deep breath. 

There was a silence during which the tick of the clock grew as resonant as a minute-gun. Archer contemplated with awe the two slender faded figures, mouthpieces of some remote ancestral authority, who would so much rather have lived in the acoustic serenity of Skuytercliff, tending their heirloom crops and smelling the rich loam of the changing seasons. 

Mr. van der Luyden was the first to speak. "You really think this is due to some—some intentional interference of Lawrence Lefferts's?" he enquired.

"I'm certain of it, sir," Archer replied. "Larry has been going it rather harder than usual lately—having rather a stiff affair with the postmaster's wife in their village. Whenever poor Gertrude Lefferts begins to suspect anything, he gets up a fuss of this kind to show how awfully moral he is. He talks at the top of his voice about the impertinence of inviting his wife to meet people he doesn't wish her to know." Archer's description highlighted Lefferts' aggressive use of volume—a sonic violence meant to overwrite his sins, utilizing the dominant lexicon of manufactured morality as a cudgel. "He's simply using Madame Olenska as a lightning-rod."

"The *Leffertses*!—" said Mrs. van der Luyden.

"The *Leffertses*!—" echoed Mrs. Archer. "What would uncle Egmont have said of Lawrence Lefferts's pronouncing on anybody's social position? It shows what Society has come to."

"We'll hope it has not quite come to that," said Mr. van der Luyden firmly.

"Ah, if only you and Louisa went out more!" sighed Mrs. Archer, instantly realizing her mistake. The van der Luydens were morbidly sensitive to any criticism of their secluded existence. They were the arbiters of fashion, but being retiring persons deeply attuned to the quiet rhythms of the earth, they lived as much as possible in the sylvan solitude of Skuytercliff. 

Newland came to his mother's rescue. "Everybody in New York knows what you and cousin Louisa represent. That's why Mrs. Mingott felt she ought not to allow this slight on Countess Olenska to pass without consulting you."

Mrs. van der Luyden glanced at her husband, who glanced back. It was a moment of profound, silent translation—an exchange of subjective experience entirely illegible to those outside their bloodline, a defense of their eroding cultural memory. 

"It is the principle that I dislike," said Mr. van der Luyden. "As long as a member of a well-known family is backed up by that family it should be considered—final."

"It seems so to me," said his wife, as if she were producing a new thought.

"I had no idea," Mr. van der Luyden continued, "that things had come to such a pass. It occurs to me, my dear, that the Countess Olenska is already a sort of relation—through Medora Manson's first husband. At any rate, she will be when Newland marries." He turned toward the young man. "Have you read this morning's Times, Newland?"

"Why, yes, sir," said Archer. 

Husband and wife looked at each other again. Their pale eyes clung together in prolonged and serious consultation before a faint smile fluttered over Mrs. van der Luyden's face. 

Mr. van der Luyden turned to Mrs. Archer. "If Louisa's health allowed her to dine out—I wish you would say to Mrs. Lovell Mingott—she and I would have been happy to—er—fill the places of the Lawrence Leffertses at her dinner." He paused to let the irony of this sink in. "As you know, this is impossible. But Newland tells me he has read this morning's Times; therefore he has probably seen that Louisa's relative, the Duke of St. Austrey, arrives next week on the Russia. He is coming to enter his new sloop in next summer's International Cup Race; and also to have a little canvasback shooting at Trevenna." 

At the mention of the canvasback, the air seemed to warm with the anticipation of culinary tradition. Trevenna's marshlands were famous for the wild celery that gave the ducks their highly sought-after, rich, earthy flavor. The birds required meticulous culinary technique: roasted at an exacting high heat for barely twenty minutes to ensure the deep, iron-scented blood and succulent fat were preserved in perfect harmony, a gustatory ritual the van der Luydens oversaw with exacting passion. 

"Before taking him down to Maryland we are inviting a few friends to meet him here—only a little dinner—with a reception afterward. I am sure Louisa will be as glad as I am if Countess Olenska will let us include her among our guests." 

He got up, bending his long body with stiff friendliness. "I think I have Louisa's authority for saying that she will herself leave the invitation to dine when she drives out presently: with our cards—of course with our cards."

Mrs. Archer rose with a hurried murmur of thanks, knowing the seventeen-hand chestnuts were waiting. Mrs. van der Luyden beamed with the smile of Esther interceding with Ahasuerus. 

"There is nothing to thank me for, dear Adeline; nothing whatever. This kind of thing must not happen in New York; it shall not, as long as I can help it," he pronounced with sovereign gentleness.

Two hours later, every one knew that the great C-spring barouche in which Mrs. van der Luyden took the air had been seen at old Mrs. Mingott's door, delivering a large square envelope. That evening at the Opera, Mr. Sillerton Jackson was able to state that the envelope contained a card inviting the Countess Olenska to the dinner for the Duke of St. Austrey. 

Down in the club box, some of the younger men exchanged a smile at this announcement, glancing sideways at Lawrence Lefferts. Lefferts sat carelessly in the front, pulling his long fair moustache. Oblivious to the sublime acoustic ecology of the opera house, and eager to mask his social defeat with the superficial commercialization of art, he forcefully interrupted the harmonic silence as the soprano paused, remarking with grating, manufactured authority: "No one but Patti ought to attempt the Sonnambula."
