"It is a part of probability that many improbable things will happen." So Aristotle spoke in the *Poetics*, and though the words were ancient, they echoed sharply in Daniel Deronda's mind on the evening following his strange encounter with Mordecai. To a youth of ordinary temperament, such an adventure—a sudden collision with a fanatic Jew on a London bridge—might have served merely as a fleeting diversion, a curious episode to be filed away and forgotten. But Deronda was not of dull susceptibility. The event had struck him with a violence that demanded immediate scrutiny. As the usual reaction of his intellect set in, he began to dissect the roots of his turmoil, wrestling with the question of whether he must yield to the currents Mordecai had set in motion.

There was a distinct alarm within him. He felt, with a sharp pang, that he was being half-dominated by Mordecai's energetic certainty, and even more so by the fervent, almost desperate trust the old man placed in him. This consciousness roused his defenses. It was his nature to recoil from the "moral stupidity" of undervaluing what stood close at hand, blind to the monumental crises unfolding in his own life that history would later recognize as sacred. If he had read of this scene as a historical account from Rome, Greece, or Palestine, involving a young man dissatisfied with neutrality and seeking a deeper purpose, it would have appeared entirely natural for that figure to be profoundly moved. Why, then, should he feel shame for his own agitation simply because he dressed for dinner, wore a white tie, and inhabited a circle where possessing a serious conscience was often mocked as the solemn folly of taking oneself too seriously? That bugbear of the fashionable world—the lack of grave emotion passing for wit—made him shrink. Yet, he also feared allowing his course to be dictated by mere contagion, without the consent of his own reason, or letting a reverential pity for spiritual struggle sweep him along a dimly lit path before he understood where it led.

"What, after all, had really happened?" he asked himself.

He knew quite accurately what Sir Hugo would have answered: A consumptive Jew, driven mad by obstacles and hastening death, had latched onto Deronda as the living embodiment of some visionary image born of wedded hope and despair. Despair over his own failing life, coupled with an irrepressible hope in spreading his fanatical beliefs. The instance might appear odd or exceptional in form, but substantially, it was not rare. Fanaticism was not uncommon, though perhaps less visible than bankruptcy; it was abundant enough. While Mordecai waited on the bridge for the fulfillment of his visions, countless other men were convinced they held the mathematical key to the universe to supersede Newton, believing all physicists conspired to silence them; others claimed the metaphysical key, differing from the old wards by only a hair's breadth. Scattered everywhere, one might find a "terrible person"—some with a glittering eye, others with a preternaturally dull gaze—seeking the man who must hear them. Most possessed volumes difficult to print, or impossible to read. Mordecai, however, possessed a more pathetic aspect, a more passionate and penetrative speech than the average monomaniac. He was more poetical than a social reformer drawing diagrams of a new moral world, or an enthusiast preaching about sewage systems. Still, he fell under the same class. A man of the world would know what to think beforehand: to indulge him a little, perhaps, offering practical comfort, but regarding the likelihood of his notions holding the value he ascribed to them as nil. And concerning Mordecai's conviction that he had found a new executive self, it might well be preparing for him the worst of disappointments—that final, crushing disillusionment.

Deronda's ear caught these negative whispers, and he repeated them distinctly to himself. It was not the first time he had faced this question of the "family likeness" among the heirs of enthusiasm, whether prophets or dreamers, from the first believer in his own unmanifested inspiration down to the last inventor of a perpetual motion machine. The kinship of human passion, the sameness of mortal scenery, inevitably filled fact with burlesque and parody. Error and folly had their hecatombs of martyrs. If one reduced the grandest type of man known to history to an abstract statement of his qualities and efforts, he appeared in dangerous company: like Copernicus and Galileo, immovably convinced in the face of hissing incredulity. But so was the contriver of perpetual motion. One could not fairly test spirits by this sort of trial. If the goal was to avoid administering hemlock or sentences of banishment in the wrong case, nothing sufficed but a capacity to understand the subject-matter on which the immovable man was convinced, and a fellowship with human travail, both near and afar, to prevent scanning any deep experience lightly. Should we say, "Let the ages try the spirits"? Alas, we are the beginning of the ages, and justice depends solely on just judgments formed in separate human breasts—separate yet combined. Even steam-engines could not have been made without this condition; they must have remained in the mind of James Watt.

This train of thought was familiar enough to Deronda to save him from any contemptuous prejudgment of Mordecai, even if their communication had been free from that peculiar claim strangely ushered in by some long-growing preparation in the Jew's agitated mind. Considered rationally, this claim might seem dismissible as illusory and preposterous; but it was precisely what turned Mordecai's hold on him from an appeal to easy sympathy into a clutch on his struggling conscience. Consciences are not all cast in the same mold; they are the voice of sensibilities as various as our memories. And Deronda's conscience included sensibilities beyond the common, enlarged by his early habit of imagining himself into the experiences of others.

What was the claim this eager soul made upon him? "You must believe my beliefs—be moved by my reasons—hope my hopes—see the vision I point to!" To take such a demand as a direct obligation would have been preposterous, bordering on dishonesty. Deronda, looking back at the agitation of those moments, felt thankful that amidst his compassion he had preserved himself from the bondage of false concessions. The claim, moreover, hung on a supposition that might well be in discordance with the full fact: the supposition that Deronda himself was of Jewish blood. Was there ever a more hypothetical appeal?

Yet, since the age of thirteen, Deronda had associated the deepest experiences of his affections with what was a pure supposition: that Sir Hugo was his father. That hypothesis had been the source of passionate struggle within him; by its light, he had learned to subdue feelings and to cherish them. He was well accustomed to finding a motive in a conception that might be disproved, and to anticipating revelations that would alter his view of duty. To exist in a state of suspense, which was also a state of emotive activity and scruple, was a familiar attitude of his conscience.

Now, suppose that wish-begotten belief in his Jewish birth, and that extravagant demand for discipleship, were the foreshadowing of an actual discovery and a genuine spiritual result. Suppose Mordecai's ideas made a real conquest over Deronda's conviction? Nay, it was conceivable that as Mordecai needed and believed this, he had found an active replenishment of himself, so Deronda might receive from Mordecai's mind the complete ideal shape of that personal duty and citizenship which lay in his own thought like sculptured fragments, certifying some beauty yearned after but not traceable by divination.

As this possibility presented itself in his meditations, he was aware it would be called "dreamy," and he began to defend it. If the influence he imagined submitting to had been that of some honored professor, some authority in a seat of learning, some philosopher accepted as a voice of the age, would thorough receptiveness toward direction have been ridiculed? Only by those who hold it a sign of weakness to be obliged for an idea, preferring to hint that they have implicitly held in a more correct form whatever others have stated with sad shortcoming. After all, what was there but vulgarity in taking the fact that Mordecai was a poor Jewish workman, perhaps to be met on a sanded floor in the parlor of the *Hand and Banner*, as a reason for deciding beforehand that no spiritual force within him could determine a white-handed gentleman? There was a legend of Emperor Domitian, who, hearing of a Jewish family of the house of David from whom the ruler of the world was to spring, sent for them in alarm but quickly released them upon observing they had the hands of workmen. Being of the opposite opinion to the Rabbi who stood waiting at the gate of Rome, confident the Messiah would be found among the destitute. Both Emperor and Rabbi were wrong in trusting outward signs: poverty and poor clothes are no sign of inspiration, said Deronda to his inward objector, yet they have accompanied it in remarkable cases. To regard discipleship as out of the question because of these trappings would be mere dullness of imagination.

A more plausible reason for dismissing discipleship was the strain of visionary excitement in Mordecai, which turned his wishes into overmastering impressions, making him read outward facts as fulfillments. Was such a temper of mind likely to accompany the wise estimate of consequences, the only safeguard from fatal error, even for an ennobling motive? But it remained to be seen whether that rare conjunction existed in Mordecai: perhaps his nature was one where a wise estimate of consequences was fused in the fires of passionate belief, thereby determining the consequences he believed in.
