Clement exhaled, a long, weary sound that seemed to carry the weight of his own uncertainty. He wondered if he had been rash in engaging with a spirit so fiercely passionate. Yet, even in his youth, he had absorbed the hard lessons of ecclesiastical prudence. He knew the value of the pause—the sacred silence required when faced with difficulty, a time to withhold both action and speech until the fog of emotion lifted slightly.

So, he held his tongue and offered a silent prayer for wisdom. His only physical movement was the gentle withdrawal of his foot.

But the penitent reacted with a sudden, desperate violence. She threw her arms around the limb, letting out a piteous cry, clutching it convulsively as tears streamed down her face. In that moment, the crushing agony of shame mingled with her penitence, flushing her throat a vivid, alarming red as she trembled beneath him.

"My daughter," Clement said, his voice softening despite the turmoil, "take courage. Do not torment yourself further over this Gerard, who is no longer of consequence. As for me, I am Brother Clement, whom Heaven has sent to you today to offer comfort and aid in saving your soul. Since you have made me your confessor, I now claim your obedience."

"Oh, yes," she sobbed, her voice breaking.

"Leave this pilgrimage immediately and return to Rome," he instructed. "Penitence sought abroad holds little value. Where we dwell lie the true temptations we must defeat or perish; we must not flee to escape them, seeking others that appear more showy but are far less lethal. It is easy to wash the feet of strangers while masked; it is hard to remain merely meek and charitable with those who surround us."

"I'll never, never lay a finger on her again."

"Nay," Clement corrected gently. "I speak not only of servants, but of dependents, kin, and friends. This is your penance: at the last hour of the night and the first after matins, recall your sin and God's boundless goodness. Let this memory make you humble and gentle toward the faults of those around you. The world courts the rich; seek instead the poor—not the beggar, for they are often neither honest nor truly destitute, but rather those who blush to ask for help yet need it desperately. To give to them is to lend to Heaven. And finally, marry a good son of the Church."

"Me? I will never marry."

"You will marry within the year," Clement declared firmly. "I entreat and command you to wed one who fears God. You are but clay; mated poorly, you shall become nothing. But wed a worthy husband, and by God's grace, you may live as a pious princess and die a saint."

"I?" she whispered, disbelief coloring her tone.

"Thou," he replied, pointing a steady finger.

He then asked her to rise and begin the good work he had assigned her. She rose to her knees, removed her mask, and cast an eloquent, searching look upon him before lowering her eyes in meek submission.

"I will obey you as I would an angel," she said, her voice trembling. "How happy I am, yet how unhappy; for oh, my heart tells me I shall never look upon you again. I cannot leave until I have dried your feet."

"It is not necessary," Clement said. "I have excused you from this bootless penance."

"'Tis no penance to me," she insisted, her voice rising. "Ah! You do not forgive me if you will not let me dry your poor feet."

"So be it then," Clement said, resigning himself to her fervor. *Levius quid foemina,* he thought to himself—a woman is a lighter burden, easily swayed. Yet, these fragile creatures, who gravitate toward the small as celestial bodies do toward the great, possess flashes of angelic intelligence that surprise even the wise.

After the princess had dried the friar's feet, she looked up at him, tears glistening in her beautiful eyes. With a singular tenderness, she murmured, "I will have masses said for her soul. May I?"

A faint blush colored the monk's cheek, and his cold blue eyes moistened. It was a sudden, unexpected grace from one he had just rated so lowly.

"It is a gracious thought," he admitted. "Do as thou wilt; often such acts fall back upon the doer like blessed dew. I am your confessor, not hers; it is your soul I must strive to save, or woe betide my own. My daughter, my dear daughter, I see good and ill angels battling for your soul this very moment. Fight on your own side. Do you remember all I have bade you?"

"Remember!" she affirmed. "Sweet saint, every syllable of yours is graved in my heart."

"But one word more, then. Pray much to Christ, and little to his saints."

"I will."

"And that is the best counsel I have to offer. So, let us part on this. You to the place that suits you best—your father's house—and I to my holy mother's work."

"Adieu," she faltered. "Adieu, thou that I have loved too well, hated too ill, known and revered too late; forgiving angel, adieu—for ever."

The monk caught her words, though they faltered in a sigh.

"For ever?" he cried aloud, his voice suddenly ardent. "Christians live 'for ever' and love 'for ever,' but they never part 'for ever.' They part as the earth and sun separate, only to meet more brightly in a little while. You and I part here for life. But what is our life? It is but one line in the great story of the Church, whose son and daughter we are; one handful in the sand of time, one drop in the ocean of 'For ever.' Adieu—for the little moment called 'a life!' We part in trouble, but we shall meet in peace; we part as creatures of clay, but we shall meet as immortal spirits; we part in a world of sin and sorrow, but we shall meet where all is purity and divine love, where no ill passions exist, but only Christ and His saints clad in white. There, in the turning of an hourglass, in the breaking of a bubble, in the passing of a cloud, she, thou, and I shall meet again. We shall sit at the feet of angels, archangels, apostles, and saints, beaming with joy unspeakable in the light of the shadow of God upon His throne, FOR EVER—AND EVER—AND EVER."

Thus they parted. The monk stood erect, his eyes turned heavenward, glowing with the sacred fire of zeal. The princess retreated slowly, turning more than once to cast a lingering glance of awe and tender regret upon that inspired figure.

She returned home subdued and purified. Clement, in due course, reached Basel and resumed his duties, teaching in the university and preaching throughout the town and its neighborhood. He led a life that could be summarized in two words: deep study and mortification. Those who knew him had already glimpsed the depths of his soul. In Basel, he advanced in holy zeal and knowledge, and the brethren of his order began to see in him a true descendant of the saints and martyrs.
