Elinor's reliance on Lucy's word was absolute; in this instance, the probability of deceit was zero. To imagine that Lucy would fabricate a falsehood of such magnitude, driven by no tangible gain, was logically impossible. Every piece of circumstantial evidence—their shared past at Mr. Pratt's, Edward's recent melancholic demeanor near Plymouth, his evident dissatisfaction with his prospects, his ambiguous conduct toward Elinor, and the startling intimacy Lucy displayed regarding Norland and the Dashwoods—conspired to form an irrefutable chain of facts. The letter, the ring, the portrait; these were not mere whispers but solid proof that Edward had wronged her. For a fleeting moment, fueled by righteous indignation, Elinor allowed herself to focus solely on her own betrayal. Yet, the tide of her reasoning quickly shifted.

Had Edward been consciously deceiving her? Had he feigned affection he did not possess? Or was his engagement to Lucy merely a contract of convenience rather than a union of hearts? Elinor dismissed the notion that his current feelings for Lucy were genuine. Whatever sentiment existed in the past, it was dead. His heart belonged entirely to hers—a truth confirmed by the collective observation of her mother, sisters, and Fanny during their days at Norland. It was not a delusion born of her own vanity. He loved her. This realization acted as a powerful solvent, dissolving the sharp edges of her anger. How much easier forgiveness became when framed as pity for a man who had squandered his own happiness.

Edward's error in staying at Norland, allowing his influence over her to grow beyond propriety, was indefensible. But if he had harmed her, he had inflicted far greater damage upon himself. While Elinor might eventually recover her tranquility, Edward's outlook seemed bleak. Could he truly find contentment with Lucy Steele? With his known integrity, delicacy, and cultivated mind, could he settle for a partner described as illiterate, artful, and selfish? The infatuation of youth might have blinded him to these flaws, but four years of rational maturity would surely have exposed them. Meanwhile, Lucy's immersion in inferior society and frivolous pursuits had likely eroded the very simplicity that once complemented her beauty. Furthermore, if familial opposition had previously weighed heavily on a hypothetical marriage between Edward and Elinor, the disparity in status and fortune now made such a match virtually impossible.

These reflections, arriving in painful succession, transformed Elinor's grief. She wept not for her own loss, but for his. Convinced that she bore no blame for their separation and believing Edward retained her esteem, she summoned the strength to conceal the crushing blow from her mother and Marianne. When she rejoined them at dinner, barely two hours after her hopes extinguished, her composure was flawless. To the outside observer, there was no trace of mourning; yet secretly, Elinor navigated a labyrinth of obstacles destined to sever her forever from her love, while Marianne dwelt on the perceived perfection of a man whose heart she believed wholly possessed.

This necessity of secrecy, though taxing, proved to be a relief. Spared the agony of explaining her pain to those who would only amplify it with their own sorrow, and protected from the harsh judgment Marianne and her mother might level against Edward based on their excessive partiality for Elinor, she found solace in isolation. Their counsel offered no aid; their tears would only deepen her distress. Alone, bolstered by her own good sense, Elinor maintained an unshakeable firmness and an invariable cheerfulness that belied the poignancy of her regret.

Despite the initial discomfort of her first conversation with Lucy, Elinor felt an urgent desire to revisit the topic. She needed further particulars of their engagement, a clearer understanding of Lucy's true sentiments, and above all, she sought to demonstrate to Lucy that she viewed the matter strictly as a friend. Elinor feared her earlier agitation had cast doubt on this neutrality. Lucy's apparent jealousy was evident; Edward's high praise of Elinor, coupled with his willingness to confide such a vital secret to someone he had barely known, suggested a rivalry. Even Sir John's jesting remarks carried weight. Given Elinor's certainty of Edward's love, Lucy's jealousy was a natural reaction, and her very confession served as proof of it. Why else reveal the affair unless to warn Elinor of Lucy's superior claims and instruct her to stay away?

Resolved to act with honor and to minimize contact with Edward, Elinor nonetheless determined to reassure Lucy that her own heart remained unwounded. Since she could expect no new information that would cause further pain, she trusted her ability to recount the particulars with equanimity. However, opportunity was scarce. The weather rarely permitted walks where privacy could be secured, and their evening gatherings at the park or cottage were orchestrated by Sir John and Lady Middleton for entertainment, not discourse. The hostess and her husband viewed such meetings as occasions for eating, drinking, laughter, and noisy games like cards or "Consequences," leaving no room for private conversation.

Two such gatherings had passed without success until one morning Sir John visited the cottage, requesting that the entire party dine with Lady Middleton at the Park. Citing his obligation to attend the club in Exeter, he noted that Lady Middleton would otherwise face solitude, save for her mother and the Miss Steeles. Elinor recognized this as a strategic opening. A mixed company, guided by the tranquil presence of Lady Middleton rather than her husband's boisterous direction, offered a safer environment for private talk. Accepting the invitation, along with Margaret (with maternal permission) and a reluctant Marianne (persuaded by her mother's inability to tolerate seclusion), the group departed.

Lady Middleton was relieved of her anticipated loneliness, but the gathering proved as insipid as Elinor predicted. The conversation lacked novelty, offering nothing of interest in the dining room or drawing room. Children trailed behind them, rendering the drawing room inaccessible for any serious discussion. Only upon removing the tea things did they retreat to the card table. Elinor wondered at her own optimism regarding the possibility of conversation amidst the park's distractions. Everyone rose for a game of Casino.

"I am glad," Lady Middleton remarked to Lucy, "that you are not finishing poor little Annamaria's basket tonight. Working filigree by candlelight must strain your eyes. We shall make amends to the dear little angel tomorrow, and I hope she will not mind the delay."

This cue was sufficient for Lucy to recall her duties instantly. "Indeed, you are greatly mistaken, Lady Middleton," Lucy replied promptly. "I am merely awaiting confirmation of whether you can manage without me, for I would not disappoint the little angel for all the world. If you require me at the card table, I intend to complete the basket after supper."

"You are very kind. I hope it does not harm your eyes. Will you ring for some working candles? My poor little girl would be terribly disappointed if the basket remains unfinished tomorrow, especially since I promised it would be done."

Lucy immediately moved her worktable closer and resumed her seat with alacrity, her cheerful demeanor suggesting that crafting a filigree basket for a spoiled child brought her no lesser delight than any other pursuit. Lady Middleton then proposed a rubber of Casino. All objected except Marianne, who, disregarding the conventions of general civility, exclaimed, "Your Ladyship will excuse me—I detest cards. I shall retire to the piano-forte; I have not touched it since it was tuned." Without further ceremony, she turned and walked to the instrument. Lady Middleton looked visibly relieved that she had never uttered such a blunt remark.

"Marianne can never stay away from that instrument for long, ma'am," Elinor interjected, smoothing over the offense. "Nor do I wonder; it is the finest-toned piano-forte I have ever heard."

With the remaining five drawing their cards, Elinor seized the moment. "Perhaps," she continued, "if I should cut out, I could assist Miss Lucy Steele in rolling her papers. There is still so much work remaining on the basket that it seems impossible for her to finish it alone this evening. I should be delighted to contribute if she permits me a share."

"Indeed, I shall be immensely obliged for your help," Lucy cried. "I find there is more to do than I anticipated, and it would be dreadful to disappoint dear Annamaria."

"Oh! That would be terrible," Miss Steele agreed. "Dear little soul, how I adore her!"

"You are very kind," Lady Middleton said to Elinor. "Since you enjoy the work, perhaps you would prefer not to cut in until the next rubber, or shall you take your chance now?"

Elinor joyfully accepted the first proposal. Through a touch of address that Marianne could never muster, she achieved her goal while pleasing Lady Middleton. Lucy made room for her with ready attention, and the two rivals sat side by side at the same table, harmoniously collaborating on the same task.

Nearby, the piano-forte played on. Marianne, lost in her own music and reverie, had forgotten everyone else's existence. Seizing the cover of the instrument's noise, Elinor judged it safe to introduce the subject of her concern without risking eavesdropping at the card table.
