The dust of Solomon's Great Road settled into the cracks of our boots, marking the final stretch of our pilgrimage into the heart of Kukuanaland. It required no exhaustive chronicle to recount the two days of travel; suffice it to say that the landscape itself seemed to breathe wealth as we advanced. The kraals multiplied, vast enclosures ringed by belts of cultivated earth, each a testament to a society where war was not a profession but a state of being. Here, as among the Germans, the Zulus, and the Masai, every able-bodied man was a soldier, rendering the entire nation a living army poised for offense or defense. Thousands of warriors surged ahead of us, a tidal wave of humanity rushing toward Loo for the great annual review, a spectacle of martial splendor that rivaled any vision of glory I had ever witnessed.

As the sun dipped below the horizon on the second day, we paused atop a ridge overlooking the valley. Below us lay Loo, a native metropolis of staggering magnitude, stretching five miles in circumference with outlying camps projecting like the ribs of a giant beast. A peculiar horseshoe-shaped hill loomed to the north, a landmark we were fated to know intimately. Through the center of the settlement, bisecting the grassy expanse, flowed a river bridged at multiple points, a familiar sight from our vantage on Sheba's Breasts. Far in the distance, sixty or seventy miles away, three snow-capped peaks jutted from the plain, forming a triangle that defied the gentle rolling hills of the region. Unlike the smooth, rounded contours of Sheba's Breasts, these mountains were sheer, precipitous walls of stone.

Infadoos broke our reverie, pointing a gaunt finger toward the jagged trio. "The road ends there," he announced.

"Why?" I pressed.

"Who knows?" he shrugged, his expression unreadable. "The mountains are riddled with caves, separated by a great pit. It is there the sages of old descended to retrieve whatever treasures they sought, and it is there now that our kings lie in the Place of Death."

"What did they seek?" I asked, my curiosity sharpening.

"Nay, I know not. Only those who have fallen from the Stars possess that knowledge," he replied, his eyes darting away with a sudden intensity. Clearly, he held secrets he deemed unfit for casual discourse.

"Yes," I continued, seizing the thread, "you are correct regarding the Stars. I have heard the ancients climbed these peaks for bright stones, precious playthings, and yellow iron."

"My lord is wise," Infadoos retorted, his tone chillingly detached. "I am but a child, unworthy to converse with my lord on such matters. Seek Gagool the Old at the king's residence; she possesses wisdom equal to your own." With that, he departed, leaving us to our speculations.

Turning to my companions, I identified the peaks. "There," I declared, "are Solomon's diamond mines."

Umbopa, lost in one of his characteristic trances, snapped out of it at my words. "Yes, Macumazahn," he interjected in Zulu, "the diamonds are certainly there, and you shall claim them, for white men love toys and coinage."

"How do you know this, Umbopa?" I demanded sharply, distrust rippling through me at his cryptic demeanor.

"I dreamed it in the night, white men," he laughed, turning on his heel before I could press further.

"Now what," Sir Henry muttered, "is our black friend scheming? He knows far more than he chooses to reveal. By the way, Quatermain, has he heard anything of my brother?"

"Not a word," I replied. "He has questioned every ally he has made, yet none have seen a white man within these borders."

"Do you believe he reached this place at all?" Good suggested grimly. "We arrived here by sheer miracle; is it plausible he navigated this labyrinth without the map?"

"I do not know," Sir Henry said, his face clouded with gloom, "yet I harbor a conviction that I shall find him."

The sun vanished abruptly, plunging the land into darkness as if a curtain had been drawn. There was no gradual twilight, no soft transition; in these latitudes, the shift from day to night was as absolute and irreversible as the passage from life to death. Yet, the void did not linger. A glow ignited in the west, followed by shafts of silver light, until the full moon erupted, bathing the plain in a luminous radiance that shot arrows of brilliance across the sky. We stood transfixed, watching the stars dim before this chastened majesty, our spirits lifted by a beauty beyond description. Though my life had been rugged, I counted this moment among the few things worth living for: to witness the moon shine over Kukuanaland.

Our meditation was shattered by Infadoos's polite interruption. "If my lords are rested, we shall proceed to Loo, where a hut awaits. The moon is bright, ensuring we shall not falter on the path."

We agreed, and within an hour we reached the town's outskirts. The settlement appeared infinite, a sea of campfires mapping out its vast boundaries. Good, ever prone to dark humor, dubbed it "Unlimited Loo." Soon we encountered a moat guarded by a drawbridge, where the clash of steel and a hoarse sentinel's challenge greeted us. Infadoos supplied a password I failed to catch, earning a respectful salute before we crossed into the central thoroughfare of this great grass city. After nearly half an hour of traversing endless rows of huts, Infadoos halted before a cluster of dwellings surrounding a courtyard of powdered limestone. "Here are your 'poor' quarters," he informed us.

Inside, we found huts superior to any we had previously encountered, each furnished with a comfortable bed of tanned skins atop mattresses of aromatic grass. Food awaited us; once we had washed in water provided in earthenware jars, handsome young women emerged bearing roasted meats and corn cobs served delicately on wooden platters. They presented the offerings with deep bows, their movements fluid and graceful.

We ate and drank, and upon our request to consolidate our sleeping arrangements into a single hut—a decision that elicited amused smiles from the attentive maidens—we collapsed onto the beds, utterly exhausted by our long journey.

Awakening revealed a sun high in the heavens. The female attendants, seemingly unburdened by conventional modesty, stood within the hut, ready to assist us in our preparations.

"Make ready, indeed," Good grumbled. "With nothing but a flannel shirt and boots, the task is trivial. Pray, ask them for my trousers, Quatermain."

I inquired, only to be told that these sacred garments had already been delivered to the king, who intended to receive us in the forenoon. To their evident surprise, we summoned the young ladies outside and proceeded to dress in the best manner circumstances allowed. Good even shaved the right side of his face anew, while we forbade him touching the left, where a respectable crop of whiskers now grew. For myself, a vigorous wash and combing sufficed; Sir Henry's yellow locks cascaded nearly to his shoulders, transforming him into an ancient Dane, while my own scruffy beard had doubled in length, exceeding my preferred limit of half an inch.

Breakfast and a pipe later, a messenger arrived—not less than Infadoos himself—to announce that King Twala was prepared to receive us. We demurred politely, citing our lingering fatigue and requesting a delay until the sun climbed higher. It is prudent, when dealing with cultures unfamiliar with diplomatic nuance, to avoid appearing hurried; they often mistake haste for arrogance or servility. Thus, though we burned with eagerness to meet Twala, we sat waiting an hour, utilizing the time to assemble gifts from our meager stock: the Winchester rifle formerly wielded by poor Ventvögel and a supply of beads. We resolved to offer the rifle and ammunition to His Royal Highness, saving the beads for his wives and courtiers. We had already distributed a few to Infadoos and Scragga, observing their delight at seeing such novelties. Finally declaring ourselves ready, we departed for the audience hall, Umbopa carrying the rifle and beads.

After a few hundred yards, we entered an enclosure fifty times larger than our lodging area, covering at least six or seven acres. Along the perimeter fence stood rows of huts housing the king's wives. Directly opposite the gateway, across the open space, rose a massive, solitary hut where the sovereign resided. The remainder of the grounds was filled with companies of warriors, mustered to the tune of seven or eight thousand. These men stood motionless as statues as we advanced, presenting a tableau of terrifying grandeur with their waving plumes, glinting spears, and iron-backed ox-hide shields.

Before the great hut, several stools were arranged. Upon Infadoos's signal, we took our seats, Umbopa standing behind us. Infadoos positioned himself by the hut's entrance. We waited, amidst a silence so profound it felt heavy, aware of eight thousand pairs of eyes fixed upon us. It was a trying ordeal, yet we endured it with dignity. Eventually, the hut doors swung open. A gigantic figure emerged, draped in a splendid tiger-skin kaffir, followed by the boy Scragga and a withered creature resembling a monkey, swaddled in a fur cloak. The giant seated himself on a stool, Scragga took his station behind, and the monkey scurried into the shade to squat.
