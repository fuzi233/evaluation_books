The last of General Braddock’s party had been escorted to their chambers, the candles guttering low in the heavy Virginia summer air that clung like damp linen to Castlewood’s wood panelling, when the two Warrington brothers slipped away to their own garret bedroom to unburden the full weight of their indignation at the day’s shocking revelation. Neither would abide the match their mother was clearly contemplating: the heir to the ancient line of Esmond marquises reduced to marrying a 23-year-old ex-land-surveyor of no great birth, who would step straight into the role of their stepfather, lording it over two nineteen-year-old boys of noble blood? It was monstrous, unthinkable.

Harry’s first rash impulse was to storm directly to his mother’s bedchamber, where her two favourite bondswomen were even then unpinning her lace fichu and unlatching the paste diamond buckles she had worn for the feast, to declare that if she went through with the unnatural union, they would abandon her forever and live off their small inherited tract of upcountry land alone.

George, cooler and sharper, advocated a far more decisive solution, which he laid out for his admiring younger brother. “No lady can possibly marry a man who has crossed swords or exchanged a pistol shot with her own son,” he reasoned, his pale cheeks flushed bright from the stolen sips of their grandfather’s old Bordeaux he had snuck at dinner. “Not if he wounded us, or we wounded him, or either of us fell dead on the field. Harry, we must call him out.”

Harry saw the perfect logic of the plan at once, and gaped at his brother’s immense sagacity. “You are right, George! Mother would never marry her own son’s murderer, would she? If we pink him the whole question is settled, just as old Mr. Dempster used to say in our Latin lessons. Shall I send my boy off this very instant with a challenge to Colonel Washington?”

“My dear Harry,” the elder replied, with quiet complacency over the duel he had already fought and survived at Quebec, “you are not yet accustomed to affairs of honour. We cannot insult a guest under our own roof – the rules of honour forbid such inhospitality. But once we ride out through the park gates? The moment the iron latch clicks shut behind us, we may speak our full minds.”

“That we can, by George!” Harry cried, squeezing his brother’s hand tight. A moment later his face flushed bright crimson, and he leaned in to plead: “This ought to be my turn, brother. You are already bound for the campaign with Braddock, you cannot claim every scrap of glory. I deserve this affair, I do.”

“The head of the house must take the lead, my dear,” George declared with a haughty, regal air. “If I fall, you will have your chance to avenge me. But I must fight George Washington. I hate him the worst, after all – it was he who counselled mother to set that brute Ward on me and lock me up like a common criminal.”

“Ah, George, you ought to forgive and forget,” the more easy-going Harry protested.

“Forgive? I will never, not as long as I can draw breath. You cannot command a man to throw his own memory out the window. A wrong that stung yesterday is still a wrong tomorrow. I never did a man harm intentionally in my life, and I will not suffer one to go unpunished if I can help it. Washington is our enemy, mine above all others. He has already interfered in my life to do me harm, and now he means to steal our mother and our home. We must punish him.”

Harry, who worshipped his brother as a near-perfect hero, bowed to his judgement as ever, and they retired for the night, George giving strict instructions that they must remain perfectly civil to their mother’s guests for the rest of their stay, for decency’s sake.

We shall name no names to say which of Braddock’s staff ended the night carried tipsy to their beds by grinning enslaved grooms – the habit of heavy drinking was universal among 18th century British army officers, and no gentleman of the time thought twice about tipping a dozen bottles of Bordeaux between a dinner party. Even General Braddock, a famously hard drinker, was soon snoring peacefully off the night’s feast. Madam Esmond saw her guests’ inebriated state without the least shock, and was up before dawn brewing spiced, cooling toddy to soothe their throbbing heads, which the servants carried up to their chambers. At breakfast, one young English officer teased Benjamin Franklin (who never touched wine at all) for turning down the morning draught, saying he missed two equal pleasures: the pleasant buzz of drunkenness, and the delicious toddy that cured the hangover.

Braddock’s new Virginian aide-de-camp, Colonel Washington, sat perfectly sober and calm at the table, unruffled and clear-eyed. The British officers laughed and vowed they would soon break him in to the ways of the regular army, but Washington only gravely replied that he had no interest in learning that particular part of British military education.

Madam Esmond, run ragged overseeing the great dinner and then the follow-on breakfast, had barely time to watch her sons closely, and only noted that George was being scrupulously polite to her favourite Colonel Washington, just as he was to all their guests. Before Braddock left, he held a private audience with the widow, formally offering to take George onto his personal staff for the coming campaign. Madam Esmond did not make a fuss over the known hard drinking habits of the British officer corps, saying stoutly to her companion Mrs. Mountain later: “My father never drank, that is true, but half the finest company in England do, and my boy must take his chances with the rest of the world for good and ill.” Braddock clapped George heartily on the back, bade him report to the camp at Frederick three days hence, when the expedition would march.

At last the General’s great coach was pulled around, his cavalry escort pranced into formation, and the other guests mounted their horses. The household stood on the veranda steps to see them off: the trumpeter blew a shrill blast, the enslaved servants cheered “Huzzay! God save the King!” as Braddock’s coach rumbled away down the drive.

As the boys turned back up the steps, Washington was lingering to take one last private farewell of their mother. The widow was tearful, visibly moved, for she would not see him again before he left for the dangerous campaign, and George watched the exchange with a sharp, bitter twinge of angry scorn. He put on a perfectly bright, amiable face, and said: “We will go boot up at once, Colonel, and ride a little ways with you to speed you on your journey.” Their horses were already saddled at the stables, and the three men set off at once, their grooms trailing behind them. Mrs. Mountain watched them ride away, sighing in huge relief that Washington had left the house without an open quarrel with the boys, and without making a formal declaration of love to their mother.

Washington was delighted and confused by George’s sudden friendliness. The long history of kindness between the Esmonds and him, the tender farewell with Madam Esmond, the shared danger of the coming campaign, all made him eager to forget the past months of petty friction between him and the boys. He talked amiably about how certain the expedition’s success was – two thousand crack British regulars would easily crush any force the French and their native allies could muster, he argued, and no loyal subject of King George II had more faith in the power of the regular army’s veteran tactics. Only Harry was quiet and withdrawn, hanging back at his brother’s side instead of pressing eagerly to ride next to Washington as he had always done before, which the Colonel mistook for simple anxiety over George’s coming departure.

They rode through the sweltering, hazy afternoon until they reached Benson’s, a rough log roadhouse that doubled as a recruiting station for Halkett’s regiment, where a handful of officers were already loitering on the porch over a big bowl of Virginian toddy. The boys’ horses were led off to be watered and fed, and they stepped into the taproom for a quick bite. George was unnaturally loud and lively, while Harry’s face was pale and drawn, as if he was the one riding off to war.

“One would think it was you off to fight the French and Indians, Squire Harry,” the tavern keeper Benson joked.

“I may fear for my brother, even if I would bear my own share of danger well enough,” Harry mumbled.

Colonel Washington was quick to leap to his defence. “No one here doubts the courage of any man of the Warrington family.”

“You do us great honour to say so, Colonel,” George bowed low, a thin, cold smile on his face. “I assure you, we can hold our own if need be.”

Harry caught his brother’s eye, and read the intent there, so alarming he whispered: “You are not going to do it here and now? For God’s sake let me take this turn. You are going off to campaign, you cannot risk this. We might be wrong, George.”

“Psha, we are not,” George muttered back, steady as steel. “It must be now. Do not be afraid. I will find an excuse that leaves no trace of our original plan.”

He called over to the two half-drunk officers on the porch, Captain Grace and Captain Waring, and invited them to share the toddy bowl, declaring the drink was on him. George called Washington over to join them, and when the Colonel politely said he was not thirsty, George jeered that he need not fear paying for the drink. “When the King’s health is proposed, no gentleman officer can refuse a sip,” he announced, raising the bowl. The toast was unassailable: Grace, Waring, Harry all drank, and Washington had to take a small sip to avoid looking disloyal. Next Grace proposed a toast to the Duke of Cumberland and the Army, and again Washington was forced to drink.

“You do not seem to stomach the toast very well, Colonel,” George sneered.

“I tell you again I do not wish to drink,” Washington snapped, stamping his foot. “The army would serve the crown far better if we spent less time drinking endless toasts and more time drilling.”

Drunk and indignant, Captain Grace slammed his fist on the table. “A British officer never neglects that duty! Any man who refuses to drink the Duke’s toast ought to be court-martialled!”

“Man, you are completely intoxicated!” Washington roared, jumping to his feet.

“Insult me, you provincial militia captain!” Grace shrieked.

“We all heard it,” George cut in smoothly. “This is my liquor, my invitation, and I am shocked that Colonel Washington would speak so abusively to my guest Captain Grace.”

The last of Washington’s patience snapped. “You insolent young jackanapes! I have put up with your impudence for months now, only out of respect for your mother, your grandfather, and your brother, or I would have – I would have dragged you over my knee and whipped you like the snarling little puppy you are!”

Harry sprang to his feet, face blazing red. “That is an insult to both of us, George Washington! You will apologise, or give us the reparation due to gentlemen.”

For a second, Washington’s rage melted, a deep tenderness filling his breast for the two lads he had watched grow up from boys. He stepped back, bewildered. “My words were hasty, I did not mean – tell me what has been the meaning of George’s behaviour to me for months, and I will make amends.”

But George’s rage was unyielding, his black eyes blazing with pure unforgiving hate. “You dodge every question just as you dodged the toast. I am no small boy to suffer your arrogance. You have publicly insulted me in a public room, and I demand satisfaction.”

Before Washington could reply, the slurring Captain Grace staggered forward, wailing that Washington had insulted him too, and Captain Waring declared he could not stand by while a visiting militia colonel called his comrade a hog. Washington shouted that it was a full conspiracy, all of them drunk and ganging up on him, and he would meet any of them, all of them, drunk or sober, for a duel, one after another.

“Pistols, not small swords,” George said coldly. “We beat you every time at fencing from our old bouts when we were boys. Pistols are fairer.”

“As you wish,” Washington said, his face falling with deep, unfeigned sorrow. “God forgive you, George. God pardon you, Harry, for dragging me into this quarrel.”

“Forgive? I will never forgive that boyhood slight, when you acted as overseer at Castlewood once and threatened to whip me like a negro field hand for climbing that forbidden apple tree!” George snarled, gritting his teeth. “I am no slave, George Washington, and I never will be. That insult is long past due to be settled.”

Washington stared at him, stunned, realising the entire months of friction had stemmed from one petty, forgotten boyhood quarrel. He turned to Harry, heartbroken. “And you bear this malice too?”

“I stand by my brother,” Harry mumbled, turning away from Washington’s sad face, and squeezing George’s hand.

Washington sighed heavily. “Very well. I have my own pistols at my saddle. You did not bring yours, so send your groom back to Castlewood to fetch them. That will give me time to write my last letters. I will not run from this.”

Benson the tavern keeper, an old frontier veteran who owed Madam Esmond his wife’s life after she tended her through a dangerous lying-in years before, nodded. “I’ll stand by to see fair play, Colonel. No cheating here. And I don’t need to tell you those boys are both crack shots – George can hit an ace of spades at a hundred paces, no trouble at all.”

The summer afternoon hung thick and heavy over the clearing, the air still and sweltering, as the three men made their arrangements for the duel, under the haze of Virginia’s hot, angry sun.
