All through the wild thunderstorm that raged over medieval Sevenbergen, Ghysbrecht Van Swieten tossed on his bed, too gnawed by anxious, greedy dread to sleep a single wink. He was a man whose nerve always crumpled at the roar of thunder and flash of lightning, else he would have marched at the head of the party sent to ransack Peter’s cottage the night before. No sooner had the last distant rumble of the tempest died away entirely, and the first pale streaks of dawn bleached the storm clouds thin, than he crept down his creaking oak stairs without rousing his household, saddled his own stubborn mule, and rode hard for the sign of the Three Kings at the edge of the village.

When he pushed open the tavern door, the reek of stale ale and smoldering peat hit him, and there were his men, dead to the world: some splayed across the rough wooden chairs, some draped over the table tops with half-empty tankards still clutched loose in their fists, others curled atop the dirty rush-strewn earthen floor. He kicked the nearest man hard in the ribs to rouse them all, snarling in a frothing fury, and listened to their bungled tale of the failed search, every line larded with their own inflated praise for their supposed zeal and diligence.

“Fools! To think I ever sent you off without me!” the burgomaster roared. “I’d stake every penny I own the man we hunt was hiding right under your noses the whole time. Did ye think to look under the girl Margaret’s bed?”

“Pah,” grunted one of the men, yawning and rubbing the fresh bruise on his side. “There’s not space enough under there for a small child, let alone a full grown man.”

“How dare ye speak of what ye do not know, when ye never stooped to look?” Ghysbrecht snapped, his round face purpling with rage. “Ye should have looked under her bed, pried apart the very frame of it if need be, and tapped every last panel in the house with your knives to seek out hollow hiding spots. Up with you now, the whole lot of you, and I will show you how a proper search is done.”

The burly Dierich Brower climbed to his feet, shaking straw off his breeches and cracking his thick knuckles. “If that man is still hiding in that little hovel when we get there, you may call me a plough horse and no man for the rest of my days.”

Within minutes, Peter’s cottage was ringed by the whole party. The fiery old burgomaster tossed his mule’s reins to Jorian Ketel, and stormed into the house with Dierich Brower and the rest of his men at his heels. The place was utterly empty. Not a living soul to be seen, not even old Peter himself. They tramped up the narrow creaking stair, when suddenly one of the men gave a sharp shout, and pointed through Peter’s wide-open casement window. The rest crowded to look, and there, a short way off, walking calmly across the dew-damp fields side by side with Margaret and Martin, was the very man they had torn the house apart to find.

Ghysbrecht let out a yell of ferocious triumph, stumbled headlong back down the stairs, and flung himself astride his mule. In moments he and his whole band were galloping across the pasture, thundering in hot pursuit of their quarry.
