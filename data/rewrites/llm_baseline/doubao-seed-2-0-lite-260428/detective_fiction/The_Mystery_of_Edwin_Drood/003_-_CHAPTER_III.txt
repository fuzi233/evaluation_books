For reasons that will unfurl as this tale unfolds, the old cathedral town at the heart of our story will bear a fictitious name here: Cloisterham. Its bones, laid down over millennia, have borne half a dozen other names – names the Druids whispered, the Romans carved on stone markers, the Saxons scrawled in runes, the Normans stamped into charters – so one more swapped alias will not trouble its dust-caked chronicles.

Cloisterham is an ancient, drowsy place, no fit home for anyone who craves the hum and rush of the wider world. The entire town carries the damp, earthy tang of the cathedral crypt at its core, so thickly strewn with forgotten monastic graves that local children tend small salad patches in the dust of long-dead abbots and abbesses, and bake dirt pies crusted with the dust of nuns and friars. Ploughmen working its outlying fields turn the soil over the bones of once-powerful Lord Treasurers, Archbishops and Bishops, grinding their remains into the earth for grain much the same way the ogre in the old tales planned to grind up his uninvited dinner guests.

The residents of Cloisterham have long clung to a strange, contradictory belief: that every great change the town will ever see has already happened, and no new shifts will ever trouble their quiet lives. A very odd moral to draw from living in the middle of history, but one far older than any traceable ruin in the city limits. The streets are so still that the shop sunblinds on summer days barely dare to flap in the south wind; sun-browned tramps passing through hurry their limps a little just to escape the town’s oppressive, unyielding respectability. This is no great feat, for Cloisterham’s public roads barely amount to one single narrow thoroughfare to enter and exit by. The rest of the streets lead to dead-end yards with rusted pumps, save for the cathedral close and a small paved Quaker settlement tucked in a shady corner, as plain and muted as a Quaker matron’s bonnet.

In short, Cloisterham is a city stranded in a bygone age: its cathedral bell sounds hoarse and worn, the rooks circling the tower caw hoarsely, and the rooks nesting in the stalls far beneath the floor echo them in even rougher, muffled croaks. Fragments of old walls, saint’s chapels, chapter houses, and ruined convents have been clumsily built into the town’s houses and gardens, much the same way half-remembered, jumbled old superstitions have become tangled in the minds of every local resident. Everything here belongs to the past. Even the town’s only pawnbroker hasn’t taken a new pledge in years, just puts his long-unredeemed stock up for sale: slow-dimming old watches that look like they’re sweating in their cases, tarnished sugar tongs with wobbly bent legs, tattered odd volumes of grim, forgotten novels. The only sign of new, living growth in the whole place is the greenery pushing up through the cracks in every garden – even the tiny, drooping local theatre has a scraggly little patch behind its stage, where the actor playing the devil makes his dramatic “descent to hell” straight into a bed of scarlet runner beans or a pile of discarded oyster shells, depending on the season.

Right in the heart of town stands the Nuns’ House: a venerable old brick building, its name drawn from the convent that stood on the site centuries before. A polished, blazing brass plate nailed to the trim wooden gate leading to its courtyard glows bright with the words *Seminary for Young Ladies. Miss Twinkleton.* The house front is so worn, weathered and faded that the stark, shining plate has long struck first-time visitors as a battered old dandy with a brand new, oversize monocle stuck straight into his blind eye.

No one living can say for sure if the old nuns who once lived here went about with their heads permanently bowed to avoid banging them on the low ceiling beams, if they sat for hours at the long narrow windows telling their beads for penance instead of stringing them into necklaces, or if any of them were once bricked up alive in the building’s odd angles and jutting gables for daring to hold on to a spark of ordinary human life that would go on to outlive the whole convent. Those stories might be of interest to any ghosts haunting the halls – but they never appear anywhere in Miss Twinkleton’s half-yearly accounts. They are not part of her regular curriculum, nor charged as extra lessons. The part-time poetry teacher on her staff has no recital pieces about such unprofitable, unladylike topics on her class list.

Much like people in cases of drunkenness or animal magnetism who hold two separate streams of memory that never cross – where if you hide your watch while drunk, you have to get drunk again to remember where you put it – Miss Twinkleton leads two entirely separate lives. Every night, the second her young boarders have retired to their dormitories, she tidies her curls, brightens her eyes, and becomes a far sprightlier, more light-hearted woman than the girls ever see by day. Every evening at the same hour, she picks right back up on the gossip she left off the night before: all the soft, tender local scandal she claims to know nothing about during school hours, and endless stories of a long-ago season at Tunbridge Wells – which she calls airily “The Wells” – when a gentleman she refers to compassionately as “Foolish Mr. Porters” laid his heart at her feet, a fact her daytime headmistress persona is as unaware of as a solid granite pillar. The only person who moves seamlessly between both of Miss Twinkleton’s worlds is her companion, Mrs. Tisher: a deferential widow with a perpetually weak back, a chronic sigh, and a soft, suppressed voice, who oversees the girls’ wardrobes and lets them all assume she once lived in far finer circumstances. The domestic staff, in a legend passed down through generations of maids, are all completely convinced that the late Mr. Tisher worked as a hairdresser.

The pet favourite pupil of the Nuns’ House is Miss Rosa Bud – called universally, of course, Rosebud: wonderfully pretty, wonderfully childish, and wonderfully whimsical. All the other girls harbour a soft, awkward romantic fascination for her, for every one of them knows Rosa is betrothed by a dead man’s will to a husband she has never even chosen for herself, and her legal guardian is bound by law to give her to him the moment he comes of age. Miss Twinkleton, in her prim daytime headmistress persona, tries to stamp out the romance of the situation by shaking her head mournfully behind Rosa’s dimpled shoulders, and muttering about the sad fate of this “poor doomed little victim” – but it does no good. The only result is that every girl in the dormitory giggles and declares out loud that Miss Twinkleton is nothing but a silly, pretending old thing.

The whole school flies into a flutter the only time it ever does: when that appointed fiancé comes to call on little Rosebud. The girls are all completely convinced he has every legal right to visit, and that if Miss Twinkleton ever tried to stop him, she would be dragged straight off to prison and transported to the colonies. The second they expect his ring at the gate bell, or the bell actually rings, every girl with even the flimsiest excuse crowds to a window, every girl supposed to be practising the piano plays off-beat and out of time, and the French class descends into such chaos that the disciplinary classroom mark gets passed around as fast as a wine bottle at a raucous 18th century dinner party.

On the afternoon the day after the two men’s quiet dinner at the gatehouse, that bell rings, and the usual flutter ripples through every corner of the seminary.
“Mr. Edwin Drood to see Miss Rosa,” the lead parlour maid announces.
Miss Twinkleton, putting on an exemplary face of gentle melancholy, turns to Rosa like she is sending a lamb to the slaughter, and says, “You may go down, my dear.”
Rosa heads for the stairs, every eye in the room following her.
Edwin Drood is waiting in Miss Twinkleton’s private parlour: a dainty, over-furnished room where the only even remotely scholastic items are one globe of the earth and one globe of the stars. Those two items are carefully positioned to reassure parents and guardians that even in her most private moments, duty could compel Miss Twinkleton at any time to globe-trot across the earth and soar through the skies in search of knowledge for her pupils. The new maid, who has never met Rosa’s famous fiancé before, is peeking at him through the gap in the half-open parlour door when she trips guiltily down the kitchen stairs – just as a tiny, charming little apparition glides into the room, her face completely covered by a thin silk apron thrown over her head.
“Oh! This is so ridiculous!” the apparition gasps, stopping short and shrinking back. “Don’t come any closer, Eddy!”
“Don’t what, Rosa?”
“Don’t come near me, please. The whole situation is absurd. It’s absurd being an engaged orphan, it’s absurd all the girls and servants are skittering around after me like mice in the wainscoting, and it’s absurd that I even have to receive visitors at all!”
The little apparition has her thumb tucked in the corner of her mouth as she complains.
“That’s a very warm welcome to give me, Pussy, I must say,” Edwin says.
“I’ll be affectionate in a minute, Eddy, I just can’t right now. How are you?” she adds, very shortly.
“I can’t exactly say I’m the better for seeing you, Pussy, when I can’t even see a single bit of your face.”
That second complaint makes a single bright, dark, pouting eye peek out from under the edge of the apron – only to vanish again instantly when Rosa exclaims, “Oh my goodness! You’ve had half your hair chopped off!”
“I think I’d have been better off if they’d chopped my whole head off,” Edwin grumbles, rumpling his cropped hair, throwing a fierce glare at the wall mirror, and stamping his foot impatiently. “Should I just leave?”
“No, don’t go yet, Eddy. The girls would all pester me for hours about why you stormed off.”
“Once and for all, Rosa, will you take that ridiculous little apron off your head and give me a proper welcome?”
Rosa yanks the apron off her pouting childish face. “You’re very welcome, Eddy. There! That’s nice, isn’t it? Shake hands. No, I can’t kiss you – I’ve got an acidulated sweet in my mouth.”
“Are you even a little bit glad to see me, Pussy?”
“Oh yes, dreadfully glad. Go sit down. Quick – Miss Twinkleton!”
It is Miss Twinkleton’s custom, whenever these visits happen, to glide in and out of the room every three minutes, either by herself or sending Mrs. Tisher to take her place, under the pretence of searching for some lost small object, all to uphold the sanctity of propriety. On this occasion, Miss Twinkleton drifts past the doorway, calling out over her shoulder, “How do you do, Mr. Drood? So glad to have the pleasure. Do excuse me, I was looking for a pair of tweezers. Thank you!”
“I got the gloves you bought me last night, Eddy, I love them. They’re absolute beauties,” Rosa says.
“Well that’s something, I suppose,” Edwin replies, half-grumbling. “I’ll take the smallest scrap of encouragement I can get. And how did you spend your birthday, Pussy?”
“It was perfect! Everyone gave me a present. We had a feast, and then we had a little dance after dark.”
“A feast and a dance, huh? Sounds like you all had a grand old time without me.”
“It was de-lightful!” Rosa cries, completely unpretending and genuine in her excitement.
“Ha! What did you have at the feast?”
“Tarts, oranges, jellies, and shrimps.”
“Did you have any proper partners for the dance?”
“We danced with each other, obviously. But some of the girls pretended to be their own brothers. It was so funny!”
“Did any of them pretend to be –”
“Pretend to be you? Oh, of course!” Rosa cackles, laughing so hard she doubles over. “That was the very first thing we did.”
“I hope they did a halfway decent job of it,” Edwin says, sounding faintly offended.
“Oh it was perfect! But I wouldn’t dance with you, you know.”
Edwin blinks, confused. He begs her to explain why not.
“Because I was so tired of you,” Rosa says – then sees the hurt look cross his face, and adds quickly, pleading, “Dear Eddy, you were just as tired of me, you know you were.”
“When did I ever say that, Rosa?”
“Say it? You never had to say it! You just showed it. And she did the imitation so well!” Rosa gushes, suddenly giddy over her pretend version of Edwin.
“That girl sounds like a dreadfully impudent little wretch,” Edwin mutters. “And so, Pussy, that was your last birthday you’ll ever spend in this old house.”
“Ah, yes.” Rosa clasps her hands, sighs, and shakes her head.
“You look sad about it.”
“I’m sad for this poor old place. I keep feeling like it’s going to miss me, when I’m gone far away, while I’m still so young.”
“Maybe we don’t have to go through with all this, Rosa?”
She glances up at him quickly, eyes bright, then shakes her head again, sighs, and stares at the floor.
“So that means we’re both still resigned to it, then?”
She nods. After a short silence, she speaks up, quaint and earnest: “You know we have to get married, and we have to get married from this house, Eddy, or all the poor girls here will be so dreadfully disappointed.”
For a second, more compassion than romance flickers across Edwin’s face – compassion for her, and for himself, trapped in this pre-written fate. He shakes the look off, and asks, “Would you like me to take you out for a walk, Rosa dear?”
Rosa tilts her head, comically thoughtful for a moment, then her face lights up.
“Oh yes, Eddy, let’s go for a walk! I have an idea. You pretend you’re engaged to some other girl, and I pretend I’m not engaged to anyone at all, and then we won’t fight at all.”
“You really think that will stop us bickering, Rosa?”
“I know it will. Hush! Pretend to stare out the window – Mrs. Tisher is coming.”
Sure enough, the matronly Mrs. Tisher drifts into the room, rustling in her silk skirts like the ghost of a old dowager in a folk tale, and purrs, “I hope Mr. Drood is well? I needn’t even ask, judging by the colour of his cheeks. I hope I’m not disturbing anything, but I seem to have mislaid a paper-knife – oh, thank you, I do have it now!” and vanishes back out the door.
“There’s one more favour you have to do for me, Eddy,” Rosa says. “The second we step out on the street, you have to press yourself right up against the side of the house, and keep me out on the far side, where you’re completely hidden behind me.”
“Whatever you want, Rosa. But why?”
“Because I don’t want the girls to see you.”
“It’s a lovely sunny day. Would you like me to carry a big umbrella up over my head to hide?”
“Don’t be silly, sir. You’re not wearing polished leather boots,” she pouts, one shoulder hunching up.
“Maybe the girls wouldn’t even notice that detail, even if they saw me,” Edwin says, suddenly staring down at his scuffed boots with deep dislike.
“Nothing ever escapes their notice. And then you know what will happen. Half of them will start declaring right then that they will never, ever agree to marry any suitor who doesn’t own a pair of polished leather boots. Hush! Miss Twinkleton’s outside the door. I’ll ask permission to go out.”
Sure enough, Miss Twinkleton’s voice drifts past the door, asking no one in particular in her bland, conversational tone, “Eh? Really? Are you quite certain you saw my mother-of-pearl button case on the worktable in my bedroom?” Rosa immediately asks for leave to take a walk, and Miss Twinkleton grants it graciously. Before long, the young couple are outside the Nuns’ House, taking every absurd precaution to hide Edwin’s supposedly defective boots – precautions, one hopes, that will prevent any future domestic strife for the future Mrs. Edwin Drood.
“Which way do you want to go, Rosa?”
“I want to go to the Lumps-of-Delight shop.”
“The what?”
“It’s a Turkish sweet, sir. Goodness me, don’t you know anything? You call yourself an engineer and you’ve never heard of Lumps of Delight?”
“Why on earth would I have heard of them, Rosa?”
“Because I love them more than anything. Oh wait, I forgot our pretend game. No, you don’t need to know anything about them, never mind.”
Edwin is glumly escorted to the sweet shop, where Rosa buys a bag of the sticky confections, offers him a piece (which he turns down with indignant offence), then tucks in with obvious relish. She rolls down her tiny pink gloves that look like rose petals, and every so often dabs her pink fingers to her rosy lips to brush off the fine white sugar dust sticking to them.
“Now, be a good-tempered Eddy, and keep pretending. So you’re engaged to this other woman?”
“Yep, I’m engaged.”
“Is she nice?”
“Charming.”
“Tall?”
“Extremely tall!” – which makes Rosa, who is very short, snort.
“Must be awfully gawky, then,” she says dryly.
“Pardon me, not at all,” Edwin says, immediately jumping to his imaginary fiancée’s defence. “She’s what everyone calls a fine woman, a very splendid woman.”
“Big nose, I suppose,” Rosa says, still perfectly calm.
“Not a tiny little button nose, certainly,” Edwin shoots back – which is exactly what Rosa’s nose is.
