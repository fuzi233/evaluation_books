The Thursday night passed quietly, without incident. But on Friday morning, two pieces of news arrived—one from the village, the other by post.

First: the baker’s boy claimed he had seen Rosanna Spearman, veiled and walking toward Frizinghall across the moor, the previous afternoon. It was odd—Rosanna’s broad shoulder was unmistakable—but clearly mistaken, for she had been unwell all day, confined to her room upstairs.

Second: a message from the postman informed us that Mr. Candy, the family doctor, had fallen ill after his ride in the rain on the birthday night. He’d caught a chill and now lay feverish and delirious. Though we felt sympathy for him, Mr. Franklin seemed particularly concerned—for Miss Rachel’s sake. From what he said at breakfast, it appeared he feared her distress over the missing Moonstone might worsen if no progress were made soon.

Breakfast barely finished when a telegram arrived from Mr. Blake, the elder, replying to his son’s urgent appeal. He wrote that he had secured the right man—the very best help available—through the Commissioner’s assistance. His name was Sergeant Cuff, and he would arrive by the morning train from London.

At the mention of this name, Mr. Franklin started slightly. He had heard curious tales about Sergeant Cuff during his stay in London, from his father’s lawyer.

“I begin to hope our troubles are nearing an end,” he said. “If half the stories I’ve heard are true, there isn’t a better detective in England than Sergeant Cuff.”

We all grew restless with anticipation as the time drew near for his arrival. Superintendent Seegrave, returning to the house at his appointed hour, heard of the expected guest and immediately retired to a private room with pen and paper, preparing his report—surely to be demanded by the new officer.

I wished I could have gone to meet him myself, but my lady’s carriage was not to be used even for such a distinguished figure, and the pony-chaise was needed later for Mr. Godfrey. He regretted leaving his aunt at such a tense moment and delayed his departure until the last possible train, eager to hear the celebrated officer’s thoughts on the case. Yet he must leave by Friday night, as a charity matter awaited his attention in town on Saturday.

When the hour came, I went down to the gate to wait.

A fly pulled up just as I reached the lodge. Out stepped a gaunt, elderly man—so thin he seemed scarcely to hold flesh on his bones. Dressed in sober black with a white cravat, his face was sharp as a knife, yellow and dry like a dried leaf. His eyes, pale grey and steely, carried a strange look—when they met yours, they seemed to expect more from you than you realized. His walk was quiet; his voice low and melancholy; his fingers long and claw-like. He might have passed for a clergyman or a undertaker—but not a policeman. A sharper contrast to Superintendent Seegrave, or a less reassuring figure for a household in distress, I cannot imagine.

“Is this Lady Verinder’s?” he asked.

“Yes, sir.”

“I am Sergeant Cuff.”

“This way, sir.”

As we walked toward the house, I introduced myself and my position, hoping to establish trust. But he said nothing about the business. Instead, he praised the grounds and remarked how refreshing the sea air felt. I found myself wondering how such a man could have earned his reputation.

We reached the house, both of us silent and uneasy—like two strangers linked together for the first time.

He asked for my lady, and upon learning she was in one of the conservatories, we turned toward the gardens at the back. While waiting, Sergeant Cuff glanced through an evergreen arch and spied our rose garden. Without hesitation, he stepped inside—his first real sign of interest.

To the gardener’s astonishment and my own dismay, this renowned detective proved deeply knowledgeable about roses.

“Ah, you’ve got the right exposure here—south and southwest,” he said, nodding slowly. “This shape is ideal for a rosery—nothing like a circle within a square. Yes, yes—walks between the beds. But gravel? No, no. Grass walks—grass! Gravel is too hard for roses.” He paused, then added with quiet warmth, “That’s a lovely bed of white and blush roses—mix well together, don’t they? Here’s the white musk rose, Mr. Betteredge—our old English rose standing tall beside the newest varieties. Pretty dear!” He gently touched the bloom as if speaking to a child.

It was a curious sight—a man famed for catching thieves, fondling flowers.

“You seem fond of roses, Sergeant?” I ventured.

“I haven’t much time for fondness,” he replied. “But when I do have a moment, the roses often get it. I began life among them—in my father’s nursery—and I hope to end it there too. One day, God willing, I’ll retire from chasing criminals and grow roses instead. Grass walks between the beds,” he added firmly, as though still annoyed by our gravel paths.

“It seems an odd passion for someone in your profession,” I said.

“If you take time to observe,” he answered, “you’ll see that a man’s tastes are often the opposite of his work. Show me anything more unlike a thief than a rose—and I’ll change my mind—if I can still manage it at my age. The damask rose makes a good stock for tender varieties, doesn’t it, Mr. Gardener?”

Just then, he noticed Lady Verinder approaching.

“He has seen her before either I or the gardener,” I thought. “And yet neither of us had spotted her.” I began to suspect he was sharper than he appeared.

My lady seemed slightly flustered by his presence—uncharacteristic for her. But Sergeant Cuff put her at ease immediately. He asked whether any other officers had been involved before their call, and upon hearing that Superintendent Seegrave had already been engaged, requested permission to speak with him before proceeding.

She led the way back. Before following, the Sergeant gave the gardener one final instruction: “Get her ladyship to try grass walks. No gravel—no gravel!”

Why Superintendent Seegrave suddenly looked diminished when presented to Sergeant Cuff, I cannot explain. But the fact remains. They withdrew into a room and remained shut away for a long while.

When they emerged, Seegrave appeared agitated; Cuff, yawning.

“The Sergeant wishes to see Miss Verinder’s sitting-room,” Seegrave announced with pomp. “He may have questions. Attend him, please.”

As I led the way upstairs, I watched Cuff—quiet, observant, his eyes fixed on Seegrave with that same still expectancy. I couldn’t say he was watching for Seegrave to fail—but I suspected it.

Cuff moved slowly through the Indian cabinet and around the boudoir, questioning me and occasionally Seegrave, though his inquiries seemed baffling to both of us. Then he reached the door and stopped before the painted panel beneath the lock—the one marked with the smear.

He placed a thin finger on it.

“That’s a pity,” he said. “How did it happen?”

I told him the women-servants had crowded into the room the previous morning, and some of their petticoats had caused the mark. “Superintendent Seegrave ordered them out,” I added, “before further damage.”

“Right,” Seegrave said sharply. “The petticoats did it, Sergeant—the petticoats did it.”

“Did you notice which petticoat?” Cuff asked—not looking at Seegrave, but at me.

“No, sir.”

He turned to Seegrave. “You noticed, I suppose?”

Seegrave hesitated. “I can’t charge my memory, Sergeant—a mere trifle—a mere trifle.”

Cuff looked at him—just as he had looked at the gravel paths—with quiet disdain.

“I made a private inquiry last week,” he said. “One end was a murder. The other—a spot of ink on a tablecloth no one could explain. In all my years, I’ve never met a ‘trifle’ yet. Before we go further, we must see the petticoat that left the mark—and know exactly when the paint was wet.”

Seegrave, sullen, asked if he should summon the servants. Cuff considered, sighed, and shook his head.

“No. Let’s start with the paint. It’s a simple yes or no. The petticoat question is long. What time were the servants in this room yesterday morning? Eleven o’clock—eh? Is anyone in the house who knows whether the paint was wet or dry at that time?”

“Mr. Franklin Blake does,” I said.

“Is he here?”

Franklin was nearby—eager to meet the great detective. Within minutes, he entered.

“That door,” he said, “was painted by Miss Verinder under my supervision. I helped mix the paint, which dries any colour in twelve hours.”

“Do you remember when the smeared section was finished?” Cuff asked.

“Perfectly. It was the last part. We wanted it done Wednesday—and I completed it by three in the afternoon.”

“Today is Friday,” Cuff said to Seegrave. “Let’s count back. At three on Wednesday, the paint was fresh. Twelve hours—dried by three Thursday morning. You held your inquiry at eleven Thursday morning. Eleven minus three—eight hours. That paint had been dry for eight hours when you blamed the petticoats.”

The blow fell. If Seegrave hadn’t already suspected Penelope, I would have pitied him.

With the paint settled, Cuff dismissed Seegrave entirely and turned to Franklin.

“It’s quite possible,” he said, “that you’ve handed us the clue.”

At that moment, the bedroom door opened. Miss Rachel stepped out.

She addressed Cuff directly, ignoring the fact that he was a stranger.

“Did you say,” she asked, pointing to Franklin, “that _he_ had given you the clue?”

(“This is Miss Verinder,” I whispered.)

“That gentleman,” Cuff replied, studying her closely, “may have done so.”

She turned briefly toward Franklin—then quickly looked away. Her face flushed, then paled. A strange expression crossed her features—one that startled me.

“Having answered your question,” Cuff said, “I may ask one of my own. There’s a smear on the painting. Do you know when it happened—or who did it?”

Instead of answering, she continued:

“Are you another police officer?”

“I am Sergeant Cuff, miss, of the Detective Police.”

“Do you think a young lady’s advice worth hearing?”

“I shall be glad to hear it.”

“Then do your duty—and don’t let Mr. Franklin Blake assist you!”

She spoke with sudden venom, her tone harsh, her eyes filled with unexpected hostility toward him. Though I had known her since childhood, and loved her dearly, I felt ashamed—ashamed of her.

Cuff’s gaze never wavered. “Thank you, miss,” he said. “Do you know anything about the smear? Might you have caused it?”

“I know nothing about it.”

She turned and retreated into her room. I heard her cry—as Penelope had once before—once she was alone.

I couldn’t meet Cuff’s eyes. I looked at Franklin, who stood beside me, visibly distressed.

“I told you I was worried about her,” he said. “Now you see why.”

“Miss Verinder appears somewhat upset,” Cuff remarked calmly. “It’s natural—such a valuable jewel. Quite natural.”

The excuse I had offered earlier—her grief over the diamond—was echoed now by a stranger. A cold shiver ran through me. I didn’t understand it then—but now I know: I had just sensed, in Cuff’s mind, a new and terrible light, sparked solely by what he had seen and heard from Miss Rachel.

“A young lady’s tongue is privileged,” Cuff said to Franklin. “Let us forget what passed. We know when the paint dried. Now we must find when it was last seen without the smear. You have a mind—understand what I mean.”

Franklin composed himself.

“I believe I do,” he said. “The narrower the time, the smaller the field.”

“Exactly,” Cuff said. “Did you notice the work after finishing it?”

Franklin shook his head.

“And you, Mr. Betteredge?”

“I cannot say I did.”

“Who was the last person in the room Wednesday night?”

“Miss Rachel, I suppose.”

Franklin interjected: “Or possibly your daughter, Betteredge.” He explained that my daughter was Miss Verinder’s maid.

“Ask your daughter to come up,” Cuff said. Then he pulled me aside. “Your superintendent has given me a full account. Among other things, he admitted he alienated the servants. It’s vital to mend that. Tell them, with my compliments: First, I have no proof the Diamond was stolen—only that it’s missing. Second, my purpose is simply to ask them to help find it.”

I recalled how the servants had reacted when Seegrave restricted their rooms.

“May I add a third thing, Sergeant?” I asked. “Are they free to move about freely, as usual?”

“Perfectly free.”

“That will calm them,” I said. “From cook to scullion.”

“Go and do it now.”

I returned in five minutes. Only one difficulty arose—when I mentioned the bedrooms, the entire female staff nearly followed me upstairs, eager to volunteer as witnesses.

Cuff seemed pleased with Penelope. He looked less grim—almost as he had when seeing the white musk rose.

Here is her testimony, drawn carefully by Cuff:

She had taken interest in the painting, having helped mix the colours. She remembered the section under the lock—it was the last part done. She had seen it hours later, without a smear. She had left it at midnight, still clean. She had bid Miss Rachel goodnight, heard the clock strike in the boudoir, and held the door handle at the time. She knew the paint was wet (having mixed it) and took care not to touch it. She swore she lifted her dress and saw no stain. She could not swear her dress hadn’t brushed it accidentally—but she remembered the dress: it was new, a gift from Miss Rachel. Her father confirmed it. She brought it—examined thoroughly. No trace of paint anywhere.

End of evidence. Convincing, clear. Signed, Gabriel Betteredge.

Next, Cuff questioned me about large dogs that might have entered the room. Hearing none existed, he fetched a magnifying glass. Under it, no skin impression—only signs of fabric brushing the paint. Thus, the smear must have been made by clothing—between midnight and three Thursday morning.

Having reached this point, Cuff noticed Seegrave still present. He summarized:

“This ‘trifle’ of yours, Mr. Superintendent,” he said, pointing to the smear, “has grown in importance. Three tasks remain: First, find any garment in the house bearing the paint. Second, identify its owner. Third, determine how that person explains being here, between midnight and three, smearing the paint. If they can’t account for it—look no further for the thief. I’ll handle this. You’re free to return to your duties. You have a man here—leave him at my disposal. Good morning.”

Seegrave respected Cuff—but himself more. As he left, he struck back.

“I’ve kept my opinions to myself,” he said, “but now I must remark: there is such a thing as making a mountain out of a molehill. Good morning.”

“There is also such a thing,” Cuff replied, “as making nothing of a molehill—because your head is too high to see it.”

With that, he turned and walked to the window alone.
