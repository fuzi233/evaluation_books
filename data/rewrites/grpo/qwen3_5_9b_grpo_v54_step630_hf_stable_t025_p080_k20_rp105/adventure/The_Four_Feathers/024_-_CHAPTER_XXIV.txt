The river cut through a landscape of indifference, a callous country inhabited by a callous race, observed Calder as he drifted downstream from Wadi Halfa toward Assouan on his three-month leave. Leaning against the railing of the upper deck, he watched the barge lashed tightly to the steamer's side. Below, amidst the native passengers, sat an *angareb*—a rough matting bedstead—bearing a motionless figure swathed in a black veil. Two Arabs had hauled this burden aboard at Korosko that morning; now, seated in the stern, they laughed and chattered, paying no heed to the weight they carried. To the casual eye, the occupant might have been a dead man or woman, so utterly neglected was the form. Calder scanned the horizon, where glaring sands and barren rocks, sculpted into the hard, silent shapes of pyramids, dominated the view. The only sign of life was a narrow, meagre strip of green clinging to the water's edge, the sole response of the Sudan to the relentless heat.

Calder's gaze returned to the *angareb*. Beneath the black cloth, the outline of a nose, hollow eyes, and a mouth was visible, but the veil concealed whether the lips bore a moustache or the chin a beard. The slanting sunlight crept closer, burning the yellow light over the black recumbent form. The natives nearby sought the shade of the upper deck, yet no one moved the *angareb*, and the two men in the stern remained oblivious to their charge. As the sun blazed pitilessly upon the face, the living creature beneath did not stir. The veil did not flutter, the legs remained rigid, and the arms lay pressed against the side.

"Move the *angareb* into the shadow," Calder shouted to the men in the stern. "And be quick!"

The Arabs rose reluctantly and complied.

"Is it a man or a woman?" Calder asked.

"A man," one replied. "We are taking him to the hospital in Assouan, but we do not think he will live. He fell from a palm tree three weeks ago."

"You give him nothing to eat or drink?"

"He is too ill."

It was a familiar tale, the logical conclusion of a fatalistic belief that life and death were pre-written scripts. The man lying quietly beneath the black covering had likely suffered nothing more than a bruise at the start, a wound curable within a week by simple remedies. Instead, he had been left to the mercy of the sun and flies, unwashed, unfed, and parched. The bruise had festered into a sore, the sore had gangrened, and when all hope was lost, the Egyptian Mudir of Korosko had discovered the accident and dispatched the man downriver. Yet, despite the familiarity of the story, Calder could not shake the fascination of the man's immobility. When a strong wind sprang up at sunset, blowing against the current, he felt a strange comfort knowing the sick man would find some relief.

That evening, as his neighbor at the dinner table spoke with a German accent, an impulse seized Calder.

"You are not a doctor by any chance?"

"Not a doctor," the man replied, "but a student of medicine at Bonn. I came from Cairo to see the Second Cataract, but was not allowed to go farther than Wadi Halfa."

Calder interrupted immediately. "Then I will trespass upon your holiday and claim your professional assistance."

"For yourself? With pleasure, though I should never have guessed you were ill," the student smiled, adjusting his eyeglasses.

"Nor am I. It is an Arab for whom I ask your help."

"The man on the bedstead?"

"Yes, if you will be so good. I must warn you—he was hurt three weeks ago, and I know these people. No one has touched him since. The sight will not be pretty. This is not a nice country for untended wounds."

The German student shrugged. "All experience is good," he said, and the two men rose to join him on the upper deck.

The wind had freshened during dinner, raising waves that tossed the steamer and its barge, breaking the water against the hull.

"He was below there," the student noted, leaning over the rail to peer into the darkness of the lower deck. Night had fallen, and save for a single lamp swinging from the center of the upper deck, the area was swallowed by blackness. Beyond the circle of uncertain light, only the bow remained visible, where the breaking water flung a white sheet of spray like driven snow.

"He has been moved," the student observed. "There is no one in the bows."

Calder stared into the gloom. "I believe the *angareb* is there. I believe it is."

Following the student, he hurried down the stairway to the lower deck and stepped onto the side. Now he could be certain. The *angareb* stood in a wash of water exactly where it had been moved that morning. Upon it, the figure beneath the black covering lay as motionless as ever, expressionless, though the cold spray broke continually upon its face.

"I thought it would be so," Calder said. He retrieved a lantern and, with the German student, climbed across the bulwarks onto the barge. He summoned the two Arabs.

"Move the *angareb* from the bows," he commanded. Once they had obeyed, he added, "Now take that covering off. I wish my friend, who is a doctor, to see the wound."

The two men hesitated. One, with an air of insolence, objected. "There are doctors in Assouan, whither we are taking him."

Calder raised the lantern and drew the veil away from the wounded man himself. "Now, if you please," he told his companion. The German student examined the wounded thigh while Calder held the lantern high. As predicted, it was a grim business; the wound crawled with infection. The student was glad to cover it up again.

"I can do nothing," he said. "Perhaps in a hospital, with baths and dressings... relief will be given at all events, but more? I do not know. Here, I could not even begin." He glanced at the Arabs. "Do these two men understand English?"

"No," Calder answered.

"Then I can tell you something. He did not get the hurt by falling out of a palm tree. That is a lie. The injury was done by the blade of a spear or some similar weapon."

"Are you sure?"

"Yes."

Calder bent suddenly toward the Arab on the *angareb*. Though the man never moved, he was conscious. Calder had watched him steadily and saw that his eyes followed the spoken words.

"You understand English?" Calder asked.

The Arab could not answer with his lips, but a look of comprehension flashed in his face.

"Where do you come from?"

The lips tried to move, but no whisper escaped. Yet his eyes spoke, vainly, with a great eagerness to answer. Calder dropped to his knees beside the man's head, holding the lantern close, and enunciated the names of towns.

"From Dongola?"

No gleam in the Arab's eyes responded.

"From Metemneh? From Berber? From Omdurman? Ah!"

The Arab answered to that word; he closed his eyelids. Calder pressed on eagerly.

"You were wounded there? No. Where then? At Berber? Yes. You were in prison at Omdurman and escaped? No. Yet you were wounded."

Calder sank back on his knees, his reflection sparking with excitement. He leaned closer to the Arab's ear, speaking in a lower key.

"You were helping someone to escape? Yes. Who? El Kaimakam Trench? No." He listed the names of other white captives from Omdurman, and to each, the Arab's eyes answered "No." "It was Effendi Feversham, then?" he said, and the eyes assented as clearly as if lips had spoken.

But this was all the information Calder could secure. "I too am pledged to help Effendi Feversham," he said, but in vain. The Arab could not speak, could not even tell his name, and his companions refused to meddle. Clinging to a story that absolved them of responsibility, they insisted they were merely following orders from kinsmen in Korosko to transport a stranger to Assouan. Calder obtained the news Durrance waited for—the confirmation of Harry Feversham's fate—but it remained hidden in a locked book within the Arab's mind. He stood beside the helpless man on the *angareb*. There he was, eager enough to speak, but the extremity of weakness laid a finger upon his lips. All Calder could do was ensure he reached the hospital in Assouan safely.

"Will he recover?" Calder asked, and the doctors shook their heads in doubt. There was a chance, perhaps a very slight one, but recovery would be slow.

Calder continued his journey to Cairo and then to Europe. An opportunity to aid Harry Feversham had slipped away. The Arab who could not speak his own name was Abou Fatma of the Kabbabish tribe, and his presence, wounded and helpless upon the Nile steamer between Korosko and Assouan, meant that Harry Feversham's carefully laid plan for the rescue of Colonel Trench had failed.
