“I have no wrong, where I can claim no right, Naught ta’en me fro, where I have nothing had, Yet of my woe I cannot so be quite; Namely, since that another may be glad With that, that thus in sorrow makes me sad.” — WYATT

Margaret had never expected to find much joy for herself in Mr. Bell’s upcoming visit; she had only looked forward to it for her father’s sake. But the moment her sharp, warm-hearted godfather stepped across their threshold, the two of them settled into the easiest, most natural friendship as if they had known each other their whole lives. He teased her that there was no merit to her being exactly the kind of girl he adored: her affection was her birthright, an inheritance of character that let her walk straight into the hold of his regard without so much as asking permission. She laughed in turn, and told him he was far more fresh and young at heart than half the lads decades his junior, hidden though that warmth was under his stuffy Oxford Fellow’s cap and gown.

“Fresh and young in kindness, you mean,” she corrected him. “I’m afraid I must admit that your opinions are the oldest, mustiest things I have run across in months.”

“Hey, Hale! Listen to this daughter of yours! Her time in Milton has quite corrupted her. She’s a democrat, a red republican, a member of the Peace Society, a socialist—”
“Papa, it’s all only because I’m standing up for the progress of commerce,” Margaret protested. “Mr. Bell would have us stuck forever bartering wild beast skins for acorns, no progress at all.”
“Nonsense, missy. I’d dig the soil and grow potatoes. I’d shave those wild hides down and spin their wool into broadcloth. Don’t exaggerate. But I am tired of all this bustle. Everyone rushing over everyone else, all scrambling to get rich as fast as they can.”
“Not everyone is fortunate enough to sit comfortably in a set of college rooms and watch their fortunes grow without lifting a finger,” Mr. Hale put in gently. “I’ve no doubt many a man in Milton would give a great deal to have property increase as yours does, without the slightest exertion on his part.”
“I don’t believe they would. It’s the scramble and the struggle they love. As for sitting still, learning from the past, or shaping the future through slow, faithful work done with a thoughtful, prophetic spirit—pooh! I don’t think there’s a single man in Milton who knows how to sit still. And that is a very great art.”
“I suspect the people of Milton think Oxford men do not know how to move at all. It would do both sides a great deal of good to mix a little more often.”
“It might do the Miltoners good, certainly. Many things would do them good that would be perfectly disagreeable to everyone else.”
“Are you not a Milton man yourself, though?” Margaret asked. “I should think you would be proud of your hometown.”
“I confess I do not see anything there to be proud of. But if you will come to Oxford with me, Margaret, I will show you a place worth glorying in.”
“Well,” Mr. Hale said, “Mr. Thornton is coming to take tea with us tonight, and he is as proud of Milton as you are of Oxford. You two shall have to try and make each other a little more liberal-minded.”
“I do not want to be more liberal-minded, thank you,” Mr. Bell huffed.

“Is Mr. Thornton coming to tea, papa?” Margaret asked, her voice soft and low.
“Either for tea or soon after. He said he could not tell for certain, and told us not to wait for him.”

Mr. Thornton had made up his mind not to pester his mother for details of the conversation she had planned to have with Margaret, over that impropriety she was so convinced Margaret had committed. He knew full well if he heard his mother’s account of any such meeting, every word would be twisted and coloured through her disapproval, leaving him chafing and angry, even as he recognised how biased her perspective was. He shrank from even hearing Margaret’s name mentioned in his own home. He blamed her, he was jealous of her, he told himself he had renounced her entirely — and still he loved her, sorely, in spite of every effort to stop. He dreamed of her: dancing towards him, arms outstretched, light and giddy, a version of her so unlike the Margaret he knew that it made him loathe her even as the vision drew him in. The false, untrue figure of her was stamped so deep into his imagination that when he woke, he could barely untangle the real, noble Margaret from that evil, giddy doppelgänger, and his dislike for the fake one seemed to taint and disfigure the memory of the real.

But he was too proud to admit his own weakness by avoiding her. He would not seek out her company, and he would not run from it, just to prove he had full control over himself. That afternoon, he dragged out every piece of business on his desk, forcing every movement to unnaturally slow, deliberate pace, so that it was past eight o’clock before he finally reached the Hales’ door. He was then kept trapped in the study with Mr. Bell, sorting through old business accounts, and Bell sat on by the fire rambling on wearily long after all the work was done, both of them perfectly free to head upstairs. Thornton said nothing to suggest moving, chafing in silence all the while, convinced Bell was the most tedious, prosy companion he had ever met. Bell returned the favour in secret, deciding Thornton was as brusque and short-tempered a fellow as he had ever encountered, and had lost half the intelligence and good manners he used to have. At last, a faint sound of movement from the room above reminded them they ought to join the others.

They found Margaret seated at the table, an open letter spread out before her, deep in excited discussion of its contents with her father. The gentlemen’s entrance made her fold the letter away at once, but Thornton’s sharp, eager ears caught the last few words Mr. Hale said to Bell. “A letter from Henry Lennox. It makes Margaret very hopeful.”

Bell nodded. Margaret’s cheeks flooded bright as a rose the second she met Thornton’s gaze. He was halfway out of his chair, ready to walk straight out the door and never set foot in this house again.

“We were thinking,” Mr. Hale said, “that you and Mr. Thornton must have taken Margaret’s advice this morning, and set about trying to convert each other, you were so long shut away in the study.”
“And no doubt you thought there would be nothing left of us but our respective opinions, like the famous Kilkenny cat that ate its own tail. Tell me, which of our opinions did you think would prove the most stubborn and hard to kill?”

Thornton had no idea what they were talking about, and disdained to ask. Mr. Hale politely filled him in. “Mr. Thornton, we were teasing Mr. Bell earlier today for that old Oxford mediaeval bigotry of his against his own native Milton. We — Margaret, I believe — suggested it would do him a great deal of good to spend more time in the company of Milton’s manufacturers.”
“Pardon me,” Margaret said, smiling. “I said I thought it would do the Milton manufacturers good to spend more time with Oxford men. Is that not right?”
“No,” Bell countered. “You argued that both sides would benefit from a little more familiarity. But never mind that. Now, Mr. Thornton, I wonder when you Milton men intend to actually live. All your lives seem to be spent gathering all the materials for life, and never getting round to living it at all.”
“By ‘live’ I suppose you mean seeking enjoyment?” Thornton said.
“Yes, enjoyment — not empty, idle pleasure, of course, I am sure we both agree mere trivial amusement is very poor fare for a full life.”
“I would rather you defined exactly what kind of enjoyment you are referring to.”
“Very well — leisure, the quiet enjoyment of the power and influence money brings. All of you in Milton are striving for wealth. What do you want it for?”

Thornton went silent for a long moment. Then he said, “I truly do not know. But money is not what I strive for.”
“What, then?”
“That is a home question. I do not think I am prepared to lay myself open to that kind of catechism this evening.”
“No,” Mr. Hale intervened quickly. “Let us not make our questions personal. Neither of you is a perfect representative of your respective worlds — you are both far too individual for that.”
“I am not sure if that is a compliment or not,” Bell laughed. “I would love to be the representative of Oxford, with all its beauty and learning, and its proud, ancient history. What do you say, Margaret? Ought I to be flattered?”
“I do not know Oxford well enough to say,” she replied. “But there is a very great difference between being the representative of a city, and being the representative man of all its inhabitants.”
“Very true, Miss Margaret. Now I remember, you were against me this morning, all Miltonian and manufacturing in your loyalties.”

Margaret caught the quick, surprised glance Thornton shot her, and grew annoyed at the wrong, unkind meaning he might read into Bell’s offhand remark. Bell went on, oblivious. “Ah, I wish I could show you our High Street, our Radcliffe Square. I am leaving out the colleges just as I give Mr. Thornton leave to omit all his factories when he tries to sell Milton’s charms. I have every right to make fun of my birthplace, remember — I was born and raised there.”

Thornton was far more annoyed by all this talk than he ought to have been. He was in no mood for light joking. On any other day, he would have enjoyed Bell’s half-testy condemnation of a town whose busy, brutal life ran so counter to the slow, leisurely Oxford rhythms he loved — but tonight, he was raw and stinging, and found himself trying to defend a town that had never been meant as a serious target for mockery. “I do not hold Milton up as a perfect model of a town.”
“Not in the matter of architecture, then?” Bell asked slyly.
“No! We have all been far too busy to waste time on mere outward appearances.”
“Do not call them mere outward appearances,” Mr. Hale said gently. “They impress upon every one of us, from the day we are born until the day we die, every single hour of our lives.”
“Wait a few years,” Thornton said. “Remember we are a different race from the ancient Greeks, for whom beauty was everything, and to whom you might speak of a life of leisure and serene enjoyment taken in through the senses. I do not despise them, any more than I would try to copy them. We Teutons, in this part of Darkshire, do not see life as something to be enjoyed — we see it as a time for action and exertion. Our glory, our beauty, comes from the strength of our work, that lets us overcome material resistance, and far greater obstacles still. We are Teutonic in another way here, too: we hate having laws made for us from far away in London. We wish people would let us right our own wrongs, instead of constantly meddling with their half-baked legislation. We stand for self-government, and oppose centralisation.”
“In short, you want to bring back the old Saxon Heptarchy. Well, at any rate, I take back what I said this morning about you Milton folk not respecting the past. You are full-on worshippers of Thor.”
“If we do not revere the past the way you do at Oxford, it is because we want lessons that apply directly to the present,” Thornton went on, heated. “It is a fine thing when study of the past leads to a vision of a better future. But for men groping their way through entirely new circumstances, it would be far finer if old wisdom could tell us exactly how to act in the matters that touch us most closely, full of hard difficulties to meet and conquer — not just push aside for a while — because the way we face them defines our entire future. But no! People can prattle on about Utopia far easier than they can tell you what your duty is tomorrow. And when that duty has already been done by other people, they are the first to cry out ‘Fie, for shame!’”

“And all this time I have no idea what you are talking about,” Bell laughed. “Would you Milton men ever condescend to send your latest troubles up to Oxford to solve? You have not even tried us yet.”
Thornton laughed outright at that. “I suppose I was thinking of all the trouble we have had lately, the strikes we have been through — troublesome, costly things, as I am finding out to my own detriment. Even the most recent strike, the one I am still stinging from, was a respectable one.”
“A respectable strike?” Bell echoed, amused. “That sounds like you are far gone in that worship of Thor I mentioned.”

Margaret could sense, more than see, how deeply Thornton was galled by every light joke made about the subject that was so serious, so personal to him. She forced herself to speak, to turn the conversation away before he grew any more upset. “Edith says the printed calicoes they sell in Corfu are both better quality and cheaper than anything you can get in London.”
“Does she?” her father said. “I think that must be one of Edith’s famous exaggerations. Are you sure that is true, Margaret?”
“I am only sure that is what she says, papa.”
“Then I am sure it is fact,” Bell put in. “I have so much faith in your truthfulness, Margaret, that it extends to cover even your cousin’s character. I will not believe any cousin of yours is capable of exaggeration.”

“Is Miss Hale so remarkable for her truth?” Thornton said, sharply and bitterly. The words were no sooner out of his mouth than he could have bitten his tongue out. What was he doing? Why was he stabbing her with the secret shame she had carried for her brother, right in front of her father? He was possessed by ill humour tonight: cross at being delayed so long before coming to see her, infuriated by the mention of Henry Lennox’s name, which he was sure belonged to a rival more fortunate than him. He could not meet the gentle, light-hearted Bell on his own ground of easy banter, and now he had lashed out at Margaret.

She did not get up and leave the room, as she had done in the old days when his brusque temper had annoyed her. She sat perfectly still, for a moment staring at him with the bewildered hurt of a child struck for no reason, her eyes wide and soft with mournful, reproachful sadness, before they dropped. She bent her head over her sewing, and did not speak again. Thornton could not help but stare at her, and saw a faint sigh tremble over her whole frame, as if she had been caught in an unexpected cold chill. He gave short, sharp answers to every question, restless and cross, unable to tell jest from earnest, desperate only for one look, one single word from her to let him throw himself at her feet in penitence. But she never looked up, never spoke. Her long, taper fingers moved steadily in and out of her stitching, as fast and steady as if sewing was the whole purpose of her life. She cannot care for me, he thought. If she did, the fierceness of my regret would have forced her to raise her eyes, if only for an instant, to see how sorry I am. He almost wanted to do something deliberately rude before he left, to earn the right to tell her the remorse eating him alive. It was a mercy that the long, cold walk home under the open sky sobered him, leaving him with a grave resolution: he would see as little of her as possible from now on. The very sight of her face, the very sound of her voice, had far too much power to unbalance him. He knew what this love was: a sharp pang, a fierce, burning experience he was struggling through in the flames. But he would fight his way through the furnace, out to the calm serenity of middle age, richer, more human, for having felt such a great passion.

As soon as he had abruptly left the house, Margaret rose from her seat and slowly folded up her sewing. The heavy seams felt far too weighty for her languid, tired arms. The soft round lines of her face had gone long and sharp, and she looked as if she had been labouring through a day of unendurable fatigue. As the three of them prepared for bed, Bell muttered a little condemnation of Thornton. “I never saw a man so spoiled by success. He cannot bear a single word of joke. Everything seems to touch some raw, sore spot in his overinflated dignity. Once he was as simple and noble as open daylight. You could never offend him, because he had no vanity at all.”
“He is not vain now,” Margaret said, turning slowly from the table, her voice clear and quiet. “Tonight he was not himself at all. Something must have upset him terribly before he even arrived here.”

Bell shot her one of his sharp glances over the top of his spectacles. She met it calmly, without flinching. After she had left the room, he leaned over to Hale and said, “Hale! Did it ever strike you that Thornton and your daughter have what the French call a tendresse for each other?”
“Never!” Mr. Hale said, first startled, then flustered by the suggestion. “No, I am sure you are wrong. I am almost certain you are mistaken. If there is any feeling at all, it is all on Mr. Thornton’s side. Poor fellow! I hope and pray he is not thinking of marrying her, because I am sure she would never accept him.”
“Well, I am a bachelor, and have steered clear of all love affairs my whole life,” Bell said, yawning. “So perhaps my opinion is worthless. Otherwise I would swear there are very pretty symptoms of feeling on her side too.”
“Then you are definitely wrong,” Hale insisted. “He may care for her, even though she has been almost rude to him at times. But Margaret! Margaret would never think of him, I am sure. The idea has never once entered her head.”
“Entering her heart would do, of course,” Bell said. “I merely threw out the suggestion. No doubt I was wrong. And whether I am or not, I am very sleepy. I have disturbed your night’s rest with my silly, untimely fancies, so I will go off to bed with an easy mind.”

Mr. Hale resolved not to let the nonsensical idea bother him — but he lay awake for a long time, unable to stop turning it over in his head.
