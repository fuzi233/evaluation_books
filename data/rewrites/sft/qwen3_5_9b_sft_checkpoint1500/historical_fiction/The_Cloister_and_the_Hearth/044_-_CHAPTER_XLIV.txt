Experience teaches a woman not to tremble when another falls, nor to rush to supernatural conclusions for a faint, but to treat it merely as a failure of the body, something she has witnessed often. Catherine moved with brisk efficiency. She laid the girl flat upon the floor, her head resting squarely on the boards, flung open the casement to let the raw air in, and loosened the bodice where the girl lay helpless. Only after these tasks were complete did she turn to the door and call out, her voice ringing with command:

"Come here, if you please."

Margaret Van Eyck and Reicht entered, finding their mistress prostrate and Catherine striking the girl's hands together.

"Oh, my poor soul! What mischief have you wrought?" cried Margaret, her face flushing with sudden anger.

"Myself?" Catherine replied, her tone sharp and defensive. "What is the matter, then?"

"Nothing, Madam," Catherine answered, though her eyes burned. "Nothing save what nature dictates in her present state."

"You speak with too much composure!" Margaret retorted, stepping forward. "And you, who are the architect of this wretched plight."

"I am not," Catherine declared bluntly. "Nor is any woman born."

"But surely it was you and your husband who drove them apart? Now he has fled to Italy alone. A 'situation' indeed! You have shattered her heart within your own walls."

"Why, Madam? Who else could it be? By Heaven's mercy! If I heard you right, I'd swear this was Gerard's own girl. But that cannot be. Look at this fur; it costs no less than five crowns per ell. Besides, this young lady is a wife, or ought to be."

"Certainly she ought. And who, pray, is the cause that she remains unmarried? Who stood before them at the very altar?"

"God forgive whomever it may be," Catherine said solemnly, her expression grave. "It was neither I nor my man."

"Well," the other woman said, her fury softening slightly, "now that you see her, perhaps you will not hold such bitterness against her, Madam. She is waking, thank God."

"Bitterness against her?" Catherine laughed softly. "That is long past. Poor creature! Trouble behind her, trouble ahead of her; yet to think I set her, of all living women, to read Gerard's letter aloud. Yes, that was why she fainted away, I swear it. She is coming to. What, sweetheart? Fear not; there is no enemy here, only friends."

They raised her gently into an easy chair. As color returned to her cheeks and lips, Catherine drew Margaret Van Eyck aside into a corner.

"Does she stay with you, if you please?"

"No, Madam."

"I would not have her return to Sevenbergen tonight, then."

"That rests upon her wish. She still refuses to spend the night."

"Ay, but you are older; you can persuade her. See, she begins to take notice."

Catherine leaned close, pressing her lips to Margaret Van Eyck's ear for a fleeting moment. It seemed insufficient time to whisper a single word, far less a sentence. Yet between women, secrets travel like lightning, carried by thought alone.

The old lady started back, her eyes wide.

"It is a lie! A slander! Monstrous! Look at her face. It is blasphemy to cast suspicion on such countenance."

"Tut! Tut! Tut!" Margaret snapped. "You might as well deny that hand is yours. I must know better, and I tell you it is true."

Then, to Margaret Van Eyck's utter shock, she rose and took the girl in both arms, kissing her fervently.

"I suffered for Gerard," Margaret wept, her voice breaking. "And you have shed your blood for him, I hear; his own words confess that I have been wrong, the very words you read to me. Ay, Gerard, my child, I have stood aloof from her, but I shall make it right the moment I begin. You are my daughter from this hour."

A second, warmer embrace sealed this impromptu pact, and the woman of impulse vanished.

Margaret sank back into her chair, a faint, weary smile touching her lips. Gerard's mother had kissed her and named her daughter. But the next instant, she saw her old friend regarding her with a troubled, vexed gaze.

"I wonder you let that woman kiss you."

"His mother!" Margaret murmured, half-reproachful.

"Mother, or no mother, you would not have allowed her to touch you if you had known what she whispered in my ear concerning you."

"Concerning me?" Margaret asked faintly.

"Aye, concerning you, whom she had never seen until tonight." The old lady hesitated, choosing her words carefully, preparing to share her indignation, when an unexpected interruption silenced her.

The young woman slid from the chair to her knees and began to pray piteously for forgiveness. In the cadence of her penitence and the humility of her posture, any bystander would have guessed that she had inflicted some cruel wrong, some intolerable insult, upon her venerable friend.
