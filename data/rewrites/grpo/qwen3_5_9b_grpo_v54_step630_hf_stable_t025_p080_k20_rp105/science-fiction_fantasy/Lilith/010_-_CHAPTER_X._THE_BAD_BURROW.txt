The air turned a suffocating black as winter pressed its icy fingers shut around me, yet the fluttering fire burned brighter, more arresting, halting its flight to hover in patient wait. As soon as I stepped beneath its radiance, it drifted forward slowly, pausing occasionally above the jagged rocks. Every time I lifted my gaze, it seemed to swell, until finally, it cast a companion shadow beside me. It was plainly a bird-butterfly, gliding with a swallow-like double beat, its vast, nearly square wings flashing through the entire spectrum of the rainbow. Captivated by their splendor, I became so transfixed by their beauty that I tripped over a low stone and lay stunned. When consciousness returned, the creature hovered directly above my head, radiating a chord of light filled with multitudinous gradations and hues I had never witnessed before. I rose to continue, but unable to tear my eyes from the shining entity to watch my footing, I struck my foot against another stone. Fearful of another fall, I sat down to watch the little glory, and a desperate longing surged within me to possess it. To my unspeakable delight, it began to descend toward me. First slowly, then swiftly, it sank, growing larger as it approached. I felt as though the treasure of the universe was surrendering itself to me—I reached out and grasped it. But the instant my fingers closed around it, its light vanished; all was darkness as pitch. A dead book with boards outspread lay cold and heavy in my hand. I tossed it into the air, hearing it crash among the heather. Burying my face in my hands, I sat in motionless misery.

Yet the cold grew so biting that, fearing to freeze, I forced myself up. The moment I stood, a faint sense of light awakened within me. "Is it coming to life?" I cried, a great pang of hope shooting through me. Alas, no! It was merely the edge of a moon peering up keen and sharp over the level horizon. She brought me light, but no guidance! She would not hover over me, would not wait for my faltering steps! She could offer me nothing but an ignorant choice.

With a full face she rose, and I began to discern my surroundings. Westward of her, and not far away, a range of low hills broke the horizon line; I set out for them.

What a night I endured before reaching them! The moon seemed to know something, for she stared at me oddly. Her gaze was indeed icy-cold, yet full of interest, or at least curiosity. She was not the same moon I had known on Earth; her face was strange to me, and her light even stranger. Perhaps it emanated from an unknown sun! Every time I looked up, I found her staring at me with all her might. At first, I was annoyed, as by the rudeness of a fellow creature; but soon I perceived—or fancied I did—a certain wondering pity in her gaze: *Why was I out in her night?* Then, for the first time, I understood the awful nature of being awake in the universe: I WAS, and I could not help it.

As I walked, my feet left the heather and trod upon bare, spongy soil, something akin to dry, powdery peat. To my dismay, it gave a momentary heave under me; then, presently, I saw what appeared to be the ripple of an earthquake running ahead of me, shadowy in the low moonlight. It passed into the distance, but while I stared after it, a single wave rose and crept slowly toward me. A yard or two away, it burst, and from it, with a scramble and a bound, emerged an animal like a tiger. Clots of mold hung about his mouth and ears, and his eyes winked and flamed as he rushed at me, baring white teeth in a soundless snarl. I stood fascinated, unconscious of either courage or fear. He turned his head to the ground and plunged into it.

"That moon is affecting my brain," I muttered as I resumed my journey. "What life can exist here but the phantasmic—the stuff of which dreams are made? I am indeed walking in a vain show!"

Thus I strove to keep my heart above the waters of fear, unaware that the very entity I distrusted was my defense against the realities I mistook for phantoms: her light controlled the monsters; without it, I would have scarce taken a second step on the hideous ground. "I will not be appalled by that which only seems!" I told myself, yet felt it a terrible thing to walk on a sea where such fishes disported themselves below. With that, a step or two from me, the head of a worm began to emerge slowly from the earth, as big as a polar bear's and much resembling it, with a white mane on its red neck. The wriggling draws with which its huge length extricated itself were horrible, yet I dared not turn my eyes away. The moment its tail was free, it lay as if exhausted, wallowing in feeble effort to burrow again.

"Does it live on the dead," I wondered, "and is it unable to hurt the living? If they scent their prey and come out, why do they leave me unharmed?"

I now know it was the moon that paralyzed them.

All night long as I walked, hideous creatures, no two alike, threatened me. In some, beauty of color enhanced the loathsomeness of their shape: one large serpent was covered from head to distant tail with feathers of glorious hues.

I became so accustomed to their hurtless menaces that I fell to beguiling the way with inventions of monstrosities, never suspecting that I owed each moment of my life to the staring moon. Though hers was no primal radiance, it so hampered the evil things that I walked in safety. For light is still light, even if but the last of a countless series of reflections! How swiftly would not my feet have carried me over the restless soil had I known that, if still within their range when her lamp ceased to shine on the cursed spot, I should that moment be at the mercy of such as had no mercy—the center of a writhing heap of hideousness, every individual of it as terrible as before it had merely seemed! Fool of ignorance, I watched the descent of the weary, solemn, anxious moon down the widening vault above me, with no worse uneasiness than the dread of losing my way—where, as yet, I had indeed no way to lose.

I was drawing near the hills I had made my goal, and she was now not far from their skyline, when the soundless wallowing ceased, and the burrow lay motionless and bare. Then I saw, slowly walking over the light soil, the form of a woman. A white mist floated about her, now assuming, now losing, then reassuming the shape of a garment as it gathered to her or was blown from her by a wind that dogged her steps.

She was beautiful, yet with such a pride mixed with misery on her countenance that I could hardly believe what I saw. Up and down she walked, vainly endeavoring to lay hold of the mist and wrap it around her. The eyes in the beautiful face were dead, and on her left side was a dark spot against which she would now and then press her hand, as if to stifle pain or sickness. Her hair hung nearly to her feet, and sometimes the wind would so mix it with the mist that I could not distinguish one from the other; but when it fell gathering together again, it shone a pale gold in the moonlight.

Suddenly pressing both hands to her heart, she fell to the ground, and the mist rose from her and melted into the air. I ran to her. But she began to writhe in such torture that I stood aghast. A moment more, and her legs, hurrying from her body, sped away as serpents. From her shoulders fled her arms, also as serpents in terror. Then something flew up from her like a bat, and when I looked again, she was gone. The ground rose like the sea in a storm; terror seized me; I turned to the hills and ran.

I was already on the slope of their base when the moon sank behind one of their summits, leaving me in its shadow. Behind me rose a waste and sickening cry, as of frustrated desire—the only sound I had heard since the fall of the dead butterfly; it made my heart shake like a flag in the wind. I turned, saw many dark objects bounding after me, and made for the crest of a ridge where the moon still shone. She seemed to linger there that I might see to defend myself. Soon I came in sight of her and climbed the faster.

Crossing the shadow of a rock, I heard the creatures panting at my heels. But just as the foremost threw himself upon me with a snarl of greedy hate, we rushed into the moon together. She flashed out an angry light, and he fell from me a bodiless blotch. Strength came to me, and I turned on the rest. But one by one, as they darted into the light, they dropped with a howl; and I saw—or fancied I saw—a strange smile on the round face above me.

I climbed to the top of the ridge: far away shone the moon, sinking to a low horizon. The air was pure and strong. I descended a little way, found it warmer, and sat down to wait for the dawn.

The moon went below, and the world again was dark.
