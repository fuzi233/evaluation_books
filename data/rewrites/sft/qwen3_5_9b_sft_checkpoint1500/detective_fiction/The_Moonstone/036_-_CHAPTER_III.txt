Among the distinguished guests at the dinner party, the figure who immediately commanded attention was Mr. Murthwaite. Upon his return to England following years of wandering, society had held him in high regard as a survivor of numerous perils. His recent announcement to return to the scenes of his former exploits, venturing into previously uncharted territories, had reignited a fervent, albeit somewhat reckless, admiration. There was a calculated indifference in his resolve to place his safety in jeopardy a second time—a quality that only served to bolster the faith of his admirers. After all, encountering an eminent traveler at dinner carries with it a grim irony: the probability that the next major news concerning him will be his murder is, statistically speaking, non-negligible.

Once the ladies retired, leaving the gentlemen to the private quarters of the dining room, the atmosphere inevitably shifted. With the civilizing restraint of female company absent, the conversation naturally drifted toward politics, a universal pastime for British males. As for myself, I am perhaps the least "English" of Englishmen regarding this specific topic. To me, political discourse is often tedious and devoid of substance. Observing Mr. Murthwaite as the wine circulated, I noted that he shared this sentiment. Though he managed his disinterest with diplomatic grace, sparing the host's feelings, it was evident he was preparing for a siesta. I decided to test a hypothesis: would a subtle reference to the *Moonstone* suffice to rouse him, and if so, what perspective would he offer on the latest developments of the Indian conspiracy, as detailed in my official records?

"If I am not mistaken, Mr. Murthwaite," I ventured, "you were acquainted with the late Lady Verinder, and you recall the peculiar sequence of events culminating in the loss of the *Moonstone*?"

The eminent traveler sat upright instantly, his composure shattered as he inquired after my identity. I introduced myself, detailing my professional ties to the Herncastle family and recalling my unique role during Colonel Herncastle's lifetime and the diamond's history. Mr. Murthwaite adjusted his posture, subtly positioning himself between the remaining guests—a mix of Conservatives and Liberals—and focused intently on me alone.

"Have you heard anything recently of the Indians?" he asked.

"I have cause to believe," I replied, "that an interview occurred between one of them and me in my office yesterday."

Mr. Murthwaite, a man of considerable fortitude, was momentarily stunned. I recounted the encounter with Mr. Luker and my own, describing the details precisely as they stood. "It is evident," I added, "that the Indian's final question possessed a specific intent. Why was he so eager to know the precise hour at which a borrower returns capital?"

"Do you truly fail to discern his motive, Mr. Bruff?"

"I confess, sir, my intellect is currently fogged. I see none."

The traveler leaned forward, seemingly delighted by the depth of my confusion. "Allow me to pose a question. In what state does the conspiracy to appropriate the *Moonstone* currently exist?"

"I cannot determine," I admitted. "The Indian plot remains an enigma to me."

"The plot, Mr. Bruff, is an enigma solely because you have not subjected it to rigorous scrutiny. Let us reconstruct it, chronologically, from the drafting of Colonel Herncastle's will to the Indian's visit to your office. Given your position, it is vital for Miss Verinder's security that you possess a lucid understanding of this matter, should necessity arise. Will you endeavor to unravel the Indian's motive independently, or shall I spare you the labor of investigation?"

I recognized the pragmatic utility of his proposal and selected the first option.

"Agreed," Mr. Murthwaite declared. "First, consider the ages of the three Indians. I can attest they appear roughly coeval. You concur they are not yet forty? Correct. Now, reflect upon Colonel Herncastle's arrival in England and the scheme he devised to secure his life. I need not count the years, but it is undeniable that these current men are the successors to three other Brahmins who accompanied the Colonel to these shores. Had they merely replaced their predecessors, the matter would be trivial. However, they have gone further. They have inherited the organization established by their forebears here. Do not dismiss it as insignificant; while it may seem modest by our standards, it encompasses financial resources, the services of shady individuals operating in London's fringes, and the clandestine sympathies of a select few locals, formerly of their own faith, employed within the city. While not formidable, this network warrants our attention. Now, I propose a query requiring your expertise: What was the catalyst that afforded the Indians their initial opportunity to seize the Diamond?"

I grasped the implication of his reference.

"The first opportunity," I responded, "was undeniably presented by Colonel Herncastle's demise. Naturally, they would be apprised of his death?"

"As a matter of course. Consequently, his death provided their maiden chance. Until then, the *Moonstone* remained secure within the bank's vault. You drafted the will bequeathing the jewel to his niece, and it was probated conventionally. As a legal practitioner, you understand the course the Indians would pursue, acting under English counsel, following that event."

"They would procure a certified copy of the will from Doctors' Commons," I surmised.

"Precisely. An intermediary of questionable reputation would facilitate this acquisition. That document would reveal that the *Moonstone* was destined for Lady Verinder, entrusted to Mr. Blake senior or his appointee. Information regarding Lady Verinder and Mr. Blake would be readily accessible. The crux of the dilemma lay in timing: Should they strike while the jewel was being transported from the bank, or await its delivery to Yorkshire? The latter path, obviously, offered the greatest safety. Hence, we find the Indians at Frizinghall, disguised as jugglers, biding their time. In London, their organization kept them abreast of affairs. Two agents were sufficient: one to shadow anyone departing Mr. Blake's residence, and another to bribe the domestic staff for gossip. These mundane measures quickly informed them that Mr. Franklin Blake had visited the bank and was the sole individual intending to visit Lady Verinder. The subsequent actions, you recall, do they not?"

I recalled that Franklin Blake had intercepted one of the spies in the street, thereby accelerating his departure for Yorkshire by several hours. Thanks to Old Betteredge's sound advice, Franklin had secured the *Moonstone* in the bank at Frizinghall prior to the Indians' anticipated arrival. The narrative held together logically up to this point. Yet, a puzzle persisted: Being unaware of these precautions, why had the Indians made no attempt on Lady Verinder's residence—the presumed location of the jewel—during the intervening months preceding Rachel's birthday?

I posed this difficulty to Mr. Murthwaite, noting that I had become cognizant of the incidents involving the child and the inkdrop, and expressing my skepticism toward any explanation rooted in clairvoyance.

"Nor do I subscribe to such notions," Mr. Murthwaite conceded. "The alleged clairvoyance in this instance is merely an elaboration of the romantic and mystical aspects inherent in the Indian character. It would serve as a refreshing diversion and a source of encouragement for those men—qualities entirely alien to the English mindset—to envelop their arduous and hazardous mission here with a veil of the marvelous. Their boy is undoubtedly sensitive to mesmeric influence; under such sway, he likely projected thoughts already present in the mind of the mesmerizer. I have tested theories of clairvoyance, and their manifestations rarely transcend this boundary. The Indians do not investigate via such methods; they regard their boy as a 'Seer' of invisible realities, and within this marvel, they discover a novel impetus uniting their purpose. Observe this as a fascinating glimpse into human nature, surely unfamiliar to you. Our inquiry relies strictly on rational deduction, tracing effects back to natural causes, eschewing anything difficult to believe. Thus far, have I satisfied your expectations?"

"Not a shred of doubt," I affirmed. "However, I remain anxiously awaiting the rational resolution to the difficulty I recently articulated."

Mr. Murthwaite smiled. "It is the simplest obstacle of all," he remarked. "Permit me to validate your account of the case as accurate. The Indians were indeed oblivious to Mr. Franklin Blake's actions with the *Moonstone*; hence, their initial blunder on the first night of Blake's arrival at his aunt's house."

"Their first error?" I echoed.

"Certainly! The mistake of permitting themselves to be discovered, hiding on the terrace at night, by Gabriel Betteredge. Nevertheless, they demonstrated commendable insight by recognizing their misstep. As you noted, possessing ample time, they refrained from approaching the house for weeks thereafter."

"Why, Mr. Murthwaite? That is the core of my inquiry!"

"Because no Indian, Mr. Bruff, ever incurs an unnecessary risk. The clause in Colonel Herncastle's will, which you drafted, stipulated that the *Moonstone* should pass irrevocably into Miss Verinder's possession on her birthday. Consider, then: Which course was safest? To assault the jewel while it was under the guardianship of Mr. Franklin Blake, who had already proven capable of suspicion and outwitting them? Or to wait until the jewel fell into the hands of a young girl, who would innocently delight in adorning herself with such magnificence?"
