The rumors had persisted for centuries—whispers among the Canons of Steinfeld of a hidden treasure buried by Abbot Thomas in his prime. Gold, they said, a vast hoard concealed somewhere within the monastery. When questioned, the Abbot would only laugh and reply: *“Job, John, and Zechariah will tell either you or your successors.”* Sometimes he added, darkly, that he would bear no grudge against those who found it.

Among his works, the great east window of the south aisle stood out—filled with exquisitely painted glass, his likeness and arms proudly displayed. He restored the Abbot’s lodging, dug a well in its courtyard, adorned it with carvings in marble. Then, in 1529, death took him suddenly.

The mystery might have remained forgotten had not an antiquary, poring over the *Sertum Steinfeldense Norbertinum*, stumbled upon the passage. The words seized him. *Job, John, and Zechariah.* He had seen them before—three towering figures in stained glass, each bearing a cryptic inscription:

- **Job**: *"Auro est locus in quo absconditur."* (*There is a place for gold where it is hidden.*)
- **John**: *"Habent in vestimentis suis scripturam quam nemo novit."* (*They have on their raiment a writing which no man knoweth.*)
- **Zechariah**: *"Super lapidem unum septem oculi sunt."* (*Upon one stone are seven eyes.*)

The glass was unmistakably from Steinfeld. And if the Abbot’s riddle pointed anywhere, it pointed here.

The letter arrived at dawn, its script hurried, its tone desperate.

*Master has suffered a nasty shock. He keeps to his bed, fevered and unlike himself. He begs you come at once. Take the short way—drive to Coblenz, then a trap to Steinfeld. I dare not say more, only that I fear the night here. An honest English face would be a comfort.*

The journey was swift, the village dreary—a husk of its former self, the abbey’s grandeur long stripped away. The inn was cramped, its shadows too deep. Brown, usually unshakable, was pale, his fingers twitching.

“He’s better now,” he muttered, “but last night—” He cut himself off as a cry came from within.

Somerton’s voice was raw with fear. The door creaked open to reveal a man diminished—eyes wild, skin slick with sweat. He clutched Gregory’s hand like a drowning man.

“You must do something for me,” he whispered. “No questions. Brown will show you tomorrow. It must be put back—where we found it.”

Gregory agreed. That night, his door locked, he heard fumbling outside—slow, deliberate, as if something tested the handle.

At first light, they went. The task was simple, though its purpose unclear. By evening, as wheels clattered toward Coblenz, Somerton finally spoke.

“The glass was the key,” he began, voice low. “Under layers of grime and paint, I found letters—hidden words on their robes. A cipher.”

He laid out the puzzle: the inscriptions, the numbers scratched beside them—*xxxviii, xxxviii, xxxviii.* The figures’ fingers gave the method—skip one letter, then two, then three.

The first attempt yielded Latin:

The Abbot’s well. The courtyard. The rest of the cipher, once unraveled, led them there.

“But what we found—” Somerton’s breath hitched. “It was not gold.”

He would not speak of it further, only that the thing had been *put back*, sealed away where the seven eyes watched.

And sometimes, in the dark, he still heard the slow drag of footsteps outside his door.
