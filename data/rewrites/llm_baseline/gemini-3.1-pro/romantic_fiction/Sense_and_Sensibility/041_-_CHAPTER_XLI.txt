Edward, having carried his profound gratitude to Colonel Brandon, brought the harvest of his fortune to Lucy. By the time he reached Bartlett’s Buildings, the very air seemed thick with the scent of rising dough and simmering possibilities; Lucy’s spirits were so fermented with joy that she assured Mrs. Jennings she had never seen him so robust. Her own happiness was a vigorously boiling pot. She eagerly anticipated their settling into Delaford Parsonage before Michaelmas—the time of the great autumn harvests, when the generational rhythms of the land yielded their richest bounty. Yet, her anticipation lacked the reverence of a true agrarian steward. While she spoke of Elinor with a sugary warmth, openly declaring her limitless gratitude, her secret resolves betrayed a strictly utilitarian hunger. She viewed Colonel Brandon not as a guardian of the soil, but as an endless larder to be raided; she planned to exploit his servants, his carriage, and particularly his cows and poultry, reducing the mythic, ancient magic of Delaford’s dairy and coop to mere commodities meant to fuel her own ascent.

Over a week had passed since John Dashwood’s brief call in Berkeley Street. With no further nourishment offered beyond a single, hollow verbal enquiry regarding his wife’s indisposition, Elinor recognized the bitter necessity of a visit. It was an obligation that curdled in her stomach. Marianne, violently allergic to the superficial commerce of their relations, refused to partake of it; Mrs. Jennings, though her carriage was always at Elinor’s disposal, found the prospect of Mrs. John Dashwood as unpalatable as a spoiled broth. Consequently, Elinor departed alone. She rode through the jarring, metallic clatter of London’s streets—a harsh acoustic ecology of iron wheels, shrieking hinges, and shouting hawkers that eroded the mind’s quiet sanctuaries, so far removed from the resonant, grounding sounds of a mortar and pestle or the rhythmic crackle of a hearth fire.

Arriving at the Dashwood residence, Elinor stepped into a home devoid of the deep olfactory landscapes of roasted herbs or browned butter. The house felt stripped of culinary soul; meals here were merely fuel, served on fine porcelain to mask their spiritual emptiness. John Dashwood accidentally emerged just as the carriage prepared to turn away. He expressed great, if flatly seasoned, pleasure in seeing her, ushering her upstairs to an empty drawing room. 

“Fanny is in her own room, I suppose,” he announced. “I will go to her presently, for I am sure she will not have the least objection in the world to seeing you. Why would not Marianne come?” 

Elinor offered a tactful dilution of the truth, translating her sister’s deep, visceral aversions into the dominant lexicon of polite excuse, knowing the rich complexities of Marianne's soul could never be parsed in this sterile room. 

“I am not sorry to see you alone,” John continued, lowering his voice into a conspiratorial hum that lacked the rich timbre of genuine intimacy. “This living of Colonel Brandon’s—can it be true? Has he really given it to Edward?” 

Upon her confirmation, he sputtered like fat dropped in a cold pan. “Really! Well, this is very astonishing! No relationship! And now that livings fetch such a price! What was the value of this?” 

“About two hundred a year.” 

“Very well—and for the next presentation to a living of that value—supposing the late incumbent to have been old and sickly—he might have got fourteen hundred pounds. I wonder he should be so improvident in a point of such common concern!” 

John’s native dialect was commerce; the ancient, unspoken language of communal care and agrarian patronage was entirely lost on him, an eroded cultural memory he could no longer interpret. He attempted to rationalize the profound gift into his own transactional worldview, assuming Edward was merely holding the living until a rightful purchaser came of age. When Elinor positively contradicted him, citing her own role in conveying the offer—a memory anchored in her mind with the sensory clarity of snapping fresh beans—he was forced to submit to her authority. 

“It is truly astonishing!” he cried. “What could be the Colonel’s motive?” 

“A very simple one—to be of use to Mr. Ferrars.” 

“Well, well; whatever Colonel Brandon may be, Edward is a very lucky man. You will not mention the matter to Fanny, however; she will not like to hear it much talked of.” 

Elinor tasted the bitter iron of the family’s cruelty on her tongue, barely refraining from observing that Fanny might have stomached an acquisition of wealth to her brother that cost her nothing. 

John lowered his voice further, creating a grating sonic intrusion in the quiet room. “Mrs. Ferrars knows nothing about it at present. When the marriage takes place, I fear she must hear of it all.” 

“But why should such precaution be used?” Elinor asked, fighting the dominant power dynamics embedded in his hushed tones. “She cast him off forever. She cannot be interested in anything that befalls him. She would not be so weak as to throw away the comfort of a child, and yet retain the anxiety of a parent!” 

“Ah! Elinor,” said John, “your reasoning is founded on ignorance of human nature. Mrs. Ferrars is one of the most affectionate mothers in the world.” 

Elinor remained silent, letting the hollow echo of his words fade into the room’s dead acoustic space. 

“We think now,” said Mr. Dashwood, after a short pause, “of Robert’s marrying Miss Morton.” 

Elinor, noting the decisive importance of her brother’s tone, calmly replied, “The lady, I suppose, has no choice in the affair.” 

“Choice! How do you mean?” 

“I only mean that I suppose it must be the same to Miss Morton whether she marry Edward or Robert.” 

“Certainly, there can be no difference; for Robert will now to all intents and purposes be considered as the eldest son; and they are both very agreeable young men: I do not know that one is superior to the other.” 

To John, souls were like unseasoned gruel—indistinguishable and meant only to sustain structural alliances. Then, taking her hand and speaking in an awful whisper, he confided a secret meant to gratify her. He had it from the best authority that Mrs. Ferrars believed a connection with Elinor would have been far preferable to Lucy—it would have been, she had said, "the least evil of the two." John spoke of this as a highly pleasing compliment, entirely deaf to the insult embedded in his lexicon. Her subjective worth, her profound capacity to appreciate the very marrow of life, was translated by them into merely a slightly less offensive transaction. 

Elinor had heard enough to agitate her nerves, and was relieved from the danger of hearing more by the entrance of Mr. Robert Ferrars. John soon departed in quest of Fanny, leaving Elinor with Robert, who carried with him the scent of expensive, manufactured colognes—a sterile olfactory mask over a decaying moral core. 

They had scarcely been alone for two minutes before Robert began to speak of Edward's living. Upon hearing the particulars, Robert emitted an immoderate, braying laugh. In the delicate acoustic ecology of Elinor’s mind, the sound was a violent industrial clatter, abrasive and meaningless. The very idea of Edward anchored to a small parsonage, embedded in the circular, multi-generational rhythms of an agrarian parish, diverted him beyond measure. He conjured the fanciful imagery of Edward reading prayers in a white surplice and publishing banns for country folk named John Smith and Mary Brown, viewing such profound, earthy domestic routines as utterly ridiculous. 

Elinor sat in immovable gravity, her eyes fixing upon him with a contempt that required no translation. Her silence was a deep, resonating bell of judgment, though Robert was too deafened by his own superficiality to hear it. Recovering from his affected laugh, he pivoted to a patronizing pity. 

“Poor Edward! He is ruined forever. I know him to be a well-meaning fellow, but his manners are certainly not the happiest. He has done for himself completely—shut himself out forever from all decent society! From his style of education, it was always to be expected.” 

“Have you ever seen the lady?” Elinor asked. 

“Yes; once. The merest awkward country girl, without style, or elegance. I offered immediately to talk to him, to represent it in a very strong light. ‘You are making a most disgraceful connection,’ I should have said. But now it is all too late. He must be starved, you know, that is certain; absolutely starved.” 

Robert used the word *starved* without any comprehension of true sustenance; to him, starvation was merely a lack of social currency and commercialized glamour, entirely ignoring the rich, sustaining harvest of a life lived with purpose, seasonal reverence, and connection to the earth. 

He had just settled this point with supreme self-complacency when the entrance of Mrs. John Dashwood abruptly terminated the subject. Though Fanny never spoke of the fractured family dynamics outside her own walls, Elinor could read the dominant lexicon of guilt and confusion in her countenance. Fanny attempted a highly artificial cordiality, her words like a beautifully plated but utterly flavorless dish. She expressed concern that Elinor and her sister were so soon to leave town—an exertion of feigned warmth in which her husband, trailing behind her and hanging enamoured upon every shallow, metallic syllable, seemed to perceive the absolute height of graceful affection.
