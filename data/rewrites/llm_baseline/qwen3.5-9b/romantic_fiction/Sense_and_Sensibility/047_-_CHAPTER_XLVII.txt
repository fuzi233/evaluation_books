Mrs. Dashwood listened to Willoughby's defense with a heavy heart. While she rejoiced that he was absolved of the specific charge laid against him, and felt genuine sorrow for his plight, she could not pretend to forgive him. The past remained an immutable barrier; no amount of current pleading could restore the unblemished faith Marianne once held, nor erase the pain she had endured through his actions. Nor could it wash away the stain of his treatment toward Eliza. Consequently, Willoughby could never regain his place in Marianne's esteem, nor could Colonel Brandon's reputation suffer further injury from his presence.

Had Mrs. Dashwood heard Willoughby's tale firsthand, witnessing his distress and believing the sincerity of his manner, her pity might have been deeper. Yet, unlike her daughter, she was not swayed by emotion alone. Elinor, guided by reflection and a sober judgment, refused to embellish the truth with tender fictions that might mislead. She sought only to state the facts clearly, allowing her mother to form her own conclusions based on reality rather than sentiment.

That evening, when the three women sat together, Marianne broke her silence regarding Willoughby. It required visible effort; her restlessness, flushed cheeks, and trembling voice betrayed the turmoil within. "I wish to assure you both," she declared, "that I see everything exactly as you desire."

Elinor, wishing to hear her sister's unbiased thoughts, signaled her mother to remain silent. Marianne continued slowly, her voice gaining a strange calmness. "What Elinor told me this morning has been a great relief. I have heard precisely what I needed to hear. I am now perfectly satisfied and wish for no change. I never could have been happy with him, even if I had known sooner what I must inevitably discover. There would have been no confidence, no esteem. Nothing could ever have bridged that gap."

"My dear Marianne!" cried her mother, tears in her eyes. "To find happiness with a man of libertine practices! With one who destroyed the peace of our dearest friend and the finest soul we know! No, my child, your sensitive conscience would never allow you to marry such a man."

Marianne sighed deeply. "I wish for no change."

"You view this matter," Elinor interjected gently, "with the clarity of a sound mind. You must realize, as I do, that your marriage would have guaranteed you endless trouble and disappointment, unsupported by any reliable affection. Had you wed him, poverty would have been your constant companion. His extravagance is admitted even by himself; his life proves he understands little of self-denial. Your limited income, combined with his demands and your own inexperience, would have plunged you into distresses unknown until then. Your strong sense of honor would have driven you to extreme economy, retrenching on your own comfort. Perhaps he would have tolerated this for a time, but eventually, faced with ruin, he would have resented your interference. Instead of winning his love, you would have diminished your influence, making him regret the very bond that trapped him in difficulty."

Marianne's lips quivered as she whispered, "Selfish?" implying, *Do you truly believe him selfish?*

"The entire course of his behavior," Elinor replied firmly, "was rooted in selfishness. From the moment he toyed with your affections, to the delay in confessing his engagement to another, to his sudden departure from Barton—it was all dictated by his own pleasure or convenience."

"It is true," Marianne agreed. "My happiness was never his objective."

"At present," Elinor continued, "he regrets his actions solely because they have failed to bring him personal satisfaction. Now that his financial burdens are lifted, he merely complains that he married a woman of a less agreeable temperament than yours. But does this mean he would have been happier with you? The hardships would differ. He would have suffered under the crushing weight of poverty, which he currently ignores. He would have had a wife of perfect temper, yet he would have been perpetually destitute. Soon, he would likely learn to value the comforts of wealth far more highly than the kindness of a partner."

"I have no doubt of it," Marianne said. "I regret nothing but my own foolishness."

"Perhaps you should blame your mother's imprudence, my child," Mrs. Dashwood offered softly. "She bears the responsibility."

Marianne stopped her mother before she could elaborate. Elinor, sensing that dwelling on the past would only weaken her sister's spirit, steered the conversation elsewhere. "There is one lesson we can draw from this tragedy," she said. "All of Willoughby's troubles stem from his initial violation of virtue in his dealings with Eliza Williams. That original sin spawned every subsequent failure and his current misery."

Marianne nodded in solemn agreement. This prompted Mrs. Dashwood to list Colonel Brandon's virtues and the wrongs done to him, speaking with warmth born of friendship and justice. However, Elinor noticed that Marianne barely seemed to listen. Over the next few days, Marianne's recovery stalled. Though she maintained a brave front, appearing cheerful and composed, Elinor trusted that time itself would heal her.

Margaret returned, and the family resettled quietly at the cottage. While their studies lacked the vigor of their earlier days at Barton, they planned to resume them with renewed energy. Meanwhile, Elinor grew increasingly anxious about Edward. Since leaving London, she had received no news of his plans or whereabouts. Letters from her brother John had confirmed only that they knew nothing of their "unfortunate" Edward and assumed he remained at Oxford—a fact never explicitly stated in subsequent correspondence.

Then, one morning, the household servant returned from Exeter. After answering questions about his errand, he volunteered a startling piece of news. "I suppose you know, ma'am, that Mr. Ferrars is married."

Marianne jumped violently, locking eyes with Elinor, who turned deathly pale. Before Elinor could react, Marianne collapsed into hysterics. Mrs. Dashwood, seeing the horror on her daughter's face, realized the full impact of the news. Distressed by both Marianne's breakdown and Elinor's shock, she struggled momentarily to decide whom to aid first. The servant, noticing Marianne's collapse, wisely summoned a maid. Together, they helped the hysterical girl to another room.

By the time Marianne had calmed slightly, her mother left her in Margaret's care and returned to Elinor. Though shaken, Elinor had regained her composure and began questioning the servant. Mrs. Dashwood took over the interrogation, sparing Elinor the strain.

"Who told you Mr. Ferrars was married, Thomas?"

"I saw Mr. Ferrars myself this morning in Exeter, along with his lady, Miss Steele," the servant explained. "They were waiting in a carriage at the New London Inn when I arrived with a message for her brother, a post-boy. As I passed, I recognized Miss Steele and tipped my hat. She greeted me warmly, asking after you and the young ladies, particularly Miss Marianne. She conveyed her and Mr. Ferrars' best wishes and expressed regret that they were in too much of a hurry to visit, noting they were traveling further west for a short while but promised to return and call."

"But did she mention the marriage?"

"Yes, ma'am. She smiled and said she had changed her name since visiting these parts. She is always so affable and straightforward. I congratulated her accordingly."

"Was Mr. Ferrars in the carriage?"

"Yes, ma'am. He leaned back, looking away. He is rarely talkative."

"Were there others?"

"No, just the two of them."

"Do you know where they came from?"

"They came straight from town, as Mrs. Ferrars told me."

"And are they heading west?"

"Yes, ma'am, but not to stay long. They'll be back soon, and then they'll surely call here."

Mrs. Dashwood glanced at Elinor, expecting her to ask for details, but Elinor knew better. She recognized the manipulative nature of Lucy's message; she was confident Edward would never approach them again. Speaking in a low voice to her mother, she noted they were likely heading to Mr. Pratt's near Plymouth.

Thomas's report ended there. Elinor appeared to want more, but the servant shook his head. "Did you see them off?"

"No, ma'am. The horses were just starting, but I couldn't wait longer; I feared being late."

"Did Mrs. Ferrars look well?"

"Yes, ma'am. She said she was very well. To me, she has always been a handsome young lady, and she seemed immensely content."

With no further questions arising, Thomas and the tablecloth were dismissed. Marianne had already informed them she could eat nothing. Both Mrs. Dashwood and Elinor lost their appetites. Margaret, however, felt fortunate; despite the emotional turmoil and the sisters' frequent neglect of proper meals, she had never gone hungry.

Once dessert and wine were served, and the servants withdrew, Mrs. Dashwood and Elinor sat in a profound silence, sharing a similar heaviness of thought. Mrs. Dashwood dared not speak, fearing any words might cause harm. She realized she had erred in trusting Elinor's earlier assurances. Everything had been softened then to protect Elinor from additional grief, especially since she was already suffering greatly for Marianne's sake.
