In those years it was no light undertaking to ride from Plymouth northward into Devon, unless a man chose to lose half a day by wide detours and safer lanes. For between a traveller and his destination lay a tract of moor and broken down where authority went thin as watered ale, and where a sort of petty prince—no anointed king, yet feared as if crowned—kept his own rough court. Folk called him, when he could not hear, the King of the Gubbings; and the name, like a fishmonger’s insult flung with relish, clung because it carried shame in it and because the country itself seemed to answer to it: a Scythia within England, so men said, a heathen patch on Christian soil, near Brent-Tor on the skirts of Dartmoor.

Amyas Leigh, therefore, rode out of Plymouth as if he meant to tilt at an army. He sat in full armor on a good horse, with lance and sword, and, slung ready besides, two great dags. Behind him came Salvation Yeo and five or six North Devon men, old comrades from the Irish work, home on furlough—head-pieces over their brows, quilted jerkins on their backs, pikes and swords in hand; and Yeo with arquebuse and match as carefully tended as a cook’s slow fire. Two sumpter ponies, patient as any dairy-cow, carried their baggage.

They meant to sleep at Lydford; but horses had to be bought, straps mended, petty delays paid for in coin and temper, and they did not start till noon. Night slid down as they reached the frontier of that lawless country. Sunset threw a wild glare over the heath—a high tableland where the wind ran clean and hard, banked on the right by Dartmoor’s crags and swelling hills, and sloping away toward Brent-Tor, which reared up like a black cone of some dead volcano, topped with a tiny church. Far down the waste, thin blue threads of smoke rose from scattered dens: the Gubbings’ homes, if holes might be called so. More than once Amyas called a halt, staring across furze and pony-shapes in the distance, asking himself whether they were only moor-beasts or men posted like wolves to watch a fold.

Laughing at such fears is easy in a later, louder age that fancies itself “civil” because its noises are constant and its roads are mapped. But on that down, where the only steady sound was the wind’s low grind through heath and stone, every foreign clink or shout struck the mind like a thrown pebble into still water. Their ears were as wary as their eyes; and within two miles they learned that caution was not cowardice.

Midway on the down stood a wayside inn—more a lump of lichen-spotted granite than a house, with paper patched into broken panes, and a rotting thatch pinned down by stones and straw-banks as if the roof had to be bullied into staying. Behind it sprawled a rambling court of barns and walls. Pigs nosed among muck; barefoot children grunted and rolled in a brotherhood of dirt.

At the door, as if lost in admiration of the last orange light touching the moor, stood the innkeeper—brawny and sodden, blear-eyed, six feet of brutishness. He held up his hose with one hand for want of points, and clawed at elf-locks in which feathers stuck—witnesses enough, for any who cared to read them, that he had slept lately on straw and had geese enough to lend him their down in more ways than one.

He saw Amyas’s armored figure crest the rise, counted the party with a slow blink, and gave a grunt. Then, with the sleepy howl of a man who keeps his words for oaths, he called:

“Mirooi!—Fushing pooale!”

A strapping lass came out at once—Mary, they named her—her working dress as scant as the times allowed countrywomen, green bodice over red petticoat, both too little for modesty and too stained for pride. She brought him a fishing-rod and basket. He examined the footlink and snarled.

“Don vlies’ gone!”

“May be,” Mary answered, without much concern. “Shouldn’t hay’ left mun out to coort. May be old hen’s ate mun off. I see her chocking about a while agone.”

He took the news with an oath and a cuff at her head. She ducked, and, as quick as a cook’s spatula when grease flames, brought her own blow home upon his shock of hair. He shuffled away howling, accustomed to such domestic music.

“Tell Patrico!”

Mary ran in, combed her hair with fingers that knew more of dough and soot than silk, slipped stockings over her legs and her best gown over her dirt, and waited the guests with the practiced face of one who can smile while counting what may be stolen.

Amyas’s men drew long faces at the place—“mucksy,” one muttered—but chose its roof over a bivouac on the edge of the enemy’s smoke. So the cooking began; and in that kitchen, as in many a poor house, the day’s fear was managed the only way fear can be managed—by fire and food.

The old hen who had swallowed the dun fly was killed, plucked, and set to roast; her skin hissed and tightened above the coals, fat spitting like small shot on the hearthstones. Black Dartmoor mutton was slapped on the gridiron; it sizzled and smoked, and—forced by that fiery torment into confession—betrayed itself to every honest nose as stolen red-deer venison. Outside, the wind kept up its moaning sermon along the eaves; inside, the crackle and hiss of meat, the clink of knives, and the low talk of hungry men laid a domestic spell over the savage land.

Amyas had put his horse and ponies into a shed where he found neither lock nor key, and came back grumbling, uneasy for the steed’s safety. The baggage was heaped in a corner. He stretched his legs before a turf fire while Salvation Yeo, whose conscience had an iron edge, planted himself at the door and watched the yard as if watching for Amalekites. The men, seized with a sudden zeal for household management, pressed close to the cooking—perhaps because Mary held the knife, and the air around her was thick with smoke, salt, and warm fat.

Presently Yeo stepped in.

“There’s a gentleman just coming up, sir, all alone.”

“Ask him to make one of our party, then, with my compliments,” Amyas said.

Yeo went out and returned.

“Please, sir, he’s gone in back ways, by the court.”

“Well, he has an odd taste, if he makes himself at home here.”

Out went Yeo again, and back he came in high excitement, as if the very soundscape had shifted under his feet and warned him.

“Come out, sir; for goodness’ sake come out. I’ve got him. Safe as a rat in a trap, I have!”

“Who?”

“A Jesuit, sir.”

“Nonsense, man!”

“I tell you truth, sir. I went round the house, for I didn’t like the looks of him as he came up. I knew he was one of them villains the minute I saw him—by the way he turned in his toes, and put down his feet so still and careful, like as if he was afraid of offending God at every step. So I put my eye between the wall and the dern of the gate, and I saw him knock at the back door and call ‘Mary!’ quite still, like any Jesuit; and the wench flies out ready to eat him; and ‘Go away,’ I heard her say, ‘there’s a dear man;’ and then something about a ‘queer cuffin’’—that’s a justice in these canters’ thieves’ Latin; and with that he takes out a somewhat—I’ll swear it was one of those Popish Agnuses—and gives it her; and she kisses it and crosses herself and asks him if that’s the right way, and then puts it into her bosom, and he says, ‘Bless you, my daughter;’ and then I was sure of the dog. And he slips to the stable, peeps in, and when he sees no one there, in he goes; and out I go, and shut to the door, and back a cart up against it, and call one of the men to watch the stable, and the girl’s crying like mad.”

“What a fool’s trick, man!” Amyas burst out, hurrying after him. “How do you know he is not some honest gentleman, after all?”

“Fool or none, sir; honest gentlemen don’t give maidens Agnuses. I’ve put him in; and if you want him let out again, you must come and do it yourself. My conscience is against it, sir. If the Lord’s enemies are delivered into my hand, I’m answerable. ’Tis written, ‘If any let one of them go, his life shall be for the life of him.’”

Amyas dragged the cart back, opened the stable-door, and began a string of apologies—only to find his cousin Eustace Leigh, trapped indeed, sitting with a face half foolish, half venomous, like a fox uncovered at last by the terriers.

For a moment neither spoke. Then Amyas, trying to keep it light, said:

“Well, cousin hide-and-seek, how long have you added horse-stealing to your other trades?”

“My dear Amyas,” Eustace replied, very meek, “I may surely go into an inn stable without intending to steal what is in it.”

“Of course, old fellow,” Amyas answered, mollified. “I was only in jest. But what brings you here? Not prudence, certainly.”

“I am bound to know no prudence save for the Lord’s work.”

“That’s giving away Agnus Deis, and deceiving poor heathen wenches, I suppose,” growled Yeo.

Eustace turned on him roundly.

“Heathens? Yes, truly; you Protestants leave these poor wretches heathens, and then insult and persecute those who, with a devotion unknown to you, labor at the danger of their lives to make them Christians. Mr. Amyas Leigh, you can give me up to be hanged at Exeter, if it shall so please you to disgrace your own family; but from this spot neither you, no, nor all the myrmidons of your queen, shall drive me, while there is a soul here left unsaved.”

“Come out of the stable, at least,” Amyas said. “You don’t want to make the horses Papists as well as the asses, do you? Come out, man, and go to the devil your own way. I sha’n’t inform against you; and Yeo here will hold his tongue if I tell him, I know.”

“It goes sorely against my conscience, sir; but being that he is your cousin—of course—”

“Of course; and now come in and eat with me; supper’s just ready, and bygones shall be bygones, if you will have them so.”

Whatever forgiveness Eustace felt—or did not feel—he knew what he ought to enact, and he knew also that a meal shared might serve the Church as well as any sermon. So he went in. Yet he never forgot the scar upon his cheek; and Amyas could not meet his eye but Eustace fancied he saw Amyas’s gaze slide to it, and peeped up from under his lids to catch, if he could, some triumph on that honest face.

They ate. The venison—mutton turned deer by theft and fire—came off the gridiron in dark strips, seared without, red within, smelling of smoke and marrow. Cider, sour as the place, went round. And as mouths were filled and bellies warmed, their talk, guarded at first, loosened and drifted back to old lanes and old rooms, to boyhood haunts and kin: uncles, aunts, cousins spoken of like dishes remembered—some sweet, some bitter, all flavored with time.

Then Eustace, with a suddenness that showed his heart had been crouching like a hare in fern, asked Amyas why he rode to Bideford while Frank and his mother were in London.

“To tell you the truth,” Amyas said, “I cannot rest till I have heard the whole story about poor Rose Salterne.”

“What about her?” Eustace cried, and the words tore out of him.

“Do you not know?”

“How should I know anything here? For heaven’s sake, what has happened?”

Amyas told him; and as he spoke he wondered at the other’s eagerness, for he had never once suspected what lay beneath Eustace’s guarded piety.

Eustace shrieked aloud.

“Fool, fool that I have been! Caught in my own trap! Villain, villain that he is! After all he promised me at Lundy!”

He sprang up, stamped up and down, gnashing his teeth, tossing his head and clutching at the empty air with that dreadful blind gesture of despair still hunting for what it knows is lost. The room rang with it. Even the fire seemed to crackle louder, as if startled.

Amyas sat thunderstruck. His first impulse was to demand: Lundy? What knew you of him? What had he—or you—to do there? But pity, for once, beat curiosity.

“Oh, Eustace! And you then loved her too?”

“Don’t speak to me! Loved her? Yes, sir, and had as good a right to love her as any one of your precious Brotherhood of the Rose. Don’t speak to me, I say, or I shall do you a mischief!”

So Eustace knew of the brotherhood as well. Amyas felt the question rise like bile; he swallowed it down. If Eustace knew, he knew; and what harm in that compared with the harm already done?

“My good cousin,” Amyas said, forcing his voice steady, “why be wroth with me? If you really love her, now is the time to take counsel with me how best we shall—”

Eustace cut him off. Conscious he had betrayed more than one secret, he stopped, drew himself together by a great effort, and eyed Amyas from under his brows with his old down-look.

“How best we shall do what, my valiant cousin?” he asked, his voice meaning and half-scornful. “What does your most chivalrous Brotherhood of the Rose purpose in such a case?”

Amyas, nettled, stood on his guard.

“What the Brotherhood of the Rose will do, I can’t yet say. What it ought to do, I have a pretty sure guess.”

“So have I,” Eustace flung back. “To hunt her down as you would an outlaw, because forsooth she has dared to love a Catholic; to murder her lover in her arms, and drag her home again stained with his blood, to be forced by threats and persecution to renounce that Church into whose maternal bosom she has doubtless long since found rest and holiness!”

“If she has found holiness,” Amyas answered, “it matters little to me where she has found it; but that is the very point that I should be glad to know for certain.”

“And you will go and discover for yourself?”

“Have you no wish to discover it also?”

“And if I had, what would that be to you?”

“Only,” Amyas said, grinding down his temper, “that, if we had the same purpose, we might sail in the same ship.”

“You intend to sail, then?”

“I mean simply, that we might work together.”

“Our paths lie on very different roads, sir!”

“I am afraid you never spoke a truer word,” Amyas said. “In the meanwhile, ere we part, be so kind as to tell me what you meant by saying that you had met this Spaniard at Lundy?”

“I shall refuse to answer that.”

“You will please to recollect, Eustace, that however good friends we have been for the last half-hour, you are in my power. I have a right to know the bottom of this matter; and, by heaven, I will know it.”

“In your power? See that you are not in mine!” Eustace shot back. “Remember, sir, that you are within a—within a few miles, at least, of those who will obey me, their Catholic benefactor, but who owe no allegiance to those Protestant authorities who have left them to the lot of the beasts which perish.”

Amyas’s anger rose hot and quick. He wanted only a touch more to seize Eustace by the shoulders and shake him till the crookedness rang out. But to take him was to doom him; to doom him was to drag disgrace and death upon the family. Remembering others’ mercy shown once before, he held his hand.

“Take me,” Eustace said, with cold courage, “if you will, sir. You, who complain of us that we keep no faith with heretics, will perhaps recollect that you asked me into this room as your guest, and that in your good faith I trusted when I entered it.”

The plea was thin, but it bit because it sounded like honor. Amyas went to the door, flung it open, and bowed.

“Walk out,” he said, “and go to the devil, since you seem set on ending your days in that company.”

Eustace vanished into the night.

Amyas stood a moment listening. The moor-wind pressed its long breath against the house; somewhere a loose shutter tapped a dull beat. In such quiet, even a man’s thoughts sound loud, and his own words—devil, honor, duty—seemed to echo back altered, as if the very land translated them into a harsher dialect.

“Pooh!” he said to himself at last. “I can find out enough, and too much, I fear, without the help of such crooked vermin. I must see Cary; I must see Salterne; and I suppose, if I am ready to do my duty, I shall learn somehow what it is. Now to sleep; to-morrow up and away to what God sends.”

He called down the passage:

“Come in hither, men, and sleep here. Haven’t you had enough of this villainous sour cider?”

The men came yawning and flung themselves on the floor. Amyas laid himself on the settle, his saddle for a pillow.

“Where’s Yeo?”

No one knew. He had gone out to say his prayers and had not returned.

“Never mind,” Amyas muttered, suspecting some plot of vigilance in the old Puritan. “He’ll take care of himself, I’ll warrant him.”

“No fear of that, sir,” one of the tars answered; and soon four were snoring in concert round the fire while Amyas drifted into sleep, the turf-smoke in his nostrils and the taste of venison still on his tongue.

It was about midnight when he started up—no, rather fell backward, upsetting saddle, settle, and then table, under the belief that ten thousand flying dragons had burst at the window with howls. The “dragons,” rushing past in the dark, proved only terror-stricken geese flapping and screaming around the corner of the house. But the sound that had scared them did not cease. Another moment made it plain: a sharp fight in the courtyard, and Yeo hallooing for help.

Out tumbled the men, swords in hand. They burst the back door, stumbled over pails and pitchers, and rushed into the yard. There was Yeo, his back against the stable-door, sword and buckler up, holding his own against a dozen men.

The night became a kind of brutal orchestra: geese screaming, chickens screaming, pigs screaming, donkeys braying in outrage, Mary screaming from an upper window; and, as if drawn by the racket, plovers wheeled overhead, their wild cries sewing the noise to the dark. The court rang with it all—the acoustic misery of a place where even terror had a chorus.

But as Amyas charged in, the ruffians broke. They fled over a low hedge at the far end of the yard and vanished into the moor like smoke sucked into wind.

“Are you hurt, Yeo?”

“Not a scratch, thank Heaven! But I’ve got two of them, the ringleaders, I have. One of them’s against the wall. Your horse did for t’other.”

The wounded man was lifted—huge, nearly Amyas’s own size. Yeo’s sword had gone through him. He groaned and choked, fighting for air like a fish on stones.

“Carry him indoors. Where is the other?”

“Dead as a herring, in the straw. Have a care, men, have a care how you go in! the horses are near mad!”

After some struggling and soothing words that did little, they brought the dead man out. No pulse, no breath.

“Carry him in too, poor wretch,” Amyas said. “And now, Yeo, what is the meaning of all this?”

Yeo told it quickly. He could not get it out of his head that Eustace had come for the horses. He had seen the innkeeper sneak off when they arrived; expecting a night-attack, he had taken his lodging in the stable.

As he expected, the attempt came. The door was opened—how, he could not say, though he had fastened it inside—and two fellows slipped in and began to loose the beasts. Yeo seized the big one; the man drew a knife and broke away; the horses, terrified, kicked right and left. One man fell. The other ran out calling for help, with Yeo at his heels.

“Whereon,” said Yeo, “seeing a dozen more on me with clubs and bows, I thought best to shorten the number while I could, ran the rascal through, and stood on my ward; and only just in time I was, what’s more. There’s two arrows in the house wall, and two or three more in my buckler, which I caught up as I went out, for I had hung it close by the door, you see, sir, to be all ready in case.”

So the cunning old Philistine-slayer spoke as they went in after the wounded man, stepping from yard-noise into kitchen-stink—blood, sweat, and the cooling reek of roast.

They had scarcely ducked through the low doorway into the back-kitchen when fresh shouting rose inside. Amyas ran forward, struck his head on the lintel, and blinked through the sparks in his eyes to see an old acquaintance held fast, one sailor on each side.

With one arm thrust into his doublet sleeve, the other bare in a none-too-clean shirt; holding up his hose with one hand and a candle with the other—lighted, it seemed, to show his own confusion—stood Mr. Evan Morgans, alias Father Parsons. His face was fiery with rage; his half-dressed figure, between disordered cloth and swelling pride, looked (as Yeo told him bluntly) like “the very moral of a half-plucked turkey-cock.” Behind him, dressed and composed, stood Eustace Leigh.

“We found the maid letting these here two out by the front door,” said one of the sailors.

Amyas’s eyes narrowed.

“Well, Mr. Parsons,” he said, “and what are you about here? A pretty nest of thieves and Jesuits we seem to have routed out this evening.”

“About my calling, sir,” Parsons answered stoutly. “By your leave, I shall prepare this my wounded lamb for that account to which your man’s cruelty has untimely sent him.”

On the floor the wounded ruffian heard the priest’s voice and moaned thickly for the “Patrico.”

“You see, sir,” said Parsons, with pompous certainty, “the sheep know their shepherd’s voice.”
