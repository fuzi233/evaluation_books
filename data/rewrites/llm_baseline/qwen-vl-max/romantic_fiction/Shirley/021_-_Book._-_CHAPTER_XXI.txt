While Shirley was engaged in conversation with Moore, Caroline returned to Mrs. Pryor upstairs. She found her governess deeply troubled, though she would not admit that Miss Keeldar’s hasty words had wounded her. Yet the pain was evident—quiet, persistent, like a bruise beneath the skin. To anyone else, Mrs. Pryor might have seemed indifferent to the gentle, tender efforts Caroline made to comfort her; but Caroline understood that beneath her stillness lay deep feeling, and that every kind word was noticed, valued, and slowly mended her spirit.

“I lack self-confidence,” Mrs. Pryor said at last, her voice low and steady. “Always have. And yet I thought Miss Keeldar knew me well enough by now to understand that I never act without care—never without a desire to do what is right, even when it costs me.” She paused, then added, “The situation was so unusual—coming hard on the heels of last night’s alarms. I couldn’t make a decision for someone else’s sake. But I trust no real harm will come from my hesitation.”

A soft knock sounded at the door. It opened slightly.

“Caroline,” came a quiet voice. “Come here.”

Miss Helstone stepped out into the gallery. There stood Shirley, looking small and contrite, as if she were a child caught in a misdeed—ashamed, sorrowful, and full of regret.

“How is Mrs. Pryor?” she asked.

“Not very cheerful,” Caroline replied gently.

Shirley looked down. “I’ve behaved terribly—unfairly, ungratefully. How could I turn on her like that? It wasn’t her fault at all—only her kindness, her over-scrupulousness. I’m truly sorry. Please tell her that—and ask if she can forgive me.”

Caroline went back with a heart full of relief. Mrs. Pryor rose and stood at the doorway. She disliked scenes; they frightened her, as they do all who are timid. “Come in, my dear,” she said, her voice trembling slightly.

Shirley entered quickly, throwing her arms around her governess and kissing her warmly. “You must forgive me, Mrs. Pryor,” she whispered. “I couldn’t bear it if there were any distance between us. I need you too much.”

“There’s nothing to forgive,” Mrs. Pryor replied. “Let it go. The only thing this has shown me is how ill-equipped I am for moments like these.”

And that was the truth she could not shake off. No amount of Shirley’s or Caroline’s affection could erase the ache of her own perceived failure. She could forgive her pupil—but not herself.

Miss Keeldar, busy with callers throughout the morning, was soon summoned downstairs again. The rector arrived first, greeted with both warmth and playful reproach. He expected both, and in his good humour, accepted them with equal grace.

In the short time he stayed, he forgot entirely to ask after his niece. His mind was filled with the events of the night—the riot, the mob, the mill, the magistrates, the heiress. He spoke of his role in defending the Hollow.

“The wrath of the Pharisees will fall upon our heads for standing firm,” he declared. “But I defy every slanderer. I was there to uphold the law—to act as a man and a Briton. These roles, I believe, are fully compatible with those of priest and Levite, in their noblest sense.”

He praised Moore: “A cooler commander I’ve never seen, nor one more resolute. He showed sound judgment—first in preparing thoroughly for what happened, and later, when victory was secured, in knowing how to use power wisely. Some magistrates are now afraid, and fear makes men cruel. But Moore restrains them with admirable calm. Once despised, he may now find favor turning his way. People will realize they underestimated him, and rush to correct their mistake. And once he sees public opinion shifting, he’ll likely show more grace than he ever has before.”

Mr. Helstone was about to add a half-joking warning to Shirley about her rumored affection for Moore when a ring at the door interrupted him. A visitor arrived—a white-haired gentleman with a stern face and sharp eyes. It was Mr. Yorke, the rector’s old adversary.

At sight of him, the rector snatched up his hat, gave a brief farewell to Shirley, and nodded curtly at Yorke before leaving abruptly.

Mr. Yorke was in no mood for diplomacy. He launched into a fierce critique of the night’s events. Moore, the magistrates, the soldiers, the mob leaders—all were condemned in turn. But he reserved his harshest words—rich with Yorkshire dialect—for the clergy. The “sanguinary, demoniac” parsons, he called them. The church, he said, was now in a “bonny pickle.” It was time it fell when ministers began swaggering with soldiers, firing guns, taking lives.

“The church,” he declared, “is in a mess. It’s time it came down when parsons start acting like warriors, killing honest folk who’re better than themselves.”

“What would Moore have done if no one helped him?” Shirley asked.

“He’d have been drunk as he brewed, eaten as he baked.”

“Which means you’d have left him alone to face the mob. Well, he has courage—but even the bravest heart couldn’t stand two hundred angry men.”

“He had soldiers—poor souls who sell their blood and spill others’ for pay.”

“You condemn soldiers almost as fiercely as you condemn clergymen,” Shirley retorted. “To you, red coats are national refuse, black gowns are national swindlers. You say Moore was wrong to call in troops—and worse, to accept help from anyone. So your logic says he should have abandoned his mill and his life to a mob of madmen, while Mr. Helstone and every other gentleman watched silently as the building burned and its owner died—without lifting a finger.”

“If Moore had treated his workers like a true master from the start,” Yorke insisted, “they wouldn’t feel as they do now.”

“Easy for you to say,” Shirley shot back, warming to her defense of Moore. “You’ve lived at Briarmains for six generations. The people know you. You understand their ways, their fears, their habits. But Mr. Moore came here as a stranger—poor, friendless, with only his talent and industry to rely on. Is it a crime that he couldn’t instantly win their love? That he didn’t become jocular and familiar with a peasantry he barely knew? That he introduced improvements without bending every change to suit their whims? Should he be punished by mob violence for such mistakes? Denied even the right to defend himself? And should those who stand beside him—like Mr. Helstone, however flawed—be mocked as criminals for doing what’s just?”

“Now, now,” Yorke said, smiling faintly at her intensity. “Be calm.”

“Calm? Must I stay calm while listening to nonsense—dangerous nonsense? No. I like you, Mr. Yorke, I really do. But I detest some of your principles. All that talk—excuse me, but I’ll say it again—*that cant* about soldiers and parsons—it grates on my ears. Ridiculous, irrational glorification of one class—whether aristocrat or democrat—and denigration of another—clerical or military—without regard for individuals, without justice. It’s sickening. All division, all party hatred, all tyranny masked as liberty—I reject it all. You think you’re a philanthropist, a champion of freedom. But let me tell you—Mr. Hall, the parson of Nunnely, is a far greater friend to humanity and liberty than Hiram Yorke, reformer of Briarfield.”

From most men, such words would have drawn anger. From many women, silence or defiance. But Yorke saw something else in Shirley—honesty and beauty, fire and charm. Her passion amused him. And secretly, he took pleasure in watching her defend Moore—because he cared deeply for Robert Moore himself. If he wished to wound her, he knew how: a single word could silence her, flush her cheeks, dim her eyes with shame.

“What more have you to say?” he asked, as she paused—not because she was spent, but to gather breath.

“Say? I have much to say—if only I could put it clearly. But I never can. I must say this: your views, and those of extreme politicians, are only possible for men who don’t have to govern. They’re opposition ideas—meant for speeches, not action. Make you Prime Minister tomorrow, and you’d abandon them. You condemn Moore for defending his mill. But if you were in his place, you’d have done exactly the same—honourably, sensibly. You attack Mr. Helstone for everything he does. He has flaws, yes—but more often he does right. If you were vicar here, you’d find it hard to carry on all the good works your predecessor started. Why can’t people judge each other fairly?

“When I hear Messrs. Malone and Donne speak of the church’s authority, the dignity of the priesthood, their outrage at Dissenters, their petty jealousies, their obsession with forms and traditions, their arrogance toward the poor, their servility to the rich—I see the Establishment in dire need of reform. And then I recall your sneers at ‘fat bishops,’ ‘pampered parsons,’ ‘old mother church.’ Your sweeping judgments against whole classes, without mercy for circumstances—then I wonder: are there any men left who are wise, fair, and just enough to lead reform? I don’t believe *you* are one.”

“You have a poor opinion of me, Miss Shirley,” Yorke said. “You’ve never told me this before.”

“I never had the chance. But I’ve sat on Jessy’s stool by your side in the back parlour, listening to your words—half admiring, half rebelling. You’re a fine old Yorkshireman. I’m proud to share your county, your parish. You’re truthful, upright, unshakable as a rock beneath the sea. But you’re also harsh, narrow, merciless.”

“Only to the proud and high-minded,” he replied.

“And what right have you to draw such lines? You’re prouder than anyone I know. You speak kindly to your inferiors—but you’re too proud, too ambitious, too jealous to treat those above you with respect. But you’re not alone. Mr. Helstone is proud and prejudiced. Moore, though fairer than either of you, is still stern, distant, selfish in public. It’s good there are men like Mr. Hall—men with wide hearts, who can love all mankind, who can forgive others for being richer, stronger, more powerful. Such men may lack your force, your originality—but they are better friends to humanity.”

“And when is it to be?” Yorke asked, rising.

“When is what?”

“The wedding.”

“Whose wedding?”

“Only that of Robert Gérard Moore, Esq., of Hollow’s Cottage, with Miss Keeldar, daughter and heiress of Charles Cave Keeldar of Fieldhead Hall.”

Shirley stared at him, her face flushing. But her eyes did not falter—they shone with a quiet fire, deep and bright.

“That’s your revenge,” she said slowly.

“Would it be an unworthy match?” she added. “Unworthy of the late Charles Cave Keeldar’s heir?”

“My lass,” Yorke answered, “Moore is a gentleman. His blood is as pure and ancient as mine or yours.”

“And we value ancient blood? We have family pride—even if one of us calls herself a republican?”

Yorke bowed. His lips remained silent, but his eyes confessed the truth. Yes—he had pride. It was written in every line of his bearing.

“Moore *is* a gentleman,” Shirley echoed, lifting her head with quiet joy. Then she stopped. Words surged in her mouth, but she held them back. Her look said everything. Yorke tried to read it—but failed. It was a poem in a language unknown—fervent, complex, not mere confession. It was deeper than love, more intricate than longing. He felt his revenge had missed. She was not humbled—she triumphed. He was baffled, uncertain. *She* owned the moment.

“And if Moore is a gentleman,” he said, “you can only be a lady. Therefore—”

“Therefore, no inequality in our union.”

“None.”

“Thank you for your approval. Will you give me away when I leave the name Keeldar for Moore?”

Instead of answering, Yorke stared at her, puzzled. Could she mean it? Was it jest or earnest?

“I don’t understand you,” he said, turning away.

She laughed softly. “Take courage, sir. You’re not alone in your confusion. But if Moore understands me—that will suffice, won’t it?”

“Moore may manage his own affairs from now on,” Yorke replied. “I’ll meddle no more.”

A sudden thought struck her. Her expression changed—eyes darkened, features set with gravity.

“Have you been asked to interfere?” she demanded. “Are you questioning me as someone else’s representative?”

“The Lord save us!” Yorke exclaimed. “Whoever weds you must watch his step! Keep your questions for Robert. I’ll answer none more. Good-day, lassie!”

* * * * *

The day was fair—soft clouds veiled the sun, and a blue haze rested gently on the hills. While Shirley was occupied with visitors, Caroline had persuaded Mrs. Pryor to put on her bonnet and summer shawl and take a walk up the narrow end of the Hollow.

Here, the sides of the glen drew close, thick with brushwood and stunted oaks, forming a wooded ravine. At its bottom, the mill-stream ran in broken course—tumbling over stones, chafing against banks, foaming through gnarled roots. Half a mile from the mill, the path led into deep solitude. Trees offered shelter, birds sang in abundance, and wildflowers bloomed untouched by foot. The air was fresh, the roses seemed to bloom unseen, like secrets in a hidden garden. Bluebells shimmered in sweet azure, and tiny white blossoms dotted the grass like stars fallen to earth.

Mrs. Pryor loved quiet walks. She avoided main roads, preferring winding lanes and secluded paths. Solitude frightened her—vague fears of encounters spoiled her peace. But with Caroline, she was safe. When they walked together, away from human habitation, a change came over her. Her face softened, her spirits lifted. With Caroline—only Caroline—her heart lightened, her thoughts loosened. She became cheerful, tender, willing to share knowledge, glimpses of her past, her understanding of nature and life.

Today, as they walked, she pointed out the birds singing in the trees—naming them, describing their habits. English natural history seemed part of her soul. Every wildflower along the path had a name, a story. Tiny plants growing near stones or in wall crevices—ones Caroline had never noticed—were identified and explained. She had studied botany closely, quietly, over years.

They reached the head of the ravine and sat on a moss-covered rock beneath a steep green hill. Mrs. Pryor looked around, speaking of the landscape as she remembered it long ago. She compared it with other parts of England, revealing a subtle eye for beauty, a quiet appreciation of nature’s contrasts—wild and cultivated, grand and plain. Her words painted scenes with simple grace.

Caroline listened with reverent attention—so quiet, so sincere, yet unmistakable. It stirred Mrs. Pryor’s spirit. Rarely had she known what it was to inspire such genuine affection in someone she could love. To see a young girl look up to her, lean on her, trust her—this was a rare delight. With a slight warmth in her voice, she gently brushed a curl from Caroline’s forehead.

“I hope this mountain air is good for you, my dear,” she said. “I wish I could see more colour in your cheeks. Perhaps you were never florid?”

“I had rosy cheeks once,” Caroline smiled. “Two years ago, I used to see a different face in the mirror—rounder, brighter. But when we’re young,” she added, “our minds are careless, our lives easy.”

“Do you worry about the future at your age?” Mrs. Pryor asked, forcing herself to speak boldly. “Believe me, it’s better not to. Let tomorrow take care of itself.”

“True, dear madam. I don’t pine for the future. But the present—sometimes it weighs too heavily. I long to escape it.”

“That is—the evil of the day—is it perhaps… your uncle? Do you find it hard to understand him? Does he not appreciate…”

She couldn’t finish. But Caroline understood.

“Oh, that’s nothing,” she replied. “My uncle and I get along well. We never quarrel. I don’t think he’s harsh. He never scolds me. Sometimes I wish someone loved me—but I don’t particularly wish for him to love me more than he does. As a child, I might have missed attention—but the servants were kind. And when people are indifferent to us for long, we grow indifferent to their indifference. It’s just his way—not to care for women and girls unless they’re ladies he meets in society. He couldn’t change, and I don’t want him to. I think it would frighten me if he tried to be affectionate now.”

“But you know, Mrs. Pryor,” she continued, “it’s scarcely *living* to measure time as I do at the rectory. The hours pass, and I endure them—but I don’t *live*. Since Miss Keeldар and you came, I’ve been… I was going to say happier—but that would be untrue.”

She paused.

“How untrue?” Mrs. Pryor asked gently.

“I *do* love Shirley,” Caroline said. “I admire her, I care for her. But I’m painfully trapped. For reasons I can’t explain, I want to leave this place—and forget it.”
