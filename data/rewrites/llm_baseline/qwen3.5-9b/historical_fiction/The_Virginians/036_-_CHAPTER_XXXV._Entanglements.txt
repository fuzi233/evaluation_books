Our worthy Colonel, undoubtedly in consultation with his equally virtuous spouse, had resolved to remove their little Hetty from the vicinity of the "charmer" with all possible haste. While complaints of a similar nature often prove curable in men through the simple remedies of absence and distance, I maintain that women are not so readily healed by the alibi treatment. Some will travel ever so far and stay away forever, yet the stubborn malady remains attached to them, defying both geography and climate. One may whip, abuse, torture, or insult them, and still these deluded creatures will persist in their fidelity. Indeed, based on my profound and extensive observations, there are few more effective methods of securing the faithfulness and admiration of our fair partners than a judicious application of mild ill-treatment, a brisk dose of occasional violence as an alterative, and, for the general diet, a cooling but persistent neglect. Administer small quantities of love and kindness at sparing intervals; but avoid daily doses, for this medicine, when taken too frequently, loses its potency. Those who are most indifferent to their husbands are precisely those who have become cloyed by an excess of sugar-plums and lollipops. I have witnessed a young woman, with every desire gratified, yawn in her adoring husband's face while preferring the trivial conversation of a mere booby; conversely, I have seen Chloe—whom Strephon has thrown his bootjack at in the morning or cursed before the servants at dinner—creeping fondly to his knee at tea-time, playing his favorite tunes, and looking around proudly when the butler enters, as if to say, "Now, John, observe how good my dearest Henry is!"

Make your choice, gentlemen! There is the path of coaxing and fondling when one is henpecked and bored out of existence; and there is the manly, selfish, and effectual system where she responds to the whistle, comes in at "Down Charge," knows her master, frisks about him, nuzzles his knees, and "licks the hand that is raised"—that hand raised to do her good, as Mr. Pope so finely observed. What did the late lamented O'Connell remark, over whom a grateful nation has erected such a magnificent monument? "Hereditary bondsmen, know ye not, who would be free, themselves must strike the blow?" Naturally, this applies to both political and domestic spheres. So raise your cudgels, my enslaved and injured boys! Women will appreciate these remarks due to their taste for humor and understanding of irony. I should not be surprised if young Grubstreet, who writes for three-penny papers and chronicles the conversations of gentlemen at his clubs, declares, "I told you so! He advocates thrashing women! He lacks nobility of soul! He has no heart!" Nor do I, my eminent young Grubstreet! any more than you have ears. Ladies, please accept my assurance that I am merely joking; I do not advocate beating your sex at all. Since you cannot comprehend the simplest jest, allow me to state plainly that I consider your sex a hundred times more loving and faithful than ours.

So, what purpose is served by Hetty's parents taking her home if the little maid intends to be just as fond of Harry in his absence as in his presence? Why not let her see him before Ball and Dobbin depart, saying, "Good-bye, Harry! I was wilful and fractious last night, and you were kind; but good-bye, Harry!" She would show no special emotion, too ashamed of her secret to betray it. Harry, too preoccupied to notice, would not detect the grief lurking behind Hetty's glances or hidden beneath the artifice of her innocent smiles. He, perhaps, harbors cares of his own. He would part from her calmly, assuming she is happy to return to her music, poultry, and flower garden. He did not even ride partway home beside his friend's carriage. Having other engagements that afternoon, when he returned, the good Lamberts were gone from Tunbridge Wells. Their windows stood open, and a card indicated the apartments were once more to let. A fleeting sorrow at the blank appearance of rooms recently enlivened by such frank and friendly countenances might cross the young gentleman's mind; but he dined at the White Horse at four, ate his meal, and demanded his bottle fiercely.

Poor little Hester would choke over her tea at roughly the same hour when the Lamberts arrived to sleep at their friends' house in Westerham. By morning, the young roses would be wan in her cheeks, and black circles would surround her eyes. It was the thunder; the night was hot; she could not sleep. She would recover by the next day. And so they came home. There was the gate where he had fallen; the bed he had lain in; the chair in which he used to sit. What ages seemed to have passed! What a gulf separated today from yesterday! Who was that little child calling chickens or watering roses yonder? Was she and that girl the same Hester Lambert? Why, she appeared so much older than Theo now—Theo, who had always been so composed, clever, and prematurely aged. Yet in a night or two, Hester had lived—oh, long, long years! So had many others; and neither poppy nor mandrake could medicate them to the sweet sleep they had tasted the previous evening.

Maria Esmond watched the Lambert cavalcade drive away and felt a grim relief. She looked with hot eyes at Harry when he entered his aunt's card-room, flushed with Barbeau's fine wine. Laughing and rattling in response to his aunt's query about which girl was his sweetheart, he gaily declared he loved them both like sisters. He had never seen a better gentleman or finer people than the Lamberts. "Why isn't Lambert a general?" he remarked. "He has been a most distinguished officer; His Royal Highness the Duke is very fond of him." Madame Bernstein noted that Harry must make interest with Lady Yarmouth for his protégé. *"Elle ravvole de fous, cher bedid anche!"* Madame Bernstein exclaimed, mimicking the Countess's German accent. The Baroness was delighted with her son's success. "You carry off the hearts of all the old women, don't you, Maria?" she asked, sneering at her niece, who quivered under the sting. "You were quite right, my dear, not to perceive that she cheated at cards, and you play like a grand seigneur," continued Madame de Bernstein. "Did she cheat?" cried Harry, astonished. "I am sure, ma'am, I saw no unfair play." "No more did I, my dear, but I am sure she cheated. Bah! Every woman cheats, I and Maria included, when we can get a chance. But when you play with the Walmodens, you do no wrong to lose in moderation; and many men cheat in that way. Cultivate her. She has taken a fancy to your *beaux yeux*. Why should your Excellency not be Governor of Virginia, sir? You must go and pay your respects to the Duke and His Majesty at Kensington. The Countess of Yarmouth will be your best friend at court."

"Why should you not introduce me, aunt?" asked Harry. The old lady's rouged cheek grew redder. "I am not in favor at Kensington," she replied. "I may have been once; and there are no faces so unwelcome to kings as those they wish to forget. All of us want to forget something or somebody. I dare say our ingenu here would like to wipe a sum or two off the slate. Wouldst thou not, Harry?" Harry turned red, as did Maria, and his aunt laughed one of those wicked laughs not altogether pleasant to hear. What did those guilty signals on the cheeks of her nephew and niece signify? What account was scored upon the memory of either that they desired to erase? I fear Madame Bernstein was correct, and that most people possess some ugly reckonings written on their consciences, which they are glad to be rid of. Had Maria known one of the causes of Harry's disquiet, the middle-aged spinster would have been far more uneasy.

For several days, Harry had missed a pocket-book. He remembered possessing it the day he drank so much claret at the White Horse, when Gumbo carried him to bed. He searched for it in the morning, but none of his servants had seen it. He inquired at the White Horse, but found no trace. He dared not advertise the loss and could only make very cautious inquiries. He must not have it known that the book was missing. Imagine the condition of mind Lady Maria Esmond would be in if she knew that the outpourings of her heart were in the hands of the public! The letters contained all sorts of disclosures: a hundred family secrets narrated by an artless correspondent; ample satire and abuse of persons connected with her and Mr. Warrington; expostulations regarding his attentions to other ladies; scorn, scandal, jokes, appeals, and protests of eternal fidelity—the usual farrago, dear madam, which you may remember you wrote to your Edward when engaged, before becoming Mrs. Jones.
