Must I, before this tale draws near its close, tell you what became of that bright taste of freedom and new life I thought I’d won on the park fête-night? Must I recount how the two grand, bold strangers named Freedom and Renovation, whom I’d brought home with me that starry evening, fared when the giddy glow of the festival faded?

I tested them the very next morning. They’d shouted loud boasts of their power to tear me free from the heavy chains of unspoken longing, but when I demanded proof—proof of lighter days, of a heart unshackled—Freedom stammered excuses that he was too poor, too weak to help me right now. Renovation never spoke a single word at all. He’d died quiet in the dark of night, before the sun was high.

There was nothing left for me then but to cling to the faint, secret hope that I had hurried myself to despair too fast, that the old gnawing pain of jealousy had warped my vision. After a short, useless struggle to keep my spirits light, I found myself dragged right back to the old rack of suspense, tied tight and stretched thinner than ever.

Would I see him before his ship sailed? Would he remember me? Did he mean to say goodbye? Would this day, would the next hour, bring him to my door? Or would I have to endure that searing pain of long, empty waiting, that brutal final agony of parting, that silent, mortal wrench that rips up hope and doubt both at once and shakes your whole life to its core—all while the hand that hurts you cannot be soothed into pity, because miles of absence have already fallen between you?

It was the Feast of the Assumption, so no classes were held. The boarders and all the other teachers had attended morning mass, then set off on a long country walk to take their afternoon snack of bread and jam at a friend’s farm. I did not go with them. Only two days remained before the *Paul et Virginie* was due to sail, and I clung to that last, thin chance to see him the way a half-drowned wreck survivor clings to his last splinter of raft, his last frayed cable.

There were carpenters scheduled to do repairs on a broken bench in the first classe that day, the kind of work you can only do when the pupils are all gone. I sat alone, half meaning to drift out to the garden to leave the workmen space, but too listless to even lift myself to my feet, when I heard their footsteps coming down the hall.

Labassecourien artisans always go about their work in pairs—you’d swear it takes two of them just to drive a single nail. As I fumbled to tie the ribbons of my bonnet, which I’d been letting hang idle from my hand, I was vaguely surprised to hear only one pair of footsteps. I noticed too—the sort of trivial, sharp detail you notice when you’re as cooped up and lonely as a prisoner in a dark dungeon—that this man wore soft leather shoes, not the clunky wooden sabots every local workman wore. I assumed it was the master carpenter, coming to inspect the space before sending his journeymen in, so I slung my light scarf around my shoulders. He stepped closer, pushed open the door, my back was turned to him—and a tiny, sharp thrill zipped up my spine, fast and fleeting, before I could even name the feeling. I turned, and there in the doorway, filling the whole frame, was M. Paul Emanuel.

Hundreds of the prayers we mumble heavenward wear out the air and bring us nothing at all. But once, just once in a lifetime, one perfect golden gift drops straight into your lap—bright, whole, unflawed, straight from the mint of fruition.

He wore the dark travelling coat trimmed with velvet he must have planned to wear for his voyage, and for a second I thought he was about to leave that very instant, though I’d been told the ship would not sail for two more days. He looked well, bright, cheerful, kind, his face soft and benign. He stepped toward me eagerly, across the room in a single second, all open warmth and amity. It might have been the bright giddiness of a bridegroom lighting up his face, but whatever it was, I could not meet his sunshine with cold distance. If this was our last moment together, I would not waste it on stiff, pointless formality. I loved him far too much to shove even the demon Jealousy out of my path, not when all she would do was block a kind final farewell. One warm word from his lips, one soft look from his eyes, would sustain me for every remaining day of my life; it would be my comfort through the loneliest, hardest years ahead. I would take that gift. I would drink that sweet elixir, and no silly pride would tip the cup over and spill it.

I thought the interview would be short, of course. He would say the same polite goodbye to me that he had said to every assembled pupil, hold my hand for two minutes, brush a soft kiss to my cheek for the first and only time—and then it would be over. Then the final parting, the wide uncrossable gulf between us, across which he would never glance back to remember me.

He took my hand in one of his, and with the other he pushed my bonnet back off my face, staring deep into my eyes. His bright, luminous smile faded, and his lips took on that soft, wordless expression a mother wears when she finds a child she thought well has wasted away, broken by illness or grief. A shadow fell over his face.

“Paul, Paul!” a woman’s hurried voice called from the hall behind us. “Paul, come to the salon at once! I have so much to say to you—we could talk all day, and Victor is waiting for you, and Josef is here. Come at once, Paul, your friends are waiting.”

Madame Beck, her vigilance or some uncanny sixth sense having led her straight to the door, pressed so close she nearly thrust herself right between M. Paul and me.

“Come, Paul!” she repeated, her sharp, hard gaze grazing me like the point of a steel stiletto. She pushed at his arm. I thought he would recede. I thought he would go with her. The pain pierced me deeper than I could bear, and I could not suppress the cry that tore out of my throat:

“My heart will break!”

It felt like literal, actual heartbreak—but then M. Paul breathed one single word against my ear, a soft whisper: “Trust me.” The weight of it lifted. I wept, great shaking sobs, cold shivers running through me, strong trembling wracking my frame, but all the while I felt a strange, light relief.

“Leave her with me,” the calm, cool Madame Beck said. “This is just a fit of nerves. I will give her a cordial, and it will pass.”

To be left alone to her and her “cordial” felt no different than being left to a poisoner and her bowl. But then M. Paul answered, his voice deep, harsh, brief, ringing with a sound that was strange and strong and life-giving all at once:

“Laissez-moi.” Let me be.

“Laissez-moi!” he repeated, his nostrils flaring, every muscle in his face quivering with restrained fire.

“But this is ridiculous,” Madame Beck snapped, stern. More sternly still, he replied:

“Sortez d’ici.” Get out of this room.

“I will send for Père Silas right this instant,” she threatened, stubborn. “I will call him to intervene.”

“Woman!” he cried, not in his low deep tones now, but his highest, most excited key. “Woman! Sortez à l’instant! Leave this room this very second.”

He was fully roused, and I loved him in that righteous wrath more fiercely than I had ever loved him before.

“What you are doing is wrong,” Madame Beck insisted. “This is exactly the sort of impulsive, unwise, inconsistent nonsense you unreliable, imaginative men always pull—vexatious, unworthy of anyone steady and resolute.”

“You have no idea how steady and resolute I can be,” he told her, his tone softening a little, “but you will see. The outcome will teach you. Modeste, be gentle. Be kind. Be a woman. Look at this poor face, and relent. You know I am your friend, and the friend of all your family. You know deep down you can trust me. I have never hesitated to sacrifice myself, but my heart aches at what I see here. It must give comfort, and it must receive it. *Leave me.*”

That time, the “leave me” was so sharp, so imperative, so full of bitter frustration, I was shocked that even Madame Beck dared to hesitate. But she stood her ground, stared him down dauntless, met his fixed, forbidding gaze as unyielding as stone. She opened her lips to retort, and I saw a quick, bright fire flare across M. Paul’s whole face. He moved gently, no violence, all courtesy, held out his hand toward her—and she spun, whirled out of the room, and the door clicked shut behind her in a single heartbeat.

That flash of temper faded as quickly as it had risen. He smiled at me, told me to wipe my eyes, and waited quietly until my sobs died down, dropping soft, soothing words every now and then until I was myself again, reassured, no longer desperate, no longer desolate, no longer friendless, no longer hopeless, no longer sick of life and ready to welcome death.

“It made you that sad, then, to lose your friend?” he asked.

“It kills me to be forgotten, Monsieur,” I told him truthfully. “All those long, weary days I didn’t hear a single word from you, and I was crushed by the growing certainty that you would sail away without saying goodbye at all.”

“Must I tell you the same thing I just told Modeste Beck? That you do not know me at all? Must I prove to you what kind of friend I can be? You will not rest easy, you will not let your hand stay soft in mine, you will not trust my shoulder as a safe place to lean, until you have seen clear proof? Good. The proof is already ready. I came here to justify myself to you.”

“Say anything. Teach anything. Prove anything, Monsieur. I can listen now.”

“Then first, you must come out walking with me, far out into the town. I came here on purpose to fetch you.”

I did not question his meaning, did not push to know his plan, did not offer a single objection. I re-tied my bonnet ribbons immediately, and was ready.

We walked along the wide tree-lined boulevards, and several times he guided me to sit on the stone benches under the blooming linden trees. He never asked if I was tired. He just looked, and drew his own conclusions.

“‘All these weary days’,” he repeated my own words back to me, mimicking my soft foreign accent with a gentle, playful tease that had never once wounded me, even when he liked to joke that no matter how well I wrote French, I would always speak it hesitantly. “I have not forgotten you for a single hour. Faithful women make the mistake of thinking they are the only faithful souls on God’s green earth. I myself, until very lately, hardly dared to hope such a living, fervent love could ever be returned. But—look at me.”

I lifted my eyes, and they were happy now, bright enough to show him exactly what was in my heart.

“Well,” he said, after staring at my face for a few quiet seconds, “there is no denying the mark written all over you. Constancy wrote it, with a pen of iron. Was it very painful, to bear that writing?”

“Severely painful,” I said, truthfully. “Take her hand away, Monsieur. I cannot bear that sharp, scratching pressure a moment longer.”

“Elle est toute pâle,” he muttered to himself. “That pale face hurts my heart.”

“Ah, I am not pleasant to look at, am I?” The words slipped out unbidden. I had carried a quiet, haunting dread of my own plainness for as long as I could remember, and that fear weighed heavier on me that day than it ever had before.

A great softness spread across his face, his deep violet eyes glistened under their thick dark lashes, and he stood up quickly. “Let us walk on.”

“Do I displease your eyes very much?” I dared to ask, for that question felt like the most vital thing in the world to me in that moment.

He stopped, and gave me a short, firm, unshakable answer that silenced every last one of my old insecurities, subdued all my silly fears, and satisfied me more deeply than I could ever put into words. After that day, I knew exactly what I was to him—and I stopped caring what the rest of the world thought of my looks, one way or another. Was it weak to care that much about one man’s opinion of my face? I suppose it was. But if that is weakness, I own it fully. I had a great fear of displeasing him, and a great, burning wish to please him, and no shame in that at all.

I hardly noticed where we rambled. Our walk was long, but it felt like five minutes. The path was soft, the day was fair and warm. M. Paul talked of his voyage—he thought he would be gone for three full years, and when he returned from Guadeloupe, he would be free of all his old debts and obligations, with a clear path laid out ahead of him. He asked what I planned to do in the long years while he was gone. He reminded me I had once talked of striking out on my own, of opening a tiny little school of my own. Had I dropped that idea?

“Not at all,” I told him. “I’ve been saving every penny I can, to make it happen.”

He said he hated the thought of leaving me behind in the Rue Fossette, that he feared I would miss him far too much there, that I would grow desolate and sad all alone. That was certainly true, but I promised I would do my very best to bear it.

“Still,” he said, his voice soft and low, “there is another problem with you staying there. I want to write to you sometimes. I can’t have any uncertainty that my letters will reach you safe. In the Rue Fossette, well—their strict Catholic rules about private correspondence, while well-meant, could easily be misused, or abused, when you least expect it.”

“If you write me letters,” I declared, “I *will* have them. Ten principals, twenty directresses, will not keep them from me. I am a Protestant. I will not abide that sort of surveillance. Monsieur, I will not.”

“Easy, easy,” he laughed, patting my hand. “We will work out a plan. We have our little tricks, our little resources. Don’t worry. Everything is arranged.”

As he spoke, we came to a stop. We were halfway through a neat, quiet suburb, where the houses were small but bright and welcoming, and he paused right on the white doorstep of a very tidy little cottage.

“I’m paying a call here,” he said.

He did not knock. He pulled a key out of his coat pocket, turned it in the lock, and stepped right inside, ushering me in behind him and shutting the door. No servant came to greet us. The tiny vestibule was freshly painted in soft, pale colors, and at the end of it a French window was draped with climbing vines, green leaves and tender tendrils pressing soft against the glass. The whole house was wrapped in deep, gentle silence.

He opened an inner door to show me the parlor: it was tiny, but the prettiest room I had ever seen. The delicate walls were the soft pink of a blush, the floors were waxed to a shine, a bright square carpet sat dead in the center, and the little round table gleamed as bright as the mirror hung above the fireplace. There was a tiny couch, a small chiffonnière, its crimson silk door half open to show shelves of pretty porcelain, a French clock, a lamp, little biscuit china ornaments, and the recess of the one large window held a green plant stand heavy with three blooming flower pots. In one corner, a small marble-top side table held a workbox and a glass full of fresh violets. The lattice window was open, letting in soft fresh air, and the violets filled the whole room with their sweet scent.

“It’s lovely! So lovely!” I breathed, and M. Paul smiled to see me so delighted.

“Must we sit here and wait for someone?” I whispered, half awed by that deep, still hush.

“First we can peek into all the little nooks of this tiny nutshell house,” he replied.

“Am I allowed to wander all through it?” I asked.

“Yes,” he said, quiet and sure.

He led the way. I saw the tiny kitchen, with its little stove and oven, bright clean brass cookware, two chairs and a table, and a small cupboard holding a neat, matching set of earthenware. “There’s a full china coffee service in the parlor,” he told me, as I stared at the six green-and-white dinner plates, four serving dishes, and the matching cups and jugs.

Up a narrow spotless staircase, he showed me two tiny, cozy bedrooms. Then he led me back downstairs, and paused with a little ceremony before a door larger than all the rest.

He pulled out a second key, turned it in the lock, opened the door, and pushed me gently in before him.

“Voici!” he cried.

I was standing in a much larger room, spotlessly clean, no carpet on the well-scoured wooden floors, lined with two rows of little green benches and desks, a wide aisle down the middle leading up to a raised platform with a teacher’s chair and table, and a blackboard behind it. Two maps hung on the walls, a few hardy flowers bloomed on the window sills. In short, it was a perfect, miniature schoolroom, neat, bright, welcoming.

“So this is a school?” I said, bewildered. “Who runs it? I never heard of any little girl’s school in this suburb.”

“Would you be so kind as to accept a stack of prospectuses to hand out on behalf of a friend of mine?” he asked, pulling a thick stack of printed papers from the pocket of his travelling coat and pressing them into my hand. I looked down, and read the neat, clear printed words:
*Externat de demoiselles. Numéro 7
