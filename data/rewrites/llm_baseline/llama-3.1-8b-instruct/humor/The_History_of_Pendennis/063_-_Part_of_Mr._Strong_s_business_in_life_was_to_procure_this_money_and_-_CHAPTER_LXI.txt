As the sun dipped below the horizon, casting a warm orange glow over the streets of London, the weight of Sir Francis Clavering's duplicity bore down upon his wife, the Begum. Her eyes blazed with indignation, her countenance a mask of disdain, as she contemplated the latest instance of her husband's reckless behavior. "He will not be saved," she declared, her voice laced with a hint of desperation, "by my aid or my tolerance." The tumultuous events of the previous day at the Derby still lingered, a festering wound that threatened to consume her every waking moment.

In the days that followed, Sir Francis became a recluse, avoiding the very people whose debts he had accumulated with such reckless abandon. His valet, discreetly informed by a messenger, discreetly forwarded his master's belongings to the Short Hotel in Blackfriars, where Strong, ever the pragmatist, waited anxiously for his friend's arrival. Strong, aware of the Baronet's propensity for evasion, dispatched a note to the hotel, entreating him to seek refuge beneath the name of Francis, a ruse that would allow him to temporarily escape the consequences of his actions.

As the message was carried by the Boots to Grosvenor Place, the news spread like wildfire through the household staff. The footman, tasked with laying the breakfast table, shared the tidbit with the other servants, who, in turn, informed Mrs. Bonner, the Begum's trusted housekeeper and confidante. Thus, the entire establishment was privy to Sir Francis's whereabouts, their whispers and conjectures painting a dire picture of the Baronet's impending fate: a tour of the Levant, a desperate bid to escape the consequences of his actions.

The day wore on, and the mail arrived, bearing an assortment of letters and bills, each one a poignant reminder of Sir Francis's profligacy. The French cook submitted his account, as did the tradesmen who supplied the Begum's table, and Messrs. Finer and Gimcrack, the mercers and ornamental dealers, and Madame Crinoline, the celebrated milliner. Even Miss Amory's private account was included, a testament to the extent of the Baronet's debts.
