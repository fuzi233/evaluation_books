My days were suddenly filled with purposeful industry. Between instructing others and sharpening my own intellect, I possessed scarcely a moment of leisure. Yet, it was a delightful occupation. I felt myself advancing, no longer stagnating like prey rotting in the damp, but honing my faculties through constant exercise. Before me lay a vast field of experience. Villette is a cosmopolitan hub, and within Madame Beck's school gathered girls from nearly every European nation, hailing from ranks ranging from the highest nobility to the humblest bourgeoisie. At Labassecour, equality was practiced not merely in theory but in substance; though the republic remained a distant ideal, the reality at the desks was striking: the young countess sat shoulder-to-shoulder with the common citizen. One could rarely distinguish the noble from the plebeian by appearance alone. Indeed, the latter often displayed a frankness and courtesy that the former lacked. While the lower-born possessed a natural grace, the aristocracy frequently exhibited a delicate blend of insolence and deceit. Often, a dash of fiery French blood mingled with the local phlegm produced a glib, oily eloquence, where flattery and fabrication flowed effortlessly from the lips—light, lively, yet utterly heartless and insincere.

To be fair, the native daughters of Labassecour possessed their own brand of hypocrisy, albeit a coarse one capable of deceiving few. When a lie served their purposes, they delivered it with a casual breadth, entirely unburdened by conscience. No one in Madame Beck's house, from the scullery maid to the director herself, harbored shame regarding a falsehood. They deemed invention not a virtue, but the most forgivable of sins. "I have lied several times" was a standard item in every girl's monthly confession, met by the priest with no shock and granted absolution without reluctance. Conversely, missing Mass or reading a forbidden novel were treated as grave crimes, warranting stern rebuke and penance.

Though I was only half-aware of this moral landscape, I progressed well in my new role. After the initial perilous lessons—taught amidst the rumbling of a moral volcano sending sparks of anxiety into my eyes—the turbulent spirit eventually subsided. My mind was fixated on success; I could not tolerate the thought of failing due to mere indiscipline or stubbornness in my first attempt at independence. Sleepless nights were spent devising strategies to gain control over these mutinous spirits, to bring this stiff-necked tribe under permanent influence.

First and foremost, I realized that assistance from Madame was impossible. Her strategy was to maintain popularity with the pupils at any cost to justice or teacher comfort. Seeking her alliance during an uprising was tantamount to inviting one's own dismissal. Madame reserved for herself only the pleasant and agreeable interactions, leaving her lieutenants to handle crises with sufficient promptitude, regardless of the unpopularity involved. Consequently, I had to rely solely on myself.

It was evident that brute force would fail against this swinish multitude. They required patience, humor, and a courteous demeanor. A rare flash of wit worked wonders, whereas severe or relentless intellectual demands were rejected outright. Where an average English girl would quietly accept a challenge to master a subject, a Labassecourienne would laugh in one's face, retreating with the cry, "God, how difficult! I refuse. It bores me too much."

A skilled teacher would immediately withdraw the demand, smoothing every difficulty until it matched the student's understanding, only to return it modified. Then, with a hearty, unsparing sarcasm, she would expose their incapacity, ignorance, and laziness. Provided the sneer was bold and clear, not sour, the students would feel the sting but bear no malice. Their fragile self-respect, trained to be crushed, actually preferred the pressure of a firm heel.

Gradually, as I gained fluency in their language and mastered their nervous idioms, the older and smarter girls began to favor me. Whenever I succeeded in igniting a spark of emulation or honest shame within a pupil, she was won. If I could make their ears burn beneath their thick, glossy hair, victory was assured. Soon, bouquets began appearing on my desk. In exchange for this foreign attention, I occasionally walked with a select few during breaks. However, when I attempted to correct their distorted views of morality—particularly condemning the baseness of a lie—an awkward silence fell between us. The girls, taught to report everything a Protestant teacher said to their Catholic ears, interpreted my words differently. Bouquets continued, but conversation became impossible. As I strolled the alleys or sat in the pergola, a teacher would magically appear at my left flank, and Madame, silent as a zephyr, would loom behind me.

One day, a pensionnaire named Isabelle, to whom I had rendered a small kindness, exclaimed naively, "Mademoiselle, what a pity you are a Protestant!"

"Why, Isabelle?"

"Because, when you die, you will burn straightaway in Hell."

"Do you believe that?"

"Certainly everyone knows it; moreover, the priest told me so."

Isabelle was an odd, blunt child. Adding *sotto voce*, she whispered, "To ensure your salvation up there, one might well burn you alive here below."

I laughed, for there was no other response possible.

Have the readers forgotten Miss Ginevra Fanshawe? If so, I must reintroduce her as a thriving pupil of Madame Beck's. Upon arriving in the Rue Fossette, shortly after my own sudden settlement, she encountered me with barely a flicker of surprise. She must possess excellent blood, for no duchess ever exuded nonchalance more perfectly or radically. A weak, fleeting amazement was the limit of her wonder. Most of her faculties seemed equally flimsy; her likes, dislikes, loves, and hates were mere cobwebs. Yet, she possessed one enduring trait: her selfishness.

She was not proud. Being naturally fond of children, she instantly sought to make me a friend and confidante. She teased me with endless complaints about school quarrels and household economics: the cooking displeased her; the staff, both teachers and pupils, were despicable foreigners. I endured her tirades against the Friday salt fish, hard eggs, soup, bread, and coffee with some patience initially, but eventually grew crusty. I finally set her straight—a correction I should have administered sooner, as it always suited her.

Her demands for labor were even more burdensome. While her wardrobe of outerwear was elegant, her undergarments required constant repair. Hating needlework herself, she piled her stockings and other items onto my lap to mend. Threatened with weeks of intolerable drudgery, I firmly declared she must mend her own clothes. She burst into tears, accusing me of ending our friendship, but I stood my ground, letting the hysteria pass.

Despite these foibles—and others of a less refined nature—how beautiful she was! How charming she looked on a sunny Sunday morning, dressed in pale lilac silk, her fair long curls resting on her white shoulders. Sundays were holidays spent with friends in town, among whom she quickly revealed one who desired to become more than a friend. Through hints and glances, it became clear that ardent admiration, perhaps genuine love, was at her disposal. She called her suitor "Isidore," though she noted this was a pseudonym, as his real name was not "very pretty." Once, boasting of "Isidore's" fervor, I asked if she loved him in return.

"As that," she replied casually. "He is handsome, and he loves me to distraction, so I am well amused. That is enough."

Finding she prolonged this game longer than her fickle nature suggested, I inquired seriously whether her parents, and especially her uncle upon whom she depended, would approve. She admitted doubt, noting "Isidore" apparently lacked funds.

"Do you encourage him?" I asked.

"Furiously, sometimes," she answered.

"Without certainty that you will be permitted to marry him?"

"Oh, how dowdyish you are! I don't want to be married. I am too young."

"But if he loves you as much as you claim, and it ends in nothing, he will be devastated."

"Of course he will break his heart. I would be shocked and disappointed if he didn't."

"I wonder if this M. Isidore is a fool?"

"He is, regarding me; but he is wise in other things, as they say. Mrs. Cholmondeley considers him extremely clever; she believes he will advance through his talents. All I know is that he does little more than sigh in my presence, and I can wind him around my little finger."

Seeking a clearer picture of this love-stricken gentleman, whose position seemed precarious, I requested a physical description. She could provide none, lacking the vocabulary or ability to construct graphic phrases. She seemed not to have truly noticed him; the only assertion she could make was that he was "handsome, but rather a fine man than a pretty boy." My patience often faltered listening to her, save for one thing: every hint and detail she dropped unconsciously proved that M. Isidore's homage was offered with great delicacy and respect. I plainly informed her I believed him far too good for her and implied she was merely a vain coquette. She laughed, shook her curls from her eyes, and danced away as if I had paid her a compliment.
