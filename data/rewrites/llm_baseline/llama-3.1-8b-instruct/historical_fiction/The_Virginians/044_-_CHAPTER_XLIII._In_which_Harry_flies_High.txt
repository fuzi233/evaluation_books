In the chill of winter, 1756-57, London's vibrant social scene offered its finest treasures to Mr. Harry Warrington, a young man from Virginia, residing in the heart of Bond Street. His reputation as a Fortunate Youth was well-deserved, for he possessed a winning combination of youth, wealth, good looks, and an innate charm that drew people to him. As he navigated the complexities of high society, Harry discovered that the fashionable world was indeed kind to those who were young, handsome, and fortunate, often overlooking those who may have been less fortunate in their circumstances. However, Harry's affability and generosity towards others earned him a special place in the hearts of those around him.

As a man of refined taste, Harry enjoyed the opera, although he found it somewhat lacking in depth. While some critics derided the Italian operas as foolish, Popish, and unmanly, the crowds still flocked to them. He also frequented the theaters, where he marveled at the talents of Mr. Garrick and Mrs. Pritchard at one house, and Mrs. Clive at another. The masked balls and ridottos, attended by the cream of society, were another favorite haunt of Harry's, although he preferred the camaraderie of White's Club, where the wit and humor were always sharper.

One day, his kinsman, Lord Castlewood, took Harry to the palace to present him to their monarch, who had returned to town from Kensington. However, the King's demeanor was far from gracious, and his words, spoken in German, left Harry perplexed. The King's comment about Harry playing too much seemed to stem from a dislike of his lordship rather than any concern for Harry himself. Lord Castlewood was taken aback by the cold reception, and Harry, sensing his kinsman's unease, asked for an explanation.

"What did His Majesty say?" Harry inquired.

Lord Castlewood hesitated before responding, "His Majesty merely told me to inform you that you spend too much time gaming."

The encounter left Harry feeling puzzled and a bit disheartened, but he knew that, in the rigid hierarchy of their time, such encounters were a mere trifle in the grand tapestry of life.
