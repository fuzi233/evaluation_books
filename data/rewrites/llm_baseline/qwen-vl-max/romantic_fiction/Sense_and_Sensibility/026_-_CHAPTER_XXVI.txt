CHAPTER XXVI. Elinor sat beside Mrs. Jennings in the carriage, journeying to London as her guest, and could not help marveling at how swiftly their lives had changed. Just days before, she had been skeptical of such an arrangement—so brief their acquaintance, so different their temperaments, so contrary her own inclinations—but now, swept along by Marianne’s fervent hope and her mother’s eager consent, she found herself en route to a city that promised both uncertainty and possibility. Though she still harbored quiet doubts about Willoughby’s sincerity, she could not deny the radiant joy that lit Marianne’s face, the way her eyes sparkled with anticipation, the very breath she took seemed charged with longing. In contrast, Elinor felt only a hollow stillness within her own heart, a loneliness that deepened each time she saw her sister’s delight. If only she too could have such a reason to hope, such a passion to chase—she would gladly bear every worry, every fear, just to feel that kind of fire.

They traveled for three days, and during the journey, Marianne remained mostly silent, lost in thought, speaking only when some beautiful landscape stirred her soul into sudden exclamation—always directed at Elinor, never at Mrs. Jennings. To balance this reserved behavior, Elinor made it her duty to be warm and attentive to their hostess. She chatted, laughed, and listened whenever Mrs. Jennings spoke, showing kindness in every gesture. In return, Mrs. Jennings doted on them both, fussing over their comfort, insisting on their preferences at every inn, though frustrated that they wouldn’t reveal whether they favored salmon or cod, fowl or cutlets.

On the third day, they arrived in London by three o’clock, relieved to step from the carriage and into the warmth of a real fire. Their lodgings were pleasant—a well-appointed room once occupied by Charlotte, whose artistic touch lingered in the silk-embroidered landscape above the mantel, a reminder of years spent in a grand school in town. Dinner was not ready for two hours, so Elinor decided to write home to her mother. As she began, she noticed Marianne doing the same.

“I’m writing to Mama,” Elinor said gently. “Wouldn’t you like to wait a day?”

“No,” Marianne replied quickly, almost sharply, as if wanting to end the conversation. Her tone told Elinor everything: she was not writing to their mother. She was writing to Willoughby.

The realization struck Elinor like a quiet certainty: they must be engaged. The secrecy, the haste, the urgency—it all pointed to a secret bond. Though the thought brought no peace, it stirred something deeper—an ache of longing, yes, but also a flicker of hope for her sister’s happiness. With renewed purpose, Elinor continued her letter.

Marianne finished hers in minutes—short, folded, sealed, and addressed with trembling hands. Elinor caught sight of a large "W" on the envelope. No sooner was it done than Marianne rang for the footman and sent it off by the two-penny post. There was no doubt now.

Her spirits soared, yet beneath them lay a nervous flutter that unsettled Elinor. As evening fell, the tension grew. Marianne barely touched her dinner. Back in the drawing room, she leaned forward, ears straining at every sound from the street. Each carriage passing outside sent her heart racing.

Elinor was grateful that Mrs. Jennings was occupied in her own room, unaware of the storm brewing in her daughter’s mind. Tea was served, and again, Marianne jumped at the sound of a knock—only to find it came from a neighbor’s door. But then, a louder, unmistakable rap echoed through the hall. This one could belong to no other.

Elinor knew instantly it was him. Marianne sprang up, rushing toward the door. Silence followed—then she returned, breathless, eyes wide with emotion. “Oh, Elinor,” she breathed, “it’s Willoughby! It really is!”

She turned, ready to throw her arms around him—only to see Colonel Brandon standing there instead.

The shock was too much. Marianne fled from the room without a word.

Elinor, too, felt a pang of disappointment. Yet she could not help but respect Colonel Brandon. His presence brought no joy to her sister, and she regretted that he might sense her grief. He looked after Marianne with concern, his expression softening with surprise and sorrow. When he turned back to Elinor, he asked quietly, “Is your sister unwell?”

Elinor hesitated, then offered excuses—headache, fatigue, low spirits. He listened intently, then composed himself, shifting the conversation to pleasantries about their journey and their friends. They spoke politely, but neither truly heard the other. Elinor longed to ask if Willoughby were in town, but feared it would wound him. Instead, she ventured, “Have you been here since I last saw you?”

“Yes,” he replied, slightly uneasy. “Almost constantly. I’ve visited Delaford a few times, but never returned to Barton.”

His words stirred memories—the abruptness of his departure, the unease it caused Mrs. Jennings. Elinor winced, realizing her question had carried more weight than she’d intended.

Mrs. Jennings soon joined them, bursting in with her usual cheer. “Colonel! How lovely to see you! I’ve been so busy settling things—Cartwright, the house, the servants—you know how it is!” She chattered on, asking about his life, his business, his travels. He answered with quiet dignity, but gave little away.

Elinor began making tea, and Marianne reappeared, pale but composed. Colonel Brandon became quieter, more distant. Mrs. Jennings pressed him to stay longer, but he excused himself shortly after.

No one else came that evening. The girls agreed unanimously to go to bed early.

The next morning, Marianne woke refreshed, her face bright with expectation. The night’s disappointment seemed forgotten, replaced by the thrill of what might come today.

Breakfast was barely over when Mrs. Palmer’s barouche pulled up at the door. Moments later, she burst into the room, laughing, full of joy and contradictions—delighted to see them, annoyed they hadn’t accepted her invitation first, furious they’d taken her mother’s place, yet unable to forgive them if they hadn’t come at all.

“Mr. Palmer will be so thrilled!” she declared. “I can’t remember what he said when he heard you were coming—but it was hilarious!”

After an hour of lively chatter—mostly inquiries from Mrs. Jennings and giggles from Mrs. Palmer—Mrs. Palmer suggested they go shopping. Both Mrs. Jennings and Elinor agreed readily; they had errands of their own. Marianne hesitated, then relented.

Everywhere they went, Marianne’s gaze darted around, searching. In Bond Street especially, her eyes scanned the crowds, her thoughts far from the silks and trinkets displayed before her. She offered no opinion on purchases, showed no interest in anything, only grew more restless, impatient to return home. Mrs. Palmer, captivated by every new thing, dawdled endlessly, leaving Marianne seething with frustration.

By late morning, they returned. No sooner had they stepped inside than Marianne dashed upstairs. When Elinor followed, she found her sister turning from the table, her face heavy with sorrow.

“Has any letter been left for me?” Marianne asked the footman, who entered with the parcels.

He shook his head. “None, ma'am.”

“Are you certain? No servant? No porter?”

“Quite certain.”

“How odd,” she murmured, voice low and broken, as she walked to the window.

“How odd indeed,” Elinor whispered to herself, watching her sister with growing anxiety. *If she believed Willoughby was in town, why write to him? Why not send a note to Combe Magna? And if he is here… why hasn’t he come? Why hasn’t he written?*

Her heart ached. She thought of her mother’s indulgence, the secrecy, the reckless trust placed in a man so unknown. *This cannot continue.* She resolved, after careful thought, that if this silence persisted, she would speak plainly to her mother—insist on knowing the truth, demand answers. For Marianne’s sake, she would do whatever was necessary.

That evening, Mrs. Palmer dined with them, along with two elderly acquaintances of Mrs. Jennings. After tea, Mrs. Palmer left for her engagements, and Elinor helped set up a whist table. Marianne refused to play—she had never learned—and though she had free time, the evening offered no relief. She tried reading, but soon tossed the book aside. Instead, she paced the room, pausing at the window every few moments, listening, hoping, waiting—for a knock that never came.
