“I ask Thee for a thoughtful love, Through constant watching wise,
To meet the glad with joyful smiles, And to wipe the weeping eyes;
And a heart at leisure from itself To soothe and sympathise.” —ANON.

Margaret listened attentively as her mother spoke of small plans to ease the burdens of the poorest in their parish. She could not help but listen, even though each new idea pierced her heart like a fresh wound. By the time winter’s frost settled in, they would be far from Helstone. Old Simon’s rheumatism was worsening, his sight failing—no one would read to him now, no one would bring him broth or warm flannel. If someone did come, it would be a stranger, and the old man would sit by his window, waiting in vain for the familiar face he once knew so well. Mary Domville’s little crippled boy would crawl to the door every day, peering through the trees, hoping to see her return. These people would never understand why she had left them—and there were many others like them. “Your father has always spent his income here, supporting the parish,” Mrs. Hale said. “I may be stepping beyond what is strictly due, but the winter looks harsh, and our poor must be cared for.”

“Oh, mamma,” Margaret replied eagerly, her voice trembling with emotion, “let us do all we can! We may not be here long.” She didn’t see the practical concerns behind her mother’s words—only the urgency of helping those who might never again know her kindness.

“Do you feel unwell, my darling?” Mrs. Hale asked, alarmed by the pallor in her daughter’s face. “You look tired. It’s this damp, soft air—it’s not good for you.”

“No, mamma,” Margaret answered gently, pressing her lips into a faint smile as she kissed her mother’s cheek. “It’s not that. The air is delicious—so fresh, so pure after the smoke and noise of Harley Street. I’m just tired. It must be nearly bedtime.”

“It is half-past nine,” Mrs. Hale said. “You should go straight to bed. Ask Dixon for some gruel. I’ll come up later to check on you. I fear you’ve caught something—perhaps from the stagnant ponds near the woods.”

“Oh, mamma,” Margaret murmured, still smiling, “I’m perfectly well. Don’t worry. I’m simply worn out.”

She climbed the stairs slowly, accepting the gruel only to calm her mother’s fears. Lying in bed, she felt weak and heavy. When Mrs. Hale came in to say goodnight, Margaret watched her leave, then waited until the click of the locked door echoed in the silence. At once, she sprang from the bed, threw on her dressing gown, and began pacing the room. The creak of a floorboard reminded her to be quiet. She slipped onto the deep window seat, where earlier that morning she had seen the church tower glowing under bright sunlight—a promise of warmth and clarity. Now, only sixteen hours later, the sky was dark and cold, the towers stark against an endless blue void.

She sat there, unable to cry, but gripped by a dull ache that seemed to drain every spark of joy from her soul. Mr. Henry Lennox’s visit—the proposal—felt like a dream, distant and unreal. But the truth was raw and undeniable: her father had allowed doubt to take root, and now stood outside the Church, rejected by the community he once served. All the changes that followed flowed from that single, devastating choice.

Outside, the church towers loomed like silent sentinels. She stared into the vastness of space, feeling a terrible emptiness. Where was God? Was He hidden beyond those infinite depths, unreachable, indifferent? The thought filled her with despair. If the heavens stretched endlessly, how could human cries ever reach Him? What use was prayer if the cosmos swallowed sorrow whole?

Then she heard footsteps—soft, hesitant. Her father stood beside her, unseen until he touched her shoulder.

“Margaret,” he whispered, “I heard you were awake. I couldn’t stay away. Will you pray with me? Just the Lord’s Prayer. It will help both of us.”

They knelt together by the window seat. He looked upward. She bowed her head, ashamed. God was near, listening. Her father might have lost faith—but hadn’t she, just moments before, questioned everything herself? She said nothing. After he left, she crept back to bed like a child afraid of being punished.

That night, dreams tormented her. Mr. Lennox climbed a tree too high to reach—her bonnet hung from a branch. He fell, and she rushed to save him, only to be held back by an invisible hand. He died. Yet in another scene, they were in the drawing-room at Harley Street, talking as if nothing had changed. But she knew—he was dead.

A restless, painful night. No rest for the coming day. She woke suddenly, unrefreshed, the weight of reality heavier than any dream. It wasn’t just grief—it was the horror of her father’s departure, not just from place, but from belief. How far had he wandered? Had doubts led him into darkness? She longed to ask, but feared the answer more than anything.

The next morning, the crisp air lifted her mother’s spirits. At breakfast, Mrs. Hale chattered about village matters, unaware of the silence between her husband and daughter. Before the dishes were cleared, Mr. Hale rose, leaning heavily on the table.

“I won't be home until evening,” he said. “I’m going to Bracy Common. I’ll ask Farmer Dobson for dinner. I’ll return by seven.”

He didn’t look at either of them, but Margaret understood. By seven, the truth would be told. Her father had delayed, but she could not endure the suspense. Better to face it now. As she stood by the window, planning how to begin, her mother hurried upstairs to prepare for school.

“Mother,” Margaret said softly, slipping her arm around her mother’s waist, “let’s walk through the garden—just once.”

They stepped outside through the open window. Mrs. Hale began to speak, but Margaret didn’t hear. Her eye caught a bee entering a deep-throated flower. She waited—when the bee emerged, that would be the signal.

It flew out.

“Mamma!” Margaret burst out. “Papa is leaving Helstone! He’s quitting the Church. We’re moving to Milton-Northern.”

The words were spoken quickly, sharply—three truths laid bare.

“What makes you say that?” Mrs. Hale asked, stunned. “Who’s been telling you such nonsense?”

“Papa himself,” Margaret said, wanting to soften the blow but finding no words. They reached a bench. Mrs. Hale sank down and began to weep.

“I don’t understand,” she said, shaking her head. “Either you’re mistaken—or I’m missing something.”

“No, mother,” Margaret insisted. “He wrote to the bishop. He says his doubts make it impossible for him to remain a priest. He’s resigning. He’s spoken to Mr. Bell—Frederick’s godfather—and it’s decided: we’re going to Milton-Northern.”

Mrs. Hale looked up, her face pale. The truth had struck her.

“I don’t believe it,” she whispered. “He would have told me first.”

Margaret felt a pang of guilt. Her father had kept this from her mother—not out of cruelty, but out of tenderness, perhaps even cowardice. Still, it was wrong. She sat beside her, cradling her mother’s head in her arms, her own cheeks brushing gently against hers.

“Dear, dear mamma,” she murmured, “we were afraid of hurting you. Papa suffers deeply. You’re not strong, and the wait must have been agony.”

“When did he tell you?” Mrs. Hale asked.

“Yesterday,” Margaret replied, sensing the jealousy beneath the question. “Poor papa.” She tried to shift her mother’s thoughts toward pity.

“What does he mean by ‘doubts’?” Mrs. Hale asked. “Does he think differently? Does he believe better than the Church?”

Margaret shook her head. Tears welled up. That question touched the deepest part of her pain.

“Can’t the bishop help him?” Mrs. Hale asked, almost impatiently.

“I’m afraid not,” Margaret said quietly. “I didn’t ask. I couldn’t bear to hear what he might say. It’s already decided. He’s leaving in two weeks. I’m not sure if he’s sent in his resignation.”

“In two weeks!” Mrs. Hale gasped. “This is outrageous. Unfeeling. He leaves without consulting me. If he’d told me sooner, I could have helped him.”

Margaret winced. She couldn’t defend her father’s silence—but she couldn’t let her mother condemn him so harshly.

“I almost hoped you’d be glad to leave Helstone,” she said gently. “You’ve never been well here.”

“The smoky air of a factory town?” Mrs. Hale scoffed. “All chimneys and dirt? How could that be better than this pure, sweet air? Living among mill workers? Though, of course, if your father leaves the Church, we’ll be cut off from society everywhere. It’s a disgrace! Poor Sir John—he’d be horrified. Every day after dinner, when I was young at Beresford Court, he’d toast—‘Church and King, and down with the Rump!’”

Margaret was relieved her mother had shifted focus—from her father’s silence to the social fall-out. But this, too, hurt her deeply. It was not just faith that was breaking—it was identity.

“We have little society here, mamma,” she said. “The Gormans are our nearest neighbors—we hardly see them. And they’ve been in trade just as long as these Milton people.”

“Yes,” Mrs. Hale snapped, “but the Gormans built carriages for gentlemen. They had connections. These factory folk—what kind of people wear cotton who can afford linen?”

“Well, mamma,” Margaret said, “I’m not defending the cotton-spinners. But we’ll likely have little to do with them.”

“Why on earth did your father choose Milton-Northern?”

“Partly because it’s so different from Helstone,” Margaret sighed. “And partly because Mr. Bell thinks there’s a chance for a private tutor.”

“A tutor in Milton?” Mrs. Hale scoffed. “Why not Oxford? Why not teach gentlemen?”

“You forget, mamma,” Margaret said softly. “He’s leaving the Church because of his beliefs. His doubts wouldn’t serve him well at Oxford.”

Mrs. Hale fell silent, tears tracing slow paths down her cheeks. Then she whispered:

“And the furniture? How shall we move everything? I’ve never moved before. Only two weeks!”

Margaret felt a surge of relief. Her mother’s distress had narrowed to something manageable. She could help. She made plans, offered promises, guided her mother through decisions, filling in gaps while awaiting further news from her father.

All day, Margaret stayed close to her mother, absorbing every shift in her mood. By evening, she grew anxious for her father’s return—afraid he’d find no comfort waiting for him after a long, weary day. She thought of the secret suffering he must have endured. Her mother, however, only repeated, coldly, that he should have told her earlier—that she could have advised him.

When she heard his step in the hall, Margaret froze. She dared not approach. She saw her mother’s lips twitch, her color change—she, too, knew he was home. The door opened. He stood uncertain, pale and weary, his eyes shadowed with fear. A look of utter helplessness on a man’s face—something almost unbearable.

His wife rushed to him, throwing herself into his arms.

“Oh, Richard, Richard—you should have told me sooner!”

Margaret fled, rushing upstairs to collapse on her bed, burying her face in the pillows to choke back sobs that finally broke free after a day of rigid control.

How long she lay there, she didn’t know. The housemaid entered silently, arranged the room, then slipped out, whispering to Dixon that Miss Hale was crying as if her heart would break. She’d surely make herself ill, the girl warned.

At the sound, Margaret jerked upright, startled. She saw the familiar room, the silhouette of Dixon holding a candle behind her, cautious not to startle her.

“Oh, Dixon! I didn’t hear you come in!” she said, regaining composure. “Is it very late?”

“I can’t tell,” Dixon replied, voice sharp with grievance. “Since your mamma told me the news, I’ve lost track of time. I don’t know what will become of us. When Charlotte said you were sobbing, I thought, *No wonder*. And master—turning Dissenter at his age? If he hasn’t done well in the Church, he hasn’t done badly. I had a cousin who became a Methodist preacher at fifty, after being a tailor all his life. But he’d never made a pair of trousers fit—so no surprise. But master? I said to missus, ‘What would poor Sir John have said? He never liked your marrying Mr. Hale, but if he’d known it would come to this, he’d have sworn worse than ever!’”

Dixon had long commented freely on Mr. Hale’s actions to her mistress. She never noticed Margaret’s sudden tension—the flash of her eyes, the flare of her nostrils.

“Dixon,” Margaret said, voice low and steady, like thunder gathering in the distance. “You forget whom you speak to.”

She stood tall now, facing the maid with unwavering gaze. “I am Mr. Hale’s daughter. Go. You’ve made a grave mistake—one I know your own sense will regret.”

Dixon hesitated, torn between anger and shame. She had never seen such authority in Margaret before. “Miss Margaret has a touch of the old gentleman,” she muttered to herself. “And poor Master Frederick. I wonder where they get it?”

She lowered her voice, half apologetic, half offended: “May I unfasten your gown, miss? Do your hair?”

“No,” Margaret said firmly. “Not tonight. Thank you.” She lit the way for Dixon to leave, then bolted the door behind her.

From that moment, Dixon obeyed and admired her. She said it was because Margaret reminded her of Frederick. But the truth was simpler: she liked being ruled by strength.

Margaret needed that strength now. She had no time for words—only action. In the days that followed, Dixon retreated into silence, showing her disapproval by saying nothing. So Margaret worked harder, directing the packing, organizing the removal, making decisions no one else could. A fortnight was too short to plan such a move. As Dixon muttered, “Anyone but a gentleman—indeed, almost any other gentleman—” she caught Margaret’s stern glance and coughed the rest away, meekly accepting the horehound drop offered to soothe her chest.

Mrs. Hale, overwhelmed by the sudden flood of decisions, fell ill. Margaret felt a strange relief when her mother took to her bed—now she could lead.

Mr. Hale returned each evening more broken than before, visiting each parishioner in farewell. Margaret, inexperienced in practical matters, didn’t know where to turn. The cook and Charlotte worked tirelessly. Margaret’s clear mind guided them, but the bigger question remained: where would they go?

In a week, they must leave. Straight to Milton? Or somewhere else?

One evening, Margaret gathered her courage and asked her father.

“Papa,” she said, “this uncertainty is killing you both. We need to know what to plan for. Have you found a house in Milton?”

“No,” he replied, weary. “We’ll need lodgings first. Look for a house later.”

“And pack the furniture so it can wait at the station?”

“I suppose so. Do what you think best. Remember—we’ll have less money.”

Margaret knew they had never had much to spare. This burden was crushing. Four months ago, her biggest decisions were which dress to wear or who to invite to dinner. Life had been smooth, predictable. Only Captain Lennox’s offer had shaken her world. Now, every day brought a new crisis—each decision vital, each choice shaping their future.

But she would face it. Not with certainty, but with resolve. For her father. For her mother. For the life they were leaving behind—and the one they would build ahead.
