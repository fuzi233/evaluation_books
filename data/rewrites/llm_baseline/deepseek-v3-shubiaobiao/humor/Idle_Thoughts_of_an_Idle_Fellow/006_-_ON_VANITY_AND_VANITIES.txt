Let’s face it: the world runs on vanity. Women are vain. Men are worse. Children? Don’t get me started. At this very moment, a tiny tyrant is pounding my shins, demanding admiration for her new shoes—shoes that look like they were cobbled together by a blind cobbler and strapped to the wrong feet. But do I say this? Of course not. I shower her with praise so thick it would make a used-car salesman blush. One ill-advised attempt at honesty—"No, I am *not* pleased with you"—ended in tears, accusations, and a swift betrayal to her mother. Now, I’ve learned my lesson: flattery is the currency of survival.

Even animals aren’t immune. I once saw a Newfoundland dog preening before a shop window, admiring his reflection with the smug satisfaction of a politician at a ribbon-cutting. Then there was the cow at a country fair, prancing about like a debutante in her floral crown, only to sulk when they took it off. Cats? They’re the worst. Insult one, and she’ll stalk off in a huff. Compliment her, and she’ll purr like a malfunctioning engine. Dogs are simpler souls—pat their heads, call them names, roll them over, and they’ll adore you forever. But a cat? She’ll demand a formal courtship: *"Poor pussy… did 'ums?"* (whatever that means), followed by strategic chin scratches. Cross her, and she’ll remind you of your place with a well-placed claw.

Vanity, my friends, is the glue of society. Without it, we’d all be insufferable saints—respectable, perhaps, but dreadfully dull. It’s our flaws that make us human, our shared vanity that unites us. From the toddler boasting of her "doodness" to the general weighed down by medals, from the dandy obsessing over his cravat to the poet basking in his own sonnets—we’re all chasing the same illusion. Flattery is the universal lubricant. Tell a man he’s wise, a woman she’s divine, a cat she’s regal, and you’ll have them eating from your hand. Even those who claim to despise flattery—"Oh, I *hate* sycophants!"—will melt if you praise their *humility*.

And love? Love is flattery in its purest form. Try wooing a woman with brutal honesty: *"Your nose is… acceptable. Your hands are… functional."* See how far that gets you. No, the path to romance is paved with hyperbole. Call her an angel, a goddess, a creature too radiant for mortal eyes, and she’ll sigh and declare you *"such a dear."* (Whether she believes you is irrelevant. The game is the thing.)

But let’s not dismiss vanity as mere folly. It’s the engine of ambition! Why else would men toil for fortunes they’ll never spend, or artists slave over masterpieces? Not for bread, but for bragging rights. Vanity built cathedrals, launched ships, and—occasionally—inspired decent behavior. After all, what is honor but vanity in a starched collar?

So let us embrace our vanity—not for fripperies and follies, but for nobler things. Let us pride ourselves on kindness, on courage, on refusing to be petty or cruel. Let us be vain of our good hearts and clean hands. And if, in moments of weakness, we still sneak a glance in the mirror or bask in idle praise? Well, at least we’re in excellent company.

The world may be vain, but without it, we’d all be insufferably perfect—and where’s the fun in that?
