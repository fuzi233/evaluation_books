By a Street Lamp.

The evening descended with a brooding heaviness, the sky bruised purple and threatening rain. Romola emerged from the Hospital of San Matteo alongside Maso, the lantern swinging between them like a fragile beacon against the gathering gloom. Just as they reached the threshold of the Via de' Bardi, her husband, Tito, stepped forth from the monastery of San Marco. Tito had departed earlier that afternoon, barely crossing paths with Romola since his return, and now he immediately dismissed Maso, irritated by the servant's short, shuffling gait. Such dismissal was routine; Tito reserved his official attentions strictly for moments when duty demanded it. Between Tito and Romola, there existed no friction, no heated debate. They were hopelessly estranged in their souls, rendering any genuine contest for mutual understanding impossible. Instead, they navigated their shared existence with the rigid precision of diplomats adhering to a treaty. When Tito desired a supper prepared in the old library, now converted into a banquet hall, Romola acquiesced silently, ensuring every detail was flawless. In return, Tito granted her absolute autonomy over her daily rhythms, accepting her assistance with transcription and digesting texts, while simultaneously fulfilling her charitable needs with supplies. Yet, even in this arrangement, intimacy remained a closed book. As he had that morning, Tito avoided eye contact, feigning ignorance of her presence within the house, and playfully attributing to her a permanent preference for solitude over his company.

In the fervent early stages of her internal conquest, after abandoning her resolve to flee, Romola had made tentative attempts to restore an open relationship. However, she realized that reconciliation required a frank confrontation regarding their divergent paths and a moral realignment—a demand Tito could not meet. For Tito, salvation from alienation depended solely on her resuming an effusive tenderness that would necessitate forgetting their fundamental differences. He desired no explanation; he deemed thorough discourse impossible. He wished for Romola to love him again, yet for her, love was an impossibility. She could offer submission and gentleness, suppressing any trace of repulsion, but tenderness could not be fabricated. Helpless before this realization, she acknowledged the inevitable outcome: her husband was irrevocably alienated from her.

This distance provided further justification for her exclusion from his secrets, particularly those concerning his political maneuvers. Tito sought to reassure her that he viewed the Medici cause as lost, arguing that he served the popular government with sincerity, both practically and theoretically—a stance in which she now harbored a passionate interest. Nevertheless, subtle impressions, akin to shifting scents, gnawed at her unease regarding his dealings with San Marco. She was torn between the terror of witnessing proof that would ignite her suspicions and the compulsion to observe closely, fearing that unseen harm might befall someone she could potentially protect.

Walking together under the encroaching twilight, Tito announced, "The day's business is not yet concluded. I will escort you to our door, my Romola, before attending to another commission requiring an hour or so. I require rest urgently." He then shifted to amusing anecdotes of his recent visit to Pisa, guiding her toward a quiet loggia where a single lamp burned before an image of the Virgin. Suddenly, the street erupted with the sound of numerous approaching footsteps and confused voices.

"We shall not reach home dry unless we take shelter," Tito declared, hastening Romola up the steps of the loggia with a start. "Unless we take shelter under this convenient loggia."

"Is it truly necessary to wait for this mere drizzle?" Romola asked, surprised.

"No," Tito replied, his mind sharp with the instinctive caution that defined him. "The rain is intensifying. Wait a moment." Through the flickering lamplight, he had spotted the leader of the approaching group wearing a red feather and a glittering sword-hilt. In all likelihood, this was the last person Tito wished to encounter beside Romola at this hour. Earlier that day, he had met Dolfo Spini; the current mission involved a second, critical interview with the Compagnaccio captain, a sequel to his visit at San Marco. Under a prearranged scheme, Tito had delivered forged letters to Savonarola—one bearing the authentic signature and seal of the Cardinal of Naples, whose influence in Rome favored the friar. These documents claimed the Cardinal was traveling from Pisa, reluctant to enter Florence due to grave concerns, yet eager to consult Savonarola at this precarious juncture. He intended to halt at San Casciano, ten miles distant, and ride out the following morning disguised as a simple priest, meeting Savonarola five miles along the road to Florence, two hours after sunrise. The plot relied on Dolfo Spini and his band of Compagnacci ambushing the friar at a secluded spot near the gates, seizing him along with his Dominican companion, and handing him over to a detachment of Milanese cavalry stationed near San Casciano, destined to carry him into Roman territory.

There was a significant probability that the astute friar would detect the trap and refuse the perilous journey, a risk he had recently avoided. Even during preaching, he was typically guarded by armed men; now, he was invited to traverse a solitary road accompanied only by a fellow monk. Time for deliberation was minimal. The strategy banked on the Friar's zeal for uniting forces within and without the Church to convene a General Council, combined with his expectation of urgent aid from the Cardinal in his struggle against the Pope, overpowering his natural shrewdness and caution within the narrow window of decision-making.

Tito had previously interviewed Savonarola, declining to entrust the letters to anyone else. With consummate skill, he had hinted at their content, leading the Friar to infer a rendezvous outside the gates. Consequently, Tito urged Savonarola to request an armed guard from the Signoria, offering to convey the request discreetly. Savonarola had responded curtly that an armed guard contradicted the necessity of privacy. Speaking with a flashing eye, he signaled his intent to accept the risk.

Tito himself cared little for the outcome. His acumen ensured that whatever eventuality arose would ultimately benefit him. Whether Savonarola fell or rose, Tito secured favor and wealth. This was a crude truth, though Tito dressed it in diplomatic language: his keen intellect, recognizing the hollow foundations of all factions, chose the only logical path—making them subservient to his own ends.

If Savonarola succumbed to the trap, diamonds and papal patronage awaited; if not, Tito's agency would strengthen his standing with both the Friar and Spini, rendering him invaluable to the Medicean faction through the confidences gained.

However, Spini was an unpredictable variable. Cunning enough to enjoy plotting, he lacked the self-control required for such intricate schemes involving secrecy. Prone to drunken excursions—even sober Florence produced its *beoni*, or boisterous drinkers, both lay and clerical—Spini often became loud in taverns and private banquets. Despite agreements with Tito to maintain a cool demeanor in public, the risk remained that an evening encounter might trigger sudden, affectionate bluster. The subtle gesture of casting the *becchetto* over the left shoulder was understood in the morning, but a firm warning might fail to deter a fraternal grasp of the shoulder in the evening.

Tito's primary hope was that Dolfo Spini had not yet spotted him. This hope would have been justified had Spini possessed no clearer vision of Tito than Tito had of Spini. Yet, hidden in the shadows, Tito had seen Spini illuminated momentarily by the direct rays of the lamp. In his own right, Tito was a striking figure, comparable to the captain of the Compagnacci. Romola's black-clad form had escaped notice, and she now stood behind her husband's shoulder in the corner of the loggia. Tito's hopes were not left to linger.

"Hah! My carrier-pigeon!" Spini's harsh voice grated, intended as an aside, as his hand clamped onto Tito's shoulder. "Why hide? You didn't realize it was comrades arriving. Lucky I caught you; it saves time. What of the hunt tomorrow? Will the bald-headed game rise? Are the falcons ready?"

Had Tito possessed a temper prone to rage, it would have flared against this bull-faced accomplice, unfit to lead or follow. His lips whitened, but his agitation stemmed from the urgent need to select a safe response. Attempting to silence Spini would only deepen Romola's suspicion; she was too perceptive to be hoodwinked if alarm struck. Conversely, repelling Spini angrily might provoke the wine-soaked Compagnaccio into savagery, as he was quicker to resent than to divine motives. Tito adopted a third course, demonstrating that Romola retained a specific power over him—the power of dread.

He pressed her hand, signaling a hint to her, and spoke in a genial tone of camaraderie: "Yes, my Dolfo, you may prepare with confidence. But take no trumpets with you."

"Don't worry," Spini retorted, slightly offended. "No need to play the learned scholar with me. I know where the devil hides his tail as well as you. Did he swallow the bait whole? Didn't the prophetic nose smell the hook?" He lowered his voice, attempting a clumsy display of secrecy.

"The brute won't be satisfied until he empties the sack," Tito thought. Aloud, however, he said, "Swallowed it as easily as you drink a cup of Trebbiano. Hah! I see torches. A corpse must be coming. The plague is spreading, I hear."
