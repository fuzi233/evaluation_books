A profound transformation seized Margaret Brandt. Her movements became mechanical, performed as if she were drifting through a dream. Should Peter utter a word with any suddenness, she would start violently, her gaze locking onto him with eyes wide with terror. Her visits to her beloved friend, Margaret Van Eyck, dwindled until they ceased entirely. When forced to endure the old lady's presence, Margaret offered no warmth in return; instead, she stood passive and trembling, occasionally shrinking back as though burned by the affection she could not reciprocate. Yet, the strangest alteration of all was her refusal to venture outdoors during the light of day. She would only emerge after dusk, wandering toward Tergou, and would not return until the dawn broke. She avoided even the sanctuary of morning prayers.

Eventually, Peter, though usually oblivious to such shifts, grew suspicious. "Why do you avoid the world?" he asked. "It seems to me the people stare at you."

One afternoon, Margaret Van Eyck pressed her gently. "Child, what troubles you? Why hide your sorrow from me?"

Margaret cast a frightened glance upward, and tears flooded her cheeks. "I have no sorrows, madam, save those I have wrought myself. I am treated with kindness I do not deserve, especially within this household."

"Then why not visit me more often, my dear?"

"I come more often than I ought," she sighed heavily. "Reicht is crying for you," the old lady interrupted, pointing toward the nursery. "Go, child—what on earth is wrong?"

Speculating on the cause, Margaret Van Eyck concluded that Margaret must be humiliated by the disdain shown to her by Gerard's family. Resolved to intervene, she decided to reprimand the women of the house. The following day, donning her hood and cloak, accompanied by the weeping Reicht, she marched to the hosier's residence.

Catherine received her with great deference, thanking her profusely and shedding tears for her past kindness toward Gerard. Encouraged by this reception, Margaret Van Eyck turned her attention to Margaret Brandt. Instantly, Catherine's demeanor hardened; her eyes lost their moisture, her lips thinned, and her expression settled into the rigid, unyielding mask typical of obstinate, ignorant women. To try to soften such a visage was futile, akin to attempting to melt a brick wall. Margaret Van Eyck persisted, but in vain. Unable to tolerate being thwarted, the old lady grew provoked. With an abrupt, truncated curtsey, she turned and departed, leaving behind a Catherine who returned the gesture with an air of defiant insolence.

Stepping outside, Margaret Van Eyck encountered Reicht speaking quietly with a pale girl leaning on crutches. Pushing past them with flushed cheeks and a scornful toss of her head directed at the entire family, she was suddenly halted. A small, delicate hand slipped timidly into hers. Looking around, she met two eyes like doves', filled with tears that pleaded and thanked her simultaneously. Reading the complex depth of that gaze, Margaret's anger dissolved instantly. She stopped, kneeling to kiss Kate's forehead.

"I understand," she said softly. "Leave it to me."

Returning home, she recounted her journey. "I visited a house today where I witnessed both the commonplace and the miraculous. I saw a foolish, stubborn woman, and beside her, an angel incarnate—a face so beautiful that if I held my paints here, I would wish to repaint the world just to capture its likeness."

Little Kate lived up to this high praise. Seizing a quiet moment later, she shared what she had learned from Reicht Heynes: that Margaret had given her very blood for Gerard in the woods.

"Look, Mother, how she loves him!" Kate exclaimed.

"Who would not love him?"

"Oh, Mother, consider it! Poor creature."

"Aye, wench. She carries her own burdens, just as we do. I cannot bear the sight of blood, let alone my own."

Though this concession was gained, Kate's attempts to pursue the subject were abruptly cut short. Approximately a month later, a soldier of the Dalgetty clan, returning from service in Burgundy, delivered a letter to the hosier's house. Though he was away on business, the family gathered for supper. The soldier placed the envelope upon the table beside Catherine, declining any reward before departing for Sevenbergen.

Curiously, though none possessed the skill to decipher the script, they all knew immediately whose handwriting it was.

"And your father must be absent," cried Catherine. "Are you not ashamed? Not one among you can read your brother's letter."

While the words remained hieroglyphics to them, the message transcended language. There exists an art that speaks without words, unbound by the limitations of ink, capable of slipping directly from the eye into the heart and mind of both scholar and peasant. It crosses seas and frontiers losing nothing, needing no translator, for it writes a universal tongue. Upon seeing the drawing Gerard had sketched between the two brief paragraphs—a pair of hands clasped tightly—they understood instantly. Gerard was bidding them farewell.

As they gazed upon the simple sketch, recognizing his manner in every curve and line, Gerard seemed present, saying goodbye. The women wept until the image blurred before them. Giles whispered, "Poor Gerard!" in a tone foreign to his usual boisterous self. Even Cornelis and Sybrandt fell silent, overcome by a fleeting remorse and gloom.

But how could they obtain the written words without exposing their ignorance and vulnerability to a stranger?

"The Dame Van Eyck?" Kate suggested timidly.

"Yes, I shall go to her, Kate. She possesses a good heart. She loved Gerard dearly; she will be glad to hear of him. I was harsh with her when she visited, but I shall make my submission, and then she will tell me what my poor child wishes to convey."

She arrived swiftly at Margaret Van Eyck's home. Reicht ushered her into a room, announcing, "Wait a moment; she is praying."

Inside, a young woman sat pensively by the stove. Rising gracefully, she made way for the visitor.

"Thank you, young lady; these winter nights are biting, and your fire is a blessing," Catherine remarked, warming her hands while casting furtive glances from head to toe. The girl wore a plain wimple, yet her gown was trimmed with fur—a mark of superior rank in those times. What struck Catherine most, however, was the candor and modesty radiating from the young woman's face. Feeling certain of sympathy from such a noble countenance, she launched into conversation.

"Now, what brings you here, young lady? It is a letter! A letter from my poor boy, far away in some savage land. I blush to admit that none of us can read it. Do you perhaps know how?"

"Yes."

"You can? That is much to your credit, my dear. I fear she will not be long, but every minute feels like an hour to a longing mother."

"I will read it to you."

"Bless you, my dear! Bless you!"

In her genuine eagerness, Catherine failed to notice the suppressed urgency with which the hand extended to retrieve the letter, nor the tremor that gripped the fingers as they closed around it.

"Begin, then, please. I am wearying for it."

"The first words are, 'To my honoured parents.'"

"Ay! He always honored us, poor soul."

"'God and the saints have you in His holy keeping, and bless you by night and by day. Your one harsh deed is forgotten; your years of love remembered.'"

Catherine laid her hand over her bosom and sank back in her chair, letting out a long, shuddering sob.

"Then comes this, madam. It speaks for itself: 'a long farewell.'"

"Ay, go on. You give me sad comfort, but still, it is comfort."

"'To my brothers Cornelis and Sybrandt—Be content; you will see me no more!'"

"What does that mean? Ah!"

"'To my sister Kate. Little angel of my father's house. Be kind to her—for poor Gerard's sake: since grief to her is death to me.'"

Nature, rebelling against the girl's desperate struggle to maintain composure, finally broke. She collapsed from her chair, lying insensible with the letter clutched in her hand and her head resting on Catherine's lap.
