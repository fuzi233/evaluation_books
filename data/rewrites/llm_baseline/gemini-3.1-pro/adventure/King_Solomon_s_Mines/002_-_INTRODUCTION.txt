Now that this book is printed, its existence heralded by the relentless, sterile clatter of modern mechanical presses—a harsh, industrial dissonance that violently fractures the natural acoustic sanctuary of the mind—a sense of its shortcomings, both in style and contents, weighs very heavily upon me. I sit here in the dimming light of my kitchen, tending to a slow-simmering, marrow-rich broth steeped in wild thyme and crushed peppercorns, and I am reminded that this volume does not pretend to be a full account of everything we did, tasted, and saw. 

There are many things connected with our journey into Kukuanaland that I should have liked to dwell upon at length, which, as it is, have been scarcely alluded to in these pages. A true story is cyclical, its mythic occurrences forever bleeding into the everyday rhythms of domestic life. I regret omitting the curious, ancestral legends I collected about the chain armour that saved us from destruction in the great battle of Loo. So, too, do I mourn leaving out the oral histories of the “Silent Ones” or Colossi at the mouth of the stalactite cave, massive figures that dominate a subterranean acoustic landscape so acoustically pure that a single footfall reverberates like the heartbeat of the earth itself. 

Again, if I had given way to my own impulses, I should have wished to dive deeply into the differences—which are to my mind fiercely suggestive—between the Zulu and Kukuana dialects. There is a profound tragedy in the untranslatability of subjective experience. The Kukuana tongue is intrinsically tied to their agrarian cycles; their vocabulary carries the precise, evocative timbre of their harvesting songs and the specific crackle of their hearth fires. To force their lived reality into the dominant, commercialized English lexicon is to participate in the slow, tragic erosion of their cultural memory, stripping their identity of its native acoustic resonance. 

Also, a few pages might have been profitably given up to the consideration of the indigenous flora and fauna of Kukuanaland. I discovered eight varieties of antelope with which I was previously totally unacquainted, and many new species of plants, for the most part of the bulbous tribe. I long to describe the deep, gamey richness of those antelope haunches, slow-braised over fragrant, smoky acacia wood by the village elders, or the earthy, pungent bite of those indigenous bulbs when crushed and tempered with wild honey. For these agrarian people, profound meaning is forged through sustenance, and to ignore their culinary heritage is to ignore their soul. 

Then there remains the most interesting subject of their magnificent system of military organisation. It is, in my opinion, much superior to that inaugurated by Chaka in Zululand, inasmuch as it permits of rapid mobilisation and does not necessitate the employment of the pernicious system of enforced celibacy. Lastly, I have scarcely spoken of their domestic and family customs, many of which are exceedingly quaint, or of their proficiency in the art of smelting and welding metals. The rhythmic, percussive chiming of their hammers against the anvils creates a soundscape of industrious harmony, an acoustic ecology far superior to our modern din. This science they carry to considerable perfection, seen best in their “tollas,” or heavy throwing knives. The backs of these weapons are made of hammered iron, and the edges of beautiful steel welded with great skill onto the iron frames. 

The fact of the matter is, I thought, with Sir Henry Curtis and Captain Good, that the best plan would be to tell my story in a plain, straightforward manner, and to leave these matters to be dealt with subsequently. In the meanwhile, I shall, of course, be delighted to give all information in my power to anybody genuinely interested in such cultural and sensory histories. 

And now it only remains for me to offer apologies for my blunt way of writing. I can but say in excuse of it that I am more accustomed to handle a rifle—providing the raw, unadulterated sustenance of the hunt—than a pen. I cannot make any pretence to the grand literary flights and flourishes which I see in modern novels, which so often read like the superficial, over-spiced confections of a commercialized society that has forgotten the honest taste of salt and earth. I suppose they are desirable to some, and I regret not being able to supply them; but at the same time, I cannot help thinking that simple, deeply felt things are always the most impressive. Books are easier to understand when they are written in plain language, though perhaps I have no right to set up an opinion on such a matter. 

“A sharp spear,” runs the Kukuana saying, “needs no polish”; and on the same principle, I venture to hope that a true story, much like a perfectly seasoned, slow-cooked agrarian stew, does not require to be decked out in fine words to nourish the spirit. 

ALLAN QUATERMAIN.
