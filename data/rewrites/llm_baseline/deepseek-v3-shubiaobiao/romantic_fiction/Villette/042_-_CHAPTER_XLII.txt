The future is a mystery no man can unravel. Love does not speak in prophecies, and fear often conjures phantoms where none exist. Those years of separation—how I had trembled at the thought of them! The sorrow they would bring seemed as certain as death itself. I had imagined their weight, their crushing toll, as inevitable as the path of a juggernaut rolling over the earth. And yet—

Strange, is it not? Strange, and yet true, as life so often proves—the dread itself was sharper than the blow. The great chariot passed, its shadow looming, its wheels grinding—but when it had gone, I still breathed. I still lived.

Monsieur Emanuel was gone three years.

And those three years, dear reader, were the happiest of my life.

Do you doubt me? Listen.

I opened my school. I worked—not merely with my hands, but with my heart. I was the steward of what he had left me, and I resolved to prove myself worthy. Pupils came—first the daughters of tradesmen, then, in time, those of a higher station. In the second year, fortune smiled upon me unexpectedly: a letter arrived from England, bearing a sum of one hundred pounds. It was from Mr. Marchmont, the cousin and heir of my late mistress. Recovering from illness, his conscience had stirred—some papers found after her death had mentioned me, and he sought to make amends. I asked no questions. I took the gift, and with it, I expanded.

I leased the house beside mine, for I would not leave the home M. Paul had chosen for me. My day school became a boarding school, and it flourished. But my strength did not come from myself—no, it came from across the sea, from a promise, from a hope, from letters that arrived like clockwork, filled not with empty courtesies but with words that fed the soul. He wrote as he loved—freely, fully, without restraint or artifice. There were no excuses, no deceptions, only truth. And his faith in me was unshaken, even in matters of religion.

"Remain a Protestant," he had said. "My little English Puritan, I love your steadfastness. There is beauty in your creed—let it remain yours."

Rome could not make him a zealot; the Propaganda could not twist his honest heart. He was a man of tenderness, not tyranny—of devotion, not dogma.

And now, the three years have ended. Autumn has come, and with it, the time of his return. My school thrives, my house is prepared. I have gathered his books, tended his favorite flowers—some still in bloom, as if waiting for him. I loved him when he left; I love him more deeply now, for absence has woven his memory into the very fabric of my being.

The equinox has passed. The days shorten; the trees shed their leaves. But—he is coming.

Frost creeps over the ground at night. November sends its mists ahead. The wind carries the season’s lament. But—he is coming.

The skies darken, the clouds twist into strange shapes—arches and radiant bands. Mornings dawn in royal purples, the heavens aflame, wild as battle, bloody as victory. I know the language of the sky; I have read it since childhood.

The wind shifts. The storm rises—howling, relentless. For seven days it rages, leaving wreckage in its wake. Only when the tempest has spent its fury does it still.

But here, I will pause. Let us leave the rest to hope. Let brighter hearts imagine the joy of reunion, the warmth of a future unchained from fear. Let them picture happiness, hard-won and enduring.

As for Madame Beck, she prospered. Père Silas, too. Madame Walravens lived to see ninety.

And so, farewell.
