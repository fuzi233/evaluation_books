Ghysbrecht Van Swieten was a man of subtle design, one who often cast a wide net to catch a fish he intended to spear with precision. Addressing the novice, he feigned a concern for historical preservation. "The town records," he remarked, his tone dripping with feigned reverence, "are penned in a crabbed hand, the ink long since turned to rust by the passage of time." With a flourish, he extended an invitation to Gerard: the honor of transcribing these ancient documents into fair copy.

Gerard, ever practical, immediately inquired of the compensation.

Van Swieten named a sum so meager it barely covered the cost of pens, ink, and parchment.

"But, Burgomaster," Gerard pressed, his voice steady despite the slight chill in the air, "what of my labor? This is a year's work."

"Your labor?" Van Swieten scoffed, leaning back with a dismissive wave. "Do you call the marking of parchment labor? Little sweat goes to that, I trow."

"It is labor, and skilled labor to boot," Gerard countered, his eyes narrowing. "In every craft, the hand that guides the pen is paid more generously than the brute strength that breaks stone, regardless of the sweat shed. Furthermore, there is the matter of my time."

"Your time?" Van Swieten laughed, a sharp, brittle sound. "Why, what is time to a boy of twenty-two?" He fixed his gaze upon Gerard, searching for the tremor of guilt he expected to find. "Let us say, rather, that you have grown idle. That your heart has wandered. Your body remains among these chanting monks, but your spirit is elsewhere—with Peter Brandt and his red-haired paramour."

"I know no Peter Brandt," Gerard denied, the words tumbling out before he could check them.

This denial only hardened Van Swieten's resolve. The suspicion that the exorcist was playing a dangerous game now took root in the burgomaster's mind.

"You lie!" Van Swieten roared, slamming his hand onto the desk. "Did I not find you at her elbow on the road to Rotterdam?"

"Ah!" Gerard gasped, caught off guard.

"Ah! And you were seen at Sevenbergen but the other day."

"Was I?"

"Ah, and at Peter's house."

"At Sevenbergen?"

"Aye, at Sevenbergen."

In the parlance of the modern world, this was a bold gamble—a statement of fact offered merely to provoke a reaction that would reveal the truth. Van Swieten waited, his eyes gleaming with anticipation.

The result was unexpected. Instead of the calm denial he anticipated, Gerard sprang to his feet, seized by a strange, electric agitation.

"Burgomaster," he stammered, his voice trembling with a mixture of fear and defiance, "I have not set foot in Sevenbergen for three years. I know not the names of those you claim to have seen me with, nor where they dwell. But since my time is precious, even if yours is not, I bid you good day." With that, he darted from the room, his eyes sparkling with a feverish intensity.

Van Swieten rose in a fit of towering rage, only to sink back into his chair as the fury drained away, leaving behind a cold, calculating dread.

"He fears me not," he muttered, his voice low. "He knows something, if not everything."

He signaled frantically to his trusted servant, nearly dragging the man to the window.

"Do you see that man?" Van Swieten commanded, pointing a shaking finger toward the retreating figure. "Haste! Follow him! But let him not perceive you. He is young, yet old in cunning. Keep him in your sight all day. Tell me where he goes and what he does."

Night had fallen by the time the servant returned, breathless and weary.

"Well? Well?" Van Swieten cried, leaning forward eagerly.

"Master," the servant panted, "the young man went from you straight to Sevenbergen."

Van Swieten let out a groan that seemed to echo through the silent chamber.

"To the house of Peter the Magician."
