The rain fell on London as a continuous, grey curtain, turning the city into a wet, monochromatic blur. Inside the lodging house, the breakfast table was dominated by the massive, white silhouette of a Britannia ware pot, a monument to domestic utility that separated the couple.

"Of course we must dine with Mrs. Carfry, dearest," Archer said.

His wife looked up, her brow furrowing with a specific, anxious tension. Across the distance of the table, she scrutinized him, measuring the weight of his suggestion against the invisible laws of propriety.

In the vast, rainy desert of autumnal London, there existed only two souls whom the Newland Archers acknowledged. Yet, they had carefully constructed a wall around themselves, adhering to the rigid New York code that it was undignified to force one's presence upon strangers in foreign lands. Mrs. Archer and Janey had mastered this art with ruthless efficiency. During their European tours, they had met the friendly overtures of fellow travelers with an impenetrable reserve, achieving nearly the impossible feat of exchanging not a single word with a "foreigner" save for the staff of hotels and train stations. Even their own countrymen, unless they were Chivers, Dagonets, or Mingotts, were met with a disdain so pronounced that their months abroad dissolved into an unbroken silence.

Precautions, however, are not always sufficient. One night in Botzen, a knock came at their door. A woman from the room across the passage, whose name, dress, and social standing Janey already knew intimately, asked if Mrs. Archer possessed a bottle of liniment. The visitor's sister, Mrs. Carfry, had succumbed to a sudden attack of bronchitis. Mrs. Archer, who traveled with a complete family pharmacy, produced the remedy without hesitation.

Mrs. Carfry was grievously ill. Traveling alone with her sister, Miss Harle, they were profoundly grateful to the Archer ladies, who provided ingenious comforts and whose efficient maid nursed the invalid back to health. When the Archers eventually left Botzen, they harbored no expectation of ever seeing the sisters again. To Mrs. Archer, forcing oneself upon a stranger to whom one had rendered an accidental service was the height of undignified behavior. But Mrs. Carfry and Miss Harle, operating under a different moral compass, viewed this act as a bond of eternal gratitude. They seized every opportunity to intercept the "delightful Americans" on the continent, displaying a supernatural acuity in tracking their movements. By the time Newland became engaged, the tie between the families was indissoluble. Wedding invitations were exchanged, followed by bouquets of pressed Alpine flowers under glass. On the dock, as the Archers sailed for England, Mrs. Archer's final instruction was clear: "You must take May to see Mrs. Carfry."

Newland and his wife had no intention of obeying. Yet Mrs. Carfry, with her characteristic sharpness, had tracked them down and issued an invitation to dine. Now, May Archer sat before her tea and muffins, her brow wrinkled in distress.

"It's all very well for you, Newland; you _know_ them. But I shall feel so shy among a lot of people I've never met. And what shall I wear?"

Newland leaned back in his chair, smiling at her. In the damp English air, she appeared handsomer, more Diana-like than ever. The moisture seemed to deepen the bloom of her cheeks and soften the slight hardness of her virgin features, or perhaps it was simply the inner glow of happiness shining through her like light beneath ice.

"Wear, dearest? I thought a trunkful of things had come from Paris last week."

"Yes, of course. I meant to say that I shan't know _which_ to wear." She pouted. "I've never dined out in London; and I don't want to be ridiculous."

He attempted to enter into her perplexity. "But don't Englishwomen dress just like everybody else in the evening?"

"Newland! How can you ask such funny questions? When they go to the theatre in old ball-dresses and bare heads."

"Well, perhaps they wear new ball-dresses at home; but at any rate Mrs. Carfry and Miss Harle won't. They'll wear caps like my mother's—and shawls; very soft shawls."

"Yes; but how will the other women be dressed?"

"Not as well as you, dear," he rejoined, wondering what had suddenly awakened in her a morbid interest in clothing.

She pushed back her chair with a sigh. "That's dear of you, Newland; but it doesn't help me much."

An inspiration struck him. "Why not wear your wedding-dress? That can't be wrong, can it?"

"Oh, dearest! If I only had it here! But it's gone to Paris to be made over for next winter, and Worth hasn't sent it back."

"Oh, well—" said Archer, rising. "Look here—the fog's lifting. If we made a dash for the National Gallery we might manage to catch a glimpse of the pictures."

This was the conclusion of their three-month wedding tour. May had summarized it in letters to her girl friends as "blissful." They had bypassed the Italian Lakes; Archer could not envision his wife in that particular setting. Her own inclination, after a month with the Paris dressmakers, favored mountaineering in July and swimming in August. They fulfilled this plan punctually, spending July at Interlaken and Grindelwald, and August at a little place called Étretat on the Normandy coast, recommended as quaint and quiet. Once or twice in the mountains, Archer pointed southward and said, "There's Italy." May, her feet resting in a bed of gentians, smiled cheerfully and replied, "It would be lovely to go there next winter, if only you didn't have to be in New York."

In reality, traveling interested her even less than he had anticipated. Once her clothes were ordered, she regarded the journey merely as an enlarged opportunity for walking, riding, swimming, and attempting the fascinating new game of lawn tennis. When they finally returned to London, intending to spend a fortnight while he ordered his own attire, she no longer concealed the eagerness with which she looked forward to sailing.

In London, nothing captivated her except the theatres and the shops. She found the theatres less exciting than the Paris _cafés chantants_, where, beneath the blossoming horse-chestnuts of the Champs Élysées, she had enjoyed the novel experience of looking down from a restaurant terrace onto an audience of "cocottes," while her husband interpreted the songs for her bridal ears.

Archer had reverted to all his old inherited ideas about marriage. It was less trouble to conform with tradition and treat May exactly as all his friends treated their wives than to try to put into practice the theories with which his untrammeled bachelorhood had dallied. There was no use in trying to emancipate a wife who had not the dimmest notion that she was not free; and he had long since discovered that May's only use of the liberty she supposed herself to possess would be to lay it on the altar of her wifely adoration. Her innate dignity would always keep her from making the gift abjectly; and a day might even come, as it once had, when she would find strength to take it altogether back if she thought she were doing it for his own good. But with a conception of marriage so uncomplicated and incurious as hers, such a crisis could be brought about only by something visibly outrageous in his own conduct; and the fineness of her feeling for him made that unthinkable. Whatever happened, he knew, she would always be loyal, gallant, and unresentful; and that pledged him to the practice of the same virtues.

All this tended to draw him back into his old habits of mind. If her simplicity had been the simplicity of pettiness, he would have chafed and rebelled; but since the lines of her character, though so few, were cast in the same fine mold as her face, she became the tutelary divinity of all his old traditions and reverences.

Such qualities were scarcely of the kind to enliven foreign travel, though they made her so easy and pleasant a companion. He saw at once how they would fall into place in their proper setting. He had no fear of being oppressed by them, for his artistic and intellectual life would continue, as it always had, outside the domestic circle. Within it, there would be nothing small and stifling—coming back to his wife would never be like entering a stuffy room after a tramp in the open. And when they had children, the vacant corners in both their lives would be filled.

These thoughts occupied him during their long, slow drive from Mayfair to South Kensington, where Mrs. Carfry and her sister lived. Archer too would have preferred to escape their friends' hospitality. In conformity with family tradition, he had always traveled as a sight-seer and looker-on, affecting a haughty unconsciousness of the presence of his fellow beings. Once only, just after Harvard, he had spent a few gay weeks at Florence with a band of queer Europeanized Americans, dancing all night with titled ladies in palaces and gambling half the day with the rakes and dandies of the fashionable club. But it had all seemed to him, though the greatest fun in the world, as unreal as a carnival.
