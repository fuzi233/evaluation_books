Though Mr. Crisparkle and John Jasper encountered one another daily beneath the vaulted roof of the Cathedral, their interactions remained strictly devoid of any mention regarding Edwin Drood since the day nearly six months prior, when Jasper had silently presented the Minor Canon with the final entries of his diary. It is improbable that they ever met, despite their frequent crossings, without each harboring a private fixation on the other's hidden agenda. As the accuser pursuing Neville Landless and the steadfast defender protecting him, they stood in natural opposition, yet neither dared to probe the depths of the other's intentions. False pretense was not in the Minor Canon's nature; he surely would have seized every opportunity to revive the topic, eager to dissect it. Yet, Jasper's resolve was impenetrable. Immovable, morose, and utterly solitary, he was consumed by a single, rigid purpose, refusing to share his burden with any fellow creature. While his art required a mechanical harmony with those around him, his spirit remained morally isolated from humanity—a state he had previously confessed to his lost nephew before his current inflexibility hardened.

Jasper undoubtedly knew of Rosa's sudden departure and guessed its cause. Whether he believed he had terrorized her into silence or suspected she had confided in someone—perhaps Mr. Crisparkle himself—remained unknown to the Minor Canon. As a man of justice, Crisparkle admitted that loving Rosa was no crime, nor was placing love above vengeance. The dreadful suspicion that haunted Rosa found no foothold in Crisparkle's mind. Neither Helena nor Neville voiced such fears aloud. Even Mr. Grewgious, though radiating an implacable hatred for Jasper, carefully avoided linking his animosity to such dark sources. A reticent and eccentric man, Grewgious made no mention of that specific evening when he warmed his hands by the gatehouse fire, gazing intently at a pile of torn, muddy garments on the floor.

Cloisterham, drowsy and indifferent, oscillated in its collective opinion regarding the fate of the deceased nephew. Was he slain by a treacherous rival, lost in a violent struggle, or had he simply vanished for his own reasons? The town would briefly lift its head to note that the grieving Jasper remained obsessed with discovery and revenge, only to drift back into slumber. Thus stood the matter when the narrative reached this juncture.

As the Cathedral doors sealed shut for the night, the Choir-master departed on a brief leave, heading toward London via the same route Rosa had taken. He arrived on a hot, dusty evening at a peculiar establishment tucked behind Aldersgate Street, near the General Post Office. It was a hybrid venture—part hotel, part boarding-house, part lodging-house—announcing itself in railway guides as a novel experiment. Bashfully, almost apologetically, it informed travelers that it did not adhere to the traditional "pint of sweet blacking" hospitality but offered boot-polishing services alongside beds, breakfast, and overnight porters for a fixed fee. From such premises, many downtrodden Britons concluded that the times were leveling, save for the high roads, which were destined to vanish entirely.

With little appetite, Jasper ate sparingly and ventured forth again, walking eastward through stagnant streets until he reached a particularly wretched courtyard. Ascending a broken staircase, he opened a door into a dark, stifling chamber and asked, "Are you alone here?"

"A lone, deary; worse luck for me, and better for you," replied a rasping voice. "Come in, whoever you be. I cannot see you until I strike a match, yet I seem to know the sound of your speech. Am I acquainted with you, ain't I?"

"Strike your match and try."

"So I will, deary, so I will; but my hand shakes, and I cannot lay it on a match instantly. And I cough so violently that, wherever I place them, they vanish. They jump and start like living creatures as I cough and cough. Are you off on a voyage, deary?"

"No."

"Not seafaring?"

"No."

"Well, there are land customers and water customers. I am a mother to both. Unlike Jack Chinaman across the court, who is a father to neither. He lacks the true secret of mixing, though he charges as much as I do, and more if he can. Here is a match, but where is the candle? If my cough takes hold, I shall burn twenty matches before finding a flame."

She eventually located the candle and lit it, but the success triggered a seizure. Seizing her in the moment of victory, she sat rocking back and forth, gasping intermittently, "Oh, my lungs are terrible! My lungs are worn away to cabbage-nets!" until the fit subsided. Deprived of sight during the convulsion, she strained her eyes as the attack faded. Regaining her ability to speak, she stared in shock, "Why, it's you!"

"Are you so surprised to see me?"

"I thought I would never see you again, deary. I thought you were dead and gone to Heaven."

"Why?"

"I didn't suppose you could keep away, alive, so long from the poor old soul who knows the real recipe for mixing it. And you are in mourning! Why didn't you come for a pipe of comfort? Did they leave you money, perhaps, so you didn't need comfort?"

"No."

"Who died, deary?"

"A relative."

"Died of what, lovey?"

"Probably, Death."

"We are short tonight!" cried the woman with a propitiatory laugh. "Short and snappish we are! But we are out of sorts for lack of a smoke. We've got the 'all-overs,' haven't we, deary? But this is the place to cure them; this is where the 'all-overs' are smoked off."

"You may prepare as soon as you like," the visitor replied.

He removed his shoes, loosened his cravat, and lay across the foot of the squalid bed, his head resting on his left hand.

"Now you begin to look like yourself," the woman approved. "Now I truly recognize my old customer! Have you been trying to mix for yourself this whole time, poppet?"

"I have been taking it now and then in my own way."

"Never take it your own way. It isn't good for trade, and it isn't good for you. Where is my ink-bottle, and where is my thimble, and where is my little spoon? He intends to take it in an artful form now, my deary dear!"

As she began her process, bubbling and blowing at the faint spark trapped in the hollow of her hands, she spoke in tones of snuffling satisfaction without ceasing. When he spoke, he did so without looking at her, his thoughts already drifting elsewhere.

"I've prepared quite a few smokes for you, first and last, haven't I, chuckey?"

"A good many."

"When you first arrived, you were quite new to it, weren't you?"

"Yes, I was easily disposed of then."

"But you rose in the world and soon took your pipe with the best of them, didn't you?"

"Ah; and the worst."

"It is ready for you. What a sweet singer you were when you first came! Used to drop your head and sing yourself off like a bird! It is ready for you now, deary."

He accepted the pipe with great care and placed the mouthpiece to his lips. She seated herself beside him, ready to refill it. After inhaling several whiffs in silence, he questioned dubiously, "Is it as potent as it used to be?"

"What do you speak of, deary?"

"What should I speak of, but what I have in my mouth?"

"It is exactly the same. Always the identical same."

"It doesn't taste so. And it is slower."

"You have become accustomed to it, you see."

"That may be the cause, certainly. Look here." He paused, became dreamy, and seemed to forget he had invited her attention. She bent over him, speaking in his ear.

"I am attending to you. You said just now, 'Look here.' I say now, I am attending to you. We were discussing your becoming accustomed to it."

"I know all that. I was merely thinking. Look here. Suppose you had something in your mind; something you intended to do."

"Yes, deary; something I was going to do?"

"But hadn't quite decided to do."

"Yes, deary."

"Might or might not do, you understand."

"Yes." With the point of a needle, she stirred the contents of the bowl.

"Would you do it in your fancy while lying here smoking?"

She nodded. "Over and over again."

"Just like me! I did it over and over again. I have performed it hundreds of thousands of times in this room."

"It is to be hoped it was pleasant to do, deary."

"It *was* pleasant to do!"

He uttered this with a savage air and a sharp start at her. Unmoved, she retouched and replenished the bowl with her small spatula. Observing her intent focus, he sank back into his previous posture.
