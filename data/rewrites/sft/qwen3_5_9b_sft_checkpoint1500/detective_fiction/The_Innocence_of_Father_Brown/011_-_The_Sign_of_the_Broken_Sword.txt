The forest stood as a thousand grey arms, its million fingers silvered with frost. Above, a sky of dark, slate-green held stars bleak and brilliant like splintered ice. The countryside, thick with wood and thin with life, was stiff with a bitter, brittle chill. Between the trunks, black hollows yawned like bottomless caverns of a Scandinavian hell—a realm of incalculable cold. Even the square stone tower of the nearby church looked northern, pagan, and barbaric, resembling some ancient watchtower rising from Iceland's sea rocks. It was a strange night for exploration, yet perhaps a necessary one.

From the ashen wastes of the forest, a green shoulder of turf rose abruptly, grey in the starlight. Most graves slanted, leading up a path as steep as stairs to the summit. There, in the sole flat and prominent spot, stood the monument that made the place famous. It contrasted sharply with the featureless graves surrounding it, crafted by one of modern Europe's greatest sculptors, yet his fame was eclipsed by the man he depicted. Under the small silver pencil of starlight, the massive metal figure of a soldier appeared recumbent, strong hands sealed in everlasting worship, his great head pillowed upon a gun. The venerable face, bearded in the heavy, old-fashioned style of Colonel Newcome, wore the simplified strokes of a modern military uniform. Beside his right leg lay a sword, its tip broken; beside his left, a Bible. On glowing summer afternoons, wagonettes full of Americans and cultured suburbans visited this sepulchre, yet even they felt the vast, dumb wilderness and the single, dumpy dome of the churchyard as oddly neglected. In this freezing midnight, one might expect solitude. Instead, a wooden gate creaked, and two figures clad in black climbed the path.

Faint as the frigid starlight was, nothing could be traced of them save that one was enormously big and the other, by contrast, startlingly small. They approached the great tomb of the historic warrior and stood staring for minutes. No living soul existed within a wide circle, giving rise to a morbid fancy that they, too, might be of the earth but not of it. After a silence, the small man spoke to the tall one: "Where does a wise man hide a pebble?"

"On the beach," the tall man answered softly.

The small man nodded. "Where does a wise man hide a leaf?"

"In the forest," came the reply.

Another stillness fell, then the tall man resumed, "Do you mean that when a wise man must hide a real diamond, he hides it among sham ones?"

"No, no," laughed the little man. "We will let bygones be bygones." He stamped his cold feet and added, "I'm not thinking of that. Something peculiar strikes me. Strike a match, will you?"

The big man fumbled in his pocket. A scratch and a flare painted gold the entire flat side of the monument, revealing black letters carved into the stone: "Sacred to the Memory of General Sir Arthur St. Clare, Hero and Martyr, who Always Vanquished his Enemies and Always Spared Them, and Was Treacherously Slain by Them At Last. May God in Whom he Trusted both Reward and Revenge him."

The match burned the big man's fingers, blackened, and dropped. He reached for another, but his small companion stopped him. "That's all right, Flambeau, old man; I saw what I wanted—or rather, I didn't see what I didn't want. Now we must walk a mile and a half to the next inn, and I'll try to tell you all about it. For Heaven knows a man needs a fire and ale to dare tell such a story."

They descended the precipitous path, relocked the rusty gate, and set off at a stamping, ringing walk down the frozen forest road. Before a quarter-mile had passed, the smaller man spoke again. "Yes, the wise man hides a pebble on the beach. But what if there is no beach? Do you know anything of the great St. Clare trouble?"

"I know nothing of English generals, Father Brown," answered the large man, laughing, "though a little about English policemen. I only know that you've dragged me a precious long dance to all the shrines of this fellow, whoever he is. One would think he got buried in six different places. I've seen a memorial in Westminster Abbey, a ramping equestrian statue on the Embankment, a medallion in his birthplace, and another where he lived. Now you drag me after dark to his coffin in a village churchyard. I'm beginning to tire of his magnificent personality, especially as I don't know who he was. What are you hunting for in all these crypts and effigies?"

"I am only looking for one word," said Father Brown. "A word that isn't there."

"Well," asked Flambeau, "are you going to tell me anything about it?"

"I must divide it into two parts," remarked the priest. "First, what everybody knows; then, what I know. What everybody knows is short and plain, yet entirely wrong."

"Right you are," said Flambeau cheerfully. "Let's begin at the wrong end. Let's start with what everybody knows, which isn't true."

"If not wholly untrue, it is at least very inadequate," continued Brown. "All the public knows is that Arthur St. Clare was a great and successful English general. After splendid campaigns in India and Africa, he commanded against Brazil when the patriot Olivier issued an ultimatum. On that occasion, St. Clare, with a very small force, attacked Olivier's vastly larger army and was captured after heroic resistance. Following his capture—and to the abhorrence of the civilized world—he was hanged on the nearest tree. He was found swinging there after the Brazilians retired, with his broken sword hung round his neck."

"And that popular story is untrue?" suggested Flambeau.

"No," said his friend quietly. "That story is quite true, so far as it goes."

"Well, I think it goes far enough!" said Flambeau. "But if the popular story is true, what is the mystery?"

Hundreds of grey, ghostly trees had passed before the little priest answered. He bit his finger reflectively. "Why, the mystery is a mystery of psychology. Or, rather, two psychologies. In that Brazilian business, two of the most famous men of modern history acted flat against their characters. Mind you, Olivier and St. Clare were both heroes—the old thing, and no mistake; it was like the fight between Hector and Achilles. Now, what would you say to an affair where Achilles was timid and Hector was treacherous?"

"Go on," said the large man impatiently.

"Sir Arthur St. Clare was a soldier of the old religious type—the type that saved us during the Mutiny. He was always more for duty than for dash; with all his personal courage, he was a prudent commander, particularly indignant at needless waste of soldiers. Yet in this last battle, he attempted something a baby could see was absurd. One need not be a strategist to see it was as wild as wind; just as one need not be a strategist to avoid a motor-bus. Well, that is the first mystery: what became of the English general's head? The second riddle is, what became of the Brazilian general's heart? President Olivier might be called a visionary or a nuisance, but even his enemies admitted he was magnanimous to the point of knight-errantry. Almost every prisoner he captured was freed or loaded with benefits. Men who had wronged him came away touched by his sweetness. Why should he diabolically revenge himself only once in his life, and for the one blow that could not have hurt him? Well, there you have it. One of the wisest men in the world acted like an idiot for no reason. One of the best men in the world acted like a fiend for no reason. That's the long and the short of it."

"No, you don't," said the other with a snort. "I leave it to you; and you jolly well tell me all about it."

"It's not fair to say the public impression is just what I've said without adding that two things have happened since. They threw a new kind of darkness. First, the family physician of the St. Clares quarreled with the family and began publishing violent articles claiming the late general was a religious maniac. The story fizzled out; everyone knew St. Clare had eccentricities of puritan piety. Second, a much more arresting incident occurred. In the unlucky regiment that made the rash attempt at the Black River, there was Captain Keith, engaged to St. Clare's daughter and later married her. Captured by Olivier, he was treated bounteously and promptly released. Twenty years later, as Lieutenant-Colonel Keith, he published an autobiography titled 'A British Officer in Burmah and Brazil.'
