Ghysbrecht Van Swieten was consumed by a feverish insomnia, his mind trapped in a loop of dread that refused to yield to rest. A superstitious terror held him fast; he feared the very elements that now raged outside—the thunder and lightning—and thus, paralyzed by this irrational awe, he had withheld himself from joining the party scouring Peter's dwelling. Yet, as the storm's fury finally subsided into a trembling silence, the urgency seized him. He crept downstairs, saddled his mule with trembling haste, and galloped to the "Three Kings" inn at Sevenbergen.

There, he found his men slumbering in a chaotic tableau of exhaustion: some sprawled across chairs, others atop tables, and a few curled directly upon the floorboards. Ghysbrecht roused them with furious shouts, demanding answers for their failure. Their responses were a disjointed tapestry of unsuccessful searching, clumsily interwoven with boasts of their own zealousness.

"Fool! To let you go without me!" the burgomaster cried, his voice cracking with indignation. "My life depended on him being there all along. Did you look under the girl's bed?"

"No; there was no room for a man there," one replied dismissively.

"How can you claim such certainty if you did not look?" Ghysbrecht snarled, his patience evaporating like mist before the sun. "You should have looked beneath her mattress, inside the folds of the bedding, and probed every panel with your knives until the wood sang. Come, rise now, and I shall teach you the art of searching."

Dierich Brower rose, shaking the dust from his clothes with a defiant posture. "If you find him, call me a horse and no man."

In moments, Peter's house was once more encircled by the group. Ghysbrecht, the fiery old man, dismounted and entrusted his mule to Jorian Ketel. Clad in determination, he stepped forward alongside Dierich Brower and the others, entering the silent, hollow shell of the home. Inside, nothing stirred. No creature moved, not even Peter himself. They ascended the stairs, the air thick with anticipation, when suddenly one of the men broke the tension with a sharp shout, pointing a finger through the open window of Peter's chamber.

The others turned, their eyes widening as they gazed outward. There, at a modest distance, walking calmly across the golden fields accompanied by Margaret and Martin, stood the fugitive they had hunted. Seizing the moment, Ghysbrecht erupted in an exultant yell. He scrambled down the stairs and threw himself onto his waiting mule. With a unified cry, he and his men spurred their animals, launching themselves into a hot, relentless pursuit.
