Edward stayed at the cottage for a full week, though Mrs. Dashwood pleaded earnestly for him to remain longer. He seemed determined to depart the moment his time among them reached its peak, behaving as if he were undergoing some form of self-imposed penance. For the final few days, his spirits, though still fluctuating, had noticeably lifted; he grew increasingly fond of the house and its surroundings, sighing whenever the topic of leaving arose. He insisted his schedule was entirely free, yet he could not decide where he would go next. Despite this, he had to leave. No week had ever flown by so rapidly; he struggled to believe it was truly over. He spoke often of his reluctance, revealing a shift in his feelings that contradicted his rigid actions. He found no joy at Norland, detested the prospect of returning to town, yet he was bound to go to one or the other. He cherished their kindness above all else, and his greatest happiness lay in their company. Still, he was compelled to depart at the end of the week, defying their wishes and his own desires, with no constraints on his time.

Elinor attributed everything strange in Edward's conduct to his mother. It was fortunate for her that Mrs. Ferrars's character was so poorly understood by her son that she served as a convenient excuse for every oddity. Though disappointed and vexed, occasionally displeased by his inconsistent behavior toward her, Elinor remained largely willing to interpret his actions with the candor and generosity she had previously been forced to muster for Willoughby's sake. His lack of spirit, openness, and consistency was usually blamed on his lack of independence and his awareness of Mrs. Ferrars's designs. The brevity of his visit and his steadfast resolve to leave stemmed from the same fettered inclination, the same unavoidable necessity of compromising with his mother. The age-old conflict between duty and will, parent and child, was the root cause. She longed to know when these difficulties would cease, when this opposition would yield, when Mrs. Ferrars would be reformed and her son free to be happy. Forced to turn from such vain hopes for comfort, she clung to her renewed faith in Edward's affection, recalling every mark of regard in his looks and words during his stay at Barton, and above all, the flattering ring he constantly wore on his finger.

"I think, Edward," Mrs. Dashwood said as they broke their fast on the final morning, "you would be a happier man if you had a profession to occupy your time and give substance to your plans. There might be some inconvenience to your friends, indeed; you could not give them so much of your time. But (with a smile) you would be materially benefited in at least one particular—you would know where to go when you leave us."

"I assure you," Edward replied, "that I have long considered this very point, just as you do now. It has been, and remains, and likely will always be, a heavy misfortune for me to have no necessary business to engage me, no profession to provide employment or any semblance of independence. Yet, unfortunately, my own fastidiousness and that of my friends have made me what I am: an idle, helpless creature. We could never agree on a profession. I always preferred the church, and still do. But it was not 'smart' enough for my family. They recommended the army. That was far too 'smart' for me. The law was deemed genteel enough; many young men with chambers in the Temple made a fine appearance in high society and drove about town in knowing carriages. But I had no inclination for the law, even in this less abstract branch that my family approved. As for the navy, it had fashion on its side, but I was too old when the subject was first raised to join it. And finally, since there was no necessity for me to have any profession at all, as I might be just as dashing and expensive without a red coat as with one, idleness was pronounced the most advantageous and honorable course. A young man of eighteen is generally not so eager to be busy as to resist his friends' solicitations to do nothing. I was therefore entered at Oxford and have been properly idle ever since."

"The consequence of which, I suppose, will be," Mrs. Dashwood remarked, "that since leisure has not promoted your own happiness, your sons will be brought up to as many pursuits, employments, professions, and trades as Columella's."

"They will be brought up," he said seriously, "to be as unlike myself as is possible. In feeling, in action, in condition, in everything."

"Come, come; this is merely an effusion of your immediate lack of spirits, Edward. You are in a melancholy mood and fancy that anyone unlike yourself must be happy. But remember that the pain of parting from friends is felt by everyone at times, regardless of their education or station. Know your own happiness. You want nothing but patience—or give it a more captivating name, call it hope. Your mother will secure you, in time, that independence you are so anxious for; it is her duty, and it will, it must soon become her happiness to prevent your entire youth from being wasted in discontent. How much might not a few months accomplish?"

"I think," Edward replied, "that I may defy many months to produce any good for me."

This despondent turn of mind, though he hid it from Mrs. Dashwood, caused additional pain to them all during the parting, which soon took place. It left an uncomfortable impression on Elinor's feelings particularly, one that required effort and time to overcome. Yet, since it was her determination to conquer it and to prevent herself from suffering visibly more than the rest of her family, she did not adopt the method so wisely employed by Marianne on a similar occasion: to augment and fix her sorrow by seeking silence, solitude, and idleness. Their approaches were as different as their goals, yet both suited the advancement of each.

As soon as Edward was out of the house, Elinor sat down at her drawing table. She busied herself throughout the day, neither seeking nor avoiding mention of his name, appearing to interest herself almost as much as ever in the family's general concerns. If this conduct did not lessen her own grief, it at least prevented its unnecessary increase and spared her mother and sisters much solicitude regarding her.

Such behavior, so diametrically opposed to her own, appeared no more meritorious to Marianne than her own had seemed faulty to her. The business of self-command she settled very easily; with strong affections, it was impossible, and with calm ones, it could hold no merit. That her sister's affections *were* calm, she dared not deny, though she blushed to acknowledge it; and of the strength of her own, she gave a striking proof by continuing to love and respect that sister, despite this mortifying conviction.

Without isolating herself from her family or leaving the house in determined solitude to avoid them, or lying awake all night to indulge in meditation, Elinor found that every day afforded her enough leisure to think of Edward and his behavior in every possible variety that her varying moods could produce—with tenderness, pity, approval, censure, and doubt. There were abundant moments when, if not by the absence of her mother and sisters, at least by the nature of their occupations, conversation was forbidden among them, and every effect of solitude was produced. Her mind was inevitably at liberty; her thoughts could not be chained elsewhere; and the past and future, on a subject so interesting, had to be before her, forcing her attention and engrossing her memory, reflection, and fancy.

From a reverie of this kind, as she sat at her drawing table, she was roused one morning, shortly after Edward's departure, by the arrival of company. She happened to be quite alone. The closing of the small gate at the entrance of the green court in front of the house drew her eyes to the window, and she saw a large party walking up to the door. Among them were Sir John and Lady Middleton and Mrs. Jennings, but there were two others, a gentleman and a lady, who were quite unknown to her. Sitting near the window, as soon as Sir John perceived her, he left the rest of the party to the ceremony of knocking at the door. Stepping across the turf, he obliged her to open the casement to speak to him, though the space was so short between the door and the window that it was hardly possible to speak at one without being heard at the other.

"Well," he said, "we have brought you some strangers. How do you like them?"

"Hush! They will hear you."

"Never mind if they do. It is only the Palmers. Charlotte is very pretty, I can tell you. You may see her if you look this way."

Since Elinor was certain of seeing her in a couple of minutes without taking that liberty, she begged to be excused.

"Where is Marianne? Has she run away because we are come? I see her instrument is open."

"She is walking, I believe."

They were now joined by Mrs. Jennings, who had not patience enough to wait until the door was opened before telling *her* story. She came hallooing to the window, "How do you do, my dear? How does Mrs. Dashwood do? And where are your sisters? What! All alone! You will be glad of a little company to sit with. I have brought my other son and daughter to see you. Only think of their coming so suddenly! I thought I heard a carriage last night while we were drinking our tea, but it never entered my head that it could be them. I thought of nothing but whether it might not be Colonel Brandon come back again; so I said to Sir John, 'I do think I hear a carriage; perhaps it is Colonel Brandon come back again—'"

Elinor was obliged to turn from her in the middle of her story to receive the rest of the party. Lady Middleton introduced the two strangers; Mrs. Dashwood and Margaret came downstairs at the same time, and they all sat down to look at one another while Mrs. Jennings continued her story as she walked through the passage into the parlour, attended by Sir John.

Mrs. Palmer was several years younger than Lady Middleton and totally unlike her in every respect. She was short and plump, had a very pretty face, and the finest expression of good humor in it that could possibly be. Her manners were by no means so elegant as her sister's, but they were much more prepossessing. She came in with a smile, smiled all the time of her visit, except when she laughed, and smiled when she went away. Her husband was a grave-looking young man of five or six and twenty, with an air of more fashion and sense than his wife, but of less willingness to please or be pleased. He entered the room with a look of self-consequence, slightly bowed to the ladies without speaking a word, and, after briefly surveying them and their apartments, took up a newspaper from the table and continued to read it as long as he stayed.

Mrs. Palmer, on the contrary, who was strongly endowed by nature with a turn for being uniformly civil and happy, was hardly seated before her admiration of the parlour and everything in it burst forth.

"Well! What a delightful room this is! I never saw anything so charming! Only think, Mama, how it is improved since I was here last! I always thought it such a sweet place, ma'am! (turning to Mrs. Dashwood) But you have made it so charming! Only look, sister, how delightful everything is! How I should like such a house for myself! Should not you, Mr. Palmer?"

Mr. Palmer made her no answer and did not even raise his eyes from the newspaper.

"Mr. Palmer does not hear me," she said, laughing; "he never does sometimes. It is so ridiculous!"

This was quite a new idea to Mrs. Dashwood; she had never been used to find wit in the inattention of anyone and could not help looking with surprise at them both.

Mrs. Jennings, meanwhile, talked on as loud as she could and continued her account of their surprise the evening before on seeing their friends, without ceasing until everything was told. Mrs. Palmer laughed heartily at the recollection of their astonishment, and everyone agreed, two or three times over, that it had been quite an agreeable surprise.

"You may believe how glad we all were to see them," added Mrs. Jennings, leaning forward toward Elinor and speaking in a low voice as if she meant to be heard by no one else, though they were seated on different sides of the room; "but, however, I can't help wishing they had not traveled quite so fast, nor made such a long journey of it, for they came all round by London upon account of some business, for you know (nodding significantly and pointing to her daughter) it was wrong in her situation. I wanted her to stay at home and rest this morning, but she would come with us; she longed so much to see you all!"

Mrs. Palmer laughed and said it would not do her any harm.

"She expects to be confined in February," continued Mrs. Jennings.

Lady Middleton could no longer endure such a conversation and therefore exerted herself to ask Mr. Palmer if there was any news in the paper.

"No, none at all," he replied, and read on.

"Here comes Marianne," cried Sir John. "Now, Palmer, you shall see a monstrous pretty girl."

He immediately went into the passage, opened the front door, and ushered her in himself. Mrs. Jennings asked her, as soon as she appeared, if she had not been to Allenham; and Mrs. Palmer laughed so heartily at the question as to show she understood it. Mr. Palmer looked up on her entering the room, stared at her for some minutes, and then returned to his newspaper. Mrs. Palmer's eye was now caught by the drawings which hung round the room. She got up to examine them.

"Oh! Dear, how beautiful these are! Well! How delightful! Do but look, Mama, how sweet! I declare they are quite charming; I could look at them for ever." And then sitting down again, she very soon forgot that there were any such things in the room.
