"Pack clouds away."

"For joy or grief, for hope or fear,
For all hereafter, as for here,
In peace or strife, in storm or shine."

The next morning, Edith moved through the house on tiptoe, silencing Sholto whenever he raised his voice, as though a sudden noise might shatter the delicate conference underway in the drawing-room. By two o'clock, the doors remained shut, and the silence within seemed to hold its breath. Then came the sharp tread of a man's foot descending the stairs. Edith peeked out from behind the curtains.

"Well, Henry?" she asked, her gaze questioning.

"Well!" he replied, his tone curt.

"Come in to lunch!"

"No, thank you. I've wasted too much time here already."

"Then it isn't settled?" Edith asked, her shoulders slumping in despair.

"Not at all. Never will be, if 'it' refers to what I suspect you mean. That will never happen, Edith. Give up thinking about it."

"But it would be so wonderful for all of us," Edith pleaded. "I would feel secure regarding the children if Margaret were settled nearby. As it stands, I am constantly terrified she will drift off to Cadiz."

"I shall endeavor, when I marry, to find a young lady skilled in the management of children. That is all I can offer. Miss Hale will not have me, and I shall not ask her."

"So, what have you been discussing?"

"A thousand things beyond your comprehension: investments, leases, the valuation of land."

"Oh, leave if that is all. You and she would be unbearably tedious if you spent your time debating such wearisome subjects."

"Very well. I shall return tomorrow with Mr. Thornton to discuss further matters with Miss Hale."

"Mr. Thornton? What possible relevance does he have?"

"He is Miss Hale's tenant," Mr. Lennox said, turning away abruptly. "And he wishes to surrender his lease."

"Oh! Very well. I cannot grasp the details, so please spare me."

"The only detail that matters is that we secure the back drawing-room undisturbed, just as today. With the children and servants constantly coming and going, I can never conduct business satisfactorily; and the arrangements we must finalize tomorrow are of critical importance."

No one ever discovered why Mr. Lennox failed to appear the following day. Mr. Thornton arrived punctually, yet after waiting nearly an hour, Margaret entered looking pale and deeply anxious.

"I am so sorry Mr. Lennox is absent," she began hurriedly. "He could have handled this far better than I can. He is my adviser in this—"

"I am sorry I came if it causes you trouble. Shall I go to Mr. Lennox's chambers to search for him?"

"No, thank you. I wished to tell you how grieved I am to lose you as a tenant. But Mr. Lennox assures me things are bound to brighten—"

"Mr. Lennox knows little of the matter," Mr. Thornton said quietly. "Happy and fortunate in everything a man desires, he fails to comprehend what it is to realize one is no longer young, yet thrust back to the starting point requiring the hopeful energy of youth. To feel half a life gone, nothing accomplished, nothing remaining of wasted opportunity but the bitter memory of what has been. Miss Hale, I would rather not hear Mr. Lennox's opinion of my affairs. Those who are happy and successful themselves are too prone to dismiss the misfortunes of others."

"You are unjust," Margaret said gently. "Mr. Lennox has merely spoken of the high probability he believes exists for you to redeem—not merely redeem, but surpass what you have lost. Do not speak until I have finished, pray, do not." Gathering herself, she rapidly flipped through legal papers and account statements in a trembling haste. "Oh! Here it is! He drafted a proposal showing that if you were to accept eighteen hundred and fifty-seven pounds of mine, currently idle in the bank yielding only two and a half percent, you could pay me significantly better interest and continue operating Marlborough Mills." Her voice had steadied, and she continued searching for the document detailing the security proposals, desperate to view the situation strictly as a business arrangement where the advantage lay with her.

While she searched, her heart stopped at the tone of Mr. Thornton's voice. It was hoarse, trembling with tender passion as he said:

"Margaret!"

For an instant she looked up, then veiled her luminous eyes by pressing her forehead to her hands. Stepping closer, he beseeched her with another tremulous, eager call.

"Margaret!"

Her head bowed lower, her face more concealed, almost resting on the table. He approached, kneeling beside her to bring his face level with her ear, whispering, panting the words:

"Take care. If you do not speak, I shall claim you as my own in some strange, presumptuous way. Send me away at once if I must go—Margaret!"

At this third call, she turned her face, still covered by her small white hands, toward him, laying it upon his shoulder, hiding it there. It was too exquisite to feel her soft cheek against his for him to wish to see either deep blushes or loving eyes. He clasped her close. They remained silent. Finally, she murmured in a broken voice:

"Oh, Mr. Thornton, I am not good enough!"

"Not good enough! Do not mock my own profound sense of unworthiness."

After a moment or two, he gently freed her hands from her face and laid her arms as he had once placed them to protect him from the rioters.

"Do you remember, love?" he murmured. "And how I repaid you with my insolence the next day?"

"I remember how wrongly I spoke to you—that is all."

"Look here! Lift your head. I have something to show you!" She slowly faced him, glowing with beautiful shame.

"Do you know these roses?" he said, producing his pocket-book, wherein were treasured some dried flowers.

"No!" she replied with innocent curiosity. "Did I give them to you?"

"No! Vanity; you did not. You may have worn similar roses, perhaps."

She examined them, wondering for a moment, then smiled slightly.

"They are from Helstone, are they not? I recognize the deep indentations around the leaves. Oh! Have you been there? When were you there?"

"I wanted to see the place where Margaret grew into what she is, even at the worst of times, when I held no hope of ever calling her mine. I went there upon my return from Havre."

"You must give them to me," she said, attempting to take them from his hand with gentle force.

"Very well. Only you must pay me for them!"

"How shall I ever tell Aunt Shaw?" she whispered after a long, delicious silence.

"Let me speak to her."

"Oh, no! I owe her—but what will she say?"

"I can guess. Her first exclamation will be, 'That man!'"

"Hush!" said Margaret, "or I shall try to show you your mother's indignant tones as she says, 'That woman!'"
