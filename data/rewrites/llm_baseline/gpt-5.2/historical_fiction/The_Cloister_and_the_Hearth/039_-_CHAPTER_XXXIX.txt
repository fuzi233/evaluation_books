Rude travel, men say, is a salt relish to English blood, and the telling of it sets many a cold hearth glowing. Yet the true loaf of this history was kneaded in Holland, and it is time to go back to the low rooms, the familiar thresholds, the common pots and pans where most lives are boiled down—slowly, with small daily stirrings that decide great matters.

In Sevenbergen, Jorian Ketel came up to Peter Brandt’s door as if he owned the very lintel, and demanded Margaret’s promise like a debt long overdue. But Margaret lay sick abed; the fever had made her more like a pale candle than a girl, and Peter, hearing Ketel’s errand, flamed up. He affronted him openly—drove him off the premises with hard words, and one or two in the street were ready enough to take the rest of the argument to the horse-pond; for father and daughter were beloved, and the whole matter was in every mouth, and Sevenbergen was in that hot, undiscriminating temper that comes of public pity.

So Jorian Ketel went away in dudgeon, and repented of a good deed he had done once; which kind of repentance is common enough, and is at least honest.

And Dierich Brower—found at “The Three Kings,” with ale at his elbow and a poor chattering fellow opposite, making him drunk, not from generosity but to draw from him Martin Wittenhaagen’s whereabouts—was discovered, seized, and flung into a horsepond. The splash of him went through the little town like a bell-note: men laughed, women cried shame, and the water frothed round his ears while threats fell on him thicker than droplets. He was told, with more emphasis than courtesy, that if ever he showed his face again in that burgh he should taste worse than pond-water.

Municipal jealousy pricked up its ears. The burgomaster of Sevenbergen, resenting Ghysbrecht Van Swieten’s high-handed forays, sent a formal missive to the burgomaster of Tergou, telling him plain he had overstepped the law, and that, next time he had a complaint—real or imaginary—against any of Sevenbergen’s townsfolk, he must apply where he ought.

Ghysbrecht, wily as a pike in still water, swallowed his rage and sent back a civil answer: the person he had followed, he said, was no Sevenbergen man at all, but a Tergovian—one Gerard—who had stolen the town records; and that Gerard, having escaped into foreign parts and likely carried the documents with him, the whole matter was now at an end. Thus he made a virtue of necessity, and a lie into a salve.

But his smoothness was only a lid. Baffled at Sevenbergen, he turned his eyes elsewhere and set emissaries to learn, from the family at Tergou, whither Gerard had fled. To his “infinite surprise,” they did not know. That ignorance—genuine as it was—added a sour drop to his cup. It made him fear Gerard was not far off at all, but lurking in the neighbourhood like a knife in a sleeve; that he would make some sure discovery and come back to take a dreadful revenge.

From that time those about Ghysbrecht noticed a change for the worse. The man who had once strutted in his authority became moody and irritable. A dread lay on him like a low, ceaseless hum in the ear—one of those sounds you cannot point to, yet cannot escape. His eyes began to cast furtive glances, as of one who expects a blow and knows not from what quarter it will fall. Making others wretched had not made him happy. It seldom does.

At Tergou, the little household that, but for violent interference, might in time have healed its own difference without banishing its hope of the flock to far roads, kept the same outward look, but inwardly was altered. Little Kate knew what share Cornelis and Sybrandt had in driving Gerard away; and though she never told her mother—fearing to breed worse mischief—there were moments when the bare sight of those two made her shudder, and she reddened at their hypocritical regrets.

Catherine, with a woman’s vigilance, noticed this. With a woman’s subtlety she said nothing, but held her tongue and watched—watched as one watches a pot that must not boil over, hearing not only the clink of spoons and the scrape of wooden trenchers, but the pauses between voices, the quickened breath, the wrong note in a laugh. The black sheep, trying to wear mourning faces and take their share in the general sorrow, imposed well enough on their father and Giles. Yet the demure satisfaction under their gloom could not escape feminine eyes—those eyes “that, noting all, seem nought to note.”

Thus mistrust and suspicion sat at table—poor substitutes for Gerard’s quick, intelligent face that had brightened the circle, unobserved till it was gone. The old hosier’s pride had been wounded by his son’s disobedience; so he bore up stiffly and did his best never to mention Gerard’s name. Yet under his Spartan cloak Nature tugged at his heart-strings. One anxiety he never pretended to hide.

“If I but knew where the boy is,” he would say, “and that his life and health are in no danger, small would be my care.” And then a deep sigh would follow, like a bell heard far off across flat land.

It seemed to me that if Gerard had opened the door at that instant and walked in, there would have been more tears and embraces than reproaches—few, or none.

One thing took the old couple by surprise: publicity. Ere Gerard had been gone a week, his adventures were in every mouth; and to make it sharper, the town sympathy ran warmly on the lovers’ side, and against Gerard’s “cruel parents,” and against that old busybody of a burgomaster who must put his nose into a matter that nowise concerned him.

“Mother,” said Kate one day, coming in with the sound of street-talk clinging to her like flour dust to a sleeve, “it is all over the town that Margaret is down with a fever—a burning fever; her father fears her sadly.”

“Margaret? what Margaret?” asked Catherine, with a treacherous calmness that did not reach her eyes.

“Oh, mother! whom should I mean? Why, Gerard’s Margaret.”

“Gerard’s Margaret,” screamed Catherine; and the very syllables were as if someone had dashed vinegar into milk. “How dare you say such a word to me? And I rede you never mention that hussy’s name in this house, that she has laid bare. She is the ruin of my poor boy, the flower of all my flock. She is the cause that he is not a holy priest in the midst of us, but is roaming the world, and I a desolate broken-hearted mother.”

Then, seeing Kate’s lip tremble, her voice broke and softened, as fire softens when you lay a lid on it.

“There, do not cry, my girl; I do ill to speak harsh to you. But oh, Kate! you know not what passes in a mother’s heart. I bear up before you all; it behoves me swallow my fears; but at night I see him in my dreams, and still some trouble or other near him: sometimes he is torn by wild beasts; other times he is in the hands of robbers, and their cruel knives uplifted to strike his poor pale face, that one should think would move a stone. Oh! when I remember that, while I sit here in comfort, perhaps my poor boy lies dead in some savage place—and all along of that girl: there, her very name is ratsbane to me. I tremble all over when I hear it.”

“I’ll not say anything, nor do anything to grieve you worse, mother,” said Kate tenderly; but she sighed, and kept her promise with difficulty.

The name forbidden in that house was spoken freely elsewhere—and with pity. All Sevenbergen was sorry for Margaret. Young men and maidens cast many a compassionate glance, as they passed, toward the little window where the village beauty lay “dying for love.” In that familiar phrase they wronged her. Gerard was not dead, and she was too loyal to doubt his constancy. Her father was dear to her and helpless; and but for bodily weakness, all her love for Gerard would not have kept her from her duties—though she might have gone about them with a drooping head.

But the strain of body and mind had brought on a fever so fierce that nothing but youth and constitution saved her. When the malady at last released her, it left her in that terrible weakness where the patient feels life not a gift but a weight. Then love and friendship by the bedside are mortal angels—comfort in the voice, healing in the hand. Yet this poor girl had to climb back into strength as she could, largely alone.

Many days she lay with no company but the soft crackle of the fire below and the changing noises of the street—cart wheels grumbling on the stones, a distant dog’s bark, the thin cry of a hawker—sounds that should have tethered her to the world, yet seemed to come from another life. The hours rolled over her like leaden waves. In her enfeebled state existence itself felt done and over. She could not stir herself to get well. Gerard was gone. She had not him to get well for. Often she lay for hours quite still, with tears welling gently out of her eyes as if her body had learned the habit of sorrow.

One day, waking from an uneasy slumber, she found two women in her room. One was a servant; the other, by the deep fur on her collar and sleeves, was a person of consideration. A narrow band of silvery hair, spared by her coiffure, showed she was past the age when sensible women pretend to be younger than they are. Both faces were kind.

Margaret tried to raise herself, but the older lady laid a hand on her—very gently, as one sets down a warm loaf so it will not break its crust.

“Lie still, sweetheart; we come not here to put you about, but to comfort you, God willing. Now cheer up a bit, and tell us, first, who think you we are?”

“Nay, madam,” whispered Margaret, “I know you, though I never saw you before: you are the demoiselle Van Eyck, and this is Reicht Heynes. Gerard has oft spoken of you, and of your goodness to him. Madam, he has no friend like you near him now.”

At that thought her strength went out of her, and tears sprang again in a moment.

Reicht Heynes, good-natured as a warm kitchen, began to cry too—for company, as some do; but her mistress checked her sharply.

“Well, you are a pretty one for a sick-room,” said she; and then she turned all her art—not the loud gaiety of the young, but the quiet, seasoned skill of one that has seen trouble and knows its smell—toward cheering the patient. She spoke of small things, safe things, the way one coaxes appetite back: the weather that would turn, the neighbours’ doings, the comfort of a clean bed, the need for broth when strength is low, and how a little patience is a sort of prayer. She knew what to say and what to avoid; she knew when silence was the better medicine.

Ere an hour was gone, Margaret’s head lay on her shoulder instead of on the pillow, and her soft eyes dwelt on the older woman with a gratitude as quiet as it was deep.

“Ah! this is hair,” said the dame, running her fingers through it with a housewife’s approving touch, as if weighing flax. “Come and look at it, Reicht!”

Reicht came, handled it, and praised it unaffectedly. The poor girl that owned it was not quite beyond the reach of flattery—owing, doubtless, to not being dead.

“In sooth, madam,” said Margaret faintly, “I did use to think it hideous; but he praised it, and ever since then I have been almost vain of it, saints forgive me. You know how foolish those are that love.”

“They are greater fools that don’t,” said the old lady sharply.

Margaret opened her eyes wide, as if a new sound had rung in her hearing.

This was only the first of many visits. Either Margaret Van Eyck herself, or Reicht, came nearly every day until their patient was convalescent; and she mended rapidly under their hands. Reicht said it was chiefly the nourishing dishes she made in Peter’s kitchen—thin gruels strengthened with egg, broths drawn long from bone and herb till they held the very marrow of comfort, little pans of stewed fruit with spice just enough to wake the tongue, and possets that slid warm and mild down a sore throat. She was a woman who knew that healing has a smell: the clean steam of barley, the sweet breath of simmering milk, the homely sharpness of onion softened to sweetness, the honest perfume of browned crust.

Margaret herself thought more of the kind words and eyes that told her, day by day, she had friends to live for; and of the hushed, merciful quiet the two women brought into the room, as if they laid a soft cloth over harsh noises. Even the street, heard through the shutter, seemed less cruel when one was not alone.

While this homely miracle went on, Martin Wittenhaagen went straight to Rotterdam to take the bull by the horns. The bull, in this case, walked on two legs and wore a crown for horns: Philip the Good—duke of this, earl of that, lord of the other.

Arrived at Rotterdam, Martin found the court was at Ghent. To Ghent he went; and there he sought an audience, but was put off by lackeys and pages, turned aside as if he were a dog nosing at a door. So he took another tack, such as soldiers learn: he threw himself in his sovereign’s way out hunting, and, contrary to court precedents, began the conversation by roaring lustily for mercy.

“Why, where is the peril, man?” cried the duke, looking all round and laughing.

“Grace for an old soldier hunted down by burghers!” bawled Martin.

Now kings differ in character like other folk; but there is one trait they share: they are often wonderfully affable to men of very low estate. Such folk vie with them in nothing; jealousy does not wake. Their bluntness amuses them; their plain speech brings a breath of nature into courts where every word is perfumed and weighed.

So Philip reined in his horse and gave Martin almost a tête-à-tête. Martin reminded him of a certain battlefield where he had taken an arrow meant for his sovereign. The duke remembered the incident perfectly, and could afford to look on it cheerfully—having not been the one struck.

Then Martin told him of Gerard’s first capture in the church, his imprisonment in the tower, the manoeuvre by which they got him out, and all the details of the hunt; and whether Martin told it better than others might, or the duke had heard fewer good stories than common folk, certain it is Philip grew so wrapped up in it that, when courtiers came galloping up and cut across them, he swore like a costermonger, and threatened—only half in jest—to cut off the next head that came between him and a good tale.

When Martin ended, the duke cried out:

“St. Luke! what sport goeth on in this mine earldom—ay, in my own woods—and I see it not. You base fellows have all the luck.”

Fortune’s partiality offended him like a spoiled child’s. “Lo you now! this was a man-hunt,” said he. “I never had the luck to be at a man-hunt.”

“My luck was none so great,” replied Martin bluntly; “I was on the wrong side of the dogs’ noses.”

“Ah! so you were; I forgot that.” And royalty, thus comforted, was reconciled.

“What would you, then?”

“A free pardon, your highness, for myself and Gerard.”

“For what?”

“For prison-breaking.”

“Go to; the bird will fly from the cage. ’Tis instinct. Besides, coop a young man up for loving a young woman? These burgomasters must be void of common sense. What else?”

“For striking down the burgomaster.”

“Oh, the hunted boar will turn to bay. ’Tis his right; and I hold him less than man that grudges it him. What else?”

“For killing of the bloodhounds.”

The duke’s countenance fell, as if a pleasant tune had jarred.

“’Twas their life or mine,” said Martin eagerly.

“Ay! but I can’t have my bloodhounds—my beautiful bloodhounds—sacrificed to—”

“No, no, no! They were not your dogs.”

“Whose dogs, then?”

“The ranger’s.”

“Oh.” The duke drew breath again, as a man does when he learns the loss is not his own. “Well, I am very sorry for him; but as I was saying, I can’t have my old soldiers sacrificed to his bloodhounds. Thou shalt have thy free pardon.”

“And poor Gerard.”

“And poor Gerard too, for thy sake. And more—tell thou this burgomaster his doings mislike me. This is to set up for a king, not a burgomaster. I’ll have no kings in Holland but one. Bid him be more humble; or by St. Jude I’ll hang him before his own door, as I hanged the burgomaster of—what’s the name—some town or other in Flanders it was; no, ’twas somewhere in Brabant—no matter—I hanged him, I remember that much—for oppressing poor folk.”

Then he beckoned his chancellor, a pursy old fellow that rode like a sack strapped to a saddle, and bade him draw a free pardon for Martin and one Gerard. The precious document was made in form and signed next day; and Martin hastened home with it, carrying that strip of parchment as if it were a loaf for starving people.

Margaret had left her bed some days and sat pale and pensive by the fireside, the warmth on her face and the weakness in her bones, when Martin burst in waving the parchment as if it were a banner and crying:

“A free pardon, girl, for Gerard as well as me! Send for him back when you will; all the burgomasters on earth daren’t lay a finger on him.”

Color rushed into her cheeks like wine into a thin cup. Her hands trembled as she took the parchment, devoured it with her eyes, kissed it again and again—as if the ink itself were a sacrament—and then flung her arms round Martin’s neck and kissed him.

When she was calmer, she told him Heaven had raised her up a friend in the dame Van Eyck.

“And I would fain consult her on this good news; but I have not strength to walk so far.”

“What need to walk?” said Martin. “There is my mule.”

“Your mule, Martin?”

The old soldier—professional pillager, as folk called him—laughed, and confessed he had got so used to the beast that at times he forgot Ghysbrecht had a prior claim. To-morrow, he said, he would turn her into the burgomaster’s yard. But to-night she should carry Margaret to Tergou.

It was nearly dusk. The town sounds softened; the day’s sharp clatter sank into evening murmurs. Margaret ventured. And about seven o’clock she astonished and gladdened her new, ardent friend by arriving at her house with unwonted roses on her cheeks, and Gerard’s pardon warm in her bosom.
