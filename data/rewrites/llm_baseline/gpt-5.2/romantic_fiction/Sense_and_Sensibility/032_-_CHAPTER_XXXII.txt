When Elinor, as soon as she could do so without betraying either haste or cruelty, repeated to her sister the substance of what she had learnt, she had imagined—foolishly, perhaps—that truth, once set upon the table like plain bread, might at least be broken and shared with less anguish than a feast of conjecture. Marianne heard her as one hears the turning of a knife on a board: steadily, without flinching, with eyes that did not once seek escape, and with lips that offered neither defence nor denial. She made no plea for Willoughby; she did not even attempt that delicate art by which the heart tries to sweeten bitterness with a remembered kindness. Only tears, silently gathered, declared how impossible it was for her to receive the story and yet to live unhurt beneath it.

Elinor was not deceived. The conviction went home. She saw it in Marianne’s altered bearing when Colonel Brandon called—no longer the shrinking that had been half resentment, half dread, but a subdued, compassionate respect, as if she had suddenly learnt what it is to carry an old wound and walk nevertheless. She saw, too, that Marianne’s spirits were less violently shaken; the sharp feverishness was gone. Yet wretchedness remained, only changed in its manner—as a fire, when the flames are beaten down, still burns in sullen coals.

For Marianne had lost more than a lover. The fall of Willoughby’s character—his seduction and desertion of Miss Williams, the misery of that injured girl, and the dreadful doubt of what his designs might once have been toward herself—preyed on her with a kind of sickened horror. His heart, gone, was a grief; his honour, found false, was a contamination. And this, which she could scarcely bear within her own mind, she could not bring herself to speak of even to Elinor. She brooded in silence, and that silence, like a room without air, oppressed her sister more than any outcry could have done.

From Barton, their mother’s letters followed quickly, long and full, like winter stores sent up to town—carefully packed, overflowing with feeling, and meant to sustain. To repeat Mrs. Dashwood’s language would have been only to echo what her daughters already knew: disappointment scarcely less painful than Marianne’s, indignation even greater than Elinor’s, and that anxious, tender solicitude which, in a mother’s hand, turns every word into a touch. Yet Elinor, as she read, could not help being struck by one mortifying proof of the depth of Marianne’s humiliation: Mrs. Dashwood spoke of fortitude. Bad indeed must be the affliction, when her mother—who had so long indulged the poetry of sorrow—could entreat her child not to indulge regret.

Mrs. Dashwood had determined, against her own comfort, that Marianne should not be at Barton now. There, every accustomed sound and smell—each gate’s creak, each wind in the trees, each familiar path where a step had once matched another step—would bring back the past with cruel fidelity, setting Willoughby before her not as he truly was, but as she had always seen him there. In London, by contrast, the day had other noises to impose themselves: wheels on stone, the far-off calls of sellers, the thin music that escaped from windows when someone practiced without passion. Variety of objects, of company, of occupations—those common, domestic distractions by which people are sometimes “cheated,” as her mother expressed it, into interest beyond themselves—might yet, at moments, draw Marianne’s mind from its own devouring circle.

Nor did Mrs. Dashwood fear any danger of their meeting Willoughby. His acquaintance, she was persuaded, must now be dropped by all who called themselves their friends. Design could never bring him to them; negligence could never leave them exposed; chance, in the crowd of London, had less in its favour than in the retirement of Devonshire, where it might one day force him before Marianne’s eyes while paying a visit at Allenham on his marriage—an event Mrs. Dashwood, having once only foreseen as probable, had now brought herself to expect as certain.

She had yet another reason—one of family, and therefore, in her mind, sacred. A letter from her son-in-law told her that he and his wife would be in town before the middle of February. It seemed right that her daughters should sometimes see their brother.

Marianne had promised to be guided by her mother’s opinion, and she submitted without open opposition; yet she felt it wrong, founded on mistaken grounds, and cruel in its effect. It deprived her of what she believed the only possible alleviation—the personal sympathy of her mother—and doomed her to such society and such scenes as must prevent her ever knowing a moment’s rest. Still, even in her misery, she found one strange consolation: what brought evil to herself might bring good to Elinor. And Elinor, who suspected—though she did not say so—that a longer stay might make it impossible to avoid Edward entirely, quieted her own apprehensions by thinking that, however it might try her, it was better for Marianne than an immediate return into Devonshire.

Elinor’s watchfulness over her sister was unceasing. As a careful cook keeps smoke from tainting a delicate dish, she guarded Marianne from the very name of Willoughby, knowing how a single syllable might set all the bitterness rising again. Her care was not thrown away. Marianne, without knowing it, reaped the whole advantage; for neither Mrs. Jennings, nor Sir John, nor even Mrs. Palmer ever spoke of him before her.

Elinor wished such forbearance could have been extended to herself; but that was impossible. Day after day she was obliged to listen to the indignation of their friends, poured out with as much noise as if sound itself could scour a stain from a man’s conduct.

Sir John could hardly contain himself. He had always thought well of Willoughby—such a good-natured fellow, such a bold rider; and now it was “an unaccountable business,” a thing past belief. He wished him at the devil with all his heart. He would not speak another word to him, meet him where he might, for all the world—not even if it were by the side of Barton covert and they were kept watching for two hours together. Such a scoundrel of a fellow, such a deceitful dog! Why, it was only the last time they met that Willoughby had offered him one of Folly’s puppies—and this was the end of it!

Mrs. Palmer, in her own manner, was equally enraged. She was determined to drop his acquaintance immediately; she was thankful she had never been acquainted with him at all; she wished Combe Magna were not so near Cleveland—though it did not signify, for it was far too far off to visit. She hated him so much, she said, that she was resolved never to mention his name again; and she should tell everybody she saw how good-for-nothing he was.

Yet Mrs. Palmer’s sympathy, always eager for particulars, found its occupation. Having sworn never to mention his name, she nevertheless took pains to procure all she could of the approaching marriage, and communicated it all to Elinor with the complacency of one laying out a table of curiosities: at what coachmaker’s the new carriage was building; by what painter Mr. Willoughby’s portrait was drawn; at what warehouse Miss Grey’s clothes might be seen. Elinor received these details as one takes an unwanted spice—aware it will overpower everything else, and unable, from politeness, to refuse it.

In this clamour of kindness, the calm, polite unconcern of Lady Middleton was, to Elinor, a relief like cool water. It comforted her to be sure of exciting no interest in at least one person; to know there was one who would meet her without curiosity after particulars, without anxiety for Marianne’s health, without the craving to hear grief described as if it were a fashionable entertainment. There are hours in which every qualification is raised, by the circumstances of the moment, beyond its real value; and Elinor, so often worried down by officious condolence, sometimes rated good-breeding as more indispensable than good-nature.

Lady Middleton expressed her sense of the affair about once every day, or twice if the subject occurred often, by saying, with perfect composure, “It is very shocking, indeed!” And by this continual though gentle vent, she was enabled not only to see the Miss Dashwoods from the first without the smallest emotion, but very soon to see them without recollecting a word of the matter. Having thus supported, in her own opinion, the dignity of her sex—censured what was wrong in the other—she considered herself at liberty to attend to the interest of her assemblies, and therefore determined (though rather against Sir John’s opinion) that, as Mrs. Willoughby would at once be a woman of elegance and fortune, she would leave her card as soon as she married.

Colonel Brandon’s enquiries were of another kind—so delicate and unobtrusive that they never pressed, never jarred. They were never unwelcome to Elinor. He had earned the privilege of intimate discussion by the friendly zeal with which he had endeavoured to soften Marianne’s disappointment; and Elinor, who had been made the unwilling repository of painful history, could talk with him in confidence. His chief reward for the exertion of disclosing old sorrows and present humiliations was given without ceremony: the pitying eye with which Marianne sometimes observed him, and the gentleness of her voice whenever—though it did not often happen—she was obliged, or could oblige herself, to speak to him. Those slight tokens assured him that his effort had not been thrown away, that it had won him an increase of goodwill; and they gave Elinor hopes that it might be farther augmented hereafter.

Mrs. Jennings, who knew nothing of all this fine interior change, saw only that the Colonel continued as grave as ever, and that she could neither prevail upon him to make the offer himself nor commission her to make it for him. At the end of two days she began to think that, instead of Midsummer, they would not be married till Michaelmas; by the end of a week she concluded it would not be a match at all. The good understanding between the Colonel and Miss Dashwood seemed rather to declare that the honours of the mulberry-tree, the canal, and the yew arbour would all be made over to Elinor; and Mrs. Jennings had for some time ceased to think at all of Mrs. Ferrars.

Early in February—within a fortnight of receiving Willoughby’s letter—Elinor had the painful office of informing Marianne that he was married. She had taken care to have the intelligence conveyed to herself as soon as the ceremony was over, because Marianne, every morning, eagerly examined the public papers, and Elinor could not bear the thought of her learning it first from that cold, printed line, surrounded by advertisements and trifles, as if a heart could be broken between notices of new goods and theatrical amusements.

Marianne received the news with a resolute composure that frightened Elinor more than any immediate passion would have done. She made no observation, and at first shed no tears. But after a short time, the tears burst forth with a force that seemed to come from deeper than sorrow—like a spring long dammed, suddenly freed. For the rest of the day she was hardly less pitiable than when she had first learnt to expect the event.

The Willoughbys left town as soon as they were married; and Elinor, now that there could be no danger of Marianne seeing either of them, hoped to prevail on her sister—who had never once left the house since the blow first fell—to go out again by degrees, as she had done before, to let air and motion, if not heal, at least keep life from becoming wholly stagnant.

About this time, the two Miss Steeles, lately arrived at their cousin’s house in Bartlett’s Buildings, Holborn, presented themselves again before their more grand relations in Conduit and Berkeley Streets, and were welcomed by all with great cordiality. Elinor alone was sorry to see them. Their presence always brought her pain; and she hardly knew how to make even a tolerably gracious return to the overpowering delight of Lucy in finding her still in town.

“I should have been quite disappointed if I had not found you here still,” Lucy said repeatedly, laying a strong emphasis on the word, as if it were a claim. “But I always thought I should. I was almost sure you would not leave London yet awhile, though you told me, you know, at Barton, that you should not stay above a month. But I thought at the time you would most likely change your mind when it came to the point. It would have been such a great pity to have went away before your brother and sister came. And now to be sure you will be in no hurry to be gone. I am amazingly glad you did not keep to your word.”

Elinor perfectly understood her meaning—each “still” and “word” was like the sharp clink of coins, counting out possession. She was forced to use all her self-command to make it appear that she did not understand.

“Well, my dear,” cried Mrs. Jennings, “and how did you travel?”

“Not in the stage, I assure you,” replied Miss Steele with quick exultation. “We came post all the way, and had a very smart beau to attend us. Dr. Davies was coming to town, and so we thought we’d join him in a post-chaise; and he behaved very genteelly, and paid ten or twelve shillings more than we did.”

“Oh, oh!” cried Mrs. Jennings. “Very pretty indeed! and the Doctor is a single man, I warrant you.”

“There now,” said Miss Steele, affectedly simpering, “everybody laughs at me so about the Doctor, and I cannot think why. My cousins say they are sure I have made a conquest; but for my part I declare I never think about him from one hour’s end to another. ‘Lord! here comes your beau, Nancy,’ my cousin said t’other day, when she saw him crossing the street to the house. My beau indeed! said I—I cannot think who you mean. The Doctor is no beau of mine.”

“Aye, aye,” said Mrs. Jennings, “that is very pretty talking—but it won’t do—the Doctor is the man, I see.”

“No, indeed!” replied her cousin, with affected earnestness; “and I beg you will contradict it, if you ever hear it talked of.”

Mrs. Jennings directly gave her the gratifying assurance that she certainly would not, and Miss Steele was made completely happy.

Lucy, after a short cessation of her pointed civilities, returned to the subject she loved best—not sympathy, but situation.

“I suppose you will go and stay with your brother and sister, Miss Dashwood, when they come to town.”

“No, I do not think we shall.”

“Oh, yes, I dare say you will.”

Elinor would not humour her by farther opposition.

“What a charming thing it is that Mrs. Dashwood can spare you both for so long a time together!”

“Long a time indeed!” interposed Mrs. Jennings. “Why, their visit is but just begun!”

Lucy was silenced.

“I am sorry we cannot see your sister, Miss Dashwood,” said Miss Steele. “I am sorry she is not well—” for Marianne had left the room on their arrival.

“You are very good. My sister will be equally sorry to miss the pleasure of seeing you; but she has been very much plagued lately with nervous head-aches, which make her unfit for company or conversation.”

“Oh, dear, that is a great pity! but such old friends as Lucy and me!—I think she might see us; and I am sure we would not speak a word.”

Elinor, with great civility, declined the proposal. Her sister was perhaps laid down upon the bed, or in her dressing gown, and therefore not able to come to them.

“Oh, if that’s all,” cried Miss Steele, “we can just as well go and see her.”

Elinor began to find this impertinence too much for her temper; but she was saved the trouble of checking it by Lucy’s sharp reprimand—one of those corrections which, though it did not add sweetness to Lucy’s manners, was of advantage in governing her sister’s.
