"I can’t say more. Not yet." He turned to the window, his back rigid. "There’s only one way to be certain now."

Cupples hesitated. "And that is?"

Trent didn’t answer. Instead, he reached for the lamp-black, his movements precise, deliberate. The truth, it seemed, was written in the dust—and Trent was the only one who could read it.

The rain whispered against the windows of White Gables, a steady, mournful drizzle that blurred the world beyond the glass into a watercolor of grays and greens. Mrs. Manderson stood at the window, her reflection a ghostly outline against the gloom. The weather had turned unseasonably bitter—a rarity for June—and the oppressive chill seemed to seep into the room, into her bones.

A knock sounded at the door.

“Come in,” she said, straightening almost imperceptibly, as though bracing herself against an unseen weight.

The maid announced Mr. Trent. He had come at an early hour, apologetic but insistent—a matter of urgency.

*Of course it was urgent*, she thought. Everything was urgent now.

She turned from the window, caught her own gaze in the mirror—the dark eyes, the pale, composed face—and gave a faint, humorless twist of her lips before facing the door.

Trent entered, and she noted the change in him at once. The usual easy confidence was gone, replaced by a weariness that spoke of sleepless nights. His expression was shuttered, deliberate.

“May I come to the point at once?” he asked, dispensing with pleasantries. “There’s a train I ought to catch, but I can’t leave until this is settled.”

She gestured to a chair. “You look exhausted. Sit, please. If you’ve come, it must be necessary.”

He remained standing. “Mrs. Manderson, what I have to say won’t be easy. I’ve uncovered things about your husband’s death—things no one else suspects. What I *believe* I’ve proven will be a shock, possibly worse. But if you give me reason to think publication would harm you beyond bearing, I’ll suppress it.” He tapped a long envelope on the table. “This contains my report. The choice is yours.”

Her hands tightened slightly, but her face betrayed nothing. “I understand. Please go on.”

“First,” he said, his voice cool, methodical, “I must ask: was it true, what you testified at the inquest? That you had no idea why your husband had grown distant in those last months?”

Her eyes flashed. “You’re asking if I perjured myself.”

“I am.” His gaze didn’t waver. “We both know the truth isn’t always spoken under oath.”

For a moment, she was silent. Then she turned to the window, her voice low but clear.

“I was twenty when I married him. I admired his strength, his certainty. But I learned quickly that his world was his business, and I was… an accessory. A social asset, or so he thought.” She exhaled sharply. “I failed him in that. The society he wanted me to thrive in was hollow—money and pretension, nothing more. I tried to play the part, but he saw through it eventually. He *always* saw through things.”

Her words came faster now, as if a dam had broken. “He never understood how I loathed it—the endless parties, the same vapid faces. I stole moments of freedom, trips with old friends, just to breathe. But he knew. And once he did, everything between us became… polite. Distant.”

She sank into a chair, her energy spent. “That was how things stood when he died.”

Trent watched her, his mind racing. There was more here—something she hadn’t said. He steeled himself.

“Mrs. Manderson,” he said slowly, “was your husband’s coldness connected to John Marlowe?”

The effect was instant. A choked cry, her hands flying to her face as she crumpled forward, her whole body shaking with sobs.

Trent stood frozen. Every instinct screamed to go to her, to offer comfort—or worse, to confess the reckless, unthinkable words clawing at his throat.

Instead, he placed the envelope neatly on the table and walked out, shutting the door softly behind him.

Outside, the rain fell harder. He strode blindly, his jaw clenched, his heart a battlefield of duty and desire.

He had his answer.

And it shattered everything.

My Dear Molloy—

If this reaches you in my absence, know that I have uncovered the truth behind Manderson’s murder. The enclosed dispatch names the culprit—an unsuspected figure whose guilt is laid bare in these pages. I leave it to your discretion whether to publish before his arrest, though I warn you: the law may frown upon it. Share this with Scotland Yard at once. As for me, I wash my hands of the affair. Would to God I had never touched it.

***

This, my final account of the Manderson case, is written with no small measure of relief. Until now, I withheld crucial facts—facts that, if revealed prematurely, might have allowed the killer to slip through our fingers. But the time for secrecy has passed. What follows is a tale of cold treachery, a labyrinth of deceit so cunningly constructed that even now, with the truth laid bare, it leaves a bitter taste.

Recall my earlier reports: the body discovered beside the shed, the eerie silence of the household despite the violent struggle, the missing whisky, the abandoned dental plate—trifles that hinted at something darker lurking beneath the surface. At first glance, they seemed mere oddities. But oddities, in my line of work, are often the threads that unravel the whole tapestry.

Take the shoes.

Manderson’s patent leather pair, worn the night of his death, bore minute but damning signs of strain—splits in the vamp, stretched stitching. These were not the marks of a man who prized his elegant, narrow footwear. No, they had been forced onto feet too large for them. And the edges of the tears were fresh. Someone else had worn them—recently.

Why? Because footprints, my dear reader, are the alphabet of the detective’s craft. The killer needed to leave Manderson’s prints, not his own. But it wasn’t just the shoes. The dental plate, left behind though Manderson never went without it? A prop, planted to cement the illusion. The whisky, gulped down not by the abstemious Manderson but by a man steeling himself for an unholy charade.

And then, the pièce de résistance: the clothes. The jacket Martin glimpsed in the library—unmistakably Manderson’s—worn by a man who kept his back turned, his face hidden beneath the broad-brimmed hat. A performance so brazen it bordered on genius.

But performances, no matter how polished, leave cracks.

Why did "Manderson" spin an elaborate tale to his half-asleep wife about sending Marlowe to Southampton? Why mention Marlowe at all, when the real Manderson scarcely spoke to her? Because the killer *needed* that alibi—for Marlowe, and for himself.

Here, then, is the truth:

Between ten and eleven that night, Manderson was shot—far from the house, where no one would hear. His body was brought back, stripped, and staged. Then the impostor entered—through the window, to avoid Martin’s sharp ears—dressed in Manderson’s clothes, his voice a flawless mimicry. He planted the revolver (Marlowe’s, of course) back in its case. He arranged the bedroom to perfection: shoes by the door, clothes strewn carelessly, the denture placed just so.

And who was this phantom Manderson?

A man who knew the household intimately.
A man of similar build, though with slightly larger feet.
A man with a talent for acting—*Marlowe’s* talent, honed in Oxford’s theatrical circles.
A man who *had* to sustain the illusion of Manderson’s survival until past midnight.

Every fact fits. Every shadow lifts.

The rest, Molloy, is in your hands.

Trent’s pen hovered over the letter to Sir James Molloy before he committed the words to paper.

*"I’m returning the cheque for my work on the Manderson case,"* he wrote from Munich, where he had fled after submitting his final, lackluster dispatch to the *Record*. *"What I provided wasn’t worth a fraction of the sum. Normally, I’d pocket it without a second thought—but not this time. Pay your standard rate, if you will, and donate the money to a charity that doesn’t specialize in intimidation, assuming such a thing exists."*

He paused, the ink drying as he considered his next words. *"I’ve come here to clear my head. Painting is impossible—I couldn’t even manage a fence. What I need is movement, action. Send me somewhere with a story worth telling, and I’ll deliver. After that, perhaps I’ll be fit for real work again."*

Sir James replied by wire, dispatching him to the chaos of Kurland and Livonia, where revolution crackled through the countryside like wildfire. For two months, Trent followed the trail of blood and rebellion. He witnessed the assassination of General Dragilew—shot dead in the streets by an eighteen-year-old girl. He documented burnings, summary executions, the grotesque theater of collapsing order. Each night, he slept with one eye open; each dawn, he awoke to fresh horrors.

Yet through it all, one face haunted him—Mabel Manderson’s.

He had not expected this. At thirty-two, Trent prided himself on his understanding of the world, of love and its follies. He had known passion before, but never like this—a relentless, hopeless obsession. It fascinated him even as it tormented him. He had always dismissed unrequited love as a boyish delusion. Now, he knew better.

Her image came to him most often as he had first seen her on the cliff—arms outstretched, exulting in her newfound freedom. That moment had told him everything: her marriage had been a prison, her widowhood a release. And the man for whom she had been freed—Marlowe—loomed in his thoughts like a specter.

Trent had tried, desperately, to find another motive for Manderson’s murder. But the truth gnawed at him: Marlowe, consumed by love and privy to the misery of her marriage, had taken the most damning page from history’s book. The evidence was there—the escape through *her* room, the way she had collapsed when Marlowe’s name was spoken.

Yet the thought that she might have known—that she could have been complicit—was unbearable. He had seen her grief, her gentleness. He had *felt* her goodness. But doubt festered. Had she guessed? Had she turned a blind eye? Worse—had she *wanted* it?

The questions plagued him, even as he threw himself into his work. After six months, he returned to Paris, seeking distraction in its bustling streets. He reconnected with old friends—artists, journalists, policemen—and found solace in their company. But his mind wandered back to England, to the unresolved wound of the Manderson affair.

Then, one June morning, he spotted a familiar figure on the Rue des Martyrs—Mr. Bunner. The American’s cheerful greeting forced Trent into conversation, and over a long meal, Bunner spoke freely.

Marlowe, it seemed, had entered his father’s business and was thriving. He was engaged now—to an Irish girl. The news struck Trent like a blow.

Bunner knew little. She had left England after the inquest, lived briefly in Italy, then returned to London. She had bought a house in Hampstead, another in the country. She rarely appeared in society.

*"Money to burn,"* Bunner mused, *"and nothing to spend it on. A shame—she’s the finest woman I ever met."*

Trent left soon after, his thoughts in turmoil. He needed answers. Cupples would know more—but Cupples was traveling. Marlowe? No, not yet. And Mabel herself? Unthinkable.

Instead, he haunted the opera, hoping for a glimpse of her. Night after night, he scanned the crowds, telling himself it was mere curiosity.

Then, one evening, a touch on his arm—light, deliberate. He turned.

She stood there, radiant, smiling. *"I wouldn’t miss a note of *Tristan*,"* she said. *"Nor should you. Come see me in the interval."*

She gave him the number of her box and vanished into the glittering crowd.

The weeks that followed were a torment Trent could scarcely bear. Each encounter with Mrs. Manderson was a fresh ordeal. She treated him with a careful, measured warmth—neither cold nor intimate, but maddeningly poised between the two. At the opera, he had been stunned to find her in the company of Mrs. Wallace, a woman of his own social sphere. She explained it lightly—she had taken a house near his acquaintances, and thus their paths had crossed.

That night, he had been painfully aware of his own clumsiness. Words tumbled from him in a nervous flood—stories of the Baltic, half-formed observations, all directed at the wrong woman. Mrs. Manderson, by contrast, was effortlessly composed, speaking of her travels, her new home, mutual friends. But Trent scarcely heard her. His attention was fixed on the curve of her cheek, the dark cascade of her hair, the line of her shoulder against the glow of the stage lights. By the evening’s end, he was hollow with suppressed emotion.

In their later meetings, he mastered himself—outwardly, at least. He matched her tone, played the part of the casual friend. But beneath the surface, his mind churned. How could she face him so calmly, knowing what he had suspected? That she had read his manuscript was undeniable. Yet she behaved as though no shadow lay between them.

His intuition told him otherwise. There *was* a wound, carefully concealed. More than once, he sensed her steering conversation toward dangerous waters, and each time he deftly turned it aside. He resolved to leave London soon—the strain was unbearable. He no longer cared to uncover the truth. He only knew, with grim certainty, that he had been wrong.

His second resolution was simpler: he would not be alone with her.

But when her note arrived, summoning him to her home, he went without protest. This was a challenge he could not refuse.

She greeted him with easy charm, guiding him through the room’s treasures—relics of her travels, each with its story. When he asked her to play for him, she obliged without hesitation. Her fingers moved over the keys with a skill that left him spellbound.

"You were born to music," he said softly when she finished.

She smiled. "It has always been a comfort to me. But when did you first decide I was musical? At the opera, I suppose?"

"No," he murmured, then stiffened as the implication struck him. "I think I knew the first time I saw you."

The past had been invoked. A silence fell.

Mrs. Manderson’s cheeks colored. Abruptly, she rose and faced him.

"That remark will serve as well as any," she began, her voice steady. "I asked you here today because I could no longer endure the pretense. Ever since White Gables, I told myself your opinion didn’t matter—that you would never speak of your suspicions. But it *did* matter. Because what you believed was not true."

Trent met her gaze, his face unreadable.

"Since I came to know you," he said, "I have ceased to think it."

"Thank you," she replied, a flush deepening on her skin. "But I want you to know the truth."

She spoke then—of her husband’s irrational jealousy, of Marlowe’s ill-fated infatuation with another woman, of the cruel misunderstanding that had festered beneath the surface of her marriage. Trent listened, his head bowed.

When she finished, she placed the sealed manuscript in his hands. "I read it many times," she said. "I even forgot, sometimes, that it was about me. And I thank you—for sacrificing your triumph rather than risk my name."

Trent looked up at last. "I don’t know how to apologize," he said hoarsely. "I was a fool. A blind, arrogant fool."

She laughed—a bright, clear sound that lifted the weight from his chest. "Oh, don’t be so dramatic. You had only met me twice. It wasn’t unreasonable."

"It was," he insisted. "Any man who could know you and still believe such things deserves no forgiveness."

"Well, you have mine," she said lightly. "And now, Mr. Trent, the subject is closed."

He rose to leave, but she stopped him. "One more thing." She lifted the envelope. "This."

Trent hesitated. "There *is* something I must ask."

"Then ask."

He drew a breath. "Did you ever—"

She waited.

He shook his head. "No. It doesn’t matter now."

She smiled, and for the first time, he saw something like relief in her eyes. "Goodbye, Trent."

He left, the manuscript in his hand, the past finally laid to rest.

“If you insist,” Trent said with a resigned sigh, “I suppose I’ll have to humor you. But I’d much rather write this alone. Still, if you’re determined—” He gestured vaguely toward the escritoire. “Fetch me something suitably solemn—paper unmarked by any mortal sin, preferably. I warn you, I’m in no mood for correspondence.”

She rewarded him with a smile. “What will you say?” he mused, pen poised above the blank sheet. “Shall I compare him to a summer’s day? Or perhaps something sharper?”

“Say what you mean,” she suggested.

He shook his head. “What I *mean* to say—what I’ve been bursting to tell every passerby since yesterday—is ‘Mabel is mine, and the world is a farce.’ But that lacks the necessary gravitas for a letter meant to unsettle a man.” He tapped the page. “I’ve managed ‘Dear Mr. Marlowe.’ Now comes the hard part.”

“Tell him you’re sending a manuscript,” she prompted. “Something he might find… enlightening.”

Trent arched a brow. “Do you realize that entire sentence contains only two words longer than a syllable? This isn’t a chat over tea—it’s a blade wrapped in parchment. We need weight.”

“Why?” She frowned. “Every solicitor and businessman I’ve known drowns their letters in ‘pursuant to’ and ‘hereinbefore mentioned,’ yet they don’t speak like that. It’s absurd.”

“To you, perhaps.” Trent set down the pen with relief and stood. “Allow me to explain. We English, allergic to mental exertion, skate through life on a thin vocabulary. Long words? They’re either comic or ceremonial. Take ‘terminological inexactitude.’ The Continent wouldn’t blink, but here, we roar at it—why? Because it’s *long*. That’s the whole joke. Conversely, when we want to be grave, we pile syllables like sandbags. A solicitor who begins with ‘pursuant to the aforementioned instructions’ feels he’s earned his fee. Laugh if you like, but it’s truth. Abroad, even a cabman tosses around ‘functionary’ and ‘independence’ between bites of his dinner. Here, we—”

“No, no, *no*,” she cut in, snatching up the pen. “We’ll dissect linguistics another time. Right now, you’re writing that letter.” She pressed the pen into his hand.

Trent eyed it with distaste. “You’ll regret silencing me. Men who don’t talk are worse than those who do. Beware the silent ones, Mabel. Besides, this feels indecent—composing a threat while you’re near enough to remind me of everything gentle.”

She steered him firmly back to the escritoire. “Try. I want it done, and sent today. The sooner this is settled, the sooner we’re free of it. Don’t you know that relief—when the letter’s gone, irretrievable, and the fussing ends?”

“As you wish.” He bent over the paper, dating it from his hotel.

She watched him for a moment, then drifted to the piano. Soft notes filled the room, a wordless accompaniment to his thoughts.

Ten minutes passed before Trent spoke again. “What if he refuses to answer?”

Her fingers stilled. “He won’t. Not if he believes silence will cost him.”

“But I’m not actually threatening him. You’d never allow it—and neither would I. The case is too murky now.”

“Ah, but he doesn’t know that.” Her smile was faint.

Trent exhaled sharply. “Honor is a peculiar thing. I’d thrash a man for insulting me and think nothing of it, yet you’d call that disgraceful. Now you suggest I bluff Marlowe with an empty threat—something even a villain might balk at. No. I won’t do it.” He returned to his task.

The piano resumed, softer still.

At last, Trent sat back. “Done. Faithfully his. Care to see?”

She crossed to him, switching on the lamp. Leaning over his shoulder, she read:

*You may recall our meeting under unfortunate circumstances at Marlstone last June. At that time, as a journalist, I conducted an independent investigation into the death of the late Sigsbee Manderson. My conclusions, detailed in the enclosed manuscript (originally intended for publication), were known only to myself and two others.*

Here, she paused. “Two?”

“Your uncle is the second. I told him everything last night. He deserved to know—and his advice may prove useful when I confront Marlowe.”

She nodded slowly. “No one else?”

“No one.” He pressed her hand. “I want this buried, Mabel. But first, I must know *all*.”

She resumed reading:

*Recent developments, however, have compelled me to revise my decision. While I do not intend to publish my findings, I now seek a private statement from you. If you possess any information that might alter the case’s complexion, I see no reason for withholding it.*

*Kindly inform me when and where we might meet—unless you prefer my hotel. In either instance, I request Mr. Cupples (who has reviewed the enclosed) be present.*

“A very cold letter,” she observed.

“Cold enough to freeze his composure.” Trent slid the pages into an envelope. “This shouldn’t go by post. A courier, straight to his hands.”

She agreed and left to arrange it.

When she returned, Trent was rifling through sheet music. She settled beside him in a rustle of skirts.

“Philip,” she said quietly, “last night—did you tell Uncle about us?”

“No. That’s your secret to share, when you choose.”

She studied her folded hands. “I’d like *you* to tell him. Perhaps you can guess why.” She met his eyes. “Consider it settled.”

For a long moment, silence held them.

Trent leaned back at last. “What a world,” he murmured. “Mabel, play something—something pure. Not triumph, not feverish joy, but the quiet certainty of happiness. Before the mood passes.”

She moved to the piano, fingers hesitating over the keys. Then, with a deep breath, she began—the opening of paradise, in notes.

The study overlooked St. James’s Park through tall windows, its oak-paneled walls and deep leather chairs betraying the refined tastes of a man who had never softened the space with domesticity. John Marlowe unlocked the heavy desk by the window and withdrew a thick envelope from its depths.

“You’ve read this, I take it?” he said to Mr. Cupples.

“Two days ago,” Cupples replied, his mild eyes wandering the room with detached interest. “We’ve discussed it thoroughly.”

Marlowe turned to Trent, placing the envelope on the table between them. “Your manuscript. I’ve gone through it three times. I doubt any other man could have pieced together as much of the truth as you have.”

Trent didn’t acknowledge the compliment. His long frame was coiled into the chair, his gaze fixed on the fire. “You mean,” he said, drawing the envelope closer, “there’s more truth to be told now. We’re ready to hear it. The longer the story, the better—I want to understand completely. Start with Manderson. His character must be at the heart of this.”

“You’re right,” Marlowe said grimly. He crossed to the hearth and perched on the edge of the fender. “I’ll begin there.”

Trent’s eyes locked onto his. “Before you do—understand that I’m here to listen, but I’ve no reason yet to doubt my conclusions.” He tapped the envelope. “You’re presenting a defense. Keep that in mind.”

“Perfectly.” Marlowe was calm, composed—a stark contrast to the exhausted wreck Trent had met at Marlstone eighteen months prior. His athletic frame was poised, his expression steady. Only the faint tension around his mouth betrayed the gravity of his position.

“Sigsbee Manderson,” Marlowe began, “was not a sane man. Most of the American magnates I’ve known got where they were through greed, relentless work, sheer force of will, or blind luck. Few had remarkable minds. Manderson had all those traits—but what set him apart was his intellect. Some might call him ruthless, but ruthlessness alone doesn’t explain his success. He had a mind like a steel trap—memory, foresight, tenacity. They called him the ‘Napoleon of Wall Street,’ and for good reason. He digested facts like a general, planned three moves ahead, and never did the obvious thing. His opponents never saw his plays coming.”

Marlowe paused. “I used to think his sliver of Iroquois blood—remote as it was—might explain his cunning. Strangely, no one knew of it but him and me. I discovered it while tracing his genealogy. The Mandersons had intermarried with native tribes generations back. He was horrified when I told him. The idea of mixed blood disgusted him—more so as racial tensions grew after the war. He swore me to secrecy. I think, from that moment, he began to distrust me.”

Mr. Cupples interjected suddenly. “Had Manderson any religious inclinations?”

Marlowe considered. “None I ever saw. Prayer and worship meant nothing to him. But he’d been raised with strict morals. In private, he was ascetic—no vices but smoking. Oddly, though he’d deceive in business without hesitation, he never told a direct lie in conversation. It was as if he saw life as a game where certain deceptions were fair play.”

“A bleak worldview,” murmured Cupples.

“Indeed,” Marlowe agreed. “Now, as to my own standing with him—we were always on solid terms. Not friendship, but mutual respect. I became his secretary after Oxford. Chess got me the post, oddly enough.”

Trent sat forward abruptly. “Chess! That’s it.” His eyes gleamed. “When we first met, I couldn’t place your look—now I remember. You’ve got the chess-player’s eye. Cold, calculating. I’ve seen it before.”

Marlowe nodded. “It runs in the family. At Oxford, I was nearly the best. Dr. Munro recommended me to Manderson. He wanted a secretary who could ride, play chess, and spell passably. I got the job.”

He exhaled. “For years, it was a good life. I managed his estates, his horses, his travels. I learned quickly. Then, in the last months, something changed. He grew colder, more secretive. Yet he never showed me anything but his usual detached approval—until the night he died.”

Marlowe’s voice hardened. “That was when I saw the truth. He hated me. Not just disliked—*hated*. And he’d planned his own death to frame me for murder.”

Mr. Cupples stiffened. “You’re saying Manderson engineered his own demise?”

“I am.” Marlowe met his gaze squarely. “And if you’ll let me, I’ll tell you exactly how.”

The evening air was crisp as Trent and Mr. Cupples stepped out of the towering flats into the bustling London street.

“Did you say we had an appointment at half-past seven?” Cupples inquired, adjusting his spectacles. “I don’t recall any such arrangement.”

Trent grinned, clapping a hand on his friend’s shoulder. “We do now. You’re dining with me. And since I’ve just untangled a case that’s haunted me for over a year, I’d say that calls for celebration. No arguments—I’m paying.” He flagged down a cab with a flourish. “And we’re not going to my club. Tonight demands something better than their eternally dull menu. We’re going to Sheppard’s.”

“Sheppard’s?” Cupples echoed, bemused.

“A place where the mutton is so good it’s made Americans question their life choices,” Trent declared as they climbed into the cab. “But more importantly—” He lit a cigarette, exhaling smoke with theatrical satisfaction. “—I’m getting married. To the most extraordinary woman alive.”

Cupples’ eyes widened. “Mabel? My dear boy, this is splendid news!” He seized Trent’s hand, shaking it vigorously. “I’ve hoped for this for ages. She’s endured so much unhappiness—she deserves every joy. And you—well, I’ve known your feelings for some time.” A sly smile crept across his face. “Ever since that dinner at my house where you spent the entire evening glaring at poor Professor Peppmuller while stealing glances at her.”

Trent groaned. “Mabel claims she knew even before that. And here I thought I was the picture of indifference.” He sighed, then brightened. “Ah, well. If I was a fool then, I’ll be a happy fool now.”

The cab rolled to a stop outside Sheppard’s, and they were soon seated in a cozy corner of the lively dining room. After ordering—Trent with gusto, Cupples with his usual abstemious precision—their conversation turned to the day’s revelations.

“The irony isn’t lost on me,” Cupples mused, dissecting his mutton with meticulous care. “We held the key to Manderson’s madness all along—his jealous obsession. Yet Marlowe will never know how close he came to being condemned for a crime born of delusion.” He shook his head. “How many lives are shaped by the hidden judgments of others?”

Trent leaned in. “You think Marlowe’s story holds up?”

“Perfectly,” Cupples replied. “A man in peril devises an audacious escape—hardly unheard of. The true marvel is how rarely such schemes unfold in the world of crime. Most criminals lack the strategic depth to weave them.”

“And yet,” Trent countered, “had Marlowe walked blindly into Manderson’s trap, he’d have hanged. How many innocent men have met that fate?”

Cupples’ expression darkened. “Too many. It’s why I’ll never again support capital punishment on circumstantial evidence.”

Their debate meandered through historical cases of wrongful condemnation before Trent circled back. “Still, Marlowe’s defense would have been torn apart in court. No jury would believe Manderson engineered his own death.”

A quiet smile played on Cupples’ lips. “Ah, but there *was* evidence to support Marlowe’s story.” He set down his fork. “Mine.”

Trent froze. “What?”

“I would have testified,” Cupples continued, unperturbed. “You said one piece of evidence could change everything. Well, I possess it.” He took a sip of water. “Because, my dear Trent, *I* was the one who shot Manderson.”

The clatter of the restaurant faded into silence as Trent stared, the weight of the revelation crashing over him.
