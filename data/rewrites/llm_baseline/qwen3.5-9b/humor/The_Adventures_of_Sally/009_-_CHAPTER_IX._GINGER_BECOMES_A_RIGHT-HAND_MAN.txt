Sally didn't truly grasp the depth of her anxiety regarding this young man until she saw him standing right there before her—hair a complete mess, a giant smudge of dirt on the tip of his nose. Suddenly, the terrifying vision of him bobbing around in the Thames as a cold, unappreciated corpse faded into memory. She was a girl of sharp imagination, and she had let it run wild. So, when the shocking reality of his presence hit her, her initial astonishment was instantly swallowed by a wave of pure relief.

She threw herself into a nearby chair and erupted into a fit of laughter so loud and strange that even she winced at the sound. Ginger watched, worried, thinking it sounded hysterical.

"I say, you know!" Ginger ventured cautiously as the giggles showed no sign of stopping. "Are you quite alright?"

Concerned, he stepped forward. Finding blighters under a girl's bed was nasty business indeed. Sally sat up, wiping tears from her eyes while still gurgling with amusement.

"Oh, I am so glad to see you," she gasped between laughs.

"No, really?" Ginger asked, relieved. "That's... that's fine."

A polite apology suddenly crossed his mind. "I say, you know, I'm terribly sorry about barging in. I never dreamed it was your room. I thought it was unoccupied."

"Don't mention it," Sally replied, composing herself. "I shouldn't have disturbed you. You were clearly sleeping. Do you always sleep on the floor?"

"It was like this..." Ginger began, gesturing vaguely.

"Of course, if you're wearing it as a sort of beauty spot," Sally interrupted gently, pointing a finger at his nose, "then all right. But just in case you don't know, you have a smut on your nose."

"Oh, my aunt! Is it real?"

"Now would I deceive you on something so important?"

"Do you mind if I check in the mirror?"

"Certainly, if you can bear it."

Ginger hurried to the dressing table. "You're absolutely right," he announced, dabbing frantically at his face with a handkerchief. "I thought I was. I'm very quick at noticing details."

"My hair is a bit rumpled, too."

"Very much so," Sally confirmed.

"You should take my advice," Ginger said earnestly. "Never lie about under beds. There's nothing in it."

"That reminds me," Sally interjected, regaining her composure. "Won't you be offended if I ask you something?"

"No, no. Go ahead."

"It's rather an impertinent question. You might resent it."

"No, no."

"Well, then, what were you doing under my bed?"

"Oh, under *your* bed?"

"Yes. Under *my* bed. This. It's a bed, you know. Mine. My bed. You were under it. Why? Or, to put it differently, why were you under my bed?"

"I was hiding."

"Playing hide-and-seek? That explains it."

"Mrs. Beecher—Meecher—I forget her name exactly—she was after me."

Sally shook her head disapprovingly. "You mustn't encourage Mrs. Meecher in these childish games. It unsettles her."

Ginger ran an agitated hand over his forehead. "It's like this..."

"I hate to criticize your appearance," Sally said kindly, "and personally, I like it; but when you clutched your brow just then, you covered it in about a pound of dust. Your hands are probably grubby, too."

Ginger inspected his palms. "They are!"

"Why not make a proper job of it and have a wash?"

"Do you mind?"

"I'd prefer it."

"Thanks awfully. I mean to say, it's your basin, you know, and everything. Seems I'm making myself pretty comfortable."

"Oh, no."

"As for the soap..."

"Use mine. We Americans are famous for our hospitality."

"Thanks awfully."

"The towel is on your right."

"Thanks awfully."

"And I've a clothes brush in my bag."

"Thanks awfully."

*Splash!* Water flew everywhere like a sea-lion taking a dip.

"Now, then," Sally said, drying her hands, "why were you hiding from Mrs. Meecher?"

A look of genuine fear replaced the earlier humor on Ginger's face. "I say, you know, that woman is rather one of the lads, what! She scares me! Word came that she was on the prowl, so it seemed a wise move to take cover until she blew over. If she'd found me, she'd have forced me to take that dog of hers for a walk."

"Toto?"

"Toto. You know," Ginger added with a tone of deep offense, "no dog has a right to be a dog like that. I don't suppose there's anyone keener on dogs than I am, but a thing like a woolly rat." He shuddered. "One simply cannot be seen walking it in public."

"Why couldn't you have refused in a firm but gentlemanly manner?"

"Ah! There you touch the spot. See, the fact is, I'm a bit behind on the rent, and that makes it hard to take a firm stand."

"But how can you be behind on the rent? I left here the Saturday before last, and you weren't in the place then. You couldn't have been here more than a week."

"I've been here just a week. That's the week I'm behind on."

"But why? You were a millionaire when I left you at Roville."

"Well, the truth is, I went back to the tables that night and lost a good chunk of what I'd won. Somehow, when I got to America, the money just slipped away."

"What made you come to America at all?" Sally asked, posing the question every sensible person would have asked immediately.

One of his familiar blushes raced across Ginger's face. "Oh, I thought I would. Land of opportunity, you know."

"Have you managed to find any of those opportunities yet?"

"Well, I have a job of sorts. I'm a waiter at a rummy little place on Second Avenue. The salary isn't big, but I would have scraped enough to pay last week's rent, only they docked me heavily for breaking plates and whatnot. The fact is, I'm making rather a hash of it."

"Oh, Ginger! You oughtn't to be a waiter!"

"That's what the boss thinks."

"I mean, you ought to be doing something far superior."

"But what? You've no idea how well all these people manage without my help. I've tramped all over the place offering my services, but they all say they'll try to carry on as they are."

Sally pondered this for a moment. "I know!"

"What?"

"I'll make Fillmore give you a job. I wonder I didn't think of it before."

"Fillmore?"

"My brother. Yes, he'll be able to use you."

"What as?"

Sally considered. "As a—as a—as oh, as his right-hand man."

"Does he want a right-hand man?"

"Sure he does. He's a young fellow trying to get along. Sure to want a right-hand man."

"'M yes," Ginger said reflectively. "Of course, I've never been a right-hand man, you know."

"Oh, you'll pick it up. I'll take you round to him now. He's staying at the Astor."

"There's just one thing," Ginger hesitated.

"What's that?"

"I might make a hash of it."

"Heavens, Ginger! There must be something in this world you wouldn't make a hash of. Don't argue any longer. Are you dry and clean? Very well, then. Let's be off."

"Right ho."

Ginger took a step toward the door, then froze, rigid, with one leg raised in mid-air, as if a spell had been cast upon him. From the hallway outside came a shrill, frantic yapping. Ginger looked at Sally, then looked longingly back at the bed.

"Don't be such a coward," Sally said severely.

"Yes, but..."

"How much do you owe Mrs. Meecher?"

"Round about twelve dollars, I think."

"I'll pay her."

Ginger flushed awkwardly. "No, I'm hanged if you will! I mean," he stammered, "it's frightfully good of you, and I can't tell you how grateful I am, but honestly, I couldn't..."

Sally didn't press the point. She liked him the better for his rugged independence, a trait her brother Fillmore had never exhibited during their days of poverty.

"Very well," she said. "Have it your own way. Proud. That's me all over, Mabel. Ginger!" She stopped abruptly. "Pull yourself together. Where is your manly spirit? I'd be ashamed to be such a coward."

"Awfully sorry, but, honestly, that woolly dog..."

"Never mind the dog. I'll see you through."
