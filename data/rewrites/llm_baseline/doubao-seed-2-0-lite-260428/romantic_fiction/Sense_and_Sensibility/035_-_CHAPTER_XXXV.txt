Elinor’s quiet, burning curiosity to meet the formidable Mrs. Ferrars had finally been sated, and the encounter left her with no small measure of relief. She had found in Edward’s mother every unlovely trait imaginable that could make a union between their families nothing short of a nightmare: brutal unyielding pride, petty, grasping meanness, and a fixed, irrational prejudice against Elinor that would have poisoned every step of a courtship between herself and Edward, had he been a free man. As she turned the visit over in her mind, she could clearly see every barrier that would have tangled their engagement, delayed their marriage, and made their lives miserable for years after. She found herself almost grateful, for her own sake, that the far greater, unassailable obstacle of Edward’s secret bond to Lucy Steele spared her ever having to grovel for Mrs. Ferrars’ approval, spared her from being at the mercy of the woman’s random, cruel whims. Even if she could not bring herself to rejoice at Edward being tied to Lucy, she had to admit that if Lucy were a kinder, more deserving girl, she ought to count that unexpected barrier a blessing.

She could only marvel that Lucy was so giddily, blindly elevated by the faint scraps of civility Mrs. Ferrars had shown her. Lucy’s vanity and desperate hunger for status had so thoroughly clouded her judgment that she mistook the politeness granted her only because she was not Elinor, not the woman Mrs. Ferrars and her daughter Fanny loathed, as genuine, personal favour. She thought that shallow preference was proof they adored her, all because no one knew the truth of her secret engagement. And that delusion was not just written all over Lucy’s face during the visit—it spilled over the next morning, when she badgered Lady Middleton into dropping her off in Berkeley Street, on the off chance she might find Elinor alone to gush over her newfound good fortune.

Luck was on her side: no sooner had she stepped through the door than a messenger arrived summoning Mrs. Jennings to attend Mrs. Palmer, leaving the two young women completely unobserved.
“My dearest friend!” Lucy cried, the second the last footstep faded down the hall, grabbing Elinor’s hands in hers. “I simply had to come tell you how perfectly happy I am. Could anything in the world be more flattering than the way Mrs. Ferrars treated me yesterday? She was so impossibly affable! You know how terrified I was at the thought of meeting her, but the very moment they introduced us, she acted as if she’d taken an immediate, wholehearted fancy to me. Wasn’t that so? You were there, you saw it all—weren’t you completely struck by how warm she was?”
“She was certainly very civil to you,” Elinor allowed gently.
“Civil? That’s not half of it! I saw far more than mere civility. That was kindness no one else got at that party, no one but me! No pride, no icy hauteur, and Fanny Dashwood was exactly the same—all sweetness, all soft good humour!”
Elinor tried to steer the conversation elsewhere, but Lucy would not be deterred, pressing her again and again to admit that her happiness was well founded, until Elinor had no choice but to respond.
“Undoubtedly, if they knew of your engagement,” she said, “nothing could be more flattering than the way they welcomed you. But as matters stand, they are entirely unaware—”
“I knew you would say that!” Lucy cut in quickly. “But there is no reason on earth for Mrs. Ferrars to pretend to like me if she did not, and her liking me is everything. You cannot talk me out of my satisfaction. I am certain everything will end perfectly well, no of the terrible hurdles I used to fear. Mrs. Ferrars is a charming woman, and so is Fanny Dashwood! They are both perfectly delightful. I cannot believe I never heard you say before how agreeable your sister by marriage is!”
Elinor had no reply to that, and did not even attempt to make one.
“Are you unwell, Miss Dashwood?” Lucy pressed, tilting her head. “You look so low, so quiet—surely you are not ill?”
“I was never in better health,” Elinor said, forcing a small smile.
“I am so glad to hear it! I should be heartbroken if you fell ill, you who have been my greatest comfort in all the world. Heaven knows what I should ever have done without your friendship.”
Elinor struggled to make a civil response, doubting she succeeded, but it was enough to satisfy Lucy, who prattled on immediately.
“I know you truly care for me. Next to Edward’s love, your regard is the greatest comfort I have. Poor Edward! But the best of this is, we will get to meet constantly now. Lady Middleton adores Fanny Dashwood, so we will be visiting Harley Street all the time, and Edward spends half his days at his sister’s house. Mrs. Ferrars and Fanny both said more than once yesterday that they will always be delighted to see me. They are such wonderful women! If you ever mention me to your sister, you cannot praise her highly enough, you simply cannot.”
Elinor had no intention of doing any such thing, and made no promise to do so. Lucy continued, carried away by her own good fortune.
“I would have known in an instant if Mrs. Ferrars disliked me. If she had only given me a stiff, formal curtsy, never spoken a word to me, never looked at me with a hint of warmth—you know exactly the sort of forbidding treatment I mean—I would have given up all hope then and there. I could never have endured it. I know how violent her dislikes can be, once they take root.”
Elinor never got the chance to reply to that giddy, unthinking boast. The drawing room door was thrown open, the servant announced Mr. Ferrars, and Edward stepped across the threshold.
It was an excruciating, awkward moment. All three faces went bright with embarrassment, every one of them looking foolish and flustered. Edward looked half-ready to turn right around and walk back out the door instead of coming further in. The exact situation all three had dreaded more than anything had come to pass: they were all three together, with not a single other person present to soften the tension.
The two women recovered first. Lucy knew better than to draw attention to herself, to risk revealing their secret engagement, so she only looked at him with soft, demure tenderness, spoke a single, brief greeting, and fell entirely silent. Elinor, for Edward’s sake as much as her own, forced herself to pull herself together, pushing past the sting of old hurt to greet him with a manner almost easy, almost open. Another small, brave effort smoothed away the last of the awkwardness. She would not let Lucy’s presence, nor the quiet knowledge of all Edward had cost her, stop her from telling him how glad she was to see him, how sorry she had been to be out of the house the last time he had called at Berkeley Street. She would not let the sharp, observant gaze of Lucy, fixed firmly on every move she made, stop her from showing him the gentle attentions due an old, dear friend, almost family. Her warmth calmed Edward, and he found the courage to sit down—though his embarrassment still far outstripped theirs. Lucy had no stake in the tension, and Elinor had long since steeled her heart for pain, but Edward’s torn, conflicted heart could not find the same ease.
Lucy said nothing at all, determined not to make the situation any easier for the other two, so almost every word that was spoken had to come from Elinor. She volunteered all the small, obvious details Edward should have thought to ask for: news of Mrs. Dashwood’s health, the comfort of their lodgings in town, the slow progress of Marianne’s recovery. She did not stop there, either: soon after, she made a heroically selfless decision, announcing that she would go fetch Marianne to join them, giving the two secret fiancés a few minutes alone. She did it with perfect grace, loitering on the landing for several long minutes, steeling herself to the quiet hurt of it, before she went to find her sister.
Her small sacrifice was cut short almost immediately. Marianne, wild with joy at the news Edward was in the house, came bounding into the drawing room the very second Elinor opened her bedroom door. Her pleasure at seeing him was as loud, as unfiltered, as every other feeling she had ever known. She pressed his hand in hers, her voice thick with sisterly affection.
“Dear Edward!” she cried. “This is such a happy moment! It almost makes up for every last bit of misery we have endured lately.”
Edward tried to return her warmth as it deserved, but with Lucy sitting right there, silent and watchful, he dared not say half of what he truly felt. They all sat down again, and for a long heavy moment, no one spoke. Marianne’s soft, tender gaze bounced between Elinor and Edward again and again, frustrated that Lucy’s unwelcome, uninvited presence was putting a damper on the joy they should all be sharing. Edward was the first to break the silence, commenting on Marianne’s paler looks, expressing his fear that London did not agree with her.
“Oh, never mind me!” Marianne replied, earnest and spirited even as tears welled up in her eyes. “Do not worry about my health. Elinor is well, you see. That is enough for both of us.”
The remark did nothing to ease Edward or Elinor’s discomfort, and it did not endear Marianne to Lucy, who looked up at her with a sharp, unkind glare.
“Do you enjoy London?” Edward asked, grasping for any new, safe topic to discuss.
“Not at all. I came here expecting so much joy, but I have found none. The only comfort this place has ever afforded me is seeing you, Edward. Thank heaven, you are exactly the same as you always were.”
She paused, and no one spoke.
“Elinor,” she added after a moment, “we must ask Edward to escort us back to Barton when we leave, in a week or two, I think. I am sure he will not mind taking charge of our journey home.”
Poor Edward mumbled some indistinct reply that none of them, not even he himself, could make sense of. Marianne mistook his agitation for shy delight at the thought of more time with Elinor, and perfectly satisfied, she turned to other topics.
“We had such a dreadful day at Harley Street yesterday, Edward! So dull, so wretchedly dull! But I have so much to tell you about it later, things I cannot say right now.” She held her tongue about how much she had hated his mother and Fanny Dashwood, wisely saving that rant for when they could speak privately. “But why were you not there? Why did you not come see us?”
“I was otherwise engaged,” Edward said.
“Engaged? But what trivial engagement could possibly matter more than being with us, your dearest friends?”
“Perhaps, Miss Marianne,” Lucy cut in sharply, eager to get a little revenge for Marianne’s obvious favouritism, “you imagine young men never bother keeping their engagements, no matter how small, if they do not feel like honouring them.”
Elinor burned with anger, but Marianne did not even feel the barb.
“Not at all,” she said, calmly earnest. “I am very sure Edward’s conscience is the most delicate, the most scrupulous I have ever known. He will never break a promise, no matter how small, no matter how much it goes against his own interest or pleasure. He would never hurt anyone’s feelings, never act selfishly for even a moment. That is the truth, Edward, and I will say it out loud. Why should you never get to hear how wonderful you are? If you will not accept my praise, then you are no friend of mine—anyone who wants my love and esteem must put up with me saying so out loud.”
Her glowing, unreserved praise landed horribly for two-thirds of the room. Lucy resented every word, and Edward was so overwhelmed by embarrassment, he jumped straight to his feet to leave.
“Going so soon?” Marianne protested. “Dear Edward, you cannot go yet!” She pulled him a little to one side, whispering that Lucy could not possibly stay much longer, that he had only to wait a few minutes and they would be free to talk. But even that encouragement was not enough. Edward was determined to leave, and no one could persuade him to stay. Lucy, who would happily have lingered for two more hours if he had, left the house not long after he did.
“The audacity of her, coming here so often!” Marianne huffed, the second the front door clicked shut behind Lucy. “Could she not see we wanted her gone? How cruel of her, to torment Edward like that!”
“Why cruel?” Elinor said softly. “We are all his friends, and Lucy has known him longer than any of us. It is only natural he should enjoy seeing her just as much as he enjoys seeing us.”
Marianne stared at her long and steadily.
“Elinor, that is the sort of evasive, false talk I cannot bear. If you are saying that only to make me contradict you, then you ought to remember I am the last person in the world to play that game. I will not trick myself into saying things I do not truly mean, just to humour you.” She turned and left the room before Elinor could say another word.
Elinor did not dare follow her. She was still bound by her solemn promise to Lucy to keep their engagement secret, so she could say nothing that would shatter Marianne’s happy, mistaken illusion that Edward was still free, still devoted to Elinor. Painful as it was to let her sister remain in the dark, she had no choice but to bear it. All she could hope was that Edward would not keep putting all three of them through the agony of such awkward, half-spoken meetings again soon—and there was every reason to believe that shared misery would be enough to keep them far apart for a long time to come.
