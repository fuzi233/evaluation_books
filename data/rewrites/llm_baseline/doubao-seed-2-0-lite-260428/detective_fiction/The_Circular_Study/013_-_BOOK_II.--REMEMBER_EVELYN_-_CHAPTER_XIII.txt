The room hung heavy with unspoken distrust. Unpremeditated tragedies like this did happen, after all; impulsive, uncharacteristic acts of violence had broken every rule of human nature since time began. But not a single man in that room, even those who had not witnessed Bartow’s bizarre pantomime at the crime scene — a performance that had never once lined up with this story of Felix Adams’s sudden, unprovoked suicide — failed to show clear, unmistakeable disbelief at the wild, voluble tale the young man had spilled in front of them.

Thomas Adams, sharp, sensitive to every shift in mood, felt that distrust settle over him like a cold shroud. His head drooped. “I won’t add a word to what I said, or take a word away,” he muttered doggedly. “Make of it what you will.”

The inspector leading the inquiry glanced dubiously over at veteran detective Mr. Gryce as the words left his lips. “We’re sorry you’ve taken that position,” Gryce said, leaning forward slow. “There are far too many loose ends left to tie up, Mr. Adams. For one thing: if your brother stabbed himself entirely out of nowhere, why did you bolt the house the second he fell, no call for help, no announcement that you were even related to him?”

“You don’t have to answer that,” the inspector cut in. “No man in this country is required to incriminate himself.”

The saddest smile any of them had ever seen tugged at the corners of the prisoner’s pale mouth for a heartbeat. He lifted his head, eyes blazing with raw desperation. “Incrimination is the last thing I fear right now. I can read the way all of you look at me. I’m lost, and I have no way at all to prove my story is true.”

The admission sounded like the final, despairing cry of an innocent man trapped by circumstance, and both interrogators paused. “You forget,” Gryce said, “your wife was there with you. She can corroborate every word, no doubt — she’d make an invaluable witness for your side.”

“My wife!” Thomas choked on the words, they were barely audible. “Must you drag her into this? She’s sick, she’s weak, it would kill her. She loves me, she —”

“Was she in your brother’s study? Did she see him lift that dagger to his own chest?”

“No.” The denial seemed to jolt a flicker of new courage into him. “She fainted minutes before the final act, right in the middle of our fight. She never saw him die. Gentlemen, I might as well tell you the full truth — I have nothing to gain by lying now. She finds it just as impossible to believe Felix stabbed himself as you all do. I’ve tried everything to make her understand the truth, but what hope do I have of the world believing me, when the woman I love more than anything — an angel, who would do no harm — she can’t bring herself to pretend what she saw wasn’t real. She’ll never testify for me. She’s too honest, too true to what she knows. I’d lose her, it would kill her —”

Mr. Gryce tried to interrupt, to calm him, but Thomas would not stop. “Spare her, sirs! Spare my wife! Write me down as guilty, call me whatever you want, just don’t force that poor girl to speak —”

The inspector cut his ragged, heart-rending pleas short. “Have you read the newspapers over the last few days?”

“I? Yes, of course I have,” Thomas snapped. “Blood is blood. He was my brother, I left him dying on that floor. I knew how much danger I was in, I couldn’t not read the papers.”

“Then you know he was found with a large wooden cross torn off the wall resting on his chest. Who tore it down? Who laid it there?”

“I did. I’m not Catholic, but Felix was. When I saw him dying without absolution, without his last rites, I thought of that cross on the wall, tore it down, and laid it in his arms.”

“Pious act. Did he recognize it?”

“I couldn’t tell you. My fainted wife was all I could think about.”

“I see. You carried her out, so focused on tending to her that you never noticed Mr. Adams’s valet —”

“He’s innocent! Whatever you all think, he had nothing to do with this death —”

“I say you never noticed him standing in that doorway watching you?” The inspector knew full well Bartow had not been in the doorway at all, but watching from the loophole high above the study, but the chance to trap the witness was too good to pass up.

Beads of cold sweat popped out on Thomas’s pale forehead, and he fumbled to keep his story straight. “That man is crazy, and deaf and dumb, like every paper says! His testimony is worthless. You wouldn’t really make me face him, would you?”

“We’re not confronting you with anyone. We asked a question. You never saw the valet, then?”

“No, sir.”

“Or the mystery of the colored lights strung around the study walls?”

“No, sir.”

“Or the steel plate in the floor, all the other strange contraptions your brother built to amuse himself in his solitude?”

“I don’t know what you’re talking about, sir.” But his tone had gone tight, defensive.

“Fine,” the inspector said. “None of the complications that have slowed our investigation down are part of the story you’ve told us. That’s possible, technically. But there’s one last small, critical detail you haven’t explained — the detail your wife mentioned to you the second she woke up after fainting. I’m talking about the half dozen words your brother scrawled on a scrap of paper right before he died. We found that scrap, and we know it was the root of your fight that night — your wife was even upset that you’d left it behind when you fled. Do you know where we found that paper?”

The eyes Thomas lifted to the inspector’s face were completely blank, all the fight drained right out of him. The mere mention of that scrap of paper had stripped every last shred of willpower he’d used to keep his lies afloat. He shook his head, reached out a hand to grab for the scrap the inspector held up, but his fingers went weak. He burst out in a raw, broken cry.

“Enough! I can’t keep living behind a wicked lie. I killed my brother — for reasons as good as any man has ever had to take another’s life. I won’t tell you what they are. You can try me for murder, hang me, I don’t care.”

It was a total, pitiful breakdown, all the more gutting against his broad, herculean frame and fine, sharp features. Mr. Gryce’s brow furrowed deep, and he stood slow to move beside the young man, who looked ready to collapse to the floor. The inspector stayed seated, drumming his fingers on the table, when Thomas suddenly froze, his whole frame shaking violently.

“What is that?” he gasped, staring hard at the door.

“Nothing,” the inspector started to say — but the door flew open.

A woman, pale as a wraith, glowing with a fierce, almost holy passion, tore herself free of the man who’d been trying to hold her back, and stumbled into the room. She was tiny, frail, her face made for soft smiles, but raw, unmistakeable human agony blazed in her eyes. It was Thomas’s young wife, the invalid he’d left three hours earlier at their home in Belleville.

“What is he saying?” she gasped, her voice trembling. “I know exactly what he’s saying. He’s trying to tell you he killed Felix, that he drove that dagger into a monster no one but us knew how wicked he was. But you can’t believe him. You can’t. He’s innocent. I rode twenty miles from my sick bed to tell you that. He —”

Thomas clapped a hand over her mouth, wild, frantic appeal in his eyes. She looked back at him, a look so full of love every man in the room felt it settle deep in his chest, then she gently pried his hand away.

“It’s too late, Tom. I have to speak. Not my father, not my own weakness, not your strictest orders could keep me locked up at Belleville when I found out they’d arrested you. I won’t be a coward now, not when these men have heard your false confession and might hang you for a crime you didn’t commit. That would be unworthy of the love we share, of the life we were supposed to build together.”

She turned to the group of men, every one of whom was holding their breath, waiting. “Sirs — I know my husband is innocent. Because the hand that drove that dagger into Felix Cadwalader’s chest was mine. I killed him.”

No one moved. The horror of the moment settled over the room slow, bleaching the color from every man’s face. Lips curved for soft words, faces built for gentle kindness, had just admitted to a brutal stabbing. No one stirred until a shout rang out from the doorway: “Catch her! She’s fainting!”

Thomas lunged forward, all the despair that had weighed him down for hours gone, all new strength in his limbs. He caught her as she crumpled, carried her gently to a nearby couch and laid her down. Mr. Gryce, who had watched every second of the spectacle with rapt attention, suddenly understood everything — that was why Thomas had panicked so hard when the cops showed up to arrest him, not him, the poor boy had thought they were coming for his wife, the center of his whole world.

Thomas stood over her, one hand brushing the hair back from her unconscious face. “Don’t you dare think this girl is evil,” he cried, his voice cracking. “There is no purer soul on this earth than hers. She did not kill him out of malice. I worship her — I would kiss her hands, her forehead, her feet, if it meant no harm ever came to her, even after what she did. Now — sirs — you have to listen. There is a terrible, unbelievable story I have to tell you.”
