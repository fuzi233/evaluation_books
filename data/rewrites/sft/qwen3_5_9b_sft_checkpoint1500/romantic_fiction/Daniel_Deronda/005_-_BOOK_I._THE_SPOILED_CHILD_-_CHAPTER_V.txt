"Gwendolen's arrival in the neighborhood proved exactly what her uncle had predicted. From the imposing Brackenshaw Castle down to the cozy Firs at Wanchester, where Mr. Quallon, the banker, maintained a hospitable household, she was greeted with a mixture of genuine admiration and social necessity. Even those who found her difficult to warm to felt a pragmatic comfort in hosting a new, dazzling figure; for hosts who entertain frequently must assemble their gatherings like political cabinets, prioritizing balance over personal affinity. To secure Gwendolen's presence, it was unnecessary to court anyone unpleasant, for Mrs. Davilow invariably served as a quiet, picturesque chaperon, and Mr. Gascoigne remained a constant draw wherever he went.

Yet, among the homes where Gwendolen was tolerated rather than loved, stood Quetcham Hall. An invitation to a large dinner there served as her formal introduction to the local elite. In a select gathering of thirty, carefully balanced across generations, it was impossible to exclude any respectable family. Nowhere else could a youthful figure rival Gwendolen's as she drifted through the long, flower-adorned suites. Visible at first as a slender silhouette floating in white drapery, she grew in definition as she passed from shadow into light. She had never experienced such a promenade before, and she felt a surge of exultation that it suited her perfectly; to any observer, the grand galleries and lacquered servants might have seemed as inevitable to her life as breathing. Meanwhile, her cousin Anna, who was genuinely more accustomed to such affairs, felt almost as bewildered as a rabbit suddenly dropped into a brightly lit cage.

"Who is that with Gascoigne?" asked the Archdeacon, abandoning a discussion of military maneuvers on which, as a clergyman, he was naturally expected to comment. Beside him, his son—a hopeful young scholar who had recently proposed "ingenious" emendations to Greek texts—exclaimed simultaneously, "By George! Who is that girl with the awfully well-set head and jolly figure?"

But for a mind of general benevolence, wishing everyone to appear their best, it was maddening to watch Gwendolen eclipse the rest. Even the handsome Miss Lawe, the daughter of Lady Lawe, seemed to shrink under her gaze, appearing suddenly broad, heavy, and lifeless. Similarly, Miss Arrowpoint, unfortunately clad in white, instantly resembled a *carte-de-visite* where the charge had been applied solely to the skirt. While Miss Arrowpoint was generally beloved for her amiable, unpretentious demeanor, which softened her mother's eccentricities, there appeared to be a distinct dissonance in Gwendolen projecting such an aura of social importance.

"She is not really so handsome if you examine her features closely," Mrs. Arrowpoint confided later to Mrs. Vulcany. "She possesses a certain style that creates a massive initial effect, but afterwards, she becomes far less agreeable."

Indeed, Gwendolen, without intending it, had offended her hostess. Though Mrs. Arrowpoint was neither splenetic nor vindictive, she possessed strong susceptibilities. Several elements converged in the Lady of Quetcham to create an impression that the neighbors' reasoning faculties found essential: she was the heir to a fortune amassed through dubious urban business, a fact cited to explain her squat figure, her harsh, parrot-like voice, and her systematically elevated head-dress. These external quirks naturally led observers to conclude she harbored literary tendencies. A closer scrutiny, however, would reveal that these traits existed independently; daughters of aldermen are often robust and beautiful, pretty women may possess husky voices, and the creation of mediocre literature is compatible with virtually any physical form.

Gwendolen, possessing a sharp eye for the absurd in others but a kind heart for anyone capable of making life pleasant, intended to win Mrs. Arrowpoint's favor. Yet, self-confidence often addresses an imaginary dullness in others. Just as the wealthy cajole the poor or the vigorous speak artificially to the elderly, believing them deaf or imbecile, Gwendolen, despite her cleverness and intent to please, fell into this trap. Unreflectingly, she concluded that because Mrs. Arrowpoint was ridiculous, she must also lack penetration. She proceeded through her little scenes unsuspecting that every nuance of her behavior was being meticulously observed.

"You are fond of books as well as music, riding, and archery, I hear," Mrs. Arrowpoint said, approaching her for a tête-à-tête in the drawing-room after dinner. "Catherine will be delighted to have so sympathetic a neighbor." This remark, delivered in a low, melodious tone, carried a fatal twang of patronization. Gwendolen responded gracefully, yet with the air of a benefactor:

"It is I who am fortunate. Miss Arrowpoint will teach me what good music is. I shall be entirely a learner. I hear that she is a thorough musician."

"Catherine has indeed enjoyed every advantage," Mrs. Arrowpoint replied. "We have a first-rate musician in the house now—Herr Klesmer; perhaps you know all his compositions? You must let me introduce him. You sing, I believe? Catherine plays three instruments but does not sing. I hope you will let us hear you. I understand you are an accomplished singer."

"Oh, no!" Gwendolen interrupted, quoting Mephistopheles. "'Die Kraft ist schwach, allein die Lust ist gross.'"

"Ah, you are a student of Goethe. Young ladies are so advanced now. I suppose you have read everything."

"No, really. I shall be so grateful if you tell me what to read. I have examined all the books in the library at Offendene, but nothing is readable. The pages stick together and smell musty. I wish I could write books to amuse myself, as you can! How delightful it must be to write books according to one's own taste instead of reading other people's! Home-made books must be so charming."

For an instant, Mrs. Arrowpoint's gaze sharpened, but the satirical edge of Gwendolen's final sentence dissolved into girlish simplicity when she added, "I would give anything to write a book!"

"And why not?" Mrs. Arrowpoint encouraged. "Pen, ink, and paper are within everyone's reach. But I will send you all I have written with pleasure."

"Thank you. I shall be so happy to read your works. Knowing an author must grant a unique understanding of their books; one could discern which parts are humorous and which serious. I am sure I often laugh in the wrong places." Gwendolen sensed danger and hurried to reassure her, "In Shakespeare, you know, and other great masters we can never fully see. But I always crave to know more than exists in the books."

"If you are interested in any of my subjects, I can lend you many extra sheets in manuscript," offered Mrs. Arrowpoint, as Gwendolen felt herself painfully positioned like the young lady who professes to love potted sprats.

"These are things I dare say I shall publish eventually," Gwendolen admitted. "Several friends have urged me, and one dislikes being obstinate. My Tasso, for instance—I could have made it twice the length."

"I dote on Tasso," Gwendolen declared.

"Well, you shall have all my papers if you wish. So many have written about Tasso, yet they are all wrong. Regarding the specific nature of his madness, his feelings for Leonora, the true cause of his imprisonment, and Leonora's character—who, in my view, was a cold-hearted woman, for otherwise she would have married him despite her brother—all current accounts are incorrect. I differ from everyone."

"How fascinating!" Gwendolen exclaimed. "I like to differ from everyone. It seems so tedious to agree. That is the worst aspect of expressing opinions; you force people to conform." This statement revived a slight suspicion in Mrs. Arrowpoint, whose gaze once more turned examining. However, Gwendolen appeared utterly innocent, continuing with a docile air, "I know nothing of Tasso save the *Jerusalem Delivered*, which we recited by heart at school."

"Ah, his life is more intriguing than his poetry. I have reconstructed his early years as a sort of romance. Considering his father Bernardo and the circumstances, there is much that must be true."

"Imagination is often truer than fact," Gwendolen stated decisively, though she could not explain these fluent words as easily as if they were ancient Egyptian. "I shall be so eager to learn all about Tasso—and his madness, particularly. I suppose poets are always a little mad."

"To be sure—" Mrs. Arrowpoint quoted, "'The poet's eye in a fine frenzy rolling.' And someone says of Marlowe, 'For that fine madness still he did maintain, Which always should possess the poet's brain.'"

"But it was not always discovered, was it?" Gwendolen asked innocently. "I suppose some rolled their eyes in private. Mad people are often very cunning."

A fleeting shadow crossed Mrs. Arrowpoint's face, but the arrival of the gentlemen precluded any immediate confrontation with this overly quick young lady, who had overplayed her *naïveté*.

"Ah, here comes Herr Klesmer," announced Mrs. Arrowpoint, rising. Soon she brought him to Gwendolen, leaving them to a dialogue that was agreeable on both sides. Herr Klesmer was a felicitous blend of the German, Slav, and Semitic, possessing grand features, artfully floating brown hair, and brown eyes behind spectacles.
