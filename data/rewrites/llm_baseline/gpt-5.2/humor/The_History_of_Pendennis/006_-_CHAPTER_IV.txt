Back at the George, Mr. Foker conducted his guest to the coffee-room with the air of a man bringing a foreign ambassador to view the national treasure-chests. Mr. Rincer, the waiter, entered as solemnly as if he bore the Great Seal upon a salver; he bowed over the dishes with a gravity that would have sat well upon a judge pronouncing sentence, and yet the sentence was only dinner.

Dinner, however, at the George was a thing with a pedigree. The turtle came on first—dark as old mahogany, glossy as a minister’s promise, breathing a warm vapour of Madeira and pepper that climbed straight into a young man’s brain. Then the venison followed, as if the forest itself had been persuaded—by fee and argument—to send one of its best haunches to be carved in an inn parlour. The knife went through it with that yielding resistance which tells you the beast was properly hung and not bullied into tenderness by heat. The gravy ran rich and brown, and the fat had that clean, nutty sweetness which makes you forgive all the crimes of man against deer.

Foker fell to with a gusto that would have done credit to an hereditary alderman, and with a knowledge which proved he had not lived last year wholly upon smuggled polonies and ginger-beer without acquiring, at least, courage. He sniffed, sipped, frowned like a Chancellor, and pronounced the champagne “condemned gooseberry,” as if it were a prisoner at the bar. He winked at the port with one eye, tried it again, and then nodded—an approving nod that passed from his palate to his whole person.

“This,” said he, “is the right sort. There’s no humbuggin’ me.”

He addressed the waiters by their Christian names, inquired after their families with the tenderness of a parish clergyman who knows the christenings and the scandals, and declared one man’s child had “a cough last week,” and another’s wife was “as well as ever.” All this philanthropy was performed between mouthfuls, while he cracked nuts and despatched fruit with the businesslike cheerfulness of one making up accounts.

Outside, the old London coaches—those lumbering, cheerful monsters of the road—rumbled to the door in the early dusk. Their ironwork clinked; the horses stamped and snorted, shaking the frost of the evening from their manes; the ostler’s whip snapped like a pistol-shot; and through it all came that rich acoustic stew of travel—horns tooting, guards calling, harness jingling, wheel-tires grating on stone. Foker flung open the coffee-room window as if he could not bear to have so much good sound shut out.

“Hi, Tom! How’s your missus?—Bill! how’s the little un?” he bawled, and then, to Pen’s amazement, imitated the horns with such liveliness and accuracy that one might have fancied the very coach-guards had crawled into his throat. Jem the ostler whipped off the horses’ cloths; the coaches rolled away with their lanterns bobbing like fireflies; and Foker, shutting the window, turned back to the table with the air of a man who has conducted an orchestra and now resumes supper.

“A bottle o’ sherry, a bottle o’ sham, a bottle o’ port, and a shass caffy,” he said, very well satisfied with the world and his own share of it. “It ain’t so bad, hay, Pen?”

When the last nut had been cracked and the last grape reduced to a stalk, he declared it was time “to toddle.” Pen sprang up with eyes too bright for mere digestion, cheeks warmed by wine and youth, and a heart beating to a rhythm it did not yet recognise as its own.

They made for the theatre and paid their money to a wheezy old lady asleep in the money-taker’s box—so asleep that she might have been a relic exhibited for the edification of playgoers, had she not presently awoke to take the coins with a mechanical snuffle.

“Mrs. Dropsicum,” Foker whispered, “Bingley’s mother-in-law. Great in Lady Macbeth.”

Of course he knew her too.

The house was thin—country theatres commonly resemble a family dinner where half the invited guests have remembered other engagements—despite the bills promising “universal bursts of attraction” and “galvanic thrills,” as if the audience were to be shocked into rapture by a battery. A score of people dotted the pit benches; the gallery contributed a continual undercurrent of kicking, whistling, and conversation, like sparrows disputing on a roof; and in the boxes lounged a dozen more who had drifted in on free admissions.

Lieutenants Rodgers and Podgers, with young Cornet Tidmus of the Dragoons, occupied a private box opposite. Their very boots seemed to applaud; they called actors by name as if addressing troops on parade; and between the dialogue they carried on friendly debates with the stage, as though the curtain were merely a piece of bunting hung to separate two clubs.

Bingley the manager—who took every chief tragic and comic part except when he modestly withdrew to make room for the occasional London star—appeared as the Stranger. He wore the legendary tight pantaloons and Hessian boots; a huge cloak; a beaver with a hearse-feather drooping over a face so raddled that it looked as if it had been basted nightly in lamp-smoke. A great buckled brown wig perched upon him, and upon his fingers he displayed the stage jewellery, selecting the largest rings for his own use with the impartial justice of a man dividing plunder. His favourite ornament was a sham diamond of such size that, when he let his little finger quiver out from the cloak, it seemed to wag at the pit like an argument.

He made a favour of wearing it in light comedy; the younger men in his company flattered him by asking its history. It had belonged, he declared, to George Frederick Cooke, who had had it from Mr. Quin, who might have purchased it for a shilling. Bingley’s belief in its splendour was as touching as any religion: he fancied the world enthralled by its glitter.

He held the stage-book—rouged, tawdry, and unbound in that peculiar fashion which no honest volume ever wears—pointed at lines, wagged his head, rolled his eyes at the ceiling as if literature and heaven were partners in the same concern. And when he saw the young gentlemen in the box, he did what managers always do when they scent money: he acted at them.

He lay upon the stage-bank so that his hand, his ring, and his Hessians were all presented as carefully as cold meat on a sideboard. Over the gilt volume he shot solemn glances, calculating the effect of each ornament on his victims. He was determined to fascinate; they had paid their money, and he could already see their families—actual or imagined—filling the cane chairs behind them in his boxes.

As he brooded, his servant Francis made remarks upon him in that theatrical dialect which no man ever uses in a kitchen.

“Again reading,” said Francis, “thus it is, from morn to night. To him nature has no beauty—life no charm. For three years I have never seen him smile.”

Bingley’s gloom at this point was of such professional blackness that it seemed to drink up the footlights.

“Nothing diverts him,” continued Francis. “O, if he would but attach himself to any living thing, were it an animal—for something man must love.”

Enter Tobias, from a hut—Goll performing him—giving thanks with caps between hands and eyes lifted to the flies as if the rafters contained Providence. The Stranger watched him as a cat watches a mouse with pretensions to poetry.

“This old man’s share of earthly happiness can be but little,” Francis said. “Yet mark how grateful he is for his portion of it.”

“Because though old, he is but a child in the leading-string of hope,” Bingley moaned, looking steadily—steadily—at Foker, who sucked the top of his stick with the unconcern of a young gentleman examining a bootlace.

“Hope is the nurse of life,” said Francis.

“And her cradle—is the grave,” replied the Stranger, with a sound like a bassoon in agony; and at that moment he fixed his eyes on Pendennis so unwaveringly that Pen felt the whole house must be staring at him too. He cast down his eyes in confusion; when he raised them, the Stranger’s were upon him again. Throughout the scene Bingley played in Pen’s direction, bent upon subjugating him; even when he sent off Francis with the book, he marked the page carefully, as if the stage-life of the volume mattered more than the moral purity of his benevolence.

Pen was never so relieved as when the scene ended and Foker, tapping with his cane, bellowed, “Bravo, Bingley!”

“Give him a hand, Pendennis,” Foker said. “You know every chap likes a hand.”

So Pen laughed, and clapped; and the dragoons clapped; and the thin house made noise enough for a coronation. Wintersen Castle shut like a trap over Tobias’s hut and the Stranger’s boots. Servants bustled on with chairs and tables.

“That’s Hicks and Miss Thackthwaite,” Foker whispered. “Pretty girl, ain’t she, Pendennis? But stop—hurray—bravo! here’s the Fotheringay!”

The pit thumped umbrellas; the gallery fired a volley of applause; the dragoons clapped till you’d have thought the theatre packed to the ceiling. Pen, still warmed with wine and dinner and the coach-horns’ jollity, felt something leap in him at that name, as if an old story—told long before he was born—had been suddenly remembered by his blood.

From the side-scene peered a red face with ragged whiskers: Mr. Costigan. Pen’s eyes opened wider, brighter, as Mrs. Haller entered.

She came on with a downcast look; then rallied at the sound, swept the house with a grateful glance, and folded her hands across her breast before sinking into a curtsey so magnificent that it seemed to bless the whole shabby theatre at once. Umbrellas thumped again. Pen clapped and cried “bravo” louder than all, in a heat that belonged as much to his youth as to his liquor.

Old Mr. Bows, the little first fiddler—perched at his desk with his crutch beside him, and reinforced tonight by a detachment of the Dragoons’ band (by permission of Colonel Swallowtail)—looked up and smiled at the lad’s enthusiasm.

Those who have only seen Miss Fotheringay in later days have little idea what a creature she was at that time. The pencil can draw an ugly face and be content; but beauty puts it out of countenance. She was tall—commandingly so—and in the full prime and splendour of it. Her forehead was broad; her black hair waved over it with a natural ripple that later beauties attempt with hot irons and patience; it was gathered into shining braids at the back of a neck like a statue’s—one of those antique necks that make you think even the air should be filtered before it touches them.

When she lifted her eyes they shone with a tenderness and a mystery that seemed to withdraw the instant you noticed it, as if Love and Genius had looked out from a window and then, ashamed of being seen, drawn the curtain. She seldom laughed—her teeth, indeed, were not good—but a smile of endless sweetness played about her lips, her dimples, her chin, making even stillness expressive. Her nose in those days defied description. Her ears, like little pearl shells, were insulted by the theatre earrings, though they were the handsomest properties available.

She wore long flowing black robes, and managed them with such grace that the folds seemed to obey her mood. Now and then you caught sight of her sandals—rather large, perhaps; but Pen, in his new intoxication, found them as ravishing as Cinderella’s slippers. Above all, her hand and arm—those superb instruments—seemed to frame her whole being. When she folded them over her breast, dropped them in mute agony, raised them in command, or fluttered them in sportive gaiety, it was as if she conducted the entire house with her gestures. She beckoned, repelled, entreated, embraced—not one admirer only, for she carried her virtue like armour, and her father’s valour stood ready at the side-scene—but the whole audience; and the whole audience rose to her, as the phrase was, when she bowed and curtseyed again.

Pen stared as if the lights had been lit for her alone.

“I say, Pen, isn’t she a stunner?” Foker whispered.

“Hush,” Pen said, scarcely knowing he spoke. “She’s speaking.”

Her voice was deep and sweet. The play itself—those who know it will confess—was stuffed with fine words that no sane person would use in a parlour or a field; the dialogue was as sham as the Stranger’s book and wig and ring. Yet, running through the balderdash, there is that reality which never grows old: love, children, forgiveness. However absurdly dressed, that truth will always get itself listened to, like good broth served in a cracked cup.

With what smothered sorrow, with what gushing pathos, did she utter her part! When, as housekeeper preparing for Count Wintersen’s arrival, she gave orders about beds and furniture and dinner, she did it with the calm agony of despair—speaking of domestic arrangements as if each chair placed were a nail driven in. Then, when the stupid servants were gone and she could pour out her grief to pit and boxes, she overflowed to each person as if he were her only confidant, as if the whole theatre were one private kitchen where she might confess her heart while the world’s kettles boiled.

Little Bows in the orchestra—whom she did not seem to watch, though he followed her ceaselessly with his bow and his whole twitching body—nodded, pointed, twisted like a man physically moved by music. When she came to the favourite passage—“I have a William too, if he be still alive—Ah, yes, if he be still alive. His little sisters, too! Why, Fancy, dost thou rack me so? Why dost thou image my poor children fainting in sickness, and crying to—to—their mum—um—other”—the little fiddler cried “Bravo!” and then buried his face in a blue cotton handkerchief.

All the house was affected. Foker took out a large yellow bandanna and wept piteously, the tears running down over the remains of turtle and venison as if supper and sorrow had agreed to share the same tablecloth. Pen was past tears. When she was off the stage, the stage and the world went blank; the red officers and the lamps reeled before him like objects seen through heat.

He watched her at the side-scene, where she waited to go on and where her father took off her shawl with the grave care of a man handling something precious that the public thinks it has bought for a shilling. When the reconciliation arrived and she flung herself upon Bingley’s shoulders—children clinging, Countess (Mrs. Bingley) and Baron Steinforth (Garbetts, lively and spirited) grouping themselves about—Pen saw only Fotheringay. The curtain fell upon him like a pall.

He did not hear Bingley’s announcement for the next night. He was hardly aware the house called for Miss Fotheringay. Bingley, of course, received the applause as usual as if it were his private income. At last even he understood, stepped back with a grin, and presently returned with Mrs. Haller upon his arm.

How beautiful she looked then! Her hair had loosened. The officers threw flowers; she clutched them to her heart as if they were a child brought back. She put back her hair, smiled all round; and her eyes met Pen’s. The moment was no longer part of the play: it was a sound in him, a chord struck that would vibrate long after the fiddles stopped.

Down went the curtain again. She was gone.

Not one note could Pen hear of the overture that the Dragoons’ brass blew afterwards with military goodwill. The very instruments, with their bright, brazen voices, seemed suddenly like an industrial intrusion—metal shouting where a human voice had just confessed. The theatre’s soundscape changed: from sobbing strings and murmured feeling to polished noise that filled space without filling the heart.

“She is a crusher, ain’t she now!” Foker asked.

Pen answered vaguely; he scarcely knew what was said to him. He could not tell another mortal what he felt, and indeed did not quite know it himself. It was something overwhelming, delicious, maddening—a fever of wild joy and undefined longing.

Rowkins and Miss Thackthwaite came on to dance the favourite double hornpipe; Foker abandoned himself to it with the same sincerity with which he had wept a few minutes before, applauding rhythm as if it were moral virtue. Pen scarcely saw the dance at all; it was mist before his eyes. He only remembered—dimly—that Mrs. Haller had acted with that woman when she first came in, as one remembers an ingredient in a dish that mattered solely because a beloved hand had touched it.

At the end of the dance, Pen looked at his watch and said it was time for him to go.

“Hang it, stay to see The Bravo of the Battle-Axe,” Foker pleaded. “Bingley’s splendid in it; he wears red tights, and has to carry Mrs. B. over the Pine-bridge of the Cataract, only she’s too heavy. It’s great fun—do stop.”

Pen looked at the bill with a lingering, foolish hope that Miss Fotheringay’s name might be hiding somewhere among the after-piece actors. It was not. Go he must. He had a long ride home.

He squeezed Foker’s hand, choking to speak and unable. He quitted the theatre and walked frantically about the town, not knowing how long—past silent shopfronts, past lanes where the day’s noises had died and only a distant cart creaked like an old memory. Then he mounted at the George and rode homeward through the night air, while the country’s quieter sounds—hedges whispering, hoofbeats dull on the road, the occasional far bark of a dog—worked upon him like a second play, older than any theatre, older than any printed bill.

Clavering clock sang out one as he came into the yard at Fairoaks. The lady of the house might have been awake; if she was, she heard only his step in the passage as he dashed into bed and pulled the clothes over his head.

Pen had not been in the habit of wakeful nights, so he fell into a sound sleep at once. Even later, when men have anxieties and thoughtful matter enough to keep them up, they begin by going to sleep as usual and get a nap in advance of trouble; but Trouble comes after, jogs them, and says, “No more of this laziness. Wake and talk with me.” Pen was not yet at that stage. He slept like a boy who thinks tomorrow belongs to him.

He did not wake until early morning, when the rooks began to caw from the little wood beyond his window; and at that instant—at the first opening of his eyes—the beloved image stood in his mind as if it had been waiting there all night.

“My dear boy,” he heard her say (for Love, when it enters, takes whatever voice it pleases), “you were in a sound sleep and I would not disturb you; but I have been close by your pillow all this while, and I don’t intend that you shall leave me. I am Love! I bring with me fever and passion: wild longing, maddening desire; restless craving and seeking. Many a long day ere this I heard you calling out for me; and behold, now I am come.”

Was Pen frightened? Not he. He did not know what was coming; it was all pleasure and delight as yet.

And as, three years before, when he entered the fifth form at the Cistercians, his father had given him a gold watch which he drew from under his pillow at waking—polishing it, listening to its ticking in corners, hugging it at the first moment of morning—so now he exulted over this new possession; felt for it in his waistcoat as if it were a thing he could lose; wound it up at night; and at waking seized it again with joy.

That first watch, by the way, was a showy ill-made piece; it never went well and was always out of order, until at last he put it in a drawer, forgot it, and swapped it away for something more useful. Love, too, often comes to us first in glittering, ill-manufactured splendour, and makes the strangest time of our lives—yet for the moment we think it the only true clock in the world.

Pen felt ever so many years older than yesterday. There was no mistake now. He was as much in love as the best hero in the best romance he had ever read.

He told John to bring his shaving-water with the utmost confidence, as if confidence were part of the toilette. He dressed in some of his finest clothes—fine pin, beautiful new waistcoat—and came splendidly down to breakfast, patronising his mother and little Laura, who had been strumming her music lesson for hours, creating those domestic noises which, in a quiet house, feel as powerful as machinery: repeated scales, stubborn bars, the metronome of duty.

He read the prayers—of which he did not heed one single syllable—and Laura, observing his grand appearance, wondered and asked him to tell her what the play was about.

Pen laughed and declined to tell Laura what the play was about. In fact it was quite as well she should not know.

Then she asked why he had got on his fine pin and new waistcoat.

Pen blushed, and told his mother that the old schoolfellow with whom he had dined at Chatteris was reading with a tutor at Baymouth—a very learned man; and as he himself was to go to College, and as there were several young men pursuing their studies at Baymouth—he was anxious to ride over and—and just see what the course of their reading was.

Laura made a long face. Helen Pendennis looked hard at her son, troubled more than ever by the vague doubt and terror that had haunted her since Farmer Gurnett brought home the news Pen would not return to dinner. Arthur’s eyes defied her.

She tried to console herself, to drive off her fears. The boy had never told her an untruth.

Pen, meanwhile, conducted himself during breakfast with a haughty, supercilious manner, as if Love had promoted him and he expected the household to salute. Taking leave of the elder and younger lady, he was presently heard riding out of the stablecourt. He went gently at first, but galloped like a madman the moment he thought himself out of hearing—flinging his hoofbeats at the road as if the very earth must be made to hurry.

Smirke the curate, thinking of his own affairs and riding softly with his toes out, on his way to give Pen his three hours’ reading, met his pupil coming like a gust. Pen shot by him like the wind. Smirke’s pony shied at the sudden thunder; the gentle man went over its head among the stinging-nettles in the hedge. Pen laughed as they met, pointed towards the Baymouth road, and was gone half a mile before poor Smirke had picked himself up, nettles stinging him into a most unwelcome wakefulness.

Pen had resolved he must see Foker that morning—must hear about her, know about her, be with someone who knew her. And Smirke, sitting up among the nettles while his pony cropped quietly in the hedge, thought dismally whether he ought to go to Fairoaks now that his pupil was evidently gone for the day.

Yes, he thought he might go too. He might go ask Mrs. Pendennis when Arthur would be back, and hear Miss Laura her Watts’s Catechism. He got upon the little pony—both were used to his slipping off—and advanced upon the house from which his scholar had just rushed away in a whirlwind.

Thus Love makes fools of all of us, big and little: the curate had tumbled head over heels in pursuit of it without knowing, and Pen had started in the first heat of the mad race.
