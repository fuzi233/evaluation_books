Now that the dust has settled, I suppose I might as well admit that there was a moment—just one little flicker of doubt—when I thought Jeeves might let me down. The man looked genuinely flummoxed. Which, given his usual air of having seen it all and understood it all before breakfast, was quite something.

Jeeves is my man, you understand. Officially, he gets paid weekly for pressing my shirts and keeping my socks from going astray. But in reality, he’s more like what that poet Johnnie called some bird who always came to the rescue when things got sticky—a guide, if I recall correctly; philosopher, possibly; and, I suspect, friend. I lean on him harder than a housewife leans on her ironing board.

So naturally, when Rockmetteller Todd burst into my life with this whole mess, I didn’t hesitate. Jeeves was brought in at the very beginning, like a secret weapon in a game of hide-and-seek.

The affair began one spring morning, early enough to make even a hardened sleeper like me feel slightly offended by daylight. I was still in bed, letting the good old tissues recover from whatever had happened the night before (probably just being alive), when the door flew open and someone jabbed me sharply in the ribs. Then came the vigorous shaking of the bedclothes. After blinking like a startled owl and slowly regaining consciousness, I identified the intruder: Rocky Todd.

Rocky lived somewhere down on Long Island, miles from New York, which was already suspicious enough. But worse—much worse—he’d told me himself, more than once, that he never woke up before noon, and often not until one o’clock. Constitutionally lazy, he’d found a career that allowed him to maximize his sloth: poetry. Or at least, he wrote poems occasionally. Most of the time, though, he seemed to be in a kind of trance-like state where he could sit on a fence watching a worm and ponder its purpose for hours on end.

He’d worked out a perfect system. Once a month, he’d write three days of poems. The other 329 days? Rest. I wasn’t sure poetry paid enough to support such an existence—even under Rocky’s standards—but apparently, if you stick to motivational verses about leading “the strenuous life” without adding any actual rhymes, American editors go mad for it. He showed me one once:

> Be! Be! The past is dead. To-morrow is not born.
> Be to-day! To-day! Be with every nerve,
> With every muscle,
> With every drop of your red blood! Be!

It was printed beside a picture of a half-naked fellow flexing his muscles while staring adoringly at the rising sun. Rocky said they paid him a hundred bucks for it—and he stayed in bed till four in the afternoon for over a month.

As for the future, he was set. He had an aunt with money tucked away in Illinois. Since he was named after her and was her only nephew, his inheritance was practically guaranteed. When he finally came into it, he said he’d do nothing but write occasional poems urging young men to light pipes and put their feet on the mantelpiece—preferably while wearing pyjamas.

And here he was, poking me in the ribs at dawn!

“Read this, Bertie!” he shouted, waving a letter like a banner. “Wake up and read it!”

I can’t read before tea and a cigarette. I reached for the bell.

Jeeves appeared, looking as fresh as a dew-kissed daisy. How he does it remains one of life’s mysteries.

“Tea, Jeeves.”

“Very good, sir.”

He glided out like a ghost made of silk. Meanwhile, Rocky was vibrating with urgency.

“What is it?” I mumbled.

“What on earth’s the matter?”

“Read it!”

“I can’t. Not without tea.”

“Well, listen then.”

“Who’s it from?”

“My aunt.”

At that, I fell back into sleep again. I woke to hear him saying:

“So what on earth am I supposed to do?”

Jeeves returned with the tray, flowing in like a quiet stream over moss-covered stones. Light dawned.

“Read it again, Rocky,” I said. “I want Jeeves to hear it. Mr. Todd’s aunt has sent him a rather peculiar letter, Jeeves. We need your advice.”

“Very good, sir.”

He stood rigid in the center of the room, radiating devotion. Rocky began:

> MY DEAR ROCKMETTELLER.—I have been thinking things over for a long time, and I’ve decided I’ve been very thoughtless to wait so long before doing what I’ve now resolved to do.

“What do you make of that, Jeeves?”

“It seems somewhat obscure at present, sir, but no doubt clarity will emerge later.”

“It becomes as clear as mud!” Rocky snapped.

“Proceed, old chap,” I said, chewing my toast.

“You know how all my life I’ve dreamed of visiting New York and seeing the wonderful gay life I’ve read so much about. I fear it will now be impossible. I am old and worn out. I seem to have no strength left.”

“Sad, Jeeves?”

“Extremely, sir.”

“Sad nothing!” Rocky retorted. “It’s sheer laziness. I saw her last Christmas—she was bursting with health. Her doctor told me herself she was perfectly fine. But she insists she’s a hopeless invalid, so he agrees. She’s convinced the trip would kill her. So, though it’s been her lifelong ambition to come here, she stays put.”

“Like the chappie whose heart was ‘in the Highlands a-chasing of the deer,’ Jeeves?”

“The cases are in some respects parallel, sir.”

“Carry on, Rocky.”

“So I’ve decided that if I can’t enjoy New York myself, I’ll enjoy it through you. Yesterday, after reading a beautiful poem about a man who longed for something all his life and finally got it—only too late to enjoy it—I was deeply moved.”

“A thing,” Rocky added bitterly, “I haven’t managed in ten years.”

“As you know, you’ll inherit my money when I’m gone. Until now, I’ve never seen fit to give you an allowance. But I’ve decided to do so—on one condition. I’ve instructed a firm of lawyers in New York to pay you a substantial sum each month. My only condition is that you live in New York and enjoy yourself—exactly as I’ve always wished to do. I want you to be my representative, spending this money as I would. I want you to plunge into the glittering, prismatic life of New York. I want you to be the life and soul of dazzling supper parties. Above all, I insist that you write me letters at least once a week—detailed, full descriptions of everything you do and see—so I may enjoy it second-hand. Remember: no detail is too trivial. —Your affectionate Aunt,

“What about it?” Rocky asked.

“What about it?” I repeated.

“Yes. What on earth am I supposed to do?”

Only then did I realize just how absurdly ungrateful the man was. Here he was, suddenly handed a fortune from the sky, and instead of leaping for joy, he looked like he’d just been told he had to attend a Sunday school picnic.

“Aren’t you thrilled?” I asked.

“Thrilled?”

“If I were you, I’d be bouncing off the ceiling. This is pure gold for you.”

He let out a yelp, stared at me, then launched into a rant that reminded me of Jimmy Mundy, the reformer who’d come to New York to expose its sins. Only Rocky made Jimmy sound like a tourist brochure.

“Pure gold?” he shrieked. “To have to move to New York! To leave my cozy cottage and cram myself into a stuffy, smelly, overheated apartment in this festering pit of a city! To mix night after night with people who think life is a St. Vitus’s dance and believe they’re having fun because they’re louder than six people and drunker than ten! I hate New York, Bertie. I wouldn’t set foot here if I didn’t have to see editors. It’s cursed. It’s got moral delirium tremens. It’s the worst. Just the thought of staying more than a day makes me ill. And you call this *soft*?”

I felt like Lot’s friends when they dropped in for tea and their host started criticizing Sodom and Gomorrah. I never knew Rocky could be so eloquent.

“It would kill me to live here,” he went on. “To breathe the same air as six million people! To wear stiff collars and decent clothes all the time! To——” He stopped. “Good Lord! I suppose I’d have to dress for dinner. What a ghastly idea!”

I was appalled.

“My dear chap,” I said reproachfully, “do you dress for dinner every night?”

“Jeeves,” I said coldly. The man was still standing like a statue by the door. “How many evening suits do I own?”

“We have three full suits of evening dress, sir; two dinner jackets——”

“Three.”

“For practical purposes, two only, sir. The third cannot be worn. We also have seven white waistcoats.”

“And shirts?”

“Four dozen, sir.”

“And white ties?”

“The first two shallow shelves in the chest of drawers are completely filled with our white ties, sir.”

I turned to Rocky.

“You see?”

He writhed like a fan caught in a storm.

“I won’t do it! I can’t do it! I’ll be hanged if I do! How can I dress like that? Do you realize I don’t get out of my pyjamas until five in the afternoon, and then I just throw on an old sweater?”

I saw Jeeves wince. Poor fellow. That sort of revelation wounded his deepest sensibilities.

“So what are you going to do?” I asked.

“That’s what I want to know.”

“You could write your aunt and explain.”

“I could—if I wanted her to send her lawyer to cut me out of the will.”

I saw his point.

“What do you suggest, Jeeves?” I asked.

Jeeves cleared his throat respectfully.

“The crux of the matter, sir, appears to be that Mr. Todd is required, under the terms of the allowance, to write detailed letters describing his experiences. If he intends to remain in the country, the only way to fulfill this requirement is for a second party to gather those experiences and report them to him. He could then use these reports as the basis for his correspondence.”

Having delivered this, Jeeves fell silent. Rocky looked at me helplessly. He hadn’t grown up with Jeeves, so he didn’t grasp the man’s subtleties.

“Could he say that clearer, Bertie?” he asked. “It sounded promising at first, but then it kind of fizzled. What’s the plan?”

“My dear old man, it’s simple. We can rely on Jeeves. All you need is someone to go around New York, take notes, and send them to you. Then you write the letters based on those notes. That’s it, isn’t it, Jeeves?”

“Precisely, sir.”

A spark of hope lit up Rocky’s eyes. He stared at Jeeves, stunned by the man’s genius.

“But who would do it?” he asked. “It’d have to be a sharp fellow—someone who notices things.”

“Jeeves!” I said. “Let Jeeves do it.”

“But would he?”

“You would do it, wouldn’t you, Jeeves?”

For the first time in our long history, I saw Jeeves almost smile. His lips curved a quarter-inch. His eye lost its fish-like meditativeness.

“I should be delighted to oblige, sir. In fact, I have already visited several of New York’s attractions during my evening walks. It would be most enjoyable to continue.”

“Perfect!” I said. “I know exactly what your aunt wants to hear. Cabaret life. First stop: Reigelheimer’s, Forty-second Street. Anyone can show you the way.”

Jeeves shook his head.

“Pardon me, sir. People are no longer going to Reigelheimer’s. The place now is Frolics on the Roof.”

“You see?” I said to Rocky. “Leave it to Jeeves. He knows.”

We were all blissfully happy. Jeeves loved exercising his mind and enjoying the bright lights. I saw him one night at the Midnight Revels, sitting at a table near the dance floor, smoking a fat cigar and sipping a bottle of the finest. He looked almost human—his face wore an expression of serene benevolence, and he was making notes in a small book.

I was happy too—because I liked Rocky and enjoyed helping him. Rocky was content—still able to watch worms in his pyjamas. And the aunt? She seemed delighted. She was getting Broadway second-hand, but it was hitting her right.

Rocky’s letters, based on Jeeves’s reports, were electrifying. It was odd, really. I loved New York, but just mentioning it made Rocky shudder. Yet here’s a letter I wrote to a friend in London:

> DEAR FREDDIE,—Well, here I am in New York. It’s not bad. I’m not having a bad time. Everything’s pretty all right. The cabarets aren’t bad. Don’t know when I’ll be back. How’s everybody? Cheer-o!—Yours,
> P.S.—Seen old Ted lately?

Not that I cared about Ted. But if I hadn’t mentioned him, I couldn’t have gotten the letter onto the second page.

Now here’s Rocky’s letter on the same subject:

> DEAREST AUNT ISABEL,—How can I ever thank you enough for giving me the chance to live in this astonishing city! New York grows more wonderful every day. Fifth Avenue is at its best. The dresses are magnificent!

Then pages and pages of fashion commentary—something I never knew Jeeves was capable of.

“I was out with some of the crowd at the Midnight Revels the other night. We had dinner at a new place on Forty-third Street, then took in a show. Georgie Cohan dropped in at midnight and gave a great speech about Willie Collier. Fred Stone stayed only a minute, but Doug. Fairbanks did amazing stunts and made us roar. Diamond Jim Brady was there, as usual. Laurette Taylor showed up with a party. The show at the Revels is excellent. I’m enclosing a programme. Last night we went to Frolics on the Roof——

And so on. Clearly, the artistic temperament helps. It’s easier for someone used to writing poetry to spice up a letter than for someone like me. Still, Rocky’s correspondence was hot stuff. I called Jeeves in.

“Jeeves, you’re a wonder!”

“Thank you, sir.”

“How you notice everything beats me. I couldn’t tell you a thing about those places except that I had a good time.”

“It’s just a knack, sir.”

“Well, Mr. Todd’s letters ought to cheer Miss Rockmetteller up, don’t you think?”

“Undoubtedly, sir.”

And they did! They certainly did! About a month later, I was lounging in the apartment, smoking, when the door opened and Jeeves’s voice cut through the silence like a bombshell.

Not loud—Jeeves has a soft, soothing voice that drifts through the air like a distant sheep’s bleat. But what he said made me jump like a startled gazelle.

“Miss Rockmetteller!”

And in walked a large, solid woman.

I was stunned. I’d come to see Rocky’s aunt as a permanent fixture in Illinois. Seeing her in New York was like finding a glacier in a swimming pool.

She looked less like an invalid than anyone I’d ever met—except perhaps my Aunt Agatha. She had a lot of Aunt Agatha about her, actually. She looked like someone who could snap a man’s neck if provoked. And I suddenly realized she’d consider herself provoked if she ever found out about the ruse.

“Good afternoon,” I managed.

“How do you do?” she said. “Mr. Cohan?”

“Er—no.”

“Mr. Fred Stone?”

“Not absolutely. As a matter of fact, my name’s Wooster—Bertie Wooster.”

She seemed disappointed. The name “Wooster” meant nothing to her.

“Isn’t Rockmetteller home?” she asked. “Where is he?”

She had me with the first question. I couldn’t say he was in the country watching worms.

There was a faint cough—the respectful signal that Jeeves was about to speak.

“If you remember, sir, Mr. Todd went out in the automobile with a group this afternoon.”

“So he did, Jeeves,” I said, checking my watch. “Did he say when he’d return?”

“He indicated, sir, that he would be somewhat late.”

Jeeves vanished. The aunt sat down in the chair I’d forgotten to offer. She looked at me with a strange, unpleasant expression. It made me feel like a stray dog she planned to bury later. My Aunt Agatha had given me that look many times. It always made my spine curl.

“You seem very comfortable here, young man. Are you a close friend of Rockmetteller’s?”

“Oh, yes, rather!”

She frowned as if she’d expected better of Rocky.

“Well, you must be,” she said, “the way you treat his flat as your own!”

I was speechless. I’d seen myself as the charming host. Now I was being treated like a trespasser. Not just a visitor—more like a plumber who’d wandered in by mistake. It hurt.

At that moment, an idea struck me: tea. The eternal lifeline.

“Would you care for a cup of tea?” I asked.

“Tea?”

She spoke as if she’d never heard of the drink.

“Nothing like a cup after a journey,” I said. “Bucks you up! Gives you a bit of zip. Restores you, you know. I’ll go tell Jeeves.”

I stumbled down the hall to Jeeves’s domain. He was reading the paper as if nothing mattered.

“Jeeves,” I said, “we need tea.”

“Very good, sir.”

“I say, Jeeves, this is awkward, what?”

I needed sympathy. The nerves were shot.

“She thinks this place belongs to Mr. Todd. Where did that idea come from?”

Jeeves filled the kettle with dignified calm.

“No doubt because of Mr. Todd’s letters, sir,” he said. “It was my suggestion, sir, that they be addressed from this apartment to give the impression he lives here.”

I remembered. We’d thought it clever at the time.

“Well, it’s bally awkward, you know. She sees me as an intruder. By Jove! She probably thinks I’m a free-loader, borrowing his shirts and eating his food.”

“Yes, sir.”

“It’s rotten, you know.”

“Most disturbing, sir.”

“And there’s another thing: What about Mr. Todd? We’ve got to get him here immediately. When you bring the tea, go out and send him a telegram—next train.”

“I’ve already done so, sir. I wrote the message and dispatched it via the lift attendant.”

“By Jove, you think of everything, Jeeves!”

“Thank you, sir. Buttered toast with the tea? Just so, sir. Thank you.”

I returned to the living room. She hadn’t moved. Still rigid, gripping her umbrella like a weapon. She gave me another of those looks. For some reason, she clearly disliked me. Probably because I wasn’t George M. Cohan. It was hard on a chap.

“This is a surprise, what?” I said after a few minutes of silence, trying to revive the conversation.

“What is a surprise?”

“Your coming here, don’t you know.”

She raised her eyebrows and studied me through her glasses.

“Why is it surprising that I should visit my only nephew?”

That made sense.

“Oh, rather,” I said. “Of course. What I mean is——”

Jeeves entered with the tea. I was glad to see him. Business saved me.

“Tea, tea, tea—what? What?” I said.

Not what I’d intended. But it worked. I poured her a cup. She sipped it, then set it down with a shudder.

“Do you mean to say,” she said frostily, “that you expect me to drink this?”

“Rather! Bucks you up, you know.”

“What do you mean by ‘bucks you up’?”

“Well, makes you full of beans. Makes you fizz.”

“I don’t understand a word. You’re English, aren’t you?”

And so it went. The situation remained tense, but at least the tea was served.
