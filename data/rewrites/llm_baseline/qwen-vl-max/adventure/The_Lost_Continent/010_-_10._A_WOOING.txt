A murmur rose around me, quickly swelling into shouts. “Kneel,” one voice hissed, “kneel, sir, or you’ll be seen!” Another roared, “Kneel, you without beard, and bow before the only Goddess—or by the old Gods, I’ll make myself her priest and cut you down!” The cry grew into a thunderous roar.

But then the name “Deucalion” began to echo through the crowd, and the fervor softened. Deucalion—man who had left Atlantis twenty years ago to rule Yucatan—they knew little of that man. But Deucalion—the one who had ridden beside the Empress just days before in the golden castle beneath the canopy of snakes—that was a name they remembered well. And when they considered what he might do if provoked, their voices fell silent, as though struck dumb by fear.

When the noise had died and Phorenice turned to see me standing alone among the bowed worshippers, I stepped forward from the throng, passed between two great stones, and walked across the circle to where she stood beside the altar. I did not kneel. At the distance prescribed, I gave the salute she herself had ordered when she made me her chief minister, then addressed her formally as Empress.

“Deucalion, man of ice,” she replied.

“I still serve the old Gods.”

“I wasn’t speaking of that,” she said, glancing at me with a sidelong smile.

Then Ylga approached, pale-faced, trembling, pleading for my life. “If he won’t abandon the old Gods yet,” she begged, “surely you can spare him? He is strong, and conversion does not come easily. You may change him later. But consider, Phorenice—he is Deucalion. If you kill him now over this, there is no other man within all Atlantis who could serve so well—”

The Empress cut her off. “You slut,” she snapped. “I keep you near me to choose my wardrobe, carry my fan—do you dare meddle in my affairs? Back out of this circle, or I’ll have you whipped. And worse—I’ll treat you as Zaemon treated my captain, Tarca. Shall I point a finger and curse your fair skin with sudden leprosy?”

Ylga bowed her head and retreated, humbled. Phorenice turned back to me. “My lord,” she said, “I am like a young bird that has suddenly found its wings. Wings have so many uses—I wish to try them all.”

“May each flight bring good to Atlantis.”

“Oh,” she said, eyes flashing, “I know exactly what you care about most. But let us return to the pyramid and speak at leisure. For now, my lord, guide me back to my riding beast.”

It seemed I would be spared punishment for refusing to worship her, thus publicly challenging her divine status. It also seemed Ylga’s interference had been ill-timed—and though I didn’t grasp why, I accepted it as part of the path laid out by Zaemon. So when the Empress offered me her hand—warm, delicate fingers, though skilled in war—I took it solemnly and led her from the great circle, which she had defiled with her pretense. I expected the Sun to strike down the sacrilege while it was still ongoing—but nothing came. I knew He would act in His own time, with His own instrument. No need for me to raise a weak arm to defend His honor.

So I guided this beautiful, sinful woman back to the massive red mammoth waiting tamely nearby, the scent of sacrifice lingering behind us. She climbed the steps to the golden castle perched on the beast’s shaggy back, and bid me mount beside her. But the spot behind her, meant for the fan-girl, remained empty. What we spoke as we rode through the city streets, no one else heard.

She was eager to know what had happened after the attack on the gate. I told her, emphasizing Nais’s courage and intelligence, suggesting with care that she might yet be won back to loyalty. But I kept silent about Zaemon’s commands—the sacred words spoken in the tongue of the priests—since such matters are not repeated except in the High Council before the Ark of Mysteries.

“You seem oddly kind to this rebel girl,” Phorenice remarked.

“She showed more wit and thought than most.”

“Ay,” she sighed, and I sensed the truth in it, “an Empress loses much that ordinary women take for granted.”

“In what way?”

“She misses the honest courtship of equals.”

“If you claim to be a Goddess—” I began.

“Pah! I don’t want to be a Goddess to you, Deucalion. That was for the people—to give me power, to help my plans. All seven of us higher priests know how to summon fire. I simply stole the trick. Can’t you be generous? A woman may be as clever as your ancient priests at discovering nature’s laws.”

“You remain Empress.”

“Nor even that. Just think—there’s a woman sitting beside you on this cushion, Deucalion. Look at her. What comes first to your lips? Forget ceremony. Forget statecraft. Do you mean to wait until your manhood fades? It’s wise not to rush—yes, I agree. But isn’t it foolish to watch a fruit ripen, then leave it until it spoils?”

I looked at her radiant beauty—but it stirred nothing within me. Yet I recalled Zaemon’s command, and forced a smile. “I may have been choosy,” I said, “but I don’t regret waiting so long.”

“Nor I. But I’ve lived long enough as a maid. I am a woman—ripe, full-blooded. The time has come for me to be more than I’ve been.”

I clasped her hand tightly. “Then take me as husband. I will be a good man to you. But since I speak now to Phorenice the woman, not the Empress, I must warn you—I will not be a puppet.”

She glanced at me sideways. “I’ve ruled long enough. I think I’d enjoy being ruled sometimes. No, Deucalion—I promise you won’t be a puppet. Indeed, it would take a mighty lung to pipe you into dancing against your will.”

“So we shall live together in the royal pyramid, ruling this land with all the wisdom the High Gods have given us. These petty conflicts shall end. Rebels shall return to their homes, hunt beasts in the provinces, and the Priests’ Clan shall be calmed. Phorenice, you and I will govern with every ounce of our minds, and make Atlantis rise again—greater than any nation, known for peace and prosperity.”

She pulled her hand away sharply. “Oh, your conditions, your Atlantis! You carry such crude colonial manners, Deucalion. They grow tiresome after the first blunt taste. Am I to do all the wooing? Is there no spark of love beneath your coldness?”

“In truth, I do not know what love is. I’ve had little talk with women in all these busy years.”

“We were alike when you arrived,” she said. “I’ve heard sighs and vows from every bearded man in Atlantis. Some pleased me for a day—but none touched me deeper. No, I haven’t learned love from my own heart either. But tell me, sir—will you teach me soon, if you keep up this chill?”

“From what I’ve seen, love belongs to the poor, the weak, those with fleeting emotions.”

“Then I wish another woman were Empress, and I were some ragged creature from the gutter—a strong man could snatch me up by force and carry me home for pure passion. Ah! How I’d revel in it! How I’d respond if he seized my fancy!” She laughed. “But I’d lead him a wretched life if my desire weren’t stronger than his.”

“We are shaped by fate. We cannot change what stirs us.”

She stared at me, sullen. “If I don’t awaken your heart, my Deucalion, there will be trouble brewing for this Atlantis you hold so dear. There will be chaos in our household if my love grows, and yours remains dead.”

I believe she would have made me caress her right there, atop the golden castle on the mammoth’s back, before the curious crowds. She craved spectacle, not privacy. But I couldn’t. The gods know I was committed to my task—and equally repelled by it. Yet I was a true priest that day. I set aside all personal feeling to fulfill the Council’s decree. If I had known how, I might have matched her mood. But where others at court would have slipped into flattery effortlessly, I faltered—lacking even a dozen suitable words.

There was no choice but to say only what I truly felt. Diplomacy was second nature; on most matters, I could speak smoothly. But charm with women was something I had never cultivated—and if I’d invented hollow compliments, Phorenice would have seen through them instantly. She had spent years feeding on such lies, and judged their worth with expert precision.

And honesty wouldn’t have helped. To lay bare my position plainly would have worsened things. So I stayed silent, accepting the role of fool.

“I always thought,” she said, “that your colonists in Yucatan were ahead of Egyptians in art and grace. But you, sir, do little credit to your rank. Why, I’ve had nobles from the Nile visit here—some of them almost seemed never to have left their homeland.”

“They must have advanced greatly in twenty years,” I replied. “Last time I reported from Egypt, the blacks clearly ruled, and our people lived only by sufferance. Their pyramids were puny, their cities mere forts.”

“Oh,” she said mockingly, “they’re still exiles—but they remember their manners. My face pleased them. They went mad over it. One even cut off his own right hand for ten pleasant words. We made a bargain, my Egyptian gallant and I. His hand dries on a shelf in my room now—a charming keepsake.”

But fortune intervened. Outside the walls, the rebels launched a fierce assault. Several times during our ride, light projectiles from their engines whistled overhead, striking buildings and sending splinters that wounded bystanders. Still, it was nothing to alarm the seasoned. Our courtship continued undisturbed. Then, suddenly, the rebels stopped hurling stones and began loading their engines with wooden casings filled with throwing fire.

Against stone, these caused little damage—except to anyone caught in the blast, who would be scorched horribly. But when they hit the wooden stalls and reed shelters of the poor, they ignited instantly. There was no stopping the flames.

Such events were common in sieges, and neither of us gave them much thought—until the mammoth reacted. When the first blue smoke drifted toward us through the winding streets, the great red beast raised its trunk and began swaying uneasily. As the smoke thickened and flickers of flame appeared, it halted abruptly and began to trumpet.

Its guards tugged hard on the chains attached to the jagged metal collar around its neck, the spikes gouging deep into flesh. But the beast’s primal fear of fire—deep-rooted from centuries of hunting and being hunted—overpowered its training. Men had long used fire to protect themselves from mammoths. Mammoths had learned to dread it as their deadliest enemy.

Phorenice’s brow darkened as the animal grew wilder. She shook her red curls violently. “Someone will lose their head for this blunder,” she snapped. “I ordered this beast trained to ignore drums, shouting, arrows, stones, and fire. The trainers swore it was done. They brought proof.”

I unfastened my belt. “Here,” I said, “quick—let me lower you to the ground.”

She turned, eyes gleaming. “Afraid for my neck, Deucalion?”

“I don’t intend to be widowed before I’ve tasted married life.”

“Pish! There’s little danger. I’ll stay. I’m no timid woman. But go if you wish.”

“There’s no chance now.”

Blood seeped from the mammoth’s neck where the collar’s spikes tore it—and with each drop, its obedience vanished. With wild shrieks and thunderous trumpetings, it turned and charged back the way it came, trampling spearmen like straw, cutting a swath through the crowd. Many must have been crushed, many killed by its trunk—but only their cries reached us. The golden castle swayed violently, threatening to throw us off like stones from a catapult.

I braced my feet against the front, wrapped one arm around a pillar, and clutched Phorenice with the other—using my body as a shield.

She lay calmly, her lovely face beneath my chin, the faint scent of her hair filling my breath. The mammoth charged wildly through narrow streets, heedless of direction. We had escaped the smoke, but the beast was lost in panic. It raced on with enormous strides, trunk raised, deafening us with screams. We left the glittering procession far behind, carried helplessly through the city’s wards.

No one could control it. Its speed left no option but to cling to the castle. Up there, we were safe. To leap off—even if we survived—would have doomed us. The mammoth would have scented us immediately, turned (as its kind do), and crushed us in seconds.

A thought struck me: perhaps this was the High God’s judgment. The mammoth was His instrument—meant to dash her to pieces. And I, His priest, was to witness justice fulfilled. But no direct sign came. So I held on, protecting her from bruise and fall.

The beast veered wildly, confused by the twisting streets. Gradually, we left the grand palaces and pyramids behind, entering the artisan quarters. Weavers and smiths gaped from doorways as we thundered past. Then we reached the merchants’ district, where men lived above their warehouses, trading with foreign lands. Ahead, an open space shimmered with water.

“Now,” I thought, “this mad beast will stop suddenly—perhaps turn and charge back.” I braced myself, gripping her tighter. But louder screaming, wilder trumpetings—the mammoth kept straight on, reaching the harbor’s edge. With clumsy steps, it plunged into the water, sending spray flying in the sunlight.

Then its pace slowed. The harbor received the city’s sewers—drainage from ancient times, carrying solid waste that settled into a thick, invisible sludge. The beast sank deeper and deeper, unable to halt. With terrified bellows, it came to a standstill—bogged irretrievably.

It struggled madly, screamed, cried out. Water and slime churned into a foul stew. The golden castle lurched violently—we were thrown from it and shot into the water.

Yet we were safe. I welcomed the end of the jolting.

Phorenice laughed as she swam. “You handle yourself like a true man, Deucalion. I owe you thanks for using your body as a cushion. By my face! There’s more gallantry in you under pressure than one would guess from your words. How was the ride, sir? I imagine it was new to you.”

“I’d prefer to walk.”

“Pish! You’ll never be a courtier. You should have sworn that, with me in your arms, you wished the bumping had lasted forever. Ho, boat there! Hold your arrows. Deucalion, hail those fools! Tell them—if they harm a hair of my mammoth, I’ll torture them all. He’ll tire soon. Then we’ll leave him where he is to reflect on his folly, then haul him out with windlasses and tame him again. Pho! I couldn’t be Phorenice without a fine, red, shaggy mammoth for my rides.”

The boat was a ten-slave galley, churning from the far side of the harbor. At my shout, the soldiers on deck ceased firing, and the steersman altered course to rescue us. We had been swimming carefully to avoid the sewer outflow—but now we stopped, treading water, and were lifted aboard by respectful hands.

The galley belonged to the port captain—a foppish sailor whose only ambition was to grovel before greatness. He immediately prostrated himself, wishing his eyes had been blinded before seeing the Empress in peril.

“The danger may pass,” she said. “Nothing mortal will ever kill me. But I’ve ruined my clothes and lost a jewel or two. That’s annoying, as you say, good man.”

He repeated his wish to be blinded.

But she grew weary of flattery. “If you’re tired of your eyes,” she said, “let me tell you—you’re headed for having them plucked from their sockets. Kill my mammoth because he acted playfully? You and your kind need education. I’ll teach you: port captains are easy to replace, worthless once obtained. But my mammoth—mine, do you understand?—is the property of Goddess Phorenice. Sacred.”

The captain bowed low. “I am ignorant,” he said, “and heaven lost its brightest star when Phorenice descended to Atlantis. But if I may make amends—I have two prisoners in the cabin. They shall be sacrificed to the mammoth. Doubtless it will find sport in them, spill its rage upon their bodies.”

“Prisoners?” she asked. “How taken?”

“Under cover of night, they tried to slip between the two forts guarding the harbor mouth. Their boat snagged the chain. The sentries saw them by torchlight. They were captured with ropes and thrown in a dungeon. There’s an order not to abuse prisoners before trial?”

“It was my order. Did they offer news for their lives?”

“The man hasn’t spoken. I think he died during capture. The woman fought like a cat, they said. But she was caught unharmed. She claims she has nothing useful to tell. She says she’s tired of living like a savage outside the city—and that inside, there’s a man she craves deeply.”

“Tut!” Phorenice said. “Is this a romance we’ve swum to? See how affectionate we women are, Deucalion.”—The galley docked at the royal quay and fastened to golden rings. I helped the Empress ashore, but she turned back to the boat, her clothes still dripping.—“Bring out your woman prisoner, captain. Let us see if she’s a runaway wife or a lovesick girl. Then I’ll judge her—and likely surprise you all with mercy. I’m in a romantic mood today.”

The captain entered the small cabin, face pale. “The man appears dead, Your Majesty. His wounds—”

“Bring out the woman, fool. I asked for her. Keep the corpse where it is.”

I saw him reach for his knife to untie a rope—and then, stepping into daylight, was the girl I had saved from cave-tigers in the circus—the one who had drawn me strangely during our brief companionship. Clearly, Phorenice recognized her too. She made no secret of it, addressing her by name and mocking her about the rebels’ plans and her lover’s success.
