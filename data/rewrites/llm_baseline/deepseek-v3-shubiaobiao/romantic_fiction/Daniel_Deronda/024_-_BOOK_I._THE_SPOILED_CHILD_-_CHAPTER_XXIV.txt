Gwendolen was relieved to have her interview with Klesmer behind her before facing her uncle and aunt. The path ahead seemed strewn with indignities, and she braced herself with a stubborn resolve to endure whatever humiliations awaited her.

The meeting came on Monday, when she accompanied her mother to the rectory. They stopped first at Sawyer’s Cottage—a cramped, sunlit space, stripped of any softening comforts. The bare walls, the neglected garden with its wilted cabbage stalks, the dusty yew arbor—all pressed upon Gwendolen with oppressive bleakness.

"How can you bear it, Mamma?" she asked as they walked away, her voice tight. "You and the girls in that stifling little room, with that hideous wallpaper? And I won’t even be there."

"It will be some comfort knowing you are spared it, dear."

"If not for the money, I’d rather suffer there than go be a governess."

"Don’t decide against it before you try," her mother urged gently. "At the bishop’s palace, you’ll have every comfort. You’ve always cared for such things. It won’t be as hard as climbing those narrow stairs, listening to the clatter of crockery, the girls chattering—"

"It’s like a nightmare," Gwendolen burst out. "I can’t believe Uncle would allow this. He should have done something else."

"What else could he have done?"

"That was for him to discover. It’s a cruel world if people like us must sink so suddenly."

Her bitterness was sharp, her words unguarded. Yet when she faced her uncle and aunt, their unexpected warmth disarmed her. They spoke with dignified cheer about their own economies—Mr. Gascoigne’s practical adjustments, the boys’ simplified education. There was a quiet strength in their resilience that made her own resentment feel petty.

Still, when her uncle turned to her future, her stomach tightened.

"I acted quickly, Gwendolen," he said kindly. "A respectable position isn’t secured overnight. The Momperts’ offer is the best we could hope for. Mrs. Mompert wishes to meet you first—in Wanchester, as she travels to town. I know it may be trying, but you’ll have time to prepare."

"Why must she see me?" Gwendolen asked, already imagining a stern matron scrutinizing her flaws.

The rector smiled. "Don’t fret. She only wants a clearer impression than my letters provide. A mother is careful about her daughters’ companion. I’ve told her you’re young, but she oversees their education herself. She’s a woman of taste and principle—objects to French governesses. I’ve no doubt she’ll approve you."

Gwendolen’s flush betrayed her revulsion, though she forced stillness. Anna, sensing her distress, slipped a hand into hers.

Mr. Gascoigne, mistaking her silence for acceptance, added encouragingly, "It’s more a home than a mere post. The bishop is a man of influence—Low Church, but broad-minded. A friend of Lord Grampian’s. You’ll find it a respectable situation."

His assurances only deepened her dread. *Continuance of education. Privately strict. Bible Society.* Each phrase felt like a shackle. Desperation clawed at her.

"Was there not another possibility?" she asked tightly.

"The school?" Her uncle hesitated. "Far less suitable. Harder work, fewer advantages. And you might not even secure it."

Her mother shook her head. "A shared bedroom, Gwendolen. You’d hate it."

Memories of school—the lack of privacy, the drudgery—sealed her defeat. With effort, she murmured, "When does Mrs. Mompert expect me?"

"Within a fortnight, I imagine." Satisfied, her uncle departed, believing her resigned.

Left behind, Gwendolen seethed. The thought of presenting herself for approval, of bending to strangers’ whims, was unbearable. Even as a governess, she would be inspected, judged, found wanting. The bishop might examine her like a pupil. The future yawned before her—a gray, joyless servitude.

Wild thoughts flickered: escape, the stage, any path to freedom. But Klesmer’s warnings haunted her. Without protection, what awaited her but vulgarity and disrespect? Her pride revolted at the thought.

Meanwhile, her family’s kindness scraped at her raw nerves. Anna, timidly affectionate; her aunt, reading Rex’s buoyant letter—*Tell Mother she’s advertised for a hardworking son, and I apply for the place!* Even that stung. *Why should he flourish while I sink?*

She avoided the rectory, unable to feign compliance. Let Miss Merry fuss over the cottage furnishings. Let Jocosa find pleasure in it. She stayed in her room, listless, ignoring meals, snapping at well-meaning words.

Her mother watched in silent anguish. One afternoon, as Mrs. Davilow lingered over her dresses, Gwendolen abruptly fetched her jewelry case.

"These should be sold," she said flatly, lifting the tray. Then she paused, her fingers brushing the turquoise necklace—the one she had once torn from the fire. A strange pang struck her. "Keep this. The rest can go."

"But, darling, if we can manage—"

"They’re useless now. Governesses don’t wear finery. Better get me a drab uniform, like a charity-school maid."

"Don’t say that! The Momperts will appreciate elegance—"

"What they *appreciate* doesn’t matter. I must be what they want." Bitterness dripped from every word.

Her mother caught her hands. "If there’s another path—anything—tell me. We’ll face it together."

Gwendolen pulled away. "There *is* no other path. I must earn money. That’s all." Her voice hardened. "You’ll have the eighty pounds. At least you won’t have to stitch yourself blind."

No caress followed. She stared at the necklace, winding the torn handkerchief around it—the same she had used to snatch it from the flames.

Her mother’s eyes filled. "Don’t lose hope, my love. You’re so young. Happiness may yet come."

Gwendolen laughed harshly. "I see no reason to expect it."

She snapped the case shut. "I’ll keep this. Sell the rest. I’ll have no use for them." A bleak smile twisted her lips. "I might as well take the veil. I wonder if all who did felt as I do now."

"Don’t exaggerate—"

"How would *you* know what I feel?" she shot back, tucking the wrapped necklace away.

Mrs. Davilow fell silent, watching her daughter’s closed face. Something had changed—but what, she could not guess.

And Gwendolen, swallowing her despair, thought: *No one sees the storm inside. No one ever will.*
