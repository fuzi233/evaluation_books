Rebecca, the spirited mare, didn't slow down a bit. She thundered straight toward Baymouth, where Pen finally pulled her up at the stable. Without wasting a second, he bolted for Mr. Foker's lodging. He'd gotten the address from Foker the day before, a detail that saved him a lot of wandering.

When Pen arrived, he found himself outside a chemist's shop. The place smelled faintly of tobacco and soda water, which vanished quickly thanks to the enthusiastic young customers inside. Inside the rooms above, Pen discovered Mr. Spavin, Foker's pal and co-owner of the tandem carriage that had recently toured Chatteris. Spavin was lounging around, smoking a pipe and showing off some fancy tricks to a tiny dog with a crumb of biscuit.

There was a striking contrast between the two men. Pen's face was flushed a healthy, vibrant red from his ride, looking full of life. Next to him sat Spavin, whose features were pale, waxy, and clearly the result of heavy drinking. Spavin noticed the difference immediately. "Who's that guy?" he muttered to himself. "He looks fresher than a green bean. His hand doesn't shake in the morning; I'd bet five to one on it."

But Foker wasn't there. Not a soul. That was a massive letdown. Spavin shrugged, saying he had no idea when his friend would show up. Sometimes Foker disappeared for a day, sometimes for a whole week. Spavin tried to fill the silence by asking Pen which college he attended and if he had anything interesting to share. There was a decent draft of ale available, so the two young men settled in for a chat. Spavin learned Pen's name from the calling card the latter produced—a card Pen seemed rather proud of displaying. After a brief exchange, they parted ways.

Defeated, Pen headed down to the beach. He paced along the sand, nervously biting his fingernails while staring out at the vast, noisy sea. The ocean stretched out before him, bright and endless. Waves crashed into the bay, foaming and roaring loudly. Pen stared at them with a blank expression, barely registering the sound. Yet, a tidal wave was crashing into his own mind, and he felt utterly powerless to stop it. He threw stones into the water, but the tide kept surging forward. He was furious about not finding Foker. He *had* to see Foker.

"Maybe I'll just ride over to the Chatteris road and catch him," Pen thought.

An hour later, Rebecca was saddled and trotting happily along the grass beside the Chatteris road. About four miles out, the road branched off toward Clavering. Naturally, the horse wanted to take that turn, but Pen cut her sharply across her shoulder, forcing her onto the main turnpike. Still, there was no sign of the black carriage with its distinctive red wheels.

By the time he reached the tollgate, Pen decided there was no point in stopping. He pushed on to the George Inn. The hostler confirmed that Mr. Foker was definitely there. "He's been causing quite a scene last night!" the hostler chuckled. "Drinking, singing, and trying to fight the postboy named Tom. I'm guessing Tom probably beat him up." With a mischievous grin, the hostler turned to Foker's servant, who was carrying a bundle of freshly laundered clothes. "Did you bring up your master's hot water for shaving?" he teased. "Take Mr. Pendennis upstairs to see him."

Pen followed the servant into the apartment. Dominating the room was an enormous bed piled high with feathers and pillows. Lying amidst the fluff was Mr. Harry Foker, his small, yellowish face visible beneath a red silk nightcap.

"Hullo!" Pen called out.

"Who's there? Brother, spill it quick!" a voice boomed from the mattress. "Oh, it's Pendennis again! Does your Mama know you're missing? Did you eat with us last night? No, wait—who ate with us last night, Stoopid?"

"The three officers, sir, and Mr. Bingley, sir, and Mr. Costigan, sir," the servant replied, answering every question with perfect seriousness.

"Aha! The cup circulated, and the jokes flew around. We sang, and I recall wanting to fight a postboy. Did I thrash him, Stoopid?"

"No, sir. The fight never happened," the servant said gravely. He was busy organizing Foker's dressing case—a magnificent trunk gifted by a loving mother, essential for any journey. It was filled with silver dishes, mugs, bottles of essences, and a selection of razor blades waiting for Foker's inevitable beard growth.

"Do it another time," Foker yawned, stretching his thin arms overhead. "No fight today, but plenty of singing. Bingley sang, I sang, the General sang—that's Costigan, right? Have you ever heard him belt out 'The Little Pig Under the Bed,' Pen?"

"The man we met yesterday," Pen stammered, trembling slightly. "The father of—"

"Of the Fotheringays! The very man. Isn't she a Venus, Pen?"

"Sir, Mr. Costigan is in the sitting room. He says you invited him to breakfast. He's been knocking for five minutes, but he couldn't wake you up. He's been standing there since eleven..."

"What time is it now?"

"One o'clock, sir."

"What would the best mother in the world say if she saw me in bed at this hour?" the lazy young man cried dramatically. "She sent me down here with a grinder. She wants me to fix my neglected self-image! Hey, Pen, this isn't exactly seven o'clock school routine, is it, old chap?" He burst into a hearty, boyish laugh. "Go talk to the General while I get dressed. Oh, and tell him to sing 'The Little Pig Under the Bed' for you. It's fantastic."

Panicked, Pen rushed off to meet Mr. Costigan, while Foker began his elaborate grooming ritual.

Let's take a moment to understand Mr. Foker. One grandfather was a wealthy brewer; the other was an Earl, leaving him a doting mother. The Foker family had attended Cistercian School for generations. At school, our hero—whose name appeared on a pub sign advertising "Foker's Entire"—was relentlessly bullied because of his background, his awkward looks, his lack of academic talent, his dirtiness, his gluttony, and various other flaws. Anyone who knows how bullying turns a sensitive boy into a sneaky, silent creature can understand how, shortly after escaping that prison, Foker transformed. He emerged as the witty, sarcastic, brilliant character we now know. Though he remained a dunce academically (you can't learn everything by skipping school), he evolved from a sloppy student into a fastidious dandy. By the time he joined his guests, he was scented, dressed in crisp white linen, and looked absolutely splendid.

Meanwhile, in the sitting room, Captain Costigan sat by the window, holding a newspaper at arm's length. His eyes were cloudy, and he struggled to read the print, mouthing the words as best he could. His hat was tilted precariously on one side, and one foot rested casually on the window seat. Looking closely at his boots—one could tell his finances weren't exactly booming. Poverty often attacks a man's extremities first: hats, shoes, and gloves suffer before the rest of the body. Costigan's attire reflected this; his headgear, footwear, and gloves were all shabby and worn.

Upon seeing Pen, the Captain hopped down from the window. He greeted the newcomer with a stiff military salute, touching his hat with a gloved finger, then removing the hat entirely. He was balding but covered his scalp with thick, greasy gray hair, with wisps hanging down his cheeks. Years of whiskey had ruined whatever complexion he once had, giving his face a copper hue. He wore a high collar, stained and torn in several places, and a tight-fitting coat where the buttons had long since surrendered.

"I am the young gentleman you introduced yourself to yesterday in Cathedral Yard," the Captain declared with a flourish and a wave of his hat. "I hope you are doing well, sir. I spotted you at the theater last night during my daughter's performance. I missed you on my way home. I merely escorted her back, sir, for Jack Costigan, despite our poverty, is a gentleman. When I returned to pay my respects to my jovial young friend, Mr. Foker—you were already gone. We had a delightful night, sir—Mr. Foker, the three gallant young dragoons, and your humble servant. Gad, sir, it reminded me of our old days when I served His Majesty in the Fighting Hundred and Third Regiment." He produced an antique snuffbox and presented it with dignity.

Arthur Pendennis was so flustered he couldn't speak. This ragged-looking fellow was—was her father. The Captain smelled strongly of last night's cigars and twirled the tuft on his chin with the flair of a young dandy.

"I hope, Miss F—, Miss Costigan is well, sir," Pen stammered, turning red. "She—she gave me far greater pleasure than—than I've ever experienced at a play. I believe, sir—I truly believe she is the finest actress in the world," he gasped.
