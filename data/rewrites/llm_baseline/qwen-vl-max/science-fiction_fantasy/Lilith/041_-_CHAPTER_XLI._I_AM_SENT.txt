I turned to Eve and said, “Mother, one couch beside Lona is empty. I know I am unworthy—but may I not sleep tonight in your chamber with my dead? Will you forgive both my fear and my boldness, and take me in? I surrender myself. I am weary of being alive, and would fain rest in the stillness.”

“The couch next to Lona was meant for you,” she replied softly. “But there is a task to be done before you may sleep.”

“I am ready,” I answered.

“How do you know you can do it?” she asked, a faint smile touching her lips.

“Because you ask it,” I said.

“What is it?”

She turned to Adam. “Is he forgiven, husband?”

“From the depths of my heart,” Adam said.

Then he spoke to me: “Come, Mara—give me your hand.”

She placed it gently in his lap. He took it with care, as though holding something sacred.

“Come with me,” he said to me. “We will go to the cottage. There I will tell you what must be done.”

As we walked, a sudden wind swept through the valley—wild and sharp—and above us, on the roof, came the sound of great wings flapping. But like before, the storm passed quickly, fading into a low, mournful moan that lingered in the air.

When the door of the death-chamber closed behind us, Adam sat down. I stood before him, still.

“You remember,” he began, “how after leaving my daughter’s house, you came upon a dry rock marked by the scars of an ancient waterfall. You climbed it and found yourself in a vast sandy desert. Now, go back to that rock. From its peak, walk deep into the desert—but do not take many steps before lying down. Place your head upon the sand and listen. If you hear the whisper of water beneath, go a little farther and listen again. If the sound remains, you are on the right path. Every few yards, stop. Lie down. Listen. If at any point the sound fades, you have strayed. Then listen in every direction until you hear it again. Follow the murmur closely—never retrace your steps. The sound will grow stronger, guiding you to where it is loudest. That is the place. Dig with the spade I will give you until you reach moisture. In that wet earth, lay the hand. Cover it level with the desert floor, and return home.

“But heed this well: carry the hand with reverence. Never set it down, no matter how safe the place may seem. Let nothing touch it. Do not pause or turn aside for any obstacle. Never look behind you. Speak to no one. Answer none. Walk straight ahead, unswerving.

“It is dark now, and dawn is far off—but you must begin at once.”

He gave me the hand. It was cold, yet not lifeless. And then he handed me a spade.

“This is my gardening spade,” he said. “With it, I have brought many beautiful things into the light.”

I took it, and stepped out into the night.

It was bitterly cold, and utterly black. To fall here would be to vanish into nothingness. The path ahead was treacherous—even in daylight, it was perilous! Yet I had not chosen this burden. The moment I stepped forward, I knew I was not alone. A pale, ghostly light rose from the ground with each footfall, illuminating my way. Through the heather and over jagged stones, I walked without stumbling, guided by unseen hands.

The bad burrow lay silent. No wave stirred. No face broke the surface as I crossed it.

A moon rose—pale and silver—and lit the path ahead. By morning’s edge, I was near the dried channels of the river’s first branch, close, I thought, to Mara’s cottage.

The moon hung low, just above the horizon, when I saw a figure blocking the narrow trail. Cloaked from head to toe in a veil of mist, shimmering under the moonlight, it stood motionless.

I did not slow. I did not look.

The veil fell away.

“Have you already forgotten me?” whispered the princess—or what seemed her.

I said nothing. I kept walking.

“You meant to leave me in that terrible tomb!” she cried. “Do you not understand? Wherever I wish to be, I am. Take my hand—I am alive, as you are!”

For a moment, I almost said, *Give me your left hand.* But I stopped myself. I clenched my jaw and pressed forward.

“Give me my hand!” she shrieked, lunging at me. “Or I will tear you apart—you are mine!”

She seized at me—but nothing touched my skin. I shuddered, but did not falter. Her form dissolved into air, and I saw her no more.

Then, filling the path ahead, came a long column of armed men—marching with measured tread, their armor clashing in the silence. I walked through them as if they were shadows. I do not know if they parted for me, or if they were not truly there. But they turned and followed. I could feel their steps behind me, hear the clang of steel, yet I cast no glance backward. Their sounds faded into the distance.

A little farther on, where the moon was nearly gone and shadows swallowed the path, I saw a woman seated where the trail narrowed—her face wrapped in cloth.

“Oh,” she said, “you’ve come at last. I’ve waited here for you—perhaps an hour, perhaps more. You’ve done well. Your trial is over. My father sent me to meet you so you might rest. Give me your charge. Lay your head in my lap. I’ll guard both until the sun rises. I am not always bitterness—nor to all men.”

Her voice was sweet poison. I was exhausted. How tempting it was to believe her—to surrender. What if, in rigid obedience, I trampled the very essence of the Lady of Sorrow beneath my feet?

My heart trembled. It felt as though it would burst from my chest.

Yet my will hardened. My step did not waver.

I bit down on my tongue, lest I speak. I moved on.

Just as I neared her, she pulled back the cloth.

Her beauty was overwhelming—but those were not Mara’s eyes. No lie could imitate them so perfectly. I walked past her as if she were not there. My foot found only empty space.

I was almost across when a Shadow blocked the way.

I think it was *The Shadow*.

It wore a helmet—but as I drew closer, I saw it was no helmet. It was the head itself—twisted, distorted, barely human. A chill wind struck me—damp, foul, like the breath of a tomb. My limbs grew weak. My bones threatened to collapse. I felt as if I were passing through him—but now I wonder if he passed through me. For a moment, I was one of the damned.

Then—softly—a gentle breeze stirred, like the first breath of spring. And before me, the sky began to glow.

Dawn was breaking.

Now my path led past Mara’s cottage. The door stood wide open. On the table, I saw a loaf of bread and a pitcher of water. But no cry, no wail—no sign of mourning.

I reached the precipice where the river once flowed. I climbed its weathered face and entered the desert.

At last, after listening again and again, I found the spot where the hidden water murmured most clearly. I tied Lilith’s hand around my neck and began to dig.

It was a long, grueling labor. The sand was loose, and I had to make a deep hole. But finally, I struck damp earth.

I threw the spade aside and laid the hand down. Water oozed from beneath its fingers.

I leapt up and hurried to fill the grave.

Then, utterly spent, I collapsed beside it—and slept.
