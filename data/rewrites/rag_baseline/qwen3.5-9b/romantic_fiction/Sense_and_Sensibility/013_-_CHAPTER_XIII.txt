The excursion to Whitwell, meticulously planned by Elinor, dissolved into absolute nullity before it ever commenced. Her preparations had accounted for the worst: drenching rains, crushing fatigue, and the gnawing terror of exposure. Yet, the outcome proved far more calamitous than any storm could have been—they did not move at all.

By ten o'clock, the entire assembly gathered at the park gates, ready for breakfast. The morning held a deceptive promise; though the night had poured its weight upon the earth, the clouds were fracturing, allowing intermittent shafts of sunlight to pierce the grey canopy. Spirits ran high, fueled by a collective determination to endure whatever inconvenience fate might throw their way, provided they remained united.

Breakfast proceeded until the arrival of correspondence shattered the mood. A letter addressed to Colonel Brandon arrived among the pile. He seized it, scanned the handwriting, and his complexion drained of color. With sudden, jerky movements, he rose and exited the room.

"What is the matter with Brandon?" Sir John demanded, his voice cutting through the chatter.

"Nothing," Elinor whispered, though her heart hammered against her ribs. "I trust he has received no bad tidings."

"It must be extraordinary," Lady Middleton interjected, her brow furrowing. "To abandon my breakfast table so abruptly suggests a crisis of the highest order."

Five minutes elapsed before Brandon reappeared, his demeanor rigid.

"No bad news, I hope," Mrs. Jennings called out as he entered.

"None at all, ma'am," he replied, his voice tight.

"Was it from Avignon?" Mrs. Jennings pressed, her curiosity overriding her decorum. "Does it concern your sister's health?"

"No, ma'am. It originated in town. Merely a letter of business."

"But how could a routine commercial missive disturb you so profoundly?" Mrs. Jennings persisted, ignoring the sharp rebuke from Lady Middleton. "Speak plainly, Colonel. Let us have the truth."

"My dear madam," Lady Middleton snapped, "remember your station."

"Perhaps it announces that your cousin Fanny is wedded?" Mrs. Jennings guessed, oblivious to the correction.

"Not at all."

"Well, then, I deduce the sender. And I pray she fares well."

"Who do you imply, ma'am?" Brandon asked, flushing slightly.

"Oh! You know precisely who I mean."

"I am deeply sorry, ma'am," Brandon addressed Lady Middleton, "to receive this dispatch today. It concerns business requiring my immediate presence in town."

"In town!" Mrs. Jennings exclaimed. "At this season? What necessitates such haste?"

"My personal loss is significant," Brandon continued, his tone grave, "in forfeiting the company of such an agreeable gathering. However, my absence is imperative to secure your entry to Whitwell."

A collective gasp rippled through the room. This was a blow of devastating proportions.

"If you draft a note to the housekeeper, Mr. Brandon," Marianne suggested eagerly, "would that not suffice?"

Brandon shook his head firmly.

"We must proceed," Sir John declared. "Delay is not an option when we stand on the threshold. You cannot depart for town until tomorrow, Brandon—that is final."

"I wish it were so simple," Brandon replied, his voice devoid of inflection. "Yet, I possess no authority to postpone my journey by a single hour."

"If you would merely disclose the nature of your errand," Mrs. Jennings urged, "we might determine if it could be deferred."

"You would not be delayed six hours," Willoughby interjected smoothly, "were you to await our return."

"I cannot afford to lose *one* hour," Brandon stated flatly.

Elinor caught Willoughby leaning toward Marianne, his voice dropping to a conspiratorial whisper. "Some souls simply cannot tolerate a party atmosphere. Brandon fears catching a chill, I suspect. He has concocted this elaborate ruse to escape. I would wager fifty guineas the letter bears his own signature."

"I harbor no doubts," Marianne retorted instantly.

"There is no convincing you to alter your course, Brandon, as I have known you since childhood," Sir John observed, his tone laced with resignation. "Nevertheless, I trust you will reconsider. Contemplate this: the two Miss Careys have traveled from Newton; the three Miss Dashwoods have trekked from the cottage; and Mr. Willoughby rose two hours prior to his custom to join us at Whitwell."

Colonel Brandon reiterated his profound sorrow at disappointing the group, yet maintained that his departure was unavoidable.

"Very well," Sir John conceded. "When shall you return?"

"I hope we shall meet you at Barton," Lady Jennings added warmly, "the moment you can conveniently leave town. Consequently, we shall postpone the Whitwell excursion until your return."

"You are exceedingly obliging. Yet, given the uncertainty of my return date, I dare not commit to any specific timeline."

"Oh! He must and shall return," Sir John insisted loudly. "Should he not appear by the end of the week, I shall ride after him myself."

"Aye, do so, Sir John," Mrs. Jennings chimed in. "Perhaps you will finally uncover the mystery of his business."

"I have no desire to pry into another man's affairs," Brandon replied coolly. "I presume it involves some subject of shame."

His horses were announced.

"Do you intend to travel to town on horseback?" Sir John queried.

"No. Only to Honiton. From there, I shall take the post."

"As you are resolved," Sir John said, rising, "I wish you a safe journey. Though I advise you to change your mind."

"I assure you, it lies entirely outside my power."

With that, he took his leave of the assembly.

"Is there no possibility of seeing you and your sisters in town this winter, Miss Dashwood?" he asked politely.

"I fear, none whatsoever."

"Then I must bid you farewell for a duration longer than I would prefer."

To Marianne, he offered only a curt bow and silence.

"Come, Colonel," Mrs. Jennings pleaded as he moved toward the door. "Before you depart, do reveal the nature of your undertaking."

He exchanged a polite morning greeting with her and, accompanied by Sir John, withdrew.

Only then did the dam break. The polite restraint previously holding back their grievances evaporated. Complaints and lamentations erupted universally, a chorus of frustration echoing through the hall. They unanimously agreed that the disappointment was excruciating.

"I can surmise his business," Mrs. Jennings declared triumphantly.

"And who is it, ma'am?" nearly everyone asked in unison.

"Yes! It concerns Miss Williams, surely."

"And who is Miss Williams?" Marianne asked, genuinely perplexed.

"What! Do you not know Miss Williams? I am certain you have heard of her. She is a relative of the Colonel's, my dear; a very close kin. We shall not specify the degree, lest we shock the young ladies." Lowering her voice to a theatrical whisper, she turned to Elinor. "She is his natural daughter."

"Indeed!"

"Oh, yes! And she resembles him in every feature, save for her ability to stare. I daresay the Colonel will leave her his entire fortune."

When Sir John returned, he joined the chorus of regret regarding the thwarted plans, though he concluded by insisting that, since they were all assembled, they must manufacture some form of joy. After brief deliberation, it was decided that while true happiness awaited them at Whitwell, they might achieve a tolerable composure of mind by touring the countryside. Carriages were dispatched; Willoughby's led the procession, and Marianne appeared more radiant than she had ever been as she settled within.

Willoughby drove swiftly through the park, and they vanished from sight quickly. No further communication occurred until their return, which happened only after the rest of the party had resumed their activities. Both seemed utterly delighted with their excursion, offering only vague remarks that they had ventured through the lanes while the others traversed the downs.

Evening promised a dance, and the day was dedicated to merriment. Additional members of the Carey family joined for dinner, swelling the table count to nearly twenty, a spectacle Sir John observed with evident satisfaction. Willoughby claimed his customary seat between the two elder Miss Dashwoods. Mrs. Jennings took her place to Elinor's right. Hardly had they taken their seats before she leaned forward, addressing Marianne with a volume audible to both women.

"I have uncovered your secret, despite all your stratagems. I know exactly where you spent the morning."

Marianne flushed crimson and replied hurriedly, "Where, pray?"

"Did you not know," Willoughby added smoothly, "that we had driven out in my curricle?"

"Yes, yes, Mr. Impudence, I am fully aware of that fact," Marianne shot back. "I was determined to ascertain *where* you had gone. I trust you enjoy your residence, Miss Marianne. It is a spacious property, I understand; and when I next visit, I hope you have furnished it anew, for it required it desperately during my stay six years ago."

Marianne turned away, overwhelmed by confusion. Mrs. Jennings laughed heartily. Meanwhile, Elinor realized that in her fervent quest to discover the location, she had inadvertently prompted her own maid to inquire with Mr. Willoughby's groom. Through this indirect channel, she learned they had visited Allenham, spending considerable time wandering the gardens and inspecting the interior of the house.
