I attempt to write this account with a steady hand, yet the words tremble on the page. There is no composure in my soul; nothing but your repeated, earnest pleas could have dragged me back to this chair. To revisit these memories is to unstring my nerves anew, reopening wounds that, even years after my deliverance, kept my days and nights shrouded in dread. Solitude, once merely quiet, now feels insupportably terrifying.

Before I close this volume, I must speak of Baron Vordenburg. It was his obscure, antiquated learning that led us to the grave of Countess Mircalla. For years, he lived in Graz, surviving on a meager stipend—the last remnant of his once-princely estates in Upper Styria. His entire existence was consumed by the meticulous study of vampirism, a subject he treated with the gravity of sacred scripture. He knew every treatise by heart: *Magia Posthuma*, *Phlegon de Mirabilibus*, *Augustinus de cura pro Mortuis*, and John Christopher Herenberg's *Philosophicae et Christianae Cogitationes de Vampiris*. From a vast collection of judicial records, he distilled a system of laws governing the undead—a set of rules that applied either universally or intermittently depending on the creature's specific condition.

Let me correct a common misconception found in popular tales: the deadly pallor attributed to vampires is a theatrical fabrication. In the grave, and when they walk among the living, they possess the vibrant hue of health. Only when exposed to the harsh light within their coffins do they reveal the true signs of their cursed state, just as we witnessed with the late Countess Karnstein. Their ability to vanish from their tombs and return hours later, leaving not a single crack in the earth nor a shift in the linen wrappings, remains an inexplicable mystery. They sustain this amphibious existence through daily slumber in the dark, while their waking lives are fueled by a horrific hunger for fresh blood.

Furthermore, vampires are susceptible to a fascination akin to the intensity of romantic love. Driven by this obsession, they employ infinite patience and cunning to overcome obstacles, refusing to rest until they have sated their appetite and drained the very essence of their chosen victim. In such cases, they approach with the refinement of an epicure, prolonging their torment through the subtle arts of courtship, seemingly seeking a twisted form of consent. However, when faced with an unwilling prey, they revert to brute force, overpowering, strangling, and exhausting their victim in a single, brutal feast.

There are also peculiar limitations to their powers. As you know, Mircalla was bound by a strange constraint: she could only assume names that were perfect anagrams of her own—"Carmilla" or "Millarca"—with not a single letter added or omitted.

My father recounted to the Baron the tale of the Moravian nobleman and the vampire at the Karnstein churchyard. Curious as to how the Baron had located the hidden tomb, the old man's face crinkled into a cryptic smile. He gazed down at his spectacles, fumbling with the case before lifting his eyes again.

"I possess many journals," he began softly. "The most fascinating concerns the visit you mentioned. Tradition often distorts the truth. While he was called a Moravian nobleman due to his residence there, he was actually a native of Upper Styria. In his youth, he was a devoted lover of the beautiful Countess Mircalla. Her premature death cast him into an abyss of grief. Vampires, according to a ghastly law, multiply through a chain reaction: a suicide born of despair may become a vampire; this specter haunts sleepers, causing them to die and transform in the grave. This was the fate of Mircalla, haunted by a demon.

"My ancestor, the original Vordenburg, deduced that suspicion would inevitably fall upon her. Yet, he harbored a profound horror—not of her nature, but of her remains being profaned by a posthumous execution. He believed that expelling a vampire from its dual existence thrust it into a far more terrible state of being. Desperate to save his beloved, he devised a ruse: a fake exhumation followed by the total erasure of her monument.

"As age settled upon him, viewing the world from the vantage point of his declining years, he recoiled in horror at his own deception. He left behind detailed notes tracing the path to the true location, along with a confession of his fraud. Death cut short any further plans he might have had, but his work guided a distant descendant to the beast's lair, saving many souls too late."

Our conversation turned to another hallmark of the undead: the strength of the hand. The Baron recalled how Mircalla's slender fingers gripped the General's wrist like a vice of steel when he raised his axe, leaving a lingering numbness that never fully returned.

That spring, my father took me on a tour of Italy, a journey lasting over a year. Long ago, the immediate terror of our ordeal faded, though the image of Carmilla persists in my mind with shifting facets. Sometimes she appears as the playful, languid beauty I first met; at other times, she transforms into the writhing fiend I glimpsed in the ruined church. Often, I wake from a reverie convinced I hear the light, rhythmic tap of her footsteps against the drawing-room door.
