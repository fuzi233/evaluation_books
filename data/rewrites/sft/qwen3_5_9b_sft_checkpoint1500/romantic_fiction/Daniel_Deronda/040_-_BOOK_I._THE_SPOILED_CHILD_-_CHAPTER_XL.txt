"The soul possesses a faculty that, through interpositions capable of veiling and obscuring, transforms shadows into contingencies of grandeur; thus, it serves to heighten its innate radiance. Like the vast moon, rising behind a dense, towering forest in the deep stillness of a summer eve, it burns within the green canopy like an unconsumed fire, kindling the surrounding foliage and turning the dark veil into a substance as glorious as itself, wholly incorporated by a power both capacious and serene."
— WORDSWORTH: *Excursion*, Book IV.

Daniel Deronda emerged from the cramped dwelling at Chelsea in a frame of mind that craved vigorous physical exertion to dispel what he privately termed the "fumes" of his irritation. Heading toward the city, the sight of the Chelsea Stairs and the idling boats instantly resolved his dilemma: he would avoid the stifling inertia of a carriage, opting instead to hire a wherry and row himself.

His mission was twofold. First, he intended to visit Ram's bookshop, where he had arrived too late the previous day to catch Mordecai during his midday vigil, having been informed that the scholar invariably returned between five and six. Deronda deeply desired further acquaintance with this enigmatic inhabitant of the Cohens' household, viewing it as a prerequisite for redeeming his stolen ring. He dreaded that their conversations would repeatedly terminate swiftly with that specific, fleeting spark of Mordecai's interest, akin to a drawbridge retracting to cut off easy communication forever. As he warmed to the rhythm of the oar, focusing his mind on this errand and the goals he pursued regarding Mirah, he experienced a familiar, rapid shift in his perspective. He mentally shifted his viewpoint from seeing Mordecai merely as a useful instrument for his own designs to considering the other man's humanity, chiding himself for resembling an enlistment sergeant who secures recruits without caring for the drama that brings them.

"I suppose, if I obtain the information I most urgently seek from this man," Deronda mused, "I would be content even if he showed no inclination to reveal more of himself, or explain why he seems to harbor expectations of me that remain unfulfilled. Such curiosity would eventually fade; yet, perhaps, we have approached and parted like two ships, each carrying an exile who would recognize the other if they could look face-to-face. While there is little chance of a peculiar tie between me and this poor fellow, whose journey, I suspect, is nearly concluded, I wonder if there exists any significant mutual absence between people who exchange blank glances, or even long for one another's departure in a crowded space. Nevertheless, one often creates opportunities for missing by adopting the recruiting sergeant's strategy."

As the wherry neared Blackfriars Bridge, where Deronda planned to disembark, the clock struck half-past four. The grey day was dying in glorious fashion, its western clouds shattered into narrowing purple strata before a wide, spreading saffron clarity. Above, the sky held a monumental calm, but upon the river, it manifested as luminous movement—the alternating flash of ripples and currents, the sudden glow of a brown sail, the passage of laden barges shifting from blackness into color. These elements formed an active response to that brooding splendor.

Feeling thoroughly warmed, Deronda set down the oar and draped his Inverness cape over himself. As he raised his head to fasten the final button, his eyes caught a familiar face gazing down from the bridge parapet. Illuminated by the western light into startling distinctness and brilliance, it was an illuminated type of bodily emaciation and spiritual urgency. It was Mordecai. Having spotted the approaching boat while watching westward, Mordecai had kept his gaze fixed upon it, first simply noting its approach, then recovering impressions that caused him to shiver with a presentiment. Finally, as the nearing figure lifted its face toward him—the face of his visions—he immediately beckoned with an uplifted white hand, again and again.

Anxious that Mordecai should recognize and await him, Deronda had not delayed in signaling, and the answer came instantly. Mordecai lifted his cap and waved it, feeling in that instant that his inward prophecy was fulfilled. Obstacles, incongruities, and all barriers melted into the sense of completion flooding his soul with outward satisfaction. His exultation mirrored that of an experimenter witnessing the first stirrings of change corresponding to what concentrated prevision had foreshadowed. The prefigured friend had emerged from the golden background to signal him; this was actuality; the remainder was destiny.

Within three minutes, Deronda had landed, paid the boatman, and joined Mordecai, whose instinct was to stand perfectly still and wait.

"I was very glad to see you standing here," said Deronda, "for I intended to proceed to the bookshop to find you again. I was there yesterday—perhaps they mentioned it?"

"Yes," said Mordecai, with simple gravity, "that was the reason I came to the bridge."

To Deronda, this answer was startlingly mysterious. Were the peculiarities of this man truly associated with some form of mental alienation, as Cohen had hinted?

"You knew nothing of my presence at Chelsea?" Deronda asked after a moment.

"No," Mordecai replied. "But I expected you to come down the river. I have been waiting for you these five years." Mordecai's deep-set eyes were fixed upon Deronda's with a look of affectionate dependence, at once pathetic and solemn. Deronda's sensitivity responded not less intensely because he could not but believe this strangely disclosed relation rested on an illusion.

"It will be a satisfaction to me if I can be of any real use to you," Deronda answered earnestly. "Shall we take a cab and drive to—whichever you wish? You have likely walked enough with your short breath."

"Let us go to the bookshop. It will soon be time for me to be there. But now, look upriver," Mordecai said, turning back toward the water and speaking in undertones of what might be called an excited calm—so absorbed by a sense of fulfillment that he felt no barrier to a complete understanding between them. "Observe the sky; it fades slowly. I have always loved this bridge; I stood upon it when I was a child. It is a meeting-place for spiritual messengers. It is true, as the Masters said, that each order of things has its angel: meaning the full message of each from afar. Here, I have listened to the messages of earth and sky. When I was stronger, I used to linger and watch for the stars in the deep heavens. Yet, this time, just about sunset was what I loved best. It sank into me and dwelled with me—fading, slowly fading: it was my own decline. It paused, it waited, until finally it brought me my new life, my new self, who shall live when this breath is all spent."

Deronda remained silent. He felt strangely wrought upon. The first-prompted suspicion that Mordecai might be prone to hallucinations, or perhaps a monomaniac obsessed with some subject that had strained his diseased organism too severely, yielded to a more submissive expectancy. His nature was too broad, too ready to conceive realms beyond his own experience, to settle immediately on the easy explanation of "madness" whenever a consciousness displayed such fullness and conviction where his own was blank. It accorded with his habitual disposition to meet rather than resist claims arising from another's need; and this claim brought with it a sense of solemnity, appearing as a radiation from Mordecai that utterly nullified his outward poverty, lifting him into authority as if he were that preternatural guide seen in universal legends, who suddenly drops his mean disguise to stand as manifest Power. This impression was sanctioned by a sort of resolved quietude produced in Mordecai's manner by the persuasion of fulfillment. After a moment of silence, Mordecai said, "Let us go now."

Once in the cab, Mordecai added, "We will alight at the end of the street and walk to the shop. You may examine the books, and Mr. Ram will be departing shortly, leaving us alone."

It seemed this enthusiast was just as cautious, just as alert to judgments in other minds, as if he were the antipode of all enthusiasm, known as "a man of the world."

As they rattled along, Mirah was still present with Deronda in the midst of this strange experience, though he foresaw that the course of conversation would be dictated by Mordecai, not by himself. He was no longer confident of the questions he could pose. With a reaction on his own mood, he thought inwardly, "I suppose I am in a state of complete superstition, as if awaiting a destiny that could interpret the oracle. Yet, some strong relation must exist between me and this man, since he feels it so acutely. Great heaven! What relation has proven more potent in the world than faith, even when mistaken? Than expectation, even when perpetually disappointed? Will my side of the relation prove disappointing or fulfilling? Well, if it is ever possible for me to fulfill, I will not disappoint."

In ten minutes, the two men, possessing an intensity of consciousness as if they were two undeclared lovers, found themselves alone in the small, gas-lit bookshop and turned face to face. Each bare his head from an instinctive feeling that they wished to see the other fully. Mordecai stepped forward to lean his back against the little counter, while Deronda stood against the opposite wall, scarcely four feet away.
