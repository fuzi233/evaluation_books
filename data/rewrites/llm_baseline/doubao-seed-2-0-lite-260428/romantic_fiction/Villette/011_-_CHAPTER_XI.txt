The summer heat hung thick and heavy over the pensionnat, settling in folds of warm linen and clinging to the back of every neck, the air still save for the drowsy hum of flies at the windowpanes. It was then that Georgette, Madame Beck’s smallest, most spoiled child, came down with a sharp, burning fever. Désirée, whose endless list of petty ailments had vanished as if by magic at the first hint of contagion, was bundled off alongside the youngest Fifine to their grandmother’s farmhouse in the country, every last door and window propped open behind them to chase the risk of sickness from the halls.

By then, no home remedy could do anymore, and Madame chose to overlook the fact that stuffy old local physician Dr. Pillule had returned home a week prior. Instead, she sent word straight to his young English rival, begging him to keep coming to attend her household. It was only a matter of days before a handful of the pensionnaires began complaining of splitting headaches, feeling faint and unwell enough to echo little Georgette’s symptoms. “Now,” I thought to myself, surely the cautious directress would recall Dr. Pillule—no prudent woman would let such a young, unmarried man move freely among her flock of sheltered teen girls.

But Madame Beck was not merely prudent. She could be daring, unshakably bold when it suited her ends. She walked Dr. John straight through the doors of the school wing, and set him to attend to proud, beautiful Blanche de Melcy and her vain, flighty friend Angélique, who were both laid low with migraine. Dr. John bore the mark of her unusual confidence with clear, quiet gratification, his bearing so gentle and discreet no one could fairly find fault with his presence. But this was a land of convents and whispered confessions, where a young man stepping foot unchaperoned in a girls’ boarding school was unheard of. The pupils gossiped behind their textbooks, the kitchen maids whispered over their soup pots, the story leaked out into the town, and soon angry parents were writing letters, turning up on the doorstep to remonstrate. A dozen rival schools were poised to seize on her misstep to ruin her reputation entirely.

But Madame did not break. I clapped my hands silently to myself, my heart cheering brava as I watched her move through the crisis, calm, good-humored, disarming every frowning parent with that folksy, easy warmth no other woman could mimic, a frank, motherly charm that won over even the most suspicious visitor when strict gravity and long-winded reasoning would have failed entirely. “Ce pauvre Docteur Jean!” she would chuckle, rubbing her plump little white hands together gaily, “That dear, sweet boy! The kindest creature alive!” She explained that her own children loved him so fiercely they would scream themselves into fits if forced to see any other doctor, and that if she trusted him with her own darlings, it was only natural she would trust him with everyone else’s. It was no more than a temporary measure, she added: he had written a prescription for the girls’ headaches, and that was the whole of it.

Every last parent was disarmed. Blanche and Angélique soon sang loud, giddy duets in their physician’s praise, all the other pupils echoed them, declaring they would have no one but Dr. John if they ever fell ill, and soon the parents were laughing too, declaring Madame had only acted out of a motherly sort of softness that no reasonable person could fault. She came out of the scandal more beloved as a directress than she had ever been before.

To this day I cannot fully say why she risked her entire livelihood for the sake of that young doctor. The whole house—pupils, teachers, servants alike—whispered openly that she intended to marry him. The fourteen years between their ages seemed no obstacle at all to anyone. The signs all lined up, too: she went out of her way to personally greet every one of his visits, was unfailingly bright and blithe in his company, and discarded all her frumpy morning house gowns and worn nightcaps entirely. Dr. John never arrived for his early visits without finding her auburn braids neatly coiffed, her silk gown fitted crisply to her frame, neat laced leather boots on her feet instead of scuffed slippers, her whole toilette as fresh and polished as a summer bloom. She was no great beauty, but she was never dull, never insipid, her temperate blue eyes and warm, fruit-blushed cheeks pleasant to look at for hours on end, and I suspected she only dressed so carefully to prove to that handsome young man that she was not at all plain.

Rumor said she dreamed of making him her husband, of settling him in her well-furnished home, endowing him with her carefully hoarded savings that were said to amount to a comfortable little fortune, and making his life soft and easy forever. I often wondered if Dr. John guessed at those quiet visions. More than once I caught him leaving her parlor with a half-mischievous, half-frustrated smile on his lips, a glint in his eyes that spoke of masculine vanity flattered and tickled. For all his good nature and kindness, he was not perfect. But he was more than that, too: there was a watchful, expectant, eager edge to his manner that never faded, that only grew sharper with every visit, as if he was searching for something he could not find, something far beyond my ability to see.

One morning little Georgette’s fever spiked higher than usual, her wails sharp and peevish, no toy or sweet able to soothe her. I suspected the draught the doctor had ordered was not agreeing with her, and waited impatiently for his arrival to ask him to adjust it. The door bell rang, I heard his familiar voice greeting the portress, and waited for him to bound up the stairs three at a time, as was his custom, his cheerful laugh preceding him like a surprise.

Five minutes passed. Ten. No sign of him, no sound of his step. What could he be doing, lingering in the corridor below? Georgette still whimpered “Minnie, Minnie, me very poorly,” and my heart ached so much I went down to find him. The corridor was empty. He could not be in Madame’s dining room— I had left her but moments before, dressing in her own private chamber. Three pupils were practicing piano in the three adjacent salons, and further off, a whole class of a dozen girls were singing a barcarole in the oratory, their voices ringing out about freshness, stormy seas, and Venice. And there, half-hidden behind the ajar door of the small little room built as a boudoir but now given over entirely to the portress’s use, I heard a giddy treble laugh. Then a man’s voice, low, deep, and pleading, cut through the music: “For God’s sake!”

After a beat of silence, Dr. John stepped out of the room. His eyes were bright, but not with joy or triumph. His fair English cheeks were flushed deep crimson, his face twisted with a look of bafflement, tortured anxiety, and strange soft tenderness all tangled together. The half-open door hid me from his sight, but even if I had stood directly in his path, I do not think he would have seen me. Some raw, sharp pain had hold of him— not hurt pride, but wounded affection, cruelly inflicted.

I peered around the door frame, bewildered. Madame was safe in her chamber, and the only other woman who used that little cabinet was Rosine Matou, the pretty, flighty young portress, vain and fond of fine dresses and coin. It could not be her, surely, who had left him looking so broken? Her voice trilled out a light, giddy French song from within, and I glanced in. She sat at the table in a fine pink sprigged muslin gown, trimming a tiny lace cap, no other living soul in the room save for goldfish swimming in a glass bowl, potted flowers on the windowsill, and a broad shaft of hot July sunlight spilling across the floor. I could not make sense of the mystery, so I turned and went back upstairs to ask about the medicine.

Dr. John was already seated by Georgette’s crib, Madame standing over him. The little girl was calm now, settled into her sheets. Madame was fretting over his own health, pointing out a faint change in his pallor, chiding him for overworking himself and insisting he take a holiday by the sea. He laughed it off good-naturedly, told her she was far too kind, that he felt perfectly well. She turned to appeal to me, and Dr. John’s eyes flicked my way slowly, a faint look of surprise on his face that she would bother asking so insignificant a person’s opinion. “What do you think, Miss Lucie?” she said. “Is he not paler and thinner?”

Ordinarily I spoke no more than a word or two in his presence, content to let him dismiss me as the quiet, unassuming foreign teacher. But that day I chose to speak clearly, meeting no one’s eye. “He looks unwell right now,” I said, “but I think it must be from some temporary trouble. Dr. John may have been vexed, or hurt.” He did not say a word, but I could feel his gaze fixed on me, like he was realizing for the first time that I was not just a background shadow. For the first time, he noticed I spoke his own English tongue, after weeks of speaking to me only in French. He looked about to make a remark, but thought better of it, and held his peace.

He bade them good morning soon after, courteous but with an air of someone who had been smothered by too much unasked-for attention, and left. No sooner had the door clicked shut behind him than Madame dropped heavily into the chair he had just vacated, resting her chin in her hand. All her bright, amiable warmth vanished from her face, leaving it stony, stern, pinched with quiet mortification. She sighed once, a deep, heavy, shuddering breath. The bell for morning school rang loud through the halls, and she stood to leave, pausing at the dressing table mirror to glance at her reflection. A single stark white hair streaked her rich auburn braids. She plucked it out with a little shudder. In the harsh bright summer light, I could see the bloom on her cheeks was still there, but the soft texture of youth was long gone, the round contours of her face hardened with age. For the first time I pitied her. Disappointment, that cold hag, had reached out to greet her, and she was the sort of practical, sharp woman who did not know how to welcome that kind of pain.

I puzzled over Rosine and that strange encounter all day, lingering by the portress’s cabinet five different times to try and make sense of the mystery. She was young, pretty, well-dressed, no doubt the kind of girl many a young man might dote on. But I could not quite wrap my head around the raw pain on Dr. John’s face, the desperation in his plea. Madame, for her part, did not wallow. She had a school to run, a business that filled her days, a good sense most women (and men) never possessed. She did not make a fool of herself, did not pine or mope, did not force her attentions on him any longer. She had fought the good fight against that foolish, soft little predilection, and she had won. Brava, once more, Madame Beck. But the secret hidden in that sunlit little cabinet, the secret that had left Dr. John looking as if his heart had been ripped open, stayed with me, lingering like the echo of that half-heard plea, long after the summer heat faded.
