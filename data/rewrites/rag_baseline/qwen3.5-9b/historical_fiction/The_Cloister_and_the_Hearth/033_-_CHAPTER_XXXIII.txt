To truly grasp the architecture of this encounter, one must first understand the absolute poverty of Gerard's universe prior to this moment. He existed within a microscopic empire of two, subjugated by a man he was conditioned to call "Master"—a term that felt less like address and more like a brand of ownership. Now, however, the landscape of his captivity had shifted violently from the silent, star-gazing void of the wilderness to the cacophonous, performative theater of human society. They met the landlord in the passage, a figure who bowed with a mechanical precision that suggested he was acting out a role rather than offering genuine hospitality.

"Welcome, messieurs," the landlord intoned, removing his cap with a low bow that seemed to require more energy than the gesture itself warranted.

"Come, we are not in Germany," Gerard retorted, his voice cutting through the air with the sharp edge of someone accustomed to silence.

In the public room, they encountered the mistress, a buxom woman of forty whose presence immediately filled the space with a chaotic warmth. She curtsied to them, smiling with a cordiality that bordered on the predatory. "Give yourself the trouble of sitting ye down, fair sir," she addressed Gerard, dusting two chairs with her apron—a theatrical flourish that Gerard observed with a mixture of amusement and weary resignation. *This is a polite nation,* he thought, cataloging the absurdities of civilized interaction. *The trouble of sitting down? That will I endure with singular patience; and presently the labour of eating, also the toil of digestion, and finally, by Hercules' aid, the strain of going to bed, and the struggle of sinking fast asleep.*

"Why, Denys, what are you doing? Ordering supper for only two?" the mistress asked, her tone shifting effortlessly from deference to command.

"Why not?" Denys replied, embodying the reckless spirit of the traveler.

"What, can we sup without waiting for forty more? Burgundy forever!"

"Aha! Courage, camarade. Le dia—"

"C'est convenu."

Here, the Salic Law seemed to have dissolved entirely, leaving behind a realm where wimple and kirtle reigned supreme. Doublets and hose were few in number, and those present moved with a feeble, unconvincing act. The landlord himself wandered objectless, eternally taking off his cap to folk for want of thought, while the women, passing him in turn, thrust him quietly aside without looking at him, much like one removes a live twig while bustling through a dense wood.

Supper arrived, carried in by a maid whom the mistress followed, empty-handed yet commanding. "Fall to, my masters," she said cheerily, her voice ringing with false intimacy. "Ye have but one enemy here; and he lies under your knife." (Gerard suspected this to be mere formula, a script recited by the trade.)

They fell to. The mistress drew her chair slightly toward the table, providing company as well as meat, gossiping genially with them like old acquaintances. Yet, once this performance was exhausted, the busy dame departed, sending in her daughter, a beautiful young woman of about twenty who claimed the vacant seat. She was not quite so broad and genial as the elder, but gentle and cheerful, displaying a womanly tenderness for Gerard upon learning the distance the poor boy had traveled and the journey ahead. She stayed nearly half an hour, a brief interlude of softness before the storm broke again. When she left, Gerard remarked, "This an inn? Why, it is like home."

"Qui fit Francois il fit courtois," Denys burst out, swelling with gratified pride.

"Courteous? Nay, Christian," Gerard corrected him. "To welcome us like home guests and old friends, us vagrants, here today and gone tomorrow. But indeed, who better merits pity and kindness than the worn traveler far from his folk? Hola! Here comes another."

The newcomer was the chambermaid, a woman of about twenty-five with a cocked nose, a large laughing mouth, and a sparkling black eye. Her bare arm was stout but not shapely, a testament to labor rather than vanity. The moment she entered, one of the travelers passed a somewhat free jest on her; instantly, the whole company erupted in roaring laughter at his expense, so swiftly had her practiced tongue dismantled his defense. It was like a passage of arms between a novice and a master of fence; the foils clashed, and the novice was pinked. One by one, others broke lances with her, but Marion stood her ground, planting her great arms upon her haunches and holding the entire room in play.

This country girl possessed in perfection that rude and ready humor which looks mean and vulgar on paper but carries all before it when spoken aloud. It was not wit's rapier; it was its bludgeon. Nature had done much for her in this regard, and daily practice in the inn completed the rest. Yet, she shall not be photographed by me, but feebly indicated, for it was just four hundred years ago, the raillery was coarse, and she returned every stroke in kind. Though a virtuous woman, she said things without winking that no decent man of our day would utter even among men.

Gerard sat gaping with astonishment. This was to him almost a new variety of *homo*. He whispered, "Denys, now I see why you Frenchmen say 'a woman's tongue is her sword.'" Just then, she leveled another assailant, and the chivalrous Denys, to console and support "the weaker vessel"—the iron kettle among the clay pots—administered his consigne: "Courage, ma mie, le..." etc.

She turned on him directly. "How can he be dead as long as there is an archer left alive?" (General laughter at her ally's expense.)

"It is 'washing day,' my masters," she declared with sudden gravity.

"After? We travelers cannot strip and go bare while you wash our clothes," objected a peevish old fellow by the fireside, who had kept mumchance during the raillery but crept out into the sunshine of commonplaces.

"I aimed not your way, ancient man," replied Marion superciliously. "But since you ask me" (here she scanned him slowly from head to foot), "I trow you might take a turn in the tub, clothes and all, and no harm done" (laughter). "But what I spoke for, I thought this young sire might like his beard starched."

Poor Gerard's turn had come; his chin crop was thin and silky. The loudest of all the laughers this time was the traitor Denys, whose beard was of a good length, singularly stiff and bristly; so that Shakespeare, though he never saw him, hit him in the bull's-eye.

"Full of strange oaths, and bearded like the pard." —*As You Like It*.

Gerard bore the Amazonian satire mighty calmly. He had little personal vanity. "Nay, 'chambriere,'" he said with a smile, "mine is all unworthy your pains; take you this fair growth in hand!" pointing to Denys's vegetable.

"Oh, time for that, when I starch the besoms."

While they shouted over this palpable hit, the mistress returned, and in no more time than it took her to cross the threshold, the Amazon turned to a seeming Madonna, meek and mild. Mistresses are wonderful subjugators; their like, I think, breathes not on the globe. Housemaids, decide! It was a waste of histrionic ability, though, for the landlady had heard and did not at heart disapprove the peals of laughter.

"Ah, Marion, lass," she said good-humoredly, "if you laid me an egg every time you cackle, 'Les Trois Poissons' would never lack an omelet."

"Now, dame," said Gerard, "what is to pay?"

"What for?"

"Our supper."

"Where is the hurry? Cannot you be content to pay when you go? Lose the guest, find the money, is the rule of 'The Three Fish.'"

"But, dame, outside 'The Three Fish' it is thus written—'Ici-on ne loge...'"

"Bah! Let that flea stick on the wall! Look hither," and she pointed to the smoky ceiling, which was covered with hieroglyphics. These were accounts, vulgo scores; intelligible to this dame and her daughter, who wrote them at need by simply mounting a low stool and scratching with a knife so as to show lines of ceiling through the deposit of smoke. The dame explained that the writing on the wall was put there to frighten moneyless folk from the inn altogether, or to be acted on at odd times when a non-paying face should come in and insist on being served. "We can't refuse them plump, you know. The law forbids us."

"And how know you mine is not such a face?"

"Out fie! It is the best face that has entered 'The Three Fish' this autumn."

"And mine, dame?" said Denys; "dost see no knavery here?"

She eyed him calmly. "Not such a good one as the lad's; nor ever will be. But it is the face of a true man. For all that," added she drily, "an I were ten years younger, I'd as lieve not meet that face on a dark night too far from home."
