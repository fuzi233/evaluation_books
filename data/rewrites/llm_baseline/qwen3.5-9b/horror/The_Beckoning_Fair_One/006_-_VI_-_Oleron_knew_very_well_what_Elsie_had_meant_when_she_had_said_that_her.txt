Oleron understood perfectly what Elsie had intended when she mentioned that her next visit would be heralded by a postcard. He realized, with a sinking heart, that she had finally grasped the truth—that he knew, and yet, he did not want her. This realization struck him with a misery so profound it felt pitiful. Consequently, when she arrived again within a week, knocking at his door without prior notice, the blow was devastating.

She waited on the landing, speaking rather than entering immediately. She claimed she did not intend to stay long, though Oleron felt compelled to urge her inside before she would deign to cross the threshold. Her excuse was practical: she had heard of an inquiry seeking short stories and suggested he might find it wise to pursue it. He offered a polite thanks. Once her business was ostensibly concluded, however, her eagerness to depart returned with renewed vigor. Oleron saw through the pretext of the literary opportunity; he did not attempt to detain her. Even he acknowledged the charade, and so he accompanied her down the stairs.

Yet, Elsie Bengough possessed no fortune in that house. A second misfortune awaited her. Halfway down the staircase, there erupted a sharp, splintering crack of breaking wood, causing her to check a loud cry. Oleron knew the woodwork was ancient, yet he himself had climbed and descended these steps countless times without incident. Elsie had driven her foot through one of the treads.

He sprang to her side in alarm. "Oh, my poor girl!" he exclaimed.

She burst into hysterical laughter. "It's my weight—I know I'm getting fat—"

"Be still—let me clear these splinters away," he muttered tightly between his teeth.

She continued to laugh and sob simultaneously, insisting it was merely her increasing bulk. He thrust downward at the broken boards. The extraction proved difficult, and the sight of her torn boot revealed how severely her foot and ankle must have been abraded.

"Good God—good God!" he chanted over and over.

"I shall be too heavy for anything soon," she sobbed, her voice trembling.

"But refuse to reascend and inspect your injury," she pleaded instead. "No, let me go quickly—let me go quickly," she repeated.

"It's a frightful gash!" Oleron protested.

"Not so bad—let me escape quickly—I'm—I'm not wanted."

At those words, that she was unwanted, Oleron's head dropped as if she had delivered him a physical blow. "Elsie!" he choked out, broken and shocked.

But she too made a swift, violent gesture, as if discarding something repulsive. "Oh, Paul, not *that*—not *you*—of course I do mean that too in a sense—oh, you know what I mean!... But if the other cannot be, spare me this now! I—I wouldn't have come, but—but—oh, I did, I *did* try to keep away!"

It was intolerable, heartbreaking. What could he do? What could he say? He did not love her...

"Let me go—I'm not wanted—let me take away what's left of me—"

"Dear Elsie—you are very dear to me—"

Again, she made the gesture of violently rejecting his sentiment. "No, not that—not anything less—don't offer me anything less—leave me a little pride—"

"Let me get my hat and coat—let me take you to a doctor," he murmured.

She refused. She refused even the support of his arm. With another unsteady laugh, she said, "I'm sorry I broke your stairs, Paul.... You will go and see about the short stories, won't you?"

He groaned. "Then, if you won't see a doctor, will you go across the square and let Mrs. Barrett look at you? Look, there's Barrett passing now—"

The long-nosed Barrett glanced curiously down the alley, but as Oleron opened his mouth to call him, the man hurried off without a word. Elsie seemed desperate for nothing more than to clear the premises. Finally, she promised to go straight to a doctor, though she insisted on doing so alone.

"Good-bye," she said.

Oleron watched her until she passed the hatchet-shaped "To Let" boards hanging outside, watching with a fear that even they might fall upon her and maim her further.

That night, Oleron did not dine. His mind was cluttered with far too many troubling thoughts. He paced from room to room of his flat, as if walking might allow him to physically distance himself from Elsie Bengough's haunting cry that still echoed in his ears. *"I'm not wanted—don't offer me anything less—let me take away what's left of me..."*

"Oh, if he could only persuade himself that he loved her!" he thought bitterly.

He walked until twilight fell, then, without lighting candles, he stirred the fire and threw himself into a chair. Poor, poor Elsie! Yet, even as his heart ached for her, it remained impossible. If only he had known! If only he had exercised common observation! But those walks, those sisterly moments where he had taken her arm—what a fool he had been!... Well, it was too late now. It was she, not he, who must now act—act by keeping away. He would help her all he could. He himself would not sit in her presence. If she came, he would hurry her out again as fast as possible.... Poor, poor Elsie!

His room grew dark; the fire burned low and died; and he continued to sit, wincing periodically as fresh, tortured phrases rang in his ears. Then, suddenly, for reasons he could not explain, he found himself anxious for her in a new sense—uneasy regarding her personal safety. A horrible fancy seized him: perhaps even then she was looking over an embankment into dark water; perhaps she was currently glancing up at the hook on the door. Women had been known to do such things.... Then there would be an inquest, and he himself would be summoned to identify her, questioned about how she acquired an ill-healed wound on the hand and a severe abrasion on the ankle. Barrett would testify that he had seen her leaving his house....

Then he recognized his thoughts were morbid. By an effort of will, he pushed them aside and sat for a while, listening to the faint creakings, tickings, and rappings within his paneling....

If only he could have married her!... But he couldn't. Her face rose before him again as he remembered seeing it on the stairs: drawn with pain, ugly, and swollen with tears. Ugly—yes, positively blubbered; if tears were women's weapons, as they were often said to be, such tears were weapons turned against themselves... suicide again....

Then, all at once, he found himself attentively considering her two accidents. They were extraordinary, both of them. He *could not* have left that old nail protruding in the wood; indeed, he had fetched special tools from the kitchen earlier, and he was convinced that the step which had broken beneath her weight had been as sound as any other. It was inexplicable. If such things could happen, anything could happen. There was not a single beam or jamb in the place that might not collapse without warning, not a plank that might not crash inward, not a nail that might not transform into a dagger. The entire house teemed with life even now; as he sat there in the darkness, he heard its crowd of noises as if the building itself were a great microphone....

Only half-conscious of doing so, he had been sitting for some time identifying these sounds, attributing each crack, creak, or knock to its material cause. However, there was one noise which, despite his lack of full consciousness, he had failed to account for. It had last come some minutes ago; it came again now—a soft, sweeping rustle that seemed to contain an almost inaudible crackling.

For half a minute or so, it held Oleron's attention; then his heavy thoughts drifted back to Elsie Bengough. In that moment, he felt nearer to loving her than he ever had before. He pondered how, for some men, their loved ones became dearer precisely because of those poor mortal blemishes that remind us we are but sojourners on earth, facing a common fate not far distant that renders existence hardly worth living except for the time remaining. Strangling sobs, blurred vision, bodies battered by sickness, hearts and minds hardened by the world's roughness—how little love there would be were these barriers insurmountable! In that sense, he did love Elsie Bengough. What her happiness had never stirred in him, her sorrow almost awakened....

Suddenly, his meditation shattered. His ear had once more become acutely aware of that soft, repeated noise—the long sweep with the nearly imperceptible crackle embedded within it. Again and again it came, with a curious insistence and urgency. It quickened slightly as he became increasingly attentive... it seemed to Oleron that it was growing louder....

All at once, he shot bolt upright in his chair, tense and straining to listen. The silky rustle came again; he tried desperately to attach it to some object.... The next moment, he leaped to his feet, unnerved and terrified. His chair hung poised for a heartbeat before tipping over, sending the fire-irons clattering to the floor.
