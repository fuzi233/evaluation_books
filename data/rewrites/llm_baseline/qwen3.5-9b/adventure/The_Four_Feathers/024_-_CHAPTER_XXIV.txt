Calder regarded the landscape with a mixture of awe and disdain. *A callous country, inhabited by a callous race,* he thought, leaning against the railing of the upper deck as the steamer drifted lazily down the Nile from Wadi Halfa toward Assouan. Below him, lashed securely to the side of the vessel, floated a flat-bottomed barge. Upon its lower deck, amidst a crowd of native passengers, rested an *angareb*—a traditional bedstead of woven mats supported by four legs. Stretched upon it lay a motionless figure, completely obscured by a heavy black veil.

Early that morning at Korosko, two Arabs had carried this silent burden aboard. Now, they sat in the stern of the barge, laughing and chatting loudly, paying absolutely no heed to the weight they transported. To the casual observer, the occupant might have been either a dead man or a dead woman; such indifference suggested the body was already lifeless. Calder shifted his gaze upward, scanning the horizon where glaring sands and barren rocks rose in rough, pyramid-like formations. The only sign of vitality was a narrow, meager strip of green clinging desperately to the water's edge on both banks—a pathetic testament to spring and summer in a land seemingly untouched by rain.

His eyes returned to the *angareb*. Was it male or female? The black cloth draped tightly over the face outlined the nose, the hollows of the eyes, and the mouth, yet offered no clue regarding a mustache or beard. As the slanting sunlight advanced, creeping closer to the sleeping form, the natives nearby retreated into the shade of the upper deck, but the *angareb* remained unmoved. Even the two men in the stern showed no concern for their charge.

Calder watched as the golden light burned pitilessly across the black fabric, moving from the feet upward until it finally illuminated the face. Beneath the veil, the figure did not stir. The legs stayed rigidly straight; the arms lay pressed against the sides.

"Move the *angareb* into the shadow!" Calder shouted to the men in the stern. "Do it quickly!"

The Arabs rose with reluctance and complied.

"Is it a man or a woman?" Calder asked.

"A man," one replied. "We are taking him to the hospital in Assouan, but we don't think he'll survive. He fell from a palm tree three weeks ago."

"You haven't given him food or water?"

"He is too ill."

It was a familiar narrative, the logical conclusion of a fatalistic worldview where life and death were pre-written scripts. In reality, the man likely suffered only a minor bruise initially—an injury that simple remedies could have healed within days. Instead, he had been abandoned to the mercy of the sun, flies, and thirst. Unwashed and unfed, the bruise festered into a sore, which eventually gangrened. By the time the Egyptian Mudir of Korosko discovered the accident and ordered the transport to Assouan, it was far too late.

Despite knowing this grim logic, Calder found himself unable to dismiss the scene from his mind. The absolute stillness of the sufferer on the native bedstead fascinated him. When a strong wind arose at sunset, blowing against the current, he felt a strange sense of comfort knowing the patient might find some relief.

That evening, at the dinner table, a neighbor with a distinct German accent approached him.

"You aren't a doctor by any chance?" Calder blurted out impulsively.

"Not a doctor," the man replied, removing his spectacles. "But a medical student at Bonn. I came from Cairo to view the Second Cataract, but was barred from proceeding further than Wadi Halfa."

"Then I shall intrude upon your holiday," Calder said immediately. "I require your professional assistance."

"For myself? With pleasure," the student smiled kindly. "Though I wouldn't have guessed you were ill."

"I am not," Calder corrected. "It is for an Arab whom I need your help."

"The man on the bedstead?"

"Yes, if you will. Be warned: he was injured three weeks ago, and I know these people. No one has tended to him since. The sight won't be pleasant. This isn't a forgiving place for neglected wounds."

"All experience is good," the German shrugged.

They left the dining room and ascended to the upper deck. The wind had strengthened, raising choppy waves that caused the steamer and the barge to toss violently, splashing water onto the decks.

"He was down there," the student noted, peering over the rail into the gloom of the lower deck.

"It is pitch black," Calder observed. "Only one lamp hangs from the center of the upper deck, casting uncertain shadows over a tiny circle. Beyond that, everything is swallowed by darkness, save for the bow where the breaking water throws up sheets of spray like driven snow."

"He has been moved," the student said.

"No doubt. There is no one in the bows."

Calder stared into the abyss for a moment before speaking. "I believe the *angareb* is there."

"I believe it is."

Following the student, Calder hurried down the stairs to the lower deck and stepped to the side. Sure enough, the *angareb* stood waist-deep in the washing water exactly where it had been placed earlier that day. The veiled figure lay as motionless as stone, indifferent to the cold spray dousing its face.

"I expected as much," Calder muttered.

Grabbing a lantern, he climbed the bulwarks onto the swaying barge and summoned the two Arabs.

"Move the *angareb* away from the bow," he commanded. Once they had complied, he added, "Now remove that covering. My friend here needs to examine the wound."

The men hesitated. One, displaying insolence, protested, "There are doctors in Assouan, where we are taking him."

Calder raised the lantern high and pulled the veil aside himself. "Now, if you please," he urged his companion.

The German student examined the wounded thigh while Calder held the light aloft. As predicted, it was a gruesome sight; the wound appeared to crawl with infection. The student quickly covered it up again.

"I cannot do anything," he declared. "Perhaps in a proper hospital, with baths and dressings... relief might be possible. But here? I couldn't even begin."

"Do these two men understand English?" the student asked.

"No," Calder answered.

"Then let me tell you something. He did not sustain this injury by falling from a palm tree. That is a lie. This wound was inflicted by the blade of a spear or a similar weapon."

"Are you certain?"

"Yes."

Calder knelt suddenly beside the Arab on the *angareb*. Though the man never moved, he was conscious. Calder had been watching him intently and noticed his eyes tracking the movement of Calder's lips.

"You understand English?" Calder asked softly.

The Arab could not speak, but a look of profound comprehension flashed in his eyes.

"Where do you come from?"

His lips trembled, but no sound emerged. His eyes, however, pleaded silently. They conveyed a desperate eagerness to answer.

Calder dropped to his knees, bringing the lantern close. He began listing towns.

"Dongola?" No reaction.
"Metemneh? Berber? Omdurman?"

At the mention of "Omdurman," the Arab's eyes closed.

"You were wounded there? No. Where then? At Berber? Yes. Were you in prison at Omdurman and escaped? No. Yet you were wounded."

Calder sank back, his mind racing. Excitement surged through him. Leaning closer to the prisoner's ear, he lowered his voice.

"Were you helping someone to escape? Yes. Who?"

"El Kaimakam Trench?"

"No."

Calder rattled off the names of other white captives taken at Omdurman. Each name met with a silent denial from the Arab's eyes.

"It was Effendi Feversham, then?" he whispered.

The eyes assented with startling clarity, as if the lips had spoken.

"That is all I can learn," Calder admitted, standing up. "I am also pledged to help Effendi Feversham," he told the Arabs, but it was futile. The prisoner could not speak his own name, nor could his companions offer any more details. Whatever secrets they held, they refused to share them, clinging stubbornly to a fabricated story that absolved them of responsibility. Relatives in Korosko had simply asked them to transport the unknown stranger to Assouan, and they had agreed.

Calder possessed the crucial piece of intelligence—the confirmation of Harry Feversham's fate—but it remained trapped behind a locked door of silence. He stood beside the helpless man on the *angareb*, watching the eager eyes that dared not open their mouths.

"Will he recover?" Calder asked later, turning to the physicians at the hospital in Assouan.

They shook their heads, uncertainty clouding their expressions. There was a slim chance, perhaps, but recovery would be agonizingly slow.

Calder resumed his journey toward Cairo and eventually Europe. The opportunity to aid Harry Feversham had slipped through his fingers.
