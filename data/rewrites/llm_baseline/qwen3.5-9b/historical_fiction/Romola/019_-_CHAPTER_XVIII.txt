When Tito departed the Via de' Bardi that afternoon, he carried with him a profound sense of relief, convinced that the looming danger had finally passed. With Romola and her father no longer pressing upon his senses, his mind drifted back to those agonizing hours of terror. He realized with a jolt that during those moments of panic, he had acted in ways he would never have chosen had he possessed true foresight. Specifically, he regretted selling his ring. To Romola, and indeed to anyone who knew him, the act of parting with a cherished gem would appear sordid unless accompanied by a plausible excuse—perhaps a claim that he intended to buy a special gift. At that critical juncture, Tito had felt a sickening weariness toward the necessity of pretense. Fortunately, fate had intervened; the potential fallout of that initial deception had evaporated, leaving him unburdened. He reasoned that since luck had granted him immunity, it was only fair to accept it. Who truly suffered? The consequences for Baldassarre remained too murky to weigh heavily on his conscience.

Yet, despite this freedom, Tito felt a gnawing desire to shed every invisible shackle that might chafe against his newfound liberty, particularly the subtle tether binding him to Romola. Unbeknownst to him, the very joy of escaping entanglement was dulling the moral sensibilities required to avoid future ones. Still, the sale of the ring seemed trivial compared to the lingering dilemma regarding little Tessa. She lived under a charming delusion, likely filling her small mind with hopes destined for disappointment. Should he seek her out privately to correct her misunderstanding immediately, or allow time and circumstance to reveal the truth? Innocent dreams were delightful, yet they often shattered under the harsh light of daily life. That sweet, pouting, round-faced creature was impossible to forget. Tito considered taking her a gift to gauge whether her stepfather had grown crueler since her mother's death. Alternatively, he might expose the truth to her first, then inform Romola, hoping to secure a better destiny for the poor child.

No, he decided firmly. The unfortunate episode involving the *cerretano* and the forced marriage, compounded by his negligence in letting Tessa depart under false pretenses, must remain unknown to Romola. Since no explanation could fully erase Tessa's belief, there would always be a risk of accidental revelation. Moreover, even a child like Tessa might harbor a touch of bitterness upon discovering her love had been misplaced—indeed, she was undoubtedly in love with him. This realization suggested it was safer never to meet her again. Yet, he acknowledged that such a minor adventure, akin to a rural girl's fleeting infatuation, would eventually fade when a local farmhand offered genuine affection, prompting her to drop her secret and discover her freedom. "Well then," he concluded mentally, "farewell, Tessa! My kindest wishes!" He resolved that the affair of the *cerretano* would yield no further complications for him. People often believed their personal resolutions were unbreakable, and Tito was no exception.

As for the fifty-five florins received for the ring, Tito had already devised a plan. He intended to execute a clever scheme that would explain the missing ring to Romola while simultaneously shielding her mind from recurring thoughts of monastic vows—a notion he found deeply repugnant. Carrying this purposeful thought, he made his way to the Via Gualfonda to locate Piero di Cosimo, the preeminent artist of Florence, renowned for his fantastical mythological designs.

Upon entering the courtyard where Piero resided, Tito noted the heavy iron knocker wrapped thickly in wool and secured with cords. Recalling the painter's habit of stuffing his ears against unwanted noise, Tito was unsurprised by this defensive measure against visitors' clamor. He began by knocking gently, then escalated to shaking the door vigorously. To no avail. Just as he prepared to retreat, blaming himself for wasting time when he could wait until meeting Piero at Nello's, a small girl emerged carrying a basket of eggs. She approached the door, stood on tiptoes, and slid up a small iron plate set in grooves. Peering through the opening, she called out in a high-pitched voice, "Messer Piero!"

Moments later, the sound of bolts retracting echoed through the air. The door swung open, and Piero appeared clad in a red nightcap and a loose brown serge tunic, his sleeves rolled up to his shoulders. He cast a startled glance at Tito but, without acknowledging him further, reached out to take the basket from the child. After re-entering the house and emerging shortly thereafter with the empty basket, he asked, "How much to pay?"

"Two *grossoni*, Messer Piero; they are freshly boiled, my mother insists," the girl replied.

Piero extracted coins from a leather pouch at his belt, and the little maid trotted away, casting admiring looks upward at the imposing young gentleman. However, Piero's gaze lacked warmth as he addressed Tito. "What brings you to my door, Messer Greco? I saw you this morning at Nello's. Had you asked then, I would have informed you that I do not entertain strangers without prior knowledge of their business and agreement."

"Pardon, Messer Piero," Tito responded with his characteristic equanimity. "I acted without due reflection. My mind was occupied solely by your remarkable talent for conceiving whimsical inventions, and a sudden urge to commission something similar drove me here."

Piero's eccentric mannerisms were so notorious among the populace that this reception could hardly be construed as an insult. Even if Tito had sensed hostility, his natural inclination toward winning favor outweighed any spark of resentment. Piero made a habitual grimace, grinning broadly, stretching the corners of his mouth, and furrowing his brow to obscure his true feelings beneath a mask of defiance. "And what might this need be?" he asked after a brief pause. Internally, he felt tempted by the prospect of applying his inventive genius.

"I require a delicate miniature motif drawn from poetic fables, which you surely know how to synthesize," Tito explained. "It must be painted on a wooden case—I will demonstrate the scale—as a triptych. The interior may feature simple gilding; my request concerns the exterior. It is a favorite theme among Florentines: the Triumph of Bacchus and Ariadne, yet I desire a novel interpretation. A passage from Ovid will provide the necessary inspiration. Young Bacchus must be seated in a vessel, his head wreathed in grape clusters, wielding a spear entwined with vines. Dark-berried ivy should coil around the masts and sails; the oars must be thyrsi, and flowers should adorn the stern. Leopards and tigers must crouch before him, while dolphins play nearby. Furthermore, I wish to include the fair-haired Ariadne beside him, rendered immortal with a golden crown—an addition not found in Ovid, but you will grasp the vision. Above them, youthful Loves, skilled in your depiction, should shoot roses from the tips of their arrows..."

"Enough!" Piero interjected sharply. "I possess Ovid in the vernacular. Locate the passage myself. I dislike being suffocated by another's ideas. Come inside."

Piero guided Tito through the first chamber, where a basket of eggs rested on the open hearth amidst a pile of discarded shells and ash. Strangely juxtaposed with this sordid clutter was a low ebony bedstead draped carelessly with a rich Oriental carpet, appearing as though it had once covered the steps leading to a Madonna's throne. Nearby stood a carved *cassone*, or large chest, adorned with painted motifs. Beyond these essentials, the room contained merely casts, wooden steps, easels, and rough boxes, all festooned with cobwebs.

The adjoining space was even larger yet more densely packed. It appeared Piero was preparing for the Feast, for the double doors beneath the window—which allowed light to flood the studio from above—stood ajar, revealing a garden or rather a tangled thicket. Fig trees and vines sprawled wildly among nettles and hemlock, while a tall cypress rose defiantly from a stifling mass of yellowish mulberry leaves. This damp luxuriance seemed to seep inward; in one corner, amidst a chaotic heap of carved marble fragments and rusted armor, tufts of long grass and dark feathery fennel had taken root. A large stone vase, tilted precariously, appeared to pour forth ivy streaming across the floor.

Along the walls hung numerous sketches in pen and oil: fantastical sea monsters, dances of satyrs and maenads, Saint Margaret's resurrection from the dragon, Madonnas bathed in celestial light, botanical studies, and grotesque heads. On irregular, rough shelves, a few books lay scattered among drooping ears of corn, ox horns, pieces of dried honeycomb, stones encrusted with rare lichens, skulls, bones, peacock feathers, and large bird wings. Rising from the dirty floor litter were life-sized figures: one dressed as a Vallombrosan monk, bizarrely topped with a helmet bearing a barred visor; another smothered in brocade and skins hastily thrown over it. Amidst this eclectic still life, several speckled and white pigeons strutted or perched, too tame to flee at the approach of humans. Three plump toads crawled intimately near the threshold, while a white rabbit, seemingly the model for the creature frightening Cupid in the central painting of Mars and Venus, twitched its nose contentedly atop a box filled with bran.
