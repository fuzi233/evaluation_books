To understand the architecture of Mr. Yorke's household, one must first grasp the absolute duality of his spirit, a paradox that defied the simple categorization of his neighbors. Cheerfulness, it appears, is a volatile compound, dependent as much on the internal landscape of the self as on the external weather of the world. Thus, when Mr. Helstone and Mr. Moore emerged from the mill-yard gates, leading their diminutive procession, they moved with a vitality that seemed entirely disconnected from the drizzling night and the perilous expedition awaiting them. As the lanterns they carried swung low, casting fractured light upon their faces, a peculiar spark ignited in Mr. Moore's eyes—a lively, defiant flame—and a newfound vivacity bloomed across his dark countenance. Simultaneously, the rector's visage, usually etched with severity, softened into a mask of agrin and ashine glee. Yet, to any observer attuned to the logic of survival, such elation amidst the rain and the threat of mob violence appeared irrational. Had the workers of Stilbro' Moor glimpsed this party, they would have found immense satisfaction in taking aim from behind the walls, for the leaders knew this danger intimately. And yet, possessing steely nerves and steady hearts, they were intoxicated by the thrill of the confrontation.

I am acutely aware, reader, that a parson should ideally be a man of peace, a figure dedicated to reconciliation rather than conflict. I recall clearly the sacred commission entrusted to the clergyman, the servant of Christ whose message demands gentleness. Nevertheless, if you harbor a hatred for the priesthood, do not expect me to walk your dismal, downward path of anathema. Do not expect me to join your narrow, sweeping condemnations against "the cloth," nor to inflame my lungs with the venom of a Supplehough or a Barraclough in horror at the diabolical rector of Briarfield.

For he was not diabolical. The tragedy lay solely in his misplaced vocation; he was born a soldier, forged by circumstance into a priest. In truth, he was a conscientious, hard-headed, hard-handed man—brave, stern, implacable, and faithful. He lacked sympathy, was ungentle, prejudiced, and rigid, yet he remained true to his principles, honorable, sagacious, and sincere. One cannot always trim a man to fit his profession, nor should one curse him because his role hangs upon him ungracefully. Nor will I curse Helstone, the clerical Cossack he undoubtedly was. Yet Helstone was cursed, vilified by many of his own flock, even as he was adored by others—a frequent fate for those who display partiality in friendship and bitterness in enmity, clinging equally to principles and prejudices.

United in purpose and buoyed by excellent spirits, one might assume Mr. Helstone and Mr. Moore would converse amicably as they rode side by side. Alas, these two men, both of hard, bilious constitutions, chafed against one another whenever their paths crossed. Their perpetual bone of contention was the war. Helstone stood as a high Tory, a loyalist of the old order, while Moore was a bitter Whig—or at least, a Whig insofar as his opposition to the war-party served his own interests. On this singular issue, he claimed any allegiance to British politics. He delighted in infuriating Helstone by proclaiming the invincibility of Bonaparte, taunting England and Europe with the impotence of their defenses, and arguing coolly that submission was preferable to destruction, for the French eagle must eventually crush every antagonist and reign supreme.

Helstone could scarcely endure these sentiments. He tolerated them only because Moore was viewed as an outcast, an alien with insufficient British blood to temper the foreign gall corroding his veins. Furthermore, a grudging respect for the dogged consistency of Moore's contumacy allayed his disgust slightly.

As the party turned onto the Stilbro' road, battling the scant wind and the stinging rain, Moore, braced by the raw breeze and irritated by the sharp drizzle, seized the opportunity to goad his companion.

"Does your Peninsular news please you still?" he asked.

"What do you mean?" came the surly retort from the rector.

"I ask if you retain faith in that Baal of a Lord Wellington?"

"And what do you mean now?"

"Do you truly believe this wooden-faced, pebble-hearted idol of England possesses the power to send fire from heaven to consume the French holocaust you seek to offer?"

"I believe Wellington will flog Bonaparte's marshals into the sea the moment he lifts his arm."

"My dear sir, you cannot be serious. Bonaparte's marshals are great men, guided by an omnipotent master-spirit. Your Wellington is the most humdrum of commonplace martinets, his slow, mechanical movements further hamstrung by an ignorant home government."

"Wellington is the soul of England! He is the rightful champion of a noble cause, the fitting representative of a powerful, resolute, sensible, and honest nation."

"Your 'noble cause,' as I understand it, is merely the restoration of that filthy, feeble Ferdinand to a throne he has disgraced. Your 'fit representative' is a dull-witted drover acting for a duller-witted farmer, arrayed against victorious supremacy and invincible genius."

"Against legitimacy stands usurpation; against modest, single-minded, righteous, and brave resistance to encroachment stands boastful, double-tongued, selfish, and treacherous ambition to possess. God defend the right!"

"God often defends the powerful."

"What! Did the handful of Israelites standing dryshod on the Asiatic side of the Red Sea outnumber the host of the Egyptians drawn up on the African side? Were they more numerous? Better appointed? More mighty, in a word? Do not speak, or you will tell a lie, Moore; you know you will. They were a poor, overwrought band of bondsmen. Tyrants had oppressed them for four hundred years; a feeble mixture of women and children diluted their thin ranks. Their masters, who roared to follow them through the divided flood, were a set of pampered Ethiops, as strong and brutal as the lions of Libya. They were armed, horsed, and charioted; the poor Hebrew wanderers were afoot. Few possessed weapons superior to their shepherds' crooks or masons' tools; their meek and mighty leader held only his rod. But consider, Robert Moore: right was with them; the God of battles stood on their side. Crime and the lost archangel commanded the ranks of Pharaoh, and which triumphed? We know the tale. 'The Lord saved Israel that day out of the hand of the Egyptians, and Israel saw the Egyptians dead upon the sea-shore'—yea, 'the depths covered them, they sank to the bottom as a stone.' The right hand of the Lord became glorious in power; the right hand of the Lord dashed in pieces the enemy!"

"You are all correct; you merely forget the true parallel. France is Israel, and Napoleon is Moses. Europe, with her overgorged empires and rotten dynasties, is corrupt Egypt; gallant France is the Twelve Tribes, and her fresh, vigorous Usurper is the Shepherd of Horeb."

"I scorn to answer you."

Consequently, Moore answered himself, adding an observation in a lower voice.

"Oh, in Italy he was as great as any Moses! He was the right thing there, fit to head and organize measures for the regeneration of nations. It puzzles me to this day how the conqueror of Lodi should have condescended to become an emperor, a vulgar, stupid humbug; and still more how a people who once called themselves republicans should have sunk again to the grade of mere slaves. I despise France! If England had marched as far on the path of civilization as France did, she would hardly have retreated so shamelessly."

"You do not mean to say that besotted imperial France is any worse than bloody republican France?" demanded Helstone fiercely.

"I mean to say nothing, but I can think what I please, you know, Mr. Helstone, concerning France and England; regarding revolutions, regicides, and restorations in general; concerning the divine right of kings, which you often preach in your sermons, the duty of non-resistance, the sanity of war, and—"

His sentence was abruptly severed by the rapid rolling of a gig and its sudden halt in the middle of the road. Both men had been so engrossed in their discourse that they failed to notice its approach until it closed upon them.

"Nah, maister; did th' wagons hit home?" a voice demanded from the vehicle.

"Can that be Joe Scott?"

"Ay, ay!" replied another voice, for the gig contained two occupants, visible by the glimmer of its lamp. The men with lanterns had fallen into the rear, or rather, the riders of the rescue party had outrun the pedestrians. "Ay, Mr. Moore, it's Joe Scott. I'm bringing him back to you in a bonny pickle. I found him atop the moor yonder, him and three others. What will you give me for restoring him to you?"

"Why, my thanks, I believe; for I could ill afford to lose a better man. Is that you, I suppose, Mr. Yorke, by your voice?"
