My days were suddenly filled with a purposeful rhythm, a blend of instructing others and sharpening my own intellect until there was scarcely a moment left for idleness. It was a refreshing change; I felt my faculties being polished rather than allowed to gather the dust of stagnation. The experience before me was vast. Villette is a cosmopolitan hub, and within Madame Beck's school gathered girls of nearly every European nation, spanning a wide spectrum of social rank. While the establishment was not republican in name, it practiced a rough equality in substance. At the desks, the young countess sat shoulder-to-shoulder with the young bourgeoise. Yet, appearances were deceptive. Often, the plebeian possessed a frank, courteous demeanor that the noblewoman lacked. The latter, however, held the upper hand in a delicate balance of insolence and deceit. There was frequently a dash of quick French blood mixed with the marshy phlegm of the north; the result was a glib, oily fluency in flattery and fiction—light, lively, and utterly heartless.

To give all parties their due, the native daughters of Labassecour possessed their own brand of hypocrisy, though it was coarse and transparent. When a lie served their needs, they delivered it with a careless breadth, untroubled by conscience. Not a single soul in Madame Beck's house, from the scullion to the director herself, felt shame in deception. To invent a falsehood was not a virtue, but it was the most venial of sins. "I have lied several times" was a standard item in every girl's monthly confession, heard by priests without shock and absolved without reluctance. Missing mass or reading a forbidden novel were different matters; these were crimes demanding rebuke and penance.

Though I was only half-aware of this moral landscape, I progressed well in my new sphere. After the initial perilous lessons, conducted on the edge of a moral volcano that sent sparks into my eyes, the eruptive spirit subsided. My mind was fixated on success; I could not bear the thought of being baffled by undisciplined disaffection in my first attempt at independence. Many nights I lay awake, devising plans to secure a hold on these mutineers, to bring this stiff-necked tribe under permanent influence. I saw clearly that aid from Madame was impossible. Her strategy was to maintain unbroken popularity with the pupils at any cost to justice or teacher comfort. For a teacher to seek her alliance during insubordination was tantamount to securing one's own expulsion. Madame took only what was pleasant and recommendatory, while rigidly requiring her lieutenants to handle every annoying crisis with promptitude, regardless of the unpopularity it might cause. Thus, I must rely solely on myself.

It was evident that this swinish multitude could not be driven by force. They required humor and patient endurance. A courteous, sedate manner impressed them; a rare flash of raillery did good. Severe or continuous mental application they rejected outright. Where an English girl of average capacity would quietly bind herself to a task of comprehension, a Labassecourienne would laugh in your face and reject it with, "God, how difficult! I do not want it. It bores me too much."

A skilled teacher would take this rejection instantly, smoothing every difficulty to the level of their understanding, returning the lesson modified, and laying on the lash of sarcasm with an unsparing hand. They would feel the sting, perhaps wince, but they bore no malice against this attack provided the sneer was hearty, not sour, and held up in a clear, bold type that exposed their incapacity, ignorance, and sloth. They might riot over three extra lines of a lesson, but I never knew them rebel against a wound to their self-respect; the little they had of that quality was trained to be crushed, and it preferred the pressure of a firm heel.

Gradually, as I gained fluency in their language and applied its nervous idioms to their case, the elder and more intelligent girls began to like me in their own way. Whenever a pupil was roused to feel the stirring of worthy emulation or the quickening of honest shame, she was won. If I could make their large ears burn under their thick glossy hair, all was comparatively well. Soon, bouquets began to appear on my desk in the mornings. In acknowledgment of this foreign attention, I sometimes walked with a select few during recreation. In conversation, I once unpremeditatedly attempted to rectify their distorted notions of principle, specifically expressing my views on the evil and baseness of a lie. In an unguarded moment, I stated that of two errors, I considered falsehood worse than an occasional lapse in church attendance. The poor girls, tutored to report in Catholic ears whatever the Protestant teacher said, reacted with an edifying consequence. Something—an unseen, indefinite, nameless barrier—stole between myself and these best pupils. The bouquets continued, but conversation became impracticable. As I paced the alleys or sat in the berceau, a girl never came to my right hand unless a teacher magically appeared at my left. Moreover, Madame's shoes of silence brought her continually to my back, as quick, noiseless, and unexpected as a wandering zephyr.

The opinion of my Catholic acquaintances regarding my spiritual prospects was naively expressed on one occasion. A pensionnaire, to whom I had rendered a small service, exclaimed one day as she sat beside me: "Mademoiselle, what a pity you are a Protestant!"

"Why, Isabelle?"

"Because, when you die—you will burn immediately in Hell."

"Do you believe it?"

"Certainly I believe it; everyone knows it, and moreover, the priest told me."

Isabelle was an odd, blunt little creature. She added, *sotto voce*: "To ensure your salvation up there, one might well burn you alive here below."

I laughed, as it was impossible not to.

Has the reader forgotten Miss Ginevra Fanshawe? If so, I must reintroduce that young lady as a thriving pupil of Madame Beck's. Upon her arrival in the Rue Fossette, a few days after my sudden settlement, she encountered me with very little surprise. She must have had good blood in her veins, for never was any duchess more perfectly, radically, unaffectedly nonchalant than she; a weak, transient amazement was all she knew of the sensation of wonder. Most of her other faculties seemed in the same flimsy condition: her likings and dislikings, her loves and hates, were mere cobwebs and gossamer. But she possessed one thing that seemed strong and durable: her selfishness.

She was not proud; and since I was *bonne d'enfants*, she forthwith made of me a sort of friend and confidant. She teased me with a thousand vapid complaints about school quarrels and household economy: the cookery was not to her taste; the people around her, teachers and pupils alike, she held to be despicable because they were foreigners. I bore with her abuse of the Friday salt fish and hard eggs, with her invective against the soup, bread, and coffee, with some patience for a time; but finally, wearied by iteration, I turned crusty and put her to rights—a thing I ought to have done in the beginning, for a salutary setting down always agreed with her.

I endured her demands on me in the way of work for much longer. Her wardrobe, regarding articles of external wear, was well and elegantly supplied; but there were other habiliments not so carefully provided. What she had needed frequent repair. She hated needle-drudgery herself and would bring her hose and other items to me in heaps to be mended. A compliance of some weeks threatening to result in an intolerable bore led me to distinctly tell her she must make up her mind to mend her own garments. She cried upon receiving this information and accused me of having ceased to be her friend, but I held by my decision and let the hysterics pass as they could.

Notwithstanding these foibles, and various others needless to mention—but by no means of a refined or elevating character—how pretty she was! How charming she looked when she came down on a sunny Sunday morning, well-dressed and well-humoured, robed in pale lilac silk, with her fair long curls reposing on her white shoulders. Sunday was a holiday she always passed with friends resident in town; and among these friends she speedily gave me to understand was one who would fain become something more. By glimpses and hints it was shown me, and by the general buoyancy of her look and manner it was ere long proved that ardent admiration—perhaps genuine love—was at her command. She called her suitor "Isidore"; this, however, she intimated was not his real name, but one by which it pleased her to baptize him—his own, she hinted, not being "very pretty." Once, when she had been bragging about the vehemence of "Isidore's" attachment, I asked if she loved him in return.

"As that," she said. "He is handsome, and he loves me to distraction, so that I am well amused. That is enough."

Finding that she carried the thing on longer than, from her very fickle tastes, I had anticipated, I one day took it upon me to make serious inquiries as to whether the gentleman was such as her parents, and especially her uncle—on whom, it appeared, she was dependent—would be likely to approve. She allowed that this was very doubtful, as she did not believe "Isidore" had much money.

"Do you encourage him?" I asked.

"Furious sometimes," she replied.

"Without being certain that you will be permitted to marry him?"

"Oh, how dowdyish you are! I don't want to be married. I am too young."

"But if he loves you as much as you say, and yet it comes to nothing in the end, he will be made miserable."

"Of course he will break his heart. I should be shocked and disappointed if he didn't."

"I wonder whether this M. Isidore is a fool?" I said.

"He is, about me; but he is wise in other things, *à ce qu'on dit*. Mrs. Cholmondeley considers him extremely clever; she says he will push his way by his talents; all I know is, that he does little more than sigh in my presence, and that I can wind him round my little finger."

Wishing to get a more definite idea of this love-stricken M. Isidore, whose position seemed to me of the least secure, I requested her to favor me with a personal description; but she could not describe. She had neither words nor the power of putting them together so as to make graphic phrases. She even seemed not properly to have noticed him; nothing of his looks, of the changes in his countenance, had touched her heart or dwelt in her memory—that he was "beau, mais plutôt bel homme que joli garçon," was all she could assert. My patience would often have failed, and my interest flagged, in listening to her, but for one thing. All the hints she dropped, all the details she gave, went unconsciously to prove, to my thinking, that M. Isidore's homage was offered with great delicacy and respect. I informed her very plainly that I believed him much too good for her and intimated with equal plainness my impression that she was but a vain coquette. She laughed, shook her curls from her eyes, and danced away as if I had paid her a compliment.

Miss Ginevra's school studies were little better than nominal; there were but three things she practiced in earnest: music, singing, and dancing; also embroidering the fine cambric handkerchiefs which she could not afford to buy ready-worked. Such mere trifles as lessons in history, geography, grammar, and arithmetic, she left undone or got others to do for her. Very much of her time was spent in visiting. Madame, aware that her stay at school was now limited to a certain period which would not be extended whether she made progress or not, allowed her great license in this particular. Mrs. Cholmondeley—her *chaperon*, a gay, fashionable lady—invited her whenever she had company at her own house and sometimes took her to evening parties at the houses of her acquaintances. Ginevra perfectly approved this mode of procedure; it had but one inconvenience: she was obliged to be well-dressed, and she had no money to buy a variety of dresses. All her thoughts turned on this difficulty; her whole soul was occupied with expedients for effecting its solution. It was wonderful to witness the activity of her otherwise indolent mind on this point, and to see the much-daring intrepidity to which she was spurred by a sense of necessity and the wish to shine.

She begged boldly of Mrs. Cholmondeley—boldly, I say: not with an air of reluctant shame, but in this strain: "My darling Mrs. C., I have nothing in the world fit to wear for your party next week; you *must* give me a book-muslin dress, and then a *ceinture bleu celeste*; *do*—there's an angel! will you?"

The "darling Mrs. C." yielded at first; but finding that applications increased as they were complied with, she was soon obliged, like all Miss Fanshawe's friends, to oppose resistance to encroachment. After a while I heard no more of Mrs. Cholmondeley's presents; but still, visiting went on, and the absolutely necessary dresses continued to be supplied, along with many little expensive *etcetera*—gloves, bouquets, even trinkets. These things, contrary to her custom and even nature—for she was not secretive—were most sedulously kept out of sight for a time. But one evening, when she was going to a large party for which particular care and elegance of costume were demanded, she could not resist coming to my chamber to show herself in all her splendour.

Beautiful she looked: so young, so fresh, and with a delicacy of skin and flexibility of shape altogether English, and not found in the list of continental female charms.
