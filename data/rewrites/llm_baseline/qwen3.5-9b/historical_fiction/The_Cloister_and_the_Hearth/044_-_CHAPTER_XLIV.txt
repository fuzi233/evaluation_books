Experienced women possess a calmness that frightens neither themselves nor others when a comrade faints; they do not hastily conjure supernatural explanations, but rather attribute the collapse to known physical causes, having witnessed them too often. Catherine moved with brisk efficiency. She laid the girl flat upon the floor, ensuring her head rested squarely on the ground, flung open the casement to admit fresh air, and loosened the tight bodice of the unconscious form. Only after these measures were complete did she turn to the door and call out, her voice ringing clearly through the room: "Come here, if you please."

Margaret Van Eyck and Reicht entered promptly, finding the young woman prone and Catherine striking her palms together in a rhythmic beat. "Oh, my poor girl!" cried Margaret, her face pale with alarm. "What have you done to her?"

"Me?" retorted Catherine, her tone sharp with irritation. "What has happened, then?"

"Nothing, madam," replied Margaret, though her cheeks flushed with rising anger. "Nothing more than is natural in her situation."

"You do well to speak so coolly," Margaret snapped, "especially since you are the cause of her situation."

"That I am not," declared Catherine bluntly. "Nor is any woman born."

"What!" exclaimed Margaret, indignant. "Was it not you and your husband who kept them apart? And now he has gone to Italy all alone. 'Situation' indeed! You have broken her heart amongst you."

"Why, madam? Who is it then? In Heaven's name! To hear you speak, one would think this was my Gerard's own lass. But that cannot be. This fur never cost less than five crowns the ell; besides, this young gentlewoman is a wife—or ought to be."

"Of course she ought," Catherine agreed coldly. "And who is the cause that she is none? Who came before them at the very altar?"

"God forgive them, whoever it was," said Catherine gravely. "It was not me, nor my man."

"Well," said the other, her anger softening slightly as she observed the girl stirring, "now that you have seen her, perhaps you will not be quite so bitter against her, madam. She is coming to, thank Heaven."

"Bitter against her?" laughed Catherine. "No, that is all over. Poor soul! Trouble behind her and trouble ahead of her; and to think of my setting her, of all living women, to read Gerard's letter to me! Ay, and that was what made her go off, I'll be sworn. She is coming to. What, sweetheart! Be not afraid; none are here but friends."

They raised the girl into an easy chair. As color began to creep back to her cheeks and lips, Catherine drew Margaret Van Eyck aside into a corner.

"Is she staying with you, if you please?" asked Catherine.

"No, madam."

"I wouldn't let her go back to Sevenbergen tonight, then."

"That is as she pleases. She still refuses to spend the night there."

"Ay, but you are older than she is; you can make her stay." Catherine leaned close, putting her mouth to Margaret Van Eyck's ear. It seemed hardly long enough to whisper a single word, let alone a full sentence. Yet, on certain topics, females can exchange thoughts between them with the speed of lightning. The old lady started, her eyes widening, and whispered back urgently:

"It is false! It is a calumny! It is monstrous! Look at her face. It is blasphemy to accuse such a countenance."

"Tut! Tut! Tut!" dismissed the other impatiently. "You might as well say this is not my hand. I ought to know; and I tell you it is exactly so."

Then, to Margaret Van Eyck's utter astonishment, she stepped forward, took the girl by the neck, and kissed her warmly. "I suffered for Gerard," she declared, her voice trembling with emotion, "and you shed your blood for him. His own words show me that I have been to blame—the very words you have read to me. Ay, Gerard, my child, I have held aloof from her; but I shall make it up to her once I begin. You are my daughter from this hour."

Another warm embrace sealed this hasty compact, and the impulsive woman departed. Margaret lay back in her chair, a feeble smile stealing over her face. Gerard's mother had kissed her and claimed her as a daughter; yet the next moment, she saw her old friend regarding her with a deeply vexed expression.

"I wonder you let that woman kiss you," Margaret murmured, half reproachful.

"Mother, or no mother, you would not have let her touch you if you knew what she whispered in my ear about you," Margaret continued softly.

"About me?" Margaret asked faintly.

"Ay, about you, whom she has never seen until tonight."

The old lady hesitated, choosing her words carefully, preparing to share her indignation, when an unexpected interruption silenced her. The young woman slid from her chair to her knees and began to pray piteously for pardon. From the fervor of her words and the anguish of her manner, a bystander would have gathered that she had inflicted some cruel wrong, some intolerable insult, upon her venerable friend.
