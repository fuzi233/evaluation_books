As soon as their visitors departed, Margaret ascended the stairs with impatience, donning her bonnet and shawl. Her sole purpose was to inquire after Betsy Higgins and spend whatever time remained before dinner sitting with her. Walking through the crowded, narrow thoroughfares, Margaret felt a profound sense of connection taking root; simply by learning to care for a resident of these streets, the environment had ceased to feel alien.

Inside the Higgins dwelling, Mary, the younger sister whose appearance was often described as slatternly, had managed to tidy the space as best she could for the impending visit. Rough-stoning covered the center of the floor, yet the tiles beneath the chairs and table, and those lining the walls, remained dark and unwashed. Though the air outside was stiflingly hot, a large fire burned brightly within the grate, transforming the interior into an oven-like chamber. Margaret failed to recognize this extravagance with coal as a gesture of hospitality; instead, she assumed the oppressive heat was necessary for Bessy's condition.

Bessy lay upon a small sofa positioned beneath the window. She appeared significantly weaker than the previous day, exhausted from repeatedly rising to gaze out the pane, hoping against hope to spot Margaret's approach. Now that her visitor had arrived and seated herself beside the bed, Bessy sank back into silence, her gaze fixed intently on Margaret's face. With a childlike wonder, she traced the textures of Margaret's clothing, marveling at their refinement.

"I never understood why people in the Scriptures cherished soft garments before," Bessy remarked. "It must be delightful to appear dressed as you do. It differs entirely from the common attire. While most wealthy ladies tire my eyes with their garish colors, somehow yours bring me peace. Where did you acquire this frock?"

"In London," Margaret replied, amused by the question.

"London! Have you been there?"

"Yes. I resided there for several years. However, my true home lies in the countryside, amidst forests."

"Tell me about it," urged Bessy. "I delight in hearing tales of nations, trees, and nature." Leaning back, she closed her eyes and interlaced her hands across her chest, settling into a state of perfect repose, ready to absorb every image Margaret might conjure.

Though Margaret had rarely spoken of Helstone since departing, save for incidental mentions, the place haunted her dreams with a vividness surpassing reality. As sleep claimed her, her memories drifted through its most cherished corners. Yet, her heart found an immediate openness to this young girl. "Oh, Bessy, I loved the home we left so dearly! I wish you could witness it. I cannot describe half its beauty. Great trees stand everywhere, their branches stretching long and low to create a deep, restful shade, even at midday. Yet, despite the apparent stillness of every leaf, a continuous rush of motion surrounds them—an endless hum. Sometimes the grass feels as soft as velvet; at other times, it is lush, nourished by the perpetual moisture of a hidden, tinkling brook nearby. In other sections, vast expanses of billowing fern stretch out, some in verdant shadow, others bathed in long streaks of golden sunlight, resembling the sea itself."

"I have never seen the sea," murmured Bessy. "Continue."

"And scattered throughout are wide commons, elevated as if soaring above the treetops themselves—"

"That pleases me," interrupted Bessy. "Down below, I felt suffocated. Whenever I ventured out, I desired to ascend high, gaze afar, and inhale deeply the fullness of the air. Milton makes me feel trapped, and the sounds you describe—the eternal, driving noise—would surely leave me dizzy; it was precisely that cacophony that caused my head to ache so terribly within the mill. Upon these commons, however, I imagine there is little disturbance?"

"No," Margaret assured her. "Save for a lark occasionally singing high overhead. Occasionally, I might hear a farmer shouting sharply at his servants, but the distance renders it merely a pleasant reminder that others toil diligently elsewhere, while I sat upon the heather doing nothing."

"I once believed that a single day of absolute idleness, spent in such a serene sanctuary as you describe, might restore me. Perhaps it would have revived me. Yet, now I have endured countless days of leisure, and I find myself as weary of them as I ever was of labor. At times, I am so utterly drained that I fear I cannot appreciate heaven without a prior period of earthly rest. I am somewhat terrified of arriving directly there, unprepared by a final, deep slumber in the grave to strengthen me."

"Do not fear, Bessy," Margaret said, placing her hand gently upon the girl's. "God can grant you a far more perfect rest than either the idle days of earth or the somnolence of the grave."

Bessy shifted restlessly. "I wish Father would cease addressing me as he does. He intends kindly, as I told you yesterday, and I repeat it now. Yet, observe: though I disbelieve him entirely during the daylight hours, by night—when fever grips me, hovering between wakefulness and dream—it returns with terrible force! Oh, it is so dreadful! And I think, if this existence marks the end, if everything I was born for amounted only to exhausting my heart and soul, and wasting away in this dreary locale with the mill noises ringing in my ears forever until I screamed to be silenced, denied the tranquility I crave... and with dust filling my lungs until I thirst for the clear air you spoke of... and Mother gone, unable to tell her again how deeply I loved her, and all my sorrows piled high..." She sat up abruptly, seizing Margaret's hand with a violent, fierce grip. "I suppose I could go mad! I could kill you, I truly could!" She collapsed back, completely overwhelmed by the surge of her emotions.

Margaret knelt beside her. "Bessy, we have a Father in Heaven."

"I know it! I know it!" she moaned, turning her head uneasily from side to side. "I am exceedingly wicked. I have spoken wicked words. Oh! Do not be frightened, and never return. I would not harm a strand of your hair. And," opening her eyes to stare earnestly at Margaret, "I believe, perhaps more than you do, in what awaits us. I have recited the Book of Revelation until I know it by heart, and I never doubt, when I am awake and sensible, of the glory awaiting me."

"Let us not discuss the fancies that haunt you during your fevers," Margaret advised gently. "I would prefer to hear of your activities when you were well."

"I consider myself well when Mother passed, though I have never possessed robust strength since that time. I commenced work in a carding-room shortly thereafter, and the fluff infiltrated my lungs, poisoning me."

"Fluff?" Margaret queried.

"Fluff," Bessy repeated. "Tiny fragments that detach from the cotton during carding, filling the air until it resembles a thick layer of fine white dust. They claim it coils around the lungs, tightening them. Indeed, countless workers in carding-rooms succumb to waste, coughing and spitting blood, simply poisoned by the fluff."

"But cannot this be remedied?" asked Margaret.

"I know not. Some establishments install a massive wheel at the terminus of their carding-rooms to generate a draft, carrying the dust away. Such a machine costs a fortune—perhaps five or six hundred pounds—and yields no profit. Consequently, only a handful of masters afford to erect them. Moreover, I have heard of men who, accustomed to swallowing fluff, refused to work in places equipped with wheels, claiming the absence of dust made them feel famished, insisting their wages must rise if they were to labor in such conditions. Thus, between the masters and the men, the wheels are never installed. I know I wished fervently for a wheel in our own factory."

"Did your father not know of this arrangement?"

"He certainly did, and he regretted it. Nevertheless, our factory was, on the whole, a decent establishment, staffed by a steady, reliable group of individuals. Furthermore, Father feared sending me to a foreign location. Although you would scarcely believe it now, many considered me a respectable girl at the time. I was reluctant to be deemed weak and delicate, and Mary required schooling, as Mother insisted. Father also harbored a penchant for purchasing books and attending various lectures—all expenses that consumed funds. Therefore, I continued working until I shall never, in this life, rid myself of the whirring in my ears or the fluff in my throat. That is all."

"How old are you?" Margaret asked.

"Nineteen, approaching July."

"And I, too, am nineteen." Margaret reflected more sorrowfully than Bessy regarding the disparity between them.
