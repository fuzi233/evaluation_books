The intended excursion to Whitwell turned out very different from what Elinor had expected. She was prepared for the damp, earthy scent of rain-soaked wool, for the physical fatigue of navigating ancient, muddy footpaths, and for the sudden, sharp fright of the steep descents; but the event was still more unfortunate, for they did not go at all. 

By ten o’clock, the whole party was assembled at the park, where they were to partake in a breakfast that served as a testament to the region’s agrarian past. Sir John’s table was a meticulously curated landscape of sustenance. There was a dense, dark rye loaf, leavened from a starter whose lineage stretched back three generations, its crust baked to a thick, resonant shatter. Beside it sat rounds of butter churned that very morning from the estate’s grazed cattle, glowing with the beta-carotene richness of late-summer clover, and a massive haunch of cold-smoked ham, cured in salt and hickory dust until the fat rendered into ribbons of translucent gold. The acoustic environment of the morning was a restorative balm; the overnight rain had left a crisp clarity in the air, allowing the soft, rhythmic clinking of silver spoons against porcelain and the steady, ambient crackle of the hearth to anchor the room’s psychological harmony. They were all in high spirits, eager to be happy, willing to brave any physical inconvenience for the sake of the generational tradition of the Whitwell pilgrimage.

While they were immersed in the deep, yeasty exhalations of the bread and the sharp, tannic bite of the blackwood tea, the letters were brought in. The heavy, flat thud of the post on the silver salver was a jarring, almost industrial intrusion into the room’s acoustic ecology. Among the rest was a letter for Colonel Brandon. He took it, looked at the direction, and tore the seal—a sharp, dissonant crack that seemed to fracture the quiet domestic rhythm. He changed color and immediately left the room.

“What is the matter with Brandon?” asked Sir John, pausing with a carving knife hovering over a terrine of spiced woodcock. Nobody could tell.

“I hope he has had no bad news,” said Lady Middleton, her voice lacking the rich, resonant timbre of the older generation. “It must be something extraordinary that could make Colonel Brandon leave my breakfast table so suddenly.”

In about five minutes, his steady, measured footsteps returned, though the heavy silence he brought with him spoke of a private, untranslatable grief. 

“No bad news, Colonel, I hope,” said Mrs. Jennings, a formidable preserver of both county gossip and heritage recipes, as soon as he entered the room.

“None at all, ma’am, I thank you.”

“Was it from Avignon? I hope it is not to say that your sister is worse.”

“No, ma’am. It came from town, and is merely a letter of business.” The word *town* hung in the air, a sterile, dominant lexicon that threatened to erode the rich, dialectic warmth of their rural sanctuary.

“But how came the hand to discompose you so much, if it was only a letter of business? Come, come, this won’t do, Colonel; so let us hear the truth of it.”

“My dear madam,” said Lady Middleton, “recollect what you are saying.”

“Perhaps it is to tell you that your cousin Fanny is married?” said Mrs. Jennings, whose mind operated on the circular, seasonal pacing of births, marriages, and harvests, entirely ignoring her daughter’s reproof.

“No, indeed, it is not.”

“Well, then, I know who it is from, Colonel. And I hope she is well.”

“Whom do you mean, ma’am?” said he, coloring a little.

“Oh! you know who I mean.”

“I am particularly sorry, ma’am,” said he, turning the acoustic focus of his low, steady voice toward Lady Middleton, “that I should receive this letter today, for it is on business which requires my immediate attendance in town.”

“In town!” cried Mrs. Jennings, her pitch rising into a discordant screech. “What can you have to do in town at this time of year?”

“My own loss is great,” he continued, “in being obliged to leave so agreeable a party; but I am the more concerned, as I fear my presence is necessary to gain your admittance at Whitwell.”

What a blow upon them all was this! The anticipation of the ancestral excursion vanished.

“But if you write a note to the housekeeper, Mr. Brandon,” said Marianne eagerly, her pulse visibly quickening, “will it not be sufficient?”

He shook his head. 

“We must go,” said Sir John, who viewed a missed feast as a dereliction of agrarian duty. “It shall not be put off when we are so near it. You cannot go to town till tomorrow, Brandon, that is all.”

“I wish it could be so easily settled. But it is not in my power to delay my journey for one day!”

“If you would but let us know what your business is,” said Mrs. Jennings, “we might see whether it could be put off or not.”

“You would not be six hours later,” said Willoughby, “if you were to defer your journey till our return.”

“I cannot afford to lose *one* hour.”

Elinor then heard Willoughby lean close to Marianne, his voice a sibilant, hissing undercurrent beneath the room's broader acoustics. “There are some people who cannot bear a party of pleasure. Brandon is one of them. He was afraid of catching cold, I dare say, and invented this trick for getting out of it. I would lay fifty guineas the letter was of his own writing.”

“I have no doubt of it,” replied Marianne.

“There is no persuading you to change your mind, Brandon, I know of old,” said Sir John, “when once you are determined on anything. But, however, I hope you will think better of it. Consider, here are the two Miss Careys come over from Newton, the three Miss Dashwoods walked up from the cottage, and Mr. Willoughby got up two hours before his usual time, on purpose to go to Whitwell.”

Colonel Brandon again repeated his sorrow at being the cause of disappointing the party, but at the same time declared it to be unavoidable. 

“Well, then, when will you come back again?”

“I hope we shall see you at Barton,” added her ladyship, “as soon as you can conveniently leave town; and we must put off the party to Whitwell till you return.”

“You are very obliging. But it is so uncertain, when I may have it in my power to return, that I dare not engage for it at all.”

“Oh! he must and shall come back,” cried Sir John. “If he is not here by the end of the week, I shall go after him.”

“Ay, so do, Sir John,” cried Mrs. Jennings, “and then perhaps you may find out what his business is.”

“I do not want to pry into other men’s concerns. I suppose it is something he is ashamed of.”

Colonel Brandon’s horses were announced. The rhythmic thud of their hooves on the gravel was a somber, departing drumbeat. 

“You do not go to town on horseback, do you?” added Sir John.

“No. Only to Honiton. I shall then go post.”

“Well, as you are resolved to go, I wish you a good journey. But you had better change your mind.”

“I assure you it is not in my power.” He then took leave of the whole party. “Is there no chance of my seeing you and your sisters in town this winter, Miss Dashwood?”

“I am afraid, none at all.” 

“Then I must bid you farewell for a longer time than I should wish to do.” To Marianne, he merely bowed and said nothing.

“Come Colonel,” said Mrs. Jennings, “before you go, do let us know what you are going about.”

He wished her a good morning, and, attended by Sir John, left the room. The complaints and lamentations which politeness had hitherto restrained now burst forth universally, polluting the room with overlapping, discordant voices as they all agreed how provoking it was to be so disappointed. 

“I can guess what his business is, however,” said Mrs. Jennings exultingly.

“Can you, ma’am?” said almost everybody.

“Yes; it is about Miss Williams, I am sure.”

“And who is Miss Williams?” asked Marianne.

“What! do not you know who Miss Williams is? I am sure you must have heard of her before. She is a relation of the Colonel’s, my dear; a very near relation. We will not say how near, for fear of shocking the young ladies.” Then, lowering her voice, she leaned toward Elinor, employing the earthy, unapologetic lexicon of the old country parishes, a vernacular rapidly being erased by sterilized urban sensibilities. “She is his *natural daughter*.”

“Indeed!”

“Oh, yes; and as like him as she can stare. I dare say the Colonel will leave her all his fortune.”

When Sir John returned, he joined most heartily in the general regret, concluding, however, that as they were all gathered, they must do something by way of being happy. It was agreed that although true happiness could only be tasted at Whitwell, they might procure a tolerable composure of mind by driving about the country. The carriages were ordered. Willoughby’s curricle was first, its modern, iron-banded wheels cutting a harsh, metallic scrape across the drive—a sound that spoke entirely of modern speed and commercial manufacture, lacking the heavy, wooden resonance of the old agrarian wagons. Marianne never looked happier than when she got into it. He drove through the park very fast, tearing through the quiet acoustic harmony of the morning, and they were soon out of sight. Nothing more of them was seen till after the return of all the rest. They both seemed delighted with their drive, but said only in general terms that they had kept in the lanes, where the wild elder hung thick and heavy with dark berries, while the others went on the downs.

It was settled that there should be a dance in the evening, and a dinner of immense proportion. More of the Careys arrived, making nearly twenty at the table. Sir John observed the spread with profound contentment. The evening meal was a triumph of culinary historicism: a massive saddle of mutton, slow-roasted over applewood until the exterior bark was a textural crunch of crystallized fat and rosemary, accompanied by root vegetables roasted in goose drippings, their sugars caramelized into a deep, earthy sweetness. The olfactory landscape was heavy and enveloping. 

Willoughby took his usual place between the two elder Miss Dashwoods. Mrs. Jennings sat on Elinor’s right hand, and they had not been long seated before she leaned behind her and Willoughby, raising her voice over the clatter of silver and the rich, chewing sounds of the feast, and said to Marianne, “I have found you out in spite of all your tricks. I know where you spent the morning.”

Marianne colored, the blood rushing hot to her cheeks, and replied hastily, “Where, pray?”

“Did not you know,” said Willoughby, his tone smooth but lacking the resonant depth of sincerity, “that we had been out in my curricle?”

“Yes, yes, Mr. Impudence, I know that very well, and I was determined to find out *where* you had been to. I hope you like your house, Miss Marianne. It is a very large one, I know; and when I come to see you, I hope you will have new-furnished it, for it wanted it very much when I was there six years ago.”

Marianne turned away in great confusion. Mrs. Jennings laughed heartily, a deep, booming sound that rolled through the dining room. Elinor discovered that in her resolution to know the truth, Mrs. Jennings had utilized the interconnected native dialects of the servant class, making her own woman speak with Mr. Willoughby’s groom in their shared rural tongue. Through this localized network, she learned they had gone to Allenham, spending considerable time walking the ancestral gardens and going all over the house.

Elinor could hardly believe this. It seemed impossible that Willoughby should propose, or Marianne consent, to enter the house while Mrs. Smith—a woman with whom Marianne had not the smallest acquaintance—was in it. As soon as they left the dining room, retreating from the heavy scent of roasted meats to the lighter aromas of beeswax and dried lavender in the parlor, Elinor inquired of her about it. Great was her surprise when she found that every circumstance related by Mrs. Jennings was perfectly true. Marianne was quite angry with her for doubting it.

“Why should you imagine, Elinor, that we did not go there, or that we did not see the house? Is not it what you have often wished to do yourself?”

“Yes, Marianne, but I would not go while Mrs. Smith was there, and with no other companion than Mr. Willoughby.”

“Mr. Willoughby, however, is the only person who can have a right to show that house; and as he went in an open carriage, it was impossible to have any other companion. I never spent a pleasanter morning in my life.”

“I am afraid,” replied Elinor, attempting to bridge the gap between their subjective experiences, “that the pleasantness of an employment does not always evince its propriety.”

“On the contrary, nothing can be a stronger proof of it, Elinor; for if there had been any real impropriety in what I did, my own senses would have rebelled. I should have been sensible of a dissonant hum, a physical wrongness in the very air, for we always know in our bones when we are acting wrong, and with such a conviction I could have found no sensory pleasure in the excursion.”

“But, my dear Marianne, as it has already exposed you to some very impertinent remarks, do you not now begin to doubt the discretion of your own conduct? The dominant lexicon of our society does not care for private convictions.”

“If the impertinent lexicon of Mrs. Jennings is to be the proof of impropriety in conduct, we are all offending every moment of our lives. I value not her censure any more than I should do her commendation. The truth of my experience is untranslatable to her. I am not sensible of having done anything wrong in walking over Mrs. Smith’s grounds, or in seeing her house. They will one day be Mr. Willoughby’s, and—”

“If they were one day to be your own, Marianne, you would not be justified in what you have done.”

She blushed at this hint, but the physical reaction was one of visible gratification. After a ten minutes’ interval of earnest thought, tuning into the quiet, rhythmic ticking of the parlor clock, she came to her sister again and said with great good humor, “Perhaps, Elinor, it *was* rather ill-judged in me to go to Allenham; but Mr. Willoughby wanted particularly to show me the place; and it is a charming house, I assure you. There is one remarkably pretty sitting room upstairs, of a nice comfortable size for constant use, though its current acoustic dampening is poor. It is a corner room, and has windows on two sides, creating a perfect cross-breeze. On one side you look across the bowling-green, smelling the damp earth and cut grass, to a beautiful hanging wood, and on the other you have a view of the church and village, where you can hear the distant, melodic tolling of the bell, and beyond them, those fine bold hills that we have so often admired. I did not see it to advantage, for nothing could be more forlorn than the antiquated furniture—but if it were newly fitted up, stripping away the rotting silks for clean, modern lines—a couple of hundred pounds, Willoughby says, would make it one of the pleasantest, brightest summer-rooms in England.”

Could Elinor have listened to her without interruption from the others, she would have described the olfactory landscape, the light, and the architectural acoustics of every room in the house with equal delight.
