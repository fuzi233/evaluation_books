Four days later Romola went along the Via Gualfonda toward the dwelling of Piero di Cosimo. The way thither lay through streets that had begun to feel like a narrow throat in which two breaths struggled for mastery. Here were French soldiers, planted in knots, staring as if Florence were a curious dish set before them—tasting it with their eyes, judging its temper; and here were Florentines, pausing with folded arms, letting their own glances answer back like the rasp of a file. The air had that strained, metallic clearness that comes before a storm; and underneath it ran the city’s sound—footfalls on broad flags, the clink of a spur against stone, the blunt thud of a staff, the burr of many voices gathered into a continual murmur that made the ear tired. Even the bells, when they spoke, seemed to do it with a kind of wary reserve, as if the old bronze throats knew there were foreign ears listening.

At first Florence had played the host with all the lavish cunning of a great house that understands ceremony as it understands bread—something that must be kneaded and offered at the right moment, fragrant, warm, flattering to appetite. There had been compliments, and feasts, and those Mysteries in the churches where clouds and angels were hoisted and made to float as if Heaven had been hired by the day. There had been balls, and suppers, and the Most Christian guest had scattered his attention among Florentine dames with a practised gaze, as though desire itself were a coin to be spent. But when the sweetmeats of festivity were cleared away and the talk became a talk of business and power, it appeared the new Charlemagne meant to eat Florence as one eats a conquered city—entering with lance in rest, speaking of leaving a viceroy behind, letting fall that he might bring back the Medici.

To Florentine minds, this tasted like sour wine poured into a cup that had been offered in good faith. Piero de’ Medici’s policy, disowned by the people, had been the only offence Florence had committed against France; yet the remedy proposed was the return of that very house. The logic of an “elect instrument of God” was singular. And Florence, whose pride was as sensitive as a palate trained on fine oil and clean wheat, was resolved not to submit. The resolve was being beaten out in consultations within the Old Palace; and outside, on the piazzas and in the streets, it began to show itself whenever a chance offered for some small flouting of a Frenchman too insolent in his air of conscious pre-eminence.

In such a temper of the city, the streets were no pleasant promenade for well-born women; but Romola, shrouded in black veil and mantle, with old Maso at her side, moved with a guarded security. If the world’s looks slid over her, the world’s sounds did not: she heard the sharpened accents, the quick laughs that were half a challenge, the sudden hush when a knot of men saw her pass. Yet her impatience drew her onward. She was going to Piero di Cosimo.

The copy of her father’s portrait as Oedipus—her father blind, yet still a presence, still a face that must not fade—was not finished. Piero was capricious in his work: now pushing forward with a feverish zeal, now leaving a painting for months as if it were a loaf forgotten in the oven’s mouth until it hardened past comfort. He might thrust a panel into a corner or coffer where it could lie like a buried bone, utterly unremembered. Romola was a favourite with him; he would bend to her wishes when they were before his eyes. But a general good-will was no safeguard against the sudden whim that made him turn away and let the world drift.

He had said a week before that perhaps the picture would be finished by now. Romola clung to that “perhaps” with nervous hunger. It seemed to her that if she did not get the image into her keeping, the lines of her father’s face—those lines the darkness had deepened, that listening look she remembered—might grow dim in her mind. And with this desire there was a sharpness of compunction: a sense of defect in her devotedness, a craving to be loyal not merely in deeds but in memory. Love, she felt, asks for completeness; it cannot bear a gap, even if the gap be only in the heart’s picture.

By special favour, Romola might intrude on the painter without warning. She lifted the iron slide and called “Piero!” in a flute-like tone—the tone of that little maiden who had brought eggs when Tito was present. The answer came quickly, and when the door opened, Piero’s quickness explained itself in a way that made Romola laugh.

“Ah, Madonna Romola—is it you? I thought my eggs were come; I wanted them.”

“I have brought you something better than hard eggs, Piero. Maso has got a little basket full of cakes and confetti for you,” she said, smiling as she drew back her veil. She took the basket from Maso, uncovered it, and stepped within. “I know you like these things when they come to you without trouble. Confess you do.”

“Yes—when they come to me as easily as the light does,” said Piero, folding his arms and looking down at the white and amber sweets, the crisp little cakes dusted like flour, the sugared seeds that crack between the teeth and send up a faint breath of fennel and anise. He lifted his eyes to her face and hair with the painter’s frank delight; her hood, dragged backward by the weight of her veil, had fallen so that the light touched her as it touched fresh bread—revealing.

“And they are come along with the light now,” he added. Then, with a suspicious narrowing of the eyelids, “But I know what the sweetmeats are for. They are to stop my mouth while you scold me.”

“Well—go on into the next room,” he continued, already turning, “and you will see I have done something to the picture since you saw it, though it’s not finished yet. But I didn’t promise, you know. I take care not to promise:—

“‘Chi promette e non mantiene
L’anima sua non va mai bene.’”

The door that opened on the wild garden was closed now, shutting out the rough green and the freer air. Within, the painter had been at work. Yet not at Romola’s portrait. That stood on the floor propped against the wall. Piero stooped to lift it, peering at some detail as he took it in his hands to carry it to better light.

And in lifting it away he disclosed another picture behind it—an oil-sketch of Tito, smaller, set far within the larger panel like a secret within a secret. Piero, careless of where he left things and now intent only on placing Romola’s portrait on the easel, did not at first know what he had shown.

But Romola, catching the face in a flash, stopped as if a hand had seized her.

“That is Tito!”

Piero looked round and gave a silent shrug—the shrug of a man vexed with his own forgetfulness. Romola’s astonishment held her silent for an instant; then, as her eyes took in the whole—Tito’s handsome head, and with it the grim figure beside him, the noose-like rope, the crouched misery of the other face—she felt a puzzled alarm rise like bitterness at the back of the tongue.

“What a strange picture!” she said. “When did you paint it? What does it mean?”

“A mere fancy of mine,” Piero answered, lifting off his skull-cap, scratching his head, and making the familiar grimace with which he pushed away any chance of betraying feeling. “I wanted a handsome young face for it, and your husband’s was just the thing.”

He stepped forward, stooped, and lifted the sketch with its back toward her, pretending to glance at it as if it were a thing not worth attention before putting it aside. But Romola had already fixed on what pierced her—the rope, the old savage face—and behind it surged the remembrance of chain-armour, that cold, secret protection Tito had taken on the very morning in question. The painter’s image joined itself to her own fear, and the joining made a sharp, unmistakable sound in her mind, like two stones struck together.

“Don’t put it away,” she said quickly, going to his elbow. “Let me look again. That man with the rope round his neck—I saw him. I saw you come to him in the Duomo. What was it that made you put him into a picture with Tito?”

Piero’s eyes shifted; he saw no better shelter than a portion of truth.

“It was a mere accident,” he said. “The man was running away—running up the steps, and caught hold of your husband. I suppose he had stumbled. I happened to be there and saw it, and I thought the savage-looking old fellow was a good subject. But it’s worth nothing—it’s only a freakish daub of mine.”

He ended with an affected contempt, moving the sketch away decisively and setting it on a high shelf, as if to thrust it out of sight and mind alike. “Come and look at the Oedipus.”

Yet the very haste with which he hid the picture gave it weight. Romola felt it as one feels a coarse grain under fine cloth: the effort to smooth it only makes the roughness more certain. It seemed to say there was something unpleasant—something that told against Tito—in the circumstances out of which the sketch had grown.

But her pride and delicacy drew back. To press questions might seem to confess that she could harbour suspicion against her husband. Her voice, when it came, was quiet, held in as if she feared its own tremor might betray her.

“He was a strange, piteous-looking man, that prisoner. Do you know anything more of him?”

“No more,” said Piero. “I showed him the way to the hospital—that’s all. See now—the face of Oedipus is pretty nearly finished. Tell me what you think of it.”

Romola turned and gave her whole attention to her father’s portrait. The room smelt of oil and pigments, of damp wood, of the stale edge of eggs that had been broken and beaten for tempera in hard times; but the basket of cakes and confetti had begun to shed its own delicate fragrance—almond and sugar, a faint hint of citrus peel—softening the harsher odours, as kindness softens harsh speech. She stood long before the painted face, letting her eyes travel slowly over the brow, the lids, the mouth that had learned to hold itself in patience. The world’s noises seemed to sink away, and she heard instead the remembered sound of her father’s voice, the particular cadence of his Greek, the old words that had made a home within her even when his sight was gone. The portrait did not give her the language—no painting could restore that untranslatable music—but it gave her the listening look: the head inclined as if toward some inward utterance the eyes could never read.

“Ah,” she said at last, softly, “you have done what I wanted. You have given it more of the listening look.” She turned to Piero with bright moist eyes. “My good Piero, I am very grateful to you.”

“Now that’s what I can’t bear in you women,” Piero burst out, turning impatiently and kicking aside the litter on the floor—broken sticks, rags stiff with colour, a brush that had dried to a blunt tail. “You are always pouring out feelings where there’s no call for them. Why should you be grateful to me for a picture you pay me for, especially when I make you wait for it? And if I paint a picture, I suppose it’s for my own pleasure and credit to paint it well, eh? Are you to thank a man for not being a rogue or a noodle? It’s enough if he himself thanks Messer Domeneddio, who has made him neither the one nor the other. But women think walls are held together with honey.”

“You crusty Piero! I forgot how snappish you are,” Romola said, smiling through her tears. She reached into the basket and took out a crisp sweet—one that would crack cleanly and melt into syrup on the tongue. “Here—put this nice sweetmeat in your mouth.”

Piero accepted it with the surly gratefulness of a creature that prefers its pleasures hidden. His mouth worked, and the sugar softened him in spite of himself; his fingers went back into the basket for another with the inevitableness of appetite after long restraint. He had eaten nothing but hard eggs for a fortnight, and these cakes, light as if the flour had been sifted through air itself, seemed to him a festival.

“It’s good, Madonna Antigone,” he said, with his mouth full, letting the old playful name fall as naturally as a crumb.

Romola stood opposite him, and for a little while the new anxiety in her breast was suspended by the sight of this naïve enjoyment—by the simple truth that hunger, answered honestly, makes a brief peace. The city’s harsh murmur and the hidden sketch, the foreign gaze and the rope about the prisoner’s neck, all receded before the homely fact of a man eating.

“Good-bye, Piero,” she said at last, setting down the basket. “I promise not to thank you if you finish the portrait soon and well. I will tell you you were bound to do it for your own credit.”

“Good,” Piero answered curtly. He helped her with deft hands to fold the mantle and veil about her, as if dressing a figure for a picture. When he had closed the door behind her, he stood a moment with the sweetness still on his tongue, and thought, not without relief: “I’m glad she asked no more questions about that sketch. I should be sorry for her to guess that I thought her fine husband a good model for a coward. But I made light of it; she’ll not think of it again.”

He was too sanguine, as open-hearted men are apt to be when they attempt a little simulation. For the thought of the picture pressed more and more on Romola as she walked homeward. Outside, the street’s sounds met her again—the tramp of boots, the scrape of a chair dragged in some tavern, the sharp call of a vendor threading between groups, the French speech striking her ear with its alien consonants like stones in a loaf. The city’s acoustic life, once a familiar net that held her, felt torn and knotted; even the echo beneath an archway seemed to have changed, as if the very stone were learning a new language against its will.

She could not help putting together two facts: the chain-armour, and the encounter Piero had described between Tito and the prisoner—an encounter on the morning when the armour was adopted. The painter had given Tito a look of terror. Had he seen it—seen something true and caught it, as painters sometimes catch what words cannot hold? What could it all mean?

“It means nothing,” she tried to assure herself. “It was a mere coincidence.” Yet the assurance tasted thin, like watered wine. “Shall I ask Tito about it?”

Her mind answered at last, sternly: No. She would not question him about anything he had not told her spontaneously. It would be an offence against the trust she owed him.

Her heart answered, more faintly, as if speaking in a dialect she dared not let anyone else hear: I dare not ask him.

There was a terrible flaw in the trust. She felt herself afraid of any hasty movement, as men are who hold something precious and want to believe it is not broken—lest a single touch should make the crack widen, and the thing fall into irreparable fragments with a sound that all the city could hear.
