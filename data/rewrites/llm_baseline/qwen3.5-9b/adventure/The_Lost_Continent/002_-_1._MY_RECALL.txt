The public reception had concluded. The decree had been proclaimed, the name of Phorenice, the Empress, sung with adoration, and the new Viceroy installed amidst the vast, ponderous ceremonial that had accrued its pomp and majesty over centuries. Formally, I had surrendered the reins of government; formally, Tatho had taken his seat upon the snake-throne and donned the chain of gems symbolizing supreme authority. Then, while drums and trumpets blared a cacophony of proclamation, he rose to his feet to begin his first state progress around the gilded council chamber as Viceroy of the Province of Yucatan.

With folded arms and bowed head, I followed him between glittering lines of soldiers and a brilliant throng of courtiers, chiefs, and statesmen. The roof-beams quivered under the cries of "Long Live Tatho!" and "Flourish the Empress!"—cries uttered as duty bound—and the new ruler acknowledged the welcome with stately inclinations of the head. In turn, he approached the three lesser thrones of the regional governors in the East, North, and South, receiving homage from each as ritual dictated. I, the man whose arrival had deposed me, followed in his train with the prescribed meekness.

It was a bitter pill to swallow, yet those who hold high office learn to wear a passionless mask before the people. Twenty years prior, these same fine obeisances had been rendered to me. Now, the Gods had seen fit to turn the wheel of fortune. Yet, as I walked bent and humbly behind the heels of Tatho, though etiquette forbade noisy greetings directed at myself, it could not suppress the kindly glances falling upon me. They came from every soldier, every courtier, and every chief standing in that gilded hall, and they landed upon me with profound gratitude. It is rare for the fallen to meet such tender looks.

Custom dictates that on such great ceremonial days of succession, those present may bring forward petitions, accuse their retiring leader with immunity from vengeance, or propose theories for the future governance of the State. I confess, my vanity compels me to record that not a single voice was raised against me, nor against any item of my twenty-year tenure. Nor did anyone speak of necessary reforms. Even as we circled the chamber three times, all present offered their approval in generous silence.

One by one, the new Viceroy and the old marched with formal steps over the golden tiles of the council hall beneath the pyramid. The great officers of state left their stations to join our train, and at the far wall, we arrived at the door of the private chambers—an hour ago, my own domain.

Well! I possessed no home now among the wondrous cities of Yucatan. A bitterness welled within me, though I should have been grateful enough to return to the Continent of Atlantis with my head still firmly attached.

Tatho issued his formal summons: "Open ye to the Viceroy," a command required by ritual. The slaves within responded, swinging the massive stone valves of the door wide open. Tatho entered, I trailing at his heels; the others halted, offering valedictions from the threshold, before the heavy doors clanged shut behind us.

We proceeded to the chamber beyond. Only then, for the first time alone together and free from the forced etiquette of the court, did the new Viceroy turn, folding his arms meekly, and bow low before me.

"Deucalion," he said, "believe me, I have not sought this office. It was thrust upon me. Had I refused, my head would have paid the forfeit, and another man—your enemy—would have been sent out as Viceroy in your stead. The Empress permits that her will shall never be questioned."

"My friend," I answered, "my brother in all but blood, there is no man living in all Atlantis or her territories to whom I would rather entrust my government. For twenty years I have ruled this country of Yucatan and Mexico beyond, first under the old King, then as minister to this new Empress. I know my colony like the back of my hand. I am intimate with all her wonderful cities, their palaces, pyramids, and people. I have hunted beasts and savages in the forests; I have built roads and diverted rivers to carry shipping; I have fostered arts and crafts like a merchant; I have preached the cult of the Gods three times daily with my own lips. Through evil and good years, I have ruled here, striving solely for the prosperity of the land and the strengthening of Atlantis, growing to love these peoples like a father. To you, Tatho, I bequeath them, with tender supplications for their welfare."

"It is not I who can carry on Deucalion's work with Deucalion's power," he replied softly. "Rest content, my friend, that I shall do my humble best to tread exactly in your footsteps. Believe me, I came to this government with a thousand regrets. I would have died sooner than take your place had I known how violently this supplanting would trouble you."

"We are alone here," I said. "Away from the formalities of assembly, a man may give vent to his natural self without fear of tarnishing a ceremony. Your coming was sudden. Until an hour ago, when you demanded audience, I intended to rule longer. And even now, I do not know the cause of my deposition."

"The proclamation stated: 'We relieve our well-beloved Deucalion of his present service, because we have great need of his powers at home in our kingdom of Atlantis.'"

"A mere formality."

Tatho glanced uneasily around the hangings of the chamber, drew me toward its center, and lowered his voice. "I do not think so," he whispered. "I believe she needs you. Troubles lie ahead, and Phorenice requires the ablest men in the kingdom ready to her call."

"You may speak openly," I assured him. "Without fear of eavesdroppers. We are in the heart of the pyramid here, constructed entirely of solid stone laid by human hands—I oversaw every course. Besides, in Yucatan, we lack the niceties of your old-world diplomacy; we do not listen, for we consider it shameful to spy."

Tatho shrugged. "I acted according to my education. At home, a loose tongue invites a loose head, and there are those whose trade is carrying tales. Still, here is the truth: The throne shakes, and Phorenice sees the need for sturdy props. Hence, this proclamation."

"But why come to me? It has been twenty years since I sailed to this colony. From that day until now, I have not returned to Atlantis once. I know little of the old country's politics. The scant news that drifts across the ocean holds slender interest here. Yucatan is another world, my dear Tatho—a realm of new interests, new people, new everything. To us here, Atlantis is merely a figment, a shadow far across the waters. It is for this new world of Yucatan that I have striven through all these years."

"If Deucalion has little time to spare from his government to brood over his fatherland, Atlantis, at least, has found leisure to admire the deeds of her brilliant son. Why, sir, over yonder at home, your name carries magic. When you and I were lads, it was customary in the colleges to teach that the men of the past were the greatest this world has ever seen. Today, that teaching has changed. It is Deucalion who is held up as the model and example. Mothers name their sons Deucalion, considering it the most valuable gift they can bestow. Deucalion is a household word. Indeed, there is only one name nearly as familiar."

"You trouble me," I said, frowning. "I have tried to do my duty for its own sake and for the country's, not for the petting and fondling of the vulgar. Moreover, if there are to be names on everyone's lips, they should be the names of the Gods."

Tatho shrugged again. "The Gods? They occupy us very little these latter years. With our modern science, we have grown past the tether of the older Gods, and no new deity has appeared. No, my Lord Deucalion, if it were merely the Gods competing for men's tongues, your name would be a thousand times better known."

"Of mere human names," I countered, "the name of this new Empress should come first in Atlantis, our late Lord the Old King having now passed."

"She certainly would have it so," replied Tatho, and there was a weight in his tone that suggested more lay beneath the words.

I drew him to a marble seat and leaned in familiarly. "I am speaking," I said, "not to the new Viceroy of Yucatan, but to my old friend Tatho, a member of the Priests' Clan like myself, with whom I worked side by side in scores of smaller home governments—from hamlets to great towns—as we gained experience in war and the art of ruling, and so tediously won our promotions. I speak in your private abode, which was mine but two hours ago, and I demand an answer with that plainness we once shared."
