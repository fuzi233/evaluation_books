Mrs. Shaw harbored a visceral, almost physical aversion to Milton, a sentiment born of the city's oppressive atmosphere. To her gentle sensibilities, the town was a cacophony of noise and smoke, a place where poverty manifested in the grime of the streets and wealth in the garish excess of the gentry. Above all, she detested the disarray of male attire; not a single man, high or low, wore clothing that fitted him properly. Convinced that Margaret could never recover her shattered strength within these walls, and fearing a relapse of her own nervous ailments, Mrs. Shaw insisted on immediate departure. Her urgency was so compelling that Margaret, frail and broken-spirited, eventually yielded a reluctant promise. By the following Wednesday, she would depart with her aunt, leaving Dixon to manage the final arrangements for settling debts and closing the house. That Wednesday marked a somber milestone: the burial of Mr. Hale, a man now interred far from any home he had ever known, leaving behind a wife isolated among strangers—a prospect that tormented Margaret deeply. She feared that if she had not succumbed to her grief-stricken stupor in those initial, devastating days, she might have orchestrated a different future for her father.

Before the funeral, however, a letter arrived from Mr. Bell.

"My Dear Margaret: I intended to return to Milton on Thursday, but unfortunately, circumstances arise requiring me to fulfill certain duties as a Fellow of Plymouth College, preventing my absence from my post. Captain Lennox and Mr. Thornton are currently in town. The former appears to be a sensible, benevolent individual who has offered to travel to Milton to assist you in searching for a missing will; naturally, since you have not found it thus far, I suspect it does not exist, or you would have located it had you adhered to my previous instructions. The Captain further proposes transporting you and his mother-in-law home, and given her current condition, I perceive that he cannot remain away beyond Friday. However, your Dixon is trustworthy and capable of managing affairs until my arrival. Should there prove to be no will, I shall entrust the matter to my attorney in Milton, as I harbor doubts regarding this 'smart' Captain's acumen in business matters, notwithstanding his magnificent moustaches. A sale of effects will inevitably ensue; therefore, please designate any items you wish to reserve, or transmit a list subsequently.

Two further points remain. You are, I am confident, aware—or if not, your late father certainly did—that upon my demise, you shall inherit my monies and possessions. While I harbor no intention of dying prematurely, I mention this merely to clarify the impending transfer. The Lennox family appears exceedingly fond of you at present, though whether this affection persists remains uncertain. Consequently, it is prudent to establish a formal agreement stipulating an annual payment of two hundred and fifty pounds from you to them, contingent upon the mutual enjoyment of residing together. Naturally, this sum encompasses Dixon; ensure you are not unduly influenced into offering a higher figure for her services. Such an arrangement safeguards you against being cast adrift should the Captain desire sole possession of his residence in the future, allowing you to relocate with your assets elsewhere, unless, of course, I have already secured your services to maintain my household. Regarding attire, domestic staff, personal expenditures, and confectionery—which all young ladies consume until maturity brings wisdom—I intend to consult a distinguished acquaintance to determine your allowable budget, considering your father's estate.

Now, Margaret, have you not already fled the page, bewildered by the audacity of an elderly bachelor to dispose of your affairs so casually? I am certain you have. Yet, he possesses a rightful authority. He has cherished your father for thirty-five years, stood sentinel at his nuptials, and witnessed his passing. Furthermore, he is your godfather; lacking spiritual efficacy due to his concealed awareness of your superior moral stature, he seeks to compensate by providing material security. Lacking earthly kin, 'Who shall mourn for Adam Bell?' his entire existence revolves around this singular devotion, and Margaret Hale is not the sort of girl to deny him. Compose a reply by return post, containing merely two lines to indicate your decision. However, offer no thanks."

Margaret seized a pen, her hand trembling as she inscribed, "Margaret Hale is not the girl to say him nay." In her debilitated state, no other words presented themselves, yet she felt a pang of resentment at their necessity. Exhausted by even this minor effort, she collapsed onto the bed, striving to silence her thoughts.

"My dearest child, has this letter distressed you?"

"No!" Margaret murmured feebly. "I shall feel better once tomorrow has passed."

"I am convinced, darling, that you will find no respite until I remove you from this dreadful air. I struggle to comprehend how you have endured it for two years."

"To where could I possibly go? I cannot abandon Papa and Mama."

"Do not torment yourself, my love. Perhaps it was all for the best, though I failed to grasp the severity of your situation. Even our butler's wife resides in a more comfortable dwelling."

"It is occasionally charming, particularly in summer; one cannot judge it by its current state. I have been quite happy here," Margaret whispered, closing her eyes to forestall further discourse.

The house, though stripped of most furnishings, exuded a semblance of comfort compared to its former desolation. Evenings had grown chilly, and fires burned brightly in every chamber per Mrs. Shaw's orders. Her aunt doted on Margaret, procuring every delicacy and soft luxury she deemed necessary for her recovery. Yet, Margaret remained largely indifferent to these comforts; if they registered in her mind, it was solely as grounds for gratitude toward her aunt, who labored so tirelessly to accommodate her needs. Restlessness plagued her despite her weakness. Throughout the day, she wandered aimlessly from room to room, idly setting aside objects she wished to retain. Dixon trailed in her wake, ostensibly seeking instructions but privately charged with soothing Margaret into repose.

"These volumes, Dixon, I shall keep. The remainder may be dispatched to Mr. Bell; they possess intrinsic value, both for themselves and for my father's memory. As for this item... I request you deliver it to Mr. Thornton after I have departed. Wait; I shall compose a brief note." She seated herself abruptly, seemingly terrified of lingering contemplation, and penned:

"DEAR SIR,—The enclosed volume I am certain will be appreciated by you, particularly in honor of my father, to whom it originally belonged.

Yours sincerely,

Resuming her perambulations through the house, Margaret touched upon familiar artifacts from her childhood, embracing them with a reluctant tenderness. Though old-fashioned, worn, and shabby, they held a poignant weight. She spoke scarcely a word, and Dixon reported to Mrs. Shaw that "she suspected Miss Hale heard not a syllable spoken, despite her constant chatter, merely to distract from her intentions." The consequence of remaining upright throughout the day was profound physical exhaustion by evening, affording her the deepest sleep she had enjoyed since learning of Mr. Hale's demise.

At breakfast the following morning, Margaret expressed a desire to bid farewell to one or two acquaintances. Mrs. Shaw demurred:

"I am assured, my dear, that you possess no friends here sufficiently intimate to warrant such a visit so promptly, prior to attending church."

"But today is my only opportunity. If Captain Lennox arrives this afternoon, and if we must—and if I truly must depart tomorrow..."

"Oh, yes, we shall proceed tomorrow. I am increasingly convinced that this climate is detrimental to your health, rendering you so pallid and ill-composed. Besides, Edith awaits my arrival, and you cannot remain unattended at your age. No; if you insist on these calls, I shall accompany you. Dixon can arrange a carriage, I presume?"

Thus, Mrs. Shaw accompanied Margaret, bringing Dixon to attend to shawls and air-cushions. Margaret's countenance was too sorrowful to soften into a smile amidst these preparations for two visits she had frequently undertaken alone at all hours. She hesitated, nearly admitting that one of her destinations was Nicholas Higgins' lodging, hoping fervently that her aunt would decline alighting from the carriage and traversing the courtyard, risking exposure to damp laundry strung between houses.

A subtle internal conflict arose within Mrs. Shaw between her desire for ease and a sense of maternal propriety, but the former prevailed. With numerous admonitions to preserve her health and avoid fevers endemic to such quarters, her aunt granted Margaret permission to visit locations she had frequented previously without precautions or authorization.

Nicholas was absent; only Mary and a few of the Boucher children remained. Margaret reproached herself for failing to time her visit more judiciously. Mary possessed a direct intellect, though her emotions were warm and compassionate. Upon comprehending Margaret's intent, Mary burst into tears with uninhibited sobbing, rendering Margaret's attempts at consolation futile. She could only vaguely suggest the possibility of reunion at some indeterminate time and place, urging Mary to convey to her father how ardently she wished, should circumstances permit, for him to visit her after his evening labors.

"I crave a small token to remind me of Bessy."
