The "To Let" signs had leaned against the low fence for so long that the residents of the cramped triangular square couldn’t recall a time they hadn’t been there. They sagged now at jagged angles, like a row of rusted axes waiting to drop—yet never quite claiming a victim. Not that there were many victims to claim. The square was a forgotten pocket of the city, choked off by a tangle of alleys and tenements that had crept in over the years, leaving the old house stranded, a relic awaiting demolition.

It was a house of crumbling red brick, its façade adorned with the faded emblems of long-dead insurance firms—crowns, clasped hands, symbols of security that now seemed like grim jokes. The children of the square had swung on the broken gate until only the top bar remained, and the narrow alley leading to the entrance was scarred by the scrawls of vagrants on the boarded-up basement windows. Rain had warped the path, and the musky scent of animals clung to the damp stone.

For six months, Oleron had passed the place without a second glance, always taking the opposite side of the square. But one morning, by chance or some quiet pull of fate, he found himself pausing before one of the tilting signs. The agent’s name was still legible, along with a note—yellowed with age—that the key could be collected at Number Six.

Oleron hesitated. His current lodgings were already a strain on his meager earnings, and the constant division between his bedroom and workroom was a persistent irritation. The thought of a single, undisturbed space was tempting. He studied the house again—the blind, grime-coated windows, the sagging roof—then crossed the square to Number Six.

The door was ajar. He knocked. No answer. He knocked again, louder.

A gaunt man with a long nose finally appeared, chewing as if interrupted mid-meal. "I was saying grace," he muttered, as though Oleron had committed some sacrilege.

Oleron asked for the key.

"Lost," the man said flatly. "But you won’t need it. The door’s unlocked. A good shove’ll open any of the others." He wiped his mouth with the back of his hand. "I’m the agent, if you’re thinking of taking it."

Oleron returned to the house. The entrance yawned wide, the steps down to the cellar gaping like a throat. The staircase ahead was broad, its carved banister thick with dust. He climbed, avoiding contact with the walls, and stopped at the first landing. One door was boarded shut; the other gave way with a groan.

The rooms were empty, thick with silence. He walked through them slowly, running a hand along the walls, testing the floorboards. Then, without venturing higher, he left.

The agent named a rent so low it was almost suspicious. Oleron inquired about taking just a single floor.

The man shrugged. "You’d have to ask the solicitors. Lincoln’s Inn. Mention my name—Barrett."

The lawyers, when Oleron visited, seemed baffled by his interest in only part of the house. They offered him the entire property for a paltry sum, as if eager to be rid of it. It took time to convince them he wanted no such burden. Eventually, they agreed—provided the "To Let" signs remained, and provided he understood that if the whole house was taken, his tenancy would end without warning.

Oleron barely considered it a risk.

The next day, he returned, pacing every room, imagining the space as his own. Stripped of grime, repainted, furnished with the pieces he had stored away—it could be perfect. He visited the warehouse where his grandmother’s things had been kept, measuring, remembering. Then he arranged for the work to begin.

Two weeks later, the first floor gleamed in pale, ghostly white. Oleron moved in, polishing wood, arranging chairs, positioning the china cupboard with its delicate, lattice-paned doors. The ceilings arched high above him, faint stars etched into the plaster. The fireplace’s ironwork curled like blackened lace.

"Charming," he murmured to himself, wandering from room to room. "Absolutely charming."

He installed a lock on his door, sealing off his sanctuary from the rest of the house. Now, if he wanted a book in the night, it was only steps away.

Late passersby in the square might have glimpsed the glow behind his red blinds—or seen the sudden shift of light as he moved through the rooms, candle in hand, settling into his new home.

And perhaps, if they had watched long enough, they might have wondered why the shadows in the upper windows seemed to shift as well, though no one lived there.

Yet.
