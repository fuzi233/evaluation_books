XIV. The Meeting

“My beloved child,” he continued, his voice heavy with sorrow, “was slipping away from me—fast. The physician who had been attending her could not so much as slow the tide of her decline. I knew then that it was no ordinary sickness, though I dared not name it. He saw my distress and proposed a second opinion. I summoned a more experienced doctor from Gratz. Days passed before he arrived. When he did, he was both learned and devout—a man of great wisdom and calm demeanor.

They examined my ward together, then withdrew to my library to discuss her condition. From the next room, where I waited for their call, I heard their voices rise—not in debate, but in sharp contention. I knocked, and stepped inside.

The old doctor from Gratz stood firm in his diagnosis. His counterpart mocked him openly, laughing as if the matter were mere folly. The laughter died when I entered.

“Sir,” said the first physician, “your learned colleague seems to think you need a conjuror, not a healer.”

“Pardon me,” replied the Gratz doctor, his face darkening, “I shall speak plainly another time. But I grieve, Monsieur le Général, that my science cannot help her. Before I leave, I offer one suggestion—though I do so with great caution.”

He sat at the table, took up a quill, and began to write. I bowed deeply, heartbroken, and turned to go. As I did, the other doctor glanced over his shoulder at his colleague, then gave a slow shrug—his hand drifting upward to his temple.

That consultation left me no closer to salvation than before. I walked into the gardens, mind shattered. Minutes later, the doctor from Gratz caught up with me. He apologized for following, but said he could not depart without speaking further.

“I am certain,” he said, “that this is not illness. No natural disease bears these signs. Death is near—perhaps only a day or two remains. If the seizure is halted now, with care and skill, she may yet recover. But one more attack… and all will be lost.”

“And what seizure?” I begged.

“I have written it all,” he said, handing me a folded letter. “But I give it on one condition: you summon the nearest priest. Open it only in his presence. Do not read it alone—you would mock it. This is not a matter of medicine, but of life and death. If no priest can be found… then you may read it.”

Before he left, he asked whether I would wish to see a man who had studied this very subject—someone whose knowledge might prove invaluable after reading the letter. He urged me to invite him.

The priest was absent. So I read the letter alone.

At another time, perhaps, I would have laughed. But when every remedy fails, and the one you love fades before your eyes—what desperate hope will not seem reasonable?

The letter was monstrous. It declared that my daughter was being visited by a vampire. The punctures near her throat? The marks of those long, needle-thin teeth—famously associated with such creatures. The livid, blue-tinged spot she described? That, too, matched the legends. Every symptom aligned perfectly with ancient accounts of vampiric visitation.

I, myself, had always dismissed such tales as superstition. Yet here was a man of learning, a man of science, offering this as truth. And I was too broken to reject it.

So I followed his instructions.

I hid in the dark dressing room adjacent to her chamber, where a single candle burned. I waited until she slept deeply. My sword lay ready upon the table. At just past one o’clock, I saw it—a shadowy form, indistinct, crawling over the foot of the bed. It rose swiftly toward her neck, swelling into a pulsing, black mass.

For a moment, I froze. Then I lunged forward, sword raised.

The creature contracted, slid back down the bed, and stood on the floor—just below the footboard. Its eyes glowed with a feral, knowing hatred. And there, beneath the dim light, I saw her—Millarca.

I struck. She stood unharmed, near the door. I chased her—again I swung the blade. She vanished. My sword shattered against the wood.

I cannot tell you what followed. The house erupted in panic. Millarca was gone. But my daughter—she grew weaker still. By dawn, she was dead.

Now, standing in this ancient cemetery, the General spoke with trembling hands. We said nothing. My father wandered off, reading the names carved into weathered stones, eventually stepping into a side chapel. The General leaned against the wall, wiping his eyes, sighing like a man near collapse.

Then—voices. Carmilla’s voice, soft and familiar, and Madame’s gentle tone. They were coming. But they faded.

Alone again, I felt the chill settle deeper. The story he had told—of noble blood, of cursed lineage, of death that came not from disease, but from something older, darker—echoed through the silence. Around us, ivy choked monuments, and the trees loomed like sentinels. And I thought: *She is not here. Not yet.*

The General stared at the ground, his hand resting on the base of a crumbling tombstone. Beneath a narrow archway, carved with grotesque, demonic faces—the kind that made even the most rational tremble—I saw her. Carmilla stepped into the shadowed chapel, radiant, smiling.

I smiled back, about to rise—when the General roared.

He seized the woodman’s hatchet and lunged forward.

In an instant, her face twisted. The sweetness vanished. Her features contorted into something animal, something ancient. She crouched backward, eyes wide and black.

Before I could scream, he swung the axe.

She slipped beneath the blow—and caught his wrist in her tiny fingers. He struggled, but his grip faltered. The hatchet fell. She was gone.

He staggered, breath ragged, hair standing on end, sweat glistening across his face. He looked as if he’d seen the gates of hell open.

It lasted only seconds.

Then—Madame stood before me, pale and frantic. “Where is Mademoiselle Carmilla?” she demanded, repeating it over and over.

“I don’t know,” I stammered. “She was here—only moments ago.” I pointed to the doorway. “She went that way.”

“But I’ve been standing in the passage since she entered,” Madame insisted. “She never returned.”

She called out—“Carmilla!”—through every door, every window, every corner. No answer.

“She called herself Carmilla?” the General whispered, still shaking.

“Yes,” I said.

“Aye,” he breathed. “That is Millarca. That is Mircalla, Countess Karnstein. Go—my child—leave this place at once. Drive to the clergyman’s house. Wait there. Do not return. Begone! May you never see Carmilla again. For if you do… you will not find her here.”
