To truly grasp the architecture of Harry Feversham's desperation, one must first visualize the absolute erasure of his identity within the microscopic empire of the Wells of Obak. This was a landscape defined not by geography, but by the violent oscillation of nature itself—a place where the sun blazed with intolerable fury from dawn until dusk, only to retreat into a night pierced by winds that sculpted the sand into shifting pyramids and hollowed valleys, leaving the face of the country perpetually altered. Here, amidst the bleached skeletons of camels and the ghostly remains of a branch-woven hut, silence brooded like a physical weight, creating a vacuum of mystery where the only inhabitants were vultures and sand-grouse.

Yet, within this desolation, a secret defiance simmered. For five consecutive mornings, a solitary figure emerged from the treeline, driving two swift Bisharin mares to the well-mouth. He watered them, sat in watchful vigil for three hours, and retreated into the shadows, feeding them delicately on *dhoura* before vanishing. No one approached him; he was a phantom haunting the edge of the known world. But on the sixth morning, the equilibrium shattered. As the man prepared to withdraw, the silhouette of a stranger appeared against the sky atop a dune crest: a man leading a grey donkey.

The Arab, Abou Fatma, observed the intruder with detached scrutiny. He noted the grey beast, then fixed his gaze on the driver's exposed legs—a stark contrast of ebony blackness against the pale sand. The driver was a Negro. Abou Fatma lowered himself back to the sand, feigning an indifference that masked a calculating stillness. When the stranger descended, halting beside him, he spoke the greeting: *"Salam aleikum."* Carrying a long spear, a short one, and a hide shield, the stranger laid his weapons aside and sat.

*"Aleikum es salam,"* Abou Fatma replied, waiting.

*"Is it Abou Fatma?"* the stranger asked.

Abou Fatma nodded. Two days prior, the stranger explained, a Bisharin named Moussa Fedil had intercepted him in the marketplace of Berber. Hunger had driven the encounter, but Fedil, having fed him, had issued a command: deliver this donkey to Abou Fatma at the Wells of Obak.

Abou Fatma glanced at the animal with casual dismissal. *"Tayeeb. The donkey is mine."* He sat motionless, signaling the transaction was concluded. But the stranger held firm. *"I am to meet Moussa Fedil again on the third morning in Berber's market. Give me a token to prove fulfillment and secure my reward."*

From the small of his back, Abou Fatma drew his knife. Picking up a stick from the ground, he carved three distinct notches at both ends. *"This shall be your sign to Moussa Fedil,"* he stated, handing the stick over. The stranger secured it to his wrap, filled his water-skin at the well, and vanished up the sandy slope. Only then did Abou Fatma rise, his movements hurried and precise.

What followed was a revelation of profound strategic depth. Six days prior, Harry Feversham had departed Obak, trusting this grey donkey to carry his supplies across the fifty-eight miles of barren desert to the Nile. Abou Fatma led the beast among the trees, inspecting its left shoulder. There, a tiny incision had been made and stitched with fine thread. With surgical precision, Abou Fatma cut the stitches, prying open the wound to extract a package no larger than a postage stamp. Inside a goat's bladder lay a note written in Arabic.

Abou Fatma, trained during his years as Gordon's body-servant to read, unfolded the paper. The message was chilling in its clarity: *"The houses which were once Berber are destroyed, and a new town of wide streets is building. There is no longer any sign by which I may know the ruins of Yusef's house from the ruins of a hundred houses; nor does Yusef any longer sell rock-salt in the bazaar. Yet wait for me another week."*

The author of this note was Harry Feversham himself. Clad in the patched *jubbeh* of the Dervishes, his hair frizzed and matted like seaweed against his neck, he navigated the wide, dangerous streets of New Berber. To the south lay the old town, a warren of ruins where front walls had been torn down and roofs swept away, leaving only bare inner shells that merged into indistinguishable corridors of decay. Foxes already burrowed beneath the crumbling foundations. Feversham had calculated his stay to be a single night, intending to slip through the gate at dusk and vanish into the desert before the stars faded. Instead, he found himself moving steadily among the crowds, a man with urgent business, terrified that a stray word might betray him. He listened intently for the name Yusef, haunted by despair and a paralyzing fear.

A madness born of these emotions seized him—a fanatical obstinacy, a belief that the universe could not contain such injustice as placing this impossible burden on the one man least fit to bear it, only to discard him for his effort. It was on the morning three days after leaving Abou Fatma that the truth crashed upon him. Coming over a slope, he saw the sand stretch like a lagoon toward the dark brown walls of the town, framed by the towering, ominous foliage of date palms on the Nile bank.

Within those walls dwelt the Dervishes. The realization hit him with the force of a physical blow: it was sheer madness to imagine escape there, even for an hour. Was it right, he wondered, to attempt this? The longer he stood, the more sinister the low mud walls appeared; the vibrant green of the palms transformed from a symbol of life into an ironic invitation to death. Had he already done enough for honor by venturing so close? The sun beat down relentlessly, draining his strength as if his veins were severed.

If captured—and he knew he would be, oh, very surely—he imagined the fanatic faces closing in, the mutilations practiced upon dissenters. The great loneliness of the place struck him with such violence that his knees trembled. Turning abruptly, he began to run, leaping in a panic across the naked desert under the scorching sun, emitting feeble, inarticulate cries. But the very violence of his flight halted him after only a few yards.

Four years of anticipation meant nothing? All his schooling in the local tongue, his immersion in the bazaars—all for naught? Was he still the craven who had submitted his resignation papers? The quiet confidence with which he had unveiled his plan to Lieutenant Sutch in the Criterion grill-room dissolved into the mere vainglory of self-deception. And Ethne?

Collapsing to the ground, he drew his coat over his head, becoming a brown spot indistinguishable from the surrounding earth, an irregularity in the vast wasteland. Shutting out the present, he projected Ethne's face across thousands of miles of continent and sea. Soon, he was transported back to Donegal. The summer night whispered through an open doorway; nearby, people danced to music. He saw the three feathers fluttering to the floor; he read the deepening turmoil in Ethne's expression. If he could endure this ordeal, and the far greater trial awaiting him, perhaps someday he would witness that troubled face clear. Significant words echoed in his mind: *"I should have no doubt that you and I would see much of one another afterwards."*

As the sun began to set, Feversham rose from the sand. Walking toward Berber, he passed silently between the gates, carrying the weight of his secret mission and the hope of a future reunion.
