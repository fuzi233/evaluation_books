Flush with his easily won fortune, generous to a fault, and warmly hospitable to every soul he met, our young Virginian could not play the part of a gentleman of fashion without hosting a grand entertainment for all his fellow countrymen and fellow visitors to the Wells, according to the well-loved custom of the day. He booked the Assembly Rooms outright, sending invitations to nearly every remaining guest staying at the spa that season. Card tables were set up in the front parlour for all who could not bear to spend an evening without the pastime then ubiquitous across every drawing room in Europe; a supper chamber was heaped with crates of champagne and frothy spiced bowls of negus, while the great high-ceilinged main assembly hall was cleared entirely for dancing.

It was hardly a raucous affair, by our modern standards. The evening opened with minuets, reserved by unspoken rule for the highest ranking ladies in attendance. As the Earl's daughter, Lady Maria Esmond was the most noble guest present save for the lame Lady Augusta Crutchley, so Harry Warrington stepped out to dance the very first opening minuet with his cousin, acquitting himself so gracefully that the whole room broke into quiet applause, far outshining stiff, earnest Mr. Wolfe who had partnered Miss Lowther across the floor. The moment that dance ended, Harry turned next to blushing little Theo Lambert, asking for the honour of walking the following minuet with her. She glowed so bright with happiness as she moved through the stately steps that her parents could barely contain their delight, while Miss Humpleby, the vain daughter of Sir John Humpleby of Liphook, seethed on the sidelines, fully convinced she ought to have been the next partner called after an earl's daughter.

When the minuets drew to a close, the country dances began, their music played by a ragged trio of harp, fiddle and flageolet tucked high up in a dusty little balcony, plucking thin, melancholy tunes well into the small hours. Anyone who pulls out a tattered 18th century songbook today will marvel that anyone ever danced to such soft, plaintive strains. And yet the men and women of that era fell in love, frisked, laughed, and courted each other to those same sad notes, every air carrying a faint tang of amari aliquid, a hint of old forgotten sorrow. Perhaps it is the dust of the past clinging to them, their echoes drifting back to us from a limbo long abandoned. Perhaps they were bright and lively to the ears that first heard them; our own descendants, no doubt, will one day listen back to the chart-topping music of our time and exclaim, *Good heavens, was this what amused our forefathers?*

Harry's guest list boasted a true duchess that night: the famed, fiery Duchess of Queensberry, the muse of Pope and Prior herself. The old duchess pointedly turned her back on a certain other countess across the room, cackling loudly, glancing over her shoulder to jab her fan at the woman she snubbed. And yet nearly every other guest in the room bowed, scraped, cringed, and swarmed around that very countess, paying her far more deference than they gave Queensberry and all her sharp, witty airs. This was no other than the Countess of Yarmouth-Walmoden, the royal favourite whom King George II delighted to honour. She had met Harry on the promenade that morning, singled him out for great graciousness, and invited him to join her card table that night. Purblind old Colonel Blinkinsop, half deaf and half blind, had mistaken that invitation for his own and bowed so low he nearly toppled over, until she laughed and said, "Pooh, pooh, not you, old man! The young Firshinian!" and the whole crowd had clapped Harry on the back to congratulate him on his great good fortune.

Every respectable name in the room hovered around her that night: Lord Bamborough begged to take a seat at her quadrille party, the famously virtuous Lady Blanche Pendragon, the vaunted valiant Sir Lancelot Quintain, the pious Dean of Ealing, all the great generals, matrons, noblemen and high-born spinsters waited breathlessly for a smile from her, ready to leap up and fetch her a glass of negus at the flick of a finger. Lady Maria waited on her meekly as a lamb, and old Madame de Bernstein greeted the Hanoverian lady with profound, stately courtesy.

Harry had bowed to her no more than basic hospitality demanded, but Hetty Lambert chose to take fierce offence at the sight. She barely spoke a word to him all through the country dance they shared, and when he led her toward the supper chamber, she was no more communicative. To reach the refreshments they had to pass right by Lady Yarmouth's card table, and the countess called out good-naturedly to Harry, "Mr. Warrington, is your pretty little partner enjoying the dancing?"

"I thank your ladyship, I do not like dancing, and I do not like cards," Hetty snapped, tossing her head, dropping a stiff, block-like curtsey, and storming right past the table.

Harry was mortified, deeply stung. He was unfailingly courteous to every soul he met, and expected that same politeness in return, especially to an elder woman of rank. He saw the flash of indignation reddening his honest face as Hetty glanced sideways at him, well aware of the offence she had given, yet she still wore her artless, ingenuous little smile as she reached the sideboard.

"What a horrid, vulgar old woman that is, don't you think so?" she said brightly.

"What woman?" Harry asked, gruff.

"That German woman -- Lady Yarmouth -- that all these men are bowing and cringing to like dogs."

"Her ladyship has been very kind to me," Harry said, grim. "Won't you try some of this custard?"

"And you were bowing to her too, weren't you?" Hetty went on, unperturbed. "That negus you're drinking looks as if it has gone bad."

"It is very bad negus," Harry said, gulping it down anyway.

"And the custard is spoiled too!" Hetty cried. "I swear it's made with rotten eggs!"

"I am very sorry the entertainment and the company are not to your liking, Hester," poor Harry groaned.

"Oh, it's no fault of yours, I'm sure," she tossed back, flicking her curly little head. "You could not help it."

Harry clenched his fists, grinding his teeth, his whole frame tight with fury. "You look quite upset. Shall we go find your mother now?"

"Yes, let's go to your mother," he bit out, nearly stumbling over an unlucky waiter loitering in his path. "Curse you, get out of my way!"

"La, is that the way you speak to folks in Virginia?" Hetty teased.

"We are rough sometimes, there, madam," Harry said slowly, fire flashing from his eyes. "And it is hard not to be disturbed." Hetty could barely see straight the rest of the walk to her mother, never having seen him look so tall, so handsome, so noble in his anger. She told Mrs. Lambert she was just overheated, curtsied faintly to Harry, who bowed so deep his wig slipped an inch, and stormed off back to circulate among his other guests.

He was soon jolted out of his ill temper by a brewing altercation between his aunt Madame de Bernstein and the Duchess of Queensberry. As Lady Yarmouth passed the duchess's group, the old noblewoman gave her a withering stare out of eyes that had once set the whole ton ablaze, before breaking into peals of affected giggles, firing off a ceaseless stream of sneers and jabs at the royal favourite. Lady Yarmouth kept her eyes fixed on her cards, appearing not to notice the insult. The feud between the Duchess of Queensberry and the Hanoverian royal family stretched back decades.

"Look at all of you bowing down to that idol! Do not lie to me, you are as bad as the rest, my good Bernstein!" the Duchess cackled. "What a fine Christian country we live in! Your first husband the Bishop would have loved to see this sight!"

"Forgive me, your Grace, if I fail to follow your meaning," Bernstein said, playing demurely dumb.

"We are both old now, my dear, or else we pretend not to understand when we do not choose to," the Duchess laughed. "That is the way of us women. I was saying what fine Christians we are, because we are so wonderfully forgiving. Don't you remember the story in the scriptures, how the Pharisees wanted to stone the woman taken in sin? Far from stoning such a woman now, we all crawl on our knees for her! Any man in this room would go crawling around the whole floor on his hands and knees if yonder creature bade him!"

"Madam," Harry cut in, stepping forward with spirit, "I can name two or three men here who would do no such thing."

"Quick, bring them to me, and let me hug them to my heart!" the Duchess crowed. "Which are they, my dear Virginian? We shall make a party of four honest folk, if we can only find two more partners!"

"Here are three of us already," Bernstein said, with a forced laugh. "We can play a dummy hand."

"Where is the third?" the Duchess asked, looking around.

"Madam," Bernstein said, drawing herself up. "I will leave your Grace to boast of your own perfect honesty, but I will thank you not to cast doubts on mine before my own nephew and children!"

"See how she fires up at a mere jest!" the Duchess said, grinning. "I am sure you are as honest as the rest of the company, my dear creature."

"Which may not be good enough for your Grace of Queensberry and Dover, who might have chosen to stay away if you thought so poorly of us," Bernstein returned, with a stately curtsey. "But it is the very best my nephew could procure, and he has given you all that he has. He is not used to our tricky ways, madam."

"She is right to teach you, Harry," the Duchess said, rapping her fan against her palm. "To treat every guest, old or young, rich or poor, with equal welcome. That is the Virginian way, is it not? And we two old girls were friends long ago, when we were young, and there is no sense in quarrelling now that we are both grey." With that, the two dowagers exchanged a final grand curtsey, and the looming battle vanished entirely, before a single sharp word could spark an all-out war.

"Egad, that was like the standoff between Byng and Galissoniere!" Chaplain Sampson laughed the next morning, as Harry recounted every detail of the night's events to him. "I thought no power on earth could have kept those two seventy-four-gun warships from opening fire on each other!"

"And your Baroness Bernstein sailed clean out of the line of fire with the most perfect skill imaginable," Harry grinned. "Why should she be afraid? I have heard you say she is wittier than any woman alive, and has no reason to fear the tongue of any dowager in England."

Sampson hemmed, and hesitated. He knew very well why Bernstein had chosen to make peace, rather than join the Duchess in mocking Lady Yarmouth: her own long, scandal-ridden past meant every jibe levelled at the royal favourite would have landed squarely on her own reputation too.

"Sir," Harry cried, his face flushing bright with anger, "you do not mean to say there is any whisper against the character of my aunt, the Baroness de Bernstein!"

The chaplain stared at the young Virginian's perfect, unjaded innocence, and hesitated to speak further. "Stories much older than you or I, my boy. The sort of gossip that attaches to every soul in this wicked world, de me, de te. You yourself have had more than your fair share of slander since you arrived here, after all. No one escapes it. The best we can do is keep a clear conscience, and forgive."

"Forgive? That is your cloth, sir, but by George, I will not!" Harry thumped his fist down on the table. "Let any scoundrel say a single word against that dear old lady in my hearing, and I will pull his nose as sure as my name is Harry Esmond." It was at that exact moment that Colonel Lambert walked in, followed by his slow-moving servant Gumbo, just in time to see Harry's fist come down.

"Who is the poor fellow whose nose you are pulling now, Mr. Warrington?" the Colonel laughed.

Sampson winked at Lambert, signalling not to disillusion the boy about his aunt. "I was only telling Mr. Harry that every soul in this town is the victim of slander," the chaplain said, in his most unctuous clerical tone.

"True enough," Lambert nodded, playing along. "No one escapes it. Remember that silly story that went around about you and the dancing girl, Harry? The only soul in all our party who refused to believe a word of it was Hetty. She even stood up for you the other day, when someone came gossiping that you had been playing cards on a Sunday morning, when you ought to have been in church."

Harry stared, confused. "She scolded me half out of my wits last night," he said. "She snapped at everyone in the room, young and old, so that I told her in Virginia we do not suffer the young to speak so disrespectfully to their elders. We ended up having a regular quarrel, and I am very glad to hear she spoke up for me," he added, his voice softening, his cheeks flushing, his eyes growing misty.

Colonel Lambert looked at him, his warm, kind gaze softening. He saw at once that the honest, comely young Virginian was half head over heels in love with his sharp-tongued younger daughter. "You will never find a worse enemy than Hetty, Harry, you may be sure of that," he said, gravely.

The conversation turned to the Duchess, to Lady Yarmouth, who had been singing Harry's praises at the Pump Room that morning, declaring she had "a leedel boy in Hannover dat is wery like you, you sharming young mans," and who had won forty guineas off Harry at quadrille the night before. Before long, Lambert revealed that his funds were run out, and his whole family was leaving for their home at Oakhurst that very day. "You must come visit us, once our son Tom is home from the army," he told Harry. "My wife and I care for you very deeply, you know that -- and the girls do too, no matter how much they scold you. And if you ever get yourself into a scrape, as young men often do, you come straight to me, you understand? Count on me."

He dragged Harry out for a long walk across the common soon after, and Chaplain Sampson, looking out his upper window, saw the two men deep in earnest talk: first Lambert grinned and looked roguish, then he flung up both hands in shocked surprise. "The boy is asking for Hetty's hand," Sampson thought to himself.

Harry returned an hour later, his face grave and gloomy, flinging his hat down hard on the table and dropping into his armchair with a curse.

"The girls are leaving, and your heart is sore?" the chaplain teased, looking up from his half-written sermon.

"Heart!" Harry sneered. "That little termagant! What does she mean by treating me like a complete fool?"

By and by, Sampson asked after the little leather notebook Harry had lost a few nights before, the one stuffed full of bank notes and important private papers, which had vanished after a night of too much drinking. Harry admitted he had searched everywhere, and found no trace of it, kicking himself for getting so far gone that he had lost track of his own property.

"Confound all women, I say!" he growled at last, as Sampson joked about the charm of young country girls. It was a very cross, ungenerous sentiment, no doubt, and every right-thinking reader will surely rebuke the poor lovestruck boy for it.
