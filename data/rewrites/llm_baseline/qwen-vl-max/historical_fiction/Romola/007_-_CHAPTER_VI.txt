When Maso opened the door once more and ushered in the two visitors, Nello stepped forward first, bowing deeply to Romola before gently guiding Tito ahead of him toward her father.

“Messer Bardo,” he said, his voice measured and respectful—unlike his usual manner—“I have the honour of presenting to you a Greek scholar who has long desired an audience with you. Not only because I have spoken of your learning and your rare collections, but also because your patronage might offer him support during this difficult time, brought on by shipwreck. His name is Tito Melema—at your service.”

Romola’s astonishment was as great as if the stranger had entered wearing a panther-skin and carrying a thyrsus. The barber had told her nothing of the man’s age or appearance; among her father’s scholarly guests, she had seen only middle-aged or grey-haired men. There was just one face she remembered vividly—one young, beautiful man: her brother, who years ago had lifted her onto his knee, kissed her forehead, and vanished forever. A fair face, golden hair like her own. But the pride instilled in her by her father’s complaints about life’s injustices—a proud independence that refused even a smile to beg for anything—was like a snowbank holding back a sudden flood of admiration.

Tito’s face, bright and radiant, showed its rich beauty beneath his simple black *sajo* and tunic that reached to his knees. It seemed like a spring garland dropped into Romola’s young, wintry world—world built on memories: of a dead mother, a lost brother, a blind father’s past joys, and far-off light, love, and beauty buried deep within books, waiting to be lit again by some known joy. Yet she returned Tito’s bow with her usual pale composure. But as he approached, the ice melted. When he glanced at her again while Nello spoke, a soft flush spread across her cheeks—only to vanish instantly, as though her will had retracted it.

Tito’s gaze, however, held a quiet, pleading admiration—the kind most likely to soften a proud, shy woman, perhaps the only compensation a man can offer when he is too handsome. His charm came not from boldness, but from absence of demand. He seemed like a swift, dark-eyed animal with soft fur—delighting you not by fleeing, but by unexpectedly resting its chin in your palm and looking up, yearning to be touched—as if it already loved you.

“Messere, welcome,” Bardo said, with gentle condescension. “Misfortune joined to learning—especially Greek learning—is a letter of credit worthy of every educated Florentine. As you know, since your countryman Manuelo Crisolora brought the light of Greek scholarship to Italy nearly a century ago, no true scholar is thought worthy unless he knows more than the Latin tradition alone. Those who study only the transplanted literature are called *opici*, or barbarians—just as the Romans themselves admitted they drew their wisdom from the source. I am, as you see, and as Nello has surely warned you, completely blind—an affliction common among us Florentines, whether due to the cold winds sweeping down from the Apennines in spring, or the harsh contrast between our dim homes and the blinding summer sun, which once made so many *lippi* among the Romans. Or perhaps it is some hidden cause beyond our understanding. But please, take seats—Nello, my friend, sit as well.”

Bardo paused, listening carefully until he sensed the others had settled—and that Romola had taken her usual place beside him. Then he asked:

“From what part of Greece do you come, Messere? I had thought your homeland had given up most of its scholars, those who still carry the memory of ancient glory. Though, truth be told, the Ottoman Sultans seem lately willing to graft precious vines onto their wild stock—though they themselves have cut them down and trampled them.”

“I sailed last from Nauplia,” Tito replied. “But I’ve lived in Constantinople and Thessalonica, and traveled through regions rarely visited by Western Christians since the Turks rose to power. I should add, Messere, that I was born not in Greece, but in Bari. I spent my first sixteen years in southern Italy and Sicily.”

As Tito spoke, a subtle tremor passed over Bardo’s delicate features. He leaned forward, reaching out his hand toward Romola, turning his head as if to speak to her—but then corrected himself, turning away and speaking softly.

“Forgive me—may I ask… are you young?”

“I am twenty-three,” Tito answered.

“Oh,” Bardo murmured, his voice low with emotion. “And you must have had a father who cared for your early education—one perhaps a scholar himself?”

There was a pause before Tito replied—long enough for Romola and Nello to notice a visible shudder pass through him, a quiver at the corner of his lip. Clearly, something painful stirred in his memory.

“Yes,” he said. “At least, a father by adoption. He was Neapolitan, highly learned in both Latin and Greek. But…” He hesitated again. “…he is gone—lost at sea, on a reckless journey to Delos.”

Bardo sank back, too sensitive to press further. He knew the sorrow was fresh. Romola, watching closely, understood how deeply Tito’s words had touched her father—how swiftly this stranger had breached the wall between them and the outside world.

Nello, sensing the lull in conversation, saw his chance to excuse himself.

“In truth, it’s clear as Venetian glass that this young man has had excellent training. The Cennini brothers already have him working on their Greek manuscripts. And I say, if there are two things that cannot be hidden—love and a cough—then there is a third: ignorance. Once a man must do more than nod, he cannot hide it. The clumsy barber always reveals himself when he picks up the shears. Is it not so, Messer Bardo? I speak like a barber, but as Luigi Pulci says—

‘Perdonimi s’io fallo: chi m’ascolta
Intenda il mio volgar col suo latino.’”

“Nay, my good Nello,” Bardo replied, with mild severity, “you are not entirely illiterate. You might have advanced farther had you avoided street gossip, so dear to our Florentines, and especially had you not cluttered your mind with frivolous works—of which Luigi Pulci offers the worst example. A jumble of absurdities and incongruities, far removed from classical models. They resemble the *grylli*—those fanciful conceits of a time when mystic meaning justified monstrous form—except here, the monstrosity remains, but the meaning is gone. A poor contrast to Virgil, whose great poem, as I once held with Filelfo—before Landino claimed the same view—wove profound philosophy into graceful, coherent fable. And I cannot help seeing the rise of such babbling, lawless writings—even supported by Lorenzo himself, otherwise a patron of real learning—as a sign that the hopes of our age are fading into darkness. Worse than iron, perhaps: an age of tinsel and gossamer, where thought lacks substance to take lasting shape.”

“Once more, forgive me,” Nello said, palms open, shoulders shrugging. “I find myself speaking Tuscan before I can recall the Latin. And Messer Luigi’s rhymes slip from my customers’ lips—they corrupt me. Besides, I’ve left my shop and reputation too long in the hands of slow Sandro, who deserves not even the title *tonsor inequalis*, but simply a bungler in the vulgar tongue. So, with your permission, Messer Bardo, I shall take my leave—understanding I remain at your service whenever Maso calls. It feels like a thousand years until I dress and perfume the damigella’s hair, which deserves to shine in the heavens like a constellation—though truly, it would be a pity if it ever went so far beyond reach.”

Three voices chimed together in farewell as Nello bowed to Romola and nodded to Tito. The sharp-minded barber saw that the young man, who had somehow charmed him, had already won Bardo’s favor. Satisfied his introduction had succeeded, he withdrew.

The brief burst of irritation sparked by Nello’s quote had shifted Bardo’s mood. Now, he addressed Tito calmly.

“Ah, young man, you are fortunate to have combined travel with study. You will be welcome among us—bearer of fresh news from a land now alien to us, save through limited trade or fleeting pilgrims. For the days I once knew—when men like Aurispa and Guarino journeyed to Greece as to a treasure house, returning laden with manuscripts every scholar coveted—seem distant. Even the time when learned Greeks fled to our shores for refuge now feels remote—farther than the onset of my blindness. But I suppose research into antiquity was not foreign to your travels?”

“Certainly not,” Tito replied. “On the contrary, my companion—my adopted father—was eager to risk his life in seeking inscriptions and traces of ancient civilization.”

“And I trust there is a record of his work?” Bardo asked eagerly. “For such records must be more valuable than those of Ciriaco, whom I have studied diligently—though his insights are not always matched by depth of learning.”

“There *was* a record,” Tito said, “but it perished in the shipwreck near Ancona. Everything was lost. The only record now is what remains in my memory.”

“You must write it down without delay,” Bardo urged, growing more animated. “You doubtless remember much—if you helped with transcription. At your age, I absorbed words as if carved in stone. That is why I often marvel at my daughter’s memory: it clings to certain things fiercely, yet lets slip the very details that matter for accuracy—the soul of scholarship. But I suspect no such flaw in you, if your will matches your training.”

When Bardo mentioned his daughter, Tito turned his eyes toward her. At the remark about her memory, his face broke into a warm, bright smile—reflected instantly in Romola’s. How soothing that smile was to her! She had never imagined a scholar could smile at a weakness she was constantly blamed for. It felt like the awakening of a new sense—the sense of kinship. They did not look away immediately, as if the moment were stolen; they held each other’s gaze, smiling freely.

“She is not really so cold and proud,” Tito thought.

“Does *he* forget too?” Romola wondered. “Yet I hope not—else he’ll displease my father.”

But Tito had to turn away and answer Bardo.

“I have practiced transcription,” he said. “But in places marked by danger and emotion—where fear of the Turk loomed like a vulture—my memory may have weakened, even if my hand wrote faithfully. On the plain of Eurotas, or amid the colossal stones of Mycenae and Tiryns, the mind wanders, though the eye dictates. Still, I believe I retain something—something that might serve, if enriched by deeper learning than mine.”

“Well said, young man,” Bardo replied, delighted. “And I will gladly lend you my aid, if you wish to share your recollections. I foresee a work that could complement Christoforo Buondelmonte’s *Isolario*, rivaling Ciriaco’s *Itineraria* and Ambrogio Traversari’s admirable studies. But beware—such a book will draw criticism. If it contains novelties, you’ll be accused of forgery. If my interpretations clash with another’s, we’ll be attacked personally—our characters slandered, our lives impugned. You may hear that your mother was a fishwife, your father a renegade priest or hanged criminal. I myself, for correcting a single preposition, was vilified with charges of treason, fraud, indecency, and worse. Such, my young friend, are the flowers that line the path of scholarship! But tell me—what of Byzantium and Thessalonica? I learned much from Demetrio Calcondila, who recently left Florence. But you, it seems, have seen less familiar lands?”

“Yes,” Tito said. “We undertook what I call a dangerous pilgrimage—to visit places almost forgotten by the West. Places beyond the paths of pilgrims. My father believed scholars barely imagine them exist outside books. He dreamed of a new era in learning, when men would seek commentaries not in texts, but in ruins, rivers, valleys, mountains.”

“Ah!” Bardo exclaimed, fervently. “Your father was no ordinary man. Was he fortunate? Did he have friends?”

“No,” Tito said quietly. “He made enemies—mostly, I think, through blunt honesty. They blocked his advancement, so he lived in obscurity. He refused to compromise—he never forgot an insult.”

“Ah,” Bardo murmured, deeply moved.

“One memory stands out,” Tito continued, wanting to shift the subject. “A hurried visit to Athens. Our haste, the danger of capture by Turks, and the threat that our galley might sail without us made it feel like a fever-dream. The wide plain, the surrounding mountains, ruined columns and porticos—some standing far apart, as if retreating from us, others jammed among Christian homes, or Turkish forts and towers. The Acropolis itself is now a stronghold of the Moslems.”

“You surprise me,” Bardo said. “Then Athens is not utterly destroyed, as I feared?”

“Few even among Greeks beyond Attica know its condition today. As we rounded Sunium, our Greek pilot pointed to the great columns atop the rock—the remains of Athena’s temple. ‘That,’ he said, ‘was the school of Aristotle.’ In Athens itself, our monk-guide insisted on showing us where Saint Philip baptized the Ethiopian eunuch—or some such tale.”

“Do not speak of monks and their legends,” Bardo interrupted sharply. “To think that ground once trodden by philosophers and poets is now crawled over by fanatic insects or howling hypocrites—this alone freezes human hope.”

“_Perdio_, I have no love for them,” Tito said, shrugging. “Servitude suits a religion that rejects all that makes life meaningful. Their chants are drowned by the muezzin’s call from the tower on the Acropolis—built upon the Parthenon itself. Every time we looked toward it, our guide cried out in grief that a temple once freed from pagan uses to honor the Virgin Mother of God was now twisted to the cursed ends of Islam. It was seeing those walls of the Acropolis, revealed from afar as we leaned over the galley in Piraeus, that fired my father’s resolve to see Athens at any cost—despite warnings that if we waited for wind to change, they’d leave without us. But we could not approach the Acropolis. The sight of men examining ‘old stones’ raised suspicion—we were feared as Venetian spies, and we fled back to the harbor.”

“We shall speak more of these things,” Bardo said, eager. “Recall everything—every trace. Your memory will earn gratitude from future generations. You will record Greece as it was before the barbarians erased every trace described by Pausanias and Pliny. Take them as your guides. And whatever critical insight my older mind can offer, I will give freely. You have traveled in the Peloponnesus?”

“Yes—and in Boeotia. I rested in the groves of Helicon, drank from Hippocrene. But every sacred site bears the mark of conquest—ruins layered with conflicting claims, only careful study can untangle. From Lacedaemon to Thermopylae, towering above every hillside, stands some massive Frankish fortress—once home to French or Italian lords, now abandoned or held by Turkish bands.”

“Stay!” Bardo exclaimed, his thoughts now fixed on the book they might write. “Will you write in Latin or Greek? Greek is natural to your thoughts, noble in its form. But Latin reaches more readers, more rivals. And if you’re less fluent, I will assist you—yes, I will pour into this work the knowledge I saved for another project, one in which I hoped to have a partner.”

He paused, then added softly:

“But who knows? That work may yet be done. For you too, young man, were shaped by a father who poured his life’s learning into your mind. Our help might be mutual.”

Romola, watching her father’s excitement, felt a strange anxiety. She kept glancing at Tito, fearing he might dismiss her father’s dreams. But no—Tito looked calm, gentle. He must feel, as she did, that the old man’s passion deserved patience. How much more so if he knew about her brother! A girl of eighteen imagines the feelings behind a face that moves her—just as ancient peoples read the gods’ moods in the sky. What else could she believe in, if not this vision spun from within?

And Tito was indeed far from impatient. He enjoyed sitting there, knowing Romola watched him, knowing he could glance at her. He pleased in Bardo’s interest. He did not take the prospect of their collaboration seriously enough to feel burdened—only amused, agreeable.

“I shall be proud and happy,” he said, answering Bardo, “if my efforts can serve your mature scholarship. But surely”—he looked toward Romola—“your lovely daughter makes all other help unnecessary. I heard from Nello she has been trained in the highest studies from childhood.”

“You are mistaken,” Romola said, her voice steady, proud. “I am no match for my father. I lack the gifts needed for true scholarship.”
