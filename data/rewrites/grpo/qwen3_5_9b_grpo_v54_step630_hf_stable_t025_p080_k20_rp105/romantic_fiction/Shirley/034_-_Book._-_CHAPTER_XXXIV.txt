Martin, having tasted the intoxicating flavor of excitement and the intoxicating weight of power, found himself loath to relinquish either. Miss Helstone—the girl he had always dismissed as plain, whose face now haunted his waking hours and dreams alike—had finally entered his orbit. The thought that their brief encounter might never be repeated gnawed at him. Though he was merely a schoolboy, Martin possessed the restless soul of an original; he knew, with a premonition that would later haunt him, that many life-romances are doomed to stall before the second chapter. For now, however, he sat idly at his desk, his mind already drafting the next scene of his unfolding drama.

He harbored a distinct, almost religious aversion to Sunday. While his parents, though critical of the Establishment, dutifully filled the large pew at Briarfield Church with their entire brood, Martin found the morning service tedious and the sermons uninspiring. Yet, this particular Saturday afternoon, a heavy snowfall transformed the day into something unexpected. Mrs. Yorke, convinced the depth of the drift made travel impossible, announced that the children would remain indoors, sitting in silence in the back parlor while Rose and Martin alternated readings from John Wesley's sermons.

"I shall do as I please," Martin declared, not lifting his gaze from the book he habitually studied over his bread and milk.

"Rose will do as she is told, and so will Martin," his mother countered firmly.

"I am going to church," Martin replied, with the absolute, terrifying quietude of a Yorke who knows his will is law and who, if cornered, would rather be crushed than capitulate.

"It is unfit weather," his father interjected.

Martin offered no reply, simply continuing to break his bread with studied gravity. His voice, naturally low and plaintive, softened further as his stubborn mood deepened, rising only to a whisper. He rang the bell for his walking shoes.

"But, Martin," his father urged, "there is drift everywhere; a man could hardly wade through it."

Seeing the boy rise as the church bell began its toll, the father relented. "There is a pitiless wind and frozen sleet, but since you prefer the storm to a warm fireside, go. I shall not balk the obdurate chap of his will."

Martin quietly donned his cloak, comforter, and cap, stepping out into the blizzard. "My father has more sense than my mother," he muttered to himself, watching the snow swirl. "Women drive nails into flesh, thinking they are striking stone."

He arrived at the church early. The air was biting, the silence profound. As the chimes faded, the elderly and the poor trickled in, the only ones brave enough to brave the elements for the sake of their faith. The affluent families were nowhere to be seen; the cushioned pews remained empty, occupied only by gray-haired elders and feeble paupers.

*I'll scorn her if she doesn't come,* Martin thought savagely. *She ought to come. If she has a mind worthy of her eyes, she will.*

He scanned the room. The rector's shovel-hat passed the porch; Mr. Helstone and his clerk were in the vestry. The doors closed, the service began. The rectory pew stood void. She was not there.

*Worthless thing! Vapid! Commonplace humbug!* Martin's internal monologue turned cruel. *Like all other girls—weak, selfish, shallow.* So consumed was he by his cynicism that he forgot to rise when the litany ended, remaining in a posture of false devotion until the first hymn was sung. When the organ swelled and the congregation rose, the church doors burst open. A hundred small feet trotted in—the Sunday scholars. Finally, trailing the children, came a tall class of young women. Their teacher led them to their seats, then moved toward the rectory pew.

Martin's breath hitched. The French-gray cloak and the small beaver bonnet were unmistakable. Miss Helstone had not let the storm deter her. She was there. A whisper of satisfaction escaped his lips, hidden behind his hymn book.

Yet, before the sermon concluded, anger flared anew. She had not once looked his way. Had she come solely for the sake of the children, ignoring him entirely?

*If she takes no notice of me,* he fumed, *I shall despise her more than ever.*

The service ended, the benediction pronounced, and the congregation dispersed. She had not approached him. As Martin set his face homeward, the sleet felt sharper, the east wind colder.

He chose a dangerous, untrodden path through the fields, drawn by a desperate hope. Near the second stile, a clump of trees stood out against the white expanse. Was that an umbrella? Yes, held with visible struggle against the gale. Behind it fluttered the familiar French-gray cloak.

Martin grinned, a look of triumph on his face, as he climbed the steep, icy slope. Seating himself coolly atop the stile, he launched into a conversation he intended to prolong indefinitely.

"I think you had better strike a bargain. Exchange me for Mrs. Pryor."

"I wasn't sure if you'd come this way, Martin, but I thought I'd risk it. There is no getting a quiet word spoken in the church or churchyard."

"Will you agree? Make over Mrs. Pryor to my mother, and put me in her skirts?"

"As if I could understand you! What brings Mrs. Pryor to your mind?"

"You call her 'mamma,' don't you?"

"She *is* my mamma."

"Not possible—not so inefficient, so careless a mother. I'd make a five times better one. You may laugh; I have no objection to seeing you laugh. Your teeth... I hate ugly teeth, but yours are as pretty as a pearl necklace."

"Martin, what now? I thought the Yorkes never paid compliments?"

"They haven't until this generation. I feel it is my vocation to create a new variety of Yorke. I am tired of my own ancestors. We have traditions going back four ages—tales of Hiram, son of Hiram, son of Samuel... all the way down to my father. Before him, there was a Godfrey. His picture hangs in Moore's bedroom; he looks like me. Of his character, we know nothing, but I am sure it was different. He has long, curling dark hair; he is dressed cavalierly. Having said he looks like me, I need not add that he is handsome."

"You are not handsome, Martin."

"No; but wait. Just let me take my time. I intend to begin cultivating, polishing, from this day forward. We shall see."

"You are a very strange, unaccountable boy, Martin. But don't imagine you will ever be handsome; you cannot."

"I mean to try. But we were talking about Mrs. Pryor. She must be the most unnatural mother in existence, letting her daughter out in this weather. Mine was in such a rage because I insisted on going to church; she was fit to fling the kitchen brush after me."

"Mamma was very concerned, but I fear I was obstinate. I *would* go."

"To see me?"

"Exactly. I thought of nothing else. I greatly feared the snow would hinder you. You don't know how pleased I was to see you alone in the pew."

*"I"* came to fulfill my duty and set the parish a good example. And so you were obstinate? I should like to see you obstinate. I should. Wouldn't I have you in good discipline if I owned you? Let me take the umbrella."

"I can't stay two minutes; our dinner will be ready."

"And so will ours. We always have a hot dinner on Sundays. Roast goose today, with apple-pie and rice-pudding. I always contrive to know the bill of fare. Well, I like these things uncommonly, but I'll make the sacrifice, if you will."

"We have a cold dinner. My uncle allows no unnecessary cooking on the Sabbath. But I must return; the house would be in commotion if I failed to appear."

"So will Briarmains, bless you! I think I hear my father sending out the overlooker and five dyers to search for the body of his prodigal son in the snow; and my mother repenting her many misdeeds now that I am gone."

"Martin, how is Mr. Moore?"

*"That"* is what you came for, just to say that word."

"Come, tell me quickly."

"Hang him! He is no worse, but as ill-used as ever—mewed up, kept in solitary confinement. They mean to declare him either an idiot or a maniac and take out a commission of lunacy. Horsfall starves him; you saw how thin he was."

"You were very good the other day, Martin."

"What day? I am always good—a model."

"When will you be so good again?"

"I see what you are after, but you won't wheedle me—I am no cat's-paw."

"But it must be done. It is quite a right thing, a necessary thing."

"How you encroach! Remember, I managed the matter of my own free will before."

"And you will again."

"I won't. The business gave me far too much trouble. I like my ease."

"Mr. Moore wishes to see me, Martin, and I wish to see him."

"I dare say" (coolly).

"It is too bad of your mother to exclude his friends."

"Tell her so."

"His own relations."

"Come and blow her up."

"You know that would advance nothing. Well, I shall stick to my point. See him I will. If you won't help me, I'll manage without help."

"Do; there is nothing like self-reliance, self-dependence."

"I have no time to reason with you now, but I consider you provoking. Good-morning."

She departed, the umbrella shut, unable to carry it against the wind.

"She is not vapid; she is not shallow," Martin observed, watching her retreat. "I shall like to watch, and mark how she will work her way without help. If the storm were not of snow, but of fire—such as came refreshingly down on the cities of the plain—she would go through it to procure five minutes' speech of that Moore. Now, I consider I have had a pleasant morning. The disappointments gave time to settle; the fears and fits of anger only made that short discourse pleasanter when it came at last. She expected to coax me at once. She'll not manage that in one effort. She shall come again, again, and yet again. It would please me to put her in a passion—to make her cry. I want to discover how far she will go, what she will dare, to get her will. It seems strange and new to find one human being thinking so much about another as she thinks about Moore. But it is time to go home; my appetite tells me the hour. Won't I walk into that goose? And we'll try whether Matthew or I shall get the largest cut of the apple-pie today."
