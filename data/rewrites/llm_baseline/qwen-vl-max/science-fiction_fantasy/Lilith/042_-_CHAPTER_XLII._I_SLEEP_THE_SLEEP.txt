When I awoke, the earth was damp beneath me, and the path to the grave was turning into quicksand. The ancient river swelled with urgency, pushing against its burden. Soon it would roar over the precipice, splitting into two currents—one plunging into the orchard valley to reclaim it, the other sweeping toward the monstrous horde, perhaps drowning them. Between these torrents, the Evil Wood would stand as an island of silence.

I set out immediately for those who had sent me. At the edge of the precipice, I chose the path between the water’s branches—my way led past Mara’s cottage. I wanted one last glimpse of her before I surrendered to sleep. Now I knew how to cross the channels, even if the river had already filled them.

But when I arrived, the door stood open. Bread and water remained untouched on the table. Silence wrapped the place like a shroud. I called aloud, but no answer came. I turned away.

A little farther on, I found a gray-haired man sitting on the sand, weeping. “What troubles you, sir?” I asked. “Are you abandoned?”

“I weep,” he said, “because they will not let me die. I’ve been to the house of death, and though I am old, its mistress refuses me. Intercede for me, if you know her.”

“I cannot,” I replied. “She accepts only those she is allowed to take.”

“How do you know this? You’ve never sought death! You’re too young to desire it.”

“Perhaps,” I said, “your words mean that if you were young again, you still wouldn’t wish to die.”

“Indeed, young sir, I wouldn’t! And I’m certain you never will.”

“I may be too young to want death,” I answered, “but I am young enough to desire life. That is why I come now—to see if she will finally receive me. You wish to die because you no longer care to live. She wonches you, for no one dies who does not long to live.”

“It ill becomes your youth to mock an old man in need. Cease your riddles!”

“Did not the Mother tell you something similar?”

“In truth, she did—but I paid little heed to her words.”

“Then,” I said, “it is clear you have not yet learned how to die. And I grieve for you. I once stood where you are—until I met the Lady of Sorrow. I am young, yes, but I have known sorrow deeply. Please, hear me: go to her cottage. It lies yonder. Her door stands open. There is bread and water on her table. Go inside. Sit. Eat. Drink. Wait. When she comes, ask her counsel. She is true. Her wisdom is great.”

He began to weep anew. I left him there.

The sun had set, the moon not yet risen, when I reached the abode of the monsters. The air was still as stone until I stepped across the threshold. Then, behind me, I heard the rush of many waters—and a great cry—but I did not turn.

Before I reached the house of death, the cold bit deep, the darkness thick. Cold and dark merged into one, seeping into my bones. Yet the candle of Eve glowed from a window, guiding me, shielding my heart from frost and shadow.

The door stood open. The cottage was empty. I sat down, disconsolate.

And then—loneliness rose within me like a tide. Thousands were near, yet none were with me. True, I was dead, not they. But by their life or my death, we were separated. They lived; I was not dead enough to feel their presence. Doubt crept in. They were distant, beyond reach. No one could help lay me beside them.

Never before had I known desolation so fully.

I tried to reason with myself: “This solitude is only seeming. You are awake; they sleep. That is all. They lie still. They do not speak. But they are here. Soon, soon you will be with them.”

I dropped Adam’s old spade. Its dull thud echoed through the clay floor, reverberating from the chamber beyond. A childish fear seized me. I stared at the coffin door.

But father Adam, mother Eve, sister Mara—soon they would come. Then—welcome the cold world, the white neighbors!

I forgot my fear. I lived, briefly. I loved my dead.

Something stirred in the chamber of the dead. From within came a sound—not quite sound, yet felt. My soul leapt into my ears. Was it merely a tremor in the air, too faint for hearing, yet vibrating through every sense?

I *knew*, without hearing, without feeling—it was coming. Closer.

In the depths of my isolation, a fragile hope awakened.

The silent thrum reached the coffin door—then became sound, striking my ear.

The door creaked open slowly, softly.

I stopped listening. I watched.

It opened just a crack—and a face appeared.

It was Lona’s.

Her eyes were closed, yet her face looked upon me, as if she saw.

White as Eve’s, white as Mara’s—but without their light.

She spoke. Her voice was like a night wind drifting through grass.

“Are you coming, king?” she whispered. “I cannot rest until you join me, gliding down the river to the great sea, to the dream-land. Sleepiness is full of beauty. Come and see.”

“Ah, my darling!” I cried. “Had I known—how could I have thought you were dead?”

She lay on my chest—cold as ice fused to marble. Her arms, pale and thin, curled weakly around me. She sighed.

“Carry me back to my bed, king. I want to sleep.”

I carried her, clutching her tightly, afraid she might dissolve from my grasp. Without knowing, I walked straight to her couch.

“Lay me down,” she murmured, “and cover me from the warm air. It hurts—just a little. Your bed is next to mine. I’ll see you when I wake.”

She was already asleep.

I fell onto my own couch—blessed beyond measure, as if on the eve of a wedding.

“Come, sweet cold,” I whispered, “still my heart swiftly.”

Instead, a soft light filled the room. I saw Adam approaching. He held no candle, yet I saw him clearly.

At the side of Lona’s bed, he smiled down at her—then greeted me across the space.

“We went to the top of the hill,” he said. “We heard the waters rushing. They will reach the den of the monsters tonight. But why didn’t you wait for us?”

“My child couldn’t sleep,” I replied.

“She is fast asleep,” he said.

“Yes—now,” I said. “But she was awake when I laid her down.”

“She was asleep all along,” he insisted.

“Perhaps dreaming of you—and came to you?”

“She did.”

“And you didn’t notice her eyes were closed?”

“Now that I think of it… I did.”

“If you’d looked before laying her down, you’d have seen her sleeping on the couch.”

“That would have been terrible.”

“You would have found she was no longer in your arms.”

“That would have been worse.”

“Perhaps it’s worth thinking about. But seeing it wouldn’t have troubled you.”

“Dear father,” I said, “why am I not sleepy? I expected to fall asleep the moment I laid my head down—like the Little Ones.”

“Your hour has not yet come. You must eat first.”

“Oh—I should not have lain down without your leave. I cannot sleep without your help. I’ll get up at once!”

But I could not move. My body felt heavier than stone.

“There’s no need,” Adam said. “We’ll serve you here. Do you feel cold?”

“Not cold enough to move, but perhaps too cold to eat.”

He came to my side, leaned over, and breathed upon my heart. Instantly, warmth returned.

As he stepped away, I heard a voice—soft, low, sweet. It was the Mother’s song. I thought she sat beside my bed in the dark. But as the melody rose, it soared—becoming the voice of a woman-angel, higher than larks, higher than any human heart had ever climbed.

I heard every word, but only one line stayed:

*Many a wrong, and its curing song;
Many a road, and many an inn;
Room to roam, but only one home
For all the world to win!*

I felt I had heard it before.

Then all three came—Adam, Eve, Mara—bearing bread and wine. I sat up to eat.

Adam stood at my right. Eve and Mara at my left.

“You are truly kind, father Adam, mother Eve, sister Mara,” I said. “I am ashamed and sorry.”

“We knew you would return,” Eve said.

“How could you know?”

“Because I was born to care for my brothers and sisters,” Mara smiled.

“Every creature must one night surrender,” Adam said. “He was made for freedom, and must not remain a slave.”

“It will be late, I fear, before all have lain down,” I said.

“There is no early or late here,” Adam replied. “True time begins only when one lays himself down. Men return slowly. Women come faster. A vast, desolate space divides him who dies from him who lives. The former may hurry—but here, there is no haste.”

“To us,” Eve said, “you were always coming. We knew Mara would find you. You had to come.”

“How long since my father lay down?”

“Years mean nothing here,” Adam said. “We do not count them. Your father will wake when his morning arrives. Your mother—lying beside you—”

“Ah! So it *is* my mother!”

“Yes—the one with the wounded hand,” Adam said. “She will rise long before your morning ripens.”

“I am sorry.”

“Be glad.”

“It must be a sight for God Himself—to see such a woman awaken!”

“It is indeed,” Adam said. “A sight that brings joy to her Maker. He sees the travail of His soul—and is satisfied. Look at her once more… and sleep.”

He let the candlelight fall upon her face.

“She looks younger,” I said.

“She *is* younger,” he replied. “Even Lilith begins to look younger.”

I lay down, drowsy with bliss.

“But when you see your mother again,” Adam continued, “you will not recognize her at first. She will grow steadily younger—until she reaches the perfection of her womanhood—a splendor beyond imagining. Then she will open her eyes, see her husband on one side, her son on the other—and rise to go to a father and brother more dear than they.”

I listened as in a dream. I was cold—but the cold no longer hurt.

I felt them clothe me in the white garment of the dead.

Then I forgot everything.

The night around me was pale with sleeping faces.

But I was asleep too—nor knew that I slept.
