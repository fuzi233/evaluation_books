Margaret returned to Rotterdam long before Gerard awoke, leaving her young son behind. She dispatched the loyal, sturdy Reicht immediately to Gouda, entrusting him with a vicar's grey frock and a large felt hat, along with detailed instructions on how to care for his new charge.

Then she visited Jorian Ketel. To herself, she reasoned, "He is the closest confidant I have ever known; therefore, he is the man for me." Together, they orchestrated two acts of mortal cunning—though, in my view, not malicious, despite her belief that they were. If pressed to name these unnamed deeds, the answer remains silent, for she, being merely a woman, guarded them until her deathbed, whereas I, a man—"Verbum non amplius addam."

She deliberately avoided the Gouda parsonage.

Matters often overlooked during heated arguments can later rankle deeply. Upon reviewing their history, Margaret was offended that Gerard had assumed she could ever forget the priest who had once been her lover. "For what did he take me?" she asked herself. This realization bred a profound shyness she would not otherwise have felt, given her inherent innocence. Pride joined forces with modesty, whispering, "Do not return to the Gouda parsonage."

She left little Gerard there to complete the spiritual conquest her maternal heart attributed to him—not to her own eloquence or wisdom—but to secure his father forever within the fold of humanity.

Yet, this generous act of policy cost her dearly. She had never been parted from her boy for an hour, and she felt strangely desolate in his absence. By the second day, the separation became unbearable. What did the poor soul do but creep to the Gouda parsonage at dusk, lurking around the grounds like a thief until she spotted Reicht Heynes alone in the kitchen? She tapped softly at the window and pleaded, "Reicht, for pity's sake, bring him out to me unseen." At that moment, the person occupying Margaret's thoughts lost her name and sank into a mere pronoun.

Reicht soon found an excuse to retrieve little Gerard. A scene of mutual rapture ensued, followed by shared tears when mother and child were forced apart again.

An arrangement was made: Reicht would take the boy halfway to Rotterdam daily at a fixed hour, meeting Margaret on the journey. During these encounters, after the initial joyous reunion and playful gamboling between mother and child, the boy would occasionally amuse himself alone at their feet. Seizing this opportunity, the two women engaged in serious conversation regarding Luke Peterson.

"Reicht," Margaret began, "I practically promised him I would marry Luke Peterson. 'Say the word,' I told him, 'and I will wed him.'"

"Poor Luke!" Reicht sighed.

"Prithee, why 'poor Luke'?"

"To be tossed back and forth between yes and no."

"Why, Reicht, have you never been so foolish as to cast an affectionate glance upon the boy, thereby taking his part?"

"Me?" Reicht replied, tossing his head dismissively.

"Oh, forgive me. Well, then, you can do me a favor."

"Hush! Whisper! That little darling is listening to every word, with eyes wide as saucers."

Upon hearing this, both women instinctively ducked their heads under a single cap. Two women plotting against one boy? Oh, you great, cowardly serpents!

However, after approximately five days of these clandestine meetings, Margaret began to feel the injustice of the situation, growing irritated and unhappy.

She was weeping over it when a cart arrived at her door. Inside sat the Vicar of Gouda, dressed in the grey frock and large felt hat she had sent him. He appeared as clean as a new penny, his beard freshly shaved, his hands white as snow, with a faint color returning to his pale face.

She rushed upstairs immediately, washing away the traces of her tears, and changed into a cap that was theoretically cleaner, having just been removed from the drawer. Descending to greet him, he seized both her hands and kissed them, a tear falling upon them. She turned her head away to conceal the one starting in her own eyes.

"My sweet Margaret," he cried, "why this distance? Why hold yourself aloof from your own good deed? We have waited for you every day, and no Margaret appears."

"You said things."

"What! When I was a hermit and a donkey?"

"Ay! No matter, you said things. And you had no reason."

"Forget everything I said there. Who listens to the ravings of a madman? For I now see that in a few months I should have become a gibbering idiot; yet no mortal could have persuaded me away but you. Oh, what an expenditure of wit and goodness was yours! But it is not here that I can thank and bless you as I ought. No, it is in the home you have given me, among the sheep whose shepherd you have made me. Already I love them dearly; there it is I must thank 'the truest friend a man ever had.' So now I say to you, as once you said to me, come to the Gouda manse."

"Hmph! We shall see about that."

"Why, Margaret, do you think I ever kept the dear child so long unless I was certain you would return to him day after day? Oh, he curls round my very heartstrings, but what claim do I have to him compared to yours? Confess now, you have harbored hard thoughts of me for this."

"Nay, nay, not I. Ah! You are yourself again; you have always been thoughtful of others. I have half a mind to go to the Gouda manse simply because you said so."

"Come then, with half your mind; 'tis worth the whole of other folk's."

"Well, I suppose I will; but there is no such urgent hurry," she said coolly (though she was literally burning to go). "First, tell me how you agree with your people."

"Why, already my poor have taken root in my heart."

"I thought as much."

"And there are such good creatures among them; simple, rough, and superstitious, yet wonderfully good."

"Oh, I leave you alone for finding a grain of good amidst a bushel of ill."

"Hush! Hush! And Margaret, two of them had been enemies for four years, each coming to the manse hoping to gain advantage over me on the other's blind side. But give the glory to God, I gained their bright side, made them friends, and caused them to laugh at their own folly."

"But are you truly their vicar? Answer me that."

"Certainly; have I not gone to the bishop, taken the oath, rung the church bell, touched the altar, the missal, and the holy chalice before the church-wardens? And they have handed me the parish seal; see, here it is. Nay, 'tis a real vicar inviting a true friend to the Gouda manse."

"Then my mind is at ease. Tell me oceans more."

"Well, sweet one, nearest to me of all my parish is a poor cripple that my guardian angel and hers (whose name you know, even by this turning of your head away) have placed beneath my roof. Sybrandt and I are brothers now, something we never were before. 'Twould gladden you, yet sadden you, to hear how we kissed and forgave one another. He is full of your praises and wholly in a pious state; he says he is happier since his affliction than ever he was in the days of his strength. Oh! Out of my house, he shall never go anywhere but to heaven."

"Tell me something that happened to you yourself, poor soul! All this is good, but yet no news to me. Do I not know you of old?"

"Well, let me see. At first, I was much dazzled by the sunlight and could not go abroad (an owl!), but that has passed. And good Reicht Heynes—hmmph!"

"What of her?"

"This to your ear only, for she is a diamond. Her voice cuts through me like a knife, and all voices seem loud save yours, which is so mellow and sweet. Stay, now I will fit you with tidings; I spoke yesterday with an old man who pretends to be ill-tempered, sweating to appear so to others, but oh, so threadbare, and possessing the best good heart beneath."

"Why, 'tis a parish of angels," Margaret remarked ironically.

"Then why do you keep aloof from it?" Gerard retorted. "Well, he was telling me there is no parish in Holland where the Devil holds such sway as at Gouda. Among his examples, he said, 'We had a hermit, the holiest in Holland; but being Gouda, the Devil came for him this week and took him, bag and baggage; not a scrap of him left but a goodish piece of his skin, just like a hedgehog's, and a piece of old iron polished up.'"

Margaret smiled.

"Ay, but," continued Gerard, "the strange thing is, the cave has truly collapsed. Had I been so perverse as to resist you, it would surely have buried me dead there where I had buried myself alive. Therefore, in this, I see the finger of Providence, condemning my former path and approving my current way of life. What sayest thou?"
