When Sally left Detroit on the following Saturday, accompanied by Fillmore (who was returning to New York for a few days to secure offices and generally make his presence felt along Broadway), her spirits had fully rebounded. She felt guilty for having been so melodramatic, even morbid. Naturally, men wanted to climb the ladder of success. It was their job. She told herself she was deeply invested in Gerald’s triumph, and that the last thing she should complain about was the energy he poured into endeavors that would benefit them both. Her mood had been lifted by the excitement of the past few days.

Detroit—city of warm-hearted audiences—had embraced *The Primrose Way* with open arms. The theatre had opened on Tuesday, just as Teddy had predicted, and a full house, starved of entertainment during its enforced closure, greeted the play with rapturous applause. The critics, who usually disagreed with first-night crowds, this time all sang from the same hymn sheet: Gerald was hailed as the rising playwright, Elsa Doland as the future queen of the stage, and even Fillmore got a mention—though only as “the coming manager,” which, honestly, was better than nothing.

But every joy has its tiny snag. The one line devoted to Gladys Winch read: “Also included: Miss Wunch.”
“Wunch?” Fillmore said, seething. “One of the greatest character actresses on the stage!”
Sally laughed. “You’re being dramatic. It’s just a typo.”
“It’s an insult! A slander!”
But Fillmore’s spirit was too robust to be crushed by such petty indignities. Life was too bright to dwell on one dark spot. Business had been booming all week. Elsa Doland improved with each performance. They received a frantic telegram from Mr. Cracknell, begging to buy the play back—apparently Miss Hobson had softened over time. And best of all, the great Ike Schumann—owner of half the theatres in New York—had seen the show and declared it a winner.

On the train, Fillmore radiated importance. He sat across from Sally, puffing contentedly on a cigar like a man who’d just inherited a kingdom.
“You know what you should do?” Sally said, breaking a long silence.
“What?” Fillmore asked, startled out of a reverie involving fur coats and Fifth Avenue.
“Buy a fur coat. With an astrakhan collar. You’ve earned it.”
“I’m not thinking about a fur coat,” he said loftily.
“Oh, yes you are. I saw it in your eyes.”
“No, I wasn’t. I was thinking about Ike Schumann.”
“You mean *Ike*?”
“I mean *Ike*,” Fillmore snapped. “Everyone calls him Ike. Even *you* call him Ike.”
“He wears a fur coat,” Sally murmured.
Fillmore bristled. “Don’t bring up the fur coat again. And why shouldn’t I have one? I’ve got the money now.”
“Well then,” Sally said, grinning, “when you roll down Fifth Avenue in your car, I’ll point and say, ‘That’s my brother!’”
“‘Your brother?’” Fillmore scoffed. “No way.”
“He is, really.”
“You’re joking. That’s the great Fillmore Nicholas.”
“I know. But he really is my brother. And I was there when he bought that coat.”
“Stop it!”
“And it isn’t just the coat,” Sally continued, undeterred. “It’s what’s underneath. Tucked away inside that mass of fur, hiding behind that dollar cigar, is a man we can all be proud of… a man whose name is spoken in hushed tones…”
Fillmore checked his watch. “I’ve got to go see Ike.”
“We’re in hourly consultation with Ike,” Sally said sweetly.
“He wants to talk about the show. He thinks we should take it to Chicago before New York.”
“Oh no!” Sally gasped.
“Why not?”
She paused. She’d imagined Gerald would have to stay with the company if they opened in Chicago—meaning more separation. But then she realized: Gerald wouldn’t need to stay beyond the first few performances.
“You’re thinking we should establish a reputation in New York first,” she said. “That makes sense. But Chicago could help us too. I’ll think about it.”
He frowned dramatically. “All wrong,” Sally said. “You need to look pensive. Compress your lips. Lay your right forefinger against your temple. Like this.”
“Stop it!”
“Fillmore Nicholas,” she said solemnly, “if you knew how much pain it gives me to tease my only brother, you’d feel terrible. But it’s for your own good. Now go. I know Ike’s waiting with his watch out. ‘You do think he’ll come, Miss Nicholas?’ were his last words to me. And oh, Fill, the yearning in his voice! ‘Of course he will,’ I said. ‘For all his exalted position, my brother is kindness itself.’ ‘If I could only think so!’ he said, gulping. ‘But you know what these managers are—thousand calls, brooding over fur coats, forgetting everything.’ ‘Have no fear, Mr. Schumann,’ I said. ‘Fillmore Nicholas is a man of his word.’”

She would have gone on, but Fillmore had already stalked off, rigid with sisterly indignation. Sally watched him disappear, then picked up a magazine. She’d just tracked a gripping story through a jungle of ads—only to discover it was part one of two—when a voice spoke.

“How do you do, Miss Nicholas?”

Into the seat before her—formerly occupied by the coming manager—slid Bruce Carmyle, of all people. He did so with that air of polite restraint that never left him, as though he were stepping into a drawing room rather than a train compartment.

Sally nearly dropped her magazine. Everyone travels, of course, and finding someone you thought was in Europe in America isn’t exactly shocking—but still, it felt like time had rewound. A chapter reopened. A ghost returned.

“Mr. Carmyle!” she exclaimed.
She hadn’t thought of him since their parting on the Paris express. In fact, she’d barely remembered him at all—until now.
“We always meet on trains, don’t we?” she said, regaining her composure. “I never expected to see you here.”
“I came over,” he said.
She almost said, “Well, I gather that,” but caught herself. She’d been rude to him last time. Very rude. And she always regretted it afterward. So she settled for a flat, “Yes.”
“Yes,” he said, “it’s been many years since I took a real holiday. My doctor said I was run down. Seemed a good opportunity to visit America.”
“Ah,” Sally said, trying to sound interested. “And what do you think of our glorious country?”
Carmyle seemed relieved to shift focus. “I’ve been to Chicago,” he said. “A wonderful city.”
“Oh!”
“I heard you were in Detroit,” he added.
Sally blinked. “You heard? How?”
“I called at your New York address and made inquiries.”
“But how did you know where I lived?”
“My cousin—er—Lancelot told me.”

Sally froze. This was beginning to feel like a detective novel. Not that Bruce Carmyle had followed her across the Atlantic, necessarily—but the idea that he’d taken such interest in her whereabouts was unsettling. It was flattering, sure, but also a little unnerving. She didn’t care about Bruce Carmyle. Yet here he was, tracking her down.

She seized on Ginger as a distraction. “How is Mr. Kemp?”
Carmyle’s face darkened. “We’ve had no news of him.”
“No news? What do you mean?”
“He’s disappeared.”
“Disappeared? When?”
“Shortly after I saw you last.”
“Good heavens!”
“He insulted his uncle,” Carmyle said grimly. “His uncle Donald. Grossly. The one man he should have kept in with.”
“Keeping in with?”
“Yes. His future depended on it.”
“But what did he do?”
“I don’t know the details. My uncle won’t speak of it. He invited Lancelot to dinner to discuss plans—and Lancelot defied him. Rude. Insulting. My uncle refuses to have anything more to do with him. Apparently, he won some money at the tables in Roville. It turned his head completely. My uncle insists he’s mad. I agree. Since that night, nothing has been heard.”

Carmyle fell silent, lost in thought. Before Sally could respond, Fillmore loomed in the aisle, looking suspicious.
“Oh, hullo, Fill,” Sally said. “This is Mr. Carmyle. We met abroad.”
“Mr. Carmyle,” Fillmore said, sizing him up. “Pleased to meet you.”
He liked the man—something about the air of distinction appealed to him.
“Strange meeting again like this,” Fillmore said affably.
The porter hovered nearby, ready to make berths.
“You two should go to the smoking room,” Sally suggested. “I’m going to bed.”
She needed to be alone. To think.

Once in her berth, Sally lay awake, buzzing with strange emotions. Ginger had listened to her! Excellent Ginger! She felt proud. She felt like a creator—she had sparked something, unleashed it. She had transformed a meek, dependent young man into a rebellious firebrand who insulted uncles. It was a feat. Something attempted, something done. By all poetic rules, it deserved a peaceful night.

But the train jolted violently, as if testing new dance steps. Sleep refused to come. And then, in the quiet darkness, the Spectre of Doubt appeared—gaunt, questioning.

Had she done the right thing? Had she meddled too much? Was she a menace to society?

“What about it?” the Spectre whispered.

Daylight brought no answer. Breakfast failed to soothe her. At Grand Central, she stepped off the train in a state of remorseful dread. She declined Mr. Carmyle’s offer to drive her to the boarding-house and walked instead, hoping fresh air would clear her mind.

Now she wondered how she could ever have approved of her own rashness. What demon had possessed her to interfere in another’s life? She had egged Ginger on to ruin himself. She remembered that mad wireless message she’d sent him from the boat—*“Do it! Break free!”*—and blushed hotly.

Miserable Ginger! She pictured him—money gone, wandering London, seeking work, rejected by footmen, sleeping on the Embankment, staring into the Thames, climbing the parapet…

“Ugh!” she muttered.
She’d reached the boarding-house. Mrs. Meecher stood at the door, welcoming her with wide eyes, unaware that Sally had effectively murdered a red-headed young man of amiable manners and excellent behavior—except when influenced by meddling, muddling women.

Mrs. Meecher was chatty. *Variety* had reported the Detroit success. The old alumni were making headlines. Toto wore a blue ribbon in honor of the triumph. There was also, unseen, a chicken dinner inside Toto’s stomach. And was it true Mr. Fillmore had bought the play? “A great man,” Mrs. Meecher declared. “Mr. Faucitt always said so.”

“Oh, how is Mr. Faucitt?” Sally asked, ashamed she’d forgotten him.
“He’s gone,” Mrs. Meecher said, with such relish that Sally went white.
“Gone?”
“To England.”
Sally sighed with relief. “Oh, I thought you meant…”
“Oh no, not that,” Mrs. Meecher said, disappointed. “He’s well. Never seen anyone healthier. Seems this Spanish flu is a tonic. Though,” she added, “he’s had good news. His brother died.”
“What?”
“Not that that was good news, far from it. But the brother left him a fortune. Mr. Faucitt had to catch the Wednesday boat and go straight over. Wind up the estate. Left in a hurry. Sent his love. Said he’d write. Funny him having a brother, wasn’t it? Not that folks don’t have brothers. I’ve got two myself—one in Portland, Oregon, and the other goodness knows where.”

Sally escaped upstairs. For a moment, the news about Mr. Faucitt lifted her spirits. Dear old Mr. Faucitt! His later years would be comfortable. But soon, the melancholy returned. She threw herself on the bed, exhausted. She couldn’t sleep. Remorse kept her awake. And then—creaking boards. Yapping dogs. Someone prowling.

She turned over—and froze.

From under her bed, a shoe peeked out. A grey trouser leg. Then a dusty coat. And finally—a head of hair so red it could only belong to one person.

“Ginger!”

Mr. Lancelot Kemp, on all fours, blinked up at her.

“Oh, hullo!” he said.
