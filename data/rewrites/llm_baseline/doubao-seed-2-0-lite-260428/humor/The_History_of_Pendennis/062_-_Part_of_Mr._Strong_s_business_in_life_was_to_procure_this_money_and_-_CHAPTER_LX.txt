A full year’s slipped past since that silly little episode we recounted a few chapters back, as you’ve no doubt noticed if you haven’t been napping through the book. Arthur’s funereal all-black mourning coat is soon getting swapped out for a very dashing blue cavalry uniform, and the rest of his person has seen far nicer upgrades: the ridiculous ill-fitting wig he had to hide his post-fever thinning hair under has been tossed in the back of a wardrobe, and his own slightly scanty hair is finally out in public, no longer making him look like a dodgy elderly clerk skulking to a pawn shop. He even had the grand honour of trotting up to Buckingham Palace as a Cornet of the Clavering Yeomanry Cavalry, presented to the King himself by none other than the Marquis of Steyne.

His old uncle the Major basically badgered him into that, by the way. The old gentleman flatly refused to let a year go by without his nephew properly “qualifying as a gentleman” by doing the whole court presentation bit. He thought Arthur ought to belong to clubs far fancier than the rowdy, beer-soaked Megatherium, and he’s been announcing to every single club bore, dinner party guest and random stranger he meets that he’s bitterly disappointed Arthur’s inheritance only comes to fifteen hundred a year, not the fancy fortune he’d daydreamed about snagging for the lad.

On Arthur’s little 1500 a year income, he’s suddenly the most popular bloke on the edge of polite society. His publishers stop treating him like a forgetful intern and start bowing and scraping every time he drops off a manuscript. Mamas with marriageable daughters don’t exactly shove their prettiest, most ambitious girls at him – those ones are all gunning for earls with 10,000 acre estates and private race tracks, duh – but the mamas stuck with the plain ones, the ones with a slightly lopsided shoulder or a habit of reciting amateur poetry mid-roast dinner? They’re tripping over their own silk skirts to get Arthur to propose. One Mrs. Hawxby even forced her daughter Beatrice, who fancied herself a literary genius, to take all her billiard lessons exclusively from Arthur, and refused to ride in any pony cart other than his, until the Major showed up unannounced at her country house party, dragged Arthur out by the scruff of his neck before she could lock them in the linen closet to force a wedding. She still yells about Arthur “leading her innocent daughter on” at every village tea party, for the record.

The Major wanted Arthur to spend Christmas back at Clavering Hall, but the lad couldn’t bear it. Clavering was too close to old Fairoaks, and every corner of the place was stuffed full of too many sad memories of his late mum, so he skipped the trip entirely.

We haven’t checked in on the Claverings in a hot minute, and poor things, they’ve had an absolutely garbage twelve months. Lady Clavering, the sweetest soul who ever spilled gravy on a duchess’s dress and mispronounced every French menu item, has had her famously good temper tested to breaking point by nonstop family drama. “I’d rather eat a plain boiled turnip for dessert, Strong, than that fancy pineapple and all those expensive Muscatel grapes Sir Francis keeps ordering,” she’d confide to her loyal friend, “if only I got ten minutes of quiet to eat it in. I was far happier back when I was a widow, before all this stupid money landed on my lap!”

Their big London social launch was a flop worse than an overbaked pancake. No one liked them, no one gave them credit for the endless fancy dinners they hosted, and everyone gossiped behind their backs nonstop. “Sir Francis Clavering? Total deadbeat,” all the high society snobs said. “Gambles, cheats at cards, runs away from any fight, and his whole household reeks of tacky new money. Who even is that Lady Clavering, with her dropped h’s and random Calcutta fortune? Her daughter Blanche prances around acting like she’s a Parisian aristocrat when she was raised in a boarding school in Bengal! All their friends are shifty bookies and drunk ex-army men!”

The great Lady Rockminster, who’d originally promised to sponsor Lady Clavering into the highest circles, dumped her faster than a hot potato after three parties. All the fancy great ladies refused to bring their daughters to Clavering parties, the young men who did turn up got drunk, ate all the canapes, and acted like they were crashing a cheap pub night. Poor Lady Clavering ended up inviting what she called “the canal set” – nice, unpretentious folks who don’t care about your great-great-grandfather’s title – to her parlour, because none of the top-tier ton would come. The canal set was way more fun, in fairness, but nothing stings worse than high society ditching you first, rather than you ditching them.

We all thought Harry Foker, that silly rich brewery heir, would be the one loyal friend who stuck around. But his huge, ridiculous crush on Blanche Amory that he thought was private somehow leaked all over town, got back to his soft doting mother, and then landed on the desk of his bald, no-nonsense father who ran the Foker brewery. The two of them had a screaming match so loud the neighbours three houses down could hear, and Harry got banished from England for a full year. His dad gave him an ultimatum: come back in 12 months and marry his boring, rich cousin, or you get 300 pounds a year allowance and never see me, your mum, or the brewery ever again. Poor Harry dragged himself off to Europe, so miserable that even the glitzy Paris boulevards looked drab and the famous Italian sun felt like it was drizzling.

That same year, Sir Francis Clavering blew seven thousand pounds on the Derby. For all you turf fans out there, that’s the ridiculous year a random horse called Podasokus – named after some random warrior from the Iliad by a pretentious young lord who thought no one else had read Homer – won the race, while every single so-called “expert” bet on a favourite called Borax that finished so far back no one even saw it cross the line. Sir Francis, who’d been hanging around the shadiest bookies in town bragging he had “top secret inside information”, bet the whole seven grand on the favourite. Poor Lady Clavering had already paid off his card debts a dozen times, listened to a hundred of his oaths promising he’d never gamble again, bailed him out of trouble with horse dealers and money lenders over and over, and now she was on the hook for that massive bill.

Major Pendennis, who’d already nosed his way into every corner of the Clavering household and knew exactly where all their family skeletons were locked up (including the ugly old story of Lady Clavering’s first husband Amory, back in Calcutta) had kept all those secrets quiet this whole time, and let Lady Clavering know he’d never spill them. He told her straight the only reason he’d been such a loyal family friend was that he’d been keen to arrange a mutually beneficial marriage between Arthur and Blanche. “Sentiment’s all well and good, my dear lady,” he’d said, “but Arthur’s a smart, ambitious lad who needs a leg up. What’s the point of Sir Francis’s useless Parliament seat? He never shows up to the house, never says a single word there. Everyone at Oxbridge said Arthur was a brilliant orator. Give him that seat, and he’ll make a name for himself so grand your grandkids will be proud. Love marriages? Rubbish! People elope to Gretna Green over moonlit gazes, then two years later they’re fighting over the dinner menu and one of them runs off to Switzerland with a new lover. The only sensible union is two people who agree to scratch each other’s backs.”

Lady Clavering blubbered that she thought Arthur ought to marry the nice, gentle Laura instead, who everyone adored, but the Major just laughed. “Marry on 500 pounds a year and sentiment alone? My good soul, you’ve gone mad!”

He gave Blanche the exact same speech later, and she thought it was perfectly reasonable. Arthur was perfectly nice, after all, until someone richer came along to catch her eye.

Arthur, for his part, wandered through that spring half-convinced his old fiery romantic days were over. He couldn’t believe he was the same lad who’d had that grand all-consuming passion years ago, and last year was running around like a loon over that actress Fanny. Now he just hung around the Clavering house, was polite to Blanche, didn’t even formally court her, and everyone just sort of accepted he was part of the furniture there.

We don’t really change all that much as we get older, you know. We just stop hiding the traits we’ve always had. The wild passionate teen turns into the jaded, laid-back middle aged bloke who shrugs at empty sentiment. The ambitious guy climbing the social ladder, even when he’s got all the medals and the crowds cheering, still has a quiet little secret ache for the days he was a small kid playing in the garden. The poor wretch shipwrecked and clinging to a floating spar in the middle of the ocean is thinking the exact same thing. No matter where life lands you, deep down, you’re still that same small kid you remember being, before the whole messy voyage of adulthood even started.
