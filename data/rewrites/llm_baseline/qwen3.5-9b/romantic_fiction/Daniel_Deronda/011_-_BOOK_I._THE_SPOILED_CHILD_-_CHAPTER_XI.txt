Getting to know someone—or something—is simply the process of turning a blank page into a defined sketch. For Gwendolen Harleth, the desire to be formally introduced to Mr. Grandcourt had been building slowly, a quiet anticipation rather than a sudden impulse. Yet, when Lord Brackenshaw stepped aside to make way for the anticipated stranger, and Gwendolen found herself face-to-face with the reality of the man, a sharp jolt ran through her. It flushed her cheeks and burned deeper as she became acutely aware of the sensation. The shock stemmed entirely from the reversal of her expectations; Grandcourt was far removed from every imaginary portrait she had constructed.

He stood slightly taller than she did, their eyes seemingly level. There was not a hint of a smile upon his face as he regarded her, nor any trace of self-consciousness or anxiety in his posture. As he lifted his hat, revealing an extensive baldness framed only by a sparse fringe of reddish-blonde hair, she also noted a perfect hand. The line of his features, from brow to chin, was undeniably handsome, stripped of beard and marked by angles that deviated only moderately from the vertical. Even his slight moustache hung perpendicularly. It was impossible for a human countenance to be so utterly free of grimaces or nervous wriggles; equally, it seemed impossible for a living, awake man to appear so devoid of animation.

One might argue that the quintessential Englishman, stiffening himself after a bow into rigid formality, agrees with severity and appears internally drilled, suggesting a suppressed vitality that might explode violently once released from parade. But Grandcourt possessed no such rigidity; his bearing leaned instead toward the flaccid. His complexion held a faded fairness, reminiscent of an actress's skin once the artificial rouge and powder had washed away. His long, narrow gray eyes expressed nothing but indifference.

Attempts at precise description are foolish; who can capture a human being in a single instant? Even when presented to us, we merely begin the study of their appearance, a knowledge to be completed by countless impressions gathered under varying circumstances. We learn the alphabet, but we remain unsure of the language. What Gwendolen grasped in the first moments of their meeting was summarized by a simple contrast: *He is not ridiculous.*

Immediately, however, Lord Brackenshaw departed, and what is termed "conversation" commenced. Its primary and constant element was that Grandcourt looked at Gwendolen with a persistent, slightly exploratory gaze, his expression unchanged, while she occasionally met his stare with a flash of observation, softened just enough by coquetry. Following her replies, there would invariably be a pause, longer or shorter, before he spoke again.

"I used to think archery was a great bore," Grandcourt began. He spoke with a fine accent, though delivered with a certain broken drawl, as if a distinguished personage suffered from a distinguished cold lodged in his chest.

"Are you converted today?" asked Gwendolen.

*(A pause ensued, during which she imagined various degrees and modes of opinion regarding herself that Grandcourt might hold.)*

"Yes, since I saw you shooting. In matters of this sort, one generally observes people missing and simpering."

"I suppose you are a first-rate shot with a rifle."

*(Another pause. Having taken a rapid assessment of Grandcourt, Gwendolen mentally drafted a brief, graphic description of him for an indefinite audience.)*

"I have left off shooting."

"Oh, then you are a formidable person. Those who accomplish things once and then abandon them make one feel very contemptible, as if one were wearing cast-off fashions. I hope you haven't abandoned all follies, for I practice a great many."

"What do you call follies?"

"Well, in general, I believe whatever is agreeable is classified as a folly. But I hear you haven't left off hunting."

*(Here, Gwendolen recalled rumors concerning Grandcourt's status and concluded he was the most aristocratically appearing man she had ever encountered.)*

"One must do something."

"And do you care about the turf? Or is that among the things you've discarded?"

*(In this interval, Gwendolen reasoned that a man of such cold, calm manners might actually be preferable as a husband—he wouldn't likely interfere with her preferences.)*

"I run a horse now and then, but I don't pursue it as fervently as some men do. Are you fond of horses?"

"Yes, indeed. I never enjoy my life more than when I am on horseback, having a great gallop. I think of nothing. I only feel myself strong and happy."

*(Gwendolen paused, wondering if Grandcourt approved of her sentiments, yet reassuring herself that she had no intention of disguising her tastes.)*

"Do you like danger?"

"I don't know. When I am riding, I never consider danger. It seems to me that if I broke my bones, I shouldn't feel it. I would go at anything that came in my path."

"You would perhaps enjoy tiger-hunting or pig-sticking. I witnessed some of that for a season or two in the East. Everything here is poor stuff compared to that."

"_You_ are fond of danger, then?"

*(Gwendolen speculated that men of the coldest manners were often the most adventurous, feeling the strength of her own insight as she weighed the possibility.)*

"One must have something or other. But one gets accustomed to it."

"I begin to think I am very fortunate, because everything is new to me: it is only that I cannot get enough of it. I am unused to anything except being dull, which is precisely what I would like to leave off, just as you have left off shooting."

*(It occurred to Gwendolen that a man of cold, distinguished demeanor might prove a dull companion; yet, on the other hand, she considered that most people were dull, that she had rarely observed husbands acting as companions—and ultimately, she resolved not to accept Grandcourt.)*

"Why are you dull?"

"This is a dreadful neighborhood. There is nothing to be done here. That is why I practiced my archery."

*(Reflecting that the life of an unmarried woman, unable to move freely and lacking command over anything, must inevitably grow dull regardless of comparison as time passes.)*

"You have made yourself queen of it. I imagine you will carry the first prize."

"I don't know that. I have great rivals. Did you not notice how well Miss Arrowpoint shot?"

*(Gwendolen thought that men were known to choose someone other than the woman they most admired, recalling several instances from novels.)*

"Miss Arrowpoint. No—that is, yes."

"Shall we go now to hear the scoring? Everyone is moving to the other end—shall we join them? I think my uncle is looking toward me. He perhaps wants me."

Finding relief in shifting the situation, Gwendolen ended the tête-à-tête. It was not that the private conversation had been disagreeable; rather, while it lasted, she could not shake the unusual flush on her cheeks or the sense of surprise that made her feel less mistress of herself than usual. This Mr. Grandcourt, who seemed to value his own importance far more than hers—a trait few can tolerate—must not assume he was of great significance to her, nor that because others speculated on him as a desirable match, she placed herself entirely at his disposal. How Grandcourt had filled the silences would become clearer later.

"You have just missed the gold arrow, Gwendolen," said Mr. Gascoigne. "Miss Juliet Fenn scores eight points above you."

"I am very glad to hear it. I should have felt that I was making myself too disagreeable—taking the best of everything," Gwendolen replied quite easily.

It was impossible to feel jealousy toward Juliet Fenn, a girl as mediocre as a midday market in every respect save her archery and plainness, in which latter quality she was notable, much like her father: underhanging jaw and a receding brow resembling that of the more intelligent fish species. (Considering the immense weight placed on such physical accidents in female offspring, surely marriageable men—or, as the new England calls them, "intending bridegrooms"—should examine themselves dispassionately in the mirror, since their natural instinct to select a mate prettier than themselves is not guaranteed to shield them from the effects of their own ugliness.)

Now, a lively movement swept through the mingling groups, carrying the conversation along with it. Everyone spoke to everyone else in turn. Gwendolen, choosing to survey her surroundings, observed that Grandcourt was having Klesmer presented to him by someone unknown to her—a middle-aged man with a dark, full face and fat hands, who appeared on the easiest terms with both parties.
