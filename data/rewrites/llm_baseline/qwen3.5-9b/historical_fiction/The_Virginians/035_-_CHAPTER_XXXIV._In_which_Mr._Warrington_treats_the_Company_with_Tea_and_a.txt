Generous with his easily acquired wealth, hospitable, and cordial to all, our young Virginian felt bound by the customs of a man of fashion to treat his country friends to an entertainment at the Assembly Rooms. According to the tradition of the day, he invited nearly the entire remaining company gathered at the Wells.

Card tables were arranged in one apartment for those who, as was common across European society, could not endure an evening without pastime. Another chamber was prepared for supper, featuring champagne in profusion and bowls of negus. The large assembly room was reserved for the dance, enjoyed by Harry Warrington's guests in the homely fashion of our ancestors. I cannot imagine the amusement was particularly lively. Minuets were called first, two or three of which were performed by various couples. The spinsters of the highest rank withdrew for the minuet, and since Lady Maria Esmond, being an earl's daughter, held the position of highest rank present—save for the lame Lady Augusta Crutchley—Mr. Warrington danced the first minuet with his cousin. He acquitted himself to the satisfaction of the entire room, moving far more elegantly than Mr. Wolfe, who stood up with Miss Lowther.

Having finished with Lady Maria, Mr. Warrington requested the honor of Miss Theo for the next minuet. Accordingly, Miss Theo, blushing and looking radiant, performed her exercise to the great delight of her parents and the sheer fury of Miss Humpleby, Sir John Humpleby's daughter of Liphook, who had expected, at the very least, to follow Lady Maria.

Following the minuets came the country dances, accompanied by a harp, fiddle, and flageolet perched in a small balcony. They thrummed through the evening with rather feeble and melancholy tunes. If one takes up an old book of music today and plays these airs, one wonders how people ever found them anything but sad. Yet, they loved, frisked, laughed, and courted to this somber accompaniment. Scarce one of the airs lacks an *amari aliquid*, a tang of sadness. Perhaps it is because they are old and defunct; their plaintive echoes call out to us from the limbo of the past, to which they have been consigned for this century. Perhaps they were gay while alive, and our descendants, hearing the works of certain modern masters, will exclaim, "Bon Dieu, is this the music which amused our forefathers?"

Mr. Warrington had the distinction of a duchess's company at his tea—the Duchess of Queensberry, the heroine of Colonel Lambert's and Mr. Prior's affections. Though the Duchess carefully turned her back upon a countess present, laughed loudly, glanced over her shoulder, and pointed at her with her fan, almost the entire company pushed, bowed, cringed, smiled, and backed away from this countess, paying scant attention to Her Grace of Queensberry, her jokes, her fan, and her airs. This countess was none other than the Countess of Yarmouth-Walmoden, the lady whom His Majesty George II, King of Great Britain, France, and Ireland, Defender of the Faith, delighted to honor.

She had met Harry Warrington in the walks that morning and had been exceedingly gracious to the young Virginian. She had promised a game of cards that night. Purblind old Colonel Blinkinsop, mistaking the invitation for himself, had made the profoundest bow.

"Pooh! Pooh!" declared the Countess of England and Hanover. "I do not mean you. I mean the young Firshinian!"

Everybody congratulated the youth on his good fortune. At night, to demonstrate their loyalty, the world thronged around Lady Yarmouth. My Lord Bamborough was eager to make her a partner at quadrille. Lady Blanche Pendragon, that model of virtue; Sir Lancelot Quintain, that pattern of knighthood; Mr. Dean of Ealing, that exemplary divine; along with numerous gentlemen, noblemen, generals, colonels, matrons, and spinsters of the highest rank, watched for a smile from her or jumped up to join her card table. Lady Maria waited upon her with meek respect, and Madame de Bernstein treated the Hanoverian lady with profound gravity and courtesy.

Harry's bow had been no lower than hospitality required, but Miss Hester chose to be indignant. She scarce spoke a word to her partner during their dance, and when he led her to the supper room, she was little more communicative. To enter, they had to pass Madame Walmoden's card table. Good-naturedly, she called out to her host as he passed, asking if her "breddy liddle bardner liked tanzing?"

"I thank your ladyship, I do not like tanzing, nor do I like cards," replied Miss Hester, tossing up her head. Dropping a curtsey like a cheese, she strutted away from the Countess's table.

Mr. Warrington was deeply offended. Sarcasm from the young toward the old pained him; flippancy toward himself hurt him. Courteous in his simple way to everyone he met, he expected similar politeness in return. Hetty perfectly understood the offense she caused; she could see the displeasure reddening on her partner's honest face with a sidelong glance, yet she wore her most ingenuous smile. As she approached the sideboard where refreshments were set, she artlessly said, "What a horrid, vulgar old woman that is; don't you think so?"

"What woman?" asked the young man.

"That German woman—my Lady Yarmouth—to whom all the men are bowing and cringing."

"Her ladyship has been very kind to me," Harry said grimly. "Won't you have some of this custard?"

"And you have been bowing to her, too! You look as if your negus was not nice," Miss Hetty continued harmlessly.

"It is not very good negus," Harry admitted with a gulp.

"And the custard is bad too! I declare 'tis made with bad eggs!" cried Miss Lambert.

"I wish, Hester, that the entertainment and the company had been better to your liking," said poor Harry.

"'Tis very unfortunate; but I dare say you could not help it," cried the young woman, tossing her little curly head.

Mr. Warrington groaned in spirit, perhaps in body, clenching his fists and teeth. The little torturer artlessly continued, "You seem disturbed: shall we go to my mamma?"

"Yes, let us go to your mamma," cried Mr. Warrington, with glaring eyes and shouting, "Curse you, why are you always standing in the way?" at an unlucky waiter.

"La! Is that the way you speak in Virginia?" asked Miss Pertness.

"We are rough there sometimes, madam, and can't help being disturbed," he said slowly, with a quiver in his whole frame, looking down upon her with fire flashing from his eyes.

Hetty saw nothing distinctly afterward until she reached her mother. Never had she seen Harry look so handsome or so noble.

"You look pale, child!" cried her mother, anxious, like all *pavidae matres*. "'Tis the cold—or no, I mean the heat. Thank you, Mr. Warrington." She made him a faint curtsey as Harry bowed tremendously and walked elsewhere among his guests.

He hardly knew what was happening at first, so angry was he. He was aroused by another altercation between his aunt and the Duchess of Queensberry. When the royal favorite passed the Duchess, Her Grace gave her Ladyship an awful stare out of eyes no longer bright as in the young days when they "set the world on fire." Turning round with an affected laugh to her neighbor, she shot a ceaseless fire of giggles and sneers at the jolly Hanoverian lady.

The Countess pursued her game at cards, either unaware or unwilling to know how her enemy was gibing at her. A feud of many years' date existed between their Graces of Queensberry and the family on the throne.

"How you all bow down to the idol! Don't tell me! You are as bad as the rest, my good Madame Bernstein!" the Duchess said.

"Ah, what a true Christian country this is! And how your dear first husband, the Bishop, would have liked to see such a sight!"

"Forgive me, if I fail quite to understand your Grace."

"We are both of us growing old, my good Bernstein, or perhaps we won't understand when we don't choose to. That is the way with us women, my good young Iroquois."

"Your Grace remarked that it was a Christian country," said Madame de Bernstein. "I failed to perceive the point."

"Indeed, my good creature, there is very little point in it! I meant we were such good Christians because we were so forgiving. Don't you remember reading, when you were young, or your husband the Bishop reading in the pulpit, how when a woman amongst the Jews was caught doing wrong, the Pharisees were for stoning her out of hand? Far from stoning such a woman now, look how fond we are of her! Any man in this room would go round it on his knees if yonder woman bade him.
