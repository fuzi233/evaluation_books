Cheerful as my godmother was by nature, and as industrious as she could be, for our sakes, in setting a brightness upon the evening at La Terrasse, enjoyment did not thoroughly take hold of us till the winter itself announced deliverance. The wind had been crying all along the eaves like a creature shut out; the snow kept striking the panes with a dry, stinging sound, and, now and then, the blast—finding some hollow under the roof—set it to a long, organ-like moan. Those who sit warm in lamplight know that comfort can still be divided from the heart: imagination is not chained to the hearth; it will go pacing with the absent—out where the road is lost in drifts, where the gates are buried, where listening becomes pain.

At last, through that wild chorus, came the sharper, welcome notes—iron on stone, a horse’s hoof striking firm ground; a jingle of bit and chain; a voice lifted short against the wind. It was as if the whole house leaned to hear. We ran down into the hall; yet even while we hurried, the storm’s violence made us cautious. They entered with a warning gesture, as if they brought the weather with them.

Father and son came at last to the château: the Count de Bassompierre had ridden with Dr. Bretton. When the door opened, we saw not men, but two white shapes—two walking mounds of snow, the folds of their greatcoats stuffed, their hats and shoulders thick as if winter had laid hands on them and would not let go. Mrs. Bretton took them in with one glance, and her motherly authority rose like a staff.

“To the kitchen—instantly. Not one step on my staircase—at your peril—till you have left off that mask of Old Christmas.”

And so, without appeal, they were driven to the kitchen; and we, obedient only in the letter, followed them there. It was a large old Dutch kitchen, with dark beams, warm brick, a wide hearth where the flame did not merely burn but lived—crackling, hissing, and then dropping suddenly to a red, steady breathing. Pots hung ready; a copper pan caught the firelight on its belly; a smell of woodsmoke, and something faintly sweet—orange-peel, perhaps, and clove—lingered as if the room remembered former seasons.

The little Countess, white too with blown snow where she had dashed near the door, danced a circle round her equally white sire, clapping her hands.

“Papa, papa, you look like an enormous Polar bear!”

The bear shook himself; a shower of ice-dust flew out and pattered on the flags, and the sprite fled, squealing and laughing, only to return the next instant, bold again, eager to help tear away the arctic disguise. When the Count, at last, got free of his dreadnought of a greatcoat, he lifted it as if he would drop it upon her like an avalanche.

“Come, then,” she said, bending her head as if inviting doom.

He advanced it playfully; she bounded away—quick as a little chamois; soft too, with that velvet grace which makes speed look like play. Her laugh rang clear, silver on crystal. She took his hands—those cold, gloveless hands—and rubbed them hard between her own, as if she meant, by sheer affection, to force warmth back into the fingers. When she rose on tiptoe and reached his lips for a kiss, there shone round her a kind of light—no miracle, only that human radiance which love gives off without asking leave. The grave and reverend seigneur looked down on her as men look on what is indeed the apple of their eye.

“Mrs. Bretton,” said he, “what am I to do with this daughter—or daughterling? She neither grows in wisdom nor in stature. Don’t you find her pretty nearly as much the child as she was ten years ago?”

“She cannot be more the child,” returned Mrs. Bretton, “than this great boy of mine,”—for she was already at war with her son about some alteration of dress she deemed prudent, and which he resisted with laughing obstinacy, leaning against the Dutch dresser and keeping her at arm’s length.

“Come, mamma,” he said, “by way of compromise—and to get warmth inside as well as out—let us have a Christmas wassail-cup, and toast Old England here on the hearth.”

At that word the kitchen took on its ceremonial air. Mrs. Bretton herself moved with decision to the region of jars and spices, and instructed Martha in the mysteries as if they were ancient rites: the bowl was set to warm; sugar was measured; a cinnamon stick snapped and gave out its dry perfume; cloves pricked the air with a dark sweetness; and, when the liquor met the heat, it sent up a breath like winter made friendly—steam carrying ale and spice, orange and nutmeg, rich and rough together. The sound of it—the first low simmer, the spoon scraping the bottom, the small clink of ladle against rim—seemed to lay a soft hand over the wind’s howl outside.

She poured the draught into a Bretton flagon, and it was served round—reaming hot—by means of a small silver vessel which I recognized at once: Graham’s christening-cup, lifted now to mouths grown grave, and yet capable of holiday.

“Here’s to Auld Lang Syne!” cried the Count, holding the glancing cup aloft; and then, turning to Mrs. Bretton, he began, in that homely, mountain-scented Scots which carries the taste of heather and peat even when spoken in a foreign room:

“We twa ha’ paidlet i’ the burn
Fra morning sun till dine,
But seas between us braid ha’ roared
Sin’ auld lang syne.

“And surely ye’ll be your pint-stoup,
And surely I’ll be mine;
And we’ll taste a cup o’ kindness yet
For auld lang syne.”

“Scotch! Scotch!” cried Paulina. “Papa is talking Scotch; and Scotch he is—partly. We are Home and de Bassompierre, Caledonian and Gallic.”

“And is that a Scotch reel you are dancing, you Highland fairy?” asked her father. “Mrs. Bretton, there will be a green ring growing up in the middle of your kitchen shortly. I would not answer for her being quite cannie: she is a strange little mortal.”

“Tell Lucy to dance with me, papa; there is Lucy Snowe.”

Mr. Home—there was still quite as much plain Mr. Home about him as proud Count de Bassompierre—held out his hand to me with a kindness that had no condescension in it. He said he remembered me well; and, even had his memory been less trustworthy, my name had been so often on his daughter’s lips, and he had heard so many long tales of me, that I must seem an old acquaintance.

All had tasted the wassail but Paulina; nobody wished to profane her airy movement with a hot draught. Yet she would not be overlooked nor baulked of mortal privileges.

“Let me taste,” she demanded of Graham, as he was setting the cup on the dresser shelf out of her reach.

Mrs. Bretton and Mr. Home were already deep in talk. Dr. John, though he said little, had been watching the fairy’s dance, and he liked it. Not only the beauty and ease of those movements pleased his grace-loving eye; that liberty in his mother’s house charmed him because it set him at ease too: again she seemed a child for him—again almost his playmate. I wondered what his first words would be to her; they came with a half-mocking deference that revived the old days as surely as a tune revives a room.

“Your ladyship wishes for the tankard?”

“I think I said so. I think I intimated as much.”

“Couldn’t consent to a step of the kind on any account. Sorry for it, but couldn’t do it.”

“Why? I am quite well now: it can’t break my collar-bone again, or dislocate my shoulder. Is it wine?”

“No; nor dew.”

“I don’t want dew; I don’t like dew: but what is it?”

“Ale—strong ale—old October; brewed, perhaps, when I was born.”

“It must be curious: is it good?”

“Excessively good.”

He took it down, administered to himself a second dose, let his mischievous eyes confess extreme contentment, and replaced the cup with solemnity.

“I should like a little,” said Paulina, looking up. “I never had any old October: is it sweet?”

“Perilously sweet,” said Graham.

She continued to look up—exactly with the face of a child longing for a forbidden dainty. At last the Doctor relented, and—because indulgence is often sweetest to the giver—he let her taste from his hand. His eyes, always frank in revealing pleasurable feelings, smiled their confession; and he prolonged the sport by managing the cup so that only a drop at a time could reach the rosy lips that courted its rim. The ale smelled of spice and warmed grain; it had that deep, yeasty breath of barley long kept, and the wassail’s cinnamon and citrus rode above it like a holiday.

“A little more—a little more,” she said, petulantly touching his hand with one forefinger to coax a more generous tilt. “It smells of spice and sugar, but I can’t taste it; your wrist is so stiff, and you are so stingy.”

He indulged her, but whispered with mock gravity, “Don’t tell my mother or Lucy; they wouldn’t approve.”

“Nor do I,” she answered; and as soon as she had fairly assayed the draught, her tone altered as if some disenchanting potion had undone the fairy-work. “I find it anything but sweet; it is bitter and hot, and takes away my breath. Your old October was only desirable while forbidden. Thank you—no more.”

With a slight bend—careless, yet as graceful as her dance—she glided away to her father.

I believed her. The child of seven lived still in the girl of seventeen, ready to start up at a touch; but she was not ruled by it.

Graham looked after her a little baffled, a little puzzled. His eye returned to her more than once through the rest of the evening; she did not seem to notice him. When we went up to the drawing-room for tea, she took her father’s arm as if it were the natural place appointed to her. Her eyes and ears were dedicated to him. He and Mrs. Bretton did most of the talking; Paulina did the best listening—attending closely, prompting him with eager little questions that drew out recollection as one draws out a thread.

“And where were you at such a time, papa? And what did you say then? And tell Mrs. Bretton what happened on that occasion.”

She yielded no more effervescence of glee; the infant sparkle had exhaled with the kitchen heat. Now she was soft, thoughtful, docile. Her good-night was pretty to see. To Graham her manner had a touch of dignity; in her slight smile and quiet bow spoke the Countess. Graham bowed in return with gravity, as if unsure how to reconcile, in one image, the dancing fairy and the delicate dame.

Next morning we assembled round the breakfast-table, all of us shivering and fresh from the chill bravery of washing in winter water. Outside, the day made a noise like an adversary. Mrs. Bretton, after one look at the casements, pronounced a decree: nobody, unless driven by dire necessity, should quit her house.

Indeed, egress seemed next to impossible. The drift darkened the lower panes; and when one peered out, the sky looked vexed and dim, as if powdered glass were blown through it. The snowfall had ceased, but what had already fallen was being torn up again and whirled about by brief shrieking gusts, cast into a hundred fantastic shapes—now a wall, now a wave, now a sudden white blindness across the garden paths.

The Countess seconded the decree with zeal.

“Papa shall not go out,” she said, drawing her chair beside her father’s. “I will look after him. You won’t go into town, will you, papa?”

“Ay—and No,” he answered. “If you and Mrs. Bretton are very good to me, Polly—kind, you know, and attentive—if you pet me in a very nice manner and make much of me, I may be induced to wait an hour after breakfast and see whether this razor-edged wind settles. But, you see, you give me no breakfast; you offer me nothing; you let me starve.”

“Quick! please, Mrs. Bretton—pour out the coffee,” begged Paulina, while she set herself to the Count’s plate with the air of a small, passionate housekeeper. “Since he grew into a Count, he has needed so much attention.”

She split and prepared a roll with quick, neat fingers.

“There, papa—your pistolets are charged,” said she. “And there is some marmalade—just the same sort we used to have at Bretton, and which you said was as good as if it had been conserved in Scotland—”

“And which your little ladyship used to beg for my boy,” Mrs. Bretton interposed, looking at Graham with a mother’s treacherous delight. “Do you remember? How you would come to my elbow and touch my sleeve, whispering, ‘Please, ma’am, something good for Graham—a little marmalade, or honey, or jam?’”

“No, mamma,” broke in Dr. John, laughing and yet reddening. “It surely was not so. I could not have cared for these things.”

“Did he or did he not, Paulina?”

“He liked them,” Paulina asserted, without mercy.

“Never blush for it, John,” said Mr. Home. “I like them myself yet, and always did. And Polly showed sense in catering for a friend’s material comforts: it was I who put her in the way of such manners—nor do I let her forget them. Polly, offer me a small slice of that tongue.”

“There, papa: but remember you are only waited upon with this assiduity on condition of being persuadable, and reconciling yourself to La Terrasse for the day.”

“Mrs. Bretton,” said the Count suddenly, “I want to get rid of my daughter—to send her to school. Do you know of any good school?”

“There is Lucy’s place—Madame Beck’s.”

“Miss Snowe is in a school?”

“I am a teacher,” I said; and I was glad—strangely glad—of the chance to say it plain. For a little while, I had been feeling as if I sat among them under some borrowed light. Mrs. Bretton and her son knew my circumstances; the Count and his daughter did not. I spoke readily; yet, at the words, there rose in me—uninvoked—a swarm of thoughts, dim and thick; and I sighed in spite of myself.

Mr. Home kept his eyes on his plate for a time; he said nothing. Perhaps he had not caught my words; perhaps, being Scotch, and proud after his own fashion, he judged that on such a confession politeness should not fasten. Whether his pride was pseudo or dignity real, I leave wide and undecided. As it touched me, I can answer simply: then and always he showed himself a true-hearted gentleman.

He was by nature a feeler and a thinker; over his emotions lay a mellow melancholy, deepened—where trouble and bereavement had passed—into cloud. He did not know much about Lucy Snowe; what he knew he did not very accurately comprehend; his misconceptions of my character could have made me smile on another morning. But he saw my walk lay on the shady side of the hill. He gave me credit for keeping my course honestly straight. He would have helped me if he could; having no opportunity to help, he wished me well. When at last he did look at me, his eye was kind; and when he spoke, his voice was benevolent.

“Yours,” said he, “is an arduous calling. I wish you health and strength to win in it—success.”

His fair little daughter did not take the knowledge so quietly. She fixed on me wide eyes of wonder—almost dismay.

“Are you a teacher?” she cried; and, after a pause, as if tasting the idea and finding it sharp, “Well, I never knew what you were, nor ever thought of asking: for me, you were always Lucy Snowe.”

“And what am I now?” I asked, unable to forbear.

“Yourself, of course. But do you really teach here, in Villette?”

“I really do.”

“And do you like it?”

“Not always.”

“And why do you go on with it?”

Her father looked at her as if he might check such questioning; but he only said, indulgent, “Proceed, Polly—proceed with that catechism: prove yourself the little wiseacre you are. If Miss Snowe were to blush and look confused, I should have to bid you hold your tongue; and you and I would sit out this meal in disgrace. But she only smiles—so push her hard. Well, Miss Lucy, why do you go on with it?”

“Chiefly, I fear, for the sake of the money I get.”

“Not then from motives of pure philanthropy? Polly and I were clinging to that hypothesis as the most lenient way of accounting for your eccentricity.”

“No—no, sir. Rather for the roof of shelter I am thus enabled to keep over my head; and for the comfort of mind it gives me to think that while I can work for myself, I am spared the pain of being a burden to anybody.”

“Papa,” said Paulina, low and with feeling, “say what you will, I pity Lucy.”

“Take up that pity, Miss de Bassompierre,” he returned, not harshly but with a firm tenderness. “Take it up in both hands, as you might a little callow gosling squattering out of bounds without leave; put it back in the warm nest of the heart whence it issued, and receive in your ear this whisper: if my Polly ever came to know by experience the uncertain nature of this world’s goods, I should like her to act as Lucy acts—to work for herself, that she might burden neither kith nor kin.”

“Yes, papa,” she said, pensively and tractably. Yet her glance stole to me again, as if she could not help tasting compassion, even when bid to set it down.

“But this school,” pursued Mr. Home, changing grave to gay with the ease of a man who knows how to protect feeling by humour, “would Madame Beck admit my Polly, do you think, Miss Lucy?”

I answered that there needed only to try; Madame was fond of English pupils. “If you, sir,” I added, “will but take Miss de Bassompierre in your carriage this very afternoon, I think I can answer for it that Rosine, the portress, will not be very slow in answering your ring; and Madame, I am sure, will put on her best pair of gloves to receive you in the salon.”

“In that case,” said Mr. Home, “I see no necessity for delay. Mrs. Hurst can send what she calls her young lady’s ‘things’ after her; Polly can settle down to her horn-book before night; and you, Miss Lucy, I trust, will not disdain to cast an occasional eye upon her, and let me know, from time to time, how she gets on. I hope you approve of the arrangement, Countess de Bassompierre?”

The Countess hemmed and hesitated.

“I thought,” she said, “I thought I had finished my education—”

“That only proves how much we may be mistaken in our thoughts. I hold a far different opinion, as most of these will who have been auditors of your profound knowledge of life this morning. Ah, my little girl, thou hast much to learn; and papa ought to have taught thee more than he has done. Come—there is nothing for it but to try Madame Beck; and the weather seems settling, and I have finished my breakfast—”

“But, papa!”

“Well?”

“I see an obstacle.”

“I don’t at all.”

“It is enormous, papa; it can never be got over; it is as large as you in your greatcoat and the snowdrift on the top.”

“And, like that snowdrift, capable of melting?”

“No—of too solid flesh. It is just your own self. Miss Lucy, warn Madame Beck not to listen to any overtures about taking me, because, in the end, it would turn out that she would have to take papa too. As he is so teasing, I will tell tales about him. Mrs. Bretton—and all of you—listen. About five years ago, when I was twelve, he took it into his head that he was spoiling me; that I was growing unfitted for the world, and I don’t know what; and nothing would serve but I must go to school. I cried, and so on; but M. de Bassompierre proved hard-hearted—quite firm and flinty—and to school I went. What was the result? In the most admirable manner, papa came to school likewise. Every other day he called to see me. Madame Aigredoux grumbled; it was of no use; and so, at last, papa and I were both, in a manner, expelled. Lucy can tell Madame Beck this trait: it is only fair to let her know what she must expect.”

Mrs. Bretton demanded what the Count had to say in answer. As he made no defence, judgment was given against him, and Paulina triumphed.

Yet she had other moods besides archness and naïveté. After breakfast, when the two elders withdrew—I suppose to speak of certain of Mrs. Bretton’s affairs—and the Countess, Dr. Bretton, and I were left for a short while together, the child dropped from her as if laid aside with the napkin. With us, nearer her in age, she rose at once into the little lady. Even her face seemed to change: that candid play of feature and dimpled roundness she wore for her father yielded to a more thoughtful cast; the lines grew distinct, less mobile; the look steadied.

No doubt Graham noted it as well as I. He stood some minutes by the window, watching the snow worry the garden; the wind outside made a thin, saw-like music against the glass. Then he came to the hearth and tried conversation—but not with his usual ease. Topics did not rise readily; he chose fastidiously, hesitatingly, and so spoke infelicitously: of Villette, its inhabitants, its sights and buildings. Miss de Bassompierre answered in quite womanly sort—intelligently, with a manner not wholly stripped of individuality: here and there a glance, a quick gesture, a tone rather animated than measured recalled little Polly; yet over all was a fine polish, a calm courtesy, such as would forbid a less sensitive man than Graham to seize those childish relics as handles for intimacy.

Still, though Dr. Bretton remained subdued—sedate, for him—he was observant. Not one small impulse, not one natural break escaped him. He did not miss a characteristic movement, a hesitation, or a lisp. At times, when she spoke fast, she still lisped; and then she coloured, and, in a painstaking conscientious way—almost as amusing as the slight error—repeated the word more distinctly, as if correcting not only speech but self.

Whenever she did so, Dr. Bretton smiled.

Gradually the restraint on each side slackened. Had the conference but been prolonged, it would soon have grown genial: already to Paulina’s lip and cheek returned the wreathing, dimpling smile; she lisped once and forgot to correct it. And Dr. John—how he changed I can scarcely define; he did not grow gayer, no raillery sparkled, no levity darted; but he seemed to find the position more pleasant, and his comfort spoke in readier language, in tones more suave. Ten years ago, those two had always found abundance to say; the decade between had not narrowed experience nor impoverished intelligence. Some natures act thus upon each other: the more they say, the more they have to say. Out of association grows adhesion; out of adhesion, amalgamation.

Graham, however, must go. His profession makes demands that do not wait upon weather or inclination. He left the room; but before he could leave the house there was a return. I am sure he came back—not for the paper or card in his desk, which served as his excuse—but to assure himself, by one more glance, that Paulina’s aspect was truly such as memory was carrying away; that he had not been viewing her by some partial light and making a fond mistake. No: the impression held; indeed it gained. He took away a parting look—shy, but very soft—as beautiful and innocent as any little fawn lifting its head from fern, or lamb rousing from its meadow-bed.

When we were alone, Paulina and I kept silence for a time. We both took out work and set to it with mute diligence. The white-wood workbox of old days had been replaced by one inlaid with precious mosaic and furnished with golden implements. The tiny fingers that once trembled over the needle—tiny still—were now swift and skilful. Yet the same intent knitting of the brow remained; the same dainty mannerisms; the quick turns—now to replace a stray tress, now to shake from the silken skirt some imaginary atom of dust, some clinging fibre of thread.

That morning I was disposed for quiet. The austere fury of the day had an awing, hushing power. The passion of January—so white, so bloodless—was not spent: the storm had raved itself hoarse, yet seemed no nearer exhaustion. Had Ginevra Fanshawe been there, she would not have suffered me to muse and listen undisturbed; the presence just gone would have been her theme, and she would have rung changes on it till my thoughts were chased like leaves. But Paulina—though she cast once or twice towards me a quiet, penetrating glance from her dark full eye, her lips half parting as if speech were coming—saw, and delicately respected, my inclination to silence.
