Now, let us record a fact concerning Arthur Pendennis—so shocking, so mortifying, that if it were told of any other hero in fiction, the reader would instantly suspect a plot or a hoax. But since this is the true and unvarnished memoir of our dear Pen, we must lay bare the truth: after suffering from fever and the tender affliction of love (a most inconvenient combination), he recovered—not just his health, but his sense.

Yes, indeed. After being bled, blistered, shaved like a monk, and dosed with every known remedy under the sun (and some not yet invented), the young man rose from his sickbed—and found himself utterly cured of his passion for Fanny Bolton. Not merely cooled off, mind you—completely over her. As if she had never existed. As if he had never lain awake at night imagining her eyes, her voice, her hand upon his brow during delirium (which may have been a dream, or possibly a hallucination involving a pigeon). He now looked back on his former infatuation with the same pity one might feel for a man who once believed in phrenology or believed he saw a ghost in the gaslight.

He laughed—gently, as one laughs when recovering from a fever dream—as he lay propped up on pillows, contemplating how absurd it was to have loved Fanny Bolton. She was nice enough, certainly. Pleasant company. But not exactly a goddess. No wit, no charm, no beauty to speak of—unless one counted her ability to keep a kettle warm and her skill in mending socks. And yet! There he’d been, burning with desire, heart pounding, letters written and then torn up, all because of her. It wasn’t her fault. It was him. The illness had burned out the flame. His own internal furnace had gone cold. The fever took the fire with it, along with the headache, the nausea, and perhaps even the last vestiges of his dignity.

And what comfort it gave him—this selfish comfort, which we all know well—that he hadn’t given in. That he hadn’t done anything rash or improper while half-mad with sickness. That he could look back and say, “I resisted.” Like a man escaping a pit of vipers by falling into a tub of lukewarm soup. He felt proud—though ashamed of feeling proud. Because, really, isn’t it humiliating to admit you no longer love someone? It’s almost as bad as admitting you’ve stopped believing in fairies.

Meanwhile, the presence of his mother at his bedside was a soothing balm. Her gentle smiles, her tireless care, her constant offer of tea and toast—these things filled the room with peace. To see him getting better was all she asked for. To fulfill his whims—whether it be a cold compress, a book read aloud, or a request for a slice of cake at midnight—was her joy. He felt wrapped in maternal warmth, as though he were five again, and she was still scolding him for leaving his toys on the floor.

But here comes the rub. Pen had vague memories—foggy, half-formed dreams—of Fanny Bolton tending him. Was she really there? Or was that just a fever-induced fantasy where she appeared as a nurse, a saint, a vision in blue cotton? He couldn’t tell. And since he’d never mentioned Fanny to his mother before, he couldn’t bring it up now. So silence reigned—on both sides. A silence that, in time, would cause more pain than any confession ever could.

Mrs. Pendennis, seeing Fanny Bolton installed as nurse, naturally drew conclusions. And what conclusions? That Arthur had been unfaithful. That the rumors were true. That her son, whom she adored, had broken every rule of decency. Why didn’t she ask? Because women often believe the worst about men they love. Especially their sons. Isn’t it true that wives are usually the first to accuse their husbands of infidelity? Poor Pen got a double dose of that kind of love—the kind that watches too closely and suspects too much. His mother thought he’d not only fallen ill, but also fallen into sin. That he was weakened by disease *and* stained by moral failure. And so she bore her sorrow silently, masking her despair with cheerful smiles and endless cups of tea.

Meanwhile, across the Channel, Captain Shandon, lounging in Boulogne with a glass of brandy (two francs a bottle—what a bargain!), read the latest issue of the *Pall Mall Gazette*. “Jack Finucane’s hand is gone,” he remarked to Mrs. Shandon. “Warrington’s back.”

“Oh, Charles!” said Mrs. Shandon, “how can you talk like that? Mr. Warrington is such a kind gentleman. He was so good to the children.”

“Kind to children,” replied Shandon, “but savage with men. You don’t understand writing, my dear. It’s like hunting. Warrington doesn’t slash—he dissects. He cuts straight down the spine. One line bleeds. Another finishes the job. I call it elegant slaughter.”

“Good heavens, Charles!”

“Ah, but it’s better here. Wine flows freely. We’re happy. Mix another tumbler, Mary. Soon we’ll return to the fray. ‘Cras ingens iterabimus aequor’—bad luck to it.”

Back in London, Warrington had taken over Pen’s duties at the *Gazette* with gusto. He wrote articles, reviews, critiques of plays and concerts—all with the force of a bull charging through a china shop. Yet when he spoke of Arthur, he became oddly tender.

“The people here don’t understand style,” he told Mrs. Pendennis. “They wouldn’t recognize genius if it bit them. But Arthur has it. He’s graceful. Light. Elegant. I call him mine—I raised him. I’m proud of him. Yes, he’s wilful, selfish, dandyish—but honest, loyal, gentle. His pen can be cruel, but his heart is soft as a lady’s glove. I swear he’d never harm a fly.”

At this, Helen sighed deeply. Laura blushed. But both were grateful. They loved Warrington for loving Pen.

Major Pendennis, meanwhile, was positively ecstatic. “He’s a gentleman,” he declared, “every inch. Suffolk Warringtons. Barons. Sir Miles Warrington—ran away with—oh, beg your pardon, Miss Bell.” He paused, embarrassed. “Sir Miles was a great man. Friend to the Prince of Wales. This man? Brilliant. Talented. Sure to rise—if he had a motive.”

Laura blushed again—this time not for shame, but for something else. For she had begun to wonder about Warrington. About his dark eyes, his quiet strength, his melancholy smile. Had he been hurt? By love? By life? She wondered, and blushed.

Warrington took rooms nearby—Grenier’s chambers in Flag Court. In the mornings, he worked hard. In the afternoons, he came to visit Pen. And sometimes, when Laura asked permission to walk in the Temple Gardens with him, Major Pendennis cried, “Of course! Everyone walks there. Beadles, police, ladies, gentlemen—it’s like the country!”

So why not? And so they walked. And talked. And laughed. And Warrington, who had never met a woman like Laura—pure, intelligent, untouched by city gossip—began to see her differently. And she, in turn, found him fascinating—raw, real, full of energy and honesty, unlike Pen, who seemed to glide through life like a bored prince, indifferent to everything but fashion and flirtation.

What made Pen such a dandy? The women, of course. They spoiled him. Praised him. Obedied him. Until he grew tired of flattery, like a king tired of courtiers. Abroad, he was lively, passionate—because abroad, no one bowed to him. At home, he yawned. He was used to being worshipped. And when worship becomes routine, it loses its charm.

But Laura? She loved him anyway. Why? Because she saw little else at Fairoaks except Doctor Portman and Captain Glanders (the latter of whom, rumor had it, kept a pet raccoon). Because her mother praised Arthur constantly. Because he was handsome, witty, and noble. And because, above all, she was built to love someone. It’s human nature. You meet someone. You hear them praised. You sit beside them in church. You ride together. You fall in love. It’s not rational. It’s instinct. Like hunger. Like thirst. You don’t fall in love because the person is perfect—you fall in love because they’re *there*.

And so, when she came to London and met Warrington—strange, serious, brilliant—she found him odd, original, and rather delightful. Who could blame her?

Years later, when fate had scattered them all—some married, some dead, some forgotten—they might have looked back and remembered those evenings in Lamb Court: the music, the laughter, the games of ecarte, the sherry brought by the Marquis of Steyne’s Amontillado (a wine so precious it could make a man weep), the piano played by Laura, the clock chimes of St. Clement’s ringing out at ten o’clock, the slippers knitted in worsted yarn, and the Damascus scimitar hanging in Sibwright’s room, ready to defend the honor of the household against any intruder—even a particularly persistent mouse.

One evening, as the chimes tolled, Laura folded her knitting. Martha from Fairoaks arrived with a candle. The Major sighed. “God bless my soul, is it so late?” Warrington stood. They shook hands. Martha lighted them down the stairs. And as they descended, she bolted the door behind them—“that thar hooky soord” ready, should danger come.

And outside, in the quiet dusk, the pale girl in the black bonnet stood near the lamp-post, listening to the music fade. Perhaps she smiled. Perhaps she sighed. Perhaps she wished she could stay. But no. She was led away by an old gentleman, probably her father, who clearly thought her too young to be out after dark.

And so ended another day in the life of Arthur Pendennis—convalescent, cured, somewhat wiser, and already forgetting the very thing that had once consumed him.

Good night, Laura. Good night, Fanny. Good night, Warrington. Good night, Pendennis. Sleep well. And tomorrow—well, tomorrow is another story.
