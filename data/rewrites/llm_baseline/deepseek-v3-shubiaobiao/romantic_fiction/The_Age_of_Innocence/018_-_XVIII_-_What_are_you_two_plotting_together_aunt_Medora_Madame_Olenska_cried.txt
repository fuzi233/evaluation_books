Ellen Olenska swept into the room like a storm wrapped in silk, her gown shimmering as if spun from moonlight. She held herself with the poised defiance of a woman who knew the weight of every gaze upon her.

“What are you two conspiring over, Aunt Medora?” she asked, her voice light but edged with something sharper.

Mrs. Manson fluttered to her feet, gesturing theatrically toward the lavish bouquet on the table. “A surprise, my dear! Something beautiful to lift your spirits.”

Madame Olenska froze. Her face remained still, but Archer saw the flash of anger—bright and sudden as summer lightning—before she laughed, a brittle sound.

“Who on earth would send me flowers? Am I a debutante? A blushing bride?” Her fingers tightened around her fan. “Nastasia!”

The maid appeared at once, and Ellen’s next words were deliberate, slow Italian—for his benefit, he realized. “Throw these away. No—wait.” A pause, then a softer tone. “Take them to the Winsetts. Their lady is ill; let them bring her some joy. And *don’t* say they’re from me.”

The velvet cloak she draped over Nastasia’s shoulders was a dismissal. When the door shut, her composure wavered—just for an instant—before she turned to them with a smile that didn’t reach her eyes.

“Well? Have you two made peace?”

Archer could only stare, caught between admiration and unease. This was not the woman who had once seemed so adrift in New York’s drawing rooms. This was someone who burned.

---

Later, with the fire between them and the clock ticking toward her departure, she lifted a cigarette to her lips. “What do you think of me when I’m angry?”

He answered without hesitation. “It makes me believe every word your aunt has ever said about you.”

Her exhale was a pale ghost between them. “Medora adorns the truth like a Christmas tree. What has she told you?”

“That your husband wants you back.”

The cigarette stilled. Ash drifted to the floor.

“Ah,” she said at last. “I suppose it was inevitable.”

The words hung there, heavy. Archer moved closer, the heat of the fire doing nothing to dispel the chill in his chest. “Do you believe she has a letter from him?”

Ellen’s smile was thin. “Does it matter?”

“It matters,” he said fiercely, “if you let them persuade you to return.”

Her laugh was a broken thing. “What choice do I have? You, of all people, made sure of that.”

He recoiled. “*I* did?”

“Who else?” Her voice trembled now. “You showed me how cruel it would be—to my family, to May, to you. And so I stayed.”

The confession struck him like a blow. All this time, he had believed himself powerless. Now he understood: he had been complicit.

---

When the telegram came, its contents were a blade. May’s joy leapt from the page—*Granny’s telegram successful. Papa and Mamma agree marriage after Easter.*

Ellen handed it to him without a word.

He read it, then crushed it in his fist. “This changes nothing.”

“It changes *everything*,” she whispered.

For a heartbeat, he thought of defiance. Of tearing up the world’s expectations and choosing her, consequences be damned. But then he met her eyes—resigned, sorrowful, *final*—and knew the battle was already lost.

The clock struck the hour.

“You should go,” she said softly.

And he did.

---

That night, alone in his room, Archer unfolded May’s second telegram: *Parents consent wedding Tuesday after Easter at twelve Grace Church.*

The date mocked him.

April.

So soon.

He laughed, sharp and humorless, until his sister rushed in, alarmed. “Newland? What’s happened?”

He wiped his eyes. “Nothing,” he lied. “Only that I’m to be married in a month.”

And as Janey embraced him, her joy ringing in his ears, he wondered if Ellen was right—if some choices, once made, could never be undone.
