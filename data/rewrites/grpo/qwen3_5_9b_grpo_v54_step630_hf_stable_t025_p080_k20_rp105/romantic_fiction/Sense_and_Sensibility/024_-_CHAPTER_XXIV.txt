Elinor approached the conversation with a steady, albeit guarded, composure. "I would be unworthy of the confidence you have placed in me," she began, "if I did not share your desire to continue this dialogue, nor lack further curiosity regarding its subject. Therefore, I need offer no apology for raising it once more."

"Thank you!" Lucy exclaimed warmly, her relief palpable. "You have broken the ice; my heart is at ease now. I confess I was terrified that what I told you on Monday had offended you."

"Offend me?" Elinor replied with genuine warmth. "How could you possibly imagine that? Believe me, nothing was further from my intention than to give you such an idea. Could there be any motive for your trust that was not honorable and flattering to me?"

"And yet," Lucy countered, her sharp eyes gleaming with a mixture of vulnerability and calculation, "I felt a distinct coldness and displeasure in your manner. It made me quite uncomfortable. I was convinced you were angry with me, and I have been tormenting myself ever since for taking the liberty of troubling you with my affairs. But I am so glad to find it was merely my own fancy, and that you truly do not blame me. If you knew how much solace it was to relieve my heart by speaking to you of what occupies every moment of my life, your compassion would surely make you overlook everything else."

"I can easily believe that it was a great relief to you to acknowledge your situation to me," Elinor said gently. "And you may be assured you shall never have reason to repent it. Your case is indeed unfortunate; you appear surrounded by difficulties, and you will require all your mutual affection to sustain you. Mr. Ferrars, I believe, is entirely dependent on his mother."

"He possesses only two thousand pounds of his own," Lucy explained, her voice dropping slightly. "It would be madness to marry upon that sum, though for my part, I could give up every prospect of more without a sigh. I have always been accustomed to a very small income and could struggle with any poverty for him. Yet, I love him too well to be the selfish instrument of robbing him, perhaps, of all that his mother might bestow if he married to please her. We must wait; it may be for many years. With almost every other man in the world, this would be an alarming prospect, but Edward's affection and constancy are treasures nothing can deprive me of."

"That conviction must be everything to you," Elinor observed carefully, ensuring her countenance remained impassive. "And he is undoubtedly supported by the same trust in yours. Had the strength of your reciprocal attachment failed—as it naturally might, under many circumstances, during a four-year engagement—your situation would indeed have been pitiable."

Lucy looked up, her gaze intense. "Edward's love for me has been thoroughly tested by our long, very long absence since we were first engaged. It has stood the trial so well that I should be unpardonable to doubt it now. I can safely say he has never given me one moment's alarm on that account from the beginning."

Elinor found herself torn between a smile and a sigh at this assertion.

Lucy continued, her tone shifting to one of defensive pride. "I am rather of a jealous temper by nature. Given our different stations in life, his being so much more 'in the world' than I, and our continual separations, I was naturally inclined toward suspicion. Had there been the slightest alteration in his behavior when we met, or any inexplicable lowness of spirits, or if he had spoken more of another lady, or seemed less happy at Longstaple than usual, I would have discovered the truth instantly. I do not claim to be particularly observant in general matters, but in such a case, I am certain I could not be deceived."

*All this,* Elinor thought, *is very pretty; but it can impose upon neither of us.*

After a brief silence, she pressed further. "But what are your views? Or do you have none but that of waiting for Mrs. Ferrars's death—a melancholy and shocking extremity? Is her son determined to submit to this, and to all the tediousness of the years of suspense it may involve, rather than risk her displeasure for a while by owning the truth?"

"If we could be certain it would be only for a while!" Lucy sighed. "But Mrs. Ferrars is a very headstrong, proud woman. In her first fit of anger upon hearing the truth, she would very likely secure every inheritance to Robert. The mere idea of that, for Edward's sake, frightens away all my inclination for hasty measures. And for your own sake too, or you are carrying your disinterestedness beyond reason."

Lucy looked at Elinor again, falling silent.

"Do you know Mr. Robert Ferrars?" Elinor asked.

"Not at all; I never saw him," Lucy replied dismissively. "But I fancy he is very unlike his brother—silly and a great coxcomb."

"A great coxcomb!" repeated Miss Steele, her attention suddenly arrested by the words amidst a pause in Marianne's music. "Oh, they are talking of their favorite beaux, I dare say."

"No sister," cried Lucy, indignant, "you are mistaken there; our favorite beaux are *not* great coxcombs."

"I can answer for it that Miss Dashwood's is not," said Mrs. Jennings, laughing heartily. "He is one of the modestest, prettiest-behaved young men I have ever seen. But as for Lucy, she is such a sly little creature, there is no finding out who *she* likes."

"Oh," cried Miss Steele, casting a significant glance around the room, "I dare say Lucy's beau is quite as modest and pretty-behaved as Miss Dashwood's."

Elinor blushed involuntarily. Lucy bit her lip and shot an angry look at her sister. A mutual silence ensued for some time until Lucy broke it in a lower tone, though Marianne was then providing them with the powerful protection of a magnificent concerto.

"I will honestly tell you of one scheme which has lately come into my head for bringing matters to a conclusion. Indeed, I am bound to let you into the secret, for you are a party concerned. I dare say you have seen enough of Edward to know that he would prefer the church to every other profession. My plan is that he should take orders as soon as he can, and then, through your interest—which I am sure you would be kind enough to use out of friendship for him, and I hope out of some regard to me—your brother might be persuaded to give him the Norland living. I understand it is a very good one, and the present incumbent is not likely to live much longer. That would be enough for us to marry upon, and we might trust to time and chance for the rest."

"I should always be happy," replied Elinor, "to show any mark of my esteem and friendship for Mr. Ferrars; but do you not perceive that my interest on such an occasion would be perfectly unnecessary? He is the brother of Mrs. John Dashwood—that must be recommendation enough to her husband."

"But Mrs. John Dashwood would not much approve of Edward's going into orders."

"Then I rather suspect that my interest would do very little."

They fell silent again for many minutes. Finally, Lucy exclaimed with a deep sigh. "I believe it would be the wisest way to put an end to the business at once by dissolving the engagement. We seem so beset with difficulties on every side that, though it would make us miserable for a time, we should perhaps be happier in the end. But you will not give me your advice, Miss Dashwood?"

"No," answered Elinor, with a smile that concealed very agitated feelings. "On such a subject, I certainly will not. You know very well that my opinion would have no weight with you unless it were on the side of your wishes."

"Indeed you wrong me," replied Lucy with great solemnity. "I know nobody of whose judgment I think so highly as I do of yours. I do really believe that if you were to say to me, 'I advise you by all means to put an end to your engagement with Edward Ferrars; it will be more for the happiness of both of you,' I should resolve upon doing it immediately."

Elinor blushed at the insincerity of Edward's future wife and replied, "This compliment would effectually frighten me from giving any opinion on the subject had I formed one. It raises my influence much too high; the power of dividing two people so tenderly attached is too much for an indifferent person."

"'Tis because you are an indifferent person," said Lucy, with some pique, laying particular stress on those words, "that your judgment might justly have such weight with me. If you could be supposed to be biased in any respect by your own feelings, your opinion would not be worth having."

Elinor deemed it wisest to make no answer, lest they provoke each other to an unsuitable increase of familiarity and unreserve. She was even partly determined never to mention the subject again. Another pause of many minutes followed, and Lucy was still the first to end it.

"Shall you be in town this winter, Miss Dashwood?" she asked with her customary complacency.

"Certainly not."

"I am sorry for that," returned the other, her eyes brightening at the information. "It would have given me such pleasure to meet you there! But I dare say you will go for all that. To be sure, your brother and sister will ask you to come to them."

"It will not be in my power to accept their invitation if they do."

"How unlucky that is! I had quite depended upon meeting you there. Anne and I are to go the latter end of January to some relations who have been wanting us to visit them these several years! But I only go for the sake of seeing Edward. He will be there in February; otherwise, London would have no charms for me; I have not the spirits for it."

Elinor was soon called to the card-table by the conclusion of the first rubber, and the confidential discourse of the two ladies came to an end. Both submitted to the interruption without reluctance, for nothing had been said to make them dislike each other less than they had done before. Elinor sat down to the cards with a melancholy persuasion that Edward was not only without affection for the person destined to be his wife, but that he had not even the chance of being tolerably happy in marriage, which sincere affection on her side would have secured. For self-interest alone could induce a woman to keep a man to an engagement of which she seemed so thoroughly aware that he was weary.

From this time forth, the subject was never revived by Elinor. When entered upon by Lucy, who seldom missed an opportunity of introducing it and was particularly careful to inform her confidante of her happiness whenever she received a letter from Edward, it was treated by the former with calmness and caution, and dismissed as soon as civility would allow. She felt such conversations to be an indulgence which Lucy did not deserve, and which were dangerous to herself.

The visit of the Miss Steeles at Barton Park was lengthened far beyond what the first invitation implied. Their favor increased; they could not be spared; Sir John would not hear of their going. In spite of their numerous and long-arranged engagements in Exeter, and in spite of the absolute necessity of returning to fulfill them immediately—a necessity in full force at the end of every week—they were prevailed upon to stay nearly two months at the park, assisting in the due celebration of that festival which requires a more than ordinary share of private balls and large dinners to proclaim its importance.
