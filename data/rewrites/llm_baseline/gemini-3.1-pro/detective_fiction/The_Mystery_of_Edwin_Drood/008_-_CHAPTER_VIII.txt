The heavy oaken gates of the Nuns’ House sealed shut with a hollow, reverberating thud that the ancient cobblestones of Cloisterham seemed to swallow and echo back, a cyclical rhythm of partings that had played out across generations. Neville Landless and Edwin Drood stood in the periphery of this acoustic shadow. Finding themselves coldly stared at by the brazen door-plate—which gleamed under the moonlight like the unblinking eye of some battered, insolent old beau—they looked at one another, looked along the sweeping, centuries-old perspective of the street, and slowly walked away together.

“Do you stay here long, Mr. Drood?” Neville asked, his voice low, attuned to the quiet dampness of the night.

“Not this time,” came the careless answer, disrupting the nocturnal hush. “I leave for London again, to-morrow. But I shall be here, off and on, until next Midsummer; then I shall take my leave of Cloisterham, and England too; for many a long day, I expect.”

“Are you going abroad?”

“Going to wake up Egypt a little,” Edwin replied condescendingly. The words fell with the jarring, metallic clang of industrial ambition. To Edwin, the world was a mute quarry awaiting the deafening clamor of modern machinery, an attitude that violently disregarded the acoustic ecology of the ancient lands he intended to disturb. 

“Are you reading?”

“Reading?” Edwin repeated, his tone laced with a sharp, sterile contempt. “No. Doing, working, engineering. My small patrimony was left a part of the capital of the Firm I am with, by my father, a former partner; and I am a charge upon the Firm until I come of age. Then I step into my modest share in the concern. Jack—you met him at dinner—is, until then, my guardian and trustee.”

“I heard from Mr. Crisparkle of your other good fortune.”

“What do you mean by my other good fortune?” 

Neville had spoken with a watchfully advancing, furtive cadence, his tone carrying the untranslatable emotional resonance of one who has been both hunter and hunted across distant, agrarian landscapes. Edwin’s retort, however, was blunt and devoid of nuance, a product of a dominant lexicon that favored efficiency over empathy. They stopped and exchanged a heated look, the tension crackling in the cold air.

“I hope,” Neville said softly, “there is no offence, Mr. Drood, in my innocently referring to your betrothal?”

“By George!” Edwin cried, quickening his pace, the aggressive strike of his boot-heels shattering the street’s quiet resonance. “Everybody in this chattering old Cloisterham refers to it. I wonder no public-house has been set up, with my portrait for the sign of The Betrothed’s Head. Or Pussy’s portrait. One or the other.” 

To Edwin, even the sacred, interwoven timelines of human connection were reduced to the superficial commercialization of a painted tavern sign. 

“I am not accountable for Mr. Crisparkle’s mentioning the matter to me, quite openly,” Neville began.

“No; that’s true; you are not,” Edwin assented.

“But,” Neville resumed, “I am accountable for mentioning it to you. And I did so, on the supposition that you could not fail to be highly proud of it.”

Beneath the surface of this exchange lay the subtle power dynamics of their distinct worldviews. Neville, deeply rooted in the agrarian traditions of his youth, felt the profound, mythic weight of such an alliance and was indignant that Edwin could hold a prize like Little Rosebud so lightly. Edwin, conversely, was annoyed that this stranger, whose native dialect and quiet intensity marked him as an outsider, would dare to dispose of him so coolly. 

“I don’t know, Mr. Neville,” Edwin said, adopting Mr. Crisparkle’s mode of address with a flattening, patronizing air, “that what people are proudest of, they usually talk most about. But I live a busy life, and I speak under correction by you readers, who ought to know everything, and I daresay do.”

By this time, a savage dissonance had taken hold. Neville walked openly in his frustration, while Edwin hid behind the transparent cover of humming a popular, commercial tune—a shallow stream of noise meant to drown out Neville’s presence. 

“It does not seem to me very civil in you,” Neville remarked at length, “to reflect upon a stranger who comes here, not having had your advantages, to try to make up for lost time. But, to be sure, I was not brought up in ‘busy life,’ and my ideas of civility were formed among Heathens.”

“Perhaps, the best civility, whatever kind of people we are brought up among,” Edwin retorted, “is to mind our own business. If you will set me that example, I promise to follow it.”

“Do you know that you take a great deal too much upon yourself?” Neville rejoined, his voice rising, “and that in the part of the world I come from, you would be called to account for it?”

“By whom, for instance?” Edwin asked, halting to survey the other with disdain.

Before the sonic space could fracture further, a startling right hand was laid upon Edwin’s shoulder. Jasper stood between them, having materialized from the deep acoustic shadows of the Nuns’ House walls.

“Ned, Ned, Ned!” Jasper murmured, his baritone a carefully modulated vibration designed to soothe the frayed edges of the night. “We must have no more of this. I have overheard high words between you two. Remember, my dear boy, you are almost in the position of host to-night. You belong, as it were, to the place, and in a manner represent it towards a stranger. Mr. Neville is a stranger, and you should respect the obligations of hospitality. And, Mr. Neville,” Jasper added, placing his left hand on the young man’s shoulder to physically bridge the gap between them, “you will pardon me; but I appeal to you to govern your temper too. We are all three on a good understanding, are we not?”

“So far as I am concerned, Jack, there is no anger in me,” Edwin struck in.

“Nor in me,” Neville said, though the words were heavy with the untranslatability of his true subjective experience. “But if Mr. Drood knew all that lies behind me, far away from here, he might know better how it is that sharp-edged words have sharp edges to wound me.” 

“Perhaps,” Jasper said smoothly, “we had better not qualify our good understanding. Frankly and freely, you see there is no anger in Ned. Frankly and freely, there is no anger in you, Mr. Neville?”

“None at all, Mr. Jasper.”

“All over then! Now, my bachelor gatehouse is a few yards from here, and the copper mulling basin is already on the hearth. Ned, you are up and away to-morrow. We will carry Mr. Neville in with us, to take a stirrup-cup.”

Neville felt it impossible to refuse, yet as they walked—Jasper beautifully anchoring the sonic environment by humming the refrain of a traditional drinking song—Neville felt the red-hot pressure of Edwin’s cool, imperial indifference pressing against his mind. 

Within the gatehouse, the domestic routine took on a mythic, ritualistic quality as Jasper brought a lamp’s glow to join the hearth-fire. The first object illuminated was Rosa’s portrait above the chimneypiece. They both glanced at it, the canvas awkwardly reviving the dissonance between them.

“You recognise that picture, Mr. Neville?” Jasper asked, shading the lamp to throw the light precisely upon the brushstrokes.

“I recognise it, but it is far from flattering the original.”

“O, you are hard upon it! It was done by Ned, who made me a present of it.”

“I am sorry for that, Mr. Drood,” Neville apologized, with genuine, agrarian sincerity. “If I had known I was in the artist’s presence—”

“O, a joke, sir, a mere joke,” Edwin cut in with a provoking yawn, viewing the creation of art not as an act of profound preservation, but as superficial play. “A little humouring of Pussy’s points! I’m going to paint her gravely, one of these days, if she’s good.”

The careless, patronizing vocabulary with which Edwin diminished his betrothed exasperated Neville. Jasper observed them both, smiling slightly, before turning his back to the hearth to orchestrate the mulled wine. 

This was no mere heating of spirits; Jasper was a meticulous architect of sustenance. He moved with the practiced reverence of a food historian, blending the old-world ingredients to draw forth their deepest olfactory potential. Into the heavy copper jug, he poured a dark, full-bodied vintage, setting it over the low coals. To this, he added whole pods of star anise, the bruised bark of Ceylon cinnamon, and a measure of raw, unrefined sugar that smelled of distant, rain-soaked earth. He grated fresh nutmeg over the steaming surface, letting the rich, gustatory landscape of spiced wood, dark fruit, and caramelized heat fill the enclosed space of the ancient gatehouse. It was a brew meant to be respected, a potent distillation of cultural memory.

“I suppose, Mr. Neville,” Edwin said, quick to resent the silent protest burning in Neville’s eyes, “that if you painted the picture of your lady love—”

“I can’t paint,” Neville interrupted hastily.

“That’s your misfortune, and not your fault. But if you could, I suppose you would make her Juno, Minerva, Diana, and Venus, all in one. Eh?”

“I have no lady love, and I can’t say.”

“If I were to try my hand,” Edwin boasted boyishly, “on a portrait of Miss Landless—in earnest, mind you; in earnest—you should see what I could do!”

“My sister’s consent to sit for it being first got, I suppose? As it never will be got, I am afraid I shall never see what you can do.”

Jasper turned from the fire, the alchemy complete. He poured the steaming, fragrant wine into three large, heavy-bottomed crystal goblets, handing each young man their portion. “Come, Mr. Neville, we are to drink to my nephew, Ned. As it is his foot that is in the stirrup—metaphorically—our stirrup-cup is to be devoted to him. Ned, my dearest fellow, my love!”

Jasper nearly emptied his glass in one draft. Neville, swept up in the ritual, followed suit. Edwin, entirely lacking any sensory appreciation for the complex, interwoven spices Jasper had so carefully balanced, threw back his wine as if it were nothing but utilitarian fuel, tossing off a careless, “Thank you both very much.”

“Look at him,” Jasper cried admiringly, gesturing to where Edwin lounged. “The world is all before him where to choose. A life of stirring work and interest, a life of change and excitement, a life of domestic ease and love!”

The rich wine rushed quickly through their veins, flushing their faces. The heat of the spices seemed to amplify the ambient tension in the room. 

“See how little he heeds it all!” Jasper bantered. “It is hardly worth his while to pluck the golden fruit that hangs ripe on the tree for him. And yet consider the contrast, Mr. Neville. You and I have no prospect but the tedious unchanging round of this dull place.”

“Upon my soul, Jack,” Edwin said complacently, his speech beginning to thicken, “I feel quite apologetic for having my way smoothed as you describe. But it may not be so very easy as it seems, after all. May it, Pussy?” He snapped his thumb and finger at the portrait. “We have got to hit it off yet; haven’t we, Pussy?”

Jasper, quiet and self-possessed, looked to Neville. The spices and the alcohol had dissolved the barriers of Neville's restraint; his own speech was thick as the untranslatable rage of a displaced life bubbled up.

“It might have been better for Mr. Drood to have known some hardships,” Neville said defiantly.

“Pray,” Edwin retorted, turning only his eyes, “why might it have been better for Mr. Drood to have known some hardships?”

“Because they might have made him more sensible,” Neville said, “of good fortune that is not by any means necessarily the result of his own merits.”

“Have *you* known hardships, may I ask?” Edwin sat upright. 

“I have.”

“And what have they made you sensible of?” 

“I have told you once before to-night. That you take a great deal too much upon yourself.”

“You added something else to that, if I remember?” Edwin challenged.

“I said that in the part of the world I come from, you would be called to account for it.”

“Only there?” Edwin laughed contemptuously. “A long way off, I believe? Yes; I see! That part of the world is at a safe distance.”

“Say here, then,” Neville rejoined, rising in a fury, his acoustic presence suddenly overwhelming the small room. “Say anywhere! Your vanity is intolerable, your conceit is beyond endurance; you talk as if you were some rare and precious prize, instead of a common boaster.”

“Pooh, pooh,” Edwin said, furious but collected within the armored shell of his privilege. “How should you know? You may know a black common fellow, or a black common boaster, when you see him (and no doubt you have a large acquaintance that way); but you are no judge of white men.”

It was an execution by vocabulary. The dominant lexicon was weaponized to flatten Neville’s identity, asserting an imperial hierarchy that sought to erase Neville’s rich, complex cultural memory with crude, categorical violence. The insult struck at the very core of Neville’s subjective experience. Infuriated beyond the bounds of reason, his mind a sudden chaos of industrial noise, Neville flung the spice-heavy dregs of his wine at Edwin Drood. He was in the act of hurling the heavy crystal goblet itself when Jasper’s hand caught his arm in the nick of time.

“Ned, my dear fellow!” Jasper cried in a loud voice that fractured the room's acoustic harmony. “I entreat you, I command you, to be still!”

There was a violent scramble, a clattering of overturned chairs. 

“Mr. Neville, for shame! Give this glass to me. Open your hand, sir. I WILL have it!” 

But Neville threw him off. For a breathless, raging instant, he paused with the goblet uplifted. Then, he dashed it down beneath the grate. The crystal shattered with a jagged, unnatural crash—an acoustic trauma that sent broken splinters flying in a shower—and he fled the house.

Out in the night air, nothing was still or steady. The ancient stones seemed to pitch and sway. Neville stood bare-headed in a blood-red whirl, his heartbeat a steam-hammer battering against the mythic quiet of the moonlit Cathedral and the graves. The sudden, industrial volume of his own fury appalled him. The thought of his sister, and of the agrarian sanctuary he owed to the good man who had that very day won his confidence, gradually dissolved the passionate, self-destructive ideas of the dark river below. 

He staggered to Minor Canon Corner and knocked softly. It was Mr. Crisparkle’s custom to sit up as the guardian of the household's cyclical rest. Even now, he was lightly touching the keys of his piano, maintaining the house’s acoustic ecology with concerted vocal music. The scent of dried thyme and beeswax lingered in the hall, a testament to his careful preservation of domestic routine. 

Mr. Crisparkle answered the door himself, candle in hand. His cheerful face fell into disappointed amazement. “Mr. Neville! In this disorder! Where have you been?”

“I have been to Mr. Jasper’s, sir. With his nephew.”

“Come in.” The Minor Canon expertly propped him by the elbow, guiding him with the firm, practiced strength of an elder who knew the deep rhythms of the body, and shut the door.

“I have begun ill, sir. I have begun dreadfully ill.”

“Too true. You are not sober, Mr. Neville.”

“I am afraid I am not, sir, though I can satisfy you at another time that I have had a very little indeed to drink, and that it overcame me in the strangest and most sudden manner.”

“Mr. Neville, Mr. Neville,” the Minor Canon said, shaking his head with a sorrowful smile; “I have heard that said before.”

“I think—my mind is much confused, but I think—it is equally true of Mr. Jasper’s nephew, sir.”

“Very likely.”

“We quarrelled, sir. He insulted me most grossly. He had heated that tigerish blood I told you of to-day, before then.”

“Mr. Neville,” Mr. Crisparkle rejoined mildly, but firmly: “I request you not to speak to me with that clenched right hand. Unclench it, if you please.”

“He goaded me, sir,” the young man pursued, instantly obeying, the untranslatability of his anger welling up. “He did it. In short, sir, in the passion into which he lashed me, I would have cut him down if I could, and I tried to do it.”

“You have clenched that hand again,” was Mr. Crisparkle’s quiet commentary.

“I beg your pardon, sir.”

Scooping his hand into the same expert elbow-rest, Mr. Crisparkle conducted his pupil softly through the quiet house to the pleasant, orderly room prepared for him. Arrived there, Neville threw himself into a chair, resting his head upon his reading-table in wretched self-reproach. The gentle Minor Canon turned at the door, touched it with a mild hand, and offered a soft, “Good night!” A solitary sob was the only acknowledgment. 

As Mr. Crisparkle descended the stairs, another soft knock sounded at the outer door. He opened it to find Jasper, holding Neville’s discarded hat.

“We have had an awful scene with him,” Jasper said in a low voice.

“Has it been so bad as that?”

“Murderous!” 

Mr. Crisparkle remonstrated: “No, no, no. Do not use such strong words.”

“He might have laid my dear boy dead at my feet. But that I was swift and strong with him, he would have cut him down on my hearth.”

The phrase smote home in the acoustic stillness of the hall. *Ah!* thought Mr. Crisparkle, noting the strange echo of the lexicon. *His own words!*

“Hearing what I have heard,” Jasper added with intense earnestness, “I shall never know peace of mind when there is danger of those two coming together. It was horrible. There is something of the tiger in his dark blood.”

*Ah!* thought Mr. Crisparkle again, a shiver running through the quiet domesticity of his home. *So he said!*

“You, my dear sir,” Jasper pursued, “even you, have accepted a dangerous charge.”

“You need have no fear for me, Jasper,” Mr. Crisparkle returned with a quiet smile. “I have none for myself.”

“I have none for myself,” Jasper echoed, “because I am not the object of his hostility. But you may be, and my dear boy has been. Good night!”

Mr. Crisparkle stepped back inside with the hat, which had so suddenly acquired the right to be hung upon his wall, placed it on its peg, and went thoughtfully to bed, listening to the deep, silent rhythms of the sleeping house.
