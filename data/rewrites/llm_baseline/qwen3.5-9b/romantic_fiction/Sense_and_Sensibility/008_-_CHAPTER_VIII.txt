Mrs. Jennings was a widow possessing an ample income, having seen both of her daughters happily settled into respectable marriages. With no family left to manage, her sole ambition turned outward: to marry off everyone else. She pursued this goal with zealous energy, seizing every opportunity to engineer unions among the youth within her circle. Possessing a keen instinct for detecting romantic attachment, she delighted in stirring the blushes and inflaming the vanities of young ladies by hinting at their secret affections for specific gentlemen. This talent allowed her to arrive at a definitive conclusion regarding Colonel Brandon and Miss Marianne Dashwood shortly after settling at Barton. Indeed, she suspected his feelings from their very first meeting, noting his rapt attention while she sang, and confirmed it when the Middletons hosted dinner at the cottage and he listened to her once more. It was inevitable, she decided; he was wealthy, and she was beautiful—a match made in heaven.

Mrs. Jennings had long desired to secure a husband for Colonel Brandon, ever since Sir John introduced him to her society, and she held the same fervent wish for every pretty girl under her roof. The personal benefit to herself was substantial, providing endless material for jests. At the park, she mocked the colonel; at the cottage, she teased Marianne. While the colonel likely found her ribbing harmless, Marianne initially struggled to comprehend it. Once the target became clear, she oscillated between laughing at the sheer absurdity and censuring the impertinence, viewing the comments as cruel reflections on the colonel's advanced years and his status as a lonely bachelor.

Mrs. Dashwood, unable to accept that a man merely five years younger than herself could appear so antiquated to her daughter's youthful imagination, stepped in to defend him. "But surely, Mama, you cannot deny the absurdity of the accusation, even if you don't mean it maliciously," she argued gently. "Colonel Brandon is indeed younger than Mrs. Jennings, but he is old enough to be *my* father. If he ever possessed the courage to fall in love, he must have long ago outgrown such sentiments. It is preposterous! When will a man finally be safe from such wit if age and infirmity offer no protection?"

"Infirmity!" exclaimed Elinor. "Do you truly consider Colonel Brandon infirm? I can imagine his age seems much older to you than to my mother, but you cannot fool yourself about his physical capabilities."

"Did you not hear him complaining of rheumatism?" Marianne countered. "And isn't that the most common ailment of declining years?"

"My dearest child," Mrs. Dashwood laughed, "if we proceed at this rate, you shall live in perpetual fear of *my* own decay. It must seem miraculous to you that I have survived to the advanced age of forty."

"Mama, you are not giving me credit," Elinor replied calmly. "I know Colonel Brandon is not so aged that his friends need worry about losing him naturally. He could easily live another twenty years. However, thirty-five has little place in matrimony."

"Perhaps," Elinor continued smoothly, "thirty-five and seventeen should not be paired either. But if, by some chance, a woman remains unmarried at twenty-seven, I see no objection to Colonel Brandon marrying her."

"A woman of twenty-seven," Marianne mused after a pause, "can never hope to feel or inspire affection again. If her home is uncomfortable or her fortune small, she might endure the duties of a nurse merely for the security of a wife. Thus, in his marrying such a woman, there would be nothing improper. It would simply be a contract of convenience, satisfying the world. In my eyes, however, it would not be a marriage at all—it would be a mere commercial exchange where each party seeks profit at the other's expense."

"It is impossible," Elinor responded, "to convince you that a woman of twenty-seven could feel for a man of thirty-five anything approaching true love, making him a desirable companion. Yet, I must object to your sentencing Colonel Brandon and his future wife to a lifetime of confinement in a sickroom solely because he complained yesterday—on a cold, damp day—of a slight ache in his shoulder."

"But he spoke of flannel waistcoats!" Marianne insisted. "To me, a flannel waistcoat is forever linked with aches, cramps, rheumatism, and every affliction that plagues the elderly and weak."

"If he had been suffering from a violent fever, you would not despise him nearly so much. Confess, Marianne—is there not something fascinating to you in the flushed cheek, the hollow eye, and the rapid pulse of someone running a fever?"

Shortly thereafter, as Elinor prepared to leave the room, Marianne confided a different anxiety. "Mama, I have a dread concerning illness that I cannot hide. I am certain Edward Ferrars is unwell. We have been here nearly two weeks, yet he has not visited. Only genuine indisposition could explain such an extraordinary delay. What else could keep him at Norland?"

"Had you expected him sooner?" Mrs. Dashwood asked.

"I had no notion," Marianne admitted. "On the contrary, whenever I mentioned his coming to Barton, he seemed to show a lack of enthusiasm. Does Elinor expect him already?"

"I have never hinted at it to her, but perhaps she assumes it."

"I doubt you are correct," Mrs. Dashwood replied. "When I spoke yesterday of installing a new grate in the spare bedroom, Elinor remarked there was no rush, as the room would likely remain unused for some time."

"How peculiar this is! What could account for it? Their behavior toward one another has been utterly inexplicable! How cold and composed were their final farewells! How listless their conversation during their last evening together! In Edward's goodbye, there was no distinction between Elinor and myself; it was merely the warm wishes of a loving brother. Twice I deliberately left them alone earlier that morning, and each time he followed me out of the room with uncanny precision. And Elinor, when departing Norland and saying goodbye to Edward, did not cry as I did. Her self-control remains unshaken. When does she ever appear dejected or melancholy? When does she seek solitude or display restlessness amidst society?"
