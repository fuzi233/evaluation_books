News of Pen's latest escapade spread faster than wildfire, sparking fury in Doctor Portman while merely tickling the funny bone of Major Pendennis. For Mrs. Pendennis, however, the revelation was catastrophic. Upon hearing of the scuffle and her son's "unchristian" behavior, she teetered on the brink of distraction. In her mind, this single transaction spawned a universe of misery, discomfort, and potential crime. She desperately wished to see Pen shipped out of Chatteris immediately, anywhere but near the woman who, in her view, had dragged him into this maelstrom.

When the fond mother remonstrated and the stern Doctor rebuked him for his violence, Pen treated the situation with the deadly seriousness typical of youth. With a gravity that belied his age, he insisted he was deeply sorry, claiming the insult came entirely unprovoked. He vowed that no man could insult him regarding his honor without facing a dignified appeal to his uncle. He asked rhetorically whether any gentleman could have reacted differently to the outrage he suffered, or offered less satisfying restitution to the chastised party.

"You are moving too fast, my good sir," Uncle Pendennis replied, looking slightly baffled. Having recently indoctrinated his nephew with his own archaic views on honor—views steeped in the smell of campfire smoke and pistol powder rather than modern sobriety—he added, "Between gentlemen of the world, perhaps. But between two schoolboys, this sort of nonsense is perfectly ridiculous, my dear boy."

"It is wicked, and utterly unlike my son," Mrs. Pendennis sniffled, tears welling in her eyes as she struggled with her son's stubbornness.

Pen kissed her cheek and delivered a line of dialogue dripping with pomposity: "Women, dear mother, simply do not understand these matters. I placed myself entirely in Foker's hands; I had no other honorable course to pursue."

Major Pendennis grinned and shrugged. To him, the boys were making excellent progress. Meanwhile, Mrs. Pendennis declared Foker a wicked, horrid little wretch, convinced he would drag her beloved boy into further mischief if they attended the same college. "I have a great mind not to let him go at all," she lamented. Only the memory that her husband had always destined him for that specific university prevented her from issuing a total veto.

His attendance was already settled by all concerned. Foker had promised to introduce him to the elite circles, and the Major placed immense faith in this arrangement. "Mr. Foker knows the finest young men currently at the University," the Major boasted. "Pen will form connections that will benefit him for the rest of his life. There is the Young Marquis of Plinlimmon, eldest son of the Duke of Saint David's; Lord Magnus Charters, son of Lord Runnymede and a first cousin of Mr. Foker (you recall Lady Runnymede was formerly Lady Agatha Milton); and Lady Agnes will surely invite him to Logwood. Far from fearing his association with her son—who is singular, humorous, yet prudent and amiable, and for whom we owe a debt of gratitude regarding the Fotheringay marriage—I consider it one of the luckiest breaks Pen could have had."

Helen sighed, assuming the Major knew best. She acknowledged Foker's kindness in the recent Miss Costigan affair and felt grateful, yet a dim, unsettling presentiment of evil gnawed at her. The constant quarrels, riots, and displays of worldliness terrified her for her boy's future.

Doctor Portman, conversely, was adamant that Pen attend college. He hoped the lad would apply himself to studies while enjoying a moderate dose of high society. Confident that Pen would distinguish himself based on Smirke's glowing reports and his own observation of the boy's impressive Latin constructions, the Doctor agreed that leaving Chatteris was essential. Even Pen, usually distracted by his private griefs and local rowdiness, gloomily agreed to obey.

August and September brought assizes, races, and a flood of visitors to Chatteris. Miss Fotheringay continued her performances, bidding farewell to audiences until the final curtain fell. Strangely, aside from the few we've named, no one seemed particularly moved by her presence or impending departure. Even the polite county folk, who likely adored the Fotheringay star in London thanks to fashion trends, found nothing remarkable about her acting on the tiny Chatteris stage. History is littered with geniuses and quacks who met similar fates. Yet, Miss Fotheringay endured the public neglect and life's petty cruelties with her characteristic equanimity. She ate, drank, acted, and slept with a rhythmic comfort born of her temperament. How much grief and harmful excitement a healthy dullness avoids! I am not saying virtue isn't virtuous simply because it faces no temptation; rather, dullness is a magnificent gift often underrated, and some are blessed with Nature's finest anodyne.

During this season, Pen wandered dully between the theater and home, doing exactly as he pleased. His erratic behavior tormented his mother, who would have interfered frequently had not the Major constantly checked her impulses while simultaneously encouraging her. The wily man of the world sensed a favorable shift in Pen's condition. The violent outpouring of verse was a key symptom giving his guardian and physician cause for optimism. Pen could be heard declaiming poetry in the shrubberies or muttering rhymes between his teeth while sitting with the household in the evenings.

One day, while prowling the house in Pen's absence, the Major discovered a massive book filled with verses in Pen's study. Containing both English and Latin, complete with scholarly footnotes quoting classics, the book confirmed the Major's suspicions. *He can't be very bad,* reasoned the Pall-Mall Philosopher. Consequently, he pointed out to Mrs. Pendennis—with a hint of secret disappointment, for she loved romance like other soft-hearted women—that the young gentleman had returned home hungry for dinner nightly and displayed a hearty appetite at breakfast. "Gad, I wish I could," the Major muttered, thinking ruefully of his own medicinal pills. "The boy begins to sleep well; depend upon it." It was cruel, but undeniably true.

With no one else to confide in—unable to discuss his loves and disappointments with his mother—Uncle Pendennis treated the topic with a scornful, worldly tone. Though polite, this attitude grated heavily on Pen's sensitive feelings. Furthermore, Foker was far too coarse to appreciate such refined sentiments. Thus, Pen's friendship for the Curate deepened; he never grew tired of using Smirke as a listener. What is a lover without a confidant? Pen employed Mr. Smirke, much like Corydon uses an elm tree, to carve out his mistress's name. He made the Curate echo the name of the beautiful Amaryllis. Once men stop playing the tune, they rarely care for the pipe anymore, but Pen believed he possessed a profound friendship for Smirke precisely because he could pour his loves and sorrows into the tutor's ears. Smirke, in turn, had his own compelling reasons to always be available.

Pen's affection erupted in a flood of sonnets addressed to his "friend of the heart," which Smirke received with great sympathy. The Curate was bombarded with Latin Sapphics and Alcaics. The love songs multiplied under Pen's fluent pen, and Smirke declared them beautiful, believing wholeheartedly in their quality. Grateful that Heaven had sent him such a friend at such a pivotal moment, Pen showered his tutor with his best-bound books, a gold guard-chain, and even attempted to gift him a double-barreled gun. He ventured into Chatteris on credit to purchase a gold pencil-case—which he couldn't afford due to existing debts to Smirke from previous gifts—and presented it with an inscription declaring his unalterable, eternal devotion. Naturally, the Curate was delighted by every token of attachment.

However, the poor Curate was naturally dismayed by the prospect of his pupil's departure. When Arthur left, Smirke's occupation and joy would vanish. What pretext could he find for his daily visits to Fairoaks and the kind words or glances from the widow there, which were as vital to him as the frugal dinners served by Madame Fribsby? Without Arthur, he would be reduced to visiting like any ordinary acquaintance. Little Laura could not teach him the Catechism more than once a week. He had curled himself around Fairoaks like ivy; the thought of losing his grip on the place caused him to pine. Should he speak his mind and kneel before the widow? He weighed every indication in her behavior that might flatter his hopes. Three weeks prior, she had praised his sermons; she had thanked him profusely for a melon gift and for hosting a small dinner party; she had expressed enduring gratitude for his kindness to Arthur. When he declared his boundless love for the boy, she had responded romantically, indicating her own strong gratitude and regard for all her son's friends. Should he speak out? Or wait? If he spoke and was rejected, it was terrifying to imagine the gates of Fairoaks closing forever—behind that door lay the entire world for Mr. Smirke.

And so, dear readers, we observe how every man possesses private griefs that weigh heavier upon him than anyone else's sorrow.
