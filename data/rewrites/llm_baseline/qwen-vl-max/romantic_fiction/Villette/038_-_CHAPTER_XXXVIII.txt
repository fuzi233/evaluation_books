It is not so for all. Yet, what then? His will shall be done—done it surely will, whether we bow to it or not. The force of creation moves forward; unseen and seen powers fulfill their purpose. Proof of life beyond must be given—and if need be, written in fire and blood. We see this record etched across nature: in fire and blood it crosses our own lives. Sufferer, do not faint before this burning truth. Tired traveler, strengthen your limbs, lift your eyes, press on. Pilgrims and fellow mourners, walk together. For most of us, the path through this world is dark and long—let our steps be steady, equal, unshaken. Let our cross be our banner. Our staff is His promise: “His word is tried, His way perfect.” Our hope now is His providence: “He gives the shield of salvation, whose gentleness makes great.” Our final home is His bosom, who “dwells in the height of Heaven.” Our crown is a glory that surpasses, that endures. Let us run so that we may win. Let us endure hardship as good soldiers. Let us finish our course, keep the faith, and trust that in the end we shall come out more than conquerors. “Art thou not from everlasting mine Holy One? WE SHALL NOT DIE!”

On a Thursday morning, we gathered in class, waiting for the literature lesson. The hour had arrived—we expected the master.

The students of the first class sat still, quiet as stone. Their neatly penned compositions, tied with ribbon, lay ready before them, awaiting the professor’s swift glance as he passed each desk. It was July, the morning bright and warm. The glass door stood slightly open, letting in a fresh breeze that stirred the plants at the lintel—leaves swaying, bending, seeming to whisper secrets.

M. Emanuel was not always punctual. We did not think much of his delay. But when the door finally opened, it was not he who entered—not with his usual fire and haste—but Madame Beck, calm and cautious, stepping into the room like a shadow.

She approached M. Paul’s desk, stood before it, drew her shawl tighter around her shoulders. With a fixed gaze and a low, firm voice, she began: “This morning there will be no literature lesson.”

After a pause of about two minutes, she continued:
“It is likely the lessons will be suspended for a week. I shall need at least that time to find a suitable replacement for M. Emanuel. In the meantime, let us fill the gaps wisely.”

She paused again, then spoke further:
“Your professor, ladies, intends, if possible, to take leave of you properly. At present, he lacks the time for such a farewell. He is preparing for a long journey. A sudden, urgent call of duty has summoned him far away. He has decided to leave Europe for an indefinite period. Perhaps he will speak to you himself. In place of the usual lesson, you will read English today under Mademoiselle Lucy.”

She bowed slightly, adjusted her shawl, and left the room.

A silence fell—then a murmur spread through the hall. I believe some girls wept.

Time passed. Whispers grew louder. Sobbing came here and there. Discipline slipped—like a thread unraveling. It felt as though vigilance had vanished, surveillance lifted. Habit and duty helped me regain control. I rose, spoke in my usual tone, ordered quiet, and enforced it. I made the English reading long and intense, keeping them occupied all morning.

I confess—I felt irritation toward those who wept. Their grief seemed hollow, only hysteric emotion. I told them so plainly. I almost mocked them. I was stern. Truthfully, I could not bear their tears, that gasping breathlessness. I could not stand it. One weak-hearted girl kept sobbing after the others had ceased. Duty compelled me to confront her—so sharply, so firmly—that she dared not continue, forced to suppress the storm within.

That girl might have hated me—except that, once school ended and her companions departed, I called her to stay. When they were gone, I did something I had never done before: I pulled her close, pressed her to my chest, kissed her cheek. But the moment I yielded to that impulse, I quickly sent her away—for upon that tender act, she wept even harder.

I filled every minute of that day with work. I would have stayed awake all night if I could have lit a candle. But the night brought no peace—only restless shadows and a deep weariness, leaving me ill-prepared for the next day’s torment: relentless gossip.

Of course, the news became the talk of everyone. At first, there was some restraint—then it vanished. Every mouth opened, every tongue wagged. Teachers, students, servants—all spoke of “Emanuel.” He, who had been part of the school since its beginning, suddenly leaving? Everyone found it strange.

They talked endlessly, over and over, until from the chaos of words and rumors, a few truths emerged. By the third day, I heard he would sail in a week—then, that he was bound for the West Indies. I looked at Madame Beck—her face, her eyes—searching for confirmation or denial. I studied her closely, but nothing revealed itself except composure, indifference.

“This departure is a great loss to me,” she said publicly—once in class, again at dinner, speaking aloud to Zélie St. Pierre. “I do not know how I shall fill this gap. I am used to my cousin—he has been my right hand. What shall I do without him? I opposed his decision, but M. Paul convinced me it was his duty.”

She repeated this everywhere, calmly, clearly.

Why was it his duty? I wanted to ask. I had the urge to grab her by the arm as she walked past me in class, to hold her fast and say: “Stop. Let us hear the full truth. _Why_ must he go into exile?” But she never looked at me. Never seemed aware I cared.

The week passed. No one mentioned him coming to say goodbye. No one seemed to care. No one asked if he would come. No one showed distress at the thought of him vanishing without a word. They chattered constantly—yet never touched on the heart of it.

Madame Beck, of course, could see him. Speak freely with him. What did she care whether he appeared in the classroom?

The week ended. We learned he was departing on a certain day—his destination: Basseterre, Guadaloupe. The reason for his journey involved a friend’s affairs, not his own. I believed that.

“Basseterre, Guadaloupe.” I barely slept during this time. But whenever I did, I woke abruptly—startled—as if someone whispered those names over my pillow, or saw them flash across the darkness in jagged red or violet light.

There was no help for what I felt. How could there be? M. Emanuel had been kinder to me lately—growing ever more gentle, more considerate. A month had passed since we settled our theological dispute, and since then, no quarrel had arisen. Our peace was not cold or distant. We had grown closer. He came often, spoke more freely, spent hours with me—calm, content, almost familiar. We shared gentle topics. He asked about my plans. I told him. He liked the idea of opening a school—it made me repeat it several times, though he called it an Alnaschar dream. The rift was healed. Understanding deepened. Feelings of unity, of hope, took root in my heart. Affection, respect, trust—each wove a stronger bond.

What peaceful lessons we had! No more mockery of my “intellect,” no more threats of public humiliation. Instead, silent kindness, gentle guidance, patient forbearance—forgiving, yet never praising. There were moments when he would sit beside me, saying nothing at all. When dusk came or duty called, he would leave with words like these: “Il est doux, le repos! Il est précieux le calme bonheur!”

One evening, less than ten days ago, he joined me walking in my private alley. He took my hand. I looked up. I thought he meant to catch my attention.

“Bonne petite amie!” he murmured softly. “Douce consolatrice!”

But in his touch, in his words, something new stirred—a strange thought, a deeper feeling. Was he becoming more than brother or friend? Did his gaze carry affection beyond kinship?

His eyes spoke volumes. His hand pulled me forward. His lips trembled—almost as if to speak. No. Not now. Then—into the twilight came interruption: two figures, ominous and dual—a woman’s and a priest’s—Madame Beck and Père Silas.

I shall never forget the look on the latter’s face. At first, it showed a Jean-Jacques sensibility, disturbed by the signs of intimacy just caught. Then, instantly, it darkened with the jealousy of the church. He spoke to me with solemnity. He looked at his pupil with severity. As for Madame Beck—she saw nothing. Nothing. Though her kinsman held the hand of the foreign heretic, refusing to let go, pressing it tightly, even in her presence.

After that, when the announcement of his departure came, I found it hard to believe. Only constant repetition, the belief of the hundred and fifty minds around me, forced me to accept it fully. And that week of silence—of blank, burning days—without a word from him—its passage I remember, but cannot describe.

The last day dawned. He would come. He would speak his farewell—or vanish without a sound, never to be seen again.

This choice seemed clear in every mind in the school. All rose at the usual hour. All ate breakfast as usual. All went to their duties, indifferent, as if nothing had changed.

So blind, so numb, so routine-bound was the house—I scarcely knew how to breathe in such suffocating stillness. Would no one give me a voice? Was there no wish, no word, no prayer I could echo with an “Amen”?

I had seen them united in demanding the smallest things—treats, holidays, skipped lessons. Yet now, not one would join in demanding a final meeting with a teacher they had loved—at least some of them—loved as they could love. But oh—what is the love of the crowd?

I knew where he lived. I knew where he could be reached. The distance was mere steps. If he’d been in the next room—uninvited, I could not have used my knowledge. To follow, to seek, to remind, to recall—these were powers I did not possess.

M. Emanuel might have passed within reach of my hand. Had he walked silently by, unnoticed, I would have let him go—silent and still.

Morning passed. Afternoon came. I thought it was over. My heart trembled. My blood churned. I was sick, barely able to remain at my post. Yet the world around me moved on, unconcerned. All seemed cheerful, free of worry, fear, or thought. Even the girls who had wept hysterically seven days before seemed to have forgotten everything.

Just before five o’clock—the hour of dismissal—Madame Beck summoned me to her chamber. She had received an English letter and needed me to translate it and write a reply. Before beginning, I noticed she quietly closed both doors of her room. She even shut and locked the window, though it was hot and air circulation was usually essential to her. Why this caution? A sharp suspicion, almost fierce distrust, rose in me. Was she trying to block sound? What sound?

I listened—more intently than ever. Like a winter wolf sniffing snow, scenting prey, hearing the tramp of a traveler far off. Yet I could listen and write at the same time. Midway through the letter, I heard it—a step in the vestibule. No bell rang. Rosine, no doubt acting on orders, had anticipated the call. Madame saw me stop. She coughed, made noise, raised her voice. The step moved on—toward the classrooms.

“Continue,” she said. But my hand was frozen, my ear captive, my thoughts stolen.

The classrooms were in another building, separated by a hall. Still, I heard the sudden stir—a whole group rising at once.

“They are putting away their work,” Madame said.

It was indeed time—but why such sudden silence? That instant stillness?

“Wait, Madame—I will see what it is.”

I put down my pen and left her. Left her? No—she followed, close as my shadow. I turned on the last step of the stairs.

“Are you coming too?” I asked.

“Yes,” she replied, meeting my gaze with a peculiar look—clouded, yet resolute.

We walked—not side by side, but she stepped behind me, following my footsteps.

He had come. Entering the first class, I saw him. There, once again, stood the familiar figure. I doubt they had tried to keep him away—but he was here.

The girls formed a semicircle. He walked among them, giving farewells, shaking hands, kissing cheeks. This custom—foreign, yet accepted—was solemn, prolonged.

I felt bitter that Madame Beck should follow me so closely—watching, trailing. My neck and shoulder burned under her breath. I was driven to fury.

He was nearing the end. The circle nearly complete. He turned. But Madame stepped forward suddenly—towering, spreading her drapery, blocking me. She eclipsed me. I was hidden.

She knew my weakness. She understood the paralysis of spirit that seized me in crisis. She rushed to her cousin, overwhelmed him with words, captured his attention, hurried him toward the glass door leading to the garden. I think he glanced back. If only I had met his eye—courage might have surged. There might have been a charge, a rescue. But already the room was in chaos. The semicircle broke into groups. I was lost among thirty others. Madame had her way. Yes—she took him away. He did not see me. He thought I was absent.

Five o’clock struck. The dismissal bell rang. The school scattered. The room emptied.

To my memory, there follows a darkness, a confusion in certain minutes—grief beyond expression, loss unbearable. _What_ should I do? Oh, _what_ should I do—when all my life’s hope was torn from my heart, ripped by the roots?

I did not know what I should do—until a child, the youngest in the school, burst into my silent storm with innocence.

“Mademoiselle,” she lisped, “I am to give you this. M. Paul said I must search the whole house—from attic to cellar—and when I found you, give you this.”

She handed me a note. The little dove dropped its olive branch on my knee.

No address. No name. Only these words:

“It was not my intention to say good-bye to you when I bid the others farewell, but I hoped to see you in class. I was disappointed. The meeting is delayed. Be ready for me. Before I sail, I must see you alone, speak with you at length. Be ready—my time is short, and now, taken up. Besides, I have private business I will not share with anyone—even with you.—PAUL.”

“Be ready?” Then tonight. But wasn’t he sailing tomorrow? Yes—of that I was certain. I had seen the ship’s departure date posted. Oh—I would be ready. But could this meeting truly happen? Time was short. The schemers were vigilant, active, hostile. The path to him was narrow as a gully, deep as a chasm—Apollyon stood guard, breathing flames. Could my Greatheart prevail? Could my guide reach me?

Who could say? Yet I began to gather courage. It seemed I felt his heartbeat still true to mine.

I waited. Apollyon came, dragging Hell behind him. I think if Eternity held torment, it would not be fire or despair. I think that on a certain day—among those days that never dawned nor set—an angel entered Hades. He stood, shone, smiled, delivered a prophecy of conditional pardon, sparked a fragile hope of bliss to come—not now, but at a day unknown. He revealed His glory, His promise—spoke—then rose, became a star, and vanished into Heaven. His gift? Suspense—a worse blessing than despair.

The first hours stretched long and slow. In spirit, I clung to the tail of the last. They passed like drifting clouds—like wreckage before a storm.

They passed. The long, hot summer day burned away like a Yule log. Its crimson glow faded. I was left bent in cool blue shadows, watching the pale, ash-like gleams of night.

Prayers were over. Bedtime came. My roommates were asleep. I remained in the dim first class, forgetting rules I had never broken before.

How long I walked I cannot say. Hours, surely. Mechanically, I pushed benches aside, cleared a path down the center. There I paced—until I was certain all were asleep, out of hearing. Then, relying on Night, trusting Solitude, I let my tears fall. No longer sealed, no longer chained. They heaved my heart. They tore their way. In this house, what grief could be sacred?

Shortly after eleven—a very late hour in the Rue Fossette—the door opened quietly, not furtively. A lamp flame cut through the moonlight. Madame Beck entered, composed as if arriving at any ordinary hour. Instead of speaking to me, she went to her desk, took her keys, and pretended to search for something. She lingered too long. Too calm. My mood could not endure the pretense. Two hours ago, I had cast aside all usual fears and respect. Led by a touch, ruled by a word—no yoke could now be borne.

“It is past time for rest,” she said. “The rules have already been broken too long.”

She received no answer. I did not stop walking. When she blocked my path, I moved her aside.

“Let me persuade you to calm, Meess,” she said, trying to soften her voice. “Let me lead you to your chamber.”

“No!” I said. “Neither you nor anyone else shall persuade or lead me.”

“Your bed shall be warmed. Goton is still awake. She will make you comfortable. She will give you a sedative.”

“Madame,” I burst out, “you are a sensualist. Beneath your serenity, your peace, your decorum—you are an undenied sensualist. Warm your own bed. Take your sedatives, your sweet drinks, your spiced meats. If you suffer—if you are disappointed—and perhaps you are—nay, I _know_ you are—seek your own remedies. Leave me. _Leave me_, I say!”

“I must send someone to watch you, Meess. I must send Goton.”

“I forbid it. Let me be alone. Keep your hand off me, off my life, off my pain. Oh, Madame! In your hand lies both chill and poison. You envenom and paralyze.”

“What have I done, Meess? You must not marry Paul. He cannot marry.”

“Dog in the manger!” I said. For I knew she secretly desired him—had always wanted him. She called him “insupportable.” She railed at him as a “dévot.” She did not love him—but she wanted to marry him, to bind him to her interests. Into some of Madame’s deepest secrets I had penetrated—I know not how—by intuition, by inspiration. Through living with her, I had slowly learned: unless with an inferior, she must always be a rival. She was my rival—heart and soul—though hidden beneath the smoothest mask, known only to her and me.

For two minutes I stood over her, feeling I held her entire being in my power. In moments like this, when perception sharpened, her habitual disguise—her mask, her domino—became to me a net of holes. I saw beneath: a heartless, self-indulgent, ignoble soul. She retreated quietly—meek, composed, though deeply uneasy. “If you will not be persuaded to rest,” she said, “I must reluctantly leave you.” And she did—perhaps more relieved to escape than I was to see her go.

This was the only moment of raw truth between us—the only confrontation that ever occurred. It was never repeated. It did not change her manner toward me. I do not know if she sought revenge. I do not know if she hated me more for my blunt honesty. I think she armored herself with the secret philosophy of her strong mind, resolved to forget what pained her to remember. I know that until the end of our lives, there was never another word, never another hint of that fiery exchange.

And so, the night passed.
