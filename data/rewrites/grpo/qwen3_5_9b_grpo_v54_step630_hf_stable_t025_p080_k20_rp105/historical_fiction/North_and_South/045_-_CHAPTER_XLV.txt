The melody of youth had faded into silence, leaving behind only the hollow echo of a world where the listeners were gone. As William Bell lay awake, the ghost of Helstone haunted his mind, summoned by his conversation with Mr. Lennox. In the theater of his sleep, he was once again the tutor at the college, a Fellow in his prime, visiting his newly wedded friend, the Vicar of Helstone. They leaped across babbling brooks that seemed to suspend them in an eternal, weightless afternoon. Time and space dissolved; reality was measured not by the clock, but by the swelling tide of emotion. The trees blazed with autumn fire, and the air was thick with the perfume of wild herbs. There, he saw the young wife moving through the house, caught in that peculiar tension of a woman proud of her devoted husband yet acutely aware of her precarious financial standing—a dynamic Mr. Bell had observed with such clarity a quarter-century ago.

The dream was so vivid, so indistinguishable from truth, that when he jolted awake, his present existence felt like a phantom. Where was he? In the stifling, ornately furnished room of a London hotel. Who were the figures who had moved around him, touched him, spoken to him just moments before? Dead. Buried. Lost forever to the reach of the earth. He was an old man, stripped of the exultant strength of his manhood, drowning in an utter loneliness that threatened to crush him. With a gasp, he rose, forcing himself to dress in a frantic rush for the breakfast awaiting him in Harley Street.

In the lawyer's office, the weight of the situation pressed down on Margaret. She watched as Mr. Bell's presence failed to soften the blow; instead, her eyes widened and her lips grew pale as each piece of evidence, meant to exonerate Frederick, was revealed to be missing. Even Mr. Lennox, usually so composed, softened his professional tone as he guided her toward the final extinction of hope. It was not that Margaret had been unaware of the outcome; she had known it all along. It was the relentless, minute-by-minute dismantling of every possible defense that finally broke her resolve, reducing her to tears.

"I had better not go on," Mr. Lennox said, his voice thick with concern. "It was a foolish proposal of mine. Lieutenant Hale..." He paused, granting him the title of the service from which he had been so harshly cast aside, a small courtesy that seemed to soothe Margaret. "Lieutenant Hale is happy now. More secure in fortune and future prospects than he could ever have been in the navy; and he has, doubtless, adopted his wife's country as his own."

"That is it," Margaret whispered, forcing a fragile smile. "It seems so selfish in me to regret it... and yet he is lost to me, and I am so lonely."

Mr. Lennox turned over his papers, wishing silently for the prosperity he believed awaited him someday. Mr. Bell blew his nose but remained silent, respecting the gravity of the moment. Within minutes, Margaret appeared to regain her composure. She thanked Mr. Lennox with a courtesy that bordered on graciousness, keenly aware that her breakdown might have seemed ungrateful to a man who had done his duty. Yet, it was pain she would not have been without.

As Mr. Bell rose to bid her farewell, he hesitated, fumbling with his gloves. "Margaret!" he began. "I am going down to Helstone tomorrow to look at the old place. Would you like to come with me? Or would it give you too much pain? Speak out, don't be afraid."

"Oh, Mr. Bell," she managed, unable to find further words. But she reached out, taking his old, gout-afflicted hand and pressing a kiss to it.

"Come, come; that's enough," he said, flushing with awkwardness. "I suppose your aunt Shaw will trust you with me. We'll go tomorrow morning, and we shall arrive about two o'clock, I fancy. We'll take a snack, order dinner at the little inn—the Lennard Arms, it used to be—and go and get an appetite in the forest. Can you stand it, Margaret? It will be a trial, I know, to both of us, but it will be a pleasure to me, at least. And there we'll dine—it will be but doe-venison, if we can get it at all—and then I'll take my nap, while you go out and see old friends. I'll give you back safe and sound, barring railway accidents, and I'll insure your life for a thousand pounds before starting, which may be some comfort to your relations; but otherwise, I'll bring you back to Mrs. Shaw by lunch time on Friday. So, if you say yes, I'll just go upstairs and propose it."

"It's no use my trying to say how much I shall like it," Margaret replied, her voice trembling through her tears.

"Well, then, prove your gratitude by keeping those fountains of yours dry for the next two days," Mr. Bell insisted with a grin. "If you don't, I shall feel queer myself about the lachrymal ducts, and I don't like that."

"I won't cry a drop," Margaret promised, winking her eyes to shake the moisture from her lashes and forcing a smile.

"There's my good girl. Then we'll go upstairs and settle it all."

Margaret left the office in a state of almost trembling eagerness. Meanwhile, Mr. Bell discussed his plan with Aunt Shaw. She was first startled, then doubtful and perplexed, her mind racing with propriety and safety. Yet, in the end, she yielded to the rough force of Mr. Bell's conviction rather than her own lingering fears. Until Margaret's safe return and the happy fulfillment of the project, she could not settle whether it was right or wrong, proper or improper. But when the plan was set, her decision crystallized instantly. "She was sure it had been a very kind thought of Mr. Bell's," she declared, "and just what she herself had been wishing for Margaret, as giving her the very change which she required, after all the anxious time she had had."
