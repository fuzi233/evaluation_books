Captain Willoughby was a well-known figure at his club—not for charm, but for endurance. He had a peculiar gift: the ability to hold an audience captive with stories so unremarkable they seemed to drain the very air of interest. His tales revolved around people no one in the room had ever heard of, delivered with such solemn conviction that listeners felt compelled to nod along, even as their minds drifted elsewhere. He didn’t listen—he only spoke. And he spoke on, undeterred by silence, indifference, or outright glares. A snub would leave him unruffled, his expression blankly pleasant, while inside he remained lost in a world of his own dullness.

At the supper table or in the smoking-room, he was like a steamroller rolling over conversation—crushing it flat under the weight of his endless monologue. There was no escaping him. Trite anecdotes were sandwiched between stale aphorisms from old books, each delivered with the theatrical surprise of a man who believed himself profoundly wise. If you stayed long enough, you’d lose the will to flee. You’d sit trapped in a web of tedium, your escape blocked by sheer inertia.

Only those who didn’t know him stayed long enough. The others, upon spotting him enter, would rise and vanish into the next room or down the stairs, leaving behind a sudden emptiness where once there had been chatter.

So it was that within half an hour of his arrival, Captain Willoughby found himself alone in a large corner of the club—the same corner that had been bustling moments before. He had made it a habit to select the liveliest group as his audience, not out of desire for attention, but because it gave him the illusion of being wanted.

He sat there one afternoon in early October, enjoying the quiet, when the waiter approached and handed him a card.

Willoughby took it eagerly. He welcomed company—his usual circle had scattered to urgent engagements, leaving him isolated. But as he read the name, his face fell.

"Colonel Durrance!" he muttered, scratching his head thoughtfully.

Durrance had never visited him before. Why now? It felt suspicious. Had Durrance somehow learned of his trip to Kingsbridge?

"Does Colonel Durrance know I’m here?" he asked.

"Yes, sir," the waiter replied.

"Very well. Show him in."

Durrance’s visit must be about something—probably questions. And questions were dangerous. Willoughby had no intention of getting tangled again in the affairs of Miss Ethne Eustace. Feversham and Durrance could settle their own matters. He wasn’t opposed to diplomacy—but he wasn’t eager to use it here.

As Durrance entered, Willoughby studied him warily. But Durrance showed no signs of inquiry. Instead, he moved with calm assurance.

Willoughby rose and guided him toward the empty corner.

"Will you smoke?" he asked, then paused. "I beg your pardon."

"Oh, I’ll smoke," Durrance said. "It’s not true that a man can’t enjoy tobacco without seeing the smoke. If I let my cigar go out, I’d know it at once. But you’ll see—I won’t let it out."

He lit the cigar slowly, deliberately, and leaned back in his chair.

"I’m lucky to find you, Willough.” he said. “I’m only in town today. I come up from Devonshire every now and then to see my oculist. I’ve been anxious to meet you if I could. On my last visit, Mather told me you were away. You remember Mather? He was with us in Suakin."

"Of course," Willoughby replied warmly. He was glad to talk about Mather—perhaps this would distract Durrance from other things.

"We’re both curious," Durrance continued, "and you might clear something up. Have you ever met an Arab named Abou Fatma?"

"Abou Fatma?" Willoughby repeated slowly. "One of the Hadendoas?"

"No—a Kabbabish man."

"Abou Fatma?" he echoed, as though hearing the name for the first time. "No, I never came across him." Then he stopped.

Durrance sensed the pause was unnatural. Normally, Willoughby would have asked why he was inquiring. But he said nothing.

In truth, Willoughby was wondering how Durrance had even heard of Abou Fatma. The name had surfaced only once—on the veranda of the palace in Suakin, a year ago. He hadn’t spoken of it since.

But he had spoken the truth—he hadn’t met Abou Fatma. Though Feversham had mentioned him.

"That makes me more curious," Durrance said. "Mather and I were together during the last reconnaissance in '84. We found Abou Fatma hiding near the Sinkat fort. He told us about the Gordon letters—how he’d hidden them in Berber. Ah! You remember his name now."

"I was just getting my pipe out," Willoughby said, hoping to cover the delay. "But yes—I remember now, thanks to the letters."

"They were brought to you in Suakin, fifteen months ago," Durrance said. "Mather showed me the article in the *Evening Standard*. I’ve been wondering—did Abou Fatma return to Berber and recover them?"

"Since I never met him," Willoughby replied awkwardly, "it seems he wasn’t the one."

He regretted denying knowledge so quickly. Now he feared the next question.

But Durrance didn’t ask it. Instead, he said slowly, "I wondered—Abou Fatma wouldn’t likely return to Berber. After all, whoever retrieved the letters did so at great risk. And since Gordon was dead… what reason would there be?"

"Quite so," Willoughby said, relieved. "No obvious reason."

"For no obvious reason," Durrance corrected gently.

Willoughby turned, eyes narrowing. Was Durrance aware of his trip to Kingsbridge? Of his meeting with Ethne?

But Durrance only smoked, his head tilted back, eyes closed. He seemed satisfied. No further questions.

Yet in his mind, Durrance was piecing together the puzzle. He knew Harry Feversham had disappeared after a moment of disgrace—some act of cowardice that had earned him three white feathers. But Feversham had vanished quietly, without explanation. And now, five years later, he was in the Sudan—broken, destitute.

Durrance suspected the redemption came through the Gordon letters. That someone had retrieved them from Berber—and that Feversham had done it. Not for glory, but for atonement.

And the only way that story made sense was if Feversham had heard of Abou Fatma’s tale—heard it in Suakin, perhaps, from Willoughby himself.

Willoughby’s silence confirmed it. His hesitation, his careful speech—Durrance recognized the signs. Just like Ethne used to speak—guarded, wary, full of unspoken meaning.

And then came the trap: "For no obvious reason," Durrance had said. A tiny flicker in Willoughby’s eyes—then a sharp turn of the head. Caught.

But Durrance didn’t press. He had no wish to provoke Willoughby into warning Ethne. So he changed tack.

"I wanted to tell you why I sought you out," he said. "It’s about Harry Feversham."

Willoughby stiffened.

"Something happened to him," Durrance continued. "More than five years ago. He did something—or failed to do something. The secret has been kept tight. He dropped out of sight. Disappeared."

"You're going back to the Sudan?" Durrance asked.

"Yes—in a week."

"Then I need you to look for him," Durrance said, leaning forward. "He’s there. At Wadi Halfa this spring, I ran into him. He’d fallen hard. I’ll tell you what happened."

He recounted their meeting outside Tewfikieh café—Feversham thin, worn, almost unrecognizable.

"It’s strange," Durrance said. "A man you knew well—gone in a second. Vanished like smoke. I want you to help him, Willoughby. If you find him, pull him back. Let me know. Harry Feversham was a friend of mine—one of the few real ones."

"All right," Willoughby said cheerfully. "I’ll keep an eye out. I remember he was a good friend of yours."

He reached for the matches.

Durrance watched as the phosphorus scraped and the match flared. Willoughby lit his pipe—a seasoned briar that bubbled as he sucked at it.

"Yes," Durrance said, "a great friend. You and I dined with him in his flat above St. James’s Park—just before we left England."

At that, the pipe stopped bubbling.

Silence.

Then—*swear!*—and a sharp stamp on the carpet.

Durrance smiled inwardly. He saw the scene clearly now: Willoughby lighting his pipe, frozen mid-action by a careless remark, staring at Durrance’s face, forgetting the match until it burned his fingers.

And then the dinner party came back to him.

"Feversham had just returned from Dublin," Durrance said. "He asked us to dinner to tell us he was engaged to Miss Eustace. That was almost the last time we saw him."

He took a slow draw of his cigar.

"And there was a third man present."

"Was there?" Willoughby asked. "It’s so long ago."

"Trench."

"To be sure—Trench was there. Poor old Trench—we may never dine together again."

His voice was light, casual. He struck another match and lit his pipe.

As he did, Durrance placed his cigar on the edge of the table.

"And we shall never dine with Castleton again," he said slowly.

"Castleton wasn't there!" Willoughby snapped—too fast.

"Not even expected," Durrance corrected. "He was dining elsewhere. He sent the telegram, you remember."

"Ah, yes," Durrance said. "A telegram came."

Now Durrance was certain. That dinner party held the key.

Willoughby, Trench, Castleton—three men whose actions had led to Feversham’s downfall.

He remembered standing by the window, watching the park, hearing the tattoo from Wellington Barracks—then the telegram arriving.

The pieces clicked.

Castleton had dined with a high official at the War Office that night. And yet—had sent a telegram.

To whom? To Feversham.

Feversham had read it silently, crumpled it, thrown it in the fire.

No word spoken.

But now Willoughby confirmed it: Castleton had sent it.

And the news? That Feversham’s regiment was ordered to active service.

That was it.

The moment Feversham received that message—engaged to Ethne, proud, strong—his courage broke. He resigned. He fled.

Not because he was weak—but because he was human.

And now, five years later, he was paying for it.

Durrance stood.

"Well," he said, "I can count on you, Willoughby. If you find him, help him. Draw on me for money."

"I’ll do my best," Willoughby said.

"You’re going?" Durrance asked.

"I could have won a bet this afternoon."

"How?"

"You said you never let your cigars go out. This one’s stone cold."

"I forgot," Durrance said. "I was thinking of Feversham."

He stepped out, hailed a cab, and drove away.

Willoughby exhaled. Relief washed over him. He had managed it—kept the secret. He’d outmaneuvered Durrance.

Or so he thought.

Durrance, however, had seen everything.

The final piece had fallen into place.

The telegram had come from Castleton. Castleton had been with a War Office official. The news was service—immediate, unavoidable.

Feversham had known. And panicked.

Durrance understood now. The cowardice wasn’t born of fear—it was born of weakness in a moment of triumph.

But the redemption? That was real.

Harry Feversham was atoning.

With every step, every hardship, every silent day in the Sudan—he was making amends.

Durrance chuckled softly.

"I’ll astonish old Sutch," he murmured.

That evening, he boarded the night mail. By midday the next day, he was home in Devonshire—quiet, content, and finally knowing the truth.
