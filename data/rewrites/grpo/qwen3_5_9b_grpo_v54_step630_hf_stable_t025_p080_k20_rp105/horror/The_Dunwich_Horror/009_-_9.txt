Friday morning, Armitage, Rice, and Morgan departed by automobile for Dunwich, arriving at the village around one in the afternoon. Though the day offered a deceptive brightness, a suffocating dread hung over the landscape, clinging to the strangely domed hills and the deep, shadowed ravines of the stricken region. Gaunt circles of stones occasionally pierced the skyline atop the mountains, silent sentinels of ancient folly. The air at Osborn's store was thick with hushed terror; within moments, the grim reality of the Elmer Frye household's annihilation became known to them.

Throughout the afternoon, the trio rode the perimeter of Dunwich, interrogating the locals and witnessing with rising horror the desolation of the Frye ruins. They saw the lingering tarry slickness on the ground, the blasphemous tracks gouged into the earth, the wounded cattle of Seth Bishop, and the vast swathes of vegetation torn apart as if by some unseen giant. To Armitage, the trail winding up and down Sentinel Hill possessed a cataclysmic significance; he stared long at the sinister, altar-like stone crowning the summit.

Eventually, learning that a party of State Police from Aylesbury had arrived earlier in response to the initial reports, the visitors resolved to locate them and exchange information. Yet, finding the officers proved more difficult than planning it. The police car stood empty near the Frye ruins, abandoned by its five occupants. The villagers, initially as bewildered as the outsiders, suddenly fell silent. Old Sam Hutchins turned pale, nudging Fred Farr and pointing a trembling finger toward the dank, yawning hollow of Cold Spring Glen.

"Gawd," he gasped, his voice cracking, "I told 'em not to go down into the glen. I never thought anyone'd do it with those tracks, that smell, and the whippoorwills screeching down there in the dark of noonday..."

A cold shudder rippled through the crowd, and every ear strained instinctively, listening to the unnatural silence. Armitage, now confronted with the full weight of the horror, trembled under the crushing responsibility of containment. Night approached, the hour when the mountainous blasphemy would lumber upon its eldritch course. *Negotium perambulans in tenebris...* The old librarian recited the formulas he had memorized, clutching the paper with the alternatives. He verified his electric flashlight, while Rice produced a metal insecticide sprayer and Morgan unslung his big-game rifle, ignoring warnings that no physical weapon could suffice.

Armitage, having read the diaries of the madman, knew precisely what manifestation to expect, yet he withheld any clues from the terrified populace, hoping to contain the secret until the creature could be neutralized. As shadows lengthened, the natives dispersed, retreating indoors despite the futility of locks against a force capable of bending timber and crushing stone. They shook their heads at the Arkham men's plan to guard the ruins, offering little hope that the watchers would survive the night.

That night, the hills groaned with subterranean rumbling, and the whippoorwills piped with threatening urgency. Occasionally, a wind sweeping from Cold Spring Glen carried a touch of ineffable fetor—the same stench the watchers had encountered fifteen and a half years prior above a dying thing. But the expected terror remained dormant. Whatever lurked in the glen bided its time, and Armitage deemed any nocturnal assault suicidal.

Morning broke wanly, the night sounds ceasing to leave behind a gray, bleak day punctuated by a drizzle. Heavy clouds piled up beyond the northwest hills, and the rain intensified. The men from Arkham debated under the shelter of a surviving Frye outbuilding: should they wait for the storm to pass, or take the aggressive path into the gloom? Thunder rumbled in the distance, and sheet lightning flashed, illuminating the accursed glen before a forked bolt descended nearby, plunging the sky into darkness.

Less than an hour later, a confused babel of voices erupted down the road. A dozen men, running, shouting, and whimpering hysterically, appeared in the gloom. The leader choked out a sobbing confession: "Oh, my Gawd, my Gawd! It's a-goin' agin, and this time by day! It's aout—it's aout an' a-movin' this very minute!"

Another man took up the tale, recounting how Zeb Whateley had heard the phone ring, only for Mrs. Corey to report that her hired boy, Luther, had seen trees bending at the mouth of the glen while driving cows after the lightning strike. Luther had smelled the same awful odor as the previous Monday and heard a swishing, lapping sound that defied the movement of the brush. Suddenly, the trees along the road had been pushed aside, accompanied by awful stomping and splashing in the mud. Luther had seen nothing but the bending foliage, yet the sound of wood cracking and splitting echoed from where Bishop's Brook crossed the road.

The narrative escalated as Sally, Seth Bishop's housekeeper, screamed into the phone. She described a mushy sound like an elephant puffing and trudging toward the house, followed by a fearful smell that triggered screams from her son, Chancey. The dogs went mad. Then, with a terrible yell, she reported the shed caving in—not by wind, but by some unseen force. The front yard picket fence crumpled without cause. Finally, heavy impacts battered the house repeatedly, invisible yet devastating, until a terrible crashing and a hullaballoo of screaming echoed over the line, worse than anything that had happened to the Fryes.

Silence followed, absolute and deafening. The crowd, convinced this was divine judgment for their iniquities, gathered around the Arkham men, unsure of what to do. Armitage saw the moment for decisive action had arrived.

"We must follow it, boys," he commanded, striving for a tone of reassurance. "I believe there is a chance to put it out of business. You know the Whateleys were wizards; this thing is of wizardry and must be met by the same means. I have read Wilbur Whateley's diary and the strange books he studied; I think I know the spell to make this thing fade. It is invisible—I knew it would be—but this sprayer contains a powder that might reveal it for a second. It is a frightful thing to have alive, but it is not as bad as what Wilbur would have unleashed had he lived longer. You will never know what the world has escaped. We must fight this single enemy; it cannot multiply, but it can do immense harm. We must not hesitate."

He directed them to the site of the latest wreck. Earl Sawyer, pointing through the diminishing rain, suggested a shortcut across the lower meadow, wading the brook and climbing through Carrier's mowing to reach the upper road near Seth's place. Armitage, Rice, and Morgan began the trek, followed slowly by the natives. As they scrambled up the twilight-covered, perpendicular wooded hill, twisting among fantastic ancient trees, courage and confidence mounted, tested severely by the oppressive atmosphere.

Emerging onto a muddy road just beyond the Seth Bishop place, they found the sun breaking through. Bent trees and hideously unmistakable tracks marked the path of the invisible destroyer. The scene was a grim repetition of the Frye incident; no bodies were found in the collapsed shells of the house and barn, only the stench and the tarry residue. Instinctively, the group turned from the ruins toward the line of horrible prints leading to the wrecked Whateley farmhouse and the altar-crowned slopes of Sentinel Hill.

Passing the site of Wilbur Whateley's former abode, the men shuddered visibly, hesitation mixing with their zeal. Tracking something as large as a house, yet invisible and possessing the vicious malevolence of a demon, was no joke. At the base of Sentinel Hill, the tracks left the road, leaving a fresh swath of bent and matted vegetation marking the monster's route to and from the summit.

Armitage produced a powerful pocket telescope and scanned the steep green side of the hill. He handed the instrument to Morgan, whose sight was keener. After a moment of intense gazing, Morgan cried out sharply, passing the glass to Earl Sawyer and indicating a specific spot on the slope. Sawyer, clumsy with optics, fumbled for a moment before Armitage helped him focus. When he finally saw, his cry was less restrained than Morgan's.

"Gawd almighty, the grass an' bushes is a-movin'! It's a-goin' up—slow-like—creepin' up ter the top this minute, heaven only knows what fer!"

Panic, the seed of which had been sown, began to spread among the seekers. It was one thing to chase a nameless entity; it was quite another to find it. Spells might work—or they might not. Voices began questioning Armitage about the nature of the thing, seeking answers that seemed to satisfy no one. Everyone felt they stood in close proximity to phases of nature and existence utterly forbidden, wholly outside the sane experience of mankind.
