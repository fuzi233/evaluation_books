The Thames shimmered under the midday sun, its waters shifting from dull gray to liquid gold as two men crossed Westminster Bridge. One was towering, the other diminutive—Hercule Flambeau, private detective, and Father Brown, the unassuming priest from Camberwell. Flambeau led the way to his new office in a towering building that loomed over the Abbey, its modern facade interrupted by one glaring oddity: a massive golden eye, gilded and radiant, mounted above the windows of the office directly above Flambeau’s.

“What in heaven’s name is that?” Father Brown asked, halting mid-step.

“A new religion,” Flambeau replied with a chuckle. “One of those fashionable creeds that absolves you by insisting you were never guilty to begin with. The fellow upstairs calls himself Kalon—some sort of prophet. He worships the sun, believes in staring straight into it without blinking. That eye is his symbol.”

Father Brown frowned. “The sun was never a kind god. But why the eye?”

“They claim a man can endure anything if his mind is unshaken. Health, strength, defiance of pain—all through sheer will.”

“And if a man were truly healthy,” mused Father Brown, “he wouldn’t feel the need to prove it.”

Flambeau paid little heed to the prophet’s theatrics, but the office below his own held his interest—a typewriting agency run by two sisters. Pauline Stacey, the elder, was a striking woman with a blade-sharp profile and a temper to match. She was an heiress who had abandoned aristocratic comforts for what she deemed a sterner, nobler life—modern work, modern machinery, no concessions to weakness. Her sister Joan, quieter and grayer, followed her with doglike devotion.

Flambeau had witnessed Pauline’s fierce independence firsthand—her impatience with lifts, her disdain for spectacles (she once shattered her sister’s pair in a fit of zeal). She spoke of courage, of defying limits, of staring down the sun itself.

It was no surprise, then, when she ended up dead at the base of the lift shaft.

The discovery sent the building into chaos. Flambeau, grim-faced, directed onlookers to fetch a surgeon while Father Brown lingered, his gaze drifting up to the balcony where Kalon stood, resplendent in white robes, his solar litany still ringing through the air.

The prophet descended, radiating calm. Joan Stacey, pale and composed, joined them.

Father Brown, ever the quiet observer, turned to Kalon. “Tell me about your religion.”

Kalon’s reply was a sermon in itself—grand, disdainful. He spoke of light, of purity, of Pauline’s love for him, even of a will she had drafted that very morning, leaving him half her fortune. He had an alibi—dozens would swear he had been on his balcony, praying, when she fell.

“Then how did she die?” Flambeau demanded.

Kalon’s voice softened with sorrow. “She believed in levitation—that the will could defy gravity. I think she tried to step off the ledge, trusting her faith to bear her up. It did not.”

Father Brown listened, his face creased with pain. At last, he murmured, “If that’s true, then take the will and go.”

But his eyes, blinking rapidly, told another story.
