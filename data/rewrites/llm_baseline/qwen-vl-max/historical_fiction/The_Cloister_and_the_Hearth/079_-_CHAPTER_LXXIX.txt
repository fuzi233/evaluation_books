She who had wept for poor old Martin could not endure this new sorrow with the quiet resignation that often marks the passing of an elder. Catherine tried, in vain, to comfort her with the usual phrases—how it was a blessed release, how the tree had ripened at last, as he himself had once said. But her most ill-timed words were those suggesting that now there were only two mouths to feed and one less burden to bear.

“Such cares are all the joys I have,” Margaret replied, her voice trembling. “They fill my hollow heart, which now feels as empty as it is wasted. Oh, empty chair—my bosom aches to see thee! Poor old man, how could I love him by halves? I used to sit and gaze at him, thinking, *But for me, thou wouldst die of hunger.* Once so wise, so learned, he grew helpless as my own child—and I loved him as if he had been my son instead of my father. Oh, empty chair! Oh, empty heart! Well-a-day! well-a-day!”

And though she tried to hold back, the tears came freely. Catherine stood silent, head bowed, knowing her words had failed.

Then, after a long pause, she spoke again—not from wisdom, but from the depths of her own feeling: “I speak to thee out of my head, not my heart. Thou dost well to be deaf to me. Were I in thy place, I should mourn him as thou dost.”

Margaret clung to her, and in that simple truth lay a small comfort. The platitudes had brought none.

Catherine’s heart softened further. “My poor girl,” she said, “you were not born to live alone. I have come to think of you as my own daughter. Waste not your youth on Gerard. He is either dead—or a traitor. It breaks my heart to say it, but how can one blind oneself to the truth? Thy father is gone, and I cannot always be beside thee. And here is a good lad—honest, steady—who has loved thee these many months. I’d take him and Comfort together. Heaven sends us creatures to torment and comfort us; without them, we are nothing.”

Seeing Margaret look bewildered, Catherine pressed on: “Why, surely you are not so blind as not to see it?”

“What?” asked Margaret. “Who?”

“Luke Peterson.”

“Our Luke? The boy who carries my basket?”

“Nay, he is over nineteen now—a strong, healthy young man. I’ve looked into his life, and they all say he is honest, hardworking, and never touches a drop. That’s no small thing in Rotterdam, where most lads are half man, half sponge.”

A faint smile touched Margaret’s lips—the first in days. “Luke loves dried puddings dearly,” she murmured. “And I make them just to his taste. That’s why he comes courting.”

Then she flushed crimson. “But if I thought he came for my son’s wife—be it past or present—I’d send him away sharp.”

“Nay, nay,” Catherine said quickly. “Let me not cause trouble. Poor lad! Fancy cannot be tamed. Bless you, I drew it out of him near a year ago.”

“Oh, mother!” Margaret exclaimed. “And you let him?”

“I thought of you,” Catherine answered. “I said to myself, *If he’s fool enough to serve her faithfully, then so much the better for her. A woman alone is lost without a man to carry her burdens.* But now I think the best thing you can do is marry him.”

“So then,” Margaret said bitterly, “even his own mother opposes him and would wed me to the first comer. An, Gerard, thou hast but me. I will not believe thee dead till I see thy tomb, nor false till I see thee holding another’s hand. Foolish boy—I shall never be kind to him again.”

Thus burdened by Catherine’s meddling kindness, Luke Peterson found the door closed to him. Where once he had been welcomed, he now met silence, and when spoken to, it was with sharp irritation. The gentle giant, unused to such coldness, began to tremble. One day he burst out, “Mistress, have I done anything to offend you?”

“Nothing,” she said softly. “It is I who am to blame. I am cursed. If you’ll listen to me, keep away for a time.”

“It’s all because of me, Mistress,” he said, turning to Catherine. “You, Mistress Catherine—what have I done to make her turn against me?”

“I meant well,” Catherine replied. “I told her I saw you looking at her through a wedding ring. But she won’t hear of it.”

“There was no need to tell her that,” Luke said. “She knows I’ve been courting her for a year.”

“No,” Margaret said firmly. “Or I should never have opened the street door to you.”

“But I come every Saturday night,” Luke insisted. “That’s how we court in Rotterdam. We sup with a lass on Saturdays—that’s wooing.”

“Oh, is it Rotterdam, is it?” Margaret said dryly. “Then next time, come on Thursday or Friday. For my part, I thought you came for my puddings, boy.”

“I like your puddings well enough,” Luke said gently. “You make them better than my mother does. But I like you more than the puddings.”

Then she rose, her voice soft yet unyielding: “You have seen the last of them. How dare you speak so to another man’s wife, especially when he is far away?”

“You need not trouble yourself to come here again, Luke,” she added. “I can carry my basket myself.”

“Oh, very well,” he said quietly. After a moment of silence, he stood up and said sadly to Catherine, “Dame, I daresay I’ve got the sack,” and left.

The next Saturday, Catherine found him sitting on the doorstep, weeping.

He confessed he had grown used to coming there—every other place felt strange.

She went in and told Margaret, who sighed and said, “Poor Luke—he might come in, if only he knew his place and treated her as a married wife.”

When this reached Luke, he hesitated.

“Pshaw!” Catherine snapped. “Promises are like pie-crusts—crumbly and useless. Promise her the world, rather than sit outside like a fool. A word will bring you inside now—when you humor her in everything. And if poor Gerard never returns to claim her, you’ll have her—eventually. A lone woman is always worn down, thou foolish boy.”
