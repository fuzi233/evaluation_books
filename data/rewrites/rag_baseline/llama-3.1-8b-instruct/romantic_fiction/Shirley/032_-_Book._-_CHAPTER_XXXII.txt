In the faint, flickering light of dawn, the household stirred within the walls of Briarmains. The night's darkness had barely lifted, yet the Yorke estate was already alive with the murmur of servants, the clinking of dishes, and the soft hum of the kitchen fire. It was here, amidst this quiet bustle, that a peculiar transformation took place. A young man, his body battered and bruised, lay supine on the best bed in the house, a testament to the violence that had befallen him on the rain-soaked road outside. The Yorkshire gentleman, Mr. Yorke, stood over him, his eyes fixed on the boy's pale face, his expression a mask of concern and calculation.

As the first light of morning seeped through the windows, Mr. Yorke's countenance softened, and his demeanor transformed. He became the devoted guardian, the benevolent patriarch, and the master of the household. His wife, Mrs. Yorke, emerged from her chambers, her nightcap askew, her eyes bleary with sleep, yet her countenance a study in contrast to her husband's. While some women might have been aghast at the sight of a wounded stranger in their midst, Mrs. Yorke was drawn to the drama, the pathos, and the sense of power that came with caring for this broken youth.

She watched with a calculating gaze as Mr. Yorke tended to the young man, his hands gentle, his voice soothing. She observed how Louis Moore, the injured boy's brother, hovered anxiously nearby, his eyes red-rimmed from lack of sleep. And she noted, too, the way the servants scurried about, eager to please, yet wary of intruding upon the scene. The air was thick with the scent of cooking bread, roasting meats, and simmering stews, but it was the aroma of opportunity that wafted through the room, drawing Mrs. Yorke in like a moth to flame.

As the morning wore on, the household settled into a routine of care and devotion. Mrs. Yorke proved to be a skilled nurse, her touch firm, her words reassuring.
