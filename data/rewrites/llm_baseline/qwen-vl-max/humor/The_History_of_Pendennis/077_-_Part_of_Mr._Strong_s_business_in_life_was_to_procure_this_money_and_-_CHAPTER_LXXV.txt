CHAPTER LXXV. A Chapter of Match-Making (With a Side of Drama and a Dash of Deception)

On the Tunbridge platform, Pen was practically vibrating with impatience—like a kettle left on too high a flame—until the evening train to London finally arrived. Half an hour passed, but to him it felt like six years, or possibly the entire duration of human history. The train came, it sped off into the twilight, and just as the lights of London blinked into view like a city’s sleepy eyes, a gentleman—forgetting his carpet bag in the process—dashed out, hopped into a cab, and bellowed, “Drive as fast as you can to Jermyn Street!”

The cabman, despite being a hansom-cabman (which, let’s be honest, is no small thing), accepted the tip with a polite “Thank you” and drove off at a speed that made the horses look like they were auditioning for *The Flying Dutchman*. Meanwhile, Pen bounded up the stairs of the hotel, heart hammering like a drum solo, straight to Lady Rockminster’s apartments.

Inside, Laura sat alone in the drawing-room, reading by lamplight, her face pale as a winter moon. She looked up when he entered. Should we follow? Well, why not? Life’s great moments are usually just ordinary ones dressed up in dramatic lighting and emotional music. Sometimes all it takes is one word, one glance, or a hand squeeze so intense it could power a small nation.

As Lady Rockminster stirred from her post-dinner nap and sauntered into the sitting room, we might as well tag along. “Upon my word, young people!” she declared, her voice sharp as a pair of scissors, while her attendant peered over her shoulder with eyes wide enough to swallow a walnut. And rightly so. Because there, in the middle of the room, stood Pen—on his knees—holding Laura’s trembling hand like a man who’d just discovered the secret of eternal life.

It had all happened in a flash: Pen burst in, saw Laura, didn’t even let her say “Back so soon?” before snatching her hand and collapsing to the floor like a fallen oak tree. “I’ve seen her,” he gasped. “She’s engaged to Harry Foker—and—and now, Laura?”

Laura’s hand squeezed back. Her eyes sparkled. Her lips trembled—but said nothing. And then, like a scene from a particularly melodramatic play, Pen buried his head in her lap and sobbed, “Come and bless us, dear mother.”

And then—cue the tender arms of Helen, wrapped around him like a warm blanket of forgiveness.

Just as things were getting emotionally charged, Lady Rockminster marched in and boomed, “Upon my word, young people! Beck! Leave the room. What do you think you’re doing, poking your nose in here?”

Pen leapt up, still clutching Laura’s hand like a lifeline. “She’s consoling me for my misfortune, ma’am,” he announced proudly. “What do you mean by kissing her hand? I don’t know what you’ll do next.”

“Oh,” said the old lady, narrowing her eyes. “You kissed *my* hand?”

“Yes, ma'am,” Pen said, unrepentant. “I’ve been to Tunbridge. I saw Miss Amory. And I found—well, a villain has stolen my place in her affections.”

“Is that all?” Lady Rockminster snapped. “Is that what you were crying about on your knees? You might have waited till tomorrow.”

“But another has taken my place,” Pen continued, adopting a tragically noble tone. “But why call him a villain? He’s brave, constant, young, wealthy, and beautiful.”

“What nonsense are you spouting?” the old lady demanded. “What’s really happened?”

“Miss Amory has jilted me,” Pen confessed, “and accepted Henry Foker, Esq. I found her singing love songs to him while he lay at her feet. Presents exchanged. Vows sworn. For ten days. Harry was old Mrs. Planter’s rheumatism, which kept dear Laura out of the house. He’s the most generous man alive—he promised Lady Ann’s husband the living of Logwood and gave her a magnificent wedding gift. And as soon as he was free, he rushed to throw himself at Blanche’s feet.”

“And so,” Lady Rockminster said, folding her arms, “since you can’t have Blanche, you settle for Laura? Is that it?”

“No, ma'am,” Pen replied. “He acted nobly. I did as she asked.”

“You may have done,” the old lady muttered. “But if you think I’m not worthy of Laura, I know it. And I pray Heaven to make me better. And if loving the best and purest creature in the world helps, then I shall have that joy to help me.”

“Hm, hm,” Lady Rockminster grumbled, eyeing them both. “All very well. But I should have preferred Bluebeard.”

At this point, Pen, sensing the conversation was veering toward emotional minefields, thought quickly. He remembered his morning chat with Huxter—about Fanny Bolton’s sudden marriage and the looming arrival of her father. So, to shift the mood, he launched into a story about how Huxter had married Fanny, and how terrified they were of Mr. Huxter senior showing up.

He told it with such comic flair—especially emphasizing Fanny’s flirtatious nature and her irresistible charm—that it was clear he was trying to say: *See, Laura? I wasn’t the one chasing after her. It was she who chased me. And now she’s gone off to charm someone else. Let’s forget that little incident, shall we? Or punish me gently, if you must.*

Laura caught on immediately. “If you did anything wrong,” she said softly, “you repented, dear Pen. And you know…”—she blushed—“that I have no right to reproach you.”

“Hm,” grumbled Lady Rockminster. “Still, I should have preferred Bluebeard.”

Pen smiled. “The past is gone. The future is ours. I’ll do my best to make your future happy, dear Laura.”

His heart swelled—not with pride, but with awe. He loved her more now that she’d admitted feeling something for Warrington. It showed her honesty, her depth. And Laura? She was probably thinking: *How strange that I ever cared for anyone else. I barely feel sorry he’s gone. In fact, I’m almost annoyed I care so little. Oh, Arthur—I’ve learned to love him so much in these two months. My thoughts are all about him. He’s never far from me. And soon… he’ll be mine. Mine! Oh, mother, if only you were here!*

She glowed. Literally. A halo of happiness seemed to surround her. She walked differently, talked differently—like a woman reborn. Arthur noticed. Lady Rockminster noticed. And the old lady whispered, “What a sly little wretch you’ve been, keeping your secret so well.”

Pen, meanwhile, was laughing about Huxter’s wedding and the chaos of it all.

“So,” Laura said, “what are we going to do for the young couple?”

Of course she would care—any true lover loves lovers. “We must go see them,” Pen said.

“Of course,” Laura agreed. “I intend to be very fond of Fanny. Let’s go right now. Lady Rockminster, may I borrow the carriage?”

“Go now?” the old lady scoffed. “It’s eleven o’clock at night. They’re probably in their nightcaps. And it’s time for you to go. Good night, Mr. Pendennis.”

Arthur and Laura begged for ten more minutes.

“Then tomorrow morning,” Lady Rockminster relented. “I’ll come and fetch you with Martha.”

“An earl’s coronet,” Pen mused, “will have quite an effect in Lamb Court and Smithfield. Wait—Lady Rockminster, will you join us in a little conspiracy?”

“A conspiracy?” she raised an eyebrow.

“Will you pretend to be ill tomorrow? When Mr. Huxter senior arrives, I’ll bring him in. If he thinks he’s attending a baronet in the country, what influence won’t a countess have? Once he’s softened, we’ll reveal the truth, bring in the couple, extract the paternal blessing, and finish the comedy.”

“A parcel of nonsense,” Lady Rockminster said. “Now take your hat, sir. Come away, miss. There—my head is turned another way. Good night, young people.”

And perhaps, as she walked away arm-in-arm with Laura, humming softly to herself, she thought of her own youth—of love, loss, and the quiet triumphs of the heart.

Next morning, Laura and Martha arrived as planned. The desired sensation—let’s hope—was achieved in Lamb Court. Then the trio headed to Charterhouse Lane to visit Mr. and Mrs. Samuel Huxter.

Fanny greeted them with a mix of nervousness and pride. She hadn’t seen Pen since her wedding. “Samuel told me how kind you were,” she said. “You were always kind, Mr. Pendennis. And—uh—how is your friend who was ill at Shepherd’s Inn?”

“My name is Laura,” the other woman said, blushing. “I’m Arthur’s sister. We’ll always love you for caring for him when he was sick. When we live in the country, we’ll see each other often. And I’ll always be happy to hear of your happiness, Fanny.”

“We’re going to do what you and Huxter did,” Pen said cheerfully. “Where is Huxter? What lovely lodgings! What a sweet cat!”

As Fanny answered, Laura thought: *Well, now—this is the girl everyone was so worried about? What did he see in her? She’s plain, yes—but what manners! Bless her, she was kind to him.*

Mr. Samuel had gone to meet his father. Mrs. Huxter said the old man would arrive at the Somerset Coffee-house. Fanny was trembling. “If he disowns us, what will we do?” she whispered. “I’ll never forgive myself for bringing shame on my husband’s head. You must intercede for us, Mr. Arthur. If anyone can sway him, it’s you.”

She still saw Pen as a superior being. Arthur, watching her, thought of the past—the little dramas, the tiny fears, the vanity, the sweetness of a new bride.

Afterward, Messrs. Linton and Blades arrived, puffing tobacco smoke like dragons. They’d seen the coronet on the carriage and were awestruck. “Who was that heavy swell who just drove off?” they asked. “And the countess—she’s the right sort.” They’d heard Pen’s father was “only a sawbones” and that he “gave himself confounded airs.”

They also recalled the night Huxter had argued with Pen in the Back Kitchen.

Later, returning through Fleet Street, Laura remarked, “Fanny is very nice, but honestly—no beauty. Maybe there is, but I can’t see it.”

Just then, near Temple Bar, they saw young Huxter returning to his bride. “The governor’s arrived,” he reported. “He’s at the Somerset Coffee-house. He’s in good spirits—something about the railway. But he’s afraid to talk about—about that business. Will Mr. Pendennis try it?”

Pen said he’d go right away.

Huxter junior hid outside while the interview took place. The coronet on the carriage inspired awe in him. And old Mr. Huxter, looking from the coffee-house window, grinned at the Strand—a sight he adored.

“And I can afford to give myself a lark, sir,” he said, shaking hands with Pen. “We got our bill. Our branch line’s approved. Our shares are up. And we’re buying your three fields along the Brawl—putting a pretty penny into your pocket, Mr. Pendennis.”

“Indeed!” Pen said, remembering a letter from Mr. Tatham that he’d neglected to open. “I hope you don’t plan to grow rich and abandon practice.”

“We can’t lose you at Clavering, Mr. Huxter,” Pen added. “Though I hear your son’s doing well. Dr. Goodenough speaks highly of him. It’s hard that a man of your eminence should be stuck in a country town.”

“The metropolis would have been my sphere,” Huxter said, gazing at the Strand. “But a man takes his business where he finds it. I succeeded to my father’s.”

“It was my father’s too,” Pen said. “Sometimes I wish I’d followed it.”

“You’ve chosen a loftier path,” the old man said. “You aspire to the senate. You write poetry. You move in fashionable circles. We keep an eye on you at Clavering. We read your name in aristocratic party lists. Why, only yesterday my wife wondered why your name wasn’t mentioned at the Earl of Kidderminster’s gathering.”

“Whose equipage was that?” Huxter asked, pointing. “The Countess Dowager of Rockminster? How is her Ladyship?”

“She’s not very well,” Pen said. “That’s why I urged her to see you.”

Old Huxter nearly fainted with joy. “There’s an old friend of yours in the carriage—a Clavering lady. Will you come out and speak to her?”

The old surgeon, thrilled, ran out, bowing and grinning.

Huxter junior watched from afar as his father spoke with Laura, saw her extend her hand, and then—after a brief exchange with Pen—saw his father actually jump into the carriage and drive away with Miss Bell.

No room for Arthur. He returned, laughing, and told his father where he’d gone.

During the ride, Laura charmed the old man with flattery, wit, and gentle manipulation. By the time they reached Jermyn Street, he was ready to grant her anything.

Lady Rockminster, meanwhile, played the role of dignified patient, complimenting him on his skill and expressing interest in consulting him. “What are your symptoms?” she asked. “Should I see my usual doctor? Mr. Jones is out of town?”

He would have treated her with the finest remedies—Spir: Ammon: Aromat: with a touch of Spir: Menth: Pip: and orange-flower water. He wrote home raving about her, told Samuel all about her, and even suggested taking her to the theatre.

Next day, at twelve, Mr. Huxter arrived at Jermyn Street. Lady Rockminster was still in bed, but Pen and Laura waited.

She’d had a comfortable night. How had he spent his evening? At the theatre? With his son? “A capital piece,” she said. “Mrs. O’Leary sang beautifully. And young Huxter—such a good fellow. Liked by all. An honour to his profession.”

“He ought to practise in the country,” Arthur said. “He ought to marry—others are doing so. Settle down.”

“Exactly what her Ladyship said yesterday,” Huxter replied. “Sam should marry.”

“The town is full of temptations,” Pen added.

The old man thought of Mrs. O’Leary.

“There’s no better safeguard than early marriage with an honest, affectionate creature.”

“Indeed,” said Miss Bell.

“And love is better than money, isn’t it?”

“Indeed it is,” Miss Bell said.

“I agree with so fair an authority,” the old man bowed.

Then Pen leaned in. “Suppose I had news to tell you.”

“God bless my soul, Mr. Pendennis! What do you mean?”

“Suppose I told you that a young man—carried away by an irresistible passion for an admirable, virtuous young woman—whom everyone falls in love with—consulted reason and heart, and married. Suppose I told you that man is my friend. That the Countess Dowager of Rockminster is deeply interested in him—and you can imagine what a young man can achieve with THAT family behind him. Suppose I told you that you know him—that he’s here—that he is——”

“Sam married! God bless my soul, you don’t mean that!”

“And to such a nice creature, dear Mr. Huxter.”

“Her Ladyship is charmed with her,” Pen said—lying for the first time in this entire story.

“Married? The rascal!” the old man thought.

“They will do it, sir,” Pen said, and opened the door.

Out stepped Mr. and Mrs. Samuel Huxter. Both knelt before the old man.

Fanny, kneeling, somehow found favour in his sight. There must have been something about her—despite Laura’s opinion.

“Will never do so again, sir,” Sam said.

“Get up, sir,” the old man said.

They rose. Fanny crept closer, tears and laughter mingling. And somehow, Mr. Huxter found himself kissing her cheek.

“What’s your name, my dear?” he asked.

“Fanny, papa,” she said.

And so it was.
