Before the first ray of dawn pierced the gloom, Gerard rose, his spirit hardened by the terror of the night. He roused Martin, the old soldier, who stared at him in bewilderment. "Did you not slip out by the window?" Martin asked, incredulous. "I thought you vanished into the dark."

Gerard, however, spoke of necessity. "We must flee," he urged, "but not blindly. There is but one safe path. I must guide you through the Great Forest to a bridle-road I know. From there, a hostelry awaits a swift horse, and within a day's gallop, we shall be free of Holland." Before the villagers stirred from their slumber, they had to move.

Their home lay a mere half-furlong from the timberline. Martin drew his bow, fletching three arrows, though it was already Thursday—a day marked for departure. For Gerard, Peter offered only a sturdy oak staff, a weapon of simple wood for a journey fraught with danger. Margaret, practical and quick, pinned up her kirtle and farthingale against the wet roads. Peter walked them to the edge of his garden, his face etched with a gravity rarely seen, and bestowed his blessing upon the young man.

As the sun crested the eastern horizon, bathing the stony fields in pale gold, they pushed toward the wood. Halfway across, Margaret froze. Her eyes darted backward, then widened in horror. With a strangled cry, she turned and sprinted toward the tree line, her screams cutting through the morning air. Behind them, the heavy tread of Ghysbrecht and his men pounded relentlessly.

To resist would have been suicide. Martin and Gerard followed Margaret's lead. Though the pursuers closed the gap, Martin's voice rang out, a beacon of desperate hope: "Only win the wood! Only win the wood!"

With their fresh start, the fugitives surged ahead, their hearts leaping. To them, the towering oaks stretched like benevolent guardians, their canopy weaving a screen against the world behind. Yet, a shadow emerged from the periphery. The fiery old burgomaster, Ghysbrecht, flung himself onto his mule, spurring it into a wild gallop. He did not merely chase his men; he aimed to cut off the fugitives entirely. Galloping in a wide semicircle, the burgher positioned himself squarely between Gerard and the open space, forcing the young man into a corner.

Margaret screamed, clutching at Gerard in a futile attempt to shield him, but he brushed her hands away with rough urgency. In his feverish state, Ghysbrecht forgot the primal truth of the hunt: that prey often turns upon the hunter, and hatred breeds a ferocity that transcends reason. Two men who hated one another could find a motive to kill.

Instead of dodging as Ghysbrecht anticipated, Gerard launched himself forward, a savage, exulting cry tearing from his throat. He swung the oak staff with every ounce of his soul, striking the burgomaster's face with a bone-shattering crash. Ghysbrecht tumbled beneath his mount, beaten by the beast's hooves, his face streaming with blood, his collar spattered red.

In an instant, the trio dove into the treeline. The yells of dismay and vengeful fury erupting from Ghysbrecht's men signaled that the game had shifted to a race for survival.

"Why run?" Gerard gasped, chest heaving, shaking the bloody staff. "You have your bow, and I have this!"

"Boy!" Martin roared, his voice raw. "The gallows! Follow me!" He plunged deeper into the woods. Soon, the baying of hounds echoed through the trees, a chorus of hunters closing in. Margaret moaned, her breath coming in ragged gasps, while Gerard gritted his teeth, gripping his staff until his knuckles turned white. They reached a dense thicket of hazel, where Martin shoved aside the young saplings as if they were dry stalks of grain.

Within fifty paces, four blind paths converged. Martin chose one, signaling them to bend low. They moved as ghosts, gliding along twisting trails that seemed to loop back on themselves, until finally, they entered a thick pine grove. Here, the ground was hard, the air stifling, and the young firs grew so close together that visibility dropped to mere yards.

After some distance, Martin halted. Having learned in war to separate the sensation of danger from the reality of it, he calmly unpacked a piece of bread and a slice of ham from his wallet, beginning to eat. The younger pair stared at him in stunned disbelief.

"All Sevenbergen could not find you now," Martin said, chewing slowly. "You will lose your purse, Gerard, long before you reach Italy. Is that the way to carry a purse?"

Gerard looked down. Attached to his wallet's buckle and strap was a large, triangular purse, entangled by chains. "This is not mine," he whispered, trying to free it. But the straps had tightened around it during the flight; it was impossible to remove without cutting. "It seems loath to leave me," Gerard murmured, drawing his knife to sever the bonds.

Upon examining the contents, Gerard found silver coins of various sizes, but the bag was swollen with crumpled brown paper. A sudden realization struck him. "It must belong to that old thief. See how it is stuffed with paper to fool the world!"

The mystery solved itself quickly. The purse had hung at Ghysbrecht's saddlebow. As Gerard charged his enemy, he had unconsciously torn it away, felling the burgomaster and stripping him in a single, fatal motion.

Gerard beamed with delight at his accidental triumph, but Margaret's face remained troubled. "Throw it away, Gerard, or let Martin take it back. They will call you a thief already. I cannot bear the stain."

"Throw it away? Give it back?" Gerard recoiled. "Not a stiver! This is spoil won lawfully in battle from an enemy. Is it not, Martin?"

"Why, of course," Martin agreed easily. "Send him back the brown paper, but keep the purse or the coin—it is a sin to return it."

"Oh, Gerard!" Margaret pleaded, tears welling. "You are going to a distant land. We need the goodwill of Heaven. How can we hope for grace if we take what is not ours?"

But Gerard saw the world through a lens of divine providence. "It is Heaven that gives it to me by a miracle, and I shall cherish it accordingly," he declared, echoing the scriptural tale of Israel spoiling Egypt. "Thus, the favored people were blessed."

"Take your own way," Margaret yielded humbly, lowering her gaze. "You are wiser than I. You are my husband..." Her voice trailed off, soft and submissive.

"These humble words," Martin noted internally, "from a woman who usually holds the whip-hand... they surprise me."

Gerard kissed her gently, grateful for her yielding nature, and they pressed onward, hand in hand, led by Martin into the vast, silent depths of the forest. As they ventured further, the sense of security grew absolute. Townsmen dared not venture so far into the trackless wilds.

Yet, impetuous spirits repent quickly. As soon as the immediate threat faded, Gerard's conscience pricked him. "Martin, would I had not struck quite so hard."

"Whom? Let that pass; he was cheap served."

"I saw his grey hairs as my stick fell," Gerard confessed, trembling. "I doubt I shall see them again."

Martin grunted with disdain. "Who spares a badger for his age? The greyer your enemy, the older he is; the older, the craftier. And the craftier, the better for a little killing."

"Killing? Killing, Martin? Speak not of killing!" Gerard shuddered, the word seeming to haunt him.

"I am much mistaken if you haven't killed," Martin said cheerfully.

"Now Heaven forbid!"

"The old vagabond's skull cracked like a walnut. Aha!" Martin grinned ruthlessly. "He rolled off his mule like a stone shot from a cart. I thought to myself, 'There is one wiped out.'"

Gerard fell to his knees, begging for mercy for his enemy's soul.

Martin's patience snapped. "Here's mummery! You, who set yourself up for learning, do you not know that a wise man never strikes unless to kill? What is this fuss over killing an old man? If it were a youth, full of wine, women, and life's pleasures, I might hesitate. But an old fellow at the edge of the grave? Why not shove him in? He goes today or tomorrow, and what better place for a greybeard? If I ever live to be so ancient as Ghysbrecht, walking on a mule's legs with a back like this—" he tapped his bow "—and a string like this, I'd thank and bless any young fellow who knocked me on the head, just as you did that shopkeeper. May his memory be cursed!"

"Oh, *culpa mea*! *Culpa mea*!" Gerard cried, smiting his breast in penitence.

"Look there!" Martin scoffed at Margaret. "He is a priest at heart even now—and when he isn't angry, St. Paul, what a milksop!"
