Arthur Pendennis was about sixteen when his reign truly began. To judge by the artist commissioned to illustrate this biography—a man whose likeness of my friend is, frankly, a disaster—one might assume he possessed nothing but flaws. Yet, in reality, he had what his peers called a "dumpy" figure, while his mother insisted on calling it "neat." His hair was a healthy brown that shimmered like gold in the sun; his face was round, rosy, and speckled with freckles, radiating a perpetual good humor. When nature finally granted him the whiskers he so ardently desired, they proved to be a distinctly reddish hue. Though not conventionally beautiful, he possessed a frank, open countenance and a merry laugh that lit up his honest blue eyes. No wonder Mrs. Pendennis considered him the pride of the entire county.

Between the ages of sixteen and eighteen, he grew from five feet six to five feet eight inches, at which majestic height he paused. His mother, however, remained baffled. How could any man possibly grow three inches taller than his father, Mr. Pendennis?

One thing was certain: Arthur never returned to school. The rigid discipline of the establishment simply did not suit him; he preferred the comfort of home. The issue was debated fiercely, with his uncle advocating for a return to Greyfriars. The Doctor argued passionately that a thorough knowledge of a Greek play was essential for Arthur's future success. However, Pen adroitly hinted to his mother about the dangers of Greyfriars—the wild fellows, the rowdy atmosphere. Taking the alarm immediately, the timid soul of the mother relented, allowing him to remain at home.

Soon after, Pen's uncle offered to use his influence with His Royal Highness, the Commander-in-Chief, who was known for his kindness toward the family. The proposal was to secure a commission in the Foot Guards for Pen. His heart leaped with joy. He recalled a visit to St. James's Park, where he had seen Tom Ricketts, a fourth-former notorious for wearing jackets and trousers so comically tight that older boys treated him as a living target or 'cockshy.' Pen remembered seeing this same Ricketts, now transformed, arrayed in crimson and gold, staggering beneath a massive bear-skin cap and the heavy colors of the regiment. Tom had recognized Pen, offering a patronizing nod. This was the same wretch Pen had once struck over the back with a hockey-stick. Now, here he was, center stage amidst bayonets, crossbelts, and scarlet uniforms, rallying around the national flag while the band blared trumpets and cymbals. Surrounded by immense warriors sporting chin-tufts and Waterloo medals, Tom spoke familiarly to giants. What would not Pen have given to wear such epaulettes and join such a glorious service?

But Helen Pendennis, upon hearing this proposal, donned a face of pure terror. "I do not quarrel with those who think differently," she declared, "but in my opinion, a Christian has no right to make the army a profession. Mr. Pendennis never, never would have permitted his son to be a soldier. Finally, I should be exceedingly unhappy if you entertained such thoughts." Now, Pen would sooner cut off his nose and ears than deliberately, and with premeditated malice, make his mother unhappy. Being of such a generous disposition that he would give anything to anyone, he instantly surrendered his vision of red coats and epaulettes, trading his ardor for military glory to his mother.

She deemed him the noblest creature in the world. Meanwhile, Major Pendennis, upon learning that the commission offer had been acknowledged and refused, replied with a curt and somewhat angry letter to the widow, concluding that his nephew was merely a "spooney."

Pen was content enough, however, when he displayed his talents during the Christmas hunt, when the Major descended to Fairoaks as usual. Pen rode a magnificent mare with uncommon pluck and grace. He cleared his fences with great coolness and judgment, devoid of unnecessary bravado. He wrote boastful letters to his schoolmates about his top-boots and his exploits across the countryside. He began to dream seriously of a scarlet coat; his mother admitted it would look remarkably well on him, though she spent hours in anguish during his absences, daily expecting to find him brought home on a stretcher.

Amidst these amusements, it must be noted that Pen did not neglect his studies entirely. He possessed a natural appetite for reading every conceivable book outside his school curriculum. Only when forced to drink from the waters of formal knowledge did he refuse to swallow. At home, he devoured everything from *Inchbald's Theatre* to *White's Farriery*, ransacking neighboring bookcases. At Clavering, he discovered an old stockpile of French novels, which he consumed with vigor. He would perch for hours atop the highest step of Doctor Portman's library, a folio on his knees, whether it was *Hakluyt's Travels*, *Hobbes's Leviathan*, *Augustine's Opera*, or *Chaucer's Poems*.

Pen and the Vicar became fast friends. From his Reverence, Pen learned that honest appreciation for port wine which characterized his adult life. As for the dear Mrs. Portman, who was not in the least jealous despite her husband's avowed love for Mrs. Pendennis—which he proclaimed made her the finest lady in the county—her sole grief was that her daughter, Minny, was already too old for Pen. Miss Myra Portman was only two years younger than Pen's mother and weighed as much as both Pen and Mrs. Pendennis combined.

Are these details insipid? Look back, good friend, to your own youth and recall the truth. I like to imagine a well-nurtured boy: brave, gentle, warm-hearted, loving, facing the world with kind, honest eyes. What vibrant colors the world wore then, and how purely one enjoyed it! A man possesses few years of such time, often unaware of their value while they last. It is only when they are long past that he remembers how dear and happy they were.

To prevent Mr. Pen from succumbing to the idleness predicted by his friend, the Doctor of the Cistercians, Mr. Smirke, the vicar's curate, was hired at a liberal salary to ride or walk over from Clavering, spending several hours daily with the young gentleman. Smirke was a man perfectly faultless at a tea table, wore a curl on his fair forehead, and tied his neckcloth with a melancholy grace. He was a decent scholar and mathematician, teaching Pen as much as the lad was willing to learn, which was not much. Pen quickly measured his tutor, finding him ridiculous. When Smirke rode into the courtyard on his pony, he turned his toes out absurdly and left such a gaping space between his knees and the saddle that no lad with a sense of humor could respect his equestrian skills. Pen nearly terrified him to death by mounting him onto his mare, Rebecca (named after Pen's favorite heroine, the daughter of Isaac of York), and taking him for a ride over a common where the county fox-hounds, led by the staunch old sportsman Mr. Hardhead of Dumplingbeare, happened to appear. Smirke, astride Pen's mare, astounded the hounds and disgusted the huntsman, laming one of the dogs by persisting in riding among the pack and earning a speech from the huntsman more energetic than any oration he had heard since leaving the bargemen on the banks of the Thames.

Smirke confided his Latin and English poems to his pupil and presented Mrs. Pendennis with a volume of the latter, printed at Clapham, his hometown. Together, the two read the ancient poets at a pleasant, rapid pace, vastly different from the slow, methodical grubbing employed by the Cistercian monks, who scented out every word and dug up every root. Pen never liked to halt; instead, he made his tutor construe whenever he stumbled, galloping through the *Iliad*, the *Odyssey*, the tragedians, and the charmingly wicked Aristophanes (whom Pen vowed was the greatest poet of all). So swift was his pace that although he covered considerable ground in the ancient classics, he eventually forgot it entirely, retaining only a vague memory akin to a Member of Parliament keeping two or three quotations handy, or a reviewer hinting at a bit of Greek for decency's sake. We humans are the most prosaic creatures, yet the most faithful; with curious reverence, we transmit from generation to generation the superstition of what constitutes a gentleman's education.

Beyond the ancients, Pen read English literature with great gusto. Smirke sighed and shook his head sadly regarding Byron and Moore. But Pen was a sworn fire-worshipper and a Corsair; he had them by heart. He would take little Laura into the window and recite, "Zuleika, I am not thy brother," in tones so tragic that the solemn little maid would open her wide eyes even further. She would sit, sewing at Mrs. Pendennis's knee until bedtime, listening to Pen read aloud to her night after night without comprehending a single word.
