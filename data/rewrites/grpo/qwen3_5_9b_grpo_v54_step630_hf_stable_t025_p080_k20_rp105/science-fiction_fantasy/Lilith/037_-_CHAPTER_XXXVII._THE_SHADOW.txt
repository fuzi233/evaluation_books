The low hum of delight that rippled through my companions snapped me back to awareness; they had spotted their kin in the distance. The two riders atop Lona's horse spurred forward to join the gathering, only to be met by a wavering shout that instantly dissolved into silence. As we closed the gap, the sound of their weeping reached us, rising like the breaking of tiny, desperate billows.

When I stepped into the circle, the truth of their plight became undeniable. Something terrible had struck them, etching a haggard, hollow terror onto their childish faces that no ordinary grief could produce. Several of them moved slowly toward me, arms outstretched to receive my burden. I yielded it to them. The tender, hopeless smile with which they accepted her made my own heart swell with pity, drowning my personal desolation in theirs. Their sobs over their mother-queen were futile; their attempts to coax even a flicker of recognition from her were in vain. They kissed her, fondled her, and carried her away, but she remained stone-cold. Two bore her arms, stroking them gently; as many as could reach placed their limbs beneath her body; the rest crowded close, a wall of small, grieving forms. They laid her down upon a patch of thick, soft grass, and there, surrounded by the Little Ones, the silence of her death settled over them.

Beyond the weeping crowd stood the elephants, and I stood near them, peering over the sea of small heads to gaze at Lona. Those nearest me caught sight of the princess and recoiled, trembling. Odu was the first to break the silence.

"I have seen that woman before!" he whispered to the boy beside him. "It was she who fought the white leopardess the night they woke us with their screaming!"

"Silly!" his companion retorted. "That was just a wild beast with spots!"

"Look at her eyes!" Odu insisted, his voice tight. "I know she is a bad giantess, but she is a wild beast all the same. I know she is the spotted one!"

The other boy took a step closer; Odu yanked him back with a sharp tug.

"Don't look at her!" he cried, shrinking away yet drawn by a hate-filled longing in her unseeing eyes. "She would eat you up in a moment! It was HER shadow! She is the wicked princess!"

"That cannot be! They said she was beautiful!"

"Indeed it is the princess!" I interposed, my voice steady. "Wickedness has made her ugly!"

She heard me, and the look that passed across her face was a storm of recognition and rage.

"It was very wrong of me to run away!" Odu murmured thoughtfully.

"What made you run away?" I asked. "I expected to find you where I left you!"

He hesitated.

"I don't know what made me run," another child admitted. "I was frightened!"

"It was a man that came down the hill from the palace," a third offered.

"How did he frighten you?"

"I don't know."

"He wasn't a man," Odu corrected, his eyes wide. "He was a shadow; he had no thickness to him!"

"Tell me more about him."

"He came down the hill very black, walking like a bad giant, but spread flat. He was nothing but blackness. We were frightened the moment we saw him, but we didn't run; we stood and watched. He came on as if he would walk right over us. But before he reached us, he began to spread and spread, growing bigger and bigger, until he was so vast he vanished from our sight entirely. Then... then he was upon us!"

"What do you mean by that?"

"He was all black through between us, and we couldn't see one another. And then he was inside us."

"How did you know he was inside you?"

"He did me quite differently. I felt like bad. I was not Odu anymore—not the Odu I knew. I wanted to tear Sozo to pieces—not really, but like!"

He turned and hugged Sozo tightly.

"It wasn't me, Sozo," he sobbed. "Really, deep down, it was Odu, loving you always! And Odu came up, and knocked Naughty away. I grew sick, thinking I must kill myself to get out of the black. Then came a horrible laugh that had heard my thoughts, and it set the air trembling around me. And then I suppose I ran away, but I didn't know I had run until I found myself moving, fast as I could, and all the rest running too. I would have stopped, but I never thought of it until I was out of the gate and among the grass. Then I knew I had fled from a shadow that wanted to be me and wasn't, and that I was the Odu who loved Sozo. It was the shadow that got into me, hating him from within; it was not my own self! And now I know I ought not to have run! But indeed I didn't quite know what I was doing until it was done! My legs did it, I think: they grew frightened, forgot me, and ran away! Naughty legs! There! And there!"

With a kick to each of his "naughty" legs, Odu finished his tale.

"What became of the shadow?" I asked.

"I do not know," he answered. "I suppose he went home into the night where there is no moon."

My mind wandered to where Lona had gone. Dropping to the grass, I took the dead princess into my lap and whispered into her ear, "Where are you, Lona? I love you!" Her lips gave no answer. I kissed them, feeling them not quite cold, laid her body down once more, and appointed a guard to watch over her. Rising, I prepared to ensure the safety of Lona's people throughout the coming night.

Before the sun dipped below the horizon, I had established a watch over the princess outside the camp, placing sentinels around her. Intending to patrol the perimeter myself all night, I ordered the rest of the army to sleep. They threw themselves onto the grass and were asleep in an instant.

When the moon rose, I caught a glimpse of something white; it was the leopardess. She swept silently around the sleeping camp, passing three times between the princess and the Little Ones. Seeing this, I made the watch lie down with the others and stretched myself beside the still form of Lona.
