If my Readers have followed me with any attention through the winding, multi-generational chronicles of our world, they will not be surprised to hear that modern life in Flatland is an aesthetically starved existence. I do not mean we lack the cyclical tumults of history—the factions and conspiracies that simmer like a perpetual, bitter broth across the centuries. Nor do I deny that our relentless mathematical conjectures offer a certain utilitarian sustenance. But to a meticulous historian of the senses, our current era is utterly bereft of flavor. We are trapped in an environment of sterile, industrial scraping, where all landscapes, portraits, and domestic still-lifes are reduced to a single, monotonous line. We consume degrees of brightness and obscurity as though they were mere fuel, our daily routines devoid of any rich, sensory marination. 

It was not always such a barren table. If the agrarian elders of our most ancient traditions speak the truth, there was a span of half a dozen centuries where a transient, mythic splendor infused the everyday domestic routines of our ancestors. A certain private individual—a Pentagon by the name of Chromatistes, whom history remembers less as a geometrician and more as a passionate, pioneering chef of the visual realm—unearthed the raw constituents of the simpler colors. Foraging minerals and clays from the earth, he macerated and steeped these elements, developing a rudimentary recipe for painting. He began by seasoning his own house, then his slaves, his Father, his Sons, and Grandsons, and finally himself, treating the flat geometry of his body like a canvas ready to be glazed with vibrant, aromatic hues.

The profound sustenance of this discovery immediately commended itself to the populace. Wherever Chromatistes turned his richly spiced, variegated frame, he offered a banquet for the senses. Moreover, his innovation fundamentally healed the psychological well-being of his community by transforming the acoustic ecology of the neighborhood. No longer did anyone need to physically "feel" him, grinding their sharp angles against him in a jarring, abrasive friction. No one mistook his front for his back. Best of all, his voice was saved the labor of that exhausting, continuous shouting by which we colorless Squares and Pentagons are today forced to proclaim our individuality. In our modern age, moving through a crowd of ignorant Isosceles requires a deafening, industrial noise pollution—an endless, anxious cacophony of self-identification. But with color, the sonic environment grew peaceful. Chromatistes moved in a harmonious, visually resonant silence.

The fashion spread like the intoxicating aroma of roasted garlic through an agrarian village. Before a week had turned, every Square and Triangle in the district had copied the recipes of Chromatistes, partaking in the feast. A month or two found even the Dodecagons marinating in the innovation. Within a year, the habit had nourished all but the very highest of the Nobility. Over two generations, the practice rippled outward in circular pacing, until no one in all Flatland was left unseasoned, save for the Women and the Priests. 

Here, the dominant lexicon of our natural order erected a barrier. A robust complexity—a "many-sidedness"—was deemed the essential platter upon which these colors could be served. "Distinction of sides is intended by Nature to imply distinction of flavors and colors," ran the common proverb, a sophism that fed whole towns and converted them to the new culture. But to our Priests and Women, this agrarian wisdom was inherently untranslatable. The Women possessed only one side, and therefore, in the pedantic grammar of our realm, *no sides* to hold the pigment. The Priests—those High Circles who boasted of a singular, continuous circumference—argued that they, too, lacked the partitioned facets necessary for the feast. Unable to digest the so-called axiom of distinct sides, these two classes alone remained untouched by the mortar and pestle, starving themselves of paint while condemning the practice as immoral, licentious, and unscientific.

Yet, to look back through the lens of aesthetic history, the days of the Colour Revolt were the glorious, simmering childhood of Art in Flatland—a recipe that, tragically, was never allowed to ripen to its full, complex maturity. To live was to constantly taste the visual world. Even a humble domestic gathering was a rich delicacy to behold. The varied hues in a church or theatre presented an olfactory and gustatory landscape so intoxicating it often distracted our greatest teachers.

Most ravishing of all was the unspeakable, multi-course magnificence of a military review. Imagine a battle line of twenty thousand Isosceles suddenly facing about, exchanging the bitter, charred-black of their bases for the citrus-rind orange and crushed-plum purple of their acute angles. Picture the militia of Equilateral Triangles glazed in vivid cherry-red, salt-white, and blueberry; the Square artillerymen dusted in mauve, rich ultra-marine, saffron-gamboge, and burnt roasted umber, rapidly rotating near guns the vermilion color of raw, pounded spice. The dashing and flashing of five- and six-colored Pentagons and Hexagons careering across the field in their offices of surgeons and aides-de-camp created a sensory banquet so overwhelming that, according to mythic lore, an illustrious Circle dropped his marshal’s baton and royal crown, hungering only for the artist’s brush.

The profound tragedy of this lost era is most acutely felt in the erosion of its cultural memory, a slow language death that haunts our modern tongue. The sensuous development of those days birthed a native dialect steeped in the precise, physical sensations of color and light. The everyday utterances of common citizens were suffused with a richer tinge of thought, bubbling with idioms of pigment and shade that are fundamentally untranslatable to our modern, utilitarian minds. The dominant, sterile lexicon of today’s scientific Flatland has paved over that acoustic and visual poetry. We are indebted to that brief, colorful epoch for whatever rhythm and lyricism still survives in our speech, but the true, flavorful essence of their words—the subjective experience of living within a painted world—has evaporated from our pots, leaving us only the dry, flavorless residue of a forgotten feast.
