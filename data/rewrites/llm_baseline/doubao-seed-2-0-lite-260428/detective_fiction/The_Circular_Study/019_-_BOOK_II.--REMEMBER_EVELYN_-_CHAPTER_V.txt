The rest of this story can only be told in Thomas Adams’ own words, exactly as he spoke it under sworn oath to the assembled detectives after his arrest.

“I kept my promise. I brought my new young wife Eva to my brother Felix’s house, exactly at the hour we’d agreed. We walked from our nearby hotel, and the door was opened by that old deaf-mute servant I’d been warned about. When Eva caught sight of him, then the long, shadow-carpeted old hall stretching dark and silent ahead of us, she paled fast and threw me a confused, frightened look. I forced a smile to reassure her—though my own gut was twisting with a dread I could not name—and tucked her arm tight under mine, following the old man past the open drawing room where I’d expected us to stop, into a plain, windowless little back vestibule that made me frown. That was when Bartow, the servant, tossed aside a thick velvet portière and showed us into Felix’s study. It would have looked like something out of a fairy tale, all gilded lantern light and silk Oriental hangings, if Felix hadn’t stood dead center of the room, his personality so sharp and overpowering it swallowed every other detail of the space the second we stepped over the threshold.

Eva barely knew anything about my brother—nothing that would have prepared her for this tall, sharp-faced man with a gaze so steady it felt like a physical weight. She looked half awed, half scared, but stepped forward bravely when I introduced her, smiling that soft, sweet smile that had won me over from our first meeting. I’d thought Felix would soften at the sight of her. Maybe he did. His already pale face went the color of wax in the lantern’s yellow glow, the hand he’d lifted to greet her fell limp at his side, and he could barely stammer out a line of welcome. His eyes were glued to her face, though, and I could see the raw feeling in them—though Eva never guessed how much her whole future, every bit of her happiness, was balanced on the edge of that iron will of his.

I knew—or thought I knew—what that feeling was tied to. I threw Felix an imploring look, and said I had some private business to discuss with him, and asked if Eva could rest here for a few minutes while we talked.

He gave me a strange, searching look. Did he pick up on the rebellion in my tone, guess even then that I’d never go through with the oath he’d forced me to swear on our father’s deathbed? I’ll never know. All I saw was him stop fumbling with some small metal object on his desk, bow to me with a sarcastic, cold grace that made me feel small, lesser than him for the first time in our lives, and lead me to a tiny door I’d never even noticed before.

“Your wife will be more comfortable in here,” he said, slow, deliberate, like he was holding back a storm of excitement under a flat, calm surface. He swung the door open to show a small, plush, perfectly appointed bedroom. “Our talk won’t take long. She won’t get bored waiting.”

“Of course not,” Eva laughed, bright and easy, happy as only a new bride can be. Even so, she must have thought it odd—my behavior, his, the whole strange greeting, that my first act introducing my brand new wife to my only living brother was to lock her alone in a back bedroom.

“I’ll call you when we’re done—” I started to say, but I caught sight of her dimpled cheeks, the great cluster of roses she was holding at her belt, and I forgot to finish the sentence. The door clicked shut. I was alone with Felix.

He breathed out slow, like a man who’d been holding his breath the whole time Eva was in the room. He didn’t speak at first, just glanced slowly around the weird, mosque-like study, and nodded like he’d just confirmed everything was in place, though I had no idea what he was checking for. Then he walked over to the big round table at the center of the room that he’d been staring at like it held some uncanny magnetism.

“Come further over here,” he said. “It’s too stuffy by the door. We both need air. Besides—she shouldn’t hear what we’re about to say. She’ll know the worst soon enough. She seems gentle.”

“She’s an angel,” I burst out, but he cut me off with a sharp, imperious gesture.

“We won’t talk about *your wife*. Where is John Poindexter?” he asked.

“Back at the hotel, probably,” I said. “Maybe he already went home. I don’t care what he’s doing anymore, Felix. I’m never leaving Eva. I’d rather break that oath I swore, the one I took before I ever met her, before I realized how good, how innocent she is. That’s what I came here to tell you. Make this easy for me. You’ve loved, you’ve grieved. Let’s bury the past. Let’s—”

I’d been fool enough to think I could reason with him, that I could beg him out of the decades-long grudge he’d nursed. But the second the words left my mouth, I saw it: his face was set like cast bronze. I might falter, but he was firmer than ever, completely unmoved.

What did I have to fear, anyway? A fight? A bit of unpleasantness? Losing the fortune I’d already started to think of as mine? None of that mattered next to Eva. She was going to find out eventually about the old wrong her father had done to our family, anyway. We could start over poor. It would still be a life together. Anything was better than being separated from her.

Felix spoke, in a tone I never expected. “I saw this coming. The second you begged me to let you marry her, I knew this moment would arrive. I knew you’d go soft on me. I planned for it. That oath you swore? It’s going to be kept.”

The weight of his words crushed me. There was no fighting that power, not with ordinary strength. I glanced around the room, copying the slow look he’d taken a minute earlier. There was nothing obviously threatening in sight—but a cold, creeping dread settled in my bones anyway. Not for me. For Eva. We could hear her humming a bright, silly tune in the bedroom next door. I bit down on my panic, even though I could feel the eyes of Evelyn’s portrait on my back, the portrait of our dead sister that I’d never stopped seeing out of the corner of my eye since we walked in. Felix faced that portrait, but he didn’t look up at it. He waited for me to speak, and when I stammered too long, he repeated it, sharp as a knife: “That oath must be kept.”

“*Impossible*,” I roared, throwing every last scrap of caution I’d held onto to the wind. “I swore that oath when I was grieving our father, when all the weird, macabre stuff you’d filled that house with had my head turned. But I’ve spent four months living with Eva, four months of normal, happy life. I can’t force her into this tragedy. You can’t make me abandon my own wife for a stupid, wicked oath I took before I even knew she existed. If you think your money—”

“Money?” he laughed, full of contempt, like he couldn’t believe I’d even mentioned it. “I forgot all about the money. I wouldn’t trade her happiness for every cent I own. But *her life*? That’s a different story. Walk out on this oath, refuse to do what you promised, and John Poindexter is going to come looking for his daughter. And he’ll find her either dead on her honeymoon, or completely disgraced. Which do you think he’ll prefer? Tell me.”

I thought I was dreaming. That wasn’t Felix talking. That wasn’t my brother. I lunged for him, where he stood pressed up against the big round table, and I groaned more than yelled: “You wouldn’t hurt her! You can’t murder an innocent girl here, in your own house—”

“I won’t lay a finger on her. *You* will,” he said, perfectly calm. “You’re not going to want to watch her waste away, go mad, starve to death in here with you. I’m no raving madman. I’m just a man who keeps his oaths. And I’m good at building things. This whole house is proof of that. This study? The only exits are those high windows above the tapestries, and the door to the hall. The door you came through? Behind that curtain, there’s a 500-pound steel plate that slides across the entire opening the second I press this little button under my hand. Once it slides home, no man short of a team with crowbars and dynamite can pry it open. Not even me. I built the mechanism to shoot the plate forward, but there’s no way to pull it back. Bartow has already been told to lock every door, shutter every window, and leave the house for a trip that will keep him gone for two full weeks. By the time John Poindexter comes back here, we’ll all be long dead.”

“God protect us,” I gasped, frozen stiff with horror, staring at the button under his finger.

He kept explaining, like he was describing a new bookshelf he’d built. “Those windows? They’re hidden behind those carved goblin faces woven into the tapestry, right up under the ceiling. No one outside will see in, no one inside can get out. Do you doubt my mechanical skill? The electrical setup I wired for this? You shouldn’t. And you shouldn’t doubt that I will press this button if I have to. Should I press it now, brother?”

“NO!” I screamed, crazy with terror, because I knew better than anyone how stubborn, how unbreakable his will was. “Give me a minute to think. Tell me exactly what you’re asking me to do. What happens if I agree?”

He pulled his hand back from the button, that soft, pale hand I’d always admired for its delicate work on his inventions. He smiled. “I knew you wouldn’t be stupid. Life’s sweet, even for a hermit like me.”

“Shut up,” I snarled, brave enough now to risk his anger. “You’re not just killing Eva and me. You’re planning to die in here with us, aren’t you?”

“Of course I am,” he said, cold as ice. “What, do you think I could live after watching the two of you perish? I’m no assassin. I don’t make other people die for my grudges while I walk free.”

I stared at him, completely stunned. That level of devotion to a grudge, that willingness to die for it, was something I couldn’t wrap my head around. “Why? Why kill all three of us?”

A high, shrill voice echoed down from the hollow vault above our heads, and I went still, cold all over. “REMEMBER EVELYN!”

It wasn’t Felix. It wasn’t my own guilty conscience. It was muffled, strange, like it was coming from miles up in the dark ceiling. “What the hell is that?” I stammered. “Another one of your tricks?”

He smiled. I’d rather he’d screamed at me. “Not exactly. My pet starling. He just repeats what he’s heard echoing through this empty house for thirty years. I wrapped him up in cloth to muffle him for this meeting, but he broke free. That means I don’t have time to waste explaining. John Poindexter got a telegram ten minutes ago, sent in your name. It tells him you need him to come to this house immediately, that you have something critical to tell him. He’ll be here in half an hour, at most. And once Bartow locks the front door behind him, there’s no way in or out. I already wrote the note you’re going to sign. Look.”

He pushed a sheet of paper across the table at me. The words were written in his neat, slanted handwriting, left there before Eva and I even walked in the door. I read them slow:

“Sign that,” he said. “Leave it on this table for Poindexter to find when he walks in. He won’t believe a word I say if it’s not in your handwriting.”

The whole world flashed through my head in that second. All my love for Eva, all our plans for the future, every happy little moment we’d had over the last four months. There was no way I could sign that note. It was a blasphemy against every good, true thing I’d ever known. I crumpled the paper up and threw it right in his face.

“You’ve gone too far!” I yelled. “Better we all die right now than shame Eva like that. I refuse, Felix. I refuse!”

The whole room went dead quiet. His face twisted. His finger started to press down on the fatal button. I was about to lunge for him, to tackle him before he could trigger the plate, when I heard a soft footstep behind me. I spun around. Eva was standing in the doorway of the bedroom, staring at us, wide-eyed.

The yellow lantern light flared bright red, cast a warm flush over her pale cheeks that looked like the roses at her belt. She walked right up to us, steady and sweet as the day she told me she loved me.

“I heard you yelling,” she said. “It sounds like a nightmare, not real life. What is this, Thomas? What does this mean, Mr. —?”

“Cadwalader,” Felix said, his face not shifting an inch.

“Cadwalader?” she said. I could see her connect the dots. “I heard that name at my father’s house. That was Evelyn’s name, the Evelyn who —”

“Who you see painted on the wall behind you,” Felix finished. “My sister. Thomas’s sister. The girl your father destroyed. I’m not going to spare you the details, child. You were never supposed to be born, and now you get to choose. You can leave here in shame, never see your husband again, or we all three starve to death locked in this room. You have five minutes. Talk it over. But don’t step one foot left or right. Thomas knows what happens if you do.”

Five minutes. I felt my head swim. I couldn’t speak. Then Eva looked up at me, her eyes full of trust, and I burst out talking, explaining everything, the whole terrible story of what her father had done to Evelyn, the oath Felix had forced me to swear. She listened, half understanding from the look on my face more than my stammering words. When Felix lifted a hand to warn us we only had one minute left, she turned to him.

“You’re serious,” she said. “We either separate and spend the rest of our lives in shame, or we die here with you?”

Felix said, “That is correct. You have one minute.”

She shrank back, looked small and fragile for half a second — then something snapped in her. She looked right at me, like she was checking if I had the guts to fight my own brother, and I hesitated. I didn’t understand what she was about to do. She reached for that old Turkish dagger Felix kept sitting out on the table, and she drove it straight into his side, hard.

“We’re too young, too happy to die, too young to be torn apart!” she screamed.

Felix was so stunned he didn’t hit the button. His hand flew up to his wound, clutching at his chest instead of the trigger. Thirty years of carefully laid plans, every contingency accounted for, and one impulsive, desperate blow from a delicate young woman had wrecked all of it.

I didn’t care about the plans, though. I didn’t care that we were seconds away from being sealed in that tomb forever. All I saw was Felix, the man who had never once broken, still standing, held up only by the iron will he’d nursed for thirty years. He looked down at Eva, that strange, unnameable look on his face I’ll carry with me to my grave. Then he saw her go white and shiver, that soft, gentle girl I loved coming back to the surface, and he turned his eyes on me. A single tear rolled down his cheek, the first tear I ever saw him cry.

“Eva! Eva! I love Eva!” the starling screamed from the ceiling.

Felix smiled, faint, as the blood started to soak through his shirt. I realized then. He’d fallen in love with Eva too. That was the secret, the thing he’d never told anyone.

His legs gave out. He collapsed on the floor, gurgling his last words. “Take her away. I forgive you. Save her. Save her. She didn’t know I loved her.”

Eva stared at him, frozen. “I killed him,” she whispered, over and over. “I killed him.” Then her eyes landed on the small Catholic crucifix hanging on the wall behind us. “Bring it to him,” she gasped. “Let him hold it. It might give him peace.”

I ran to grab the cross, and Eva fainted, falling in a heap on the floor. Felix’s voice, faint, came again, from his deathbed: “Save her.” I understood then. The steel plate never had a timer. The mechanism would only trigger if he pressed the button. We were free. I grabbed Eva in my arms, laid the cross in Felix’s cold, dead hands, and ran out of the house, out onto the busy street, past crowds of people going about their day. I flagged down a carriage, got her in, and drove away as fast as the horses could go.

We never told anyone what happened, not even the detectives, until today. Don’t blame Eva for that silence. When she woke up days later, she wanted to turn herself in immediately, to take full responsibility for what she’d done. But I wouldn’t let her. I loved her too much to drag her name through the newspapers, to let the whole city call her a murderess. Her father John Poindexter agreed with me, too. He’s not a man who cares about anyone, not his dead daughter Evelyn, not his living Eva. He doesn’t feel remorse. He doesn’t feel anything. That’s why Felix’s grudge burned for thirty years, and that’s why none of this ever had to happen.”
