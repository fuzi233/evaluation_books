The next afternoon brought Dr. Donaldson to Marlborough Street, and with him the old, sharp-edged privacy that had lately seemed to soften within the Hale rooms. The drawing-room door closed; Margaret was left outside it as if she were again a child, while Dixon—keys, apron, and authority—passed in without so much as a glance.

Margaret would not sit. She went into her mother’s bedchamber—the little room that backed the drawing-room like a pantry behind a kitchen—and walked its narrow length, heel to toe, heel to toe, counting the boards without meaning to. The house had its usual London undertone: a far cart-rumble, a hawker’s call blurred by distance, the faint, ceaseless hiss of the city that never truly paused. Over it, she strained for the smallest domestic sound—her mother’s breath, the drag of a slipper, the sigh that might become a groan.

Once—surely—there was a moan. It threaded through the wall like steam through a cracked lid. Margaret stopped so abruptly that the blood seemed to stop with her; she held her breath and pressed her clenched hands against her own ribs, as if she could keep her heart quiet by force. Silence followed—too complete, too careful. Then the scrape of a chair-leg, voices raised into the courteous pitch of departure, the polite little disturbances that always came when someone had been told more than they wished to hear.

The drawing-room door opened. Margaret was out of her mother’s room before the latch had finished clicking.

“My father is from home, Dr. Donaldson,” she said. “He is with a pupil at this hour. Will you be so good as to come into his room—downstairs?”

Dixon made a sound like a disapproving cough, and half turned as if to interpose herself—housekeeper against daughter; keeper of the sick-room against an intruder. But Margaret, all at once, took up her place as if she had been born into it and had merely forgotten to claim it. There was something in her carriage—head lifted, voice steady, step decisive—that checked Dixon more effectually than argument. For an instant the very oddity of it—Margaret feeling herself look grand, Dixon looking scandalised—sent a flash of amusement through Margaret’s terror, like tasting sharp lemon in the middle of bitterness. It held her for the length of the staircase.

Then she was in her father’s study, and the business of breath and blood returned. The familiar smell of books—leather and dust and the faintest tang of ink—seemed suddenly too dry, too lifeless. It was a moment before she could make her tongue obey her.

“What is the matter with mamma?” she said at last. “You will oblige me by telling me the simple truth.”

Dr. Donaldson had taken his place with the ease of a man accustomed to sorrowful parlours, accustomed to being made both confessor and enemy. He looked at her, measured her, and Margaret felt the measurement as a kind of pressure.

“My dear young lady,” he began, in the tone that was meant to soothe, “your mother seems to have a most attentive and efficient servant—more like her friend—”

“I am her daughter, sir.”

A pause—his eyes narrowing a fraction, as if he had expected this.

“But when I tell you she expressly desired you might not be told—”

“I am not good or patient enough to submit to the prohibition,” Margaret answered; and though her voice shook, it did not waver. “Besides, I am sure you are too wise—too experienced—to have promised to keep the secret.”

His mouth twitched, half smile and half sorrow. “There you are right. I did not promise. In fact, I fear the secret will be known soon enough without my revealing it.”

Margaret’s hands—so still—tightened upon each other. She did not sit down. She did not plead. She waited with the stillness of someone who will be told whether she begs or not.

He spoke at last—two short sentences, lowered as if the words themselves were indecently loud. He watched her while he spoke. The pupils of her eyes widened until they seemed to swallow all colour; the pale skin of her cheeks turned livid, as if the life had been drained in one sudden draught. When he stopped, he did not at once speak again. He waited for her breath to return.

She drew it in with effort, as if her lungs had become too small for London air. “I thank you most truly, sir,” she said. “That dread has haunted me for many weeks. It is a true, real agony. My poor, poor mother!”

Her lips began to tremble; tears came, not in a storm but in a brief, hot relief, and Dr. Donaldson—who had seen women collapse and scream and rage—let them come, certain she would arrest them when she must. She wiped them away with the back of her hand, almost impatient with herself, and her need for particulars broke through.

“Will there be much suffering?”

He shook his head. “That we cannot tell. It depends on constitution—on a thousand things. But the late discoveries of medical science have given us large power of alleviation.”

“My father,” Margaret said, and the words made her tremble all over. “Oh—my father!”

“I do not know Mr. Hale well,” he replied slowly. “It is difficult to advise. But I should say—bear on for a little while with the knowledge you have forced me to give you so abruptly, until the fact has become in some degree familiar to you. Then you will be able, without too great an effort, to give what comfort you can to your father. Before then—my visits, which I shall repeat from time to time, though I fear I can do nothing but alleviate—a thousand little circumstances will occur to awaken his alarm, to deepen it. He will be the better prepared.”

He softened then, as if finding his way past professional caution. “Nay—my dear young lady—nay. I saw Mr. Thornton, and I honour your father for the sacrifice he has made, however mistaken I may believe him to be. Well—this once, if it will please you—my dear. Only remember: when I come again, I come as a friend. You must learn to look upon me as such; for seeing each other—getting to know each other—at such times as these is worth years of morning calls.”

Margaret could not answer; her throat closed on every word. But at parting she took his hand and wrung it with a strength that surprised him.

“That’s what I call a fine girl,” Dr. Donaldson thought, once he was seated in his carriage and had leisure to look down at his ringed fingers, slightly sore from her grip. “Who would have thought that little hand could give such a squeeze? But the bones are well put together; that gives immense power. What a queen she is—head thrown back at first to force me into the truth; then bent forward, eager as a hawk, to catch it. Poor thing. I must see she does not overstrain herself. Though it’s astonishing what these thorough-bred creatures can do and suffer. That girl’s game to the backbone. Another, gone that deadly colour, would have fainted or fallen into hysterics. But she wouldn’t—not she. The very force of her will brought her round. Such a girl would win my heart, if I were thirty years younger. Too late now. Ah—here we are at the Archers’.”

And he was out, ready to be called upon as if no other household in all London had pain but this.

Margaret went back into her father’s study for one instant, as if she must gather herself like a cook gathering her wits before a spoiled dish—no time to lament, only time to begin again. The air in the room seemed suddenly stale; she could almost taste the soot in it, the city’s fine ash settling on every surface, even on the tongue. Noise outside thickened and thinned as a cart passed, iron-hooped wheels singing harshly over stone. She pressed her hands to her face.

“Oh, my God—my God—this is terrible,” she whispered. “How shall I bear it? Such a deadly disease—no hope! Oh, mamma, mamma! I wish I had never gone to Aunt Shaw’s, and been all those precious years away from you. Poor mamma—how much you must have borne—alone. Oh—pray, my God, that her sufferings may not be too acute, too dreadful. How shall I bear to see them? How can I bear papa’s agony? He must not be told yet—not all at once. It would kill him. But I won’t lose another moment of my own dear, precious mother.”

She ran upstairs.

Dixon was not in the room. Mrs. Hale lay back in an easy-chair, wrapped in a soft white shawl that had been folded about her with practised hands, and in a becoming cap set on with that particular care women used to take when illness had begun to steal their dignity. The exertion of the examination had left her with a faint flush; exhaustion gave her, for the moment, a kind of peace that was like the quiet after bread has been kneaded and set to rise—stillness that is not rest, but waiting.

Margaret was struck by the calm.

“Why, Margaret—how strange you look! What is the matter?” Mrs. Hale said; and then suspicion slid into her voice, as if it pained her to suspect. “You have not been seeing Dr. Donaldson and asking him questions—have you, child?”

Margaret did not answer; she could only look, her whole face confessing what her lips would not.

Mrs. Hale’s quiet turned to displeasure. “He would not surely break his word to me—and—”

“Oh yes, mamma, he did,” Margaret said quickly. “I made him. It was I—blame me.”

She knelt by the chair, caught her mother’s hand, and held it as if she could anchor her there. Mrs. Hale tried to draw it back; Margaret would not let go. She kissed the thin fingers again and again, and tears—hot, salt, shamefully abundant—fell on the skin and made it shine.

“Margaret, it was very wrong of you. You knew I did not wish you to know.”

But the struggle tired her; her hand remained in Margaret’s grasp, and after a moment her fingers pressed faintly back, as if, against her will, comfort had begun to creep in.

That small return of pressure gave Margaret courage. “Oh, mamma—let me be your nurse. I will learn anything Dixon can teach me. But you know I am your child, and I do think I have a right to do everything for you.”

“You don’t know what you are asking,” Mrs. Hale said, with a shudder, as if the word nurse carried with it images of pain that could not be spoken.

“Yes, I do,” Margaret answered—too urgently, too earnestly. “I know a great deal more than you are aware of. Let me be your nurse. Let me try, at any rate. No one has ever—shall ever—try so hard as I will do. It will be such a comfort, mamma.”

“My poor child! Well—you shall try. Do you know, Margaret—Dixon and I thought you would quite shrink from me if you knew—”

“Dixon thought!” Margaret’s lip curled before she could stop it; the bitter taste of Dixon’s judgement rose in her mouth like burnt sugar. “Dixon could not give me credit for enough true love—for as much as herself. She thought, I suppose, that I was one of those poor sickly women who like to lie on rose leaves and be fanned all day. Don’t let Dixon’s fancies come any more between you and me, mamma. Don’t—please!” The last word broke into entreaty.

“Don’t be angry with Dixon,” Mrs. Hale said anxiously, as if even now she must keep the household peace.

Margaret caught herself, ashamed. “No. I won’t. I will try and be humble—and learn her ways—if you will only let me do all I can for you. Let me be in the first place, mother—I am greedy of that. I used to fancy you would forget me while I was away at Aunt Shaw’s, and cry myself to sleep at nights with that notion in my head.”

“And I used to think,” Mrs. Hale answered, her voice softening into something like confession, “how will Margaret bear our make-shift poverty after the thorough comfort and luxury in Harley Street; till I have many a time been more ashamed of your seeing our contrivances at Helstone than of any stranger finding them out.”

“Oh, mamma—and I did so enjoy them!” Margaret said, and for a moment her smile came as naturally as if sorrow had not moved in at all. “They were so much more amusing than all the jog-trot Harley Street ways. The wardrobe shelf with handles that served as a supper-tray on grand occasions! And the old tea-chests stuffed and covered for ottomans! I think what you call make-shift contrivances at dear Helstone were a charming part of the life there.”

“I shall never see Helstone again, Margaret,” Mrs. Hale said; tears brimmed slowly, as if they had a long way to travel.

Margaret could not reply. The word Helstone rose in her mind with its remembered sounds—the clean hush of fields after rain, the birds’ calls that seemed to set one’s own breathing right, the church-bells that rang over green, not over stone. London, by contrast, was always grinding—always rattling, clanging, pushing its harsh music into every corner. She thought, with a pang that was almost anger, how the city’s ceaseless noise left no space for the gentle acoustics of a life remembered; as if grief itself required quiet to speak properly, and here one must shout it down.

Mrs. Hale went on, in the tone of one pronouncing sentence on herself. “While I was there, I was for ever wanting to leave it. Every place seemed pleasanter. And now I shall die far away from it. I am rightly punished.”

“You must not talk so,” Margaret said impatiently, more afraid of her mother’s resignation than of her mother’s pain. “He said you might live for years. Oh, mother! we will have you back at Helstone yet.”

“No—never! That I must take as a just penance.” She drew a quick breath, as if bracing herself, and then the word that had been kept, like a spice too strong to be brought out, leapt from her. “But, Margaret—Frederick!”

At the sound of that name her composure broke as if a cord had snapped. She cried out—not softly, not as a woman meant to cry in a sick-room, but loud, sharp, as if the cry itself were a pain.

“Frederick! Frederick!” It came again and again, each time more wild, more urgent. “Come to me. I am dying. Little first-born child—come to me once again!”

The room filled with her voice, and Margaret’s terror rose until it seemed to stop her hands from working. Mrs. Hale’s body shook with violent hysterics; her fine fingers clawed at the shawl as if it were choking her.

Margaret ran for Dixon.

Dixon came in on the high wind of resentment, as if called from a kitchen where something had boiled over. One look at Mrs. Hale’s agitation, and her eyes flashed at Margaret.

“There now!” she cried. “I told you! You’ve over-excited her—just because you would be meddling and prying—”

Margaret took the blame without a word, as if punishment could purchase calm. “Only help her,” she begged in her look and in the quick obedience of her hands. She fetched what Dixon demanded, held what Dixon bade her hold, moved when Dixon ordered her to move. She did not once defend herself. She only listened for the sound of her father’s step in the passage—dreading it as one dreads the sudden slam of a door in a quiet sick-room.

Between them they got Mrs. Hale into bed at last; the violent storm wore itself out into sobs, and the sobs into heavy sleep. Margaret sat by the bedside while the breathing deepened and steadied, watching each rise and fall of her mother’s chest as if counting it could keep it going. The city noise outside went on—wheels, voices, a distant hammering—but within the room there was, for a little while, the frail sanctuary of sleep.

At length Dixon beckoned her out, shutting the door with careful hands, and took her into the drawing-room. Her face was sour, as if she were about to do something distasteful, though her very distaste carried kindness. On the table stood a cup of coffee—fresh made, strong, the bitter scent rising at once, comforting and reproachful; Dixon had roasted it too far, perhaps, as anxious people do, so that it smelled of scorched beans and necessity. She stood over Margaret like a commander while Margaret drank.

“You shouldn’t have been so curious, Miss, and then you wouldn’t have needed to fret before your time,” Dixon said. “It would have come soon enough. And now, I suppose, you’ll tell master, and a pretty household I shall have of you!”

“No, Dixon,” Margaret answered, sorrowfully. “I will not tell papa. He could not bear it as I can.”

And as if to prove she bore it well, she burst into tears, sudden and helpless.

“Ay! I knew how it would be,” Dixon said, but her own voice broke into something thinner. “Now you’ll waken your mamma, just after she’s gone to sleep so quietly. Miss Margaret, my dear—I’ve had to keep it down this many a week; and though I don’t pretend I can love her as you do, yet I loved her better than any other man, woman, or child—no one but Master Frederick ever came near her in my mind.”

She wiped at her eyes with her apron, fierce with shame at her own softness, and went on as if speech were a dam holding back a flood.

“Ever since Lady Beresford’s maid first took me in to see her dressed out in white crape, and corn-ears, and scarlet poppies, and I ran a needle down into my finger and broke it in—such a fright I had!—and she tore up her worked pocket-handkerchief, after they’d cut it out, and came in to wet the bandages again with lotion when she returned from the ball—where she’d been the prettiest young lady of all—I’ve never loved any one like her. I little thought then I should live to see her brought so low. I don’t mean no reproach to nobody. Many a one calls you pretty and handsome, and what not. Even in this smoky place, enough to blind one’s eyes, the owls can see that. But you’ll never be like your mother for beauty—never; not if you live to be a hundred.”

“Mamma is very pretty still,” Margaret whispered. “Poor mamma!”

“Now don’t you set off again,” Dixon said hastily, and a whimper slipped through in spite of her. “Or I shall give way at last. You’ll never stand master’s coming home and questioning, at this rate. Go out and take a walk, and come in something like. Many’s the time I’ve longed to walk it off—the thought of what was the matter with her, and how it must all end.”

“Oh, Dixon!” Margaret cried, with the sudden generosity that sorrow brings. “How often I’ve been cross with you, not knowing what a terrible secret you had to bear!”

“Bless you, child!” Dixon said, rallying, glad to clutch at familiar pride. “I like to see you showing a bit of spirit. It’s the good old Beresford blood. Why, the last Sir John but two shot his steward down, there where he stood, for just telling him that he racked the tenants—and he’d racked the tenants till he could get no more money off ’em than he could get skin off a flint.”

“Well, Dixon,” Margaret said, through her tears, “I won’t shoot you. And I’ll try not to be cross again.”

“You never have,” Dixon replied quickly, as if to wipe out her own harshness. “If I’ve said it at times, it has always been to myself—just in private—by way of making a little agreeable conversation, for there’s no one here fit to talk to. And when you fire up you’re the very image of Master Frederick. I could find it in my heart to put you in a passion any day, just to see his stormy look coming like a great cloud over your face.”

Then, brisk again, she pointed Margaret to the door. “But now you go out, Miss. I’ll watch over Missus. And as for master—his books are company enough for him, if he should come in.”

“I will go,” Margaret said.

She lingered for a minute, hovering near Dixon as if she were afraid to step beyond the circle of lamplight and familiar walls. Then, suddenly, she bent and kissed Dixon—quick, grateful, unexpected—and went out at once, as if speed could outrun the thought of what waited upstairs.

“Bless her!” Dixon said, standing alone in the drawing-room. “She’s as sweet as a nut. There are three people I love: it’s Missus, Master Frederick, and her. Just them three. That’s all. The rest be hanged, for I don’t know what they’re in the world for. Master was born, I suppose, for to marry Missus. If I thought he loved her properly, I might get to love him in time. But he should ha’ made a deal more on her, and not been always reading, reading, thinking, thinking. See what it has brought him to. Many a one as never reads or thinks neither gets to be Rector, and Dean, and what not; and I daresay master might, if he’d just minded Missus and let the weary reading and thinking alone.”

She went to the window as the front door shut, the sound of it carrying up the stairwell like the closing of a lid.

“There she goes,” Dixon muttered, watching Margaret’s figure fall into the stream of London. “Poor young lady! her clothes look shabby to what they did when she came to Helstone a year ago. Then she hadn’t so much as a darned stocking or a cleaned pair of gloves in all her wardrobe. And now—and now—!”
