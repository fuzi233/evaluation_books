As Calder stepped into a carriage at Assouan, his heart heavy with the failure to find Harry Feversham, miles away in Hampshire, Lieutenant Sutch was traversing a high white road. The landscape stretched out beneath him, a vast common of heather and gorse, but Sutch's mind was far from the rolling fields. Like many who spend their lives in solitude, he had cultivated the habit of voicing his internal monologue. As he guided his pony slowly and reluctantly forward, the words escaped him again and again: "I foresaw there would be trouble. From the very beginning, I foresaw it."

The road dipped suddenly into a hollow, revealing a steep descent flanked by dense forests of pine leading down to a small railway station. The sight of the gleaming rails cutting straight into the afternoon sun, converging in the distance like a single line of destiny, unsettled Sutch further. He reined in his pony, staring with a furrowed brow at the red-tiled roof of the station.

"I promised Harry to say nothing," he murmured, finding a fragile comfort in the repetition. "I promised faithfully in the Criterion grill-room."

A distant whistle pierced the air, sharp and clear, breaking his gloom. He watched the white smoke of an approaching train stretch across the horizon like a ribbon.

"I wonder what brings him," he muttered, doubt coloring his voice. Then, steeling himself, he added, "Well, it's no use shirking." With a flick of his whip, he urged the pony down the hill, reaching the station just as the train pulled to a halt. Only two passengers disembarked: Colonel Durrance and his servant. They emerged immediately onto the road. Sutch hailed Durrance, who walked purposefully to the side of the trap.

"You received my telegram in time, then?" Durrance asked.

"Luckily it found me at home."

"I have brought a bag. May I trespass upon you for a night's lodging?"

"By all means," Sutch replied, though the stiffness in his tone betrayed the reluctance in his heart. Durrance, however, anticipated a cool reception; he had sent his telegram at the last possible moment, suspecting that an explicit address might have invited a refusal. His suspicion was well-founded. The telegram had merely announced a visit, omitting its purpose, yet its dispatch served as a warning that something grave had transpired regarding Ethne Eustace and Durrance. Sutch knew Durrance had come to renew his inquiries about Harry Feversham—questions Sutch was sworn to parry. Yet, as Durrance probed for the step of the trap with his raised foot, the reality of his blindness struck Sutch anew. Reaching out, he caught Durrance by the arm and helped him up. After all, he reasoned, it would not be difficult to deceive a blind man. Ethne herself had harbored the same thought, feeling a similar relief. The lieutenant was so relieved that a pang of pity surfaced within him.

"I was very sorry, Durrance, to hear of your bad luck," he said as they drove up the hill. "I know what it is to be suddenly stopped and put aside just when the world is smoothing itself out, though my wound in the leg is nothing compared to your blindness. I don't speak of compensations and patience; that is the chatter of the comfortable who have never suffered. We know that for a young, active man in a career where action is vital, there are no compensations if his path is abruptly cut short through no fault of his own."

"Through no fault of his," Durrance repeated slowly and thoughtfully. "I agree. It is only the man whose career ends through his own fault who receives compensation."

Sutch glanced sharply at his companion. Was Durrance referring to Harry Feversham? Did he possess enough knowledge to make such a reference, or was it mere coincidence that his words were so strikingly apt?

"Compensations of what kind?" Sutch asked, unease tightening his chest.

"The chance of knowing oneself, for one thing—the chief thing. He is halted, stopped in his career, perhaps disgraced." Sutch flinched at the word. "Yes, perhaps... disgraced," Durrance echoed. "Well, the shock of disgrace is, after all, his opportunity. Do you see? It is his opportunity to know himself at last. Until that moment, his life has been a sham and an illusion; the man he believed himself to be never existed. Now, at the end, he knows the truth. Once he knows it, he can begin to retrieve his disgrace. Oh, there are compensations for such a man. You and I know a case in point."

Sutch no longer doubted that Durrance was deliberately speaking of Harry Feversham. He possessed some knowledge, though Sutch could not fathom how he had acquired it. The information was not entirely accurate, and the inaccuracy did Harry a disservice. This was the primary reason Sutch did not feign ignorance regarding Durrance's allusion. Years had not dimmed his profound regard for Harry; he loved him with a woman's concentrated intensity and could not bear for his memory to be slighted.

"The case you and I know of is not quite in point," Sutch argued. "You are speaking of Harry Feversham."

"Who believed himself a coward and was not one. He commits the fault that stops his career, discovers his mistake, and sets himself to the work of retrieving his disgrace. Surely it is a case quite in point."

"Yes, I see," Sutch agreed. "There is another view—a wrong view, as I know—but I thought for a moment it was yours—that Harry fancied himself brave and was suddenly brought up short by discovering he was a coward. But how did you find out? No one knew the whole truth except myself."

"I am engaged to Miss Eustace," Durrance said.

"She did not know everything. She knew of the disgrace, but not of the determination to retrieve it."

"She knows now," Durrance replied sharply. "You are glad of that—very glad."

Sutch was unaware that any movement or exclamation had betrayed his joy. His face undoubtedly showed it, but Durrance could not see. Puzzled, Sutch did not deny the imputation.

"It is true," he said stoutly. "I am very glad that she knows. I can quite see that from your point of view it would be better if she did not. But I cannot help it. I am very glad."

Durrance laughed, not unpleasantly. "I like you the better for being glad," he said.

"But how does Miss Eustace know?" Sutch asked. "Who told her? I did not, and there is no one else who could."

"You are wrong. There is Captain Willoughby. He came to Devonshire six weeks ago. He brought with him a white feather, which he gave to Miss Eustace as proof that he withdrew his charge of cowardice against Harry Feversham."

Sutch stopped the pony in the middle of the road. He no longer concealed the joy this news inspired. In fact, he forgot Durrance's presence entirely. He sat silent and still, radiating a happiness he had never known in his life. He was an old man now, well into his sixties, an age when blood runs slow and pleasures are sober, gray things, and joy has lost its feverish edge. Yet, a buoyant gladness welled up in his heart, a sensation reserved only for youth. Five years ago, on the pier of Dover, he had watched a mail packet steam away into darkness and rain, praying that he might live to witness this great moment. He had lived, and it had come. His heart swelled with gratitude. It seemed to him that a great burst of sunlight had broken across the world, transforming it into a place of vibrant color and joy. Ever since the night he stood outside the War Office in Pall Mall, watching Harry Feversham touch his arm and speak his despair, Sutch had been oppressed by a sense of guilt. Harry was Muriel Feversham's son, and Sutch, for that reason alone, should have watched over him in his boyhood since his mother was gone, and fathered him in his youth since his father did not understand. But he had failed. He had failed in a sacred trust, imagining Muriel's eyes looking down from the sky with reproach. He had heard her voice in his dreams, gentle yet insistent: "Since I was dead, since I was taken away to where I could only see and not help, surely you might have helped. Just for my sake you might have helped—you whose work in the world was at an end." The long tale of his inactive years had stood as an accuser. Now, however, the guilt was lifted from his shoulders, removed by Harry Feversham's own act. The news was not entirely unexpected, but the lightness of spirit he felt revealed how much he had counted on its arrival.

"I knew," he exclaimed. "I knew he wouldn't fail. Oh, I am glad you came today, Colonel Durrance. It was partly my fault that Harry Feversham ever incurred that charge of cowardice. I could have spoken—there was an opportunity on one of the Crimean nights at Broad Place, and a word might have been of value—and I held my tongue. I have never ceased to blame myself. I am grateful for your news. You have the particulars? Captain Willoughby was in peril, and Harry came to his aid?"

"No, it was not exactly that."

"Tell me! Tell me!"

He feared to miss a word. Durrance recounted the story of the Gordon letters and their recovery by Feversham. It was all too brief for Lieutenant Sutch.

"Oh, but I am glad you came," he cried.

"You understand at all events," Durrance said, "that I have not come to repeat the questions I asked in the courtyard of my club. On the contrary, I am able to give you information."

Sutch spoke to the pony and drove on. He had said nothing that could reveal to Durrance his fear that renewing those questions was the object of the visit; he was slightly perplexed by the accuracy of Durrance's conjecture. But the great news he had listened to hindered him from dwelling on that perplexity.

"So Miss Eustace told you the story," Sutch said, "and showed you the feather?"

"No, indeed," Durrance replied. "She said not a word about it, she never showed me the feather, she even forbade Willoughby to hint of it, sending him away from Devonshire before I knew he had come. You are disappointed at that," he added quickly.

Sutch was startled. It was true he was disappointed; he was jealous of Durrance, wishing Harry Feversham to stand first in the girl's thoughts. It was for her sake that Harry had undertaken his difficult and perilous work. Sutch wished her to remember him as he remembered her. Therefore, he was disappointed that she did not immediately bring her news to Durrance and break off their engagement. It would be hard for Durrance, no doubt, but that could not be helped.

"Then how did you learn the story?" Sutch asked.

"Some one else told me. I was told that Willoughby had come, that he had brought a white feather, and that Ethne had taken it from him. Never mind by whom. That gave me a clue. I lay in wait for Willoughby in London. He is not very clever; he tried to obey Ethne's command of silence, but I managed to extract the information I wanted. The rest of the story I was able to piece together myself. Ethne now and then was off her guard. You are surprised that I was clever enough to find out the truth by the exercise of my own wits?" Durrance laughed.

Sutch jumped in his seat. It was mere chance, of course, that Durrance continually guessed with such singular accuracy; still, it was uncomfortable.

"I have said nothing which could in any way suggest that I was surprised," he said testily.

"That is quite true, but you are none the less surprised," Durrance continued. "I don't blame you. You could not know that it is only since I have been blind that I have begun to see. Shall I give you an instance? This is the first time I have ever come into this neighborhood or gotten out at your station. Well, I can tell you that you have driven me up a hill between forests of pines, and are now driving me across open country of heather."

Sutch turned quickly toward Durrance.

"The hill, of course, you would notice. But the pines?"

"The air was close. I knew there were trees. I guessed they were pines."

"And the open country?"

"The wind blows clear across it. There's a dry, stiff rustle besides. I have never heard quite that sound except when the wind blows across heather."

He turned the conversation back to Harry Feversham's disappearance and the cause of it. He made no mention, Sutch noted, of the fourth white feather that Ethne herself had added to the three. But the history of the three that had come by post to Ramelton he knew to the last letter.

"I was acquainted with the men who sent them," he said. "Trench, Castleton, Willoughby.
