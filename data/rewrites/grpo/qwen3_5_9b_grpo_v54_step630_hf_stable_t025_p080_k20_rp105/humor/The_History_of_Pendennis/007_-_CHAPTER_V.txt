Rebecca, the mare, did not break her stride. She thundered down the road to Baymouth, carrying Pendennis straight to the inn stables, and from there, he bolted for Mr. Foker's lodgings. These quarters sat precariously above a chemist's shop, a place where the stock of cigars and sodawater vanished faster than the rent money, thanks to the voracious appetites of Foker's young tenants.

Upon arriving, Pen found the place empty of its master. Instead, he encountered Mr. Spavin, Foker's chum and co-owner of the tandem that had recently driven into Chatteris. Spavin was smoking a pipe and teaching a small dog to perform tricks with a biscuit. The contrast was stark: Pen's face was flushed with the healthy red of a gallop, while Spavin's features were waxy, pale, and distinctly debauched.

"Who's that man?" Spavin muttered, eyeing Pen. "He looks fresh as a bean. His hand doesn't shake in the morning. I'd bet five to one he's got the constitution of a saint."

Foker was nowhere to be found. A disappointment washed over Pen. Spavin shrugged, admitting Foker was a creature of erratic habits, sometimes staying a day, sometimes a week. When Pen revealed his own identity via a calling card—something he was currently quite proud of—Spavin offered a fair tap of ale and dismissed him.

Left alone, Pen walked down to the beach, biting his nails as he stared at the vast, roaring sea. The blue waters foamed and crashed against the shore, indifferent to his turmoil. A tidal wave was surging within his own mind, and he possessed no power to check it. He threw stones into the surf, but the tide kept coming. Rage consumed him at Foker's absence. He *had* to see Foker.

"Suppose I go on the Chatteris road," Pen thought, mounting Rebecca. "Just to catch him."

He rode hard, passing the turn for the Clavering road without noticing the black tandem and red wheels that should have been there. At the turnpike, logic dictated he continue. He rode to the George Inn, where the hostler confirmed Foker was indeed present, having caused a "tremendous row" the night before involving drinking, singing, and a failed attempt to fight a postboy.

Pen was ushered into an apartment dominated by an immense feather bed. Mr. Harry Foker lay amidst the pillows, his sallow face obscured by red silk nightcaps and bolsters.

"Hullo!" Pen called out.

"Who goes there? Brother, speak quickly!" Foker's voice rang from the depths of the bedding. "Pendennis again? Is your mother aware of your absence? Did you sup with us last night? No stop—who supped with us last night, Stoopid?"

"There were three officers, sir, Mr. Bingley, and Mr. Costigan, sir," the servant, Stoopid, replied with grave seriousness.

"Ah, yes! The cup and merry jest went round. I wanted to fight a postboy. Did I thrash him, Stoopid?"

"No, sir. The fight didn't happen," Stoopid answered, arranging Foker's dressing case—a trunk gifted by a doting mother and filled with silverware and razors.

"Do it another day," Foker yawned, throwing his lean arms over his head. "No fight, but chanting. Bingley chanted, I chanted, the General chanted—Costigan, I mean. Did you ever hear him sing 'The Little Pig under the Bed,' Pen?"

"The man we met yesterday," Pen stammered, trembling. "The father of—"

"Of the Fotheringay! The very man. Ain't she a Venus, Pen?"

"Please sir, Mr. Costigan is in the sitting-room," Stoopid interrupted. "He says you asked him to breakfast. He's been here since eleven."

"How much is it now?" Foker asked.

"One, sir."

"What would the best of mothers say if she saw me in bed at this hour?" the young sluggard cried, bursting into a boyish laugh. "She sent me down with a grinder. She wants me to cultivate my neglected genus. He, be! I say, Pen, this isn't quite like seven o'clock school, is it, old boy?"

Pen fled to the sitting-room in great perturbation while Foker began his toilette.

The narrative of Mr. Foker is one of transformation. Born to a brewer and an earl, he had endured the harshest bullying at Cistercian School due to his trade, uncomely face, and gluttony. Released from that tyranny, he blossomed into the humorous, sarcastic, and brilliant Foker known to the world. Though a dunce by academic standards, he had become a dandy, arriving in his sitting-room scented and splendid.

In the room sat Captain Costigan, a man of questionable stability. His eyes were dim, his hat cocked askew, and his boots shabby, signaling that poverty had already claimed his extremities. He was spelling the newspaper with his lips, a rare feat for anyone.

As Pen entered, Costigan descended from the window seat, saluting with a broken glove and a jaunty twist of his chin. He spoke of Pen's introduction in the Cathedral Yard and his daughter's performance, claiming it reminded him of his glory days in the Hundred and Third. He produced an old snuffbox, presenting it with stately air.

Arthur Pendennis was flustered. This shabby-looking man was the father of the actress he had admired. Costigan, however, was perfumed with cigar smoke and spun tales of his past, boasting of his voice and his ability to charm generals. He confessed that his daughter, Miss Fotheringay, earned her living despite his pride, noting that her manager, Bingley, paid her merely two guineas a week.

Costigan's history was a blur of fact and fiction, a muzzy tale of bravery, drunkenness, and survival. Yet, he managed to extract two sovereigns from Pen for tickets to his daughter's benefit performance. Pen, possessing only two pounds, handed them over, terrified of offending the Captain's delicacy. Costigan scribbled an order for a box, slipped the gold into his waistcoat, and slapped his hand over the spot, warming his old sides.

"Faith, sir," Costigan grinned, "the bullion's scarcer with me than it used to be. I won six hundred of 'em in a single night in Gibraltar."

He regaled Pen with stories of claret, races, and the Duke of Kent until Foker arrived for breakfast. The Captain's spirits soared over deviled turkey and mutton chops, prating endlessly of his past splendor and a challenge sent to a Captain Shanty Clancy. He announced that sitting with such noble young fellows was the pride of an old soldier's existence. After a second glass of Curacoa, he began to cry, and the trio walked out, the Captain linking arms with both young men, winking at tradesmen's shops as if to say, "See the company I'm in—sure I'll pay you."

On the street, Costigan inquired about Foker's fortune. Pen, eager to please, revealed that Foker's father was a celebrated brewer and his mother Lady Agnes Milton. Costigan erupted in exaggerated praise of Foker's "native aristocracie," though he believed not a word of it.

Pen, amused and puzzled, listened to the Captain's prattle. The Captain, finding such an artless and honest listener, invited Pen to his humble abode to introduce him to his daughter. Pen was struck dumb with delight and shock. He was going to see her! She was the center of his universe.

They walked to Prior's Lane, a quiet street near the cathedral. Costigan lived in a low gabled house formerly occupied by a tailor. Two choir-boys, sweet-voiced and mischievous, greeted him at the door, jumping at his coat tails.

Inside, Pen followed the Captain up the creaking stairs, his knees trembling. He entered a room where a figure stood, waving as if making a curtsey.

"It is very fine weather," Miss Fotheringay said, her voice a deep, rich Irish melancholy.

"Very," Pen replied.

She looked even more striking off-stage. Her movements were naturally grand; she resembled a Muse contemplating or an Empress seated. Her hair was blue-black, her complexion dazzling, her eyes grey with long lashes, and her mouth a staring red that rivaled the finest geranium.

"And very warm," she continued.

The conversation flowed. She asked about the evening at the George, and he recounted the punch. She mentioned studying Ophelia for the twenty-fourth. Pen noted her natural Irish inflection, unaware that it was her native tongue. Costigan tapped his pocket, revealing Pen's sovereigns, and winked.

"My name is Pendennis," Pen blushed, declaring himself a lover of poetry with a feeling heart.

"Pendennis," she answered slowly, looking him full in the eyes. Her glance was clear, bright, and killing, shooting him through with pleasure.

"I never knew the name was so pretty before," Pen said.

"'Tis a very pretty name," Ophelia agreed. "Pentweazle's not a pretty name. Remember, papa, when we were on the Norwich Circuit, Young Pentweazle... His name was Harrington; that is, his real name was Potts..."

She spoke of her comrade Pentweazle, a man named Potts who played second old men and married a Mrs. Rancy. Costigan interjected that Pentweazle should have been Julia, or his name wasn't Jack Costigan. Emily modestly admitted she wasn't fit for the role until Bows taught her.

Costigan then revealed he had been a fencing-master in Dublin, where his friend Mr. Bows had discovered Emily's capabilities. "What have ye done since Bows went, Emily?" he asked.

"Sure, I've made a pie," Emily said simply, pronouncing it "Poy."

"If ye'll try it at four o'clock, sir, say the word," Costigan gallantly offered.

Pen, who had promised to be home by six, accepted the invitation with delight. He watched Ophelia arrange the glasses and smooth the cloth with a quiet grace that enchanted him. The "poy" arrived from the baker, and at four o'clock, Pen found himself at dinner with the greatest tragic actress in the world and her father. He ate a crust of her making, poured her a glass of beer, and watched her drink a glass of punch from the tumbler she mixed for her papa. She offered to mix one for him; it was prodigiously strong, intoxicating him more than any spirit.

During dinner, Pen tried to discuss poetry and her profession. He asked about Ophelia's madness and whether she loved Hamlet. "In love with such a little wretch as that stunted manager of a Bingley?" she bristled indignantly. Pen clarified he meant the character, not the manager. She admitted she didn't value Bingley. When Pen mentioned Kotzebue, the author of the play she performed, she asked who he was. "The author of the play in which she had been performing so admirably," Pen explained.

"She did not know that—the man's name at the beginning of the book was Thompson," she said. Pen laughed at her adorable simplicity, telling her of the author's melancholy fate and how Sand had killed him. For the first time, Miss Costigan heard of Kotzebue, and her sympathy sufficed for honest Pen.

The hour and a quarter passed too quickly. Pen left, riding Rebecca homeward.

"What was that he was talking about, the madness of Hamlet?" Emily asked her father later.

"'Deed then I don't know, Milly dear," Costigan answered. "We'll ask Bows when he comes."

"He's a nice, fair-spoken pretty young man," Emily said. "How many tickets did he take?"

"Faith, then, he took six, and gev me two guineas, Milly," Costigan replied. "I suppose them young chaps is not too flush of coin."

"He's full of book-learning," Emily continued, mentioning Kotzebue again. "Did ye ever hear such a thing? I'll ask Bows about it, papa, dear."

"A queer death, sure enough," Costigan ejaculated, changing the subject to the elegance of Pen's mare and the grand breakfast Foker had given them.

"He's good for two private boxes, and at leest twenty tickets, I should say," Emily cried, keeping her fine eyes on the main chance.

"I'll go bail of that," answered the papa.

At half-past six, Emily appeared at the theatre, accompanied by her father, who stood in the side-scene watching her.

"How beautiful she is," Pen thought, cantering home. "How simple and how tender! How charming it is to see a woman of her commanding genius busying herself with the delightful, though humble, offices of domestic life... How rude it was of me to begin to talk about professional matters, and how well she turned the conversation!"

He rode down the avenue, laughing as he saw Reverend Mr. Smirke coming demurely away from Fairoaks on his pony. Smirke had dawdled over Laura's lessons and Mrs. Pendennis's gardens, leaving just in time to miss the dinner invitation he fondly expected.

Pen was full of kindness and triumph. "What, picked up and sound?" he cried out laughing. "Come along back, old fellow, and eat my dinner—I have had mine: but we will have a bottle of the old wine and drink her health, Smirke."
