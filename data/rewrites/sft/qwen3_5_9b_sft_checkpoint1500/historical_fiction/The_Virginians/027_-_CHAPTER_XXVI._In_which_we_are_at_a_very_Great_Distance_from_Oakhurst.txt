Before the windows of the tavern's eating-room lay a bowling-green, dotted with tables where guests sipped punch or tea. Having concluded their meal simultaneously, Mr. Morris proposed they adjourn to the green to drink a cool bottle. "Jack Morris would adjourn to the Dust Hole as a pretext for a fresh drink," remarked my lord. To this, Jack replied that surely each gentleman possessed his own favored method of proceeding to the deuce, admitting openly that his weakness was a bottle.

"My Lord Chesterfield's deuce is deuce-ace," observed my Lord March. "His lordship cannot keep away from cards or dice."

"My Lord March possesses not one devil, but several," countered Jack. "He loves gambling, horse-racing, betting, drinking, eating, money, and women. You have fallen into bad company, Mr. Warrington, by lighting upon his lordship. He will play you for every acre you hold in Virginia."

"With the greatest pleasure in life!" interjected my lord eagerly. "And for all your tobacco, spices, slaves, oxen, and asses, and everything else that is yours."

"Shall we begin now?" Jack asked, noting that Mr. Warrington was never without a dice-box or bottle-screw. "I will set Mr. Warrington for whatever he pleases."

"Alas, my lord," Harry protested, "the tobacco, slaves, asses, and oxen are not yet mine. I am just of age, and my mother, scarcely twenty years older, has as good a chance of long life as I."

"I will bet you that you survive her," my lord offered. "I will pay you a sum now against four times the sum payable at her death. I will set a fair stake over this table against the reversion of your estate in Virginia upon her departure. What do you call your place?"

"Castlewood."

"A principality, I hear," my lord mused. "I will bet its value has been exaggerated tenfold by the gossip-mongers here. How came you by the name of Castlewood? Are you related to my lord? Ah, I know—my lady, your mother, descends from the true head of the house. He took the losing side in '15. I have heard this story a dozen times from my old Duchess. She knew your grandfather; he was a friend of Addison, Steele, Pope, and Milton, I dare say, and the great figures of his time. It is a pity he did not stay home and transplant the other branch of the family to the plantations."

"I have just been staying at Castlewood with my cousin there," Harry noted.

"Hm! Did you play with him? He is fond of pasteboard and bones."

"Never, save for sixpences and a pool of commerce with the ladies."

"So much the better for both parties. But did you play with Will Esmond if he was at home? I will lay ten to one you did."

Harry blushed and admitted that evenings past, he and his cousin had played a few hands at cards.

"And Tom Sampson, the chaplain?" cried Jack Morris. "I wager Tom made a third, and may the Lord deliver you from Tom and Will Esmond together!"

"Nay, the truth is, I won of both of them," Harry declared proudly.

"And they paid you? Well, miracles will never cease!"

"I did not speak of miracles," Harry smiled over his wine.

"And you do not tell tales out of school—the *volto sciolto*—hey, Mr. Warrington?" asked my lord.

"I beg your pardon," said straightforward Harry. "French is the only language besides my own of which I know aught."

"My Lord March has learned Italian at the Opera, and a pretty penny his lessons have cost," Jack Morris remarked. "We must show him the Opera—must we not, March?"

"Must we, Morris?" my lord asked, seeming to accept the invitation only half-heartedly.

Both gentlemen wore identical attire: small scratch-wigs unpowdered, blue frocks with plate buttons, buckskins, riding-boots, and little hats adorned with narrow cords of lace, devoid of fashionable markers.

"I care little for the Opera, my lord," Harry said, warming to his Burgundy, "but I should like to go to Newmarket and behold a good English hunting-field."

"We will show you Newmarket and the hunt, sir. Can you ride well?"

"I believe I can," Harry answered. "I can shoot fairly well and jump some."

"What is your weight? I bet we weigh even, or I weigh most. I bet Jack Morris beats you at bird or mark, at twenty-five paces. I bet I leap farther than you on level ground, right here on this green."

"I know not Mr. Morris's shooting—I have never seen either gentleman before—but I take your bets, my lord, at whatever you please," cried Harry, now thoroughly heated by the wine.

"Ponies on each!" shouted my lord.

"Done and done!" echoed my lord and Harry in unison. The young man felt it was for the honor of his country not to be ashamed of any wager made.

"Now we may try the last bet, if your feet are steady," my lord said, rising, stretching his limbs, and surveying the crisp, dry grass. He removed his boots, followed by his coat and waistcoat, buckling his belt tight around his waist before casting his garments onto the ground.

Harry held greater respect for his garments, which were his finest suit. Removing the velvet coat and waistcoat, he folded them carefully. Seeing that the nearby tables were sloshed with drink, he carried them to a table within the eating-room, whose windows stood open.

Then a new guest appeared: none other than Mr. Wolfe, soberly consuming a chicken and salad with a modest pint of wine. Harry, in high spirits, told the Colonel he had a wager with my Lord March and asked if the Colonel would stand him halves. The Colonel, being too poor to bet, nonetheless agreed to witness fair play. He set down his glass and stalked through the open window after his young friend.

"Who is that tallow-faced Put with carroty hair?" Jack Morris asked, the effects of Burgundy fully manifest.

Mr. Warrington explained this was Lieutenant-Colonel Wolfe of the 20th Regiment.

"Your humble servant, gentlemen!" the Colonel bowed rigidly.

"Never saw such a figure in my life!" cried Jack Morris. "Did you, March?"

"I beg your pardon, I think you said March?" the Colonel looked surprised.

"I am the Earl of March, sir, at Colonel Wolfe's service," the nobleman bowed. "My friend, Mr. Morris, is so intimate with me that, after dinner, we are quite like brothers."

"Why is not all Tunbridge Wells present to hear this?" thought Morris. Delighted, he shouted, "Two to one on my lord!"

"Done!" called Mr. Warrington, forcing the enthusiastic Jack to cry "Done!" as well.

"Take him, Colonel," Harry whispered.

"But the Colonel said he could not afford to lose, and therefore could not hope to win," Harry noted later.

"I see you have won one of our bets already, Mr. Warrington," my Lord March observed. "I am taller than you by an inch or two, but you are broader round the shoulders."

"Pooh, my dear Will! I bet you you weigh twice as much as he does!" cried Jack Morris.

"Done, Jack!" said my lord, laughing. "The bets are all ponies. Will you take him, Mr. Warrington?"

"No, my dear fellow—one's enough," said Jack.

"Very good, my dear fellow," said my lord. "Now we will settle the other wager."

Already arrayed in his best silk stockings, black satin-net breeches, and neatest pumps, Harry refused to remove his shoes, as his antagonist's heavy riding-boots and spurs were ill-suited for leaping. Before them stretched a fine, even green turf of about thirty yards, sufficient for a run and a jump. A gravel walk circled this space, beyond which stood a wall and gate sign—a field azure bearing the Hanoverian White Horse rampant between two skittles proper, with the motto naming the landlord and the beast.

My lord's friend placed a handkerchief on the ground as the mark from which the leapers were to start, while Mr. Wolfe stood at the far end of the grass plat to measure where each landed.

"My lord went first," Mr. Warrington wrote in a letter to his mother, Mrs. Mountain, at Castlewood, Virginia, which remains extant. "He wished for me to take the lead; but, recalling the story of the Battle of Fontenoy which my dearest George used to tell, I said, 'Monseigneur le Comte, tirez le premier, s'il vous plaît.' Thus he took his run in stocking-feet, and for the honor of Old Virginia, I had the gratification of beating his lordship by more than two feet—namely, two feet nine inches. I jumped twenty-one feet three inches, by the drawer's measured tape, whereas his lordship leaped only eighteen-six.
