The royal pyramid loomed before us, its polished stone catching the last gleams of sunlight as the cavalcade drew to a halt in the vast square. The mammoth knelt, and a stair was placed against its flank. At the blare of trumpets, we descended—Phorenice, the fan-girl, and I—onto the paving stones.

Instinctively, I turned toward the Sacred Mountain, its jagged peaks cutting into the darkening sky. My hand moved in the old gesture of reverence, my lips forming the silent prayer of thanks to the Gods. But as I straightened, a ripple of laughter slithered through the crowd.

I turned sharply, searching for the offender, but Phorenice stood motionless beside me, her chin high, her gaze fixed ahead. She had not bowed.

“You find something amusing in piety?” I asked, my voice low.

Her lips curled. “The old ways are cumbersome, Deucalion. We move beyond them now.”

I said nothing. The weight of her words settled coldly in my chest.

The massive doors of the pyramid groaned open, and we stepped inside, leaving the murmurs of the crowd behind. The hall of waiting was dim, lit only by the flickering blue flame of earth-breath that burned at its center. The air hung thick with perfume, cloying and oppressive.

“You are to be my honored guest,” Phorenice said, her voice smooth. “Your chambers are prepared. Ylga will show you.”

The fan-girl bowed and led me through the labyrinth of passages, her lamp casting long shadows on the walls. The pyramid was a maze of secrets, its corridors twisting, its hidden doors responding only to the touch of those who knew their mechanisms.

At last, we reached my quarters—a spacious room lined with chests of rich garments and jeweled ornaments.

“A gift from the Empress,” Ylga said, her tone unreadable.

I dismissed the slaves who hovered, eager to adorn me in finery I had no taste for. When we were alone, Ylga’s demeanor shifted. She closed the door, her dark eyes intent.

“You do not understand what game you walk into,” she said quietly.

“Then enlighten me.”

She hesitated, then spoke in a rush. “I am Ylga, daughter of Zaemon. My father was your friend once. I tell you this because I see the storm gathering around you, Deucalion. Phorenice is not what she pretends.”

Before I could answer, a deep tremor shook the pyramid, vibrating through the stone. The walls groaned as if in protest.

Ylga’s face paled. “She dares even this.”

“What?”

“The sacred earth-tremor—the call of the Gods. She uses it now as a dinner bell.”

Disgust curled in my gut. To pervert such a portent into a summons for gluttony was sacrilege beyond measure.

The feast was a spectacle of excess. The hall glittered under the glow of earth-fire, its guests draped in silks and jewels, their laughter sharp and brittle. I was placed at Phorenice’s side, the seat of highest honor, though the food turned to ash in my mouth.

A governor, drunk on wine, stumbled forward, proclaiming his devotion in florid words. Phorenice rewarded him with a sip from her own cup, and the man drank greedily, as though it were a sacrament.

“You disapprove,” she murmured, watching me.

“I see no purpose in such displays.”

Her smile was knife-edged. “Purpose? Must every act have one? Perhaps I indulge simply because I can.”

Before I could reply, a stir at the entrance silenced the hall. The guards moved to block the intruder, but he passed them untouched—an old man, frail and ragged, his white hair wild about his shoulders. In his hand, he carried the symbol of the Sun.

A priest.

The room held its breath as he stopped before Phorenice’s throne.

“Your crimes have mounted,” he said, his voice thin but unshaken. “The Gods have granted you power, but you have squandered it. Feed your people. Restore the land. Or you will be cast down.”

Phorenice leaned forward, her eyes alight with cold amusement. “And who will do this casting down? You?”

The old man did not flinch. “The earth will shake. The fires will rise. Atlantis will not endure your rule.”

A murmur of unease rippled through the courtiers.

Phorenice’s laughter rang out, bright and terrible. “Then let it tremble.”

The priest’s gaze swept the room, lingering last on me. There was warning in his eyes—and pity. Then he turned and walked away, his bare feet silent on the stone.

The feast resumed, but the air had thickened. The guests drank harder, laughed louder, as if to drown the omen in noise.

And beneath it all, the pyramid seemed to hum, as though the earth itself remembered the curse.
