In the velvety softness of a summer's day, a boy of thirteen sprawled on the lush green grass of a cloistered courtyard, his curly head cradled in his arms as he devoured the pages of Sismondi's _History of the Italian Republics_. The warm sunlight danced across the cobblestones, casting dappled shadows that seemed to whisper secrets to the ancient stones. His tutor, a young Scotchman with a passion for the world beyond the horizon, sat on a stool beneath the shelter of a Gothic arch, immersed in the intricate dance of economic theory. The air was heavy with the sweet scent of blooming roses, their delicate petals falling like confetti on the grass below.

As Deronda's gaze drifted from the printed words to his tutor's profile, a question burst forth from his lips like a sunbeam breaking through the clouds. "Mr. Fraser, how was it that the popes and cardinals always had so many nephews?" The query hung in the air like a challenge, and the tutor's eyes, red-rimmed from late nights spent poring over dusty tomes, snapped into focus as he rose to meet the inquiry.

"Their own children were called nephews," he declared, his voice dripping with the rhythmic cadence of the Scottish hills. Deronda's eyes sparkled with curiosity as he pressed for an explanation. "Why?" The tutor's response was laced with a hint of impatience, but his words tumbled forth with the ease of a well-worn melody. "It was simply a matter of propriety; for, as you know, priests do not marry, and the children were born out of wedlock."

As the tutor's gaze returned to the pages of his book, Deronda's expression darkened, like a storm cloud gathering on the horizon. He had always known Sir Hugo Mallinger as his uncle, a benevolent figure who had taken him in as a child and showered him with love and attention. But now, a nagging doubt crept into his mind like a thief in the night. When he had once asked about his parents, Sir Hugo had spoken of them only in hushed tones, his words veiled in mystery. "You lost your father and mother when you were very young," he had said, his eyes clouded with a sorrow that Deronda couldn't quite understand.
