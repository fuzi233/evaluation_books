I rose before dawn the next morning, kneeling beside the dormitory’s center stand to finish my task by the fading glow of the night-lamp. Every bead and strand of silk had been woven into the chain, doubled in length and richness to suit the exacting taste of its intended recipient. A final touch was needed—a small gold clasp, which I sacrificed from my only necklace, fastening it securely before coiling the completed guard into a gleaming box of nacarat shell, adorned with a circlet of blue stones. Inside the lid, I carved initials with the tip of my scissors.

The tradition of Madame Beck’s fête was well known—each year, the school presented her with a gift. Yet M. Emanuel, her kinsman, received no such formal tribute. His honors came unbidden, proof of the affection he inspired despite his tempers and peculiarities. He would accept no jewels, no finery; a simple sketch or a wildflower, given sincerely, pleased him more than gilded offerings. There was an unworldly purity in him, a kinship with something brighter than the age he lived in.

His fête fell on the first of March—a Thursday, bright and unseasonably warm. Mass was attended, and the promise of a half-holiday lent the morning an air of festivity. Even the frugal Labassecouriens had indulged: collars were fresh, somber wool exchanged for lighter hues. Mademoiselle Zélie St. Pierre, in a rare surrender to vanity, wore silk and summoned a coiffeur. Rumor held that her eye lingered on M. Emanuel, and his, often, on her—watching with an intensity that left her flustered. He had a way of piercing through pretense, laying bare the flaws and fractures of the soul. Yet for all his severity, he was quick to forgive where honesty was offered. It was deception that provoked his wrath.

When the bell rang, the classroom brimmed with color and fragrance—every pupil held a bouquet, save me. I preferred flowers rooted in earth, their brief plucking a melancholy reminder of life’s fragility. Zélie’s gaze darted about, incredulous at my bare hands.

*"How wise to spare your coins, Miss Lucie,"* she remarked, displaying her hothouse blooms. *"I’ve wasted two francs on these."*

A step silenced her—*his* step, brisk as ever, yet today touched with something warmer. He entered not as the brooding specter in his habitual paletôt, but crisply attired, bare-headed, a hat in hand. His blue eyes were clear, his manner almost buoyant.

*"Bon jour, mes amies,"* he said, and the words carried an unspoken kindness.

Zélie, ever the spokeswoman, swept forward. *"We wish Monsieur joy on his fête,"* she declared, laying her lavish bouquet before him. The others followed, their offerings mounting into a fragrant pyramid that soon obscured him entirely. Then—silence.

Minutes passed. At last, from behind the floral barricade, a voice emerged, low and hollow:

Zélie turned. *"All have presented their bouquets,"* she assured him, adding with a glance my way, *"Save Meess Lucie, who, as a foreigner, likely did not grasp the custom."*

I suppressed a smile. *Well played, Zélie.*

The response was a dismissive wave. Then M. Paul stepped forward, his gaze fixed on the far wall. A third time, with tragic weight, he demanded:

I might have relented then, slipping the shell-box into his hand—had Zélie’s meddling not stiffened my resolve. Let him be vexed. He deserved it.

*"It is well,"* he pronounced at last, and the storm passed over his face before he launched into his lecture.

I paid little heed until his tone shifted, his words veering across the Channel to *les Anglaises*. Never had I heard such venom—our height, our morals, our *impious skepticism* derided with relish. The class tittered. Labassecour, it seemed, nursed a quiet hatred for England.

Then he struck too deep.

*"Vive l’Angleterre, l’Histoire et les Héros!"* I cried, slamming my desk. *"À bas la France, la Fiction et les Faquins!"*

The room froze. M. Paul, hiding a smirk behind his handkerchief, brightened at having provoked me. His mood shifted like quicksilver. Soon he was rhapsodizing over flowers, comparing the girls to blossoms, praising Zélie’s bouquet with florid charm. Then, the coup de grâce: an invitation to a country breakfast—*for his friends only.*

*"Then I shall not be there,"* I muttered.

*"Soit!"* he snapped, gathering his flowers and vanishing.

My anger flared and faded. By afternoon, I regretted the withheld gift. Returning to the empty classroom to secure the box—its initials, *P. C. D. E.*, too revealing—I found the door ajar. Inside, a figure hunched over my desk: M. Paul, in his ink-stained coat, rifling through my papers with familiar ease, a cigar between his lips.

He had always left traces—corrected exercises, borrowed books redolent of tobacco. Now, caught in the act, he turned without surprise.

*"I thought you were in town,"* he said, reclaiming his composure. *"Do you imagine I care if you find me here? I come often."*

*"And you read what I leave? Even after this?"* He tapped the cigar.

He exhaled. *"This morning, I was happy. You ruined it."*

I said nothing.

His jaw tightened, but his hands, still resting among my papers, had deposited two new books—offerings of reconciliation. The sight disarmed me.

*"I do not dislike you, Monsieur,"* I admitted.

He studied me, then sighed. *"No. I suppose you do not."*

And with that, the tension broke—leaving only the scent of cigars and the quiet understanding that some battles were never meant to be won.
