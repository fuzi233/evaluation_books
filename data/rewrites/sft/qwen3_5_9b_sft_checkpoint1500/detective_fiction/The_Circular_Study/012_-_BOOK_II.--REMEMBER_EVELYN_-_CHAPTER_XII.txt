Mr. Gryce played his game with a quiet, calculated grace. Though he had declared his intent to trail Mr. Adams, he did not merely shadow him from afar. Instead, he boarded the same carriage and claimed the seat directly behind the young man. It was a position chosen not for immediate confrontation, but for observation—a chance to study the subject at leisure. To Gryce's surprise, this boy possessed a quality that cut deeper than any criminal profile he had ever read; it struck a chord within the old detective's own conscience, making him acutely aware of the weight of his own age, a burden he felt he had earned through a lifetime of intricate pursuits now concluded.

From his vantage point, Gryce watched the transformation of the prisoner's face with growing fascination. The handsome countenance, which moments ago had twisted in the throes of deepest torment, now bore a terrifying calm. Gryce marveled at the source of this fortitude. How could a soul so recently shattered sit so immovably under the gaze of authority figures whose judgment he had previously fled from in panic? Was it a courage forged only in desperation? Or was the boy so utterly consumed by internal ruin that he failed to see the shadow his grief cast upon the world around him? His brow furrowed in brooding silence, his eyes vacant, suggesting a mind retreated far beyond the reach of present surroundings. One could not guess the abyss of remorse he inhabited. Not even an old detective like Gryce, hardened by decades of exposure to the criminal classes—many of whom shared this young man's cultivated appearance and social standing—could fully comprehend the depth of this tragedy.

At the Brooklyn station, Gryce stepped forward to join his charge, who barely lifted his head as the detective approached. Within an hour, they stood within the hallowed, echoing halls of Police Headquarters, where the interrogation of Mr. Adams commenced.

The young man offered no resistance. If anything, he appeared eager to speak, driven by a heavy burden he longed to unload. Yet, the truth he sought to confess was not what the police expected. He did not admit to the murder of his brother; rather, he confessed to being the accidental architect of that brother's demise.

"My name is Cadwalader, not Adams," he began, his voice steady despite the tremor in its roots. "My father, a Scotsman by birth, became a naturalized citizen of Pennsylvania, settling in a town named Montgomery when he was a young husband. He had two children then; one perished in infancy, and the survivor was my brother, Felix—the man whose violent death under the alias of Adams you have summoned me to explain. I am the fruit of a later union, contracted by my father several years after he left Montgomery. Born in Harrisburg, I know nothing of that city, for my father departed when I was three. My earliest memories are not of America, but of the Old World, to which I was sent following the premature death of my mother. I returned to England only this past year, finding my father near the end of his life and my brother, Felix, a wealthy man with whom I had hoped to share his fortune. After my father passed, Felix arrived in New York, leaving me adrift without resources, without hope, and without a path forward."

His narrative shifted, the tone darkening as he recounted the fateful encounter. "In a search for my father's old friends, I stumbled upon a young lady in the household of a former companion from Scotland. She captured my heart so completely that I resolved to marry her. But I was penniless, unemployed, and homeless. I wrote to Felix, pleading for a sum sufficient to wed her with dignity. He gave me only a vague, evasive promise, never a concrete offer. Youth, however, is stubborn and hopeful; I decided to proceed anyway, betting that her beauty and charm would eventually soften his resolve.

"The fatal flaw of my conduct, gentlemen, is that I entered this alliance without informing either her or her father. They believed me prosperous, perhaps wealthy. Her father, Mr. Poindexter, is a man of ambition who views his daughter as worthy of a millionaire's devotion. Had he known the truth—that I possessed nothing more than the clothes on my back and a handful of coins—he would have stopped the wedding cold.

"Felix was notified of the marriage, yet he refused to witness it and forbade any interference. Consequently, the ceremony was performed quietly in a hotel parlor, attended by strangers. Two days later, armed with both hope and dread, I led my bride to the residence that would soon become infamous. I had miscalculated the nature of my host.

"Felix, brilliant as polished steel, proved to be as hard as iron. He feigned indifference to my wife's charms, dismissing love and marriage as frivolities suited only for idle minds, wholly unworthy of men of our talent and attainment. He turned his back upon us. Angered by his cruelty, I lashed out in a fit of frenzy, an assault for which he was ill-prepared. He crumpled like a man under a blow, his composure crumbling moment by moment until, in a final, desperate act, he seized a dagger lying nearby. With a cry of, 'You want my money? Well, then, take it!' he drove the blade into his own heart."

He paused, looking at the detectives with an expression of weary resignation. "I fear you will not believe me, but that is the story of this crime."
