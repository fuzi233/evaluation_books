The carriage ride to London, undertaken under the guardianship of Aunt Jennings, presented a stark contrast to the reality Elinor perceived. To embark on this journey as her aunt's guest, following so brief an acquaintance and amidst a disparity of ages and temperaments that had previously raised grave objections, seemed absurd. Yet, those doubts, swept away by the fiery ardour shared by Marianne and their mother, now haunted Elinor. Watching the radiant, delirious hope that illuminated Marianne's face, Elinor felt a profound desolation. Her own prospects seemed bleared and grey compared to her sister's animated vision of a future with Willoughby. Though she harboured lingering fears about the young man's fidelity, she resolved to suppress them. Time, she knew, was short; Willoughby was likely already in town. Marianne's impatience confirmed this. Elinor vowed to observe everything with ruthless scrutiny: she would glean every scrap of information from the city and watch Willoughby's treatment of her sister with unyielding vigilance. If her observations proved him unworthy, she would tear the veil from Marianne's eyes; if he proved true, she would force herself to celebrate her sister's joy, silencing her own private sorrows.

For three days, the journey unfolded. Marianne remained largely silent, lost in a reverie of romantic anticipation, offering exclamations of delight only when a picturesque landscape caught her gaze. To compensate for this solitude, Elinor adopted the role of the perfect companion to Aunt Jennings. She spoke, laughed, and attended to her aunt with exaggerated politeness, while the older woman, delighted by their company, fretted over their dining choices, insisting on salmon over cod despite the girls' apparent indifference. By the third afternoon, they arrived in London, eager to escape the coach and bask by a warm fire in a handsome, newly furnished house. Their quarters were spacious and comfortable, a former residence of Charlotte Palmer, evidenced by a silk painting of a landscape hanging above the mantle—a testament to seven years of artistic training in the capital.

With dinner delayed for two hours, Elinor seated herself to write to her mother. Almost simultaneously, Marianne took up her pen. "I am writing home, Marianne," Elinor remarked gently, "might you not postpone it until tomorrow?" "No!" Marianne replied sharply, avoiding further comment. Instantly, the truth dawned on Elinor: Marianne was writing to Willoughby. The implication was undeniable; they must be engaged. While this revelation offered no comfort to Elinor, it thrilled her with a sudden, sharp sense of certainty. Marianne finished her brief note with frantic speed, sealing it and directing it with a trembling hand. As Elinor watched, the direction clearly bore a large 'W'. Before the ink was dry, Marianne rang the bell, ordering the footman to dispatch the letter via the two-penny post. The matter was settled.

Marianne's spirits soared, yet beneath the surface lay a fluttering anxiety that failed to bring peace to her sister. As evening deepened, her agitation grew palpable. She ate little, and when they returned to the drawing-room, her ears strained to catch the roll of wheels on the pavement. To Elinor's relief, Aunt Jennings remained largely confined to her own room, unaware of the storm brewing below. Tea was served, and just as Marianne's heart sank after several false alarms, a heavy knock shattered the silence. It was unmistakably Willoughby's approach. Marianne sprang up, rushing toward the door. Silence followed the knock, stretching agonizingly. She peered out, stepped onto the stairs, and listened. Returning to the room, her countenance transformed by ecstatic disbelief, she cried out, "Oh, Elinor! It is Willoughby! He is here!" She stood poised to rush into his embrace, but the door opened, and Colonel Brandon entered.

The blow to Marianne was shattering. She fled the room, leaving Elinor in a state of bewildered frustration. Yet, Elinor's regard for Colonel Brandon tempered her resentment; she felt a stinging injustice that a man so devoted to her sister should mistake her composure for indifference. She quickly realized that Brandon had noticed her turmoil, observing her retreat with a mixture of astonishment and concern that bordered on forgetting basic courtesy. "Is your sister ill?" he asked. Elinor, distressed, attributed Marianne's state to headaches and fatigue, carefully deflecting any suspicion of deeper causes. Brandon listened intently, then shifted the conversation to the pleasures of London and inquiries about their journey, his tone calm and uninterested. Elinor, desperate to know if Willoughby was in town, hesitated to ask, fearing to wound Brandon. Finally, she ventured, "Have you been in London since I last saw you?" With evident embarrassment, he admitted he had been there almost constantly, visiting Delaford briefly but unable to return to Barton. These words, coupled with his demeanor, jolted Elinor's memory to the circumstances surrounding Willoughby's departure and the unease it had caused. She feared her question had revealed a curiosity far greater than she intended.

Aunt Jennings soon entered, her noise and cheerfulness masking the tension. She expressed her delight at seeing Colonel Brandon, mentioning her recent struggles with settling affairs and a debt to Mr. Cartwright. When Brandon mentioned having heard of her presence through Mr. Palmer, she turned her attention to the Palmers, asking after Charlotte, who, she assured them, was "a fine size" by now. Brandon, commissioned to deliver news of Mrs. Palmer's health, promised a visit the following day. Turning to the sisters, she asked where they had been, noting the absence of Miss Marianne. "Do not worry," she mused, "about what you and Mr. Willoughby intend for her. Youth and beauty are a powerful combination." She reflected wistfully on her own past, lamenting her lack of handsomeness but boasting of a "very good husband," now deceased for eight years. Brandon responded with his customary mildness to her barrage of questions, offering no real answers. As Elinor prepared the tea, Marianne reluctantly reappeared.

Once the guests departed, Colonel Brandon fell silent, his brow furrowed with thought, and Aunt Jennings struggled to persuade him to stay. With no other visitors, the ladies retired early, united in exhaustion. The next morning, Marianne awoke with renewed spirit, the previous night's disappointment seemingly erased by the promise of what lay ahead. Soon after breakfast, Mrs. Palmer's carriage arrived. The hostess burst into the room, effervescent and delighted, though her surprise at their arrival in town betrayed a hint of anger at having ignored her earlier invitation. After a brief, cheerful interlude with the Palmers, the group set off shopping. Mrs. Palmer led them to various boutiques, her eye catching every novelty, leading to a parade of indecision and impulsive desires. Marianne, however, was a study in restless abstraction. In Bond Street, her gaze scanned the crowd not for goods, but for signs of Willoughby. She refused to engage with any item, impatient for the journey to end, driven by a singular, consuming longing.

They returned home late, weary and empty-handed. The moment they crossed the threshold, Marianne ascended the stairs with feverish haste. Elinor followed to find her standing by the table, her face etched with sorrow. "Has no letter been left for me?" she demanded of the footman who entered with their parcels. "None," he replied. "Are you quite sure?" Marianne pressed, her voice dropping to a whisper. "Certain," the man affirmed. "How odd," she murmured, turning to the window. Inside, Elinor echoed the sentiment, her heart heavy. If Marianne had not believed Willoughby was in town, she would not have written; if he were there, why neither his presence nor a reply? The mystery gnawed at her. "My dear mother," Elinor thought, "you must be wrong to allow such an engagement, conducted in such shadow, to proceed. I long to speak, but how shall I intervene?" Resolving that if the suspense persisted, she would demand a serious inquiry from her mother, she watched her sister pace the floor, pausing only to stare out the window, hoping against hope for a knock that would never come.
