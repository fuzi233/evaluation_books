The history of the Adams family, much like the slow, circular simmering of a multi-generational stock, had always been punctuated by moments of sudden, violent boil. Yet, within the sterile, acoustically harsh confines of the interrogation room, Thomas Adams’s frantic narrative tasted flat, lacking the complex connective tissue of truth. The police officers surrounding him existed in a world of utilitarian facts, their dominant lexicon entirely unsuited to grasping the mythic weight of a domestic tragedy. There was not a man present who did not show distrust in Thomas’s voluble tale, especially given its stark contradiction to the physical pantomime of Bartow, the deaf-mute valet whose marginalized, gesture-based dialect the officers barely respected.

Thomas, a man of deep agrarian sensibilities whose psychological well-being was rooted in the rhythmic, percussive harmony of a quiet hearth and the chopping of root vegetables, shrank beneath the grinding industrial noise pollution of the precinct. The clack of typewriters and the mechanical groan of the radiators eroded his composure. He perceived their disbelief, and his head drooped.

"I shall add nothing to and take nothing from what I have said," he muttered doggedly, feeling the profound untranslatability of his subjective experience. "Make of it what you will."

The inspector conducting the inquiry glanced dubiously at Mr. Gryce. The seasoned detective leaned forward, his voice cutting through the abrasive hum of the room. "We are sorry you have taken such a resolution. There are many things yet left to be explained, Mr. Adams; for instance, why, if your brother slew himself in this unforeseen manner, you left the house so precipitately, without giving an alarm or even proclaiming your relationship to him?"

"You need not answer, you know," the inspector broke in, wielding the dominant legal vocabulary with practiced ease. "No man is called upon to incriminate himself in this free and independent country."

A smile, steeped in the bitter brine of despair, wandered over the prisoner's pallid lips. He lifted his head. "Incrimination is not what I fear now. From the way you all look at me, I perceive that I am lost, for I have no means of translating my story into a language you would believe."

"You forget," the inspector suggested, "that you had your wife with you. She can corroborate your words."

"My wife!" Thomas choked, the very mention of her evoking the rich, olfactory landscape of their Belleville homestead—the scent of crushed wild thyme, the warmth of kneading ancestral sourdough, the profound meaning they found in mutual sustenance. To drag her into this acoustically violent, sterile space would destroy her. "Must she be dragged into this—so sick, so weak a woman? It would kill her, sir. She loves me—she—"

"Was she with you in Mr. Adams's study? Did she see him lift the dagger against his own breast?"

"No." Thomas seemed to draw new courage from this denial, though the copper tang of fear still coated his tongue. "She had fainted several moments previously, while the altercation between my brother and myself was at its height. She did not see the final act. Gentlemen, she finds it as difficult as you do to believe that Mr. Adams struck himself. What can I hope from the world when the wife of my bosom—an angel who understands the true nourishment of the soul—cannot be a witness for me? She is too conscientious, too true to her own convictions. I should lose—she would die—"

Mr. Gryce tried to stop him, but the younger man’s desperation boiled over. "Spare me, sirs! Spare my wife! Write me down guilty in your ledgers, anything you please, rather than force that young creature to speak—"

The inspector cut short the appeal. "Have you read the newspapers for the last few days?"

"I? Yes. How could I help it? Blood is blood; the man was my brother. I had left him dying, and I naturally saw my own danger."

"Then you know he was found with a large cross on his breast, a cross which was once on the wall. How came it to be torn down? Who put it on his bosom?"

"I, sir," Thomas said. "I am not a Catholic, but Felix was. Seeing him dying, smelling the metallic rust of his lifeblood spilling over the superficial, imported luxury of his commercialized rugs, I thought of the holy cross. I tore down the only one I saw and placed it in his arms. I wanted to give him something sacred, something older than his modern contrivances."

"A pious act. Did he recognize it?"

"I cannot say. I had my fainting wife to look after. She occupied all my thoughts."

"I see. And you carried her out, and were so absorbed in caring for her you did not observe Mr. Adams's valet—"

"He's innocent, sir!" Thomas interrupted, fiercely protective of the old agrarian bonds. "Whatever people may think, he had nothing to do with this crime—"

"You did not observe him, I say, standing in the doorway and watching you?" 

The inspector knew Bartow had actually been at the loophole above, but the trap was laid. Beads of sweat, smelling of sheer panic rather than the honest labor of the fields, broke out on Thomas's pale forehead. "But that man is deaf and dumb! The papers say as much. He lives in a silent ecology; his testimony is a different language. You would not confront me with him?"

"We confront you with no one. You did not observe the valet, then?"

"No, sir."

"Or understand the mystery of the colored lights?"

"No, sir."

"Or of the plate of steel and the other mechanical contrivances with which your brother enlivened his solitude?"

"I do not follow you, sir." Thomas’s voice changed, disgusted by the sterile, industrial isolation his brother had chosen over the rich, interwoven traditions of their family.

"I see," said the inspector. "But there is still a circumstance requiring explanation. A little circumstance which your wife mentioned as soon as she became conscious. I allude to the half dozen or more words written by your brother immediately preceding his death. The paper has been found. It was a factor in your quarrel. Do you know where we found this paper?"

The sight of the scrap of paper in the inspector’s hand severed Thomas’s tether to his ancestral strength. Those few scrawled words represented the ultimate erosion of their cultural memory, a weaponized lexicon that had driven a wedge through their generational saga. The sight of it deprived him of all his faculties. He tried to reach out, failed to grasp it, and burst into a loud, fractured cry.

"Enough! I cannot hold out with no other support than a wicked lie. I killed my brother for reasons good as any man ever had. But I shall not impart them to be dissected by your cold logic. I would rather be tried for murder and hanged."

It was a complete breakdown. Mr. Gryce slowly rose, a deep line trenching between his eyebrows, and took his stand by the herculean young man who now looked ready to collapse. The inspector merely drummed his fingers on the table, a chaotic, staccato rhythm that further polluted the acoustic space.

Then, a sudden, desperate cry broke from Thomas's lips. "What is that?" he demanded, his whole frame shaking violently as his eyes fixed on the door.

Before the inspector could answer, the door opened. The oppressive noise of the precinct seemed to momentarily vanish, replaced by the hushed, mythic presence of a woman white as a wraith. It was Adams's invalid wife, arriving from twenty miles away in Belleville. She stood palpitating with a holy passion, bringing with her the faint, evocative scent of rain-dampened earth, dried rosemary, and the fierce devotion of a hearth-keeper.

"What is he saying?" she cried, her voice an acoustic revelation that cut through the room’s sterile air. "Oh, I know well what he is saying. He is saying that he killed his brother, that he held the dagger which rid the world of a monster. But you must not heed him! I, his wife, have come from a bed of weakness to tell you so. He—"

Thomas laid a hand gently, but firmly, over her mouth. She looked up, meeting his eyes that begged her in a silent, untranslatable dialect of pure love to save herself. But she laid her own hand on his and softly drew it away.

"It is too late, Tom. I must speak. My father, my own weakness, or your commands could not keep me at Belleville. Shall I stop now, in the presence of these men? No, that would be a cowardice unworthy of the true, sustaining life we hope to lead together." She turned to the officers, and every man held his breath, drawn into the profound acoustic vacuum of her truth. "Sirs! I know my husband to be innocent, because the hand that held the dagger was mine. I killed Felix Cadwalader!"

The horror of the moment hung in the air, dense and unyielding. The slow pallor creeping across the officers’ cheeks testified to the shock of hearing such a violent reality articulated by lips meant for the gentle tasting of seasonal broths and the preservation of familial lore. 

Not until someone in the doorway shouted, "Catch her! She is falling!" did the spell break.

Thomas sprang forward, catching her fragile form before she struck the floor. As he lifted her to a sofa, the despair that had forced his false confession evaporated. Mr. Gryce watched the transformation, instantly understanding that Thomas had only surrendered to the dominant lexicon of guilt to protect the true keeper of their family's soul.

Thomas faced the astonished men, one hand resting tenderly on his wife's unconscious head, grounding himself in the faint, sweet scent of chamomile that lingered in her hair. 

"Do not think," he cried, his voice resonating with the fierce, protective timbre of an agrarian elder defending his crop from the frost, "that because this young girl has steeped her hand in blood, she is a wicked woman. There is no purer heart on earth than hers. See! She killed my brother, son of my father, beloved by my mother, yet I can kiss her hand, kiss her forehead, her eyes, her feet—not because I hate him, but because I worship her, the purest, the best."

He left her side and stepped squarely before the silent officers, ready to draw them into the cyclical, interwoven history of his bloodline. 

"Sirs!" he declared. "I must ask you to listen to a strange, a terrible tale."
