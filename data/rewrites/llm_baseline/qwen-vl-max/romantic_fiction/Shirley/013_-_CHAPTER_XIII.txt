There were moments when Shirley allowed herself to drift into a gentle stillness, as though the world had paused just for her. She found joy in doing nothing—no task to complete, no thought to chase. In those quiet hours, simply being alive was enough: the sky above, the earth beneath, the rustle of leaves, the hum of bees—all woven together into a tapestry of peace. Often, after a busy morning, she would lie beneath a broad tree, letting the sun warm her face and the breeze stir her hair. She needed no one near but Caroline; even that was not necessary—only knowing she could call if she wished. The sky, vast and blue, held clouds like drifting dreams; the silence was rich with life. Her only books were memory and hope—two volumes she read with eyes brightened by wonder, her lips curling now and then in soft smiles, as if catching glimpses of stories yet to unfold. There was no sorrow in her heart, no shadow on her mind. Fate had smiled upon her, and she believed it would continue to do so. Her past was sweet, her future glowing with promise.

Yet one afternoon, when Caroline approached to rouse her from her repose, she found Shirley’s cheek glistening with tears—fresh as dew. Her eyes shimmered, brimming with unshed emotion.

"Shirley," Caroline whispered, the word “you” emphasized with sudden concern, "why are you crying?"

Miss Keeldar lifted her head, her expression calm, almost amused. "Because I find it deeply satisfying," she replied. "My heart is both heavy and light at once. But why don’t you join me? I shed only joyful tears—easy to wipe away. You might weep bitterness, if you chose."

"Why should I weep bitterness?"

"Mateless, solitary bird," Shirley murmured, half to herself.

"And aren’t you too, Shirley?"

"At heart—no."

"Oh! Then who dwells there?"

Shirley laughed—a clear, sparkling sound—and sprang up. "I’ve been dreaming," she said. "Just a daydream—bright, perhaps, but likely baseless."

* * * * *

Miss Helstone, meanwhile, had long since abandoned illusions. She faced the future with sober clarity, sensing the path her own life and others’ were taking. Yet old habits clung to her—especially the evening ritual of walking to the field-style and the ancient thorn tree overlooking the Hollow. That night, the night after the mysterious note, she stood as usual, waiting for the beacon she once trusted. But no lamp lit the hillside. She waited until stars rose high, signaling the hour had passed. On her way back, moonlight caught her attention—Fieldhead glowed under the silver orb. Trees and halls stood serene, bathed in pearly light; shadows deepened around the oak-wreathed roof, and the stone pavement gleamed like polished marble. Two dark figures cast sharp silhouettes on the ground—still at first, then moving slowly, speaking in low tones.

Caroline watched them emerge from behind the cedar. "Is that Mrs. Pryor and Shirley?"

It was Shirley—there was no mistaking her slender, proud figure, or the grace with which she walked. Her face was visible—thoughtful, playful, tender, all at once. Her curls tumbled freely, framing her neck and shoulders. A gold chain sparkled through the folds of her scarf, and a large gem glittered on her white hand. Yes—definitely Shirley.

And beside her—was it Mrs. Pryor?

No. The figure was tall, young, stately—a man. Robert Moore, her tenant.

They spoke softly, their words lost in the night. To linger was not spying, not when the moon revealed every detail. And Caroline could not help staying.

She remembered how, in summer nights past, Moore had walked with her—holding her hand, sharing quiet talks beneath the stars. He had never touched Shirley like that. Not yet. And yet Shirley was not haughty when she loved. There was dignity in her posture, yes—but no arrogance. Robert must see her beauty clearly, with a man’s gaze. She had fire in her eyes—soft, generous, magnetic. She smiled—what stirred that smile? Caroline saw Robert respond, drawn in by it. They looked like spirits bound together, walking on a shore beyond death’s flood. And she—she stood in shadow, hidden, her mind darker than her hiding place. What was she? A mortal, burdened, asking why she lived, what purpose she served. Her thoughts circled endlessly: how would she face death? Who would stand beside her?

"This is the hardest part I’ve reached," she thought. "But I knew it would come. I gave Robert up the moment I saw Shirley—rich, young, radiant. She has him now. He loves her. She will be his darling, more so when they marry. The more he knows her, the deeper he’ll love her. They will be happy. I don’t begrudge them happiness—but I suffer. Some pain is unbearable. Truly, I wish I had never been born. They should have silenced me at birth."

As Shirley stepped aside to pick a dew-damp flower, the pair turned toward a path near the gate. A few words drifted to Caroline. She didn’t stay to listen. She slipped away quietly, and the moon kissed the wall where her shadow had lingered. But the reader may remain—and hear what passed between them.

"I can’t understand why nature didn’t give you a bulldog’s head," Shirley said. "You have all its tenacity."

"Not a kind comparison. Am I so low?"

"And something of its silent ways. You move without warning, strike without noise, hold fast."

"You’re guessing. I’ve done no such thing before you."

"Your silence speaks for you. How little you say—yet how much you plan. You’re far-seeing, calculating."

"I know these people. I’ve gathered their intentions. My note last night told you Barraclough was convicted and sentenced. His allies will seek revenge. I’ll prepare for them. That’s all. Now—do you approve?"

"I’ll stand with you—as long as you’re defending, not attacking. Yes."

"Good. Even without your support, I’d act this way—but differently. Now I feel satisfied. I enjoy this role."

"I suppose you do. It shows. You relish this work more than making army cloth."

"It feels right."

"So would old Helstone. True, your motives differ—perhaps greatly. Should I speak to Mr. Helstone? If you wish."

"Do as you please. Your judgment is sound. I’d trust it in worse crises. Though I warn you—he’s biased against me."

"I know. But it will fade. He can’t resist an alliance like this."

"I’d welcome him. He’s made of true stuff."

"I think so too."

"An old blade—rusty, but still sharp."

"Well, you shall have him, Mr. Moore—if I can win him."

"Who can’t you win?"

"Perhaps not the rector. But I’ll try."

"Effort? He’ll yield to a word—a smile."

"No. It will take tea, toast, cake, and endless persuasion. It’s growing cold."

"I see you shiver. Am I keeping you too long?"

"It’s calm—almost warm. And your company is rare pleasure. If you wore a thicker shawl—"

"I might stay longer. But it’s late. Mrs. Pryor would chide. We keep early hours here. I’m sure your sister does too."

"Yes. Hortense and I agree—we each do as we please."

"How do you please?"

"Three nights a week I sleep at the mill. I need little rest. When it’s moonlit, I often walk the Hollow till dawn."

"When I was a child, my nurse told me fairies appeared in that Hollow. Before the mill was built, it was wild and lonely. You’ll fall under enchantment."

"I fear it’s already done," Moore said softly.

"But there are worse things than fairies to beware of," Shirley added.

"More dangerous ones," he replied.

"Far more so. For instance—what if you met Michael Hartley, that mad Calvinist weaver? They say he poaches, walks the woods at night with a gun."

"I’ve already met him. We argued one night. Strange little incident—it intrigued me."

"Intrigued you? I admire your taste! Michael isn’t sane."

"His madness holds a wild charm. He’d be half-poet if not wholly mad—perhaps prophet, if not profligate. He told me hell was foreordained for me. He saw the mark of the beast on my brow. Said I’d been an outcast from the start. God’s vengeance was preparing—through a vision, he saw my doom. I asked more—but he left with, 'The end is not yet.'"

"Have you seen him since?"

"About a month later, returning from market, I found him and Barraclough, both drunk, praying wildly by the roadside. They called me Satan, demanded deliverance. Recently, Michael came to the counting-house—hatless, shirt-sleeved—his coat pledged. He told me to set my house in order. My soul, he said, would soon be required."

"Do you take it lightly?"

"He’d been drinking for weeks—bordering on delirium tremens."

"Then he might act on his prophecies."

"We mustn’t let such things unsettle us."

"Mr. Moore, go home!"

"So soon?"

"Go straight down the fields—not by the lade."

"It’s early."

"It’s late. I’m going in. Promise not to wander in the Hollow tonight?"

"If you wish."

"I do. May I ask—do you think life is worthless?"

"Not at all. On the contrary—I value it more than ever."

"Recently?"

"Existence once felt aimless, hopeless. Three months ago, I was drowning—wishing it over. Then a hand reached out—so delicate I barely dared trust it. But its strength saved me."

"Are you truly saved?"

"For now. Your help gave me another chance."

"Then live well. Don’t offer yourself to Michael Hartley. Goodnight."

* * * * *

The next day, Miss Helstone had promised to spend the evening at Fieldhead. She kept her word, though the hours leading up to it had been heavy with sorrow. Most of the time, she stayed locked in her room, leaving only for meals. Fanny, curious, asked about her plans. Caroline lied: "I’m busy altering a dress. Prefer sewing upstairs to avoid interruptions."

She did sew. Needle flew, thread pulled, fabric stitched—her hands moved without pause. But her mind raced faster. Again, she craved purpose—any purpose, however harsh. She would talk to Mrs. Pryor, then her uncle. Her brain labored as hard as her fingers, weaving plans while stitching muslin. Tears sometimes welled—quickly wiped away. The pain passed. She re-threaded her needle, adjusted the trim, and worked on.

Late in the afternoon, she dressed and arrived at Fieldhead just as tea was served. Shirley noticed her tardiness.

"Why so late?" she asked.

"Making my dress," Caroline replied. "These sunny days made me ashamed of my winter wool. So I refreshed a lighter one."

"You look lovely," Shirley said. "You’re a lady-like little person, Caroline. Isn’t she, Mrs. Pryor?"

Mrs. Pryor rarely praised appearance. This time, she brushed Caroline’s curls aside, touched her cheek gently, and said, "You’re getting thin, my dear. Pale. Do you sleep well? Your eyes look weary."

"I dream troubling dreams," Caroline confessed. "When I wake in the night, I think of the rectory—how dreary it is. It’s near the churchyard. The back is ancient—some say the kitchens were once inside the graveyard. Graves lie beneath. I want to leave."

"My dear, you’re not superstitious?"

"No. But I feel… nervous. Things seem darker. I fear omens, disasters. A weight presses on me—unbearable. I’d give anything to shake it off. I can’t."

"Strange," Shirley said. "I never feel that way." Mrs. Pryor remained silent.

"Sunshine, pleasant days—they bring me no joy," Caroline continued. "Calm evenings feel restless. Moonlight, once mild, now looks mournful. Is this weakness, Mrs. Pryor? I struggle. I reason. But nothing helps."

"Exercise," Mrs. Pryor suggested.

"Exercise? I exercise until I collapse."

"My dear, you should leave home."

"I would, Mrs. Pryor—but not aimlessly. I want to be a governess, like you. Would you speak to my uncle about it?"

"Nonsense!" Shirley burst in. "Be a governess? Better be a slave. Why dream of such pain?"

"My dear," Mrs. Pryor said gently, "you’re too young, not strong enough. Governesses endure hard duties."

"And I believe I need hard duties—to occupy me."

"Occupy you?" Shirley exclaimed. "When are you idle? I’ve never seen a girl so industrious. Come—sit by me. Have some tea. Eat something. Laugh. Stay home."

Caroline shook her head, sighed. She knew how hard it would be to convince anyone to help her change her life. If only she could follow her own judgment—she believed she could find a cure, harsh but effective. But this judgment, rooted in secrets she couldn’t explain—even to Shirley—seemed absurd to others. They saw no need. Her uncle might provide for her. Their logic was sound, but they didn’t understand her torment—her sleepless nights, her bleak days. Explanation was impossible. Endurance was her only choice. Many who lacked food and clothes lived brighter lives than she.

"Now, is your mind calmer?" Shirley asked. "Will you stay?"

"I won’t leave against my friends’ wishes," Caroline replied. "But I believe they’ll come to see my view."

Mrs. Pryor looked uneasy. Her usual reserve kept her from speaking plainly. Alone, she might have said more. But Shirley’s presence silenced her. She showed concern indirectly—asking if the fire was too warm, placing a screen, closing a window, watching Caroline closely.

Shirley resumed: "Having destroyed your plan—thankfully—I’ll create a new one. Every summer, I take a trip. This year, I propose two months at the Scottish lochs or English lakes—provided you come with me. If not, I won’t go."

"You’re very kind, Shirley."

"Drink tea. Eat. Laugh. Stay home."
