No one in the Dashwood household could quite make sense of how Edward Ferrars had come to be freed from the secret engagement that had hung over him for four long, miserable years, but the truth of his liberty was unshakable. And every last one of them knew exactly what he would do with that freedom: after suffering through one ill-considered, mother-disapproved attachment that had brought him nothing but strife, he would come straight to Barton to lay his heart at Elinor’s feet.

That was precisely why he had ridden up to the cottage gate that afternoon, his boots dusty from the journey, his cuffs smudged where he’d anxiously worried at them through a full hour of pacing the sun-dappled lanes of the Barton valley to work up his courage. It felt absurd to him that he, who had once stumbled through a proposal of sorts to Lucy Steele years before, could be so nervous now—so desperate for fresh air, so in need of a quiet moment to steady his breath before he could speak the words he’d carried locked in his chest for months.

There is no need to recount every stammered, blushing line of that proposal, every quiet pause where Elinor’s eyes glistened so bright he almost forgot what he meant to say. All that matters is this: by four o’clock, three hours after he had arrived, he had secured Elinor’s yes, won Mrs. Dashwood’s tearful, eager blessing, and was not just the newly rapturous adoring lover, but one of the happiest men alive. His joy was far greater than the ordinary triumph of having a love returned. He walked away free of a entanglement that had haunted him, free of a woman he had not loved for years, and lifted at once to the quiet security he had once dared only to dream of, a future with Elinor he had long thought was hopelessly out of his reach. The change radiated off of him, genuine and warm and grateful, a cheer none of his friends had ever seen from him before.

He laid his whole heart bare to Elinor that afternoon, confessing every foolish mistake, every soft weakness, talking through that boyish attachment to Lucy with the gentle hindsight of a twenty-four year old who had finally grown into himself. “It was nothing but idle, silly infatuation,” he told her, his hand folded tight in hers. “All because I was eighteen, fresh from Mr. Pratt’s care at Longstaple, and my mother had given me nothing to do, no profession to aim for. I was idle, I was lonely, Lucy seemed kind and obliging, and I’d met too few other women to ever see her flaws. It was foolish, no doubt, but not an inexcusable folly, given how little I had to occupy me back then.”

The whole of the Dashwood family had turned upside down in those few short hours, their happiness so bright none of them expected to sleep a wink that night. Mrs. Dashwood was too giddy to sit still: she did not know how to lavish enough love on Edward, or enough praise on Elinor, how to thank him for his freedom without wounding his pride, or how to balance leaving the two of them to their private talks without hovering nearby, drinking in the sight of their smiling faces. Marianne could not speak for joy at all, her tears falling freely the whole afternoon, her comparisons to her own heartbreak and soft quiet regrets tangled up in the purest, most wholehearted happiness for her sister.

As for Elinor? It took her hours to steady her spirits. From the moment she first heard Lucy had married someone else, to the moment Edward knelt before her and said he loved her, she had been tossed between hope and disbelief, giddy and breathless and never once tranquil. When every last doubt fell away, when she realized he was truly hers, released honorably, and had come to her first the second he was free, the weight of that felicity almost overwhelmed her. It was hours before her heart stopped fluttering like it might fly out of her chest, before she could feel settled in the certainty that this was not a dream.

Edward made plans to stay at the cottage for at least a full week: no other claim on his time could possibly come before this, before making up for all the lost time, talking over every little detail of the past, the present, all the years stretching ahead of them. Lovers, after all, never tire of repeating the same stories twenty times over, each time a little sweeter than the last.

The first thing they turned to was the unthinkable absurdity of Lucy’s marriage to Edward’s own brother, Robert. Elinor could scarce wrap her head around it: she had heard Robert scoff at Lucy’s lack of beauty, mock her unsophisticated manners, and now he had run off and married her, the same girl who had been engaged to Edward, the very reason their mother had disowned Edward entirely. It was delightful, a little ridiculous, and utterly incomprehensible to her sense. Edward could only suppose that their meeting had been accidental at first: Lucy’s flattery feeding straight into Robert’s vanity, till the silly foolish game of him acting as mediator for Edward had spiraled into an elopement. Elinor remembered what Robert had told her in Harley Street, about how he was certain he could fix Edward’s whole mess if only he was allowed to step in, and they both laughed at how perfectly that suited his foolish, vain character.

Edward had not had the slightest hint it was coming, stuck up at Oxford since he’d left London, all his letters from Lucy remaining as frequent and affectionate as ever right up till the very end, when he’d received the note that left him half stunned between shock, horror, and overwhelming joy. He handed the letter to Elinor to read:
> Being very sure I have long lost your affections, I have thought myself at liberty to bestow my own on another, and have no doubt of being as happy with him as I once used to think I might be with you; but I scorn to accept a hand while the heart was another’s. Sincerely wish you happy in your choice, and it shall not be my fault if we are not always good friends, as our near relationship now makes proper. I can safely say I owe you no ill-will, and am sure you will be too generous to do us any ill offices. Your brother has gained my affections entirely, and as we could not live without one another, we are just returned from the altar, and are now on our way to Dawlish for a few weeks, which place your dear brother has great curiosity to see, but thought I would first trouble you with these few lines, and shall always remain,
> Your sincere well-wisher, friend, and sister,
> I have burnt all your letters, and will return your picture the first opportunity. Please to destroy my scrawls—but the ring with my hair you are very welcome to keep.

Elinor handed it back without a single comment, pressing her lips together to hide her amusement. Edward blushed, saying he would never have let her see a letter of Lucy’s in the old days, it was so poorly written, so absurdly self-assured. They agreed that Mrs. Ferrars had brought the whole mess on herself: she had settled a thousand a year on Robert out of spite to punish Edward, only for Robert to turn around and do the very exact thing she had disinherited Edward for daring to do. Edward knew his mother, who had always spoiled Robert as her favorite, would be far more hurt by Robert’s marriage than she ever would have been by Edward’s, and would forgive him far sooner, too.

Neither of them had made any solid plans for approaching Edward’s family yet. He had left Oxford less than twenty-four hours after receiving Lucy’s letter, his only thought to ride straight for Barton, so he had not had a spare minute to scheme anything else till he knew Elinor would have him. He even confessed, with a soft sheepish smile, that he had half expected her to turn him away, to remember all his old doubts, all the time he had spent pining when he thought he could never have her. Elinor teased him for it, chiding him gently for lingering at Norland back when he was still engaged to Lucy, leading all the Dashwoods to hope for what he thought he could never give. He could only plead that he had been too ignorant of his own heart, too certain that his promise to Lucy would keep him safe from falling for her.

When Colonel Brandon arrived at Barton four days after Edward, he completed the whole group’s joy. For the first time since they had moved to the cottage at Barton, Mrs. Dashwood had more guests than she had rooms for: Edward got the spare bedroom inside, and the Colonel walked back every night to his old quarters at Barton Park, returning early each morning just in time to interrupt the two lovers’ quiet pre-breakfast tête-à-tête. The Colonel, who had spent three weeks at Delaford calculating the unfair gap between his thirty-six years and Marianne’s seventeen, arrived in a low, somber mood, but soon revived entirely in the warmth of the Dashwood’s welcome. He had not heard a single whisper of Lucy’s elopement, and when the whole story was laid out for him, he could only rejoice that the living he had gifted to Edward would now secure Elinor’s happiness too. The two gentlemen grew fast friends immediately: their shared good sense, gentle principles, and devotion to two sisters who loved each other more than anything made their bond inevitable and instant.

The letters that arrived from London soon after, once sure to make Elinor’s heart race with anxiety, now made her laugh. Mrs. Jennings wrote first, chattering about the whole absurd scandal, declaring poor heartbroken Edward must be shattered at Oxford, crying that Lucy had been so sly no one had suspected a thing, even stealing poor Nancy Steele’s money so she could not afford to get to Plymouth, forcing Mrs. Jennings to give her five guineas to go stay with Mrs. Burgess in Exeter and chase after the doctor she’d set her cap for next. John Dashwood’s letter was far more solemn: he described Mrs. Ferrars as completely prostrate with grief, Fanny in agonies of sensibility, declared that Robert and Lucy’s crime was unpardonable and their names would never be spoken in her hearing, and suggested Edward send a proper groveling letter of submission to Fanny, for Fanny to show their mother, to win back her favor.

Edward scoffed at the idea of a groveling submission, saying he would not beg his mother’s pardon for Robert’s treachery and his own honor. But Elinor gently talked him around to a compromise: he could go to London in person, not write a letter, ask for forgiveness for the old imprudent engagement to Lucy, and break the news of their engagement to Fanny face to face. Even Marianne, soft and newly reasonable, chimed in that if John and Fanny actually helped reconcile Edward to his mother, she would even admit they were not entirely without merit.

Colonel Brandon and Edward left Barton together only four days later, bound first for Delaford, so Edward could see the little parsonage that would be his home with Elinor, to walk its grounds and map out small, happy improvements for their future, before Edward went on to London to make his peace with his family. The road ahead was bright, and every one of them knew it.
