The whole of that Thursday night dragged by without a single unexpected event to ruffle the house. When Friday dawned, though, two pieces of news broke that settled soft and unsettling over all of us.

First off, the baker’s delivery lad swore blind he’d run into Rosanna Spearman the previous afternoon, wrapped in a thick veil, tramping the moor footpath toward Frizinghall. Now, you’d think no soul in the county could mix Rosanna up with anyone else, not with that twisted, hunched shoulder marking her plain as day, poor girl. But the lad had to be wrong—Rosanna, as we all knew, had been laid up ill in her bedroom the entire Thursday afternoon, never once putting a foot downstairs.

The second bit of news came by way of the postman. That cheery little Mr. Candy, our local doctor, had joked on the birthday night as he drove off through the lashing rain that a doctor’s skin was naturally waterproof. Turns out his skin wasn’t half as waterproof as he claimed. He’d caught a fierce chill that night, and now he was laid low with a raging fever, light-headed in delirium, chattering nonsense as easily as he used to spout silly quips when he was in his right mind. We all felt for the little man, but Franklin Blake seemed far more upset on Rachel’s account than anything else. Over breakfast, right there in front of me while I waited on the table, he told Lady Verinder that if the mystery of the Moonstone didn’t get sorted soon, Rachel was going to fray so bad she’d need the very best medical attention we could get our hands on.

Barely half an hour after breakfast cleared away, a telegram arrived from old Mr. Blake in reply to his son’s earlier note. He told us he’d pulled strings through his friend the Metropolitan Police Commissioner, and tracked down exactly the right man for the job: Sergeant Cuff. He’d be arriving on the morning train from London, due before noon.

Franklin jumped clean out of his chair when he read the Sergeant’s name. Turns out he’d heard wild, hair-raising anecdotes about Cuff from his father’s lawyer back when he was staying in London.
“I’d dare say we’re near the end of all this mess already,” he said, grinning for the first time since the Diamond went missing. “If half the stories are true, there isn’t a man alive in all England better at untangling a messy mystery than Sergeant Cuff.”

Every last one of us was buzzing, impatient to lay eyes on this legendary detective. Superintendent Seegrave, rolling back up to the house right on schedule as he was told, heard the news about Cuff and immediately locked himself in a private room with pen, ink, and paper, scribbling a long, detailed report so he wouldn’t look like a fool in front of the London expert. I’d have happily trundled down to the station myself to meet the Sergeant, but Lady Verinder’s best carriage and horses were far too fancy to waste on a police officer, even the great Cuff, and the small pony chaise was reserved for Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite later that day. Godfrey was gutted he had to leave his aunt mid-crisis, so he’d kindly pushed his departure back to catch the very last train that night, just long enough to hear what the clever London copper made of the case. He had a committee of struggling ladies’ charities waiting to consult him first thing Saturday morning, so he couldn’t put off leaving any longer.

When the train was due, I tramped down to the front gate to keep watch.
A hired fly pulled up the drive just as I reached the lodge, and out stepped a grizzled, elderly man so painfully thin he looked like he didn’t carry a single spare ounce of flesh anywhere on his bones. He was dressed head to toe in sober black, a plain white cravat knotted tight around his neck. His face was sharp as a hatchet, his skin yellow, dry, and withered like an autumn oak leaf. His pale steely-grey eyes had a very disconcerting habit: when they locked onto yours, he looked like he was digging out a secret you didn’t even know you were hiding from yourself. His footsteps were soft as a cat’s, his voice low and mournful, his long lanky fingers hooked like a bird’s claws. He could have passed for a country parson, or an undertaker—any respectable man you could name, except what he really was. You’d hunt the whole country over and never find a man more different from blustery, red-faced Superintendent Seegrave, or less comforting to look at when your whole household is tangled up in a terrible theft.

“Lady Verinder’s house, is this?” he asked.
“Yes, sir.”
“I’m Sergeant Cuff.”
“This way, if you please, sir.”

On the walk up to the manor, I told him my name and my position as the household steward, to let him know he could speak freely about the case with me. Not a single question about the case did he ask, though. He just admired the landscaped grounds, and commented that the sea air off the moors was brisk and refreshing. Privately, I found myself wondering how on earth this quiet, odd fellow had ever earned his famous reputation. We walked up to the house like two strange hounds chained together for the very first time, neither one quite trusting the other.

We asked for Lady Verinder, and found out she was in the back conservatory, so we cut around the side of the house to the gardens, and sent a servant to fetch her. While we waited, Sergeant Cuff peered through the arch of evergreen shrubs on our left, spotted our rose garden, and tramped straight into it, showing the very first spark of genuine interest I’d seen from him yet. To the gardener’s total shock, and my absolute disgust, this famous detective turned out to be a walking encyclopedia of rose-growing trivia.

“Ah, you’ve got the perfect south and south-west exposure here,” the Sergeant said, nodding his grizzled head, a faint little flicker of pleasure warming his mournful voice. “This is exactly the right layout for a rosery—nothing beats a circular bed set inside a square, with wide paths between every bed. But these gravel paths won’t do, my man. Grass walks, gardener—grass between the roses. Gravel’s far too harsh for them. That bed there, mixing white roses and blush pink roses? That’s perfectly lovely, they always complement each other. And here’s the old English white musk rose, Mr. Betteredge—holding its own right alongside all the fanciest new imported varieties. Precious thing,” he said, stroking the musk rose gently with his long hooked fingers, talking to it like it was a small child.

A fine sort of man to get back Rachel’s Moonstone and track down the thief who stole it, I thought to myself.

“You’re very fond of roses, Sergeant?” I asked.
“I haven’t got much spare time to be fond of anything,” he said. “But when I do get a moment of spare affection to dole out, nine times out of ten, it goes to the roses. I grew up running around my father’s nursery garden, and please God, I’ll end my days there too. One of these days, I’ll hang up my thief-catching boots, and spend the rest of my life growing roses. Grass walks, gardener, between every single bed,” he added, still clearly sore about our gravel paths.

“Seems an odd hobby for a man in your line of work, sir,” I dared to say.
“If you bothered to look around you, which most folks won’t,” he said, “you’d see a man’s favourite pastime is almost always the exact opposite of his day job. Show me two things more opposite than a rose and a thief, and I’ll rethink my tastes—though it’s probably too late for me to change them at my age. The damask rose makes a fine sturdy stock for the more tender varieties, right, gardener? I thought so. There’s a lady coming now. That’s Lady Verinder, isn’t it?”

He spotted her before either me or the gardener did, even though we knew exactly which direction to look for her, and he didn’t. That’s when I started to suspect he was a good deal sharper than he’d seemed at first glance.

Either the sight of him, or the news of his errand, put Lady Verinder in a rare fluster. For the first time in all the years I’d known her, she was lost for words meeting a stranger. Sergeant Cuff put her at ease in two seconds flat. He asked if anyone else had been called in to work on the robbery before him, and when he found out Superintendent Seegrave was still on the premises, he asked permission to speak to the Superintendent first before doing anything else at all.

Lady Verinder led the way back to the house. Before he followed her, Cuff paused to get one last word in with the gardener about the paths. “Get her ladyship to let you put down grass,” he said, glowering at the gravel. “No gravel! No gravel at all.”

I can’t explain why Superintendent Seegrave looked two sizes smaller than life the second he was introduced to Sergeant Cuff. It’s just a fact of the day, that’s all. The two of them shut themselves in a room together for what felt like hours, no intruders allowed. When they finally came out, Superintendent Seegrave was bright-eyed and over-excited, and Sergeant Cuff was yawning like he was bored out of his mind.

“The Sergeant wishes to see Miss Verinder’s sitting room,” Seegrave announced to me, puffing out his chest like he was a general addressing a private. “He may have questions to ask. You are to attend him, if you please!”

While he was ordering me around like that, I glanced over at the great Sergeant Cuff. He was staring back at Seegrave with that same quiet, waiting look he’d given everyone else so far. I won’t say I knew for sure he was just sitting there waiting for Seegrave to make a total fool of himself—I’ll only admit I strongly suspected it.

I led the way upstairs. The Sergeant moved softly, checking every inch of the Indian cabinet and the whole of Rachel’s little decorated boudoir, firing off questions—hardly any for Seegrave, almost all for me—that neither of us could make head nor tail of. Before long, he reached the door, and came face to face with that famous decorative paintwork we all knew so well. He laid one lean, inquisitive finger on the tiny smear just below the lock—the very same mark Superintendent Seegrave had noticed when he’d yelled at all the maids for crowding into the room.

“That’s a shame,” Cuff said. “How did this happen?”

He asked me, not Seegrave. I told him the maids had crowded into the boudoir the previous morning, and some of their long petticoats must have brushed the paint and left the mark. “And Superintendent Seegrave kicked them all out before they could do any more damage, sir,” I added.

“Right!” Seegrave barked in his gruff military tone. “I ordered them out. Petticoats did it, Sergeant—plain and simple, the petticoats did it.”

“Did you notice which petticoat made the mark?” Cuff asked me again, still ignoring the Superintendent.

“No, sir.”

He turned to Seegrave then. “And you noticed, I suppose?”

Seegrave looked completely caught off guard, though he tried to recover fast. “I can’t recall it off the top of my head, Sergeant. It’s a mere trifle, after all—just a tiny trifle.”

Sergeant Cuff stared at Seegrave the exact same way he’d stared at the gravel paths in the rosery, and spoke in that same soft mournful voice, giving us the first taste of just how sharp he really was.
“I was working a private inquiry last week, Mr. Superintendent,” he said. “At one end of the case, there was a murder. At the other end, there was a random spot of ink on a tablecloth no one could explain. In all my years working the darkest, messiest corners of this world, I have never once come across such a thing as a trifle. Before we take one more step in this investigation, we are going to find the exact petticoat that made that smear, and we are going to prove for certain exactly when that paint was wet.”

Seegrave took the dressing-down pretty sulkily, and asked if he should round up all the maids for questioning. Sergeant Cuff thought for a full minute, then sighed and shook his head.
“No,” he said. “We’ll sort out the paint timeline first. That’s a simple yes-or-no question, quick work. Questioning all the maids about their petticoats will drag on for hours. What time was it when the servants crowded into this room yesterday morning? Eleven o’clock, was it? Is there anyone in this house who knows for certain whether that paint was wet or dry at eleven o’clock yesterday morning?”

“Mr. Franklin Blake, her ladyship’s nephew, knows,” I said.

“Is the gentleman in the house?”

Franklin was hovering right nearby, waiting impatiently for his chance to meet the famous Sergeant. He was in the room half a second later, and laid out his evidence right away:
“That door, Sergeant,” he said, “Miss Verinder painted it herself, under my eye, with my help, using a special varnish formula I mixed myself. Whatever colours you mix with that varnish dry completely, to the touch, in exactly twelve hours.”

“Do you remember exactly when the smeared patch under the lock was finished, sir?” Cuff asked.
“Perfectly,” Franklin said. “That was the very last little bit of the whole door left to paint. We were determined to finish it Wednesday, and I myself put the final touches to it around three o’clock that afternoon, or not long after.”

“Today is Friday,” Cuff said, turning back to Seegrave. “Let’s do the sums, sir. At three o’clock Wednesday afternoon, that patch of paint was finished. The varnish dries it completely in twelve hours—so that paint was fully dry by three o’clock Thursday morning. At eleven o’clock Thursday morning, you held your little inquiry in this room. Eleven minus three is eight. That paint had been dry for eight whole hours, Mr. Superintendent, when you decided the maids’ petticoats had smeared it.”

That was the first total knockout blow for poor blundering Seegrave. If he hadn’t singled out my daughter Penelope as a suspect earlier, I might even have felt sorry for him.

Once that paint timeline was sorted, Sergeant Cuff clearly wrote Seegrave off as a lost cause completely, and started talking only to Franklin, who was far more use to him.
“You’ve very likely just handed us the whole clue to this mess, sir,” he said.

The second the words left his mouth, the bedroom door flew open, and Rachel Verinder stepped out among all of us without warning.
She stared straight at the Sergeant, acting like she didn’t even notice he was a total stranger.
“Did you say,” she asked, pointing straight at Franklin, “that he had put the clue into your hands?”

“This is Miss Verinder,” I whispered, right behind the Sergeant’s shoulder.

“That gentleman, miss,” Cuff said, those steely grey eyes of his not leaving her face for a single second, “may very well have put the clue into our hands.”

Rachel turned for half a second like she was going to look at Franklin, but she snapped her gaze away again before their eyes could even meet. There was something wild and unsettled running through her. She flushed bright red, then went deathly pale. When the colour drained from her face, there was a new, sharp look in her eyes that made my blood run cold.

“Thank you for answering my question, miss,” Cuff said. “Now I’d like to ask you one in turn. There’s a strange smear on the paintwork of your door, right there. Do you know when it was made? Or who made it?”

Instead of answering him at all, Rachel just kept right on talking, like he hadn’t spoken, like she hadn’t even heard him.
“Are you another police officer, then?” she asked him.
“I’m Sergeant Cuff, miss, of the Detective Police.”
“Do you think a young lady’s advice is worth listening to?”
“I’d be very glad to hear what you have to say, miss.”
“Do your duty all on your own—and don’t take a single bit of help from Mr. Franklin Blake!”

She said those words so spitefully, so viciously, with such a burst of raw, unprovoked anger directed at Franklin ringing in her voice, that even though I’d known her since she was a tiny baby, even though I loved and respected her next to Lady Verinder herself, I was ashamed of her for the very first time in my whole life.

Sergeant Cuff’s unblinking eyes never moved an inch from her face. “Thank you, miss,” he said. “Do you know anything about that smear on the door? Could you have made it by accident yourself?”
“I know nothing at all about the smear.”

With that, she turned on her heel, slammed the bedroom door shut, and locked herself inside. This time, I heard what Penelope had heard the day before: the second she was alone, she burst out sobbing uncontrollably.

I couldn’t bear to look at the Sergeant, so I stared at Franklin, who was standing nearest to me. He looked far more broken and distressed by Rachel’s outburst than I was.
“I told you I was worried about her,” he said. “Now you see why.”

“Miss Verinder’s just a little out of sorts about losing her valuable diamond,” Cuff remarked, calm as anything. “It’s a very expensive jewel. It’s only natural! Perfectly natural!”

That was the exact same excuse I’d made for Rachel, back when she’d snapped at Superintendent Seegrave the day before—and now a total stranger, a man with no personal stake in her reputation, was making the very same excuse for her. A cold, unaccountable shudder ran right through me. I realise now that was the exact moment I felt the first flicker of a terrible, unspoken new suspicion bloom in Sergeant Cuff’s mind—all from what he’d seen Rachel do, and heard Rachel say, in that first strange, hostile meeting.

“A young lady’s tongue is a privileged thing, sir,” Cuff said to Franklin. “Let’s forget that outburst ever happened, and get straight back to business.
