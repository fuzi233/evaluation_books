An earnest letter, once loosed from the hand, would not lie quietly in the mind of her that wrote it. Margaret had poured her whole strength into Luke’s charge—her father’s dying sight, whether vision or sweet delusion, and her own faith harnessed to it like a cart-horse to a heavy wain. But when the words were gone and Luke with them, the heat ran out of her as from an oven when the door is left ajar. Doubt blew in cold. She began to marvel at her own credulity; then her conscience, sharp as vinegar on a raw tongue, stung her about Luke—of all men, Luke, whom she had sent on a road of danger for a hope that might be no more than smoke.

Catherine came and rated her soundly, and Margaret paid, as folk do, for that sudden lifting of spirits—by a deeper sinking after. She sat in a stupor that was not rest, hearing too much and too little at once: the thin domestic sounds that should have steadied her—the scrape of a chair-leg, the creak of a latch, the soft patter of the babe’s little breath—seemed to fall away behind a louder inward noise, a roaring as of a mill-wheel in flood. It made her long for one true, wholesome sound to lay upon her heart like a warm cloth: a fire crackling, a kettle singing, the firm rhythm of work done at the hearth.

In that ebb of spirit Joan Ketel found her.

Joan came in as if she brought summer with her, cheeks bright from walking, eyes alight with an idea. She was one of those women whose very step makes a room sound fuller of life: her skirts brushed, her shoes tapped briskly, and she spoke in a voice that had never learned to whisper despair.

“Margaret,” she cried, “I know the cure for thine ill. The hermit of Gouda—a wondrous holy man. Why, he can tell what is coming, when he is in the mood.”

“Ay, I have heard of him,” said Margaret, not lifting her eyes. Hope, to her, was like a dish she had once loved and now could not bear the smell of.

But Joan, who had learnt what kind of physic Margaret could give, had the courage to press. She coaxed and urged with that obstinate tenderness which is half mothering, half command, until at last Margaret let herself be led, as a sick body lets itself be carried into air.

They made ready as for a little errand of housewifery—because women’s lives, even at the edge of tragedy, must be tied down with baskets and bread. Joan brought butter and eggs, the best they had, laid in a basket with a clean cloth over them, as if the very gift must be wholesome, yellow, and sound. The butter was firm and sweet, smelling of churn and cool dairy; the eggs warm from the nest. Margaret’s fingers, handling them, remembered for a moment that the world was still made of tangible things.

What had made the pair such fast friends, these last weeks, was itself a story of the hearth—the kind of miracle that hides in a kitchen garden.

Some six weeks ago Jorian Ketel had fallen ill of a bowel disease. It began with raging pain; and when that went off, leaving him like a wrung rag, an awkward symptom came after—nothing, either liquid or solid, would stay in his stomach a minute. Bread, broth, even water, all returned as fast as fear. The doctor had come in his great importance and had said, as if death were a matter of recipe, “He must die if this goes on many hours; therefore boil thou now a chicken with a golden angel in the water, and let him sup that!”

So they had fetched a chicken and killed it. The scalding water steamed; the feathers came away; the bird went into the pot. A gold coin—holy money, they said, for its stamping—clinked in the simmer. The broth rose and fell, slick with fat, smelling rich and promising. Joan had ladled it out with trembling hands.

Alas! Gilt chicken broth shared the fate of the humbler viands that had gone before it. It stayed with him no more than the rest.

Then, because men love far-fetched marvels, the cure was changed: they steeped the thumb of St. Sergius in beef broth, as if sainthood could be boiled into a pan and swallowed. The beef had its deep, brown savor; the relic lay in it like a pale root. Same result.

Then Joan, spent and weeping, ran to Margaret to borrow linen to make his shroud.

“Let me see him,” said Margaret.

She went in, and the room struck her first by its staleness: a sick-sweet heat, close as a shut cupboard. The air had no honest sound in it either—no breeze at the casement, only the wet swallow and retch of the man fighting his own body.

She took his wrist and felt his pulse under her fingers; her face did not change much, but there was a certain quickening in her eye, as when a cook tastes a pot and knows at once what is missing.

“Ah!” said she. “I doubt they have not gone to the root. Open the window! Art stifling him; now change all his linen.”

“Alack, woman, what for?” Joan had cried, half in despair, half in household thrift. “Why foul more linen for a dying man?”

“Do as thou art bid,” said Margaret, dully; and she left the room as if to fetch something common, not a marvel.

Joan, whose hands were used to kneading dough and wringing cloth, found herself obeying like a child under a schoolmistress’s eye. The casement was thrown wide; new air came in with a rush and a whole choir of outside sounds—the distant clop of a hoof, the chirr of sparrows in the eaves, a cart somewhere on the road. The room seemed to sigh.

Margaret returned with her apron full of a flowering herb, freshly gathered, still damp at the stalk, smelling green and faintly sweet when bruised. Her eyes had seen it from the window—the very kind of seeing that marks a housewife and an herb-wife both: the knowledge that remedies may grow under one’s nose while folk send to saints and cities.

She set water to heat and made a decoction, stirring as if she had done it all her life—watching the first shiver, then the steady simmer, not letting it boil too hard lest the virtue be driven off in steam. The scent came up: sweet at first, then with a bitterness that tightened the back of the throat like good ale. She strained it, careful as a cook skimming a sauce, and carried it to the bed.

Before giving it, she took a spoonful herself and smacked her lips—too openly pleased for sincerity.

“That is fair,” said Jorian, with a feeble attempt at mirth. “Why, ’tis sweet, and now ’tis bitter.”

She drew him into talk as soon as he had swallowed it—because the mind, held in company, helps the body keep its hold. And lo, this bitter-sweet stayed by him. Seeing which, she built on it as cards are built: the third spoonful she strengthened with a very little schiedam, just enough spirit to warm the stomach without whipping it; in the seventh she slipped a little beaten yolk of egg, as gently as one enriches a thin broth, to give it body and gentleness. So, with the patience of her sex and the cunning of a good cook who will not spoil a dish by haste, she coaxed his body out of Death’s grasp; and finally Nature—being patted on the back instead of kicked under the bed—set Jorian Ketel on his legs again.

But the doctress made them both swear never to tell a soul her guilty deed.

“They would put me in prison, away from my child.”

The simple that saved him was called sweet feverfew. She had gathered it in his own garden.

That was the root of Joan’s devotion. Gratitude, when it is true, is like a loaf baked in a poor oven: small maybe, but it feeds.

So now Joan and Margaret went on toward Gouda, the basket on Joan’s arm, their steps making two different musics on the road—Joan’s brisk certainty, Margaret’s slower tread, as if her thoughts weighed at her ankles. The land lay domestic around them: hedges, ditches, flat fields, the smell of wet earth and cow-breath, and now and then the clean cry of a bird cutting the air like a knife. Even sorrow walked among these ordinary things.

They reached the hermit’s cave and found the little platform where folk set their offerings. They placed the butter and eggs there with a kind of reverence—food, the honest tribute of women.

Margaret then stooped, and put her mouth to the aperture made for that purpose, and spoke into the darkness:

“Holy hermit, we bring thee butter and eggs of the best; and I, a poor deserted girl—wife, yet no wife—and mother of the sweetest babe, come to pray thee tell me whether he is quick or dead, true to his vows or false.”

For a moment there was only the damp silence of stone. Then a faint voice issued from within, thin as breath through reeds:

“Trouble me not with the things of earth, but send me a holy friar; I am dying.”

“Alas!” cried Margaret, and the cry was not for herself alone. “Is it e’en so, poor soul? Then let us in to help thee.”

“Saints forbid! Thine is a woman’s voice. Send me a holy friar.”

So ended the errand, like a pot that will not come to boil no matter how long it sits on the fire.

They went back as they came. Joan could not help saying, with rough justice, “Are women imps o’ darkness then, that they must not come anigh a dying bed?”

But Margaret was too deeply dejected to answer. The refusal had struck her like a slap—another door shut in her face, another tongue saying in effect: you are earth, you are unclean, you are not fit. Words have their power; they set ranks in the world. Friar, hermit, woman: three names, and in them a whole order of who may speak and who must be silent. Margaret, who had lived between tongues and stations, felt again how a dominant lexicon can bruise the soul without drawing blood.

Joan tried her homely consolations, as one tries to cheer a sick child with broth and warm covering; but Margaret did not seem to hear until Joan spoke of Jorian.

“And Jorian will speak out ere long,” Joan said. “He is just on the boil. He is very grateful to thee, believe it.”

“Seeing is believing,” replied Margaret, quietly bitter; for she had been fed on assurances and found them thin.

“Not but what he thinks you might have saved him with something more out o’ the common than yon. ‘A man of my inches to be cured wi’ feverfew,’ says he. ‘Why, if there is a sorry herb,’ says he. ‘Why, I was thinking o’ pulling all mine up,’ says he.”

Joan’s voice took on the relish of repeating a husband’s foolishness, the way women season their talk with what makes it go down.

“I up and told him remedies were none the better for being far-fetched; you and feverfew cured him, when the grand medicines came up faster than they went down. So says I, ‘You may go down on your four bones to feverfew.’ But indeed, he is grateful at bottom; you are all his thought and all his chat. But he sees Gerard’s folk coming around ye, and good friends, and he said only last night—”

“Well?” Margaret’s head lifted a little at that name, as a starving person lifts at the word bread.

“He made me vow not to tell ye.”

“Prithee, tell me.”

“Well,” said Joan, wrestling between oath and pity, “he said: ‘An’ if I tell what little I know, it won’t bring him back, and it will set them all by the ears. I wish I had more headpiece,’ said he; ‘I am sore perplexed. But least said is soonest mended.’ Yon is his favourite word; he comes back to’t from a mile off.”

Margaret shook her head. The road seemed to widen into dark water under her feet.

“Ay,” said she low, “we are wading in deep waters, my poor babe and me.”

It was Saturday night, and no Luke.

“Poor Luke!” said Margaret at last, with a tenderness that was almost pain. “It was very good of him to go on such an errand.”

“He is one out of a hundred,” replied Catherine warmly.

Margaret’s hand went, almost without her knowing, as if to the place where her child lay, to that little warm weight that was both comfort and fear.

“Mother,” she said, “do you think he would be kind to little Gerard?”

“I am sure he would,” Catherine answered. “So do you be kinder to him when he comes back! Will ye now?”

“Ay,” said Margaret.

And the house settled again into its small sounds—the soft stir of bedding, the faint creak of a board—domestic noises that tried to hold together a life being pulled apart.

Brother Clement, directed by the nuns, had avoided a bend in the river and strode lustily forward, his sandals beating a steady monk’s rhythm on the road. He reached a station some miles nearer the coast than that where Luke lay in wait for Gerard Eliassoen. The next morning he started early and was in Rotterdam at noon.

He made at once for the port, not to keep Jerome waiting. The air there was full of salt and tar, and the clang of work—ropes slapping, men shouting, gulls crying overhead with their harsh laughter. A port is a place where sound rules: every call is command, every creak and groan of timber a warning. Clement felt it in his bones; it set the nerves on edge, and he longed for the cloister’s measured chant, where each syllable has its appointed place and silence is not emptiness but peace.

He observed several monks of his order on the quay and went to them; but Jerome was not amongst them. He asked one whether Jerome had arrived.

“Surely, brother,” was the reply.

“Prithee, where is he?”

“Where? Why, there!” said the monk, pointing to a ship in full sail.

Clement now noticed that all the monks were looking seaward. The ship was already drawing away, its canvas belly full, its ropes singing in the wind. It was as if the sea itself had swallowed Jerome up between one heartbeat and the next.

“What, gone without me! Oh, Jerome! Jerome!” cried Clement, and his cry was torn by the wind and scattered among the port’s other noises, a small human anguish lost under commerce.

Several friars turned and stared.

“You must be brother Clement,” said one at length; and then they kissed him and greeted him with brotherly warmth, and gave him a letter Jerome had charged them with.

It was a hasty scrawl. The writer told him coldly that a ship was about to sail for England and he was loth to lose time. Clement might follow if he pleased, but would do much better to stay behind and preach to his own country folk.

“Give the glory to God, brother; you have a wonderful power over Dutch hearts; but you are no match for those haughty islanders: you are too tender.

“Know thou that on the way I met one, who asked me for thee under the name thou didst bear in the world. Be on thy guard! Let not the world catch thee again by any silken net. And remember, Solitude, Fasting, and Prayer are the sword, spear, and shield of the soul. Farewell.”

Clement stood with the salt wind worrying the paper, and felt as if he had been struck across the face. Deserted; not only left behind, but measured and found wanting—too tender—as if tenderness were a fault in Christ’s service. The letter’s tone was like a knife wiped clean: no warmth, no fellowship, only counsel and dismissal.

He promised the good monks to sleep at the convent and to preach wherever the prior should appoint—for Jerome had raised him to the skies as a preacher—and then withdrew abruptly, for he was cut to the quick and wanted to be alone.

Walking, he questioned himself as a penitent questions his soul in the dark: Was there some incurable fault in him, repulsive to so true a son of Dominic? Or was Jerome himself devoid of that Christian Love which St. Paul had placed above Faith itself? They had been shipwrecked together, saved on the same fragment of the wreck; Jerome had been his pupil, his penitent, his son in the Church; for four hundred miles they had travelled as fellow-servants in Christ—and now he was shaken off like dirt at the first opportunity, with harsh and cold disdain.

“Why, worldly hearts are no colder nor less trusty than this,” Clement said aloud, and the port’s racket answered him without pity. “The only one that ever really loved me lies in a grave hard by. Fly me, fly to England, man born without a heart; I will go and pray over a grave at Sevenbergen.”

Three hours later he passed Peter’s cottage. A troop of noisy children were playing about the door—shouting, clapping, chasing one another, their cries bright as sparrows. The house had been repaired, and a new outhouse added; prosperity had laid new boards where misery had once creaked. That quick, careless music of children, so like laughter and so unlike prayer, struck him with a pain he could not bear. He turned his head hastily away, not to disturb a picture his memory treasured, and went on to the churchyard.

There the air changed: fewer voices, more wind, the thin rustle of grass. He sought among the tombstones for Margaret’s. He could not find it. He could not believe they had grudged her even a stone, so he searched the churchyard all over again, counting names and reading them as if the dead could answer in their carved letters.

“Oh poverty! stern poverty!” he murmured. “Poor soul, thou wert like me: no one was left that loved thee when Gerard was gone.”

He went into the church; the hush there folded round him like a cloak. After kissing the steps, he prayed long and earnestly for the soul of her whose resting-place he could not find. In the stillness he could hear the faintest sounds—his own breath, the little tick of cooling stone, the distant cough of a sparrow in the rafters. Such quiet is a balm, and yet it made his loneliness ring louder inside him, as if the absence of human voice were itself an accusation.

Coming out he saw a very old man looking over the little churchyard gate. Clement went to him and asked did he live in the place.

“Four score and twelve years, man and boy,” the old fellow answered. “And I come here every day of late, holy father, to take a peep. This is where I look to bide ere long.”

“My son,” said Clement, “can you tell me where Margaret lies?”

“Margaret? There’s a many Margarets here.”

“Margaret Brandt. She was daughter to a learned physician.”

“As if I didn’t know that,” said the old man pettishly. “But she doesn’t lie here. Bless you, they left this a longful while ago. Gone in a moment, and the house empty. What, is she dead? Margaret a’ Peter dead? Now only think on’t. Like enow; like enow. They great towns do terribly disagree wi’ country folk.”

“What great towns, my son?”

“Well, ’twas Rotterdam they went to from here, so I heard tell; or was it Amsterdam? Nay, I trow ’twas Rotterdam. And gone there to die!”

Clement sighed; the sound seemed to sink into the earth between the graves.

“’Twas not in her face now, that I saw,” the old man went on, softer now, as if memory had taken the edge off his fret. “And I can mostly tell. Alack, there was a blooming young flower to be cut off so soon, and all old weed like me left standing still. Well, well, she was a May rose yon; dear heart, what a winsome smile she had, and—”

“God bless thee, my son,” said Clement; “farewell!” And he hurried away, because the man’s country words—so plain, so rooted—had made Margaret too alive again, and grief too sharp.

He reached the convent at sunset. The bell sounded, deep and measured, gathering the day into prayer; the chant rose, ordered and familiar, as if the very air of the chapel had been trained to carry holy syllables without waste. Clement watched and prayed there for Jerome and for Margaret until it was long past midnight, and his soul, after much beating, recovered its cold calm.
