Margaret severed a generous portion of venison and hurled it through the open window, where it landed squarely in the emerald gaze of the fire-leopards. With a savage, guttural snarl, the beasts lunged forward, tearing and crunching the meat until a sudden, piercing bay from a hound echoed through the hall, causing the trio at the sill to recoil in terror. Realizing her supper was compromised, the leopard slipped away with furtive grace into the treeline. Moments later, the clamor of horses, men, and curs erupted past the window as they pursued the beast across the countryside. Safe in the knowledge that the animal was too swift to be caught within a league of their home, Martin and his companions exhaled, clasping hands in relief. Seizing the brief respite, Margaret allowed herself a quiet sob, which Gerard gently wiped away with a kiss.

Back at the table, Gerard raised his cup to toast "Woman's Wit," declaring it far superior to raw masculine strength. "Only when those she loves are in peril," Margaret replied softly. That night, Gerard lingered beside her longer than custom allowed, returning home buoyed by a pride that felt akin to royalty. Yet, scarcely a mile from home, beneath the canopy of trees, two figures materialized, blocking his path. It was his father and mother. A sudden chill seized him. They stood immovable, grim and silent. Stammering inquiries died on his lips.

"Why ask?" his father growled. "You know why we follow."

"Oh, Gerard!" his mother interjected, her voice a tangled weave of reproach and tenderness.

Gerard's heart hammered against his ribs, silence falling over him like a shroud. His father, however, softened the blow, chiding him gently: "Do not bow your head like a guilty fool. You are not the first young man ensnared by a rosy cheek and azure eyes." Catherine added quickly, attributing their union to witchcraft, citing the fame of Peter the Magician. Her husband, Elias, pressed for a promise that Gerard would cease visiting Sevenbergen, offering leniency for one fault.

"I cannot promise that, Father," Gerard whispered.

"Not promise it, you young hypocrite!" Elias snapped.

"Nay, Father, do not misname me," Gerard pleaded, his voice breaking. "I lacked the courage to speak of what I feared would grieve you. I am grateful to the friend who revealed your knowledge. It is a heavy burden lifted. Yes, Father, I love Margaret. Call me not a priest, for I shall never be one. I would sooner die."

"That we shall see," Elias muttered, his patience fraying. "Come, do not defy me further. You shall learn the price of disrespect."

The return walk was a somber procession, broken only by Catherine's occasional, heavy sigh. From that moment, the tranquil sanctuary of the Tergou house dissolved into a battlefield. The following day, Gerard was summoned before the entire family, assailed by a chorus of condemnation save for little Kate and the dwarf, whose loyalty stemmed from Kate's influence. Cornelis and Sybrandt, his cousins, were particularly venomous. Gerard, stunned by the sudden shift in alliances, cast a desperate glance at his younger sister. Her eyes, usually warm, filled with tears at the harsh words directed at him, who had been the household's favorite just weeks prior. Yet, she offered no solace, turning her face away to mutter, "Pray to Heaven to cure you of this madness!"

"What, am I your enemy too?" Gerard asked, his voice thick with sorrow. Rising with a deep, weary sigh, he departed the house and headed toward Sevenbergen.

Conflict between those bound by affection yet divided by opposing interests begins innocently, often with both parties claiming moral superiority. However, as discord endures, it widens and deepens, fueled by human fallibility and irritability, inevitably leading both sides to error. Gerard, possessing a broader and more cultivated mind, perceived nuances that his hot-tempered kin missed. While Elias raged and the cousins plotted with malice, Gerard suffered in quiet unhappiness, plagued by indecision and a profound loneliness. He had no confidant. Margaret, spirited and proud, he dared not burden with his domestic torment; she might, to save her own future, side with his relatives and abandon him. Though she had been a beacon of comfort in the past, her own history—a lifelong sacrifice of marriage and motherhood for the sake of painting—made him hesitate. Could he ask her to choose between him and the artistic solitude she had chosen?

Just as Gerard teetered on the brink of surrender, a formidable adversary emerged: Catherine Senior. Good-hearted yet unrefined, she lacked the steadiness of her daughter, oscillating wildly between irritation and self-serving attacks. On one occasion, Cornelis suggested that Gerard wed Margaret merely to satisfy hunger. Catherine retorted fiercely, asking what provision the lazy cousins had made for themselves, accusing them of fearing Gerard would claim their inheritance. These moments sparked fleeting, slinking smiles in Gerard, momentarily confusing Catherine's allies. But after six months of relentless friction, the pressure reached a breaking point.

In front of the assembled family, Elias announced that he had ordered the Burgomaster to imprison Gerard in the Stadthouse, preventing his marriage to Margaret. Gerard paled, his blood running cold, yet he forced himself to remain silent. His father continued, decreeing that Gerard would be ordained a priest within the year, regardless of his will.

"Is this so?" Gerard cried, his voice ringing with sudden, fierce resolve. "Hear me, all! By God and Saint Bavon, I swear I will never wear the habit of a priest so long as Margaret lives. If force decides this, then let it be tried, Father! But know this: the day the Burgomaster arrives to drag me away, I shall leave Tergou forever, forsake Holland, and abandon your house, where I have been valued not for myself, but for what you can extract from me."

Spurning the room, he fled, his face white with anguish and despair. Outside, Catherine lamented the cruelty of driving young hearts too hard, fearing he would leave them unmarried or married alike.

Emerging from the house, Gerard met Reicht Heynes, who bore a message from Margaret Van Eyck requesting an audience. Finding the elderly woman sitting sternly, Reicht Heynes questioned why Gerard had neglected her lately. Before Gerard could respond, Reicht accused him in a sneering tone of failing to confess his love.

"It is because you never told her," Reicht cooed, feigning pity.

Gerard, confused and stammering, admitted his fear of being called a fool. Reicht scoffed at the notion, recounting her own youthful rejection of love for art, leaving her a childless widow surrounded by grandchildren while her former lovers thrived. "I look upon you as my son," she declared warmly. "You are good, handsome, and a painter. I will not let you waste your youth as I did yours. Marry this Margaret. I have inquired; she is a dutiful daughter."

Moved by her wisdom, Gerard poured out his heart, and Reicht and Margaret both shed tears of empathy. Women possessed a unique, vicarious courage, unshaken by the tremors that paralyzed men. Through their support, Gerard realized he must seize the initiative. He resolved to marry Margaret immediately, hoping the deed would settle the matter. Yet, listening to their counsel, he hesitated. Fear of his father's wrath, not imprisonment, haunted him. If he defied his father openly, Elias would disown him entirely, condemning Margaret to a lifetime of poverty under a father's curse.

Could he propose a different path? He imagined eloping with Margaret to a foreign land where his art was highly valued, perhaps Italy. After a year or two, free from his family's shadow and financially secure, he could return, begging forgiveness not for rebellion, but for survival, offering only his love and labor, not their wealth.

"The idea is sound," Reicht whispered, her eyes gleaming. "There lies a realm where fortunes await you. Here, art freezes; there, it flourishes like never before."

"Italy!" Gerard exclaimed. "Italy!"

"Yes, Italy!" Reicht affirmed. "Where painters are honored as princes and scribes earn three hundred crowns for a single copy. The Pope seeks skilled hands to transcribe the treasures fleeing Constantinople."

"But the journey costs a fortune," Gerard protested.

"I shall find the means," Reicht promised. "Ten golden angels to buy you passage to Rome. And bring the girl with you, if she loves you truly."

For hours, they sat discussing the plan, and afterward, Gerard carried a secret talisman against the storms of home. Beyond the funds, Margaret Van Eyck gifted him invaluable technical secrets. She spoke of Jan van Eyck's mastery, revealing that the artist's longevity was due to his refusal to rush. He ground his own pigments, preparing panels a year in advance. She warned him against boiling oils, explaining that heat melted vegetable impurities into the heart of the medium, killing the color. Instead, she taught him to wash oil in bottles, decanting the muddy water, and then exposing it to sunlight in a glass trough to clarify it further without turning it to varnish. "Jan never hurried," she concluded, "and neither will the world forget him."
