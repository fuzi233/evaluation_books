Marianne, whose generous spirit had never suffered impertinence, vulgarity, poor wit, or even a trivial difference of taste to pass without quiet irritation, was now so raw with the unspoken grief of Willoughby’s desertion that she could not bear the forward, giddy advances of the Miss Steeles at all. Her unwaveringly cold behaviour snuffed out every attempt the sisters made to grow intimate with the Dashwood household, and Elinor soon noticed that this chill had pushed the two women to favour her above all others—none more so than Lucy, who seized every spare moment to draw Elinor into conversation, chattering freely of her own thoughts and small adventures in an obvious bid to win her friendship.

Lucy was naturally quick-witted, with remarks that were often sharp and surprisingly amusing, and Elinor frequently found her pleasant enough company for half an hour’s stroll. But her native cleverness had never been nurtured by any formal education: she was ignorant, ill-read, and her total lack of general knowledge could not be hidden even by her constant, obvious efforts to appear polished and accomplished. Elinor pitied her, at first, for the wasted gifts a proper education might have turned into something respectable. But that soft pity faded when she saw the total absence of delicacy, honesty, and integrity in the girl, the calculated assiduity and hollow flattery she deployed to win favour at Barton Park. There could be no real pleasure in the company of someone who paired ignorance with total insincerity, who could never meet Elinor on equal ground in conversation, and whose obsequious deference to her felt valueless, given how slyly she treated everyone else around them.

“You will think this a very odd question, I dare say,” Lucy said to her one afternoon, as the two walked back from the grounds of Barton Park to the Dashwood cottage, “but pray tell me—are you personally acquainted with your sister-in-law’s mother, Mrs. Ferrars?”

Elinor did think it an exceedingly odd question, and her face betrayed her surprise even as she replied that she had never once met Mrs. Ferrars.

“Indeed!” Lucy said, blinking. “I wonder at that— I was certain you must have seen her at Norland sometimes. Then I suppose you cannot tell me what sort of a woman she is?”

“No,” Elinor answered carefully, not wanting to voice her quiet private opinion of Edward’s mother, and disinclined to satisfy what felt like impertinent curiosity. “I know nothing at all of her.”

“I am sure you think me very strange, prying about her like this,” Lucy said, her eyes fixed intently on Elinor’s face. “But there are reasons— I wish I might dare to tell you them! But at least you will do me the justice to believe I do not mean to be rude.”

Elinor gave her a civil reply, and they walked on in silence for several minutes, before Lucy spoke again, her voice wavering with apparent hesitation.
“I cannot bear for you to think me nosy and unkind. I would do anything in the world rather than earn a bad opinion from someone whose good opinion is so worth having as yours. I should not fear at all to trust you, and I would be so grateful for your advice on how to manage a situation as uncomfortable as mine— but there is no need to trouble you with it now. I am only sorry you do not know Mrs. Ferrars.”

“I am sorry I do not, too,” Elinor said, utterly bewildered. “If knowing my opinion of her could be of any use to you. But I never had the slightest idea you were connected to that family at all, I confess— so I cannot help being surprised by how seriously you are asking after her character.”

“I do not blame you for being surprised, not at all,” Lucy said, looking down with a soft, bashful little air, only flicking a quick glance at Elinor’s face to gauge her reaction. “But if I dared tell you everything, you would not be half so shocked. Mrs. Ferrars is nothing to me, now— but the time may come, and how soon it comes depends entirely on her, when we will be very closely connected indeed.”

“Good heavens!” Elinor exclaimed, her heart jumping. “What do you mean? Are you acquainted with Mr. Robert Ferrars? Is that possible?” The thought of such a frivolous, scheming girl as a sister-in-law struck her as deeply unpleasant.

“No,” Lucy replied, her eyes now locked steadily on Elinor’s. “Not Mr. Robert Ferrars. I have never even met him. I am acquainted with his eldest brother.”

For a beat, Elinor could not breathe. Shock washed over her so sharply it would have been agonizing, if she had not immediately rejected the claim as impossible. She turned to stare at Lucy in silence, her mind reeling, unable to fathom what lie the girl could be telling or why. Though her face flushed pale then hot, she stood firm in her disbelief, no hint of a swoon or fit clouding her resolve.

“You are quite right to be surprised,” Lucy continued. “You could never have suspected this before, I am sure— for he never dropped the smallest hint of it to you or any of your family. We meant this to be a great secret, and I have kept it perfectly faithfully until this very hour. Not a single soul in my family knows of it but my sister Anne, and I would never have told you if I did not trust your secrecy completely. I thought it only fair to explain, after I asked you so many odd questions about Mrs. Ferrars. And Mr. Ferrars will not be displeased when he learns I trusted you— he has the highest respect for all your family, and looks on you and your sisters as if you were his own.”

Elinor was silent for long moments, too stunned to speak. At last she forced herself to respond, her tone calm enough to hide most of her astonishment and quiet dread. “May I ask how long your engagement has stood?”

“We have been engaged these four years.”

“Four years!” Elinor echoed, reeling, but still refusing to believe it. “I did not even know you were acquainted until just the other day.”

“Our acquaintance stretches back many years, long before that,” Lucy said. “He was under my uncle’s care for a long while, you know.”

“Your uncle?”

“Mr. Pratt, at Longstaple, near Plymouth. Did he never speak to you of Mr. Pratt? He lived there four years, as a pupil. My sister Anne and I often stayed at my uncle’s house, that is how we met. We became engaged a year after he left his studies there, and he spent almost all his free time visiting us afterwards. I was most unwilling to agree to an engagement without his mother’s knowledge and approval, as you may well imagine, but I was too young, and loved him too well, to be as prudent as I ought to have been. You do not know him as well as I do, Miss Dashwood, but you have surely seen enough of him to know how easy it is for a woman to grow sincerely attached to him.”

“Certainly,” Elinor heard herself say, though she scarcely knew what she spoke. Then a surge of faith in Edward’s honour washed over her, and she was certain Lucy was lying. “Engaged to Mr. Edward Ferrars! I must beg your pardon, but there must be some mistake of name or person. We cannot possibly mean the same Mr. Ferrars.”

“We mean no other,” Lucy laughed softly. “Mr. Edward Ferrars, eldest son of Mrs. Ferrars of Park Street, brother to your sister-in-law John Dashwood’s wife. You cannot possibly think I would be mistaken in the name of the man all my happiness depends on.”

“It is most strange,” Elinor said, caught in a painful, confused tangle of doubt, “that he never once mentioned your name to me.”

“Considering how carefully we hid our connection, that is not strange at all,” Lucy replied. “You knew nothing of me or my family, so there was no reason to speak my name, and he was always so afraid his sister Fanny would suspect something— that was more than enough reason for him to keep quiet.”

Elinor’s fragile hope of Lucy’s falsehood crumbled, though her self-possession did not break. “Four years you have been engaged,” she said, her voice steady.

“Yes, and heaven knows how much longer we will have to wait,” Lucy sighed. “Poor Edward! It makes him so hopeless.” She pulled a tiny, painted miniature out of her pocket. “To make sure there is no mistake, please look at this face. It does not do him justice, I know, but I am sure you cannot fail to recognise who it is for. I have carried it now for more than three years.”

She passed the painting to Elinor, and no lingering doubt could survive the sight of it: it was undeniably Edward’s face. Elinor handed it back almost instantly, confirming the likeness.

“I have never been able to give him my own portrait in return, which vexes me terribly— he has begged me for it a hundred times,” Lucy prattled on. “But I shall sit for it the very first chance I get.”

“You are quite right,” Elinor said, her tone perfectly calm. They walked on a few paces in silence, before Lucy spoke again.
“I am sure you will keep this secret faithfully, for you must know how much depends on it. If his mother found out, she would never approve— I have no fortune at all, and I hear she is an exceedingly proud woman.”

“I did not ask you for your confidence,” Elinor said, “but you do me no more than justice to trust me. Your secret is safe with me. But you must pardon my saying I do not see why you chose to tell me this. You cannot possibly have thought my knowing of it would keep it any safer.” She looked hard at Lucy, hoping to spot a flicker of deception in her face, but the girl’s expression never shifted.

“I was afraid you would think I took a great liberty, telling you all this,” Lucy said. “I have not known you long in person, but I have heard descriptions of you and your family for years, and the moment I met you, I felt as if we were old acquaintances. Besides, after asking you so many odd questions about Mrs. Ferrars, I felt I owed you an explanation. I am so unfortunate— I have no one else to turn to for advice. Anne is the only other person who knows, and she has no judgment at all, she does far more harm than good. I am always terrified she will blurt the secret out. You saw the other day, when Sir John mentioned Edward’s name at dinner, I thought my heart would stop, sure she would spill everything. You cannot imagine what I have suffered these four years, all this suspense, seeing him hardly more than twice a year. I wonder my heart has not broken entirely.”

She pulled out a handkerchief to dab at her eyes, but Elinor felt no spark of compassion.
“Sometimes,” Lucy went on, wiping her cheeks, “I think it would be kinder for both of us to break off the engagement entirely.” She stared directly at Elinor as she spoke. “But I never have the resolution to do it. I cannot bear the thought of hurting him so badly. And for my own sake too— he is so dear to me, I do not think I could bear to let him go. What would you do in my place, Miss Dashwood? What advice would you give me?”

“Pardon me,” Elinor said, startled, “but I can give you no advice at all in these circumstances. You must trust your own judgment.”

“Of course,” Lucy said, after a long silence between them. “Someday his mother must provide for him, but poor Edward is so low-spirited. Did you not think him dreadfully down when he stayed at Barton? He was so miserable when he left us at Longstaple to come visit you, I was afraid you would think he was ill.”

“Did he come straight from your uncle’s house to visit us, then?”

“Oh yes, he stayed a full fortnight with us. Did you think he came directly from London?”

“No,” Elinor said, every new detail of Lucy’s account settling over her like cold lead. “I remember he told us he had spent a fortnight with some friends near Plymouth.” She remembered, too, how confused she had been at the time that he would never name those friends, never speak of them further.

“Did you not think him sadly out of spirits?” Lucy repeated.

“We did, especially when he first arrived.”

“I begged him to cheer up for fear you would suspect what was wrong, but he could not help being melancholy— knowing he could not stay more than a fortnight with us, seeing how upset I was. Poor fellow! I am sure he is just as down now; he writes in the lowest of spirits. I got a letter from him just before I left Exeter.” She pulled a letter out of her pocket, letting Elinor glance casually at the handwriting on the address. “You know his hand, I dare say? It is usually so neat, but this is scrawled, he was tired, I expect, he had just filled a whole sheet writing to me.”

Elinor saw that the handwriting was unmistakably Edward’s. All last remaining doubt vanished. The portrait, she had told herself, might have been an accident, not a gift from him. But secret letters, regular correspondence, could only exist under a formal, binding engagement. For a second her head swam, her heart sank so heavy she could barely stand, but she summoned every last scrap of resolution, fighting back the wave of agony that threatened to overwhelm her, and steadied herself almost at once.

“Writing to each other is the only comfort we have in all these long separations,” Lucy said, tucking the letter back into her pocket. “Well, I have his portrait, at least, but poor Edward has none of mine. He says if he had my likeness he would be perfectly content. Last time he visited Longstaple, I gave him a lock of my hair set in a little ring, he says that is some comfort, but not half as good as a portrait. Did you happen to notice that ring on his finger when he stayed with you?”

“I did,” Elinor said, her voice perfectly composed, though a storm of agony, humiliation, and shock raged beneath it, worse than anything she had ever felt in her life.

Thankfully, they reached the cottage just then, and the conversation could not go any further. The Miss Steeles sat with the Dashwoods for a few minutes more before returning to Barton Park, leaving Elinor at last free to sit alone, and grieve the quiet, tender hope of a future with Edward Ferrars that had just been utterly, irrevocably destroyed.
