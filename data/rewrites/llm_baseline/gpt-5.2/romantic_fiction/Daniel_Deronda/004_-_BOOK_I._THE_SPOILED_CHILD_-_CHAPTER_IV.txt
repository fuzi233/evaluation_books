It would have been cruelly unfair to charge the rector of Pennicote with any singular worldliness because, as he turned a matter over and over in his mind like a conscientious housekeeper testing a loaf for hollowness, he concluded that Gwendolen Harleth was a girl meant for a brilliant marriage. He lived among people who weighed daughters as they weighed acres, and thought themselves benevolent in hoping the best price should be offered. Why should he be required, alone of his time and station, to wish his niece a fate less applauded than the common ideal?

Besides, Mr. Gascoigne’s feeling in the matter was kindly—kindly as a well-ordered table is kind, where every dish has its proper hour and the servants know their steps. He was not for fantastical recipes. He did not advise that Gwendolen should dress in meek rags like some sainted Griselda, as if a marquis were to be hooked by the bait of shabbiness; nor did he propose that she should hide herself away, as if a fair fruit kept in a dark pantry grew sweeter by neglect. His calculations were those of a man who believed in rational management: show the best goods in the best light, and the neighborhood’s best society would do its part. He did not so much as imagine contriving melodramatic accidents—no acquiring of frisky horses in order that a gentleman of property might be forced into heroism by saving her from a fall. He wished her well, and meant her to be seen to advantage.

In that, his intention chimed exactly with Gwendolen’s own desire—yet not in the plain way everybody assumed. If you had asked her, she would have admitted, with the air of one granting a tiresome point in conversation, that she must be married some day; and she would have been quietly sure—sure as a woman is sure of the salt in a well-kept cellar—that her marriage could never be of the middling kind most girls were content to serve up as their life’s portion. But her imagination did not dwell on marriage as the end of her drama. In the plays she staged within herself, she was a heroine long before any last act of wedding bells. To be much sued, to have hopeless sighs spent on her as if she were a rarity no one could afford—this was an agreeable proof of power, like the power to lift a whole room’s attention with a single clear word. Yet to become a wife—tied into the daily harness, hemmed in by a domestic “must,” burdened with more children than were desirable, and drowned at last in humdrum—seemed, on the whole, a vexatious necessity.

Her own observation of matrimony had left her with the taste of something over-boiled: nourishment perhaps, but dulled, without savor, and served in portions one could not choose. Marriage was undeniably a social promotion; she could not picture for herself a single life. But promotions have their bitter herbs. A title will not satisfy the man who meant to lead; and this delicate-limbed creature of twenty meant to lead. Such passions do not belong to men only.

In Gwendolen they dwelt amid entirely feminine furniture. They had no relation to learning, to public good, to constitutions balanced and nations improved. Her knowledge was the brisk schooling that lays down rules like hard crackers—dry but making ignorance feel less limp—and the rest of the world she felt she had tasted sufficiently through novels, plays, and poems. Her French and her music—those approved accomplishments that were supposed to give a young lady the right bouquet—did not trouble her. Add to these the native, happy confidence that some people are born with, so that whatever subject they turn to seems immediately to prove their own talent for judging it, and it is not strange that Gwendolen felt ready to manage destiny as another woman manages a household, making everything come when she chooses and in the form that pleases her.

There were, of course, many subjects in which she took no interest—because they were stupid. Subjects often look stupid to the young, as light looks dull to the old. Yet she never felt helpless before them; if they rose in conversation, she could meet them as she met any ordinary dish set before her—touch it, taste it, pronounce upon it with an air of having always known.

No one had ever disputed her power. From the moment of their settling at Offendene, and indeed always, the first thought in the house had been, What will Gwendolen think? If the footman’s boots creaked too heavily along the boards, or if the laundress’s work showed a clumsy seam, the maid’s dismay took the form of prophecy: “This will never do for Miss Harleth.” If the wood smoked in the bedroom grate, Mrs. Davilow—whose own eyes suffered most—apologized to Gwendolen, as if smoke were a personal affront to her daughter’s fastidiousness rather than a common misery to them all.

Even under the small violences of traveling, if Gwendolen did not appear to breakfast until everyone else had finished, the only question became how her coffee and toast might still arrive as if they had just come from the fire—hot, crisp, and fragrant with that clean, comforting smell of browned bread which can make a shabby room feel momentarily civilized. And when she did come in, her light-brown hair freshly brushed and streaming back, waiting for her mother’s hand to coil it up, her large brown eyes flashing—dark and bright as a polished stone washed by a wave—she was the one expected to be tolerant. It was she who must beg Alice, sitting ready to serve, not to hunch her shoulders into that frightful posture, and insist that Isabel should stop pressing close with questions and go away to Miss Merry.

Always she was treated like a princess in exile. If famine came, her breakfast-roll would be made of the finest-bolted flour gleaned, by miracle, from the seven thin ears; if there were a general packing up, her silver fork was to be kept out of the baggage as if it were a relic no rough hand might risk. One might say the reason lay plainly on the surface: her beauty, and something uncommon in her; her decided will making itself felt in the way she moved—graceful, certain—and in the tone of her voice, clear and unhesitating. If she entered a room on a rainy day when everyone else seemed softened and slackened by damp, when the very purpose of things felt blurred, her presence was like a sudden command that life should keep its forms. The very air seemed to tighten; and even hotel-waiters, who can be sluggish as if their ears were clogged with stale clatter, would show quicker alacrity—whisking away crumbs, smoothing creases, clearing the dregs where flies struggled, as if her notice were a kind of judgment.

Add to this that she was the eldest daughter, and her mother had long lived in an apologetic tenderness toward her for the evils a step-father had brought. Surely that might seem explanation enough—sunlight enough to account for daylight.

And yet—beware of conclusions that have not been tested by comparison.

I have seen the same trembling, assiduous service offered to people who were not beautiful, not uncommon, not melodious, not even daughters at all. Some were, in truth, very ordinary men. What they shared with Gwendolen was not charm but a fierce, undisguised determination to have what pleased them, and an entire fearlessness about becoming disagreeable—becoming even dangerous—when they were crossed. Who is so coaxed and waited on, so anxiously soothed, as the unscrupulous creature in a household—especially the male—of whom it is said with a shiver that, if he is not given his way at home, he will go and do worse elsewhere? That fear, like the continual grinding hum of a bad machine, can force even tender hands into obedience.

So it is difficult not to suspect that, even without her beauty and her special place as eldest child, Gwendolen might still have ruled like a queen in exile, simply by virtue of her inborn energy of egoistic desire and her power of inspiring apprehension about what she might say or do. Only—she had the charm as well; and those who feared her were also fond of her, the fear and fondness heightened by that shifting, many-colored quality in her—an iridescence of character, where contrary tendencies played over each other like light over the surface of a changing sea.

For the impossibility of being opposite things “in the same moment” belongs to the clumsy necessities of action, not to the finer, more secret possibilities of feeling. We cannot speak a loyal word and remain meanly silent in the same breath; we cannot kill and not kill at once. But a moment is wide enough for the loyal desire and the mean desire together—for the lash-out of a murderous thought, and the sharp backward stroke of repentance that follows as quickly as a knife drawn back from the board.
