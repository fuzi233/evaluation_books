It was late afternoon when Tito returned home. Romola sat across from the cabinet in their narrow room, her pen moving steadily over parchment as she copied documents. The light was fading, and she was about to pause when he entered—silent, unannounced, with a purpose that seemed carved into his posture. He did not remove his cap or mantle, but leaned one arm on the cabinet, standing directly before her, his gaze fixed.

Romola had spent the day certain of the Frate’s safety, and now felt a quiet remorse for the anger and distrust that had driven her to confront her husband publicly—a matter he had always wished kept private. She replayed the moment in her mind: her outburst, her accusations, the way she had spoken before others. Had she been wrong? Could the political machinations she’d heard even her godfather speak of—those cunning shifts of loyalty, those alliances born of necessity rather than truth—explain Spini’s connection without implicating Tito in any deeper plot?

She wanted to confess her haste, to ask forgiveness. For hours, she had hoped this moment might open a door—perhaps not only to apology, but to words long buried, truths never spoken. Two years of silence had passed between them, so complete that Tito did not know she had once fled from him, nor that she had returned. Their past—the tenderness of youth, the sudden fracture—remained sealed away, like a feast interrupted by death, its table still set but no longer touched.

Now, she looked up at him with softness in her eyes, the kind born of regret. But something in his face stopped her. A subtle shift—tighter lips, colder eyes—made her breath catch. They stood in silence, staring at each other, the air thickening.

Tito knew this was a turning point. Beneath his usual calm, beneath his gentle manner, lay a will that had surfaced permanently now. It showed in the rigid line of his jaw, in the way his body seemed to hold itself back—not from violence, but from yielding. He was not angry. He was decisive.

“Romola,” he said, voice cool and clear, like water over stone, “it is time we understood each other.”

“Yes,” she whispered, her voice barely audible. “That is what I most desire.”

Her face, pale and composed, bore no trace of the fury she had worn earlier. Only sorrow and doubt remained. In contrast, Tito’s presence loomed larger—strong, unyielding. He continued.

“You took a step this morning,” he said, “one that was useless. It drew attention. And it may bring trouble upon me.”

“I acknowledge it,” she said, speaking more firmly now. “I was too hasty. I am sorry if I wronged you.” She hoped this would soften him. Then, perhaps, she could say more.

But Tito did not relent. “I wish you to understand once and for all,” he said, tone unchanged, “that such actions are incompatible with our marriage. Think carefully about how you came to act. Reflect so this does not happen again.”

“That depends chiefly on you, Tito,” she replied, surprised by the heat in her own voice.

He smiled faintly. “You would say, then, that nothing should arise to stir your suspicions? You were frank enough last night to admit you do not believe in me. I am not shocked by your conclusions—only by the consequences they may bring. Do you hear me?”

“Yes,” she answered, flushing with irritation at his cold superiority.

“Then listen. It may not be long before some word or incident sets your imagination running again—imagining crimes, plotting disasters. You might rush to the Palazzo Vecchio, alarm the Signoria, throw the city into chaos. Let me tell you what might follow. Not just my disgrace—something you seem ready to face—but the arrest, the ruin, of many men in Florence. Including Messer Bernardo del Nero.”

Tito had made his move. And it struck deep.

The color drained from Romola’s face. Her lips turned white—an unusual pallor for one so resilient. Tito saw it. He had won.

“You may think yourself noble,” he went on, “a heroine saving the city. But you might as well try to unlock doors with prayers as expect your emotions to shape Florentine politics. The question is not whether you can trust me—but whether, having been warned, you will still charge forward blindly into affairs you do not understand.”

Romola felt her mind trapped. The possibilities he painted rose before her—clear, terrifying. She could see the scaffold, the arrests, the collapse of families, all because of her impulse.

“I am rash,” she murmured. “I will try not to be.”

“Remember,” Tito said, voice sharp now, “your act this morning might have caused worse harm than the sacrifice you’ve learned to face without flinching.”

“Tito,” she burst out, rising and stepping closer, her hand reaching for his arm, “it is not true that I would willingly destroy you. I have fought harder than I ever thought possible to stay with you. I left two years ago in anger. I came back because I loved you more than anything. But it is useless. You shut me out. You treat me as someone too irrational to share in your world. You reveal nothing. Nothing.”

She looked like an angel pleading—eyes wide, voice trembling. But Tito did not feel tenderness. He felt only distance. The man who had once found joy in her presence now found it hard to endure her nearness. His nature was gentle, yes—but his intellect was sharp, his purpose firm. And Romola resisted him. No man, unless weak beyond measure, endures being thwarted by his wife. Marriage must be either harmony or dominion.

No emotion flickered across his face when she spoke of fleeing. Only a slight tightening of the mouth, a faint smile.

“My Romola,” he said, “when conditions are known, we must accept them. Wishes cannot fill the Arno, nor turn plums into oranges. I have seen no prayer change fate. You carry strong impressions—ones reason cannot touch. I cannot share them. You withdrew trust. So I have withdrawn myself. There is no need to look back. We must adapt.”

“But Tito,” she pleaded, “we could speak! We could be honest!”

“It was deception,” she insisted, “that changed me. And it has kept us apart. And it was not I who changed first. You changed the night you wore that chain-armour. You hid something from me—about that old man. And I saw him again yesterday.”

“Tito,” she whispered, voice breaking, “if you would only tell me everything—no matter what—I would bear pain, anything. Just let there be no wall between us. Can’t we begin again?”

This time, a flicker crossed Tito’s face—swift, almost imperceptible. He stood motionless, but his skin seemed to whiten. Yet he did not answer her plea.

After a pause, he said quietly, “Your impetuosity, Romola, has a freezing effect. It could chill the baths of Nero.”

At those words, Romola recoiled. She straightened, returning to her usual self-contained poise.

Tito continued. “If by ‘that old man’ you mean Jacopo di Nola—the madman who tried to kill me and accused me of something I did not tell you, because it would have alarmed you—he is dead. I saw his name on the list of the deceased.”

“I know nothing of his accusation,” Romola said. “But I know who he is. The man I saw in the Duomo, with the rope around his neck—the one Piero di Cosimo painted, grasping your arm as he did the day the French came in, the day you first wore the armour.”

“And where is he now?” Tito asked, still pale, but controlled.

“He lay dying in the street,” Romola said. “Starved. I gave him bread and wine. I brought him to our door. He refused to come in. I gave him money. He left without speaking. But he knew I was your wife. Who is he?”

“A fool, half-mad, half-idiot,” Tito said. “Once my father’s servant in Greece. I dismissed him for theft. He hates me. That is the whole story. And now you know why I wear the armour—and why I feared him. Did you think that sight led you to seek him out? To invite him into our house?”

Romola said nothing. Speech felt futile. Like throwing herself against a shield.

Tito stepped back, removed his cap and mantle, pushed his hair from his forehead. He was preparing for final words.

“We need not speak of this again,” he said, voice steady, as before. “But remember—if your passion leads you to interfere in politics again, you may not save anyone. You may raise scaffolds, burn houses. You are not yet so devoted to the Piagnone cause that you believe Bernardo del Nero is the prince of darkness, and Francesco Valori the archangel Michael. I do not need a promise from you.”

“I have understood you too well,” Romola said.

“Enough,” he replied, turning and leaving.

She sank into her chair, face shadowed with despair.

“Oh God,” she whispered, “I have tried. I cannot help it. We shall always be divided.”

Then, suddenly, aloud—“Unless… misery should come and join us.”

Tito, too, had new thoughts after closing the door. He had long planned to leave Florence—once his position here had served its purpose, to move on to Rome or Milan. But now, for the first time, he felt a desire to leave Romola behind. She no longer fit into the life he wanted. An easy relationship required honesty. Honesty meant confession. Confession meant changing course. And Tito, like a leopard with grown teeth, had no taste for milk.

He avoided discomfort. Why cling to what strained him? And Romola had already complicated things with others—Dolfo Spini, furious after a fruitless ambush, waiting in the rain. The scene at Nello’s door, once reported, could grow into something worse than suspicion.

Yet now, Tito believed he had mastered her—not through force, but through fear. He had touched her deepest instincts: her love, her conscience, her sense of responsibility. He had shown her the vast, tangled web of power where moral judgment was useless. He had silenced her mind.

Still, he was not at peace. The world was not yet soft enough. And even if it were, he could not rest fully in comfort. Before he left that evening, he put on his coat of chain-armour.
