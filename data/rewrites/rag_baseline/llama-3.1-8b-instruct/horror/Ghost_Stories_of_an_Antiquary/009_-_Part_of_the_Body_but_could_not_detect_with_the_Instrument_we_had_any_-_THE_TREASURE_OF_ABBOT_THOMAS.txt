As I stood before the imposing façade of the Steinfeld Abbey, the chill of the Rhine Valley evening air bit at my skin like a damp, unyielding shroud. My eyes wandered to the magnificent stained-glass window, its colors a kaleidoscope of blue, red, and gold, as if the very essence of the setting sun had been distilled within its intricate panes. It was said that Abbot Thomas, a man of boundless energy and wisdom, had commissioned this masterpiece in the prime of his life. His effigy, positioned alongside the window, seemed to gaze out upon the world with an air of quiet confidence, as if aware of some secret known only to himself.

The story of Abbot Thomas's hidden treasure had long been a topic of whispered speculation among the Canons. It was said that he had concealed a vast sum of gold within the monastery walls, a treasure that had eluded the grasp of many a determined seeker. The tale was often told with a mixture of awe and trepidation, as if the very thought of uncovering such a secret was a daunting task. According to tradition, Abbot Thomas would often respond to inquiries about the treasure's location with a knowing smile, saying, "Job, John, and Zechariah will tell either you or your successors." Some believed that these words held a deeper meaning, a hidden message waiting to be deciphered.

As an antiquarian, I had dedicated myself to unraveling the mysteries of the past, and the Steinfeld Abbey was a treasure trove of stories waiting to be uncovered. My current quest was to identify the origin of a series of stained-glass panels that had found their way to England after the Revolution. The Steinfeld Abbey had been a significant contributor to the artistic heritage of our country, and I aimed to shed light on the provenance of its beautiful glasswork. The passage I had come across in the Sertum Steinfeldense Norbertinum had set me on this path, and I was determined to follow it through to its conclusion.

My eyes returned to the window, and I noticed the intricate patterns etched into the glass. The subjects depicted within the panels told a story of their own, a narrative woven from the threads of faith and tradition. Job, John, and Zechariah, the three figures that had captured my attention, seemed to embody the very essence of the human experience – perseverance, faith, and redemption.
