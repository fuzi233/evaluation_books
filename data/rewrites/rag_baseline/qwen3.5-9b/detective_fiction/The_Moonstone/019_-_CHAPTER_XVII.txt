The night passed in a vacuum of silence; neither Miss Rachel nor Rosanna attempted the forbidden communion that had plagued the previous watch, and Sergeant Cuff's vigilance yielded no fruit. Yet, I had anticipated that the Sergeant would depart for Frizinghall at the break of dawn. Instead, he lingered, a coiled spring of potential action, seemingly weighing a task he deemed necessary before his journey. Leaving him to his solitary deliberation, I ventured into the grounds shortly thereafter, finding Mr. Franklin pacing his favored path along the shrubbery. Our exchange was brief, cut short by the abrupt arrival of the Sergeant. He approached Mr. Franklin with a deference that was immediately rebuffed; the gentleman received him with a hauteur that bordered on icy disdain.

"Have you anything to say to me?" was the sharp retort to the Sergeant's polite greeting.

"I have something to say to you, sir," the Sergeant replied, his tone shifting from courtesy to professional gravity, "regarding the inquiry I am conducting here. You discerned the trajectory of this investigation yesterday. Naturally, in your position, you are shocked and distressed. Equally naturally, you project your own anguished sense of familial scandal upon me."

"What do you want?" Mr. Franklin interjected, his voice cutting through the air.

"I seek to remind you, sir, that I have thus far not been *proven* incorrect. Bear that in mind, and simultaneously recall that I am an officer of the law acting under the explicit sanction of the mistress of this house. Under these circumstances, is it, or is it not, your duty as a conscientious citizen to assist me with any special information you may possess?"

"I possess no special information," Mr. Franklin stated flatly.

Sergeant Cuff dismissed this response as if it had never been uttered. "You may spare my time from being wasted on inquiries at a distance," he continued, pressing forward, "if you choose to understand me and speak out."

"I don't understand you," Mr. Franklin countered, "and I have nothing to say."

"One of the female servants—I shall not name her—spoke to you privately, sir, last night."

Once more, Mr. Franklin cut him short; once more, he declared, "I have nothing to say."

Standing silently nearby, I recalled the subtle movement in the swing-door from the previous evening and the ghostly trail of coat-tails vanishing down the passage. The Sergeant had undoubtedly gathered sufficient evidence to suspect that Rosanna had unburdened her conscience to Mr. Franklin Blake. This hypothesis had scarcely taken root in my mind when who should emerge from the end of the shrubbery walk but Rosanna Spearman herself! Trailing behind her was Penelope, clearly attempting to guide her back toward the safety of the house. Observing that Mr. Franklin was not alone, Rosanna halted, paralyzed by a profound perplexity regarding her next move. Penelope paused behind her. Mr. Franklin perceived the girls the moment I did. The Sergeant, displaying his diabolical cunning, feigned total obliviousness to their presence.

All of this transpired in the span of a heartbeat. Before either Mr. Franklin or I could utter a syllable, Sergeant Cuff intervened smoothly, adopting the guise of merely continuing our prior conversation.

"You need not fear harming the girl, sir," he addressed Mr. Franklin, raising his voice deliberately so that Rosanna might catch every word. "On the contrary, I commend you to honor me with your confidence, should you harbor any genuine interest in Rosanna Spearman."

Instantly, Mr. Franklin masked his awareness of the girls. Speaking loudly, he declared: "I take no interest whatever in Rosanna Spearman."

My gaze drifted toward the distant end of the walk. All I could discern was Rosanna turning abruptly the moment Mr. Franklin spoke. Unlike her resistance earlier, she now allowed my daughter to seize her arm and escort her back toward the house. The breakfast bell chimed as the two figures vanished inside—and even the astute Sergeant Cuff was compelled to concede defeat.

"I shall proceed to Frizinghall, Mr. Betteredge," he murmured quietly to me. "And I shall return before two."

With that, he departed without further comment, and for several hours, we were fortunate to be free of his scrutiny.

"You must rectify matters with Rosanna," Mr. Franklin said to me once we were alone. "I seem fated to utter or perform something awkward whenever that unfortunate girl appears. You witnessed it yourself: Sergeant Cuff laid a trap for both of us. Had he succeeded in confusing me or irritating her into a breach of composure, either she or I might have divulged something serving his ends. On the spur of the moment, I saw no viable escape but the path I chose. It prevented the girl from speaking, and it demonstrated to the Sergeant that I saw through his ruse. He was evidently listening, Betteredge, when I was conversing with you last night."

"He had done worse than listen," I thought privately. He had retained my confession that the girl loved Mr. Franklin and had calculated precisely on that fact when appealing to Mr. Franklin's supposed interest in Rosanna, all within her earshot.

"As to listening, sir," I remarked, withholding the deeper truth, "we shall all be rowing in the same boat if this continues. Prying, peeping, and eavesdropping are the natural occupations of those situated as we are. Within a day or two, Mr. Franklin, we shall all be rendered speechless—for we shall all be listening to surprise one another's secrets, fully aware of our complicity. Forgive my outburst, sir. The horrid mystery enveloping this house infiltrates my mind like potent liquor, driving me wild. I shall not forget what you have told me. I will take the first opportunity to make amends with Rosanna Spearman."

"You have said nothing to her yet about last night, have you?" Mr. Franklin asked.

"No, sir."

"Then say nothing now. I deem it inadvisable to invite the girl's confidence while the Sergeant watches for us to be caught together. My conduct is inconsistent, Betteredge—is it not? I perceive no exit from this predicament, which is dreadful to contemplate, unless the Diamond is traced to Rosanna. And yet, I cannot, and will not, aid the Sergeant in discovering the girl."

Unreasonable, undoubtedly. Yet, it mirrored my own state of mind perfectly. I comprehended him entirely. If you will, for once in your life, remember your mortality, you may comprehend him as well.

While the Sergeant traversed the road to Frizinghall, the domestic atmosphere unfolded thus: Miss Rachel awaited the carriage destined for her aunt's, remaining obstinately confined to her chamber. My lady and Mr. Franklin shared breakfast. Post-prandially, Mr. Franklin made one of his impulsive resolutions and departed precipitately for a long walk to soothe his agitated spirit. I was the sole witness to his departure; he assured me he would return before the Sergeant's arrival.

The weather, ominously foreshadowed the night prior, had shifted. Heavy rain, followed swiftly by dawn, gave way to a high wind. It blew fresh as the day progressed. Though clouds threatened intermittently, the rain held off. It was a tolerable day for a stroll, provided one was youthful and robust enough to withstand the great gusts sweeping in from the sea.

After attending to my mistress following breakfast, I assisted her in settling our household accounts. She alluded to the Moonstone only once, issuing a prohibition against mentioning it between us. "Wait until that man returns," she said, gesturing toward the Sergeant. "We *must* discuss it then; we are not obligated to speak of it now."

Leaving my mistress, I discovered Penelope awaiting me in my room. "I beseech you, father, to come and speak to Rosanna," she pleaded. "I am exceedingly uneasy concerning her."

I suspected the cause readily enough. However, it is a maxim of mine that men, being superior creatures, are bound to improve women—if they can. When a woman requests my assistance, be it my daughter or anyone else, I invariably insist upon knowing the 'why.' The more frequently you compel them to excavate their own minds for reasons, the more manageable you shall find them in all relations of life. It is not their fault—that poor souls—that they act first and think afterward; it is the fault of the fools who indulge them.

Penelope's reasoning, in this instance, was delivered in her own words. "I fear, father," she said, "that Mr. Franklin has wounded Rosanna cruelly, albeit unintentionally."

"What drew Rosanna to the shrubbery walk?" I inquired.

"Her own madness," Penelope replied. "I can conceive of no other explanation. She was bent on addressing Mr. Franklin this morning, regardless of the consequences. I did my utmost to prevent her; you witnessed that effort. If I could merely have removed her before she heard those dreadful words..."

"There! There!" I soothed. "Do not lose your composure. I cannot recall anything occurring to alarm Rosanna."

"Nothing to alarm her, father. But Mr. Franklin stated he took no interest whatsoever in her—and, oh, he said it in such a cruel voice!"

"He said it to silence the Sergeant's mouth," I explained.
