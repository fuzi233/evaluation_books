Though Elinor had often questioned the reliability of Lucy's word in the past, the gravity of the current situation rendered such doubt impossible. To invent a falsehood of such magnitude, involving a secret engagement and a ruined reputation, would require a level of audacity that Lucy simply did not possess. Every probability pointed to the truth of her assertion; every piece of evidence—their history at Mr. Pratt's, Edward's recent melancholy demeanor near Plymouth, his vague behavior toward her, and the intimate knowledge Lucy held of Norland—conspired to form an undeniable chain of facts. The letter, the ring, and the picture were no longer mere rumors but tangible proofs of Edward's ill-treatment of her.

For a fleeting moment, anger flared within her, a hot indignation at having been the dupe of such deception. She asked herself if Edward had feigned affection, if his engagement to Lucy was one of the heart. Yet, as quickly as the doubt arose, it was dispelled. Whatever sentiment had once existed between them, it was clearly dead. His affection belonged entirely to her; she could not be deceived about that. Her mother, her sisters, and even Fanny had witnessed his devotion at Norland. It was not a vanity-induced illusion. He loved her. This realization acted as a balm to her wounded spirit, softening the blow and making forgiveness seem not only possible but necessary.

He had been foolish, undeniably so, in allowing his feelings to cloud his judgment while residing at Norland. He could not be excused for that imprudence. But if he had caused her pain, he had inflicted far greater harm upon himself. While she might eventually find peace, his future seemed bleak. Could he truly be happy with Lucy Steele? With a woman who was illiterate, artful, and selfish, lacking the refinement and education that years of rational living had bestowed upon her? The youth of nineteen might have blinded him to these defects, but four years of growth should have opened his eyes. Instead, Lucy's time in inferior society had likely eroded the very simplicity that once made her charming.

Furthermore, the obstacles to their union had grown insurmountable. If his mother's opposition had previously seemed daunting, it was now compounded by the reality that Lucy was inferior in both connection and fortune. Yet, the irony was bitter: while Elinor faced family opposition with dread, Edward found a strange relief in the certainty of it. Melancholy settled over her as she considered his hopeless state. Weeping for him became her primary emotion, overshadowing her own grief. Convinced that she had done nothing to deserve this unhappiness and that Edward retained her esteem, she summoned the strength to conceal the full extent of her despair from her mother and sisters.

Two hours after the news broke, she joined them at dinner with an air of such composed cheerfulness that no one suspected the storm raging within. To spare them the agony of knowing the truth, and to shield herself from their excessive, perhaps unfair, condemnation of Edward, she bore the burden alone. She knew their sorrow would only deepen her own, and their counsel would offer no solace. Alone, bolstered by her own sense, she remained unshaken.

Driven by a need to clarify the situation, Elinor soon felt a strong desire to speak with Lucy again. She needed to hear the details of their engagement anew, to gauge the sincerity of Lucy's feelings, and most importantly, to demonstrate through her calm demeanor that she was no longer a rival but a friend. She suspected Lucy's jealousy; Edward's high praise of her, coupled with Sir John's teasing, suggested it. But even without those hints, Lucy's eagerness to reveal the secret proved her intent: she wished to warn Elinor away. Resolved to honor her principles and avoid Edward, Elinor nonetheless sought to reassure Lucy that her own heart remained unwounded. She believed she could recount the particulars with composure, for there was nothing more painful to hear than what had already been told.

Opportunity, however, was scarce. The weather rarely permitted walks where they might speak privately, and their evenings at the park or cottage were dominated by the noisy games and laughter of Sir John and Lady Middleton, leaving no room for serious discourse. They met to eat, drink, and play, not to converse.

It was only when Sir John called one morning to invite them all to dine at the cottage, citing Lady Middleton's impending solitude, that Elinor saw a potential opening. She anticipated that a quieter gathering, guided by Lady Middleton's gentle manners rather than her husband's boisterous energy, might yield better results. Margaret and Marianne, the latter reluctantly persuaded by her mother, accepted the invitation.

The dinner was, as Elinor predicted, devoid of novelty or interest. The conversation in the drawing room was equally dull, interrupted only by the arrival of the children. Realizing that engaging Lucy's attention here was futile, Elinor waited until the tea things were cleared and the card tables set. As the group prepared for a game of Casino, Lady Middleton remarked kindly to Lucy that she should not work on Annamaria's basket by candlelight, promising to make amends the next day.

Lucy, seizing the moment, declared she would not disappoint the "little angel" and insisted on finishing the basket after supper. Lady Middleton, delighted by her compliance, offered working candles. Lucy immediately moved her table closer, her alacrity suggesting she found no greater joy than crafting a gift for a spoiled child.

Marianne, ever the outlier, refused to play, declaring her hatred for cards and retreating to the piano. Lady Middleton looked relieved, while Elinor smoothly intervened to praise the instrument. Seizing the lull, Elinor proposed that if she were to cut out, she would assist Lucy in rolling her papers, noting that the basket required more work than Lucy could manage alone.

Lucy gratefully accepted the offer, lamenting the prospect of disappointing Annamaria. Elinor joyfully took her seat beside her, utilizing a tact she knew Marianne lacked. Side by side, the two rivals worked in harmony on the same project. The piano, where Marianne was lost in her own music, provided a perfect shield of noise. Under its cover, Elinor felt safe to introduce the subject she had come to discuss, hoping to find the clarity she sought before the evening ended.
