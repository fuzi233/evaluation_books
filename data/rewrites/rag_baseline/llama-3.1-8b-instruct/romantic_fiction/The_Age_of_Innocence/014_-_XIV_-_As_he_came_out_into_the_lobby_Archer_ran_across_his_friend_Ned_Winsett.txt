The silence of the afternoon seeped into Archer's apartment like a chill. He stood by the window, gazing out at the row houses opposite, their brick facades a monotonous repetition of identical patterns. The faint scent of smoke from the chimney next door drifted in, mingling with the musty smell of his own books. His mind wandered back to the previous night, to the Beaufort ball, where Ellen Olenska had reappeared, her presence a subtle vibration in the crowded room.

As he turned away from the window, he heard a knock on the door. It was Ned Winsett, his friend from the world of journalists and theatrical people. Winsett's eyes, usually bright with curiosity, seemed dimmed today, his expression subdued. They exchanged a few pleasantries, and Winsett mentioned the Countess Olenska, his tone tinged with a mixture of fascination and unease.

"I've seen her, you know," Winsett said, his voice low. "She's been kind to my little boy, who got a nasty cut chasing his kitten. She rushed in bareheaded, carrying him in her arms, and was so sympathetic and beautiful that my wife was too dazzled to ask her name."

Archer felt a warmth spread through him, a sense of recognition that was almost painful. It was just like Ellen, he thought, to have rushed in bareheaded, carrying the boy in her arms, and to have captivated poor Mrs. Winsett with her kindness.

"That's the Countess Olenska," Archer said, his voice barely above a whisper. "A granddaughter of old Mrs. Mingott's."

Winsett whistled softly. "A Countess! Well, I didn't know Countesses were so neighborly. Mingotts ain't."

"They would be, if you'd let them," Archer replied, a hint of amusement in his voice.

The two men stood there for a moment, the silence between them thickening like the fog outside. Then Winsett spoke up again, his tone tinged with a hint of melancholy. "I wonder how a Countess happens to live in our slum?"

"Because she doesn't care a hang about where she lives – or about any of our little social sign-posts," Archer said, his voice filled with a quiet admiration for Ellen's independence.

Winsett nodded thoughtfully, his eyes drifting away from Archer's face. "Been in bigger places, I suppose," he muttered.
