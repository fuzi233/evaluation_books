When new mayor Thomas Sapsea finds the self-congratulatory contemplation of his own profundity growing a little stale on a damp Cloisterham evening, he is wont to take a slow airing through the Cathedral Close. He ambles past the churchyard with the swagger of a landed proprietor, warm with the soft, self-satisfied glow of a benevolent landlord who has been unusually generous to a most deserving tenant: his late wife, whose monumental inscription he wrote and unveiled to great local fanfare. He loves to spot a stray stranger peering through the iron railings, likely pausing to read his handiwork on the stone markers. If he ever sees a visitor hurrying away from the churchyard, he is fully convinced they are exactly as his monument directs: “retiring with a blush,” humbled by the grandeur of his tribute.

His status as mayor has inflated his ego tenfold. He is fully certain he invented the brilliant observation that the entire framework of society would collapse entirely without mayors—plenty of them, all duly solemn, all duly self-important. Mayors have been knighted, after all, for trotting up to London with flowery addresses, firing off grandiloquent nonsense straight into the laps of the crown. Why shouldn’t Sir Thomas Sapsea be next? He has long since decided he is the very salt of the local earth.

His friendship with choirmaster John Jasper has deepened considerably since that first evening of port wine, epitaph debates, backgammon and roast beef. Jasper has hosted him at the gatehouse too, sat at his piano to play for him long enough to tickle his ears—figuratively, of course—for hours. What Sapsea likes most about the young man is that he is always eager to learn from the wisdom of his elders, and is sound, sir, sound to the very core. He proved it that night, Sapsea will tell anyone who listens, by playing none of the frivolous foreign ditties favoured by national enemies, but only rousing old loyalist George III era ballads, exhorting all brave British boys to smash every other island, continent, peninsula, isthmus and promontory on the map, and sweep every sea clean of verminous foreign folk. It was quite clear Providence had made a terrible mistake making the nation of oak hearts so small, and all those other useless countries so large.

This particular damp evening, Sapsea is strolling past the churchyard, hands behind his back, still on the lookout for that blushing retiring stranger, when he turns a corner and stumbles straight into the Dean, deep in conversation with verger Tope and Jasper. He bows so low he might as well genuflect, instantly adopting a far more pious air than any Archbishop of York or Canterbury could ever muster.

“You are clearly planning to write a book about us, Mr. Jasper,” the Dean teases. “Well, we are extraordinarily ancient, we ought to make a very good book. We are not nearly as rich in endowments as we are in years, so you would do well to highlight that little fact, call attention to our neglected state, if you do put pen to paper.”
Verger Tope snorts with perfectly dutiful, respectful amusement.

“I have no intention at all of becoming an author or archaeologist, sir,” Jasper replies smoothly. “It is nothing but a silly whim of mine. And even that whim is more Mr. Sapsea’s fault than mine.”

“How so, Mr. Mayor?” the Dean says, nodding good-naturedly at him. “What are you responsible for here?”

“I am not at all aware,” Sapsea fumbles, glancing around for an out, “of what the Very Reverend the Dean does me the honour of referencing.”
“Durdles,” Tope mutters, helpfully.

“Ah, Durdles, of course!” the Dean echoes.

“The truth is,” Jasper explains, “my curiosity about that strange old man was first stoked entirely by Mr. Sapsea. His unmatched knowledge of human nature, and his gift for drawing out the quirkiest, most reclusive characters around this town, is what first made me pay him any mind, even though I’ve passed him a hundred times loitering around the cathedral grounds. You would not be surprised at all if you had seen Mr. Sapsea charm Durdles right out of his shell in his own parlour, as I did.”

“Oh!” Sapsea exclaims, latching onto the praise with unutterable pomp and glee. “Yes, yes. The Very Reverend the Dean means that incident, of course. I happened to introduce Durdles and Mr. Jasper to one another. I consider Durdles a most distinct Character.”
“A character you can turn completely inside out with a few well-chosen, skillful words, Mr. Sapsea,” Jasper flattered him.

“Nay, not quite that,” the old auctioneer huffs, lumbering a little. “I may have a little influence over him, perhaps, and a little insight into his ways. You must remember, Very Reverend Sir, that I have seen the world.” He shuffles a step behind the Dean immediately after that, pretending to be fascinated by the buttons on his coat.

“Well,” the Dean says, glancing around for his scribe who has wandered off somewhere, “I do hope, Mr. Mayor, that you use your expertise in Durdles’ character to make sure he does not break our very valuable choir-master’s neck. We cannot afford to lose him. His voice and his wits are far too precious to the cathedral.”
Tope is once more highly entertained, dissolving into respectful chuckles before murmuring deferentially that any man would count it an honour to have his neck broken, if it earned such a compliment from the Dean.

“I take full responsibility, sir,” Sapsea declares loftily. “I will answer for Mr. Jasper’s neck. I will tell Durdles to be careful. He listens to what I say. How is his neck endangered, exactly?” he asks, looking around like he is dispensing a great favour by inquiring.

“Only by a moonlight expedition I am planning to make with Durdles, wandering among the tombs, vaults, old towers and ruins,” Jasper answers. “You remember suggesting when you introduced us that as a lover of the picturesque, it might be well worth my while to see all these old hidden corners? You were entirely right.”
“I remember perfectly!” Sapsea says. The solemn fool truly believes he does.

“Following your excellent advice,” Jasper goes on, “I have taken several daytime rambles with the extraordinary old fellow, and tonight we are off on a secret, hole-and-corner exploration under the full moon.”

“And here comes Durdles himself,” the Dean says.
Sure enough, there he is, slouching towards them, his dinner bundle tucked under his arm. He pulls off his hat when he spots the Dean, and tries to slouch away, but Sapsea stops him.

“Mind you take good care of my friend,” Sapsea orders him.
“What friend of yours is dead?” Durdles grunts. “I ain’t had no orders for carving a headstone for no friend of yours.”
“I mean my living friend, right there.”
“Oh, him?” Durdles says. “Mister Jarsper can take care of hisself, easy.”
“Still, you take care of him too,” Sapsea insists.
Durdles glares at him, unamused, from head to toe. “With all respect to his Reverence the Dean there, you mind what’s your business, Mr. Sapsea, and Durdles will mind his own.”

“You are just out of temper,” Sapsea says, winking at the rest of the group to show how smoothly he is about to defuse him. “My friend here is my business. Mr. Jasper is my friend. And you are my friend, too.”
“Don’t you get into that bad habit of boasting,” Durdles says, nodding at him gravely, warningly. “It’ll grow on you before you know it.”

“You are out of temper,” Sapsea repeats, reddening a little, and winking at the others again.
“Fair enough,” Durdles replies. “I don’t much care for liberties.”
Sapsea winks a third time, as if to say See, I’ve settled him, no problem, and struts off to end the conversation.

Durdles bids the Dean good evening, claps his hat back on, and says, “You’ll find me at home, Mister Jarsper, all ready like we agreed, when you need me. I’m off home now to clean myself up.” He slouched out of sight. That “cleaning himself” is one of the town’s great unsolved mysteries; he, his hat, his boots, and his ragged coat never seem the least bit less caked in dust and grit, no matter how many times he claims to have washed up.

The lamplighter is now darting around the quiet Close, leaving little golden specks of light behind him as he zips up and down his tiny ladder. Generations of Cloisterham residents have grown up tripping over that ladder, and no local would ever dream of getting rid of it. Soon the Dean heads off to his dinner, Tope to his tea, and Jasper back to his gatehouse. He sits down at his piano, nothing but firelight to see by, and sings soft, beautiful choir music under his breath for two or three hours, until it is fully dark outside, and the moon is just about to rise.

Then he closes the piano lid very gently, changes his coat for a thick pea-jacket with a big wicker-cased bottle tucked in its largest pocket, pulls on a low-crowned, flapped brimmed hat, and slips out the door. He moves so softly, almost stealthily, that there is no obvious outward reason for it. Is there something dark, unspoken, and coiled deep inside him that makes him move like that?

He makes his way to Durdles’ half-finished little hut built into the old city wall, spots a light inside, and picks his way softly through the graveyard, where the rising moon is just now gilding the edges of old stone monuments and scattered stony debris. The two stonemason journeymen left for the night, their big saws still stuck half-buried in their blocks of stone. It looks for all the world like two skeletons stepped straight out of the Dance of Death, grinning in the shadows behind their sentry boxes, waiting to start carving the gravestones for the next two people fated to die in Cloisterham. The two workmen are alive right now, probably off drinking and laughing, with no thought for the fate waiting for them. It would be quite a game, Jasper thinks, to guess which two names those saws will cut first. Or just the name of one.

“Ho! Durdles!” he calls.
The light shifts inside, and Durdles appears in the doorway holding it. It looks very much like he has been “cleaning himself” with the help of a bottle, a jug, and a chipped tumbler; there are no other signs of soap or water anywhere in his bare brick room, exposed rafters overhead, no plaster on the walls.

“Are you ready?”
“I’m all set, Mister Jarsper. Let all the old dead folks come out and show their faces if they dare, tonight. My spirit’s ready for ’em.”
“Do you mean your animal spirits, or the ardent kind in the bottle?”
“One and the same,” Durdles grins. “I mean both.”
He grabs a lantern off its hook, tucks a couple of matches in his pocket, and they head out together, his dinner bundle still tucked under his arm.

It is a strange, unaccountable kind of expedition. No one finds it odd that Durdles, who prowls graveyards and ruins like a ghoul night after night, should be out wandering aimlessly, but it is a very strange thing for the respectable young choirmaster to tag along, tramping through the dark with the town’s most disreputable drunken stonemason to hunt for moonlight views.

“Mind that there mound by the yard gate, Mister Jarsper.”
“I see it. What is it?”
“Lime.”
Jasper stops short, waiting for him to catch up. “Quicklime, you mean?”
“Ay,” Durdles nods. “Quick enough to eat right through your boots. Stir it a little, and it’s quick enough to eat right through your bones.”

They walk on, passing the red lit windows of the Travellers’ Twopenny lodging house, and step out into the clear bright moonlight that floods the old Monks’ Vineyard. They cross it, and come to Minor Canon Corner, most of which is still deep in shadow, until the moon climbs higher in the sky.

They hear a house door click shut, and two men step out: Mr. Crisparkle, the minor canon, and Neville Landless. A strange, sudden, sharp smile crosses Jasper’s face. He presses a hand hard to Durdles’ chest, stopping him dead in his tracks.
The far end of Minor Canon Corner is buried in deep shadow at this hour, and there is a low, breast-high dwarf wall, all that is left of an old garden boundary, running alongside the path. Jasper and Durdles were about to step past it, but now they duck down behind it, hidden from view.

“Those two are just out for a saunter,” Jasper whispers. “They’ll walk out into the moonlight soon. Let’s stay quiet here, or they’ll stop us, make us chat, insist on joining us, and ruin the whole expedition.”
Durdles nods, and starts munching bits of food out of his dinner bundle. Jasper rests his arms on top of the wall, his chin propped on them, and watches. He pays no attention at all to good Mr. Crisparkle. His eyes are locked entirely on Neville Landless, as if his gaze is the sight on a loaded rifle, and he has the young man dead in his crosshairs, about to pull the trigger. The look of unspoken, destructive hunger on his face is so obvious that even Durdles pauses mid-bite, stares at him, half-chewed food still stuck in his cheek.

Crisparkle and Neville pace back and forth, talking quietly. Jasper cannot make out every word, but he hears his own name mentioned, over and over again.
“This is the first day of the week,” Crisparkle says clearly, as they turn back towards the shadow. “And the last day of the week is Christmas Eve.”
“You can be perfectly certain of me, sir,” Neville replies.
The echoes carry those two lines perfectly, but the rest of their conversation blurs together, until Jasper catches the word “confidence” floating through the air, then Neville’s half-answer: “I have not earned it yet, but I will, sir.” When they turn away again, Jasper hears his own name once more, and Crisparkle saying, “Remember I told you I would answer for you, confidently.” Then their voices fade. They stop for a minute, Neville gesturing earnestly at the sky, before they turn and walk slowly out of the shadow, disappearing into the moonlight at the far end of the Corner.

Jasper does not move until they are completely gone. Then he bursts out laughing, wild, uncontrollable laughter. Durdles still has that half-chewed lump in his cheek, sees nothing funny at all, and just stares at him until Jasper drops his face onto his arms, laughing so hard his shoulders shake. Only then does Durdles finally swallow that lump, like he is resigned to having indigestion for the rest of the night.

There is hardly a soul stirring in these old nooks after dark. Even at noon the streets are quiet, but at night the whole area around the cathedral is wrapped in a thick, eerie hush that no local cares to disturb. Ask a hundred Cloisterham residents at midday if they believe in ghosts, and every one of them will say no. Offer them a choice after dark between cutting through these eerie, silent precincts, or taking the long, detoured route along the busy high street, and ninety-nine of them will pick the long way. It is not just local superstition about the headless lady, or the ghost of a mother with a rope around her neck that haunts the cloisters. It is the basic, universal human instinct: the living do not like lingering too long in places thick with the dust of the dead. No one will admit it, but every last person here thinks: if the dead ever do walk, this is exactly the sort of place they would choose to do it. That is why, when Jasper and Durdles pause before a small side door leading down into the crypt, Durdles’ key in hand, the entire stretch of moonlit churchyard around them is completely, utterly deserted. You would think the whole tide of living people is blocked off by Jasper’s own gatehouse. You can hear the hum of distant traffic beyond the archway, but not a single soul crosses it, and Jasper’s red lamp glows behind his gatehouse curtains, like a lighthouse warning people away from the dark grounds.

They unlock the door, step inside, and clamber down the rough stone steps into the crypt. No lantern is needed, because the moonlight slants straight through the groined, broken-glass windows, their cracked empty frames casting lacy patterns across the stone floor. The heavy pillars that hold up the roof cast huge pools of inky black shadow, but between them, narrow lanes of bright silver light stretch out. They walk up and down those lanes, Durdles chattering away about all the “old uns” he is still planning to dig up, slapping a solid stone wall where he swears a whole entire family of dead people are sealed up inside, like they are old personal friends of his. Durdles’ usual taciturnity has vanished entirely, thanks to the wicker bottle. He passes it back and forth freely, its contents vanishing quickly into Durdles’ bloodstream, while Jasper only ever rinses his mouth with a sip, and spits it back out onto the stone.

They climb the steps up towards the main cathedral, planning to ascend the great central tower. Durdles stops on a step halfway, to catch his breath. The steps are pitch black, but they can still see the lanes of moonlight they just walked through, glinting far below. Durdles sinks down onto a step, and Jasper sits down on the step next to him.
