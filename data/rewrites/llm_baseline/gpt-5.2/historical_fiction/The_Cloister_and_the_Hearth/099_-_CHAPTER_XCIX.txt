Jerome was morose by nature—his brow seemed carved for disapproval—yet toward Gerard he had softened a little, as winter yields a finger’s breadth to sun. In England he had missed him more than pride would let him name; and afterward, at odd hours, he found himself listening for his brother’s step in the cloister passage, as if sound could undo distance.

What he heard at Gouda had raised Gerard in his esteem. Above all, men told how Clement—so they called him now—had stood firm against Pope and Minorites upon that “infernal heresy of the immaculate conception,” as Jerome named it, grinding the words between his teeth like hard grain. That was the sort of resistance Jerome could honour: not the soft rebellion of appetite, but the stern refusal that cost a man friends and favour.

Then a young monk, wet-faced, came to him with the cause of Gerard’s sickness. Jerome’s brief tenderness snapped back into its old disdain.

“Dying for a woman?”

He resolved to stop the scandal if he could, and if he could not, to fence it in with doctrine. Twice a day he went to Clement’s cell, where the air was thin with candle-smoke and old lime, and tried all his former influence—his iron logic, his stern exhortations—to make Gerard shake off what he called unspiritual despondency, and not rob the Church of piety and eloquence at so critical a season.

Gerard listened, approved the reasoning, admired Jerome’s strength, confessed his own weakness—and yet, before Jerome’s very eyes, continued to ebb. It was like watching a hearth-fire whose embers will not take, no matter how patiently you coax them: you add the kindling, you breathe, you shelter it from draft, and still it sinks to grey.

One day Jerome came with his argument sharpened to a point.

“I have heard thy story,” he said, “and heard it with pride. But now thou spoilest it all, Clement; for this is the triumph of earthly passion. Better thou hadst yielded and repented, than resist while she lived, and succumb under it now, body and soul.”

“Dear Jerome,” said Clement—so gently that the words ceased to be rebuke and became a plea—“here I think thou dost me some injustice. Passion there is none; but a deep affection, for which I will not blush here, since I shall not blush for it in heaven. Bethink thee: the poor dog that dies of grief upon his master’s grave—is he guilty of passion? Neither am I.

“Passion had saved my life and lost my soul. She was my good angel. She sustained me in duty and charity; her face encouraged me in the pulpit; her lips soothed me under ingratitude. She intertwined herself with all that was good in my life; and after leaning on her so long, I could not go on alone.

“And, dear Jerome, believe me, I am no rebel against Heaven. It is God’s will to release me. When they threw the earth upon her poor coffin, something snapped within my bosom here that mended may not be. I heard it, and I felt it. And from that time, Jerome, no food that I put in my mouth had any savour. With mine eyes bandaged now I could not tell thee which was bread and which was flesh, by eating of it.”

Jerome’s mouth fell open an instant, as if he had smelt something impossible—like honey turned to smoke.

“Holy saints!”

“And again,” Clement went on, as if reporting a change in the weather, not the sundering of a life, “from that same hour my deep dejection left me, and I smiled again. I often smile—why? I read it thus: He in whose hands are the issues of life and death gave me that minute the great summons. ’Twas some cord of life snapped in me. He is very pitiful. I should have lived unhappy; but He said, ‘No; enough is done, enough is suffered; poor feeble, loving servant, thy shortcomings are forgiven, thy sorrows touch their end; come thou to thy rest!’ I come, Lord, I come!”

Jerome groaned, not in argument now but in defeat.

“The Church had ever her holy but feeble servants,” he said. “Now would I give ten years of my life to save thine. But I see it may not be. Die in peace.”

And so it was. In a few days more Gerard lay a-dying in a mind so holy and happy that more than one aged saint came to gather his last sayings, as men go out at harvest to glean what the reapers miss.

In the evening he had seen Giles, and begged him—his voice already loosened from the world—to do small homely mercies, the sort that keep a life from falling utterly into neglect: not to let poor Jack starve; to see that little Gerard’s trustees did their duty; to kiss his parents for him; and to send Denys to his friends in Burgundy.

“Poor thing,” he murmured, “he will feel so strange here without his comrade.”

After that he had speech with Jerome alone. What passed between them was never clearly known; but it must have been something remarkable, for Jerome came from the door with his hands crossed upon his breast, his high head lowered, sighing as he went—as if some proud instrument in him had been re-tuned, and he did not yet know how to bear the new music.

Two monks watched with Clement till matins. They said that all through the night he broke out now and then into pious ejaculations and praises and thanksgivings; only once, they said, he wandered and thought he saw her walking in green meadows with other spirits clad in white, and beckoning him; and they all smiled and beckoned him. The watchers smiled too, half in awe, half in the tender foolishness that takes comfort from another’s comfort.

And both of them—though it might have been fancy born of candle-flame and vigil—declared that just before dawn there came three light taps against the wall, one after another, very slow. In that hush the monastery’s smallest sounds were magnified: the creak of timber settling, the far drip from a gutter, the faint brush of a mouse behind wainscot. Yet these taps were distinct, as measured as a knuckle upon a door.

The dying man heard them, and said, very clearly, “I come, love, I come.”

This at least is certain: he did utter those words, and having uttered them, prepared for departure as a man rises and sets his cloak when the time has come.

He sent for all the monks who at that hour were keeping vigil. They came and hovered about him like gentle spirits, their voices braided of prayer. Some prayed in silence with faces to the ground; others supported his head tenderly, as if afraid that the least roughness might bruise the soul on its way out.

When one said something of his life of self-denial and charity, Gerard stopped him. His voice—worn these days to a thread—suddenly cleared and rang with a force that seemed not his own.

“My dear brethren,” he said, “take note that he who here dies so happy holds not these new-fangled doctrines of man’s merit. Oh, what a miserable hour were this to me an I did! Nay, but I hold, with the Apostles and their pupils in the Church, the ancient fathers, that we are justified not by our own wisdom, or piety, or the works we have done in holiness of heart, but by faith.”

Silence fell. The monks glanced at one another, each hearing in those words not only comfort but a challenge, a shaping of memory, a refusal to let his last breath be translated into some other man’s triumph. In the cloister, language was power; the phrase that survived would govern how the dead were used by the living.

Then Gerard, still with that strange lucidity, said, “Please you sweep the floor.”

They obeyed at once, with awe and curiosity. The broom rasped over stone, raising a dry scent—dust, chalk, and the faint sharpness of old ash that had lodged in cracks. In that sound there was something domestic, almost kitchen-like, as if a hearth were being cleared for the next baking, and the act steadied them.

“Make me a great cross with wood ashes,” said Clement.

They fetched ashes—grey, light, and powder-fine, the residue of yesterday’s fire. They strewed them upon the floor in the form of a great Cross. The ash lay like flour on a board, dulling the stone’s cold shine, and the air took on that particular smell of extinguished warmth: char without flame, the ghost of fuel.

“Now lay me down on it,” he said, “for so will I die.”

They lifted him gently from the bed. He was so wasted that he seemed to weigh nothing, as if grief had consumed him the way a slow fire consumes wood from within. They laid him upon the cross of ashes.

“Shall we spread out thine arms, dear brother?” one asked, trembling.

“Now God forbid!” he answered. “Am I worthy of that?”

He lay silent, eyes raised, not staring but seeing. Presently he spoke, half to them and half to himself, with rapture gathered tight as a fist.

“Oh,” he said, “I feel it buoyant. It lifts me floating in the sky whence my merits had sunk me like lead.”

Day broke and showed his face cast upward in silent joy, hands together—like Margaret’s, they said afterward, remembering that other death as men remember the taste of a bread once eaten in hunger: never quite describable, never forgotten.

And just about the hour she died, he spoke his last word in this world.

“Jesu!”

And even with that word he fell asleep.

They laid him out for his rest. Under his linen they found a horse-hair shirt, rough as penance itself.

“Ah!” cried the young monks, “behold a saint!”

Under the hair-cloth they found a long thick tress of auburn hair.

They started back, horrified; and a babel of voices rose—some condemning, some excusing—each man trying to force the ungovernable fact into the lexicon he trusted. The lock, mute and fragrant with some ghost of the past, resisted them all.

In the midst of the dispute Jerome came in. He listened a moment, his face hardening at the sound of eager judgment. Then he turned to an ardent young monk called Basil, who was crying scandal the loudest.

“Basil,” said he, “is she alive or dead that owned this hair?”

“How may I know, father?”

“Then for aught thou knowest it may be the relic of a saint.”

“Certes it may be,” said Basil, sceptically.

“Thou hast then broken our rule,” Jerome replied, “which saith, ‘Put ill construction on no act done by a brother which can be construed innocently.’ Who art thou to judge such a man as this was? Go to thy cell, and stir not out for a week by way of penance.”

He carried off the lock of hair. And when the coffin was to be closed, he cleared the cell and, alone with the dead, put the tress upon Clement’s bosom.

“There, Clement,” he said to the still face.

Then, as if he feared even that mercy might be pride, he set himself a penance for doing it—and nailed the coffin up with his own hands.

The next day Gerard was buried in Gouda churchyard. The monks followed in procession from the convent, their footsteps and chant making a slow river of sound through streets that otherwise held their tongues. Jerome, evidently fulfilling the wishes of the deceased, read the service.

The grave was deep, and at the bottom of it lay a lead coffin already waiting. Poor Gerard’s—light as a feather, so had he wasted—was lowered and placed beside it.

After the service Jerome spoke a few words to the crowd of parishioners who had come to take the last look at their best friend. When he named the departed man’s virtues, loud wailing broke out, and tears fell upon the coffin like rain—salt upon wood, grief upon grief.

The monks went home. Jerome gathered them in the refectory, that room of daily bread and measured silence, and there spoke to them—not as one triumphing, but as one chastened.

“We have this day laid a saint in the earth,” he said. “The convent will keep his trentals, but will feast, not fast; for our good brother is freed from the burden of the flesh; his labours are over, and he hath entered into his joyful rest. I alone shall fast, and do penance; for to my shame I say it, I was unjust to him, and knew not his worth till it was too late.

“And you, young monks, be not curious to inquire whether a lock he bore upon his bosom was a token of pure affection or the relic of a saint; but remember the heart he wore beneath. Most of all, fix your eyes upon his life and conversation, and follow them an ye may: for he was a holy man.”

Thus, after life’s fitful fever, these true lovers were at peace. The grave—kinder to them than the Church—united them for ever; and now a man of another age and nation, touched with their fate, has laboured to build their tombstone and rescue them from long and unmerited oblivion. He asks for them your sympathy, but not your pity. No: put this story to a wholesome use.

Fiction must often give false views of life and death; here, as it happens—curbed by history—she gives you true ones. Let the barrier that kept these true lovers apart prepare you for this: that here on earth there will nearly always be some obstacle or other to your perfect happiness. To their early death apply your Reason and your Faith, by way of exercise and preparation. For if you cannot bear to be told that these died young—who, had they lived a hundred years, would still be dead—how shall you bear to see the gentle, the loving, and the true glide from your own bosom to the grave, and fly from your house to heaven?

Yet this is in store for you. In every age the Master of life and death, kinder as well as wiser than we are, has transplanted to heaven, young, earth’s sweetest flowers. I ask your sympathy, then, for their rare constancy and pure affection, and their cruel separation by a vile heresy in the bosom of the Church; but not your pity for their early yet happy end.

Beati sunt qui in Domino moriuntur.
