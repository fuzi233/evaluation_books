You, the fortunate resident of Three-Dimensional Spaceland, blessed with both light and shadow, gifted with two eyes, a native understanding of perspective, and the full spectrum of colour to parse the world around you, you who can actually *see* an angle laid out before you, and take in the full curved circumference of a Circle all at once—how could I possibly convey the absurd, constant difficulty we Flatlanders face when trying to tell one another’s shapes apart?
Call back to what I told you earlier: every single being in our flat world, animate or inanimate, no matter how many sides or points their form has, appears to our sight as nothing more than a straight (or nearly straight) line segment. If every single thing looks like a line, how do we tell anyone apart?
We rely on three distinct methods.
The first is hearing, and our sense of sound is far more finely tuned than yours. It lets us pick out personal friends by their unique voices instantly, and even reliably distinguish between the three lowest regular classes: Equilateral Triangles, Squares, and Pentagons. We do not bother counting the lowly Isosceles Triangles as a class for this purpose. But as you move up the social ladder, telling voices apart gets far harder: higher ranks deliberately cultivate nearly identical, uniform tones, and the ability to identify people by voice alone is written off as a coarse, plebeian skill that the Aristocracy never bothers to hone. Worst of all, low-class Isosceles Triangles have far more developed vocal organs than their sense of hearing, and with a little practice, they can mimic the voice of any Polygon, even a Circle himself, with perfect accuracy. That means we can never fully trust hearing alone, especially if there is any risk of imposture.
Which is why a second method is far more widely used: *Feeling*. For all Women and the vast majority of our lower and middle classes, feeling is the default way to identify strangers, to confirm someone’s class, the exact equivalent of what you call an “introduction” in Spaceland.
In remote rural districts, old-fashioned country gentlemen still use the full, formal introduction formula: “Permit me to ask you to feel and be felt by my friend Mr. So-and-so.” But in towns and among business folk, the “be felt by” clause is dropped, and the line is shortened to “Let me ask you to feel Mr. So-and-so,” with everyone taking for granted that the feeling will be reciprocal. Even trendier, brash young upper-class men, who hate wasting even a second of effort and care nothing for the purity of our native tongue, have further mangled the phrase into slang that uses “to feel” as a transitive verb meaning “to formally recommend two people to verify each other’s shapes by touch.” Right now, the fast set of upper class polite society thinks nothing of saying the absurd, barbaric line: “Mr. Smith, permit me to feel Mr. Jones.”
Do not make the mistake of assuming feeling is a slow, tedious chore, as it would be for you. We do not have to run our fingers along every single side of a stranger to tell what they are. The skill is drilled into us in elementary school, refined over a lifetime of daily use, and we can instantly distinguish by touch the angles of an equal-sided Triangle, Square, and Pentagon. Even the dullest touch can pick out the sharp, tiny vertex of an acute Isosceles Triangle. As a rule, we only need to brush a single one of your points to confirm exactly what class you belong to—unless you are part of the highest echelons of the nobility. There, the difficulty spikes sharply. Even a Master of Arts from our prestigious Wentbridge University has been documented mixing up a ten-sided Polygon and a twelve-sided one, and barely any Doctor of Science in the entire kingdom can claim to tell a twenty-sided and twenty-four-sided member of the Aristocracy apart instantly, no hesitation.
If you recall the laws I shared earlier regarding Women, you will immediately see why introductions via feeling require strict care and discretion. An unexpected angle could easily inflict irreparable harm on the person doing the feeling. It is absolutely mandatory for safety that the person being felt stands perfectly, completely still. A sudden jolt, a fidgety shift of position, even a violent sneeze, has been known to kill incautious feelers out of hand, nipping a perfectly promising new friendship in the bud. This risk is most extreme with the lowest class of Triangles: their eyes sit far up near their wide, blunt base, so they can barely tell what is happening at the far end of their sharp vertex. They are also rough, coarse, and far less sensitive to the light, delicate touch of a highly organized Polygon. Is it any wonder an involuntary toss of the head has more than once robbed the State of a valued, productive life?
My own venerable grandfather, one of the least irregular members of his unfortunate Isosceles class, who won four out of seven votes from the Sanitary and Social Board right before he died to finally be promoted to the ranks of the Equilateral, often cried as he told the story of his great-great-great-grandfather: a respectable working man with a vertex angle of 59 degrees 30 minutes. That unfortunate ancestor, suffering from rheumatism, jolted by accident while a high-rank Polygon was feeling his angle, and skewered the nobleman right through his diagonal. The accident led to my ancestor’s long imprisonment and formal degradation, and the entire family was hit with a full 1.5 degree setback on their slow climb up to regular status. The next generation’s family vertex angle was formally registered at only 58 degrees, and it took five full generations to recover the lost ground, hit the full 60 degree mark, and finally escape Isosceles serfdom. All that cascading disaster from one tiny, clumsy misstep during a routine feeling.
I can already hear well-educated Spaceland readers protesting: “How do you people even know what an angle is, let alone measure them down to the minute? We can see an angle, because we in the third dimension can look at two straight lines slanted relative to each other at once. But you can never see more than one straight line at a time, or at most a bunch of line segments stacked flat into one single straight line. How could you ever tell angles apart, let alone measure them so precisely?”
My answer is simple: we do not *see* angles, we *infer* them, and with a level of precision your naked 3D sight could never match, without the aid of a protractor. Our sense of touch, sharpened by centuries of absolute necessity and refined by lifelong training, lets us distinguish tiny differences in the slant of two lines meeting far better than your eyes can.
Nor can I leave out the natural helps Nature provides us. It is a fundamental law of our Flatland that the vertex of the lowest Isosceles starts at half a degree (thirty minutes), and if the family advances at all, that angle grows by half a degree a generation, until it hits 60 degrees, the point where they leave serfdom behind and become full free Regular citizens. That means Nature herself gives us a built-in ascending scale of angles from half a degree all the way up to 60, and we place dozens of these specimens in every elementary school across the country. Thanks to occasional generational backsliding, stagnant moral and intellectual growth, and the extraordinary high birth rate of our Criminal and Vagabond classes, we always have a massive surplus of half-degree and one-degree individuals, and plenty more specimens all the way up to 10 degrees. These unfortunates have zero civic rights, and far too many lack the basic intelligence to even serve as soldiers in our wars, so the State assigns them to the education system. They are chained completely immobile, to eliminate any risk of them stabbing a child, and placed in infant school classrooms to train the touch sensitivity of middle class children, the very intelligence the specimens themselves will never be capable of.
In some lenient states, these specimens are fed, and allowed to live for several years. But in our more sensible, well-regulated regions, the education boards long ago realized it is far better to never feed them, and replace the entire stock of specimens once a month — that is the average lifespan of a criminal Isosceles without food anyway. The cheap, low-budget schools claim they save money by keeping specimens alive longer, but they lose far more on feeding costs, and the angles of the specimens wear down and become inaccurate after weeks of thousands of children touching them over and over. That “expensive” monthly replacement system also has a quiet, massive benefit: it slowly but steadily reduces the surplus of the low Isosceles population, a goal every Flatland statesman works toward constantly. Though I am aware many popularly elected school boards push for the so-called “cheap system,” I firmly believe this is one of many cases where spending a little more up front is the truest form of economy.
I will not get dragged into school board politics any longer. I think I have said enough to prove that recognition by feeling is nowhere near as tedious or unreliable as you might have assumed, and it is far more trustworthy than recognition by voice alone. But as we have already established, the method carries unavoidable, deadly risk. That is why almost all of the middle and lower classes, and every single member of the Polygonal and Circular aristocracy, prefer a third, far safer method of recognizing one another — which I will describe for you in the next section.
