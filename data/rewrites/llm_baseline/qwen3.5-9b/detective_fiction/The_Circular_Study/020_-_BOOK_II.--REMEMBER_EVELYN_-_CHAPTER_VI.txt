A heavy silence, thick with unspoken emotion, settled over the room following that final plea. While the listeners of this extraordinary tale murmured amongst themselves and Thomas Adams turned toward his wife with a mixture of affection and deep worry, Inspector Gryce returned the memorandum he had taken from Mr. Adams. The document listed twelve critical points regarding the murder, most of which now bore the stamp of resolution.

*   **Point 1:** Why a woman capable of calmly arranging her hair at the start of an encounter could suddenly descend into such frenzied rage as to slay a man with whom she shared no prior grievance? *(Answered)*
*   **Point 2:** What significance lay in the words written by the deceased moments before the interview: *"I return you your daughter. Neither you nor she will ever see me again. Remember Evelyn!"* *(Answered)*
*   **Point 3:** Why was the pronoun "I" utilized in that communication? What standing did Mr. Felix Adams hold vis-à-vis this young girl that entitled him to speak such terms after her marriage to his brother? *(Answered)*
*   **Point 4:** Having penned those words, why did he, upon being assaulted, attempt to swallow the paper itself, dying with it clamped firmly between his teeth? *(Answered)*
*   **Point 5:** If he was slain in a fit of anger and met his end like a monster, as the killer claimed, why did his face reflect sorrow rather than hate, and a resolve seemingly untouched by the chaotic surge of emotions one expects from receiving a fatal blow from an unexpected foe? *(Answered)*
*   **Point 6:** Possessing the strength to grasp the paper and bring it to his mouth, why did he not use that same vigor to reach for a light switch—calculated to summon aid—instead of leaving the room bathed in the crimson glow of the signal lamp, which currently meant: *"Nothing more required just now. Keep away?"* *(Answered)*
*   **Point 7:** What was the purpose of the massive steel plate discovered wedged between the door casings, and why did it remain motionless in its socket at this climactic moment of his life? *(Answered)*
*   **Point 8:** How did old Poindexter arrive on the scene so swiftly after the incident? Overheard words attributed to him were: *"It is Amos' son, not Amos!"* Did he not know whom he was destined to meet in that house? Was the sight of the man lying there, bearing a cross on his chest and a dagger in his heart, any less surprising to him than the identity of the victim? *(Not Answered)*
*   **Point 9:** Recall the deductions drawn from Bartow's pantomime. Mr. Adams was struck down by a left-handed thrust. We await confirmation that the young woman is indeed left-handed, and we require an explanation for why she held her other arm stretched rigidly behind her for so long. *(Answered)*
*   **Point 10:** Why does the bird, whose primary cry is *"Remember Evelyn!"*, occasionally alter its song to *"Poor Eva! Lovely Eva! Who would strike Eva?"* For this tragedy to hold truth, the narrative must establish that Mr. Adams knew his brother's bride intimately, both in duration and depth. *(Answered)*
*   **Point 11:** If Bartow is, as we suspect, innocent of any direct involvement in this crime aside from witnessing it, why does he display such palpable joy at its outcome? While this may not fall strictly within the scope of Thomas Adams's confession, it cannot be overlooked by us. This deaf-and-dumb servant was driven mad by the very fact that filled him with delight. Why? *(Answered)*
*   *Note:* It must be remembered that the fragments of writing in Felix's hand had not yet been recovered by the police. Allusions within them suggest Bartow was consumed by a jealousy that likely curdled into triumph upon seeing his master felled by the object of that master's love and his own hatred. How he recognized in the bride of another man the owner of the name so frequently whispered by his master remains a puzzle for more astute observers of human perception. Perhaps he spent countless hours at the stairwell loophole, studying his master until he deciphered every gesture and expression.
*   **Point 12:** Observe the following schedule, compiled after repeated trials with Bartow and the enigmatic lamp slides that cast varied lights into Mr. Adams's study:
*   *White Light:* Water desired.
*   *Green Light:* Bring coat and hat.
*   *Blue Light:* Return books to shelves.
*   *Violet Light:* Arrange study for the night.
*   *Yellow Light:* Await the next signal.
*   *Red Light:* Nothing wanted; depart.

The red light was active during the final scene. Can this fact be reconciled with Mr. Adams's account?

Only two paragraphs remained without full explanation. The first, Number 9, was particularly vital. The description of the stroke delivered by Mrs. Adams failed to account for the peculiar posture observed in Bartow's pantomime. Consulting with the inspector, Mr. Gryce finally approached Mr. Adams and asked if he possessed the strength to reenact the blow exactly as witnessed by his wife.

The startled young man hesitated, dreading the question he feared to voice. Like everyone else present, he knew Bartow had performed specific gestures attempting to describe the crime, yet he remained unaware of their nature, as this crucial detail had been deliberately withheld by the police. Consequently, he did not react immediately to the suggestion. Instead, he pondered intensely for a moment before extending his left hand, snatching an object from the inspector's table, and lunging across his body toward an imaginary adversary on his right.

He then scanned the faces around him with profound anxiety, finding little reassurance in their expressions.

"You would make your wife out left-handed," Mr. Gryce suggested gently.

"Now I have watched her since she entered this place," Mr. Adams replied earnestly. "I have seen no evidence of this."

"She is not left-handed," Mr. Adams continued, his voice trembling slightly. "But she thrust with her left hand because her right was held fast in mine. I had seized her instinctively as she leaped forward for the weapon, and the convulsive grip of our two hands was not released until the horror of her deed caused her to faint. She fell away from me to the floor, crying: 'Tear down the cross and lay it on your brother's breast. I would at least see him die the death of a Christian.'"

Mr. Gryce glanced at the inspector with an air of immense relief. The mystery surrounding the constrained position of the right hand—which had rendered Bartow's pantomime so baffling—was now naturally resolved. Picking up the blue pencil the inspector had set down, Mr. Gryce smiled and wrote a decisive "answered" across paragraph number nine.
