To truly grasp the architecture of this evening, one must first understand the suffocating weight of the silence that preceded it. The Crimean night outside was merely a backdrop, a canvas for a drama played out within the gilded cage of Broad Place. Lieutenant Sutch arrived as the vanguard of General Feversham's retinue, stepping into a sanctuary that felt less like a home and more like a museum dedicated to obsolescence. The old red-brick house, perched precariously on the southern slope of the Surrey hills, glowed with the feverish warmth of a relic, its windows reflecting the golden afternoon sun that dared not penetrate the dark, impenetrable forest of pines surrounding it.

Sutch limped across the hall, his movement a rhythmic reminder of the war that had claimed his youth—a war now reduced to gossip and anniversaries. Above him, the portraits of the Fevershams ascended the wall like a lineage of ghosts, watching with painted indifference. On the stone-flagged terrace behind, he found his host. General Feversham sat erect, rigid as a statue carved from marble, gazing southward toward the Sussex Downs with the detached curiosity of a man observing a specimen in a jar.

"How's the leg?" Feversham asked, rising with a briskness that belied the stiffness in his joints. He was a small, wiry creature, his white hair offering no softening to a face that was nothing more than bone and shadow. A high, narrow forehead and steel-blue eyes that held no reflection suggested a mind entirely barren of empathy, a landscape devoid of vegetation.

"It gave me trouble during the winter," Sutch replied, the admission tasting like ash. "But that was to be expected."

Feversham nodded, a mechanical gesture, and for a moment, the two men stood in a silence that pressed against the eardrums. Below them, the ground fell away into a vast, indifferent plain of brown earth and emerald fields, dotted with dark clumps of trees. Voices drifted up from the distance, small and clear, belonging to a world that did not know the weight of history. Far away, near Horsham, a coil of white smoke from a train snaked rapidly through the trees, a fleeting symbol of progress moving on regardless. On the horizon, the Downs rose, patched with white chalk, mocking the grim reality of the present.

"I thought that I should find you here," Sutch said.

"It was my wife's favorite corner," Feversham answered, his voice devoid of emotion, as if reciting a fact from a ledger. "She would sit here by the hour. She had a queer liking for wide and empty spaces."

"Yes," Sutch murmured, feeling the ghost of a smile tug at his lips. "She had imagination. Her thoughts could people them."

Feversham glanced at his companion with a look of mild confusion, as though the concept of internal landscapes was foreign to him. But he asked no questions. What he did not understand, he habitually discarded, letting it slip from his mind as unworthy of comprehension. Instead, he shifted the focus immediately to the inevitable.

"There will be a leaf out of our table tonight."

"Yes," Sutch acknowledged, easing his crippled leg with a wince. "Collins, Barberton, and Vaughan went this winter. Well, we are all permanently shelved upon the world's half-pay list as it is. The obituary column is just the last formality which gazettes us out of the service altogether."

"I am glad that you came before the others," Feversham continued, his tone shifting slightly, acquiring a hint of performative gravity. "I would like to take your opinion. This day is more to me than the anniversary of our attack upon the Redan. At the very moment when we were standing under arms in the dark—"

"To the west of the quarries; I remember," Sutch interrupted, drawing a deep, involuntary breath. "How should one forget?"

"At that very moment Harry was born in this house," Feversham declared. "I thought, therefore, that if you did not object, he might join us tonight. He happens to be at home. He will, of course, enter the service, and he might learn something, perhaps, which afterward will be of use—one never knows."

"By all means," Sutch said with alacrity. For years, his visits to the General had been limited to these anniversary dinners, occasions where he had never yet seen Harry Feversham. Sutch had long been puzzled by the qualities in General Feversham that had attracted Muriel Graham, a woman renowned for the refinement of her intellect and the beauty of her person. He could never find an explanation for why such a refined spirit had married a man so much older, so utterly alien in character. Personal courage and an indomitable, almost pathological self-confidence were the only traits that surfaced in the General. Now, Sutch retreated into memory, tracing the lines of his own past back to a season in London, fresh from the China station, driven by a curiosity that he carefully masked as the natural interest of a disabled man studying human nature. He wanted simply to see if the boy took after his mother or his father—that was all.

That night, Harry Feversham took his place at the dinner table, listening to the stories his elders spun. The tales were all of that dark winter in the Crimea, a relentless stream of death, hazardous exploits, the pinch of famine, and the chilling bite of snow. Yet they were told in clipped words, with a matter-of-fact tone that treated these atrocities as distant curiosities, mere entries in a logbook. There was seldom a comment more profound than a "That's curious," or an exclamation more significant than a nervous laugh.

But Harry Feversham sat listening as though the incidents thus casually narrated were unfolding in real-time within the four walls of the room. His dark eyes—the eyes of his mother—darted from speaker to speaker, wide-open and fixed, waiting for every syllable to land. He listened, fascinated and entrapped. So vividly did the changes of expression shoot and quiver across his young face that it seemed to Sutch the boy must actually hear the drone of bullets in the air, actually resist the stunning shock of a charge, actually ride down into the thick of a squadron where guns screeched out tongues of flame from the fog.

Once, a Major of Artillery spoke of the agonizing suspense of the hours between the parading of the troops and the first command to advance. Harry's shoulders worked under the intolerable strain of those lagging minutes, trembling with a tension that bordered on hysteria.

But he did more than tremble. He threw a single, furtive, wavering glance backward. Sutch was startled, and indeed more than startled—he was pained. For this was Muriel Graham's boy.

The look was too familiar to Sutch. He had seen it on the faces of recruits during their first exposure to battle too often to misunderstand it. A specific image rose before his mind: an advancing square at Inkermann, a tall, big soldier rushing forward from the line in the eagerness of his attack, then stopping suddenly as though he suddenly realized he was alone, facing the charge of a mounted Cossack. Sutch remembered clearly the fatal, wavering glance the soldier had thrown backward toward his companions—a glance accompanied by a queer, sickly smile. He remembered, with equal vividness, its consequence. Though the soldier carried a loaded musket with a bayonet locked to the muzzle, he had, without an effort of self-defense, received the Cossack's lance-thrust in his throat.

Sutch glanced hurriedly about the table, fearing that General Feversham or one of his guests had noticed the same look, the same smile, on Harry's face. But no one paid the lad any heed; each visitor was too eager to seize an opportunity to tell a story of their own. Sutch drew a breath of relief and turned to Harry. But the boy was sitting with his elbows on the cloth, his head propped between his hands, lost to the glare of the room and its glitter of silverware. He was reconstructing, out of the swift succession of anecdotes, a world of cries and wounds, maddened riderless chargers, and men writhing in a fog of cannon-smoke. Even the curtest, least graphic description of the biting days and nights in the trenches set the lad shivering. His face grew pinched, as though the iron frost of that winter was actually eating into his bones.

Sutch touched him lightly on the elbow.

"You renew those days for me," he said softly. "Though the heat is dripping down the windows, I feel the chill of the Crimea."

Harry roused himself from his absorption.

"The stories renew them," he said.

"No. It is you listening to the stories."

Before Harry could reply, General Feversham's voice broke sharply from the head of the table.

"Harry, look at the clock!"

Instantly, all eyes turned upon the lad. The hands of the clock made the acutest of angles. It was close upon midnight; from eight, without so much as a word or a question, he had sat at the dinner table listening. Yet even now, he rose with reluctance.

"Must I go, father?" he asked, and the General's guests intervened in a chorus. The conversation was clear gain to the lad, a first taste of powder which might stand him in good stead later.
