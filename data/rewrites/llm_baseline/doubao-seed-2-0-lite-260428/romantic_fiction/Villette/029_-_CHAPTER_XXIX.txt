I rose an hour before the first streak of dawn seeped through the dormitory windows, and knelt on the cold bare floor beside the centre lamp stand to finish my work, working by the last glimmer of the sputtering night-lamp as it guttered to its end. Every bead, every skein of coloured silk I owned was exhausted before the guard I wove reached the length and richness I had dreamed of: I stitched it double over, knowing full well that to catch the eye of the sharp, exacting man I designed it for, nothing short of a lush, intentional finish would do. For a final touch I pried the small gold clasp from my only necklace, fastened it neatly to the end of the chain, coiled the finished piece soft and compact, and tucked it into a tiny polished box I had bought for its rare nacarat-pink sheen, carved from a tropical shell and crowned with a circlet of tiny sparkling blue stones. With the sharp point of my scissors I carefully scored his full initials deep into the inside of the lid: P. C. D. E., for Paul Carl David Emanuel, that long string of baptismal names every continental gentleman seemed to carry.

You will remember the lavish annual fête held for Madame Beck, no doubt, and how every pupil contributed a small sum to buy her a grand gift. That honour was reserved only for her, save for the spontaneous, unscripted tributes offered each year to her kinsman, the fiery, adored Professor Emanuel. No one planned or pressured the girls to participate for his day: it rose of its own accord, proof of the quiet fondness everyone held for him, even in spite of his quick temper, stubborn prejudices, and bouts of unsparing sharpness. He would not accept costly plate or jewellery, he had made that perfectly clear—no pompous gift of gold or diamonds would please him half so much as a single wild flower, or a simple sketch, offered honestly and without fanfare. That was his nature: far from the worldly, calculating men around him, there was a tender, unguarded spot deep in his chest that leaned toward soft, unvarnished goodness.

Monsieur Paul’s fête fell on the first of March, a Thursday, and the day broke bright and sun-warmed. It was a day set aside for morning mass, with a half-holiday promised for the afternoon to walk the streets, shop, or call on friends, and the whole school buzzed with uncharacteristic excitement. Clean new collars appeared on every girl’s uniform, the usual dingy workday wool dresses were swapped out for lighter, brighter fabrics. Mademoiselle Zélie St. Pierre went so far as to wear a full silk gown, a luxury almost unheard of in thrifty Labassecour, and sent for a professional coiffeur to arrange her hair that morning; sharp-eyed pupils spotted her dabbing expensive new perfume on her handkerchief and wrists. Poor Zélie! She had complained for months that she was sick to death of seclusion and drudgery, that she dreamed of a husband who would pay off her mounting debts, stock her wardrobe, and set her free to “taste a little of life’s pleasures.” Everyone had long suspected she set her cap for Monsieur Emanuel. And it was true, his eyes often lingered on her, for minutes at a stretch, while the class worked silent at their desks and he sat idle on his raised platform. She would squirm under that unblinking gaze, half-flattered, half-confused, while he read right through every petty lie and hidden desire she tried to veil, that terrible, unerring instinct of his peeling back every soft, flowery pretense to lay bare the warped, empty corners of the soul no one else ever saw. If you owned your flaws to him openly, he would pity and forgive you anything—but if he met with denial and deceit? He could be cruel, brutally so, snatching the screen from cowering pretenders and dragging them out for all to see their own falsity, convinced he was doing righteous justice. More than once I had cried for his victims, and snapped back at him in anger, and he never once understood why I would side with the liars he thought he had a right to unmask.

When breakfast was over and mass attended, the school bell rang and the classroom filled, every pupil and teacher neatly dressed, holding a fresh bouquet of spring blooms that perfumed the whole air. Only my hands were empty. I love to see flowers growing in the earth, but plucked roots are nothing to me: they are rootless, perishable, too much a reminder of something already dying, and they make me sad. I never offer cut flowers to those I love, and I never wish to receive them from dear hands. Zélie spotted my empty hands instantly, and her eyes darted around me in greedy triumph, certain I must have hidden at least a small knot of violets somewhere. When she saw I truly had nothing, no bloom, no leaf at all, as bare as a winter tree, she smiled, well pleased.
“How wise of you to save your money, Miss Lucy!” she trilled. “Foolish me, I threw two whole francs away on this hothouse bouquet!” She held it aloft, preening at the size and richness of it.

Hush. There was that step, the familiar quick, sharp footfall that belonged only to him. It was as prompt as ever, but it carried a lightness we all liked to think was born of happy anticipation, not his usual nervous vehemence. He stepped through the door in a mood that was brighter than the morning sun spilling over the classroom walls. He had dressed for the occasion, no shapeless, soot-dark conspirator’s overcoat hiding his frame, no defiant old Greek cap perched on his head. Instead he wore a neat, well-cut coat and a soft silk waistcoat that looked almost pretty, bare-headed, holding his formal hat in gloved hands. The little man looked unexpectedly fine: there was a warm glow of good humour in his bright blue eyes, a flush of gentle feeling on his dark cheeks that outshone conventional beauty, and no one cared to notice his slightly lopsided nose, thin cheeks, square marked brow, or un-rosebud mouth. He was himself, and his presence was warm, alive, impossible to ignore. He set his hat and gloves on his desk.
“Bon jour, mes amies,” he said, in a soft, rough tone that made amends for every sharp snap and savage snarl we had endured from him before. It was his voice, the one he only used when his heart was speaking before his lips, the tender spot deep in his chest coming to the surface, the part that was softer to women and little children than most men ever dared to be.
“We all wish Monsieur a happy fête day, and offer him our warmest congratulations,” Zélie announced, stepping forward uninvited as spokeswoman for the whole room, laying her lavish two-franc bouquet on his desk. He bowed over it, and the line of pupils filed past after her, leaving their small tributes behind, until the pile of blooms rose into a tall, lush pyramid, so tall it completely hid the man behind it. We all sat silent, waiting for his speech.

Five minutes passed. Ten. No sound. He was completely concealed behind the flowers, still as a statue. At last a deep, hollow voice rumbled out from behind the petals:
“Est-ce là tout?” (Is that all?)
Zélie glanced around, asked all the girls if they had given their bouquets. Every last one, from the eldest senior to the tiniest seven year old, nodded. “Est-ce là tout?” he repeated, his voice dropping several notes lower, heavier.
“Monsieur,” Zélie purred, rising with her sweetest, most affected smile, “I have the honour to tell you every single person in class has offered their bouquet, save one. Miss Lucy, as a foreigner, probably does not know our customs, and thinks this silly little ceremony too frivolous to honour with her presence.” I bit back a snort of amusement. Famous, Zélie, you are a very fine orator when you want to be.
From behind the flower pile, a hand waved dismissively, ordering silence. A moment later he stepped out from behind the petals, planted himself on the edge of his platform, and stared straight ahead at the giant wall map of the world, his tone tragic, sharp with hurt, as he asked a third time:
“Est-ce là tout?”
I still could have stepped forward, slipped my little pink shell box into his hand, and made everything right. That was what I had planned all along. But the comedy of his dramatic show had made me delay, and now Zélie’s petty, lying jab pricked my stubborn streak. I would not defend myself. I would not give him my gift just yet, not when he was being so dramatic, not when he so clearly thought I had snubbed him. Let him be vexed a little. I sat still, face blank as stone, clutching the shell box tight in my lap.

“C’est bien,” he said at last. A shadow of wrath, of bitter disappointment, crossed his brow, rippled over his lips, lined his cheeks. He swallowed down all the words he wanted to snap, and launched into his expected speech. I did not listen. The force of him gulping back his hurt sent a strange little jolt through me, half-laughable, half tender. Toward the end of his speech, I fumbled my thimble, dropped it on the floor, and hit my head hard on the sharp corner of my desk when I stooped to grab it. The small clatter was all it took to break his fragile forced equanimity. He threw all his careful dignity to the wind, and suddenly found himself ranting across the English Channel, furious about “les Anglaises.”
I have never heard English women handled with such vicious, spiteful glee. He spared nothing: our height, our long necks, our thin arms, our slovenly dress, our pedantic education, our impious scepticism, our unendurable pride, our self-righteous pretence. He bared his teeth and snarled, repeating every ridiculous, slanderous false continental myth about our greatest men and our national history, dragging the Union Jack through the mud. Zélie and the whole class grinned, delighted, for all of Labassecour secretly harboured a silly, petty hatred for England. I could not bear it. I slammed my fist down sharp on my desk, and yelled at the top of my voice:
“Vive l’Angleterre, l’Histoire et les Héros! A bas la France, la Fiction et les Faquins!”

The whole room froze, as if I had gone mad. Monsieur Paul hid his face in his handkerchief, and smiled a fiendish, triumphant little smile. He had won. He had provoked me out of my stubborn silence, and now his whole sour mood vanished. He turned soft and bland again, singing poetic praises of the flowers’ sweetness and purity, comparing each pupil to the blossoms on his desk, paying Zélie a grand, fulsome compliment on her bouquet, and announcing that the first warm mild spring morning, he would take the whole class out for breakfast in the country. “That is,” he added, sharply, “only those of you I can count among my friends.”
“Then I will not be coming,” I blurted out, before I could stop myself.
“Soit!” (So be it.) he snapped back. He gathered his whole armful of flowers, and stormed out of the classroom. I stuffed my neglected shell box back into my desk, grabbed my things, and ran up the stairs. I was hot, angry, my cheeks burning. And yet, an hour later, as I sat on the edge of my bed replaying the whole ridiculous scene over and over in my head, I found myself smiling. Only one small, soft pang of regret pricked me: I had meant to give him my gift, to make him happy, and fate had tangled everything up.

Later that afternoon, I remembered the classroom desks were never perfectly secure. I had better go retrieve the pink shell box, before someone found it and saw his initials scored into the lid. The school was quiet, fast asleep in its holiday repose: all day pupils had gone home, all boarders were out walking, all other teachers were off shopping or calling on friends. The great salle was empty, the grand piano closed and silent, the giant globe hanging still in the middle of the room. I was surprised to find the first classe door ajar—usually it was locked when empty, and only Madame Beck and I had keys to it. I heard movement inside: a step, a chair scraping, the soft click of a desk lid opening.
I peeked through the crack. It was not Madame Beck in her shawl and clean cap. It was Monsieur Paul, back in his familiar ink-stained old overcoat, that silly Greek cap of his lying discarded on the floor at his feet. His olive hand was propped in my open desk, his face buried in my papers, his back turned to me. I had known for ages he was my secret brownie, sneaking in when no one was around to go through my things. I would leave exercises full of mistakes on my desk overnight, and wake to find them carefully corrected. Tucked between a tattered sallow dictionary and worn grammar, I would find a brand new novel, or a well-loved classic, smelling strongly of his cigar smoke. Once I had tried to air one of those books out the window, and he had caught me, snatched it out of my hand, and almost threw it into the glowing stove before I could fight it back from him. I never fussed about the cigar smell after that.

And now I had caught him red-handed, the little friendly phantom, wreathed in pale blue cigar smoke, carefully tucking two beautiful, brand new volumes I had been dying to read into the back of my desk, his hands moving over my papers gently, so he would not bend or tear a single page. All the anger from that morning melted right out of me. I did not dislike him at all.
He heard my quiet breath, turned sharp as a flash. His nerves were frayed, but he never started, never paled. “I thought you were off to town with the other teachers,” he said, gripping hard to his slipping composure. “It is just as well you are not. I do not care if you catch me. I visit your desk all the time.”
“I know that, Monsieur.”
“You find a new book sometimes, I suppose. But you do not read them, not when they smell of this?” He tapped the cigar between his fingers.
“They do, and they are no worse for it. I read every one.”
“Without pleasure?”
“I would never dare contradict Monsieur.”
“Do you like them? Are they a welcome little amusement for you? You have seen me read them a hundred times. You know I have so few small recreations, I would never undervalue anything you give me.”
“I mean well,” he said, his voice softening. “If you can see that I mean well, if my little gifts do make you smile, why can we not be friends?”
“Any fatalist would say—because we cannot.”
“This morning,” he went on, his voice dropping, wounded, “I woke up in such a bright, happy mood. I came to class full of joy, and you spoiled my whole day.”
“No Monsieur, only an hour or two. And not on purpose.”
“Not on purpose! It was my fête day. Every last person wished me happiness, but you. Even the smallest little girls in the third division came up to me, lisped their congratulations, gave me a tiny knot of violets. You? Nothing. Not a bud, not a leaf, not even a kind glance. And that was unintentional?”
“I meant no harm.”
“Then you truly did not know our custom? You were not prepared? You would willingly have spent a few centimes on a flower to give me pleasure, if you had known it was expected? Say that, and all the hurt is gone, all is forgiven.”
“I did know it was expected. I was prepared. And I spent no centimes on flowers.”
