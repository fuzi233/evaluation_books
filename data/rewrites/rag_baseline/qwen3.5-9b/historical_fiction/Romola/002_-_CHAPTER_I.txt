The Loggia de' Cerchi anchored the eastern edge of old Florence, a stone sentinel guarding a labyrinth of narrow arteries behind the Badia. In peacetime, these thoroughfares were ghost towns, rarely traversed by outsiders seeking only the obscure entrance marked *Qui Nacque Il Divino Poeta*. Yet, in the fifteenth century, the same stones had once echoed with the thunder of factional warfare; today, they vibrated only with the chaotic, unscripted friction of wool-carders in the industrial districts of San Martino and Garbo.

At dawn on April 9, 1492, two figures occupied the space beneath the archway. One stood, leaning forward with the predatory focus of a scavenger assessing potential loot. The other lay prone on the cobblestones, eyes snapping open in the startled reflex of a creature dragged abruptly from sleep.

The standing man spoke first. He was a grey-bearded giant, his physique forged by the fist and refined by the pickaxe, embodying the Tuscan archetype of the artisan. Deep furrows carved his brow and mouth, etching a mask of self-importance designed to counteract the rough, utilitarian features Nature had assigned him. At his feet sat a substantial leather sack, bulging with trade goods, while a pedlar's basket hung from his arm, laden with trivialities: spools of thread, sewing needles, and shards of glass salvaged from broken vessels.

"Young man," he commanded, gesturing sharply at a gold ring adorning the finger of the prostrate figure, "once your beard hardens, you'll learn better judgment than to nap in public corners wearing jewelry like that. By the Holy Gospels! Had anyone else stood over you two minutes ago, I'd have taken it. But Bratti Ferravecchi is not a thief. The cat cannot devour a mouse unless it catches it alive, and Bratti cannot savor profit unless he secures a genuine bargain. Consider this: on San Giovanni's feast day, three years past, a blind beggar crossed my path, his cap lined with silver coins. My stomach recoiled at the unsolicited wealth until I realized San Giovanni owed me the value of my annual festival expenses anyway. So I buried the corpse, funded a Mass, and called it a fair transaction. But pray tell, how does a youth with the visage of Messer San Michele find himself sleeping on a stone slab with the wind as his only curtain?"

His deep, guttural cadence was thick with local dialect, rendering the words unintelligible to the dazed sleeper. However, the gesture toward the ring was universal. With mechanical compliance, the stranger removed the band, shoved it into his doublet, and rose, stretching his limbs with a groan.

"Your tunic and hose clash violently with that gem," Bratti observed, his tone deliberate. "One might argue the Saints dispatched *you* as the corpse. But if you pilfered the jewels, I trust you interred the body—and you certainly can afford a few Masses to balance the scales."

A jolt of pain, sharp and electric, shot through the stranger's frame, halting his stretch. For a fleeting second, he locked eyes with Bratti, his expression tightening into a frown of suspicion. Then, the tension evaporated, replaced by a practiced indifference. He reached up, removing the red Levantine cap that drooped heavily over his left ear, tossed back his long, dark-brown curls, and glanced critically at his attire. A smile touched his lips.

"You speak truth, friend," he replied. "My garments are weather-stained like an ancient sail, though not aged. Like such a sail, they have absorbed the brine of the sea as readily as the rain. The fact is, I am a stranger in Florence. Last night, footsore and weary, I deemed it wiser to collapse in a corner of this hospitable porch than to continue hunting for a hostel that might prove a den of bloodsucking parasites."

"A stranger, truly," Bratti conceded, his voice dropping to a conspiratorial register. "For your speech melts from your throat like wax, leaving a Christian Florentine unable to distinguish a hook from a hanger. Are you not from Genoa? Your cut suggests Venice."

"At this precise moment," the stranger countered, his smile unwavering, "my origin matters less than my destination. Can you direct me to a livelier quarter where I might procure breakfast and lodging? This city currently regards me with a grim countenance."

"I can," Bratti affirmed. "It is your stroke of luck that I arrived from Rovezzano this morning and detoured through Mercato Vecchio to pay respects at the Badia. That is your fortune. But remember, young man, nothing is free. If I guide you to Mercato Vecchio, you shall swear by your patron saint to offer me the first bid on that stained ensemble when you acquire finer raiment—which, doubtless, you will."

"Agreed, by San Niccolò," the stranger laughed. "Let us proceed to this alleged Mercato, for I feel an urgent need to line this doublet you covet."

"Coveting? Nay," Bratti retorted, hoisting his sack onto his shoulder and stepping forward. Suddenly, his demeanor shifted. He erupted in a cacophony of harsh, grinding tones, mimicking the squeak of a cart wheel: "*Chi abbaratta—baratta—b'ratta—chi abbaratta cenci e vetri—b'ratta ferri vecchi?* Who wants to exchange rags, broken glass, or scrap iron?"

"It yields little value," he murmured, returning to his conversational pitch. "Your hose and attire are worth a pittance. Nevertheless, if you aspire to a lute surpassing any new instrument, a sword wielded by a Ridolfi, or a paternoster of the highest order, I could offer a magnificent deal, deducting the cost of your current wardrobe. Though I stand here in simplicity, I possess the best-furnished shop in the Ferravecchi district, situated adjacent to the Mercato. Blessed be the Virgin! I do not carry a pumpkin on my shoulders. Yet, I am not confined to my shop; I have a wife and a raven to manage the inventory. *Chi abbaratta—baratta—b'ratta?* ... Now, young man, whence do you hail, and what brings you to Florence?"

"I assumed you delighted in nothing gratis," the stranger remarked, his tone tinged with skepticism. "You have offered nothing in exchange for that intelligence."

"Well, well," Bratti shrugged. "A Florentine pays a fair price for news; it keeps the stomach content even if no hose result from the transaction. If I escort you to the fairest maiden in the Mercato for a cup of milk—that constitutes a fair bargain."

"Nay," the stranger dismissed. "If she resides in the Mercato, I shall locate her myself; pretty heads tend to peer from doorways and windows. Moreover, a shrewd trader knows that he who bids for nuts and news risks finding them hollow."

"Ah!" Bratti exclaimed, casting a sideways glance of genuine admiration. "You were not born on a Sunday; the salt shops were open when you drew breath. Are you not a Hebrew? From Spain or Naples? Let me inform you: the Minor Friars are attempting to incinerate Florence with Spanish fervor, driven by dogs of hell who seek to monopolize usury profits while denying Christians their due. When you stride along the Calimara draped in yellow cloth, it mars your beauty far more grievously than a sword slash across that smooth olive cheek. *Abbaratta, baratta—chi abbaratta?* Tell you what, young man: grey cloth opposes yellow cloth. There is ample grey cloth in Florence to clothe the Duomo, yet insufficient yellow cloth to make hose for Saint Christopher—blessed be his name, and grant me a vision of him today! *Abbaratta, baratta, b'ratta—chi abbaratta?*"

"All this is delightful intelligence, dispensed freely," the stranger scoffed. "Yet it concerns me not. I am no Hebrew."

"See now!" Bratti triumphed. "I've secured a bargain with mere words. I've extracted information from you, though you remain slippery as a lamprey. Praised be San Giovanni! A blind Florentine matches two one-eyed men. But here we arrive at the Mercato."

They emerged from the claustrophobic alleys into the vast, sun-drenched expanse of the Mercato Vecchio. Historically, this piazza served as the provisioning heart of the city, a site where ancestral Fesuleans allegedly descended from their mountain fastnesses to trade with valley dwellers. By the early decades of the fifteenth century, however, it had ceased to be solely a marketplace. The Medici and the *popolani grassi*, the commercial nobility, maintained residences here, seemingly unbothered by the deafening roar of mixed dialects or the visceral display of butcher stalls—a feature the poet Antonio Pucci hailed as the market's supreme dignity. Yet, the glory of mutton and veal was absent; Lent still held sway. The butcher guild remained inactive, leaving the stage to gardeners, cheesemongers, and vendors of macaroni, corn, eggs, milk, and dried fruit. Consequently, the chorus of commerce was dominated by female voices.
