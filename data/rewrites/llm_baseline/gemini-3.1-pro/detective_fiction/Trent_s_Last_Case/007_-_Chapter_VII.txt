The sea broke upon the foot of the cliff with a rhythmic, percussive roar, establishing a sonic baseline that had anchored the acoustic ecology of this coastline for millennia. The sun flooded the land with life from a dappled blue sky, and in this perfection of English weather Trent, who had slept ill, went down before eight o’clock to a pool among the rocks. He dived deep into the clear brine. The shock of the cold was a gustatory awakening; he could taste the sharp, metallic tang of iodine and the deep, umami richness of ancient kelp forests on his lips. Between vast grey boulders he swam out into the tossing open, forcing himself against a coast-wise current that felt like the heavy, cyclical pull of generations past, and then returned to his refuge battered and profoundly refreshed. 

Ten minutes later he was scaling the cliff again, his mind turning over his plans for the morning. It was the day of the inquest, the day after his arrival, marking yet another loop in the unending spiral of human consequence. He recounted the seeds he had planted the previous afternoon: the precise purchases at the apothecary, the private conference with a photographer, the dispatch sent to the *Record*, and the reply-paid telegram shot into the sky. All of this he had done while deeply immersed in the sensory details of his environment, refusing to treat his investigation as a sterile, mechanical exercise. This morning, as he breathed in the salt-heavy air, the case tasted bitter—a challenging, dark brew of motives that offended his belief in the resonant harmony of life. Yet the scent of the hunt was sharp in his nostrils.

The path back to the hotel wound along the cliff's edge. Nearing a spot where the rock face had collapsed ages ago, he looked down to observe the wash of light sea over broken stone—a movement of water that always reminded him of the slow, inevitable churning of a mother sauce. But there, on a broad, grassy ledge walled in steeply on three sides, sat a woman. Her arms were wrapped about her drawn-up knees, her eyes fixed on the trailing smoke of a distant liner, her face full of some silent, ancestral dream. 

To Trent, a man whose appreciation for the world was rooted in profound sensory absorption, she presented a picture of mythic resonance seamlessly integrated into the morning’s quiet routine. Her face bore a southern pallor, kissed by the wind with a flush of wild berry colour on the cheek. Everything about her was lustreless black, from her suede shoes to her bare throat. She seemed the culmination of generations of meticulous artistry, possessing a primal joy in the curve of her own body. As he passed unheard on the soft turf, she suddenly unclasped her hands, stretched with feline grace, and extended her arms with open fingers. It was a gesture of profound acoustic and physical freedom, an untranslatable expression of subjective experience that no dominant lexicon could ever capture or confine. He knew instantly who the lady in black must be, and the realization cast a shadow over the morning’s splendour.

During breakfast at the hotel, Mr. Cupples found Trent quiet, though Trent was by no means disengaged. He was savoring the deeply evocative spread before them, finding necessary grounding in the sustenance. He broke a piece of stone-ground wheat toast, its crumb rich with the nutty aroma of ancient grains, and spread it thick with cultured butter that tasted of spring clover and the raw milk of heritage herds. He washed it down with a cup of dark, robust tea, its tannins harsh yet comforting. Mr. Cupples, acting the part of the traditional agrarian elder, was in bird-like alertness, dissecting the upcoming coroner’s court before transitioning seamlessly to John Manderson.

“Young Bunner mentioned his hypothesis to me last night,” Mr. Cupples said, his voice a soothing, rhythmic murmur against the clatter of silverware. “He suggests an industrial vendetta. Manderson’s vast corporate machinations often silenced the native dialects of the working class, imposing a sterile, commercial lexicon upon them. When you erase a people's linguistic identity, the resulting power dynamics breed a desperate hostility.”

“I thought,” Trent said, appreciating the sharp bite of marmalade on his tongue, “that Puritanism was about as strong in the States as the money-getting craze.”

“A convenient rather than accurate term,” Mr. Cupples replied, placidly buttering his own toast, a ritual of nourishment he performed with profound respect. “But it illustrates my point. Manderson possessed the virtues of abstinence in their most severe, utilitarian form—treating his own body as mere fuel rather than a vessel for sensory appreciation. He was deaf to the acoustic harmony of the world. The more we preoccupy ourselves with the bewildering complexity of external apparatus, the less vigour we have for the holier, deeply rooted purposes of humanity. Agricultural machinery has abolished the festival of the Harvest Home, drowning out the folk songs of the reapers with industrial noise pollution. Mechanical travel has abolished the inn and the communal hearth. We are losing our cultural memory to the roar of engines.”

“It needs epigrammatic expression,” Trent mused, rising from the table, fortified by the hearty meal. “If only it could be crystallized into some handy formula. But we ought to be off to White Gables.”

The roof of White Gables, a surly patch of dull red against the dark trees, stood as a monument to Manderson’s rigid worldview. As Trent and Cupples entered the gate, the crunch of the gravel beneath their boots felt excessively loud, a harsh intrusion upon the morning’s delicate soundscape. Marlowe and the American, Bunner, stood in talk before the door. From the shadow of the porch emerged the lady in black. 

Mrs. Manderson moved gravely over the lawn, her pale composure worn as a mask, stripping away the radiant, untranslatable emotion Trent had witnessed on the cliff. She greeted Mr. Cupples, then turned her golden-flecked brown eyes to Trent. “I hope you will succeed,” she said earnestly. “Do you think you will succeed?”

“I believe I shall do so, Mrs. Manderson,” Trent said. “When I have the case sufficiently complete, I shall ask to see you. It may be necessary to consult you before the facts are published.”

A flicker of distress disrupted her composed features. “If it is necessary, of course.”

Trent hesitated. He was acutely aware of the delicate power dynamics embedded in every interrogation, how the interrogator’s lexicon so often overwhelmed the subject’s organic truth. “I am going to ask leave to put a question or two to yourself,” he said gently. “We know your husband lately drew an unusually large sum of ready money from his bankers. Have you any idea why?”

She opened her eyes in astonishment. “I cannot imagine. I did not know he had done so.”

“Why is it surprising?”

“He made it a rule to carry a hundred pounds or so in a note-case. Yet on Sunday night, just before he went out in the motor, he came to me. He seemed irritated, his voice sharp—an abrasive sound—and asked if I had notes or gold. I unlocked my escritoire and gave him nearly thirty pounds.”

“And he did not tell you why?”

“No. He simply said Mr. Marlowe had persuaded him to go for a run in the motor by moonlight to help him sleep. Then he went off. I thought it odd he should need money, but I soon forgot it.”

The mechanical roar of a motorcar in the moonlight—an acoustic violation of the night’s natural rest—hung heavy in Trent’s mind. Mr. Cupples and Mrs. Manderson began discussing the inquest, leaving Trent to drift toward Marlowe. 

Trent steered their talk toward Bunner’s theory of industrial revenge. Marlowe’s tired face grew grave. “I don’t find myself convinced by it,” the young man said, “but I’ve lived in the States long enough to know such melodramatic revenge is characteristic. Americans have a taste for secret societies. You know *Huckleberry Finn*? The most American thing in that epic is Tom Sawyer’s elaborate, romantic scheme for securing the escape of the slave Jim. Look at the Ku Klux Klan, or Brigham Young’s tyranny in Utah. It’s an attempt to impose their own violent vocabulary upon the world. I take it very seriously.”

“It has a hideous side,” Trent agreed, “especially when it intersects with vice or mere luxury. But to return to the matter: was Manderson’s mind affected by this menace? It was extraordinary to send you posting off in the middle of the night.”

“About ten o’clock,” Marlowe corrected. “He had a strong streak for dramatic, ruthless directness. He suddenly decided he wanted word from a man named Harris—”

“Who is Harris?”

“Nobody knows. I booked a deck-cabin for a Mr. George Harris on the Monday boat at Manderson’s request. Manderson wanted news from him too secret for the telegraph. So I was sent off.”

Trent ensured the acoustic space around them was clear before speaking. “Martin the butler caught a few words before you started in the car. He heard Manderson say, ‘If Harris is there, every moment is of importance.’ I must ask if you truly know nothing of this business.”

Marlowe shook his head. “I know nothing. Manderson simply told me to find Harris and bring back a letter. And now you know as much as I do.”

“That talk took place *before* he told his wife you were taking him for a moonlight run. Why did he conceal your errand?”

“I can guess no better than you.”

Trent gazed at the earth. “Why did he conceal it—from Mrs. Manderson?” 

“And from Martin,” Marlowe amended coolly. “He was told the same thing.”

Trent extracted his letter-case, the leather smelling faintly of old woodsmoke and oil. He drew out two clean slips of paper. “Look at these, Mr. Marlowe. Any idea where they come from?”

Marlowe examined them. “They seem cut with scissors from a small diary for this year, from the October pages. I see no writing. Nobody here has such a diary. What about them?”

“There may be nothing in it,” Trent murmured.

Mrs. Manderson approached, signalling the departure for the inquest. Mr. Cupples and Bunner strolled toward the gate. 

“You will excuse me, I hope,” Trent said to her. “I came up to look about me here for some indications. I had not thought of attending the court just yet.”

“Please do exactly as you wish,” she said with perfect candour, before entering the house.

Trent watched her go, turning to Marlowe. “That is a wonderful woman.”

“You say so without knowing her,” Marlowe replied softly. “She is more than that.”

Silence returned to the grounds, save for the wind in the trees, until a disruptive, hobnailed haste rose on the still air. A boy appeared, trotting from the hotel, the violent orange of a telegram envelope in his hand. Trent watched the rhythmic pumping of the boy's legs, the cycle of the runner echoing the ancient footfalls on this agrarian soil. 

“A propos of nothing,” Trent said to Marlowe, “were you at Oxford?”

“Yes. Why?”

“I wondered if I was right in my guess. Each of us is marked by our native dialect, our particular lexicon. I should have said you were an artist, if I hadn’t known it.”

“Why?”

“You look at things with an eye that moves steadily from detail to detail—tasting the whole picture.”

The boy arrived, panting, handing over the telegram. Trent tore open the crinkling paper, his eyes alighting upon the words. 

“It must be good news,” Marlowe murmured.

Trent turned a glance upon him that defied translation. “Not exactly news,” he said. “It only tells me that another little guess of mine was a good one.”
