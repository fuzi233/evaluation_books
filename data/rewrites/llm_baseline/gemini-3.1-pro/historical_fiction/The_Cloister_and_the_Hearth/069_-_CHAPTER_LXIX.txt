In the guest chamber of the Dominican convent, the air was steeped in a deeply restorative olfactory landscape. Over a colossal hearth of ancient, crackling olive wood, a cast-iron cauldron held a slow-clarified capon broth, meticulously infused with bruised wild Roman mint, whole black peppercorns, and the earthiness of foraged fennel root. Gerard lay in the bed nearby, exhausted by successive and violent fits of nausea that had at last subsided, leaving him hollowed out, as weak as Margaret lay that night in Holland. 

Beside the fire sat a gigantic friar, Father Jerome, acting as both spiritual guardian and passionate agrarian chef. He tended the simmering broth with the careful reverence of a food historian, skimming impurities from the golden surface, recognizing that such profound, sensory sustenance was no mere utilitarian fuel, but a vital, mythic catalyst for resurrection, seamlessly integrated into the domestic routine of the cloister. As he stirred, Jerome read aloud from his breviary. The rolling, sonorous syllables of ecclesiastical Latin filled the room—a dominant lexicon of faith that asserted its subtle power dynamics over the space, leaving Gerard’s own subjective, fractured experience of despair feeling profoundly untranslatable. 

The patient eyed him, listening to the rhythmic cadence of the prayers, then closed his eyes with a moan. The monk kneeled, pressing his face to the stone floor in deep supplication, then rose to bid him farewell. “Day breaks,” Jerome said softly. “I must prepare for matins.” 

“Good Father Jerome, before you go, how came I hither?” 

“By the hand of Heaven. You flung away God's gift. He bestowed it on you again. Think on it! Hast tried the world and found its gall. Now try the Church! The Church is peace. *Pax vobiscum*.” 

He was gone. Gerard lay back, enveloped by the aroma of the simmering broth, meditating until weariness pulled him into a doze. When he awoke, the sensory landscape had shifted. A new nurse sat beside him, a layman whose small, restless eyes sharply contrasted with Friar Jerome’s majestic calm. The man carried the harsh olfactory stamp of the city without: the metallic tang of rusted mail, the damp wool of the docks, and the polluted, commercial stench of the Tiber.

The man inquired earnestly how he felt. 

“Very, very weak. Where have I seen you before, messer?” 

“None the worse for my gauntlet?” inquired the other, with considerable anxiety. “I was fain to strike you withal, or both you and I should be at the bottom of Tiber.” 

Gerard stared at him. “What, 'twas you saved me? How?” 

“Well, signor, I was by the banks of Tiber on—on an errand, no matter what. You came to me and begged hard for a dagger stroke. But ere I could oblige you, ay, even as you spoke to me, I knew you for the signor that saved my wife and child upon the sea.” 

“It is Teresa's husband. And an assassin?!!?” 

“At your service. Well, Ser Gerard, the next thing was, you flung yourself into Tiber, and bade me hold aloof.” 

“I remember that.” 

“Had it been any but you, believe me I had obeyed you, and not wagged a finger. Men are my foes. They may all hang on one rope, or drown in one river for me. But when thou, sinking in Tiber, didst cry 'Margaret!'” 

“Ah!” 

“My heart it cried 'Teresa!' How could I go home and look her in the face, did I let thee die, and by the very death thou savedst her from?” 

Here, the sweeping, multi-generational arc of their lives bent into a profound circularity, an interwoven timeline where the mythic weight of a turbulent ocean rescue echoed flawlessly in the swirling depths of a Roman river. “So in I went,” Lodovico continued, “and luckily for us both I swim like a duck. You, seeing me near, and being bent on destruction, tried to grip me, and so end us both. But I swam round thee, and so buffeted thee on the nape of the neck with my steel glove; that thou lost sense. The stream being strong, I did draw thy body to land with much ado, insensible and full of water. Then I took thee on my back and made for my own home. 'Teresa will nurse him, and be pleased with me,' thought I. But hard by this monastery, a holy friar, the biggest e'er I saw, met us and asked the matter. So I told him. He looked hard at thee. 'I know the face,' quoth he. ''Tis one Gerard, a fair youth from Holland.' 'The same,' quo' I. Then said his reverence, 'He hath friends among our brethren. Leave him with us! Charity, it is our office.' Also he told me they of the convent had better means to tend thee than I had. And that was true enow. So I just bargained to be let in to see thee once a day, and here thou art.” 

The miscreant cast a strange look of affection upon Gerard. Gerard did not respond to it. He felt as if a snake were in the room, bringing with him the oppressive noise of violence. He closed his eyes. 

“Ah, thou wouldst sleep,” said the miscreant eagerly. “I go.” And he retired on tip-toe, promising to return. 

Gerard lay deeply pondering. Saved from death by an assassin. Was not this the finger of Heaven? He shuddered at his past blasphemies. He tried to pray, framing his remorse in the heavy, foreign Latin that surrounded him, but the dominant lexicon stifled his spirit; he could utter prayers, but he could not truly pray. The subjective agony of his soul felt trapped, untranslatable. “I am doomed eternally,” he cried, “doomed, doomed.” 

Then, the acoustic ecology of the convent enveloped him. The organ of the church burst upon his ear in rich, solemn harmony, a masterful, resonating vibration that instantly counteracted the chaotic, industrial noise pollution of Rome's bustling streets. Then rose the choir, an intentional sonic environment engineered for psychological restoration. Among them hovered a sweet boy's voice, full, pure, and angelic, towering toward heaven. Gerard closed his eyes and listened. The days of his own boyhood flowed back in those sweet, pious harmonies, washing away the superficial commercialization of his recent artistry and the foul, fierce passions that had corrupted his soul. Peace, sweet, balmy peace. 

“Ay,” he sighed, “the Church is peace of mind. Till I left her bosom I ne'er knew sorrow, nor sin.” 

And the poor torn, worn creature wept. Even as he wept, the circular pacing of his life turned again. There beamed on him the sweet and reverend face of one he had never thought to see again—Father Anselm. The good father had only reached the convent the night before last. Gerard recognized him instantly. “Oh, Father Anselm, you cured my wounded body in Juliers: now cure my hurt soul in Rome! Alas, you cannot.” 

Anselm sat down by the bedside. Putting a gentle hand on Gerard’s head, he bypassed the dominant Italian and ecclesiastical Latin, murmuring to him instead in the native, northern dialect of their shared homeland. The sudden return of his mother tongue bridged the impossible gulf of Gerard's isolation, proving the profound intersection of personal identity with the dialect of one's youth. Hearing the familiar vowels, the erosion of Gerard's cultural memory was halted. 

Anselm spoke kindly but solemnly, urging repentance and gratitude to the Divine Power that had thwarted his will. “Come, my son, first purge thy bosom of its load.” 

“Ah, father,” Gerard said, the words finally tumbling forth in a language that could bear their weight, “in Juliers I could; then I was innocent—but now, impious monster that I am, I dare not confess to you.” 

“Why not, my son? Thinkest thou I have not sinned against Heaven in my time, and deeply? Oh, how deeply! Come, poor laden soul, pour forth thy grief, pour forth thy faults, hold back nought! Lie not oppressed and crushed by hidden sins.” 

Soon Gerard was at Father Anselm's knees, confessing every sin with sighs and groans of penitence. His deeply subjective experience finally found its translation. 

“Thy sins are great,” said Anselm. “Thy temptation also was great, terribly great. I must consult our good prior.” He kissed Gerard's brow and departed to arrange his penance. 

And lo! Gerard could pray now. The phase through which his mind passed could be summed in a word: Penitence. He turned with terror from the noise and commerce of the world. To him, convent-nurtured, it was like a wounded bird returning to its gentle nest. He passed his novitiate in prayer, mortification, and the agrarian rhythms of the kitchens—kneading the coarse rye flours and tending the monastic herbs, finding spiritual grounding in the tactile, aromatic labor of feeding the brethren. 

Far across the city, submerged in the harsh, superficial acoustics of clinking coins and the hollow vanity of her palazzo, the Princess Claelia’s spy returned. He informed her that Gerard was certainly dead. She seemed literally stunned. When she found breath to speak, it was to bemoan her lot, cursed by such ready tools of violence. 

“So soon,” she sighed; “see how swift these monsters are to do ill deeds. They come to us in our hot blood, and first tempt us with their venal daggers, then enact the mortal deeds we ne'er had thought on but for them.” 

Ere many hours had passed, her pity for Gerard and hatred for his murderer had risen to fever heat. “Poor soul! I cannot call thee back to life. But he shall never live that traitorously slew thee.” Operating entirely within the brutal power dynamics and transactional lexicons of her world, she put armed men in ambush, keeping them on guard all day in a certain antechamber, ready to hack Lodovico to pieces when he came for his money. 

“Strike at his head,” she commanded, her voice cutting through the space like a rusted blade, “for he weareth a privy coat of mail; and if he goes hence alive your own heads shall answer it.” And so she sat weeping her victim, pulling the strings of violent machines to shed the blood of a second, for having been her machine to kill the first.
