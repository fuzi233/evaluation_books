Cousin Maria made her entrance, accompanied by two gardener’s boys carrying baskets brimming with flowers—roses, sweet-williams, and other blooms—to adorn Madame de Bernstein’s drawing-room in anticipation of the evening’s guests. Three footmen in livery, their coats trimmed with elaborate worsted lace, set up twice as many card-tables as were needed. A major-domo, dressed in black with a powdered wig and ruffled sleeves that seemed to demand a sword at his side, followed behind, bearing bundles of wax candles. He placed pairs on each table and lit the silver sconces along the wainscoted walls, which now shimmered under the slanting golden rays of the afternoon sun. Beyond the windows, the green common stretched out, dotted with rocks, clusters of trees, and houses gleaming in the light. Figures in bright silks, hoops, and powdered wigs strolled across the grass, casting dappled shadows upon the earth.

On the opposite side of the Baroness’s windows lay the Pantiles, where a constant fair buzzed with activity. The clatter of voices and the hum of conversation drifted through the air, mingling with the strains of a band playing for the visitors at the Wells. Madame de Bernstein’s sitting room might not have suited a recluse or scholar, but for those who thrived on liveliness, elegance, and a view into the heart of society, it was an ideal retreat. When the lights were lit, passersby below could see that her ladyship was entertaining—and that admission to her gathering was within reach.

It struck one as curious, reflecting on the past, how dimly society lived just a century earlier. For every candle we now burn in a drawing-room, there had been scarcely one back then—let alone gaslight or electric illumination. Tallow lamps smoked and reeked in hallways; the candle-snuffer was a familiar figure even in theatres. Look at Hogarth’s prints: how dark they are! How grimy the feasts appear, as if coated in grease. In *Marriage à la Mode*, Lord Viscount Squanderfield’s grand salons—where he and his wife sit yawning before their horrified steward after a soirée—are lit by only eight candles: one per card-table, half a dozen in a brass chandelier. If Jack Briefless hosted friends for oysters and beer in his chambers at Pump Court, he might have doubled that number. Let us be grateful that Louis XIV, in all his splendour, once held court in near darkness—and bless Mr. Price and others who brought light to our lives, banishing the foul mutton-fat tapers of old.

Maria, radiant herself like a bloom among flowers, arranged the blossoms in vases and ornate jars around the room. She lingered over each vessel, her eyes darting shyly toward young cousin Harry, whose own flush would have flattered any maiden. One might have thought she meant to stay longer than her aunt intended. But the Baroness, seated firmly in her armchair with her crooked tortoiseshell cane in hand, gave orders with imperious precision. She scolded Tom for a darned stocking, John for greasing his hair too lavishly from the candle-box—maintaining strict control over her staff.

Another truth about life a hundred years ago: people shared beds—two in one, four in a single room, often in cellars—and washed in wooden troughs such as one might still find only in military barracks today.

Had Maria hoped for a private moment with Harry? Her hopes were dashed. “Where will you dine, Harry?” asked Madame de Bernstein. “My niece Maria and I shall enjoy a simple chicken in the little parlour. You should go to the best ordinary—the White Horse serves at three o’clock. We’ll hear their bell soon enough. And understand this, sir: do not stint yourself. Behave as if you were the son of Princess Pocahontas. Your trunks have already been taken to the lodging I’ve secured for you. It does no good for a young man to hover about two old women. Does it, Maria?”

“No,” replied her ladyship, lowering her eyes meekly—while the other woman’s gaze flashed triumphantly.

Andromeda, one might say, had suffered greatly beneath the Dragon’s shadow these past days. Had Perseus struck off its head, it would have been no unjustifiable act. Yet Harry did not draw sword or shield. He merely watched the lacqueys in their tawny and blue liveries creaking about the room.

“And there are fine mercers and tailors from London always present here, ready to serve the company at the Wells,” continued the Baroness. “You’d best visit them, my dear—your suit is not quite up to fashion. A touch of lace…”

“I cannot leave mourning, ma'am,” said the young man, glancing down at his black garments.

“Ho, sir!” exclaimed the Baroness, rising sharply on her cane. “Wear black for your brother until you’re as old as Methuselah, if you wish. I care not to stop you. I only insist that you dress properly, like others, and make a fitting appearance for your name.”

“Madam,” replied Mr. Warrington with solemn dignity, “I believe I have done nothing to bring shame upon it.”

Why did the old woman pause, as if struck?

Let bygones be bygones. Between them had passed many such exchanges—sharp words, quick retorts, like swords crossed and thrusts parried. Yet she respected him more for standing firm against her.

“Surely,” she said, forcing a laugh, “a finer shirt than that one won’t disgrace you, sir.”

Harry bowed, his face warming. The linen was a gift from his Oakhurst friends—a token of simplicity and sincerity. He felt oddly proud to wear it, remembering those kind souls: so pure, so honest, so full of gentle strength. In their presence, evil seemed powerless.

He promised to return after dressing in his best linen.

“Come back here, sir,” said Madame de Bernstein, “and if our guests haven’t arrived, Maria and I shall find you some ruffles!”

Under the guidance of a footman, Harry left for his new lodgings.

There he found not only spacious, elegant rooms but also a groom awaiting employment and a second valet, should he choose to hire one for Mr. Gumbo. Within minutes, messengers from a London tailor and bootmaker arrived—bearing cards and greetings from Messrs. Regnier and Tull. Gumbo laid out the finest items from Harry’s wardrobe, including the delicate linen his thrifty Virginian mother had carefully provided.

Images of home rose before him—the snow-covered fields of Castlewood, the crackling fire, the quiet ladies knitting by the hearth. For the first time, a flicker of doubt crossed his mind: perhaps his plain clothes weren’t smart enough, his homemade linen not as fine as it might be. To feel ashamed of anything connected to Castlewood? That was strange. At home, everyone took pride in what was made there. Madam Esmond, when sending her son abroad, believed no noble youth needed better provisions.

True, the clothes might have fit better or been more modern—but when Harry emerged from his chamber, fully dressed, he carried himself with grace. Gumbo summoned a chair, walked beside it, and escorted him to the White Horse ordinary, where he was to dine.

He expected to meet the gentleman he had briefly encountered earlier at his aunt’s—someone who had recommended the White Horse as the most fashionable place to dine. He mentioned the man’s name to the host, but the landlord and waiters greeted him with smiles and bows, assuring him he needed no introduction. They helped him hang up his coat and sword, offered wine—Burgundy, Pontac, or champagne—and led him to a table.

Though the most popular ordinary in Tunbridge Wells, the White Horse was not crowded that day. Monsieur Barbeau, the landlord, informed Harry that a grand entertainment at Summer Hill had drawn most of the guests away. As Harry entered, only four other men occupied the room. Two had finished their meals and were drinking wine; the other two were mid-dinner. The landlord must have whispered Harry’s name to them, for they glanced his way with interest and offered a polite bow across the table as the host bustled off to fetch his dinner.

Mr. Warrington returned their greeting. One of the younger men welcomed him to Tunbridge Wells and expressed hope he would grow fond of the place. Then they exchanged knowing looks—glances Harry could not interpret, nor understand why they directed them toward the two older men at the far end of the room.

One of these elders wore a worn velvet coat, a long queue, voluminous ruffles, and heavy embroidery. The other was short, broad-shouldered, with beetle brows and a hooked nose. His companion addressed him as “milor”—my lord—in a loud voice.

The lord, sipping wine, barely acknowledged Harry before turning to his friend.

“And so you know the nephew of the old woman—the Croesus who has just arrived?”

“You’re mistaken there, Jack,” replied the younger man. “Never could master the language.”

“Indeed,” said Jack. “But surely, my dear lord, you’ve shown energy. He’s been here less than two hours. My servants told me of him only as I came to dinner.”

“I knew him before,” said the Baron. “I’ve met him often in London with the Baroness and my lord, his cousin.”

A steaming soup arrived for Harry, carried by the smiling host.

“Behold, sir! Behold a potage of my own creation!” declared Monsieur Barbeau, placing the dish before Harry and whispering the name of the nobleman across the table.

Harry thanked him in English. The foreign gentleman turned, grinned broadly, and said, “Fous bossedez notre langue barfaidement, monsieur.”

Harry had never heard French spoken so strangely in Canada. He bowed politely in return.

“Tell me more about the Croesus, my good Baron,” continued the lord, speaking with a tone of condescension, ignoring Harry entirely—perhaps irritating the young man.

“What shall I tell you, my dear lord? Croesus is a youth like any other—tall, awkward, with black hair, as most from the Indies have. Lodgings have been taken for him at Mrs. Rose’s toy-shop.”

“I have lodgings there too,” Harry thought. “Who is Croesus? And how delicious this soup is!”

“He travels with a large retinue—four servants, two postchaises, outriders. His chief attendant is a Black man who saved his life from savages in America and refuses, under any circumstances, to be freed. He wears mourning for his elder brother, from whom he inherits his principality.”

“Could anything console you for the loss of yours, Chevalier?” asked the elder gentleman.

“Milor,” replied the Chevalier, “his property might—though you know it is not small.”

“Your brother lives on his patrimony—which you’ve told me is immense. You, by your industry, my dear Chevalier.”

“Milor!” said the Chevalier, raising his voice. “By your industry—or your esprit—how much more noble! Shall you be at the Baroness’s tonight? She ought to be a little of your parents, Chevalier?”

“Again I fail to comprehend your lordship,” said the other, rather sourly.

“Oh, come now,” the lord replied. “She is a woman of wit, noble birth, great adventures—though lacking principle. But what do we men of the world care? You intend to play cards with the young Creole, no doubt, and drain him dry. By the way, Baron—suppose this young Creole is a trap? Suppose our dear friend invented him in London, bringing him down with a reputation for wealth to prey on innocent folk here?”

“J’y ai souvent pense, milor,” said the little Baron, touching his nose knowingly. “That Baroness is capable of anything.”

“A Baron—a Baroness, que voulez-vous? I mean the late lamented husband. Do you know who he was?”

“Intimately. A more notorious villain never dealt a card. At Venice, Brussels, Spa, Vienna—he knew the prisons of every city. I knew the man, my lord.”

“I thought you would. I saw him at the Hague, where I first had the honour of meeting you. A more disreputable rogue never entered my doors. A minister must open his gates to all sorts—spies, sharpers, ruffians.”

“Parbleu, milor, how you treat them!”

“A man of my rank—of the rank I held then—must see all sorts. Entre autres, your acquaintance. What could his wife possibly want with such a name?”

“Apparently, it was better than hers.”

“Effectively! So I’ve heard of my friend Paddy swapping clothes with a scarecrow. I don’t know which name is more distinguished—the English bishop’s or the German baron’s.”

“My lord,” the other gentleman said, rising and laying a hand on a large star pinned to his coat, “you forget—I too am a Baron and a Chevalier of the Holy Roman Order.”

“—Of the Spur! Not in the least, my dear knight and baron! You will have no more wine? We shall meet at Madame de Bernstein’s tonight.”

The knight and baron left the table, fumbling in their embroidered pockets as if searching for coins. The waiter handed him his laced hat, but the nobleman waved him away with a hand adorned with rings and wide ruffles, then strode out of the room.

Only when the man called “my lord” began speaking of the bishop’s widow and the German baron’s wife did Harry realize his aunt and himself had been the subject of their conversation. Before the thought fully settled, one speaker had departed. The other turned to a nearby table and said, “What a little sharper he is! Everything I said about Bernstein applies mutato nomine to him. I knew the fellow was a spy and a rogue. He’s changed religions God knows how many times. I had him expelled from the Hague myself when I was ambassador—and I know he was caned in Vienna.”

“I wonder my Lord Chesterfield associates with such a villain!” Harry burst out.

The other diners looked up. To his surprise, the nobleman continued talking as if nothing had happened.

“There cannot be a more fieffe coquin than this Poellnitz. Why, Heaven be thanked, he has actually left me my snuff-box! You laugh? The fellow is capable of taking it.”

And my lord assumed the laughter was at his own satire.

“You are quite right, sir,” said one of the two diners, turning to Harry. “Though, saving your presence, I don’t know what business it is of yours. My lord plays with anyone who will set a pack of cards before him. Don’t worry—he’s as deaf as a post and didnI hear a word. That’s why he plays with anyone who offers cards—that’s why he consorts with this rogue.”

“Faith, I know other noblemen who aren’t particular about their company,” said Mr. Jack.

“Do you mean because I associate with you? I know my company, my good friend, and I defy most men to have the better of me.”

Meanwhile, the nobleman opposite paid no attention to Harry’s outburst. He spoke in French with Monsieur Barbeau, complimenting him on the dinner.

The host bowed repeatedly, delighted that his Excellency approved—remembering well the art he’d learned during his youth in Ireland.

“The salmi suits your lordship?” asked Barbeau.

“I have just served it to the young American seigneur opposite—the gentleman from Virginia.”

“To whom?” the lord asked, his pale face flushing slightly as he turned toward Harry.

“To the young gentleman from Virginia who has just arrived—and who speaks our beautiful language perfectly!” said Barbeau, hoping to please both parties.

“And to whom your lordship will be answerable for slanderous remarks about my family, spoken in the presence of these gentlemen!” Harry shouted, determined his opponent would hear.

“You must call into his ear,” said one of the younger guests. “Then he may perchance hear you.”

“I will ensure he understands my meaning—one way or another,” Harry declared with dignity. “I will not allow calumnies about my relatives to be spoken by him or any man!”

As Harry spoke, the nobleman opposite had not heard him—but had time to prepare his reply. He rose, wiped his mouth with a handkerchief, and placed his fingers lightly on the table.

“Sir,” he said, “you will believe, on the word of a gentleman, that I had no idea before whom I was speaking. It seems my acquaintance, Monsieur de Poellnitz, knew you no better than I. Had I known you, I assure you I would never have uttered a syllable to cause you distress. I offer my deepest regrets and apologies before my Lord March and Mr. Morris here present.”

To this, Harry could only bow and mutter thanks. The nobleman pretended to hear, bowed deeply, and said he would have the honour of visiting Mr. Warrington at his lodgings. Then he saluted the company and departed.
