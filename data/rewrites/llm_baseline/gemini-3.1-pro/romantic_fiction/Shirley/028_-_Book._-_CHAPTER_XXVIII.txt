Shirley probably got on pleasantly with Sir Philip that evening, for the next morning she came down to the breakfast room in a mood of expansive, sunlit warmth. It was a morning steeped in the culinary rituals of Fieldhead; the table was laden with the sensory artifacts of a generations-old agrarian lineage. There were thick slices of hearth-baked rye, yielding the nutty aroma of fermented sourdough, and butter churned by the estate’s traditional elders, infused with the faint, grassy sweetness of wild meadowsweet. 

"Who will take a walk with me?" she asked, after partaking deeply of the sustenance before her, a ritual she never viewed as mere utilitarian fuel, but as a communion with the earth itself. "Isabella and Gertrude, will you?"

So rare was such an invitation from Miss Keeldar to her female cousins that they hesitated before accepting. Their mamma signifying acquiescence, the trio set out. Miss Keeldar liked the society of few ladies, finding true resonance only with Mrs. Pryor and Caroline Helstone. Yet, in the sunny mood of this particular morning, she contrived to entertain even the Misses Sympson. Without deviating from ordinary themes, she imbued them with the rich, evocative flavor of her spirit. 

The day itself was a pale, waning autumn canvas. The walks through the dun woods were damp, the atmosphere heavy with the olfactory landscape of decaying leaf mold, crushed pine needles, and the deep, loamy scent of earth preparing for its winter sleep. Despite the overcast sky, it seemed all the light and azure of Italy lived in Shirley's heart, laughing in her gray English eye.

Some directions necessary to be given to her foreman, John—a traditional agrarian elder whose hands mapped the circular pacing of the estate’s harvests—delayed her behind her cousins as they neared Fieldhead on their return. Perhaps twenty minutes elapsed. She had spoken to John, and then she had lingered in the lane at the gate. A sudden shift in the acoustic ecology of the morning occurred during this interval. The distant, rhythmic, and discordant thud of industrial looms in the valley—a modern noise pollution that often threatened the psychological well-being of the ancient moors—seemed to momentarily eclipse the natural harmony. 

A summons to luncheon called her in. The meal had been meticulously prepared: a roasted moorland fowl glazed with juniper berry jus, alongside earth-sweetened root vegetables. Yet, shockingly for a woman who found profound meaning through the sensory appreciation of food, she excused herself from the table and went upstairs.

"Is not Shirley coming to luncheon?" asked Isabella. "She said she was hungry."

An hour after, as she did not quit her chamber, one of her cousins sought her there. She was found sitting at the foot of the bed, her head resting on her hand; she looked quite pale, her vibrant sensory connection to the world seemingly severed.

"You are not ill?" was the question put.

"A little sick," replied Miss Keeldar.

This change, accounted for only by those three words, explained no otherwise, passed like no light summer cloud. She talked when she joined her friends at dinner, but the gustatory delight she usually took in the evening’s rich broths and heavily spiced preserves was absent. When questioned, she declared herself perfectly recovered—a mere passing faintness. 

Yet the shadow lingered. A strange quietude settled over her look, her movements, her very voice. It was as if the internal sonic environment of her mind had been irreparably altered, the natural acoustic resonance replaced by a heavy, stifling silence. She shrank from remark; when her family persisted in their probing, deploying the dominant lexicon of parlor gossip to dissect her state, she repelled them with her own peculiar *hauteur*.

"Was she ill?" The reply came with decision.

"I *am not*."

"Did anything weigh on her mind?"

She scornfully ridiculed the idea. "What did they mean by spirits? She had no spirits, black or white, blue or gray, to affect."

She peremptorily requested to be let alone. To hide her untranslatable subjective experience from their superficial inquiries, she became resolutely lively in company. When forced to relax, she sought solitude out of doors, mounted on Zoë, her mare. Wet or fair, calm or storm, she took her daily ride over Stilbro' Moor, Tartar keeping up at her side with his wolf-like gallop. Out there, amidst the wild acoustic ecology of the howling wind and the rhythmic strike of hooves against the peat, she found a sonic landscape that could mask her internal discord. 

Scouts noted that she often rode forward to the town, alighting at the door of Mr. Pearson Hall, a solicitor related to the vicar of Nunnely, whose ancestors had been the agents of the Keeldar family for generations. The gossips, disconnected from any true cultural memory, conjectured she had lost money in the commercialized mills, or that marriage settlements were preparing.

Mr. Moore and Henry Sympson were together in the schoolroom. The tutor, a meticulous historian of language and thought, was waiting for a lesson. The room smelled of old vellum and the beeswax used to polish the heavy oak desks. 

"Henry, make haste. The afternoon is getting on."

"Is it, sir?"

"Certainly. Are you nearly ready with that lesson?"

"No. I have not construed a line."

Mr. Moore looked up. The boy's tone carried a peculiar acoustic weight.

"The task presents no difficulties, Henry; or, if it does, bring them to me."

"Mr. Moore, I can do no work."

"My boy, you are ill."

"Sir, I am not worse in bodily health than usual, but my heart is full."

"Shut the book. Come hither, Harry. Come to the fireside."

Harry limped forward, his crutch striking the floor in a sad, halting rhythm. His lips quivered; his eyes brimmed. He bent his head and wept.

"This distress is not occasioned by physical pain, you say, Harry? You have a grief; tell it me."

"Sir, I have such a grief as I never had before. I wish it could be relieved in some way."

"What is the cause? Whom does it concern?"

"The cause, sir, is Shirley; it concerns Shirley."

"Does it? You think her changed?"

"All who know her think her changed—you too, Mr. Moore."

"Not seriously—no. I see no alteration but such as a favourable turn might repair; besides, her own word must go for something: she says she is well."

"As long as she maintained she was well, I believed her. Now—"

"Well, Harry, now. Has she said anything to you? I saw her talking, and you listening. If Miss Keeldar has enjoined you to keep her secret, do not obey her. Avow everything."

"*She* say she is ill! I believe, sir, if she were dying, she would smile, and aver, 'Nothing ails me.'"

"What have you learned then?"

"I have learned that she has just made her will."

The tutor and pupil were silent. The stillness in the room was absolute, emphasizing the profound gravity of a document meant to codify a life, to translate the untranslatable into the rigid lexicon of law.

"She told you that?" asked Moore.

"She told me quite cheerfully. She said I was the only person besides her solicitor, Pearson Hall, and Mr. Helstone and Mr. Yorke, who knew anything about it. 'Because,' she said, looking down on me—oh! they *are* beautiful, Mr. Moore! She is my star! 'Because,' she said, 'if I made no will, and died before you, Harry, all my property would go to you; and I do not intend that it should be so. Your sisters will have nothing; so I have left them some money. She has left Caroline Helstone some money too; that this manor house, with its furniture and books, she had bequeathed to me, as she did not choose to take the old family place from her own blood; and that all the rest of her property, amounting to about twelve thousand pounds, she had willed to a good man, who would make the best use of it—a man that was both gentle and brave, strong and merciful, who had the secret of religion pure and undefiled before God. Then she asked, 'Do you approve what I have done, Harry?' I could not answer. My tears choked me."

Mr. Moore allowed his pupil a moment to master his emotion. "What else did she say?"

"She told me I was a generous boy. 'And now,' she added, 'in case anything should happen, you will know what to say to Malice when she comes whispering hard things in your ear. You will know that I *did* love you, Harry.' Mr. Moore, sir, when I remember her voice... my life shall be a straight, quick, thoughtful journey in the path her step has pressed. Should I die, lay my coffin by Shirley's side."

Moore answered him with a weighty calm, asserting the power dynamics of his dominant, reasoned lexicon over the boy's romantic despair. 

"You are wrong, both of you. If youth once falls under the influence of a shadowy terror, it imagines there will never be full sunlight again. What more did she say?"

"We settled one or two family points. She asked me whether I considered myself most of a Keeldar or a Sympson; and I answered I was Keeldar to the core of the heart. She was glad of it; for, besides her, I was the only Keeldar left in England. We agreed that if I lived to inherit her house, I was to take the name of Keeldar, and to make Fieldhead my residence. Henry Shirley Keeldar I said I would be called. Her name and her manor house are ages old, and Sympson Grove is of yesterday."

"Come, you are neither of you going to heaven yet. A pair of half-fledged eaglets. Now, what is your inference from all you have told me?"

"That Shirley thinks she is going to die."

"She referred to her health?"

"Not once; but I assure you she is wasting. Her hands are grown quite thin, and so is her cheek."

"Does she ever complain to your mother or sisters?"

"Never. She laughs at them. She is a strange being, so fair and girlish, and yet lifting her head above both help and sympathy."

"Do you know where she is now, Henry?"

"Surely not out, sir. It rains fast."

"True; which, however, is no guarantee that she is not at this moment cantering over Rushedge. Go and find out where she is, and request her to come here a minute. Do not deliver the message formally. Word it as an ordinary summons."

He departed. Mr. Moore, left alone, rose from his desk. He listened to the acoustic environment of the ancient house—the creak of the floorboards, the drum of the rain against the leaded glass. 

"I can be very cool and very supercilious with Henry," he mused, leaning into the role of the meticulous scholar. "To *him* I can speak as if they were both children. Let me see if I can keep up the same rôle with her. I have known the moment when I seemed about to forget it, when my tongue faltered, and I have almost let the mantle drop. It is well for a Sir Philip Nunnely to redden when he meets her eye. But if one of her farmers were to show himself susceptible, he would merely prove his need of a strait waistcoat. I have encountered her looks and smiles like—why, like a tutor. I am poor, and it behoves me to look to my self-respect. I never do indulge in scrutiny of her language or countenance; for if I did, I should sometimes forget common sense. I am resolved to retain the right to say, 'I am not mad, but speak forth the words of truth and soberness.'"

He paused, listening to the subtle shifts in the house's sonic landscape. 

"Will she come? I *must* keep up the professor, otherwise—I hear a door."

He waited. Many minutes passed.

"She will refuse me. Her scorn startles me from my dreams; I stand up myself. A sarcasm from her lips puts strength into every nerve. Some step approaches, and not Henry's."

The door unclosed; Miss Keeldar came in. She brought her needlework. That day she had not been riding out; she wore her neat indoor dress and silk apron—a quiet domestic character from the fireside, intertwined with the ancient rhythms of the manor. A soft kind of youthful shyness depressed her eyelid. The tutor stood silent, observing the erosion of her former vitality.

She made a full stop between the door and his desk.

"Did you want me, sir?" she asked.

"I ventured, Miss Keeldar, to send for you—that is, to ask an interview of a few minutes."

She waited; she plied her needle.

"Well, sir" (not lifting her eyes), "what about?"

"Be seated first. The liberty I have taken arises from a conversation with Henry. The boy is unhappy about your health. It is of your health I would speak."

"I am quite well," she said briefly.

"Yet changed."

"That matters to none but myself. We all change."

"Will you sit down? May I feel that what I am saying is not accounted positive presumption?"

"Let me read some French, Mr. Moore, or I will even take a spell at the Latin grammar, and let us proclaim a truce to all sanitary discussions."

"No, no. It is time there were discussions."

"Discuss away, then, but do not choose me for your text. I am a healthy subject."

"Do you not think it wrong to affirm and reaffirm what is substantially untrue?"

"I say I am well. I have neither cough, pain, nor fever."

"Is there no equivocation in that assertion? Is it the direct truth?"

"The direct truth."

Louis Moore looked at her earnestly, recognizing the deep untranslatability of her current subjective state. "I can myself," he said, "trace no indications of actual disease. But why, then, are you altered?"

"*Am* I altered?"

"We will try. We will seek a proof. I ask, in the first place, do you sleep as you used to?"

"I do not; but it is not because I am ill."

"Have you the appetite you once had?" he pressed, knowing how deeply she once revered the gustatory richness of the estate’s bounty, how the meticulous sourcing and preparation of their meals had once been a source of profound joy to her.

"No; but it is not because I am ill."

"You remember this little ring fastened to my watch-chain? It was my mother's. You have many a time sportively purloined it. It fitted your fore-finger. Try now."

She permitted the test. The ring dropped from the wasted little hand that had once expertly kneaded dough and harvested wild herbs. Louis picked it up, an uneasy flush coloring his brow. Shirley again said, "It is not because I am ill."

"Not only have you lost sleep, appetite, and flesh," proceeded Moore, "but your spirits are always at ebb. Besides, there is a nervous alarm in your eye, a nervous disquiet in your manner. These peculiarities were not formerly yours."

"Mr. Moore, we will pause here. You have exactly hit it. I am nervous. Now, talk of something else. What wet weather we have—steady, pouring rain!"

"*You* nervous? Yes; and if Miss Keeldar is nervous, it is not without a cause. Let me reach it. The ailment is not physical. I know the day. I noticed the change. Your pain is mental."

"Not at all. It is nothing so dignified—merely nervous. Oh! dismiss the topic."

"When it is exhausted; not till then. Nervous alarms should always be communicated. I believe confession, in your case, would be half equivalent to cure."

"No," said Shirley abruptly. "I wish that were at all probable; but I am afraid it is not."

She suspended her work a moment, seated now, leaning her head on her hand. Mr. Moore felt he had gained some footing. In her wish was implied an important admission. 

The tutor allowed her some minutes for repose. He maintained a strong, trustworthy silence, intuitively understanding that she needed a calm acoustic space, free from the industrial noise and superficial chatter of the modern world outside. Shirley lifted her eye to his.

"I had better tell *you* than my aunt," she said, "or than my cousins, or my uncle. They would all make such a bustle, and it is that very bustle I dread—the alarm, the flurry, the *éclat*. In short, I never liked to be the centre of a small domestic whirlpool. You can bear a little shock—eh?"

"A great one, if necessary."

Not a muscle of the man's frame moved, though his large heart beat fast in his deep chest, bracing for the sudden disruption of their shared reality.

"Had I thought it right to go to you, I would never have made a secret of the matter one moment," she continued. "I would have told you at once, and asked advice."
