The moment we were seated, Lady Verinder broke the silence. Her voice was steady, but I could see the effort it cost her.

"Sergeant Cuff," she began, "I spoke to you earlier in a manner I now regret. I make no excuses. If I wronged you, I sincerely apologize."

The Sergeant, ever the professional, accepted her words with a respectful nod. He was not a man easily moved, but even he could not deny the dignity in her tone.

"Permission to justify myself, my lady," he replied. "My conduct in this investigation has been guided by one principle—not to alarm Rosanna Spearman prematurely. Mr. Betteredge can attest to that."

I confirmed it. Yet the Sergeant, relentless, pressed further. He was steering toward a confrontation, and none of us could avoid it.

"I’ve heard theories about Rosanna’s death," he said, "but my own belief remains unchanged. The Diamond drove her to despair. I don’t know the nature of that despair, but I suspect someone in this house does."

A silence fell, thick and suffocating. The storm outside rattled the windows, as if nature itself braced for what came next.

"Speak plainly," Lady Verinder commanded.

"The person I refer to," said Cuff, "is your daughter."

Her ladyship’s hand, resting near her cheque-book, trembled slightly. It pained me to see it—this woman who had always been strength itself.

"I had hoped," she said, her voice low but unwavering, "to settle this matter without Rachel’s name being spoken between us. But you leave me no choice. Proceed."

Cuff consulted his watch. "Ordinarily, I’d prefer to submit my findings in writing. But time is against us. What I must say will be painful—for you to hear, and for me to speak."

Lady Verinder lifted her chin. "Then let me make it easier. You suspect my daughter of hiding the Diamond for her own purposes. Is that correct?"

"Unquestionably."

"Then know this," she said, her gaze unflinching. "As her mother, I tell you she is *incapable* of such deceit. You have known her days; I have known her a lifetime. State your suspicions—you will not offend me. But you are wrong."

She turned to me, offering her hand. I kissed it, wordless.

"Continue, Sergeant," she said.

And so he did, with methodical cruelty.

"For twenty years," he said, "I’ve dealt with the secrets of families. Young women of rank, burdened by debts they dare not confess—it is not uncommon. Milliners, jewelers… or other, darker obligations." He paused. "Now consider Miss Verinder’s behavior. She refused to speak to Superintendent Seegrave. She resented Mr. Blake’s efforts. She barred the examination of her wardrobe. These are not the actions of an innocent woman. They are the actions of one hiding something."

My lady’s expression did not change. "You are misled by circumstance."

Cuff pressed on, relentless. He spoke of Rosanna—her history, her possible role as an accomplice. He painted a picture so damning that even I, loyal as I was, felt a chill.

When he finished, my mistress was pale but composed. "Your proposal?"

"Two paths," said Cuff. "First, surveillance—watch Miss Verinder’s movements, her correspondence. Place a discreet agent in this house. Trace the Diamond if she tries to sell it."

Lady Verinder’s eyes flashed. "Unacceptable."

"Then the alternative," said Cuff smoothly. "A shock. Tell her of Rosanna’s death—abruptly, without warning. If guilt or remorse stirs her, she may confess."

To my astonishment, my lady agreed. "I will do it myself."

And with that, she rose, summoned her carriage, and departed for Frizinghall—leaving Sergeant Cuff, for once in his career, utterly speechless.
