Captain Willoughby retired.

Ethne had utterly erased Colonel Durrance from her mind. Since the moment Captain Willoughby placed the soiled, yellowed feather into her hand, her entire world had narrowed to Harry Feversham. She had dragged Willoughby into that secluded enclosure, and his tale had consumed her, stretching her memories thin like taffy as she tried to fill the gaps in his story with recollections of Harry's gestures, his voice, his gaze. She had been whisked away from the August garden of sunlight and vibrant blooms; the five agonizing years during which she had borne her head high, masking her grief with a brave smile, seemed to vanish from her consciousness. Perhaps she never truly grasped how exhausted she had been until she lifted her head and spotted Durrance standing at the mouth of the hedge.

"Hush!" she hissed to Willoughby. Her complexion drained, and her eyes squeezed shut for a fleeting second against a sudden, sharp pang. Yet, there was no room for weakness. Their brief conversation with Captain Willoughby had been a respite, but it was ended. She had to resume the heavy burdens of those five years immediately. If she could not perform the arduous task of forgetting—which she now realized she never could—she must at least ensure she gave no indication that she hadn't. Durrance must continue to believe that her love for him remained pure, untainted by anything other than friendship.

He stood right at the threshold of the enclosure and stepped inside. He was perceptive enough that it was unwise for him to encounter Captain Willoughby, let alone learn of his presence. Ethne scanned her surroundings for an exit, knowing full well that none existed. The stream lay ahead, flanked by three towering walls of dense, thorny hedgerow behind and to either side. There was only one entrance, and Durrance himself blocked the path.

"Stay quiet," she whispered. "Do you know him?"

"Of course. We served together at Suakin for three years. I heard rumors he'd gone blind, but I'm relieved to see he walks freely." He noted the ease of his stride.

"Lower your voice," Ethne urged urgently. "It is true. He *is* blind."

"One would never guess. Comforts seem pointless. What words do I use for him?"

"Use none!"

Durrance remained just inside the enclosure, appearing to stare directly at the two figures seated on the bench.

"Ethne," he said, not shouting but speaking with a calm, unwavering tone that created the illusion of perfect vision.

"It's impossible he is blind," Willoughby interjected. "He sees us."

"He sees nothing."

Again, Durrance called out, "Ethne!" but this time with a note of uncertainty in his voice.

"Do you hear? He isn't certain," Ethne whispered, gripping Willoughby's arm tightly to keep him still. "Remain absolutely motionless."

"Why?"

"He must not realize you are here." Willoughby, sensing the gravity in Ethne's demeanor, fell silent. She sat perfectly still, as rigid as she wanted him to be, curled slightly inward in a strange, defensive posture. She held her breath, her face strained forward, not a muscle twitching. Willoughby felt a growing unease, a thrill of apprehension born of her intense fear. Unknowingly, he began to share it, fearing exposure alongside her.

"He is moving toward us," he breathed.

"Not a word, not a single movement."

"Ethne!" Durrance cried again. He advanced further into the clearing, closing the distance to the bench. Ethne and Willoughby watched him, frozen. He passed in front of them and halted, facing them squarely. Willoughby convinced himself, surely he must see. His gaze was fixed upon them; he stood with an easy grace, ready to speak. Even Ethne, who knew the truth, began to waver, doubting her own knowledge.

"Ethne!" he called once more, reverting to the quiet, steady tone he had used initially. When silence met his call, he shrugged and turned toward the stream. His back was to them now, yet Ethne, having learned to read the subtle shifts in his bearing, understood that his face revealed little. Something in the set of his shoulders, the tilt of his head, the careless swing of his cane, told her he was listening intently. Her grip on Willoughby's arm tightened. They stayed like statues for a minute, and then Durrance spun abruptly and took a brisk step toward the seat. Ethne, anticipating his tricks, did not stir. Not a rustle of her skirt disturbed the air, and her hold on Willoughby remained firm.

"I wonder where in the world she could be," Durrance muttered to himself, and he backed out of the enclosure. Ethne did not release Willoughby's arm until he vanished from view.

"What a narrow escape," Willoughby remarked when he finally spoke. "If Durrance had sat atop us—"

"Why speculate, since he didn't?" Ethne asked, her composure restored. "Did you tell me everything?"

"As far as I recall."

"And all of this transpired last spring?"

"The spring of last year," Willoughby confirmed.

"Yes. I have a question. Why did you not bring this feather to me last summer?"

"My leave was brief. I spent it hunting ibex in the hills north of Suakin."

"I see," Ethne said softly. "I trust you enjoyed the sport."

"It was decent."

Last summer Ethne had been free. If Willoughby had returned with news instead of stalking wild goats on Jebel Araft, the trajectory of her life would have shifted irrevocably, and a scream would have torn from her throat: "Why didn't you come?" Yet outwardly, she masked the terrible damage caused by his delay. Possessing the steel-like self-command of a woman who has suffered deeply, she spoke with such nonchalance that Willoughby assumed her inquiry sprang from simple curiosity.

"You might have written," she suggested.

"Feversham implied there was no urgency. Explaining this via letter would have been tedious and fraught. He instructed me to deliver it when I had the chance, and I had no opportunity until now. Truth be told, I feared finding Feversham had already returned."

"Oh, no," Ethne replied. "There was no chance of that. Two feathers remain to be redeemed before he dares ask me to reclaim mine."

Willoughby shook his head. "Feversham will never convince Castleton and Trench to retract their charges as he persuaded me."

"Why not?"

"Major Castleton died when the square broke at Tamai."

"Dead?" Ethne gasped, then burst into a short, satisfied laugh. Willoughby turned, stunned, unable to reconcile the evidence of his ears with the look on her face. Yet her countenance told him plainly she was delighted. Ethne was of Celtic stock, and to her, death held little weight. She could harbor hatred, and toward the men she despised, she was as hard as iron. These three men she loathed profoundly. While she had ostensibly handed over the fourth feather to Harry Feversham merely to signal agreement, she cared little for that formality. She was thrilled to hear Major Castleton was removed from the world forever.

"And Colonel Trench?" she pressed.

"No," Willoughby answered, his tone severe. "You are disappointed? He fares worse. He was taken during a reconnaissance. He is currently a prisoner in Omdurman."

"Ah!" Ethne murmured.

"You have no conception," Willoughby declared sternly, "of what imprisonment in Omdurman entails. If you had, despite whatever animosity you feel for a captive, you would feel pity."

"Not I," Ethne insisted stubbornly.

"I will describe to you what it involves."

"No. I refuse to listen to tales of Colonel Trench. Besides, you must depart. Before you go, I require one piece of information," she said, rising from the bench. "What became of Mr. Feversham after he gave you that feather?"

"I advised him he had fulfilled every reasonable expectation, and he accepted my counsel. He boarded the first steamer touching at Suakin en route to Suez, thus leaving the Sudan."

"I must locate him. He must return. Did he require funds?"

"No. He continues drawing his allowance from his father and claimed to have ample resources."

"I am relieved to hear that," Ethne said. She dismissed Willoughby, instructing him to wait within the enclosure while she ventured out alone to ensure the coast was clear. The garden lay empty. Durrance had vanished, and the massive stone terrace of the house, along with the building itself and its striped sun-blinds, appeared slumberous. It was approaching one o'clock; the birds among the trees were hushed. The profound stillness of the garden struck Ethne as almost unnatural. Only the drones of bees hovered lazily over the flowerbeds, and the faint cry of a boy echoed from the meadows across the creek. She returned to Captain Willoughby.
