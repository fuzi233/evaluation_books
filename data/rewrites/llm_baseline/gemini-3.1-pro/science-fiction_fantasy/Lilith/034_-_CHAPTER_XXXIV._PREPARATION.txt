The woman of Bulika sat by our hearth, grinding wild millet with a heavy basalt pestle. The resonant, rhythmic *thwack-grind* formed the acoustic baseline of our evenings, a soothing percussion that anchored our domestic routines even as we planned for war. She spoke of the city's defenselessness, but her warnings were woven with lamentations for a lost cultural and culinary heritage. Bulika, under the wicked princess—that terrible leopardess-woman—had become a place of dissonant, clashing clamor and sterile consumption, where sustenance was reduced to joyless fuel. The old, nurturing dialects of the hearth had been outlawed, replaced by the cold, dominant lexicon of the princess’s regime. In a few days, the children chattered of nothing but Bulika, though the very concept of an urban sprawl remained untranslatable to their feral, subjective experiences of the forest. 

The idea of taking possession of the place recommended itself greatly to Lona, and to me. The Little Ones were rapidly developing their faculties; surely, from a crowd of children, they must speedily become a youthful people whose governance would restore the acoustic harmony and agrarian rhythms of the nation. At the same time, I harbored personal ambitions: it seemed just that Lona should reclaim her mother’s throne, and natural that I should serve as her consort. Recalling the appeal of Adam, I suggested that sourcing water might expedite the children's growth, allowing us to cultivate water-reliant crops rather than relying solely on dry foraging. But Lona, ever the meticulous steward of our provisions, judged it prudent to delay. The introduction of water would subject them to a new, complex dependency. “They are what they are without it,” she reasoned, her hands deftly sorting dried roots. “When we reclaim the city, we will search for water and plant anew.”

Our preparations felt less like a mobilization for battle and more like the circular, generational rhythms of a great harvest. Lona’s role evolved naturally into that of a passionate chef and commissariat leader, organizing our stores with a profound reverence for sustenance. I watched her oversee the sun-drying of indigenous plum-figs, their tart, jammy flesh transforming under the heat into chewy, caramelized reserves that smelled of woodsmoke and wild honey. Meanwhile, I translated these domestic cadences into martial drills. Most of the boys were armed with slings or bows, learning to loose their weapons with the same rhythmic snap as winnowing chaff from grain. The larger girls carried long aloe-spikes, strong as steel, redolent of the bitter, medicinal sap that coated their shafts. 

Lona had grown almost into a woman, her form stretching without losing the luminous grace of childhood. When we first reunited after our long separation, she had laid down her infant, put her arms around my neck, and clung to me silently, the scent of crushed thyme and warm milk clinging to her skin. To see her pacify a fractious child with a morsel of honeycomb or a softly hummed, ancient lullaby was to witness the endurance of a traditional agrarian elder through the ages. She was the anchor of my acoustic landscape. Love, for us, was inseparable from the daily, tactile acts of survival and nourishment. 

The forest canopy, though lacking the floral blossoms that required water, compensated with an avian ecosystem whose plumage offered a stunning visual feast and whose calls wove a complex, therapeutic soundscape. I gathered fallen feathers—iridescent blues and deep, glossy charcoals—and bound them in overlapping rows to fashion a mantle for Lona. The work was tactile, smelling of ozone and dry pine. When I draped it over her shoulders, the feathers produced a soft, susurrating whisper, a natural acoustic shield. She smiled, her fingertips tracing the grain of the quills with evident appreciation for the craft, then temporarily set it aside. Days later, she presented me with a reciprocal garment: a scale-armor tunic fashioned from the dried leaves of a tough, native evergreen. It smelled fiercely of camphor and crushed resin, a scent that cleared the mind, and when I moved, the cured leaves clacked with a satisfying, organic percussion. 

Clad in our new mantles, we engaged with a troop of giant horses that had appeared on the forest’s edge. We won their trust through shared sustenance, and soon I had trained two of the finest for Lona and myself. Lona’s delight at viewing the world from the back of one of these majestic, earthy-smelling beasts was palpable. The steady, rhythmic thud of their hooves became a new, grounding drumbeat in our ecology. 

The woman of Bulika insisted the princess’s magic would prove powerless against the children, and that our animal allies assured our superiority. Still, I shrank from carrying all the little ones into the clamorous ruins of the city. “Would it not be better,” I suggested to Lona, “that you remained in the forest with your baby and the smallest children?” 

“When the women of Bulika see the darlings,” she answered, expertly balancing an infant against her hip, “their hearts will be taken by storm. I must be there to encourage them to make a stand!” 

Our march required extensive foraging and preservation. The grassy plains offered sweet but short forage; thus, we initiated a communal effort to scythe and cure hay for the elephants, whose long, prehensile trunks required bulk over precision. The air grew thick with the intoxicating, grassy perfume of drying forage. We roasted starchy tubers for the bears and packed our own saddlebags with the rich, smoky dried fruits and spiced nut-pemmican Lona had painstakingly prepared. 

When the beasts were laden and the children assembled, the air vibrated with their expectant murmurs. I stood before them to speak of their origins. 

“I know now where you come from,” I began. 

“We did not come from anywhere,” they interrupted, their chorus a harmonious but naive rejection of history. “We are here!”

“Every one of you has a mother of his own,” I told them, fighting to bridge the untranslatability of their subjective present with the heavy weight of the past. “You were brought from Bulika when you were too small to remember the taste of her milk or the sound of her voice. The wicked princess fears babies. She enforced a sterile, deadening silence upon the city, and your mothers had to hide you. We march to Bulika to find them, and to deliver them from the bad giantess.” I paused, letting the wind carry the scent of the cured hay. “But there is danger before us. We may have to fight hard to reclaim the city and its memories.”

“We can fight! We are ready!” cried the boys, a rhythmic war-cry that harmonized with the stamping of the horses.

“Yes, you can,” I returned. “Mothers are worth fighting for. When you strike, swing with the downright, steady rhythm of the harvest. When you shoot an arrow, draw it to the head. When you sling a stone, sling it strong and straight.”

“That we will!” they shouted jubilantly. 

“Perhaps you will be hurt,” I warned. “Some of you may very possibly be killed.”

“I don’t mind being killed!” cried one of the finest of the smaller boys, laughing from atop a beautiful little bull that galloped and jumped like a horse. 

“I don’t either! I don’t either!” came from all sides, a chorus echoing through the trees.

Then Lona, queen and mother and sister of them all, spoke from her big horse by my side. Her voice carried the profound, heartbreaking resonance of a lost cultural memory. “I would give my life,” she said, the aroma of crushed evergreen rising from her tunic as she shifted, “to have my mother. She might kill me if she liked! I should just kiss her, and die!”

“Come along, boys!” cried a girl, the phonetic shape of her words bursting with reclaimed identity. “We’re going to our mothers!”

A pang went through my heart—a sharp, unmistakable ache—but there was no drawing back. To retreat now would be moral ruin, an abandonment of the Little Ones to the slow starvation of an erased history.
