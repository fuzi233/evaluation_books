Shortly thereafter, as the small household at Tergou gathered around the dinner table, Luke Peterson burst through the door, his clothes heavy with road dust.

"Good people," he gasped, "Mistress Catherine is required instantly in Rotterdam."

"My name is Catherine, young sir," the woman corrected him firmly. "Call me Kate, or rather, Margaret."

"Ay, dame," Luke replied breathlessly. "She spoke to me thus: 'Good Luke, hasten to Tergou, seek out Eli the hosier, and beg his wife Catherine to come to me forthwith, for God's love.' I made no delay until daylight broke."

"Holy saints!" exclaimed the hostess, her composure fracturing. "He has returned home? No, surely she would have said so. What on earth could possibly bring her here?" She launched into a rapid succession of wild guesses.

"Perhaps the young man can enlighten us," Kate ventured timidly.

"I can certainly explain," Luke declared. "Her baby is dying, and she was utterly consumed by worry over it."

Margaret sprang to her feet. "What is the matter with him?"

"Nay, I know not the specifics," Luke admitted. "But he has been wasting away, growing weaker and weaker for some time past."

A fleeting, furtive exchange of satisfaction passed between Cornelis and Sybrandt. Fortunately, Catherine did not notice; her gaze was fixed intently upon her husband.

"Now, Eli," she cried, her voice rising in fury, "if you utter a single word against this, we shall quarrel, after all these years of marriage!"

"Who gainsays thee, foolish woman?" Eli retorted calmly. "Quarrel with your own shadow while I go fetch Peter's mule for you."

"Bless thee, my good man! Bless thee! Have you never yet failed me in a crisis?" she exclaimed, her anger melting into gratitude. "Eat your dinner whoever can, while I prepare to depart."

She ushered Luke into the cart, where she interrogated him relentlessly—alternating between severe questioning and seductive coaxing—until she had extracted every scrap of information from his mind.

Upon arriving, Margaret met them at the threshold, pale and trembling. She flung her arms around Catherine's neck, looking up with desperate pleading.

"Come," Catherine said softly after examining her friend's face, "he lives, thank God."

She glanced first at the failing child, then at the hollow-eyed mother, switching her gaze between them. "Fortunate that you summoned me," she noted. "The child is poisoned."

"Poisoned! By whom?" Margaret asked, horror-struck.

"By you. You have been fretting."

"Nay, indeed, Mother. How can I avoid fretting?"

"Do not tell me," Catherine snapped. "A nursing mother has no business to fret. She must turn her mind entirely away from her grief toward the comfort resting in her lap. Do you not know that the child wastes away if the mother vexes herself? This is the result of your reading and writing. Such idle pursuits befit a man; they keep all practical wisdom far from a woman. The child must be weaned."

"Oh, you cruel woman!" Margaret cried vehemently. "I am sorry I called for you. Would you rob me of the only solace I possess in this world? While I nurse my Gerard, I forget that I am the most miserable creature under the sun."

"That you do not," Catherine shot back. "Or he would not be as he is."

"Mother!" Margaret implored.

"'Tis hard," Catherine relented finally. "But consider: would it not be harder to look down and see his lovely little face gazing up at you from within a coffin?"

"Oh, Jesus!"

"And how could you face your other sorrows with your heart perpetually full and your lap empty?"

"Oh, Mother, I consent to anything. Only save my boy."

"That is a good girl," Catherine assured her. "Trust me. I see things more clearly than you do."

Yet, there remained one final consent to secure—that of the infant himself—and he proved more stubborn than his mother.

"There," Margaret tried to feign regret at his behavior, "he loves me too dearly."

Catherine, however, was a match for both. Having noticed a robust young woman seated outside her own door, holding an infant nearby, Catherine approached her. She explained the situation and asked if she might nurse the starving child temporarily until arrangements could be made to wean him. The stranger agreed with a gentle smile, placed her own child in the cradle, and entered Margaret's home.

She curtsied gracefully, and Catherine handed the suffering infant to her. The stranger examined the baby, filled with pity, cooed over him, and immediately began to nurse him as though he were her own.

Margaret, paralyzed by shock at such assurance, cast a sorrowful glance at Catherine and burst into tears. The visitor looked up.

"What is the matter? Wife, you told me the mother was unwilling."

"She is not," Catherine interjected sharply. "She is merely a fool. Pay her no heed. And you, Margaret, I am ashamed of you."

"You are a cruel, hard-hearted woman," Margaret sobbed.

"Those who undertake to guide the weak must necessarily be stern," Catherine replied bluntly. "And forgive me; you are not my flesh and blood, whereas your boy is."

After allowing this harsh truth to settle, she added gently, "Come now. She is sacrificing her own to save yours, and you can think of nothing better than to burst into tears in the woman's face. Out, fie, for shame!"

"Nay, wife," the nurse responded kindly. "Thank heaven, I have enough for myself and hers to spare. And pray, do not blame her! Perhaps the trials of life have soured her own milk."

"And her heart as well," Catherine remarked mercilessly.

Margaret stared her full in the face, then lowered her eyes.

"I know I ought to be very grateful to you," Margaret whispered to the nurse, then turned her head away, leaning over the chair to avoid witnessing the unbearable sight of another woman nursing her Gerard, who showed no distinction between this new mother and the one he had rejected.

The nurse replied, "You are very welcome, my poor woman. And so are you, Mistress Catherine, who is my townsfolk, though you seem unaware."

"What, are you from Tergou? All the better. Yet I cannot recall your face."

"Oh, you do not know me. My husband and I are very humble folk compared to you. But true Eli and his wife are known and respected throughout the town. So, I am at your call, Dame; at yours, Wife; and at yours, my pretty poppet, night or day."

"There is a woman of the right old sort," Catherine murmured as the door closed behind her.

"I HATE her," Margaret whispered with terrible fervor. "I HATE her. I HATE her."

Catherine merely laughed at this outburst. "That is correct," she said. "Better to speak it openly and hold it in thought than to scheme silently. It is very natural, after all. Come, here is your bundle of comfort. Take it and hate it, if you can," she added, placing the child in Margaret's lap.

"No, no," Margaret protested, turning her head halfway away. She could not bear to turn the other side. "He is not my child now; he is hers. I do not know why she left him here, for my part. It was very kind of her not to take him to her own house, cradle and all... Oh! Oh! Oh! Oh! Oh! Oh! Oh!"

"Well," Catherine said soothingly, "one comfort remains: he is not dead. This gives me hope. Some other woman has taken him from me; like father, like son..."

Catherine felt compassion for her friend and allowed her to weep undisturbed. From that point forward, whenever she needed assistance from Joan, she would take Gerard outdoors for fresh air. Margaret never objected, nor did she express any disbelief; yet upon returning indoors, she was invariably in tears.

This tacit arrangement lasted only briefly. Margaret grew entirely eager to wean little Gerard. Once accomplished, the child regained his health and vigor. Soon, however, a new trial awaited him: the onset of teething. Here, Catherine's experience proved invaluable. Amidst her grief regarding the missing father, Margaret found moments of pure bliss watching her son's tiny teeth emerge.

"Teeth, Mother? I call them not teeth, but pearls of pearls," she would exclaim. Each pearl that peeked out and sparkled on his red gums seemed to her the greatest marvel nature had ever wrought. Her companion shared this illusion. If we had told them that standing corn was equally admirable, Margaret would have transformed into a reproachful gazelle, and Catherine would have thrown us out of the room; so, the arrival of each tooth was heralded with a shriek of triumph by whichever of the two happened to discover it first.

Catherine chatted with Joan and learned that she was the wife of Jorian Ketel of Tergou, who had once served Ghysbrecht Van Swieten but had fallen out of favor, returning to Rotterdam, his birthplace. His friends had secured him the position of sexton for the parish, and combined with carpentry, he managed quite well.
