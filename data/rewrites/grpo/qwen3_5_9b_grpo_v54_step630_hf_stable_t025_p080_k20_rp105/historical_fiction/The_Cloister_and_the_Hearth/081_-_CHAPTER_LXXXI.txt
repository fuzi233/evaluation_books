"Eli," Catherine commanded, her voice cutting through the quiet of the room. "Answer me one question like a man, and I shall ask no more today. What is wormwood?"

Eli stared at her, momentarily paralyzed by the sudden demand on his wits. He recovered quickly, offering a practical definition. "It is a herb that tastes of main bitterness."

"That is a fair answer, my man, but not the one I seek."

"Then answer it yourself."

"And shall," Eli groaned, the weight of her words settling heavily upon him. "Wormwood is... to have two men in the house doing naught but waiting for your shoes and mine." The shaft struck home, piercing the pretense of idleness.

"Methinks waiting for their best friend's coffin, that and nothing to do, are enough to make them worse than Nature intended. Why not set them up somewhere, to give them a chance?"

Eli hesitated, admitting he was willing but fearful that they would drink and gamble their very shelves away before they could earn a crust.

"Nay," Catherine replied sharply. "Do you take me for a simpleton? Of course I intend to watch them at the start, driving them with a loose rein, as the saying goes."

"Where did you think of going? Not here; to divide our own custom."

"Not likely. I say Rotterdam against the world. Then I could start them."

Oh, self-deception! The true motive behind this bold declaration was simply to draw near to little Gerard.

Following many discussions and eager promises of amendment from Cornelis and Sybrandt, Catherine traveled to Rotterdam to hunt for a shop, taking Kate with her for a change. Soon they found a suitable location in a good street, though it was sadly out of order. Yet, they secured it at a lower price and immediately set to work, brushing it up, fitting proper shelves for the business, and rendering the dwelling habitable.

Meanwhile, Luke Peterson was constantly asking Margaret what he could do for her. Her answer, delivered in a sad tone, was invariably, "Nothing, Luke, nothing."

"What, you who are so clever, can you think of nothing for me to do for you?"

"Nothing, Luke, nothing."

But finally, she varied her reply: "If you could make something to help my sweet sister Kate about."

The slave of love consented joyfully. Soon, he crafted a little cart for Kate, cushioned it, and yoked himself into it. At eventide, he drew her out of the town and along the pleasant boulevard, with Margaret and Catherine walking beside them. It appeared a happier party than it truly was.

Kate, for one, enjoyed the outing keenly. Little Gerard was placed in her lap, and she doted on him; it seemed like a cherub carried by a little angel, or a rosebud resting in the cup of a lily.

The vulgar jeered, asking Luke how a thistle tasted and mocking the fact that his mistress could not afford a horse with four legs.

Luke did not mind these taunts; Kate minded them for him.

"You have made the cart for me, good Luke," she said. "'Twas much. I did ill to let thee draw me too; we can afford to pay some poor soul for that. I love my rides, and to carry little Gerard, but I'd liefer ride no more than thou be mocked for it."

"Much I care for their tongues," Luke retorted. "If I did care, I'd knock their heads together. I shall draw you until my mistress says give over."

"Luke, if you obey Kate, you will oblige me."

"Then I will obey Kate."

An honorable exception to the popular humor was Jorian Ketel's wife. "That is strength well laid out, to draw the weak," Joan observed. "And her prayers will be your guerdon; she is not long for this world; she smileth in pain."

These were the words of Joan, spoken with a mixture of pity and resignation.

Single-minded Luke answered that he did not want the poor lass's prayers; he did it to please his mistress, Margaret.

After that, Luke often pressed Margaret to give him something to do, without success. But one day, seemingly tired with his importuning, she turned on him with a look and an accent she would later try in vain to convey.

"Find me my boy's father."
