My fair friend, Miss Clack, having laid down her pen, there are two compelling reasons for me to take up the quill in my turn.

First, I am uniquely positioned to shed light on several critical points that have hitherto remained obscured. Miss Verinder broke her marriage engagement for a private reason—one that I uncovered. Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite withdrew his claim to his charming cousin for a similarly private reason—one that I also discovered.

Second, fate—or perhaps misfortune—has cast me directly into the vortex of the Indian Diamond mystery at this very moment. I recently hosted an interview in my own offices with an Oriental gentleman of distinguished bearing, who was none other than the leader of the three Indians. Furthermore, I encountered the renowned traveler, Mr. Murthwaite, the very next day, engaging him in a conversation regarding the Moonstone that bears significant weight on subsequent events. These are the credentials that justify my presence in this narrative.

The chronology demands that we address the broken engagement first, as it precedes all other events. To trace the full arc of this tragedy, I must begin, somewhat counterintuitively, at the bedside of my esteemed client and friend, the late Sir John Verinder.

Sir John possessed many of the harmless, amiable weaknesses common to mankind. Most pertinent to our current inquiry was his invincible reluctance, while in robust health, to confront the solemnity of drafting his will. Lady Verinder urged him repeatedly, and I did likewise. He conceded the logic of our arguments but took no action until illness finally claimed him. Only then was I summoned to draft his testament. The instructions were deceptively simple: "Leave everything to my wife."

Sir John was dozing when I entered. Upon seeing me, he roused himself. "How do you do, Mr. Bruff?" he asked. "I shall not linger long; then I shall sleep again." He watched with keen interest as I prepared my stationery. "Are you ready?" he queried. I dipped my pen and awaited his final decree.

"I leave everything to my wife," Sir John declared simply. "That is all." Turning onto his pillow, he settled back into slumber.

I was compelled to interrupt his repose. "Do you intend," I asked, "to leave the entirety of your estate, in every form, absolutely to Lady Verinder?"

"Yes," replied Sir John. "I have phrased it concisely. Why cannot you do the same and allow me to sleep? Everything to my wife. That is my Will."

His assets comprised land and capital. In most cases, I would have advised a more complex distribution. However, I knew Lady Verinder to be not only virtuous but exceptionally capable of managing wealth—a rarity among women. Within ten minutes, the will was drafted, signed, and sealed, allowing Sir John to resume his nap.

Lady Verinder honored her husband's trust implicitly. Shortly after becoming a widow, she consulted me to draft her own will. Her judgment was impeccable; my role was merely formalizing her clear intentions. Before Sir John had been buried a fortnight, his daughter's future was secured with wisdom and affection.

This will resided safely in my fireproof safe for many years. It was not until the summer of 1848, under somber circumstances, that I revisited it. Physicians had delivered a fatal prognosis to Lady Verinder. As her first confidant, she requested a review of her documents. While the core provision benefiting her daughter remained unchanged, her wishes regarding minor legacies to other relatives had evolved. To prevent confusion, I drafted a second will incorporating new codicils. Regarding Rachel Verinder's financial standing, the new document mirrored the first perfectly. Changes were limited solely to guardianship arrangements, made under my counsel. Following Lady Verinder's passing, the will was submitted to my proctor for probate.

Approximately three weeks later, a subtle alarm bell rang. Visiting my proctor's office, I noticed an air of unusual agitation.

"I have news for you," he announced. "What do you think I heard at Doctors' Commons this morning? Lady Verinder's will has already been requested and examined!"

This was startling. The will was irrefutable, and no one seemed to have a legitimate stake in scrutinizing it. (For those unaware, the law permits anyone to inspect a will at Doctors' Commons for a nominal fee.)

"Who requested it?" I asked.

"The clerk identified the visitor immediately," he replied. "Mr. Smalley, of the firm Skipp and Smalley. Since the official registers had not yet recorded the copy, he was granted access to the original. He studied it intently and jotted notes in his pocket book. Do you have any notion of his purpose?"

I shook my head. "I shall discover soon enough."

Returning to my office, I utilized a unique leverage against the firm of Skipp and Smalley. My common-law clerk was Mr. Smalley's brother. For years, my firm had inadvertently subsidized theirs by referring cases they handled, though I deemed them too trivial for my own practice. This patronage was valuable.

Immediately upon returning, I dispatched my clerk to deliver a message: *Mr. Bruff requests to know why Messrs. Skipp and Smalley felt compelled to examine Lady Verinder's will.*

Mr. Smalley arrived shortly thereafter, accompanied by his brother. Admitting he acted on client instruction, he initially balked at breaching confidentiality. We engaged in a sharp debate. He was technically correct, but I was driven by anger and suspicion. I refused to accept vague assurances, insisting on total transparency. Pushing my advantage ruthlessly, I presented him with a stark choice: lose his client's business or lose mine. An act of tyranny, admittedly, but effective.

Smalley sighed resignedly and yielded the name of his client: **Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite.**

That was sufficient for me.

To ensure fairness, I must now align the reader's knowledge with my own regarding Lady Verinder's will. Let it be stated clearly: Rachel Verinder held only a life-interest in the property. Her mother's foresight and my legal acumen had shielded her from the dangers of financial mismanagement. Neither she nor a potential husband could touch the principal assets—land or cash. They were entitled only to the residence and the income generated by it.

Reflecting on this discovery, I was deeply perplexed. Less than a week prior, I had learned of Miss Verinder's impending marriage with profound sorrow. I admired her dearly and was devastated by her decision to unite with Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite, a man I viewed as a smooth-tongued fraud. Now, evidence suggested his motives were purely mercenary. Was this a common occurrence? Perhaps. But would you treat it so lightly if it involved your own sister?

My primary concern was whether Ablewhite would honor the engagement after learning the extent of the inheritance. If his finances were dire, the prospect of marrying for her income would be irresistible. Conversely, if he faced an urgent deadline requiring a massive sum, the will would solve his problem instantly, leaving Rachel vulnerable to a scoundrel.

If the latter were true, I should delay revealing the truth during her initial mourning. If the former, remaining silent would be complicity in a lifetime of misery.

Doubts aside, I traveled to London to the hotel where Mrs. Ablewhite and Miss Verinder were staying. They informed me they were departing for Brighton the following day, noting that Mr. Godfrey could not accompany them due to an unforeseen obstacle. Seizing the opportunity, I volunteered to take his place.

While contemplating Rachel, hesitation lingered. Yet, upon meeting her, my resolve hardened. I sought her out on a walk the day after my arrival.

"May I speak to you," I asked, "regarding your marriage engagement?"

"Yes," she replied indifferently, "provided you have nothing more fascinating to discuss."

"Will you forgive an old friend and servant of your family, Miss Rachel, if I inquire whether your heart is truly set on this union?"

"I am marrying in despair, Mr. Bruff—hoping to stumble upon a stagnant happiness that might reconcile me to existence."

Such strong language hinted at underlying turmoil. However, I steered clear of tangents.

"Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite likely does not share your sentiments," I countered. "Is his heart set on this marriage at least?"

"He claims so, and I presume I should believe him. He would not propose after my disclosures unless he cared for me."

Poor soul! The notion that a man might marry her solely for selfish gain had never crossed her mind. The challenge ahead loomed larger than anticipated.

"It sounds peculiar," I continued, "in my old-fashioned ears..."

"What sounds peculiar?" she asked.

"To hear you speak of your future husband as if you harbor doubts regarding the sincerity of his attachment. Are you aware of any reason within yourself to distrust him?"

Her perceptiveness caught the shift in my tone. She paused, pulling her arm from mine to gaze searchingly at my face.

"Mr. Bruff," she said, "you have something to tell me about Godfrey Ablewhite. Speak."

Knowing her directness, I confessed everything.

She resumed holding my arm, walking slowly beside me. I felt her grip tighten mechanically, observing her growing pallor, yet she remained silent throughout my confession. Once finished, she stayed quiet, her head bowed, seemingly lost in a private storm, oblivious to her surroundings.
