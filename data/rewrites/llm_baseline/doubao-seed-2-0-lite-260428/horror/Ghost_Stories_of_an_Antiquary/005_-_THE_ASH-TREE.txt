Any soul who has trundled the rutted lanes of Eastern England knows the small, damp country houses dotting the landscape like half-forgotten tombs: stucco-faced Italianate blocks, red-brick bones peeking through cracks, set in parks of eighty to a hundred acres where the mist never fully lifts even at midday. For years I have been drawn to them, the grey split-oak palings sagging under ivy, the meres fringed with reeds that whisper after dark, the distant woods that hold shapes you cannot quite make out at the edge of your vision. I have daydreamed of owning one, of wandering its galleries, of turning the pages of a 13th-century Psalter in its dim library, of imagining the lives lived there long ago—though none of those daydreams ever brushed against the dark truth of Castringham Hall in Suffolk.

Much has been altered to the building since the events I recount, but the hallmarks remain: pillared portico, white stucco over far older brick, park fringe of gnarled oak, the mere that glints like dull steel at dusk. Only the one feature that set it apart from every other Suffolk country house is gone. From the park lawn, if you stood on the right side of the drive, you could once see a great ancient ash growing no more than six yards from the Hall’s west wall, its thick boughs brushing the plaster so close it looked like the tree had pressed its whole weight against the building, leaning in to listen to every word spoken inside. It had stood there since the moat was filled in and the Elizabethan hall raised, and by 1690 it had grown to a monstrous, full-grown bulk, its bark ridged like the skin of a leper.

That year, the Suffolk countryside was roiling with witch trials. To this day we cannot untangle how much of that universal, rabid fear was rooted in nothing but mass cruelty, how much was born of something far older and far worse. This story makes me loath to dismiss the old testimonies as mere superstition. You may judge for yourself.

Castringham gave up one victim to the noose. Her name was Mrs. Mothersole, and she was no ragged beggar: she owned a good cottage, had respectable farmers who swore to her good name, pleaded with the jury to spare her. But it was the testimony of the then-owner of Castringham Hall, Sir Matthew Fell, that sealed her fate. He swore under oath that he had watched her, three full moon nights running, climb the ash tree outside his west window in nothing but her thin shift, a curved black knife in her hand, snipping off tiny twigs and muttering to something hidden in the branches, something he could never see. Each time he tried to rush down and catch her, she heard his step, and by the time he reached the lawn, all he saw was a hare that ran across the path toward the village, moving faster than any hare had any right to move. The third night he chased it all the way to her cottage, banged on her door for 15 minutes, and when she opened it she was yawning, half-asleep, her bed still warm behind her, no twigs, no knife anywhere to be seen.

On that evidence, stacked on top of dozens of petty tales of blighted crops and sick children, Mrs. Mothersole was found guilty. She was hanged a week later, along with five other wretches, on the gallows hill outside Bury St Edmunds.

Sir Matthew, Deputy-Sheriff that year, stood on the drizzly March grass to watch her die. The other prisoners wept, or hung their heads, but Mrs. Mothersole stared at the crowd with a snarl on her face that a local diarist wrote made even the hangman flinch: every man who laid a hand on her said later her eyes glowed like no human’s should. She did not struggle. The only words she spoke, under her breath, repeated three times, were “There will be guests at the Hall.”

Sir Matthew could not shake the look she had given him, the venom in that quiet line. He rode home with the parish Vicar, Mr. Crome, that evening, saying he had never believed in witch-finding fanaticism, that he had only told the truth of what he saw, that the whole business had turned his stomach. The Vicar told him he had done his duty, and the pair tried to put it out of their minds.

A month and a half later, the May moon hung full and fat over the park. Sir Matthew’s wife was away tending her dying mother, so he invited Mr. Crome to stay for late supper. The two talked of estate business, Sir Matthew scribbling down notes of his last wishes for his lands that would prove very useful before long. When the Vicar made to leave at half past nine, they took a turn on the gravel walk behind the house, in full sight of the great ash.

Sir Matthew stopped dead.
“What is that scuttling up and down the trunk?” he said. “No squirrel would be out at this hour, they’re all asleep in their nests.”

Mr. Crome squinted at the shifting shape in the moonlight. He could not make out its colour, but he swore until his dying day that the thing had more than four legs. Before he could say a word, it vanished into a crevice in the bark, and the two men parted, neither speaking of what they had seen. They would never share another conversation again.

The next morning, Sir Matthew did not come down for breakfast. His servants banged on his bedchamber door until their knuckles ached, then broke it down. They found him dead in his bed, his skin black and bloated, his limbs twisted into impossible angles as if he had fought something invisible to the last breath. The casement of his west window was standing wide open.

The Vicar was fetched at once, and his surviving notes of that day still turn the blood cold. The physician from Bury could find no poison in Sir Matthew’s nightly tankard of small ale, no mark of intrusion on the chamber lock. The two respectable lay women who had come to lay out his corpse screamed and ran from the room before they had even washed his chest, their palms burning as if they had plunged them into a fire, their forearms swelling up so badly they could not work for six weeks after, no mark or puncture on their skin to explain the agony. When the Vicar and the physician pressed a magnifying crystal to the dead man’s chest, they found two tiny, almost invisible pricks, nothing more.

Helpless, the Vicar picked up the small Bible Sir Matthew read every night before bed, and tried the old forbidden practice of Sortes Biblicae, three times opening the book at random: the first line his finger landed on was Luke 13:7, *Cut it down*. The second, Isaiah 13:20, *It shall never be inhabited*. The third, Job 39:30, *Her young ones also suck up blood*.

The whole parish decided the death was the work of Papist conspirators, leftover malice from the Popish Plot, and Sir Matthew was buried with full honors. His son, the second Sir Matthew, inherited the estate, and never once slept a night in that west chamber. For forty years, no one did. A strange sickness plagued his cattle: every morning, any beast left out in the park overnight was found dead, black, twisted, those same two tiny pricks on their necks. He finally locked every cow, sheep and horse in sheds after dark, and the sickness stopped preying on livestock, moving only to wild birds and deer, no hunter ever catching sight of a predator to explain the deaths.

He died in 1735, and his son Sir Richard inherited. Sir Richard was a man obsessed with all things Italian, and set about ripping out the old red brick facade of Castringham to cover it in stucco, planting cheap Roman marbles in the gardens, building a silly little imitation Sibyl’s temple on the far bank of the mere. When he expanded the family pew in the parish church, the workmen disturbed a number of old unmarked graves—including Mrs. Mothersole’s, whose position was marked on a map Mr. Crome had drawn decades before. They pried open her oak coffin, and found nothing inside. No bones, no dust, no shred of cloth. It was completely empty.

The village whispered, crossing themselves when they passed the empty plot. Sir Richard laughed, and ordered the empty coffin burned.

One blustery 1754 morning, he woke after a miserable sleepless night: his chimney smoked, something scuttled and scraped at his window nonstop, and all his game in the park was dying off fast, ruining his reputation as a sportsman. He stormed down to breakfast, announcing he would not sleep another night in his current chamber, and demanded his housekeeper find him a west-facing room far from the bustle of the household.

“There is only one such room, Sir Richard,” the housekeeper, Mrs. Chiddock, said reluctantly. “It’s Sir Matthew’s old west chamber. No one has slept there in forty years.”

“Then move my furniture in there this afternoon,” he snapped. He pushed past her, wrenched open the long-shut door, threw back the shutters, and flung the casement wide. The smell that rolled out was close, earthy, sharp with something like rot. The great ash tree stood not a dozen feet outside the pane, its branches swaying slow.

He was still ordering Mrs. Chiddock to air the room out when a young man in black stepped into the doorway: William Crome, grandson of the old Vicar, newly elected Fellow of Clare Hall, riding through to Bury to drop off a packet of his grandfather’s old papers, including the notes of the first Sir Matthew Fell’s death.

Sir Richard read the old Sortes predictions, and snickered. “Cut it down, eh?” he said. “That’s fine advice. That infernal ash tree is coming down tomorrow, no two ways about it.” He grabbed the old family Bible from the shelf, flipped it open at random to test his luck. The line stared up at him black and stark: *Thou shalt seek me in the morning, and I shall not be*. He laughed it off, called it a silly coincidence, saw young Crome on his way, and went back to planning for his guests that evening.

Dinner was at five, with the Bishop of Kilmore, Lady Mary Hervey, Sir William Kentfield, and the rest of the party drinking late over cards before they all trundled off to their separate chambers.

The next morning, Sir Richard met the Bishop on the terrace, bleary-eyed. The Bishop nodded toward the open window of the west chamber, where he had spent the night.

“None of my Irish parishioners would ever sleep in that room, Sir Richard,” he said, smiling. “They say it is the worst luck in the world to sleep with an ash tree so close to your window.”

Sir Richard huffed. “It was no luck, it was the branches scraping against my glass all night that kept me awake from midnight to four. But the tree is coming down tomorrow, so I shall not hear any more of it.”

The Bishop frowned, and gestured at the great ash, standing clear across the lawn.
“Forgive me, Sir Richard,” he said, “but look for yourself. None of the nearest branches reach within a foot of that casement. There was no gale last night. Not a twig could have touched that glass.”
