The poor widow, aided by her loyal maid Martha—who marveled at the German customs and managed the simple household's affairs—had prepared a modest feast to welcome Major Pendennis. Only the Major and his two younger companions ate; Helen had sent word that she was too unwell to dine, and Laura accompanied her. During the meal, the Major chatted cheerfully, oblivious—or perhaps choosing to ignore—the heavy silence and gloom shared by the other two guests.

Only when evening fell did Helen and Laura enter the sitting-room. Helen leaned heavily on Laura, her back turned to the dying light, hiding the pallor and despair etched onto her face. As she approached Pen, whom she hadn't seen all day, she wrapped her arms around his neck and kissed him tenderly. Laura stepped aside, leaving the scene. Pen noticed immediately that his mother's voice and entire frame trembled; her hand was ice-cold as she pressed it to his forehead, clutching him with pathetic intensity. Yet, instead of soothing her, the sight of her misery only fueled a surge of wrath and irritability in the young man. He barely returned her kiss, meeting her desperate gaze with a hardness that felt cruel. *She persecutes me,* he thought bitterly. *She comes to me acting like a martyr!*

"You look dreadfully ill, my child," she whispered. "I cannot bear to see you looking like that." She stumbled toward a sofa, still gripping his limp hand with her thin, freezing fingers.

"I've had so much to annoy me lately, Mother," Pen said, his chest heaving. As he spoke, Helen's heart hammered so violently against her ribs that she sat nearly dead with terror.

Warrington, Laura, and the Major stood frozen, sensing the storm was about to break.

"I received letters from London," Arthur continued, his voice rising. "And one has caused me more pain than anything in my life. It says that my previous letters were intercepted and stolen—that a young woman who showed me nothing but love and care has been cruelly mistreated... by you, Mother!"

"For God's sake, stop!" cried Warrington. "Look at her! She's ill!"

"Let him finish," the widow murmured faintly.

"Let him go on and kill her!" Laura shouted, rushing to her mother's side. "Speak, sir! See her die!"

"It is you who are cruel!" Pen roared, his normally soft nature revolting against the injustice laid at his feet. "It is you who blame me for this pain! You heap wicked reproaches, cast unjust doubts, and persecute those who love me—those who risk everything for me, only to be despised and trampled because they are of lower birth! Now that I know your conduct, tell me what I'll do! I'm resolved to go back to that poor girl you threw out of my house and ask her to return and share my home. I'll defy the pride that drives her away and the suspicion that insults us both!"

"Do you mean, Pen, that you—" Helen began, her eyes wide and hands outstretched, but Laura cut her off. "Silence, hush, dear mother," she pleaded, and the widow obeyed. Despite Pen's savagery, she hung on his every word, almost swooning.

"By Gad, he shan't go on, or I won't listen!" the Major thundered, trembling with rage. "After all we've done for you, after everything I've sacrificed for you personally, to insult your mother and disgrace the Pendennis name by marrying a low-born kitchen-maid? Go ahead and do it, by Gad—but consider yourselves washed clean of me! I'm an old man, not long for this world. My family is ancient and honorable, by Jove, and I hoped that the boy I raised and nursed would prove our name wasn't left dishonored behind. But if he won't, then damn it, amen. Both my father and my brother Jack were the proudest men in England, and I never thought such disgrace would touch us. Never! And I'm ashamed it's Arthur Pendennis."

The old man's voice cracked into a sob—the second time Arthur had drawn tears from those wrinkled eyelids. The sound instantly halted Pen's fury. He stopped pacing. Laura stood by Helen's sofa, and Warrington, previously a silent observer, watched the family tempest. Darkness had fallen as they argued. Following the Major's passionate outburst, George Warrington's deep voice broke into the twilight room, filled with emotion.

"May I tell you something about myself, my kind friends?" he asked gently. "You've been so good to me, ma'am, so kind to me, Laura—I hope I may call you so sometime. Pen and I have been such close friends that I've long wanted to share my true story. It's sad and holds another's secret, but it might help Arthur understand. It's right everyone knows. Perhaps it will distract you from a topic born of fatal misunderstanding that has caused so much pain. May I speak, Mrs. Pendennis?"

"Please, go on," Helen said softly. Though she tried to listen, her mind raced with the possibility that Pen's words hinted at the truth she secretly feared.

Warrington filled a glass of wine and drained it before speaking. "You all see me now—a man with no ambition, careless of reputation, living in a garret from hand to mouth. I have friends and a name, and surely the capability to succeed if I chose. But I have no mind for it. I'll probably die alone in that garret. I nailed myself to this fate early on. Do you know what sparked my interest in Arthur years ago? College gossip mentioned his early affair with the Chatteris actress—about whom Pen has spoken since. Without the Major's intervention, she might have been your daughter-in-law, ma'am. I can't see Pen in the dark, but I'm sure he blushing; Miss Bell likely does too; and Major Pendennis, I expect, laughs appropriately—he won, after all. Imagine Arthur's lot had he been tied at nineteen to an illiterate woman older than himself, lacking any common ground, equality, confidence, or love. What could he have been but miserable? When he threatened such a union just now, believe me, it was anger speaking—a very natural reaction after generous, manly, self-denying conduct (rare in him!) met with unkind suspicion from friends and unfair treatment of an innocent person to whom you are all indebted."

Helen rose to interrupt, but Warrington saw her move. "Do I tire you, ma'am?"

"Oh no—go on, go on," Helen urged, delighted.

"I liked him because of that early history, and because I admire a man who shows unreasonable attachment to a woman. That's why we're friends—all of us. Pen has been a great comfort to a lonely, unfortunate man."

"I'm not complaining of my lot; no man gets what he wants. Up in my garret, with the flowers you left there, my old books, and my pipe for a wife, I'm fairly contented. Occasionally, I envy men with brighter careers or those who can soothe their misfortune with the affection of a woman or child—things Fate and my own faults denied me." A sigh drifted from near Warrington in the dark, followed by a hand reaching out toward him. Instantly, it withdrew. Female prudery dictates that before any expression of feeling, a woman is taught to prioritize propriety and blush at the slightest attention. Modesty checked the spontaneous gesture, shrinking back ashamed, and Warrington resumed his tale.

"My fate is what I made, unlucky for me and others. Like Arthur, I had an adventure before college. No one saved me as the Major saved Pen. Pardon me, Miss Laura, if I confess this. At eighteen, working as a private tutor, I became attached to a woman of lower station and advanced age. You recoil from me..."

"No, I don't," Laura declared firmly. This time, her hand went out resolutely, taking his. She had guessed his story from earlier hints.

"She was a yeoman's daughter nearby," Warrington said, his voice faltering. "I fancied her—as all young men do. Her parents knew my father and encouraged me with crude artifices and scoundrel-like flattery. To her credit, she never cared for me; she was forced into it by her family's threats. Oh, that I hadn't been deceived! But in these matters, we deceive ourselves because we wish to. I thought I loved that poor woman."

"What could such a marriage bring? Soon I realized I'd wed a boor. She couldn't grasp a single subject I cared about. Her dullness bored me until I loathed it. After a wretched, secretive union, I found letters—terrible letters!—revealing her heart had never been mine, but belonged to someone of her own class. Upon my father's death, I paid my college debts and settled every remaining shilling in an annuity for those bearing my name, on the condition they hide and never use the title. They honored it, mostly, unless they needed more money. Had I gained fame, she would have claimed it; had I made a name, impostors would wear it.
