The air in Bond Street was thick with the scent of beeswax and expensive wool, a static weight that seemed to press against the lungs of anyone unfortunate enough to breathe it. Harry Warrington sat within this atmosphere, a figure of considerable girth and even greater fortune, drinking bumpers of vintage wine as if the liquid were merely water. To the fashionable world, he was the Fortunate Youth, a title worn as comfortably as a tailored coat. He was young, handsome, and possessed a specific brand of luck that allowed society to treat him with a deference usually reserved for saints or kings.

Do not mistake this for hypocrisy. Let us not cry out against the selfishness of the world for welcoming the young and the beautiful while ignoring the old and the broken. Society is an aggregate of choices, a collection of preferences where the lively, the well-dressed, and the amusing naturally crowd out the slouching, the ill-washed, and the misanthropic. Harry was liked because he was likeable; because he was rich, jovial, and brave; because he sang with drunkards and hunted with gentlemen; because he possessed a modest, blushing timidity around ladies that made him intriguing; and because he was magnificently generous to those beneath him. He was high and mighty, certainly, but in an era where rank dictated the very posture of one's spine, distance from the inferior was not a flaw but a feature. The Secretary of State knelt to the King; the Under-Secretary stood while his superiors reviewed papers. Such lines must be drawn. Yet, there remains a peculiar friction when Bob Bowstreet or Tom Garbage, men of obscure literary connections, slap one on the back and demand to be called "old boy" or by one's Christian name. It is a breach of protocol, a small rebellion against the necessary hierarchy.

Winter 1756–57 offered the town its usual fare of distractions. There were operas, assailed by satirists as foolish and popish, yet attended nonetheless. There were theaters hosting Garrick and Pritchard, and masquerades frequented by lords and ladies. There were private assemblies ending in cards, though Harry preferred White's Club, where the stakes were higher and the fairness more absolute.

One day, his kinsman Lord Castlewood escorted him to court, presenting him to the Sovereign, who had recently descended from Kensington. The reception was icy. The King, turning to his lordship in German, remarked that the Earl played too high at White's, then turned his royal back. Lord Castlewood recoiled, frightened by the cold dismissal.

"What does he say?" Harry asked.

"The Majesty thinks they play too high," the nobleman whispered, trembling.

"If he does not want us, we had better not come again," Harry replied simply. "I never considered that German fellow a real King of England."

"Hush!" cried Lord Castlewood, panic rising in his voice. "Hold your confounded colonial tongue! Do you not see the walls have ears?"

"And what then?" Harry countered, observing the courtiers. "Look at them! Just moments ago they shook my hand, bowed, and flattered me. Now they avoid me as if I carried the plague."

A broad-faced gentleman in a scarlet-laced waistcoat and an old-fashioned wig stepped forward. "Shake hands, nephew," he said, extending a large brown hand. "I heard what you said. I have ears like the wall. Since others show you the cold shoulder, I offer my hand." He grasped Harry firmly. "There is something of my brother in your eyes. Though I suppose in your island you grow more wiry and thin. I am your uncle, child. Sir Miles Warrington."

Lord Castlewood looked yellow with fright. "Yes, my dear Harry. Your paternal uncle."

"Might as well have come to see us in Norfolk as dangle about playing the fool at Tunbridge Wells," the Baronet grunted. "The old lady calls herself Madam Esmond, doesn't she?"

"My mother is not ashamed of her father's name, nor am I, uncle," Harry said, pride coloring his tone.

"Well said, lad! Come home and eat mutton with Lady Warrington at three, unless you cannot do without your White's kickshaws." The Baronet turned to Lord Castlewood. "You need not look frightened. I shall tell no tales."

"I—I am sure Sir Miles Warrington will act as a gentleman!" the lord stammered.

"Belike, he will," the Baronet growled, turning on his heel. "And thou wilt come, young man, at three. Mind, good roast mutton waits for nobody. Thou hast a great look of thy father. Lord bless us, how we used to beat each other! He was smaller, younger, yet many a time he had the best of it. Take it he was henpecked when he married, and Madam Esmond took the spirit out of him when she got him in her island. Virginia is an island. Ain't it an island?"

Harry laughed. "No!"

The jolly Baronet chuckled. "Island or not, thou must come and tell all about it to my lady. She'll know whether 'tis an island or not."

"My dear Mr. Warrington," Lord Castlewood pleaded as they exited the King's apartments onto Saint James's Street, "in this great city, every man has enemies. Detraction and scandal abound. I never mentioned Sir Miles to you precisely because we have had differences. Should he make remarks to my disparagement, you must receive them *cum grano salis*, remembering they come from an enemy."

By the time Harry reached White's, the news of his rejection had already circulated. The King's jealousy regarding Harry's favor with Lady Yarmouth was a settled fact. A score of gentlemen congratulated him on the conquest. By nightfall, the cold shoulder at court was merely another victory for the Fortunate Youth.

That evening, Harry appeared at the Baronet's dinner table. Sir Miles rallied him in his simple, rustic fashion. Lady Warrington, initially a grand and stately figure, admitted that the world's bad reputation had given her a frigid welcome. Her daughters greeted him with stiff, prim curtseys—"How d'ye do, cousin?" and "No, thank you, cousin"—before departing. Their little boy, the heir, dined under the watchful eye of his governor. After dinner, with a glass of port, the child loosened his tongue. Looking hard at Harry, he asked, "Are you wicked, cousin Harry? You don't look very wicked!"

"My dear Master Miles!" the tutor exclaimed, turning red.

"But you said he was wicked!" the child insisted.

"We are all miserable sinners, Miley," the father explained gently. "Have you not heard the clergyman say so every Sunday?"

"Yes, but not so very wicked as cousin Harry. Is it true that you gamble, drink all night with wicked men, and frequent wicked women? You know you said so, Mr. Walker—and mamma said so, too, that Lady Yarmouth was a wicked woman."

"And you are a little pitcher," the father cried, dismissing the child to his sisters. "Take Miles to his sisters, Mr. Walker, and Topsham shall give thee a ride in the park on thy little horse." The idea of the horse consoled the boy, who had begun to cry bitterly, bawling that he would rather stay with his wicked cousin.

"They have made you a sad reputation among 'em, nephew!" Sir Miles noted later, pouring wine. "My wife, since the death of my poor eldest son, has taken to Tottenham Court Road and Mr. Whitfield's preaching. We have had a ward recount sad stories about you and your brother."

"About me, Sir Miles, as much as he pleases," Harry declared, warm with port. "But I'll break any man's bones who dares say a word against my brother! That fellow was not fit to buckle my dear George's shoe. If I find him repeating what he dared say in our house in Virginia, I promise him a second caning."

"You seem to stand up for your friends, nephew Harry," the Baronet observed. "Fill thy glass, lad. Thou art not as bad as thou hast been painted. I always told my lady so. I drink Madam Esmond Warrington's health, of Virginia, and will have a full bumper for that toast."

Harry drained his glass, refilled it, and toasted Lady Warrington and Master Miles.

"Thou wouldst be heir to four thousand acres in Norfolk did he die, though," the Baronet added.

"God forbid, sir, and be praised that I have acres enough in Virginia of my own!" Harry replied.

He moved to take coffee with Lady Warrington, talking easily to the young ladies. One resembled Fanny Mountain and became his special favorite. When he left, they agreed their wicked cousin was not nearly as wicked as imagined. Lady Warrington hoped to rescue him from the pit. That evening, while Harry was at White's, she sent him a copy of *Law's Call* with a note hoping it would serve him well. Once dispatched, she and her daughters went to a rout at a minister's house.

Before heading to White's, Harry drove to Tavistock Street to visit his friend Mr. Sparks. He purchased more trinkets for his female cousins, claiming they were gifts "from their aunt in Virginia." He was full of kindness, kindled and warmed by his prosperity. Not all men respond to wealth with such grace
