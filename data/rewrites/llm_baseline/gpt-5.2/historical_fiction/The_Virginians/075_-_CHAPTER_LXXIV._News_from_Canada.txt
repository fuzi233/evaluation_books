Our Castlewood kinsfolk kept us to the turn of the year; and when at last we had been a fortnight away—an age, as any absurd and infatuated young man will reckon time—my feet carried me back to my enchantress as surely as a hungry fellow finds the door of a kitchen by smell alone.

Madame de Bernstein, for her part, wore no sorrow at quitting her father’s house. Indeed she loosened, as it were, the strings of her tongue the moment the coach-wheels had fairly begun to sing upon the road and the wind, taking us in flank, changed the very note of the world. What had passed in that private audience where she and her niece had fought their battle-royal she never told; yet the proof of it was plain enough. She spoke no more of “forming” cette petite; and when she named the young Countess, it was with a nervous little laugh, sharp as a spoon on a china cup, and without the old sourness. Her nephew Eugene, she said, was plainly marked out to be henpecked as long as he lived—she could see it as one sees salt in a clear broth. A little order in the house would do all the good possible. The little old vulgar American gentleman—so she chose to call Mr. Van den Bosch—seemed shrewd, and might serve advantageously as steward, keeping accounts as neat as a row of spice-jars.

As for the Countess’s mother, Madame had heard a story—one of those stories that are flung like kitchen slops from one alley of talk into another—that the woman was a convict, shipped out from England, where no doubt she had beaten hemp in most of the gaols. “But,” says my aunt, with that worldly mercy which can be cruel and kind in one breath, “this need not be cried by the town-crier. And after all, with certain people, what matters birth? The young woman will be honest now for her own sake; she is shrewd enough, and will learn English presently; and the name she has a right to is big enough to carry her into any society.” A grocer, a smuggler, a slave-dealer—what mattered Mr. Van den Bosch’s pursuit? The Countess of Castlewood, says she, could afford to be anybody’s daughter; and once Eugene produced her, it was our duty to stand by her.

Mr. Warrington, who was always hoping to make kindness do the work which pride had neglected, ventured to think that the same tie which bound my aunt so strongly to her nephew might dispose her to some affection toward her niece; and he spoke of my visit to Mr. Hagan and his wife, praying her favour for them. But Madame was flint there. She begged that Lady Maria’s name might never again be mentioned; and, as though that very prohibition had set the wheel going, she talked for two hours afterwards of nobody else. She related a string of anecdotes—sharp, salted, and not fit, as books say, for virginibus puerisque—which I will not set down. This only I will say: if that poor creature sinned, she did not sin alone in our family; and if she repented, others might do well to follow her example.

Hagan, it is known, after he quitted the stage, led an exemplary life; and in the pulpit had an elegance and eloquence which showed how well he understood the cadence and music of speech. His lady adopted extreme views and was much esteemed in the sect she joined; and when I saw her last she spoke to me of enjoying a peculiar spiritual illumination, which I suspected then to be lit by the too free use of liquor. Yet I remember—as one remembers the honest taste of bread in a lean season—how she and her husband were good to me and mine when sympathy was needful and many a Pharisee turned away.

I have told already how easy it was to rise and fall in my fickle aunt’s favour; how each of us brothers in turn was embraced and neglected. My season of glory came after the success of my play. I was introduced to the town-wits; held my place among them tolerably; was pronounced pretty well bred by the macaronis and people of fashion; and might have run a career in that bright, noisy world had my purse been longer, had I chosen such a life, had I not loved at that time a pair of kind eyes better than all the painted beauties of Ranelagh, and the brightest orbs of the Gunnings or Chudleighs.

Because I was fond of your mother—will it be believed, children?—my tastes were called low by my genteel family, and deplored as though I had taken to eating with my fingers in public. So it was; and I know that my godly Lady Warrington and my worldly Madame Bernstein put their elderly heads together and lamented my way of life.

“Why, with his name, he might marry anybody,” says meek Religion, which had ever one eye on Heaven and one on the main chance.

“I meddle with no man’s affairs, and admire genius,” says my uncle; “but it is a pity you consort with those poets and authors and that sort of people, and that, when you might have had a lovely creature with a hundred thousand pounds, you let her slip and make up to a country girl without a penny-piece.”

“But if I had promised her, uncle?” says I.

“Promise, promise! These things are matters of arrangement and prudence, and demand a careful look-out. When you first committed yourself with little Miss Lambert, you had not seen the lovely American lady whom your mother wished you to marry—as a good mother naturally would. And your duty to your mother, nephew—your duty to the Fifth Commandment—would have warranted your breaking with Miss L. and fulfilling your excellent mother’s intentions regarding Miss—What was the Countess’s Dutch name? Never mind. A name is nothing; but a plumb, Master George, is something to look at! Why, I have my dear little Miley at dancing-school with Miss Barwell, Nabob Barwell’s daughter; and I don’t disguise my wish that the children may contract an attachment which may endure through their lives! I tell the Nabob so. We went from the House of Commons one dancing-day and saw them. ’Twas beautiful to see the young things walking a minuet together! It brought tears into my eyes, for I have a feeling heart, George, and I love my boy!”

“But if I prefer Miss Lambert, uncle, with twopence to her fortune, to the Countess, with her hundred thousand pounds?”

“Why then, sir, you have a singular taste—that’s all,” says the old gentleman; and he turned on his heel and left me, as a cook leaves a customer who insists on sugar in his gravy.

I could understand his vexation at my not seeing the world as he saw it. Nor did my Aunt Bernstein relish my engagement, nor the family with whom I passed so much time. Their simple ways wearied her; perhaps even offended her nice nostril. She no more liked their company than a certain person—who is not so black as he is painted—likes holy water. The old lady chafed at my perpetual attendance by my sweetheart’s lap. Having risen in her favour, I began to fall again; and once more Harry was the favourite, and his brother—Heaven knows—felt no jealousy. He was now our family hero.

From the seat of war where he was engaged he sent brief letters—plain fare, not seasoned with fine phrases; and at first Madame cared little for them or the writer, the facts not being, to her mind, amusing enough. Yet a plain letter can carry more life than a gilded one, as brown bread can hold the whole field in it.

We had early learned in London of the action on the glorious first of August at Minden, where Wolfe’s old regiment was one of the British six that helped to achieve victory on that famous day. At the same hour the young General lay in his bed within sight of Quebec, stricken with fever, and perhaps with rage and disappointment at the check his troops had just received.

In June the fleet that carried Wolfe and his army had entered the St. Lawrence; and on the last day of the month they landed on the Island of Orleans, opposite the great cliff of Quebec that rises like a wall out of the river mist. After the great action in which his General fell, my dear brother—who had accompanied the chief—wrote me one of his simple letters describing his modest share in that glorious day. He added little to what the town already had by a hundred pens, save only this: from the testimony of a brother aide-de-camp who stood by Wolfe’s side, the General spoke not a word after receiving his death-wound; so that the famous phrase put into the dying hero’s mouth is no more authentic than one of those set speeches of Livy or Thucydides which scholars love because nobody living can contradict them.

From his position on the Island—the great channel running between it and the northern shore—the General watched for a chance to strike, hungry as a man on campaign who hears, far off, the clatter of mess-tins and smells some other regiment’s stew. Above the city and below it he landed—now here, now there—bent on attacking wherever he saw an opening. It seems now a prodigious fault in the Marquis of Montcalm to accept a battle on equal terms; for Wolfe had no artillery, and when we made our famous escalade of the heights and stood on the Plains of Abraham, we were nearer the city, certainly, yet as far as ever from being within it.

The match played between those brave chiefs and their two gallant little armies from July until Wolfe won the crowning hazard in September was as eager a game as ever men played—stakes being provinces, and the counters human lives. On the first night after the landing, as Harry narrates it, the sport began. At midnight the French sent a flaming squadron of fireships down upon the British vessels discharging stores at Orleans. Our seamen, taking the matter as if it were a fair, towed the fireships clear and grounded them on shore, where they burned out with a roar and crackle that must have made the whole river-bank seem a smithy. When the French commander learned our ships had entered the river, he marched to Beauport in advance of the city and there took a strong position.

When our stores and hospitals were established, Wolfe crossed from his island to the left shore and drew nearer to his enemy. The ships were behind him in the river, but the whole country in front was in arms. Indians in the forest seized our advanced parties as they strove to clear it and murdered them with horrible tortures; and the French, for all their polish and prayers, were as savage as their red allies in that business. The Montmorenci River rushed between Wolfe and the enemy: a loud, unceasing voice in the landscape, as though the land itself counselled delay. He could neither attack the entrenchments nor the city behind them.

Bent on finding some other assailable point, the General skirted the left shore beyond Quebec. Everywhere it was guarded. Having run the gauntlet of batteries up and down the river, he returned to his post at Montmorenci.

On the right of the French position, across the Montmorenci—fordable at low tide—stood an enemy redoubt. He would have that. Perhaps, to defend it, Montcalm would be forced out from his lines, and a battle brought on. Wolfe resolved to play those odds. He would fetch over the body of his army from the Island of Orleans and attack from the St. Lawrence; and he timed his assault so that, at shallow water, his lieutenants Murray and Townshend might cross the Montmorenci. Thus, on the last day of July, he played his desperate game.

First he and General Monckton, his second in command—setting out from Point Levi—crossed the St. Lawrence from their respective stations, and were received with a storm of shot and artillery as they rowed for shore. No sooner were the troops landed than they rushed at the French redoubt without order, were shot down before it in great numbers, and were obliged to fall back. At the preconcerted signal, the troops on the other side of the Montmorenci advanced across the river in perfect order. The enemy even evacuated the redoubt and fell back to their lines; but from those lines the assailants were met with so severe a fire that to make an impression was hopeless, and the General had to retreat.

The battle of Montmorenci—Harry and I have fought it again many a time over our wine, the clink of glasses keeping time with the remembered volleys—formed the dismal burden of Wolfe’s first despatch that reached England and plunged us all into gloom. What more might one expect of a commander so rash? What disasters might one not foretell? Was ever scheme so wild—to bring three great bodies of men across broad rivers under murderous batteries merely on the chance of inducing an enemy, strongly entrenched, to quit his strength and come out to fight? It was the talk of the town. Grave people shook their heads over their dishes, and prophesied fresh ruin with their mouths full.

The General, who took to his bed after this failure, shuddering with fever, was to live barely six weeks longer—and die immortal.

How is it—by what, and whom—that Greatness is achieved? Is it Merit—is it Madness that is patron? Is it Frolic or Fortune? Is it Fate that awards successes and defeats? How did the French gain Canada from the savage, and we from the French; and after which of the conquests was the right time to sing Te Deum? We are always for dragging Heaven into our quarrels, and making the gods intervene whatever the knot may be. Does Broughton, after pummelling Slack, lift up a black eye to Jove and thank him for victory? And if ten thousand boxers are to be heard, why not one? And if Broughton is to be grateful, what is Slack to be?

Wolfe wrote from his headquarters at Montmorenci Falls on the 2nd of September—letters so noble in cadence and plainness that you know my dear scribe was not his author nor secretary:

“By the list of disabled officers (many of whom are of rank) you may perceive, sir, that the army is much weakened. By the nature of this river the most formidable part of the armament is deprived of the power of acting, yet we have almost the whole force of Canada to oppose. In this situation there is such a choice of difficulties, that I own myself at a loss how to determine. The affairs of Great Britain, I know, require the most vigorous measures; but then the courage of a handful of brave men should be exerted only where there is some hope of a favourable event. The admiral and I have examined the town with a view to a general assault: and he would readily join in this or any other measure for the public service; but I cannot propose to him an undertaking of so dangerous a nature, and promising so little success.... I found myself so ill, and am still so weak, that I begged the general officers to consult together for the public utility. They are of opinion that they should try by conveying up a corps of 4000 or 5000 men (which is nearly the whole strength of the army, after the points of Levi and Orleans are put in a proper state of defence) to draw the enemy from their present position, and bring them to an action. I have acquiesced in their proposal, and we are preparing to put it into execution.”

On the 14th of October following the Rodney cutter brought this melancholy intelligence to England: the attack had failed; the chief was sick; the army dwindling; the threatened city so strong that assault seemed almost impossible; the only chance to fight Montcalm on terms less disadvantageous than storming entrenchments, and, if possible, to draw him from his position. Would the French chief, whose great military genius was known in Europe, walk into such a snare? Pale looks were seen in the City; doubt and gloom lay in coffee-houses where the cups clattered and yet the talk sank low, as though even the room’s familiar hum had lost heart.

Three days after came the famous letters announcing that wonderful consummation of fortune with which Wolfe’s wonderful career ended. If no man is to be called happy till his death, what are we to say of this one? His end was so glorious that I protest not even his mother nor his mistress ought to have deplored it—or at any rate to have wished him alive again. I know it is a hero we speak of; and yet I scarce know whether in that last act I admire the result of genius, invention, and daring, or the boldness of a gambler winning astonishing odds.

Suppose the ascent discovered half an hour sooner, and his people—surely—beaten back. Suppose Montcalm not to quit his entrenched lines to accept that strange challenge. Suppose these points—and none of them depended on Wolfe at all—what then becomes of the hero’s glory, of the great minister who found him, of the intoxicated nation that rose frantic with self-gratulation at the victory? What fate is it that shapes our ends—or those of nations? In the many hazardous games my Lord Chatham played, he won this prodigious one. And as the greedy British hand seized the Canadas, it let fall the United States out of its grasp. Wisdom after the event is easy: we marvel at one man’s rashness when the deed is done, and at another man’s fault. What generals we are upon paper! what repartees come when the talk is finished! the game over, how clearly we see how it should have been played!

Writing of an event at thirty years’ distance, it is not hard to criticise. But at the time, when first we heard of Wolfe’s deeds upon the Plains of Abraham—of the army marshalled in darkness and carried silently up the midnight river, as if the oars themselves had learned to whisper; of the rocks scaled; of the miraculous security of the enemy; of his acceptance of our challenge; of his defeat on the open plain by sheer valour—we were intoxicated in England by the news.

The whole nation rose and felt itself stronger for the victory. Not merely those engaged in the battle, but those at home who had condemned its rashness, felt themselves heroes. Friends embraced in the street when they met. Coffee-houses and public places were thronged with people eager to talk; yet it was not only the words that made the rejoicing—there was a change in the very sound of the town, as though bells and wheels and footsteps had taken on a brisker measure. Courtiers rushed to the King and to the great Minister whose will had decreed the campaign; when he showed himself, the people followed with shouts and blessings. They did not deplore the dead warrior so much as admire his euthanasia. Should James Wolfe’s friends weep and wear mourning because a chariot had come from the skies to fetch him away? Let them watch with wonder and see him departing radiant, rising above us.

To have been near him was distinction. Every soldier who fought with him became, in public talk, a hero. In our fond little circle it was a distinction to be Harry’s brother. We did not wonder but that, from his previous knowledge of the place, he had found the way up the heights which the British army took, and pointed it out to his General. Promotion, we said, must follow as a matter of course.

Why, even our Uncle Warrington wrote letters blessing Heaven and congratulating me and himself upon Harry’s share in the achievement. Our Aunt Beatrix opened her house and received company on the strength of the victory. I became a hero by resemblance to my brother. Parson Sampson preached such a sermon that his auditors—some of whom had been warned beforehand—were with difficulty restrained from huzzaing the orator, and were mobbed as they left the chapel.

“Don’t talk to me, madam, about grief,” says General Lambert to his wife, who, dear soul, was for allowing herself some small indulgence of her favourite sorrow on the day Wolfe’s remains were buried with glory at Greenwich. “If our boys could come by such deaths as James’s, you know you wouldn’t prevent them from being shot, but would scale the Abraham heights to see the thing done! Wouldst thou mind dying in the arms of victory, Charley?” he asks of the little hero from the Chartreux.

“That I wouldn’t,” says the little man; “and the doctor gave us a holiday, too.”

Our Harry’s promotion was insured after his share in the famous battle; and our aunt announced her intention of purchasing a company for him.
