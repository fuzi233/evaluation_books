A profound metamorphosis seized Margaret Brandt, transforming her spirit until she moved through her domestic sphere like a ghost haunting her own life. Her household duties were performed with the detached rhythm of a dreamer, her movements devoid of their usual vitality. When Peter, ever the blunt observer, spoke to her with any rapidity, she flinched violently, locking two wide, terrified eyes upon him as if he were a specter. Her visits to her beloved friend Margaret Van Eyck dwindled; when she did arrive, the warmth of the old lady's embrace met only with a trembling, passive resistance, causing Margaret to recoil as though burned. Yet, the most harrowing symptom of her affliction was her refusal to step beyond the sanctuary of her own roof under the sun. She ventured to Tergou only after dusk had swallowed the world, returning before the dawn broke, shunning even the sacred light of matins. Finally, Peter, though naturally unobservant, pierced the veil of her silence. "Methinks the folk all look at me," she confessed, her voice barely a whisper.

One afternoon, driven by concern, Margaret Van Eyck pressed her friend for the source of her torment. A look of sheer terror and a cascade of tears were the only response; the elder woman pleaded gently, asking why she feared to confide her sorrows. "I have no sorrows, madam, save those of my own making," Margaret replied, her voice breaking. "I am treated with a kindness I do not deserve, especially within these walls." "Then why not come oftener, my dear?" urged the old lady. "I come oftener than I deserve," Margaret sighed, the weight of her guilt crushing her further. "There, Reicht is crying for you," Margaret Van Eyck interrupted, pointing to the child. "Go, child—what on earth could possibly be wrong?"

Margaret Van Eyck, turning the possibilities over in her mind, concluded that Margaret must be mortified by the contemptuous treatment she endured from Gerard's family. Resolute, she vowed to reprimand the women responsible. The following day, clad in her hood and cloak, accompanied by the weeping Reicht, she marched to the hosier's house. Catherine received her with feigned respect, thanking her profusely with tears for her past kindness to Gerard. However, the moment Margaret attempted to pivot the conversation to Margaret Brandt, the hosier's demeanor shifted grotesquely. Her eyes dried, her lips thinned to a rigid line, and she adopted the stony visage of an obstinate, ignorant woman—a mask so impenetrable that one might as well try to soften a brick wall. Margaret Van Eyck, unused to such defiance, grew provoked. With an abrupt, mutilated curtsey that lacked all grace, she turned and fled, leaving behind Catherine's defiant glare.

Outside, Margaret Van Eyck encountered Reicht speaking with a pale girl leaning on crutches. Pushing past them with flushed cheeks and a scornful toss of her head directed at the entire family, she was suddenly halted by a small, delicate hand slipping timidly into hers. Looking around, she beheld two dove-like eyes filled with tears, gazing at her with a mixture of grateful relief and desperate pleading. Reading this complex, heartbreaking expression, the old lady's anger dissolved instantly. She stopped, kneeling to kiss Kate's brow. "I see," she murmured softly. "Very well, then, I leave it to you."

Returning home, Margaret recounted her journey with a mixture of pity and awe. "I visited a house today where I witnessed both the commonplace and the miraculous," she declared. "I saw a stupid, obstinate woman, and I also saw an angel incarnate, possessing a face so beautiful that if I held my paints here, I would take down my brushes once more and strive to capture its likeness."

Little Kate did not betray the high regard so swiftly formed of her. Seizing a better opportunity, she revealed what she had learned from Reicht Heynes: that Margaret Brandt had shed her very blood for Gerard in the woods. "See, Mother, how she loves him," Kate whispered. "Who would not love him?" "Oh, Mother, think of it! Poor thing." "Ay, wench. She has her own troubles, no doubt, just as we have ours. I cannot bear the sight of blood, let alone my own." This concession was a victory, yet when Kate pressed further, she was abruptly silenced.

About a month later, a soldier of the Dalgetty tribe, returning from service in Burgundy, delivered a letter to the hosier's house. Though he departed immediately to attend to business in Sevenbergen, the family gathered around the table as supper was served. The soldier placed the envelope beside Catherine, refusing any reward. Unfolding the paper, they discovered, with curious unanimity despite their inability to read, that the handwriting belonged to Gerard. "And your father must be away," cried Catherine, her voice rising in indignation. "Are you not ashamed? Not one among you can read your brother's letter." Although the words remained hieroglyphics to them, the letter carried a silent, universal language—an art that speaks without words, crossing frontiers and seas to strike directly at the heart. It required no translator, for it wrote in a tongue understood by all. Upon seeing the simple sketch Gerard had drawn between the two short paragraphs—a picture of two hands clasped tightly—they understood immediately. Gerard was bidding them farewell. As they gazed upon the lines, recognizing his manner in every curve, he seemed present, saying goodbye. The women wept until the image blurred before them. Giles spoke "Poor Gerard!" in a voice unnaturally low, while even Cornelis and Sybrandt felt a fleeting remorse, sitting in somber silence.

Desperate to decipher the message without exposing their ignorance or vulnerability to the stranger, Kate timidly suggested, "The Dame Van Eyck?" "And so I will, Kate," the soldier agreed. "She has a good heart. She loves Gerard, too. She will be glad to hear of him. I was sharp with her when she came here, but I shall make my submission, and then she will tell me what my poor child says." Soon, Margaret Van Eyck stood before the hosier's door. Reicht ushered her into a room, warning, "Wait a moment; she is at her prayers." Inside, a young woman sat pensively by the stove. Rising gracefully, she made way for the visitor. "Thank you, young lady; the winter nights are cold, and your stove is a treat," the newcomer remarked warmly.

While warming her hands, Catherine scrutinized her companion furtively. The young woman wore a plain wimple, yet her gown was trimmed with fur—a rare sign of superior rank or wealth in those times. What struck Catherine most profoundly, however, was the candor and modesty radiating from the girl's face. Feeling certain of sympathy from such a noble countenance, Catherine began to chatter nervously. "Now, what brings you here, young lady? It is a letter! A letter from my poor boy, far away in some savage land. I take shame to say that none of us can read it. I wonder if you can?" "Yes," the girl replied simply. "Can you, now? It is much to your credit, my dear. I dare say she won't be long, but every minute feels like an hour to a poor, longing mother." "I will read it to you," the girl promised. "Bless you, my dear; bless you!"

In her unfeigned eagerness, the girl failed to notice the suppressed urgency with which the hand was slowly extended to take the letter, nor the tremor that gripped the fingers as they closed around it. "Come, then, read it to me, pray. I am wearying for it," Catherine implored. "The first words are, 'To my honoured parents,'" the girl began. "Ay! And he always did honour us, poor soul," Catherine sobbed. "'God and the saints have you in His holy keeping, and bless you by night and by day. Your one harsh deed is forgotten; your years of love remembered.'" Catherine laid her hand over her bosom and sank back in her chair, overcome by one long, rending sob. "Then comes this, madam. It speaks for itself: 'a long farewell.'" "Ay, go on; bless you, girl, you give me sorry comfort. Still, 'tis comfort." "'To my brothers Cornelis and Sybrandt—Be content; you will see me no more!'" "What does that mean? Ah!" "'To my sister Kate. Little angel of my father's house. Be kind to her—for poor Gerard's sake: since grief to her is death to me.'"

At the mention of Margaret Brandt, the dam broke. "That is Margaret Brandt, my dear—his sweetheart, poor soul. I've not been kind to her, my dear. Forgive me, Gerard!" Nature, resenting the girl's struggle to maintain unnatural composure, suddenly gave way. She sank from her chair, collapsing into insensibility, the letter clutched in her hand, her head resting heavily on Catherine's knees.
