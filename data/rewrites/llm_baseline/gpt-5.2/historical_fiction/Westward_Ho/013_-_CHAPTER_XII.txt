Every soul in Bideford—aye, and every stranger who has but once smelt the river at low tide, brackish and weedy, with the tar of boats and the sharp bite of oakum—learns soon enough that the town is not built round its market, nor its church, nor even its quay, but round its bridge. The bridge stands there as if it had been set down by a hand older than masons’ craft: a backbone of stone spanning the Torridge, taking into itself the tramp of hoof and boot, the creak of cart-wheels, the cries of fishwives, the bell of the parish, the soft slap of water under the arches, and the hiss of wind through the parapets. It is the town’s ear as much as its spine; for on a still day you can tell by the bridge’s hum whether the river runs full or thin, whether the tide comes in easy or fretting, whether peace sits on the houses or quarrel.

And if men were honest, they would confess that Bideford Bridge has never been a mere work of stone. It has been, by turns and all at once, a thing blessed and a thing feared: a bridge that saved souls by letting pilgrims pass dry-foot; a bridge that took alms and gave them again; a bridge that paid for schooling; a bridge that could, so the old tell it, feel the weight of a liar’s foot more sharply than a true man’s. Folk will still whisper, lowering their voices as if the very arches might be listening, how the first stones were carried away each night from the place the builders chose, and set down—without the mark of crowbar or rope—half a mile lower, where the sand lies treacherous and shifts like flour under a baker’s palm. Sir Richard Gurney, parson of the parish, fretted himself into sleeplessness over that theft, dreaming of devils busy in his sheepfold; and then, at last, came his vision—an angel, so the tale goes, who spoke plain sense with heavenly authority: build it where the stones go of their own will, for there alone is foundation amid the false, sliding bed.

Bishop Grandison of Exeter, a man who knew that bodies have their claims as surely as souls, made the work holy by proclamation: indulgence and benediction, and “participation in all spiritual blessings for ever,” to any who would help bridge that dangerous ford. Thus did Bideford, with a piety not untouched by prudence, contrive to “make the best of both worlds.”

What queer sayings have grown up about the bridge since then! That its foundations are laid upon wool, and yet it quivers under a horse’s lightest step; that though it bears three-and-twenty arches, a certain William Alford—another Milo, if you like to believe it—carried for a wager four bushels, salt-water measure, all the length thereof. Stranger still (and truer), that the bridge is, in law, an esquire in its own right, bearing arms like a gentleman—ship and bridge on a plain field—and owning lands and tenements in many parishes, by which it has founded charities, built schools, and fought suits at Westminster as stoutly as any flesh-and-blood litigant.

But of all its works, the one that concerns us most is this: it has given dinners. Yearly dinners. And for that purpose, being (as Bideford folk say with a wink) a bridge of a very good palate, it kept the best-stocked cellar of wines in all Devon—bottles lying cool as river-stones, casks breathing out their oak and their long, sleepy sweetness in the dark.

To one of these bridge dinners, in the year of our Lord 1583, were bidden all the notabilities of Bideford; and with them Mr. St. Leger of Annery, whose house sat close by, looking down upon park and river, and whose open hand was so busy in his lifetime that he saved his children, after him, from the temptation of riches by leaving them none to tempt.

It fell out, as often at table where men’s tongues are warmed, that a trustee of the bridge and Mr. St. Leger came to words—civilly, but with heat—upon a matter of great weight to Devon stomachs: whether a salmon caught below the bridge was better or worse than one caught above. No argument can live long when a fish is in question and honest appetites are present; proof must be had. Mr. St. Leger swore that since the bridge had fed him well, he would feed the bridge in return; he offered five pounds that he would find them, out of the pool below Annery, a salmon as firm and flaky as the Appledore fish they had just eaten; and then, in that overflow of hospitality which is as sudden as the lifting of a lark, he bade the whole company dine with him at Annery three days thence, and bring each man a wife or daughter. Don Guzman being at table, he was invited too.

So it came to pass that there was a mighty feast in the great hall at Annery, such as had been rare since Judge Hankford feasted Edward the Fourth there. The house itself seemed to remember old banquets: oak-panelling dark with age, a hearth wide enough to roast an ox, and the air full of mingled scents—wood-smoke, rushes strewn newly underfoot, rosemary bruised by skirts, and the rich, steady perfume of meat brought to the board.

Servants went and came with that soft, trained swiftness that adds its own music to a feast: trenchers set down, knives laid, cups filled. Pewter flashed; napery whitened the long table like a river of linen. There was no starving of the eye nor cheating of the nose. Dishes arrived steaming—capons browned and crackling, their skin salted just so; pasties whose crusts had been pinched and glazed to a deep shine; platters of river-fish set in order, the flesh pearled and ready to lift in clean flakes with the knife; herbs chopped fine and strewn; bowls of sharp sauces—verjuice and mustard, and one sweet-sour with fruit that made the mouth water before it touched the tongue. When the salmon came—pink as dawn under its skin, and smelling of clean river and salt—it drew a murmur from those who knew how to praise without words.

While every one ate their best and drank their worst, Rose Salterne and Don Guzman played a more dangerous game than any wager over fish. They pretended not to see each other, and watched each other all the more. Rose, at least, had to keep her eyes bridled; for not only did her father sit at the table, but just opposite her were two young men newly returned on furlough from her Majesty’s Irish army—Mr. William Cary and Mr. Arthur St. Leger—whose soldierly faces and quick, measuring looks made a girl feel as if even her silence could be questioned.

Rose and the Spaniard had not exchanged one word in six months, though they had met many times. Don Guzman did not avoid her company—save in her father’s house. There he obeyed her too carefully, as if she were air: always the stateliest of salutations on coming and going, never a glance that could be accused in open day. Yet whenever he was in her presence elsewhere, he set himself—deliberate as a cook seasoning a dish—to show his best: more witty, more eloquent, more full of romantic wonder and far-off tales than ever he had been. He had tried one way and failed; now he tried another, more subtle and more perilous.

For in his mind Rose had deserved punishment for daring to refuse a Spanish nobleman; and what punishment, he thought, could sting more sharply than the withdrawal of his smiles—the closing of his sunshine? There was vanity in that, but cunning too. He knew well how the world values a man at the price he puts upon himself, and how women—especially proud women—are tempted to pay dearly for what seems withheld.

So he did not stoop to gross tricks, such as paying attention to another woman—too coarse a bait, that might have raised disgust or despair but would never have brought Rose to his feet. Instead, he showed her, quietly and without fuss, that he could do without her. And Rose, poor soul, began at once to ask herself why. What secret treasure did he possess that made him independent of her, while she could not say she was independent of him? Was there some mystery at his heart? Some great ambition? Some knowledge he kept locked away like spice in a chest? Some capacity of love beyond common men? And worse—was he not, in all things, greater than she: wiser, richer-hearted, better-born? If his wealth could but mend her poverty—

Step by step she was being led to sue, as a pauper, to the very man she had once dismissed. The temptation of being the keeper of some hidden sanctuary—of knowing what other women did not, of feeling herself set apart—grew too strong for her, as it grows strong for most. She tired of her mask. At table that day, between the clatter of knives and the thick talk of men, Don Guzman saw her unease and downcast looks; and thinking, in his conceit, that she was punished enough, he let his eyes steal to hers now and then. When she dropped her gaze at meeting his, he was not abashed; he only watched her blush, watched her grow more silent, more abstracted, as if her very breath were busy with his name.

Then he, too, played at abstraction—till at last he caught her looking at him to see whether he looked at her. In that instant he knew she was snared. With his eyes he asked, Do you forgive me? And she stopped short in talk with her neighbour, faltered, lowered her eyes, and then lifted them again—searching his—so he might ask it once more. What could she do but answer with all her face, with the bend of her pretty neck: And do you forgive me in turn?

Upon that, Don Guzman burst forth bright as a bird at dawn, and became the life of the company—story, jest, repartee flowing from him like wine from a tilted cup. And Rose knew, with a tremor of guilt that was half delight, that she was the cause of his sudden joy. She blamed herself, and yet her heart leapt. She longed for the feast to end that she might flee home and hide herself alone with sweet fancies whose reality she dared not face.

After dinner the company drifted out to the great terrace. It was a fair sight: the stately house behind, warm stone in the midsummer glare; before them the park with old oaks thick as history, deer moving like brown shadows between trunks, and the broad, land-locked river spread below like a lake of hammered brightness. The air held the scent of hot grass, distant water, and the faint sour-sweet breath of fermenting ale from within. Laughter rose and fell in little bursts. Dresses rustled; ribbons flickered; women walked in twos and threes, peering, gossiping, praising, pricking one another with smiles. The day had its own soundscape: the far caw of rooks, the small insistence of insects, the soft thud of hooves in turf, and beneath it all the steady, soothing voice of the river—nature’s music, not the harsh hammering that later ages would call progress and sell as triumph.

Mrs. St. Leger, the hostess, did the honours with a kindly state; beside her moved fair Lady Grenville, a presence that drew eyes as flame draws moths. Rose kept apart, as if she could find a corner of shadow on a sunlit terrace, longing to laugh or cry, she knew not which.

“Our pretty Rose seems sad,” said Lady Grenville, coming to her with an ease that made kindness look like grace. “Cheer up, child! We want you to come and sing to us.”

Rose answered she scarce knew what, and obeyed as if she were walking in a dream.

She took the lute and sat down on a bench beneath the house, where the wall gave a strip of shade and the stone held a coolness against her skirts. The others gathered round her, voices softening, as if music demanded a gentler tongue.

“What shall I sing?” she asked.

“Let us have your old song, ‘Earl Haldan’s Daughter.’”

She shrank from it. It was loud and dashing, ill-matched to her thoughts; and Frank—Frank—had praised it in happier days long gone. The words felt ominous in her mouth. Yet she dared not refuse, for fear that refusal might call notice where no one had yet suspected. So she began, forcing steadiness into her fingers and voice—

“It was Earl Haldan’s daughter,
She look’d across the sea;
She look’d across the water,
And long and loud laugh’d she;
‘The locks of six princesses
Must be my marriage-fee,
So hey bonny boat, and ho bonny boat!
Who comes a wooing me?’

“It was Earl Haldan’s daughter,
She walk’d along the sand;
When she was aware of a knight so fair,
Come sailing to the land.
His sails were all of velvet,
His mast of beaten gold,
And ‘hey bonny boat, and ho bonny boat,
Who saileth here so bold?’

“‘The locks of five princesses
I won beyond the sea;
I shore their golden tresses,
To fringe a cloak for thee.
One handful yet is wanting,
But one of all the tale;
So hey bonny boat, and ho bonny boat!
Furl up thy velvet sail!’

“He leapt into the water,
That rover young and bold;
He gript Earl Haldan’s daughter,
He shore her locks of gold;
‘Go weep, go weep, proud maiden,
The tale is full to-day.
Now hey bonny boat, and ho bonny boat!
Sail Westward-ho, and away!’”

As the last note died, a measured voice, foreign in accent and yet trained to please, slid into the hush and made it tremble.

“In the East,” said Don Guzman, “they say the nightingale sings to the rose; Devon, more happy, has nightingale and rose in one.”

“We have no nightingales in Devon, Don Guzman,” Lady Grenville answered, lightly, as if to clip his compliment before it could fly too near Rose. “But our little forest thrushes sing, as you hear, sweetly enough to content any ear. What brings you away from the gentlemen so early?”

“These letters,” he said, holding them up. “They have just been put into my hand; and as they call me home to Spain, I was loath to lose a moment of that delightful company from which I must part so soon.”

“To Spain?” half-a-dozen voices echoed at once; for the Don was a general favourite.

“Yes; and thence to the Indies. My ransom has arrived, and with it the promise of an office. I am to be Governor of La Guayra in Caracas. Congratulate me on my promotion.”

A mist swam over Rose’s eyes. The Spaniard’s voice sounded hard, almost flippant, as if the whole world were a gaming-table and this but a throw of dice. Did he care for her at all? And if he did—was it hopeless? Heat rose in her cheeks, and she feared every woman there must see it. In a nervous haste—when silence would have been wisdom—she blurted out, to turn attention from herself:

“And where is La Guayra?”

“Half round the world,” he answered, “on the coast of the Spanish Main. The loveliest place on earth, and the loveliest governor’s house—set in a forest of palms at the foot of a mountain eight thousand feet high. I shall only want a wife there to be in paradise.”

“I do not doubt you may persuade some fair lady of Seville to accompany you thither,” said Lady Grenville.

“Thanks, gracious madam; but the truth is, that since I have had the bliss of knowing English ladies, I have begun to think they are the only ones on earth worth wooing.”

“A thousand thanks for the compliment; but I fear none of our free English maidens would like to submit to the guardianship of a duenna. Eh, Rose? How should you like to be kept under lock and key all day by an ugly old woman with a horn on her forehead?”

Rose turned so scarlet that Lady Grenville understood at once and would have steered the talk away; but before she could, some burgher’s wife blundered in with a commonplace about the jealousy of Spanish husbands, and another—giggling—spoke something more true than delicate of West Indian masters and fair slaves.

Don Guzman reddened. “Ladies,” he said, with a tight pride, “believe me these are but calumnies of ignorance. If we be more jealous than other nations, it is because we love more passionately. If some of us abroad are profligate, it is because they—poor men—have no helpmate, which, like the amethyst, keeps its wearer pure. I could tell you stories, ladies, of the constancy and devotion of Spanish husbands, even in the Indies, as strange as ever romancer invented.”

“Can you?” cried Lady Grenville. “Then we challenge you to give us one at least.”

“I fear it would be too long, madam.”

“The longer the more pleasant, señor. How can we spend an hour better this afternoon, while the gentlemen within are finishing their wine?”

In those days, when books were fewer and the tongues of travellers served as libraries, story-telling was an art as valued as carving at table; and as Don Guzman’s skill in it was known, the ladies drew in, chairs and benches were fetched, skirts settled, fans stilled. The terrace quieted into an attentive ring, the river’s murmur below and the faint clink from within making a distant accompaniment. Don Guzman seated himself in the midst with a proud humility, at Lady Grenville’s feet, and began:

“Your perfections, fair and illustrious ladies, must doubtless have heard how Sebastian Cabota, some five-and-forty years ago, sailed forth by commission of my late master, the Emperor Charles the Fifth, to discover the golden lands of Tarshish, Ophir, and Cipango; but being in want of provisions, stopped at the mouth of that mighty South American river to which he gave the name Rio de la Plata, and sailing up it discovered the fair land of Paraguay. Yet you may not have heard how, on the bank of that river, at the mouth of the Rio Terceiro, he built a fort which men still call Cabot’s Tower; nor, perhaps, the strange tale which will ever make that tower a sacred spot to all true lovers.

“For when Cabota returned to Spain the year after, he left in his tower a garrison of a hundred and twenty men under Nuno de Lara, Ruiz Moschera, and Sebastian da Hurtado—old friends and fellow-soldiers of my invincible grandfather Don Ferdinando da Soto; and with them a jewel more precious than Spain ever owned: Lucia Miranda, the wife of Hurtado, who, famed in the emperor’s court no less for wisdom and modesty than for unrivalled beauty, had flung aside palace pomp to marry a poor adventurer and follow him round the world.

“Mangora, cacique of the neighbouring Timbuez Indians—whose friendship Lara had contrived to win—cast eyes upon this fair creature; and no sooner saw than he coveted, no sooner coveted than he plotted with the devilish subtilty of a savage to seize by force what he knew he could never gain by right. She soon discovered his passion—she was wise enough (what every woman is not) to know when she is loved—and telling her husband, kept as much as she could out of her new lover’s sight. Meanwhile the savage pressed Hurtado to visit him and bring his lady. Hurtado, suspecting the snare yet fearing to offend, excused himself courteously on duty; and Mangora, mad with desire and disappointment, began to plot against his life.

“So passed several weeks, till food grew scarce; and Don Hurtado and Don Ruiz Moschera, with fifty soldiers, were sent up the river on a foraging party. Mangora saw his hour and leapt at it.

“The tower, ladies—so I have heard from those who have seen it—stands on a knoll where two rivers meet; on the land side stretches a dreary marsh covered with tall grass and bushes, a fit place for ambuscade. There Mangora hid four thousand Indians about the tower, while he himself went boldly up, followed by thirty men laden with grain, fruit, game, and all the delicacies his forests could afford.

“With smiling face he told the unsuspecting Lara his sorrow for the Spaniards’ want; begged him to accept the provision; and was, as he had expected, invited within to taste the wines of Spain.

“In went he and his thirty fellow-bandits; and the feast continued—songs and libations—far into the night. Often Mangora looked round; at last he asked boldly for fair Miranda; but she had shut herself in her lodging, pleading illness.

“A plea, fair ladies, which little availed. For no sooner had the Spaniards retired to rest—leaving, by I know not what madness, Mangora and his Indians within—than they were awakened by the cry of fire, the explosion of their magazine, and the inward rush of the four thousand from the marsh outside.

“Why pain your gentle ears with details of slaughter? A few fearful minutes sufficed to exterminate my bewildered and unarmed countrymen, to bind the only survivors—Miranda, the innocent cause of the tragedy, and four other women with their infants—and to lead them away in triumph through the forest towards the Indian town.

“Stunned by the suddenness of the evil, and more by thought of the worse to come, Miranda travelled all night and at daybreak was brought before the Indian king to receive her doom. Judge her astonishment when she saw it was not Mangora.

“A ray of hope flashed. She asked where he was.

“‘He was slain last night,’ said the king; ‘and I, his brother Siripa, am now cacique of the Timbuez.’

“It was true. Lara, maddened with drink, rage, and wounds, caught up his sword, rushed into the fight, singled out the traitor, slew him on the spot; and then, forgetting safety in revenge, continued to plunge his sword into the corpse, heedless of blows, till he fell pierced with a hundred wounds.

“A ray of hope, as I said, flashed across Miranda for a moment; but the next she found she had been freed from one bandit only to be delivered to another.

“‘Yes,’ said the new king in broken Spanish; ‘my brother played a bold stake and lost. But it was worth the risk. You cannot be his queen now; you must content yourself with being mine.’

“Miranda, desperate, answered with fierce taunts against his treachery and crime, asking how he could dream the wife of a Christian Spaniard would become the mistress of a heathen savage—hoping, unhappy lady, to exasperate him into killing her at once. But in vain. She only prolonged her misery. Whether her virtue and beauty awed him; whether some ambition moved him to make her wisdom serve his ends; or whether—though I will scarce believe it of one of those dark children of the devil—some pride within him made him too proud to take by force what he could not win by persuasion, certain it is the savage answered her with smiles; bade his men unbind her; told her she was no slave, and it lay with her alone to become sovereign of him and his vassals; he gave her a hut to herself, loaded her with ornaments, and for weeks treated her with courtesy—as miraculous as love itself—almost as if he had been a cavalier of Castile.

“Three months and more passed in this misery; each day Miranda grew more desperate of deliverance, seeing nearer and nearer some hideous end. When one day, going with the cacique’s wives to draw water from the river, she saw on the opposite bank a white man in a tattered Spanish dress, drawn sword in hand. No sooner did he spy her than, shrieking her name, he plunged into the stream, swam across, landed at her feet, and clasped her. It was—credible or no—Don Sebastian himself, returned with Ruiz Moschera to the tower to find only a charred and bloodstained heap.

“He guessed, as by inspiration, what had passed and whither she was taken; and without a thought of danger—like a true Spanish gentleman and a true Spanish lover—darted alone into the forest, guided by his loyal heart, and found again his treasure, still unstained, still his.

“Who can describe the joy—and then the terror—of that meeting? The Indian women fled. For ten short minutes the lovers were left together, and life was one long kiss. But what to do they knew not. Inland was the enemy. He would have swum with her across the river and tried; but his strength, worn with hunger and travel, failed. He drew her back with difficulty, sat down by her, and awaited doom with prayer—the first and last resource of virtuous ladies, as weapons are of cavaliers.

“Alas for them! May no true lovers ever have to weep over joys so soon lost, after being so hardly found! For ere a quarter of an hour had passed, the women returned with all the warriors of the tribe. Don Sebastian, desperate, would fain have slain his wife and himself; but his hand sank again—and whose would not but an Indian’s?—as he lifted it against that fair and faithful breast. In a few minutes he was surrounded, seized from behind, disarmed, and carried in triumph to the village. And if you cannot feel for him in that misery, fair ladies—who have known no sorrow—yet I, a prisoner, can.”

He paused, as if the tale had tightened his throat; and though no one spoke, the terrace did not fall silent—rather it seemed to listen harder: to the river’s voice below, to the birds in the oaks, to the distant laughter from the hall where men still drank, careless of how words can bind more surely than ropes. And Don Guzman, pausing, did not forget—no, not for the space of one breath—to see whether Rose Salterne’s eyes were on him. They were.
