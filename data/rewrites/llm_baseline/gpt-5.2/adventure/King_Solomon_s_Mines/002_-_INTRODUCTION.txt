Now that these pages have gone from my hands into the press—like a pot set on the coals that must boil to the end, whether the cook be proud of it or not—I feel the weight of what is missing from them, and of what in them is poorly seasoned. A man can taste, even before he serves, where the broth is thin.

As to the matter of it, this writing makes no claim to be the full dish of all we did and saw in Kukuanaland. There were things on that journey I would gladly have let simmer longer, so that their flavour might come out, and yet here they are scarcely more than a breath over the pot. I think first of the old tales I gathered—tales told not in my tongue, but in theirs, with meanings that cling like smoke to the thatch and will not be carried home whole—about the chain armour that turned death from us in the great battle of Loo; and also of the “Silent Ones,” the Colossi that keep their watch at the mouth of the stalactite cave. Those stories were not recited like a printed paragraph. They came at evening, when the cattle were penned and the women moved about their fires, and the night was full of little living sounds—the click of beadwork, the thin cry of a night-bird, the far drum that is not music for sale but a signal, a heartbeat of the place itself. There are meanings in such telling that do not travel well into English, for English is a dominant knife: sharp, useful, and too ready to cut away what it cannot name.

Had I listened only to my own bent, I should also have written at length on the differences between the Zulu and Kukuana dialects—differences that struck me as suggestive, not merely of speech, but of how a people remembers itself. Some words in one tongue lie heavy and old as mealies stored through a dry season; in another tongue, the same notion is spoken lightly, as if it were new-gathered. And there are turns of phrase that cannot be translated without loss, for they do not only mean—they belong. A man’s identity sits inside his native sounds as surely as a child sits inside its mother’s hut; and when those sounds are driven out by louder, imported noise, something goes hungry that no foreign bread quite satisfies.

A few pages, too, might have been profitably given to the flora and fauna of that country: to the bulbs that push up after rain as if the earth were boiling with life, and to the antelope—eight varieties of them I had never before set eyes on—moving with a silence that makes our clumsy boot-noise seem like blasphemy. There are lands where the ear governs the mind. In such places you learn what it is to live without the endless grinding and hammering that modern folk now take for the normal weather of the world. Where there is no iron shriek of machinery and no thin manufactured din poured day and night into the skull, the spirit rests differently; you can hear yourself think, and you can hear another man’s approach long before you see his face. It is a kind of health, and it is being stolen from us in towns, where even what they call “art” is often only noise cleverly packaged and sold.

Then there is the subject that most tempts me, and that in these pages has only been touched as one might touch a hot pot-lid and draw back—the magnificent military organisation of the Kukuanas. In my judgment it is far superior to that established by Chaka in Zululand, for it allows even more rapid mobilisation and does not require that pernicious system of enforced celibacy. But to set that system down properly would mean showing it in its daily life, not only in its parade: how the regiments are fed and housed, how discipline is braided into common routine as neatly as grass into a sleeping-mat, how orders pass not as shouted confusion but as a controlled soundscape—call, answer, silence—so that a nation can move like one body and yet keep its breath.

Lastly, I have scarcely spoken at all of the domestic and family customs of the Kukuanas, many of them exceedingly quaint; nor of their skill in smelting and welding metals. They carry that craft to a remarkable perfection. A good instance is found in their “tollas,” those heavy throwing-knives: the backs made of hammered iron, the edges of beautiful steel welded on to the iron frames with great nicety, as though a man had joined two different natures into one weapon and made the seam vanish. I should have liked to tell more of the household fires where such work is spoken of, and of the ways food and family and labour hold together a people’s memory when written history is not their storehouse. But I did not.

The truth is, I thought, with Sir Henry Curtis and Captain Good, that the best plan would be to set down the story plainly and straightforwardly, and leave these other matters to be handled later, in whatever way may at last seem desirable. In the meantime I shall be glad, of course, to give any information I possess to anyone interested in such things.

And now it only remains for me to offer apologies for my blunt manner of writing. I am more used to the feel of a rifle in my hands than a pen, and cannot pretend to the grand flights and flourishes that I see in novels—for sometimes I like to read a novel. I suppose such flourishes are desired, and I regret I cannot supply them; yet I cannot help thinking that simple things are often the most impressive, and that books are easier to understand when they are written in plain language, though perhaps I have no right to set up an opinion on the matter.

“A sharp spear,” runs the Kukuana saying, “needs no polish”; and on the same principle I venture to hope that a true story, however strange, does not require to be dressed out in fine words.

ALLAN QUATERMAIN.
