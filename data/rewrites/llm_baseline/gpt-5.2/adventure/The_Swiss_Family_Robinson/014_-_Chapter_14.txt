My last pot had scarcely cooled on its bed of ashes when the sky gathered itself into a bruised, black frown. Great clouds shouldered up from the sea; wind worried the palms; rain came down in sheets that turned our paths to porridge and shut us indoors as firmly as if a door had been barred. The wet season had returned.

Even in our roomy dwelling, with tools to mend and stores to order, the hours began to clink and drag like an ill-made chain. The boys’ laughter dulled. The horses in the stable shifted and stamped, and the muffled drum of rain on leaf and thatch made a constant roof-song—at first comforting, then wearing, because it never varied, because it gave no quiet. When the downpour slackened, a brief ride could brighten faces; but the moment we were back under cover, the same heavy, watery hush returned, and with it the sinking of spirits.

Fritz, who had been watching us all with the keen eye he brought to every piece of work, said to me one morning as the storm muttered far off like distant cannon, “Father, why should we not build a canoe—something swifter, more manageable than what we have? I long for a light skiff that will skim where our boats labor.”

The notion ran through the house like a gust of clean air. Even the younger ones sat up. Only my wife—whose heart never grew friendly to the sea, however often it fed us—drew her hands closer about her apron as though to keep us all from sliding into the water.

“Our chances of drowning,” she declared, “are already great enough with the pinnace and the canoe. Must you add another temptation to that perfidious element?”

I could not blame her. The sea around us was as beautiful as polished steel and as treacherous as steel, too; and its voice—now a whisper, now a roar—could soothe the mind one hour and unsettle it the next. But I promised her, and I meant it, that we would build no frail toy.

“It shall be no cockle-shell,” I said. “I have in mind the Greenlander’s cajack—light, yes, but stout and safe. It will occupy the boys, and if we do it well, it will be a masterpiece of our little colony.”

So the boat-building began, and with it our house found a new rhythm. We set the skeleton first: whalebone for the ribs, split bamboo canes to stiffen the sides and curve the deck. The deck we carried nearly the whole length, leaving only a square opening where the paddler would sit, snug and low, as if the boat were part of his own body. All day the tools made their bright, honest sounds—saw rasping, knife clicking, mallet tapping—music that pushed the rain’s monotone into the background and reminded us that we were not merely waiting out weather, but shaping our future.

By the time the frame stood complete, the season itself seemed to relent. The clouds tore open, the sun returned with a brilliance that made every wet leaf flash, and we rolled our half-finished craft toward the door. The front opening was just wide enough to let her pass, as if the house had been built with this moment in mind. Outdoors, under full light, we drew the skin over her.

Seal-skin served us again—thick, tough, and willing. We stretched it tight and stitched it down; every seam we made watertight with caoutchouc, working it carefully into the needle holes until no bead of water could find a path. When at last she lay finished on the sand, she looked strange—like some sleek sea-creature turned to our service—yet she was so light that one hand could lift her. We launched her, and she bounded on the surface like an india-rubber ball, eager to be gone.

By unanimous vote Fritz was declared her owner. But my wife stood with her hands on her hips, gaze fixed on that small deck and the narrow opening.

“I will not hear of his entering it,” she said, “until I have contrived a swimming-dress. If a sharp rock punctures it, or the fin of a fish tears it, and it collapses—then what?”

I did not consider our cajack the bubble she imagined. Still, I knew what fear can do to a household: it can sour every meal, make even the gentlest night-sounds feel like threats. So I offered my help.

We made the garment like a double waistcoat: linen coated with a solution of india-rubber, every seam painted again with caoutchouc until the whole was airtight. We left a small opening so air could be forced between outer cloth and lining; inflated, it would buoy the wearer like a bladder. It was comical to look at, and I confess I doubted its perfection. Yet the care my wife gave it—fingers pressing each seam, ear turned as if to listen for any faint sigh of leaking air—was a kind of tenderness in itself.

Meanwhile the island around our home was singing its own busy song. Seeds we had scattered were up and thriving; the verandah had begun to change from bare structure to green room, as creepers climbed the pillars and breathed out sweet scents in the heat. Birds filled the air with layered calls; even the insects seemed to keep time. After weeks of rain’s unbroken beat, the many-voiced day felt like health returning.

At last the swimming-dress was ready. One fine afternoon Fritz offered, solemn as if he were volunteering for a trial of arms, to prove it before us all. We gathered on the beach. He donned the odd waistcoat, we forced air in until it stood away from him, puffed and stiff, and his brothers burst into such roars of laughter that the gulls answered with harsh cries overhead.

Fritz marched into the water with exaggerated dignity, then slipped into the cajack, took his paddle, and moved off—swift, balanced, low to the surface. The little craft cut the bay cleanly; the water made its quick, whispering hiss along the skin; and the sounds of shore—the boys’ shouts, the dogs’ barking, my wife’s anxious call—fell behind him in a widening ring. We followed in one of our boats. He reached Shark Island, turned neatly, and came back as if he had been born in that narrow deck.

The experiment succeeded so perfectly that Ernest, Jack, and little Franz—though they had laughed loudest—begged their mother for swimming-dresses of their own.

Since we were already at Shark Island, we went to see the colonists we had settled there the autumn before—our animals, our small beginnings of order. All were well. We could tell, by the neat, sharp prints, that the antelopes had found and used the shelter we had built them. Having nothing more urgent to do, we scattered maize and salt in places they frequented, then strolled along the other shore.

The beach there was a cabinet of curiosities: shells of delicate shape and shining interior, bits of coral like frozen lace. The boys gathered these for their museum, their voices rising and falling over the surf’s steady breath. Along the waterline lay seaweed in many colors—olive, red-brown, deep green—and my wife, with the practical eye that always found food in what looked like mere wreckage, had the boys collect armfuls and stow them in the boat.

As we rowed back, I noticed she had selected certain long leaves with edges notched like a saw. She held them aside as carefully as if they were herbs. At home she washed them, then dried them in the oven, and did it with such quiet secrecy that my curiosity became a constant itch.

“Are those leaves to be a substitute for tobacco?” I teased her at last. “Do you long for its refreshing smell?”

She smiled—everyone knew her dislike of that weed—and she answered lightly, “Do you not think a mattress stuffed with these would be wonderfully cool in summer?”

But the little twinkle in her eyes told me I would not learn the truth so easily.

Not long after, the boys and I returned from a long, tiring expedition. We flung ourselves down in the verandah, grateful for the shade and the soft stir of air through vine and leaf. The island’s soundscape was gentler then: the low cooing of birds, the distant wash of waves, the soft cluck of our fowls settling—no harsh metal clamor, no grinding engines, only living things and the wind’s own instruments. It soothed the mind in a way no amount of rest alone could manage.

From within, my wife called, “Could any of you enjoy a little jelly?”

She appeared carrying a porcelain dish that looked almost too fine for our rough life, and in it a clear, trembling jelly—so transparent it caught the light and held it. Cut with a spoon and set before us, it quivered like captured water.

“Ambrosia!” cried Fritz after the first taste.

He was right. It was delicious: cool on the tongue, with a clean sea-freshness tamed by citrus, sweetened but not cloying, leaving behind a faint perfume that made the mouth want more. We ate every portion she had brought and still stared at her as if she had performed magic.

“Aha!” she laughed, watching our astonishment. “Is not this an excellent substitute for tobacco—far more refreshing than that nasty weed? Behold the produce of my mysterious seaweed.”

“My dear wife,” I said, “it is a masterpiece of culinary art. But where did you meet with such a dish? What put it into your head?”

“While staying with my Dutch friends at the Cape,” she answered, “I often saw it. I recognized the leaves at once on Shark Island. Once you know, it is simple: soak the leaves in water—fresh water, changed every day—for a week. Then boil them for hours with orange juice, citron, and sugar. That is all.”

All! As if patience and exactness were nothing. Yet I understood: like preserving a dialect in a family, such recipes survive only when someone repeats them faithfully, not in vague translation but in the old cadence of practice. The taste in my mouth seemed to carry her past with it—Cape kitchens, borrowed words, remembered measures—brought alive here among palms and sea.

The boys thanked her heartily and declared they must go at once to Shark Island for more leaves. I agreed to accompany them; I wished to examine our plantations there.

Everything flourished. Palms and mangroves had shot up with a speed that still astonished me, and seeds I had tossed idly into rock clefts had germinated, promising to cloak bare boulders with green. We even found, higher among the rocks, a bright spring of delicious water. Around it were many footprints: antelopes had been drinking there, as we had hoped.

Content with that, we grew anxious about Whale Island. When we landed, it was clear at a glance that the rabbits had multiplied. Young shoots bore the ragged signatures of many little teeth. Only the cocoanut palms had been spared.

Such depredations could not be allowed. With the boys’ help I built a hedge of prickly thorn around each stem, a simple barrier but enough to discourage most nibblers. Before we embarked, I noticed some of the seaweed there had been gnawed too. Wondering what sweetness or salt had tempted the rabbits, I gathered a little to examine at home.

The whale’s skeleton still lay where it had been stranded, but now the birds and weather had picked it clean and bleached the bones to a perfect whiteness. As I stood among those pale arches, listening to the wind blow through them like a low flute, a new plan formed. The vertebrae joints looked as if they could serve in a machine.

I separated ten or twelve and rolled them down to the boat. We towed them after us like a strange necklace trailing in the water. All the way home my mind worked on a crushing-machine—something to save our hands and arms, to grind and press what needed pressing. Stone was beyond me, but bone and wood might answer.

Next morning I went out to choose a tree for the wooden blocks I would need. I expected company, but when I reached the stables I found the boys had slipped away with guns and traps, leaving me to choose between our bull and buffalo. I took Storm—reliable, if not graceful—and set off with Floss and Bruno trotting beside.

Crossing the bridge, I passed the cassava field and stopped short. It had been overrun and laid waste. The plants were torn and trampled, the ground rooted as if by ploughshares driven mad. I bent to study the tracks; they resembled those of pigs.

Annoyed, I followed the trail. It led toward our old potato field, then grew faint. I hunted back and forth, listening for grunts, for the crackle of brush. At last Floss and Bruno began to bark in a sharp, excited way that meant discovery. Ahead, in a clearing, stood our old sow with her whole family—bristling, teeth showing, grunting so savagely that even our dogs hesitated to close.

I raised my gun and fired twice into the herd. Two fell; the rest bolted, and the dogs rushed after them. I called the dogs back, lifted the fallen pigs, and went on through the forest. Soon I found a tree suited to my purpose, marked it, and returned home.

Ernest, who had remained behind, helped me flay the young porkers. We handed them to my wife, who immediately began planning supper—salt rubbed into flesh, fire laid just so, herbs chosen with that instinct that turned our island’s rough abundance into comfort. I hoped the other boys would return by then.

They did—late, with the sound of hoofs like sudden thunder. Jack came first, wild with triumph, astride his two-legged steed, the ostrich. Behind him, Fritz and Franz followed more steadily, game-bags bulging on their cruppers. The contents were displayed at once: four birds, a kangaroo, twenty musk-rats, a monkey, two hares, and half a dozen beaver rats lay in a heap before me like the day’s accounting.

Then Fritz, without explanation, threw down a bundle of thistles.

The boys were almost feverish with excitement. Jack could hardly contain himself.

“Oh, father, you cannot think what grand fun hunting on an ostrich is! We flew like the wind. Sometimes I could scarcely breathe we went so fast, and I had to shut my eyes because of the rush of air. You must make me a mask with glass eyes to ride with, or I shall be blinded one day.”

“Indeed,” I replied. “I must do no such thing.”

“Why not?” he demanded, honestly astonished.

“For two reasons. First, because I am not obliged to do whatever you demand; and second, because you are quite capable of doing it yourself.” Then, seeing their bright faces, I added what was due. “I congratulate you on your supply of game. You have worked hard. But I wish you would let us know when you plan such long expeditions. You forget that, though you know you are safe, we at home pass the hours in anxiety.”

I sent them to care for their animals and wash, and promised supper.

Supper was worth the wait. My wife had turned the young pork into a roast that filled the house with a rich, crackling fragrance, fat hissing where it met heat, skin browning into a lacquered crust. She served it with what we had—roots and greens from our fields—and we washed it down with fragrant mead whose honeyed scent seemed to catch sunlight and hold it. The very act of eating—chewing, savoring, listening to the contented silence between remarks—restored us more than any lecture could have done.

While we ate, Fritz gave the day’s account. They had set traps near Woodlands and caught musk-rats by tempting them with small carrots. Other traps, baited with fish and earthworms, had taken several beaver rats and even a duck-billed platypus. Hunting and fishing had filled the rest of the day; Jack boasted, with reason, of the kangaroo he had run down with his swift courser. They had not forgotten the garden either: Fritz handed his mother cuttings of cinnamon and sweet-apple trees, treasures that would root our future meals more deeply into this soil.

At last Fritz asked me to examine the thistles. He believed, he said, they were used in making wool.

He was right. I recognized them at once as fullers’ teazle, with sharp little hooks on stem and leaf—the very plant used to raise the nap of cloth.

We resolved to rise early and attend to preparing the booty. The boys’ eyelids were already heavy, their voices thick with tired satisfaction, so I ended the day with evening devotions, grateful for the quiet that followed: not the dead quiet of the rainy season, but the earned hush after work well done, when even the sea seems to speak more softly.

The number of creatures we had killed made skinning a long, unpleasant business. Standing before that array, I decided I would not spend our strength wastefully. Among the ship’s stores—in the surgeon’s chest—I found a large syringe. With a few alterations it could serve a new purpose. I fitted two valves inside the tube, perforated the stopper, and in my hands I had a powerful air-pump.

The boys stared when I took up the kangaroo.

“Skin a kangaroo with a squirt?” one of them cried, and laughter broke out.

I made no answer. I hung the kangaroo by its hind legs from a tree branch, made a small incision, inserted the mouth of the syringe, and began to force air between skin and flesh. Slowly the hide distended, changing the animal into a grotesque, swelling shape. I kept pumping until the skin was nearly separated. Then a bold cut down the belly and a few quick touches where the fibres still held, and the hide came away.

“What a splendid plan!” the boys shouted.

“But why does it do it?” they asked, wonder turning quickly into the hunger to understand.

“For a simple reason,” I said. “The skin is fastened to the flesh only by slender fibres. Between them are countless little chambers. When we force air in, those fibres stretch, and at last—elastic as they are—they crack. The skin is then free and may be drawn off easily. The Greenlanders have long known this; they even distend a seal’s skin to tow the animal more easily ashore.”

We treated the remaining animals the same way, and in a couple of days all the skins were off and prepared for use.

Then I called the boys to help procure wood for the crushing-machine. We set out with saws, ropes, axes, and other tools. Reaching the tree I had marked, I sent Fritz and Jack up to lop off the highest heavy branches, so that when the trunk fell it would not damage its neighbors. When they came down, Fritz and I attacked the stem with a long saw like those used in a saw-pit, one on each end. The teeth bit steadily. When the cut was deep enough, we fastened ropes to guide the fall and sawed again.

It was laborious. The forest around us carried every sound—saw-groan, rope-creak, our own breathing—so that even our effort seemed amplified by the living acoustics of the place. At last we took the ropes and pulled together. The trunk cracked, swayed, tottered, then fell with a crash that sent birds up in a startled storm.

We lopped the boughs, then sawed the trunk into blocks four feet long. It took us two days to cut down and divide the tree, and on the third we carted home four large and two small blocks. With these and the whale-vertebrae joints, I soon completed my machine.

While I labored over this, I paid too little attention to our fields of grain. One evening, when the fowls returned with obvious indifference to their usual feed and their crops full, suspicion struck me. They had come from the direction of our cornfield.

I hurried there, half-expecting ruin. Instead, joy met me: the grain stood ripe. The heads bent heavy; the color had changed to that satisfying gold that promises bread, porridge, and quiet days ahead.

My wife, seeing at once how much work had sprung up overnight, was startled into real distress. Reaping and threshing would now be added to fishing, salting, pickling—already enough to keep her hands moving from dawn to dark.

“Only think,” she cried, “of my beloved potatoes and manioc roots! It is time to take them up. How are we to manage, with all this press of work? I cannot see.”

“Do not be downhearted,” I told her. “There is no immediate hurry about the manioc. Digging potatoes in this light soil is easier than in Switzerland. And as for planting more, we need not if we leave the younger plants in the ground. For the grain we shall harvest after the Italian fashion—less economical perhaps, but quick. Since we have two crops a year here, we can afford not to be too particular.”

Without delay I leveled a broad space of firm, clayey ground for a threshing-floor. We sprinkled it with water, rolled, beat, and stamped it. As the sun dried it, we watered again and repeated the treatment until it lay flat, hard, and smooth.

Then we slung our largest wicker basket between Storm and Grumble, armed ourselves with reaping hooks, and went into the field. I told the boys not to fuss about the straw’s length. They were to grasp the corn where convenient without stooping, wind one stalk around their handful, and toss it into the basket. Speed mattered now.

The plan delighted the boys; the basket filled again and again. Soon the field was left with tall, headless stubble. My wife was horrified.

“This is dreadful!” she cried. “You have left ears on short stalks, and that splendid straw is wasted! I do not approve of your Italian fashion at all.”

“It is not waste,” I said. “The Italians use high stubble for cattle, letting them feed among it and eat what grain remains. Afterwards, when grass grows up through it, they mow it all together for winter fodder. And now we shall thresh in Italian fashion too—it will spare arms and backs.”

We laid the little sheaves in a great circle on the threshing-floor. The boys mounted Storm, Grumble, Lightfoot, and Hurry, and set off at a brisk trot, laughing and calling as they went. Round and round they rode, trampling and stamping grain free, while dust and chaff rose in clouds. My wife and I worked with hayforks, lifting and turning the sheaves so the animals would pass over them again and again. Now and then the beasts snatched mouthfuls of what they were beating out; we let them, remembering the old command: Thou shalt not muzzle the ox that treadeth out the corn.

For winnowing we waited for a strong land- or sea-breeze, then threw the threshed corn high with shovels. The wind carried chaff away in a pale stream and the heavier grain fell clean. Our poultry visited constantly, clucking and pecking with avid interest, until my wife had to keep them back.

“Let them enjoy themselves,” I told her. “What we lose in grain we gain in flesh. I anticipate delicious chicken-pie, roast goose, and boiled turkey.”

When the harvest was housed, we found we had reaped sixty-, eighty-, even a hundred-fold what we had sown. Our garner was filled with good store. Expecting a second harvest, we at once prepared the field for sowing again and began mowing down the stubble. As we did, flocks of quails and partridges came to glean among the scattered ears. We caught only a few, but resolved that next season we would spread nets and take them in quantity.

My wife’s satisfaction returned when she saw the straw carried home and stacked neatly. Our maize crop, which we did not thresh like the other grain, gave us soft leaves for stuffing mattresses; the stalks, when burnt, left ashes rich in alkali—useful in their own way. I changed what we sowed next to rye, barley, and oats, hoping they would ripen before the rainy season returned.

Just as we finished these agricultural labors, shoals of herring appeared. This year we pickled only two barrels. But we showed far less mercy to the seals that arrived directly afterwards. We hunted them vigorously, needing their skins for many purposes—most especially to complete the cajack.

On the tiny deck of that little vessel I had made a sort of magazine to hold pistols, ammunition, water, and provisions, and I intended to cover it with seal-skin so it would be quite watertight. A couple of harpoons furnished with seal bladders were to hang alongside—small preparations, perhaps, but in our life every careful detail was the difference between a bold excursion and a reckless one.
