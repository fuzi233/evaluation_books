The spring of 1849 found me adrift in the East, my travel plans altered unexpectedly. No longer stopping in a certain city as originally intended, I dispatched a servant to retrieve my letters and funds from the English consul there, with instructions to rejoin me at a designated time and place. But fate intervened—a delay, through no fault of his own, left my camp waiting at the desert’s edge for a week.

When he finally appeared at my tent, the weight in his voice was unmistakable. *“I’m afraid I bring bad news, sir.”* His finger tapped a letter edged in black, addressed in the precise hand of Mr. Bruff. Suspense is a torment I’ve never endured well. That mourning-bordered envelope was the first I opened.

My father was dead. His fortune—now mine—came shackled with duty, and Bruff’s message was clear: *Return to England at once.* By dawn, I was on my way home.

Betteredge’s account of my departure, though earnest, paints me with too broad a brush. He took my cousin’s jests about my “continental eccentricities” as gospel, weaving a fiction of German sternness and French flair that never existed outside his own imagination. But on one point, he was painfully accurate: Rachel’s rejection had cut deep. I left England resolved to forget her—or to let time and distance dull the ache.

And they did, in their way. The sharpness faded, replaced by the distractions of new places, new faces. Yet the moment my course turned homeward, the old wound throbbed anew. The closer I drew to England, the more her ghost tightened its grip.

Upon landing, Rachel was my first question to Bruff. He relayed the events in my absence—most of them, at least. One detail he withheld: the true reason she and Godfrey Ablewhite had broken their engagement. I didn’t press. It was relief enough to know she had reconsidered marrying him.

But where was she now? Under the care of Mrs. Merridew, a widow and sister of the late Sir John Verinder, residing in Portland Place. Within the hour, I stood on her doorstep.

The servant’s blank stare told me everything. *“Miss Verinder is not at home.”* Not once, but twice. No message. No explanation. Only the cold certainty: Rachel refused to see me.

Mrs. Merridew received me with polite regret but no answers. A letter to Rachel met with a single, icy line: *“Miss Verinder declines any correspondence with Mr. Franklin Blake.”*

Insult sharpened my resolve. Bruff, when I confronted him, could shed no light. Had slander touched her ears? Had she spoken of me at all? Nothing. Then I showed him Lady Verinder’s old letter—the damning lines accusing me of “unpardoned offence” in the Diamond affair.

“Is that hatred still alive?” I demanded.

Bruff’s hesitation was answer enough.

I rang for my bags. *“I’m going to Yorkshire. Tonight.”*

“For what purpose?”

“To finish what I started. If Rachel’s silence is rooted in that cursed jewel, I’ll unearth the truth myself—link by missing link. And where better to begin than with Betteredge?”

By sunset, I stood once more on the terrace of the old house. The gardener pointed me to the backyard, where I found him: the old man in his chair, pipe in mouth, *Robinson Crusoe* in lap, the dogs sprawled at his feet.

A shadow fell across the gate. The dogs growled. He looked up, hand shading his eyes against the fading light.

Mine were too blurred with memory to speak at once.
