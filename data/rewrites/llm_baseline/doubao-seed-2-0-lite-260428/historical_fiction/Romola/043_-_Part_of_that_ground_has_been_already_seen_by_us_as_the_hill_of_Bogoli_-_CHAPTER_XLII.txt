It was the thirtieth of October, 1496. The Florentine sky dawned clear, gilded by crisp autumnal sun, and a soft land breeze drifted down the Arno valley, carrying the sharp scent of crushed vine leaves and woodsmoke from the hills. But none of the city’s inhabitants spared it more than a passing glance. Their minds were fixed on the gales howling out at sea, gales that had conspired with every other ill turn of fortune to prove Fra Girolamo Savonarola’s bold claim of Heaven’s special protection for Florence a cruel mockery.

Those tempestuous off-shore winds had driven a fleet of Marseilles supply ships off course, ships loaded down with soldiers and, most desperately needed of all, grain. Florence was reeling: famine walked her cobblestone streets, and every one of her territory’s borders bristled with hostile forces. The French king Charles VIII, that would-be new Charlemagne who had swept through Italy unopposed to seize Naples fifteen months prior, had slipped away north soon after, and word had spread that the grief of losing his newborn son had all but erased his half-hearted intention to return and set the Italian states to rights. A new Holy League had been forged against him, led by Pope Alexander Borgia, framed as a patriotic pact to “drive out the barbarian” French garrisons still holding Naples’ fortress. The fine rhetoric hid a far uglier truth: it was no more than a pack of wolves agreeing to chase off all competing packs, then tear the carcass of Italy apart between them. And Florence, stubbornly clinging to her old French alliance and refusing to join the League, was marked out not as a fellow wolf, but as the juiciest, most desirable carcass of all.

Pisa, still fighting tooth and nail for its independence from Florentine rule, was now backed by thousands of Venetian and Milanese troops, and the German Emperor Maximilian, invited by the League, had arrived to join the Pisan forces in a full siege of the port of Leghorn, while Venetian and Genoese galleys blockaded the entire coast. If Leghorn fell, Florence was doomed: that single port was her only outlet to the sea, her only lifeline for imported grain, and every land route surrounding her was already closed off by the Pope’s bitter enmity and the greedy jealousy of smaller neighbouring states.

The Signoria, dominated by Savonarola’s Piagnone followers, had met the crisis with remarkable courage: they raised new funds, levied fresh militia, dispatched conciliatory embassies to neighbouring courts, and made the radical, unheard-of choice to not bar their gates against the starving rural peasants and homeless beggars being turned out of every other city in Tuscany. But every act of mercy only stoked the anger of their political rivals, who grumbled loudly in every piazza and tavern that the government was dooming the city: Florence should join the League at once, they insisted, instead of courting ruin for a useless foreign ally, and they should feed their own citizens first before letting hordes of starving strangers drag famine and pestilence into the streets.

Day by day the hunger grew sharper, the murmurs louder. To make matters far worse, Fra Girolamo had been silenced by a papal mandate a month earlier, forbidden from preaching. But when word arrived that the Marseilles grain ships had been lost at sea, the city’s desperate hunger for the friar’s unshakable, fire-infused faith became too strong to ignore. Defying the Pope’s order, the Signoria summoned Savonarola back to the Duomo’s pulpit, and two days prior he had stood before the packed crowds and told them only to remain steadfast in piety and good citizenship, and divine deliverance would surely come. He had even dared to vow publicly that he would let his friar’s frock be stripped from his back in the streets if God did not come to Florence’s aid.

But no sign of that deliverance had appeared on this crisp October morning. So the city had turned to its oldest, most beloved intercessor: the miraculous hidden image of the Madonna dell’ Impruneta, whose sacred tabernacle had been brought into the outskirts of Florence the previous evening, escorted by a procession of priests and noble patrons and left in the church of San Gaggio just outside the San Piero gate. For a hundred and fifty years, Florentines had fetched her image into the city in times of drought, flood, plague, and war, and every time they had been granted relief. The saying went that the Madonna dell’ Impruneta loved Florence so well she would grant whatever the city asked of her. Surely she would not turn away from them now, when hunger bit so deep it was turning to plague, when hospital wards overflowed and even private house courtyards had been converted to makeshift infirmaries, when the burial brotherhoods were out before dawn every day gathering the corpses of those who had dropped dead of hunger in the gutters overnight.

Moving among those streets that morning was Romola de’ Bardi. Clad in the plainest black serge gown, a simple length of unadorned black drapery drawn over her head to hide almost all her hair, save for the faint ripples of gold that peeked out across her brow, she was walking west from the Ponte Vecchio along Por’ Santa Maria when her path was blocked by a halted bier carried by members of the San Jacopo del Popolo burial brotherhood. The brethren at the front of the bier were stooped over a huddled figure they had found collapsed by the roadside, surrounded by a crowd of gaunt, sharp-featured workmen, all chattering at once in the raw dialect of the city’s poorer quarters.

“He’s dead, I tell you! The Lord loved him so much he took him straight home.”
“Ah, wouldn’t it be a blessing for all of us to stretch out our legs and follow him? Standing upright on nothing but empty air in your stomach is no way to live.”
“He’s an old fellow anyway. Death got a poor bargain of him. Life already took all that was worth having.”
“Ten to one he’s no Florentine! A beggar thrown out of Siena. San Giovanni defend us—they don’t even need to send soldiers to fight us. They send an army of starving men to eat us out of house and home.”
“No, no, he’s one of the prisoners they kicked out of the Stinche gaol. I can see the grey patch where his prison badge was sewn on.”

Romola stepped forward, pulling the small wine flask slung from her belt free. “Let me try giving him some wine,” she said. She pried the old man’s jaws gently open with a small ivory aid she carried for this exact purpose, trickling a few drops of spiced vernaccia past his lips. He swallowed, his head lolled a little towards her, and his dark wild eyes fluttered open.

In that instant, Romola’s blood ran cold. She knew that face: the deep-lined sallow cheeks, the long white beard, those fierce unmistakeable eyes. This was the escaped prisoner she had seen in the Duomo months before, the stranger at whose very grasp Tito had blanched so pale she had thought he would faint, the figure whose likeness Piero di Cosimo had once sketched in his studio, caught mid-leap to lunge for her husband. For months she had sensed a terrible, unspoken secret coiled at the heart of all Tito’s betrayals, and now here he was, half-dead at her feet. She felt a wild impulse to turn and run, as if from a waking nightmare—then a fiercer, more unshakable resolve to stay, for some deep, unnameable instinct told her this old man had been hurt, gravely, irreparably hurt, by Tito’s treachery.

She did not run. She lifted his head gently with one arm, still dribbling wine between his lips, her hands trembling but guided by the long habit of care she had built for herself in her months of charity work. Baldassarre, for his part, looked up at her in dazed recognition. The descriptions he had heard of the golden-haired wife who lived in the de’ Bardi palace had slipped from his mind like water, and for the first time, he was looking straight at her.

“It would be best to carry him just a little further, to the steps of San Stefano,” Romola suggested. “We will not block the street there, and your brethren can go on with your work.”

They moved the thirty yards to the church steps, and by then Baldassarre had strength enough to prop himself up against the stone doorframe. The burial brethren went on their way, but the crowd of onlookers swelled, their murmurs growing bolder now that it was clear the old man was not a corpse after all. A rough man in a tattered red night-cap elbowed his way to the front of the circle that had narrowed tight around Romola, who had just pulled a hunk of soft bread from her basket, soaked a chunk of it in wine, and held it out to Baldassarre.

“Madonna, why waste that good bread on a thief who was half dead five minutes ago?” the man jeered. “We’re all starving here. Give it to us, we could do a far better job eating it than he could.”

Romola stood, her pale face drawn thin by weeks of shared fasts, her hazel eyes steady and unflinching as she held her basket of bread out to the crowd. “I know exactly how cruel hunger is,” she said, no trace of anger in her voice. “You are all strong men. You could take this bread from me right now, if you chose. But these loaves were saved for sick women, for hungry children who cannot fight for themselves. You can take all of it, if you will—but I will not let you take the piece I have set aside for this helpless old man. I will stand between you and him, for as long as I can.”

The crowd fell dead silent. The men nearest her stepped back, suddenly ashamed, each of them trying to look as if someone else had pushed them forward. At that moment, the armed patrols of the Signoria, clearing the streets ahead of the Madonna dell’ Impruneta procession, rounded the corner, waving their maces to disperse the loiterers.

A ragged, broad-shouldered man with a bristly black beard and a red rag tied around his head stepped up to Romola, bowing awkwardly. “Don’t you worry, Madonna. If you’ve got errands to run, I’ll watch the old fellow for you. I’ve got a hungry old mother waiting for me at home, I know what it is to look out for people who can’t look out for themselves. He’ll be right here when you get back.”

Romola smiled, penitent for the flicker of doubt she had felt at his rough appearance. She turned back to Baldassarre, who was already nibbling at the soaked bread, and touched his arm gently. “Wait for me here,” she said. “I will come back, and take you to a warm place to rest.”

He nodded, faint and slow, and Romola turned to make her way towards the Hospital of San Matteo, in the Piazza di San Marco, her mind already racing with the knowledge that the truth she had spent so long running from was waiting for her, there on the stone steps of San Stefano.
