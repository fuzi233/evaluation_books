"The general man is easier to know than the particular man." — La Rochefoucauld.

An hour after Grandcourt had departed, the news of Gwendolen's engagement rippled through the rectory. Mr. and Mrs. Gascoigne, accompanied by Anna, spent the evening at Offendene. The atmosphere was thick with a mixture of relief and speculative calculation.

"My dear, let me congratulate you on having secured a powerful attachment," the rector observed, his tone warm yet analytical. "You look serious, and I do not wonder at it; a lifelong union is a solemn undertaking. Yet, from the way Mr. Grandcourt has conducted himself and spoken, I believe we may already perceive some good emerging from our adversity. It has afforded you an opportunity to observe your future husband's delicate liberality."

Mr. Gascoigne nodded sagaciously, referring to Grandcourt's subtle implication regarding provisions for Mrs. Davilow—a detail Gwendolen had previously recounted to her mother with precise accuracy.

"But I have no doubt that Mr. Grandcourt would have behaved quite as generously had you not fled to Germany," Mrs. Gascoigne interjected, sensing the necessity of this diplomatic reassurance. "Had you been engaged to him a month ago, as you might have been, surely he would have acted handsomely. Now, however, there is no room for caprice; indeed, I trust you harbor no inclinations toward any. A woman owes a profound debt of gratitude to a man who persists in offering himself despite obstacles. No doubt you feel properly."

"I am not at all certain that I do, Aunt," Gwendolen replied with a gravity that bordered on the saucy. "I do not know precisely what feelings are proper to entertain upon being engaged."

The rector patted her shoulder, smiling at what he deemed innocent naughtiness, while his wife interpreted his demeanor as permission not to be displeased. Anna, meanwhile, approached Gwendolen with a tender kiss, whispering, "I do hope you will be happy," before sinking quietly into the background, fighting back tears. In recent days, she had nurtured a private romance involving Rex—a fantasy wherein, if he still longed for Gwendolen, hardship might soften her heart until they could marry. That dream had transmuted into a prayer for her role as a faithful sister, useful only so long as Rex remained poor. Now, however, she required grace to celebrate something else.

The evening gathered around the honor and glory of Gwendolen. Miss Merry and four younger girls—Alice with her high shoulders, Bertha and Fanny the whisperers, and Isabel the listener—were present, rendering real life as absorbing as a novel like *Sir Charles Grandison*. The conversation flowed chiefly through the rector's decisive remarks, answering the conjectures of the two elder ladies. According to him, the matter of financial settlements need not be mentioned; everything must, and would undoubtedly, safely be left to Mr. Grandcourt.

"I should like to know exactly what sort of places Ryelands and Gadsmere are," Mrs. Davilow asked curiously.

"Gadsmere, I believe, is a secondary residence," Mr. Gascoigne offered. "But Ryelands is one of our finest seats. The park is extensive, the woods of rare value. The house itself was built by Inigo Jones, with ceilings painted in the Italian style. The estate yields twelve thousand a year, and there are two livings attached, including a rectory, in the gift of the Grandcourts. There may be some burdens on the land, yet Mr. Grandcourt is an only child."

"It would be most remarkable," Mrs. Gascoigne mused, counting off items on her fingers, "if he were to become Lord Stannery in addition to everything else. Consider: the Grandcourt estate, the Mallinger estate, _and_ the baronetcy, _and_ the peerage"—she paused on the fourth digit, adding, "though they say there will be no land accompanying the peerage." It seemed a pity there was nothing for the fifth finger.

"The peerage," the rector noted judiciously, "must be regarded as a remote chance. There are two cousins between the present peer and Mr. Grandcourt. It is certainly a serious reflection how death and other causes sometimes concentrate inheritances on a single man. But an excess of that kind is to be deprecated. To be Sir Mallinger Grandcourt Mallinger, with corresponding properties, is a valuable talent enough for any man entrusted with it. Let us hope it will be wisely used."

"And what a position for the wife, Gwendolen!" Mrs. Gascoigne exclaimed. "A great responsibility indeed. But you must lose no time in writing to Mrs. Mompert, Henry. It is a good thing you have an engagement to offer as an excuse; otherwise, she might feel offended. She is rather a formidable woman."

*I am rid of that horror,* Gwendolen thought internally, finding the name of Mompert akin to Mumbo-Jumbo. She remained remarkably silent throughout the evening. That night, she could hardly sleep in her little white bed. It was a rarity in her robust youth to be wakeful, and perhaps an even greater anomaly for her to conceal her restlessness from her mother. Her state of mind was entirely new: she who had always felt secure in managing others had just taken a decisive step she had previously vowed never to take—or perhaps, one she was bound not to take. She could not retreat now; she found much allure in what lay ahead, and returning would leave nothing of value.

Yet, her resolution was haunted by the shadow of a previous resolve, once the undoubted movement of her entire being. Lying on her pillow with wide-open eyes, watching a darkness visible only to the blind, she was appalled by the realization that she was proceeding with what she had initially fled from with repugnance. It was new to her that a question of right or wrong in her conduct should rouse such terror; she had never known compunction that atoning caresses and gifts could not quell. But here stood a moment when a new consciousness was awakened. She seemed poised to adopt deliberately, for the rest of her life, what she had rashly declared in her bitterness during her flight to Leubronn: that it did not signify what she did, so long as she amused herself.

That lawlessness, that casting aside of all concern for justification, suddenly terrified her. It arrived accompanied by a shadowy array of possible calamities behind it—calamities that had ceased to be merely abstract names. All the infiltrated influences of disregarded religious teaching, alongside the deeper impressions of something awful and inexorable enveloping her, concentrated in the vague conception of avenging power. The brilliant position she had longed for, the imagined freedom of marriage, the deliverance from the dull insignificance of girlhood—all stood immediately before her, yet they tasted like sacrilegious food, which she must snatch with trembling.

In the darkness and loneliness of her bed, her more resistant self could not act against the first onslaught of dread following her irrevocable decision. Images of that unhappy-faced woman and her children—Grandcourt and his relations with her—repeated themselves in her imagination like a clinging memory of disgrace, gradually erasing all other thoughts. Only the consciousness remained that she had dragged those scenes into her life. Her long wakefulness seemed a delirium. A faint, pale light penetrated beside the window curtain; the chill intensified. She could bear it no longer and cried, "Mamma!"

"Yes, dear," Mrs. Davilow responded instantly, her voice waking.

"Let me come to you."

Soon Gwendolen fell asleep on her mother's shoulder, sleeping until late. When she opened her eyes, her mother stood by the bedside holding a small packet.

"I am sorry to wake you, darling, but I thought it better to give you this at once. The groom has brought the Criterion; he has come on another horse and says he is to stay here."

Gwendolen sat up, opening the packet. Inside was a delicate enameled casket containing a splendid diamond ring, alongside a letter enclosing a folded piece of colored paper bearing these words:

*"Pray wear this ring when I come at twelve in sign of our betrothal. I enclose a check drawn in the name of Mr. Gascoigne for immediate expenses. Of course Mrs. Davilow will remain at Offendene, at least for some time. I hope, when I come, you will have granted me an early day, when you may begin to command me at a shorter distance.—Yours devotedly,*

The check amounted to five hundred pounds. Gwendolen turned it toward her mother along with the letter.

"How very kind and delicate!" Mrs. Davilow said, her voice filled with emotion. "But I truly prefer not to be dependent on a son-in-law. My daughters and I could get along very well."

"Mamma, if you say that again, I will not marry him," Gwendolen snapped angrily.

"My dear child, I trust you are not marrying solely for my sake," Mrs. Davilow pleaded, deprecatingly.

Gwendolen tossed her head away from her mother and let the ring lie. Irritated by this attempt to strip away a motive, perhaps the deeper cause of her agitation was the consciousness that she was not marrying solely for her mother's sake—that she was drawn toward this marriage in ways that stronger reasons than her mother's renunciation could not hinder.
