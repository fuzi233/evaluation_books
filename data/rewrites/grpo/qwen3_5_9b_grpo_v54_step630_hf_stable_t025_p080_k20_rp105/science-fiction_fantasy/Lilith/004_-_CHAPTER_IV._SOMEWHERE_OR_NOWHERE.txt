The sun blazed with an intensity that felt less like warmth and more like exposure, though I suspected the day would not hold its promise. I glanced into the milky sapphire ring upon my finger, checking the star embedded within for clarity. It appeared even more nebulous than I had anticipated, a blurred point of light in a shifting sea. Rising from the breakfast table, I moved to the window, drawn back to the stone. Outside, the lawn was slick with the heavy rain of the night; a thrush struggled futilely to break the shell of a snail, its movements frantic against the damp earth.

As I twisted the ring, seeking the star's response to the solar glare, a keen black eye fixed itself upon me from the milky blue depths of the gem. The sight jolted me so violently that I dropped the ring. When I bent to retrieve it, the eye was gone. In that exact instant, the sun vanished, swallowed by a dark vapor that spread rapidly until the entire sky was choked with cloud. The air grew stifling, heavy with humidity, before a sudden gust tore through the room. A flash of lightning illuminated the gloom, followed by a single, sharp crack of thunder, and then the rain descended in torrents.

I had thrown open the window, standing there watching the precipitous deluge, when I saw a raven approaching across the grass. He moved with a solemn, deliberate gait, utterly indifferent to the falling water. Recognizing him, I felt a surge of relief that I remained on the ground floor, yet simultaneously a gnawing conviction that disaster loomed if I were not vigilant.

He drew nearer, executed a profound bow, and with a sudden, winged leap landed on the window sill. He stepped over the ledge, dropped into the room, and walked straight to the door. Assuming he intended for the library, I prepared to follow, vowing not to take a single step after him if he ascended the stairs. Instead, he turned neither toward the library nor the staircase, but toward a small door opening onto a grass patch nestled between two sections of the rambling old house. I hurried to open it for him. He stepped out onto the creeper-covered porch and stood gazing at the rain, which fell like a massive, thin cataract. I lingered in the doorway behind him. A second flash of lightning was followed by a prolonged roll of distant thunder. He turned his head over his shoulder to look at me, as if to ask, "Do you hear that?" before swiveling back to contemplate the storm with apparent approval. His posture and mannerisms were so undeniably human that I remarked almost involuntarily, "Fine weather for the worms, Mr. Raven!"

"Yes," he croaked in the familiar, gravelly voice I knew well, "the ground will be lovely for them to emerge and retreat! It must be a magnificent time on the steppes of Uranus!" he added, glancing upward. "I believe it is raining there too; it has been so all week!"

"Why would that make it a grand time?" I asked.

"Because the creatures there are all burrowers," he replied, "like the field-mice and moles here. They will thrive for ages to come."

"How do you know that, if I may be so bold?" I countered.

"As anyone would who has been there to witness it," he answered. "It is a spectacular sight, until you acclimate, when the earth heaves and a beast emerges. You might mistake it for a hairy elephant or a deinotherium, but none of the animals are what we have known here. I was nearly frightened the first time I saw the dry-bog-serpent wallow out—such a head, such a mane! And SUCH eyes! But the shower is nearly over. It will cease directly after the next thunderclap. Here it comes!"

A flash accompanied his words, followed by the thunder half a minute later. Then, the rain stopped abruptly.

"Now we must depart!" the raven said, stepping to the front of the porch.

"Depart where?" I asked.

"To where we must go," he answered. "Did you truly believe you had arrived home? I warned you that entry and exit are not pleasures to be taken at will until you are truly at home!"

"I do not wish to go," I said.

"That makes no difference—at least, not much," he replied. "This is the path!"

"I am perfectly content where I am."

"You think so, but you do not. Come along."

He hopped from the porch onto the grass and turned, waiting.

"I will not leave the house today," I declared with stubbornness.

"You will enter the garden!" he rejoined.

"I yield for the moment," I replied, stepping from the porch.

The sun pierced the clouds, and raindrops flashed and sparkled on the grass. The raven walked ahead.

"You will wet your feet!" I cried.

"And mire my beak," he answered, immediately plunging it deep into the sod and drawing out a great, wriggling red worm. He threw back his head and tossed it into the air. It spread great wings, gorgeous in red and black, and soared aloft.

"Tut! Tut!" I exclaimed. "You are mistaken, Mr. Raven: worms are not the larvae of butterflies!"

"Never mind," he croaked. "It will suffice for once! I am not a reader at present, but sexton at—a certain graveyard, cemetery, more properly—in—no matter where!"

"I see! You cannot keep your spade still: and when you have nothing to bury, you must dig something up! Only you should mind what it is before you make it fly! No creature should be allowed to forget what and where it came from!"

"Why?" said the raven.

"Because it will grow proud and cease to recognize its superiors."

No man realizes he is making an idiot of himself until it is too late.

"Where DO the worms come from?" the raven asked, suddenly curious.

"From the earth, as you have just seen!" I answered.

"Yes, last!" he replied. "But they could not have come from it first—for that would never return to it!" he added, looking up.

I looked up as well, seeing nothing but a small dark cloud whose edges were tinged red, as if with the light of a setting sun.

"The sun is not setting!" I exclaimed, struck with amazement.

"Oh, no!" returned the raven. "That red belongs to the worm."

"You see what happens when creatures forget their origins!" I cried with some heat.

"It is well, surely, if it allows them to rise higher and grow larger!" he returned. "But indeed, I only teach them to find it!"

"Would you fill the air with worms?"

"That is the business of a sexton. If only the rest of the clergy understood it as well!"

His beak plunged into the soft turf again, and out came the wriggling worm. He tossed it into the air, and away it flew.

I looked behind me and let out a cry of dismay: I had only moments ago declared I would not leave the house, and now I was a stranger in a strange land!

"What right do you have to treat me thus, Mr. Raven?" I said with deep offense. "Am I, or am I not, a free agent?"

"A man is as free as he chooses to make himself, never an atom freer," the raven answered.

"You have no right to make me do things against my will!"

"When you possess a will, you will find that no one can."

"You wrong me in the very essence of my individuality!" I persisted.

"If you were an individual, I could not; therefore, since you are not, I do not. You are merely beginning to become an individual."

Surrounding me was a pine forest, into which my eyes searched desperately for an unaccountable glimmer that might guide me home. Alas, how could I any longer call that house HOME, where every door and every window opened outward, and even the garden I could not keep inside?

I suppose I looked discomfited.

"Perhaps it may comfort you," said the raven, "to be told that you have not yet left your house, nor has your house left you. At the same time, it cannot contain you, nor do you inhabit it!"

"I do not understand you," I replied. "Where am I?"

"In the region of the seven dimensions," he answered, with a curious noise in his throat and a flutter of his tail. "You had better follow me carefully for a moment, lest you harm someone!"

"There is no one to harm but yourself, Mr. Raven! I confess I would rather like to hurt you!"

"That you see no one is where the danger lies. But you see that large tree to your left, about thirty yards away?"

"Of course I do: why should I not?" I answered testily.

"Ten minutes ago you did not see it, and now you do not know where it stands!"

"I do."

"Where do you think it stands?"

"Why THERE, where you know it is!"

"Where is THERE?"

"You bore me with your silly questions!" I cried. "I am growing tired of you!"

"That tree stands on the hearth of your kitchen and grows nearly straight up its chimney," he said.

"Now I KNOW you are mocking me!" I answered with a scornful laugh.

"Was I mocking you when you discovered me looking out of your star-sapphire yesterday?"

"That was this morning—not an hour ago!"

"I have been widening your horizon longer than that, Mr. Vane; but never mind!"

"You mean you have been making a fool of me!" I said, turning from him.

"Excuse me: no one can do that but yourself!"

"And I decline to do it."

"You mistake."

"How?"

"In declining to acknowledge yourself as one already. You make yourself such by refusing what is true, and for that you will sorely punish yourself."

"How, again?"

"By believing what is not true."

"Then, if I walk to the other side of that tree, I shall walk through the kitchen fire?"

"Certainly. You would first, however, walk through the lady at the piano in the breakfast-room. That rosebush is close by her. You would give her a terrible start!"

"There is no lady in the house!"

"Indeed! Is not your housekeeper a lady? She is counted such in a certain country where all are servants, and the liveries one and multitudinous!"

"She cannot use the piano, anyhow!"

"Her niece can: she is there—a well-educated girl and a capital musician."

"Excuse me; I cannot help it: you seem to me to be talking sheer nonsense!"

"If you could but hear the music! Those great long heads of wild hyacinth are inside the piano, among the strings, and give that peculiar sweetness to her playing!—Pardon me: I forgot your deafness!"

"Two objects," I said, "cannot exist in the same place at the same time!"

"Can they not? I did not know!—I remember now they do teach that with you. It is a great mistake—one of the greatest ever made by a wiseacre! No man of the universe, only a man of the world could have said so!"

"You, a librarian, and talk such rubbish!" I cried. "Plainly, you did not read many of the books in your charge!"

"Oh, yes! I went through all in your library—at the time—and came out the other side not much the wiser. I was a bookworm then, but when I came to know it, I woke among the butterflies. To be sure, I have given up reading for many years—ever since I was made sexton.—There! I smell Grieg's Wedding March in the quiver of those rose-petals!"

I went to the rose-bush and listened hard, hearing not the thinnest ghost of a sound, but smelling something I had never before detected in any rose. It was still the scent of roses, but altered, caused, I supposed, by the Wedding March.

When I looked up, the bird stood by my side.

"Mr. Raven," I said, "forgive me for being so rude: I was irritated. Will you kindly show me my way home? I must go, for I have an appointment with my bailiff. One must not break faith with his servants!"

"You cannot break what was broken days ago!" he answered.

"Do show me the way," I pleaded.

"I cannot," he returned. "To go back, you must go through yourself, and that way no man can show another."

Entreaty was vain. I must accept my fate! But how was life to be lived in a world of which I had all the laws to learn? There would, however, be adventure! that held consolation; and whether I found my way home or not, I should at least have the rare advantage of knowing two worlds!

I had never yet done anything to justify my existence; my former world was no better for my sojourn in it. Here, however, I must earn, or in some way find, my bread! But I reasoned that, as I was not to blame for being here, I might expect to be cared for here as well as there! I had had nothing to do with entering the world I had just left, and in it I had found myself heir to a large property! If that world, as I now saw, had a claim upon me because I had eaten and could eat again, upon this world I had a claim because I must eat—when it would in return have a claim on me!

"There is no hurry," said the raven, who stood regarding me; "we do not go much by the clock here. Still, the sooner one begins to do what has to be done, the better! I will take you to my wife."

"Thank you. Let us go!" I answered, and immediately he led the way.
