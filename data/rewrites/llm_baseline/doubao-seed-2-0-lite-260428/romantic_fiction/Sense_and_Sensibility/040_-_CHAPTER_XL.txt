The door had barely clicked shut behind Colonel Brandon before Mrs. Jennings rounded on Elinor, her round face alight with a knowing, playful smirk, like she’d just caught a secret no one else had thought to share.
“I won’t even pretend to ask what the Colonel just said to you, my dear,” she trilled, settling into the nearest armchair with a smug little bounce. “I tried to duck out of earshot, honest I did, but the bits I caught left no doubt what his business was. I could not be happier for you if I tried—every last inch of me wishes you all the joy in the world.”

Elinor’s chest felt soft and tight all at once, warmth spreading through her limbs that she’d not expected to feel so soon. “Thank you, ma’am. It is the most wonderful, surprising news. Colonel Brandon’s kindness is more than I ever dared hope for—there are so few men in the world with a heart so open, so quick to step in where others would turn away. I have not been so stunned in all my life.”
“Stunned? Pish, my love, I’m not the least bit shocked!” Mrs. Jennings waved a hand dismissively, her lace cap bobbing. “I’ve suspected for months he had it in him. No man looks at you the way he does and doesn’t end up acting on it, sooner or later.”
“You judged his character rightly, of course, you’ve known him for years,” Elinor said, a faint, sad little smile tugging at her mouth. “But no one could have foreseen an opportunity like this presenting itself quite so quickly.”
“Opportunity, bah!” Mrs. Jennings laughed. “When a man sets his heart on doing something good? He’ll carve out an opportunity faster than a needle through linen. Mark my words, soon you’ll be the happiest bride in all of Devonshire, and I’ll be trotting up to Delaford to visit the two of you before the autumn rains hit.”
“You intend to come calling at Delaford, then?” Elinor said, her smile growing a little more real.
“Of course I do! And don’t let the Colonel make out that the house is falling apart, either. I’ve seen it—great sprawling place, gardens full of roses, better than half the gentry’s homes in the shire. Why he’d claim it needed fixing I can’t imagine; if it’s broken, he’s the man rich enough to fix it, isn’t he?”

A servant popped in then to announce the carriage was waiting at the door, and Mrs. Jennings scrambled to her feet, already reaching for her bonnet. “Oh drat, I’ve not even told you half of what I meant to say! But we’ll hash the rest out tonight, all by our lonesomes, no callers to interrupt us. I won’t drag you out with me—your head’s far too full of this lovely news to care about paying visits, and besides, I know you’re dying to run find Marianne and tell her every last detail.”
Marianne had slipped out of the parlour before Colonel Brandon had even finished speaking, so Elinor nodded. “I will tell Marianne, certainly. But I would ask you not to mention it to anyone else just yet, if you please.”
Mrs. Jennings’ face fell a little. “Oh, very well—but I was planning to stop in and see Lucy Steele up in Holborn today, you know.”
“Not even Lucy, if you can keep it, ma’am. A day’s delay will do no harm. I must write to Mr. Ferrars first, to let him know what has happened, and until that is done, no one else ought to hear of it. There is no time to waste, not when he means to take his orders so soon.”

Mrs. Jennings blinked, thoroughly confused for a long moment, then her face lit up as a spark of understanding hit. “Oh! Oh, I see now! Mr. Ferrars is to officiate the ceremony, isn’t he? That’s a lovely touch, so thoughtful of you both! But my dear, isn’t that a touch odd? Shouldn’t the Colonel be the one writing to him, not you?”
Elinor hadn’t the faintest idea what she was on about, and didn’t care enough to ask, so she just replied to the last part. “Colonel Brandon is far too delicate, far too considerate of Edward’s feelings, to bring the news himself. He wanted it conveyed gently, by someone Edward already counts as a friend.”
“Ah, that’s very strange sort of delicacy if you ask me, but I won’t pester you,” Mrs. Jennings said, seeing Elinor reach for her pen and paper. “You know your own affairs best. Goodbye, my sweet—haven’t had news that made me this happy since my Charlotte had her first baby.”
She was halfway out the door when she popped back in, cheeks flushed. “Wait! I just thought of Betty’s sister—she’s an excellent housemaid, sews like an angel, I’d love to get her a place as your lady’s maid down at Delaford. You can think on it later, no rush.”

Elinor nodded, barely hearing a word. She was far too wrapped up in the letter to Edward, wondering how on earth to phrase the news—too much or too little, and she risked hurting his pride, or overstepping, when he was still bound to Lucy, when every kind word she wrote felt like a secret little ache in her own chest. She sat there, pen hovering over the page, staring at the blank paper, when the parlour door creaked open.
It was Edward.
He’d run straight into Mrs. Jennings on the steps of the house, on his way out to her carriage, and she’d practically shoved him inside, babbling that Elinor was waiting for him, that she had terribly important business to discuss, before he could protest. The two of them stood there staring at each other for a long, flustered moment—they hadn’t spoken a word since his engagement to Lucy had been dragged out into public, since he knew Elinor was fully aware of how his family had cast him off, left him penniless, with no way to support a wife. They both fumbled through their greetings, red-faced, awkward, Edward apologizing for intruding even though he’d been practically forced through the door.
“Mrs. Jennings said you wished to see me,” he said, sinking into a chair across from her, his hands twisted tight in his lap. “I would never have presumed to drop by unannounced, but I could not bear to leave London without seeing you and Marianne one last time. I leave for Oxford tomorrow, you see. It will likely be months before we cross paths again.”
“You would never have left without our well wishes, even if we could not give them to you in person,” Elinor said, steadying her voice, determined to get the news out as fast as she could to spare them both more awkwardness. “Mrs. Jennings was quite right. I do have something very happy to tell you—something Colonel Brandon charged me with, just ten minutes ago. He knows you intend to take orders, and he is delighted to offer you the living at Delaford, which has just fallen vacant. He only wishes the income was larger than the two hundred a year it brings in.”

Edward stared at her, completely stunned, his mouth hanging half open. All he could get out was, “Colonel Brandon?”
“Yes,” Elinor said, her courage growing as the first hard part was over. “It is his way of showing how deeply he sympathizes with all that your family has put you through lately—with that terrible unfair situation you were left in. All of us, Marianne, me, all your friends share that sympathy. It is also a tribute to how highly he thinks of your character, how much he admires the honourable way you have behaved through every hardship.”
“Colonel Brandon is giving me a living? That cannot possibly be real,” Edward whispered.
“Your own family has been so cruel to you, it is no wonder you struggle to believe anyone would be so kind.”
“No,” he said, his eyes locking on hers, his voice thick with emotion. “I never doubted *your* kindness. I know full well I owe this all to you. I cannot find the words to thank you—you know I was never any good at speeches.”
“Oh no, you are quite wrong,” Elinor hurried to say. “You owe this to no one but your own good character, and Colonel Brandon’s wisdom in seeing it. I had no hand in it, not at all. I did not even know the living was vacant, not five minutes before the Colonel walked in. He is a friend to our whole family, so it gives him extra joy to do this for someone we care for—but I never once asked him for a single thing on your behalf.”
She hesitated to admit she might have had a tiny, tiny influence, and that hesitation only made Edward more certain she was the one who had arranged it all. He sat quiet for a long minute, thinking, before he spoke again. “Colonel Brandon seems like a man of great worth. I have always heard nothing but good of him, and your brother John thinks the world of him. He is an honourable gentleman, no doubt.”
“You will grow to like him even more, once you get to know him,” Elinor said. “You will be very near neighbours, after all—the parsonage is barely a stone’s throw from the big house at Delaford.”
Edward did not answer. He looked away from her, his expression dark, quiet, almost sad, as if he was already dreading the thought of living so close to a man he was certain was his rival for Elinor’s heart.
“I know the Colonel lodges on St. James Street,” he said, standing up abruptly. Elinor told him the number. “I must go to him at once, to thank him. He has made me the happiest man alive.”

Elinor did not try to stop him. They parted, her voice soft as she told him she would always wish him every possible happiness, and he stammered out a half-formed reply that could not quite capture all the feeling he was holding back.
As the door clicked shut behind him, Elinor sank back into her chair, breathing out slow. When I see him again, she thought to herself, he will be Lucy’s husband. The thought ached, sharp and quiet, but there was no use fighting it.

When Mrs. Jennings came home later that afternoon, she was so bursting with curiosity about her secret that she barely even mentioned the new people she’d gone to call on. She pounced on Elinor the second she walked through the door.
“Well, my dear! I sent that young Mr. Ferrars up to you, didn’t I do a splendid job? You didn’t have any trouble popping the question to the Colonel, did you? He didn’t drag his feet, I hope?”
“Nothing of the sort happened, no,” Elinor said, biting back a laugh.
“Good! How soon will the wedding be? I thought you said he needs to get ordained first?”
Elinor blinked. “Oh, no, that’s for Edward. It takes two or three months, usually, to finish ordination, as far as I know.”
“Two or three months?” Mrs. Jennings squawked. “Good heavens, my dear! The Colonel would never wait that long! What a waste of time! Surely you could find someone else who’s already ordained to marry you lot, no need to hang about for poor Mr. Ferrars!”
“Ma’am,” Elinor said, bewildered, before the full force of the misunderstanding hit her. “Colonel Brandon is not marrying me. He is giving the living to Edward Ferrars, so that Edward will have an income to marry Lucy Steele.”

For a second Mrs. Jennings just stared, then she burst out laughing so hard she had to sit down, clutching her sides. She’d been completely under the wrong impression the whole time! Instead of being disappointed, though, she was even more delighted.
“Oh! Oh my goodness, I see now! I’ve made a perfect fool of myself! Well, that parsonage down at Delaford, it’s got five rooms on the ground floor, fifteen beds total, you know! Far nicer than your little cottage at Barton! We’ll nag the Colonel to fix it all up nice and cosy for Edward and Lucy before they move in, you’ll see. I promise you, I’ll be down there visiting them before Michaelmas, and Lucy will be right there in that parsonage, settled happy as can be. You mark my words.”
Elinor smiled, a soft, quiet thing. She thought, deep down, that Edward and Lucy would not need to wait two or three months at all. Mrs. Jennings was almost certainly right.
