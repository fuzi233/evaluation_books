Mr. Gumbo's generous and compassionate conduct succeeded in soothing the angry heart of his master. Consequently, Harry's second night in the spunging-house was passed with considerably less bitterness than the first. There was, at last, someone there to assist and sympathize with him. Yet, despite this softening in one specific corner of his soul, Harry's heart remained hard and proud toward nearly everyone else in the world. He viewed them all as selfish and ungenerous. His pious Aunt Warrington, his lordly friend March, and his cynical cousin Castlewood—all had been tested and found wanting. He would not stoop to ask another favor of any of them merely to avoid twenty years of prison. What a fool he had been to believe their promises and confide in their friendship! There was no friendship in this cursed, cold, selfish country. He resolved to leave it. He would trust no Englishman, great or small.

His options were clear: he might go to Germany to make a campaign with the King, or return home to Virginia. There, he could bury himself in the woods, spend his days hunting, become his mother's factor and land-steward, and marry either Polly Broadbent or Fanny Mountain. He could turn into a regular tobacco-grower and farmer. He would do anything rather than remain among these English "fine gentlemen."

So, he rose with an outwardly cheerful countenance but an angry spirit. Early in the morning, the faithful Gumbo attended to his master's chamber, having traveled from Bond Street to retrieve Mr. Harry's correspondence.

"I wanted to bring some more clothes," honest Gumbo said, "but Mr. Ruff, the landlord, refused to let me take any more."

Harry had no desire to examine the letters immediately. He opened one, then two, then three; they were all bills. When he opened a fourth, it was from the landlord, stating that no further items belonging to Mr. Warrington could leave the house. Unless his bill was paid, the landlord would sell Mr. W.'s goods to settle the debt and evict his black servant, forcing him to sleep elsewhere. Harry hesitated to let Gumbo remove his own clothes and portmanteau. However, the black man reported finding refuge elsewhere—among friends at Lord Wrotham's house.

"With Colonel Lambert's people," Gumbo observed, looking intently at his master. "And Miss Hetty fell into a faint when she heard you were taken up. Mr. Lambert is a very good man; he told me this morning, 'Gumbo, tell your master if he wants me, send to me, and I shall come.'"

Harry was touched to learn that Hetty had been distressed by his misfortune. Though he did not fully believe Gumbo's account of her fainting—he was accustomed to translating his black man's speech while allowing for exaggeration—the mention of the Colonel darkened the young Virginian's spirit anew.

*"I am to send to Lambert,"* Harry thought, grinding his teeth. *"The man who insulted me and threw my presents back in my face! Even if I were starving, I would not ask him for a crust!"*

Shortly thereafter, dressed and ready, Mr. Warrington requested breakfast and dispatched Gumbo with a brief note to Mr. Draper in the Temple, demanding the attorney's immediate attendance.

"The note was as haughty as if he were writing to one of his negroes, not to a freeborn English gentleman," Draper remarked later. Indeed, Harry had always treated the lawyer with insufferable condescension. "It's all very well for a fine gentleman to give himself airs, but for a fellow in a spunging-house! Hang him!" Draper muttered, adding, "I've a great mind not to go!"

Nevertheless, Mr. Draper did go. He found Mr. Warrington in his misfortune even more arrogant than during the days of his utmost prosperity. Mr. W. sat on his bed like a lord, wearing a splendid gown with his hair meticulously dressed. He motioned for Gumbo to fetch a chair for the lawyer.

"Excuse me, madam, but such haughtiness and airs I am not accustomed to!" exclaimed the outraged attorney.

"Take a chair and proceed with your story, my good Mr. Draper!" replied Madame de Bernstein, smiling as she listened to the proceedings. She was amused by the lawyer's anger and liked her nephew for being insolent in adversity.

The course Draper was to follow had been arranged the previous day between the Baroness and her man of business. Draper was generally an able man, likely to serve a client well, but he failed in this instance because he was piqued and angry—or, more likely, because he could not comprehend the gentleman with whom he had to deal.

*I presume that whoever reads these pages is the most gentle of readers.* *Gentleman, as you unquestionably are, have you not remarked in your dealings with those who are no gentlemen that you often offend them without knowing how or why?* *So the man who is no gentleman offends you in a thousand ways of which the poor creature has no idea. He does or says something that provokes your scorn. He perceives that scorn (always watching himself, uneasy about his manners) and rages. You speak to him naturally, and he fancies you are sneering. You hold indifference toward him, but he hates you, and hates you the worse because you do not care.*

"Gumbo, a chair for Mr. Draper!" commanded Mr. Warrington, folding his brocaded dressing-gown around his legs as he sat on the dingy bed. "Sit down, if you please, and let us discuss my business. Much obliged to you for coming so soon in reply to my message. Have you heard of this piece of ill-luck?"

Draper knew of the circumstances. "Bad news travels quick, Mr. Warrington," he said. "I was eager to offer my humble services the moment you required them. Your friends and family will be deeply pained that a gentleman of your rank should be in such a position."

"I have been very imprudent, Mr. Draper. I have lived beyond my means." (Mr. Draper bowed politely.) "I played with gentlemen much richer than myself, and a cursed run of bad luck has carried away all my ready money, leaving me with liabilities exceeding five hundred pounds."

"Five hundred now in the office," noted Mr. Draper. "Well, this is such a trifling sum that I thought, by sending to one or two friends yesterday, I could have paid the debt and gone home without further ado. I have been mistaken. I shall thank you to kindly put me in the way of raising the money as soon as possible."

Mr. Draper sighed gravely and pulled a long face. "Why, sir, it can be done!" said Mr. Warrington, staring at the lawyer.

Indeed, it could be done. Mr. Draper had proposed to Madame Bernstein the day prior to instantly paying the money and releasing Mr. Warrington. That lady had declared her intention to make the young gentleman her heir. Like the rest of the world, Draper believed Harry's hereditary property in Virginia to be vast both in value and extent. He had notes in his pocket and Madame Bernstein's order to pay them under certain conditions. Nevertheless, when Harry simply stated, "It can be done!" Draper pulled his long face again and said, "It can be done in time, sir; but it will require a considerable period. To touch the property in England which belongs to you upon Mr. George Warrington's death, we must prove the event and release the trustees. Who is to do either? Lady Esmond Warrington in Virginia, of course, will not allow her son to remain in prison, but we must wait six months before hearing from her. Does your Bristol agent have authority to honor your drafts?"

"He is only authorized to pay me two hundred pounds a year," replied Mr. Warrington.

"I suppose I have no resource, then, but to apply to my aunt, Madame de Bernstein. She will be my security."

"Her ladyship will do anything for you, sir; she has said so to me, often and often," the lawyer assured him. "If she gives the word at this very moment, you can walk out of this place."

"Then go to her, Mr. Draper. I did not wish to trouble my relations, but rather than continue in this horrible, needless imprisonment, I must speak to her. Tell her where I am and what has befallen me. Disguise nothing! And tell her that I confide in her affection and kindness to release me from this... this disgrace," Mr. Warrington's voice shaking slightly as he passed his hand across his eyes.

"Sir," said Mr. Draper, eyeing the young man, "I was with her ladyship yesterday when we discussed the whole of this most unpleasant—I won't say, as you do, disgraceful business."

"What do you mean, sir? Does Madame de Bernstein know of my misfortune?" asked Harry.

"Every circumstance, sir; the pawning of the watches and all," the lawyer confirmed.

Harry turned burning red. "It is an unfortunate business, the pawning of those watches and things which you had never paid for," continued the lawyer.
