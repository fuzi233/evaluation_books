OF TWO GENTLEMEN OF WALES, AND HOW THEY HUNTED WITH THE HOUNDS, AND YET RAN WITH THE DEER

“I know that Deformed; he has been a vile thief this seven years; he goes up and down like a gentleman: I remember his name.”—*Much Ado About Nothing.*

Amyas slept that night a restless and troubled sleep. His mother and Frank, watching over him, could see the storm of dreams behind his closed eyes. And no wonder. The day had been full of excitement, but it was the memory of John Oxenham—the lost sailor, the vanished adventurer—that clung to his thoughts like a shadow. All evening, seated in the bay-windowed room where he had last seen him, Amyas replayed every glance, every gesture, in his mind. He found himself wondering why he should care so deeply for a man he barely knew, until sleep came—and then the dream began.

He found himself sailing westward, chasing a tiny sail on the horizon—Oxenham’s ship. A terrible dread gripped him: if he did not catch up in time, something dreadful would happen. But the ship refused to move. Around her floated thick beds of sargasso weed, their long, snaking tendrils wrapping around the bow, dragging her down. He struggled, imagining speed, urging the sails forward—but nothing changed. The sun sank, and darkness swallowed the sea. Then the moon rose, cold and bright, and suddenly, Oxenham’s vessel loomed beside him.

Her sails were torn and flapping. Pitch oozed from her timbers. Her railings rotted away. And along the mainyard—dangling in a ghastly row—were dark figures, hanged men. From the yard-arm above, John Oxenham’s corpse stared down with grave, lifeless eyes. It beckoned, pointed—not ahead, but back—toward the course they had come. And when Amyas turned, there it was: the snow-capped peaks of the Andes glittering under the moonlight. He was in the South Seas again, America stretching between him and home.

The corpse still pointed, its lips trembling as though trying to speak, its eyes filled with agony and warning. And then Amyas woke, gasping, shouting into the silence. He lay in his little cove chamber at Burrough, the gray light of autumn morning already creeping through the window.

Feverish and wide-awake, he could not return to sleep. After an hour of tossing, he rose, dressed, and set off for his favorite pebble ridge, where the sea had built a natural breakwater of smooth boulders, two miles long, shaped by time like a work of human hands. There, beneath the crisp salt air, he stripped and plunged into the waves, diving deep, rolling in the foam, swimming with the strength of youth—until a voice called out from shore.

Looking up, he saw Eustace Leigh standing atop the ridge.

Amyas felt a pang of disappointment. He had been thinking only of Rose Salterne, dreaming of her all the way there. He didn’t want company now. Yet, not having seen his cousin in three years, he gave in to manners and scrambled ashore, dressing quickly while Eustace paced the grassy turf beyond.

Eustace was the son of Thomas Leigh, younger brother of Sir Richard’s family, who had severed ties with both kin and country by remaining a Catholic. Though born a Papist, he had once followed the Reformation under Edward VI, then returned to Rome under Mary. But when Elizabeth came to power, he refused to change again—honorable, perhaps, but stubborn. He lived quietly at Chapel House, perched on the Atlantic cliffs near Moorwinstow, far from the eye of the law. The penal statutes never touched him. For one thing, he never plotted rebellion—only practiced faith. Jesuits came and went without notice, celebrating mass in secret chambers, hiding in turrets, pretending martyrdom for sport. When the local parson complained, Sir Richard Grenville merely scolded him for being a “fantastical Puritan” and told him to mind his own business. So the priest, though offended, kept quiet—especially since Mr. Leigh paid his tithes regularly.

But the weight of conscience pressed heavily upon Thomas Leigh. He held lands once belonging to Hartland Abbey, and though he half-believed he was damned for keeping them, he couldn’t bring himself to give them up. Like many Catholics of the time, he compromised: he kept the wealth, secretly supported foreign priests, and sent his children abroad—some to be trained as seminary priests—to serve as sacrifices, offerings to God for his sins. One such child was Eustace.

And now, Eustace had returned from Rheims, where he had been taught not just theology, but the art of persuasion, secrecy, and influence. He was tall and fair, with a high forehead and small mouth, his face always striving to appear cheerful, even when it wasn’t. He smiled often—too often—because he believed joy was a duty. But his smile was stiff, forced, like a mask worn too long. Had he not been handsome, many might have thought him a skeleton dancing in chains.

He had come back in obedience to a royal proclamation requiring all Englishmen abroad to return within four months. He had arrived in Bideford the night before, on private business for the Catholic cause. Now he sat beside Amyas on the pebbles, studying him carefully from the corner of his eyes.

Amyas turned fully toward him, grinning broadly, and offered a firm handshake. Eustace took it eagerly, squeezing hard, his fingers moving violently as if trying to prove his joy. Their greetings were worlds apart.

“Hold on, old lad,” said Amyas, laughing. “You’ll break my arm. Where’ve you been?”

“Going to and fro in the earth, walking up and down in it,” replied Eustace with a faint, knowing smile.

“Like the devil, eh? Well, everyone has their model. How’s Uncle?”

And here, dear reader, lies the truth: Eustace feared Amyas more than any man. Not because he was stronger—though he was—but because Amyas carried himself with a calm, unshakable confidence. Eustace felt himself judged, despised even. They hadn’t met in three years, but Amyas had always treated him as if his beliefs were nonsense—“wind and moonshine,” he’d called them. Eustace knew it. He also knew Amyas was wrong. “He used to undervalue me,” he thought. “Let’s see if he thinks so now.”

And then, in secret, he prayed for mercy—for forgetting himself, for remembering that God’s glory, not his own pride, was the goal.

But let us not dwell too long on souls. Let me simply say this: Eustace was a man trying desperately to be good, following rules, measuring his steps, checking his heart like a weak swimmer testing his muscles. Amyas was different—he did what was right without thinking, guided not by doctrine but by instinct, as a child follows love.

And yet, Eustace was not evil. Only misled. His path had been shaped by Jesuit training, which twisted noble hearts into instruments of intrigue. Had he grown up differently, he might have stood beside his brothers—true Englishmen, loyal to queen and country—as one did in Ireland under St. Leger, or on the Crimean fields. But fate had chosen otherwise.

“Ah, my dearest cousin!” said Eustace. “I was so disappointed to arrive just a day too late to see your triumph! But I came straight to your home, learned from your mother you’d be here, and hurried down to greet you.”

“Well, old lad,” said Amyas, “it feels very natural to see you. I often thought of you pacing the deck at night. How is Uncle? Are the girls well? Is the old pony dead yet? And Dick the smith—how’s Nancy? Grown into a fine girl, I bet. Good grief, it seems like half a lifetime since I left.”

“And you truly thought of your poor cousin?” asked Eustace softly. “Know that I, too, prayed nightly for your safety—surely not without effect—before the saints, whose intercessions, I doubt not, preserved you.”

“Halt there, coz,” said Amyas. “If they’re half as good as we think, they’ll help me without asking.”

“They have helped you, Amyas.”

“Maybe. I’d have done the same for them, had I been in their place.”

“And do you not feel gratitude, then—especially to her, the star of the sea, the compassionate guide of sailors?”

“Humph,” said Amyas. “Here’s Frank. Let him answer.”

At that moment, Frank arrived. After greetings, he sat beside them.

“I say, brother,” said Amyas, “Eustace is already trying to convert me. Says I owe all my luck to the Blessed Virgin.”

“It may be,” said Frank, “but you owe it to the prayers of the purest virgin ever known—the one who commanded your voyage. Her sweet incense has risen daily for you, and for her sake, you were spared from flood and foe, so you might spread the fame of Elizabeth, our queen, champion of truth, justice, and freedom.”

Amyas chuckled—a loyal, hearty sound. Eustace smiled meekly, but replied with quiet venom:

“I am certain I speak the truth when I call my patroness a virgin undefiled.”

Both brothers frowned. Amyas, lying on his back among the stones, looked up at the gulls overhead.

“I wonder what the Frenchman I cut off at the Azores thinks now about all that.”

“Cut off a Frenchman’s head?” asked Frank.

“Yes, faith. I fleshed my sword there. Let me tell you. We were in a tavern—George Drake and I—and there sat this Frenchman, sword on the table, ready for a fight. Later I learned he was a notorious bully. He started shouting insults—about this, about that. But then, stirred by the devil, he launched into a dozen slanders against her majesty’s honor, one after another. I was ashamed to hear them, and ashamed to repeat them.”

“I’ve heard enough of such,” said Frank. “They come mostly from lewd rogues around the French ambassador—men bred in the corruption of the Medici court, where Queen Mary of Scots was schooled. To them, virtue is just a cover for vice. Let them bark. *Honi soit qui mal y pense* is our motto—and shall be forever.”

“But I didn’t let him bark,” said Amyas. “I grabbed him by the ears and dragged him out. He drew his sword. I drew mine. I nearly died that day. He didn’t fight like a Christian—no edge and buckler—but with some newfangled French trick—stabbing, lunging, stamping, tracing at me. I expected to be full of holes before I could close with him.”

“Thank God you’re safe,” said Frank. “I know that style. Dangerous.”

“Of course you do. I didn’t.”

“Well, I’ll teach you, lad—better than Rowland Yorke himself.”

“Rowland Yorke? Who’s he?”

“A reckless fellow in London making a fortune teaching fencing. As likely to die in a brawl as your Frenchman. But how did you escape?”

“He pierced my left arm before I could turn. That made me mad. I leapt on him, caught his wrist, and landed a clean blow. By luck, his head fell onto the table. End of slander.”

“So perish all her enemies!” said Frank.

Eustace, who had pretended not to listen, rose abruptly.

“I trust you do not count me among them?”

“As you speak, I do, coz,” said Frank. “But for your own sake, believe the true reports—those who live under her rule know her virtue as they know the sun rises. Don’t trust the lies of cowardly spies seeking favor with the Guises. Come, let’s say no more. Walk with us to Appledore, then home to breakfast.”

But Eustace declined. He had urgent business in Northam, then Bideford. So he left them to linger on the beach, then walk across the smooth green plain to the little fishing village of Appledore, where the Torridge and Taw rivers meet.

Yet Eustace lied. He told them he was going to Northam—but his real destination was Appledore. After passing the nearest house in Northam, he veered sharply left across the fields, murmuring prayers to the Virgin all the way. He traveled several miles out of his way—just as crooked spirits often do, especially Jesuits, who, history shows, rarely outwit themselves.

Meanwhile, Amyas and Frank walked straight across the turf, arriving at Appledore just in time to see what Eustace had gone to great lengths to hide: four horses standing outside the “Mariner’s Rest” Inn, saddled and ready. Three men were mounting—Eustace among them, and two strangers.

“There’s one lie already this morning,” growled Amyas. “He said he was going to Northam.”

“And we don’t know he hasn’t been,” Frank replied gently.

“You’re as bad a Jesuit as he is, helping him out.”

“He may have changed his mind.”

“Bless your pure imagination, my sweet boy,” said Amyas, mimicking his mother’s tone, laying a hand on Frank’s head. “Let’s step into this shop and buy a pennyworth of whipcord.”

“What do you want with whipcord?”

“To spin my top, of course.”

“Top? How long have you had a top?”

“I’ll buy one. Save my conscience. But I must see the end of this.”

So he pulled Frank into the shop, unseen by the group at the inn.

“What strange creatures has he brought now?” whispered Amyas. “Look at that three-legged fellow trying to mount—clawing the horse’s ribs like a cat scratching a tree!”

The man was tall, meek-faced, draped in flamboyant clothes, a huge feather in his hat, and carrying a sword so long and broad it seemed ill-suited to his frame.

“Young David in Saul’s armor,” said Frank. “He’d better not go in them—he hasn’t proven them.”

“Look—his third leg’s turning into a tail! Why doesn’t someone pull in half a yard of his belt?”

And thus they watched, amused and wary, as the shadows of secrets unfolded beneath the autumn sky.
