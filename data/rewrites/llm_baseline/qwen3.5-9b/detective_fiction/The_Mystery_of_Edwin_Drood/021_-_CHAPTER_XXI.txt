Nothing stirred in the night to disturb the weary bird, and when dawn broke, the dove rose renewed in spirit. At ten o'clock, as the clock chimed, Mr. Crisparkle arrived, bursting into the room as if he had leaped straight from the River Thames at Cloisterham.

"Miss Twinkleton was so distressed, Miss Rosa," he explained to her, "that she rushed around to my wife and me with your note, in such a state of bewilderment that I felt compelled to volunteer for this errand on the very first train of the morning. I initially wished for you to come to me; however, I now believe it was best that you remained with your guardian."

"I did consider coming to you," Rosa replied, "but Minor Canon Corner was so close to him—"

"I understand perfectly," Mr. Crisparkle assured her. "It was entirely natural."

"I have informed Mr. Crisparkle," Mr. Grewgious interjected gently, "of everything you shared with me last night, my dear. Naturally, I intended to write to him immediately, but his arrival proved most timely. Indeed, it was exceptionally kind of him to come, considering he had just departed moments before."

"Have you decided," Rosa asked, turning to both men with an appeal in her eyes, "what steps we must take regarding Helena and her brother?"

"Why, truly," Mr. Crisparkle admitted, rubbing his chin, "I find myself in considerable perplexity. Even Mr. Grewgious, whose intellect stretches far beyond my own and who thinks a full night ahead of me, remains undecided. What hope does a man like me have?"

Just then, the Unlimited poked her head through the doorway, having knocked and received permission to enter. "A gentleman requests a private word with Mr. Crisparkle," she announced. "If such a gentleman is present, he wishes to speak with him. If not, he begs forgiveness for his mistake."

"Such a gentleman is here," Mr. Crisparkle confirmed, "though he is currently occupied."

"Is he a dark gentleman?" Rosa asked, stepping back toward her guardian.

"No, Miss," the servant replied. "More of a brown complexion."

"You are certain he does not have black hair?" Rosa ventured, gathering courage.

"Absolutely positive, Miss. Brown hair and blue eyes."

"Perhaps," Mr. Grewgious suggested with his customary caution, "it would be wise to admit him, Reverend Sir, if you do not object. When one faces a dilemma or feels lost, one rarely knows which path leads to resolution. My business principle is never to shut off any avenue, but rather to observe every possible direction until clarity emerges. I could recount a relevant anecdote, but the timing seems premature."

"If Miss Rosa permits it, then? Please, let the gentleman enter," Mr. Crisparkle said.

The stranger entered, offering a frank yet modest apology for not finding Mr. Crisparkle alone. He turned to Mr. Crisparkle and, with a warm smile, posed the unexpected query: "Who am I?"

"You are the gentleman I observed smoking beneath the trees at Staple Inn just moments ago."

"That is correct. I saw you there. But who else am I?"

Mr. Crisparkle focused intently on the handsome, sun-browned face before him. A faint, ghostly image of a departed youth seemed to materialize slowly and dimly within the room. The visitor noticed the flicker of memory lighting up the Minor Canon's features. Smiling again, he asked, "What shall we have for breakfast this morning? You appear to be out of jam."

"Wait!" cried Mr. Crisparkle, raising his right hand. "Grant me a single instant! Tartar!"

The two men embraced with profound warmth, then went to lengths rare among Englishmen: placing their hands on each other's shoulders and gazing joyfully into one another's eyes.

"My old fag!" Mr. Crisparkle exclaimed.

"My old master!" Mr. Tartar responded.

"You saved me from drowning!" Mr. Crisparkle declared.

"After which you took to swimming yourself, you know!" Mr. Tartar quipped.

"Good heavens!" Mr. Crisparkle gasped.

"Amen!" Mr. Tartar echoed.

They resumed their vigorous handshake.

"Imagine," Mr. Crisparkle cried, his eyes shining, "Miss Rosa Bud and Mr. Grewgious, imagine Mr. Tartar, when he was merely a junior, diving for me, seizing me—a large, heavy senior—by the hair of my head, and hauling me toward the shore like a water-giant!"

"I certainly did not let him sink, as I was his fag!" Mr. Tartar insisted. "Yet the truth is, he became my finest protector and friend, doing me more good than all my teachers combined. An irrational impulse seized me to either lift him up or drown with him."

"Hem! Allow me, sir, to offer my congratulations," Mr. Grewgious stepped forward, extending his hand. "I truly value this honor. I trust you did not catch a chill. I hope you were not inconvenienced by swallowing too much water. How have you been since then?"

It was unclear whether Mr. Grewgious actually knew what he was saying, though his intent was undeniably friendly and appreciative. *If only Heaven had bestowed such courage and skill upon my poor mother,* Rosa thought. *And he was so slight and young back then!*

"I do not wish to be praised for it, thank you," Mr. Tartar said. "However, I believe I have an idea."

Mr. Grewgious announced, after pacing a few unpredictable steps across the room—an action so erratic that everyone stared, unsure if he was choking or suffering a cramp—that he believed he had seen Mr. Tartar listed as a tenant in the top set of the house adjacent to the corner building.

"Yes, sir," Mr. Tartar confirmed. "That is accurate."

"I am correct so far," Mr. Grewgious noted, ticking the point off mentally with his thumb against his palm. "Do you happen to know the name of your neighbor in the top set on the opposite side of the party wall?" He leaned in closely, ensuring he missed nothing of the other man's expression, despite his short-sightedness.

"Landless."

"Another tick," Mr. Grewgious said, resuming his peculiar trot before returning to center stage. "Do you possess any personal knowledge of him?"

"Slightly, yes."

"Another tick," Mr. Grewgious continued, repeating his dance. "What is the nature of this knowledge, Mr. Tartar?"

"I suspected he was a young man in difficult straits. Only a day or two ago, I asked his permission to share my flowers upstairs with him; essentially, to extend my flower garden to his windows."

"Will you kindly take a seat?" Mr. Grewgious requested. "For I *do* have an idea!"

They complied willingly; Mr. Tartar especially, as he seemed somewhat disoriented. Seated in the middle with his hands resting on his knees, Mr. Grewgious delivered his theory with the memorized cadence characteristic of him.

"I cannot yet determine whether it is prudent to maintain open communication under current circumstances between the fair lady present and either Mr. Neville or Miss Helena. I have cause to believe that a local associate of ours—one upon whom I bestow a passing yet heartfelt curse, with the kind permission of my reverend friend—moves stealthily about, darting up and down corridors. When he is not active himself, he likely employs an informant skulking nearby, perhaps a watchman, porter, or similar hanger-on of Staple. Conversely, Miss Rosa naturally desires to see her friend Miss Helena, making it vital that Miss Helena (or her brother, through her) learns privately from Miss Rosa exactly what has transpired and what threats loom. Do I align with general consensus on these views?"

"I entirely agree," Mr. Crisparkle affirmed, having listened attentively.

"As I surely would," Mr. Tartar added with a smile, "were I to comprehend them fully."

"Proceed cautiously, sir," Mr. Grewgious advised. "We shall place our full confidence in you shortly, provided you grant us your permission. Now, if our local friend possesses an informant on the ground, it becomes reasonably clear that such a watcher can only monitor the chambers occupied by Mr. Neville. Should this informant report to our local friend, who moves freely in and out, the latter would deduce the identities of the parties based on prior knowledge. No one can possibly guard all of Staple or track comings and goings to other sets of chambers: unless, indeed, it is mine."

"I begin to grasp your intention," Mr. Crisparkle said, "and I highly approve of your caution."

"I need not repeat that I know nothing yet of the 'why' or 'wherefore,'" Mr. Tartar clarified. "Nevertheless, I understand your aim. Therefore, let me state unequivocally that my chambers are freely at your disposal."

"There!" Mr. Grewgious cried, smoothing his head triumphantly. "Now we all possess the plan. Do you have it, my dear?"

"I believe I do," Rosa answered, blushing slightly as Mr. Tartar glanced quickly in her direction.
