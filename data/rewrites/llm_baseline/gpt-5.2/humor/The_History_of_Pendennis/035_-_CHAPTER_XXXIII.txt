Captain Shandon, being for the present lodged at Her Majesty’s expense within those brick bowels near Ludgate Hill where the air tastes faintly of wet rope and yesterday’s porter, announced—through a prospectus written with such a crack of the whip in every line—that the hour had struck for the Gentlemen of England to shoulder their rights like ancestral hams and defend the whole pantry of the Constitution from hungry new doctrines.

Foreign revolutions, he declared, were boiling up like a foreign soup of suspicious colour. Intestine radicalism (a phrase with a medical sound, as if the nation had eaten unripe fruit) was grumbling below decks. Mill-owners and cotton-lords were slandering the aristocratic roast, and the masses—those same masses whom, with a perfectly straight face, he accused the Gentlemen of having “gulled and led”—were, in his picture, a kind of perpetual, ill-trained dinner-party that would overturn the table the moment the footman looked away.

“The ancient monarchy,” he wrote and now read aloud with a theatrical toss of paper, “is insulted by a ferocious republican rabble. The Church is deserted by envious dissent, undermined by stealthy infidelity. The good institutions which made our country glorious, and the name of English Gentleman the proudest in the world, are left without defence—”

And here the Captain, as if carving a joint, laid down the knife-edge of one clause and took up the next with a flourish.

“It was because the kings of France mistrusted their gentlemen,” Mr. Shandon went on, “that the monarchy of Saint Louis went down: it was because the people of England still believed in their gentlemen that this country encountered and overcame the greatest enemy a nation ever met: it was because we were headed by gentlemen that the Eagles retreated before us from the Donro to the Garonne: it was a gentleman who broke the line at Trafalgar, and swept the plain of Waterloo.”

Mr. Bungay, the publisher—who sat planted like a dumpling in a chair too small for him, his stick upright between his knees—nodded in the knowing rhythm of a man who hears “Waterloo” as other men hear “pudding.” He even winked at that name, as if Wellington were a private joke between tradesmen. Mr. Warrington, who had been leaning back with that air of lazy judgment he cultivated as carefully as a moustache, burst out laughing.

“You see how our venerable friend Bungay is affected,” Shandon said, looking up slyly from his sheet. “That’s your true sort of test. I have used the Duke of Wellington and the battle of Waterloo a hundred times, and I never knew the Duke to fail.”

He proceeded—modest as a man can be while declaring war upon half the kingdom—to confess that gentlemen had hitherto left their political interests, like their estates and lawsuits, to paid persons; and that the day for such delegation was over. Gentlemen must become their own champions; their enemies were brave, numerous, uncompromising; and, above all, they must not be represented in the press by hireling advocates.

“They must not have Grub Street publishing Gazettes from Whitehall—” he cried, with a side glance. “That’s a dig at Bacon’s people, Mr. Bungay.”

Bungay clapped his stick on the floor as if it were a mallet bringing down the price of pork. “Hang him—pitch into him, Capting!” he said, beaming; and then, turning his heavy head to Warrington, wagged it with solemn relish. “For a slashing article, sir, there’s nobody like the Capting—no-obody like him.”

Some gentlemen, Shandon announced next—names withheld for reasons too “obvious” to be printed—had determined to bring out a journal of impregnable principles.

At “obvious reasons,” Warrington began to laugh again; and the Captain, enjoying the laugh as a cook enjoys the hiss when his pan hits the fire, flourished his paper.

“These men are proud of their order, anxious to uphold it. They are loyal to their Sovereign by faithful conviction and ancestral allegiance; they love their Church, where they would have their children worship, and for which their forefathers bled; they love their country, and would keep it what the gentlemen of England—yes, the gentlemen of England,” he insisted, turning upon Bungay with the familiarity of a man ordering a compositor, “we’ll have that in large caps, Bungay, my boy—have made it—the greatest and freest in the world: and as the names of some of them are appended to the deed which secured our liberties at Runnymede—”

“What’s that?” asked Bungay, who could smell printer’s ink at a mile but would have mistaken Magna Charta for a new brand of cheese.

“An ancestor of mine sealed it with his sword-hilt,” Pen said gravely, while the little girl—Mary Shandon—stood staring at these men as if they were all performing tricks.

“It’s the Habeas Corpus, Mr. Bungay,” Warrington said.

“All right, I dare say,” Bungay yawned, and, as if history were a long sermon, added: “Go on, Capting.”

“—at Runnymede; they are ready to defend that freedom to-day with sword and pen, and now, as then, to rally round the old laws and liberties of England.”

“Bravo!” cried Warrington.

The child, not understanding England or Runnymede, understood hands and faces. Warrington beckoned her with a large paw of a hand and patted her curls; but she shrank as if his touch were too rough a towel. She sidled away, climbed up on Pen’s knee, and began to play gravely with his watch-chain, as if its cool links had more sense than all this patriotic thunder. Pen—who had a soft heart that he tried to cover with a shy pompousness, like a tender dish concealed beneath a too-loud garnish—was pleased by her preference.

“You were laughing,” Shandon said to Warrington, enjoying his own phrase again. “About the ‘obvious reasons.’ Now I’ll show ye what they are, ye unbelieving heathen.”

And he read on, offering the audience a vision of invisible grandees and secret wires, of “influential friends in both Houses of the Senate,” allies in “every diplomatic circle in Europe,” and sources of intelligence so exquisitely confidential that no other journal—no other journal!—could possibly acquire them.

But while his words were all high corridors and velvet doors, the place they were spoken in had its own loud, uninvited commentary: the scrape of prison shoes in the passage; a distant cough; the clink of a tin mug; a hoarse laugh from some unseen room; a key turning somewhere with the finality of a guillotine. The very building kept time with his rhetoric, a kind of acoustic reminder that “gentlemen” here were measured by bolts and bars.

Nevertheless, Shandon did not miss a syllable of his music.

“The very earliest information connected with the movement of English and Continental politics,” he declaimed, “will be found only in the columns of the Pall Mall Gazette. The Statesman and the Capitalist, the Country Gentleman and the Divine, will be amongst our readers, because our writers are amongst them. We address ourselves to the higher circles of society: we care not to disown it—the Pall Mall Gazette is written by gentlemen for gentlemen; its conductors speak to the classes in which they live and were born. The field-preacher has his journal; the radical free-thinker has his journal: why should the Gentlemen of England be unrepresented in the Press?”

He then, with much modesty (the kind that manages to praise itself without seeming to look), sketched the literary and fashionable departments of the paper, to be conducted by gentlemen of acknowledged reputation—men famous at the Universities, known at the Clubs, and intimate with the very Society they would describe.

At “famous at the Universities,” Pen could not help laughing and blushing; the phrase struck him like a spoon tapped on a glass, calling attention to him in a room of strangers.

Shandon turned, as delicately as a cook suggesting sauce, to the advertisers: there would be no medium like the Pall Mall Gazette for giving publicity to their sales. He called upon the nobility, the baronetage, the revered clergy, the bar, the matrons, the daughters, the homes and hearths of England to rally. Bungay, who had fallen into a second snooze during these rolling appeals, woke at the conclusion and pronounced it all right.

Then came the practical talk—the politics, the management, the habits of the journal—where Bungay sat nodding, his approvals ready-made and indiscriminate. His opinions were plain as boiled potatoes. He wanted Bacon’s opposition house smashed; he believed Shandon could smash it. If the Captain had copied out the Church Catechism or written Junius on a sheet, Bungay would have smacked his lips at the “slashing article” all the same. He pocketed the papers with the satisfaction of a man securing a pie; and having paid for the manuscript, he called little Mary and gave her a penny as he went away—an action that sat oddly beside all the talk of ancient liberties, and yet somehow was the only coin in the room that looked like actual kindness.

With the prospectus read and pocketed, Shandon grew jaunty, putting on that second-hand fashionable air he wore in compliment to his guests, as if his tattered dressing-gown were merely an eccentric morning wrapper in St. James’s. He spoke of the great world with easy familiarity and jocular allusions: he told anecdotes of great personages, private sayings, entertainments at which this or that occurred; and Pen was amused to hear this shabby prisoner, in slippers, talking glibly of the great of the land as if he had them all in his pocket next to Bungay’s note.

Mrs. Shandon sat quietly at her work, her needle going in and out like a patient little clock. She listened with fond admiration. The great Society was the right place for her Charles; he shone in it; he was respected in it. Indeed, once, an Earl of X had asked him to dinner; and she treasured the invitation-card in her workbox still, as if it were a relic and not merely pasteboard.

At last Bungay had enough of fashionable talk and rose to take leave. Warrington and Pen rose too, to depart with him, though Pen would gladly have stayed: the poor little household, for all its imprisonment and tatters, interested and touched him. He murmured something about hoping for leave to repeat his visit.

Shandon, with a rueful grin that showed his teeth like a man pretending at cheer over cold victuals, said he was always to be found at home, and would be delighted to see Mr. Pennington.

“I’ll see you to my park-gate, gentlemen,” said Captain Shandon, clapping on his hat in spite of a deprecatory look and a faint “Charles” from his wife.

And so, in shabby slippers, he shuffled out before them, leading the way through dismal passages where the very echoes sounded poor. As he took leave of the three gentlemen at the wicket, his hand was already fiddling with the waistcoat pocket where Bungay’s five-pound note lay—warm, new, and doomed. Pen, when he found himself again on the flags of Farringdon Street, drew a freer breath, as if the air outside—though full of carts and street cries—were cleaner than those locked-in sounds behind him.

Mrs. Shandon, left at the window that looked into the court, went on with her work. She saw her husband with a couple of men at his heels run rapidly in the direction of the prison tavern. She had hoped to have had him to dinner. There was a piece of meat—poor fare, but honestly meant—and some salad in a basin on the ledge outside the window, where the cool air could keep it a little while: she had expected she and little Mary would share it with the child’s father.

But there was no chance now. The tavern would have him until closing; then there would be cards or drink in some other man’s room; then he would come back silent, glazed-eyed, reeling a little, to be nursed by the woman who had been waiting with the meat and the salad.

She went to the cupboard and, in lieu of dinner, made herself some tea.

If there is a domestic sound more sorrowful than the soft settling of tea-leaves under boiling water, it has not yet been invented. The prison had its coarse clangours—keys, bolts, footsteps—yet in their room the gentler noises were the ones that pierced: the lid of the teapot set down; the faint clink of cup to saucer; the little sigh of steam; and beyond, always beyond, the tavern’s blurred murmur, as if drink itself had a voice that called men away from the table where they were needed.

So she and little Mary drank their tea together, while the Captain went out and took his pleasure. She cared for nothing else but that, when her husband was away—nothing, that is, except the child’s quiet presence and the hot cup between her hands.

It was during this small, pale meal that Mr. Jack Finucane—already slightly known to us, and a townsman of Captain Shandon’s—came in. Jack always brought a sweetmeat for little Mary in his pocket; and he produced it now with the importance of a diplomat producing a treaty.

Jack thought Shandon the greatest genius on earth. He had had one or two helps from the good-natured prodigal—always a kind word, sometimes a guinea for a friend in need—and he never missed a day of seeing his patron. He ran errands, transacted money-business with publishers and newspaper editors, duns, creditors, holders of Shandon’s acceptances, gentlemen disposed to speculate in those securities; and in short was aide-de-camp to an embarrassed Irish gentleman, as naturally as a shadow follows a man in sunlight.

And as sure as there is an embarrassed Irish gentleman, there is a little troop behind him: subordinates of subordinates, all in pecuniary discomfort, all ready to march at the heels of their chief and share in the rough fortunes of his day.

“He won’t have that five-pound note very long, I bet a guinea,” Bungay had remarked as he walked away with the two young men. And Bungay judged rightly. When Mrs. Shandon came, as was her habit, to empty her husband’s pockets, she found but a couple of shillings and a few halfpence out of the morning’s remittance.

The note had melted like butter in a hot pan. A pound had gone to one follower; a leg of mutton with potatoes and beer had been sent to an acquaintance on the poor side of the prison—charity, yes, but charity with a loud thirst behind it; an outstanding tavern bill had been paid where the note was changed; there had been dinner with two friends there; and then cards, with half-crowns slipping away one by one as if they too were eager to get out of his pocket. So the night left him as poor as the morning found him.

Meanwhile, outside, the publisher and the two gentlemen talked as they walked. Warrington, in a mood of mischievous advocacy, reiterated to Bungay what he had already said to Bacon: that Pen was a high fellow of great genius, and what was more, well with the great world, and related to “no end” of the peerage. Bungay replied that he should be happy to have dealings with Mr. Pendennis, and hoped to see both gents cutting mutton with him before long; and with mutual politeness and protestations, they parted.

That night, Pen, thinking over the day as one thinks over a dish whose flavour lingers and troubles—salt and sweet together—said, “It is hard to see such a man as Shandon, of accomplishments so multifarious, and of such undoubted talent and humour, an inmate of a gaol for half his time, and a bookseller’s hanger-on when out of prison.”

“I am a bookseller’s hanger-on—you are going to try your paces as a hack,” Warrington said, laughing. “We are all hacks upon some road or other. I would rather be myself than Paley our neighbour in chambers, who has as much enjoyment of his life as a mole. A deuced deal of undeserved compassion has been thrown away upon what you call your bookseller’s drudge.”

“Much solitary pipes and ale make a cynic of you,” Pen answered. “You are a Diogenes by a beer-barrel, Warrington. No man shall tell me that a man of genius, as Shandon is, ought to be driven by such a vulgar slave-driver as yonder Mr. Bungay, who fattens on the profits of the other’s brains, and enriches himself out of his journeyman’s labour. It makes me indignant to see a gentleman the serf of such a creature—a man who can’t speak the language he lives by, who is not fit to black Shandon’s boots.”

“So you have begun already to gird at the publishers, and to take your side amongst our order. Bravo, Pen, my boy!” Warrington said, laughing still. “What have you got to say against Bungay’s relations with Shandon? Was it the publisher who sent the author to prison? Is it Bungay who is tippling away the five-pound note we saw just now—or Shandon?”

“Misfortune drives a man into bad company,” Pen said. “It is easy to cry ‘Fie!’ against a poor fellow who has no society but such as he finds in a prison; and no resource except forgetfulness and the bottle. We must deal kindly with the eccentricities of genius, and remember that the very ardour and enthusiasm of temperament which makes the author delightful often leads the man astray.”

“A fiddlestick about men of genius!” Warrington cried, suddenly severe, though severity sat on him like a borrowed coat. “I deny there are so many geniuses as people who whimper about the fate of men of letters assert. There are thousands of clever fellows who could, if they would, turn verses, write articles, read books, deliver judgments. The talk of professional critics and writers isn’t a whit more brilliant than that of any educated society. If a lawyer, soldier, or parson outruns his income and does not pay his bills, he must go to gaol; and an author must go too. If an author fuddles himself, I don’t know why he should be let off a headache the next morning; if he orders a coat from a tailor, why he shouldn’t pay for it.”

“I would give him more money to buy coats,” Pen said, smiling in spite of himself. “I suppose I should like to belong to a well-dressed profession. I protest against that wretch of a middle-man whom I see between Genius and his great landlord, the Public, and who stops more than half the labourer’s earnings and fame.”

“I am a prose labourer,” Warrington said. “You, my boy, are a poet in a small way, and so consider you are authorised to be flighty. What is it you want? A body of capitalists forced to purchase the works of all authors who present themselves, manuscript in hand? Everybody who writes his epic, every driveller who can or can’t spell and produces his novel or tragedy—are they all to find a bag of sovereigns in exchange for worthless reams of paper? Who is to settle what is good or bad, saleable or otherwise? Will you give the buyer leave, in fine, to purchase or not?

“Why, sir, when Johnson sate behind the screen at Saint John’s Gate and took his dinner apart because he was too shabby and poor to join the literary bigwigs round Mr. Cave’s best table-cloth, the tradesman was doing him no wrong. You couldn’t force the publisher to recognise genius in the young man who presented himself, ragged, gaunt, hungry. Rags are not proof of genius; whereas capital is absolute, as times go, and perforce the bargain-master. It has a right to deal with the literary inventor as with any other. If I produce a novelty in the book trade, I must do the best I can with it; but I can no more force Mr. Murray to purchase my book of travels or sermons than I can compel Mr. Tattersall to give me a hundred guineas for my horse. I may have my own ideas of the value of my Pegasus and think him the most wonderful of animals; but the dealer has a right to his opinion too, and may want a lady’s horse, or a cob for a heavy timid rider, or a sound hack for the road, and my beast won’t suit him.”

“You deal in metaphors, Warrington,” Pen said; “but you rightly say you are very prosaic. Poor Shandon! There is something about the kindness of that man, and the gentleness of that sweet creature of a wife, which touches me profoundly. I like him, I am afraid, better than a better man.”

“And so do I,” Warrington said. “Let us give him the benefit of our sympathy, and the pity that is due to his weakness: though I fear that sort of kindness would be resented as contempt by a more high-minded man. You see he takes his consolation along with his misfortune, and one generates the other or balances it, as the way of the world. He is a prisoner, but he is not unhappy.”

“His genius sings within his prison bars,” Pen said.

“Yes,” Warrington replied bitterly, “Shandon accommodates himself to a cage pretty well. He ought to be wretched, but he has Jack and Tom to drink with, and that consoles him. He might have a high place, but as he can’t, he can drink with Tom and Jack. He might be providing for his wife and children, but Thomas and John have got a bottle of brandy which they want him to taste. He might pay poor Snip the tailor the twenty pounds the poor devil wants for his landlord, but John and Thomas lay their hands upon his purse. And so he drinks whilst his tradesman goes to gaol and his family to ruin. Let us pity the misfortunes of genius—and conspire against the publishing tyrants who oppress men of letters.”

“What! are you going to have another glass of brandy-and-water?” Pen asked, with a humorous look; for this philosophy was taking place at the Black Kitchen, where hot things were brought in glasses and moral sentences cooled on the tongue.

Warrington began to laugh as usual. “Video meliora proboque—I mean, bring it me hot, with sugar, John,” he said to the waiter.

“I would have some more too, only I don’t want it,” Pen said. “It does not seem to me, Warrington, that we are much better than our neighbours.”

Warrington despatched his last glass; and the pair returned to their chambers.

Two notes waited in the letter-box, sent by their morning acquaintance, Mr. Bungay. That hospitable gentleman presented his compliments to each, and requested the pleasure of their company at dinner on an early day, to meet a few literary friends.

“We shall have a grand spread, Warrington,” Pen said. “We shall meet all Bungay’s corps.”

“All except poor Shandon,” Warrington answered.

Pen nodded good-night and went into his own little room. The day’s new faces and odd acquaintance had excited him. He lay awake thinking, while from the neighbouring apartment Warrington’s vigorous, regular snore declared that he had made his peace with the world in the simplest dialect known to man.
