In the rarefied world of diplomatic protocol, few individuals were as intricately entwined in the complexities of Anglo-Indian relations as Lady Clavering, a woman whose life was a testament to the intricate web of cultural exchange and economic interests that defined the era. Born to a wealthy Calcutta attorney, she had spent a brief stint in a London suburb, dubbed "Ackney" by her family, before being summoned to join her father in the Indian subcontinent at the tender age of fifteen. It was during this voyage, aboard the Ramchunder East Indiaman, that she crossed paths with her first husband, Mr. Amory, a third mate who would soon find himself embroiled in a series of mercantile misadventures.

A cursory examination of Lady Clavering's history reveals a trajectory marked by calculated risk-taking and strategic maneuvering. After a tumultuous marriage to Mr. Amory, who had worked his way out from the Cape before establishing himself as an indigo planter, editor, and ultimately, a failed entrepreneur, she found herself at the helm of a precarious financial situation. The erstwhile couple's relationship was marked by ceaseless bickering with her father and herself, culminating in a spectacular downfall that forced Mr. Amory to abandon his claims in Calcutta and seek refuge in New South Wales.

It was during this period of personal and professional upheaval that Lady Clavering likely encountered Sir Jasper Rogers, the respected Judge of the Supreme Court of Calcutta, who would later become a key player in the complex tapestry of relationships that defined her life. As the truth eventually came to light, it became clear that Mr. Amory's reliance on her father's literary skills, which he exploited for his own gain, had ultimately led to his downfall.
