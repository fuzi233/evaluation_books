Fra Colonna possessed unrestricted access to the Pope's library, and occasionally, he would cease his labors at the same hour Gerard did, choosing instead to walk the city with him. On these occasions, the fortunate artist perceived everything *en beau*, enveloped in the majestic splendor of Rome—its churches, palaces, and ancient ruins.

The friar, however, reserved his praise solely for the ruins, dousing the enthusiasm regarding the living city with a bucket of cold water.

"This place, Rome?" he declared. "It is merely the tomb of mighty Rome." He guided Gerard's gaze downward, pointing out that twenty or thirty feet of the old triumphal arches lay buried beneath the soil, while the modern thoroughfares ran directly over ancient palaces and the severed tops of columns. Coupling the relatively cramped boundaries of the contemporary city with the colossal remnants of antiquity that peeked sporadically above ground, he struck a striking comparison. "Listen to me," he said. "This village they call Rome is but as one of those swallow's nests you will see constructed upon the eaves of a decaying abbey."

"Old Rome must indeed have been magnificent then," Gerard remarked.

"Judge for yourself, my son," the friar replied. "You see the great sewer, a work of the Romans in their infancy, destined to outlast even Mount Vesuvius. You see the fragments of the Temple of Peace. Imagine how you would appear if you could behold the Capitol with its twenty-five temples! Observe this Monte Savello; what is it, if you please, but the ruins of the ancient Theatre of Marcellus? And as for Testaccio, one of the highest hills in modern Rome, it is nothing more than an ancient refuse heap; the women of old Rome discarded their broken pots and pans there, and lo—a mountain was formed."

*"Ex pede Herculem; ex ungue leonem"* ("From the foot judge the Hercules; from the claw, the lion").

Gerard listened with respectful attention, but when the holy friar proceeded by analogy to suggest that the moral superiority of the pagan Romans was proportional to their grandeur, Gerard resisted stoutly. "Has the world then suffered loss through Christ's coming?" he asked, though he immediately blushed, sensing he was reproaching his benefactor.

"Saints forbid!" exclaimed the friar. "'Twere heresy to say otherwise." Having made this direct concession, he gradually evaded the implication through subtle circumlocution until they reached the forbidden door via a spiral back staircase. Along the way, they encountered a church crowded with people in the porch, where a demon was being exorcised.

Fra Colonna had a peculiar habit of emitting a curious, low moan whenever facts that Zeno or Epicurus would reject were presented to him as truth. To those accustomed to hearing it, this moan conveyed not only strong dissent but also a profound pity for human credulity, ignorance, and error—especially when such blindness obscured the merits of paganism.

The friar sighed deeply and said, "Then let us depart."

"Nay, Father, pray stay! Pray stay! I have never seen a devil cast out."

The friar accompanied Gerard into the church, though he first gave a hearty shrug. Inside, they found the demoniac forced to his knees before the altar, a scarf tied tightly around his neck, by which the officiating priest held him fast like a dog on a leash.

Few people were present, for rumor had spread that the last demon expelled from that church had fled no further than into the lap of someone in the congregation: *"As a coney ferreted out of one burrow runs to the next."*

Upon their arrival, the priest seemed to deem the audience sufficient and commenced the ritual. Facing the demoniac, breviary in hand, he first sought to identify the entity possessing the man.

"Come out, Ashtaroth. Oh ho! Not you then. Come out, Belial. Come out, Tatzi. Come out, Eza. No; he trembles not. Come out, Azymoth. Come out, Feriander. Come out, Foletho. Come out, Astyma. Come out, Nebul. Aha! Have I found you? 'Tis thou, thou reptile, at thy old tricks. Let us pray!"

"Oh, Lord, we pray thee to drive the foul fiend Nebul out of this Thy creature: out of his hair, his eyes, his nose, his mouth, his ears, his gums, his teeth, his shoulders, his arms, his legs, his loins, his stomach, his bowels, his thighs, his knees, his calves, his feet, his ankles, his finger-nails, his toe-nails, and his soul. Amen."

The priest then rose, turned to the onlookers with quiet geniality, and announced, "Gentlemen, we have here as obstinate a devil as you might see in a summer day." Turning back to the patient, he addressed him with great severity, alternating between speaking to the man and the fiend. They answered in turn through the same mouth, now claiming hatred for the holy names the priest invoked, now complaining of internal misery.

It was the priest who first confused the victim and the culprit in the minds of the observers, assaulting the former physically—he cuffed him, kicked him, and spat repeatedly in his face. He then lit a candle, dimmed it, and burned it until it scorched his fingers, dropping it quickly thereafter. Next, he displayed the *Corpus Domini* to the patient. He lit another candle, handled it more cautiously, and finally spoke civilly to the demoniac in his human guise, dismissing him and accepting the congratulations of the assembly.

"Good father," Gerard observed, "you seem to know their names by heart. Our northern priests possess no such exquisite knowledge of the hellish squadrons."

"Aye, young man, here we know all their names, and their ways, the reptiles. This Nebul is a bitter hard one to track down."

He then recounted several of his experiences to the company in the most affable manner, concluding with a feat from the previous day when he drove a great, hulking fiend out of a woman through her mouth, leaving behind nails, pins, and a tuft of hair. The creature had cried out in anguish, *"It is not thou that conquerest me. See that stone on the windowsill. Know that the angel Gabriel, descending to earth, once alighted upon that stone; 'tis that which has done my business."*

The friar moaned. "And you believed him?"

"Certainly! Who but an infidel would discredit a revelation so precise?"

"What, believe the father of lies? That pushes credulity beyond endurance."

"Oh, a liar does not always lie."

"Aye, he does whenever he begins with an improbable story and produces a holy relic, arming you against the Satanic host. Fiends—if any exist—are not so simple. You should have answered him from antiquity: *'Timeo Danaos et dona ferentes'* ('I fear Greeks bearing gifts'). Some blackguard chopped his wife's head off on that stone, young man; you take my word for it."

With that, the friar hurried Gerard away.

"Alack, Father, I fear you have abashed the good priest."

"Aye, by Pollux," the friar chuckled, "I blistered him with a single touch of 'Socratic interrogation.' What modern mind can parry the weapons of antiquity?"

One afternoon, after completing his daily work, a fine lackey summoned Gerard to the Palazzo Cesarini. Upon entering a noble apartment, he found a girl seated, working on a tapestry. She rose, excused herself, and returned shortly with news that her mistress wished to see him.

Gerard waited a good hour in the vast room, beginning to fret. "These nobles think nothing of a poor fellow's time," he muttered. Just as he resolved to slip out, the door opened, and a superb beauty entered, followed by two maids. It was the young Princess of the House of Cesarini. She entered talking loudly and haughtily to her attendants, but upon seeing Gerard, her voice softened to a very feminine tone.

"Are you the writer, Messer?"

"I am, Signora."

"'Tis well."

She seated herself; Gerard and the maids remained standing.

"What is your name, good youth?"

"Gerard, Signora."

"Gerard? Body of Bacchus! Is that the name of a human creature?"

"It is a Dutch name, Signora. I was born at Terghou, in Holland."

"A harsh name, girls, for so well-favored a youth. What say you?"

The maids assented warmly.

"What did I summon you for?" the lady inquired with lofty languor. "Ah, I remember. Sit down, Ser Gerardo, and write me a letter to Ercole Orsini, my lover—at least, he insists I do."

Gerard seated himself, prepared paper and ink, and looked up for instructions.

The Princess, seated on a high chair that resembled a throne, looked down at him with inquiring eyes.

"Well, Gerardo."

"I am ready, Your Excellence."

"Write, then."

"But I await the words."

"And who, do you suppose, is to provide them?"

"Who but Your Grace, whose letter it is to be?"
