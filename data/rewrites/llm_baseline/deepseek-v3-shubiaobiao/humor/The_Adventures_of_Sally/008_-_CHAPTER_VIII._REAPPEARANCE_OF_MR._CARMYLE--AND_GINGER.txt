When Sally boarded the train from Detroit that Saturday, her earlier worries had melted away like morning fog. She’d convinced herself she’d been silly—of *course* Gerald was pouring his energy into his work. His success was hers too, wasn’t it? And success seemed to be the word of the hour.

Detroit had adored *The Primrose Way*. The theater, miraculously allowed to open after Teddy’s prediction, had been packed. Critics raved, calling Gerald the next big thing and Elsa Doland a rising star. Even Fillmore earned a nod as a promising manager—though he sulked for a solid hour when Gladys Winch’s name was misspelled (“Wunch!” he’d fumed. “A *talent* like hers!”).

Now, as the train rattled eastward, Fillmore radiated smug satisfaction from his seat across from Sally.

“Yes, do,” Sally said suddenly.

Fillmore blinked. “Eh?”

“Buy the fur coat. It’s practically your duty now.”

A faint pink tinged his cheeks. “I wasn’t—that is—”

“Astrakhan collar, definitely. And when you roll down Fifth Avenue, I’ll point and say, ‘That’s my brother!’ ‘*Your* brother?’ ‘Yes! The great Fillmore Nicholas! And I was there when he bought that coat—’”

“Oh, shut up,” Fillmore muttered, though his lips twitched. “I was actually thinking about what Ike Schumann said. He’s on the train—wants to talk Chicago for the show.”

“We’re on first-name terms with Ike now, are we?”

“Everyone calls him Ike,” Fillmore said loftily.

“Just like everyone will soon call *you* ‘The Man in the Fur Coat.’”

Fillmore gave up and fled to the smoking car, leaving Sally to her magazine—until a polished voice broke in.

“Miss Nicholas.”

Bruce Carmyle, impeccably tailored and just as stiffly formal as she remembered, slid into the seat across from her.

Sally nearly dropped her magazine. “Mr. Carmyle! What are you doing in America?”

“Traveling,” he said, as if that explained everything. “One ought to see the world.”

Sally eyed him. “And you just *happened* to take this train?”

A flicker of discomfort crossed his face. “I—ah—inquired at your address in New York.”

“You *what*?”

“My cousin Lancelot mentioned it.”

At the name, Sally sat straighter. “How *is* Mr. Kemp?”

Carmyle’s expression darkened. “We’ve no idea. He vanished.”

“*Vanished?*”

“After insulting his uncle Donald—a man whose goodwill was *essential* to his future.” Carmyle’s jaw tightened. “The family has disowned him.”

Sally’s pulse jumped. *Ginger had done it.* He’d actually rebelled. Pride warred with guilt—had she pushed him into disaster?

Fillmore reappeared, and introductions were made. Carmyle, ever the gentleman, excused himself shortly after, leaving Sally to her thoughts—which, by the time the train reached New York, had spiraled into full-blown panic. *Had she ruined Ginger’s life?*

By morning, her imagination had conjured Ginger penniless, sleeping on the Thames Embankment, eyeing the dark waters with tragic resolve—

“Ugh!” Sally shook the image away as she reached the boardinghouse.

Mrs. Meecher greeted her with news: Mr. Faucitt had inherited money and dashed off to England. Sally’s spirits lifted briefly—until she trudged upstairs, exhausted, and collapsed onto her bed.

Then she froze.

A shoe. A *man’s* shoe. Peeking out from under her bed.

Sally bolted upright. “*Who’s under there?*”

A sneeze. A shuffle. Then, inch by inch, a familiar figure emerged—dusty, disheveled, and grinning sheepishly.

Ginger Kemp blinked up at her. “Oh, hullo!”
