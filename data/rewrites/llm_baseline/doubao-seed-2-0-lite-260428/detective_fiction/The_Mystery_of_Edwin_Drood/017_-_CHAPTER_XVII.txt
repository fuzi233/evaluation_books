Six full months had slipped by when the Minor Canon, Mr. Crisparkle, found himself cooling his heels in a waiting room at the London headquarters of the Haven of Philanthropy, waiting for an audience with the formidable Mr. Honeythunder.

Back in his university days, when he’d been active in amateur athletics, Crisparkle had known dozens of bare-knuckle pugilists, even attended a few of their gloved exhibition matches. Now he found himself struck by a peculiar parallel: the back of every professed professional philanthropist milling through those halls was shaped almost exactly like the back of a professional boxer’s skull. Every organ tied to a compulsion to “pitch into” other people was overdeveloped, swollen to a degree he’d only ever seen among the fighting set. The men sweeping past him all carried the same swagger, the obvious readiness to pick a fight with any random novice who dared cross their path, that he remembered well from the old fight circles. They were already making arrangements for a big moral rally out in the countryside, the backers loudly touting this or that heavyweight public speaker as a sure bet to land this line or that zinger on stage, exactly the same way sporting pub owners would bet on a prizefighter’s knock-out hits, the proposed rally resolutions treated like scheduled rounds in the ring. The event’s celebrated platform tactician, in his plain black suit, was the spitting image of old Frosty-faced Fogo, a legendary fight referee he’d known years prior who used to rope off the fighting ring and supervise the match start. Only three key differences separated the “philanthropy professors” from the old bare-knuckle pugilists: first, the philanthropists were dreadfully out of shape, far too fleshy, their faces and figures stuffed full of what fight experts called “suet pudding” fat. Second, the philanthropists had none of the boxers’ natural good temper, and used far fouler language. Third, their “fighting code” was entirely unregulated: they didn’t just press their opponent to the ropes, they bored him until he teetered on the edge of total madness. They hit men when they were down, hit them anywhere, kicked them, stamped on them, gouged them, and mauled them behind their backs without a shred of mercy. The old Noble Art of boxing was far nobler than this brand of professional philanthropy.

Crisparkle was so lost turning these similarities and differences over in his head, watching the endless stream of people come and go, all seemingly on errands designed to snatch something hostilely from someone else and never give anything back, that his name was called twice before he heard it. When he finally responded, a shabby, drastically underpaid clerk — a stipendiary philanthropist so poorly treated he could not have fared worse if he’d worked for an avowed enemy of humanity — led him straight to Mr. Honeythunder’s private office.

“Sir,” Honeythunder boomed, his voice as thunderous as a schoolmaster barking orders at a pupil he already suspected of every crime imaginable, “sit down.”

Crisparkle took the offered seat. Honeythunder finished signing off on a few hundred out of thousands of circulars, which ordered thousands of cash-poor families to “step forward, stump up immediately, become a philanthropist, or go straight to the Devil”. Another equally shabby, dismally paid clerk gathered the stack of circulars into a wicker basket and trundled away.

“Now, Mr. Crisparkle,” Honeythunder said, swiveling his chair half around to face him, squaring his arms with his hands planted firm on his knees, brows knitted like he was about to get this over with fast, “we hold very different views, you and I, sir, on the sanctity of human life.”

“Do we?” the Minor Canon replied mildly.

“We do, sir.”

“Might I ask,” Crisparkle said, “what your views on the subject are?”

“That human life is a sacred thing, sir.”

“Then might I ask,” the Minor Canon pressed on, “what you suppose my views on the subject are?”

“By George, sir!” the philanthropist snapped, squaring his arms even tighter as he glowered at Crisparkle. “They’re best known to you.”

“Easily admitted. But you opened by saying our views differ. Which means you must have already assigned some specific belief to me to make that claim. What belief have you pinned on me?”

“Here is a man — a young man, mark you — swept off the face of the earth by an act of brutal violence. What do you call that?” Honeythunder said, as if the victim’s youth made the crime infinitely worse, and he could have borne the loss of an old man with no trouble at all.

“Murder,” Crisparkle said.

“Then what do you call the man who did the deed, sir?”

“A murderer.”

“I’m surprised to hear you admit even that much, sir,” Honeythunder sneered in his most offensive tone, “and I’ll be frank, I never expected it.” He leaned forward, heavy and menacing.

“Explain to me exactly what you mean by those unjustifiable remarks, please.”

“I don’t sit here, sir,” the philanthropist roared, raising his voice to a full yell, “to be browbeaten.”

“As the only other person in the room, no one could possibly know that better than I do,” Crisparkle said, perfectly calm. “But go on. I won’t interrupt your explanation.”

“Murder!” Honeythunder boomed, in a loud, performative reverie, folding his arms the same way he would on stage, nodding in feigned abhorrence after every short punchy phrase. “Bloodshed! Abel! Cain! I strike no bargains with Cain. I recoil with a shudder from any red hand that is offered to me.”

Instead of leaping out of his chair and cheering himself hoarse, as the rest of the philanthropist’s crowd of followers would have done in a public meeting, Crisparkle only shifted the quiet cross of his legs and said, “Do go on with that explanation when you get to it.”

“The Commandments say no murder. NO murder, sir!” Honeythunder thundered, pausing dramatically as if he was about to accuse Crisparkle of openly claiming the Commandments permitted “a little bit of murder” before you had to stop.

“They also say you shall bear no false witness,” Crisparkle pointed out.

“ENOUGH!” Honeythunder bellowed, with a solemn severity that would have brought the house down at any public rally. “E—e—nough! My two late wards are now of age, and I’m released from a trust I cannot even think about without a shudder of horror. These are the accounts you agreed to accept on their behalf, and this is the statement of the balance you agreed to collect — and the sooner you collect it, the better. And let me tell you, sir, as a man and a Minor Canon, you could be far better employed.” He nodded once. “Far better employed.” A second nod. “Bet-ter em-ployed!” A third, firm nod to drive the point home.

Crisparkle stood, a little flush rising in his cheeks but perfectly in control of himself.

“Mr. Honeythunder, whether my current work is better or worse than any other is a matter of personal opinion. You might think I’d be better off enrolling myself as a member of your society.”

“Ay, indeed you would!” Honeythunder snarled, shaking his head threateningly. “You’d be far better off if you’d done that years ago!”

“I disagree.”

“Or,” Honeythunder went on, shaking his head again, “I might think a clergyman of your station would be better off devoting all his time to hunting down and punishing guilty men, instead of leaving that duty to a layman.”

“I may view my clerical duties through a lens that says my first obligation is to the needy, the suffering, the desolate, and the oppressed,” Crisparkle said. “And since I have no interest in making empty performative professions of piety, I won’t argue that point further. But I owe it to Mr. Neville Landless, and to his sister Helena — and to a far lesser degree, to myself — to tell you plainly that I knew Neville’s mind and heart perfectly, completely, at the very time Edwin Drood vanished. Without glossing over his flaws, or hiding the faults he needed to correct, I know for a fact his account of that night is true. On that certainty, I stand by him. For as long as that certainty holds, I will stand by him. If any bribe or pressure could ever make me abandon that, I would be so ashamed of my own cowardice that no one’s good opinion — not a man’s, not even a woman’s — could ever compensate me for losing the respect of myself.”

He was a good man, a steady, manly fellow, and so humble there was no trace of self-importance in him, no more vanity than the schoolboy who used to stand guard at the wicket on his old school’s breezy playing fields. He was simply, staunchly true to his small, quiet duties as he was to the great, grand ones, as every truly great soul always is. Nothing petty ever feels small to a spirit that is truly large.

“Then who do you claim did the murder?” Honeythunder snapped, whirling on him abruptly.

“Heaven forbid I should set out to clear one innocent man by lightly criminate another!” Crisparkle said. “I accuse no one.”

“Tcha!” Honeythunder huffed in disgust, for the “philanthropy brotherhood” almost always operated by the exact opposite principle. “And you, sir, are not a disinterested witness. We all have to keep that in mind.”

“How am I an interested party?” Crisparkle asked, smiling innocently, genuinely confused.

“You received a stipend for tutoring Neville Landless, didn’t you? That probably warped your judgment a little,” Honeythunder said, coarsely.

“Wait, you think I’m still holding out to keep that stipend?” Crisparkle said, now understanding. “Is that the accusation you’re leveling at me?”

“Well, sir,” the professional philanthropist said, pushing himself to his feet and shoving both hands deep into his trouser pockets, “I don’t go around making custom hats for people. If folks find one I’m carrying happens to fit, they can put it on and wear it if they like. That’s their problem, not mine.”

Crisparkle looked at him, bright with just indignation, and spoke his mind plainly.

“Mr. Honeythunder, I hoped when I walked in here I would not have to denounce the rude, underhanded stage tricks of your public platform as something that has no place in the decent, respectful forbearances of private life. But you have given me such a blatant example of both that I would deserve to be lectured myself for staying silent. They are detestable.”

“They don’t suit you, I dare say, sir.”

“They are detestable,” Crisparkle repeated, ignoring the interruption. “They violate both the basic justice owed to fellow Christians, and the basic restraint expected of any gentleman. You assume a terrible murder was committed by a man I — who knows all the circumstances, who has dozens of solid reasons on my side — devoutly believe to be innocent. Because I disagree with you on that critical point, what do you pull out of your platform playbook? You immediately turn on me, and claim I don’t care about the enormity of the crime at all, that I am an aider and abettor of murder! Another time, you pull the same trick: you perform a grand, unanimously seconded profession of faith in some ridiculous delusion or dangerous, harmful hoax. When I refuse to believe it, you declare loudly to the crowd that I believe in nothing at all — that because I won’t bow down to your fake man-made god, I deny the true God! Another time, you make the obvious trivial observation that war is a disaster, then say you can abolish it with a string of twisted, meaningless resolutions tossed into the air like a kite’s tail. I point out that observation isn’t original to you, and I don’t trust your useless remedy for a second. You again pull your platform trick, and paint me as a fiend incarnate who revels in the slaughter of battlefields! Another time, on one of your undiscriminating public crusades, you want to punish every sober person the same way you punish drunkards. I ask for basic consideration for the comfort and decency of people who don’t drink, and you immediately proclaim to the crowd that I have a twisted desire to turn all of God’s creatures into wild, ravening beasts! In every single one of these cases, your followers, from the lowest to the highest, run wild like unhinged madmen, freely assigning the lowest, basest possible motives to anyone who disagrees, with no regard for the truth — take your own remark about my tutoring stipend just now, a petty falsehood you should be ashamed of — citing statistics you know are deliberately one-sided, as absurdly unbalanced as a ledger that lists all credits and no debits, or all debits and no credits. That’s why, Mr. Honeythunder, I think your public platform is already a terrible example and a terrible school even for public life. But when you drag those rude, unfair tactics into private personal interactions, it becomes an unendurable nuisance.”

“Those are strong words, sir!” the philanthropist gasped.

“I should hope so,” Crisparkle said. “Good morning.”

He strode out of the Haven of Philanthropy at a fast clip, before slowing to his usual brisk, steady pace, a smile creeping onto his face as he walked. He wondered what the little china shepherdess figurine on his mantelpiece back home would have thought if she’d seen him land those hard blows on Mr. Honeythunder. He had just enough harmless vanity to hope he’d gotten his point across sharp, and glow in the quiet satisfaction that he’d trimmed that pompous philanthropist’s sails pretty handsomely.

He made his way to Staple Inn, but not to the office of P. J. T. and Mr. Grewgious first. He climbed flight after flight of creaking stairs until he reached a set of attic rooms tucked into a far corner, turned the latch on the unbolted door, and stepped up to the table where Neville Landless sat working.

The rooms, and the young man who lived there, were wrapped in a thick, quiet air of seclusion. Neville looked gaunt and worn thin, and the rooms showed the same traces of long, unbroken solitude: slanted low ceilings, cumbrous rusty locks and grates, heavy wooden bins and beams slowly mouldering away, giving the whole place the feel of a small, comfortable prison. Neville’s face was the haggard, hollow face of a man who lived as if he was a prisoner. But bright sunlight streamed in through the ugly garret window, which jutted out from the roof tiles like its own little penthouse. On the cracked, smoke-stained parapet outside, a few scruffy local sparrows hopped about rheumatically, like tiny feathered cripples who’d left their crutches back in their nests, and the rustle of nearby living leaves stirred the air, making a faint, imperfect murmur that would have sounded like full melody out in the open countryside.

The rooms were sparely furnished, but stacked wall to wall with books. Everything in sight marked it as the home of a poor, dedicated student. Anyone who looked at Crisparkle’s face when he stepped through the door, the warm, familiar beam he turned on those books, would have easily guessed he was the one who’d chosen, lent, and gifted almost all of them.

“How goes it, Neville?”

“I’m in good spirits, Mr. Crisparkle, and working away hard.”

“I wish your eyes weren’t quite so large and bright,” the Minor Canon said, slowly releasing the young man’s hand after giving it a firm, warm shake.

“They light up the second I see you,” Neville said. “If you ever turned your back on me, they’d go dull and dark fast enough.”

“Rally, rally!” Crisparkle encouraged, his tone bright and bracing. “Fight for it, Neville!”

“If I was dying, I think a single word from you would pull me back. If my pulse had stopped, I think your touch would make it beat again,” Neville said. “But I have rallied. I’m doing wonderfully well.”

Crisparkle turned Neville so his face angled toward the light.

“I want to see a ruddier flush here, Neville,” he said, tapping his own healthy, sun-kissed cheek as an example. “I want more natural sunlight on your face.”

Neville’s head drooped suddenly, his voice dropping low. “I’m not strong enough for that yet. Maybe I will be someday, but I can’t bear the daylight out in the open yet. If you’d walked the streets of Cloisterham the way I did, if you’d seen all those averted eyes, all the respectable people silently stepping far, far out of my path so I wouldn’t accidentally brush up against them, you wouldn’t think it unreasonable that I can’t bear to go out in broad daylight.”

“My poor boy!” the Minor Canon said, his tone so purely, sincerely sympathetic that Neville caught his hand in a tight grip. “I never said it was unreasonable. I never even thought it. But I do want you to work up to it.”

“And that’s the strongest possible reason I have to try. But I can’t do it yet. Even out here in this huge city, full of strangers, I can’t convince myself that the people I pass aren’t looking at me with suspicion. I feel marked, tainted, even when I only go out after dark. But the dark hides me, then. I draw all my courage from the dark.”

Crisparkle rested a gentle hand on his shoulder, looking down at him.

“If I could legally change my name, I would have done it,” Neville said. But you wisely pointed out that would only look like proof of guilt. If I could run off to some faraway country, I think that would ease the weight, but I can’t do that either, for the exact same reason. Everyone would say I ran away because I was guilty. It feels a little cruel to be tied to the stake like this, completely innocent, but I’m not complaining.”

“And you can’t expect a miracle to rescue you, Neville,” Crisparkle said, compassionate.

“No, sir. I know that. All I can trust is the steady march of time, and the slow unfolding of circumstance.”
