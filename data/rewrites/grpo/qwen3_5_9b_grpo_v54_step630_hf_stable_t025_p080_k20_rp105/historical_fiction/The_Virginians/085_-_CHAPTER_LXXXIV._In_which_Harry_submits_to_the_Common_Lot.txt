The era of my destitution had finally ended. The death of my young charge had irrevocably altered my financial standing, transforming me from a pauper into the presumptive heir to a substantial estate. With my uncle and aunt unlikely to produce further offspring, the path to wealth seemed clear. Yet, the transition was not without its emotional turbulence. My aunt, Lady Warrington, had been shattered by the loss, her grief so profound that her spiritual advisors interpreted it as a divine chastisement for her former worldly ambitions. They reminded her that while her son sought earthly coronets, she was now assured of a heavenly crown. This tragedy rippled through the religious circles of the time, with sermons preached in chapels dedicated to the sudden shift from vanity to piety.

Despite her sorrow, Lady Warrington eventually reached out to me. A year after the accident, during a visit to London, she summoned me for a lesson on my conduct. She viewed my inheritance not merely as a windfall but as a moral imperative to reform my life. She pleaded with me to sever ties with my disreputable associates, abandon the theatre, and embrace the quiet, sacred duties I had neglected. Her speech was a torrent of scripture, a didactic homily that I found exhausting. Upon my departure, she gifted me a packet of sermons for my wife and a hymnal by Miss Dora, a woman who would later become a prominent figure in that fervent society before marrying the celebrated preacher Mr. Juffles.

In the aftermath, the family dynamic shifted. While my aunt struggled to bear the sight of our lost child, my uncle, a man of obsequious loyalty to the government, poured his attention onto our surviving son, Miles. He brought endless toys and sweets, begging the boy to call him "Godpapa." It was a strange tableau: the grieving mother, the doting uncle, and the innocent child who seemed to forget his sorrow in the delight of new clothes and fresh air. His innocent chatter, however, struck a deeper wound in my wife's heart. We returned to our lodgings in Bloomsbury, where Miles was enrolled at the Chartreux School. There, under the care of his friend Charley, he thrived, avoiding the beatings he had suffered elsewhere and excelling under a competent tutor.

With my uncle's influence secured, I was gazetted as one of His Majesty's Commissioners for licensing hackney-coaches. I held this post with honor until a future quarrel with the Minister stripped me of it. I also pursued my education at the Temple, appearing in Westminster Hall in gown and wig. Later, accompanying my friend Mr. Foker to Paris, I was warmly received by Monsieur de Florac, who introduced me to the city's elite. Though I had the spirit to mingle with the nobility, I remained loyal to Foker, whose connections lay firmly with the bourgeoisie, including Monsieur Santerre, a brewer who would later distinguish himself in blood rather than beer. Meanwhile, my wife and I resided at Foker's villa in Wimbledon, amused by the "Parisian letters" I sent home via Mr. Hume, which were eventually published as a volume.

While I discharged these duties in London, the seeds of the great separation between the colonies and the mother country were being sown. When Mr. Grenville proposed the Stamp Duties, I warned my wife of the discontent it would stir, noting our own tendency to extract as much as possible from England. Yet, I never anticipated the fury the scheme ignited. It was a classic case where a pretext for quarrel masked long-standing animosities. The habitual insolence of Englishmen toward colonists, the natural spirit of men outraged by imperial domination, and the blunders of the British system drove the colonies to revolt. Had I been in Virginia rather than London, I might have sided with the provincials, perhaps driven into rebellion by the very resolve of my sister-in-law, Lady Warrington. Was the Stamp Act the true cause? A tax no greater than that cheerfully paid in England. Ten years prior, when the French threatened our borders, would the colonies have rebelled over such a levy? Against the British in America stood thousands of high-spirited men, yet others found profit in the quarrel. I confess I cannot now determine if my own motivations were selfish or patriotic, nor which side held the moral high ground. Having lost the game, I vow that after the initial soreness, the loser felt no lasting rancour.

The true source of my domestic turmoil, however, lay in the correspondence from my brother, Hal. He wrote home with flaming patriotic fervor, a tone that seemed entirely at odds with his usual deference to my wisdom. My wife whispered that he was rebelling against "Madam Esmond," while I suspected some colonial influence. Hal begged me never to allude to the delicate subject in my letters, fearing his mother's scrutiny. Yet, when he learned that I had retained only a hundred pounds from his aunt's legacy despite my own extreme straits, he was moved to mortgage his newly purchased estate to support me, quarrelling with his mother in my defense. Only later did he discover that our mother had cut off my supplies to advance his interests; by then, my financial situation had improved, and his sacrifices were in vain.

Our thrifty parent at Castlewood meticulously archived every scrap of paper, yet we boys were careless with our own correspondence. My wife, however, preserved many of Harry's letters, including one that revealed a shocking development. Reading it, my wife exclaimed, "To think whom he has chosen, and whom he might have had! Oh, 'tis cruel!" I remarked on the beauty of Miss Fanny Mountain, but she countered, "But is she to compare to my Hetty?" We had agreed that Hetty and Harry were not destined for happiness, yet my wife sighed, wishing they had tried, fearing Hetty's superior intellect might have led Harry astray.

Harry's letter detailed his conquest of Miss Fanny Mountain. He wrote with a newfound confidence, aided by a dictionary, declaring that he had given his heart in exchange for hers. He explained that he had not long been home when he realized his fate was accomplished. He praised Fanny's growth since his European travels and noted the rough, haughty treatment she endured from Madam Esmond, who treated her worse than a servant. Harry's love for Fanny was sweet and angelic, enduring her mistreatment with patience. He recounted how Fanny thanked him for rescuing her from prison and paying her debts, expressing her gratitude for his selfless poverty.

Harry revealed that Madam Esmond had seen an opportunity to provide for him through the sale of Tom Diggle's estate, offering six thousand pounds for the farm and stock. With his aunt's legacy arriving opportunely, Harry purchased the property near Richmond, intending to return to his native country and join the House of Burgesses. Madam Esmond had made a condition: he must leave the army and live at home. She had lost one son to literature and England, she claimed, and demanded the other stay.

However, after the purchase was made, Harry's mother attempted to force him to marry Miss Betsy Pitts, a woman scarred by smallpox with crooked eyes and unequal legs. Harry rejected her, comparing her unfavorably to Fanny's graceful ankles. The turning point came when a certain dear girl, Fanny, burst into tears of happiness upon hearing of his departure from the army. She confessed her terror of losing him, her prayers for his safety during Braddock's campaign and Wolfe's expedition. Harry, touched by her sympathy, seized her hand.

Later, finding Fanny crying in her mother's room while sewing a cockade for a hat, Harry asked why her eyes were red. Fanny admitted she was a fool, crying because he was leaving them for a life of honor among gay companions, leaving them in solitude. She began to speak of remembering him, but stopped, hiding her face. Harry, flustered and moved, declared his love, asking if he could think that parting brought her tears. Fanny, realizing the gravity of the situation, cried out for him to consider the difference between them and the potential scandal.

Their mother, Madam Esmond, entered the room, accusing Harry of being an ungrateful, artful, false wretch. She insulted Fanny, calling her the daughter of a housekeeper and suggesting she marry the apothecary's son, Sam Lintot, or even the blacksmith's son. Enraged, Harry swore oaths and blasphemies, defending Fanny's honor against his own mother. He declared that if Lintot offered Fanny any liberty, he would brain him with his own pestle. Madam Esmond, leaning on her tortoiseshell stick, lamented her punishment, claiming she had centered her love on Harry to the neglect of his elder brother, taking unjustly from the eldest to enrich the younger. She accused him of seducing the housekeeper's daughter under her roof.

Harry, furious, insisted he did not intend to seduce anyone and defended Fanny's purity. He knelt before her, offering his heart and hand forever. Fanny accepted, declaring that only Madam Esmond could hold such an opinion of her honor. She refused to stay in the house any longer. Madam Esmond, pale and stunned, ordered her to leave. Harry offered Fanny his own home, nearly ready, inviting her to stay forever as reparation for the insult to her honor.

As Harry spoke to Fanny, he heard his mother outside the room repeatedly muttering, "I am punished for my crime!" He did not immediately grasp the meaning of her words, but later, at a tavern where the ladies had moved with their belongings, Fanny explained. Madam Esmond had announced her intention to return instantly to Castlewood, taking her servants with her. She had decided to leave the country entirely, bargaining for a passage on a ship bound for England. However, the cabin was mercifully engaged by a North Carolina gentleman and his family, who agreed to take the ladies along. Before the next ship sailed, they had arranged to stay with Harry.

Harry's letter concluded with hopes for reconciliation and plans to visit Europe someday. He signed off as "H. E. W." (Henry Esmond Warrington), adding a postscript that revealed the full extent of his mother's manipulation. She had stopped his allowance and charged him nearly a thousand pounds for goods provided for the Virginian property, plus the costs of his release from prison. He drew upon his brother for the sum, instructing him not to acknowledge the account in writing to avoid bothering women with details.

Upon reading the document, we concluded that the ladies had supervised the style and spelling of Hal's letter, but the postscript was added without their knowledge. We feared that the Virginian Squire was under female domination, much like Hercules, Samson, and many strong men before him.
