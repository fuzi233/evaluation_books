A week had passed since the Bonfire of Vanities, and now the Piazza della Signoria swarmed with a crowd greater than any gathered for the feast of San Giovanni. By midday, every vantage point was claimed—men clung to iron rods, perched on statues, balanced on architraves, their discomfort forgotten in the anticipation of witnessing a miracle. For today, Florence would not merely hear of divine wonders; they would see one unfold before their eyes.

At the heart of the spectacle stood a long platform, eight feet wide and twenty yards in length, piled high with dry oak, thorns, and oil-soaked rags, a pyre meant to test faith with flame. It stretched from the foot of the Old Palace, beneath the stony gaze of Marzocco, the guardian lion. Three forces stood armed and waiting: the Signoria’s soldiers before the palace, Dolfo Spini’s Compagnacci at the far end, and Marco Salviati’s loyalists near the Loggia, where the rival orders—Franciscan and Dominican—would face their trial.

The air hummed with tension. The Franciscans had already taken their place in silence, their grey robes a stark contrast to the fervor soon to arrive. Then, from across the square, rose the thunderous chant of the Dominicans: *"Let God arise, and let His enemies be scattered."* At their head strode Fra Domenico, clad in flame-colored cope, cross in hand, his face alight with conviction. Behind him came Savonarola, robed in white, bearing the Host. His voice rang clear, his expression unshaken, though every eye upon him bore either hope, doubt, or malice.

Yet beneath the prophet’s steadfast gaze lay a gnawing dread. The trial was not merely a test of faith—it was a battle for survival. The courier’s intercepted letters had reached Milan, sealing his fate in Rome. The flames ahead were but one peril; the true fire was the shifting will of Florence.

As the hours dragged on, delays mounted. Fra Domenico, denied the crucifix, now bore the Sacrament—a defiance that sparked fresh debate. The Franciscans balked; Savonarola held firm. The crowd, restless and hungry, grew impatient.

The jeers swelled into chaos. Spini’s Compagnacci surged forward, only to be repelled by Salviati’s men. Then, as if heaven itself intervened, the clouds broke. Rain poured down, drenching the pyre, the plaza, and the people’s fervor alike. The trial was over before it had begun.

Savonarola stood motionless as the storm passed. The Signoria granted him a guard, but no victory. The faces that once looked to him for salvation now twisted in scorn. As he walked from the Loggia, the Sacrament still in hand, no divine light blessed him—only the indifferent glare of the clearing sky.

*"Well parried, Frate,"* murmured Tito Melema from the shadows. *"But your time here is done."*

Niccolò Machiavelli sighed. *"A pity. With the times favoring reform, he might have achieved greatness."*

Savonarola heard none of it. The trial had ended, but the judgment had only begun.
