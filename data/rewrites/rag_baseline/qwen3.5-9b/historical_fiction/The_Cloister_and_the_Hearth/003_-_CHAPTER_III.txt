To understand the architecture of Gerard Eliassoen's ascent, one must first confront the absolute poverty of his universe prior to this moment. He existed within a microscopic empire of two, subjugated by a man he was conditioned to call "Master"—a square-built, brutal artifact of a world he barely knew, roughly sixty years of age, though time on the island seemed measured only by the decay of his humanity. Now, however, the chains of servitude have been severed, replaced by the dizzying vertigo of proximity to royalty.

When the grave white-haired seneschal approached their table, his inquiry courteous yet laden with the weight of protocol, the atmosphere shifted instantly. Faces within hearing turned sharp, bending with a mixture of curiosity and venomous envy upon the man destined to walk beside a princess. Gerard rose to obey, the motion fluid despite the turmoil in his soul.

"I wager we shall not see you again," Margaret said calmly, though a flush betrayed her composure.

"That you will," was the reply, delivered with a confidence that bordered on arrogance. Then, leaning close, he whispered, "This is my good princess; but you are my queen." Turning to the crowd, he added aloud, "Wait for me, I pray you; I will presently return."

"Ay, ay!" Peter spoke simultaneously, awakening from his reverie.

With Gerard gone, the pair whose dress was so homely, yet who stood in the presence of the man summoned by the Princess, became the cynosure of neighboring eyes. Observing this spectacle, William Johnson stepped forward, feigning surprise to claim kinship.

"And to think that there was I at your backs, and you saw me not," he declared.

"Nay, cousin Johnson, I saw you long since," Margaret replied coldly, her tone cutting through the warmth of the reunion.

"You saw me, and spoke not to me?"

"Cousin, it was for you to welcome us to Rotterdam, as it is for us to welcome you at Sevenbergen. Your servant denied us a seat in your house."

"The idiot!" Johnson muttered, his face flushing purple. He sensed Margaret's keen edge and suspected her intent. Under the circumstances, wisdom dictated action over words. He insisted they accompany him immediately, vowing to demonstrate whether they were truly welcome to Rotterdam or not.

"Who doubts it, cousin? Who doubts it?" the scholar interjected.

Margaret thanked him graciously but demurred, citing a desire to hear the minstrels once more. Yet, in about a quarter of an hour, Johnson renewed his proposal, noting that many guests had departed. Only then did her true reason surface.

"It were ill manners to our friend; and he will lose us. He knows not where we lodge in Rotterdam, and the city is large, and we have parted company once already."

"Oh!" Johnson exclaimed. "We will provide for that. My young man, ahem! I mean my secretary, shall sit here and wait, and bring him on to my house: he shall lodge with me and with no other."

"Cousin, we shall be too burdensome."

"Nay, nay; you shall see whether you are welcome or not, you and your friends, and your friends' friends, if need be; and I shall hear what the Princess would with him."

Margaret felt a thrill of joy that Gerard might be lodged under the same roof as her, yet a slight misgiving clouded her happiness. "But if your young man should be thoughtless, and go play, and Gerard miss him?"

"He go play? Leave that spot where I put him, and bid him stay? Ho! Stand forth, Hans Cloterman."

A figure clad in black serge and dark violet hose arose, taking two precise steps to stand before them without moving a muscle—a solemn, precise young man, the very statue of gravity and starched propriety. At his aspect, Margaret, overwhelmed by joy, could hardly maintain her countenance. She whispered to Johnson, "I would put my hand in the fire for him. We are at your command, cousin, as soon as you have given him his orders."

Hans was instructed to sit at the table and await Gerard, conducting him to Ooster-Waagen Straet. He complied not with words, but by calmly occupying the indicated seat. Margaret, Peter, and William Johnson then withdrew together.

"And, indeed, it is time you were abed, father, after all your travel," Margaret said, a thought that had lingered in her mind throughout the evening.

Hans Cloterman sat waiting for Gerard, solemn and businesslike. Minutes flew by, exciting no impatience in that perfect young man. Johnson dismissed the notion of his secretary leaving his post or neglecting duty in pursuit of sport or youthful hilarity with a laugh of scorn.

As Gerard proved long in coming, the patient Hans, his employer's eye no longer upon him, improved the time by quaffing goblets of Corsican wine. The wine was strong, so was Cloterman's resolve; yet, after an hour, the model secretary imbibed the notion that Creation expected him to toast the health of all good fellows, and specifically the Duke of Burgundy present. With this view, he filled his bumper nine times, rising gingerly but solemnly and slowly. Having reached his full height, he instantly rolled upon the grass, goblet in hand, spilling the cold liquor on more than one ankle—their owners frisked, but not disturbing a single muscle in his own long face. In the total eclipse of reason, his visage retained its gravity, primness, and infallibility.

Meanwhile, the seneschal led Gerard through several passages to the door of the pavilion, where young noblemen, embroidered and feathered, sat sentinel, guarding the heir-apparent while playing cards by the red light of torches held by their servants. A whisper from the seneschal prompted one of them to rise reluctantly, staring at Gerard with haughty surprise before entering the pavilion. He presently returned, beckoning the pair, and led them through a passage or two, landing them in an ante-chamber where three more young gentlemen, feathered, furred, and embroidered like pieces of fancy work, were deep in that instructive and edifying branch of learning known as dice.

"You can't see the Princess—it is too late," said one.

Another followed suit: "She passed this way but now with her nurse. She is gone to bed, doll and all. Deuce—ace again!"

Gerard prepared to retire. The seneschal, with an incredulous smile, replied, "The young man is here by the Countess's orders; be so good as conduct him to her ladies."

On this, a superb Adonis rose with an injured look and led Gerard into a room where sat or lolloped eleven ladies, chattering like magpies. Two, more industrious than the rest, played cat's-cradle with fingers as nimble as their tongues. At the sight of a stranger, all tongues stopped like one piece of complicated machinery, and all eyes turned on Gerard, as if the same string that checked the tongues had turned the eyes. Gerard was ill at ease before, but this battery of eyes discountenanced him further, and down went his eyes to the ground. Then the cowards, finding, like hares running by ponds and frogs scuttling into water, that there was a creature they could frighten, giggled and enjoyed their prowess. A duenna said severely, "Mesdames!" and they were all abashed at once as though a modesty string had been pulled. This same duenna took Gerard and marched before him in solemn silence. The young man's heart sank, and he had half a mind to turn and run out of the place.

"What must princes be," he thought, "when their courtiers are so freezing? Doubtless they take their breeding from him they serve."

These reflections were interrupted by the duenna suddenly introducing him into a room where three ladies sat working, and a pretty little girl tuning a lute. The ladies were richly but not showily dressed. The duenna went up to the one hemming a kerchief and said a few words in a low tone. This lady then turned towards Gerard with a smile, beckoning him to come near. She did not rise, but she laid aside her work, and her manner of turning towards him, slight as the movement was, was full of grace, ease, and courtesy. She began a conversation at once.

"Margaret Van Eyck is an old friend of mine, sir, and I am right glad to have a letter from her hand, and thankful to you, sir, for bringing it to me safely. Marie, my love, this is the gentleman who brought you that pretty miniature."

"Sir, I thank you a thousand times," said the young lady.

"I am glad you feel her debtor, sweetheart, for our friend would have us to do him a little service in return."

"I will do anything on earth for him," replied the young lady with ardor.

"Anything on earth is nothing in the world," said the Countess of Charolois quietly.

"Well, then, I will—What would you have me to do, sir?"

Gerard had just discovered the nature of the high society in which he now found himself. "My sovereign demoiselle," he said gently, a little tremulously, "where there have been no pains, there needs no reward."

"But we must obey mamma. All the world must obey."
