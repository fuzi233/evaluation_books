Gerard formed a friendship with a monk renowned for his ingenuity, a man who had crafted the great sundial in the prior's garden, a wheel for lifting water, and a winnowing machine for the grain. Always occupied with some clever contrivance, he had previously built several psalteries and two dulcimers, and was currently working on a set of regalles—small organs intended for the choir.

Gerard played the humble psaltery modestly, but the monk struck the instrument with divine skill, demonstrating to Gerard how green he truly was in matters of music. While the monk excelled in illumination, he admitted he could not match Gerard's calligraphy. Comparing their talents against the earnest simplicity of an era where skills reflected genuine natural aptitude, the young traveler and the aged monk quickly grew like brothers. Gerard was warmly invited to remain that night.

He consulted Denys, who agreed with a rueful shrug. Gerard explained his destination to his old companion, recounting their recent adventures while carefully softening the blow of their troubles. "Alack!" exclaimed the good old man. "I have traveled extensively in my day, yet no one ever molested me." He advised Gerard to avoid inns entirely, which were always infested with rogues and rowdiers, warning that his soul could suffer harm even if his body escaped injury. Instead, he urged him to plan daily journeys so as to rest within the peace of a monastery. Suddenly, the tone shifted; the old man looked at Gerard with the sharp intensity of a needle and asked, "How long has it been since you were shriven?"

Gerard flushed and replied weakly, "More than a fortnight."

"And thou an exorcist! No wonder perils have overtaken thee. Come, thou must be absolved immediately."

"Yes, father," said Gerard, "with all my heart." He sank to his knees, joining his hands, but the monk stopped him, half-fretful. "Not to me! Not to me! Not to me! My soul is as filled with worldly cares as yours or anyone living in it. My entire devotion lies in these wooden pipes and sorry leather stops, which will perish along with those whose minds are fixed on such vanities."

"Dear father," pleaded Gerard, "these instruments are for the service of the Church, and surely that sanctifies the labor spent upon them?"

"That is precisely what the devil has been whispering in my ear lately," the monk retorted, placing one hand behind his back and shaking his finger at Gerard with a mixture of threat and playfulness. "He was so kind and thoughtful as to remind me that Solomon built the Lord a house with rare hangings, and that this act was deemed gracious and sinless. Oh! He can quote Scripture remarkably well. But I am not so simple a monk as you think, my lad," he cried, suddenly defiant, addressing not Gerard but the empty space beside him. "This one toy finished, vigils, fasts, and prayers await me. Prayers standing, prayers lying on the chapel floor, and prayers in a right good tub of cold water."

He nudged Gerard and winked knowingly. "Nothing he hates and dreads more than seeing us monks at our devotions up to our chins in cold water. For *corpus domat aqua*. So now, go confess your little trivial sins—pardonable in youth and secular life—and leave me to mine, which are sweeter to me than honey and require equal expiation."

Gerard bowed his head but could not resist asking, "Where shall I find a confessor more holy and merciful?"

"In each of these cells," the monk replied simply, gesturing toward the corridor. "Go to Brother Anselm, yonder."

Gerard moved toward the indicated cell, but the doors stood close together, and he appears to have mistaken his mark. Just as he raised his hand to knock, he heard his old friend calling out in an agitated whisper, "Nay! Nay! Nay!"

Turning, he found the monk pacing anxiously before his own cell door, beating the air with both hands like a woodcutter swinging an axe. Gerard genuinely feared the room he sought housed some dangerous wild beast, or perhaps the very personage whose presence in the convent had been so clearly announced. Looking back questioningly, he proceeded to the next door.

Then, his old friend nodded vigorously, his face breaking into a blissful expression before shooting back into his sanctuary. He took his hourglass, flipped it, and returned to his work on the regalles. Often, he would glance upward and mutter to himself, "Well-a-day, how swiftly the sands run when a man bends over earthly toys."

Father Anselm was a venerable monk with an ample head and a countenance radiating dignity and love. Consequently, as Gerard confessed to him and answered his gentle yet probing questions, he couldn't help thinking, "Here is a head! Oh dear, oh dear! I wonder if you will allow me to sketch it once the confession is concluded." In his distraction, his own mind grew confused, and he forgot certain crimes. Nevertheless, he did not soften his account this time, nor was he so dishonest as to diminish the pagan nature of his previous embellishments.

The penance assigned was brief: Gerard was to enter the convent church, prostrate himself, and kiss the lowest step of the altar three times. Then, kneeling on the floor, he was to recite three Our Fathers and a Creed. Upon completion, he was to return instantly.

Having performed this short mortification, Gerard returned to find Father Anselm mixing plaster. "After the soul comes the body," the monk said. "Know that I serve as the surgeon here, lacking a better option. This is for your leg; to cool it, not to burn it—the saints forbid."

During the treatment, the monastic physician, having taken a keen interest in the Düsseldorf episode of Gerard's confession, leaned slightly toward Denys regarding the merits of "bleeding."

"We Dominicans seldom bleed nowadays," the monk remarked. "Lay surgeons claim it stems from timidity and lack of skill; indeed, we have long found that simples cure most ailments that can be cured at all. Furthermore, they never kill in capable hands, whereas other remedies strike like thunderbolts. As for blood, the Vulgate explicitly states it is the life of a man. And in medicine, as in law or divinity, to be wiser than the All-Wise is to be a fool. Moreover, simples are mighty. Consider the small four-legged creature that kills the poisonous snake; if bitten itself, it finds an herb powerful enough to quell that venom, acting faster and stronger than any human remedy. Taught by her wisdom and our own traditions, we still search and test the virtues of the plants God has scattered across this earth—some to feed the body, others to heal it. Only in desperate cases do we mix heavenly with earthly virtue, steeping the hair or bones of deceased saints in the medicine to achieve marvelous cures."

"Do you believe, father, it is due to the relics? For Peter a Floris, a learned physician and certainly no pagan, denies this stoutly."

"What does Peter a Floris know? And what do I know? I do not presume to claim we can command the saints, nor that they will, regardless of our wishes, draw corporeal virtue from their blessed remains. Yet, I observe that patients drinking thus in faith are often improved as if by a charm. Doubtless, faith in the recipient plays a large role in all such cures. Indeed, it always has. A sick woman whom all Jewish physicians failed to heal merely touched Christ's garment and was cured instantly. Had she not touched that sacred cloth, she would never have recovered. If she had worn it to her grave without faith, I suspect she would have gained nothing. But we do ill to investigate these matters too curiously. Everything around us demands faith. Have then a little patience. We shall soon know all. Meanwhile, I, your confessor for this occasion, strictly forbid you, under penalty to your soul's health, from listening to learned laymen on religious matters. Arrogance is their ruin; with it, they shut heaven's open door in their own faces. Mark my words: learned laics. Unlearned ones have often been my masters in humility and may become yours. Your wound is tended; in three days, it will be but a scar. May God speed you, and may the saints make you as good and happy as you are thoughtful and gracious."

Gerard hoped there was no need to depart, as he was scheduled to dine in the refectory. However, Father Anselm told him, with a barely perceptible trace of regret, that he would not leave his cell that week, being himself in penitence. With this, he gently held Gerard's head in both hands, kissed him on the brow, and almost before the cell door had closed, he was back to his pious duties.

Gerard left feeling chilled to the heart by the isolation of monastic life, deeply saddened. "Alas!" he thought. "Here is a kind face I shall never behold again on earth; a kind voice lost forever from my ear and my heart. There is nothing but meeting and parting in this sorrowful world. Well-a-day! Well-a-day!"

This pensive mood was broken by a young monk who fetched him and led him to the refectory. There, he found several monks seated at a table, while Denys stood like a sentinel, being questioned about the towns he intended to pass through.
