Tito moved with a light step, the paralyzing fear having evaporated like mist before the sun. His natural buoyancy returned, driving him toward Romola. Yet, as he approached, a shadow crossed his mind: her existence appeared an embodiment of selfless devotion and enduring patience, qualities he had previously shunned. However, he was not devoid of virtue, nor was he ready to surrender to vice entirely. In the bloom of his youth, his pulse quickened at the sight of beauty, and he still craved the simple pleasures of humanity. The poison he had swallowed worked slowly, insidiously. Though he had sold himself to evil, life currently felt unchanged to him; he remained unaware of the chains binding him. He intended for everything to proceed as before, internally and externally, winning favor through diligent effort and learned wit. He sought no discord with those he cherished above all, particularly Romola, whom he desired as his beloved wife. While alliances of greater wealth might eventually await a man of his rising stature, no woman in Florence compared to her. When she gazed upon him with those sincere hazel eyes, he felt overwhelmed by a magnetic influence, powerful and inevitable, akin to a melody that commands the soul until it is played anew.

Even before crossing the threshold, standing alone with Maso on the stone stairs, this influence began to stir, ignited merely by the anticipation of her presence.

"Welcome, Tito mio," the old man's voice called out before Tito could speak. There was a renewed vigor in it, a fresh cheerfulness in the blind face, since their meeting two months prior. "You have brought fresh manuscripts, I presume? But after our conversation last night, I've conceived new ideas: we must broaden our scope, return to our starting point."

Tito bowed respectfully to Romola as he advanced, adhering to his custom by moving straight to Bardo's chair. He placed his hand into the palm extended to receive it, settling onto the cross-legged leather seat with scrolled ends, nestled close to his master's elbow.

"Yes," Tito replied softly. "I have brought the new manuscript, but it may wait on your pleasure. I have youthful limbs, you know, and can climb the hill back without difficulty."

He did not meet Romola's gaze, though he was acutely aware that her eyes were fixed upon him with delighted attention.

"That is well said, my son." Bardo had taken to addressing Tito thus recently. "And I am glad to see you do not shrink from labor. Without toil, the poet wisely declares, life offers nothing to mortals. Too often, young ambition chases the *palma sine pulvere*—glory without the dust of the race. But what does the Greek say? 'Work in the morning of life; give counsel in the midday; pray in the evening.' Truthfully, I may seem to have reached that helpless evening, yet not so, while I possess counsel within me yet unspoken. For my mind, as I have often noted, was sealed behind a dam; the waters beneath lay dark and stagnant. But you, my Tito, have opened a channel for them, and they rush forward with a force that astonishes me. Now, I require that we review our preliminary ground again, with a broader scheme of commentary and illustration. Otherwise, I may miss opportunities I now see in hindsight, chances that will never return. Do you mark what I say?"

As Bardo stooped to retrieve his scattered manuscript, Tito's slight movement caught the jealous ear of the old man. Tito might have shrugged at the prospect ahead, but impatience was not his nature. Furthermore, he had been trained in that rigorous, exhaustive erudition that defined the age. With Romola nearby, he was buoyed by waves of pleasant sensation that rendered even the arduous task effortless.

"Certainly," Tito agreed. "You wish to expand your commentary on certain passages we have cited."

"Not merely so," Bardo interjected, his voice sharpening. "I intend to include occasional *excursus*, where we discuss authors I have studied deeply, for I may die before completing a separate work. Nor is this a season for scholarly integrity to remain idle. We must fear not only rash ignorance but men like Calderino, who, as Poliziano has demonstrated, resort to impudent falsifications of citation to serve their vanity and crown their errors with triumph. Therefore, my Tito, it is ill-advised to let this opportunity slip. Let us return to the point where we cited Thucydides. First, however, I wish you to accompany me through all my notes regarding the Latin translation by Lorenzo Valla. That incomparable Pope Nicholas the Fifth, honored by my personal acquaintance when I was young and known then as Thomas of Sarzana, paid him five hundred gold scudi—a sum, I do not say unjustly, given the honor. Yet, although Valla holds dubious fame, he is revered for his severe scholarship, prompting the epigrammatist to joke that since he descended to the shades, Pluto dares not speak ancient tongues. Thus, it is imperative that his name not serve as a stamp warranting false goods. I propose an *excursus* on Thucydides, wherein my critiques of Valla's text shall find their proper place. My Romola, you will fetch the necessary volumes—you know them well—from the fifth shelf of the cabinet."

Tito rose simultaneously with Romola. "I will fetch them, if you will direct me," he said, following her hurriedly into the adjoining chamber. Here, the walls were lined with books arranged in perfect order.

"There they are," Romola indicated, pointing upward. "Every book rests exactly where it was when my father ceased to see them."

Tito stood beside her, hesitating before reaching for the books. They had never shared this room together before.

"I hope," she continued, turning her gaze fully upon him with a look of grave confidence, "he will not tire you. This work brings him such happiness."

"And me as well, Romola—if you will allow me to say I love you—if you will deem me worthy of even a little love."

His words were a soft murmur. The dark, beautiful face, drawn closer than ever before, regarded him with beseeching tenderness.

"I do love you," Romola whispered. Her expression retained its simple majesty, yet her voice had never before sank to such a whisper. To both of them, it seemed they gazed at one another for a long time before her lips moved again. Yet, it was but a moment before she spoke: "I know *now* what it is to be happy."

Their faces met, and the dark curls intertwined for an instant with the rippling gold. Swift as lightning thereafter, Tito stepped onto a projecting ledge of the shelves and retrieved the needed volumes. Both were content to remain silent and separate; that first blissful experience of mutual consciousness was all the more exquisite for being unperturbed by immediate sensation.

Everything had unfolded as rapidly as the irreversible mingling of waters, and even the eager, jealous Bardo showed no sign of impatience.

"You have the volumes, my Romola?" the old man asked as they returned. "Now, prepare your pen. As Tito marks off the scholia we decide to extract, it is vital you copy them without delay—number them carefully to correspond with the numbers Tito writes in the text."

Romola always possessed a task granting her a share in this joint endeavor. Tito positioned himself at the *leggio*, where he both wrote and read, while she stationed herself at a table just in front, ready to hand anything her father required or relieve him of a finished volume. They had occupied these positions since the work began, yet today it felt new. So different was it now for them to sit opposite one another; so different for Tito to take a book from her hand as she lifted it from her father's knee. Yet, there was no subtlety employed to secure an extra glance or touch. Each woman fashions in her own image the tokens of love offered to her, and Romola's deep, calm happiness enveloped Tito like the rich, quiet light of evening, dissolving all unrest.

Two hours passed, and they ceased work as the light began to fail, when the door swung open. A figure entered, strangely incongruous with the flow of their thoughts and the solemn atmosphere of the room. It was a short, stout woman with black eyes, perhaps fifty years of age, wearing a black velvet *berretta* adorned with pearls. Beneath it, surprisingly massive black braids crowned her slightly bulging forehead, falling in rich, plaited curves over her ears. An equally striking carmine tint colored the upper region of her fat cheeks, contrasting with the surrounding sallowness. Three rows of pearls rested on her neck, along with a lower necklace of gold, lying on the horizontal cushion formed by her throat. The embroidered border of her trailing black velvet gown and her long, drooping sleeves of rose-colored damask were slightly faded, yet to the initiated eye, they conveyed the satisfying assurance of being the splendid result of six months' labor by a skilled artisan. The rose-colored petticoat, with its dimmed white fringe and seed-pearl arabesques, displayed a similar pleasing reflection. A handsome coral rosary hung from one side of an inferential belt, which emerged into certainty with a large clasp of silver wrought in niello; on the other side, where the belt again became obscure, hung a *scarsella*, or large purse, of crimson velvet, stitched with pearls.
