A few words from me will suffice to conclude the account left by Ezra Jennings in his journal.

As for myself, I awoke on the morning of the twenty-sixth with no memory of my actions under opium’s influence—from the moment the drug first seized me to the instant I opened my eyes in Rachel’s sitting room.

What followed requires little elaboration. Rachel and I reached an understanding before a single word of explanation passed between us. How this reconciliation occurred so swiftly, neither of us cares to justify. To those who have loved passionately, the scene that unfolded after Ezra Jennings closed the door needs no further illumination.

I will only add that we narrowly avoided discovery by Mrs. Merridew, thanks to Rachel’s quick thinking. Hearing the rustle of the old lady’s dress in the corridor, Rachel intercepted her. “What is the matter?” Mrs. Merridew demanded. “The explosion!” Rachel replied. With remarkable composure, she guided Mrs. Merridew into the garden, sparing her the supposed shock. Upon returning, the lady met me in the hall, marveling at modern science. “Explosions are far milder than in my youth, Mr. Blake! I scarcely heard Mr. Jennings’s from the garden—and not a trace of odor remains! Your medical friend must accept my compliments. He has managed it beautifully.”

Thus, after triumphing over Betteredge and Mr. Bruff, Ezra Jennings conquered even Mrs. Merridew. It seems liberal sentiment thrives in unexpected places.

At breakfast, Mr. Bruff made no secret of his desire for me to accompany him to London on the morning train. Rachel, intrigued by the bank’s surveillance and its possible outcome, resolved to join us—provided Mrs. Merridew consented. The old lady, still mollified by the “considerate” explosion, obliged without protest. Betteredge, entrusted with refurbishing the house, was too absorbed in his duties to succumb to detective fever.

Our sole regret was parting so abruptly with Ezra Jennings. He refused all persuasion to accompany us. I could only promise correspondence; Rachel insisted he visit her in Yorkshire. Though we expected to reunite within months, the sight of our dearest friend standing alone on the platform as the train departed weighed heavily upon us.

In London, Mr. Bruff was met at the station by an odd little boy clad in threadbare black, his most striking feature being his protuberant eyes—so loose and rolling, one marveled they remained in their sockets. After a brief exchange, Mr. Bruff excused us from escorting the ladies to Portland Place. I barely had time to assure Rachel I would return with a full account before the lawyer hurried me into a cab. The boy, whom Mr. Bruff affectionately called “Gooseberry,” perched beside the driver as we set off for Lombard Street.

“News from the bank?” I asked.

“News of Luker,” Mr. Bruff replied. “An hour ago, he left Lambeth in a cab with two plainclothes officers. If his fear of the Indians prompted this, the implication is clear: he means to withdraw the Diamond.”

“And we are to witness the result?”

“Or learn of it, if the affair is already concluded. Did you notice my boy?”

“His eyes were difficult to overlook.”

Mr. Bruff chuckled. “At the office, they call him ‘Gooseberry.’ I employ him for errands—and wish my clerks were half as reliable. Despite those eyes, he’s one of the sharpest lads in London.”

At twenty minutes to five, we halted before the bank. Gooseberry gazed longingly as Mr. Bruff opened the cab door.

“Coming in?” the lawyer asked kindly. The boy eagerly complied. “He’s quick as lightning,” Mr. Bruff whispered. “Two words suffice where twenty would be wasted on another.”

Inside, the outer office bustled with patrons before closing time. Two men approached Mr. Bruff immediately.

“Well?” the lawyer inquired.

“He passed through half an hour ago, sir, and entered the inner office.”

“Has he not emerged?”

“No, sir.”

Mr. Bruff turned to me. “We wait.”

I scanned the crowd for the Indians but found no trace. The sole dark-complexioned man present was a tall sailor in a pilot coat and round hat—too large to be one of them in disguise.

“They must have a spy nearby,” murmured Mr. Bruff, eyeing the sailor. “Perhaps him.”

Before he could elaborate, Gooseberry tugged his coat-tail. “Hush!” Mr. Bruff warned. “Here comes Luker.”

The moneylender emerged, flanked by his guardians. “Watch closely,” Mr. Bruff whispered. “If he transfers the Diamond, it will be here.”

Luker moved deliberately through the crowd. As he passed a stout, gray-suited man, I saw his hand flicker. The man started slightly, then stared after him. Luker proceeded to the door, his escort close behind, followed by one of Mr. Bruff’s men—and vanished from view.

I glanced meaningfully at the gray-suited man. “Yes,” Mr. Bruff whispered. “I saw it too.” He turned to locate his second man—nowhere in sight. Even Gooseberry had disappeared.

“What the devil?” Mr. Bruff muttered. “Deserted when most needed.”

The gray-suited man completed his transaction and turned to leave.

“What now?” Mr. Bruff asked. “We can’t trail him without debasing ourselves.”

“I can,” I declared. “I wouldn’t lose him for ten thousand pounds.”

“Then I shan’t lose you for twice that,” Mr. Bruff sighed. “A fine occupation for a man of my standing,” he grumbled as we followed the stranger out. “Not a word of this, Blake. Ruin awaits if it’s known.”

The man boarded a westward omnibus; we followed. Mr. Bruff, to my astonishment, flushed as he took his seat—latent youth resurfacing.

In Oxford Street, the man alighted and entered a chemist’s shop.

“My chemist!” Mr. Bruff exclaimed. “We’ve blundered.”

A brief exchange with the proprietor confirmed it. The lawyer returned, crestfallen.

“At least we may credit ourselves with one thing,” he said, leading me away.

“What?”

“We are the two worst amateur detectives ever to attempt the craft. That man has served the chemist thirty years. He was merely depositing funds and knows nothing of the Moonstone.”

I asked our next move.

“Back to my office,” Mr. Bruff said. “Gooseberry and my man must have followed someone else. Let us hope they kept their eyes open.”

At Gray’s Inn Square, the second man awaited us.

“Report,” Mr. Bruff demanded.

“A mistake, sir. I’d have sworn Luker passed something to an elderly gentleman in a light coat. Turns out he’s a respectable ironmonger of Eastcheap.”

“Where’s Gooseberry?”

The man stared blankly. “Haven’t seen him since the bank.”

Mr. Bruff dismissed him. “Either the boy’s fled, or he’s on his own hunt,” he mused. “Dine here, and we’ll see if he returns. I’ve good wine, and the coffee-house can furnish a chop.”

We dined in his chambers. Before clearing the cloth, a visitor was announced—not Gooseberry, but the man tasked with surveilling Luker. His report held no intrigue: Luker had returned home, dismissed his guards, and bolted his doors. The streets showed no sign of the Indians.

“Do you think he took the Diamond home?” I asked.

“Not likely,” Mr. Bruff said. “He’d have kept his guards if so.”

After another futile half-hour wait, Mr. Bruff departed for Hampstead, and I returned to Rachel. I left word for Gooseberry at my lodgings—but, true to form, I arrived late, finding only his note: he had grown sleepy and would return in the morning.

At nine the next day, I awaited him. Instead, Sergeant Cuff entered—as lean and dreary as ever, though now dressed as a country gentleman.

“I thought to call before writing to Yorkshire,” he said.

His sharp eyes still held their knowing gleam. He declined breakfast (shocked at my late hours) and came straight to the point.

“I misjudged the case last year,” he admitted. “But I’ve a sealed letter for you. Open it only when the truth is known—then compare my suspect with the guilty party.”

I pocketed the letter and asked his thoughts on our bank measures.

“Well-intended,” he said, “but another person required watching—the one named in that letter.”

Before he could elaborate, Gooseberry arrived. The boy, awed by the legendary Sergeant, recounted trailing a black-bearded sailor after seeing Luker pass him something.

“Why not tell Mr. Bruff?” Cuff pressed.

“No time, sir. The sailor left too quickly.”

Cuff praised the boy’s sharpness. Gooseberry beamed.

The sailor had hailed a cab; Gooseberry clung to its rear.

At that moment, Mr. Bruff’s chief clerk arrived with ill tidings: the lawyer was bedridden with gout. I sent assurances of Cuff’s involvement and the boy’s testimony.

Returning, I found Cuff ringing for a cab.

“The boy’s onto the right man,” he declared. “Time’s been lost—we must act now.”

Minutes later, we rode eastward, Gooseberry guiding the driver.

“That lad,” Cuff remarked, “will go far in my former profession. Now, let me relay what he told me in your absence…”
