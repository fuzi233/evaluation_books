Margaret stood once more in her morning attire, traveling quietly homeward with her father, who had journeyed north to assist at the wedding. Her mother remained behind, held captive by a multitude of half-reasons that defied clear explanation to anyone save Mr. Hale. He alone understood that his earnest pleas for a grey satin gown—somewhere between the faded and the fresh—had fallen on deaf ears. Lacking the funds to outfit his wife anew from head to toe, Mrs. Hale simply would not make the trip to her only sister's only child's nuptials. Had Mrs. Shaw guessed the true cause of her sister's absence, she might have flooded the parsonage with gowns; but nearly twenty years had passed since Mrs. Shaw was the poor, pretty Miss Beresford, and she had long since forgotten all grievances save the unhappiness born of age disparity in marriage. She could discourse on this topic for half an hour at a stretch. "Dearest Maria married the man of her heart," Mrs. Shaw would muse, noting the groom was merely eight years her senior, possessing a sweet temper and that rare blue-black hair. "He is one of the most delightful preachers I've ever heard, a perfect model of a parish priest." From these premises, Mrs. Shaw drew her characteristic, illogical conclusion: "Married for love, what can dearest Maria wish for?" To which Mrs. Hale, if she chose to speak truth, might have replied with a ready list: "A silver-grey glacé silk, a white chip bonnet, dozens of things for the wedding, hundreds for the house."

Margaret knew only that her mother had deemed it inconvenient to come, and she was secretly relieved that their reunion would occur at Helstone parsonage rather than amidst the chaotic whirlwind of the last few days in Harley Street, where she had played the part of Figaro, summoned everywhere simultaneously. Her mind and body ached with the recollection of everything she had done and said within forty-eight hours. The hurried farewells, especially those of those she had lived with so long, now pressed upon her with a sad regret for times that were gone forever. It mattered not what those times had been; they were lost. As the carriage rolled toward her dear home—the place and life she had yearned for since childhood—Margaret felt a heaviness in her heart she had never anticipated. Yet, she forced her mind away from the past to the bright, serene contemplation of the hopeful future. Her eyes adjusted to the present reality: her father leaning back asleep in the railway carriage. His blue-black hair was now grey, lying thinly over his brow. The bones of his face were plainly visible, too stark for beauty unless his features were less finely cut; yet, they possessed a grace of their own. His face was in repose, but it was the rest of weariness, not the serene calm of a placid life. Margaret was painfully struck by this worn, anxious expression. She traced the lines of habitual distress back to the open facts of her father's life.

"Poor Frederick!" she sighed inwardly. "Oh! if Frederick had been a clergyman instead of going into the navy, and being lost to us all! I wish I knew all about it. I never understood it from Aunt Shaw; I only knew he could not return to England because of that terrible affair. Poor dear papa! How sad he looks! I am so glad I am going home, to be at hand to comfort him and mamma."

She was ready with a bright smile, devoid of fatigue, to greet her father upon his awakening. He smiled back, faintly, as if it were an unusual exertion. His face settled back into its lines of anxiety. He had a habit of half-opening his mouth as if to speak, which constantly unsettled the form of his lips, giving his face an undecided expression. Yet, he retained the same large, soft eyes as his daughter—eyes that moved slowly and almost grandly in their orbits, veiled by transparent white eyelids. Margaret was more like him than like her mother. Sometimes people marveled that parents so handsome should have a daughter who was so regularly beautiful; occasionally, they whispered she was not beautiful at all. Her mouth was wide, not a rosebud that opened just enough to utter a "yes" or "no," but a wide curve of rich red lips. Her skin, though not white and fair, possessed an ivory smoothness and delicacy. While her general demeanor was too dignified and reserved for one so young, now, talking to her father, it was bright as the morning—full of dimples and glances that spoke of childish gladness and boundless hope.

It was the latter part of July when Margaret returned home. The forest trees were a dark, full, dusky green; the fern below caught the slanting sunbeams. The weather was sultry and broodingly still. Margaret used to tramp along by her father's side, crushing down the fern with a cruel glee as she felt it yield under her light foot, sending up its peculiar fragrance. Out on the broad commons into the warm, scented light, she saw multitudes of wild, free creatures revelling in the sunshine and the herbs it called forth. This life—at least these walks—realized all her anticipations. She took a pride in her forest; its people were her people. She made hearty friends with them, learned and delighted in their peculiar words, took up her freedom amongst them, nursed their babies, and talked or read with slow distinctness to their elders. She carried dainty messes to the sick and resolved soon to teach at the school where her father went daily as an appointed task, though she was continually tempted off to visit some individual friend—man, woman, or child—in a cottage in the green shade of the forest. Her out-of-doors life was perfect. Her in-doors life had its drawbacks. With the healthy shame of a child, she blamed herself for her keen sight in perceiving that all was not as it should be there. Her mother, always so kind and tender, seemed now and then discontented with their situation. She thought the bishop strangely neglected his episcopal duties in not granting Mr. Hale a better living, and almost reproached her husband for not wishing to leave the parish for a larger charge. He would sigh aloud, saying that if he could do what he ought in little Helstone, he should be thankful, yet every day he seemed more overpowered; the world became more bewildering. At each repeated urging from his wife to seek preferment, Margaret saw her father shrink further, and she strove to reconcile her mother to Helstone. Mrs. Hale claimed the near neighbourhood of so many trees affected her health, and Margaret would try to tempt her forth onto the beautiful, broad, upland common. For a time, this did good; but as autumn drew on and the weather grew changeable, her mother's idea of the place's unhealthiness increased, and she repined more frequently that her husband, more learned than Mr. Hume and a better priest than Mr. Houldsworth, had not met with the preferment those two former neighbours had achieved.

This marring of the peace of home by long hours of discontent was what Margaret was unprepared for. She had known, and rather revelled in the idea, that she would have to give up many luxuries which had been troubles and trammels to her freedom in Harley Street. Her keen enjoyment of every sensuous pleasure was balanced finely, if not overbalanced, by her conscious pride in being able to do without them all if need were. But the cloud never comes from the quarter of the horizon from which we watch for it. There had been slight complaints and passing regrets from her mother regarding trifles connected with Helstone and her father's position when Margaret spent her holidays there previously, but in the general happiness of recalling those times, she had forgotten the small, unpleasant details.

In the latter half of September, the autumnal rains and storms arrived, and Margaret was obliged to remain more in the house than she had hitherto done. Helstone was at some distance from neighbours of their own standard of cultivation.

"It is undoubtedly one of the most out-of-the-way places in England," said Mrs. Hale in one of her plaintive moods. "I can't help regretting constantly that papa has really no one to associate with here; he is so thrown away, seeing no one but farmers and labourers from week's end to week's end. If we only lived at the other side of the parish, it would be something; there we should be almost within walking distance of the Stansfields; certainly the Gormons would be within a walk."

"Gormons," said Margaret, "are those the Gormons who made their fortunes in trade at Southampton? Oh! I'm glad we don't visit them. I don't like shoppy people. I think we are far better off, knowing only cottagers and labourers, and people without pretence."

"You must not be so fastidious, Margaret, dear!" said her mother, secretly thinking of a young and handsome Mr. Gormon whom she had once met at Mr. Hume's.

"No! I call mine a very comprehensive taste; I like all people whose occupations have to do with land; I like soldiers and sailors, and the three learned professions. I'm sure you don't want me to admire butchers and bakers and candlestick-makers, do you, mamma?"

"But the Gormons were neither butchers nor bakers, but very respectable coach-builders."

"Very well. Coach-building is a trade all the same, and I think a much more useless one than that of butchers or bakers. Oh! how tired I used to be of the drives every day in Aunt Shaw's carriage, and how I longed to walk!"

And walk Margaret did, in spite of the weather. She was so happy out of doors, at her father's side, that she almost danced; and with the soft violence of the west wind behind her as she crossed some heath, she seemed borne onwards, lightly and easily as a fallen leaf wafted by the autumnal breeze. But the evenings were rather difficult to fill agreeably. Immediately after tea, her father withdrew into his small library, leaving her and her mother alone. Mrs. Hale had never cared much for books and had discouraged her husband's desire to read aloud to her while she worked early in their married life. At one time they had tried backgammon as a resource, but as Mr. Hale grew to take an increasing interest in his school and parishioners, he found the interruptions arising from these duties were regarded as hardships by his wife, not to be accepted as the natural conditions of his profession. So he withdrew, while the children were yet young, into his library to spend his evenings in reading the speculative and metaphysical books which were his delight.

When Margaret had been there before, she had brought down a great box of books recommended by masters or governesses, finding the summer's day too short to complete her reading before returning to town. Now, there were only the well-bound, little-read English Classics, weeded out of her father's library to fill the small bookshelves in the drawing-room. Thomson's *Seasons*, Hayley's *Cowper*, Middleton's *Cicero* were by far the lightest, newest, and most amusing. The shelves afforded little resource. Margaret told her mother every particular of her London life, to which Mrs. Hale listened with interest, sometimes amused and questioning, at others slightly inclined to compare her sister's circumstances of ease and comfort with the narrower means at Helstone Vicarage. On such evenings, Margaret was apt to stop talking abruptly and listen to the drip-drip of the rain upon the leads of the little bow window. Once or twice, she found herself mechanically counting the repetition of the monotonous sound, wondering if she might venture to ask where Frederick was now, what he was doing, and how long it had been since they had heard from him. But a consciousness that her mother's delicate health and positive dislike to Helstone dated from the time of the mutiny in which Frederick had been engaged—the full account of which Margaret had never heard, and which now seemed doomed to be buried in sad oblivion—made her pause and turn away from the subject each time she approached it. When with her mother, her father seemed the best person to apply to for information; and when with him, she thought she could speak more easily to her mother. Probably there was nothing much to be heard that was new. In one of the letters she had received before leaving Harley Street, her father had told her they had heard from Frederick; he was still at Rio, very well in health, and sent his best love. These were dry bones, not the living intelligence she longed for. Frederick was always spoken of, in the rare times his name was mentioned, as "Poor Frederick." His room was kept exactly as he had left it, regularly dusted and put into order by Dixon, Mrs. Hale's maid, who touched no other part of the household work but always remembered the day she had been engaged by Lady Beresford as ladies' maid to Sir John's wards, the pretty Miss Beresfords, the belles of Rutlandshire. Dixon had always considered Mr. Hale the blight that had fallen upon her young lady's prospects. If Miss Beresford had not been in such a hurry to marry a poor country clergyman, there was no knowing what she might have become. But Dixon was too loyal to desert her in her affliction and downfall. She remained with her, devoted to her interests, always considering herself the good and protecting fairy whose duty it was to baffle the malignant giant, Mr. Hale. Master Frederick had been her favourite and pride, and it was with a little softening of her dignified look and manner that she went weekly to arrange the chamber as carefully as if he might be coming home that very evening.

Margaret could not help believing there had been some late intelligence of Frederick, unknown to her mother, which was making her father anxious and uneasy. Mrs. Hale did not seem to perceive any alteration in her husband's looks or ways.
