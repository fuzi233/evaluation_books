Half a year had sifted away, grain by grain, since the trouble began; and in that time Mr. Crisparkle had grown practised at the patience of waiting—waiting upon law, upon rumour, upon the slow digestion of events in other men’s minds. He sat now in a London antechamber belonging to the chief offices of the Haven of Philanthropy, where the air had the peculiar odour of much paper handled in haste: ink newly dried; sealing-wax warmed and pressed; and, beneath it, the tired breath of too many bodies kept too long under one roof.

It was not a quiet place. Doors clicked; feet scuffed; voices rose and fell like bad singing in a corridor; and the soundscape—harsh with echoes and command—seemed devised to keep the nerves alert in the wrong way, as a factory keeps its wheels awake. Men passed in and out with the forward, shoulder-first manner of those who are always about to “set upon” somebody; and it struck the Minor Canon, remembering certain college days when he had looked on at gloved encounters for sport, that these professional benefactors carried the same combative shape in the back of the head as the professors of fist-work. The Haven might have been training a set of moral bruisers. They moved as if every errand were a snatch, every message a grapple, and every interview a round.

There was a stir over some coming rural demonstration—some “little Mill” of virtue, to be held on a circuit of villages—and the talk about it had the flavour of betting: this man “good” for such blows of rhetoric, that man likely to “finish” a resolution with a flourish. An official manager, black-coated and eminent for platform stratagems, carried himself with the practised air of one who could rope off a ring and keep the crowd in order. Only, for all their likeness to the Fancy, these philanthropists wanted three things the prize-men commonly had. They were not in training—too much soft flesh, too much of that suet-pudding condition that sits heavily on the spirit as well as the waistcoat. They had, next, less good temper; and, lastly, a worse code: a manner of fighting that allowed them to bore their man not merely to the ropes but to distraction—hitting when he was down, stamping, gouging, worrying him out of sight and behind his back. In that, the old brutal art looked almost clean by comparison.

So occupied was Mr. Crisparkle with these observations—and with the ugly, jarring music of the place, as if charity must always be beaten out upon a drum—that he did not hear his name until it had been called twice. Then, answering, he was guided by a shabby, underpaid stipendiary of the Haven—one of those sorry servants who, being ill-used by their own cause, might have seemed engaged by its enemy—into the room of Mr. Honeythunder.

“Sir,” said Mr. Honeythunder, in the tremendous voice with which he habitually scolded a meeting into admiration, “sit down.”

Mr. Crisparkle sat.

Mr. Honeythunder signed, with a ponderous haste, the tail-end of a few score circulars—part of a few thousand—calling upon a corresponding number of families without means to come forward instantly, find money they had not, and be Philanthropists, or go to the Devil. Another shabby stipendiary gathered the heap into a basket and carried it away like a man removing dinner from a table at which he would never eat.

“Now, Mr. Crisparkle,” said Mr. Honeythunder, wheeling half round and planting his hands on his knees, brows knitted as if the room were a platform and the Minor Canon an opposition to be shouted down, “we entertain different views, you and I, sir, of the sanctity of human life.”

“Do we?” returned Mr. Crisparkle.

“We do, sir.”

“Might I ask,” said the Minor Canon, “what your views are?”

“That human life is a thing to be held sacred, sir.”

“Might I ask,” pursued Mr. Crisparkle, just as quietly, “what you suppose mine to be?”

“By George, sir!” cried the Philanthropist, tightening his posture, “they are best known to yourself.”

“Readily admitted. But you began by saying we differ; therefore you must have set up some views as mine. Pray, what views have you set up as mine?”

“Here is a man—and a young man,” said Mr. Honeythunder, as if youth were an aggravation and age a mitigation, “swept off the face of the earth by a deed of violence. What do you call that?”

“Murder,” said Mr. Crisparkle.

“What do you call the doer of that deed, sir?”

“A murderer.”

“I’m glad to hear you admit so much, sir,” returned Mr. Honeythunder, lowering upon him with offensive satisfaction, “and I candidly tell you I didn’t expect it.”

“Be so good as to explain what you mean by those expressions.”

“I don’t sit here, sir,” roared Mr. Honeythunder, “to be browbeaten.”

“As the only other person present, no one can possibly know that better than I do,” returned Mr. Crisparkle. “But I interrupt your explanation.”

“Murder!” proceeded Mr. Honeythunder, relapsing into a kind of declamatory trance, folding his arms as on the platform, nodding after each exploded syllable as if an audience were taking notes. “Bloodshed! Abel! Cain! I hold no terms with Cain. I repudiate with a shudder the red hand when it is offered me.”

Mr. Crisparkle, instead of leaping up to cheer, merely changed the quiet crossing of his legs. “Don’t let me interrupt your explanation—when you begin it.”

“The Commandments say, no murder. NO murder, sir!” pursued Mr. Honeythunder, pausing as if Mr. Crisparkle had openly maintained the opposite: You may do a little murder, and then leave off.

“And they also say, you shall bear no false witness,” observed Mr. Crisparkle.

“Enough!” bellowed Mr. Honeythunder, with that solemnity which would have brought down applause in a hall. “E—e—nough! My late wards being now of age, and I being released from a trust I cannot contemplate without a thrill of horror, there are the accounts which you have undertaken to accept on their behalf; and there is a statement of the balance which you have undertaken to receive, and which you cannot receive too soon. And let me tell you, sir, I wish that, as a man and a Minor Canon, you were better employed.” Nod. “Better employed.” Another nod. “Bet—ter em—ployed!” A third; and the three together made a kind of blowsy cadence.

Mr. Crisparkle rose with a little colour in his face, but with his temper held as firmly as a cook holds a pan from boiling over by lifting it a hair from the fiercest flame.

“Mr. Honeythunder,” he said, taking up the papers, “my being better or worse employed than I am is a matter of taste and opinion. You might think me better employed in enrolling myself a member of your Society.”

“Ay, indeed, sir!” retorted Mr. Honeythunder, shaking his head as if to threaten.

“I think otherwise.”

“Or,” said Mr. Honeythunder, shaking it again, “I might think one of your profession better employed in devoting himself to the discovery and punishment of guilt than in leaving that duty to be undertaken by a layman.”

“I may regard my profession from a point of view which teaches me that its first duty is towards those in necessity and tribulation—desolate and oppressed,” answered Mr. Crisparkle. “However, as I have quite clearly satisfied myself that it is no part of my profession to make professions, I say no more of that. But I owe it to Mr. Neville, and to Mr. Neville’s sister—and in a much lower degree to myself—to say to you that I know I was in the full possession and understanding of Mr. Neville’s mind and heart at the time of this occurrence; and that, without in the least colouring or concealing what was to be deplored in him and required to be corrected, I feel certain that his tale is true. Feeling that certainty, I befriend him. As long as that certainty shall last, I will befriend him. And if any consideration could shake me in this resolve, I should be so ashamed of myself for my meanness that no man’s good opinion—no, nor no woman’s—so gained, could compensate me for the loss of my own.”

There was no flourish in it: no stage-smack of the palm, no turning to the gallery. It was the plain courage that belongs to an honest man, and it had the weight of plain bread—made of common flour, well kneaded, and baked through.

“Then who do you make out did the deed?” demanded Mr. Honeythunder, turning on him abruptly.

“Heaven forbid,” returned Mr. Crisparkle, “that in my desire to clear one man I should lightly criminate another! I accuse no one.”

“Tcha!” ejaculated Mr. Honeythunder, with disgust; for it was not the usual method of the Brotherhood, who liked their enemies served up ready-trussed.

“And, sir, you are not a disinterested witness, we must bear in mind.”

“How am I an interested one?” asked the Minor Canon, smiling in simple surprise.

“There was a certain stipend, sir, paid to you for your pupil, which may have warped your judgment a bit,” said Mr. Honeythunder, coarsely.

“Perhaps I expect to retain it still?” Mr. Crisparkle returned, newly enlightened. “Do you mean that too?”

“Well, sir,” said Mr. Honeythunder, rising and thrusting his hands deep into his trouser pockets, “I don’t go about measuring people for caps. If people find I have any about me that fit ’em, they can put ’em on and wear ’em, if they like. That’s their look out: not mine.”

Mr. Crisparkle eyed him with just indignation—steady as a man listening for truth in a room full of noise.

“Mr. Honeythunder, I hoped when I came in here that I might be under no necessity of commenting on the introduction of platform manners among the decent forbearances of private life. But you have given me such a specimen of both, that I should be a fit subject for both if I remained silent. They are detestable.”

“They don’t suit you, I dare say, sir.”

“They are,” repeated Mr. Crisparkle, without noticing the interruption, “detestable. They violate equally the justice that should belong to Christians and the restraints that should belong to gentlemen. You assume a great crime to have been committed by one whom I, acquainted with the attendant circumstances and having reasons on my side, devoutly believe to be innocent. Because I differ from you on that vital point, what is your platform resource? Instantly to turn upon me, charging that I have no sense of the enormity of the crime itself, but am its aider and abettor! So, another time—taking me as representing your opponent in other cases—you set up a platform credulity; a moved and seconded and carried-unanimously profession of faith in some ridiculous delusion or mischievous imposition. I decline to believe it, and you fall back upon your resource of proclaiming that I believe nothing; that because I will not bow down to a false god of your making, I deny the true God! Another time you discover that War is a calamity, and propose to abolish it by a string of twisted resolutions tossed into the air like the tail of a kite. I do not admit the discovery to be yours, and I have not a grain of faith in your remedy. Again, you represent me as revelling in the horrors of a battlefield like a fiend incarnate! Another time, in another of your undiscriminating rushes, you would punish the sober for the drunken. I claim consideration for the comfort, convenience, and refreshment of the sober; and you presently proclaim that I have a depraved desire to turn Heaven’s creatures into swine and wild beasts! In all such cases your movers, and seconders, and supporters—your regular Professors of all degrees—run amuck like so many mad Malays; habitually attributing the lowest motives with the utmost recklessness—let me call your attention to a recent instance in yourself for which you should blush—and quoting figures which you know to be as wilfully one-sided as any complicated account that should be all Creditor and no Debtor, or all Debtor and no Creditor. Therefore it is, Mr. Honeythunder, that I consider the platform a sufficiently bad example and a sufficiently bad school, even in public life; but, carried into private life, it becomes an unendurable nuisance.”

“These are strong words, sir!” cried Mr. Honeythunder.

“I hope so,” said Mr. Crisparkle. “Good morning.”

He walked out of the Haven at a great rate, as if he would leave its din behind by speed alone. Then his breath fell into its usual wholesome rhythm, and he resumed his brisk pace with a smile that came, half in amusement, half in that harmless vanity which hopes one’s blow has told. In his mind’s ear he heard, as a faint counter-music to the Haven’s brazen clamour, the quiet domestic voice of that china shepherdess at Cloisterham who had once been the witness of his gentler moods; and he wondered what she would have said if she had seen him pounding Mr. Honeythunder in that late lively affair, trimming the Philanthropic Jacket to his own satisfaction.

From the loud streets he took himself to Staple Inn; but not to the chambers of P. J. T. and Mr. Grewgious. He climbed many a stair that creaked as if it complained in a dialect of old wood—each step a different note, each landing a change of pitch—until he reached some attic rooms in a corner. The door stood unbolted. He lifted the latch and went in, and there, beside a table, stood Neville Landless.

An air of retreat lay upon the rooms, like flour-dust after a day’s baking: it dulled the edges of things, softened the light, and yet spoke of long labour. The ceilings sloped; the locks and grates were rusty; heavy bins and beams, slowly mouldering, gave the place a prisonous look. The young man wore it too: worn in face, haggard as a prisoner who measures time by shadows on a wall. But the sunlight made its own stubborn entrance at the ugly garret-window that thrust a little penthouse among the tiles; and beyond, on the smoke-blackened parapet, some sparrows hopped with rheumatic perseverance, like small feathered invalids who had left their crutches in their nests. Nearby there was a play of living leaves—some neighbour’s tree finding a way upward—making an imperfect music, not the full country melody, but enough to thin the stale air and remind one what quiet can be.

The rooms were sparely furnished, but they held a good store of books. They had the look of a poor student’s larder: plain staples, well chosen, meant to keep mind and hope alive. Mr. Crisparkle’s eyes rested on them with the friendly beam that betrayed him as chooser, lender, donor—or all three.

“How goes it, Neville?”

“I am in good heart, Mr. Crisparkle, and working away.”

“I wish your eyes were not quite so large and not quite so bright,” said the Minor Canon, slowly releasing the hand he had taken.

“They brighten at the sight of you,” returned Neville. “If you were to fall away from me, they would soon be dull enough.”

“Rally, rally!” urged Mr. Crisparkle, with the stimulating tone of one who would rather call courage up than pity down. “Fight for it, Neville!”

“If I were dying, I feel as if a word from you would rally me; if my pulse had stopped, I feel as if your touch would make it beat again,” said Neville. “But I have rallied, and am doing famously.”

Mr. Crisparkle turned him a little towards the light, as if he were gauging colour in a cheek the way a careful cook gauges the first returning blush in meat set too near the fire.

“I want to see a ruddier touch here,” he said, indicating his own healthy cheek by way of pattern. “I want more sun to shine upon you.”

Neville’s spirit drooped suddenly; his voice sank. “I am not hardy enough for that, yet. I may become so, but I cannot bear it yet. If you had gone through those Cloisterham streets as I did; if you had seen, as I did, those averted eyes, and the better sort of people silently giving me too much room to pass, that I might not touch them—you wouldn’t think it unreasonable that I cannot go about in the daylight.”

“My poor fellow!” said Mr. Crisparkle, and the pity in it was so unfeigned that Neville caught his hand. “I never said it was unreasonable; never thought so. But I should like you to do it.”

“And that would give me the strongest motive. But I cannot yet. I cannot persuade myself that the eyes of even the stream of strangers I pass in this vast city look at me without suspicion. I feel marked and tainted, even when I go out—as I do only—at night. But the darkness covers me then, and I take courage from it.”

Mr. Crisparkle laid a hand upon his shoulder and looked down at him.

“If I could have changed my name,” said Neville, “I would have done so. But as you wisely pointed out, I can’t—for it would look like guilt. If I could have gone to some distant place, I might have found relief; but it is not to be thought of, for the same reason. Hiding and escaping would be the construction. It seems a little hard to be so tied to a stake, and innocent; but I don’t complain.”

“And you must expect no miracle to help you,” said the Minor Canon, compassionately.

“No, sir. The ordinary fulness of time and circumstances is all I have to trust to.”

“It will right you at last, Neville.”

“So I believe, and I hope I may live to know it.”

Yet he saw the shadow his words cast on the other’s face, and—perhaps feeling that the broad hand upon his shoulder was not quite so steady now as its strength should have made it—he roused himself.

“Excellent circumstances for study, anyhow! And you know what need I have of study in all ways. Not to mention that you have advised me to study for the difficult profession of the law, specially, and that of course I am guiding myself by the advice of such a friend and helper. Such a good friend and helper!”

He took the fortifying hand from his shoulder and kissed it. Mr. Crisparkle smiled towards the books, but not with the earlier brightness.

“I gather from your silence on the subject that my late guardian is adverse, Mr. Crisparkle?”

“Your late guardian,” returned the Minor Canon, “is a—most unreasonable person, and it signifies nothing to any reasonable person whether he is ad-verse, per-verse, or the re-verse.”

“Well for me,” sighed Neville, half wearily and half cheerily, “that I have enough with economy to live upon, while I wait to be learned, and wait to be righted! Else I might prove the proverb, that while the grass grows, the steed starves.”

As he spoke, he opened his books, and soon was deep among interleaved pages and pencilled notes; and Mr. Crisparkle sat beside him, expounding, correcting, advising—turning dry matter into something that could be chewed and swallowed, and might one day sustain a career. The Minor Canon’s Cathedral duties made such visits difficult and rare—coming only at intervals of many weeks—but they were precious and serviceable, as bread is to a hungry man.

When their work for that time was done, they stood leaning on the window-sill, looking down upon a patch of garden that seemed a small green afterthought in the brick and slate.

“Next week,” said Mr. Crisparkle, “you will cease to be alone, and will have a devoted companion.”

“And yet,” returned Neville, “this seems an uncongenial place to bring my sister to.”

“I don’t think so,” said the Minor Canon. “There is duty to be done here; and there are womanly feeling, sense, and courage wanted here.”

“I meant,” explained Neville, “that the surroundings are so dull and unwomanly, and that Helena can have no suitable friend or society here.”

“You have only to remember,” said Mr. Crisparkle, “that you are here yourself, and that she has to draw you into the sunlight.”

They were silent a little; the leaves made their thin music again, and the city’s larger roar, muffled by height and distance, came and went like a far-off, unkind sea.

“When we first spoke together,” Mr. Crisparkle began anew, “you told me that your sister had risen out of the disadvantages of your past lives as superior to you as the tower of Cloisterham Cathedral is higher than the chimneys of Minor Canon Corner. Do you remember that?”

“Right well!”

“I was inclined to think it at the time an enthusiastic flight. No matter what I think it now. What I would emphasise is, that under the head of Pride your sister is a great and opportune example to you.”

“Under all heads included in the composition of a fine character, she is.”

“Say so; but take this one. Your sister has learnt how to govern what is proud in her nature. She can dominate it even when it is wounded through her sympathy with you. No doubt she has suffered deeply in those same streets where you suffered. No doubt her life is darkened by the cloud that darkens yours. But bending her pride into a grand composure—not haughty, not aggressive, but a sustained confidence in you and in the truth—she has won her way through those streets until she passes along them as high in the general respect as any one who treads them. Every day and hour of her life since Edwin Drood’s disappearance, she has faced malignity and folly—for you—as only a brave nature, well directed, can. So it will be with her to the end. Another and weaker kind of pride might sink broken-hearted, but never such a pride as hers: which knows no shrinking, and can get no mastery over her.”

The pale cheek beside him flushed at the comparison and at the sting of its implied rebuke.

“I will do all I can to imitate her,” said Neville.

“Do so, and be a truly brave man, as she is a truly brave woman,” answered Mr. Crisparkle stoutly. “It is growing dark. Will you go my way with me, when it is quite dark? Mind! it is not I who wait for darkness.”

Neville said he would accompany him directly. But Mr. Crisparkle, remembering courtesies owed even when one would rather avoid certain thresholds, said he had a moment’s call to make on Mr. Grewgious, and would run across to that gentleman’s chambers, rejoining Neville on his own doorstep if Neville would come down to meet him.

Mr. Grewgious, bolt upright as usual, sat taking his wine in the dusk at his open window: wineglass and decanter on the round table at his elbow; himself and his legs on the window-seat; only one hinge in his whole body, like a bootjack. The sound outside was quieter here—Staple Inn keeping, as it could, a certain old-fashioned restraint upon the street’s incessant tongue.

“How do you do, reverend sir?” said Mr. Grewgious, offering hospitality with as much seriousness as if he meant it, and receiving the decline as cordially as he made the offer. “And how is your charge getting on over the way in the set that I had the pleasure of recommending to you as vacant and eligible?”

Mr. Crisparkle replied that he was improving himself steadily.

“I am glad you approve of them,” said Mr. Grewgious, “because I entertain a sort of fancy for having him under my eye.”

As Mr. Grewgious would have had to turn his eye up a great deal to see those attic windows, the phrase was plainly figurative.

“And how did you leave Mr. Jasper, reverend sir?” asked Mr. Grewgious.

Mr. Crisparkle had left him pretty well.

“And where did you leave Mr. Jasper, reverend sir?”

At Cloisterham.

“And when did you leave Mr. Jasper, reverend sir?”

That morning.

“Umps!” said Mr. Grewgious, his preoccupied glance fixed out at the window. “He didn’t say he was coming, perhaps?”

“Coming where?”

“Anywhere, for instance?” returned Mr. Grewgious.

“No.”

“Because here he is,” said Mr. Grewgious, without shifting that outward look, “and he don’t look agreeable, does he?”

Mr. Crisparkle craned towards the window to see.
