To truly grasp the architecture of Louis Moore's suspended hope, one must first understand the absolute fragility of the atmosphere surrounding Fieldhead. The very next day following the grand, seismic quarrel regarding Sir Philip Nunnely, a tentative reconciliation was stitched together between uncle and niece—a fragile truce that defied the logic of their previous hostilities. Shirley, possessing a heart that refused to harbor the capacity for inhospitality (save in the singular, glaring instance of Mr. Donne), implored the entire party to remain. She begged with such visceral earnestness that it was palpable she sought their presence for reasons beyond mere politeness. They accepted her plea, drawn by an invisible tether. Indeed, Uncle Sympson could not bring himself to depart, leaving her entirely unguarded and free to pursue Robert Moore as soon as that gentleman might reassert his presumed claims to her hand—a prospect Mr. Sympson prayed fervently would never materialize. Thus, they stayed, anchored by the gravity of her desire.

In his initial fury against the house of Moore, Mr. Sympson had conducted himself with such coarse insolence toward Mr. Louis that the latter—patient of labor and suffering, yet intolerant of such brutality—promptly resigned his post. Now, he could only be induced to resume and retain it until the family vacated Yorkshire. Mrs. Sympson's entreaties secured this concession; his own attachment to his pupil added weight to the compromise; and likely, a third motive, stronger than either, drove him. Perhaps he knew he would find it excruciatingly difficult to abandon Fieldhead at this precise juncture.

For some time, the household operated with a deceptive smoothness. Miss Keeldar's health was restored; her spirits resumed their fluid motion. Moore had found means to liberate her from every nervous apprehension, and indeed, from the moment she entrusted him with her confidence, every fear seemed to take flight. Her heart became lightsome, her manner careless, mirroring the innocence of a child that, oblivious to its own mortality, trusts all responsibility to its guardians. He and William Farren, through whom he investigated the state of Phoebe, agreed in asserting that the dog was not mad—that it was merely ill-treatment which had driven her from home, for it was proven her master frequently chastised her violently. Their assertion might or might not be true. The groom and gamekeeper affirmed the contrary, claiming that if hers was not a clear case of hydrophobia, no such disease existed. Yet, to this evidence, Louis Moore turned an incredulous ear. He reported to Shirley only what was encouraging. She believed him; and right or wrong, it is certain that in her case, the bite proved innocuous.

November yielded to December. The Sympsons were now truly departing; it was incumbent upon them to be home by Christmas. Their packages were being packed; departure was imminent. One winter evening, during the final week of their stay, Louis Moore again produced his little blank book and discoursed with it as follows:

"She is lovelier than ever. Since that little cloud was dispelled, all temporary waste and waness have vanished. It was marvelous to witness how swiftly the magical energy of youth raised her elasticity and revived her bloom.

"After breakfast this morning, having seen her, listened to her, and, so to speak, felt her in every sentient atom of my frame, I passed from her sunny presence into the chill drawing-room. Taking up a small gilt volume, I found it contained a selection of lyrics. I read a poem or two; whether the spell resided in me or in the verse, I know not, but my heart filled genially, my pulse rose. I glowed, notwithstanding the frosty air. I, too, am young as yet. Though she claimed she never considered me young, I am barely thirty. There are moments when life, for no other reason than my own youth, beams with sweet hues upon me.

"It was time to go to the schoolroom. I went. That same room is rather pleasant in the morning. The sun then shines through the low lattice; the books are in order; no papers are strewn about; the fire is clear and clean; no cinders have fallen, no ashes accumulated. I found Henry there, and he had brought with him Miss Keeldar. They were together.

"I said she was lovelier than ever. She is. A fine rose, not deep but delicate, opens on her cheek. Her eye, always dark, clear, and speaking, utters now a language I cannot render; it is the utterance, seen not heard, through which angels must have communed when there was 'silence in heaven.' Her hair was always dusk as night and fine as silk, her neck always fair, flexible, polished; but both now possess a new charm. The tresses are soft as shadow, the shoulders they fall upon wear a goddess's grace. Once I only *saw* her beauty, now I *feel* it.

"Henry was repeating his lesson to her before bringing it to me. One of her hands was occupied with the book; he held the other. That boy receives more than his share of privileges; he dares to caress and is caressed. What indulgence and compassion she shows him! Too much. If this continued, Henry in a few years, when his soul was fully formed, would offer it on her altar, as I have already offered mine.

"I saw her eyelid flutter when I entered, but she did not look up; *now* she hardly ever gives me a glance. She seems to grow silent too; to *me* she rarely speaks, and when I am present, she says little to others. In my gloomy moments, I attribute this change to indifference, aversion, or worse? In my sunny intervals, I assign it another meaning. I say, were I her equal, I could find in this shyness coyness, and in that coyness, love. As it stands, dare I look for it? What could I do with it if found?

"This morning I dared at least contrive an hour's communion for her and me; I dared not only *wish* but *will* an interview with her. I summoned solitude to guard us. Very decisively, I called Henry to the door. Without hesitation, I said, 'Go where you will, my boy; but, till I call you, return not here.'

"Henry, I could see, did not like his dismissal. That boy is young, but a thinker; his meditative eye shines on me strangely sometimes. He half-feels what links me to Shirley; he half-guesses that there is a dearer delight in the reserve with which I am treated than in all the endearments he is allowed. The young, lame, half-grown lion would growl at me now and then, because I have tamed his lioness and am her keeper, did not the habit of discipline and the instinct of affection hold him subdued. Go, Henry; you must learn to take your share of the bitterness of life with all of Adam's race that have gone before or will come after you. Your destiny can be no exception to the common lot; be grateful that your love is overlooked thus early, before it can claim any affinity to passion. An hour's fret, a pang of envy, suffice to express what you feel. Jealousy hot as the sun above the equator, rage destructive as the tropic storm—the climate of your sensations ignores—as yet.

"I took my usual seat at the desk, quite in my usual way. I am blessed with the power to cover all inward ebullition with outward calm. No one who looks at my slow face can guess the vortex sometimes whirling in my heart, engulfing thought and wrecking prudence. Pleasant is it to have the gift to proceed peacefully and powerfully in your course without alarming by one eccentric movement. It was not my present intention to utter one word of love to her, or to reveal one glimpse of the fire in which I waste myself. Presumptuous I never have been; presumptuous I never will be. Rather than even *seem* selfish and interested, I would resolutely rise, gird my loins, part and leave her, and seek, on the other side of the globe, a new life, cold and barren as the rock the salt tide daily washes. My design this morning was to take of her a near scrutiny—to read a line in the page of her heart. Before I left, I determined to know *what* I was leaving.

"I had some quills to make into pens. Most men's hands would have trembled when their hearts were so stirred; mine went to work steadily, and my voice, when I called it into exercise, was firm.

"'This day week you will be alone at Fieldhead, Miss Keeldar.'

"'Yes: I rather think my uncle's intention to go is a settled one now.'

"'He leaves you dissatisfied.'

"'He is not pleased with me.'

"'He departs as he came—no better for his journey. This is mortifying.'

"'I trust the failure of his plans will take from him all inclination to lay new ones.'

"'In his way, Mr. Sympson honestly wished you well. All he has done or intended to do, he believed to be for the best.'

"'You are kind to undertake the defense of a man who has permitted himself to treat you with so much insolence.'

"'I never feel shocked at, or bear malice for, what is spoken in character; and most perfectly in character was that vulgar and violent onset against me, when he had quitted you worsted.'
