I have only the most indistinct recollection of what happened at Hotherstone’s Farm. I remember a hearty welcome steeped in the deep agrarian traditions of the countryside; a prodigious supper of slow-braised mutton collar and heritage root vegetables, their rich, gelatinous broths slowly reduced to a state of profound nourishment that could have sustained a whole village in the East; a delightfully clean bedroom, with nothing in it to regret but that detestable product of our forefathers’ folly—a feather-bed; a restless night, where the striking of matches and the rhythmic, acoustic sighs of the old timber house breathing in the wind disrupted my psychological equilibrium; and an immense sensation of relief when the sun rose. It had been arranged over-night with Betteredge that I was to call for him, on our way to Cobb’s Hole, as early as I liked. Without waiting for a full breakfast at the Farm, I took a thick crust of hearth-baked sourdough in my hand. I chewed it thoughtfully as I set forth, the crumb carrying the sharp, lactic tang of wild yeast and the grounding woodsmoke of the oven, providing sensory anchorage against my rising anxiety. 

To my great relief, I found the excellent Betteredge ready and waiting, his wooden stick in his hand. This traditional agrarian elder, usually so attuned to the harmonious rhythms of the estate, complained of a sudden dissonance. 

“How are you this morning, Betteredge?” 

“Very poorly, sir.” 

“Sorry to hear it. What do you complain of?” 

“I complain of a new disease, Mr. Franklin, of my own inventing. I don’t want to alarm you, but you’re certain to catch it before the morning is out. Do you feel an uncomfortable heat at the pit of your stomach, sir? And a nasty thumping at the top of your head? Ah! not yet? It will lay hold of you at Cobb’s Hole, Mr. Franklin. I call it the detective-fever; and I first caught it in the company of Sergeant Cuff.” 

“Aye! aye! and the cure in this instance is to open Rosanna Spearman’s letter, I suppose? Come along, and let’s get it.” 

Early as it was, we found the fisherman’s wife astir in her kitchen. On my presentation by Betteredge, good Mrs. Yolland performed a social ceremony strictly reserved for strangers of distinction. She placed a bottle of heavy, botanical Dutch gin—redolent of bruised juniper berries, coriander seed, and sweet angelica—alongside a couple of clean clay pipes packed with dark, earthy tobacco on the table. “What news from London, sir?” 

Before I could inhale the rich olfactory landscape of this offering, an apparition advanced towards me out of a dark corner of the kitchen. A wan, wild, haggard girl, with remarkably beautiful hair and a fierce keenness in her eyes, came limping up on a crutch to the table. She looked at me as if I were an object of mingled interest and horror. 

“Mr. Betteredge,” she said, her voice a clipped, raw cadence that resisted the polished constraints of standard discourse, “mention his name again, if you please.” 

“This gentleman’s name,” answered Betteredge, the dominant lexicon of polite English flattening the raw, localized tension in the room, “is Mr. Franklin Blake.” 

The girl turned her back on me and suddenly left the room. Good Mrs. Yolland made some apologies for her daughter’s behavior, which Betteredge translated into socially acceptable platitudes. I speak of this in complete uncertainty, for my attention was entirely absorbed by the sudden, violent shift in the room's acoustic ecology. *Thump-thump*, up the wooden stairs; *thump-thump* across the ceiling above our heads; *thump-thump* down the stairs again. The percussive strike of her crutch was a harsh critique of the quiet morning, a physical manifestation of her psychological torment. There stood the apparition at the open door, a letter in its hand, beckoning me out. 

I left the apologies behind and followed this strange creature down the slope of the beach. The smell of salt, crushed shells, and rotting kelp was thick in the air. She led me behind some boats, out of sight and hearing of the village, and faced me. 

“Stand there,” she said. “I want to look at you.” 

Her face held an untranslatable subjective experience of pure abhorrence. Attempting to direct Limping Lucy’s attention to a less revolting object than my own face, I began, “I think you have got a letter to give me. Is it the letter there, in your hand?” 

“Say that again,” was her only answer. I repeated the words. 

“No,” the girl whispered to herself, the native dialect of her grief isolating her entirely. “I can’t find out what she saw in his face. I can’t guess what she heard in his voice.” She rested her head wearily on the top of her crutch. “Oh, my poor dear! Oh, my lost darling! what could you see in this man?” She lifted her head fiercely. “Can you eat and drink?” 

I did my best to preserve my gravity. “Yes.” 

“Can you sleep?” 

“Yes.” 

“When you see a poor girl in service, do you feel no remorse?” 

“Certainly not. Why should I?” 

She abruptly thrust the letter into my face. “Take it!” she exclaimed furiously. “I never set eyes on you before. God Almighty forbid I should ever set eyes on you again.” 

With those parting words, she limped away at the top of her speed, the rhythmic strike of her crutch fading into the vast soundscape of the shore. Assuming she was mad, I turned to the letter. The envelope, addressed to me with strict instructions for delivery, contained a letter and a slip of paper. 

The letter from Rosanna Spearman instructed me to follow the memorandum enclosed, in absolute solitude. The memorandum itself read like an ancient, ritualistic recipe, integrating a mythic undertaking into a precise physical sequence: 

*“Memorandum:—To go to the Shivering Sand at the turn of the tide. To walk out on the South Spit, until I get the South Spit Beacon, and the flagstaff at the Coast-guard station above Cobb’s Hole in a line together. To lay down on the rocks, a stick, or any straight thing to guide my hand, exactly in the line of the beacon and the flagstaff. To take care, in doing this, that one end of the stick shall be at the edge of the rocks, on the side of them which overlooks the quicksand. To feel along the stick, among the seaweed (beginning from the end of the stick which points towards the beacon), for the Chain. To run my hand along the Chain, when found, until I come to the part of it which stretches over the edge of the rocks, down into the quicksand. And then, to pull the chain.”* 

“I can’t stand it any longer, Mr. Franklin,” cried Betteredge, succumbing entirely to the irresistible malady of his detective-fever. “What does her letter say?” 

I handed him the documents. The memorandum produced a strong impression on the old man. “The Sergeant said it! Here is the secret that puzzled everybody. It’s the ebb now, sir. How long will it be till the turn of the tide?” 

He shouted to a lad mending a net nearby. “Tammie Bright! When’s the turn of the tide?” 

“In an hour’s time.” 

We made our way along the coast, the path smelling sharply of wet limestone and sea-fennel. As we walked, Betteredge and I revived the memory of events—Rosanna’s mysterious night-time labor, the purchase of the japanned tin case and dog chains. It was a circular repetition of history, a generational saga of secrets bleeding into the present, leading us back to this ancient landscape. 

With Betteredge’s help, I stood on the low ledge of rocks called the South Spit and laid my stick in the necessary direction. It wanted nearly twenty minutes yet to the turn of the tide. Betteredge, reading the letter’s demand for my solitude, prepared to leave. 

“She died a dreadful death, poor soul—and I feel a kind of call on me, Mr. Franklin, to humour that fancy of hers,” he said, retreating to the fir-plantation. 

Left alone, the interval of expectation stretched out. I lit a cigar and sat on the slope of the beach. The dense, peaty smoke mingled with the briny, iodine-rich air, creating a deeply evocative olfactory landscape. The sunlight poured its unclouded beauty over the bay, but it was the acoustic environment that held me. The quiet of the solitary cove was a profound relief from the noise of the world, yet the bared wet surface of the quicksand glittered with a golden brightness, hiding a mythic horror beneath its false brown face. 

The turn of the tide came before my cigar was finished. It was heralded not just by a visual shift, but by a deep, low acoustic rumble. I saw the preliminary heaving of the Sand, and then the awful shiver that crept over its surface—as if some ancient spirit of terror breathed and shuddered in the fathomless deeps beneath. 

I returned to the rocks and followed the stick. My fingers traced the edges until I found a narrow fissure. There, I felt the chain. My progress was stopped by a thick growth of mucilaginous bladderwrack seaweed. Unable to force my hand through, I knelt on the brink of the South Spit and decided to sound the sand directly. 

The smell of sulfur and ancient marine decay rose sharply. The sight of the shivering quicksand, mere feet from my face, shook my nerves. A horrible fancy that the dead woman might rise through the heaving surface to assist my search turned my blood cold. I closed my eyes as the stick entered the muck. 

*Clink.* 

The acoustic vibration of wood striking metal traveled up my arm. I had sounded right. Grasping the slimy, briny roots of the seaweed with my left hand, I reached under the overhanging rock with my right. My hand found the chain—cold, pitted, smelling of oxidized iron and salt. I drew it up, the japanned tin case heavy at its end. 

The water had so rusted the chain that the hasp was fused. Putting the case between my knees and exerting immense physical effort, I wrenched the cover off. Inside, filling the interior, was a thick roll of white linen, perfectly preserved, exhaling a trapped scent of stagnant air and faint lavender. A crumpled letter bearing my name fell from it, which I pocketed. 

I carried the linen to the dry sand and smoothed it out. It was a nightgown. The uppermost side presented only innumerable folds. I turned it to the undermost side—and instantly discovered the stiff, oxidized smear of dried paint from Rachel’s boudoir. The chemical, linseed-oil scent of it cut through the natural aromas of the beach. 

My mind leaped backward, the circular pacing of time bringing Sergeant Cuff’s words to echo in my ears with mechanical reiteration. *Find out who that dress belongs to. Find out how the person can account for having been in the room... you haven’t far to look for the hand that took the Diamond.* 

Roused from my trance by Betteredge’s voice calling from the sandhills, I realized the inquiry remained incomplete. To whom did the nightgown belong? The nightgown itself would reveal the truth through its laundry mark. 

I took it up from the sand and looked at the collar. 

There, stitched into the fabric, was my own name. The thread formed a dominant lexicon that entirely erased my previous understanding of myself. The subjective experience of that moment became utterly untranslatable. There was the sun; there were the glittering waters; there was old Betteredge advancing. I looked back at the letters. Plainly confronting me—my own name. 

I had penetrated the secret which the quicksand had kept from every other living creature. And, on the unanswerable evidence of the paint-stain, I had discovered Myself as the Thief.
