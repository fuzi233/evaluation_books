Yes, the hermit of Gouda was indeed the vicar of Gouda, though he remained unaware of his own identity due to the absolute nature of his seclusion. My readers know that the moment the frenzy of his passion subsided, he was seized with profound remorse for having yielded to it. Yet, perhaps only those who have ascended to the highest peaks of spiritual aspiration and then suddenly tumbled down can truly comprehend the terror that gripped him. He felt as though self-confidence had betrayed him to the very brink of a precipice.

"Ah, good Jerome!" he cried aloud. "How much better you knew me than I knew myself! How bitter, yet how wholesome, was your admonition!"

Accustomed to searching his own heart, he immediately discerned that the true source of his fury lay in Margaret. "I love her then better than God," he confessed despairingly. "Better than the Church! From such a love, what can possibly spring for me, or for her?" He shuddered violently at the thought. "Let the strong battle temptation; 'tis for the weak to flee. And who is weaker than I have proven myself? What is my penitence, my religion? Merely a pack of cards built degree by degree into a fair-seeming structure; and lo! one breath of earthly love, and it lies in the dust. I must begin again, and on a surer foundation."

He resolved to leave Holland immediately and spend years of his life in some distant convent before returning. By that time, the temptations of earthly passion would be doubly baffled; older and a better monk, he would be more master of his affections. Furthermore, seeing himself abandoned, Margaret would likely marry another and love someone else. The very anguish this final thought caused him demonstrated that the self-searcher and self-denier was indeed walking the path of religious duty. However, in leaving her for his immortal good—and hers—he was not to neglect her temporal welfare. Indeed, the sweet thought that he could provide for her comfort and enrich her in this world's goods, which she was not bound to despise, sustained him through the bitter struggle of turning his back on her without offering a single kind word or glance.

"Oh, what will she think of me?" he groaned. "Shall I not seem to her, of all creatures, the most heartless, inhuman? But so best! Ay, better she should hate me, miserable that I am. Heaven is merciful, and gives my broken heart this comfort: I can make that villain restore her dowry, and she shall never lose another true lover because of poverty. Another? Ah me! Ah me! God and the saints to my aid!"

How he fared on this errand has been previously related. But first, as you may recall, he went at night to hear the confession of the hermit of Gouda. He found him dying and never left until he had closed his eyes and buried him beneath the floor of the small oratory attached to his cell. It was the peaceful end of a stormy life. The hermit had been a soldier, and even then he wore a steel corselet next his skin, declaring that he was now Christ's soldier as he had once been Satan's. When Clement had heard his confession and prayed beside him, the dying man, in his turn, sought counsel from one who was passing away in such a pious frame. The hermit advised him to become his successor in this peaceful retreat. "His had been a hard fight against the world, the flesh, and the devil, and he had never thoroughly baffled them until he retired into the citadel of Solitude."

These words, coupled with the hermit's pious and peaceful death—which swiftly followed and seemed to set a seal of immortal truth upon them—made a deep impression upon Clement. In his case, there was no prejudice to combat; the solitary recluse was still profoundly revered in the Church, whether immured as an anchorite in a cave or cell belonging to a monastery, or hidden in the more savage but laxer seclusion of an independent hermitage. Clement knew more about the hermits of the Church than most divines of his time. He had read extensively on the subject at the monastery near Tergou, devouring their lives with wonder and delight in the manuscripts of the Vatican, and conversing earnestly about them with mendicant friars of various nations. Before the invention of printing, these friars were the great circulators of local annals and biographies that accumulated in convents across every land.

Moreover, his teacher, Jerome, had spent three years as an anchorite on the heights of Camaldoli, where the Thebaid had been revived for more than four centuries. Jerome, usually cold and curt on most religious themes, burned with enthusiasm regarding this one. He had pored over the annals of St. John Baptist's Abbey, round which the hermit's caves were scattered, telling Clement the names of many nobles and famous warriors who ended their days there as hermits, and of many bishops and archbishops who passed from the see to the hermitage, or vice versa. Among the former was the Archbishop of Ravenna; among the latter, Pope Victor the Ninth. He recounted, with grim delight, their multifarious austerities and how each hermit set himself to find where he was weakest, attacking himself without mercy or remission until, even there, he became strongest. He described how seven times in twenty-four hours, in thunder, rain, or snow, by daylight, twilight, moonlight, or torchlight, the solitaries flocked from distant points over rugged, precipitous ways to worship in the convent church—at Matins, Prime, Tierce, Sext, None, Vespers, and Compline.

Under eager questioning, Clement also learned of the persons of famous anchorites he had sung the Psalter and prayed with there—the only intercourse their vows permitted, save with special permission. There was Moncada, Duke of Moncada and Cardova, and Hidalgo of Spain, who retired there in the flower of his youth from the pomps, vanities, and pleasures of the world; Father John Baptist of Novara, who had led armies to battle but was now a private soldier of Christ; Cornelius, Samuel, and Sylvanus. This last, when the great Duchess de' Medici obtained the Pope's leave—hitherto refused—to visit Camaldoli, went down and met her at the first wooden cross. Surrounded as she was with courtiers and flatterers, he remonstrated with her, persuaded her, and warned her not to profane that holy mountain, where no woman had placed her foot for so many centuries. She, awed by the place and the man, retreated with all her captains, soldiers, courtiers, and pages from that one hoary hermit.

At Basel, Clement found fresh materials, particularly concerning German and English anchorites. He had even prepared a *Catena Eremitarum* spanning from the year of our Lord 250, when Paul of Thebes commenced his ninety years of solitude, down to the year 1470. He called them *Angelorum amici et animalium*, i.e., Friends of Angels and Animals. Thus, although in those days he never intended to be a recluse, the road was paved, so to speak. When the dying hermit of Gouda blessed the citadel of Solitude, where he had fought the good fight and won it, and invited Clement to take up the breastplate of faith that now fell from his shrunken body, Clement said within himself: "Heaven itself led my foot hither to this end."

It struck him, too, as no small coincidence that his patron, St. Bavon, was a hermit—an austere one, a cuirassier of the solitary cell. As soon as he was reconciled with Ghysbrecht Van Swieten, he went eagerly to his abode, praying that Heaven might not have already occupied it during these three days. The fear was not vain; these famous dens never lacked a human tenant for long. He found the rude stone door ajar; fearing he was too late, he opened it and went softly inside. No; the cell was vacant. There lay the hermit's great ivory crucifix, his pens, ink, seeds, and, as a *memento mori*, a skull; his cilice of hair, and another of bristles; his well-worn sheepskin pelisse and hood; his hammer, chisel, and psaltery, and so forth. Men and women had passed that way, but none had ventured to intrude, far less to steal. Faith and simplicity had guarded that keyless door more securely than the houses of the laity were defended by their gates like a modern gaol, complete with iron bars at every window, and the gentry by moats, bastions, *chevaux de frise*, and portcullises.

As soon as Clement was fairly in the cell, there was a loud flap and a flutter, and down came a great brown owl from a corner, whirling out of the window and driving the cold air onto Clement's face. He started and shuddered. Was this seeming owl something diabolical, trying to deter him from his soul's good? On second thought, might it not be some good spirit the hermit had employed to keep the cell for him, perhaps the hermit himself? Finally, he concluded it was merely an owl and decided to try and make friends with it. He knelt down and inaugurated his new life with prayer.
