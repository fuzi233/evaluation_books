Catherine felt a sudden chill of dismay as she realized that Gerard must return home within a week, or at the latest, another month. The prospect terrified her. How could she possibly explain to him that she had failed even to cast a glance toward his betrothed? Beyond her own guilt lay the agonizing uncertainty of the girl's fate, a shadow that occasionally coalesced into something sickening in her mind. "Oh, Kate," she groaned aloud, "what if she has already thrown herself into the river?"

"Mother, she would never be so wicked!" Catherine protested.

"Ay, my lass, but you know not the folly of young maidens who lack a mother's hand to guide them," her mother replied, her voice heavy with experience. "They will cast themselves into the water for a man whom the next stranger they meet could cure of in a single week. I have seen them leap in like iron one moment and scream for rescue the next, unable to comprehend their own desires regarding such trifles. Worse still, there are times when their bodies ache in ways unknown to any other creature, tightening their nerves until their patience snaps. In those moments, reason vanishes, and they plunge into the depths. That is why I say men are monsters."

"Monster?" Catherine cried out.

"Monsters, and no less," her mother insisted. "They dig these very canals merely to lure poor women in. They know we cannot cut our throats, for we hate the sight of blood and value our skins and lives too highly. As for hanging, once the noose is fixed, there is often time to change one's mind. But a jump into a canal is no different than jumping into bed; the water washes everything clean, whether you wish it or not. Look at me, the mother of nine—was I not eager enough to make a hole in our canal just now?"

"Nay, Mother, I shall never believe that of you."

"You may believe it, though. It happened in the first year we kept house together. Eli had not yet discovered my frailty, nor I his; so we fell into a quarrel, pulling in opposite directions. Overcome with sorrow, I ran to tell some gossiping fool what I ought to have kept between myself and the saints. There stood one of those precious canals before me. Do they take us for ducks? Oh, how inviting it looked! I told myself, 'Since he has driven me from his door in anger, let him find me drowned next; then he will surely change his tune. He will weep bitterly, and I shall gaze down from heaven—and perhaps another world—to see him mourn, and oh, that would be sweet indeed.' My feet were already lifting, ready to step in, but then I hesitated. I had a new gown nearly finished, after all. Instead, I turned back home. What was Eli's first word? 'Let that flea stick in the wall, my lass,' he said. 'None of what angered you was true, save this: I love you.' Those were his exact words, spoken during our first fight. I threw my arms around his neck, sobbing slightly, thinking of the cold water, but he was no colder to me than I to him, being both a man and a youth. That warmth was far sweeter than lying in the river, ruining my wedding dress and my fine new shoes made by Old John Bush, his uncle who runs the shop today. And what was my grief compared to hers?"

Little Kate clung to the hope that Margaret loved her father too deeply to abandon him, especially given his advanced years. "He is father, mother, and everything to her, you know," she whispered.

"Nay, Kate," her mother countered gently. "In a moment of despair, when the sky turns utterly black, people forget all such duties. I place greater trust in the unborn than in the ripe for the grave to keep a soul from mischief. Indeed, it goes sore against nature to die when a tiny innocent pulls at one's heartstrings, feeding on our very veins."

"Well, then, keep your heart high, Mother," Catherine urged, adding privately that these fears were likely exaggerated. She concluded by earnestly begging her mother to stop insisting on naming the sex of Margaret's baby. It was bad luck, all the town gossips claimed. "Dear heart, as if there were not as many girls born as boys."

Though this reflection was reasonable, it met with immediate clamor. "Do you have the cruelty to threaten me with a girl?!" her mother exclaimed. "I want no more daughters while I have you. What use would a lass be to me? Can I hold her on my knee and see my Gerard again as I can with a boy? Tell thee, it is settled."

"How can that be?" Catherine asked.

"In my mind," her mother declared firmly. "And if I am to be disappointed in the end, it is not for you to disappoint me beforehand by telling me it is not a child, but merely a girl."
