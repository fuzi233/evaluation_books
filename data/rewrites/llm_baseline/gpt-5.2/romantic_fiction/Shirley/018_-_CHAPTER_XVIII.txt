The evening had that close, honeyed warmth which makes the very air feel poured and settled, as if it had been simmered all day in a great copper pan and now lay thick over field and hedge. The sun, sinking, bled purple and Indian-red through a gauze of cloud; the horizon wore colours too rich for Yorkshire propriety—rose and saffron, bruised plum, a last flare like coals being raked. Every surface took the tint as if it were a glaze: the white fronts of houses, the boles of trees, the curves of the road, the broad backs of pasture-ground lifting and dipping like loaves.

Shirley Keeldar and Caroline Helstone came down from the fields at a lingering pace, as though they were reluctant to leave that open, breathing space. When they reached the churchyard the bells had fallen silent; the people had flowed inside and shut the door on their own murmurs. Outside, among the grey stones, the world held its hush.

“How pleasant and calm it is,” Caroline said, letting the stillness wash her like cool water.

“And how hot it will be in the church,” Shirley returned, fanning herself with her hat as if she could stir the air. “And what a dreary long speech Dr. Boultby will make; and how the curates will hammer over their prepared orations—each syllable like a pestle pounding stale meal. For my part, I would rather not enter.”

“But my uncle will be angry if he observes our absence.”

“I will bear the brunt of his wrath; he will not devour me. I shall be sorry to miss his pungent speech, though—sense for the Church, and causticity for schism. He will not forget the battle of Royd Lane. And I should be sorry, too, to deprive you of Mr. Hall’s friendly homily, with all its racy Yorkshireisms—words like good brown bread, honest and heavy. Still—here I must stay. Look at the church, Cary. Look at the stones. Grey upon grey—yet the west is laying a crimson relish over them, as if Nature had dressed even death for supper.”

She stood among the tombs as if she belonged there—unafraid of names cut in stone, unsoftened by them either, only brightened by the light. Her eyes, fixed on the burning west, had that quick, eager fire she got when some inward fancy seized her.

“Nature is now at her evening prayers,” Shirley went on, her voice dropping. “She is kneeling before those red hills. I see her prostrate on the great steps of her altar, praying for a fair night for mariners at sea, for travellers in deserts, for lambs on moors, and unfledged birds in woods. Caroline, I see her—and I will tell you what she is like. She is like what Eve was when she and Adam stood alone on earth.”

“And that is not Milton’s Eve, Shirley.”

“Milton’s Eve! Milton’s Eve!” Shirley repeated with a little flare of impatience, as though the name were a dish too often served. “No—by the pure Mother of God, she is not. Cary, we are alone; we may speak what we think. Milton was great; but was he good? His brain was right—how was his heart? He saw heaven; he looked down on hell. He saw Satan, and Sin his daughter, and Death their horrible offspring. Angels serried their battalions; the long lines of adamantine shields flashed back on his blind eyeballs the unutterable splendour of heaven. Devils gathered their legions in his sight; their dim, discrowned armies passed rank and file before him. Milton tried to see the first woman—but, Cary, he saw her not.”

“You are bold to say so, Shirley.”

“Not more bold than faithful. It was his cook that he saw—or it was Mrs. Gill, as I have seen her, making custards in the heat of summer, in the cool dairy with rose-trees and nasturtiums about the latticed window; preparing a cold collation for rectors—preserves and dulcet creams—puzzled what choice to choose for delicacy best; what order so contrived as not to mix tastes not well-joined, inelegant, but bring taste after taste, upheld with kindliest change.”

Caroline could not help a smile; even in Shirley’s irreverence there was something domestic—something that took the grand and brought it close to hand, like a great idea tested by the tongue.

“All very well too, Shirley.”

“I would beg to remind him that the first men of the earth were Titans—and that Eve was their mother. From her sprang Saturn, Hyperion, Oceanus; she bore Prometheus—”

“Pagan that you are! What does that signify?”

“It signifies that there were giants on the earth in those days—giants that strove to scale heaven. The first woman’s breast that heaved with life on this world yielded the daring which could contend with Omnipotence; the strength which could bear a thousand years of bondage; the vitality which could feed that vulture death through uncounted ages; the unexhausted life and uncorrupted excellence—sisters to immortality—which, after millenniums of crimes, struggles, and woes, could conceive and bring forth a Messiah. The first woman was heaven-born. Vast was the heart whence gushed the well-spring of the blood of nations, and grand the undegenerate head where rested the consort-crown of creation.”

“She coveted an apple and was cheated by a snake,” Caroline said, half laughing, half vexed; “but you have got such a hash of Scripture and mythology into your head there is no making any sense of you. You have not yet told me what you saw kneeling on those hills.”

“I saw—I now see—a woman-Titan,” Shirley answered, and her tone was changed, softened by awe. “Her robe of blue air spreads to the outskirts of the heath where yonder flock is grazing; a veil white as an avalanche sweeps from her head to her feet, and arabesques of lightning flame on its borders. Under her breast I see her zone, purple like that horizon; through its blush shines the star of evening. Her steady eyes I cannot picture. They are clear; they are deep as lakes; they are lifted and full of worship; they tremble with the softness of love and the lustre of prayer. Her forehead has the expanse of a cloud and is paler than the early moon, risen long before dark gathers. She reclines her bosom on the ridge of Stilbro’ Moor; her mighty hands are joined beneath it. So kneeling, face to face, she speaks with God. That Eve is Jehovah’s daughter, as Adam was His son.”

“She is very vague and visionary. Come, Shirley, we ought to go into church.”

“Caroline, I will not. I will stay out here with my mother Eve—in these days called Nature. I love her—undying, mighty being! Heaven may have faded from her brow when she fell in paradise, but all that is glorious on earth shines there still. She is taking me to her bosom and showing me her heart. Hush, Caroline! You will see her and feel as I do, if we are both silent.”

“I will humour your whim,” Caroline said; “but you will begin talking again ere ten minutes are over.”

Shirley, on whom the warm evening seemed to work with unwonted power, leaned against an upright headstone. The stone held the day’s stored heat; it warmed her shoulder through her sleeve like a hand. She fixed her eyes on the deep-burning west and sank into a still, pleasurable trance.

Caroline wandered a little apart beneath the rectory garden wall, where the earth smelt of mint bruised underfoot and of box and old damp brick; bees went home in a low, drowsy drone; a thrush, somewhere hidden, tried a few notes and fell silent again. The word Shirley had spoken—mother—did not summon to Caroline any Titan of hill and cloud. It called up, instead, a gentle human form: a face she had never seen, yet had been shaping in fancy since childhood; a voice she had never heard, yet had longed for till longing itself felt like hunger.

Oh that the day would come when she would remember her child! Oh that she might know her—and, knowing, love her!

The craving that had kept her awake in her crib—when the house was quiet and the candle wicks died down and even the cats ceased to stir—returned with sudden, aching warmth. For years she had tried to smother it, afraid of the folly of hoping; yet now it rekindled, and her heart, like a hearth revived, glowed with the thought that perhaps one day her mother would come, would send for her, would look upon her with eyes not strange but fond, and say, in a sweet voice, “Caroline, my child, I have a home for you; you shall live with me. All the love you have needed and not tasted from infancy I have saved for you carefully. Come; it shall cherish you now.”

A sound cut across that inward softness—metal and hoof and discipline, the road speaking in an altered tongue. Both girls started, as if some unseen cook had dropped a pan in a quiet kitchen. They listened: the measured tramp of horses; then, through the trees, a glint as of steel catching fire. Between leaves they saw flashes of martial scarlet; a helm shone, a plume waved. Silent and orderly, six soldiers rode softly by, their very restraint more alarming than noise.

“The same we saw this afternoon,” Shirley whispered. “They have been halting somewhere till now. They wish to be as little noticed as possible, and are seeking their rendezvous at this quiet hour, while the people are at church. Did I not say we should see unusual things ere long?”

Scarcely had the last hoof-beat been swallowed by the hedges when another disturbance broke the night-hush—no disciplined sound, but the raw cry of impatience: a child’s scream, sharp as a knife on a plate. They turned. A man had come out of the church carrying an infant—a robust, ruddy boy of about two—roaring with all the lung-power that good milk and sound sleep can furnish. Two little girls followed, nine and ten, neat as if dressed for a fair-day. The cooler air, the change of scene, and the bribery of flowers snatched from a grave—little white heads with green stems, still damp—soon quieted the boy. The man sat down with him and dandled him on his knee as tenderly as any woman; the girls took their places close, one on either side, as if to fence their small brother in with their bodies.

Shirley narrowed her eyes a moment, then her face opened.

“Good-evening, William.”

The man lifted his hat and grinned with a broad, pleased simplicity. It was Farren—rough-headed, hard-featured, not old but weather-beaten, as if wind and work had tanned him into his own coat. His clothes were clean; his children’s, singularly neat—washed, mended, and buttoned with a thrift that had pride in it.

“You are not going into the church?” he asked, looking at them with a complacency touched by bashfulness—not awe of their station, but a kind of shy pleasure in their youth and elegance, as one might be shy before a fine flower not meant for one’s own garden.

William, with gentlemen, was often dogged; with proud or insolent ladies he could be unmanageable, resentful. But good-humour and civility melted him. His stubborn nature was repelled by inflexibility in others; for that reason he had never been able to like his former master, Moore. Unaware that Moore, behind his cold face, had done him a service—recommending him as gardener to Mr. Yorke and so opening him a path—William still nursed his grudge against austere manners as if they were an injury.

Latterly he had often worked at Fieldhead. Shirley’s frank hospitality charmed him; Caroline he had known from childhood, and without knowing it he kept her as his ideal of a lady. There was something in her gentleness—her step, her gesture, the very way her dress fell—that moved quiet artist-fibres in his peasant heart. He liked to look at her as he liked to examine rare flowers or stand and drink in a pleasant landscape. Both young women liked William; they lent him books, gave him plants, and preferred his talk to that of many coarse, hard, pretentious people immeasurably higher in station.

“Who was speaking, William, when you came out?” Shirley asked.

“A gentleman ye set a deal of store on, Miss Shirley—Mr. Donne.”

“You look knowing, William. How did you find out my regard for Mr. Donne?”

“Ay, Miss Shirley, there’s a gleg light i’ your een sometimes which betrays you. You look raight down scornful sometimes when Mr. Donne is by.”

“Do you like him yourself, William?”

“Me? I’m stalled o’ t’ curates, and so is t’ wife. They’ve no manners. They talk to poor folk fair as if they thought they were beneath them. They’re allus magnifying their office. It’s a pity but their office could magnify them; but it does nought o’ t’ soart. I fair hate pride.”

“But you are proud in your own way yourself,” Caroline put in gently. “You are what you call house-proud: you like to have everything handsome about you. Sometimes you look as if you were almost too proud to take your wages. When you were out of work, you were too proud to get anything on credit. But for your children, I believe you would rather have starved than gone to the shops without money; and when I wanted to give you something—what a difficulty I had in making you take it!”

He shifted the child higher on his knee, as if the boy’s weight steadied him.

“It is partly true, Miss Caroline. Ony day I’d rather give than take—especially from sich as ye. Look at t’ difference between us. Ye’re a little, young, slender lass, and I’m a great strong man; I’m rather more nor twice your age. It is not my part, then, I think, to tak fro’ ye—to be under obligations, as they say, to ye. And that day ye came to our house and called me to t’ door and offered me five shillings, which I doubt ye could ill spare—for ye’ve no fortin’, I know—that day I war fair a rebel, a radical, an insurrectionist; and ye made me so. I thought it shameful that, willing and able as I was to work, I suld be i’ such a condition that a young cratur about t’ age o’ my own eldest lass suld think it needful to come and offer me her bit o’ brass.”

“I suppose you were angry with me, William?”

“I almost was, in a way. But I forgave ye varry soon. Ye meant well. Ay, I am proud—and so are ye; but your pride and mine is t’ raight mak—what we call i’ Yorkshire clean pride—such as Mr. Malone and Mr. Donne knows nought about. Theirs is mucky pride. Now, I shall teach my lasses to be as proud as Miss Shirley there, and my lads to be as proud as myseln; but I dare ony o’ ’em to be like t’ curates. I’d lick little Michael if I seed him show any signs o’ that feeling.”

“What is the difference, William?” Shirley asked, not unkindly, though her eyes laughed.

“Ye know t’ difference weel enow, but ye want me to get a gate o’ talking. Mr. Malone and Mr. Donne is almost too proud to do aught for theirseln; we are almost too proud to let anybody do aught for us. T’ curates can hardly bide to speak a civil word to them they think beneath them; we can hardly bide to tak an uncivil word fro’ them that thinks themseln aboon us.”

“Now, William, be humble enough to tell me truly how you are getting on,” Shirley said. “Are you well off?”

“Miss Shirley, I am varry well off. Since I got into t’ gardening line, wi’ Mr. Yorke’s help, and since Mr. Hall—another o’ t’ raight sort—helped my wife to set up a bit of a shop, I’ve nought to complain of. My family has plenty to eat and plenty to wear.” He said it with the quiet satisfaction of a man who hears, in his own kitchen, the good sounds of well-being: the steady bubble of broth, the scrape of a knife on a board, the clink of a cup set down without fear of it being the last. “My pride makes me find means to have an odd pound now and then against rainy days; for I think I’d die afore I’d come to t’ parish; and me and mine is content. But t’ neighbours is poor yet. I see a great deal of distress.”

“And consequently there is still discontent, I suppose?” Shirley asked.

“Consequently—ye say right—consequently. In course starving folk cannot be satisfied or settled folk. The country’s not in a safe condition—I’ll say so mich!”

“But what can be done? What more can I do, for instance?”

“Do? Ye can do not mich, poor young lass! Ye’ve gi’en your brass; ye’ve done well. If ye could transport your tenant, Mr. Moore, to Botany Bay, ye’d happen do better. Folks hate him.”

“William, for shame!” Caroline cried, warmth rising in her as quickly as a blush. “If folks do hate him, it is to their disgrace, not his. Mr. Moore himself hates nobody. He only wants to do his duty and maintain his rights. You are wrong to talk so.”

“I talk as I think. He has a cold, unfeeling heart, yond’ Moore.”

“But,” Shirley said, turning the matter as neatly as a cook turns an argument in a pan, “supposing Moore was driven from the country and his mill razed to the ground—would people have more work?”

“They’d have less. I know that, and they know that. And there is many an honest lad driven desperate by t’ certainty that whichever way he turns he cannot better himself; and there is dishonest men plenty to guide ’em to the devil—scoundrels that reckons to be t’ people’s friends, and that knows nought about the people, and is as insincere as Lucifer. I’ve lived aboon forty year i’ t’ world, and I believe that the people will never have any true friends but theirseln, and them two or three good folk i’ different stations that is friends to all the world. Human natur’, taking it i’ th’ lump, is nought but selfishness. It is but excessive few—it is but just an exception here and there, now and then—sich as ye two young uns and me—that, being in a different sphere, can understand t’ one t’ other, and be friends wi’out slavishness o’ one hand or pride o’ t’ other. Them that reckons to be friends to a lower class than their own fro’ political motives is never to be trusted; they always try to make their inferiors tools. For my own part, I will neither be patronized nor misled for no man’s pleasure. I’ve had overtures made to me lately that I saw were treacherous, and I flung ’em back i’ the faces o’ them that offered ’em.”

“You won’t tell us what overtures?” Shirley asked, quickened.

“I will not. It would do no good. It would mak no difference. Them they concerned can look after theirseln.”

“Ay, we’se look after werseln,” said another voice.

Joe Scott had sauntered out of the church to get a breath of air, and there he stood—overlooker and oracle by his own valuation—his very posture a reprimand to the world.

“I’ll warrant ye, Joe,” William said, with a smile that took the edge off his bluntness.

“And I’ll warrant my maister,” Joe returned, with a stiff assurance. Then, to the young ladies, assuming a lordly air: “Ye’d better go into th’ house.”

“I wonder what for?” Shirley asked. Joe’s pragmatical manners were familiar to her; she was often at war with him. He held supercilious theories about women, and the fact that his master’s mill and his master himself were, in a manner, under petticoat government, was wormwood and gall to his secret soul. He had especially resented those business visits of the heiress to the Hollow’s counting-house, as if her very step among ledgers were an affront.

“Because there is nought agate that fits women to be consarned in.”

“Indeed! There is prayer and preaching agate in that church. Are we not concerned in that?”

“Ye have been present neither at the prayer nor preaching, ma’am, if I have observed aright. What I alluded to was politics. William Farren here was touching on that subject, if I’m not mista’en.”

“Well, what then? Politics are our habitual study, Joe. Do you know I see a newspaper every day, and two of a Sunday?”

“I should think you’ll read the marriages, probably, miss, and the murders, and the accidents, and sich like?”

“I read the leading articles, Joe, and the foreign intelligence; and I look over the market prices. In short, I read just what gentlemen read.”

Joe looked as though he considered this talk mere chatter—like a pie bird’s noise in a kitchen where men were weighing sacks. He replied by a disdainful silence.

“Joe,” Shirley continued, “I never yet could ascertain properly whether you are a Whig or a Tory. Pray, which party has the honour of your alliance?”

“It is rayther difficult to explain where you are sure not to be understood,” Joe answered, his pride stiffening his vowels; “but as to being a Tory, I’d as soon be an old woman, or a young one—which is a more flimsier article still. It is the Tories that carries on the war and ruins trade; and if I be of any party—though political parties is all nonsense—I’m of that which is most favourable to peace, and, by consequence, to the mercantile interests of this here land.”

“So am I, Joe,” Shirley said, delighted to bait him. “Partly, at least. I have rather a leaning to the agricultural interest too—as good reason is, seeing that I don’t desire England to be under the feet of France; and that, if a share of my income comes from Hollow’s Mill, a larger share comes from the landed estate around it. It would not do to take any measures injurious to the farmers, Joe, I think?”

“The dews at this hour is unwholesome for females,” Joe observed, as if changing the subject could shut a woman’s mouth like a lid on a pot.

“If you make that remark out of interest in me,” Shirley replied, “I have merely to assure you I am impervious to cold. I should not mind taking my turn to watch the mill one of these summer nights, armed with your musket, Joe.”

Joe’s chin, always rather prominent, poked out at this speech some inches farther than usual.

“But—to go back to my sheep,” she continued, “clothier and mill-owner as I am, besides farmer, I cannot get out of my head a certain idea that we manufacturers and persons of business are sometimes a little—a very little—selfish and short-sighted in our views, and rather too regardless of human suffering; rather heartless in our pursuit of gain. Don’t you agree with me, Joe?”

“I cannot argue where I cannot be comprehended,” was again the answer.

“Man of mystery! Your master will argue with me sometimes, Joe. He is not so stiff as you are.”

“Maybe not. We’ve all our own ways.”

“Joe, do you seriously think all the wisdom in the world is lodged in male skulls?”

“I think that women are a kittle and a froward generation,” Joe said, with the air of quoting a sound authority, “and I’ve a great respect for the doctrines delivered in the second chapter of St. Paul’s first Epistle to Timothy.”

“What doctrines, Joe?” Shirley demanded.

“‘Let the woman learn in silence, with all subjection. I suffer not a woman to teach, nor to usurp authority over the man, but to be in silence. For Adam was first formed, then Eve.’”

“What has that to do with the business?” Shirley cut in. “That smacks of rights of primogeniture. I’ll bring it up to Mr. Yorke the first time he inveighs against those rights.”

“And,” Joe continued, doggedly pleased with his weapon, “Adam was not deceived, but the woman being deceived was in the transgression.”

“More shame to Adam to sin with his eyes open!” Shirley cried. “To confess the honest truth, Joe, I never was easy in my mind concerning that chapter. It puzzles me.”

“It is very plain, miss. He that runs may read.”

“He may read it in his own fashion,” Caroline said, entering quietly now, but with a firmness that had its own flavour. “You allow the right of private judgment, I suppose, Joe?”

“My certy, that I do! I allow and claim it for every line of the holy Book.”

“Women may exercise it as well as men?”

“Nay. Women is to take their husbands’ opinion, both in politics and religion. It’s wholesomest for them.”

“Oh! oh!” both Shirley and Caroline exclaimed together, as if he had offered them some bitter draught and called it wholesome.

“Consider yourself groaned down and cried shame over for such a stupid observation,” Shirley said. “You might as well say men are to take the opinions of their priests without examination. Of what value would a religion so adopted be? It would be mere blind, besotted superstition.”

“And what is your reading, Miss Helstone, o’ these words o’ St. Paul’s?” Joe demanded, turning on Caroline with the satisfaction of a man who thinks he has found a softer adversary.

“Hem! I—I account for them in this way,” Caroline answered, a little flurried, yet earnest. “He wrote that chapter for a particular congregation of Christians, under peculiar circumstances; and besides, I dare say if I could read the original Greek I should find many of the words have been wrongly translated—perhaps misapprehended altogether. It would be possible, I doubt not, with a little ingenuity, to give the passage quite a contrary turn—to make it say, ‘Let the woman speak out whenever she sees fit to make an objection.’ ‘It is permitted to a woman to teach and to exercise authority as much as may be. Man, meantime, cannot do better than hold his peace;’ and so on.”

“That willn’t wash, miss.”

“I dare say it will. My notions are dyed in faster colours than yours, Joe. Mr. Scott, you are a thoroughly dogmatical person, and always were. I like William better than you.”

“Joe is well enough in his own house,” Shirley said, half conciliatory, half amused. “I have seen him as quiet as a lamb at home. There is not a better nor a kinder husband in Briarfield. He does not dogmatize to his wife.”

“My wife is a hard-working, plain woman,” Joe replied, “time and trouble has ta’en all the conceit out of her. But that is not the case with you, young misses. And then you reckon to have so much knowledge; and i’ my thoughts it’s only superficial sort o’ vanities you’re acquainted with.”

He leaned forward a little, as if to speak confidentially, yet with malice enough to season his words.

“I can tell—happen a year sin’—one day Miss Caroline coming into our counting-house when I war packing up summat behind t’ great desk, and she didn’t see me, and she brought a slate wi’ a sum on it to t’ maister. It war only a bit of a sum in practice, that our Harry would have settled i’ two minutes. She couldn’t do it. Mr. Moore had to show her how. And when he did show her, she couldn’t understand him.”

“Nonsense, Joe!” Shirley cried, quick to defend.

“Nay, it’s no nonsense. And Miss Shirley there reckons to hearken to t’ maister when he’s talking ower trade—so attentive like—as if she followed him word for word, and all war as clear as a lady’s looking-glass to her een; and all t’ while she’s peeping and peeping out o’ t’ window to see if t’ mare stands quiet; and then looking at a bit of a splash on her riding-skirt; and then glancing glegly round at wer counting-house cobwebs and dust, and thinking what mucky folk we are, and what a grand ride she’ll have just i’ now ower Nunnely Common. She hears no more o’ Mr. Moore’s talk nor if he spake Hebrew.”
