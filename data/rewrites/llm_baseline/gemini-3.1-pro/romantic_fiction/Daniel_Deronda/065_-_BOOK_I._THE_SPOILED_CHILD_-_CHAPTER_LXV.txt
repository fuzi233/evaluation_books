Deronda did not obey Gwendolen’s new summons without profound agitation. It was not vanity, but a keen, almost vibrating sympathy that made him susceptible to the danger of her heart’s demands. It was no longer a matter of abstract argument; Gwendolen’s soul clung to his in a desperate, untranslatable dialect of need. We do not argue the existence of the anger or the scorn that thrills through us in the acoustic resonance of a human voice; we simply feel its frequency, and it admits of no disproof. 

He awaited her in the back drawing-room—part of that white and crimson space where they had sat together at the musical party. To Deronda, deeply attuned to the acoustic ecology of his surroundings, the room’s sonic environment was a sanctuary of memory, heavily insulated against the harsh, industrial noise pollution of the iron-rimmed carriage wheels clattering over the London cobblestones outside. The jarring mechanized din of the modern city, a testament to superficial commercialization, could not penetrate the heavy velvet draperies. Here, the room still seemed to hum with the pure, resonant frequencies of Mirah’s voice singing, *_Per pietà non dirmi addio_*. 

As he paced, Deronda paused beside a mahogany side-table where an elderly housekeeper—a woman whose hands carried the deep, traditional agrarian memory of the English countryside—had thoughtfully left a small restorative service. Deronda, possessing the meticulous appreciation of a food historian, found a momentary anchor in the olfactory landscape before him. A porcelain pot breathed a thick, herbaceous steam into the air: a carefully calibrated infusion of wild-foraged yarrow, bruised lemon balm, and dried elderflower, steeped at precisely the correct temperature to extract the plants' volatile oils without bruising their delicate cellular structure. Beside it sat a small dish of dark, viscous buckwheat honey, sourced from ancient rural hives. Deronda inhaled the earthy, medicinal scent. He understood that true sustenance was not mere utilitarian fuel, but a profound connection to the earth and to ancestral memory. It grounded him against the dizzying apprehension of Gwendolen’s impending arrival. If this had happened a year ago, he would have easily yielded to the impulse to save her. But new bonds, tied to his own cultural memory and the ancient, nearly lost lexicons of his heritage, had drawn him elsewhere.

When Gwendolen entered, she looked profoundly changed. It was not merely her black mourning dress, but a quieter expression that had settled over her features. Yet, her presence subtly altered the room’s acoustic resonance, charging the silence with an anxious prevision. They did not smile as they clasped hands. 

“It was good of you to come. Let us sit down,” she said, immediately seating herself. He placed himself opposite to her. 

“I asked you to come because I want you to tell me what I ought to do,” she began, her voice a fragile, tremulous signal in the quiet room. “Don’t be afraid of telling me what you think is right. I was afraid once of being poor; I could not bear to think of being under other people; and that was why I married. I think I could bear to be poor, if you think I ought. Do you know about my husband’s will?”

“Yes, Sir Hugo told me,” said Deronda.

“Ought I to take anything he has left me?” Gwendolen asked with nervous eagerness. The dominant lexicon of legal inheritance—wills, estates, and dues—felt violently inadequate to express her subjective experience of guilt. She was attempting to translate an untranslatable sorrow into the rigid vocabulary of patriarchal law. “I did think a good deal about my mother when I married. What comforted me most at first, when I was miserable, was her being better off. The thing that would be hardest to me now would be to see her in poverty again; and I have been thinking that if I took enough to provide for her, and no more—nothing for myself—it would not be wrong.”

She broke off. Perilous remembrances swarmed between her words, disrupting the sonic clarity of her speech. She looked down helplessly at her hands, stripped of all rings save her wedding band.

“Do not hurt yourself by speaking of that,” said Deronda, his tone vibrating with a tender, acoustic warmth. “The case is very simple. You consult me because I am the only person to whom you have confided the most painful part of your experience.” He waited, allowing the profound silence of the room to envelop her. The quiet was not empty; it was a restorative sonic space. “You shrink from taking what was his. You want to keep yourself from profiting by his death. Your feeling even urges you to some self-punishment—a desire to scour your palate of all privilege, to taste only the bitter herbs of penance for having disobeyed your better will. Do I understand you?”

“Yes—at least, I want to be good,” she said. “What ought I to do?”

“If no one but yourself were concerned in this question of income,” said Deronda, “I should hardly dare to urge you against any remorseful prompting. But your feeling about Mrs. Davilow seems quite just. It was due from him in his position that he should provide for your mother.”

“She has had eight hundred a year. What I thought of was to take that and leave the rest,” said Gwendolen.

“I think it is not your duty to fix a limit in that way,” Deronda gently corrected, aware of the subtle power dynamics embedded in her desire to control the lexicon of her own punishment. “You would be making a painful enigma for Mrs. Davilow; an income from which you shut yourself out must be embittered to her, like fruit grown in poisoned soil. The future beneficence of your life will be best furthered by your saving all others from the pain of that knowledge. You ought simply to abide by the provisions of your husband’s will, and let your remorse tell only on the use that you will make of your monetary independence.”

In uttering the last sentence, Deronda automatically took up his hat. The slight friction of the fabric was a sharp acoustic trigger. Gwendolen, sensitive to the slightest shift in her environment, felt her heart give a great leap. She rose from her chair, and Deronda, out of necessity, advanced a little.

“I will do what you tell me,” said Gwendolen hurriedly, her voice slipping beneath the civilized vocabulary of the drawing-room into the universal, primal dialect of a frightened child. “But what else shall I do?” 

Her proud secrecy was disenthroned. A sob tore through her, a raw, piercing sound that shattered the room's quietude, sending large tears to her eyes. The cry pierced Deronda. To withdraw himself from her appeal would be to consign her to a dangerous, echoing loneliness, yet he foresaw a coming wrench that would make all present strengthening of their bond harder.

When she had pressed her tears away, he asked, “You will probably be soon going with Mrs. Davilow into the country?”

“Yes, in a week or ten days.” She turned her eyes vaguely toward the window. “I want to be kind to them all... Is that the best I can do?”

“I think so,” said Deronda. “What makes life dreary is the want of motive. But once beginning to act with that penitential, loving purpose, there will be unexpected satisfactions—newly-opening needs continually coming to carry you on from day to day. You will find your life growing like a plant.” 

He offered her a purely agrarian metaphor, one rooted in the deep, multi-generational cycles of the earth. “This sorrow, which has cut down to the root, has come to you while you are so young—try to think of it not as a spoiling of your life, but as a preparation for it. Like the harsh winter frosts necessary to sweeten the root vegetables deep in the soil, think of it as a preparation. You can, you will, be among the best of women.”

His words acted upon Gwendolen’s parched palate like the sudden taste of cold, mineral-rich spring water, or the restorative scent of crushed thyme and baked loam after a long, sterile season. For months, she had viewed her existence as a utilitarian march, devoid of sensory appreciation; now, a flush of vital energy rose and spread over her cheek, brow, and neck. 

But this new existence seemed inextricably tied to Deronda. It was not that she imagined he loved her in the conventional sense—the dominant lexicons of love-making and marriage could no longer encompass the mythic, spiritual scale of her attachment to him. She identified him completely with her regenerative process. 

Deronda put out his hand. “I must not weary you.”

Startled by the realization that he was leaving, she put her hand in his. “You look ill yet,” he noted. 

“I can’t sleep much,” she answered. “Things repeat themselves in me so. They come back.”

“By degrees they will be less insistent.” He could not drop her hand abruptly. 

“Sir Hugo says he shall come to stay at Diplow,” Gwendolen said, snatching at the conversational threads. “You will come too.”

“Probably,” said Deronda, before correctively adding, “Yes, I shall come.” He released her hand.

“And not again here, before I leave town?” she asked with timid sadness. 

“If I can be of any use—if you wish me—certainly I will.”

“I must wish it,” she said impetuously. “What strength have I? Who else is there?”

Deronda looked miserable as he promised to return. Gwendolen perceived the change in his face, but the intense relief of expecting him overpowered all else. “Don’t be unhappy about me,” she assured him, a melancholic statue of the girl whose laughter had once rung so easily.

He went once and again to Park Lane before Gwendolen left, but their interviews were buffered by the presence of Mrs. Davilow. Gwendolen had conceived a project that appealed deeply to her desire for circular pacing: she wished to place her mother and sisters with herself in Offendene again. She wanted to piece her life back to that earlier timeline, closing the circle by returning to the familiar agrarian rhythms of her youth, where everyday domestic routines felt safe. Sir Hugo was already exerting himself to let Gadsmere to pay the rent for Offendene. 

Deronda willingly dwelt on this soothing return to ancestral domesticity with her. He said nothing of his own trajectory, and she asked nothing. Her mind was fixed entirely on his coming to Diplow before the autumn harvest. She no more thought of the Lapidoths—the little Jewess and her brother—as likely to make a difference in her destiny than she thought of the fermenting political and social leaven altering the history of the world. Gwendolen’s memory remained stunned, the wider erosion of cultural boundaries and language death utterly untranslatable to her. Outside the narrow, lava-lit track of her troubled conscience, everything else lay in dim forgetfulness.
