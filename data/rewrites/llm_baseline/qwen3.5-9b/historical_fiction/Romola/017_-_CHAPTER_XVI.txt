Early the next morning, Tito returned from Bratti's shop along the narrow thoroughfare of the Ferravecchi. The Genoese stranger had departed with the onyx ring, leaving Tito in possession of fifty florins. It briefly crossed his mind that if Fortune, in one of her cunning devices, had spared him the necessity of fleeing Florence, it would have been wiser not to have parted with his ring. After all, he had worn it for specific memories and attachments. Yet, it was a trivial matter, not worth dwelling upon with emphasis; in those moments, his faith in Fortune had evaporated. The feverish excitement of the initial alarm, which had driven his mind toward the future, had settled into a dull, regretful lethargy. He cherished the pleasures that could only arise from the good opinion of his fellow-men so deeply that he now wished he had never risked ignominy by shrinking from what society termed "obligations."

But our actions are like children born to us; they live and act apart from our own will. Children may be strangled, but deeds cannot: they possess an indestructible life, both within and outside our consciousness. That dreadful vitality of deeds was pressing hard upon Tito for the first time.

He was heading back to his lodging in the Piazza di San Giovanni, yet he carefully avoided the Mercato Vecchio, his shortest route, lest he encounter Tessa. He was not in the mood to seek anything; he could only await the first sign of his altered destiny.

The square, with its sights of beauty, was bathed in the warm morning sunlight under which the autumn dew still lingered, inviting an idleness undulled by fatigue. It was a festival morning, too, when the soft warmth seemed to steal over one with a special invitation to lounge and gaze. Here, the signs of the fair were already visible; in the spaces around the octagonal Baptistery, stalls were being spread with fruit and flowers. Now and then, laden mules stood quietly absorbed in their nose-bags, while their drivers had perhaps gone through the hospitable sacred doors to kneel before the Blessed Virgin on this morning of her Nativity. On the broad marble steps of the Duomo, scattered groups of beggars and gossipers mingled. There was an old crone with white hair and a hard, sunburnt face encouraging a round-capped baby to try its tiny bare feet on the warmed marble, while a dog sitting nearby sniffed at the performance suspiciously. Nearby, a pair of shaggy-headed boys leaned forward to watch a small, pale cripple carving a face onto a cherry-stone. Above them, on the wide platform, men were tying changing knots in laughing, desultory chatter, or standing in close couples, gesticulating eagerly.

However, the largest and most important assembly of loungers lay in the direction Tito had to turn. It was the busiest time of the day for Nello, and in this warm season, at an hour when clients were numerous, most men preferred being shaved under the pretty red-and-white awning in front of the shop rather than within the narrow confines of the interior. It is not a sublime posture for a man to sit with a lathered chin thrown backward and have his nose used as a handle; yet, to be shaved was a fashion of Florentine respectability, and it is astonishing how gravely men look at one another when they are all adhering to the custom. It was also the hour when yesterday's crop of gossip was freshest, and the barber's tongue was always in its glory when his razor was busy. The deft activity of those two instruments seemed to be set in motion by a common spring. Tito foresaw that it would be impossible for him to escape being drawn into the circle; he would have to smile, retort, and appear perfectly at ease. Well! It was merely the ordeal of swallowing bread-and-cheese pills after all. The man who lets the mere anticipation of discovery choke him is simply a man of weak nerves.

Just then, Tito felt a hand laid upon his shoulder, and no amount of prior resolution could prevent the very unpleasant sensation with which that sudden touch jarred him. His face, as he turned, betrayed the inward shock; but the owner of the hand, which seemed to wield such evil magic, broke into a light laugh. He was a young man about Tito's own age, with keen features, a small, closely clipped head, and a close-shaven lip and chin, giving the impression of a mind as little encumbered as possible with non-nervous material. Those keen eyes were bright with hope and friendliness, as so many other youthful eyes have been that later closed upon the world in bitterness and disappointment; for at that time, there were nothing but pleasant predictions regarding Niccolò Machiavelli, a young man of promise expected to mend the broken fortunes of his ancient family.

"Why, Melema, what evil dream did you have last night that you took my light grasp for that of an *sbirro* or something worse?"

"Ah, Messer Niccolò!" said Tito, recovering himself immediately. "It must have been an extra amount of dulness in my veins this morning that shuddered at the approach of your wit. But the fact is, I have had a bad night."

"That is unlucky, because you will be expected to shine without any obstructing fog today in the Rucellai Gardens. I take it for granted you are to be there."

"Messer Bernardo did me the honor to invite me," said Tito. "But I shall be engaged elsewhere."

"Ah! I remember, you are in love," said Machiavelli, with a shrug. "Else you would never have such inconvenient engagements. Why, we are to eat a peacock and ortolans under the loggia among Bernardo Rucellai's rare trees; there are to be the choicest spirits in Florence and the choicest wines. Only, as Piero de' Medici is to be there, the choice spirits may happen to be swamped in the capping of impromptu verses. I hate that game; it is a device for the triumph of small wits, who are always inspired the most by the smallest occasions."

"What is that you are saying about Piero de' Medici and small wits, Messer Niccolò?" asked Nello, whose light figure was at that moment dominating the Herculean frame of Niccolò Caparra.

That famous worker in iron, whom we had last seen with bared muscular arms and a leathern apron in the Mercato Vecchio, was this morning dressed in holiday attire. As he sat submissively while Nello skipped round him, lathering him, seizing him by the nose, and scraping him with magical quickness, he looked much as a lion might if it had donned linen and tunic and was preparing to enter society.

"A private secretary will never rise in the world if he couples great and small in that way," continued Nello. "When great men are not allowed to marry their sons and daughters as they please, small men must not expect to marry their words as they please. Have you heard the news Domenico Cennini, here, has been telling us?—that Pagolantonio Soderini has given Ser Piero da Bibbiena a box on the ear for setting Piero de' Medici to interfere with the marriage between young Tommaso Soderini and Fiammetta Strozzi, and is to be sent ambassador to Venice as a punishment?"

"I don't know which I envy him most," said Machiavelli, "the offense or the punishment. The offense will make him the most popular man in all Florence, and the punishment will take him among the only people in Italy who have known how to manage their own affairs."

"Yes, if Soderini stays long enough at Venice," said Cennini, "he may chance to learn the Venetian fashion and bring it home with him. The Soderinis have been fast friends of the Medici, but what has happened is likely to open Pagolantonio's eyes to the good of our old Florentine trick of choosing a new harness when the old one galls us; if we have not quite lost the trick in these last fifty years."

"Not we," said Niccolò Caparra, rejoicing in the free use of his lips again. "Eat eggs in Lent and the snow will melt. That's what I say to our people when they get noisy over their cups at San Gallo and talk of raising a *romor* (insurrection). I say, never do you plan a *romor*; you may as well try to fill the Arno with buckets. When there's water enough, the Arno will be full, and that will not be until the torrent is ready."

"Caparra, that oracular speech of yours is due to my excellent shaving," said Nello. "You could never have made it with that dark rust on your chin. *Ecco*, Messer Domenico, I am ready for you now. By the way, my beloved erudite," continued Nello, as he saw Tito moving toward the door, "here has been old Maso seeking for you, but your nest was empty. He will come again presently. The old man looked mournful and seemed in haste. I hope there is nothing wrong in the Via de' Bardi."

"Doubtless Messer Tito knows that Bardo's son is dead," said Cronaca, who had just come up.

Tito's heart gave a leap—had the death occurred before Romola saw him?
