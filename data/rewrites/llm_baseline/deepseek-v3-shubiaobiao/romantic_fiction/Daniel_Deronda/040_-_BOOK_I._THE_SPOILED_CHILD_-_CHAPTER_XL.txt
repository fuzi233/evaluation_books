The narrow streets of Chelsea seemed to press in on Deronda as he stepped out into the fading afternoon light, his mind restless, his spirit unsettled. The encounter with Mordecai the day before had left him with an unshakable curiosity—an urgency to understand the man whose fervent gaze seemed to pierce through the ordinary fabric of life.

He needed movement, the rhythm of exertion to steady his thoughts. The river called to him, its shifting currents a mirror to his own uncertainty. Instead of hailing a cab, he turned toward the water, where a wherry waited at the Chelsea Stairs. The oarsman nodded as Deronda took his seat, and soon the boat was cutting through the Thames, the city’s skyline retreating behind them.

The physical labor of rowing was a relief, the strain of muscle replacing the churn of unanswered questions. But even as his body warmed to the effort, his mind circled back to Mordecai—his strange intensity, his conviction that Deronda was somehow part of a destiny he himself did not yet see.

*Am I only seeking him for my own purposes?* The thought pricked at him. He had come to London with Mirah’s plea in his heart, her brother’s fate tangled in his own sense of duty. Yet Mordecai’s words had stirred something deeper, a resonance he could not ignore.

The sky above the river was a shifting tapestry—gray yielding to gold, the clouds breaking into streaks of violet and saffron. The water reflected the dying light in flickers of bronze and amber, the world alight with a transient beauty.

Then, as the wherry neared Blackfriars Bridge, Deronda’s gaze was drawn upward. A figure stood at the parapet, silhouetted against the luminous sky—a figure he knew at once.

Mordecai.

The man’s face, illuminated by the western glow, was etched with a hunger that was more than physical—a yearning that seemed to reach beyond the limits of his frail body. His eyes locked onto Deronda’s, and before a word could be spoken, he lifted a hand in recognition, as though this meeting had been foretold.

Deronda signaled back, his pulse quickening. The boat touched the shore, and in moments he stood before Mordecai, who greeted him with a quiet certainty.

“I was coming to find you,” Deronda said.

“I knew you would,” Mordecai replied, as though this were the simplest truth in the world.

There was no hesitation in his voice, no doubt. To him, their meeting was inevitable.

Deronda, though moved, felt the weight of responsibility settle upon him. He did not yet understand the bond Mordecai believed in, but he could not dismiss the raw sincerity in the man’s gaze.

“Let me take you where you need to go,” Deronda offered, mindful of Mordecai’s labored breath.

“To the bookshop,” Mordecai said. “But first, look.” He turned back toward the river, where the last light clung to the water. “This bridge has always been a place of meeting for me—between earth and sky, past and future. And now, you are here.”

There was something sacred in the way he spoke, as though Deronda’s arrival completed a long-awaited prophecy.

They rode in silence through the city, the clatter of hooves and wheels a counterpoint to the quiet intensity between them. When they reached the bookshop, the proprietor, Ram, soon departed, leaving them alone among the shelves.

The gaslight flickered over Mordecai’s gaunt features as he faced Deronda, his eyes burning with an almost prophetic fire.

“You cannot yet know why I have waited for you,” he said. “But I will tell you.”

Deronda listened, his skepticism tempered by an unwillingness to shatter the fragile hope in Mordecai’s voice. The man spoke of visions, of a heritage that stretched across centuries, of a soul reborn to carry the weight of a people’s longing.

“I have been a voice crying in the wilderness,” Mordecai murmured. “But you—you are the answer.”

The claim was staggering. Deronda, though moved, felt the danger of false promises.

“You place too much faith in me,” he said gently. “I am not of your blood. I may not be who you believe me to be.”

Mordecai’s grip tightened on his arm. “You are unsure of your own past,” he insisted, as though this uncertainty only confirmed his certainty. “But I *know*.”

The conviction in his voice was unshakable.

Deronda exhaled slowly. He could not dismiss the depth of Mordecai’s need, nor the strange pull he felt toward this man who saw him as something more than he knew himself to be.

*What if he is right?* The question lingered, unanswered.

For now, there was only this: two men in a dimly lit shop, bound by something neither fully understood—one with a vision of the future, the other with the power to shape it.

And the night stretched before them, heavy with possibility.
