Doctor Portman's letter was dispatched to London, and the good clergyman busied himself with soothing Mrs. Pendennis into a semblance of composure, hoping for a reply that would vindicate her son's morality. Helen, however, deemed it impractical to travel to London immediately to confront her son with the truth of his wickedness. The apothecary forbade her even a journey to Fairoaks for the first day, and it was not until the following morning that she returned to her sofa at home, tended by the faithful, silent Laura.

Unfortunately for everyone involved, Pen never read Doctor Portman's homily until weeks later. Day after day, the widow waited for her son's response to the charges, her own health deteriorating with every delay. For Laura, the burden was heavy: to witness her dearest friend's suffering, to endure Helen's estrangement, and to bear the pain of that averted affection. Yet, she fulfilled her duty with a grace that defied noise; her prayers, the true source of her strength, were whispered in her chamber, invisible to the world. Like a flower blooming in secret, her virtue remained unseen, yet undeniably present—a testament to the fact that good women are the fairest flowers under heaven, often bowed low by grief or consumed by disease, yet purified by Heaven's chastening trials.

Pen, meanwhile, never received the letter. It was duly posted, delivered to his box in Lamb Court, and carried by the laundress to his writing table, joining the pile of correspondence that included a picture of him entering his room just as Mrs. Flanagan was drinking his gin.

Those who have tracked Mr. Arthur's career have likely identified his greatest enemy: himself. A scoundrel who indulged him all his life, this internal rogue had grown insolent, reacting with frantic rudeness to any attempt at self-denial. Unlike Laura, who sacrificed pleasure easily, Pen, unaccustomed to restraint, suffered woundily when asked to forgo his desires. He resolved not to see Fanny, attempting to drown her image in work, exercise, and dissipation. He worked too hard, walked too much, and consumed too many cigars and punches. Yet, none of this could banish Fanny from his inflamed brain. By the end of a week of this futile discipline, our young gentleman lay in bed with a fever.

Let the reader who has never suffered a fever in chambers pity the wretch bound to such a fate. One might imagine a Cruikshank illustrating the horrors of bachelor life: the lonely breakfast with a black kettle in a midsummer fire, or the return from a festive holiday to find only Christmas bills waiting on the table. But the true tragedy is illness in chambers—long nights of pain, the absence of a kind hand, and the company of one's own sickening fancies. This dismal lot befell Arthur Pendennis.

One night he went to bed ill; the next, he awoke worse. His only visitors were the laundress and a printer's devil from the *Pall Mall Gazette*, whose demands for copy only exacerbated his fever. With Shandon away at Boulogne and Warrington absent, the political columns of the Gazette looked blank until Mr. Finucane rushed to Pen's chambers. The good-natured Irishman stepped in, producing a series of political compositions filled with praise for Ireland and its genius. Shandon, reviewing the paper from the coast, recognized the hand of his friend and chuckled, "Look here, Mary, here is Jack at work again." Jack, a warm partisan, even offered to forgo his holidays to nurse Arthur, though the invalid insisted on bearing his malady alone.

On a Friday night, after finishing the paper, Finucane informed Captain Costigan of Arthur's condition. Two days later, the Captain visited Lamb Court. He found Mrs. Flanagan in tears, her grief so overwhelming she resorted to brandy to sustain her. Her intoxicated attentions became intolerable to the invalid, who begged her to leave, prompting further tears and recourse to the bottle. The Captain reprimanded her soundly for her intemperance.

Inside, Pen, in a fevered state, was delighted by the Captain's arrival. He called eagerly, thanking him and begging him to talk. The Captain felt the young man's pulse—fierce and rapid—and noted the haggard, hot face and bloodshot eyes. Pen tossed in his comfortless bed, trying to discuss the Back Kitchen, Vauxhall, and Fanny. "How is little Fanny?" he asked.

Indeed, how was she? Fanny had gone home sadly the previous Sunday, watching Arthur light his lamp after his meeting with Bows. That night, she lay restless, unable to read *Walter Lorraine* aloud in fear of waking her father. She clutched the book under her pillow, stirring with the dawn. When she finally saw Bows, the good-hearted musician, she demanded to know if he would speak ill of Arthur.

"I've been fond of you ever since we came to live here," Bows said gently. "You used to like me until three or four days ago."

"And now you're going to say ill of him?" Fanny cried, pacing the room, her cheeks flushed with defiance. "Do, Mr. Bows—that will make me like you better."

"Indeed I shall do no such thing," Bows replied. "I think he is a very good and honest young man."

"But if you said a word against him, I would never speak to you again!" she cried, her frame trembling with love, anger, and despair.

Bows explained that he knew Pen years ago, when he had a romantic attachment for Lady Mirabel, the Captain's daughter. "He wanted to marry her, but their ages and ranks were disproportionate," Bows said. "She acted wisely in refusing him. Mr. Pendennis has his way to make in the world; he must marry a lady of his own rank. An honest girl won't ruin a man's prospects or lead him into poverty for her gratification."

Fanny's defiance turned to dismay. "What do I know about marrying, Bows?" she sobbed. "It wasn't my doing... He came and rescued us... Yesterday Ma and I walked in the Temple Gardens, and—and"—she broke into tears—"Oh! I wish I was dead! I wish I was laid in my grave and had never seen him!"

"He said as much himself," Bows said softly. "He cannot and must not think of you as a wife fitting for him. You must strive to forget him."

Leaving the musician, convinced but still longing, Fanny told her mother everything. She bewailed the inequality of their conditions, citing *The Lady of Lyons* and Pauline as examples of true love transcending class. "Not that Mr. Arthur loves me—Oh no! I ain't worthy of him," she confessed. "Only a princess is worthy of such a gentleman. If I might, oh, how I'd serve him, work for him, slave for him... I'd sit up all night and learn to make myself worthy of him."

Her head fell on her mother's shoulder as she wept. Mrs. Bolton, of course, joined her, but when Mr. Bolton entered for his evening meal, the talk ceased instantly. With practiced hypocrisy, the mother dried her daughter's tears, praising the undertaker's aide-de-camp and feigning ignorance of Fanny's gathering emotion, hiding her daughter's turmoil behind a mask of demure quiet.

Thus, fate seemed determined to inflame the poor child's passion. Her mother encouraged it; Bows's words only augmented the fever. Pen was not a wicked seducer; he was high-minded, wishing to avoid a marriage with an ill-educated girl whose relations he could not acknowledge. Society would say he was right to crush such a fondness, to let the passion pass over and cure itself. Yet, perhaps there is something to be said for the blind, unreasoning passion that makes a man ready to stake his all.

Fanny waited a whole week for Arthur to come, only to hear from Costigan of his illness on the final day. That very evening, Arthur's uncle, the excellent Major, arrived in town from Buxton. He sent his valet, Morgan, to inquire for Arthur and arrange a breakfast. Morgan returned with a long face: Mr. Arthur was very bad, in bed with a fever. A doctor was needed. The Major, vexed but unable to attend due to his own ailments, despatched Morgan with a note to Doctor Goodenough.

The Major went to bed disconsolate. When Doctor Goodenough visited the next day, he found the patient in a high fever and ordered immediate bleeding. The Major wrote to Mrs. Pendennis with alacrity, declaring that if he could be of use, he would rush to the chambers, but his own health required him to stay in the country. He tried to satisfy his conscience by deputy and post, traveling by railway to Stillbrook to dine with the Marquis of Steyne, though he remained gloomy and feverish with worry.

The next morning, he received a note from Mr. Bows saying Arthur had had a tolerable night. But shortly after, as the company waited for carriages on the terrace, a fly drove up. A grey-headed, shabby old gentleman jumped out and asked for Major Pendennis. It was Mr. Bows. Most gentlemen saw the alarm on the Major's face.

"Hullo! What's the matter, Pendennis?" cried Lord Steyne.

"It's—it's—my boy that's dead," the Major sobbed, overcome.

"Not dead, my Lord; but very ill when I left London," Bows corrected in a low voice.

Just then, a britzka arrived. Lord Steyne checked his watch. "You've twenty minutes to catch the mail-train. Jump in, Pendennis; and drive like h——, sir, do you hear?"

The carriage sped away. The Major drove rapidly to the Temple, finding a travelling carriage blocking the lane. Two ladies emerged, asking their way. The Major glanced at the panel and saw the worn-out crest of the Eagle looking at the Sun, with the motto "Nec tenui penna." It was his brother's old carriage. It was Helen and Laura, asking their way to Pen's room.

He ran up, hastily clasping his sister's arm and kissing her hand. The three entered Lamb Court and mounted the long, gloomy stair. They knocked gently at the door, on which Arthur's name was written. It was opened by Fanny Bolton.
