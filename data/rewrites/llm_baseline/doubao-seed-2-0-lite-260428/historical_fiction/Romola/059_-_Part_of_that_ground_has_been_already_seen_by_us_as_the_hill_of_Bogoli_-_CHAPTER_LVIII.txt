Tito had scarcely returned to Florence from Siena before he set out on a second urgent journey, and did not come back until the seventeenth of August. Nearly a fortnight had dragged past since the arrest of the five prominent citizens accused of plotting to restore the Medici, and still they languished in the city dungeons, their fates hanging entirely unwritten, no verdict handed down to end the limbo that stretched thin the nerves of every partisan in Florence.

For Romola, those two weeks had blotted out every other care, her whole life narrowing down to watching the ebb and flow of odds that might save her godfather, Bernardo del Nero, and the other imprisoned men. At times, hope seemed almost tangible: the long delay worked in their favour, letting influential allies quietly marshal support for leniency, as every official tasked with judging the case shrank from the odium of making a final call. One faction roared that the Republic would be fatally endangered by any mercy to the plotters, that to spare them was to invite every exiled Medicean ally to march on the city gates. The other side knew full well that to sentence five men of such ancient, distinguished Florentine lineages to death and confiscation would breed generations of bitter, vengeful relatives who would never rest until every judge who signed the warrant had paid for it.

The official responsibility for criminal judgements rested with the Eight, the magistracy that oversaw Florence’s justice system, and a sentence of death required a majority of six votes – the famous sei fave, or “six beans”, named for the black and white dried beans citizens used to cast their secret votes, a small, absurd marker of the republic’s parsimonious approach to political representation. But the Eight dared not bear the weight alone, and begged the Signoria – the ruling Gonfaloniere and his eight Priors – to share the burden. The Signoria in turn shrugged off the onus, proposing instead that the accused be allowed to appeal to the Great Council, per the law Savonarola had pushed through to wide acclaim nearly three years prior.

The friends of the prisoners resisted that appeal, though, determined to drag out the delay as long as possible – and strict legal precedent did say an appeal could only be filed after a formal sentence had already been passed. Their resistance won out, and a compromise was struck: the verdict would be decided by a special, broad assembly convened that very seventeenth of August, made up of all the highest magistrates, the eighty-member Senate, and a select body of leading citizens.

That same day, Romola, her anxiety spiking at the thought her godfather’s fate might be decided before sundown, had secured permission for a second, closely supervised visit to his cell, with two guards posted within earshot the whole time. She had walked back to the Via de’ Bardi house with her cousin Monna Brigida, still entirely in the dark about what the special council had voted to do. Monna Brigida had immediately hurried off again to wait for news at the home of a relative who sat on one of the magistracies, determined to bring back the first authentic, unrumoured account of the outcome.

Romola dropped onto the first bench she spotted in the bright, frescoed saloon, too shaken, too sick at heart to care about decorum or notice the fine statues and tapestries around her. She sat with her back to the door, her head resting in her hands, waiting. It felt like an hour had passed since Brigida left, so when the door creaked open, she did not even lift her head – until the step on the stone floor told her at once this was not her cousin.

Since that terrible night she had turned Tito out of her rooms, she had held no concrete, provable evidence that he had bought his own safety by betraying his old father Baldassarre, or the secrets of the conspiracy, to the Signoria. To the contrary, all she could see was evidence that the exiled Mediceans still trusted him, believed he was running their secret errands across Romagna while posing as an agent of the Florentine republic. No official had ever named him as a conspirator, the evidence against the plotters was murky enough to leave his name entirely clear. But Romola’s suspicion would not budge: the horror of what she had seen him do to the old man blotted out every other fact about him, as if she had watched him commit a murder and could never shake the sick, certain impression his hands were still sticky with fresh blood.

A cold shudder ran down her spine as his step crossed the threshold. She could not turn, could not stand to greet him. He said nothing at first, pausing only a moment before taking a seat across the table, directly facing her. Then she lifted her eyes to meet his, still completely silent. He showed no irritation, his voice even and calm.
“This meeting mirrors our parting, Romola. But I know full well you are living through a moment of terrible, gnawing suspense. I have come, if you will listen, to bring you the relief of real hope.”

She started, shifting her weight, staring at him with open, distrustful doubt.
“The council has voted to prorogue, to delay any final verdict, until the twenty-first. The Eight were finally bullied into issuing a formal sentence of condemnation, but a formal demand has now been filed on behalf of the five prisoners to allow them to appeal to the Great Council.”

Romola’s face softened, the doubt melting away. She leaned forward, eager. “When will that appeal be heard?”
“It has not been granted yet. But it could be. The special council will meet again on the twenty-first to vote on whether to allow the appeal at all. That gives three full days, a window where favour can be called in, influence can be brought to bear, and odds can shift sharply in the prisoners’ favour.”

Romola sprang to her feet, colour flooding her cheeks, her hands trembling. In that rush of hope, all her loathing for Tito vanished for a heartbeat.
“No doubt you have already thought of what I was about to suggest,” Tito said, standing as well. “You are thinking of the Frate.”
“I am,” Romola said, surprised. “Has he acted already? Is there news of him?”
“Only this: it was Messer Francesco Valori’s rabid, violent anger that pushed most of the council to vote for condemnation today. Half the men who sided with the death sentence did it out of fear, not conviction. There are dozens of Fra Girolamo’s followers in that special council and out of it who are dead set against putting five prominent citizens to death – Piero Guicciardini, for example, who was one of the Signoria’s most vocal resisters of the sentence, and Giovan Battista Ridolfi, a devout Piagnone who will not easily forgive the death of his own brother Niccolò.”
“But how can they possibly deny the appeal?” Romola demanded, indignant. “It is the law! One of the proudest achievements of this very popular government, won by the Frate’s own preaching.”
“They claim this is an exceptional case, full of loud bluster about the Republic being on the brink of destruction. No amount of argument could stop them voting to adjourn, though. A single, powerful, well-placed influence used over these next three days could stiffen the spines of all the waverers who want to grant the appeal, and rein in Valori’s headlong hatred. I heard myself that the Frate has already sent a message to Valori to plead for mercy for Lorenzo Tornabuoni. You are the only lay person with regular, open access to the Frate. It would be more than worth your while to go to him now.”
“You are right,” Romola said, her eyes distant, lost in thought. “I cannot believe the Frate would ever stand by and let that law he fought so hard for be overturned.”
“I heard half the crowd in the Palazzo courtyard saying before I left that it would bring eternal shame on Fra Girolamo if he let a government made up almost entirely of his own followers deny that appeal, without lifting a single protest – after spending years boasting in his sermons and his books that he was the one who won that law for Florence.” Tito paused, his tone silkily smooth. “Though between us, for all your Frate’s gifts, he has fallen into the habit of preaching that violent sort of ‘human sacrifice’ that calls for killing tyrants and dissidents, and some of his followers are now wondering how to square that with showing mercy to these five men today.”

Romola winced, her face tight with pain. “I know. I know. He is driven to those excesses of speech, it was not always so. I will ask for an audience at once. I cannot rest until I speak to him. I trust the greatness of his heart.” She was not looking at Tito now, her gaze fixed on the floor, barely even registering that he was the one speaking to her.
“Then do not waste a moment,” Tito said, picking up his cap and twisting it in his hands, making as if to leave. “And now, Romola, I hope that in spite of all your prejudice against me, you can see that my own wishes are aligned with yours here. You will not hold the mere fact that I escaped implication in the conspiracy, that I kept my life, against me as an unforgivable crime.”

A jolt ran through Romola, sharp as an electric shock, dragging her back to full, sharp awareness of the man standing before her. She stared at him, silent.
“At the very least,” he added, his tone hardening a little, “you could treat our intercourse as something more than a test where if an evil deed is possible for a man, he must have done it. Am I the only person in the whole city you cannot extend your famous charity to?”

All the pain and resentment she had held back for a fortnight surged up in her, rising like a high tide that would not be dammed. She spoke with unflinching decision, unconcerned for any consequences.
“It is too late, Tito. A lie once told, a suspicion once planted, can never be killed. And I know everything now. I know who that old man was. He was your father. The man who raised you, who gave you everything, who risked his life for you more than once. Next to the horror of what you did to him, the fact that you broke my trust, and betrayed my father’s memory, is a small thing. As long as you lie about him, deny the truth of what you did, there is a chasm of loathing between us that can never be crossed. We are both human beings. I have a soul of my own, and it recoils from every action you take. Our marriage is nothing but a pretence. A perpetual lie can never be a sacred union.”

Tito did not reply at once, calculating, already spiked with alarm. When he spoke, his tone was carefully measured, cold and deliberate.
“And you intend to act on that independence by leaving me, I presume?”
“I do,” Romola said, sharp and impetuous.
“And what if I refuse to let you go?” Tito said, his tone sharpening with quiet menace. “The law gives me every right to keep my wife at my side. Will you run through every street in Florence screaming your story? Drag that mad, would-be assassin old man out of the gutters to prove your point, who everyone knows will do anything you tell him to get his revenge on me? Will you run first to the Signoria and tell them I am a secret Medicean conspirator, then run to the exiled Mediceans and tell them I betrayed their secrets? What proof do you have? Only your own word, that you are so certain I am capable of every evil imaginable. What a position that would be for a respected noble Florentine wife! If you did somehow manage to drag my name through the mud, you would be the most famous wronged heroine the Greek stage ever saw.” He paused, watching her stand mute, sensing he had the upper hand. “I do not believe you have any other grievance against me. Only that I failed to live up to some lofty, vague ideal of a husband you set for me, so you withdrew your affection, and left me to carefully tend to your public reputation as a pious, charitable leading Piagnone lady. You will find you cannot make the city believe your story. But no doubt you would start by running to your beloved godfather Bernardo del Nero with your wild tales, would you not?”

“Why am I even wasting breath talking about myself?” Romola cried out in anguish, sinking back onto her chair. It felt loathsome, selfish, to waste a single second fighting over their broken marriage when five men’s lives hung in the balance.

She did not notice when Tito slipped silently out of the room. She had no idea how long she sat there, lost in her own thoughts, until the door creaked open and Monna Brigida stepped over the threshold. Romola sprang to her feet at once.
“Cousin. We must go to San Marco this very instant. I have to see my confessor, Fra Salvestro – and the Frate.”
