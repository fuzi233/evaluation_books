News of Rosanna Spearman’s vanishing didn’t stay politely indoors. It ran, as rumor always runs, along the back passages and out into the yard, leaping from mouth to mouth with the clatter of pails and the thud of boots on stones. The out-of-door servants had been questioning each other in their own sharp dialect—words shortened by wind and salted by the sea—and they had at last caught hold of a quick little imp of a boy, nicknamed “Duffy,” who weeded in the garden when he felt like earning pennies.

Duffy swore he had seen Rosanna no more than half an hour ago—seen her in the fir-plantation, not walking as a tired girl might, but running, with her skirts snatched up and her breath likely tearing at her throat, straight in the direction of the shore.

“Does this boy know the coast hereabouts?” Sergeant Cuff asked.

“He’s been born and bred on it,” I said. You could hear it in the boy’s voice—sea-worn, bird-quick, tuned to distance and weather. There are ears in such children that notice what older people drown out with talk.

“Duffy,” says the Sergeant, “do you want to earn a shilling? If you do, come along with me. Keep the pony-chaise ready, Mr. Betteredge, till I come back.”

He set off for the Shivering Sand as if the wind pushed him from behind; and Duffy—like all our young savages when excitement gets in them—gave a wild howl and scampered after, his feet making that light staccato patter that’s almost more alarming than heavy running, because it sounds like a creature chasing rather than being led.

After they left, my mind went into a strange state—restless and stupefied together, like a kitchen fire that smokes instead of drawing. I did a dozen needless things, turning from room to room and door to door, and afterwards could not have named one of them. I only remember the house noises: the hollow complaint of the wind in the passages, the rain beginning to tick at the panes, the servants’ voices muffled, as if the very air was thickening with what we feared.

How long it was before Duffy came back I can’t truly say. Time, when trouble is brewing, turns queer—now dragging like a heavy cart-wheel in mud, now skipping whole yards like a hare. The boy came running, hair plastered already with damp, and thrust a leaf of paper at me. Sergeant Cuff had torn it from his pocket-book and scribbled in pencil:

“Send me one of Rosanna Spearman’s boots, and be quick about it.”

I sent the first woman-servant I could lay hands on up to Rosanna’s room, and I sent Duffy back with word that I would follow myself with the boot.

This wasn’t the quickest obedience—no, I know that as well as any man. But something in me would not let Rosanna’s boot go out of my sight like a mere object passed along a chain. My old foolish resolve to screen the poor girl—if there was still anything left to screen—came back upon me at the eleventh hour, and with it that detective-fever which makes a man fancy he can smell out truth as one might smell a stew that’s beginning to scorch.

When I had the boot in my hand—heavy, damp-limbed leather, with the faint sourness of worn days in it, and a whisper of blacking long gone stale—I started off at the nearest approach to a run that a man turned seventy can reasonably attempt. I could hear my own breath rasping, and the blood knocking at my ears with a dull drumlike pulse, as if my body itself had become part of the storm’s music.

As I drew nearer the shore, the sky closed in like a lid. Clouds gathered black; rain came down in great white sheets, driven so hard before the wind that it looked less like falling than like flying sideways. The sea made its own terrible sound—no polite lap of waves, but the deep thunder of water striking the sand-bank at the mouth of the bay, booming and withdrawing and booming again, as if the coastline were a great instrument played by a ruthless hand.

A little further on, I passed Duffy crouching for shelter under the lee of the sandhills. Even over the gale I caught a thin, frightened whimper in his breathing, the way a child tries not to cry.

And then I saw it all: the raging sea; the rollers tumbling in on the sand-bank; the driven rain sweeping over the waters like a garment torn loose; and the yellow wilderness of beach with one solitary black figure standing on it. Sergeant Cuff.

He waved his hand towards the north as soon as he saw me. “Keep on that side!” he shouted. “And come on down here to me!”

I went down, choking for breath, heart leaping as if it meant to jump clean out of my chest. I had a hundred questions, and not one of them would pass my lips. His face stopped them. There was something in his eyes—something fixed and dreadful—that chilled me more than the rain.

He snatched the boot from my hand and set it in a footmark on the sand, bearing south from where we stood and pointing straight towards the rocky ledge called the South Spit. The mark was not yet blurred away by the rain, and the boot fitted it to a hair—as if the ground itself remembered her weight and would not be denied.

He pointed at the boot in the footprint without speaking. The sea roared behind him, and the wind hissed through the rain, and in that monstrous noise his silence was like a knife.

I caught at his arm and tried to speak. Nothing. My throat felt sealed.

He moved on, following the footsteps down and down to where sand met rock. The South Spit lay just awash with the flowing tide; the waters heaved over the hidden face of the Shivering Sand. Now this way, now that, with an obstinate patience dreadful to watch, Sergeant Cuff tried the boot in the prints, and always found it pointing the same way—straight to the rocks. Hunt as he might, no sign could he find anywhere of footsteps walking from them.

At last he gave it up. Still silent, he looked at me again; then he looked out over the water, rising deeper and deeper over the quicksand.

I looked where he looked—and saw his thought written in his face.

A dumb trembling crawled over me. My knees gave way. I sank down on the beach, the wet sand sucking at my clothes as if it too wanted to keep what fell into it.

“She has been back at the hiding-place,” I heard him say to himself. “Some fatal accident has happened to her on those rocks.”

But as he spoke, Rosanna’s altered looks and words and actions rose before me—the numbed way she had listened and answered when I found her sweeping the corridor only hours before, as if her mind was elsewhere and her body only doing what habit required. The Sergeant’s guess struck wide of the dreadful truth; I knew it—knew it in a way that didn’t come by reasoning but by the sort of knowledge you can’t translate into neat words, because it’s made of pity, and memory, and a man’s fear for his own.

I tried to tell him. I tried to say, “The death she has died, Sergeant, was a death of her own seeking.” But my tongue would not serve me. The trembling held me. I could not feel the rain; I could not see the tide rising. Everything turned unreal, like a dream where you watch your own hands and don’t know how to move them.

The poor lost creature came back to me as she had been in earlier days—on that morning when I went to fetch her into the house, when she spoke of the Shivering Sand as if it called to her against her will, and wondered aloud whether her grave was waiting for her there. There are old country sayings for such things, in words half-forgotten now, because the young learn polished speech and lose the rough, true names that fitted the land. And yet no fine language in the world can carry what a place like that means to those who live beside it: the slow swallowing, the silence after the cry, the cruel patience of the sand.

The horror struck at me through my own child. My girl was just Rosanna’s age. If my girl had been tried as Rosanna was tried, she too might have lived that miserable life and died this dreadful death.

Sergeant Cuff, kindly enough, lifted me up and turned me away from the sight of the place where she had perished. With that small mercy, I began to fetch breath again and to notice what was before me.

Looking towards the sandhills, I saw the men-servants from out-of-doors and the fisherman named Yolland running down together, calling out through the storm to know if the girl had been found. Their voices came broken on the wind; the sea kept its own harsh chant underneath, never pausing, as if it would drown human speech and human grief alike.

In the fewest words, the Sergeant showed them the evidence of the footmarks and told them a fatal accident must have happened. Then he singled out the fisherman and asked, turning again to the sea:

“Tell me. Could a boat have taken her off, in such weather as this, from those rocks where her footmarks stop?”

Yolland pointed to the rollers tumbling in on the sand-bank, and to the great waves leaping in clouds of foam against the headlands.

“No boat that ever was built,” he said, “could have got to her through that.”

Sergeant Cuff looked one last time at the footmarks, already fast blurring under the rain.

“There,” he said, “is the evidence she can’t have left this place by land. And here is the evidence she can’t have got away by sea.”

He paused, thinking, the rain running off the brim of his hat in steady streams. “She was seen running towards this place, half an hour before I got here from the house. Some time has passed since then—call it an hour ago. How high would the water be, at that time, on this side of the rocks?” He pointed to the south side—the side not filled up by the quicksand.

“As the tide makes today,” Yolland answered, “there wouldn’t have been water enough to drown a kitten on that side of the Spit an hour since.”

The Sergeant turned northward, towards the quicksand. “How much on this side?”

“Less still,” said Yolland. “The Shivering Sand would have been just awash, and no more.”

The Sergeant turned to me and said the accident must have happened on the quicksand side.

That loosened my tongue at last, like a knot giving way.

“No accident!” I cried. “When she came to this place, she came weary of her life, to end it here.”

He started back. “How do you know?”

The others crowded close, hungry for meaning in the wind-whipped confusion, like people pressing around a hearth when the kettle begins to sing.

The Sergeant recovered instantly. He put them back with a hand and a look. “He’s an old man,” he said. “The discovery has shaken him. Let him alone a little.”

Then, to Yolland: “Is there any chance of finding her, when the tide ebbs again?”

“None,” Yolland answered. “What the Sand gets, the Sand keeps for ever.”

After saying this, the fisherman stepped nearer to me. His voice lowered, as if even now he respected the dead, and the storm’s roar made a kind of harsh privacy around us.

“Mr. Betteredge,” he said, “I have a word to say to you about the young woman’s death. Four foot out, broadwise, along the side of the Spit, there’s a shelf of rock, about half fathom down under the sand. My question is—why didn’t she strike that? If she slipped, by accident, from off the Spit, she fell in where there’s foothold at the bottom, at a depth that would barely cover her to the waist. She must have waded out, or jumped out, into the Deeps beyond—or she wouldn’t be missing now. No accident, sir! The Deeps of the Quicksand have got her. And they have got her by her own act.”

After that—spoken by a man whose knowledge came not from books but from tides, seabirds, and the sound the sand makes when it shifts—Sergeant Cuff said nothing. None of us did. The storm filled our mouths for us.

With one accord we turned and went back up the slope of the beach. At the sandhillocks we met the under-groom running from the house. He was a good lad, with an honest respect for me, and he held a little note as carefully as if it were something breakable, his face decent with sorrow.

“Penelope sent me with this, Mr. Betteredge,” he said. “She found it in Rosanna’s room.”

It was Rosanna’s last word to the old man who had tried—thank God, had always tried—to befriend her.

“You have often forgiven me, Mr. Betteredge, in past times. When you next see the Shivering Sand, try to forgive me once more. I have found my grave where my grave was waiting for me. I have lived, and died, sir, grateful for your kindness.”

No more than that. Yet it was enough to break me. Tears come easy when you’re young and beginning the world; they come easy when you’re old and leaving it. I burst out crying there in the rain, the note trembling in my hand like a living thing.

Sergeant Cuff stepped nearer—kindly meant, I don’t doubt. I shrank back.

“Don’t touch me,” I said. “It’s the dread of you that has driven her to it.”

“You are wrong, Mr. Betteredge,” he answered, quietly. “But there will be time enough to speak of it when we are indoors again.”

I followed the rest of them, leaning on the groom’s arm. Through the driving rain we went back—to meet the trouble and the terror waiting for us at the house.
