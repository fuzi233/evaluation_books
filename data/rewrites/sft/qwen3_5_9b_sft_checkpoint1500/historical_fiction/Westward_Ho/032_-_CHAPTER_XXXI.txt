"Britannia needs no bulwarks,
No towers along the steep,
Her march is o'er the mountain wave,
Her home is on the deep."

—Campbell, *Ye Mariners of England*.

Now commenced that epoch-making naval contest, destined to decide whether tyranny and idolatry or liberty and the Gospel should govern half of Europe and the future continent of America. It was a twelve-day epic, a narrative too grand for ordinary prose, demanding the thunder of Homeric verse; yet, compelled to recount it, I shall strive to employ the words of contemporary chroniclers wherever possible.

"The Lord High Admiral of England, sending a pinnace ahead named the *Defiance*, declared war by discharging her cannon; and immediately, drawing within musket-range, unleashed a tremendous barrage from his flagship, the *Ark Royal* (also known as the *Triumph*), striking at what he presumed to be the Spanish Admiral's vessel—which turned out to be the ship of Alfonso de Leon. Shortly thereafter, Drake, Hawkins, and Frobisher engaged stoutly against the rear squadron, commanded by Recalde." The Spaniards quickly recognized the superior speed and maneuverability of the English ships. Realizing they were giving more than they took, despite Recalde's efforts, the rear squadron hurried forward to join the main body. Admiral Medina, observing his fleet scattering rapidly, gathered them into a crescent formation. The Armada attempted to advance with solemn dignity, resembling a stately herd of buffalo marching across a prairie, disdainful of the wolves snarling at their heels. Yet, these were no mere wolves, but cunning hunters, swift-footed and keenly armed, determined to "shamefully shuffle" (to quote Drake's own phrase) that vast herd from the Lizard to Portland, and from Portland to Calais Roads. Even in this brief two-hour engagement, countless Spaniards began to question the boasted invincibility of their Armada.

One of the four great galliasses was already riddled with shot, causing severe disruption to her "pulpits, chapels," and the friars within. The fleet was forced to form a circle around her, lest Drake and Hawkins sink her. During this maneuver, the "principal galleon of Seville," carrying Pedro de Valdez and a host of blue-blooded nobility, ran aground upon her neighbor, tearing away her foremast. Despite Spanish chivalry, she was left to her fate. This did not appear like victory, certainly. But courage! Though Valdez remained behind, "Our Lady," the saints, and the bull *Caena Domini*—dictated by one I dare not name here—remained with them, and it would be blasphemous to doubt their presence. Meanwhile, however, if they had fared so poorly against merely a third of the Plymouth fleet, what would become of them when the forty belated ships, already whitening the blue horizon between them and the Mewstone, entered the fray?

Thus ended the first day; not a single English ship, hardly a man, was injured. It had forever destroyed, in the minds of the English, the prestige of boastful Spain. It had fully vindicated the policy advocated by the virtuous Lord Howard, following the counsel of Raleigh and Drake, to maintain a running fight rather than "clapping ships together without consideration." As Raleigh noted, had he adhered to the latter strategy, "he had been lost, if he had not been better advised than a great many malignant fools who found fault with his demeanor."

Be that as it may, the first day concluded with Amyas and the other Bideford ships having been exceedingly busy for two hours, punching holes in a massive galleon bearing on her poop a maiden with a wheel and named *Sta. Catharina*. She displayed a coat of arms on her flag at the sprit, likely those of the soldier-commandant, though they were shot away early in the fight, preventing Amyas from confirming if they belonged to Don Soto. Nevertheless, ample opportunity existed for personal revenge. Finally recalled by the Admiral's signal, Amyas retired to his hammock and slept soundly.

However, before an hour had passed, he was awakened by Cary descending to request orders.

"We were to follow Drake's lantern, Amyas; but where it is, I cannot see, unless he has been taken up aloft there among the stars for a new *Drakium Sidus*."

Amyas emerged grumbling, yet no lantern was visible. Instead, there was a sudden explosion and a terrible fire on board a Spanish vessel, which was gradually extinguished, forcing the entire fleet to lie-to for the whole night.

The next morning found them off Torbay. Amyas was hailed by a pinnace delivering a letter from Drake. Saving for the somewhat arbitrary spelling common to those days, the missive read something like this:

"DEAR LAD,—I have been wool-gathering all night after five great hulks, which the Pixies transfigured overnight into galleons, and this morning again into German merchantmen. I let them go with my blessing; and returning, falling in (God be thanked!) with Valdez's great galleon, I secured good booty—the faithful and valiant comrades the Dons had left behind, which the Lord Howard allowed to slip past him, thinking her deserted by her crew. I have sent to Dartmouth a sight of noblemen and gentlemen, perhaps fifty in number; and Valdez himself, who, when I sent my pinnace aboard, insisted on maintaining his punctilios and proposing terms. I answered him, saying I had no time to bargain; if he wished to die, I was the man for it; if he wished to live, then, *buena guerra*. He sent again, boasting that he was Don Pedro Valdez and that it stood not with his honor, nor that of the Dons in his company. I replied that, for my part, I was Francis Drake, and my matches were burning. Whereupon he found in my name a salve for the wounds of his own, coming aboard to kiss my fist, reciting Spanish lies about his fortune in falling into the hands of fortunate Drake, and much more which he might have kept to cool his porridge. But I have much news from him (for he is a leaky tub); and among others, this: that your Don Guzman is aboard the *Sta. Catharina*, commandant of her soldiery, with his arms flying at her sprit, beside the maiden with the wheel at the poop. She is a lofty-built ship of three tiers of ordnance, from which God preserve you, and grant you similar luck.

"Your deare Friend and Admirall,
"F. Drake."

"She sails in this squadron of Recalde. The Armada intended to smoke us out of Plymouth; and God's grace it was they failed: but their orders from home are too strict, and so the slaves fight like a bull in a tether, no farther than their rope, finding thus the devil a hard master, as most do in the end. They cannot match our quick handling and tacking, and take us for very witches. So far so good, and better to come. You and I know the length of their foot of old. Time and light will kill any hare, and they will find it a long way from Start to Dunkirk."

"The admiral is in a gracious humor, Leigh, to have vouchsafed you so long a letter."

"St. Catherine! Why, that was the galleon we hammered all yesterday!" exclaimed Amyas, stamping on the deck.

"Of course it was. Well, we shall find her again, doubt not. That cunning old Drake! How he has contrived to line his own pockets, even though he had to keep the whole fleet waiting for him."

"He has given the Lord High Admiral the dowry, at all events."

"Lord Howard is too high-hearted to stop and plunder, Papist though he is, Amyas."

Amyas answered with a growl, for he worshipped Drake and was not overly just to Papists.

The fleet did not find Lord Howard until nightfall; he and Lord Sheffield had held station steadfastly all night after the Spanish lanterns with only two ships. At least, there was no longer any doubt regarding the loyalty of English Roman Catholics, and indeed, throughout the fight, the Howards exhibited (as if to wipe out the slurs cast upon their loyalty by fanatics) a desperate courage that might have driven less prudent men to destruction, but led them only to victory. Soon a large Spaniard drifted by, deserted and partly burnt. Some of the men desired to leave their posts to board her, but Amyas stoutly refused. He had "come out to fight, and not to plunder; so let the nearest ship to her have her luck without grudging." They passed on, and the men pulled long faces when they saw the galleon seized by their next neighbor and towed off to Weymouth, where she proved to be the ship of Miguel d'Oquenda, the Vice-Admiral, which they had seen the previous night, almost blown up by some desperate Netherland gunner who, having been "misused," intended to settle old scores with his tyrants.

And so ended the second day, while Portland grew higher and clearer every hour. The next morning found them off the island. Would they attempt Portsmouth, though they had spared Plymouth?
