Gerard, shaken by the narrowness of his escape, rose before the break of dawn and roused Martin. The old soldier stared at him in astonishment, convinced that Gerard had somehow slipped away through the window during the night. When asked for counsel on how to flee the country and evade their pursuers, Martin declared there was but one safe path. "I must guide you through the vast forest to a bridle-path I know," he said. "From there, you will reach a hostelry that lends swift horses; a single day's gallop will carry you clear of Holland. But we must depart ere the villagers stir from their beds."

Peter's home lay merely a furlong and a half from the tree line. They set out immediately. Martin carried his bow and three arrows, for it was Thursday, a holy day. Gerard bore nothing but a sturdy oak staff Peter had lent him for the journey. Margaret pinned up her kirtle and farthingale against the damp road. Peter walked them as far as his garden hedge, then, with an emotion rarely seen on his face regarding passing events, bestowed his blessing upon the young man.

As the sun crested the horizon, casting golden light across the stony fields toward the woods, disaster struck. Having crossed roughly half the distance, Margaret, who had been nervously scanning their rear, let out a piercing cry. Acting on pure instinct, she turned and sprinted toward the timber, screaming in terror. Ghysbrecht and his men were in hot pursuit. Resistance would have been suicide. Martin and Gerard followed Margaret's lead. Though the pursuers gained ground, Martin shouted encouragement: "Only win the wood! Only win the wood!" Their early start gave them hope; the great trees seemed to stretch their branches like welcoming arms, their leaves forming a protective screen.

Yet, an unforeseen danger emerged. The fiery old burgomaster threw himself onto his mule, spurred it into a gallop, and rode not just his own men but directly at the fugitives. His intent was to cut them off. The old man circled in a wide arc until he reached the forest's edge, positioning himself squarely between the fleeing group and the exit. He cared little whether the others escaped; his focus was solely on Gerard.

Margaret shrieked, clutching at Gerard in a desperate attempt to shield him, but he shook her off without ceremony. In his fury, Ghysbrecht forgot that hunted beasts often turn upon their hunters, and that two men can harbor such hatred that they long to destroy the very thing they despise. Instead of dodging as the burgomaster expected, Gerard charged straight at him, issuing a savage, triumphant cry. With all his heart, soul, and strength, he swung the oak staff. It descended upon Ghysbrecht's face with a terrifying crash, knocking the old man beneath his mule's tail. The beast beat a frantic tattoo with its hooves while Ghysbrecht lay beaten, his face streaming with blood, his collar spattered red.

In an instant, the three fugitives vanished into the tree cover. The yells of dismay and vengeance erupting from Ghysbrecht's men signaled that the chase had become a race for absolute survival.

"Why run?" Gerard gasped, breathless. "You have your bow, and I have this," adding that he brandished his bloodied staff.

"Boy!" Martin roared. "The gallows! Follow me!" He plunged deeper into the woods.

Soon, a sound like a pack of hounds closing in on prey echoed through the trees. The pursuers had entered the forest and spotted them darting among the trunks. Margaret moaned and panted as she ran; Gerard gritted his teeth, gripping his staff tightly. They soon encountered a dense thicket of hazel coppice. Martin dashed inside, pushing aside the young wood as easily as one moves through standing grain. Within fifty yards, they faced four blind paths. Martin chose one.

"Bend low," he commanded. Half-creeping, they glided forward. Their route soon intersected with other twisting trails. Taking another, it initially seemed to loop back, but soon curved again, leading them to a thick pine grove where the footing was firm and the air heavy with scent. Here, there were no paths; the young firs grew so densely that visibility dropped to mere yards ahead.

After traveling some distance, Martin sat down. Having learned in war to shed all impressions of danger amidst the danger itself, he retrieved a piece of bread and a slice of ham from his wallet and calmly ate his breakfast. The younger pair watched him with dismay.

"All Sevenbergen could not find you now," Martin remarked casually. "You will lose your purse, Gerard, long before you reach Italy; is that the proper way to carry a purse?"

Gerard looked down. Entangled by its chains to the buckle and strap of his wallet hung a large triangular purse.

"This is none of mine," Gerard said. "What lies within, I wonder?" He attempted to free it, but since passing through the coppice, it had become inextricably bound to his gear.

"It seems loath to leave me," Gerard muttered, eventually cutting it loose with his knife. Upon inspection, the purse was well-stocked with silver coins of various denominations, though its swollen appearance was largely due to numerous sheets of brown paper folded and doubled inside.

A sudden realization dawned on Gerard. "Why, it must belong to that old thief! And see! Stuffed with paper to deceive the world!"

The mystery of how the burgomaster's purse ended up on Gerard solved itself quickly. It must have been slung at Ghysbrecht's saddlebow; when Gerard rushed his enemy, he had unconsciously torn it away, thus felling his foe and robbing him in a single, fatal gesture.

Gerard was delighted by this feat, but Margaret remained uneasy. "Throw it away, Gerard, or let Martin take it back. Already they call you a thief. I cannot bear the stain."

"Throw it away? Give it back? Not a stiver!" Gerard protested. "This is spoil lawfully won in battle from an enemy. Is it not, Martin?"

"Of course," Martin agreed. "Send him back the brown paper, but the purse or the coin—that would be a sin."

"Oh, Gerard!" Margaret pleaded. "You are going to a distant land. We need the goodwill of Heaven. How can we hope for that if we take what is not ours?"

But Gerard viewed the matter differently. "It is Heaven that gives it to me by a miracle, and I shall cherish it accordingly," the pious youth declared. "Just as the favored people spoiled the Egyptians and were blessed."

"Take your own way," Margaret submitted humbly. "You are wiser than I am. You are my husband," she added softly, "is it for me to gainsay you?"

These humble words from Margaret, who until that day had held the dominant position, surprised Martin momentarily. They lingered in his mind later, surprising him even less. Gerard kissed her tenderly in reward for her wife-like docility, and they continued their journey hand in hand, with Martin leading the way into the depths of the immense forest.

The further they traveled, the more secure they felt from pursuit. Indeed, the townsfolk never ventured so far into the trackless wilderness. Impetuous natures tend to repent quickly. As soon as Gerard was entirely out of immediate danger, his conscience began to prickle.

"Martin, would I had not struck quite so hard."

"Whom? Oh! Let that pass; he was cheaply served."

"Martin, I saw his grey hairs as my stick fell upon him. I doubt they will remain visible to me for long."

Martin grunted with contempt. "Who spares a badger for his grey hairs? The greyer your enemy, the older; and the older the craftier, and the craftier the better for a little killing."

"Killing? Killing, Martin? Speak not of killing!" Gerard trembled violently.

"I am much mistaken if you have not," Martin replied cheerfully.

"Now Heaven forbid!"

"The old vagabond's skull cracked like a walnut. Aha!"

"Heaven and the saints forbid it!"

"He rolled off his mule like a stone shot from a cart. I said to myself, 'There is one wiped out,'" Martin grinned ruthlessly.

Gerard fell to his knees and began praying for his enemy's life. At this, Martin lost his patience. "Here's mummery. What! You, who set yourself up for learning, do you not know that a wise man never strikes his enemy unless to kill him? And what is all this fuss about killing old men? If it had been a young one, full of the joys of life—wine, women, and pillaging! But an old fellow at the edge of the grave, why not shove him in? Go he must, today or tomorrow; and what better place for greybeards? Now, if I should ever be so misfortunate as to live as long as Ghysbrecht did, forced to walk on a mule's legs instead of Martin Wittenhaagen's, and with a back like this"—striking the wood of his bow—"instead of this"—tapping his bowstring—"I'll thank and bless any young fellow who knocks me on the head, as you have done that old shopkeeper. Malison on his memory."

"Oh, *culpa mea*! *Culpa mea*!" cried Gerard, striking his breast.
