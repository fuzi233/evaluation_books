Silence enveloped the dining room like a shroud, broken only by the soft clinking of silverware against plates. Mr. Welland pushed back his chair, the scrape of wood on floorboards echoing through the stillness. His wife, Mrs. Welland, sat across from him, her eyes fixed on a small, cream-colored card. She adjusted her gold-rimmed spectacles, the gesture a habitual tic in a life governed by routine.

"'A party for the Blenkers—-the Blenkers?' she read aloud, her voice a monotone, devoid of inflection. 'Professor and Mrs. Emerson Sillerton request the pleasure of Mr. and Mrs. Welland's company at the meeting of the Wednesday Afternoon Club on August 25th at 3 o'clock punctually. To meet Mrs. and the Misses Blenker.'"

Mr. Welland's face contorted in a mixture of dismay and frustration, as if the words on the card had somehow altered the fabric of reality. "Good gracious—" he muttered, the phrase hanging in the air like a challenge to the universe.

Mrs. Welland sighed, a sound that seemed to carry a weight of resignation. "Poor Amy Sillerton—you never can tell what her husband will do next." Her gaze drifted to her daughter, May, who sat quietly, her eyes fixed on some point beyond the table. "I suppose he's just discovered the Blenkers."

Professor Emerson Sillerton was a figure of discomfort, a thorn in the side of Newport society. His family connections were venerable and respected, with ties to the esteemed Sillerton Jackson and the influential Pennilow of Boston. Yet, he had chosen to defy convention, becoming an archaeologist and a professor, living in Newport in the winters, and exploring ancient tombs in Yucatan instead of more conventional destinations. His marriage to Amy Dagonet, a woman from a respectable family, seemed a mystery to the Mingott set, who struggled to comprehend why she had submitted to her husband's eccentricities without protest.

As the silence between them grew, Mrs. Welland broke it with a practical suggestion. "There's no reason why you should go at all, my dear. I have some cards to leave at the other end of Bellevue Avenue, and I'll drop in at about half-past three and stay long enough to make poor Amy feel that she hasn't been slighted."
