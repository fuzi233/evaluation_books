Outside the town, the snow lay fresh and untouched save where countless wolves had trampled paths through it. Every foot of the road bore witness to their passage, a chaotic trail of destruction.

"We did well to heed the old man's counsel, Denys."

"Aye, indeed. As I recall now, I heard them last night scurrying beneath our window, howling and whining for man's flesh in that market square. Yet not a single fat burgher showed pity, nor dropped a crust to the poor vagabonds."

Gerard offered a smile, though it carried an air of distant abstraction. They trudged forward in silence, the crunch of snow the only sound accompanying their steps.

"What dost thou muse upon so deeply?" Denys asked eventually, breaking the quiet.

"Upon thy goodness."

Denys bristled immediately. Despite his usual good humor and capacity for bearing impertinence, he was prone to flare up where no cause for anger truly existed. "A civil inquiry deserves a civil response," he said dryly.

"Alas, I meant no offense," Gerard replied gently.

"Then why feign concern for my virtue when you know I possess no goodness beneath my skin?" Denys pressed, his tone sharp.

"Had anyone else spoken thus, I would have retorted, 'Thou liest.' But to thee, I say, 'Thou hast no eye for men's qualities, save for those of women.'" Gerard raised his chin, defying the warrior's unreasoning choler. "I was thinking of thy kindness shown last night. Thou wouldst have married me to the 'Tête d'Or,' or rather to the 'Golden Head,' and left thyself solitary."

"Oh, art thou there, lad?" Denys recovered his geniality in a heartbeat. "Truly, I spoke not of goodness, but of friendship and true fellowship. Let me confess, my young master, that my conscience pricks me even now for allowing thee to turn thy back upon fortune and peaceful days. A truer friend might have steered thee differently. If I had acted sooner, thou mightest have lain smarting at the 'Tête d'Or' for a month or two; that skittish lass would have nursed thee tenderly, and all would have been well. I had the blade in hand to perform this act, but remembering how thou hatest pain, even a mere scratch, my craven heart failed me at the critical moment." Denys wore a look of humble apology for his lack of virtuous resolve when the path of duty had lain so clear.

Gerard arched his brows in astonishment at this monstrous yet thoroughly characteristic revelation. This new, delicate point of friendship—whether one ought to sever the heel-tendon of a beloved friend in times of crisis—was never to be discussed. An incident intervened before the debate could deepen.

"Behold one riding behind us upon a neighbor's mule!" Denys shouted.

Gerard turned. "And how knowest it is not his own?"

"Oh, blind fool! Because he rides it with no discretion."

In truth, the man approached galloping like a fury. But what startled the friends most was that upon reaching them, the rustic rider's eyes widened saucer-like. He jerked the reins so suddenly and powerfully that the mule stumbled, extending her forelegs and sliding between the pedestrians like a four-legged table on castors.

"I trow ye are from the 'Tête d'Or,'" the stranger called out. They assented. "Which of ye is the younger?"

"He that was born later," Denys winked at his companion.

"Glad to hear the news."

"Pray, divine then!"

"And I shall. Thy beard is ripe, thy fellow's is green; he shall be the younger. Here, youngster." He extended a paper packet. "Ye left this at the 'Tête d'Or,' and our mistress sends it unto thee."

"Nay, good fellow, methinks I left naught." Gerard felt within his pouch, finding it empty.

"Wouldest thou make our burgess a liar?" the rustic reproached, his voice dripping with indignation. "Shall I receive no *pourboire*?" He demanded it again, even more sharply, noting how he had arrived *ventre à terre*.

"Nay, thou shalt have *pourboire*," Gerard said, producing a small coin.

"*À la bonne heure*!" cried the clown, his features beaming with disproportionate joy. "May the Virgin go with ye! Come up, Jenny!" With a final "stomach to earth," he departed.

Gerard untied the packet. It measured roughly six inches square. Inside, he discovered another packet, which contained yet another, and so on. By the fourth layer, frustration overwhelmed him, and he hurled the entire bundle into the snow. Denys retrieved it, rebuking his petulance. Gerard excused himself on the grounds of hating affectation.

Denys attested, "'The great toe of the little daughter of Herodias'—there was no affectation here, but only woman's good wit. Doubtless the wraps concealed something which, out of delicacy or her sex's lovely cunning, she would not wish her hind to see bestowed upon a young man; thy garter, to wit."

"I wear none."

"Her own, then? Or a lock of her hair? What is this? A scrap of raw silk, fresh from the worm. Well, of all the love tokens!"

"Now who but thee ever dreamed she was so base as to send me love tokens? I saw no harm in her—save for her hands."

"Wait, here lies something hard lurking in this soft nest. Come forth, I say, little nestling! Saints and pikestaves! Look at this!"

It was a gold ring featuring a great amethyst, glowing and sparkling, fully colored yet pure as crystal.

"How lovely!" Gerard exclaimed innocently.

"And here is writing; read it, for I do not read as glibly as some when I know not the matter beforehand."

Gerard took the slip of paper. "'Tis a posy, and fairly written." He read the lines, blushing like a schoolgirl. They were naive, perhaps thus translated into English:

*Come back to 'the Hedde of gold.'"

"The little dove!" Denys cooed.

"The great owl! To risk her good name thus. However, thank Heaven she has played this prank with an honest lad who will never expose her folly. But oh, the perverseness! Could she not bestow her nauseousness upon thee?" Denys sighed and shrugged. "Upon thee that art as ripe for folly as herself?"

Denys confessed that his young friend had echoed his very thoughts. It was passing strange to him that a damsel with eyes in her head should pass by a man to bestow her affections upon a boy. Still, he could not but recognize in this the bounty of Nature. Boys were human beings after all; but for this occasional caprice of women, their lot would be too terrible—they would remain outside the sun, blighted and never coming to anything. Since only the fair could fashion a man out of such unpromising materials as a boy. Gerard interrupted this flattering discourse to beg the warrior-philosopher's acceptance of the lady's ring. Denys refused it flatly, insisting instead that Gerard return to the "Tête d'Or" at once, ring and all, like a man, and not allow a poor girl to hold out her arms in vain.

"Her hands, you mean."

"Her hand, holding the 'Tête d'Or.'"

Failing to persuade him otherwise, Denys considered placing the ring upon his friend's finger. Gerard declined. "I wear a ring already."

"What, that sorry gimcrack? 'Tis pewter, or tin at best: and this virgin gold, besides the jewel."

"Aye, but 'twas Margaret gave me this one; and I value it above rubies. I'll neither part with it nor give it a rival," he declared, kissing the base metal and bidding it fear naught.

"I see the owl hath sent her ring to a goose," Denys murmured sorrowfully. Nevertheless, he prevailed upon Gerard to fasten it inside his bonnet. To this, indeed, the lad consented very readily. For sovereign qualities were universally ascribed to certain jewels, and the amethyst ranked high among these precious talismans.

Once this matter was settled, Gerard earnestly requested his friend to let the subject drop. Speaking of the other sex to him made him pine so desperately for Margaret, and nearly unmanned him with the thought that each step took him farther from her. "I am no general lover, Denys. There is room in my heart for one sweetheart, and for one friend. I am far from my dear mistress; and my friend, but a few leagues hence, and I must lose him too. Oh, let me drink thy friendship pure while I may, and not dilute it with any of these stupid females."
