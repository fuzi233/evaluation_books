The "Fairoaks to let" sign stood as a grim monument to the evening's tragedy, though inside the house, the true eviction was taking place in the hearts of those gathered around the dying fire. Major Pendennis, ever the pragmatist, had organized a modest feast to welcome his son, but the table was a stage for a silent drama. Helen, too frail to eat, had sent word that she could not join them, leaving Laura to bear her mother's company in the shadows. While the Major chattered cheerfully, oblivious to the heavy silence that pressed against the two women, the atmosphere thickened as evening fell.

When Helen finally emerged into the sitting-room, she leaned heavily on Laura, her back turned to the fading light, hiding the pallor of her face from Arthur's view. As she approached Pen, placing her trembling arms around his shoulders in a gesture of desperate affection, Laura stepped back, unable to watch. Arthur felt the cold clamminess of her hand against his forehead and the violent tremor of her voice. Instead of comfort, her misery ignited a savage wrath within him. He saw not a mother seeking forgiveness, but a persecutor playing the martyr. "She persecutes me," he thought, his heart hardening against her plea. "She comes to me with the air of a martyr!"

"You look very ill, my child," she whispered, her voice breaking. "I don't like to see you look in that way." She sank onto the sofa, clutching his hand with fingers that felt like ice.

"I have had much to annoy me, mother," Pen replied, his chest heaving with suppressed rage.

The words hung in the air like a detonator. Helen's heart hammered so violently she sat frozen, terrified of what he would say. Warrington, Laura, and the Major held their breath, sensing the storm about to break.

"I have had letters from London," Arthur continued, his voice rising. "One has given me more pain than I ever felt in my life. It tells me that former letters of mine have been intercepted and stolen—that a young creature who showed me the greatest love has been cruelly used by... by you, mother."

"For God's sake stop!" cried Warrington, seeing Helen's terror. "She's ill—don't you see she is ill?"

"Let him go on," Helen whispered faintly.

"Let him go on and kill her!" Laura shouted, rushing to her mother's side. "Speak on, sir, and see her die."

"It is you who are cruel!" Pen roared, his soft nature revolting against the injustice laid at his feet. "It is you who attribute all this pain to me! You with your wicked reproaches, your wicked doubts, your wicked persecutions of those who love me—yes, those who brave everything for me, whom you despise and trample upon because they are of lower degree! Shall I tell you what I will do? Now that I know your conduct, I will go back to that poor girl you turned out of my doors and ask her to share my home. I'll defy the pride that persecutes her and the suspicion that insults us both!"

"Do you mean, Pen, that you—" Helen began, reaching out with eager, outstretched hands.

"Silence, hush, dear mother," Laura cried, stopping her.

Savagely as he spoke, Pen's words only fueled Helen's desperate need to hear him out. "Go on, Arthur, go on," she gasped, nearly swooning.

"By Gad, I say he shan't go on, or I won't hear him!" the Major bellowed, trembling with wrath. "If you choose, after all we've done for you, to insult your mother and disgrace the name of Pendennis by allying yourself with a low-born kitchen-girl, go and do it! But let us have no more to do with him. I wash my hands of you, sir! I'm an old fellow, ain't long for this world, and I come of an ancient family. I hoped, before I went off the hooks, that the fellow I'd liked and nursed would show me our name wasn't dishonoured. But if he won't, dammy, I say amen. Both my father and brother Jack were the proudest men in England, and I never thought there'd come this disgrace. I'm ashamed it's Arthur Pendennis."

The old man's voice broke into a sob, the second time Arthur had drawn tears from those wrinkled lids. The sound instantly checked Pen's anger; he stopped pacing, stunned by the vulnerability of the man who had raised him.

In the twilight, George Warrington's deep voice broke the tension. "Will you let me tell you something about myself, my kind friends?" he asked gently. "You have been so good to me, ma'am, so kind to Laura... My dear Pen and I have been such friends that I wanted to tell you my story. It is sad, contains another's secret, but it may divert you from this fatal misconception causing so much pain. May I speak, Mrs. Pendennis?"

"Pray speak," Helen said, though her mind was already racing with a terrifying hope.

Warrington filled a bumper of wine and drained it. "You see me," he began, "a man without ambition, careless of reputation, living in a garret from hand to mouth. I shall likely die there alone. I nailed myself to that doom in early life. Years ago, what interested me about Arthur was a story from Oxford gossip about his early affair with the Chatteris actress—a woman who, but for the Major's generalship, might have been your daughter-in-law. I can't see Pen blush, but I dare say Miss Bell does. And Major Pendennis, I dare say, laughs as he ought to—for he won. What would Arthur's lot have been tied at nineteen to an illiterate woman older than himself, with no equality, no love? He would have been miserable. When he threatened a similar union just now, it was anger, very natural after meeting unkind suspicion from friends. He has complained of unfair treatment toward an innocent person."

Helen tried to rise, but Warrington continued. "I liked him because of that early history, and because I like a man who shows unreasonable attachment for a woman. That is why we are friends. Pen has been a great comfort to a lonely man. I am not complaining; up in my garret with my old books and pipe for a wife, I am contented, occasionally envying men who can solace their ill fortune with the affection of a woman or child."

A sigh came from near Warrington, and a hand reached out to him, only to be instantly withdrawn by female prudery. Warrington resumed his confession. "My fate is such as I made it. Before college, at eighteen, I was at a private tutor's and became attached to a woman of lower degree and greater age. You shrink from me..."

"No, I don't," Laura said resolutely, laying her hand firmly in his. She had divined his story.

"She was a yeoman's daughter," Warrington faltered. "Her parents knew my father and encouraged me with coarse artifices. To do her justice, she never cared for me; she was forced by her family's threats. I thought I loved her, but I was married to a boor. Her dulness palled until I loathed it. Later, I found letters showing her heart had never been mine, but belonged to a man of her own degree. At my father's death, I paid my debts and settled my remaining shillings in an annuity on those who bore my name, on condition they hide themselves and not assume it. They kept the condition for more money. If I had made a name, they would have claimed it. I entered life at twenty, hopeless and ruined. I was a boyish victim of vulgar cheats. Beware how you marry out of your degree. I was made for a better lot, but God awarded me this one. So, I look on and see others happy with a heart as little bitter as possible."

"By Gad, sir!" cried the Major, suddenly in high good humour. "I intended you to marry Miss Laura here."

"And, by Gad, Master Shallow, I owe you a thousand pounds," Warrington said.

"How d'ye mean a thousand? It was only a pony, sir," the Major replied simply.

Helen, overwhelmed with delight, started up. "God bless you—God for ever bless you, Mr. Warrington!" She kissed his hands, ran to Pen, and fell into his arms.

"Yes, dearest mother," Pen said, embracing her with noble tenderness. "I am innocent, and my dear mother has done me a wrong."

"Oh yes, my child, I have wronged you!" Helen whispered. "Come away, Arthur—not here. I want to ask my child to forgive me—and my God, to forgive me; and to bless you, my son."

She led her tottering son into her room, closing the door as the three spectators watched the reconciliation in pleased silence. Ever after, the tender accents of that voice, the look of sacred eyes, and the quiver of fond lips smiling mournfully would be remembered by the young man. In his best moments and hours of trial, his mother's face would look down upon him, blessing him with a gaze of pity and purity, appearing as an angel transfigured with love.

The moon rose, lighting up Helen's sweet, pale face. Their talk was more tender and confidential than in years. Arthur told her the story of his struggle against temptation and his thankfulness for overcoming it. He promised never to wrong the girl again, nor wound his mother's pure heart. The threat to return was a momentary exasperation he repented of; he would never see her again. But his mother said yes, he should; she admitted her pride and culpability, begged his pardon for opening the letter, and promised to write to the young girl if she had time. Was it not natural she should love her Arthur? Again she kissed him and blessed him.

The clock struck nine. Helen reminded him of how, when he was a boy, she used to hear him say the *Our Father* at that hour. Once more, the young man fell to his mother's sacred knees and sobbed out the prayer echoed by millions of humbled men. As he spoke the last words, "for ever and ever" and "Amen," Helen's head fell on his shoulder, her arms closing round him.

A quarter of an hour later, Laura heard Arthur call, "Laura! Laura!" She rushed in to find him still on his knees, holding his mother's hand. Helen's head was sunk back, her face quite pale. Pen looked round, scared with ghastly terror. "Help, Laura, help! She's fainted—she's—"

Laura screamed and fell beside Helen. The shriek brought Warrington, the Major, and the servants running. The sainted woman was dead. Her last emotion was joy to be henceforth unchequered and eternal. Her tender heart beat no more; it had no more pangs, doubts, or griefs. Its last throb was love; Helen's last breath was a benediction.

The melancholy party hurried homewards. Helen was laid by her husband's side at Clavering, in the old church where she had prayed so often. For a while, Laura stayed with Dr. Portman, who read the service over his dear departed sister amidst his own sobs. Few cared for her when she was gone; scarcely more than a nun in a cloister did people know of that pious lady. A few words among the cottagers, a little talk at Clavering about the amount of property left, and speculation on whether Arthur would let Fairoaks—that was all. Except with one or two who cherished her, the kind soul was forgotten by the next market-day.

The great blank she left was in Laura's heart, to whom her love had been everything. "I am glad she gave me her blessing before she went away," Warrington said to Pen. Arthur, with humble wonder, hardly dared ask Heaven to make him worthy of it, feeling a saint was now interceding for him.

All the lady's affairs were found in perfect order. Papers showed she had long known her heart complaint and knew it would suddenly remove her; a prayer in her handwriting asked that her end might be in the arms of her son. Laura and Arthur talked over her sayings, packing up the vase destined for Dr. Goodenough, sending the silver coffee-pot to Portman, and giving a diamond ring with her hair to Warrington.

It was a hard day for Laura visiting Fairoaks, standing in the widow's blank chamber where those two had passed beloved hours. There were the clothes, the cushion, the chair, the glass that was no more to reflect her dear sad face. Pen knocked, led her downstairs, made her drink wine, and said, "God bless you." "Nothing shall ever be changed in your room," he said. "It is always your room—it is always my sister's room. Shall it not be so, Laura?" And Laura said, "Yes!"

Among the papers was a packet marked "Letters from Laura's father," which Arthur gave to her. The ink was faded, the tears dried out, the grief healed. Laura learned fully for the first time the tie that bound her so tenderly to Helen: how faithfully her more-than-mother had cherished her father's memory, how truly she had loved him, how meekly she had resigned him.

One legacy Pen remembered, unknown to Laura: Helen's wish to make a present to Fanny Bolton. Pen wrote to her, enclosing the letter under an envelope for Mr. Bows to read first. "Dear Fanny," Pen wrote, acknowledging her letters and thanking her for nursing him during his fever. "The last words of my dear mother who is no more were words of goodwill and gratitude to you... she said she would like to ask your pardon if she had harshly treated you—and that she would beg you to show your forgiveness by accepting some token of friendship."
