"Rise, my daughter," Fra Girolamo finally commanded. "Your servant waits nearby with the mules. It is time for me to proceed toward Florence."

Romola rose from her kneeling position. That silence had become a sacred ritual for her, cementing the state of yearning passivity she had recently embraced. In a single act—renouncing her plan to leave her husband—her will felt so thoroughly shattered that she required direction even for trivial matters. She lifted the edge of her cowl and spotted Maso and the second Dominican standing with their backs to her on the hill's edge, some ten yards away. Yet, she did not look at them immediately. Her gaze remained fixed on Savonarola, silent and expectant, as though the command to recall Maso must originate from him, not from her own voice.

"I will go and fetch them," he replied to her unspoken plea. "And I commend you, my daughter, to the Brother accompanying me. You seek to place yourself under guidance and to acquire that wisdom which has previously seemed foolishness to you. A primary gateway to this wisdom is the sacrament of confession. You require a confessor, my daughter, and I wish to entrust you to the care of Fra Salvestro, one of the brethren of San Marco, in whom I place my highest confidence."

"I would prefer no guidance other than yours, Father," Romola answered, her voice trembling with anxiety.

"My daughter, I do not act as your confessor. My vocation withdraws me from duties that would necessitate frequent contact with the laity and interfere with my specific responsibilities."

"Then shall I never be able to speak to you privately? If I falter, if—" Romola broke off, overcome by a sudden surge of agitation. A cold alarm seized her: she feared that the newfound strength derived from her renunciation might evaporate the moment Savonarola's direct personal influence faded.

"My daughter, if your soul requires the private word from my lips, you must inform me through Fra Salvestro, and I shall meet you in the sacristy or the choir of San Marco. Do not think I will cease to watch over you. I shall instruct my brother regarding you, ensuring he guides you into that path of labor for the suffering and the hungry to which you are called as a daughter of Florence in these times of dire need. I desire to see you among the weaker and less learned sisters as an apple tree stands out among the forest trees, so that your beauty and all your natural gifts serve merely as a lamp through which the Divine light shines more purely."

He paused, then added, "Now I shall go and summon your servant."

Once Maso had been sent ahead, Fra Salvestro stepped forward, and Savonarola led Romola toward him. She had already felt an internal shrinking away from a new guide who was a complete stranger to her; yet, to resist Savonarola's counsel would have been to adopt an attitude of independence at a moment when every ounce of her strength depended on the renunciation of that very independence. Consequently, her entire mental inclination was now directed toward performing what was painful rather than what was easy.

She bowed reverently to Fra Salvestro before meeting his eyes directly. However, when she raised her head and took him fully in view, her reluctance transformed into a palpable doubt. Some men possess a presence that naturally infuses trust and reverence; others demand that we bring our trust and reverence to them, pre-formed and ready. This distinction struck Romola sharply as Savonarola disappeared from her sight and Fra Salvestro Maruffi stood revealed in his place.

There was nothing manifestly repulsive in Fra Salvestro's face or manner—no hint of hypocrisy, no trace of coarseness. His features were actually handsomer than Fra Girolamo's, and his stature slightly taller. He was the long-standing confessor to many of Florence's leading figures, possessing vast experience as a spiritual director. Yet, his face bore the vacillating expression of a mind incapable of concentrating strongly upon one great emotion or belief—a trait fatal to influencing an ardent nature like Romola's. Such an expression does not necessarily signal insincerity; rather, it marks a shallow soul. One finds such individuals sincerely striving to fulfill a lofty vocation, earnestly composing their faces to utter sublime formulas, only to find their muscles twitch or relax despite their beliefs, much as prose intrudes where poetry should reign in those lacking divine frenzy.

Fra Salvestro possessed a peculiar susceptibility to visions, seemingly rooted in a constitution prone to somnambulism. Savonarola believed in the supernatural nature of these visions, whereas Fra Salvestro had initially resisted such interpretations, even rebuking Savonarola for his prophetic preaching. This served as further proof, if evidence were needed, that the relative greatness of men cannot be measured by their tendency to reject the superstitions of their age. Between the two, there could be no question of who was the great man and who was the lesser one. The disparity was measured precisely by the shift in Romola's feelings as Fra Salvestro began to address her with words of exhortation and encouragement.

After her initial angry resistance to Savonarola had subsided, she had forgotten the old dread of being dragged into the circle of fanaticism and sour, monkish piety. Now, however, the chilling breath of that fear stole over her once more. It could not decisively counteract the momentum her mind had just gained; it was akin to grey clouds closing over a sunrise, rendering her return path monotonous and somber. Perhaps of all somber journeys, the one undertaken after traversing a path with strong resolution is the one that most severely tests the fervor of renunciation.

As they re-entered the city gates, light snowflakes began to fall around them. The grey sister hurried homeward from the Piazza di San Marco, crossed the bridge again, and turned into the large doorway of the Via de' Bardi. Her footsteps left dark prints on the thin carpet of snow, and her cowl hung heavy and damp about her face. Inside, she ascended to her room, cast aside her serge habit, destroyed the farewell letters, restored all her cherished trifles to their places, loosened her hair, and donned her usual black dress.

Instead of embarking on a long, stirring journey, she was to sit in her accustomed spot. Snow tapped against the windows, and she was alone. She felt the dreariness, yet her courage remained high, reminiscent of a seeker who has discovered new signs of gold. She intended to navigate life by a fresh clue. She had poured all the energy of her will into renunciation. The empty tabernacle remained locked, and she placed Dino's crucifix outside it.

Nothing disrupted the outward monotony of her solitary home until night arrived like a white ghost at the windows. Yet, for Romola, this Christmas Eve of 1494 proved to be the most memorable of her life.
