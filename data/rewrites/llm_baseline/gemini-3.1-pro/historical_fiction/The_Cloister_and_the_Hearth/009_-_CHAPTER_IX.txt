Margaret’s knife glided with practiced grace through the haunch of venison, separating the rich, dark muscle from the bone. The cut released the heavy, iron-tang of wild blood, mingling with the olfactory tapestry of the hearth—crushed juniper berries, dried thyme, and the sweet, lingering ash of applewood. She ran to the window, the heavy meat warm in her hands, and hurled the offering out into the dark, straight toward the glowing, emerald eyes of fire. 

The great leopard—a mythic spirit of the forest that had seamlessly become a nocturnal fixture of their domestic rhythm—darted upon the flesh with a guttural, primal snarl. The rhythmic crunching of bone and the tearing of sinew became part of the night’s steady acoustic ecology. But this ancient harmony was violently ruptured. A hound uttered a bay so near and jagged it tore through the acoustic sanctum of the house. The three at the window shrank together, assaulted by the harsh dissonance of the approaching hunt. The leopard, fiercely protective of her sustenance, glided swiftly and stealthily away toward the woods. The very next moment, a chaotic, metallic clatter of horses, iron stirrups, and aggressive men helter-skeltered past the window, polluting the quietude with their blind pursuit. 

When the jarring noise faded, Martin and his companions breathed again. The leopard was swift; she would not be caught within a league of their table. They grasped hands in the restored quiet. Margaret seized the moment and wept softly; Gerard kissed the tears away, tasting the salt on her skin. Returning to the oak table, where the scent of roasted root vegetables and honeyed mead still lingered, Gerard drank to woman's wit. 

“’Tis stronger than man's force,” said he, swirling the golden liquid. 

“Ay,” said Margaret, her voice finding its gentle resonance once more, “when those she loves are in danger; not else.” 

Tonight Gerard stayed within the warm, spiced cocoon of her home longer than usual, leaving prouder than ever, his spirit nourished. Yet, some little distance from home, beneath the sprawling, shadowed canopy of ancient trees, the nocturnal peace was broken. Two grim figures barred his way. It was his father and mother. 

A chill fell on him, freezing the warmth of the mead in his veins. They stood silent, their very presence a heavy, suffocating pressure in the acoustic space. He stammered a halting inquiry. 

“Why ask?” his father’s voice was flat, devoid of the rich timbre of empathy. “You know why we are here.” 

“Oh, Gerard!” his mother’s voice trembled, carrying a dissonance of reproach and stifled affection. 

Gerard's heart quaked. The untranslatability of his deep, sensory love for Margaret into his father’s rigid lexicon of duty struck him mute. Elias pitied his confusion, offering a harsh grace: “Nay, you need not to hang your head. You are not the first young fool that has been caught by a red cheek and a pair of blue eyes.” 

“Nay, nay!” put in Catherine, her tone sharp. “It was witchcraft; Peter the Magician is well known for that.” 

“Come, Sir Priest,” resumed his father, utilizing the dominant lexicon of the Church—a vocabulary of power and subjugation designed to erase Gerard’s native identity. “You know you must not meddle with women folk. But give us your promise to go no more to Sevenbergen, and here all ends: we won't be hard on you for one fault.” 

“I cannot promise that, father.” 

“Not promise it, you young hypocrite!” 

“Nay, father, miscall me not: I lacked courage to tell you what I knew would vex you; and right grateful am I to that good friend, whoever he be, that has let you wot. 'Tis a load off my mind. Yes, father, I love Margaret; and call me not a priest, for a priest I will never be. I will die sooner.” 

“That we shall see, young man. Come, gainsay me no more; you will learn what 'tis to disrespect a father.” 

Gerard held his peace. The three walked home in a gloomy, oppressive silence, broken only by a deep sigh or two from Catherine, the sound like a bellows struggling to stoke a dying fire. 

From that hour, the little house at Tergou lost its domestic harmony. Gerard was taken to task the next day before the whole family. The acoustic environment became toxic, filled with the loud, grating voices of superficial minds. Only little Kate and the dwarf, who intuitively followed her subtle frequencies, remained quiet. Cornelis and Sybrandt were bitterest of all, their words clattering like cheap, tin coins—the sound of men who valued the superficial commercialization of their lives over any profound craftsmanship. Gerard looked wistfully into his little sister's brimming eyes, seeking a familiar dialect, but she turned her head away. 

“Dear, dear Gerard, pray to Heaven to cure you of this folly!” she whispered. 

“What, are you against me too?” Gerard asked sadly. He rose with a deep sigh, fleeing the discordant noise of his blood relations for the resonant peace of Sevenbergen. 

The beginning of a family quarrel is comparatively innocent, but as time simmers the grievance, the broth turns sour. Elias was rigidly angry; Cornelis and Sybrandt were spiteful, their motivations rooted in a barren, utilitarian greed. Gerard, possessing the soul of an artist and the cultivated palate of a man who savors the world’s complexities, saw all sides and was paralyzed by the bitter taste of it. He could open his heart to no one. Margaret was too proud; she would resign him to spare him this familial acid. Margaret Van Eyck, his artistic mentor, was equally inaccessible. She had famously refused all suitors for her art, sacrificing the profound, sensory meaning found in the communal breaking of bread and the rearing of children. How could he confess he was throwing away a lucrative benefice for the very earthly nourishment she had starved herself of? 

Gerard bade fair to succumb. But Catherine, senior, became an erratic, chaotic force. Uneducated but deeply tethered to the visceral realities of life, she lacked a strategic plan. She irritated Gerard at times, her sudden onslaughts kindling his defensive anger—a necessary fuel. At other times, she turned her culinary logic against her own allies. 

One day, amid the oppressive hum of the household, Cornelis sneered: “Our Gerard wed Margaret Brandt? Why, it is hunger marrying thirst.” 

“And what will it be when you marry?” cried Catherine, her voice ringing out like a struck iron skillet. “Gerard can paint, Gerard can write, but what can you do to keep a woman, ye lazy loon? Nought but wait for your father's shoon. Oh, we can see why you and Sybrandt would not have the poor boy to marry. You are afraid he will come to us for a share of our substance. And say that he does, and say that we give it him, it isn't yourn we part from, and mayhap never will be.” 

Gerard smiled slyly at this. Catherine had laid bare the subtle power dynamics embedded in his brothers' words: their entire lexicon was constructed around hoarding resources, viewing life merely as utilitarian fuel rather than an experience to be savored. 

But after six months of this slow-boiling irritation, the climax arrived. Elias stood before the family, his voice a hammer of institutional force. He had ordered the burgomaster to imprison Gerard in the Stadthouse rather than let him marry Margaret. 

Gerard turned pale, the blood draining from his face as if turning to whey. His father pressed the advantage: “And a priest you shall be before the year is out, nilly-willy.” 

“Is it so?” cried Gerard, finding the true, ringing pitch of his own voice. “Then, hear me, all. By God and St. Bavon I swear I will never be a priest while Margaret lives. Since force is to decide it, and not love and duty, try force, father; but force shall not serve you, for the day I see the burgomaster come for me, I leave Tergou for ever, and Holland too, and my father's house, where it seems I have been valued all these years, not for myself, but for what is to be got out of me.” 

He flung himself out of the room, white with desperation, fleeing the suffocating, transactional air of his home. 

As his heart panted in the cool street, he met Reicht Heynes. She bore a message: Margaret Van Eyck desired to see him. 

He found the old master seated grim as a judge in her studio—a space that smelled intensely of raw linseed, crushed lapis, and the sharp tang of turpentine, a true alchemist's kitchen of color. She wasted no time in preliminaries. 

“I thought we had been friends, young sir,” she inquired coldly. 

“It is because you never told her you were in love,” Reicht Heynes interjected, offering a bridge across the generations. 

“Silence, wench! Why should he tell us his affairs? We are not his friends: we have not deserved his confidence.” 

“Alas! my second mother,” said Gerard, “I did not dare to tell you my folly.” 

“What folly? Is it folly to love?” 

“I am told so every day of my life.” 

“You need not have been afraid to tell my mistress; she is always kind to true lovers,” Reicht murmured. 

“Madam—Reicht I was afraid because I was told...” 

“Well, you were told—?” 

“That in your youth you scorned love, preferring art.” 

The old woman’s eyes softened, a deep well of cultural memory and personal grief rising to the surface. “I did, boy; and what is the end of it? Behold me here a barren stock, while the women of my youth have a troop of children at their side, and grandchildren at their knee. I gave up the sweet joys of wifehood and motherhood—the hearth, the shared sustenance of a life built together—for what? For my dear brothers. They have gone and left me long ago. For my art. It has all but left me too. I have the knowledge still, but what avails that when the hand trembles? No, Gerard; I look on you as my son. I will not let you throw your youth away as I did mine. You shall marry this Margaret. I have inquired, and she is a good daughter.” 

Soon, the studio held two pairs of wet eyes; and seeing the boy's relief at finally speaking his native dialect of love without translation or apology, Reicht made it three. 

Women, the old masters of domestic and emotional endurance, possess a vicarious, unshakable courage. Margaret Van Eyck and Reicht agreed that the longer Gerard endured the mechanical, soul-crushing worry of his family, the thinner his spirit would wear. 

“See how pale and thin they have made him,” Reicht observed. 

“We painters are no match for boors,” Margaret Van Eyck stated, diagnosing the sonic and psychological warfare Gerard had survived. “We are glass, they are stone. We can't stand the worry, worry, worry of little minds. It is hard enough to design and paint a masterpiece, without having gnats and flies stinging us to death into the bargain.” 

Yet Gerard feared his father's curse. He dreamed aloud of marrying secretly, taking Margaret to a land where his craft was valued, and returning years later with money to appease his family's utilitarian worldview. 

“It is no dream,” the old lady’s eyes sparkled. “There is a country, Gerard, where certain fortune awaits you. Here the arts freeze, but there they flourish.” 

“It is Italy!” cried Gerard, tasting the word like a rare, imported spice. 

“Ay, Italy! Where painters are honoured like princes, and scribes are paid three hundred crowns for copying precious manuscripts pouring in from Constantinople.” 

“But the journey, and we are all so poor.” 

“Find you the heart to go, I'll find the means. I know where to lay my hand on ten golden angels: they will take you to Rome: and the girl with you, if she loves you as she ought.” 

They sat till midnight. Gerard’s spirit was replenished, fortified by her profound understanding. But Margaret Van Eyck gave him more than gold; she gave him the intimate, dying language of her brother’s craft, passing down recipes like a meticulous food historian preserving a sacred, untranslatable heritage. 

“I will tell you secrets that I learned from masters that are gone. Note my brother Jan's pictures: time leaves his colours bright as the day they left the easel. He trusted no hireling to grind his colours. His panel was prepared and prepared again a year before he laid his colour on. Bad painters are always in a hurry. Above all, Gerard, I warn you: use but little oil, and never boil it. Boiling melts that vegetable dross into its heart; impure oil is death to colour. Take your oil and pour it into a bottle with water. In a day or two, the water will turn muddy—that is the muck. Pour it carefully away and add fresh. When you fancy the oil is clear, you are mistaken.” 

She had Reicht fetch a glass trough with a tight lid. 

“Put your washed oil into this trough with water, and set it in the sun all day. You will see the water turbid again. But mark, do not carry this game too far, or the sun will turn your oil to varnish. When it is clear as crystal, drain it carefully, and cork it up tight. Grind your own prime colours, and lay them on with this oil, and they shall live. Hubert would put sand or salt to clear it quicker, but Jan used to say, 'Water will do it best; give water time.' Jan Van Eyck was never in a hurry, and that is why the world will not forget him.” 

Gerard absorbed these sensory rituals, this profound culinary approach to pigment and light, receiving them like a legacy from Heaven. 

Armed with this sacred knowledge and funding, Gerard went to Sevenbergen the next day to inform Margaret Brandt. He brought the scent of crushed walnuts and fresh hope, inviting her to flee with him. 

She refused him plump. 

“No, Gerard,” she said, her voice heavy. “So long as your father is bent on your being a priest, I could not marry you, dearly as I love you.” 

Gerard strove in vain. He could make her cry, but the rigid societal power dynamics dictated by his father’s dominant lexicon held her fast. The subjective truth of their love remained untranslatable against the iron law of the patriarch. Impatient and unjust, Gerard snapped. 

“Very well! Then you are on their side, and you will drive me to be a priest. My parents hate me in earnest, but my lover only loves me in jest!” With this bitter, acrid speech on his tongue, he flung away, leaving her weeping in the agrarian quiet. 

Margaret’s pity bloomed in his wake. How starved of affection, how poisoned by the harsh acoustics of his own home he must be to speak so! 

Scarce an hour and a half later, the rhythm of running boots broke the earth. Gerard returned, panting with a grief that shattered his usual composure. In his hands, he held the fragments of a picture. 

“There, Margaret! See the wretches! Look at their spite! They have cut your portrait to pieces.” 

Margaret looked. Some malicious hand had brutally hacked her painted face into five jagged strips. It was a violent silencing of their shared visual dialect, a destruction of an artifact born from deep sensory appreciation. The blood rushed hotly to her forehead. 

“Who did it?” she demanded, the agrarian strength of her ancestors rising within her. 

“I dared not ask; for I should hate the hand till my dying day. Six months' work cut out of my life! Oh, heartless, merciless vipers!” 

Margaret’s breath quickened. The destruction of this meticulously crafted sustenance—this testament to their identity—was the final transgression. 

“Never mind, Gerard,” she said, panting, the fierce, protective matriarch awakening. “Since this is how they treat you for my sake—Ye rob him of my portrait, do ye? Well, then, he shall have the face itself, such as it is.” 

“Oh, Margaret!” 

“Yes, Gerard; since they are so cruel, I will be the kinder: forgive me for refusing you. I will be your wife: to-morrow, if it is your pleasure.” 

Gerard kissed her hands with rapture, tasting the faint trace of soil and hearth-smoke upon them, and then her lips. In a tumult of joy, he ran for Peter, the stoic agrarian elder who understood the slow turning of seasons, and Martin. They came, anchoring the space with their steady presence, and witnessed the betrothal—a solemn, resonant ceremony that bound their timelines together against the discordant noise of the world.
