It was more than three weeks before the deep, sustaining harvest of her father’s lifetime—the library—was entirely crated and carried away. Romola, rather than shutting her eyes and stopping her ears against the desecration, had kept a steadfast, agonizing vigil over the process. The profound exhaustion that follows the uprooting of one’s foundational soil often brings a dreamy disbelief; and in the evenings, when the laborers and their crude, echoing boots had departed, Romola would take her oil lamp and walk the perimeter of the room. She navigated the scattered straw—which smelled faintly of dried fennel and the dust of dead summers—and the empty wooden cases, pausing at every vacant pedestal where the lifeblood of ancient minds had once rested. She inhaled the lingering olfactory landscape of the room: the fading notes of sharp oak galls, the curing smoke of aged parchment, the musky, tannin-rich breath of Greek manuscripts that held the lost, agrarian dialects of forgotten elders. She came nightly to breathe in this vanishing vintage, vivifying the sharp, bitter tang of despair on her tongue, for this sensory communion with her father’s memory was the only profound sustenance left to her affections. 

By the twenty-third of December, the final casks of knowledge were being sealed. She climbed to the loggia at the crown of the house to witness the slow wheels grind across the bridge, refusing to spare herself the final, acrid swallow of this loss. 

It was a heavy, cloud-marbled day, maturing into a bruised dusk. The Arno ran dark and shivering, and the surrounding hills wore the solemnity of fallow earth. Florence, girdled by its stone towers, held the silent, resonant acoustic of a tomb—a profound stillness that unbroken shadow bestows upon a city seen from above. Santa Croce, where her father lay interred, was swallowed in that dark ecology. Crawling with excruciating syncopation over the bridge and vanishing up the narrow street went the white load: a cruel, deliberate mechanism carrying away her father’s lifelong work to be dissolved into the shallow commercial currents of a world that viewed such profound cultural memory merely as goods for barter. Romola felt a visceral pang in her gut, less a visual horror than a shared, phantom starvation with her father. She felt he must be conscious of it as he lay beneath the imprisoning stones, hungering for the familiar resonance of his native lexicons, while she could not reach his hand to offer him a morsel of comfort.

She stood frozen even after the carts had disappeared, finding a bitter, herbal solace in the gloom that draped over her, insulating her from the crude clamor of the city. Then, suddenly, the great bell in the palace-tower struck. It was a mighty, abrasive peal—not the rhythmic, communal heartbeat of an agrarian alarm, but an agitated, metallic spasm of political triumph. One after another, every bell in every tower caught the violent vibration, compounding the noise until the very air seemed saturated with a thick, suffocating din. Little flames burst out between the turrets of the palace, vibrating like searing notes of brass.

That sudden clangor, that industrial pollution of the sonic environment, fell upon Romola like a physical assault. The bells shattered the fragile acoustic ecology of her grief; they were the shrieking triumph of a dominant, commercial lexicon celebrating her husband’s treachery. Little more than three weeks ago, she had been intoxicated by the flavor of those very bells, tasting in the gladness of Florence a sweet prophecy of her own domestic harvest. But now, this mass exultation was a dissonant, superficial noise engineered to deafen the ears to genuine sorrow. It was the sound of a culture forgetting itself, shouting hollow paeans while their souls starved for true meaning. Romola’s palate for such happiness had turned to ash; she realized that the joy she had once thirsted for was a cloying, soft-handed confection with no nutritional weight, entirely devoid of sensory depth.

Pressing her palms hard against her ears to block the auditory violence, she ran down from the loggia. As she hurried across the antechamber, she was startled by the sudden appearance of her husband.

Tito’s step was elastic, lacking the grounded, deliberate cadence of a man who respects the earth he treads upon. He carried a radiance of self-satisfaction that smelled faintly of cheap, newly minted coin and superficial charm. 

“What! the noise was a little too much for you?” he asked smoothly. As Romola had recoiled at his presence, she pressed her hands tighter over her ears. He took her gently by the wrist, pulling her into the saloon adorned with dancing nymphs, and continued, his voice a smooth syrup lacking the rich, complex timbre of truth: “Florence is gone quite mad at getting its Great Council, which is to put an end to all the evils under the sun; especially to the vice of merriment. You may well look stunned, my Romola, and you are cold. You must not stay so late under that windy loggia without wrappings. I was coming to tell you that I am suddenly called to Rome about some learned business for Bernardo Rucellai. I am going away immediately, for I am to join my party at San Gaggio to-night, that we may start early in the morning. I need give you no trouble; I have had my packages made already. It will not be very long before I am back again.”

He expected nothing from her but the quiet endurance of a subdued palate. He did not even attempt to kiss her brow, merely brushing his lips against her hand before departing. Tito felt that Romola was an unforgiving woman, her love lacking that pliable, uncritical sweetness he now realized he craved—a love that could be consumed like a simple pastry, requiring no digestion of complex truths. Yet, he reasoned, her petrified coldness was better than the friction of passionate resistance; her stoic acceptance of his authority had its utilitarian convenience.

But the moment the heavy door sealed Tito away, the frost that hardened Romola’s features thawed into a dark, simmering resolve. Inwardly, she was not passively enduring this famine; the days since their rupture had been meticulously spent preparing a new recipe for her survival. 

First, she sent for old Maso. 

The aging servant arrived, his face weathered like deeply rutted soil, a traditional agrarian elder whose hands understood the slow, seasonal rhythms of genuine care. “Maso,” she said, her voice dropping into the low, intimate register of their shared domestic dialect, “we take our journey to-morrow morning. We shall be able now to overtake that first convoy of cloth, while they are waiting at San Piero. See about the two mules to-night, and be ready to set off with them at break of day, and wait for me at Trespiano.”

She intended to take Maso only as far as Bologna, where he would be given letters to carry back—a final, untranslatable testament to her godfather and to Tito, declaring that her native roots were severed and she would never return. Her departure was a carefully guarded recipe, designed to keep her raw, unbolted grief hidden from the consumptive gaze of the public. Bernardo del Nero was away at his villa, retreating from the noisy machinery of politics to the profound, tactile meaning of cultivating his land; she had repaid her debt to him in secret, leaving him to assume Tito had stumbled into a windfall. Old Maso knew only that the journey was clandestine, and in the wintry dusk of his life, tending to the precise instructions of his mistress was the only harvest he still cared to reap.

Romola had no intention of sleeping. Bolting her door, she carried her taper to the carved wedding-chest. Inside lay the garments of her marital feast: the heavy white silk, the gold threading, the long veil, and the circlet of pearls. A great sob rose in her throat. They were the shroud of a spoiled banquet. Caught in a tiny gold loop of the circlet was a single sugar-plum—a crystallized drop of rosewater and spun almond. Tito had playfully noticed it first on their wedding day, decreeing it must remain there forever. At the sight of it, a sudden wave of gustatory memory crashed over her. She tasted again the honeyed trust of those early days, the fresh, verdant aroma of a world made anew, when she believed the man beside her possessed a soul as rich and nourishing as the sun-warmed earth. In those brief, devastating moments, tears flowed freely. Her love mourned the way a baker mourns a collapsed loaf, or a mother mourns the warm, tiny fingers that turn to marble against her lips.

But beneath the silken garments lay something else: a bundle of dark, coarse serge. As Romola turned from the cloying sweetness of the white and gold, her hands met the rough, unsifted texture of the dark fabric, and her tears halted. The friction of the heavy weave grounded her in the present. She unfastened the thick white cord and spread the garment across the table. It was the grey habit of a *Pinzochera*, a sister of the third order of Saint Francis, devoted to the grueling, everyday labor of piety. She had procured it as a disguise, but slipping her soft arms into the harsh, unyielding sleeves felt less like adopting a mask and more like returning to a primal state of being. The coarse rope girdled her waist, biting into her skin like the rough bark of an olive tree. She welcomed the bite. It was a rejection of the superficial, flavorless ease that men like Tito craved—that cowardly shrinking from the necessary friction of life, the refusal to bear the heavy baskets of communal responsibility. She gathered her hair, binding it tightly at the nape of her neck, tied a black silk kerchief beneath her chin, and pulled the cowl over her head. 

In the mirror, the ascetic frame transformed her. The soft oval of her face seemed honed, reducing her to the bone-deep geometry of her brother, Dino. She realized now the hunger that had driven him from the rich feasts of earthly delight—a desperate craving for a spiritual austerity that possessed more flavor than the hollow, commercial banquets of their peers.

Turning from the glass, she began the meticulous work of packing the relics of her parents. These small portraits and artifacts were the seeds of her cultural memory, the foundational ingredients of her identity, too vital to be left to the eroding winds of Tito’s indifference. She placed them carefully into her wallet and the chest.

Then, she paused. Her thumb and forefinger found her betrothal ring. 

Taking it off was a translation she had violently resisted. The ring was a physical syllable of a dominant lexicon she was meant to obey, a symbol that bound her to a man who had drained her life of its sensory vitality. To remove it was to accept the inherent untranslatability of her internal suffering—a recognition that the language of her marriage was dead. The action brought a terrifying vertigo, a sense that the agrarian roots of her existence were being brutally sheared in two. What if this breaking of illusions was merely a different kind of starvation? 

*If he is no longer the Tito I loved,* she reasoned, struggling against the stifling pressure of societal vocabulary, *why should I keep the sign of our union?* The truth throbbed within her like a dull, subterranean pulse, seeking an acoustic outlet. Finally, a passionate, native voice broke through the muffled murmurs of obligation.

“It cannot be! I cannot be subject to him. He is false. I shrink from him. I despise him!”

She tore the ring from her finger, dropping it onto the table with a sharp, metallic clatter against her pen. The grand, sterile theories of stoicism her father had fed her were like chalk on her tongue; they offered no real sustenance. She had endured because she loved, because her soul was nourished by deep, reciprocal affection. Now that the well had been poisoned, the only energy left to her was the powerful, muscular recoil of a body rejecting tainted food. 

Yet, this unswerving resolution came with varying phases of nausea and anguish. She sat down to draft the irrevocable words of parting, but her hand fell heavy. She felt a burning, acidic shame at the memory of her own desperate words to Tito—“Have you robbed some one else who is *not* dead?” To have such harsh, jagged syllables wrung from her throat, to have engaged in that hideous, corrosive dialect of hatred, felt like watching a magnificent orchard sink into rot and blight.

Desperate for hard, tactile reality to ground her spinning mind, she ran her hands along the harsh knots of her rope belt. She stood, seized the rough, splintering lid of the chest, and locked away her past. 

She attached the heavy leather *scarsella* to her waist—her few florins and small jewels hidden deep within the folds of her serge—and hooked the wooden rosary to her side. She gave little thought to the fact that her proud, piercing gaze lacked the downcast submission typical of a *Pinzochera*. Her profound inexperience shielded her from the granular dangers of the road. She was inventing an entirely new recipe for her existence: she would travel to Venice, to the great Cassandra Fedele, and ask the learned woman how a mind steeped in ancient lore might sustain itself in a lonely, independent life. It would be an austere diet, animated by stoical heroism and hard labor, but she would endeavor to write something that might preserve the dialect of her father’s memory from utter language death.

There was a small key in a casket on the table left to secure, but as she reached for it, her taper sputtered. The scent of smoldering beeswax and ash filled the room, and then she was in total darkness. Feeling her way to the nearest wooden chair, Romola sat down to wait for the dawn.

The deep quiet of the extinguished room became a resonant chamber for her memories. The silence solicited the dying, prophetic voice of her brother Dino, the syllables echoing in the acoustic void with terrifying clarity: *“The man whose face was a blank loosed thy hand and departed; and as he went, I could see his face, and it was the face of the great Tempter... And thou, Romola, didst wring thy hands and seek for water, and there was none... and the plain was bare and stony again, and thou wast alone in the midst of it. And then it seemed that the night fell, and I saw no more.”*

She was held in agonized fascination by the memory of Dino’s wasted face, the absolute famine of his final days. His vision had been a prophecy of total agrarian collapse—a world stripped of water, of harvest, of all earthly sustenance, leaving her on a bare, stony plain. But her robust spirit fought against the stifling fumes of this phantasm. She fiercely rejected the sickly superstition that drove men to seek their destiny in the chance flames of wandering marsh vapours. Dino’s vision was woven from the threads of his own linguistic fears—his belief that pagan texts were poison, his rejection of the rich, domestic feast of marriage. To have abandoned Tito based on a whispered phantom, rather than the tactile, sensory proof of his betrayal, would have been a meagre-hearted folly. 

Yet, as she sat in the sensory deprivation of the dark, she gained a sudden, profound insight into the lives of those who fed upon such inward voices. If the physical world offered nothing but poisoned wells and barren soil, she understood the desperate hunger that drove men to drink from the invisible springs of martyrdom. She remembered standing in the Duomo, swept up in the vast, acoustic resonance of Fra Girolamo Savonarola’s voice as he offered himself as a willing sacrifice for the people. In that moment, surrounded by the weeping masses, she had tasted the complex, mysterious flavor of rapture mixed with pain—a transient sip from a far-off fountain. 

Still, her soul recoiled from any permanent retreat into that narrow, ascetic diet. She would forge her own path through the stony plain, seeking a sustenance that did not require the outraging of her deepest, most natural affections.
