The beginning of any acquaintance—whether it is with a person, or with a place, or with a dish you have never tasted—is only this: you get, at last, a clean outline for what had been ignorance, a shape you can turn over in the mind as you would a loaf newly drawn from the oven, judging its crust before you ever cut it.

Mr. Grandcourt’s desire to be introduced had not come upon Gwendolen as a thunderclap; it had been in the air all day, like the distant tuning of strings before dancing begins. Yet when Lord Brackenshaw shifted aside and the prefigured gentleman became the real one advancing toward her, she felt the small shock that betrays itself in the body before pride can intercept it. Heat rose to her cheeks—annoying heat, as if she had been caught too plainly alive.

For this man was not at all the creature her fancy had been cooking up in secret: no extravagant ornament, no theatrical flourish. He was only a little taller than herself, so that their eyes met on a level that made her feel as if there were no shelter in looking down or up. He raised his hat with an ease that had neither vanity nor hesitation in it. The hat-lift disclosed a wide baldness with a mere reddish-blonde fringe, and at the same instant a hand so perfectly cared for it might have belonged to some fastidious cutter of truffles who could not bear the least grit beneath a nail. His face—clean-shaven—was handsome in a narrow, measured way, the lines running almost straight, the whisker too cut in a perpendicular that seemed less a choice than a law. There was no smile: not the faintest twitch, not the smallest grimace that might be pleaded as friendliness. Nor was there that stiff, correct drilling into which many an Englishman draws himself, hinting at some imprisoned liveliness that will leap out when parade is over. Grandcourt’s bearing was not rigid; it was rather slack with assured possession, as if he had never needed to brace himself against anything. His fairness was faded, like a stage face when the paint is washed off; his long gray eyes gave nothing away—neither anger nor pleasure—only a cool indifference that could observe without seeming to care.

It is stupid work, trying to describe a human creature as if he could be captured at once in words. We learn the alphabet quickly enough; we do not, for that, know the language. Gwendolen had only this first impression to seize on, and it condensed itself into one private sentence that steadied her: He is not ridiculous.

Then Lord Brackenshaw had moved away, and what is called conversation began—yet with a curious rhythm. Grandcourt looked at her persistently, a slow, exploring gaze without any change of expression, while she, who hated to be mastered by surprise, only returned the look now and then in quick flashes—observation softened with that habitual coquetry which was, in her, as instinctive as throwing a pinch of salt to wake the taste. And after each answer she gave, there fell a pause—sometimes long, sometimes brief—as if he let the words lie between them like a card placed down, to be considered before the next was played.

“I used to think archery was a great bore,” he began. His voice had the fine accent of a man bred to have his tones received; yet it came with a broken drawl, as if some distinguished cold sat in his chest and refused to be hurried.

“Are you converted to-day?” said Gwendolen.

And while he paused, she imagined, with rapid irritation, the different degrees and species of opinion he might be forming about her—how far admiration went, where contempt began.

“Yes,” he said at last, “since I saw you shooting. One generally sees people missing and simpering.”

“I suppose you are a first-rate shot with a rifle,” she returned.

Again that pause—during which she had time to sketch him for herself as if she were telling an unseen listener: bald crown, perfect hand, lifeless eyes, the air of a man who has never once needed to ask pardon.

“I have left off shooting.”

“Oh, then you are formidable,” she said, letting a lightness play over her words as a cook might scatter herbs over something meant to bite. “People who have done things once and left them off make one feel contemptible—as if one were wearing cast-off fashions. I hope you have not left off all follies, because I practise a great many.”

He waited again, as if he tasted her speech and found it neither sweet nor sour enough to show reaction.

“What do you call follies?”

“Well—whatever is agreeable is called a folly, I think. But you have not left off hunting, I hear.”

Pause: and in it she recollected the things said of him—his position, his fortune—and decided, unwillingly, that he had the most aristocratic look she had ever met with: not by flourish, but by a quiet assumption that the world was arranged for him like a well-served table.

“One must do something,” he answered.

“And do you care about the turf?—or is that among the things you have left off?”

“I run a horse now and then; but I don’t go in for the thing as some men do. Are you fond of horses?”

“Yes, indeed. I never like my life so well as when I am on horseback, having a great gallop. I think of nothing. I only feel myself strong and happy.”

And as she spoke it, there ran through her a bodily memory: the tightness of glove leather, the clean animal heat rising through the saddle, the keen air cutting the face, the thud of hooves that makes the blood answer like a drum. It was not thought; it was sensation—and she would not pretend otherwise for any man.

“Do you like danger?” he said.

“I don’t know. When I am on horseback I never think of danger. It seems to me that if I broke my bones I should not feel it. I should go at anything that came in my way.”

He listened as he had listened to everything—without showing the smallest quickening; yet his gaze never slackened.

“You would perhaps like tiger-hunting or pig-sticking. I saw some of that for a season or two in the East. Everything here is poor stuff after that.”

“You are fond of danger, then?” she asked.

“One must have something or other. But one gets used to it.”

“I begin to think I am very fortunate,” she said, leaning a little on her own frankness as on a rail, “because everything is new to me: it is only that I can’t get enough of it. I am not used to anything except being dull—which I should like to leave off, as you have left off shooting.”

“How are you dull?”

“This is a dreadful neighbourhood. There is nothing to be done in it. That is why I practised my archery.”

And underneath her complaint lay another, more secret: the dullness of being an unmarried girl, shut in by proprieties like a larder locked against the hungry; allowed to taste, perhaps, but never to choose the meal.

“You have made yourself queen of it,” he said. “I imagine you will carry the first prize.”

“I don’t know that. I have great rivals. Did you not observe how well Miss Arrowpoint shot?”

“Miss Arrowpoint. No—that is, yes.”

“Shall we go now and hear what the scoring says? Every one is going to the other end now—shall we join them? I think my uncle is looking toward me. He perhaps wants me.”

She found relief in the movement, in breaking the sealed little room of the tête-à-tête. It was not that the talk had been wholly disagreeable; rather that the unwonted flush would not go, and she hated the feeling of not being entirely mistress of herself. Besides, this Mr. Grandcourt—who seemed to feel his own importance more than he felt hers—must not imagine she held herself ready at his whistle because the world found him a desirable match. Few unreasonablenesses are harder to endure than being taken for granted.

“You have just missed the gold arrow, Gwendolen,” said Mr. Gascoigne as she came up. “Miss Juliet Fenn scores eight above you.”

“I am very glad to hear it. I should have felt that I was making myself too disagreeable—taking the best of everything,” she said easily.

It was impossible to be jealous of Juliet Fenn, a girl as plain and middling as a market noon—serviceable, unadorned—save for that one bright skill with the bow. There are men who speak of daughters as if beauty were a moral duty imposed upon them; yet those same “intending bridegrooms” might do well to consider what faces they contribute to the future before they bargain for loveliness as if it were a fruit one buys by the pound.

Now the groups mingled more briskly, voices crossing and recrossing like swallows. Gwendolen, choosing at last to look outward rather than inward, saw Grandcourt having Herr Klesmer presented to him by a stranger unknown to her—a middle-aged man with a dark full face and fat hands, on easy terms with both. Soon the stranger led the way to the Arrowpoints, whose acquaintance he and Grandcourt already possessed. She did not care to know his name; she wanted rather to watch Grandcourt with other people, to see if he had reserved some warmer manner for them.

But no: precisely the same bearing and tone. Only he looked little at Miss Arrowpoint; his attention lay more on Klesmer, who spoke with animation—hands stretched out, forefinger pointing down, arms folded then flung open again, the mane of hair tossed as if the very air should obey his argument. Grandcourt listened with narrow eyes, impassive, his left forefinger lodged in his waistcoat-pocket, his right lightly touching his thin whisker—a man who seemed to keep even his gestures on a short leash.

“I wonder which style Miss Arrowpoint admires most,” flickered through Gwendolen’s mind, and the thought gathered a mocking curve about her mouth. Yet she refused to linger in looking as if curious. She turned her brightness on those near her, determined not to care whether Grandcourt sought her again.

He did, however, at the moment of leaving, when he could offer his arm to conduct Mrs. Davilow to her carriage.

“Shall we meet again in the ball-room?” Gwendolen said as he raised his hat.

“Yes,” he returned, in that same drawl, with perfect gravity.

“You were wrong for once, Gwendolen,” her mother said in the brief drive back to the castle.

“In what, mamma?”

“About Mr. Grandcourt’s appearance and manners. You can’t find anything ridiculous in him.”

“I suppose I could if I tried, but I don’t want to do it,” said Gwendolen, a little pettishly; and her mother, accustomed to the shifting weather of her daughter’s mood, feared to press further.

On such occasions it was the rule that ladies and gentlemen dined apart, the separation being counted a mercy to both—rest for the women from the demand to smile, rest for the men from the demand to attend. The gentlemen, indeed, kept alive a set of archery tales about the ladies’ epicurism—stories told with mock horror, as if it were a scandal that a woman should have a palate, as if taste were a masculine monopoly like certain offices or estates. Lord Brackenshaw, who prided himself on being something of a gourmet, would each year repeat Byron’s opinion that a woman should never be seen eating—introducing it with a confidential, “The fact is,” as if he were admitting a difficult truth. Yet even as he said it, the house rang with the gentler acoustics of old prosperity: cutlery not clattering but chiming; voices kept to civilized distances; servants gliding rather than stamping—sounds that were part of comfort, as the faint fragrance of soup and baked meats was part of it. In such a place, the mind could breathe. It is the brutal modern world—its needless racket, its metal shrieks and hammering—where one’s inner life is battered thin; here, the very noise had a measure.

In the ladies’ dining-room it was plain that Gwendolen was not a general favourite with her own sex. No beginnings of intimacy sprang up around her. The other girls noticed what she said rather than spoke to her in free exchange, as if they were listening to a performer they did not mean to applaud. Perhaps she was not much interested in them; perhaps, when left among them, she felt something like empty benches—an audience that would not warm. Mrs. Vulcany once remarked that Miss Harleth was too fond of the gentlemen; yet Gwendolen was not fond of them in the least—she was fond of their homage, and women did not offer it. The single exception, quiet but persistent, was Miss Arrowpoint, who often managed to be beside her without display and talked with a mild friendliness, as if goodwill were a habit of the blood.

“She knows, as I do,” Gwendolen thought, “that our friends are ready to quarrel over a husband for us—and she is determined not to enter into the quarrel.”

“I think Miss Arrowpoint has the best manners I ever saw,” Mrs. Davilow said later, when they were in a dressing-room with Mrs. Gascoigne and Anna, but a little apart, where their voices could have privacy.

“I wish I were like her,” said Gwendolen.

“Why? Are you getting discontented with yourself, Gwen?”

“No. But I am discontented with things. She seems contented.”

“You ought to be satisfied to-day. You must have enjoyed the shooting. I saw you did.”

“Oh, that is over now, and I don’t know what will come next,” Gwendolen said, stretching herself with a sort of moan and flinging up her arms.

Her arms were bare now; it was the fashion to dance in the archery dress, the jacket thrown off. The simplicity of her white cashmere, bordered with pale green, set off her form to the utmost. A thin line of gold lay about her neck; the gold star on her breast was her only other ornament. Her smooth soft hair, piled high into a grand crown, drew a clear line around her brow. One could imagine a painter loving that steadied moment and fastening it—never mind that life is not paint and never holds still.

“The dancing will come next,” said Mrs. Davilow. “You are sure to enjoy that.”

“I shall only dance in the quadrille,” said Gwendolen. “I told Mr. Clintock so. I shall not waltz or polk with any one.”

“Why in the world do you say that all on a sudden?”

“I can’t bear having ugly people so near me.”

“Whom do you mean by ugly people?”

“Oh—plenty.”

“Mr. Clintock, for example, is not ugly.” Mrs. Davilow dared not name Grandcourt, though the name fluttered unspoken between them like a moth near flame.

“Well—I hate woollen cloth touching me.”

“Fancy!” Mrs. Davilow said to her sister, who had now come up. “Gwendolen says she will not waltz or polk.”

“She is rather given to whims, I think,” Mrs. Gascoigne replied gravely. “It would be more becoming in her to behave as other young ladies do on such an occasion as this; especially when she has had the advantage of first-rate dancing lessons.”

“Why should I dance if I don’t like it, aunt? It is not in the catechism.”

“My dear!” Mrs. Gascoigne said, sharply checking her; and Anna looked frightened at Gwendolen’s daring. But they passed on without more.

Something had altered in Gwendolen’s mood since the hour of exultation on the archery-ground, as if a shadow had crossed the sun and made her restless. Still, she did not look worse for it under the chandeliers when the ball-room filled. The soft splendour—light caught in glass and satin, flowers breathing out their damp sweetness from the conservatory—had a soothing power, like the first warm waft when the lid is lifted from a tureen: a promise that the senses will be cared for. And alongside that came the steadier, more dangerous comfort—the consciousness of being sought. Hardly a dancing man but was eager to have her for a partner, and each whom she accepted complained, half-laughing and half-wounded, that she would not waltz or polk.

“Are you under a vow, Miss Harleth?”—“Why are you so cruel to us all?”—“You waltzed with me in February.”—“And you who waltz so perfectly!” The exclamations pricked her vanity pleasantly. The ladies who waltzed told themselves that Miss Harleth only wished to seem particular. Her uncle, overhearing one refusal, supported her at once.

“Gwendolen has usually good reasons.”

He thought her more distinguished in not waltzing, and he wanted her distinguished. This archery ball was meant to keep a subdued pitch suited to dignities clerical and secular; it was not an outlet for violent youth. He himself considered the fashionable dances too much of a romp. Even the sound of the evening followed this intention: no stamping, no drunken roar of fiddles, but a regulated pulse—music that did not tyrannize the room, and talk that could still be heard as human talk, not shouted against noise. In such measured sound, people’s tempers were less frayed; one could almost believe manners were bred in the ear.

Among the remonstrant dancing men, however, Mr. Grandcourt was not numbered. After standing up for a quadrille with Miss Arrowpoint, he seemed disposed to ask no other partner. Gwendolen observed him frequently with the Arrowpoints, but he never took an opportunity to approach her. Mr. Gascoigne spoke to him sometimes—Mr. Gascoigne being everywhere, like a well-meaning bell whose sound reaches every corner—but Grandcourt remained apart.

It entered Gwendolen’s mind that she would, after all, have no trouble about him. Perhaps he had looked at her without admiration and was too used to everything in the world to consider her more than one of the girls invited in that neighbourhood. Very likely the elders were ridiculous to weave romances about what a man would do, without having seen him even through a telescope. Perhaps he meant to marry Miss Arrowpoint. Whatever happened, she told herself, she would not be disappointed. She had never committed herself—not even in silent confidence—to anything he might do. Still, she noticed that he did sometimes, quietly, gradually, change his position according to hers, so that he could see her when she was dancing. And if he did not admire her—so much the worse for him.

Late in the evening, when she had accepted Herr Klesmer as a partner, Grandcourt’s movement for sight of her became more direct. Klesmer, whose wide glance seemed to seize everything and nothing by turns, said as they walked, “Mr. Grandcourt is a man of taste. He likes to see you dancing.”

“Perhaps he likes to look at what is against his taste,” said Gwendolen, with a light laugh. She felt courageous with Klesmer. “He may be so tired of admiring that he likes disgust for variety.”

“Those words are not suitable to your lips,” Klesmer said quickly, with one of his grand frowns, shaking his hand as if to banish the discordant phrase from the air. “I should require your words to be what your face and form are—always among the meanings of a noble music.”

“That is a compliment as well as a correction,” said Gwendolen. “I am obliged for both. But do you know, I am bold enough to wish to correct you, and require you to understand a joke?”

“One may understand jokes without liking them,” Klesmer returned, terrible in his sincerity. “I have had opera books sent me full of jokes; it was just because I understood them that I did not like them. The comic people are ready to challenge a man because he looks grave. ‘You don’t see the witticism, sir?’ ‘No, sir, but I see what you meant.’ Then I am ticketed as a fellow without esprit. But in fact”—here he dropped suddenly from rapid narrative into reflective tone, and the frown became impressive—“I am very sensible to wit and humor.”

“I am glad you tell me that,” said Gwendolen, and she had a little wickedness to herself because his thoughts had already flown off on the wings of his own statement. “Pray, who is that standing near the card-room door? He is a friend of yours, I think.”

“No, no; an amateur I have seen in town. Lush—a Mr. Lush—too fond of Meyerbeer and Scribe—too fond of the mechanical-dramatic.”

“Thanks. I wanted to know whether you thought his face and form required that his words should be among the meanings of noble music?”

Klesmer was conquered; a delightful smile flashed out, and they were friendly from that moment until she begged to be deposited beside her mamma.

Three minutes afterward, all Gwendolen’s preparations for Grandcourt’s indifference were cancelled. She had turned her head after some remark to her mother, and there he was: he had made his way up to her.

“May I ask if you are tired of dancing, Miss Harleth?” he began, looking down with that same unperturbed expression.

“Not in the least.”

“Will you do me the honor—the next—or another quadrille?”

“I should have been very happy,” said Gwendolen, glancing at her card, “but I am engaged for the next to Mr. Clintock—and indeed I perceive that I am doomed for every quadrille; I have not one to dispose of.”

She was not sorry to punish Mr. Grandcourt’s tardiness; yet she would have liked to dance with him. She looked up, giving him a charming smile—one that seemed to offer sweetness as plainly as a dish set forward on the table. He stood still, looking down at her with no smile at all.

“I am unfortunate in being too late,” he said, after a moment.

“It seemed to me you did not care for dancing,” said Gwendolen. “I thought it might be one of the things you had left off.”

“Yes. But I have not begun to dance with you,” he answered—always that pause, as if each sentence were measured out with a spoon. “You make dancing a new thing, as you make archery.”

“Is novelty always agreeable?”

“No, no—not always.”

“Then I don’t know whether to feel flattered or not. When you had once danced with me there would be no more novelty in it.”

“On the contrary, there would probably be much more.”

“That is deep. I don’t understand.”

“It is difficult to make Miss Harleth understand her power?”

He had turned, while saying this, to Mrs. Davilow; and she, smiling gently at her daughter, replied, “I think she does not generally strike people as slow to understand.”

“Mamma,” said Gwendolen, in a deprecating tone, “I am adorably stupid, and want everything explained to me—when the meaning is pleasant.”

“If you are stupid, I admit that stupidity is adorable,” said Grandcourt—after the usual pause, without change of tone; but plainly he knew how to place the words where they would have effect.

“I begin to think my cavalier has forgotten me,” Gwendolen said presently. “I see the quadrille is being formed.”

“He deserves to be renounced,” said Grandcourt.

“I think he is very pardonable,” Gwendolen returned.

“There must have been some misunderstanding,” said Mrs. Davilow. “Mr. Clintock was too anxious about the engagement to have forgotten it.”

Then Lady Brackenshaw came up.

“Miss Harleth, Mr. Clintock has charged me to express to you his deep regret that he was obliged to leave without having the pleasure of dancing with you again. An express came from his father, the archdeacon; something important; he was to go. He was au désespoir.”

“Oh, he was very good to remember the engagement under the circumstances,” said Gwendolen. “I am sorry he was called away.”

It was easy to be politely sorrowful on so felicitous an occasion—like expressing regret that the dish you did not want has been removed.

“Then I can profit by Mr. Clintock’s misfortune?” said Grandcourt. “May I hope that you will let me take his place?”

“I shall be very happy to dance the next quadrille with you,” said Gwendolen.
