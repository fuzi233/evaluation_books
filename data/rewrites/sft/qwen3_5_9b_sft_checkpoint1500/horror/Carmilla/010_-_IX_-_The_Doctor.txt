The silence of the night was broken not by a scream, but by a cold, calculated order. My father, his face a mask of grim resolve, had ensured that no servant slept within the walls of Carmilla's chamber. Instead, a guard was stationed outside her door, a silent sentinel tasked with intercepting her before she could vanish once more into the dark corridors. That night, the air remained heavy with unspoken dread, yet the hours passed without incident.

By dawn, the atmosphere shifted abruptly. A man, summoned by my father without a whisper reaching me, appeared at the threshold. This was Doctor Spielsberg, a figure I had heard whispered about—a grave scholar with snow-white hair and spectacles that magnified eyes devoid of warmth. Madame, acting as my chaperone, led me to the library, where the doctor waited in the shadows of a window recess.

"I have heard your tale," he intoned, his voice dropping as I spoke. "And as you speak, the gravity within me deepens, bordering on the horrific."

He stood opposite me, leaning heavily against the stone wall, his gaze fixed upon me with an intensity that made the hair on my arms stand erect. There was a chill in his interest, a shadow of terror beneath his professional composure. After a long pause, he requested to see my father.

When the count entered, a faint smile played upon his lips, though it vanished instantly as the doctor beckoned him closer. They retreated into the same secluded corner, engaging in a low, urgent debate that burned with the heat of argument. I and Madame stood at the far end of the vast room, our hearts pounding with a feverish curiosity. The thick walls and the deep alcove of the window acted as a soundproof barrier, muffling their words until they were mere whispers lost to the silence. We could only see the count's pale, agitated features and the doctor's stooped silhouette.

Eventually, the count stepped back into the light, his expression thoughtful and shaken. "Laura, come here," he called softly, dismissing Madame. "Doctor Spielsberg has a request."

I approached, feeling a strange mixture of weakness and alertness. A malady clung to me, not with the fever of sickness, but with a lingering, ghostly presence. The count extended his hand, but his eyes remained locked on the physician.

"You have spoken of a sensation," the doctor began, his tone clinical yet ominous, "like two needles piercing the skin near your neck during your first nightmare. Does that mark remain?"

"No soreness," I replied, trying to keep my voice steady.

"Point to it," he commanded.

I lowered my sleeve, revealing the skin just below the collarbone. "Here," I indicated.

"Now, look closely," the doctor said, his gloom deepening into triumph. "Do not mind your father's instruction to lower it further. We must inspect the symptom."

"My God!" the count gasped, his face draining of color.

"There," the doctor declared, pointing a trembling finger. "A small blue spot, no larger than the tip of your little finger."

"What is it?" I cried, fear seizing my throat.

"It is nothing," the doctor lied smoothly, turning to the count. "Yet, it marks the precise locus where the sensation of strangulation begins. And recall the 'thrill' you described? Like a cold stream running against you?"

"Yes," I whispered, the memory surfacing.

"Aye," the doctor confirmed, glancing at the count. "Then the diagnosis is clear. Madame, you must ensure Miss Laura is never alone. It is indispensable."

"We will obey," Madame agreed, her voice tight with anxiety.

"And you, Laura," the doctor continued, "do not stray from the path. I have another patient, a young lady whose symptoms mirror yours, though milder. She is also a guest. You will meet her later tonight."

With final, stern instructions given to both of us, the count and the doctor departed. Outside, on the grassy platform between the moat and the road, they paced together, their conversation earnest and secret. Soon, the doctor mounted his horse and rode eastward, disappearing into the mist-shrouded forest. Almost simultaneously, a messenger arrived from Dranfield, delivering letters to the count.

Inside, speculation ran wild. Madame feared the doctor suspected a sudden, fatal seizure, urging constant vigilance. To me, however, the strictures seemed excessive, perhaps merely a precautionary measure to keep a young girl from wandering off or eating unripe fruit. But the weight of the doctor's gaze lingered.

An hour later, the count returned, holding a letter. "It is from General Spielsdorf," he announced, his demeanor uncharacteristically somber. "He may arrive today, or tomorrow, or not at all."

He handed me the open letter, his face etched with a turmoil he refused to name. "Laura, lay your hand upon my arm," he pleaded, though his eyes betrayed a desperate need for distance. "Tell me..."

"Do you think I am very ill?" I asked, clutching his sleeve.

"No," he murmured, smoothing my hair with a trembling hand. "If the proper steps are taken, you will be well again in days. But pray, let us not discuss this. The General's arrival would be disastrous if you were... compromised."

"But what is wrong?" I pressed, tears pricking my eyes.

"Nothing you need know yet," he snapped, the irritation breaking through his usual gentleness. "You shall know all in a day or two. For now, rest."

Before I could process his harshness, he returned, announcing his departure for Karnstein. He intended to visit a local priest regarding some pressing business, and he demanded that Madame and I accompany him. "We shall hold a picnic in the ruins," he said, a faint, mocking smile touching his lips. "Miss Carmilla has never seen those grounds."

At noon, the carriage was ready. We crossed the drawbridge and followed the winding road westward, over the steep Gothic arches, toward the desolate village and the crumbling fortress of Karnstein. The landscape was a tapestry of gentle hills and hollows, cloaked in wild, untamed woods, free from the rigid geometry of human cultivation. The road twisted and turned, hugging the sides of broken valleys and rising slopes, leading us deeper into the ancient, decaying heart of the land.

Suddenly, emerging from a bend in the path, we met our old friend, the General. He rode toward us, flanked by a mounted servant, his portmanteaus trailing behind in a hired wagon. As we pulled up, he dismounted, exchanging pleasantries with the count. With a nod, the General accepted the vacant seat in the carriage, sending his horse and servant ahead to the *Schloss*, leaving us three bound for the haunted ruins.
