The next morning, our first task was to return to Calabash Wood and retrieve the sledge loaded with the dishes, bowls, and baskets we had crafted. I chose to go with only Fritz, leaving the other boys with their mother. My plan was to explore beyond the rocky hills that ran along the coast, and I thought it wiser to travel light for such a venture.

As we passed through the grove of evergreen oaks, we spotted our sow grazing peacefully on acorns. She seemed none the worse for the scare we’d given her the day before—indeed, she appeared friendlier than usual, perhaps seeing us as her rescuers from the wild dogs.

Birds filled the trees around us, undisturbed by our presence—until Fritz fired his gun, bringing down a brilliant blue jay and two parakeets, one glowing scarlet, the other shimmering green and gold. As he reloaded, a strange, dull thumping sound reached our ears—like a distant drumbeat. Instantly alert, we crept forward, hiding behind low bushes and thick vines, until we reached a clearing.

There, perched on a fallen log, stood a magnificent bird about the size of a rooster. Its feathers were rich chestnut brown, mottled with dark browns and grays. On its shoulders grew curious tufts of velvety black feathers, gleaming with hints of green. It ruffled its wings, raised its tail and neck feathers, and strutted in an odd, stately dance.

After some time, it spread its tail like a fan, stiffened its wings, and began beating them rapidly—faster and faster—until a rumbling sound echoed like distant thunder. The whirring motion created a cloud-like effect around it. This was the mysterious noise that had startled us, likely amplified by the hollow log beneath it.

I watched in fascination when suddenly a shot rang out from behind me. The performance ended abruptly—Fritz, eager and impulsive, had fired without my permission. The bird dropped dead from its perch, and the crowd of watching birds scattered in panic.

I was deeply disappointed. Such a rare and beautiful creature—likely a ruffed grouse—had been destroyed senselessly. I scolded Fritz sharply for acting without caution or consent. We placed the bird on the donkey’s back and continued on our way.

The sledge was exactly where we’d left it. With daylight still strong, I decided to press forward and explore the line of cliffs and rocky hills that stretched along the coastline. I hoped to find a pass into the interior, or at least confirm whether we were trapped on this stretch of shore.

Leaving Calabash Wood behind, we moved across land covered in manioc, potatoes, and unfamiliar plants. Gentle streams wound through fertile soil, and the view was open and inviting.

Then I noticed a bush heavy with small white berries, waxy and sticky when picked. I recognized them at once: *Myrica cerifera*, a plant whose berries could be melted and strained to make wax candles. I explained to Fritz how this would solve our problem of having to stop work at sunset. While not as durable as beeswax, the wax would burn well and release a pleasant scent. With the donkey nearby, we quickly gathered enough berries to fill one of his canvas bags.

Soon after, we came upon another marvel—a tree hosting an entire colony of birds, about the size of yellowhammers but with plain brown plumage. Their nests clustered tightly around the trunk and branches, forming a large mass. A roof of grass, straw, and fibers protected them from rain and sun. Multiple openings dotted the sides, resembling rooms in a shared house. Twigs stuck out like perches, and birds darted in and out in constant motion. The whole structure looked like a giant sea sponge.

The birds swarmed everywhere. Among them were bright little parrots, often squabbling over nesting space. Fritz, eager to investigate, climbed the tree hoping to catch young birds. He reached into several empty nests, but when he plunged his hand deep into one, a sharp beak clamped down on his finger. He yelped in pain and pulled away, shaking his hand.

Recovering, he seized the bird firmly, despite its shrieks, and stuffed it into his pocket, buttoning up fast. He slid down quickly, pursued by angry relatives who circled him, screaming and pecking.

His prize wasn’t one of the nest owners—the sociable grosbeak—but a small green parrot. Fritz was thrilled and vowed to tame it and teach it to speak. For now, it stayed locked safely in his coat.

We talked about the bird community as we walked. “It’s amazing how animals cooperate,” I said. “Beavers build dams together. Bees, wasps, ants—all work in harmony. Even coral polyps create vast reefs under the sea through teamwork.”

“I’ve seen ants,” Fritz replied. “They’re always busy, carrying food, moving eggs.”

“Do you know what they do with those?” I asked.

“Oh, those aren’t eggs—they’re larvae, antworms. People call them eggs, but that’s wrong.”

“You’re right,” I said proudly. “And if you liked ants at home, imagine the giant ones in foreign lands! Some build mounds six feet high, divided into streets, chambers, nurseries. Strong enough to use as ovens!”

“But ants are no beauty,” I added. “They’re sticky, smelly, and destructive. In tropical lands, they can ruin crops. But nature balances things—birds, insects, even mammals hunt them.”

“The ant-eater!” Fritz exclaimed.

“Exactly. The tamanoir of South America—six or seven feet long, covered in coarse hair, with a long narrow head and no teeth. Its tongue is like a red worm. It tears apart ant hills with powerful claws, then flicks its tongue inside, coating it with thousands of ants, which stick and get pulled into its mouth.”

“There’s also a smaller kind,” I said, “that lives in trees.”

I was pleased that Fritz listened so intently. Our conversation carried us far down the path.

Eventually, we came to a grove of tall trees with broad, thick leaves. They bore round, fig-like fruits with tiny seeds—bitter and sour. Fritz noticed resin oozing from cracks in the bark. It reminded him of cherry gum from home, so he scraped some off.

He tried to mold it, but it wouldn’t behave like gum. Then he stretched it—and it bounced back! “Father! Look! This is elastic! Could it be india-rubber?”

“India-rubber?” I gasped. “Let me see! You might be right—it’s caoutchouc, a milky sap from certain trees in Brazil, Guiana, and Cayenne. The natives used it to coat clay bottles, then removed the shell and smoked it. They drew designs on it—those old bottles were sold in Europe as erasers.”

“It has many uses,” I said. “We could make boots, waterproof clothes—this is a valuable find!”

Just then, we reached the coconut grove, with the bay and Cape Disappointment ahead. I noticed a smaller palm among the giants—its trunk broken by wind. The pith looked mealy. I knew instantly: this was the sago palm.

Inside, I saw fat worms. “These feed on sago,” I recalled. “In the West Indies, they’re eaten as a delicacy.” I decided to try one.

I built a fire, salted half a dozen worms, skewered them, and roasted them. Soon, fat dripped from them and the smell was delicious. Any hesitation vanished. I ate one like butter on a potato—and found it tasty. Fritz joined in, and we enjoyed this unusual meal with our baked potatoes.

We turned aside from a dense thicket and headed toward the sugar brake near Cape Disappointment. We cut a bundle of sugarcanes and loaded them onto the donkey, adding to his already full load.

At last, we returned to Calabash Wood, unloaded everything onto the sledge, and set off. The donkey pulled it easily—no longer burdened by carrying all the weight himself.

We arrived at Falconhurst in the evening. Our welcome was warm, and everyone listened eagerly to our tales. The green parrot especially delighted the boys.

Supper was ready, and we ate with gratitude. Then we climbed into our tree-house, pulled up the ladder, and rested—well earned after a long day.

The next morning, candle-making was on everyone’s mind. The boys woke up chanting “candles!” Breakfast was all about candles. Afterward, they insisted on starting immediately.

“So be it,” I said. “Let’s become chandlers.” Though secretly, I doubted our success. We lacked animal fat, essential for long-burning candles. And I wasn’t sure I remembered every step.

But I said nothing. Under my guidance, the boys began. We crushed the wax berries into a shallow iron pot over the fire. The green, sweet-smelling wax rose to the surface. We skimmed it off, repeating until we had enough.

I took the wicks my wife had made and dipped them into the wax, handing each to Fritz, who hung them on a bush to dry. After several dips, they thickened into sturdy candles. We stored them in a cool place.

That night, we sat up three hours after sunset—Falconhurst lit brilliantly for the first time. Everyone was thrilled.

“My husband,” my wife said, “you’re clever. Now, can you help me with butter? Cream spoils daily. Can’t you invent something?”

“I think I can,” I replied. “Not my idea—it’s how the Hottentots do it. Jack, bring me a gourd bottle.”

I filled it with cream, sealed it tightly, and gave it to the boys. They tied four posts in the ground, fastened a square piece of sailcloth, and placed the gourd inside. Then, taking turns, they rolled it back and forth for half an hour.

“Open it!” I called. “Take it to your mother.”

They did. My wife stared in delight—there was a large lump of fresh butter.

While they worked, I built a cart using the sledge frame. It was clumsy but strong. It would prove useful for harvests.

Next, we planted fruit trees in a prepared plot. Walnut, cherry, and chestnut trees formed a shaded avenue from Falconhurst to Family Bridge. Between them, we laid a path to reach Tentholm easily.

Vines wrapped around the roots of our great mangrove. Other trees went to spots near Falconhurst and across Jackal River, to beautify Tentholm.

Tentholm had long been on my mind. I didn’t want beauty—I wanted safety. So I planted a thick, thorny hedge to block wild animals and possible attackers. Then we fortified the bridge and mounted two guns from the wreck on hillocks, ready to defend us.

Six weeks passed in hard but joyful labor. Sundays brought rest and thanks to God for our health and safety.

I saw the boys growing stronger. I encouraged running, climbing, swimming. But their clothes—once neat—were now ragged and patched.

So I decided to visit the wreck again—to gather supplies and check its condition. Three boys and I rowed out in the pinnace.

The ship was much the same—just a few more planks gone.

“Rummage her out!” I ordered. “Leave nothing of value.”

They searched every corner. Sailors’ chests, cloth, guns, ball, shutters, bolts, pitch barrels—everything piled on deck. We loaded the pinnace and returned.

We towed the tub-boat back and made several trips until nothing remained.

“One final trip,” I told my wife. “Then the ship will be gone. I left two barrels of gunpowder. We’ll blow her up.”

Before lighting the fuse, I found a large copper cauldron. I tied two empty casks to it so it would float when the explosion came. We placed the barrels, lit the fuse, and retreated.

We dined outside the tent, facing the wreck. Darkness fell. Then—a flash! A roaring blast shook the water. Our ship was gone.

We had planned this. We knew it was wise. Yet that night, we felt a quiet sadness—as though we’d lost a loyal companion.

The next morning, joy returned. The shore was lined with planks and beams from the wreck. I found the cauldron floating, secured by the casks. I hauled it ashore and stored the powder casks beneath it.

As we collected these treasures, my wife brought news: two ducks and a goose had each raised large families in the river reeds. They waddled past us, proud of their broods.

“Hurrah!” Ernest shouted. “Soon we’ll have duck and peas—like civilized people again!”

Seeing the birds reminded me of Falconhurst. I announced we’d visit.

Everyone wanted to come. As we approached, I noticed young trees bent by the wind. I decided to gather bamboo for support the next day.

Since only Fritz and I had explored Cape Disappointment, the others begged to join. I agreed.

The next morning, we set off with the cart—drawn by cow and donkey—loaded with a tent, food, ammunition, tools, and gear. I intended to gather fruits and samples from various trees.

It was a lovely day. We passed through fields of potatoes, manioc, and cassava. The birds’ nests charmed the children.

We stopped at the wax trees to collect more berries. We filled two sacks, hid them among the bushes, and marked the spot.

“Now for the caoutchouc tree,” I said. “Waterproof boots for you, Ernest!”

We found the tree and began tapping the bark, placing vessels below to catch the sap.
