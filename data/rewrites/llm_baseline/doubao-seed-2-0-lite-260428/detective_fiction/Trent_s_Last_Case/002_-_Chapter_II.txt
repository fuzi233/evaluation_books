The rest of the *Record* office reeked of damp newsprint and chilled ink, drafty and cluttered, but Sir James Molloy’s private sanctum was the only room in the whole warren that felt lived-in, stuffed with leather chairs, a half-smoked Havana smoldering in an ashtray brimming with crumpled galley slips. The telephone on his mahogany desk buzzed once, sharp as a startled wasp. Sir James waved his pen without looking up, and his secretary Mr Silver abandoned his typewriter to cross the room and lift the receiver.

“Who’s that? I can barely hear you—oh, it’s Mr Bunner, is it? Sir James is swamped this afternoon, couldn’t you possibly—wait, really? Grave news? All right, hold on one moment.” He set the receiver down in front of his boss. “Calvin Bunner, Sigsbee Manderson’s right-hand man. He’s calling straight from the millionaire’s country house outside Bishopsbridge, says he has to speak to you personally.”

Sir James glowered at the phone like it had personally interrupted his lunch, then took the receiver in one big calloused hand. “Well?” he barked, his broad Irish baritone cutting through the line. For half a minute he only nodded, grunting out half-syllable replies of “yes” as he listened. Then the colour drained from his swart, black-moustached face. “Good God,” he breathed. He leaned in, clutching the plastic casing hard enough to crack it, his eyes fixed unblinking on the wall. A quick glance at the clock, and he snapped over his shoulder at Silver without taking his ear off the line: “Go find Figgis and young Williams. Run.”

Silver bolted. The man he’d left behind was a Fleet Street legend at fifty: sharp, tireless, half-cynical but entirely unpretentious, no fake mystique, no performative wit, the managing director who ran both the powerful morning *Record* and the chaotic, high-selling evening *Sun* across the street. His staff respected him more than almost any man in a industry that worshipped nothing but a good scoop, and when his brow furrowed like this, the whole office went quiet.

A few more minutes of listening, a few sharp questions, and he finally hung up. “You’re sure the police haven’t leaked a word yet? The servants haven’t gossiped to the village? Right. Bunner, I owe you one for this—come see me first time you’re in town, I mean it.” He slammed the receiver down, grabbed a tattered railway timetable off the rack, flicked through it, and swore so loud the walls rattled. Silver burst back in seconds later, trailed by Figgis, the grizzled crime reporter with spectacles and a face hewn from granite, and Williams, the sharp-eyed cub reporter who could outrun a newsboy with a bundle of extras.

“Figgis, jot this down fast,” Sir James said, all trace of shock gone, his voice clipped and steady as a machine gun. “We’re spinning a special *Sun* edition that will hit the streets before any other outlet even hears the rumour. You’ll have the copy written before the clock hits half past three.” Figgis nodded, flipped open his shorthand notebook, and pulled a chair up to the desk, face impassive as if he’d been told it was going to rain. “Silver, wire our Marlstone local correspondent right now, tell him to drop every single story he’s working on and get to Manderson’s estate, no questions. Don’t put a single word of the news in the telegram, no leaks allowed. Williams, run across the street to the *Sun* offices, find Anthony, tell him to prep a two-column front page splash that’ll knock London flat. Tell him it’s the scoop of the decade, Figgis will be over in five minutes with all the facts. On your way back, send Miss Morgan in to me, and tell the switchboard to get Mr Trent on the line as fast as humanly possible. Report back here when you’re done.” Williams vanished like a smoke wisp.

He turned straight back to Figgis, pacing the floor with his hands clasped behind his back. “Sigsbee Manderson is dead. Murdered. Shot straight through the left eye, point blank range. He, his wife, and his two secretaries have been staying at his Marlstone estate White Gables for a fortnight. He turned in at half past eleven last night as usual, no one saw him leave the house. A gardener found his body at ten this morning, laying next to a toolshed in the grounds. Death was instant. His pockets weren’t touched, no valuables stolen, but there were deep bruise marks on his wrists—he fought his killer. The local doctor is doing the post-mortem, the Bishopsbridge cops are on site and won’t say a word, they don’t have a single lead on who did it.”

Figgis scribbled a line of shorthand. “We can add that a top Scotland Yard detective is already assigned, that’s a safe bet,” he suggested. “And Mrs Manderson—prostrate with shock, refusing to see anyone, human interest beat.”

“Leave that part out,” said a quiet, calm voice. Miss Morgan, the pale, sharp, gracefully efficient head of the newsroom morgue, had slipped into the room while they talked, no one had heard her. “I met Mrs Manderson last year. She’s a woman of iron, not the fainting type. She won’t be prostrate. She’s probably down at the shed right now, helping the police pick through the clues.”

Sir James huffed a quiet laugh. “You’re right, Figgis. Cut it. Off you go, run. Miss Morgan, you know what we need.”

“Our full Manderson biography is already fully updated,” she said, flipping through the mental index of the morgue she carried in her head. “I reviewed it three months ago, it’s ready for tomorrow’s *Record* front page. The *Sun* can run the shorter profile we put out when he sorted the Berlin potash cartel dispute two years back, it was tight, well written, perfect for their space. We have two exclusive portraits, one a charcoal sketch Mr Trent drew of Manderson on a transatlantic crossing three years ago, far better than any press photo. I’ll send them up to your desk in ten minutes to pick which one to run. The only gap we have: the last fast train down to Marlstone left half an hour ago, we can’t get a special correspondent down there in time for tomorrow’s first edition.”

Sir James groaned, leaning back in his chair. “This woman knows Bradshaw by heart,” he complained to Silver. “None of us have a single trick she doesn’t see coming. Make a mistake someday, Miss Morgan, a big silly one, just so the rest of us don’t feel like utter fools.” She gave him the flicker of a smile and vanished back to the morgue.

Sir James picked up the phone to talk to Anthony across the street, the firebrand editor who called himself the Murat of Fleet Street, who lived for beating the clock on a breaking story. Five minutes later, a copy boy poked his head in to say Mr Trent was on the line. Sir James cut his conversation short.

“Trent!” he barked into the receiver.

The voice on the other end was half amused, half annoyed. “Hullo be blowed, Molloy. I’m mid-canvas, right in the middle of the best part of a new landscape. This better be important.”

“It is. Sigsbee Manderson’s been murdered. Shot through the head, body found this morning by his gardener, cops have no leads. I want you down there on the case for us.”

There was a long grunting pause on the other end. “Look, Molloy,” Trent said, voice half-querulous, half-serious. “It might be a big clever mystery, it might be dead simple. Could be some tramp he chased off the grounds at two in the morning, who pulled a gun, knew enough not to steal anything so no one would suspect robbery. And for the record? I wouldn’t lift a finger to hang a man who put a bullet in that greedy, brutal old tyrant. Half the financial world wanted Manderson dead.”

Sir James smiled, he already knew he’d won. “Come off it. You’re dying to go poke around the scene. If it bores you, you can walk away no strings attached. The only train we can catch leaves Paddington at midnight. My fast car’s in the garage, but even that won’t get you there before dark tonight. You can crash on the slow one, we’ll wire ahead to get you a hotel room near White Gables.”

“Fine,” Trent grumbled. “I’ll be at your office in forty minutes. And for the record? I love slow trains. I’m the master of the railway nap, the king of the dining car snack. Don’t rush me.”

Sir James hung up. Just then, a high, screaming din erupted from the street below. He leaned out the open window, and saw a swarm of grinning, ragged newsboys pouring down the steps of the *Sun* building, bundles of extra editions under their arms, waving big placards scrawled in thick black ink:

The boys were already yelling the headline at the top of their lungs as they swarmed towards Fleet Street, and the first passersby were stopping mid-step, pulling out pennies to grab a copy. Sir James jingled the loose silver in his pocket, grinning. “Best headline we’ve run in ten years,” he said to Silver at his elbow.
It was Sigsbee Manderson’s epitaph.
