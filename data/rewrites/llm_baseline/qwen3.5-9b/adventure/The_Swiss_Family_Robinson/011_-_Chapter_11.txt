"Now for the finishing up of this dirty job!" I cried cheerfully as we all woke at daybreak. Once our daily routine was complete, we set to work constructing a rough scaffold to hold the tubs filled with blubber. We pressed them heavily so that the purest oil overflowed into waiting vessels below. Afterward, we boiled the remaining blubber in a cauldron over a distant fire. By skimming and straining the mixture through a coarse cloth, we secured a generous supply of excellent train oil, which we stored safely in casks and bags made from animal intestines in our makeshift cellar—the children's favorite storage room.

The work was unpleasant, and the foul odor oppressed us all, particularly my poor wife. Yet, she endured it with her characteristic good temper, though she urgently proposed establishing a new colony on the uninhabited island. She argued that it would be safer for any animals we left there, free from apes and thieves, and ideally suited for boiling blubber and processing oil. The boys approved heartily, eager to act immediately. However, remembering the rotting carcass of the whale, they wisely agreed to let nature—wind, storms, birds, and insects—purge the area and reduce the beast to a skeleton before we returned.

With the cleanup delayed, my mind turned to a new invention: a mechanical rowing machine. Using an iron bar spanning the center of our boat, I rigged a system of ribbed machinery and paddle-like spokes. By winding a jack attached to a central cogwheel, the bar would rotate rapidly, driving the paddles to propel the vessel. Though crude, the device worked brilliantly. When Fritz and I tested it across the bay, our speed thrilled the entire family, who cheered from the shores. While everyone clamored for a longer cruise, I insisted on saving energy for the next day's expedition to Cape Disappointment and Prospect Hill.

Armed with spades and mattocks, we set out to gather young coconut trees and shrubs to plant on Whale Island upon our return. The sea was calm, and our mechanical boat glided effortlessly past Safety Bay and Shark Island. Upon reaching Prospect Hill, we disembarked and ventured into the woods to collect fresh coconuts and seedlings.

Approaching our abandoned farm cottage, the crowing of roosters stirred a pang of nostalgia in my heart, reminding me of visits home. Yet, here, save for the unconscious animals, absolute solitude reigned. Our prolonged absence had transformed our return into a panic. The original pets had forgotten us, and their offspring—lambs, kids, and chicks who had never seen a human—viewed us as terrifying invaders. The boys managed to capture the goats with lassos, binding their legs and offering salt to pacify them, eventually securing a supply of milk in calabash flasks. My wife gathered eggs, and we settled down for dinner.

The centerpiece of our meal was a piece of whale tongue, specially prepared by the boys. To our dismay, the taste was horrid, prompting desperate requests for pickled herrings to mask the flavor of the train oil. Fortunately, our abundant supply of fresh coconuts and goat's milk restored everyone's spirits.

After packing up, Fritz and I collected sugar-cane shoots for planting. Before heading back to Whale Island, I attempted to scout the coast beyond Cape Disappointment, but hidden reefs forced us to turn back. On landing, I planted the cane shoots while the boys played nearby. Suddenly, Jack shouted excitedly about an "enormous skeleton" of a mammoth. Laughing, I corrected him, identifying it as the whale's remains, explaining that whales are mammals, not fish, named for their milk-producing glands ("mama"), and possessing hollow bones for buoyancy—a testament to the Creator's wisdom.

Just then, Ernest cried out about a giant turtle trying to escape. We managed to flip the massive creature onto its back. Once inspected, we realized the "mammoth" was indeed the whale. Deciding the turtle must swim, we tied an empty cask to a rope looped around its neck. As the turtle dove, the cask floated alongside, preventing it from sinking. Towing us gently toward Safety Bay, the boys compared the scene to Neptune riding dolphins. Back at Rockburg, we harvested the turtle's meat and shell, storing the latter for a future water trough.

With the rainy season approaching, I shifted focus to agriculture and craftsmanship. Unable to plough due to the animals' lack of training, I completed a sturdy loom, using isinglass instead of flour to treat the threads. I also crafted transparent window panes from the isinglass and fashioned saddles, bridles, and yokes for our livestock. The harvest of herring, seals, and other marine life provided ample resources, leading to a successful season of basket weaving. The children loved carrying small Franz in a large basket, an idea that inspired Fritz to propose building a sedan chair for their mother. After some playful resistance regarding comfort, we constructed a proper litter. Jack suggested using the oxen and buffalo to pull it.

The experiment was a chaotic delight. The animals pulled the basket steadily at first, but the boys encouraged a faster pace, causing the litter to shake violently. Ernest, terrified inside, screamed for his brothers to stop, but they merely laughed, circling the grounds until I intervened to restore peace and remind them of the importance of brotherly love.

Peace was short-lived. Standing beneath the verandah, Fritz pointed out a strange, coiling object advancing from the Jackal River. Through my spyglass, I identified it as a colossal serpent. Alarmed, we barricaded our cave dwellings and prepared firearms. The serpent, a gigantic boa constrictor over thirty feet long, moved with undulating grace, occasionally rearing its head high. Despite our best efforts, our shots failed to harm it. Startled but unharmed, the monster slipped into the reedy marsh and vanished.

For three days, we lived in suspense, confined to our cave, fearing the beast's return. Its presence was confirmed only by the agitated behavior of our geese and ducks, which fled to Shark Island. Just as hopelessness seemed to grip us, our old donkey, Grizzle, acted out of sheer stupidity rather than bravery. Sent to fetch grass across the river, Grizzle bolted straight into the marsh. The serpent emerged, seizing the donkey in a swift embrace. The animal brayed pitifully as the snake tightened its grip, swallowing it whole.

"My children, it is impossible to save him now," I said sadly. "But perhaps, once the snake is gorged, we can attack it successfully." Explaining that snakes lack teeth to chew and must swallow prey whole—including bones and hair—I reassured the frightened children that while gruesome, it was not unique to this creature. With the threat temporarily neutralized by the snake's own hunger, we waited, hoping for a final confrontation.
