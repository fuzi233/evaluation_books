The papers from which I have woven this tale came into my possession through means best left unexplained. But before I present the extracts, a word on their nature is necessary.

They began as notes for a travelogue—the sort of commonplace volume produced in abundance during the mid-19th century, filled with sketches of foreign landscapes, anecdotes of rustic hospitality, and tedious details of coach routes and inns. Yet what began as a dry compilation soon twisted into something darker: a chronicle of one man’s singular and dreadful experience.

The author was a Mr. Wraxall. From his writings, I gather he was a man of some means, adrift in the world, residing in transient lodgings with no fixed home. He had published once before—a forgettable account of a Breton holiday—but his name has since slipped into obscurity. A scholar by inclination, he had narrowly missed a fellowship at Oxford, and his most persistent flaw was an insatiable curiosity—a trait that would prove fatal.

His final journey took him to Scandinavia in the summer of 1863, a region then little-known to Englishmen. Intrigued by old Swedish histories, he conceived a book blending travel with episodes from noble family annals. Letters of introduction secured him access to certain archives, including those of an ancient manor in Vestergothland—Råbäck (though that is not its true name).

The estate was magnificent: a red-brick mansion of the early 1600s, its walls lined with portraits of the De la Gardie lineage. The current owners, descendants of the original builders, received Wraxall courteously, offering him lodging. Preferring independence, he took rooms at the village inn, from which he made daily excursions to the manor.

Nearby stood the church, a squat, shadowed structure with a grotesque fresco of the Last Judgment upon its ceiling. But more arresting was the mausoleum attached to it—an octagonal vault of black-painted copper, its dome crowned by a peculiar spire. The door was always locked. Peering through the keyhole, Wraxall glimpsed marble effigies, tarnished sarcophagi, and heraldic pomp that stirred his scholarly hunger.

The archives at the manor proved fruitful. Journals and letters painted a vivid picture of the first Count Magnus de la Gardie—a man of ruthless efficiency. When peasant revolts troubled the district, he suppressed them with executions and mutilations. His portrait, hanging in the hall, showed a face of singular ugliness, its features heavy with malice.

That evening, Wraxall dined with the family and walked back beneath a lingering twilight. Passing the churchyard, his eyes fell on the mausoleum.

*"Ah, Count Magnus,"* he murmured aloud, *"there you are. I should dearly like to see you."*

A clang from within the church startled him—some unseen hand dropping a metal object. He laughed at himself. *"Count Magnus sleeps sound enough."*

At the inn, the landlord spoke reluctantly of local lore. Count Magnus was remembered with dread. Tenants who displeased him were flogged, branded, or worse. Houses that encroached on his lands burned mysteriously, their occupants trapped inside. But most ominous was the whispered tale of the *Black Pilgrimage*—a journey the Count had undertaken, from which he returned with *something*.

Pressed for details, the landlord yielded only this:

*"Ninety-two years ago, two men ventured into his woods at night, scoffing at warnings. Those who listened heard first a scream—as if a soul were being torn out—then laughter, not human. At dawn, they found one man dead, his face *sucked clean to the bone*. The other lived, but died mad, forever pushing away unseen horrors."*

The next day, the deacon admitted Wraxall into the mausoleum. Among the grand sarcophagi lay Count Magnus’s own—a massive copper tomb, its lid engraved with scenes of battle, execution, and a hooded figure pursuing a fleeing man. The pursuer was no man, but something indistinct and terrible, its limbs unnaturally formed.

Three steel padlocks secured the tomb. Two lay loose on the ground.

That night, Wraxall returned alone. He scarcely remembered walking there, only becoming aware of himself as he stood before the gate, murmuring, *"Are you awake, Count Magnus?"*

Inside, he found the two locks still detached. The third resisted. Had it yielded, he confessed, he might have dared to lift the lid.

The next morning, urgent letters called him back to England. His work was done. Yet as he prepared to leave, he wrote one final, uneasy note:

*"I cannot account for the fascination this grim old noble holds over me. But I fear—were I to stay—I might learn more than I wish to know."*
