Twelve long, slow years had drifted by since I sealed the casket of my great-uncle, Captain John Carter of Virginia, away from mortal eyes in that uncanny, purpose-built mausoleum tucked in the shadow of Richmond’s oldest cemetery. All that time, his bizarre, unorthodox instructions for the tomb gnawed at the edge of my thoughts: rules that demanded he be interred in an open casket, that the colossal bolt mechanism securing the vault’s ton-heavy granite door could only ever be operated from the inside. No earthly logic I could name justified such a design.

Twelve years, too, had I pored over the tattered, dog-eared manuscript that man had left me – the manuscript of a man who could not recall a single childhood memory, who could not even hazard a guess as to his own age, who stayed as sharp and lithe as a thirty-year-old fighting man for as long as I had known him, and who had once bounced my great-great-grandfather on his knee. A man who spent ten full years on the dying red planet of Barsoom, fighting alongside the six-limbed green Thark hordes and against them, riding the great shaggy thoats across the crumbly rust-colored dead sea bottoms, clashing with the noble red city-states one day and allying with them the next, and who had won the heart of the incomparable Dejah Thoris, Princess of Helium, to become a beloved prince in the court of Tardos Mors, Jeddak of Helium.

Twelve years since his body was found sprawled on the wind-scoured bluff outside his Hudson-side cottage, no mark of violence on him, no trace of where he’d gone in the hours he vanished. Through every one of those years I’d wondered: was John Carter truly dead? Or was he already tramping those red dust-choked sea bottoms, racing to pry open the gates of Barsoom’s mighty global atmosphere plant – the plant he had been hurled forty-eight million miles back to Earth mere minutes from saving, on that terrible day millions of Barsoomians had choked on thinning air? I wondered if he’d found his black-haired princess waiting for him in the perfumed royal gardens of Helium, if the slender, red-skinned son he’d dreamed of while stranded on Earth was there clinging to Dejah Thoris’ hand. Or had he arrived too late? Trapped in a living death on a world already slipping into its final, cold grave? Or was he truly gone forever, never to set foot again on either his mother Earth or the red planet that had claimed all of his heart?

I was adrift in that exact useless speculation one sweltering, magnolia-scented August evening, when my old body servant Ben handed me a crumpled telegram. I tore it open at once, my fingers trembling:
> Meet me to-morrow hotel Raleigh Richmond.

I caught the very first train out for Richmond at dawn the next day, and two hours later was shown into his sunlit hotel suite. He stood to greet me the second I crossed the threshold, that familiar, warm, battle-worn smile spreading across his sharp, handsome face. He had not aged a single day. Straight, broad-shouldered, clean-limbed, the exact same fighting man of thirty I had first met decades before. His keen grey eyes were as bright and sharp as a Martian radium blade, the only lines on his face the deep-set marks of iron courage and unbreakable determination I had known there for thirty-five years.

“Well, nephew,” he laughed, clapping me on the shoulder, “do you reckon you’re staring at a ghost, or that old Ben slipped too much bourbon in your last mint julep?”
“Julep, I think,” I laughed back, “because I’ve never felt better. But it’s more the sight of you. You’ve been back to Barsoom, haven’t you? Tell me – is Dejah Thoris well? Did she wait for you?”

“I went back, aye, to Barsoom,” he said, his voice softening, “and the story is long, far too long for the few hours I have left before I must return. I have uncovered the secret now, nephew: I can stride across the empty, trackless void between worlds at will, come and go between a thousand different planets as the mood takes me. But my heart will never leave Barsoom, not while it lives safe in the keeping of my Martian princess. That dying red world is my life now.
I came back to Earth one last time only to see you, before you pass on to that other afterlife I will never know. I have died three times already, as you measure death, and I will die again tonight – but no matter how many times I cross that threshold, I can no more fathom its mysteries than you can. Even the ancient, secretive therns of Barsoom, that cult that has hidden in their impregnable mountain fastness on the near slopes of the Mountains of Otz for a million years, claiming they hold all the secrets of life and death, know no more of it than we do. I proved that, too – though I nearly lost my life doing it. You will find all of it written down in the notes I have scribbled over the last three months I spent on Earth.” He patted the bulging leather portfolio sitting on the table at his elbow.

“I know you will believe me, no matter how wild the tale sounds. The rest of the world will be curious, too, but they will not believe it for decades – for centuries, most like. Earth’s people have not yet grown to a place where they can wrap their heads around the things that happened to me on Barsoom. Publish whatever parts of the notes you think the world is ready for, whatever will do no harm. Do not feel hurt if they laugh at you for it.”

That night, the two of us walked down the tree-lined path to the old cemetery together, fireflies dancing in the thick dark air. At the heavy granite door of his mausoleum, he turned and squeezed my hand hard, calloused from a thousand battles.
“Goodbye, nephew,” he said. “I will likely never see you again. I cannot bear to leave my wife and my boy while they still live, and Barsoomians live for more than a thousand years, most of them.”

He stepped inside the vault. The massive bronze door swung slowly shut behind him. The heavy iron bolts grated home with a low, thunderous rumble. The lock clicked.

I have never laid eyes on Captain John Carter of Virginia ever since.

What follows is the full story of his return to Mars, his second great quest to find Dejah Thoris, Princess of Helium, pieced together from the thousands of pages of notes he left stacked on that hotel table in Richmond. I have left out parts too terrible, too unthinkable for mortal men of Earth to know yet – but the tale you are about to read is even more wild, more glorious, more pulse-pounding than the first manuscript I shared with a disbelieving world, the one that carried you across Barsoom’s dead sea bottoms beneath the glow of the twin Martian moons.

The cold March wind of 1886 bit through my tunic as I stood on the edge of the Hudson valley bluff, and below me the great river coiled like the silver, silent ghost of a long-dead waterway. For ten long, lonely years I had stood here, arms outstretched to the glinting red pinprick of Mars hanging high in the night sky, begging my old patron, the god of war, to drag me back across the void to the lost Dejah Thoris I had left behind on that dying, red world. Not since that other March night twenty years prior, outside a hidden Arizona cave where my own earthly body lay locked in a death-like trance, had I felt that same unshakable, thrumming pull that once wrenched me across interplanetary space.
I stood bareheaded, straining toward Mars’ lurid glow, when a wave of nausea crashed over me. My senses swam, my knees buckled, and I pitched forward so close to the bluff edge I could feel the cold air of the hundred-foot drop on my cheeks.
The paralysis hit instantly, exactly as it had in that Arizona cave. My muscles locked tight, useless to my commands, and for a split second I swore I could hear the scrape and low, hungry moans of the unnameable thing that had lurked in that cave’s dark recesses so long ago. I poured every last ounce of my superhuman will into breaking the invisible bonds holding me, and then—*click*—the sharp, clear snap of a taut wire parting, and I was standing naked and free beside my own still, lifeless earthly corpse.
I did not waste a glance on the body I was leaving behind. I lifted my hands to Mars, and waited.
The void swallowed me faster than thought. For a single, unbearable instant I was adrift in utter, freezing darkness, and then I blinked awake beneath a burning bronze sun, lying on a carpet of close-cropped, blood-red grass beneath the dome of a forest unlike any I had ever seen on my previous trips to Barsoom.
My throat tightened in sudden, cold fear: I had missed Mars, I thought, been thrown blind across the trackless wastes of space to some alien, uncharted world. The trees around me bore blooms so huge and vivid no earthly word could describe their color, winged silent shapes darted through the branches that bore no resemblance to any bird or beast I knew, and past the treeline glinted the impossible blue of an open sea—something no Barsoom explorer had ever documented on its parched, dying sea bottoms.
I tried to push myself up, and forgot. The low gravity of the planet sent me flying three feet into the air, and I face-planted straight back into the soft red grass. The silly, familiar mishap made me grin. This *was* Mars, or an unknown corner of it. After ten years of wandering its vast deserts I had only explored a tiny fraction of its surface, after all. I got to my feet again, quickly recalibrating my Earth-born, gravity-strengthened muscles to the light, thin Barsoomian air, and started down the gentle slope toward the sea.
The whole grove was tended like a king’s private park: the grass was clipped as tight as an English nobleman’s lawn, the trees pruned smooth to a uniform fifteen feet off the ground, so the forest stretched around me like a vast cathedral with a ceiling woven of leaves and blooms. Their hundred-foot-wide trunks were polished as smooth as a new piano, in hues of polished ebony, bone white porcelain, electric azure, scarlet, and deep violet. I assumed I had landed in the territory of a peaceful, civilized red Martian settlement, and that soon enough I would be welcomed with all the honor due my rank as Prince of the House of Tardos Mors.
I was almost at the treeline edge, where the meadow opened out to the sea, when the romantic glow of the beautiful landscape vanished entirely.
A score of grotesque, man-sized figures were grazing in the meadow, near the banks of a wide, slow-moving river that poured out of the foot of a sheer line of towering cliffs. The tallest stood twelve feet high, their torsos and legs shaped exactly like a human’s, but their arms were boneless, sinuous, coiling like an elephant’s trunk as they moved their strange hands over the red grass.
I ducked behind a massive polished scarlet trunk to watch, and one of the creatures wandered close enough to get a full look at. Its hairless skin was a ghoulish, sickly blue, ringed with a band of white around its single bulging eye—a dead, milky orb with no pupil, no iris, all of it pale, blind white. Its face had no mouth, only a raw, ragged circular hole of a nose that looked exactly like a fresh, unbled bullet wound. Its “hair” was a tangled mass of eight-inch-long jet-black worms, wriggling and crawling across its scalp as if each one had a mind of its own. Its feet were broad, three feet long, flat as paddles, and a six-foot blade-tipped tail trailed behind it, held at a right angle to the ground. It was not grazing with its face, I realized: the palms of its hands had mouths, lined with razor talons that snipped the grass, sucking the vegetation up through the hollow, throats that ran the length of its boneless arms. Dangling from its armpits on thin little living stems were two tiny, perfectly formed replicas of itself, no more than six inches long.
I was half-moved to step out of hiding and approach the herd, curious to test their temper, when a high, shrill shriek cut through the quiet, coming from the direction of the cliffs. Every single wriggling “hair” on the plant men’s heads snapped upright, stiff as wires. Those weren’t hair, I realized. They were ears— a thousand tiny, living ears, the last leftover trait of that strange, ancient Barsoomian race that had sprung first from the planet’s original Tree of Life.
The largest of the creatures, their leader, purred a low resonant hum from the mouth in his palm, and the whole herd bounded off toward the cliffs in 20 and 30 foot leaps, moving faster than any kangaroo on Earth. Curiosity burning, I gave chase, my own Earth muscles sending me bounding even farther across the meadow than they could go.
I ducked behind a giant weathered boulder at the base of the cliffs, and the full scene broke over my view. The plant men had surrounded a tiny party of six green Martians—two men, four women, adorned in the war ornaments of different, mutually hostile hordes. For them to stand back to back fighting a common enemy was unheard of, save for that one historic battle when Tars Tarkas of Thark had united a hundred and fifty thousand green warriors to storm Zodanga and rescue Dejah Thoris.
They were all armed with longswords and daggers, but no firearms. Before they could make a coordinated move, the plant man leader bounded ten feet over their heads, sweeping his blade tail down so hard he crushed one green warrior’s skull like an eggshell. The rest of the herd circled at bewildering speed, screeching their purring cries to confuse their prey, and two more green Martians died to the tails of leaping attackers.
Only one green warrior and two women were left, and they would have been dead in seconds if the warrior had not learned fast. As two more plant men charged, he swung his longsword in a clean, perfect arc that sliced one creature open from chin to groin. The other’s tail caved in the skulls of the two remaining women, and the lone green warrior cut a swath straight through the herd, running for the shelter of the distant forest.
Acting on the same old battle impulse that had served me well through a hundred Barsoomian fights, I sprinted from my hiding spot, snatched a longsword from the hand of a dead green warrior, and gave chase, the old battle-lust burning hot in my chest.
I caught up to the herd before they even noticed I was there. Four of the plant men dropped dead under my blade before they realized a new foe had joined the fight. The lone green warrior saw his opening, bounded to my side, and fought back to back with me, his longsword swinging in endless, looping figure-eight strokes that cut through plant man flesh, bone, and the thin metal ornaments on their bodies as if they were thin air.
The plant men’s thick, sticky sap oozed over us, green and thick as the juice from a crushed caterpillar, splattering our skin and faces. One of the monsters leapt on my back, its talons sinking deep into my shoulders, the moist sucking mouths on its palms latching to my wounds to drink my blood. The green warrior tore it off my back with a single, lightning fast sweep of his blade.
We held our ground, backs pressed to the boulder to keep the plant men from leaping over our heads to strike with their tails, and we were cutting through the last of the nearby herd when that shrill shriek blared out again. I looked up.
On a narrow natural balcony carved into the cliff face, a lone human figure was standing, shrieking the call over and over, waving one hand toward the mouth of the distant river to summon backup. I followed his gaze. Hundreds of the bounding, hideous plant men were swarming toward us from every direction, pouring out of the forest, across the meadow, and over the far banks of the river. We were outnumbered a hundred to one.
“It will be a great death,” I said to my companion. I nodded toward the horde rushing us.
He smiled, his tusks glinting in the sun. “We may at least die fighting as great warriors should, John Carter.”
I froze. No one on Barsoom who I had not known intimately would call me by my Earth name, not here, not ten years after I had vanished from the red planet. I stared at the green warrior beside me, at his familiar, broad-shouldered frame, at the proud, steady light in his lateral eyes, at the great battle scars I recognized across his chest.
Standing there covered in sticky plant man sap, his longsword still dripping with the gore of the monsters we had slain, was the greatest of all Barsoom’s green men. My oldest, truest friend. Tars Tarkas, Jeddak of Thark.

There was no time for Tars Tarkas and I to swap stories as we huddled behind the great boulder, grotesque corpses of our first assailants strewn at our feet. Down the breadth of the red valley, a roiling tide of nightmarish creatures was surging toward us, drawn by the shrill, alien call of the hooded figure shrieking from a ledge high above the cliffs.
“Run!” the Thark Jeddak roared, his blade still glistening with the ichor of a dead attacker. “The cliffs are our only chance. We’ll find a cave, a narrow ledge—two good swordsmen can hold that against this mindless, unarmored horde forever.”
We raced side by side across the scarlet sward, me slowing my Earth-honed speed so I never pulled ahead of my larger, heavier companion. Three hundred yards lay between our boulder and the cliff base, precious little space to find cover before the horde swallowed us whole. They were gaining fast when Tars Tarkas shouted for me to push ahead, scout the cliff face for shelter first to shave minutes off our search. I threw every ounce of my superhuman earthly muscle into the sprint, eating up the remaining distance in great bounding leaps that put me at the foot of the escarpment in seconds.
The cliffs rose sheer and unbroken straight from the valley floor, no loose talus, no sloped debris piles to ease a climb—only the scattered boulders half-buried in the red turf to hint that the massive rock face had ever crumbled even a fraction. My first sweeping glance across its surface sank my heart: nowhere, save for the screaming herald high up, was there even a bare fingertip of foothold to cling to. To my right the cliff base vanished into the dark, dense foliage of an ancient forest that grew right up to its foot, its canopies climbing a thousand feet up the rock’s stern yellow shadow. To my left, the cliff stretched unbroken across the valley head, bleeding into the jagged outline of the massive mountain range that walled the valley in on all sides. A thousand feet away, the river burst straight from the cliff base, no path of escape that way left to me, so I turned my gaze back toward the forest.
The cliffs towered five thousand feet above me, still half in shadow, streaked with patches of dusky red, deep green, and flecks of white quartz. They were stunning, but I had no eye for beauty then—only escape, and I scanned their expanse again and again until they felt like the smooth, impregnable walls of a prison cell.
Tars Tarkas was closing the gap fast, and the horde was closer still. It looked like the forest or death, until the sun crested the cliff’s zenith, and the slanting golden rays hit the rock face. The entire escarpment exploded into a million shards of shimmering light: burnished gold, searing ruby red, soft emerald green, gleaming diamond white. Later inspection would prove the whole cliff was shot through with veins of solid gold, crusted with outcroppings of precious stone so thick it looked like a single wall of treasure, hiding unimaginable wealth deep inside its mass. But what caught my eye far faster than the riches were the dark, black cave mouths scattered high across the glowing rock, peeking out just above the treeline.
There was only one way to reach them: through the giant, towering trees of the forest at the cliff’s base. I knew I could scale them easily, but Tars Tarkas, with his massive bulk and enormous weight, would struggle. Green Martians of Barsoom are notoriously poor climbers—their home planet’s gentle hills, all gradual slopes up from the dead sea bottoms, never gave them reason to practice the skill, and they always preferred a long, easy detour around any eminence over a hard, steep climb. But there was no other choice. The Thark grasped the plan and its risks in an instant, and we bolted for the nearest trees.
The pursuers were almost on our heels now, so close it seemed impossible Tars Tarkas would make the treeline before they swarmed him. The green men of Barsoom never flee death, no matter what form it takes, and I knew Tars Tarkas had proven his bravery in tens of thousands of mortal battles against men and beasts. He was not running from death—some new, fierce urge to live drove him, just as the thought of Dejah Thoris, my lost princess, spurred me on. The cruel, loveless Tharks almost always sought death over prolonged life, and his sudden hunger to survive was a mystery I could not solve, then.
We burst into the cool shadow of the forest, and a hundred yards ahead of the rest of the horde, the fastest of the attackers lunged: a giant plant man, writhing clawed tentacles outstretched, its blood-sucking mouths gaping. I shouted to Tars Tarkas to climb the nearest tree that brushed the cliff face, so he could get a head start to the upper branches while I cut the plant man down. But I misjudged the creature’s speed and cunning. Before my longsword could strike its killing blow, a thick, muscular tail as powerful as a grizzly bear’s arm swept across the sward, knocked my feet out from under me, and sent me crashing to the ground. The brute was on top of me in a heartbeat, but I seized two of its writhing tentacles in a vice grip. My Earth-born sinew and greater agility would have won me the fight, if we’d had time to tussle undisturbed—but over the plant man’s shoulder, I caught sight of the rest of the horde surging over us.
Now I saw what the other creatures were: the great white apes of Barsoom, the most feared horror on the whole planet. They stood fifteen feet tall, walking upright on hind legs, with a second set of arms midway between their upper limbs and their legs, their faces nearly human but for their gorilla-like snouts and bristling shock of black hair on their heads. That uncanny, half-human look was the only thing on Mars that had ever made me feel true fear.
They swept over me in a snarling, snapping, purring wave. Dozens of cruel fangs and sharp talons tore at my flesh, cold sucking lips clamped to my arteries. I fought my way back to my feet, shortened my sword to use it like a dagger, and cut through the horde until I stood free for a breath—just in time to see Tars Tarkas drop back down from the lower tree branches he’d climbed so laboriously. The great Thark leaped to my side, and we stood back to back, the two most famous swordsmen on a planet of legendary warriors, just as we’d done a hundred times before.
We beat back every charge the apes made, our blades flashing through the air to slice through ape flesh and plant man tentacles alike. But no two swords, no matter how skilled, can hold off endless waves of ferocious, unkillable brutes forever. Step by step we were forced back, until our shoulders hit the massive tree trunk we’d meant to climb. We circled its base as the horde pressed in, until Tars Tarkas let out a sharp cry of triumph.
“Here’s shelter for at least one of us, John Carter!” he said. Glancing down, I saw an opening three feet wide cut into the base of the tree, leading into its hollow core.
“Go first!” I shouted, but he refused. His broad frame was too big to squeeze through the narrow gap fast, he said, while my smaller, slighter human frame could slip right inside. “We both die out here. One of us might survive in there. You can live to avenge me.”
“Then we die together,” I told him. “You go first. I’ll hold the opening while you crawl in, then I’ll slip in right after you.” We kept fighting as we spat out our broken sentences, our blades never pausing in their bloody work. At last he yielded, a grim, rare smile pulling at his hard, cruel Thark face. The greatest Jeddak on Barsoom was obeying the orders of a man from a different world, a man less than half his size.
“Turning my back on an enemy is a thing I have never done in my whole life,” he rumbled, dropping to his hands and knees to wriggle into the hollow. “But if you fall, John Carter, I will come right back out and die beside you.”
The whole horde threw themselves at me then, the moment he turned his back. My blade flew so fast it blurred, sticky with plant man sap one second, red with white ape blood the next, cutting down every creature that got within reach until the bodies piled high around my feet. But even my strength gave out, and the weight of hundreds of thousands of pounds of ferocious, fighting flesh dragged me down for the second time that day. Cold sucking lips fastened to my throat—until powerful, calloused hands clamped around my ankles, and hauled me yanking back into the hollow dark of the tree.
It was a short, brutal tug of war between Tars Tarkas and a plant man clinging to my chest, until I drove my longsword through the creature’s vitals. Torn, bleeding, gasping for air, I collapsed on the hard flat floor of the tree’s hollow core while Tars Tarkas stood guard over the narrow entrance. The horde howled and scratched at the wood for a full hour before they gave up, leaving only a score of sentries to wait outside, trapping us for what looked like a slow death by starvation.
When our eyes adjusted to the dim dark, I explored our shelter. The hollow stretched fifty feet wide, its hard packed floor clearly worn smooth by hundreds of other inhabitants long before us. Glancing up, I saw a faint glow of light far above our heads: another opening, higher up. At the far side of the hollow, I found a rough wooden ladder leading to a set of horizontal wooden rungs set three feet apart, spanning the narrow shaft of the tree’s trunk, climbing straight up out of sight.
We decided Tars Tarkas would guard the entrance while I climbed ahead to scout. I scaled the rungs for five hundred feet, the light growing brighter with every step, until I reached the upper opening. It led out onto a broad, flat, well-worn tree limb that angled slowly toward the cliff face. I didn’t step out fully, afraid the sentries below would spot me, so I climbed back down to get Tars Tarkas. As we climbed back up together, I pulled every lower rung free after we passed, tucking them up higher to block any possible pursuit from below—a precaution that would later save our lives.
When we stepped out onto the limb, I found it curved right toward a narrow, narrow ledge on the cliff face, leading directly to a small cave mouth. The tip of the limb swayed gently two feet away from the ledge, five hundred feet above the scarlet valley sward, five thousand feet below the glowing golden cliff tops. Our combined weight pulled the limb too low to reach the cave, so we had Tars Tarkas back up the limb to lighten our load, using his long leather harness strap to pull him up onto the ledge once the limb rose high enough for me to clamber into the cave mouth.
We stood on that tiny balcony ledge a moment, looking out across the valley: crimson grass, endless forest, a distant glint that almost looked like a gilded minaret half-hidden in the trees before we decided it must be a trick of the light, while the great white apes feasted on the remains of Tars Tarkas’ old war party down by the river.
We turned to explore the cave. It was a perfectly hewn tunnel cut straight from the solid cliff, twenty feet high, five feet wide, with a smooth arched roof. We had no fire, no light, so we groped our way slowly forward, holding hands so we would not get separated if the tunnel branched. We walked for what felt like miles until we hit a hard flat obstruction across the tunnel, not made of stone, but of polished, heavy wood. My hands found the familiar raised button that marks every Barsoomian door, the same way a doorknob marks an Earth one. I pressed it gently. The door swung open silently, revealing a dimly lit empty room.
I stepped inside first, Tars Tarkas right behind me. For a breath we stood still, taking in the space around us—until a soft, faint noise behind us made me spin around. The door had snapped shut with a sharp, final click, as if pulled by an invisible hand. I scrabbled at the smooth wood, searching for the opening button, and found nothing. No way out, no mark, no crack.
And then, out of the empty dark of the chamber, a cruel, mocking peal of laughter echoed, bouncing off the rock walls all around us.

For long, taut seconds after that ghoulish, echoing laugh died away against the rock walls, Tars Tarkas and I stood frozen, every sense straining for the next sign of threat. No further sound broke the thick silence, no shadow shifted within the range of our vision.

At last the great Thark chief laughed softly, that strange, alien huff of mirth his kind reserve for horrors that would drive Earth men to tears or retching. It is no hysterical titter, but unfeigned pleasure at the sight of terror and suffering—emotion I had watched consume them in rolling, gasping fits of glee as they watched women and children writhe out their lives under the tortures of Barsoom’s hellish Great Games.

I grinned back up at him, hand resting light on the hilt of my long sword. Here there was far more use for a stiff spine than a trembling chin.
“What do you make of all that?” I asked. “Where in the name of all the old gods of Mars are we?”
He blinked at me in open surprise.
“Where are we? You mean to say John Carter, you do not recognize this place?”
“That I am still on Barsoom is the only thing I can be half sure of,” I told him. “If I hadn’t run into you and those giant white apes I would not have guessed even that. The things I’ve seen today are no more like the Barsoom I knew ten years ago than they are like my own home world. No, Tars Tarkas, I have no idea where we are.”
“Where were you all that time, after you wrenched open the great gates of the atmosphere plant? The keeper was dead, the engines had stalled, and every Barsoomian who hadn’t already asphyxiated was minutes from dying. We searched the whole planet for you for years. The Jeddak of Helium, your princess Dejah Thoris, offered rewards so vast that even royal princes joined the hunt. When no trace of you ever turned up, there was only one conclusion: you had taken the long pilgrimage down the mysterious River Iss, to wait for Dejah at the edge of the Lost Sea of Korus in the Valley Dor.”
I cut him off, my voice tight. “Thank God. I didn’t dare ask. I left her in the royal gardens of Tardos Mors that night, half dead, so far gone I barely dared hope I could reach the atmosphere plant in time before her spirit slipped away forever. She lives?”
“She lives, John Carter.”
“Then tell me where we are now,” I pressed.
“We are where I expected to find you—and another. Long ago you heard the story of the human woman who taught this cruel Thark what love was, the woman who was tortured to death by the brute Tal Hajus for that love. I thought she waited for me on the shores of Korus. You, too, the only man ever to teach me what friendship means— I thought you wandered the carefree paradise of the Valley Dor. The two people I wanted most in all the universe, at the end of the pilgrimage I knew I would have to make one day. A month ago, when the time Dejah Thoris had long clung to—when she swore you would return to her—was up, I could bear the waiting no longer, and set out down the Iss. Do you understand now where you stand?”
“Then that was the River Iss, emptying into the Lost Sea of Korus? This is the so-called heaven every Barsoomian dreams of, the end of a life of war and strife?”
“This is what they call the Valley of Love and Peace,” he said, his voice sharp with cold, bitter irony. “This is Barsoom’s Heaven.”
I laid a hand on his shoulder. The weight of that disillusionment—ten thousand years of sacred tradition crumbling to ash before his eyes—was a blow no mortal could be blamed for reeling under. “I am sorry,” was all I could say.
“Think of the billions of souls that have drifted down that river since the dawn of time,” he snarled. “Voluntarily, to fall straight into the clutches of those plant men and white apes that attacked us today. There is an old legend, you know, of a red man who once came back from the Valley Dor, sailed back up the Iss, screaming blasphemies about brutes that devoured pilgrims on the very shores where they expected peace. They stoned him to death, per tradition, for returning from the River of Mystery. Now we know he told nothing but the truth. But what good is that? Even if we escape, they will kill us for blasphemers too. We are trapped between the wild thoat of certain death behind us and the mad zitidar of certain death ahead.”
“On Earth we call that between the devil and the deep sea,” I said, and I couldn’t help a smile at our absurd, deadly predicament. “There is nothing for it but to meet whatever comes. Whatever kills us will pay for it dearly. No one—white ape, plant man, red or green Martian—will ever be able to brag that they took John Carter, Prince of the House of Tardos Mors, and Tars Tarkas, Jeddak of Thark, without losing dozens of their own to the pair of us.”
The grim joke made him laugh, that rare, unguarded burst of true joy that made him stand so far apart from the rest of his cruel, cold kind.
“But you,” he said, turning to me at last. “If you were not here in the Dor for all these years, where were you?”
“Back on Earth,” I said. “Ten long, Earth years of living death, pining for this harsh, brutal planet I love more than the one I was born on. Ten years of wondering every single day if Dejah Thoris was dead, and now that I know she is alive, fate has dumped me right in the one spot on all Barsoom there is no escape from. Even if we could get out, the price would snuff out every last chance I ever have of seeing her again. Barely half an hour before I saw you fighting those plant men on the riverbank, I was standing in the moonlight on the banks of the Hudson, in New York, on Earth. Do you believe me?”
“I believe,” Tars Tarkas rumbled. “Though I cannot understand it.”
As we spoke I was scanning the chamber. It was roughly two hundred feet long, a hundred wide, hewn straight from the solid golden cliff rock. A single tiny radium illuminator set in the ceiling cast dim, soft light over inlaid patches of polished ruby, emerald, and diamond dotted across the walls and ceiling. The floor was some unknown glass-hard material, worn as smooth as polished crystal by tens of thousands of feet that had crossed it over eons. Only two doorways broke the walls: the one we had come through, which we knew was locked behind us, and another set dead centre in the far wall.
I stepped toward that far door, reaching out to find its hidden control button, when that same cruel, mocking laugh pealed out so close to my ear I flinched back, my fingers tightening white around the hilt of my great sword.
A hollow, droning voice chanted from the far corner of the room, with no visible source: “There is no hope, there is no hope; the dead return not, the dead return not; nor is there any resurrection. Hope not, for there is no hope.”
My eyes snapped straight to the corner, but there was no one there. Cold shivers prickled down my spine, the short hairs at the base of my neck standing up stiff as a hound’s when it sees something in the dark no human can make out.
I strode toward the spot the voice seemed to come from, but it cut off before I got there. Then a shrill, piercing shriek erupted from the opposite end of the chamber.
“Fools! Fools! Do you think you can defy the eternal laws of life and death? Would you cheat the great Issus, Goddess of Death, of her rightful dues? Did not her messenger, the ancient river Iss, carry you of your own free will straight to her domain? Do you think she will ever let you escape? In all the endless ages, only one soul ever fled the Valley Dor.
“Go back the way you came, to the quick mercy of the children of the Tree of Life or the fangs of the great white apes. There your suffering will end fast. But if you are fool enough to try to thread the Golden Cliffs of the Mountains of Otz, past the fortresses of the Holy Therns, you will meet the most terrible form of death Issus can devise. A torment so fiendish that even the Holy Therns, who invented every form of life and death, will turn their eyes away and block their ears to the screams of their victim.
“Go back, O fools, the way you came.”
The mocking laugh burst out again, from yet another new corner of the room.
“Uncanny as hell,” I muttered to Tars Tarkas.
“What do we do?” he grumbled. “We cannot fight thin air. I would rather turn back and face a foe I can drive my blade into, than keep dancing to the tune of disembodied voices. At least then I can sell my life dear before I vanish into that eternal nothingness that seems to be the best any mortal can hope for.”
“If we can’t fight thin air, Tars Tarkas,” I snapped, “thin air can’t fight us either. I’ve fought and beaten thousands of hardened warriors in my time, I’m not going to be scared off by noise. And neither are you.”
“Unseen voices could belong to unseen creatures wielding unseen blades,” he argued.
“Rot,” I said, loud enough for every hidden listener in the walls to hear. “Those things are as flesh and blood as we are. Their blood runs red, same as ours. The fact that they hide instead of facing us blade to blade proves they’re mortal, and cowards. John Carter doesn’t run screaming from a foe that won’t even show his face.”
I was sick of their cheap, nerve-racking tricks. It was obvious what they were doing: scaring us back out into the valley to be torn apart by the beasts there, so they wouldn’t have to bother dealing with us themselves.
For a long stretch there was silence. Then a soft, stealthy scrape behind me made me spin around fast. A huge, ten-legged banth—Barsoom’s lion—was creeping sinuously toward me, mane of stiff bristles standing up along its thick neck, rows of needle-sharp fangs glinting in its open jaws, bulging green eyes blazing.
It lashed its tail against its yellow sides, then let out that deafening roar that freezes lesser prey into paralysis for the split second before it pounces. It launched its massive bulk at me.
But its roar did nothing to me. It met cold steel, driven straight through its heart before its jaws could close on my flesh. I wrenched my blade free, and turned to see Tars Tarkas standing over a second dead banth at his feet. Before I could call out, another one came leaping across the room at my back.
For the better part of an hour, they kept coming. One hideous banth after another, springing at us from nowhere, screaming in rage and pain as our blades tore into their vitals, gushing hot, real blood over the polished stone floor. There was nothing supernatural about these foes, and we both revelled in the tangible fight after the unnerving mind games.
I noticed none of them ever appeared while we were facing that spot. We never saw one materialize out of thin air. The trick was obvious: hidden, perfectly fitted secret doorways in the walls.
Then I spotted the small hand mirror hanging on the back of Tars Tarkas’ leather harness, right between his shoulders and his waist, a commonplace little accessory most Martian warriors wore. I glanced into it as he stood over a freshly killed banth, and what I saw made me hiss at him not to move a single muscle.
He froze, like a statue, as I watched the trick. A section of the wall behind me was pivoting on hidden axles, turning along with a perfectly circular section of the floor in front of it, fitted so tightly into the surrounding stone there had been no visible crack in the dim light. As it half swung open, I saw what was on the other side: a brightly lit chamber, where several red Martians were chained to the far wall, guarded by a wicked-faced man who was not red, nor green—he was white, like me, with a shock of long flowing yellow hair, directing the movement of the secret panel. Dozens of banths and other ferocious beasts were chained up back there with the prisoners.
The second the panel swung far enough to spit a new banth out into our chamber, I whispered my discovery to Tars Tarkas. “They’re watching us through the walls,” I muttered low, so the hidden listeners couldn’t hear. As long as we both stood facing opposite ends of the room, no new attacks came, confirming it.
I hatched a plan, whispered it fast into his ear. The great Thark grunted his assent, and we moved slowly: he backed toward the wall I was watching, I walked slowly in front of him, his back mirror giving me perfect view of the pivoting panel. When we were ten feet away, I stopped him, turned my back fully to the wall, and stared hard at the mirror.
It didn’t take long. The golden wall section began to turn. I gave Tars Tarkas the prearranged signal, and both of us leaped. I sailed straight through the swinging opening, landing in the hidden chamber face to face with the blonde white man I’d spotted earlier. He was my height, heavily muscled, armed to the teeth with two swords, a dagger, and a radium revolver at his hip.
He didn’t care about Barsoomian honour rules that said you fight a swordsman only with a sword. He drew his revolver fast, but an upward swing of my long sword sent it spinning out of his hand before he could pull the trigger. He drew his own long blade, and we duelled for our lives.
He was a brilliant swordsman, sharp and well practiced. I hadn’t held a sword in ten long Earth years, but it took only minutes for the old reflexes to flood back. Soon he was grunting in rage, his guard slipping, a dozen small cuts dripping blood down his face and arms.
“Who are you, white man?” he hissed. “You’re no red Martian from the outer world, you’re not one of us—”
I gambled on a wild guess. “What if I am from the Temple of Issus?”
His face went dead white under the blood. “May fate forbid that!”
The reaction told me everything. The people here feared the Temple of Issus more than anything, but I didn’t have time to puzzle it out. I drove my sword straight through his ribs, and he crumpled to the floor.
The chained prisoners had watched the whole duel in dead silence, nothing but the clash of blades echoing through the room. But the second he fell, a woman prisoner screamed a warning.
“Turn! Behind you!”
I spun. A second white man had crept out of a dark corridor behind me, sword raised to strike. The secret panel had snapped shut, Tars Tarkas was nowhere to be seen. I was exhausted. I had fought nonstop for hours, I hadn’t eaten in twenty four hours, hadn’t slept longer than that. I was bone tired, my vision swimming.
This new foe was even better with a sword than the first. He backed away, parrying every wild, desperate strike I threw at him, wearing me down until I could barely stand. He backed me in a circle right over the body of his dead comrade, then rushed me. My heel caught on the corpse, I toppled backward, my head cracking hard against the stone floor. The blow cleared my foggy brain, my rage flaring hot enough to tear him apart with my bare hands— and as I pushed myself up, my right hand brushed a piece of cold metal lying on the floor.

The low, wet snarl of fighting dragged me back to full consciousness, the harsh vibration thrumming up through the cold gold of the floor beneath my palms. For a dazed heartbeat I could not place where I was, nor parse the racket coming from the far side of the blank stone wall at my back: shuffling, clawed feet, the guttural growl of banths, the clink of metal armor, and the ragged, straining breath of a man I knew better than any other on this dead red world.

I stumbled to my feet, and swept a glance around the grim vault. The prisoners and their chained savage brutes huddled along the far wall, every eye fixed on me—some sharp with curiosity, some sullen with unspent rage, some soft with fragile hope. That hope burned brightest on the face of the young red Martian woman whose warning cry had saved my life minutes before. She was the pinnacle of her race: features carved with the same godlike symmetry as Earth’s finest, her skin the warm, luminous hue of polished light copper, unadorned by a single ornament so I could not guess if she had once been a queen or a common laborer, only that she was trapped here same as the rest of us.

It took a few more seconds for my foggy wits to piece the fighting noises together. Tars Tarkas. The great Thark was trapped on the other side of that wall, fighting for his life against whatever horrors the Therns had locked in there with him. I bellowed encouragement, throwing my full weight against the solid panel hard enough to jar my shoulders, but I might as well have tried to tear the side of a Martian cliff down with my bare hands. I scrabbled at the stone, hunting for the hidden trigger, longsword raised ready to hack at the unyielding gold, when the red girl called out to me, her voice clear and steady above the distant snarls.

“Spare your blade, warrior. You will need it far more where it can draw blood. Do not waste it on senseless metal that answers to a single light touch from one who knows its secret.”

“*You* know how to open it?” I asked, already hunting the ring of keys the dead Thern guard still wore on his belt.

“I do. Free me, and I will lead you through to that other death chamber. The keys to my fetters are on your first slain foe back there. But why would you rush back to face the banths, or whatever other killing thing they loosed in that trap?”

“Because my friend is fighting there alone,” I said, the keys already in my hand. She plucked the right one out of the oval ring in a breath, snapped the lock on her irons, and darted to the panel, picking out a thin, needle-thin key to push into a pinprick hole almost invisible in the gold. The whole slab swung silently on its pivot, and the patch of floor I stood on glided right with me into the second vault.

Tars Tarkas had his back wedged into the sharp angle of two walls, half a dozen massive, slavering banths crouched in a wary semicircle around him. Their shoulders and foreheads were crusted with dried blood, proof of how many wounds the Thark had dealt them, but his own glossy green hide was crisscrossed with ragged, bleeding gashes: talons had torn through leg, arm, chest, left him so weak from loss of blood I doubted he could have stood another minute without the stone bracing him. Yet his grip on his longsword never wavered, his burning eyes never left the beasts. He was the living embodiment of the old Thark proverb: leave a Thark his head and one hand, and he may yet conquer.

When he saw me, a thin, grim smile tugged at the corners of his hard lips. I never knew if it was relief, or amusement at the sight of my own scraped, blood-crusted state. I tensed to leap into the fray, when a soft hand landed light on my shoulder. I spun, shocked to see the young red girl had followed me in, no weapon, no armor, nothing.

“Wait,” she whispered. “Leave them to me.”

She stepped past me, straight toward the snarling banths, until she was within arm’s reach of the leading beast. She spoke a single sharp, peremptory word in the old, low tongue of Barsoom. The great cats spun toward her in a blur, and I lunged forward, certain I would watch her torn to ribbons before I could reach her— but instead the six massive killers slunk to her feet, heads bowed, like puppies waiting for a deserved whipping. She murmured a few more words too low for me to catch, then turned and walked toward the far panel, the whole pack trailing at her heels one by one. She herded every banth out through the hidden door, and vanished after them, leaving Tars Tarkas and me staring at each other in dumbfounded silence.

“I heard you fighting through the wall,” Tars rumbled at last, wiping blood from his brow with the back of his free hand. “I never feared for you, John Carter, until I heard the gunshot. I knew no man on all Barsoom could face you with naked steel and live. But you never carry firearms. I thought that was the end.”

I told him the full story of the girl and the banths and the dead guards, then we both turned to hunt for the panel that would lead us out of the vault, away from the prison ward. We fumbled for the lock for 20 minutes, got nowhere, until the panel slid open of its own accord. Thuvia stood on the other side, as calm as if she’d never herded a pack of man-eating banths like house pets.

“Who are you?” she asked. “What makes you mad enough to try escaping the Valley Dor, when every soul that drifts down the River Iss here comes voluntarily to die in the ‘holy’ land?”

“I chose no death, maiden,” I said. “I am not from Barsoom. I never took that fool’s pilgrimage down the Iss. My friend here is Jeddak of all the Tharks, and he is not going to rot here feeding Thern pets. I am John Carter, Prince of the house of Tardos Mors, Jeddak of Helium. You may have heard rumors of me, even in this den of monsters.”

She smiled. “Nothing that happens on the outer face of Barsoom stays hidden from here. I heard of you, years ago. The Therns wondered for decades where you vanished to, since you never took the pilgrimage, and no one could find your corpse anywhere.”

“Tell me your name,” I said. “And how is it that a slave can command the banths like that, with power no prisoner should ever have?”

“My name is Thuvia of Ptarth,” she said, and she shuddered. “I have been a slave here for fifteen years. They tired of me, and they knew I learned too many of their secrets. They condemned me to die by the plant men, a few hours from now. The Holy Therns eat human flesh, but only after the plant men have sucked every last drop of blood out of it. You interrupted that plan before they could drag me down there.”

She walked us through the grim truth of the Valley Dor: the lesser Therns we had killed, the thousand miles of tunnels the Iss flows through before it spills out into the Valley, the labyrinth of caves and sunless chambers full of humans and beasts born and died in total darkness, never seeing the red light of Barsoom’s sun. She told us the old superstition the Therns clung to: they live a thousand years, if they are not killed before that, and pass through to their own private heaven behind the walls of the Temple of Issus. If they die early, their souls get trapped in a plant man. If the plant man dies before the thousand years are up, their soul moves to a great white ape. If the ape dies early, they are doomed to spend eternity as a squirming silian in the black depths of the Lost Sea of Korus.

“Then we sent a good handful of them to the silians today,” Tars Tarkas laughed, the sound deep and rough, and Thuvia shook her head.

“You cannot escape,” she said, hopeless. “No one ever has, not in a hundred thousand years.”

“Someone did, once, centuries ago,” I countered. “And what can be done once, can be done again. Come with us. Help us get out. We can bring the truth back to the outer world, break the death grip this terrible lie of a sacred afterlife has on every nation of Barsoom. Hundreds of thousands of fools drift down the Iss to die here every year, and no one even knows they are walking straight to slaughter.”

Thuvia stood silent for a long minute, then nodded. “I would give my life a thousand times over to save even one soul from this place. I will go with you.” She glanced at Tars Tarkas.

“To the gates of Issus, or to the bottom of Korus,” the Thark rumbled, thumping his chest once with his fist. “To the snows of the north or the snows of the south. Tars Tarkas follows where John Carter leads. I have spoken.”

Thuvia led us back through the panel to the first vault, where ten more red Martian prisoners—men and women—waited. When we explained our plan, every one of them chose to join us, nervous as they were to defy a superstition every Barsoomian had been raised on since birth. Thuvia unlocked their chains, we stripped the dead Therns of their swords, daggers, and the two revolvers they carried, arming every last one of our ragtag band, giving the firearms to Thuvia and one of the other women.

We wound through twisting tunnels, past great chambers hewn straight out of solid gold cliff stone, climbing steep inclines, ducking into dark recesses every time we heard approaching footsteps. Thuvia said we were heading for a distant weapons storeroom, to load up on enough ammo and blades to fight our way through the Thern stronghold all the way to the cliff tops, where we could make a break for the outer world.

We had traveled almost an hour when we stepped into a vast empty chamber, and found a man blocking our path: a Holy Thern, ranked high, his leather harness crusted with gems, a massive gold circlet set with one of the legendary nine-rayed atmosphere plant gems set in the center of his forehead. It was the exact twin of the gem I had seen on the old custodian of the great atmosphere plant, twenty years prior, the secret to which had once let me save all of Barsoom from suffocation.

The Thern narrowed his eyes. “Stop!” he roared. “What is the meaning of this, Thuvia?”

Thuvia lifted the revolver, fired point blank into his chest. He dropped dead without a sound.

“Beast,” she hissed, spitting on his corpse. “After fifteen years, I am revenged.” Then she turned to me, eyes wide. “O Prince—look. He is the exact same height as you. Your features match almost perfectly.”

I stared. It was true, save for the mass of flowing golden hair on his head, which contrasted sharply with my own short, cropped black hair. Thuvia knelt, and to my utter shock, peeled the entire scalp and golden wig straight off his bald, egg-smooth head. She stepped over to me, set the golden wig over my black hair, and pressed the gold circlet with its nine-rayed gem onto my forehead.

“Now put on his harness,” she said. “Sator Throg was a Holy Thern of the Tenth Cycle. You can pass anywhere in their realms, unchallenged. Every one of them is bald from birth—they all wear these golden wigs. No one will suspect.”

I slipped the heavy, gem-studded harness over my shoulders, and two of the red Martians slung Sator Throg’s corpse over their shoulders to carry with us, to hide our trail. We reached the storeroom without running into a single patrol, and the keys Thuvia had taken off the dead guard let us right in. We stocked every pouch full of ammo, strapped extra swords to our hips, until we were armed to the teeth.

By that point, exhaustion dragged at every muscle in my body, after hours of fighting and running and no rest. I dropped flat onto the stone floor, told Tars Tarkas to get what rest he could, and set two of the prisoners on watch.

I was asleep before my head even hit the cold rock.

I cannot say how many hours I passed unconscious on the cold stone of the underground storeroom, but it felt like an entire cycle of Barsoom’s faint, distant twin moons. I jolted awake to sharp, shrieking cries of alarm, my wits still fumbling to place me in the dark stone cell, when a roar of gunfire split the air. The blasts bounced off the subterranean walls in a cascade of deafening, overlapping echoes that rattled my teeth in my skull.

I was on my feet before the last echo died. A dozen lesser therns blocked the far storeroom doorway, their wicked rifles still smoking. My remaining companions from the escape party lay motionless on the floor—all save Thuvia of Ptarth and Tars Tarkas, who had been asleep out of the line of the first raking fire, same as me. The therns lowered their weapons then, their faces twisted with chagrin, confusion, and raw, unmasked fear, as they realized they had nearly gunned down the very high-ranking Holy Thern they served.

I seized the gambit instantly, roaring in tones of thunderous, feigned fury: “What treason is this? Do your vassal hands dare raise arms against Sator Throg?”

One of the men dropped to his knees, the others edging backward toward the exit like rats trying to slip from a lion’s den. “Mercy, O Master of the Tenth Cycle!” he babbled.

“Demand their purpose here,” Thuvia breathed at my elbow.

“What do you trespass in this chamber for?” I snarled.

“We hunt the two fugitives from the outer world, by order of the Father of Therns!” the man stammered. “One a pale man with raven hair, the other a giant green Thark warrior—” He darted a suspicious, wary glance at Tars Tarkas as he spoke.

Thuvia stepped forward then, her voice sharp and cutting. “Here is the Thark, and if you trouble to look at the corpse crumpled by the entry, you will find the black-haired man. It was Sator Throg and his small band of slaves that did what your useless guard ranks could not: we slew one fugitive, captured the other alive, in return for the liberty he promised us. Now your fool’s rush of gunfire has butchered all the prisoners save myself, and nearly murdered the Holy Thern himself!”

The lesser therns looked so sheepish and terrified I almost laughed. When Thuvia suggested they drag all the bodies down to the plant men to dispose of, then return to their quarters, they jumped to obey at once. As one of the men stooped to hoist the corpse of the real Sator Throg, he paused, his gaze locking on the dead man’s bald, polished scalp. He flicked a furtive, sneaking glance at me from the corner of his eye, and I knew for a certainty he had guessed the truth of our disguise. He dared not speak it aloud yet, not without proof, but as he carried Sator Throg’s body out the door, a thin, cunning smile of triumphant conspiracy curved his lips.

When the last of the therns vanished, only the three of us remained. The marksmanship of the guard had wiped out every last slim chance our dead companions had held of breaking free to the surface world above. Thuvia wasted no time pressing us to run for our lives: she, too, had seen that thern’s knowing smirk. “That bodes no good, Prince of Helium,” she said, her voice tight with urgency. “He would not dare accuse you outright without proof, but he will run straight to Matai Shang, the Father of Therns. He is a high-ranking thorian among the lesser ranks—his audience will not wait long. By the time he finishes telling his tale, every gallery, chamber, garden and temple courtyard within ten miles will be swarming with searchers, and we will be hunted down like trapped banths.”

I shrugged, weary, my old wounds throbbing so sharply I could taste blood where I bit my lip. I had lost enough blood in earlier fights that the ache would not fade, and there was no salve here to knit the flesh fast—nothing like the miracle-working unguents the green Martian women of the Thark hordes used that could put a warrior back on his feet in an hour. For the first time in a hundred battles against Tharks and Zodangan raiders, a wave of utter hopelessness washed over me. But then the long, flowing yellow silk of Sator Throg’s robes blew across my face, caught by a stray underground draught. That disguise might still buy us the time we needed to outrun the alarm.

“Will darkness aid us?” Tars Tarkas rumbled, his great tusks glinting in the dim radium light. “We stand small chance of slipping past sentries in the glare of day.”

Thuvia nodded, glancing toward a cliffside window cut high in the stone wall overlooking Valley Dor. “Night is far safer here. Few souls wander the gardens after dark, and the sentries’ watch grows lax. You freed me from my chains at midnight, we reached this storeroom two hours later, and you all slept for fourteen full hours. The red sun is about to set right now—we have only to see it for ourselves.”

She led us through winding, narrow corridors until we reached that open ledge. The huge, bloated red orb of Barsoom’s sun was dipping below the western peaks of the Otz range, painting the crimson sward of the valley below in fire. The thin, rarefied atmosphere of Mars traps almost no heat: we could already feel the sharp, bone-deep cold seeping in before the last sliver of sun vanished. There is no twilight on Barsoom, no soft fading of light: the sun drops below the horizon like a snuffed lamp, and the world plunges at once into total darkness.

Below us, we spotted a canoe drifting down the sacred River Iss, carrying a dozen red pilgrims from the outer world, sailing to their fated death in Dor. The thern sentry on the balcony beneath us spotted them at the same instant, and lifted his voice in a shrill, keening wail that called every man-eating plant man in the valley to the river bank. The plant men bounded across the sward on their overgrown toes and massive, prehensile tails, their talons outstretched. The pilgrims landed, put up a brief, futile fight, and then vanished under a heaving, writhing mass of the brutes, their blood drained in seconds. Thuvia pointed further across the valley: a dozen great white apes were loping toward the bank, to feast on the flesh the plant men left behind.

Then the sun vanished, and darkness so thick you could almost press your palm against it swallowed the valley whole.

We turned back to the ascent corridors, heading thousands of feet up the winding cliff passages toward the surface. Twice, giant banths—Barsoom’s ferocious tawny lion equivalents, maned, six-legged, the top predator of the underworld—blocked our path, snarling, green eyes glowing in the dark. But Thuvia spoke a single low word of command, and the great beasts dropped their heads, growling sullenly, and slunk away to let us pass. When I asked how she tamed them so easily, she laughed, then shuddered. Sator Throg had once thrown her into a banth pit for spurning his advances, she explained, and some odd quirk of her voice had calmed the beasts so thoroughly they fawned at her feet instead of tearing her to shreds. The therns had kept her ever since to train and control their scavenger guard of banths, who disposed of unclaimed corpses in the underground levels.

A wild, brilliant idea struck me. “Call every banth you can reach,” I said. “Unleash them ahead of us in the upper gardens. The chaos they sow will draw every guard’s eye away from us, buying us a clear run for the ramparts.”

Thuvia grinned, and began to hum a low, thrumming half-purr, the kind of call a Martian beast tamer uses to summon her charges. One by one, the banths crept out of side tunnels to join us, until fifty of the massive brutes padded around our little party, their soft, noiseless paws making no sound on the stone floors. It was a surreal procession: the gem-encrusted golden walls of the therns’ underworld glinting under the dim glow of distant radium bulbs, the great green warlord Tars Tarkas towering head and shoulders above even the largest banth, me clad in Sator Throg’s yellow robes and priceless diadem, and Thuvia, the stolen princess, leading the ravenous horde like some goddess of old Mars.

We crossed through chambers crammed full of the warped, blind, half-human hybrid offspring of prisoners trapped in the underworld for generations, their skin bleached pale from lack of sunlight, their limbs twisted by centuries of inbreeding, looking less like living people than the lost souls of Dante’s Inferno. The banths sniffed hungrily at the limp, defenseless forms strewn across the floors, but held fast to Thuvia’s unspoken command. She explained that no thern would dare wander these lower levels after dark: the banths hunt free, and chained prisoners regularly break their bonds to tear any wandering thern apart.

At last we reached the top of the ascent, and Thuvia paused at a heavy carved door. “Beyond here lies the inner gardens,” she said. “Four miles to the outer ramparts, and every inch of the way is patrolled by sentries. The therns fear no one in all Barsoom, for no soul dares step foot in the forbidden Valley Dor—save their only enemy, the Black Pirates of Barsoom.”

The door swung open. The sweet scent of blooming Martian blossoms washed over us, and the cool night air bit at my cheeks. The banths caught the wild, open scent of the gardens, and burst past us in a surging, roaring tide, disappearing across the sward under the lurid light of the low, hurtling nearer moon of Mars.

A second later, a sharp, high cry of alarm burst from the nearest temple rooftop. It was picked up by the next sentry, and the next, racing east and west along every roof, wall, and rampart, echoing until the whole forbidden kingdom rang with the warning call. Tars Tarkas’s long, double-edged Thark sword flew from its scabbard with a metallic shriek, and Thuvia pressed close to my side, trembling. The hunt was on.

“What in the name of Helium’s royal guard is that?” I breathed to Thuvia, my hand drifting to the hilt of my longsword where it hung at my hip.

She did not speak. Her white, slender finger lifted, pointing straight up into the ink-dark Martian sky, where the glow of Barsoom’s twin moons—Carthoris and Thuria—painted drifting clouds silver. High above the temple spires, walled courts, and blooming thern gardens, shadowy shapes darted, weaving sharp erratic paths like giant predatory birds closing in on a flock of prey.

A split second later, stabbing pinpricks of orange light burst from the hulls of the fliers. The roar of massed rifle volleys rolled down from on high, echoing off the golden cliffs of Otz, and a wall of answering fire blazed back from the thern ramparts and temple battlements.

“The Black Pirates of Barsoom, O Prince,” Thuvia said, her voice tight with long familiarity with the carnage to come.

Airships of the raiding band swept in great descending circles, dropping lower and lower to rain fire on the thern defenders. Volley after volley tore through Barsoom’s thin crisp air, spat from the fliers’ gun ports, while thern guards returned fire so fast their muzzles glowed steady red. When the pirates dropped low enough, thousands of golden-haired, white-robed thern soldiers poured out of temple archways to crowd the gardens and open courtyards. The second they spilled out into the open, scores of small pirate scout fliers peeled off from the main formation and dove straight for us.

The therns fired back through curved shield attachments bolted to the ends of their rifle barrels, but the grim matte-black craft never wavered. Most were small two or three-man scouts, built for speed and hit-and-run raiding, while a handful of bulkier heavy cruisers hung high overhead, lobbing explosive bombs from keel-mounted launchers that blew craters into the temple domes.

On a signal no ear on the ground could catch, every pirate craft in our immediate vicinity dove recklessly for a landing, right in the thick of the massed thern soldiery. Before the hulls had even settled to the grass, the black warriors were leaping over the gunwales, howling like demons, their curved steel sabers flashing in the moonlight.

I had fought ferocious green Martian Warhoons, I had faced the beasts of the dead sea bottoms, but never had I seen fighting like this. The black pirates threw themselves into the fray with wild feral abandon that outstripped every act of ferocity I had ever witnessed. Beneath the bright glow of the twin moons, every second of the battle burned sharp and clear: pale golden-haired therns locking in desperate hand-to-hand combat with their ebony-skinned foes, their robes stained red with blood that dripped onto crushed pimalia blooms under their feet. One pirate drove his saber through a thern’s heart, the body slumping limp at the base of a towering statue carved from solid glowing ruby. A dozen therns forced a lone raider back against a bench hewn from solid emerald, its surface traced with intricate Barsoomian star charts inlaid with cut diamonds.

Thuvia, Tars Tarkas the great Thark, and I stood huddled at the edge of the fray, untouched by the tide of battle for the moment, as fighters swerved past us close enough that I could make out every detail of the raiders I had only ever heard of in old half-forgotten legends from my first time on Mars. The stories said they hailed from the tiny lesser moon, swooping down on Barsoom once every few long Martian years to wreak atrocities, carry off weapons, supplies, and young girls to sacrifice to their dark gods in cannibal orgy.

Up close, they were striking men, broad-shouldered and easily six and a half feet tall, their features sharp, symmetrical, and impossibly handsome, their large black eyes glinting beneath the moonlight, their dark polished ebony skin somehow adding to, rather than detracting from, their fierce unearthly beauty. Their bodies were built exactly the same as therns, red Martians, and my own Earthly frame—only their hearts, it seemed, were made of something far darker. Their lust for blood was a palpable hungry thing, rolling off them as they hacked their way through the thern ranks.

Their small black scout craft lay scattered all across the garden grounds, completely unguarded, and for a second I wondered why the therns did not simply smash every last one of them to splinters. Thuvia answered the unspoken question a minute later, when the roar of fighting receded a little ways from our spot.

“You see now, O Prince,” she said, “why a million warriors guard the thern borders night and day? I have watched this exact same slaughter play out a score of times in the fifteen years I have been their prisoner. The pirates never press hard enough to wipe the therns out completely. They treat us like playthings, to sate their endless hunger for battle, and collect their regular tribute of arms and captives.”

“But why don’t the therns destroy the pirate fliers?” I demanded. “That would stop these raids cold. The crafts are completely unguarded, for God’s sake.”

“They dared once, thousands of years ago,” Thuvia said, her face going pale at the old memory. “The very next night, a thousand of their giant black battleships circled the Mountains of Otz, and bombarded every temple, garden, and courtyard nonstop for an entire Martian moon, until every thern that survived fled deep into the underground catacombs to hide. The therns know they only live at the sufferance of the First Born, as the pirates call themselves. They will never risk that extermination again.”

It was at that exact moment a new unplanned force exploded into the battle. The massive banths—giant ten-legged Barsoomian lions we had released earlier from their pens—had cowered at first from the roar of gunfire and the screams of fighting men, but the scent of fresh blood had roused their rage. One great tawny shape launched itself from a thicket of flowering shrubbery, landing right in the middle of a knot of struggling warriors, and its roar of bestial fury was the signal for the whole pack to charge.

Panic spread like wildfire. Thern and pirate alike forgot their feud to turn on the common enemy, the great banths tearing through their ranks, bowling men over with the sheer weight of their bodies, slashing through armor and flesh with their giant talons, rending bodies apart with their saber-length fangs. The battle turned into a chaotic roiling mess of fur, steel, and screaming men.

I realized in a flash that this was our chance to escape. The therns were far too busy fighting for their lives against the banths to guard their walls. Even better, those unguarded pirate fliers lay right at our feet. I knew every model of Barsoomian airship that had ever been built, after nine years serving in the navy of Helium, from the tiny one-man scout skiffs to the massive dreadnoughts that could outgun any fleet on the planet. Escape was right there for the taking.

I grabbed Thuvia’s arm, jerked my head at Tars Tarkas to follow, and we slipped through the edge of the fighting to the furthest small two-man pirate flier, untouched a hundred yards from the nearest battling warrior. I vaulted onto the deck, my hand closing around the starter lever, my thumb hovering over the repulsor ray button—that miraculous Martian technology that could lift even the largest battleships against Barsoom’s weak gravity.

The craft swayed. It did not lift an inch.

A chorus of savage shouts rose behind me. A dozen black pirates had broken off from the fray and were sprinting straight for us, their sabers drawn.

I realized my mistake in a heartbeat. This was a two-man scout, its repulsor tanks only charged with enough power to lift two average 150-pound red Martians. Tars Tarkas, the 10-foot tall Thark warrior, weighed more than three ordinary men combined. He was anchoring us to the ground like a hundred-pound stone.

There was no time to argue. I jammed the repulsor lever all the way forward, locked the button down, and leaped off the deck of the flier, drawing my longsword to face the onrushing pirates. The craft lurched hard upward, its engines screaming to lift its two remaining passengers. I heard Thuvia’s voice, thin and high above the roar of the engines, “My Prince— I would rather die with you—” before the flier soared up and vanished over the treetops.

For a minute, I thought the dozen pirates would cut me down in seconds. But my Earth-born strength, honed on Virginia’s frontier, was far greater than any Martian’s. My blade wove a shimmering net of death around me, and for long minutes I held them back, my old Virginian battle fury burning hot in my veins, the old familiar fighting smile tugging at my lips.

The pirates fell back a few paces, breathing hard, staring at me with a mix of fury and reluctant respect. “Thern,” one of them snarled, “you fight like a Dator of the First Born. If it were not for your cursed yellow hair and pale skin, you would be a credit to our people.”

“I am no thern,” I called back, about to explain I hailed from Earth, that we could strike a truce to fight the therns together. That was when a heavy hard object slammed into my shoulder blades so hard I nearly face-planted into the grass. The trailing anchor of a passing 10-man pirate cruiser, drifting slow and low over our heads, had struck me. The heavy metal fluke swung past my shoulder, hitting one of the pirates square in the jaw and knocking him cold.

The cruiser was slowly pulling away, the anchor rising higher and higher. I did not hesitate. I put every ounce of my strength into a single leap that carried me right over the heads of the gaping pirates, and my hand closed tight around the last link of the anchor chain just as it was pulling out of reach.

I dangled there, one-handed, dragged through the tops of the flowering trees, as the pirates below screamed and howled in rage. The cruiser banked west, then turned south, carrying me far past the golden cliff crests, out over the vast gulf of the Valley Dor. Six thousand feet below me, the Lost Sea of Korus shimmered like a sheet of silver beneath the twin moons.

Carefully, I hauled myself up the chain, my muscles burning, until my fingers curled over the edge of the cruiser’s gunwale. I pulled myself up, and found myself staring straight into the fierce ebony face of a First Born pirate, his eyes blazing with triumphant, hungry hate.

For one suspended, breathless heartbeat, the black pirate and I hung frozen, our eyes locked, the thousand-foot void of Barsoom’s sunken Lost Sea yawning beneath my single-handed grip on the anchor chain. A grim, sharp smile curved the handsome ebony lips hovering inches above my face, as a dark hand slipped over the deck’s edge, snout of a revolver glinting in the faint moonlight to press dead-centre to my forehead.

The hollow black muzzle of the weapon bore directly between my eyes. Simultaneously, my free hand shot out to clamp tight around his exposed throat, just as his finger curled tight on the trigger. His hissing snarl of *“Die, cursed thern”* was half-choked off in his own windpipe under my bruising grip. The hammer fell with a useless, sharp click on an empty chamber. Before he could adjust to pull the trigger again, I hauled him so far over the rail that he was forced to drop his gun and cling white-knuckled to the wooden slats with both hands. My hold on his throat strangled every potential cry for help, and we struggled in grim, total silence: he fought to tear free and drag himself back onto the deck, I fought to yank him over the edge to his quiet death.

His face purpled to a livid hue, eyes bulging almost out of their sockets. He knew he was seconds from death if he didn’t tear my steely fingers off his throat. With one last, heaving effort, he threw his weight hard back onto the deck, releasing his death-grip on the rail to scrabble frantically at my wrists to pry me loose. That single split second of slack was all I waited for. With one mighty downward surge, I swept him clean off the deck’s surface. His falling weight nearly tore my one remaining hand loose from the anchor chain, yanking me half over the rail to plunge with him into the dark waters below.

I didn’t let go of him, though. I knew even one sharp scream tearing from his throat as he plummeted would rouse every one of his crewmates above to hunt me down before I could blink. I held fast, choking him, never easing the pressure, even as his flailing, spasming limbs dragged me lower and lower down the length of the rusted chain. Bit by bit his convulsions slowed, until they stopped entirely. Only then did I release my grip, and his body vanished in an instant into the inky, unlit shadows thousands of feet beneath us.

I hauled myself back up the chain to the cruiser’s rail, and this time I managed to hoist my eyes level with the deck to scan the situation fully. The smaller, nearer moon had already dipped below the horizon, but the soft, brilliant glow of the further satellite bathed the entire deck in silver, throwing the sprawled forms of six or eight sleeping black pirates into sharp, clear relief. Huddled against the base of the rapid-fire gun, bound tight with heavy silk cords, was a young white girl. Her eyes were wide, brimming with horrified anticipation, and locked directly on my face the second I peeked over the rail. Unutterable, sharp relief flooded her features the instant her gaze fell on the glowing mystic jewel set in the centre of the stolen thern headpiece I still wore. She did not speak, only flicked her eyes sideways to warn me of the sleeping men all around her.

I stepped noiselessly over the rail onto the deck, and she nodded me over to her. When I bent low, she breathed the words right against my ear. “Free me. I can help you. You’ll need every hand you can get when they wake.”
“Half of them will wake already in the bosom of Korus before they even know what’s happening,” I murmured, smiling. She caught my meaning at once, and the cruel, sharp curve of her answering smile made my skin go cold. You don’t recoil from cruelty on a hideous, twisted face — but when that same savagery shows on the features of a goddess, whose perfectly carved lineaments were made for softness and beauty, the contrast is breathtaking, appalling.

I cut her bonds in two quick swipes of my sword. “Give me a revolver,” she whispered. “I’ll pick off the ones your blade doesn’t get fast enough.” I handed her one of the pirates’ discarded weapons, then turned to the grim, necessary work ahead of me. This was no time for fine scruples, no time for chivalry these sadistic demons would never understand or repay.

I crept up on the nearest sleeper. He was half over the rail, halfway to Korus before he even stirred. His shriek of shock as he woke to the fall came faint, thin, drifting up from the black depths below. The second pirate woke the exact moment my hand touched his shoulder, and though I managed to hurl him over the edge before he could lunge, his wild cry of alarm wrenched all five of his remaining crewmates to their feet.

Phaidor’s revolver barked in sharp, staccato bursts before they could fully draw their blades, and one of them dropped back to the deck, dead, never to rise again. The rest charged at me with drawn long-swords, and I saw Phaidor hang back, not firing for fear of hitting me, moving with a silent, catlike grace to circle around to their unguarded flank. Then they were on me, and for minutes I fought in some of the hottest, closest quarters combat I’d ever experienced on any world. There was no room to dodge, no room to manoeuvre — only stand your ground, parry, strike, and take what came back. At first I took more than I gave, but soon I slipped under one pirate’s guard and sent him crumpling to the deck. The rest redoubled their attacks, the clang of steel on steel ringing loud enough to carry for miles across the silent Barsoom night, sparks flying off the blades to die instantly in the thin, cold air, until a dull, sickening crack echoed out as my sword bit clean through a man’s shoulder bone.

Three foes still faced me, but Phaidor was almost in position to take at least one of them out with a clean shot. Then everything happened so fast, I still can barely untangle the sequence even now. The three charged at me all at once, clearly intending to force me back the three short steps that would send me tumbling over the rail into the void. At the exact same second, Phaidor fired, my sword arm moved twice: one man dropped with a bullet through his brain, a second’s sword clattered across the deck and sailed over the edge into the Lost Sea as I disarmed him, and the third went down with my blade buried hilt-deep in his chest, three feet of steel protruding out his back — his falling weight wrenched the sword right out of my grasp.

I was completely unarmed now, facing my only remaining foe, who had no weapon left either, his sword lost thousands of feet below in the dark sea. He smiled, baring his gleaming white teeth, clearly pleased to find he could rush me bare-handed. The huge, rippling muscles rolling under his glossy black skin clearly convinced him I would be easy prey, not worth the effort of drawing the dagger from his harness. I let him get almost within arm’s reach, ducked under his outstretched arms, sidestepped sharp to the right, pivoted on my left toe, and swung a brutal right hook straight to his jaw. He dropped like a felled ox, out cold before his body hit the deck planks.

A low, silvery laugh rang out behind me. “You are no thern,” the sweet, clear voice of my companion said, “not with that golden hair and Sator Throg’s harness. No man alive on all Barsoom ever fought like that before. Who are you?”
“I am John Carter, Prince of the House of Tardos Mors, Jeddak of Helium,” I told her. “And whom have I had the honour of serving tonight?”

She hesitated a long moment before answering, staring at me intently, as if she was trying to read every corner of my soul, measure my code of honour and my standard of chivalry with that long, searching gaze. Apparently she liked what she saw. “I am Phaidor, daughter of Matai Shang, Holy Hekkador of the Holy Therns — Father of Therns, Master of Life and Death across all Barsoom, Brother of Issus, Prince of Life Eternal.”

It was right then I noticed the black pirate I’d knocked out was starting to stir, consciousness seeping back. I crossed over to him, stripped off his harness, bound his hands tight behind his back, tied his ankles together, and lashed him to the heavy gun carriage to make sure he couldn’t move.
“Why not the simpler way?” Phaidor asked, tilting her head.
“What simpler way?” I said, confused.
She shrugged her lovely shoulders, made a small flicking gesture with her hands, the motion of throwing something heavy over the side of the ship.
“I am no murderer,” I said. “I only kill in self-defence.” She stared at me narrowly, puckered her perfect, fine brows, and shook her head. She could not understand. Even my own Dejah Thoris had once failed to see the logic of what she’d called my foolish, dangerous leniency to enemies. On Barsoom, no quarter is ever asked, no quarter ever given — every dead man leaves a little more of the dying planet’s dwindling, precious resources for the survivors to split between them. But there was a sharp, strange difference here: Phaidor wasn’t regretting the death of a living soul, she was regretting the thrill of seeing it thrown over the rail.

The pirate had fully woken now, and lay still, watching us intently. He was strikingly handsome, clean-limbed, powerful, his features so perfectly chiselled Adonis himself might have envied their symmetry. The unguided cruiser had been drifting slow across the death valley, but now I stepped to the helm to set a proper course. I could only guess roughly where the Valley Dor lay: far south of the equator, the constellations made that clear, but without the precision charts and delicate instruments I’d used as a Heliumite Navy officer, I couldn’t pin down our exact location. A northerly course would take us straight back towards the settled, populated parts of the planet, so I turned the cruiser’s nose north at once.

The ship swung gracefully under my hand, I pressed the button that activated the repulsive rays, and we soared high, arcing out into open sky. I cranked the speed lever all the way to its final notch, and we raced north, climbing higher and higher above that terrible valley of the dead. Far below us, the flash of gunfire winked across the Thern borderlands, proof of the brutal battle still raging on that cruel frontier, but no sound of it ever reached us. At that high altitude, the rarefied Barsoom air was too thin to carry sound waves, they dissipated long before they could climb up to where we flew.

The air grew bitterly cold. Breathing became a struggle. Phaidor and the bound pirate both stared at me without speaking, until she said, evenly, as casually as if warning me of rain, “Unconsciousness comes very fast at this height. If you are not trying to kill all three of us, you had better drop altitude, right now.”
I brought the cruiser plummeting downwards fast, not a second too soon. Phaidor had already fainted. The pirate was fully unconscious, and I only stayed awake myself by sheer force of will — the one carrying all the weight of responsibility always hangs on the longest. We levelled off low, flying above the foothills of the Otz range, the air warm enough again, thick enough to fill our starved lungs. The pirate woke first, and a moment later Phaidor’s eyes fluttered open.
“That was a close call,” she said.
“It taught me two things,” I answered.
“What are they?”
“That even Phaidor, daughter of the Master of Life and Death, can be mortal,” I said, smiling.
“Immortality only belongs to the therns, granted to us by Issus,” she replied. “So I cannot truly die.” I caught a fleeting, knowing grin flicker across the bound pirate’s face when she said that. I did not understand the meaning of that smirk then — later, both her and I would learn the truth in the most horrible way imaginable.
“The other thing I learned,” I went on, “is that our dusky friend here is not a native of the nearer moon Thuria. He would have frozen to solid ice thousands of feet above the surface. If we’d kept climbing the five thousand miles all the way to Thuria, he’d be nothing but a frozen statue of a man by now.”
Phaidor stared at the pirate, shocked. “If you are not from Thuria, where are you from?” she demanded. He shrugged, turned his eyes away, and refused to answer. She stamped her small foot, imperious. “The daughter of Matai Shang does not let queries go unanswered. You, lesser breed, should feel honoured a member of the holy race born to eternal life would even deign to look at you.”
The pirate smiled that same wicked, knowing smile. “I am Xodar, Dator of the First Born of Barsoom. I give commands, I do not take them,” he said. Then he turned to me. “What do you intend to do with me?”
“I am taking both of you back to Helium,” I said. “No harm will come to you. The red men of Helium are a kind, magnanimous people — and when we make the truth known, no more pilgrims will ever set foot on the banks of the river Iss. The absurd myth they have worshipped for ages will shatter into a thousand pieces.”
“You are from Helium?” he asked.
“I am a Prince of Tardos Mors’ house,” I told him, “but I am not native to Barsoom. I come from a different world.”
Xodar studied me for long moments. “I can well believe you are not from Barsoom,” he said at last. “No man born on this world could beat eight of the First Born single-handed. But how is it you wear the golden hair and jewelled circlet of a Holy Thern?” He spat the word holy with sharp, obvious irony.
“I had forgotten I was even wearing it,” I laughed. “It’s plunder, taken from an enemy.” I swept my hand over my head and pulled the thern disguise free, revealing my own close-cropped black human hair. His eyes went wide in shock, he had clearly expected to see the bald, hairless pate of a true thern.
“You truly are from another world,” he said, a faint touch of awe in his voice. “With skin the colour of a thern, hair the colour of a First Born, and the strength of a dozen Dators, there is no shame even for Xodar to admit you defeated me. A thing I would never, ever do if you were born on Barsoom.”
“You are running far ahead of me,” I cut in. “Your name is Xodar, but who are the First Born? What is a Dator? And why could you never acknowledge defeat to a Barsoomian native?”
“The First Born are the oldest race on Barsoom, the line of black men I was born to lead as Dator — what the lesser red races would call a prince,” he explained. “Our lineage traces unbroken straight back to the Tree of Life that grew in the centre of the Valley Dor twenty-three million years ago. For countless ages, the fruit of that tree evolved, shifting slowly from pure plant life to a hybrid of plant and animal. At first, the fruit only had independent muscular movement, the stem still fixed to the parent tree. Later, the fruit grew a brain, so they hung from their long stems, thinking, moving, as individual beings.
“With the first capacity for perception came the ability to compare those perceptions, make judgements — and that is how reason was born on Barsoom. Ages passed, dozens of forms of life sprouted and died on the Tree of Life, until the tree sprouted the small, tiny plant men we now see grown to giant size roaming the Valley Dor, still hanging from the tree branches by stems growing out of the tops of their heads. The buds that grew those plant men were large, foot-wide nut-like structures, split down the middle by double partitions into four separate sections. One section held a plant man, the next a sixteen-legged worm, the third the ancestor of the great white apes, and the fourth the very first original black man of Barsoom.

Curious, he broke open the rest of the unhatched shells — and that is how Barsoom first became populated.
“The pure bloodline of that first black man has never mixed with any other creature, in the race I belong to. Every other form of life on Barsoom — the red men, the white apes, the plant men, the therns — all of them descend from that sixteen-legged worm, that first ape, and the early renegade black men. Their brains are no bigger than the tip of your smallest finger, their actions are ruled by pure instinct, no reason. He was facing the stern of the cruiser as he spoke, and for one instant, that dark gaze locked on something behind me, a flash of sharp triumph brightening his eyes.

The second I caught that deliberate, flicker of Xodar’s eyes darting upward, the whole ruse snapped into sharp focus. The black pirate had not been spinning that wild, meandering tale for entertainment. For miles he’d sensed his own people’s rescue creeping up behind us, stringing me along to keep my gaze fixed on his face rather than the sky. I was no novice to Barsoomian aerial combat, my reflexes honed as sharp on the flying skiffs of Mars as they had been on the cavalry charges of Earth’s Civil War. At that exact instant, the red dawn of Barsoom crested the horizon, spilling blood light over the battleship’s steel stern, revealing a hundred grim, ebony faces peering over the rails down at us. We hung there for barely a full second before the metal gave way, but that one second was all the black warriors needed. Phaidor was bound next, then the two of us were lashed together with barely three feet of slack between our linked wrists. Our skiff was towed alongside the crippled battleship, and we were hauled up onto its main deck.

A thousand First Born warriors crewed the leviathan, their dark bodies packed tight along the rails as far as discipline would permit, all shoving for a glimpse of their captives. My own thern-pale skin and close cropped black hair drew equal curiosity, and when Xodar told his fellow nobles how I’d fought off a dozen of their best men, and that the harness and metal medallions I wore had been stripped off a dead thern guard, they thronged around me with questions. The First Born were a uniformly handsome, powerfully built people, their officers’ harnesses crusted so thick with gold, platinum, and precious gems that no trace of the fine red leather beneath was visible. The captain’s harness was a single solid sheet of cut diamonds, blazing against his ebony skin like a fallen star. Phaidor and I were dragged below decks, thrown into a tiny, windowless compartment with only one small porthole for light. The door slammed shut, the heavy iron bars clanking home behind our escort. We could hear the crew hammering at the broken propellers overhead, and through the porthole I watched the battleship drift lazily south. Neither of us spoke for a long while. My own mind raced to Tars Tarkas and Thuvia, still loose somewhere on the banks of the River Iss. Even if they eluded pursuit, the fugitives from the Valley Dor were marked for a swift, brutal death if captured by any red or green Barsoomian party. I thought of Dejah Thoris, of Tardos Mors, of the thousands of warriors who would follow me straight through the Gates of Iss itself if I could only reach Helium and expose the therns’ cruel deception. But right then, the odds of escaping the First Born felt thinner than the rarefied air at the top of Mars’ highest peaks.

I looked over at Phaidor, and caught her staring at me, a soft, unnameable expression on her face I had never seen before. When she realized I’d caught her, her white lids fluttered down, a faint rose flush tingeing her pale thern cheeks.
“Do you find the study of the lower orders so fascinating?” I laughed.
She looked up, a nervous, relieved little huff of laughter escaping her. “Oh, very much so. Especially when they have such excellent profiles.”

It was my turn to flush, but I only laughed harder, drawn to her bright wit even on the edge of certain death. She asked if I knew where we were bound, and I joked that we were heading for the supposed eternal paradise the therns promised all pilgrims of the Iss. She shuddered, and said her fate would be far worse. No thern princess stolen by the black pirates in all their centuries of raiding had ever returned to tell their tale.
“Is that not just retribution?” I challenged her. “Do the therns not do the same to all those poor pilgrims who volunteer to drift down the River of Mystery? Thuvia was held as your plaything, your slave, for fifteen years. Why should you not suffer the same cruelty you mete out to others?”
Phaidor stared at me, bewildered. “We therns are a holy race. It is an honor for a lesser creature to serve us. If we did not save some of those fools drifting down the Iss, every last one of them would be torn apart by plant men and white apes before they reached the Dor.” When I pressed her about the lie the therns had spread to the outer world, convincing every red man that paradise waited past the Iss, she shrugged as if it was the most obvious truth in the world. “All life on Barsoom exists only to serve the therns. Why else would we waste our time on labor?”
The words curdled in my throat. “It is true then that you eat human flesh?”
She looked at me with pity for my ignorance. “Lesser humans are the beasts that feed the gods. You eat the flesh of animals, do you not? The Holy Therns are the gods of Barsoom.”
Disgust coiled hot in my chest, but she leaned in, her cheeks flushing again. “You are an unbeliever now. But if we escape the pirates, and make it back to my father Matai Shang’s temple, we will find a way to convince you of the error of your ways. We can find a place for you among us. You can be ours.”
Her eyes dropped to the floor, and I was slow to parse what she meant—Dejah Thoris had always teased me that I was a complete simpleton when it came to soft emotions. I told her plainly that if I was a thern, the very first thing I would do was station armed guards at the mouth of the Iss, and escort every deluded pilgrim back to the outer world, before wiping out all the plant men and white apes of the Dor.
Phaidor went deathly white, horror written across her face. “Do not speak such sacrilege! Not even I would be able to save you from the tortures they would inflict if they heard you say such things.”

The cell door swung open then, and Xodar stepped inside, smiling, his dark face kind for once, stripped of its usual warrior’s grimness. “There is no use keeping you locked away. You cannot possibly escape. I will cut your bonds. Come up on deck. You will see no sight any First Born slave has ever laid eyes on: the subterranean entrance to our holy land, the real heaven of Barsoom.” He glanced at Phaidor, his grin sharpening. “You will get to see the Temple of Issus, too. Perhaps Issus will favor you enough to make you her personal handmaiden.”
Phaidor drew herself up, haughty again. “Blasphemy! Issus would wipe your entire accursed breed from the face of Mars if you ever dared step foot near her temple!”
Xodar laughed, ugly and sharp. “You have far more to learn, little thern. And you will not like the lessons.”

When we stepped onto the deck, I froze. As far as the eye could see stretched nothing but endless fields of ice and snow. We were above the south polar cap of Mars—the only spot on the entire planet where frozen water ever existed. No sign of life moved across the frozen expanse.
Xodar walked beside me as I leaned on the rail, pointing south. “We are heading straight for the Otz Valley. A great sunken bowl thousands of feet below the surrounding ice, ringed by the Otz Mountains that circle the inner Valley Dor. At its very centre lies the Lost Sea of Korus, and on its shores, the Golden Temple of Issus. That is our destination.” I realized then why only one soul in all the ages had ever escaped the Valley Dor. No one could cross that wind-scoured frozen wasteland on foot and live.
We passed the deep, gashed canyon of the River Iss, its waters flowing deep below the ice, then the neutral strip of valley between the ice wall and the mountains, dotted with tiny villages. Xodar explained those were the lost souls: pilgrims who’d climbed out of the Iss canyon, escaped thern slaves, trapped forever with nowhere to run, left alone by both the therns and the First Born to war amongst themselves in their tiny, isolated settlements.
Ahead of us, rising out of the ice, was a flat-topped, truncated cone of black basalt. The battleship slowed to a hover directly over its peak, and I gaped down at the mile-wide circular well bored straight through the rock, its bottom lost in inky, endless darkness. The ship began to sink, slow and steady, down into the pit. For half an hour we fell, until the battleship’s lights pierced the dark, and suddenly we broke through into the vast domed ceiling of an entire subterranean world.
A soft blue phosphorescence lit the scene below, illuminating a buried sea that stretched to the far edges of the dome, thousands of First Born warships bobbing on its gentle swells. Xodar laughed when he saw my shock. “This sea is larger than Korus. We pump the excess water back up through the rock into the northern reservoirs the red men draw their irrigation from. For eons they called it a miracle.” The secret of the Barsoomian water “miracle” clicked into place at last.
We passed tiny, heavily guarded islets dotted with roofless prison compounds, before pulling up to a larger island where a massive oval, roofless building sat on the shore. “You will meet Issus soon,” Xodar said to Phaidor, his grin turning cruel. “Her handmaidens never serve longer than one year.” The truth of what that meant hung unspoken in the air, and Phaidor clung tight to my arm, no longer the arrogant, holy thern princess, just a terrified young girl in the hands of merciless enemies.
We walked to the edge of the long, water-filled tank at the centre of the building, and saw a strange black craft floating in its centre. Xodar shouted a command, a hatch popped open on the vessel’s deck, and we climbed aboard. The hatches sealed shut, the engines purred to life, and the craft began to sink below the surface of the water. Phaidor’s fingers dug hard into my arm, every Martian’s ancient terror of deep water rising to the surface.
“Save me,” she whispered, pressing herself against my side. “Anything the Holy Therns can give you, it is yours. Phaidor—” she hesitated, her voice so soft I could barely hear it over the gurgle of water past the portholes, “Phaidor herself is yours.” She threw both her arms around my neck, and dragged my face down to hers.

The cry of love torn from Phaidor by her fright struck straight through me, and left me tangled in a mess of sharp, awkward guilt. I’d never meant to lead her on, never so much as hinted I cared for her that way, and yet some careless word or small, kind gesture I’d long forgotten had convinced her I returned her affection.

I was never the sort of man to moon over scented gloves too small for my hand, or tuck a dead flower that stank worse than rotting Martian cabbage in my tunic pocket. My days were for fighting, for drilling, for the rough, honest work of a warrior—all the soft, tangled games of romance had never come naturally to me. I stood there frozen, far more afraid of meeting that beautiful girl’s eyes to tell her the truth than I ever was to face a charging horde of green Tharks on the dead sea bottoms. But there was no way out, so I did the clumsy best I could.

I unclasped her arms from around my neck gently, held her hands in mine, and told her the whole, unvarnished truth of Dejah Thoris. That across two entire worlds, across a hundred long years of life and battle, I had only ever loved one woman, and I would never love another.

The words did not go over well.

She sprang to her feet like a snarling tigress, chest heaving, her perfect face twisted into a mask of vicious, unholy rage. Her eyes blazed bright enough to burn. “Dog!” she hissed. “Blasphemer! Do you imagine Phaidor, daughter of Matai Shang, begs for a man’s affection? I command it. What do I care for your trivial, outer-world fancy for that silly little Heliumite you chose in some dead old life?

“I honored you with my love, and you spat it back in my face. Ten thousand deaths too foul to name could never atone for that insult. That thing you call Dejah Thoris will die the cruelest of them all. You signed her death warrant just now.

“And you? You’ll be the lowest, most abused slave in the service of the goddess you dared to mock. Tortures and humiliations will be heaped on you until you grovel in the dirt begging for the tiny mercy of death. And when at last I grant your prayer, I’ll stand on the high balconies of the Golden Cliffs and laugh as the great white apes tear you limb from limb.”

She had the whole revenge plotted out, every last gory detail. I could scarce believe that a creature so divinely beautiful could be so steeped in venom. It struck me, though, that she’d forgotten one small, inconvenient fact that put a crimp in her plans, so I nodded at the nearest port-hole, no intent to taunt her, just to help her adjust her little scheme to the reality of where we were.

She’d been so caught up in her rage she’d entirely forgotten we were still in the sinking submarine. One glance at the dark, churning, crushing waters outside the glass sent her crumpling to a low bench, her face buried in her arms, sobbing like a lost, miserable little girl rather than the proud, untouchable daughter of the Thern’s ruling god.

Down we sank, deeper and deeper, until the thick, pressure-forged glass of the port-holes grew warm against my palms, heated by the superheated water far beneath Barsoom’s outer crust. We must have been miles below the surface of the planet. At last our downward motion stopped, the propellers at the stern whirred to life, and we shot forward at high speed. It was black as pitch down there, but the light spilling from our port-holes and the blazing beam of the submarine’s nose searchlight showed we were racing through a narrow, tube-like tunnel hewn straight out of solid red volcanic rock.

A few minutes later the propellers fell silent. We drifted to a full stop, then shot upward fast toward the surface. The light outside grew brighter by the second, until we halted entirely. Xodar stepped through the cabin hatch with a squad of his black guards. “Come,” he ordered, and we followed him out.

We stood in a small, domed subterranean vault, a pool of still water at its center holding our submarine, only its matte black hull peeking above the surface. A flat stone platform ringed the pool, and the cave walls rose straight up a dozen feet before curving over into a low, vaulted roof. Dim, glowing archways lined the ledge, leading off into unlit tunnels. Our guards herded us to one of these, and after a short walk we stopped before a steel elevator cage at the bottom of a shaft that stretched up so far we could not see its top.

The car was the same magnetic elevator common across Barsoom, powered by giant electromagnets strung at the top of the shaft, their pull regulated by a simple electrical dial to control speed. With Mars’ low gravity offering barely any resistance to the magnet’s force, these lifts shot upward so fast the ride left your stomach lurching. The door had barely clicked shut behind us before we were slowing to a stop at the landing at the top of the shaft.

When we stepped out of the small stone terminal building, we found ourselves in a fairyland of splendor no words from Earth could ever properly describe. You could talk all you want about crimson Martian grass, ivory-trunked trees draped in vivid purple blooms, walkways paved with crushed rubies and emeralds and turquoise and raw diamonds, a temple of burnished gold carved over every inch with impossibly fine filigree. But where are the words to name the colors that no human eye on Earth has ever seen? Where is the imagination that can picture the scintillating glow of unheard-of light rays spilling from the thousands of strange, nameless gemstones native to Barsoom? Even my eyes, long used to the barbaric opulence of a Jeddak of Helium’s court, went wide at the sight. Phaidor stared, lips parted. “The Temple of Issus,” she whispered to herself. Xodar watched us with his grim half-smile, half amusement, half gloating over the shock we felt.

The gardens swarmed with black First Born men and women draped in rich, brilliant silks, waited on hand and foot by red and white women stolen as princesses from the outer world and Thern temples, reduced to nothing but slaves. We marched past them all toward the great gilded temple doors, where a cordon of armed black guards halted us. Xodar spoke a few quiet words to their officer, the two of them vanishing inside the temple for a long stretch before they returned, announcing Issus wished to see Phaidor, daughter of Matai Shang, and the strange man from another world who had once been a Prince of Helium.

We moved through endless corridors and great, soaring halls of impossible beauty, until we stopped in a vast antechamber at the very heart of the temple. One of our guards walked to a massive carved door at the far end, gave a secret signal, and a richly robed courtier stepped out. We were ordered to drop to our hands and knees, our backs turned to the chamber beyond. The doors swung open. We were warned that turning our heads even a fraction would mean instant death, and commanded to crawl backwards into Issus’s presence.

It was the most humiliating moment of my whole life. Only the thought of Dejah Thoris, the tiny spark of hope that I might still find her alive, stopped me from leaping to my feet, drawing my sword, and charging into that room to die like a warrior, facing my enemies with their blood soaking my own before I fell.

We crawled that disgusting, humiliating way for two hundred feet before our escort halted us. “Let them rise,” a thin, wavering old voice called out from behind us, a voice that had spent thousands of years barking orders that no one dared disobey. “Rise,” our guard echoed, “but never turn your face toward Issus.”

“The woman pleases me,” the thin voice said after a long, quiet pause. “She will serve me for her allotted time. The man may be sent to the Isle of Shador, on the northern shore of the Sea of Omean. Let the woman turn and look upon Issus. All those of lower rank who gaze upon my radiant, holy face may live no longer than one year afterward.”

I watched Phaidor out of the corner of my eye. She blanched so pale her skin looked almost white as bleached ivory. Slow as if pulled by an invisible, unbreakable string, she turned, her bare arm brushing mine as she faced the supposed goddess of life eternal. I could not see her face, but the violent shudder that wracked her body traveled straight through the arm pressed to mine, hard enough to feel. “That must be some blinding, unearthly loveliness,” I thought, “to shake a woman as beautiful as Phaidor that badly.”

“Leave the woman here. Take the man away. Go,” Issus ordered. The guard’s heavy hand fell on my shoulder, and I dropped back to my hands and knees to crawl back out of the audience chamber. It was my first audience with a “living goddess,” and I left entirely unimpressed—aside from the deep embarrassment of scrambling around on my knees like a whipped cur.

Once outside the doors they let me stand, and Xodar fell into step beside me as we walked slowly back toward the gardens. “You spared my life when you could have killed me easily,” he said after a long silence, “and I would help you if I can. I can make your bondage far easier to bear, but your fate is sealed. You will never go back to the outer world.”

“What fate?” I asked.

“It all depends on Issus. As long as she never calls you to look on her face, you might live for years, with me arranging the softest possible posts for you. But she often calls in strong, skilled fighters like you for her monthly temple rites. Men pitted against men, or against great beasts, for her amusement—and to stock her larder.”

“She eats human flesh?” I asked. I felt no shock. After what I’d seen of the Holy Therns, nothing in their hidden “heaven” could surprise me. Ages of brutal fanaticism and absolute power had stripped every last trace of mercy and humanity from the First Born. They saw all the rest of Barsoom’s inhabitants the same way we see cattle on Earth: meat, no more. So why wouldn’t they eat their flesh?

“Only the flesh of the best-bred Therns and red Barsoomians is fit for her. The rest go to our tables. The beasts go to the slaves. She has other rare delicacies, too.”

I did not grasp how deep their depravity ran then, I thought cannibalism was the worst they could possibly do. I had no idea how far absolute, unchallenged power could twist a creature to cruelty.

We were almost at the gardens when an officer ran up behind us, breathless. “Issus demands to see the man again,” he said. “The girl told her he is of unmatched strength and beauty, that he killed seven of the First Born all alone, and took Dator Xodar captive with his bare hands, binding him with his own harness.”

Xodar’s jaw tightened. He clearly hated that his humiliating defeat had reached Issus’s ears. Without a word, we followed the officer back to the closed doors of the audience chamber, went through the whole crawling ritual again, and Issus bade me stand. The silence hung thick as a tomb for minutes, while that ancient goddess stared at me. At last that thin, wavering voice droned out the same line that had sealed the fate of thousands of victims before me. “Let the man turn and look upon Issus, knowing that those of lower order who gaze upon the holy vision of my radiant face survive no longer than a single year.”

I turned, ready for the sight of divine, unearthly glory that could burn a mortal’s eyes. What I saw was a line of armed guards standing between me and a carved sorapus wood dais. Perched on that throne was a shriveled old black woman. Not a single hair grew on her wrinkled, bald skull. Save for two sharp, yellow fangs, she had no teeth left at all. Her eyes burned deep in sunken sockets flanked by a thin, hawk-like nose. Her face was crisscrossed with a million deep, cracked wrinkles, her body as emaciated and repulsive as her face. Stick-thin arms and legs jutted from a torso that was little more than a distended, sagging belly. That was the “holy, radiant divine beauty” everyone worshipped. Phaidor stood among the group of trembling female slaves at her side, white as a sheet.

“Is this the man who killed seven of the First Born, and bound Dator Xodar with his bare hands?” Issus squealed. “Most glorious divine vision, it is,” the officer at my side answered. “Bring Dator Xodar to me,” she ordered. They dragged Xodar into the room. She glared at him, malice burning in her hideous little eyes. “And you call yourself a Dator of the Immortal Race?” she shrieked. “For the shame you have brought on us, you are stripped of all rank. You are no longer a Dator. You are the lowest of all slaves, to fetch and carry for the garden slaves. Strip his harness from him. Cowards and prisoners wear no fine trappings.”

Xodar stood rigid and tall, not a single muscle twitching, as the guards tore his gorgeous ceremonial trappings off him. “Begone!” she screamed. “And you will not serve in the gardens. You will go to the Isle of Shador, to be the personal slave of the man who defeated you. Fetch and carry for him for the rest of your days. Get out of my sight.” Xodar walked out of the chamber, head held high, his pride unbroken.

Issus turned and made to leave through a side exit. Over her shoulder she called to me: “You will be sent to Shador for now. Later, Issus will watch you fight. Go.” She vanished, her whole retinue trailing after her. Only Phaidor lagged behind. As I turned to follow my guards back toward the gardens, she ran after me.

“Don’t leave me here,” she begged, breathless, half incoherent. “Forgive what I said. I did not mean it. Take me with you to Shador. Let me share your prison. We both looked on Issus—we both only have a year left to live. We can spend that whole year together, happy, while we still can. You never understood. We Therns do not marry the way outer world red men do. We could have loved each other forever.”

“Phaidor,” I said, gentle as I could, “you cannot blame me for not understanding your ways, when you do not understand mine. I do not wish to hurt you, but what you ask can never be. While I draw breath, my heart belongs only to Dejah Thoris, Princess of Helium. When I die, my heart will stop beating, and I know nothing of what comes after. Not even Matai Shang or the great Issus know any more than that.”

Phaidor stared at me for a long moment. No rage burned in her eyes now, only quiet, hopeless grief. “I do not understand,” she whispered, and turned, walking slowly back through the door where Issus’s retinue had vanished, until she was gone from my sight.

The temple guard’s sandaled feet scuffed the ruby dust of the outer gardens as they escorted me past terraces overgrown with the blood-red, thorn-studded blooms sacred to Issus, and there I found Xodar ringed by a baying horde of First Born nobles. The once-mighty Dator, stripped of his rank regalia, stood tall and unflinching as men with skins polished like obsidian slapped his cheeks, and women draped in iridescent silk spat at his bare feet, cursing him for a traitor to their eternal goddess.

The second they spotted me, their rage snapped toward me like a hunting calot’s jaws.
“Ah!” a burly black noble roared, baring his fangs. “This is the beast that bested Xodar bare-handed? Let us see if his luck holds against a true warrior of the First Born.”
The tallest of the women, her face painted with swirling gold patterns, laughed and called out, “Make him bind Thurid! Thurid is no fallen outcast cur—let the great Dator show this alien dog what it means to face a man who still walks in Issus’s favor!”
The cry of *Thurid! Thurid!* rose up from a dozen throats, and a new voice boomed over the din: “What is the meaning of this rabble?”

I turned to see him: a colossus of a black warrior, his harness crusted with rubies and platinum filigree, his sheathed long-sword heavy enough to cleave a green Martian war thoat in two, moving with the arrogant, unchallenged swagger of a man who had never known defeat. This was Thurid, highest ranked of all Issus’s fighting Dators.
Dozens of overlapping voices explained the game at once, and Thurid’s eyes slitted to narrow, venomous black shards as he rounded on Xodar. “Calot!” he hissed. “I always knew you carried the heart of a cowardly sorak in your rotting chest. For years you outmaneuvered me in Issus’s secret councils, but now your scabby true nature is bared to all. I spit on you, I spurn you with my foot—”

His boot swung hard for Xodar’s ribs, a deliberate, degrading kick meant to send the once-noble warrior sprawling in the dust. My blood had been simmering for minutes at the cowardly spectacle of a crowd tearing down a man who had once been their equal, and I saw red through a haze of righteous anger. I do not abide unfair persecution, and I acted before thought could catch up. My own foot snapped out, catching Thurid dead on the unprotected shin with a crack that made him howl and stagger, his kick flying wide of Xodar.

For a heartbeat the whole garden went dead silent. Then Thurid roared with rage and launched himself at my throat, exactly as Xodar had done on the deck of the First Born cruiser only a few days prior. The outcome was identical: I ducked under his flailing arms, drove a rock-hard right fist into the side of his jaw, and the 300-pound warrior spun like a child’s top, his knees buckling, crumpling unconscious at my feet in a cloud of ruby dust.

The crowd stared, gape-mouthed, first at the motionless Dator, then at me like they could not believe their eyes. “You asked me to bind Thurid,” I called out, loud enough for every last one of them to hear. “Behold!” I knelt, tore his own harness free, and bound his wrists and ankles tight with the leather straps. “Treat him exactly as you treated Xodar. Drag him before Issus, bound in his own regalia, so she can see there is a man here greater than her pet champions. I am John Carter, citizen of two worlds, Captain of Virginia, Prince of the House of Tardos Mors, Jeddak of Helium. Tell your goddess that I will meet any of her flower of warriors, naked-handed or armed with short-sword or long-sword, and beat them.”

The woman who had mocked me earlier whispered, awestruck, “Who are you?”
Before I could answer, the guard who had been tasked with escorting me back to the island stepped forward. “My orders are absolute,” he said, though there was no bite left in his tone, no trace of the contempt he had shown a prisoner only an hour before. “No more delays. Xodar—come with him.” For the rest of the march back to the great shaft that led down to the Sea of Omean, he walked a respectful pace behind me, his short-sword drawn, as if I was a Jeddak rather than a condemned slave.

Our journey back was uneventful. We descended the vast, echoing shaft in the same suspended car that had carried us up to Issus’s surface gardens, boarded the waiting submarine, and dove deep through dark, frigid water to traverse the tunnel that connected the outer world to the sunken sea’s vast cavern. We surfaced in the glass-smooth pool on the far side, then boarded a small, fast cruiser that carried us across miles of windless, phosphorescent green water to the Isle of Shador.

The island was nothing but bare gray rock, topped by a squat, windowless stone prison, guarded by half a dozen armed First Born. There was no ceremony to our incarceration. A guard dragged open the heavy oak door with a rusted iron key, we stepped inside, the door slammed shut behind us, and the lock grated with a sound that sent a wave of the same crushing hopelessness I had felt in the Therns’ Chamber of Mystery rolling over me. Back then Tars Tarkas had been at my side. Now I had no ally I could trust, no way to send word to my friends: neither the great Thark warrior nor Thuvia of Ptarth could possibly guess that I was trapped in a buried sea no surface dweller on Barsoom even believed existed. Even if they could, the full might of the First Born’s navy would tear any rescue fleet to shreds before it ever reached the cavern mouth. My situation looked impossible. But I straightened my shoulders, shoved the brooding despair away, and turned to survey my surroundings.

Xodar sat on a low stone bench, his head bowed, silent since Issus had stripped him of his rank. The prison was roofless, the walls rising thirty feet high, polished so smooth no hand or foot could grip them to climb. Halfway up, two small slits crisscrossed with iron bars let in faint traces of the cavern’s ubiquitous glow. The building was divided into half a dozen separate cells by 20-foot-high stone partitions, two of the connecting doors standing ajar. I wandered through the empty cells one by one, until in the furthest room I found a young red Martian boy asleep on the stone bench, his face relaxed, his limbs lean and graceful, his skin a pale, sun-kissed gold-red. There was something hauntingly familiar about his features, though I could not place his name. I did not wake him—sleep is the most precious boon a prisoner can have, and I had seen men turn feral over losing even a few minutes of it.

When I returned to our cell, Xodar had not moved. “Moping will do you no good,” I told him, clapping him on the shoulder. “There’s no shame in losing to John Carter. You saw me drop Thurid with one punch. You saw me kill three of your men on the cruiser deck. Why waste the rest of your days rotting here when two great fighters like us can win our freedom?”

He looked up at me, horrified. “You do not know what you say. Issus is omnipotent. She is omniscient. She hears every word you speak, reads every thought in your head. To even dream of defying her is sacrilege. She will strike you dead any second now, writhing in agony.”

I laughed. “Rot, Xodar. The red men of Barsoom have had the power to read thoughts for centuries. They also know how to shield their minds so no one can pry into them. I learned that trick decades ago. Your Issus cannot read my thoughts—she could not even read yours if you did not will it. When I knelt before her and pretended to gaze in awe at her ‘radiant holy face’? I thought she was the most hideous, vile old crone I had ever laid eyes on in my life.”

Xodar gasped, leapt to his feet, and lunged at me screaming “Blasphemer!” I did not want to hurt him, so I caught his wrist, swung my elbow under his chin, and bore him backward over my thigh, holding him helpless until the rage drained out of him. “I am not lying to you,” I said, setting him back on his feet. “Your goddess is no immortal. She is a mortal old woman, a fraud who has lied to you and every other First Born for centuries to keep you enslaved to her will. With your knowledge of this sunken realm and my experience fighting my way out of worse scrapes, we can escape.”

He said nothing, walked back to his bench, and sat lost in deep thought for hours. It was only when a soft sound came from the cell doorway that I looked up to see the young red Martian youth leaning against the frame, watching us with a curious smile.
“Kaor,” he said, using the traditional Barsoomian greeting.
“Kaor,” I replied. “What brings you to this prison?”
“I expect I’m waiting for my execution,” he said, grinning. “I’ve been here nearly a year, ever since I snuck a look at Issus’s so-called radiant face. I half expected to drop dead on the spot when I saw her: bloated, wrinkled, hideous, a fraud pretending to be the goddess of eternal life. No one could believe that garbage, not after seeing her.”
He told me he had been flying a one-man scout plane far south, chasing the legend of the Lost Sea of Korus, when his propeller had jammed over the southern ice cap. The First Born had swarmed him before he could make repairs, and he had fought his way through a dozen of them before they finally dragged him down. “I only regret that my mother will mourn me,” he said. “She mourned my father ten years ago, when he died before I was even born.”
I was about to ask him his father’s name when the heavy prison door creaked open. A burly guard dragged the boy back to his own cell, locked it, then came back to stand over us. “Issus commands that John Carter be given every leave to punish this fallen cur,” he said, jabbing a thumb at Xodar. “Heap every indignity you can think of on him, or you will be punished in his place.” The door slammed shut behind him.

When we were alone, Xodar lifted his head. “I have thought on every word you said, John Carter,” he said, quietly. “I see it now. All our faith, all our sacred rituals, all of it is a lie the priests and Issus have fed us for generations to steal our power. I renounce her. I am ready to fight. But how? These walls are solid polished rock, we cannot climb them, and there is no way out of this prison.”

I smiled. “I will scale that wall and carry you with me, easy. All I need from you is the best time to make the break, and a guide to the great shaft that leads up out of this cavern to the outer world.”
“Nighttime is our only chance,” Xodar said. “We are thousands of feet below the surface. The sun never reaches here. The glow you see, that faint phosphorescence, seeps out of the cavern dome rock. There is no darkness here at all. But at the hour when the outer world is dark, all the First Born sleep. Only a sleepy sentry keeps watch on the largest battleships, none at all on the small cruisers. The guards at the prison will be dozing.”
I shrugged. No darkness? That would make things trickier, but I had faced far worse odds. “We will rest first,” I said. “A solid plan will come to us when we wake.”

We stretched out on the cold hard stone floor, and fell asleep to the soft, endless lap of the windless Sea of Omean’s waves against the Isle of Shador’s rocky shores, the first spark of real hope we had known since we were dragged down into the sunken realm of Issus.

Dawn painted the vaulted inner sky of Omean in faint, lurid streaks of crimson when Xodar and I knelt on our cell’s stone floor, mapping our way out. We scratched the polar charts using my harness buckle and the razor-sharp facet of the glowing gem I’d pried from the corpse of Sator Throg. First I drew the full sweep of the south polar ice and terrain, calculating the rough bearing and distance to Helium from the hidden dome exit that opened out to the outer world. Next I mapped the whole of the sunken sea, marking Shador prison and the position of that critical exit on Omean’s vast ceiling. I traced every line until they were burned into my memory, no paper or ink needed.

From Xodar I learned the sentry patterns for the whole prison. During the four sleep cycles of the First Born, only one guard patrolled the perimeter at a time. He moved so slowly, dawdling every step of his beat, that a full circuit of the prison took ten minutes flat. That meant five full, unbroken minutes where each face of the building sat unwatched, the sentry all the way on the opposite side.

“This information is grand for after we’re out,” Xodar rumbled, brow furrowed, “but none of it tells us how we get out past these four stone walls in the first place.”

I laughed, clapping him on the shoulder. “Leave the door-breaking to me. We strike the first night a small skiff is tied up within easy reach of Shador’s shore.”

He blinked. “The windows are thirty feet off the ground. We can’t see the water from here.”

“Watch this.” I bounded straight up to the iron bars of the cell’s high window, hauled myself up the grating, and scanned the bay spread below. Two heavy First Born battleships and half a dozen small, fast sailing skiffs were moored less than a hundred yards from the prison’s docks. Tonight, I thought, the word on my lips, when the cell door slammed wide open without warning.

If that guard spotted me perched on the window bars, he’d clap me in irons before sundown. The First Born already suspected my Earth-born muscles gave me impossible speed and strength—they’d never let me run loose if they knew the full extent of what I could do. He stood facing the center of the cell, his broad black back turned fully to me, five feet of empty space below my perch on the sill, and five more feet up to the top of the dividing stone partition that separated our cell from the empty one next door. If I dropped straight down I’d land right on top of him. There was no other choice.

I hauled myself up, coiled my legs, and launched. My metal harness grated loud against the stone lip of the partition as I hauled myself over, dropping silent as a cat to the floor of the adjacent cell.

“What was that?” the guard roared, spinning around. “Where is the white man? Issus commands his presence, now.”

Xodar shrugged, putting on the perfect act of a bored, captive noble. “I do not know. He was here when you walked through the door. I am not his keeper. Search for him yourself.”

The guard grumbled something guttural I couldn’t parse, unlocked the far cell doors one by one to hunt for me, and I heard the clang of his retreat. I sprang back over the partition top before the echo of his footsteps faded, landing light on the stone of our own cell right in front of Xodar.

“Now do you see how we escape?” I whispered.

“*You* can bounce over stone walls like a white ape,” he said, bewildered, “I am no acrobat. I still have no clue how I get out of this cell.”

We heard the guard trudge back a minute later, and his eyes bulged right out of his skull when he spotted me leaning against the far wall like I’d been there the whole time. “By the shell of my first ancestor! Where the hell did you come from?”

“I’ve been in this room the whole time,” I said, grinning. “Maybe your eyes are rotting out of your head from too much sun.”

He snarled, half furious half spooked, and waved me toward the door. “Move. Issus wants you, now.”

He marched me out of the prison, and waiting by the gates were a squad more guards, herding that red Martian teen boy I’d caught a glimpse of in the adjacent Shador cell the night before. The journey to Issus’s temple was the same as the day before, but this time they didn’t lead us up the marble steps to her gilded sanctum. They turned off down a winding path through gardens thick with bioluminescent blooms, until we reached a sheer hundred-foot stone wall, its massive iron gates opening out onto a sunlit plain rimmed with the same vivid, ancient forests that fringe the base of the Golden Cliffs.

Throngs of First Born black warriors wandered the paths ahead, flanked by lurching plant men and lumbering great white apes that got shoved roughly out of the way or whacked flat with the broad of a sword blade, slinking off like whipped hounds. Our destination loomed half a mile from the garden walls: a massive sunken amphitheatre, its arena floor dozens of feet below the surrounding plain, only the top tier of seats peeking above ground level.

The crowds flooded through the main arch to their seats, while we prisoners were herded down a narrow side ramp into the undercroft pens, locked behind bars far weaker than any the First Born thought they needed—no one ever escaped the rites of Issus, after all. The guards slacked off almost immediately, and I drifted over to the strange young red boy whose face felt so impossibly familiar, like I’d known him my whole life even though I was sure I’d never met him.

“What’s this rite for?” I asked him. “Do we fight gladiator style for their amusement? Or something worse?”

“It’s the monthly Tribute and Atonement of Issus,” he said, his mouth twisting into a bitter smile. “Black warriors accused of disloyalty to her fight us outer world prisoners to wash their sins clean in our blood. If they die? They were guilty, a traitor, fit for nothing but the meat pits. If they win? Their slate is wiped clean. We never win freedom, though. No one who steps foot in the domains of the First Born ever leaves. The best we get if we fight well is a few more months of arena matches. The worst is an early grave, or worse.”

I asked if he’d fought often. “Almost a full year,” he said, his sword hand tightening on the hilt at his hip. “I’ve put down a hundred of their black devils. My father was one of the greatest warriors Barsoom ever saw. I’m going to keep his name proud, no matter how many of these gods I kill.”

“Who was your father?” I started to ask, and before he could answer a guard roared, “Move, calots! To the slaughter!” and he shoved us down the steep ramp that led to the barred cages lining the arena floor. We got separated the second we hit the line of cells.

My cage was directly across the sand from Issus’s gilded throne. The ancient, hideous goddess squatted on her padded dais, ringed by a hundred jeweled slave girls, three solid ranks of heavily armed black guards, and a crowd of her highest nobles glinting with gemmed rank insignia set in gold on their foreheads. The stands were packed wall to wall with First Born, every single one of them attended by one to three slaves they’d stolen from the therns or the outer kingdoms. They considered themselves all gods, none of them did a day’s work in their lives. Slaves bathed them, dressed them, fed them, some even had slaves that narrated the arena’s action to them because they were too lazy to open their own eyes.

The first event of the day was the Tribute: ten beautiful young slave girls who’d served in Issus’s personal retinue for a full year were led out into the sand. Their reward for that honor? Death, and the honor of being served as feast meat to the First Born court the next day. A huge brute named Thabis inspected each one like he was picking out fruit for the table, then dragged the plumpest one off toward the kitchens. “Issus dines well tonight,” a prisoner beside me muttered.

The next three cages swung open, and three 15 foot tall great white apes bounded out into the sand, shrieking and slavering. The nine remaining girls huddled together in the center of the arena, one on her knees begging Issus for mercy, and the evil goddess only leaned further forward, cackling, to enjoy the show.

A red haze bleared my vision, the same fire of rage that always blazes up before I kill a dozen foes. That cowardly, sadistic creature on the throne didn’t get to play with the lives of these innocent girls for fun. The guard lolled against the unbarred gate of my cell, half asleep. I drove my fist into his jaw hard enough to knock him out cold, snatched his long-sword, and vaulted straight out into the arena sand.

Two bounds, and I was between the apes and the cowering girls. My sword whistled through the air, and the lead ape dropped headless at my feet.

For one heartbeat the whole amphitheatre went dead silent. Then the hundreds of prisoners in their pens roared so loud the stone shook. The other two apes spun to attack me, and that was when the young red boy burst out of his own cage, sword held high. “Come, men of the outer world!” he shouted at the top of his lungs. “Grab the spare swords off the racks by your pens! Let’s turn Issus’s little feast into a revenge orgy that they’ll whisper about for ten thousand years!”

The cages blew open. The prisoners swarmed the few remaining undercroft guards, stripped the racks of every blade, and poured out into the sand to back me up. I cut down the two remaining apes in three blows, and by the time I looked up a full company of Issus’s royal guards were charging across the sand at us. The boy was faster than any red Martian I’d ever seen, his leaps almost matching the superhuman bounds my Earth-born muscles gave me on this low-gravity world. We met the charge back to back, carving through their ranks like they were made of paper.

For half an hour, all hell broke loose. The arena was a writhing, howling mass of blood and steel, dead and dying men piling up underfoot, the clash of blades drowning out every other sound. I herded our ragtag fighters into a rough defensive circle, the surviving slave girls tucked safe in the center. The First Born nobles were pouring down from the stands now to reinforce their guards, hundreds of them, thousands, swarming us from every side. I looked up and saw Issus leaning over the edge of her throne, her hideous face twisted not just with rage, but fear.

“Fifty of you stay here, defend the girls until I come back!” I shouted. “The rest of you—down with Issus! Charge the throne! Vengeance where vengeance is due!”

The young boy took up the cry at my side, and a thousand hoarse voices roared after him: “To the throne! To the throne!” We moved as one, an unstoppable wall of blades, cutting down every black that stood in our path. Half our force veered off to scale the low arena wall and storm the stands, their dripping swords hungry for every First Born they found. The whole amphitheatre exploded in screams, the clash of steel, triumphant shouts.

Then a roar rose from the stands, rolling like thunder around the arena: *Rise, slaves! Rise, slaves!* Every enslaved woman in the amphitheatre turned on her master, stabbing them with stolen daggers, bludgeoning them with heavy jeweled harness ornaments, tearing at their throats with their nails and teeth, a century of pent up, unspeakable cruelty finally spilling over.

Only me and the boy were left fighting at the base of the throne now. Every one of our comrades lay dead. A black guard snuck up behind me, his sword raised to split my skull, when a hand shot out from behind him, grabbed his elbow, and threw his swing off course. The boy drove his blade through the man’s chest a second later, and I looked past the corpse at the woman who’d saved my life: Phaidor, daughter of Matai Shang.

“Fly, John Carter!” she screamed over the noise. “There are ten thousand more First Born pouring through the palace gates right now. The slave revolt is almost crushed. You cannot win. Run for the sea of Korus, fight your way to the Golden Cliffs, find my father Matai Shang, tell him what has happened! You still might escape.”

But I wasn’t running. Not while that monster Issus cowered on her throne, safe, untouched. My sword was still in my hand, the boy was still at my side, and the handful of remaining guards between us and her were falling fast. The fire of hell that I’d helped unleash on Issus’s arena was far from burned out.

The horde of Issus’s red-robed worshippers was on me in the span of one thudding heartbeat, their serrated blades glinting crimson in the arena’s hellfire glow. I planted my boots to meet the first charge, heel slipping back half a pace—then half again, meeting nothing but yawning empty air.

I teetered on the brink of the very pit that had swallowed Issus herself, the boy I’d dragged from the arena floors still clamped tight to my chest, before gravity took us both. We tumbled backward into blackness, hit a polished, frictionless stone chute that sent us skidding fast, and the trapdoor above snicked shut behind us as seamlessly as if it had never existed at all. We spilled unharmed onto the flagstones of a low, dimly lit chamber fathoms below the temple.

The first thing my eyes locked on was Issus. She was pressed to the heavy iron bars of a grated cell set into the far wall, her ageless face twisted into a rictus of venom, eyes burning like smoldering coals.
“Blaspheming mortal!” she shrieked, voice raw with unbridled rage. “You will rot here for what you have done. You, and the boy at your side, locked in total darkness to starve, alone with his festering corpse until the loneliness and hunger gnaw what little wit you have left. You will crawl over his rotting bones to feast on the maggots that once made up his flesh, and no soul shall ever know your names or your fates.”

She winked out of sight an instant later, and the faint, sickly light that had filled the chamber died with her, swallowing every glimmer of sight in pitch-black, Cimmerian dark.
“Charming old dear,” a voice piped up right next to my ear.
I spun, sword raised. “Who speaks?”
“Me, sir. Your partner in the arena brawl, who got the honor of fighting shoulder to shoulder with the greatest warrior to ever don steel harness on all Barsoom.”
“Thank the skies you aren’t dead,” I breathed, reaching out to find his shoulder, my fingers brushing the crusted dried blood over the gash on his scalp. “I thought that axe blow to your head had finished you.”
“Stunned me, that’s all,” he laughed, quiet. “Nothing but a scratch. Though I reckon it’d be a mercy if it had done the job, huh? We’re trapped down here, no food, no water, no way out.”
“Where exactly are we?”
“Beneath the arena floors,” I told him. “We fell straight down the shaft that swallowed Issus when we had her cornered.”

He let out a soft, relieved huff of laughter, then groped through the dark until his fingers found my shoulder, pulling my ear down to his mouth to whisper.
“Couldn’t have landed anywhere better. Issus has no idea half the secrets buried under her temple exist.”
I tensed. “What do you mean?”
“Last year, I was put to work with a crew of other slaves remodeling these subterranean galleries. We dug right into a whole network of tunnels and chambers sealed tight for ages—no one had set foot there for a hundred thousand years or more. The First Born guards made us go with them to clear rubble, map the passages. I know every inch of it. Miles of tunnels honeycombing under the gardens, the temple, the arena. One of the passages slopes all the way down to the great underground sea Omean, opens right onto the water shaft that moors the First Born submarines.”
His whisper got even quieter, as if the very dark walls had ears. “If we can sneak to that sub undetected, we can sail out to the uncharted islands in Omean where no First Born ever patrols. We can hide out there, and wait for a chance to get free for good.”

“First, we go back for Shador,” I whispered back immediately. “My friend Xodar, the First Born rebel, is locked in the cells there. We swore we’d escape together. I won’t leave him behind.”
“Never leave a man who’s fought at your side,” he agreed. “I’d rather get recaptured a dozen times than abandon a friend.”
He went to work groping across the stone floor, scrabbling with his fingertips until a soft *s-s-t* called me over. I crawled toward the sound to find him kneeling over a hidden trapdoor in the stone, swung open to reveal another dark pit below.
“Ten foot drop,” he breathed. “Hang from the edge, let go, and you’ll land on soft sand, no injury.”

I lowered myself down, the dark so absolute I couldn’t see my own hand held an inch in front of my face. For a heartbeat I hung there, feet dangling over nothing, a prickle of irrational panic spiking up my spine even though I knew the drop was short—old human instinct screaming that the dark below was bottomless. I let go, landed soft as a feather on the sand four feet later, and the boy dropped down right after me.
“Climb on my shoulders,” he said. “I’ll pull the trap shut behind us, no one will ever know we came this way.”
When he hopped down, he laced his fingers through mine, leading me slow, pausing every few feet to feel the walls, make sure we didn’t take a wrong turn into a dead end. A steep, sloping incline stretched out ahead of us, and he nodded toward the dark.
“Not far now,” he said. “The lower levels are carved from the same phosphorescent rock that lights the whole of Omean. We’ll have light soon.”

That trek through the sunken pits of Issus is burned into my memory forever. Nothing dramatic happened, no monsters, no guards, but every step hummed with a wild, thrumming sense of adventure. The dark hid more than it showed, but my imagination ran wild, conjuring the long-dead civilizations that had built these halls ages before: the black First Born, the lost white races, the vanished yellow people who had once walked Barsoom’s surface when its oceans were full, before the seas dried and the world started dying. I’d always thought those three old races had been wiped out, merged entirely into the red race that populated the planet’s surface now—but I’d found thousands of whites and thousands of blacks in just four days. Could the yellow folk still be hiding somewhere, tucked in some forgotten corner of the dying world?

The boy’s quiet exclamation pulled me out of my reverie. “There! The light!”
A faint silver glow bloomed far down the tunnel, growing brighter as we walked, until we stepped out into wide, well-lit corridors glowing soft blue from the embedded rock. We moved fast after that, no more groping in the dark, until we rounded a bend and came face to face with the ledge that ringed the sub’s mooring pool. The craft bobbed at its dock, hatch wide open, no soul in sight.
The boy pressed a finger to his lips, tapped the hilt of his missing sword, and crept forward silent as a shadow. I was right on his heels. We slipped over the rail onto the empty deck, crawled on hands and knees to the hatch, peeked down into the cabin below, and found no guard. We dropped into the main hold in unison, cats through a birdcage, and slammed the hatch shut, latching it tight. The boy ducked into the pilot house, hit a single button, and the sub drifted down below the surface of the pool.

I went room to room, checking every bunk and closet, and found the entire craft completely deserted. Not a single crew member left aboard. When I reported back to the pilot house, the boy handed me a crumpled radio dispatch slip left on the console.
“Think this explains where everyone went.”
The message was scrawled in the First Born’s harsh angular script:
> The slaves have risen in the arena. Mobilize every man you have, round up all stragglers on your way back. You cannot wait for backup from Omean. They are massacring every guard in the amphitheater. Issus’s life is at risk. Haste.

“Zithad’s Issus’s head of security,” the boy explained, grinning. “We gave them one hell of a scare back there in the arena, didn’t we?”
“Let’s hope it’s the first crack in Issus’s whole rotting empire,” I said.

We cruised unchallenged down the water shaft all the way to Omean, the great underground sea, and pulled up to the dock on the far side of the pool. We debated sinking the sub to hide our tracks, but decided it would do no good—even if every last crewman came rushing back from the temple, there were thousands of First Born on Omean to hunt us if we were caught. The extra few hundred wouldn’t make the difference between success and failure.
The only problem left was the sentry line patrolling the island that ringed the mooring pool. I thought fast, turned to the boy.
“What’s the name of the guard commander on duty today?”
“Torith,” he said. “We passed him this morning when we were marched through here as prisoners. The sub’s captain is named Yersted.”

I grabbed a blank dispatch form off the console, scrawled an order in the same angular script the First Born used:
> Return these two prisoners immediately to the Shador prison.
“Got a trick to get us right past the guards,” I said, holding it up. “We’re leaving our swords here, though. There’s no way to explain why two slaves are wandering around armed.”
“Are you sure going back unarmed straight into the First Born’s custody isn’t the dumbest possible plan?” he asked, half amused.
“It’s the only plan that works,” I told him. “Once we’re in Shador, I’ll get us out. A place full of thousands of armed guards is not a hard place to pick up a new pair of swords.”
He laughed, shrugged. “I’d follow you anywhere. Let’s test this trick of yours.”

We stepped off the sub’s deck unarmed, marched straight up the path to the sentry post. The guards snapped to attention, rifles raised, ready to gun us down, but I held out the forged order. One guard took it, saw it was addressed to Torith, and handed it over to the commander himself, who was storming out of his office to see what the commotion was.
Torith read the note, narrowed his eyes, and glowered at us. “Where is Captain Yersted?” he demanded. My stomach dropped. I’d been an idiot not to sink the sub. Now he was going to check the dock, see the sub was empty, and we’d be dead before we could take three steps.
“His orders were to head straight back to the temple landing post, sir,” I said, evenly.
Torith shifted his weight, half-turning toward the pool, and for one heartbeat the whole plan hung by a thread. If he took three steps toward the water, the lie was exposed. But he hesitated, squinted at the order again, and nodded. There was no reason two slaves would voluntarily march themselves back into custody unless they had genuine orders. The very boldness of the ruse made it impossible to doubt.
“Were you part of this slave riot in the arena?” he asked.
“All the prisoners were,” I shrugged. “The guards crushed it fast. Killed most of us.”
He waved a hand at his subordinate. “Take them to Shador. Lock them back up.”

We piled into a small patrol boat, crossed the short stretch of dark water, and stepped onto the shores of Shador. The guards herded us to our old cells, threw Xodar in with me, and locked the heavy iron door shut behind us. We were prisoners of the First Born again—but this time, we held a secret map to their most closely guarded hidden tunnels, and a chance no one on Issus’s side could ever have predicted.

Xodar stared at me, jaw slack, as I recounted every second of the arena rites: the sword pressed to Issus’s wrinkled throat, the way she’d screamed not with divine wrath but the petty, spitting fury of a cornered old tyrant. For all his private doubt that the self-proclaimed goddess was anything more than a fraud, he could scarcely wrap his head around the fact any mortal had dared raise a blade to her and not been reduced to smoldering ash on the spot.

“It is the final proof,” he breathed at last. “No lingering scrap of my old superstition survives. Issus is nothing but a cruel, power-hungry crone, propped up by centuries of carefully orchestrated lies that have kept every last soul on Barsoom trapped in religious bondage.”

“For all that, she still rules every inch of this sunken underworld,” I said. “We leave the first chance we get—tonight, if possible.”

He barked a sharp, disbelieving laugh. “Good luck finding that chance. No prisoner of the First Born has ever broken free of Omean in all recorded history.”

“Tonight will be as good a night as any,” I told him. “Dark’s falling fast. Can you swim?”

“A slimy silian that haunts the black depths of Korus couldn’t outswim me,” he grinned. “I was born in these waters.”

“Perfect. Our red companion almost certainly can’t,” I said. “The outer red domains barely hold enough water to float a toy skiff. Their people haven’t seen a river or a sea in a dozen generations—deep water terrifies them. One of us will have to carry him the hundred yards out to the fliers moored off the dock. I’d hoped we could make the whole swim submerged, but there’s no way he’d survive that.”

“The red boy comes with us?” Xodar asked. At my nod, he nodded back. “Wise. Three swords beat two, and I’ve seen that kid fight in the arena a dozen times. Before I saw you move, I’d have sworn no man could fight like that, outnumbered ten to one and still laugh in his enemies’ faces. You could be father and son—you’ve got the exact same grim, unimpressed smirk when you draw a blade.”

“Be that as it may, he’s a deadly fighter,” I said. “If Tars Tarkas, Jeddak of Thark, was here with us, we could carve a path from one end of Barsoom to the other even if every living soul on the planet came at us.”

“One day soon, they will,” Xodar said darkly. “That’s just another of Issus’s lies. She never deals directly with the outer world—she works through the Holy Therns, who are every bit as deluded as the red and green Barsoomians they dupe. Her decrees appear written in blood on their most guarded altars, seemingly out of thin air. The fools think they’re receiving divine revelations, but I’ve carried those messages for her for decades. There’s a hidden tunnel dug by our slaves ages ago, running straight from Issus’s temple to Matai Shang’s main temple, a tunnel no thern has ever guessed exists.

“The therns spread their doctrine across all the surface world, preaching the sacred pilgrimage down the River Iss to the Valley Dor and the Lost Sea of Korus, tricking thousands of poor souls into walking voluntarily to their own enslavement, swelling the therns’ coffers and their slave pens. Then the First Born raid the therns whenever we want, seizing their wealth, their artisans, their warships, and their royal red women captives. We’re a non-productive race—labour or invention is a crime for a First Born, fit only for lower castes whose sole purpose is to keep us in idle luxury. We don’t die natural deaths, you know. Our women live forever unless we tire of them and discard them. Only Issus has cheated death entirely, for uncounted millennia.”

“Wouldn’t the rest of Barsoom live just as long, if not for the pilgrimage scam that drags every soul to Iss before they hit their thousandth year?” I asked.

“I’m certain of it,” Xodar said, quiet now. “And I’ll fight for them, to atone for all the harm I helped inflict, born of nothing but generations of lies.”

A weird, trilling call cut across the black waters of Omean—the same one I’d heard the night before, marking the end of the Barsoomian day, when the entire population of the sunken sea spreads their silk sleeping mats across their ship decks and drops straight into dreamless rest.

Our guard stepped in to make his final rounds before the surface world’s new day dawned, checked the three cells in less than a minute, and slammed the heavy iron door shut behind him. We were alone for the night.

I waited long enough for him to trudge back to the guardhouse, then clambered up to our grated window. A quarter mile out, a massive First Born battleship loomed, surrounded by a handful of smaller cruisers and single-man scout fliers. Only the battleship posted a watch, and even he was spreading his sleeping mat on his tiny upper deck platform, and seconds later he was stretched out, dead to the world. No wonder their discipline was so lax—no soul on the surface even suspected the First Born, or the Sea of Omean, existed. There was no possible enemy to guard against.

I dropped back down and Xodar described his personal flier, a custom 5-seater built to be the fastest craft on the entire sea. I recognized the unique gearing he described instantly—Kantos Kan had shown me that exact system back when we served undercover in Zodanga’s navy under Sab Than, prince of Zodanga. The First Born had stolen the design straight from Helium’s shipyards, and I knew no craft on Barsoom could outpace a Helium-engineered flier.

We agreed to wait an hour for every last straggler to fall asleep, before I snuck over the cell partitions to fetch the red youth. He was leaning back against his cell wall, staring up at Omean’s glowing phosphorescent dome, and his eyes went wide when he saw me balancing on the foot-wide wall above his cell. Then a wide, knowing grin spread across his face. He motioned for me to wait, and whispered that he’d spent months practicing jumps, and could almost leap high enough to pull himself up to the top of the partition on his own. I hung over the edge, grabbed his outstretched hand when he launched himself upward, and hauled him up beside me.

The three of us huddled in Xodar’s cell, swearing a blood oath to fight back to back, to the death, no matter what enemies came for us. Even if we escaped Omean, we knew the full weight of Barsoom’s centuries-old religious dogma would be hunting us. We agreed I’d pilot the flier, and head straight for Helium the second we broke through to the surface.

“You’re a prince of Helium?” the red boy asked, his expression strange, like he knew something he wasn’t saying. I didn’t press, preoccupied with our escape plans, and the moment passed.

We moved out then. I used my harness straps as a rope to haul Xodar up onto the partition wall with us, then we climbed the outer prison wall one by one, lowering each other to the ground. The sentry on his slow, snail-paced beat rounded the far end of the prison and vanished from sight, and we walked slow and casual the hundred yards toward the water, right past the guardhouse full of sleeping First Born soldiers.

Xodar and the boy slipped silently over the dock edge first, clinging to the metal mooring rings at the water line, only their noses and mouths sticking above the dark surface of Omean. I hesitated for half a second, staring at the guardhouse door. We needed swords. I couldn’t leave them there, unused. The foolhardy urge won out.

I eased the door open a crack. A dozen black-skinned First Born were stretched out on their silk mats, snoring deep. A weapon rack on the far wall was lined with swords and revolvers. I pushed the door wider, and the hinge gave a high, groaning squeak. One guard stirred, and my heart stopped dead. I froze over him, hands hovering over his throat, ready to silence him before he could yell. Seconds dragged by, and he rolled over, his breathing even and deep again.

I tiptoed across the room, grabbed three long swords off the rack, and fumbled one extra revolver off its pin. Right then, the opposite door burst open. The officer of the guard stood there, face full of rage, his revolver coming up. We fired at exactly the same time. His bullet whizzed an inch past my ear, and I saw him crumple. I dove straight through the open window right behind the weapon rack, and hit the cold water of Omean before the echoes of the shots had faded.

The three of us struck out for Xodar’s flier, 100 yards out. Xodar was towing the red boy, I was carrying all three swords, and we moved slow enough that I was certain we’d be spotted any second. But we clambered over the flier’s rail entirely unseen, right before the watch on the battleship finally noticed us.

The alarm gun boomed, its thunder rolling under Omean’s rocky dome, and thousands of sleeping First Born exploded awake. Decks of hundreds of warships swarmed with armed men, launching every craft they had in pursuit. I wrenched the flier’s controls, and we rose off the water in a surge.

“Climb high!” Xodar yelled over the roar of the engines. “They can’t fire their heavy guns up toward the dome—shell fragments will rain back down on their own fleets. Our steel keel will stop all their rifle fire.”

I climbed, and we looked down to see hundreds of men leaping off their ships, swimming for their scout fliers. A 10-man cruiser rose fast directly in front of us, angled to skim over our deck and drag us down with grappling hooks. There was no way around—every other path was blocked by onrushing pursuers. I feinted like I was climbing higher, tricking the cruiser’s pilot into tilting his craft even steeper to force me up against the dome, then I slammed the throttle to maximum and dove straight for his keel at full speed.

The crash was deafening. The cruiser, already tilted at a suicidal angle, flipped completely backward. Its crew screamed as they tumbled through the air down to the water, and the wreck, propellers still spinning wildly, nosed straight down to the bottom of the sea. Our bows were crushed in, and the jolt nearly threw all three of us over the rail. I caught the red boy by his ankle half a second before he would have vanished into the dark water. Our flier careened wildly, drifting within fifty feet of the glowing dome, until I wrenched the controls back level and aimed for the black, yawning mouth of the escape shaft.

A hundred scout fliers were right on our heels. Xodar yelled that if we used only our anti-gravity rays to climb the narrow shaft, they’d overtake us in seconds, their smaller, buoyancy-aided craft rising faster than ours could. There was only one trick left.

“Hold on!” I screamed. “Both of you, hold the boy tight—we’re not using buoyancy. We’re flooring it straight up.”

We shot under the black lip of the shaft, and I cranked the speed lever all the way to its final notch. The wind screamed in our ears as we hurtled upward at nearly two hundred miles an hour, no light to guide us but the tiny, shrinking circle of phosphorescent Omean glow far below. It was blind luck, more than skill, that kept us from scraping against the rocky shaft walls that would have smashed the flier to pieces in an instant. In forty seconds flat, we burst out of the two-mile deep tunnel, out into the open air of the surface world.

And total darkness wrapped around us. No moons, no stars. I had never seen anything like it on Barsoom, where the sky is always clear enough to see every constellation at night. Then I realized: the southern summer had arrived. The polar ice cap was melting, and the rare, almost unheard of meteorological phenomenon called clouds had rolled in, swallowing the entire sky. We had broken out of the sunken prison of Omean straight into a world of total, unseen shadow.

“My son!”
I could not believe my ears. I rose slow, stiff from the long hours at the ship’s helm, and turned to face the lithe, handsome youth standing before me. Now that I looked close, the reason his face had tugged at some half-forgotten, cherished part of my soul hit me like a blow to the chest. Dejah Thoris’s incomparable grace was etched in every clean line of his features, but it was hard, bright, unmistakeably masculine—those sharp grey eyes, the set of his jaw, the quiet, unyielding fire in his gaze, they were mine, mirror-perfect.

A lifetime of fighting across two worlds has taught me a man may seem soft where his wife and child are concerned, and still be unbreakable when the swords are drawn for sterner things.

Xodar had manned the helm the whole time we spoke, and now he called over to us, voice tight with alarm.

I ran to check the damage, and it was far worse than I feared. Not only did we have to tip the nose of the ship sharply upward to keep from diving headlong into the ground, bleeding speed every second, but the repulsive rays pouring out of the cracked tank meant in an hour or less we would be floating completely helpless, stern-down, plummeting to the ochre sea beds below.
We had eased our pace earlier, lulled into a false sense of safety, but I wrenched the engine throttle wide open the second I realized how bad the damage was, and the little craft tore north at blistering speed, while Carthoris and Xodar scrambled to patch the ragged gash in the hull, fighting a losing battle to stop the rays from escaping.
It was still dark when we cleared the northern edge of the ice cap and the thick layer of roiling clouds that hung over it. Below us stretched the familiar Barsoom landscape: rolling, ochre bottoms of seas that had dried a million years prior, low crumbling hills, and dotted across the horizon the grim, silent ruins of cities dead for longer than the human race had walked Earth, great piles of masterful architecture now only home to ancient ghosts, and the ferocious great white apes that roamed the empty wastes.
It got harder and harder to keep the craft level. The bow sagged lower and lower until I had to kill the engine entirely, lest we dive straight down and shatter against the rock below. When the sun crested the horizon and washed away the last of the night dark, the ship gave a final, lurching plunge, rolled half onto its side, and began to spin slow in sickening circles, the bow dropping further below the stern with every turn.
We clung to the handrails and stanchions as the deck tilted steeper and steeper, until all three of us snapped our safety harnesses to the iron rings bolted to the hull sides. A moment later the deck reared to a full ninety-degree angle, and we hung there suspended by our leather straps, our feet dangling a full thousand yards above the ground.
I swung close enough to reach the control levers, and my fingers closed around the bar that regulated the ship’s repulsion rays. The craft responded soft to my touch, and we began to sink slow, gentle, toward the ground below.
It took half an hour for us to touch down softly on the earth. Directly north of us rose a range of high, jagged hills, perfect for hiding from any air patrols that might stumble across our crash site, so we struck out for them at once.
An hour later we were hidden deep in the rounded, wind-carved gullies of the hills, surrounded by the bright flowering shrubs that grow even in Barsoom’s driest wastes. We found thick clusters of the great milk-yielding bushes that serve the nomadic green hordes as both food and drink—exactly the boon we needed, for all three of us were half-starved. We curled up in the shade of the bushes, completely hidden from any passing air scout, and fell asleep. It was the first real rest I had had since I woke back on Barsoom, five days prior, after being pulled straight from my cottage on the Hudson to the bizarre, beautiful, cursed valley of Dor. I had only managed two short naps the entire time, one of them within the therns’ stone storehouse.
I woke mid-afternoon to find someone pressing their lips to my hand, kissing it over and over. I jolted awake, and stared straight into Thuvia’s bright, tear-streaked face.
“My Prince! My Prince!” she cried, light bursting from her. “I mourned you dead. My ancestors have heard my prayers. I have not lived for nothing.”
Her voice woke Xodar and Carthoris. The boy stared at her in open surprise, but Thuvia did not even notice the two men standing beside me. She would have thrown her arms around my neck, smothering me in affection, if I had not disengaged her gentle but firm.

“I forget nothing, my Prince,” she replied, soft and steady. “You have never spoken a word of romantic love to me, and I never expected you to. But nothing can stop me loving you. I would never dream of taking Dejah Thoris’s place. All I want, all I ever ask, is to serve you forever as your slave. There is no greater honour, no greater happiness I could ever hope for.”
I have never been the sort of man who fancies himself a favourite of women, and I was more awkward and embarrassed in that moment than I have ever been in my life. I knew the Martian custom well, that a man of honour will always protect any woman in his household, even a slave, but I had never taken any servant but men before.
“If we make it back to Helium, Thuvia,” I told her, “you will come with us as an honoured equal, never a slave. There you will find dozens of handsome young nobles who would face Issus herself just to win one smile from you, and we will have you married to the best, bravest of them all. Forget this foolish infatuation born of gratitude and fear, that your innocent heart mistook for love. I would far rather have you as a dear friend.”

“The great Thark, I fear, is dead,” she said, her voice falling. “He fought like no man I have ever seen, but hundreds of green warriors from a rival horde overwhelmed him. The last I saw of him they were dragging him, bleeding and wounded, toward the dead city they sallied from to attack us.”

He shoved me into a shadowed doorway and told me to hide, to run for Helium if I could.

“Then he stepped out to meet them. My Prince, you have never seen fighting like it. “I will not leave Tars Tarkas to die in Warhoon hands if there is even a sliver of a chance he lives,” I said, standing and buckling my sword belt tight. “I go into that city tonight to confirm it, or rescue him.”
“I go with you,” Carthoris said at once.
“And I,” echoed Xodar.
“Neither of you will come,” I told them flat. “This is work for stealth, not brute force. One man alone can slip through their shadows unseen where three would get caught before we even crossed the city wall. I go alone. If I need your help, I will come back for you.”
They did not like it, but both were good soldiers, and we had already agreed I held command. The sun was already dipping low, and Barsoom’s short, sudden night fell fast.
I gave them final instructions for what to do if I did not return, bade them all farewell, and set off at a long, fast jog toward the dead city. As I came out of the hills, the nearer of Barsoom’s two moons tore across the sky, its bright silver light gilding the jagged, barbaric splendour of the ancient metropolis. The city had been built on these gentle foothills that once sloped down to meet the long-gone sea, which meant I could slip into its streets completely unobserved from the rear hillside, away from the main sea-facing approaches the Warhoons always used.
The green hordes that occupy these dead cities never spread far past the central plaza, so I hugged the dense, pitch-black shadows of the walls the whole way, pausing at every intersection to make sure no warrior was in sight before darting across to the shadowed side opposite. I made it all the way to the edge of the inhabited part of the city without being spotted, alerted to the horde’s presence only by the grunts and squeals of their thoats and zitidars corralled in the hollow walled courtyards of the surrounding blocks.
Those old familiar sounds of green Martian life made my chest tight, like coming home after a decade away. It was among these exact sounds that I had first courted Dejah Thoris, long ago, in the marble halls of the dead city of Korad.
I hunkered in the shadows at the far corner of the first inhabited square, and saw a stream of warriors pouring out of the surrounding buildings, all heading for the great domed structure at the centre of the plaza. I knew Warhoon customs well enough to know that was either the jeddak’s private quarters or the audience hall where he met his sub-chiefs, and whatever gathering they were holding almost certainly involved their new prisoner, Tars Tarkas.
To reach that building I had to cross the full length of one city block, a wide main avenue, and a stretch of the main plaza itself. The south side of the nearest block was completely dark, no torches burning, no sounds of life, so I slipped through the empty, abandoned building there to cut through to the inner courtyard. No one stopped me, no one spotted me, and I emerged into the courtyard right behind the occupied east-facing buildings. A whole herd of thoats and zitidars wandered there, cropping the low ochre moss that grows everywhere on uncultivated Barsoom. The wind blew from the northwest, so they could not scent me, and I crept along the east wall, tucked tight beneath the overhanging second-floor balconies, all the way to the north end of the block. The first three floors of those buildings were lit up, teeming with people, but everything above the third was pitch black.
There was no way to sneak through the lit rooms, packed full of green men and women, so my only path was up through the dark upper floors. It was trivial for me to scale the wall; one quick, agile leap, and my hands closed around the stone handrail of the second-floor balcony. I hauled myself up in a single motion.
Through the open windows I could see the green Martian squatting on their silk and fur sleeping mats, grunting short, single words at each other, their telepathic powers more than enough for conversation without long speeches. A warrior stepped through the doorway into the hall, and called to one of the men sitting there.
“Tan Gama, come. We are taking the Thark before Kab Kadja. Bring one other with you.”
The warrior named rose, gestured to a comrade beside him, and all three left the apartment.
If I followed them, I knew I would find exactly where they were keeping Tars Tarkas. I slipped through a dark unlit doorway on the balcony that led into the building’s corridor, and crept after the three warriors. They turned right at the end of the hall, vanishing from sight, and I raced after them as fast as I dared, not caring that my pace was reckless. Fate had handed me a perfect opportunity, and I could not waste it.
At the end of the corridor I found a spiral stairway leading up and down. I knew Warhoon customs well, and these ancient buildings: the Thark would not be held in an above-ground cell. He was down in the dark dungeon pits beneath the city.
I had been a prisoner of the northern Warhoons once, long ago, and I still remembered every inch of that dark underground pit they had locked me in. I crept down the stairs, and at the landing below I saw the faint glimmer of the three warriors’ torch far down the shaft, leading into the maze of underground corridors. I followed a safe distance behind them, keeping to the edge of the torchlight, as they wound through a hundred twisting, turning passages that had not seen sunlight in a million years.
After a hundred yards, they turned sharp right through an open doorway. I ran to catch up, and peered inside to see them unlocking the heavy chains that bound Tars Tarkas to the cell wall. They herded him rough out of the cell so fast I barely managed to dart back down the corridor ahead of them, out of the thin circle of their torchlight, before they spotted me.
I had assumed they would turn back the way they came, away from me, but to my horror, they turned straight toward me the second they stepped out of the cell. There was nothing for it but to run as fast as I could stay out of their torchlight, not daring to duck into any random side passage, lest they turn right into it after me. I raced through the dark, and I knew at any second I might stumble straight into a hidden pit, or run face-first into the ghoulish things that haunt the dark pits beneath Barsoom’s dead cities. The faint glow of their torch behind me was just bright enough that I could see the turns in the corridor ahead, and keep from slamming headfirst into the walls.
Suddenly I rounded a corner and came to a five-way intersection, and the faint glow of the torch vanished completely. The silence around me was total, as heavy as a tomb. The warriors must have taken a different corridor at the junction while I was still moving ahead of their light.
I groped my way back to the five-way crossroads, my hand pressed flat to the wall to guide me, fumbling until I counted five separate corridor entrances, but not a single glimmer of light peeked out of any of them. I held my breath and listened, and far down the middle corridor I thought I heard the faint clink of their side arms against their blades. I ran down that passage, pausing every few steps to listen again, but soon the silence swallowed even that faint sound. I had followed a false lead.

“Carthoris?” he said, surprised. “Your son?”
“Aye. I found him in a First Born prison beneath the Sea of Omean. I will tell you the full, strange tale once we are clear of the Warhoons.”

We made our way safely across the courtyard to the great stables at the far end, where the green warriors kept their massive war mounts. Taming five wild Barsoomian thoats is no easy task: the beasts are as ferocious and untamed as their riders, broken only by brute force and cruelty. The second they caught our unfamiliar scent, they reared back, squealing in rage, circling us on their eight long, powerful legs, their ten-foot shoulders heaving, huge gaping mouths lifted high over our heads. Their skin is smooth, hairless, dark slate across their backs, fading to a bright vivid yellow on their padded, nailless feet, their bellies pure white, broad flat tails thicker at the tip than the root: perfect, terrifying war steeds for a race of born raiders. Guided only by unspoken telepathic commands, no reins or bridles needed, they charged us in a frenzy, the racket of their screams loud enough to rouse every guard in the fortress if we did not move fast.

I made a dash for the nearest great brute, swung myself up onto his glossy back before he knew what was happening, and sent him a sharp, unyielding telepathic command to stand still. A second later Tars Tarkas had mounted a second, and between us we herded three more loose mounts toward the great gates. Tars Tarkas leaned down from his thoat to throw the heavy latch, swinging the barriers open, and we galloped out onto the wide avenue beyond, not even pausing to close the gates behind us, heading straight for the southern edge of the dead city.

Our luck held: we slipped past the outer ruins completely, reached our hidden camp without hearing a single sound of pursuit. My low prearranged whistle brought Thuvia, Carthoris, and Xodar running to meet us, overjoyed to see Tars Tarkas returned safe. We wasted no time on long stories. Carthoris and Tars Tarkas exchanged the dignified, formal greetings common to Barsoomian nobility, but I could see the quiet, deep affection between the two warriors at once. Xodar, the former First Born black jeddak, was introduced to Tars Tarkas, and then we all mounted our thoats, setting out at a hard gallop east, then curving north, speeding noiselessly across the silver ochre dead sea bottom under the light of Barsoom’s two moons, putting miles between us and the Warhoon fortress, fleeing for the hills, toward unknown new dangers.

We halted at noon the next day to rest our mounts and ourselves, hobbling the thoats so they could wander and graze on the thin ochre moss that serves them for both food and water. Thuvia volunteered to take the first watch, while the rest of us slept. It felt like I had barely closed my eyes when I felt her soft hand on my shoulder, her voice low in my ear. “Wake, Prince. There is a great mass of pursuers behind us, far on the horizon.”

I sprang up, woke the others. Tars Tarkas, his giant frame towering over all of us, squinted out across the plain. “It is thousands of mounted green warriors,” he said. “They are riding hard, and gaining fast.”

There was no time to waste. We cut the hobbles off our thoats, mounted, and rode north at the top speed of our slowest beast. For the rest of the day, and all through the long cold Martian night, we raced across that barren ochre waste, the Warhoons behind us drawing ever closer. Just before sundown we could make out clearly they were all Warhoons, and through the dark hours we could hear the clink of their harness and their ferocious war cries drifting closer. When the sun rose on the second day of our flight, the horde was less than half a mile behind us. They spotted us, and a roar of triumphant, bloodthirsty howl went up from their ranks.

A range of hills lay a few miles ahead: the far shore of the old dead sea bed we were crossing. If we could reach the broken cover of those hills, we might have a fighting chance to escape — but Thuvia’s thoat, the lightest burdened of all our mounts, staggered suddenly, lurching against mine. I snatched her off its back right before it collapsed, swinging her to sit behind me on my own thoat, her arms wrapped tight around my waist. We were almost to the hills, but the Warhoons were so close we could see their bared fangs, and we thought we would never make it. I felt Thuvia’s soft warm lips press a quick kiss to the back of my shoulder. “For you, my Prince,” she whispered. He was already off his mount by the time I reached Thuvia’s side, lifting her up to throw her across the saddle of his own animal, slapping the beast hard across the rump with the flat of his sword to send it thundering for the hills with her. He would not climb on. He stayed at my side, drawing his longsword, as did Tars Tarkas and Xodar, who had turned back at the first sign we were missing, and now all four of us lined up on foot, our four thoats turned loose beside us, ready to meet the full charge of the thousands of Warhoon raiders.

I thought of Dejah Thoris then, and how I hated to die before I could hold my princess in my arms once more. But if fate would not grant me that, then I would sell my life as dearly as possible, and give the Warhoons a tale to tell for twenty generations. The raiders were less than a hundred yards from us when a deafening explosion boomed out from over the hills behind us, and a shell burst dead in the front ranks of the charging horde.

Chaos erupted instantly. A hundred warriors toppled dead. Riderless thoats reared and stampeded over the dead and wounded, trampling their own comrades underfoot. The Warhoons broke ranks completely, staring up over our heads to spot where the attack had come from, and their confusion turned to panic, then wild headlong flight back the way they had come. We turned to look, and saw a great, gleaming battleship sweeping majestically over the tops of the hills, its bow gun roaring once more to send another shell bursting after the fleeing raiders. I let out a wild shout of joy: blazoned clear across its bow was the crimson royal sigil of Helium.

The four of us—Carthoris, Xodar, Tars Tarkas, and I—stood rooted to the red dust, staring up at the glimmering Helium battleship that hung low over the hills, the very symbol of the home we had all thought lost forever, when a second, then a third massive hull crested the ridgeline and glided gracefully down to join its sister. Before we could speak, a score of one-man air scouts launched from the nearest vessel’s upper decks, streaking down in long, screaming dives to fan out and form a ring of sharp, cold steel around us.

An officer stepped forward to bark the order for our capture, then his gaze locked on Carthoris. He gasped, surprise bursting over his face, and he bounded forward to clap both hands to the prince’s shoulders. “Carthoris! My Prince, Kaor! Kaor!” he cried, grinning so wide his fangs showed. “Hor Vastus greets the son of Dejah Thoris and John Carter. Where have you been? All Helium has wept for you these months. Calamity after calamity has crushed our great nation from the day you vanished.”

“Grieve no more, good Hor Vastus,” Carthoris laughed, turning to gesture at me. “I do not come home alone. I bring back the saviour of Barsoom, John Carter, Prince of Helium.”

Hor Vastus spun to face me, and went pale as a Thern’s robes, swaying where he stood like he might collapse from shock. “John Carter—” he started, then the words died on his lips. I knew the unspoken question hanging in the air: How are you here? You walked the banks of the Lost Sea of Korus, you stepped foot in the Valley Dor, you drank from the waters of Iss. No man returns from that. The loyal man would never force me to say it out loud. He shook off his stunned pause and bowed low. “That you are here is all that matters. Let Hor Vastus’ sword be the first to do you honour.”

He unbuckled his scabbard and laid his blade at my feet. To any red Martian, the act held the weight of a sacred, unbreakable oath: *My sword, my body, my life, my soul are yours, now and after death. Your word is my only truth. Whoever raises a hand to you answers to me*. No man save the most legendary, chivalrous Jeddaks of old had ever received that tribute from a warrior, and I knew what my response had to be. I bent to lift his blade, pressed the hilt to my lips in respect, then buckled it back at his hip with my own hands. “Hor Vastus, I will never ask you to draw this sword for anything but truth, justice, and the good of Barsoom,” I told him, and he nodded, fierce and proud, like he had never doubted it for a heartbeat.

As we spoke, shuttles darted between the ground and the battleship hull overhead, and a ten-person flier drifted down to land light as a feather beside us. Its captain saluted Hor Vastus. “Kantos Kan orders your party brought immediately to the deck of the *Xavarian*,” he said. That was when I noticed Thuvia was not among us. No one had seen her since Carthoris had sent her galloping away on her madly spurring thoat toward the hills to get her out of harm’s way. Hor Vastus dispatched a dozen scouts to comb every ridge and ravine for her at once, but a cold little knot of dread settled in my gut.

We stepped aboard the shuttle, and minutes later were greeted on the *Xavarian*’s deck by my old friend Kantos Kan himself. He had risen to command all of Helium’s aerial forces, but he was the same wild, loyal comrade who had starved beside me in a Warhoon dungeon, fought beside me through the bloody excesses of the Great Games, and run the gauntlet of Zodanga’s defences to rescue Dejah Thoris so many years before. He did not ask me where I had been. He did not want to hear the answer, not yet. He hugged Carthoris like he was his own son, clapped Tars Tarkas on the back, and told us that the whole of Helium had wept ten million tears the day word came the young prince was lost.

“Wait,” I said, his last line niggling at me. “What did you mean, ‘his poor mother’?”

He drew me aside, his face grim. “Ever since you did not return from the atmosphere plant, Dejah Thoris has carried that grief. The only thing that softened the blow was her new son—Carthoris hatched from his shell the very night you vanished. But when the boy disappeared, that last spark of hope went out. Expedition after expedition came back empty handed, no clue where he’d gone, and she started fading. All of us thought she would waste away in weeks. As a last resort, Tardos Mors and Mors Kajak took two full fleets to comb every inch of Barsoom’s northern hemisphere a month ago. We haven’t heard a word from them in two weeks. Rumour says every last ship was lost. Zat Arras, the Jed of Zodanga, has been pressuring Dejah Thoris to marry her ever since. Six days ago he met with her in secret, and the next day she was gone. She took a dozen of her guard, her whole household staff, and Sola—Tars Tarkas’ daughter—with her. No note, no message. Everyone knows what that means. She took the voluntary pilgrimage down the Iss, to join you in the Valley Dor. Zat Arras has been leading this whole fleet on a useless search for her ever since.”

My blood turned to ice. I knew exactly what horrors waited for any woman fool enough to reach the banks of the Iss, the plant men, the white apes, the lying, cruel Therns. I was just about to demand Kantos Kan send every ship he had to hunt for her when a dispatch flier dropped from the fleet flagship, carrying an order directly to Kantos Kan from Zat Arras.

“Zat Arras commands us to bring you all before him as prisoners,” Kantos Kan spat, furious. “He has no right. He should be down here kneeling to honour the Prince of Helium, not summoning us like common thieves.”

“Zat Arras has hated me since the day I beat him in the pits of Zodanga,” I said, grinning. “Nothing would make him happier than to humiliate me before he kills me. Let’s go give him the chance to try.”

We boarded the small flier, and moments later stepped onto the deck of Zat Arras’ flagship. He did not smile, he did not bow, he did not even greet his missing prince Carthoris, his face cold and haughty. “Why were these prisoners not disarmed?” he snapped at Kantos Kan.

“They are not prisoners,” Kantos Kan shot back. “Two of them are of Helium’s royal blood. Tars Tarkas is our oldest, most valued ally. The fourth is John Carter. That is all the justification I need.”

“Not enough for me,” Zat Arras snarled. “Speak, John Carter. Where have you been?”

“I came from the Valley Dor, from the land of the false gods the Therns call the Land of the First Born,” I said plainly.

He lit up, like I had handed him the rope to hang myself. “So you do not deny it? You returned from the bosom of Iss, you blasphemer! Do not think you will fool us with your lies to save your cowardly carcass—”

No man calls John Carter a coward and a liar and lives to boast of it. I was across the deck before any of his guards could move, one hand locked tight around his throat, hauling him half over my knee. “I don’t care if I came from heaven or hell, Zat Arras,” I growled in his ear. “No man speaks that way to me and walks away without begging forgiveness.”

His officers lunged to pull me off, but Kantos Kan was at my side in an instant, hissing in my ear. “Stop, John Carter! My whole crew will turn on them to defend you. We’ll have mutiny, civil war, the whole of Helium tearing itself apart while Dejah Thoris is lost. For the sake of the Jeddaks, for the sake of Barsoom, stop.”

I released him, turned my back on the shaking, sputtering Jed, and walked for the rail. “Come, Kantos Kan. The Prince of Helium is returning to the *Xavarian*.” No one dared lay a hand on us. One old officer who had served Tardos Mors for forty years leaned in to murmur as I passed, “My blade is yours, John Carter.” I nodded my thanks, and we boarded the flier back.

Fifteen minutes later, we received our orders: the whole fleet was sailing for Helium. The trip was quiet, heavy with dread. Carthoris and I were both sick with fear for Dejah Thoris. Kantos Kan knew Zat Arras was bound to invoke the old, terrible law that condemned anyone who returned from the Valley Dor to a hideous public death. Tars Tarkas grieved for his lost daughter Sola. Only Xodar, the fugitive First Born, was unconcerned. “What do I have to lose?” he shrugged. “At least we’ll all die with good red Martian blood on our blades.”

The crew of the *Xavarian* was already splitting into factions long before we sighted Helium’s mile-high crimson spires. Half the men clustered around us whenever they could, loyal to their prince out of years of love and shared memory. The other half kept their distance, polite but distant, their eyes full of superstitious horror. I could not blame them. The old religion of Iss and Korus had held Barsoom in its grip for ten thousand years, and I could not ask them to throw that all away overnight for the word of a man who came back from the dead.

We descended in wide, slow circles over Helium’s naval docks, and the streets below were packed shoulder to shoulder with millions of people, notified by radio-aerogram of our arrival. We were transferred to a tiny, unmarked flier, not to be paraded down the broad Avenue of Ancestors like heroes or condemned criminals, but flown directly to the roof landing pad of the Temple of Reward—where Martian justice is meted out, to decorate heroes and condemn felons alike. Zat Arras dared not let the crowd see us, afraid their love for Carthoris and me would override their superstitious fear and tear his whole scheme apart.

They locked us in a south-facing chamber, looking out over the five mile stretch of the Avenue of Ancestors all the way to the far Gate of Jeddaks. The crowds below stood silent, no jeers, no cheers, many of them covering their faces and weeping when they spotted us at the window. Late that afternoon, a messenger arrived from Zat Arras, bearing our formal notice: we would be tried by a council of noble judges in the temple’s great hall at the first zode of the following morning, roughly 8:40 A.M. Earth time.

[Translator’s Note: For the convenience of Earth readers, all Martian units of time, distance, and weight have been converted to their nearest equivalent earthly values, to avoid confusion. For reference, the Martian day runs just over 24 hours 37 minutes Earth time, and is split into ten equal segments called zodes, each divided into fifty shorter units called xats, each of which holds 200 individual tals, roughly equal to an Earth second. The first crimson rays of Barsoom’s dying sun were just gilding the obsidian spires of the Temple of Reward when a squad of Zat Arras’ elite Zodangan officers arrived at our quarters, their long radium rifles glinting in the dawn, to march us to judgement. We filed out in pairs, flanked on all sides by armed guards, and tramped down the broad, marble Aisle of Hope, its worn surface polished smooth by ten thousand pilgrims’ feet. Three unbroken ranks of Zodangan mercenaries lined every inch of the aisle on both sides, a steel wall between us and the rest of the ten thousand spectators packed shoulder to shoulder in the temple’s circular coliseum.

When we stepped up onto the raised dais called the Throne of Righteousness, my blood ran cold at the sight of the 31 judges assembled in a great ring around the platform’s edge. By Barsoomian custom they were supposed to be drawn by lot from Helium’s noble class, to judge their fellow nobles fairly—but not one friendly face looked back at me. Almost to a man they were Zodangans, every last one seething with the old, bitter grudge of the day I’d led Tars Tarkas’ green hordes over their walls, sacked their avenues, and dragged their proud city into vassalage to Helium. There would be no justice for John Carter here, nor for my son Carthoris, nor for the great Thark warlord who’d overrun their streets.

The temple had hummed with low, restless conversation until that moment, but the second we halted on the dais, a silence as thick as the cold vacuum of Barsoom’s empty dead sea beds fell over the crowd. Our guards retreated to the foot of the dais stairs, leaving us facing the ring of judges and the thousands of eyes fixed on us, our backs to the tiny Pedestal of Truth set dead-center in the platform. Zat Arras, the pretender Jed of Helium who’d usurped power in Tardos Mors’ absence, sat perched in the gilded presiding magistrate’s chair, his cruel red lips curling as he called my name.

“John Carter, once Prince of Helium,” he boomed, his voice echoing off the domed ceiling, “step to the Pedestal of Truth, that you may be judged for your deeds, and receive the reward you have earned.” He turned to address the crowd, his tone rising to a fiery, accusatory pitch. “Judges, people of Helium! This man admits he has returned from the forbidden Valley Dor, from the very inner sanctum of Issus herself! In front of dozens of your own citizens he has blasphemed against Iss, against the Lost Sea of Korus, against the Holy Therns, and against Issus, Goddess of Death and Life Eternal. The ancient law of Barsoom is clear: he who has passed through the gates of the afterlife may not return. Any man who violates that sanctity must be made dead forever. What sentence do you pass on this blasphemer?”

“Death!” the shout ripped from the throat of a Zodangan judge, and a roar of agreement rose from his colleagues before anyone else could speak.

Then a man in the crowd vaulted over the low barrier separating the audience from the aisle, his sword drawn high over his head. It was Kantos Kan, the loyal head of Helium’s navy. “Justice!” he screamed. “Justice! Justice!” He dodged past the stunned Zodangan soldiers and leaped clean onto the Throne of Righteousness, facing Zat Arras down. “You dare call this a trial? The man has not spoken a single word in his own defense, not been allowed to call a single witness! In the name of all Helium, I demand fair treatment for your prince.”

Ten thousand voices roared back his cry, the stone floors shaking under the weight of it, and Zat Arras dared not refuse. “Speak then,” he snarled at me, “but utter no blasphemy against Barsoom’s sacred truths.”

I turned my back on the sneering Zodangan judges, and spoke directly to the crowd. “Men of Helium, I do not beg justice from the men of Zodanga. I do not ask it. I speak to you, not for myself, but for your wives, your daughters, your children yet unborn. I have seen what waits for them beyond the River Iss. I have seen the coiling, sucking tentacles of the plant men that drag pilgrims down to be devoured. I have seen the fangs of the great white apes of Dor. I have seen the Holy Therns drag your fairest women off to their mountain lairs, to be tormented and killed for sport. The afterlife your priests tell you of is a lie, a cruel sham built on the blood of your loved ones.”

I went on, recounting my days as a stranger from Earth, fighting beside their people, sacrificing my own joy to save Helium from certain destruction years before. For two full hours I spoke, and the crowd hung on every word, their cheers shaking the temple so hard the guttering incense torches flickered on their brackets. A great noble from the far tiers shouted “Speak on, John Carter, Prince of Helium!” and the whole temple echoed him.

But when the last word left my mouth, Zat Arras sneered and turned to his judges. “You have heard his so-called defense. He has used every second he was given to spew more blasphemy. What is your verdict?”

“Death to the blasphemer!” the first judge roared, and in a heartbeat all 31 were on their feet, their swords raised high in unanimous, glittering agreement.

A low, sullen murmur rippled through the crowd, swelling fast to a roar of rage. Kantos Kan, who had stood at my side the whole time, threw up a hand for silence. “You see the fate the Zodangan occupiers would serve on your greatest hero!” he called. “Every man here may choose what to do. This is the answer of Kantos Kan, High Admiral of Helium’s Navy, to Zat Arras.” He unbuckled his sword, and laid it clattering at my feet.

Hundreds of Helium’s citizens, soldiers and nobles and laborers alike, surged past the stunned Zodangan line, swarming up onto the dais, until a hundred sharp steel blades lay in a glittering pile at my boots. Zat Arras turned purple with rage, screaming for his Zodangan soldiers to arrest us, but the whole temple drew their blades in answer. A shout went up that split the air: “Tardos Mors is dead! A thousand years to John Carter, Jeddak of Helium!”

I saw a civil war seconds away, brother slaying brother in the temple, and I leaped back onto the Pedestal of Truth, screaming for silence. “Hold! Not a single sword moves! A single blow here today will tear Helium apart for generations. No man’s life is worth that. I will not be the cause of blood between our own people. We will strike a bargain. The sentence stands, but no execution will be carried out for one full year—until Tardos Mors, or his son Mors Kajak, returns to Helium. They will judge me fairly.” I leaned close to Zat Arras, my voice low and sharp enough to cut steel. “You take this offer, or this crowd tears you and every last one of your Zodangan guards to shreds right now. You have ten seconds to choose.”

Zat Arras paled. He turned to the crowd, and yelled the respite, ordering them to disperse. They did not move, every eye fixed on me, waiting for my order to attack. I stepped to the edge of the dais, and bade them go home peacefully. As one man, they turned, filing out silent and still raging, past the Zodangan guards who could do nothing but glower in helpless fury.

Kantos Kan led us out of the temple, down the Aisle of Hope, a hundred loyal Heliumites flanking us. The crowds in the city streets lined the route to my palace, flinging their swords at my feet in token of their loyalty, my old slaves rushing to kiss my hands the second we crossed the gates. They cared nothing for the blasphemy charges, only that their prince had come home.

The welcome feast was a somber, quiet affair, held in my great dining hall around the triangular table custom built for three: Carthoris, Dejah Thoris, and myself. Dejah’s carved high-backed throne sat empty at the third side, draped in her glittering wedding jewels, a slave standing behind it just as he had when she sat laughing in that seat, her bright voice ringing through the hall. Tars Tarkas, his bulk easily filling the oversized carved chair at Dejah’s right hand, had just learned that his own daughter Sola had followed Dejah Thoris on her pilgrimage to Dor. No flicker of grief crossed his green, hard features, but I knew the pain in his chest matched my own. No man spoke much: every heart was heavy with Dejah’s absence, and fear for the missing Jeddak Tardos Mors.

Then we heard it: distant shouting, swelling fast, growing louder until the whole palace shook with it. A slave burst into the hall screaming, “A messenger! A messenger from the Valley Dor! Dejah Thoris is found!”

I did not waste a single second walking all the way around the three-hundred-foot long table. I bounded, cleared the entire spread of dishes and diners in a single leap, and crashed through the far windows onto the balcony, vaulted thirty feet down to the scarlet lawn, and sprinted for the gates. The rider dismounting from the great thoat was Sola, her green face streaked with tears.

“Where is the Princess?” I cried, skidding to a halt before her.

“O my Prince,” she sobbed, running to me, “she is gone. The black pirates of Barsoom stole her. Even now she may be a prisoner on the lesser moon, and we may never get her back.”

The cool, mineral-thick air of the Helium palace hall wrapped around us as I hauled Sola past the marble pillars, her green Martian hide still dust-stained and crumpled from her weeks of flight. She paused first to greet her father Tars Tarkas, bowing low in the rigid, time-honored formal custom of the Thark green men—tapping her tusks to the hilt of his great sword, the quiet gesture heavy with the weight of the unspoken peril she had survived. When we settled in the grand dining hall, lamps strung along the walls glowing with soft Barsoomian bioluminescence, she spoke, and the story she wove of Dejah Thoris’s pilgrimage and capture hung thick in the air like the smoke of burning rykor.

“Seven days ago, right after Dejah Thoris’s private audience with Zat Arras, she slipped out of the palace in the dead of moonless night. I never heard exactly what was said between her and the false regent, but I saw the black, hopeless pain etched into her face for hours after, and when I caught her creeping toward the outer gates with only Woola the great calot hound padding silent at her heels, I knew exactly where she meant to go: the long, suicidal pilgrimage south to the Sacred Iss, the Valley Dor that every Barsoomian has been taught holds the afterlife of Issus.

“I did not hesitate. I roused a dozen of her most loyal personal guards in minutes, laid out my fear that she meant to die rather than face whatever fate Zat Arras had plotted for her, and every single one of them swore to follow her, even to the forbidden Dor. We overtook her less than a mile outside the palace walls. She raged, pretended fury, ordered us back at sword point, but for the first time in her life her command fell on deaf ears. When she saw we would not leave her to walk that final path alone, she broke down weeping, embraced each of us, and we turned south together, riding hard under the first faint light of the rising moon.

“On the second day we stumbled on a wild herd of small, fast thoats, and after that we covered hundreds of haads a day, racing straight south, until the dawn of the fifth day broke over a great armada of black-painted battleships sailing north over the dead sea bottoms. They spotted us before we could duck into the shelter of the low hills, and hundreds of the black pirates of the First Born swarmed down on us on fliers. Dejah’s twelve guards fought till their last breath, cutting down twice their number before they fell. Only the Princess and I were spared.

“When Dejah realized she was in their clutches, she drove her dagger toward her own heart before any man could stop her—she would have died rather than be their prisoner— but a black warrior wrenched the blade from her hand at the last second, and they bound our wrists tight so we could not strike at ourselves or our captors.

“The fleet turned north then, carrying us with them: twenty massive warships, plus a dozen of the small, fast scout cruisers. That evening one of those cruisers returned to the fleet with a second captive, a young red woman they had grabbed from a mountain range right under the noses of three patrolling Helium warships. From the scraps of talk we overheard between the pirates, they had been hunting a band of fugitives that had escaped them days earlier, and they treated this new prisoner like a prize of the highest order. Their fleet commander spent an hour in closed, urgent talk with her before they dragged her to our cell and bound her alongside us.

“She was Thuvia, Princess of Ptarth. She told Dejah she had set out on her own voluntary pilgrimage years prior, and that only that very morning she had stood beside John Carter—you, my prince— and your son Carthoris. Dejah would not believe her at first, until Thuvia recounted every wild, impossible adventure you had lived in the hidden domain of the First Born, every daring escape, every battle, and Dejah knew. ‘There is no other man on all of Barsoom who could have done the things you describe,’ she wept. When Thuvia spoke of her own quiet, honorable love for you, and your unshakable loyalty to your Princess, Dejah broke, cursing Zat Arras and the cruel fate that had driven her to flee Helium only days before you returned. She told Thuvia she held no grudge for that love, that she could see how pure and true it was.

“The fleet kept sailing north, nearly to the walls of Helium, before they realized you had slipped their trap after all, and turned south again. Not long after, a black guard wrenched me out of our cell and dragged me to the deck. ‘No green vermin gets to see the Land of the First Born,’ he snarled, and threw me as hard as he could over the rail.

“I thought I was dead. The fleet had been cruising thousands of feet above the dead sea floor all day, and I expected to smash to pulp on the rocks below. But fate favored me. The ship had drifted so low in the total dark that its keel was nearly brushing the crest of a high, hidden mountain peak, and I fell barely twenty feet into the thick, soft ochre moss that grows on the highest Barsoomian summits. I was knocked half unconscious, but only bruised. I lay there all night, and at sunrise I saw the whole vast expanse of the dead sea floor spread out thousands of feet below me, and understood the miracle that had spared me. I walked west for half a day until I reached a grand canal flying the crimson and gold banner of Helium, seized a stray thoat, and rode straight back here. The rest you know.”

No one spoke for long minutes. Dejah Thoris, my princess, in the clutches of the mad goddess Issus and her bloodthirsty First Born followers? A cold rage surged through my veins, then burned away to the old, unbreakable fire that had carried me across a dozen battlefields on two worlds. I sprung to my feet, my long sword flashing as I drew it, the tip pointing to the vaulted ceiling, and swore a solemn oath that I would cross the hidden sea Omean, storm the very gates of the Valley Dor, and tear Issus from her throne if that was what it took to bring my Dejah home. A hundred swords screamed out of their scabbards a heartbeat later. Red and green warriors alike leapt up onto the table tops, their blades raised high, and swore to give me their lives, their ships, their every last breath to make that rescue happen.

I thanked each of my loyal men, left Carthoris to host the crowd of cheering fighting men, and withdrew to my private audience chamber with Kantos Kan, Tars Tarkas, Xodar the renegade First Born, and Hor Vastus to hammer out the details of our campaign. We talked long past midnight, the plans all falling into place. Xodar confirmed that Issus would almost certainly select both Dejah and Thuvia to serve as her personal handmaidens for a full year, a grace period that would keep them alive and relatively unharmed while we prepared our invasion.

The work of refitting our warships to sail the hidden, waterlogged depths of Omean fell to Kantos Kan and Xodar. Xodar had spent decades refitting captured red Martian battleships for the First Born, modifying them with sealed hulls and powerful underwater propellers to navigate the secret sea, so he knew every inch of the required design. With total secrecy required to keep our plans hidden from Zat Arras’s spies, we calculated the work would take six full months.

“Zat Arras’s greed has eaten all of his sense now,” Kantos Kan warned us. “Nothing short of the title of Jeddak of all Helium will sate him. He would not even welcome Dejah’s return, not when it puts her next in line for the throne ahead of him. With you and Carthoris dead, there is nothing stopping him from seizing power entirely. He will hunt you both to the ends of Barsoom to make that happen.”

Hor Vastus grinned, leaning forward. “There is a way to crush his claim forever.” When we pressed him, he said, low and quiet, “Crown John Carter Jeddak of Helium. The entire navy, half the army, all the green hordes would follow him in a heartbeat. Zat Arras would have no leg to stand on.”

Every eye in the room turned to me, bright with approval. But I shook my head. “No,” I said. “Not yet. Until we know for certain that Tardos Mors and Mors Kajak are never coming home, I will not steal their throne. The people of Helium will choose their next Jeddak freely when the time comes, and whoever they pick will have my sword’s full loyalty. But until then, Zat Arras is no more than a regent, and we will bide our time.”

Hor Vastus opened his mouth to reply, then froze, his head snapping toward the open balcony overlooking the gardens. He pressed a finger to his lips. “Did you hear that?” He was over the rail and onto the balcony before any of us could move. “Spy! Guards, below!”

We were right on his heels, and we all saw the figure, cloaked in dark silk, dart across the moonlit sward and vanish into the thick shrubbery. We scoured the entire palace grounds with every man of the guard for hours after, but we found no trace of him.

“Zat Arras’s man, no doubt,” Tars Tarkas rumbled.

“Let’s pray he only heard us talking about crowning a new Jeddak,” I said. “If he overheard our plan to sail for Omean, he will try to stop us, even if it means tearing Helium apart in civil war. I will not be stopped, not by anyone, not even by Tardos Mors himself if he stands between me and Dejah. If I die before we can rescue her, will you all swear to finish the mission, and bring my Princess home safe to her people?”

Every man there knelt, pressing his lips to the hilt of his drawn sword, and swore that oath.

We mapped out the rest of our tasks before we adjourned. The battleships slated for refit would be sent to the secret docks of Hastor, a remote Heliumite city far to the southwest, where they could be modified in total seclusion. Tars Tarkas would travel back to Thark, rally his entire horde of green warriors, and assemble thousands of transport ships to sail straight for the Valley Dor once we struck. Hor Vastus would build a secret army of a million loyal men, all sworn to follow me anywhere, to man our thousand warships and our transport fleets.

When they all left, I bid Carthoris good night, exhausted at last, and stumbled to my private chambers, looking forward to the first full night’s sleep I had known since I had stepped back onto Barsoom.

I never got that sleep. I woke suddenly, half-drugged, to find half a dozen powerful men pinning me to my sleeping silks, a gag already jammed in my mouth, my arms and legs bound so tight I could not twitch. They worked so fast, so silent, that I had no chance to draw my sword or cry out. Their faces were all swathed in layers of dark silk— I could not recognize a single one of them. They lifted me like a sack of grain, carried me down the corridor, and stopped at a hidden secret panel I had thought no one outside my most intimate inner circle knew about. The lead man pressed the hidden button without a moment’s hesitation, the panel swung open, and they dragged me down a winding, unlit stairway deep into the ancient pits beneath the palace.

He rapped three quick times on a blank wall, paused, rapped three more times, paused, then rapped twice. The stone swung inward, and they pushed me sprawling onto the floor of a brilliantly lit, richly decorated chamber. Three men sat on a carved obsidian dais at the far end. One turned to face me, a thin, cruel, sardonic grin spreading across his pale lips. It was Zat Arras.

“Ah,” purred Zat Arras, his feline smile sharp as a radium blade, “to what happy accident do I owe this unlooked-for visit from the runaway Prince of Helium?”
The guards had already torn the gag from my jaws, but I said nothing. I stood tall, gaze locked level on the treacherous Jed of Zodanga, the full weight of my unmasked contempt curling my lip. The rest of the chamber’s attendants glanced back and forth between us, until a hot, crawling flush of anger crept up Zat Arras’ neck and stained his cheeks.
“Leave us,” he snapped at the last of my escorts. When only his two closest confidants and I remained, his voice dropped to glacial, deliberate pauses, each word weighed and sharpened like a throwing knife.
“John Carter,” he said, “custom, the sacred edicts of the First Born, and a full, impartial court have all condemned you to die. The people of Helium cannot save you. *I* alone can. Your life, your freedom, all of it lies in the hollow of my hand. I could slit your throat this instant and no soul would ever know. Or I could walk you out of this palace unshackled this very minute.
“Free for a year, as your reprieve promised, the Helium mob will forget all about that silly execution sentence. They’ll cheer for you, follow you, never dream of laying a hand on you again.
“You’ll have that freedom in two minutes. For one condition. Tardos Mors, Mors Kajak, and Dejah Thoris never come back to Helium. The nation picks a new Jeddak within the year. And that Jeddak is Zat Arras. Swear your loyalty to my cause. That is the price. I have spoken.”
I knew well the cruelty coiled under Zat Arras’ smooth words. If he killed me, there would be no one left to stop him from seizing Helium’s throne. If I lived, I could still hunt for Dejah Thoris, lead the rescue our comrades had spent months planning. To refuse outright meant my death, and very likely Dejah’s too – left to the white apes in Issus’ arena, all for my pride.
I hesitated for all of a heartbeat. The princess of a thousand Jeddaks would never barter her honor to a traitor. Neither could John Carter barter the honor of Helium for my own life.
“There is no alliance possible,” I told him, “between a man who stabs his own Jeddak in the back and a prince of the House of Tardos Mors. And I do not believe your lies about the great Jeddak being dead.”
Zat Arras shrugged, unimpressed. “Your opinions will not matter for much longer, John Carter. By nightfall you will be in the pits, to rot in dark and silence until you see the wisdom of accepting my offer. If you refuse long enough, a dagger will come out of the blackness one day, no warning, no sound, and you will never see the light of Barsoom again.”
He clapped his hands. The guards returned at once.
“To the pits,” he waved a dismissive hand at me, and that was all. Four armed men marched me out, a single glowing radium handlight leading the way down winding, endless tunnels, deeper and deeper beneath Helium’s bedrock.
At last we stopped in a rough hewn rock chamber, iron rings bolted into the walls, each one strung with a heavy chain. Skeletons still clad in tattered armor hung from their ends. One guard kicked a yellowed skull aside, unlocked the massive padlock fastening a chain to a long-dead warrior’s ankle, and snapped the iron band tight around my calf. Then they turned, took the light, and left me alone.
Utter, absolute darkness swallowed every trace of them. For a minute I could hear the faint clink of their harness fading down the corridor, until even that died. The silence was as total as the dark, and I was left with nothing but my ghoulish roommates – the bones of men who had suffered exactly the fate that waited for me. I did not know how long I stood there, straining my ears for any sound at all, before I sank to the cold stone floor, rested my head against the rock wall, and fell asleep.
I woke hours later to find a young man standing over me, holding a light in one hand and a bowl of thin, grey prison gruel in the other.
“Zat Arras sends his greetings,” he said, quiet and polite. “He knows all about the plot to name you Jeddak of Helium, but his offer still stands. All you need do is tell me you accept his terms, and you will walk free.”
I only shook my head. He set the bowl down beside me, and left, taking the light with him.
Twice a day, every single day for months, that same boy came to my cell, food in hand, the same unchanging message from Zat Arras. I tried for weeks to draw him into conversation, to learn anything about the world above, but he would not speak. At last I gave up – but never stopped working. Every waking minute, I scraped away at a single link of my chain, grinding it thin against the rough rock of the wall, hoping to wear it through. I was half mad with worry for Dejah Thoris, for Carthoris, for Kantos Kan and Tars Tarkas, not knowing if they were all dead or prisoners in Zat Arras’ other pits. The spy that had overheard us planning the rescue must have told Zat Arras every last detail.
The young guard was handsome, about the same height and age as my son Carthoris, and his shabby, rusted harness did not match the quiet dignity of his bearing. I hatched a gambit.
“You have been kinder to me than any man in this prison,” I told him on his next visit. “I will not live much longer, and I want to repay that kindness. In the guard wing of my palace there are harnesses fit for a prince. Go there, pick the one you like best. It is yours. All I ask is that you wear it, so I know my gift was received.”
His eyes lit up, and he glanced down at his own tattered trappings, hesitating. “If I went to your palace asking for that, they would laugh me off the steps and throw me in the street. If Zat Arras even suspected I was thinking of it, he would carve my heart out.”
“Come with a note from me, at night,” I urged him. “You can read the whole thing before you deliver it, there is no harm to Zat Arras in it. My son Carthoris will give you the harness, and the jeweled short sword I took from the northern Jeddak I killed years ago. There will not be a finer accoutred warrior in all Zodanga. Bring writing materials when you come next, and you will have both by sundown.”
He stood silent for a long minute, thinking, then turned and left me without a word. I waited for hours, sick with hope and fear. If he delivered my note, it would mean Carthoris was alive and free. If he came back wearing that harness, Carthoris would know exactly where I was, and that I still lived.
When his footsteps echoed down the tunnel on his next visit, I could barely contain my excitement. He set the gruel down beside me – and dropped a set of writing materials next to it. My heart soared. I scribbled a short, simple order for Carthoris to give the young man, Parthak, the harness and the jeweled sword I had promised. That was all, but it held my whole life in those few lines. Parthak picked the note up, and left without a sound.
By my count, I had been in the pits 300 days. Dejah Thoris had less than two months left before Issus would sentence her to the arena.
The next footsteps I heard down the tunnel did not belong to Parthak. It was a different guard, sullen, silent, who would not answer a single one of my questions. Day after day he came, dropped my food, and left without a word. Parthak was gone. I did not know if he had betrayed me to Zat Arras, if he was dead, or if Carthoris had found him. 330 days since I was locked up. 30 days left before they threw Dejah Thoris to the white apes in Issus’ arena, before her body was dragged through the dust, cut up, and served as meat to the black nobles of the First Born. I buried my face in my arms, and for the first time in a hundred years of fighting across two worlds, I almost wept. The black despair of that dark rock cell swallowed me whole.
I would escape. No more waiting. When my next jailer knelt to check on me, I would kill him with the chain, and fight my way out to the surface. I threw myself on the floor, twisting my body into a frozen, limp pose as if I had died of a convulsion, the slack of the heavy chain coiled tight in my right hand.
Footsteps drew closer. He stopped over me. Muttered a curse. Stepped forward. Knelt beside me. I tensed, opened my eyes – and swung the heavy chain as hard as I could, only to see the face of my own son, Carthoris, inches from mine.
I had checked the blow at the very last split second, but the impact still sent me blacking out across his body, sick with the horror of what I had almost done.
When I came to, a cool hand was pressed to my forehead. I forced my eyes open, terrified of what I might see – and Carthoris was leaning over me, a dark purple bruise blooming on his forehead where the chain had struck, alive, whole, grinning down at me. I pulled him into my arms, and thanked whatever god watches over lost souls on Mars with every fiber of my being.
“How did you find me?” I asked, once I could speak.
“Parthak,” he laughed. “We thought you dead, until he showed up at the palace asking for that harness you promised him. I gave him the harness and the sword, but he still would not tell me where you were – he was fiercely loyal to Zat Arras, would not crack for any bribe or threat. So Xodar came up with a trick. We had Hor Vastus shave his hair, put on a Zodangan private’s armor, and get locked in the cell right next to Parthak. Fifteen days they spent together in the dark, until Parthak trusted him completely, and told him exactly where your cell was hidden. It was easy to pull the old pit blueprints from the archives. I had to slip past the guards at the pit entrance, and the Zodangan sentry saw me. I had no choice but to kill him. So here I am – nearly brained by my own father, for my trouble.”
As he spoke, he picked the padlock on my chains, and the heavy iron band clattered to the floor, free. He handed me a long-sword and a sharp stabbing dagger, and we set off back up the tunnels. We hid the dead sentry’s body in a tiny side cell to delay the search parties, and half an hour later, we stepped out of the pit entrance right into my palace’s audience chamber. Kantos Kan, Tars Tarkas, Hor Vastus, and Xodar were all waiting there, jumping to their feet the second they saw me.
No time for stories. I demanded to know how the rescue plans were going.
“Everything is ready,” Kantos Kan said. “A thousand of the greatest battleships ever built on Barsoom are docked at Hastor, each fitted with both air and water propellers to sail the skies and dive straight down into the Omean sea. There are five ten-man cruisers, ten five-man scouts, a hundred one-man scouts on every ship – over a hundred thousand craft total. Nine hundred giant troop transports wait at Thark, loaded down with Tars Tarkas’ green warriors. We have been ready for seven days, waiting for you to be found so you could lead the raid.”
“The Thark council met with me, and they stand with you,” Tars Tarkas rumbled. “Two hundred and fifty thousand green fighting men from a hundred hordes spread all the way across Barsoom, from the northern ice cap to the south, are camped in Thark right now. They will fight for you, all they ask is the loot they take and a ride home when it is done.”
“Over a million veteran Helium man the ships,” Hor Vastus added. “Every single one of them sworn to secrecy. Not a whisper of our real mission has leaked to Zat Arras.”
“Good,” I said. “We leave for Hastor at once. We sail before sunrise tomorrow.”
“A cruiser is waiting at your private dock–” Kantos Kan started to say, but a sudden crackle of gunfire from the gardens outside cut his words short.
We all ran for the balcony in time to see a dozen of my palace guards chase a fleeing figure into the dark shrubbery. Down below, a handful of guards knelt over a motionless body sprawled on the crimson grass of the lawn. They lifted the man up and carried him into the audience chamber at my order, laying him out on the floor at our feet. He was a red man in his prime, wearing plain, unmarked soldier’s armor, meant to hide his identity.
“Another of Zat Arras’ spies,” Hor Vastus grunted.
“Take the body away,” I ordered the guards.
“Wait,” Xodar held up a hand. “Prince, send a man for a soft cloth and a flask of thoat oil. Do not move him yet.”

Two hours after I quit my palace in Helium, the red sun hanging low toward midnight, Kantos Kan, Xodar, and I set down our cruiser at Hastor. Carthoris, Tars Tarkas, and Hor Vastus had already peeled off on a separate warship, bound straight for Thark to rouse the green horde that would march with us against the gods of Barsoom.

The troop transports had orders to weigh anchor immediately and drift slowly south, to give the battleship fleet time to overhaul them at dawn of the second day. At Hastor, every last gear, every crate of rations, every charged ray cannon was already in place. So meticulous had Kantos Kan planned every thread of the campaign that ten minutes after our boots hit the dock, the first great battleship lifted gracefully into the thin dark sky. One a second after that, the rest of the fleet followed, spilling out over the southern wastes to form a silvery line that stretched miles long, curving toward our destination.

It was not until we had settled into Kantos Kan’s cabin that I thought to ask the date. Stuck in the absolute blackness of Zat Arras’s pits, I’d lost all track of time, and the thought that I might have miscalculated had been gnawing at the edge of my mind. When Kantos Kan spoke the number, a cold fist clamped around my heart. Three hundred and sixty-five days had passed by Earth’s reckoning. I was too late to save Dejah Thoris.

The mission was no longer a rescue. It was revenge. I said nothing to my friends of the blow. What use to heap my private grief on men who had already borne so many of my sorrows with me? This expedition could still do more than tear a path through the thern temples: it could rip away the cruel lie that had tricked generation after generation of Barsoom’s people into throwing their lives away on the false pilgrimage to Issus’s supposed paradise. If we could pry open the fertile Valley Dor, land that needed no irrigation to spill forth rich harvests, the broad unspoiled acres between the Otz Mountains and the northern ice cap would feed millions who starved on the dying planet’s parched outer plains. That stretch of land, the only place on Barsoom that had natural rain, dew, and an open freshwater sea, had been hoarded for eons by the therns and the First Born, barred to all the rest of the world by nothing but the weight of religious superstition. If I could break that barrier down, it would be a monument worthy of Dejah’s name. Her supposed martyrdom would not have been for nothing.

Dawn of the second day spilled pink over the ice cap, and we spotted the great spread of the transports and their screening convoy ships bobbing low over the frost. We drew close enough to exchange visual signals, for no Barsoomian commander ever dares send secret dispatches via radio-aerogram in wartime. Every cipher ever invented, every wireless transmission trick, has been cracked by rival powers so many times over centuries that no soul trusts a wireless message of any importance. Tars Tarkas flashed back that all was well with the green warriors crowded in the transports’ holds. Our battleships slid past them to take the vanguard, and the whole combined fleet hugged the ice surface so tight their keels stirred the fine frost dust, to dodge the eyes of thern lookouts posted on the northern ridges of Dor.

Thin lines of one-man scout fliers ranged far ahead of us, flanked our sides, and fanned out twenty miles behind the last transport to guard against surprise. We’d flown for hours this way, the cone-shaped peak that marked the mouth of the Omean shaft rising on the horizon ahead, when a scout from the left flank came screaming back for the flagship. His flier was moving so fast its hull glowed faint red from air friction. Kantos Kan and I waited for him on the forward open deck, the Barsoomian equivalent of an Earth battleship’s bridge. The tiny craft had barely touched the landing deck before the scout was bounding up the stairs, gasping out his report.

“A great fleet of battleships south-southeast, my Prince—thousands of them, bearing dead straight for us.”

“Thern spies in your Helium palace did not sneak in for nothing,” Kantos Kan said, turning to me. “Your orders, Prince.”

“Dispatch ten battleships to seal the Omean shaft. Let no hostile craft enter or leave that passage, and bottle up the entire First Born navy in their underground sea.” I snapped the orders out fast, my mind already mapping the battle plan. “Form every remaining battleship into a sharp V, apex pointed straight at the oncoming therns. The transports, wrapped tight in their convoy screen, will stick to the wake of the battleships until the point of the V punches clean through the thern line. Then the apex splits open, the two arms of the V tear hard to left and right, carving a broad lane through the enemy ranks. The transports sprint down that lane at full speed, and take up position directly above the therns’ glittering temple spires. Land every warrior there, and give the Holy Therns a lesson in ferocity they will not forget for a thousand years.”

Kantos Kan saluted and was barking the orders to his aides before the words were fully out of my mouth. In less than a minute the entire formation shifted. The ten ships assigned to the shaft were streaking south, the rest of the fleet surging forward like a pack of unleashed hounds. We hit the thern line so fast it was as unexpected as lightning out of a cloudless sky. Their ragged, three-deep battle formation stretched as far as the eye could see in either direction, but they had not had time to even warm their ray cannons. Our hulls sliced clean through their ranks, the V split open, and the transports poured through the gap to streak for the temples. By the time the disoriented therns managed to rally, a hundred thousand green warriors were already pouring over their gardens and temple courtyards, while another hundred and fifty thousand leaned out of the low-hanging transport hulls to pick off every thern soldier on the ramparts with their legendary, uncanny marksmanship.

Far above the din of the ground battle, the two massive fleets locked into the swirling circular dance that is the signature of Barsoomian aerial warfare. Kantos Kan’s ships looped round and round each other’s tracks until they formed a near-perfect spinning death circle, moving at such blistering speed the therns could barely get a firing lock on them. Broadside after broadside tore through thern hulls, sending one enemy ship after another into that sickening, plummeting dive that marked total destruction, no survivors left to pull out. Slowly we maneuvered our death circle down over the thern gardens, flashed the recall signal for our green warriors, and pulled them aboard into the safe hollow at the center of the spinning ring.

The therns’ fire died off. They’d had enough, and seemed content to let us go on our way without further fight. But our luck was not going to hold. Barely had we turned our prows toward the Omean shaft again when a thick black line topped the northern horizon, stretching across the entire sky. No mistaking it: another war fleet. Before they were even close enough to make out our hull markings, Kantos Kan’s signal officer handed me a captured radio-aerogram. It read: *Surrender in the name of the Jeddak of Helium, for you cannot escape. Signed Zat Arras.*

The therns had intercepted the message the same second we did. Scenting that we were about to be crushed between two foes, they roared back into the fight, all guns blazing. Now we were sandwiched: therns ahead, Zat Arras’s five thousand warships at our backs. Our hulls reeled under the combined fire, ship after ship tearing open and spilling crewmen into the thin air. There was no way we could hold this for long. I sent a signal down to the transports: descend back to the gardens, wreak every last ounce of vengeance they could, for none of us would be going back across the northern ice cap alive.

Then the ten ships we’d sent to guard the Omean shaft came tearing back into view, their stern batteries spitting continuous fire, chased by the entire great fleet of the First Born. Our last hope was gone. I thought for a wild, bitter second that the curse of Issus hung over me, that some malign power in that hideous old goddess’s carcass had turned every possible chance against me. But then a thern battleship slammed broadside into our flagship, grappling irons biting into our hull, and white-robed thern boarders came swarming over the rail. I drew my longsword and leaped into the fray, my men roaring behind me. The old battle lust surged hot in my veins, and with every thern that fell beneath my blade, the old unbreakable hope I’d thought crushed flickered back to life. Our men tore through the boarders like a whirlwind, stormed the enemy’s deck, and the thern commander leaped from his prow in surrender rather than face our wrath.

Kantos Kan, who had watched the brawl, got a sudden idea. He ordered my personal standard—the red banner of the Prince of Helium, emblazoned with the gold sun of Helium’s house—run up every mast of the flagship, then flashed the order to the rest of our fleet. A great cheer went up that rippled across every remaining ship we had. Then Kantos Kan strung a huge, unmissable visual signal high on the flagship’s topmast, plain for every sailor in the sky to read: *MEN OF HELIUM, FOR THE PRINCE OF HELIUM, AGAINST ALL HIS ENEMIES.* One by one, Zat Arras’s ships began hauling my colours up their masts. The Heliumetic crews forced to serve under Zodangan occupation mutinied, fighting bloody deck battles against Zat Arras’s loyal soldiers, until only Zat Arras’s own flagship still flew his standard.

The sky was a churning brawl now, so packed with ships there was no room for fleet maneuvers at all. The therns and the First Born, hereditary enemies who had hated each other for thousands of years, had completely forgotten about us, turning their guns on each other the second they realized they were tangled together. That was our salvation. I flashed orders to every last one of my ships: peel away, slip west-southwest, draw any enemy that tried to chase you straight into the path of their old hereditary foe, then break off and fall back to our rally point. The stratagem worked flawlessly. By the time the red sun sank below the horizon, every remaining ship of my fleet had gathered twenty miles southwest of the still-roaring battle between therns and the black First Born navy.

I transferred Xodar to a command battleship, and sent him away with all the transports and the bulk of our remaining warships, bound straight for the Temple of Issus. Tars Tarkas’s green horde and Hor Vastus’s red Helium warriors, guided by Xodar, would land at dawn in the gardens of Issus. Carthoris, Kantos Kan, and I took our small, fast remaining squadron and headed for the mouth of the Omean shaft. Carthoris knew every tunnel and underground passage leading from the submerged sea straight up into the pits beneath the Temple of Issus, and we would strike for the heart of the temple through that hidden route.

I learned then why our ten ships had fled the shaft mouth: the entire First Born navy had already been pouring out of it when they arrived. Twenty of our men’s ships had fought a holding action as long as they could, but the odds were far too great, and they had been forced to run. We crept up on the shaft mouth under cover of full dark. Carthoris took his one-man scout flier ahead alone to reconnoiter, and returned half an hour later to report no enemy patrols, no guards, nothing. We slipped forward to the shaft, lined all our ships up at the mouth, then the flagship dove down into the absolute black of the great tunnel, the rest of the fleet following one by one in her wake. We left no guard at the shaft mouth. There was no point; our entire force could not have held off the returning First Born navy if they caught us. We gambled that the guards posted at Omean would mistake us for their own returning fleet, and the bet paid off. Four hundred of our five hundred ships were floating unchallenged on the dark surface of the buried sea before a single cannon shot was fired. The First Born had left nothing but a handful of ancient, obsolete hulks to guard their harbor, secure in their delusion no enemy would ever dare slip past the therns. We towed the captured hulks back to the shaft, wedged a dozen of them tight inside the narrow tunnel mouth, then activated the buoyance rays on the rest so they would drift up and jam the passage even tighter when the returning fleet tried to force its way back in.

I took a strong raiding party straight to the small island that held the First Born’s secret submarine, seized the tiny guard force without a fight, and secured the vessel. The sub’s commander, Yersted, recognized me from my days as a prisoner of the First Born. He smiled a grim, knowing smile when he saw me. “You do not hold us for long, John Carter,” he said. “We knew you were coming.”

I asked him, half-afraid to hear the answer, if he had ever transported a woman called Dejah Thoris to the Temple of Issus. His face softened. “I saw her yesterday, at the monthly rites. She sat at the foot of Issus, as she always does.”

A roar of joy exploded in my chest. I had been an idiot! I had counted the days as Earth days, not Martian ones. A Martian year is six hundred and eighty-seven days long. Three hundred and sixty-some Earth days was barely half a Martian year. I was not too late. I was in time. But Yersted shook his head, his face grave. “Issus knows you march for her temple. If you so much as step foot in the outer courtyards, she will lock Dejah Thoris in the Temple of the Sun. Six hundred and eighty-seven chambers, one stacked below another. A single corridor leads to each, and the whole temple revolves once every full Martian day. Each corridor’s mouth only lines up with the exit to the outer world once every six hundred and eighty-seven days. You will never reach her for a full year, by which time—”

He did not need to finish the sentence. But the fire in my veins was still burning. Dejah was alive. And I would tear every rock in that temple apart, if that was what it took, to get to her before the year was out.

Yersted’s warning burned hot in my bones: there was not a single heartbeat to waste. Dawn would break before long, and Tars Tarkas’s horde of Green Martian warriors would hurl themselves at the Temple of Issus’s outer ramparts. If I did not slip past its sealed, accursed walls before that assault began, Dejah Thoris would be dust on the altar long before my old Thark friend ever scaled a single battlement. I had five thousand seasoned red Heliumite fighters at my back, every one of them forged in a hundred border wars, and I was certain we could cut through the temple’s guard lines like a radium blade through vellum to tear my princess free.

The moment Carthoris and his scouting party rejoined us, we began ferrying every man through the submerged tunnel, shuttling them across the sunken Omean basin to the secret gangway mouth that opened from the temple’s underwater pool straight into the labyrinth of Issus’s pits. It took dozens of trips, but at last we all stood packed in that low, stone-walled chamber, staring down the final leg of our months-long quest. Carthoris was the only soul alive who knew the hidden twist of these tunnels, so we could not split our force to strike the temple from multiple sides as we had planned. We formed up in tight column of twos, ready to follow him as fast as the narrow passages would let us, straight to the temple’s heart.

We were seconds from stepping into the lead corridor when a dwar of the 3rd Utan called my eye to the dark water sloshing around the hull of our last submarine. At first I thought a second enemy sub was rising beneath us to cut off our retreat. Then I saw it: the whole surface was climbing. Slow, unrelenting, no wave, no ripple of a moving hull, just the water line creeping higher, soon to spill over the pool’s edges and submerge every flat stone floor in the chambers around us.

For a few stunned seconds I missed the full horror of it. Carthoris saw it immediately.
“Haste!” he screamed, his voice raw. “The Omean pumps are sabotaged. They mean to drown us all like rats in a trap. If we don’t hit the upper pits above the flood line right now, none of us will ever see the sun again. Move!”
“Lead on, boy!” I shouted back. “We are right behind you.”

He bolted into the corridor first, the columns filing after him in perfect order, each company only moving forward on its captain’s sharp command. Before the last utan had cleared the chamber, the water was up to our ankles. Red Martians almost never encountered standing water deeper than their hip, save for the small bathing pools and drinking cisterns of their cities, and every man flinched from the cold, churning dark creeping around his boots. Not one of them broke ranks, though—their discipline and courage held strong, even when the current tugged at their greaves.

I was the last man to leave the submarine chamber, wading through water up to my knees as I brought up the rear of the column. The corridor was level with the pool for dozens of yards, so the flood rose just as fast there. No matter how fast the men marched, we could not outpace it. I knew the physics of Barsoom’s sealed domed basins well: as the water level climbed up Omean’s curved dome, the empty space left for it to fill shrank, making its rise speed exponentially. Long before the front of the column could reach the high, dry upper pits, the flood would surge over the top of every man in the lower passages, snuff out half our force before they even saw a single priest of Issus.

Panic was rippling through the last ranks now. A mad stampede would pile hundreds of men on top of each other in the narrow corridor, trample them under the flood, and clog the passage tight enough to trap every soul ahead. I spotted a steep, upward-sloping side corridor cut into the right wall, and bellowed over the roar of the rising water for the rear companies to peel off. Nearly three thousand men turned at the order, pushing back through the current to crowd into the new passage. Gur Tus, the battle-hardened dwar of the 10th Utan, and Djor Kantos—son of my old friend Kantos Kan, a padwar barely out of his teens—paused to stand rear guard with me. We dragged floundering, drowning men back to their feet, held the line against the rising tide until the very last of them had scrambled up the side corridor. By the time we turned to follow, the water was surging around our necks.

A hundred yards up the steep grade, we were above the flood line entirely. We panted for breath, pressed on fast toward what I thought were the upper pits leading directly to the temple, when a scream of “Fire!” tore down the corridor ahead. Then came choking shouts, the sharp snap of officers yelling orders, and a wave of thick, acrid chemical smoke billowed back at us, blinding every man in its path.

The First Born had laid a second trap. They had ignited stores of incendiary powder in the passage ahead, and we were wedged tight between walling flames in front and a rising flood behind. There was no choice but to break off onto the first new upward gallery I could spot, herding as many men as possible down it to escape the smoke. When the last of the two thousand men who had followed me through the side passage had cleared the turn, I raced back past the smoke to the edge of the fire, just to make sure no wounded soldier had been left to burn alive. The heat seared my throat, but I confirmed no one lay trapped there between us and the roaring chemical inferno, and turned to run back to my men.

A solid, cold steel grating slammed down across the corridor mouth before I could step through. I was trapped.

There was no doubt left now. The First Born had watched every single move we made, the second our fleet was spotted off Omean’s shores. They had stopped the pumps exactly when we reached the far end of the tunnel, lit the incendiary fires the exact second we were all clustered in the lower pits, and dropped that gate the second I stepped away from my men. They had orchestrated every single second of our destruction, invisible eyes following every footfall. I berated myself for walking straight into the obvious snare, for not bringing the whole assault down on the temple’s outer walls from the valley side instead of sneaking through the tunnels. It was too late now. The smoke was pushing me back down the dark corridor, no torches left, no glowing phosphorescent rock to light my way—proof I was almost directly under the temple, in the unlit pits the priests used for their secret rites.

Soon the cold lapping water of Omean was curling around my ankles. The smoke burned my lungs, and I had nowhere left to go but into the dark flood. I waded in, letting the water close over my shoulders, and swam out into total blackness. The instinct to survive burned hot even there, even when I was sure I would drown in that lightless grave. I kept swimming until my head cracked against the rocky ceiling: the corridor dipped down into a full submerged stretch, who knew how long, no air pocket anywhere. I filled my lungs to bursting, dove, and pulled through the icy dark. Over and over I stretched my hand up, only to hit solid rock. My lungs were screaming, starved for air, when at last my hand broke through empty space. I could breathe.

I dragged myself up onto the dry stone floor, gasping, and ran as fast as I could toward the first door I spotted leading up toward the temple levels above. I did not care where it led. I would kill Issus herself first, before I died, if I could not reach Dejah Thoris in time. I burst through the short, steep upward incline, threw open the unbarred doorway at its top, and stumbled into a brilliantly lit, richly draped chamber.

A woman lay on a low couch on the far side, half-asleep. I thought for a wild, feral second that it was Issus, unguarded, that I could take her hostage and force the priests to hand Dejah Thoris over unharmed. I crept toward her, silent as a Thark stalking prey, and she stirred, rose, and turned to face me.

Terror blazed across her face first, then shock, then a bright, overflowing joy that I would have sworn only my princess could ever wear. My heart swelled so big I thought it would burst through my ribs. I crossed the room in three strides, and my arms closed around Dejah Thoris, the woman I had crossed dead seas and burning pits to find, safe at last.

“John Carter—John Carter,” Dejah Thoris sobbed, her dear head pressed to my shoulder, the faint, familiar sandalwood of her Barsoomian hair tangled against the tattered crimson of my warrior tunic. Even now I can scarce believe my own eyes, she told me. When Thuvia of Ptarth first babbled that you had somehow crossed the gulf of space back to Barsoom, I could not let myself hope. Too many long cycles of silent, lonely imprisonment had worn away every last scrap of faith that happiness could ever find me again. When I finally accepted the news was true, I thought even then that no man, not even you, could ever breach the hidden, guarded walls of the Temple of Issus to reach me.

Moon after moon drifted by, no rumour of rescue reaching my cell, and I resigned myself to being a plaything for the mad false goddess forever. For an hour before you burst through that door, I heard the clash of fighting echo through the halls. I did not know what it meant, but I clung to the faintest, foolish hope that the men of Helium were coming, led by their prince. Tell me—what of our son, Carthoris?”

“He fought beside me less than an hour ago, my Princess,” I answered, pressing my lips to her forehead. “It was his warriors you heard cutting their way through the temple precincts. Where is Issus now?”

Dejah Thoris’s shoulders lifted in a weary, bitter shrug. “She dragged me under guard to this room right as the first fighting broke out, saying she would send for me later. She was shaking, John Carter—wild with anger and raw, unmasked fear. In half a year of watching her cruel, capricious reign, I never saw her act so unhinged. Now I know she learned you were coming to call her to account for stealing Helium’s princess.”

The din of battle rolled through the halls: the clash of obsidian blades against steel, the war cries of red Heliumites, the thunder of booted feet over polished marble. I knew my men needed me on the front lines, but I could not bring myself to drag Dejah Thoris into that roiling, bloody chaos, and I could not bear to leave her unguarded out in the open. Then I thought of the shadowed pits I had just climbed out of, hidden away from the main paths of the temple. I told her my plan to tuck her there until the fighting ended, so I could come back for her and carry her away from this cursed place forever.

For a heartbeat, she clung tighter to me. “I cannot bear to be parted from you, even for a single second,” she whispered. “I shudder to think of being alone here, where that monster Issus could find me. You have no idea of the cruelty she has shown, day in and day out, for half a year. No one who has not seen it for themselves could believe it possible.”

“Then I will not leave you at all,” I said, and began to turn toward the door to join the fighting with her at my side.

She was silent for a long moment, then she pulled my face down to hers and kissed me. “Go, John Carter,” she said, her voice steady even as her eyes glistened. “Our son is out there, fighting for the Princess of Helium beside our soldiers. Your duty is with them. I will not stand in the way of what you must do. Hide me in the pits, then go.”

I led her to the low door leading down to the tunnels below. I crushed her to my chest for one long, desperate moment, my gut twisted with a terrible, unplaceable foreboding, then guided her across the threshold, kissed her one last time, and pulled the heavy door shut behind her.

I did not hesitate a second longer, sprinting down the halls toward the loudest roar of battle. I had barely crossed six chambers before I rounded a corner and found the full force of the Black First Born massed at the mouth of a great hall, blocking the advance of a column of red warriors pushing toward Issus’s inner sacred precincts. Emerging from the tunnels as I did, I found myself directly at their rear. I did not stop to count their numbers, or consider how foolhardy it was for one man to charge a hundred armed black warriors alone. I drew my long steel sword, screamed “For Helium!”, and cut through their ranks before they even knew I was there.

Blow after blow rained down on their unprotected backs. The red men outside heard my shout, roared my name so loud the walls shook, and redoubled their assault so fiercely that the black ranks shattered in minutes, letting the Heliumites pour into the chamber. That fight would go down in the annals of Barsoom as one of the grimmest tests of her warlike people’s ferocity—five hundred men, red against black, no quarter asked, no quarter given, as if they had all agreed to fight to the death to settle once and for all whether the cruel old superstition of Issus would outlive the outer world’s advance. The floor ran ankle-deep in Barsoom’s crimson blood, dead bodies piled so thick that half the time we fought standing on the corpses of the fallen.

When we surged toward the great windows overlooking Issus’s walled gardens, a sight hit me that sent a wave of wild exultation surging through my chest. “Look!” I shouted. “Men of the First Born, look!”

Fighting stopped dead. Every head turned, and every pair of eyes stared at a sight no black warrior of Issus had ever dreamed they would see: a wavering line of black soldiers being slowly forced back across the gardens by a seething horde of green Thark warriors, mounted atop their massive, twenty-foot thoats. At their head, fiercer and grimmer than any warrior there, rode Tars Tarkas, Jeddak of Thark, roaring a command to his legion. He couched his forty-foot, metal-shod lance, and every green warrior behind him did the same.

Twenty yards separated the green horde from the black line. Tars Tarkas shouted once more, and the green men charged with a wild, earth-shaking battle cry. The black line held for no more than a heartbeat before the great thoats and their terrible riders tore straight through their ranks, shattering them completely. Utan after utan of red men poured through the breach behind the green horde, the green warriors fanning out to secure the outer gardens, the red men surging inward to clear the temple halls. Our remaining black defenders had vanished entirely.

My first thought was Dejah Thoris. I called over my shoulder to Carthoris that I had found his mother, then broke into a run toward the chamber I had left her in, my son hot on my heels, our surviving warriors trailing after us. The second I stepped through the chamber door, I knew someone had been there after I left. A scrap of her favourite silk sash lay crumpled on the floor, a heavy ceremonial dagger and half a dozen of her royal metal ornaments scattered across the marble as if torn from her in a struggle. Worst of all, the heavy door leading down to the pits where I had hidden her was standing ajar.

I lunged for it, threw it open, and rushed down the tunnels. Dejah Thoris was gone. I screamed her name over and over, no answer coming back but the echo of my own voice. For a second I teetered on the edge of madness, a wild, maniacal rage burning through every vein in my body. “Issus!” I roared. “Find Issus! No man harms her but John Carter. Carthoris—show me the way to her private apartments, now.”

Carthoris did not wait to confirm I had heard him, sprinting full tilt deeper into the temple’s stone bowels. I kept pace right beside him, pushing him to run faster, until we skidded to a stop before a great carved sandalwood door. Carthoris burst through a step ahead of me, and we found the throne room of Issus laid out before us just as I had seen it months prior: the goddess’s reclining slave attendants, the ranks of her royal guard lining the walls, the great sorapus wood dais holding her gilded throne. We gave them no time to draw their weapons. My first swing of the sword cut down two of the front rank guards, and the weight of my charge carried me straight through the rest of the ranks to leap onto the dais right beside Issus’s throne.

The repulsive old false goddess squatted there, wild with terror, and tried to dart around me to dive into the hidden trapdoor behind her throne. This time I would not let her escape. Before she could get halfway to the door, I had her by the wrist, my dagger pressed hard against her foul, shrivelled breast. “Back!” I screamed at the guards, who were surging toward the dais. “The first black man who steps foot on this platform dies before I drive this blade through Issus’s heart.”

They hesitated. An officer barked an order to fall back, just as a thousand red warriors under Kantos Kan, Hor Vastus, and Xodar the former First Born high priest poured into the throne room behind us.

“Where is Dejah Thoris?” I snarled, shaking the old crone in my grip.

Her eyes rolled wildly in her head. It took her long seconds to process that her temple had fallen, that the men she had claimed were all condemned to eternal joyless slavery in her afterlife had broken down her walls. The realization of what that meant hit her like a blow—her power gone, her fraud exposed, all the eons of lies crumbling to dust around her. Then the highest priest of her old cult stepped forward, his voice shaking. “Issus, Goddess of Death and Life Eternal!” he shrieked. “Rise in your wrath, smite these blasphemers dead with one wave of your omnipotent hand! You are all-powerful—save your people!”

That was the final snap. The false goddess went completely, raving mad. She shrieked and gibbered in my grip, biting and clawing at my arms, then let loose a weird, unearthly cackle that turned my blood cold. She lunged at her terrified slave girls, gnashing her teeth, froth dripping down her chin. I shook her hard, trying to yank her back to lucidity long enough to get an answer. “Where is Dejah Thoris?” I screamed again.

A sudden flicker of cunning sparked in her close-set, hideous eyes. “Dejah Thoris?” she cackled. “And Thuvia, and Phaidor, daughter of Matai Shang! All three of them love John Carter, isn’t it funny? They’re locked inside the Temple of the Sun, to meditate for a full year. When the food runs out, they’ll have nothing left to eat but each other. Ha-ha! Divine entertainment, isn’t it?”

The horror of it nearly paralyzed me. I shook her like a terrier shakes a rat, my rage so fierce I could barely speak. “Countermand the order! Call them back, or you die right now.”

“It’s too late!” she screamed, dissolving back into cackling gibberish.

My dagger flew up over her chest of its own accord, but I held back. Killing a raving mad old woman with my own hands would have fouled my soul forever. I had a far fitter fate for her. “Men of the First Born,” I called out to the black warriors watching, “you have seen Issus is no god. She is a cruel, lying old wretch who has deceived you for ages. John Carter of Helium will not sully his hands with her blood. She is yours.” I shoved the raving creature off the dais, right into the waiting clutches of the people she had betrayed and tormented for centuries.

I spotted Xodar in the crowd of red officers, and called him over to lead me to the Temple of the Sun immediately. We did not stop to see what vengeance the First Born visited on their fallen goddess, sprinting out of the throne room with Xodar, Carthoris, Kantos Kan, Hor Vastus, and a score of Helium’s finest nobles at my heels. Xodar led us through winding inner passages until we burst out into a vast circular central courtyard, paved with translucent, pure white marble, the great golden Temple of the Sun rising before us. Its walls were inlaid with diamonds, rubies, sapphires, and the thousands of rare, iridescent Barsoomian gems that outshine every precious stone on Earth, glinting in the torchlight.

“This way!” Xodar shouted, leading us to a dark tunnel mouth set right beside the temple’s gilded base. We were about to climb down when a deep, thunderous roar burst out from the direction of Issus’s main temple, and a young padwar named Djor Kantos came sprinting through the courtyard gate screaming for us to stop. “The blacks have set fire to the temple!” he yelled. “It’s burning in a thousand different places! If you don’t get back to the outer gardens now, you’ll all burn alive.”

Smoke was already pouring from a dozen windows overlooking the courtyard, a great roiling black pall rising over the temple’s highest minarets. “All of you go back!” I ordered my warriors. “Xodar—show me the way into the Temple of the Sun tunnels, then leave me. I am not leaving here without my Princess.”

“Follow me!” Xodar shouted, already sprinting down the tunnel. I ran at his heels through half a dozen tiers of galleries, until we reached a flat, straight corridor at the end of which I could see a lit chamber blocked off by thick iron bars. Beyond the bars, I saw her: my Dejah Thoris, standing beside Thuvia and Phaidor. When she saw me, she ran straight for the bars separating us.

The great rotating chamber of the Temple of the Sun had already turned so that only a narrow sliver of its outer opening lined up with the corridor’s end. The gap was shrinking faster by the second. Within hours it would close entirely, and the chamber would slowly, silently revolve for 687 long Barsoomian days before the aperture would line up with the corridor once more. A full year locked inside, no way in or out, for the three women inside.

“Xodar!” I shouted. “Is there no way to stop this terrible turning mechanism? No one knows how to open these bars fast enough?”

“The keeper of the keys burned to death in the fires above, his body lost under tons of falling rubble,” Xodar answered. “But I will try to find another way. Wait here for me.”

While he was gone, I pressed my face to the bars, holding Dejah Thoris’s hand as she stretched it through the shrinking gap. Thuvia stepped back to the far side of the small chamber to give us privacy, but Phaidor, the Princess of the Yellow Martian city of Zodanga, hung back a few feet away from us. “John Carter,” she said, her voice soft and sharp with longing, “this is the last time you will ever see any of us. Tell me you love me, so that I may die happy.”

“I love only Dejah Thoris, Princess of Helium,” I answered gently. “I have told you that from the very beginning. I am sorry.”

She bit her lip hard, turned away, and I caught a glimpse of a black, ugly scowl twisting her beautiful face. For a few minutes more we talked in low, quiet tones, the gap between the bars shrinking smaller and smaller. Smoke began curling down the corridor, thick and acrid, the distant roar of the burning temple growing louder behind us. Then our warriors came sprinting down the tunnel toward us, coughing, the smoke so thick we could barely see ten feet in front of our faces.

“There is no other way, John Carter!” Carthoris yelled, grabbing my arm. “The pits are flooding right now. If we don’t leave in three minutes, we will all drown or burn alive.” Xodar had tossed half a dozen sealed tins of concentrated rations through the narrow gap to the three women inside the chamber. The opening was barely an inch wide now. Dejah Thoris was pressed as close to it as she could get, whispering for me to save myself.

Then I saw Phaidor, behind her, her face twisted with unbridled hatred. “You will never hold Dejah Thoris in your arms again!” she shrieked, raising a sharp dagger high over her head. “When the year ends, it will be my arms that welcome you, not hers!”

Before I could scream a warning, Thuvia lunged between them. A great billow of smoke blotted out the chamber completely. A single, high shriek cut through the roar of the fire. When the smoke cleared, the gap was gone. A blank, seamless gilded wall faced us. The chamber had turned fully sealed.

My men dragged me away, fighting and screaming in my madness, half-conscious as they carried me up the tunnels and out into the gardens to safety. I do not remember the rest of that day, and no one who was there has ever spoken of it to me, for fear of reopening the terrible wound in my heart.

Ah, if I could only know one thing! Which woman did that dagger find, in that last split second before the door slammed shut? Only time, the long, slow year of waiting ahead, will ever tell.
