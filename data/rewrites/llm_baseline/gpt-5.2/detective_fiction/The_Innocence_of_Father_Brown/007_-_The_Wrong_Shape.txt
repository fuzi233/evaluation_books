The north roads out of London do not end so much as they fray; they turn into a long, thinning ribbon of respectability—shops, then a paddock with a smell of wet earth, then an inn, then a nursery garden where the air tastes of bruised leaves—until suburb and country overlap like two voices heard at once.

Along one of those roads stood a house that made passers-by slow their step without knowing why. It lay low and long beside the street, painted white and a pale medicinal green, with a veranda and sun-blinds that suggested noon heat even under English cloud. Over the porches were those quaint cupolas like wooden umbrellas, as if the place had been designed to keep a tropical glare from striking the eyes. It was, for all its English comfort, built with a foreign reflex—a hint of an Anglo-Indian dream translated into Clapham money.

On the Thursday before Whit-Sunday, at half-past four, the front door opened, and the house revealed its trick. One could see straight through the entrance hall down the “tail” of the building—because the whole thing was built like a T, a long cross-bar along the road and a short stem running back. The stem held two rooms in a line: first a study; beyond it a glass conservatory blazing with flowers so rich they looked invented. When the door stood open, the view was like a corridor cut through domesticity into a scene of purple cloud and crimson star, all transparent and unreal in the sunlight; and the air that drifted outward carried not only polish and paint, but the warmed sweetness of sap and some faint, stale bitterness like crushed poppy.

Father Brown stepped out first, small and sober in his black, a large pipe in his mouth as if it were a homely tool. Beside him came Flambeau, very tall, elegant even in repose, with a very small cigarette. They paused, not because the garden invited them, but because the house behind them pressed on the senses as a spice presses on the tongue—too much, too layered, too deliberately arranged.

Leonard Quinton, who owned the place, had arranged it all with a poet’s impatience for plain form. He lived for colour as some men live for wine: saturations, contrasts, violent harmonies. His books overflowed with Eastern splendours—mitred heroes on purple elephants, jewels too huge to be true, skies like beaten copper and blood. The art in his house had the same fever: carpets where the patterns curled as if they wished to be snakes; bronzes, lacquer, shards of strange beauty. But the morbidity that made such imagery was more visible in his habits than in his verse: opium, languor, and a household strained thin by his indulgences.

His wife held it together. She was handsome in a hard-working way—square-faced, fair-haired, strong in the jaw as if she had learned to carry other people’s weakness like a pail. What she objected to was not merely the drug. It was the “holy man,” the Indian in white and yellow, whom Quinton kept as if he were a private key to an Eastern heaven and hell.

They had scarcely taken two steps from the threshold when the garden gate clattered and flew open, and a young man lurched in as if the noise of his own urgency drove him. He wore a billycock pushed back on his head, a vivid red necktie twisted as though he had slept in it, and he snapped and worried at the air with a little jointed cane. His face had the half-bright vacancy of dissipation.

“I say,” he panted, “I must see old Quinton. Has he gone?”

“Mr. Quinton is in,” Father Brown said, taking his pipe from his mouth and cleaning it with slow care, “but I don’t know if you can see him. The doctor is with him at present.”

The youth—Atkinson—stumbled into the hall; and at that moment Dr. Harris emerged from the study, shutting the door behind him and tugging on gloves. He was bull-necked, ordinary to the point of invisibility, the sort of man whose competence was felt rather than noticed.

“See Mr. Quinton?” he said, briskly amused. “No, you can’t. In fact, you mustn’t. Nobody must. I’ve just given him his sleeping draught.”

“No, but look here, old chap—” Atkinson tried to grab him by the lapels with a drunken affection that was mostly greed.

“It’s no good, Mr. Atkinson,” Harris said, pushing him back with practised impatience. “When you can alter the effects of a drug I’ll alter my decision.”

He settled his hat and stepped into the light with Flambeau and Father Brown, leaving Atkinson on the doorstep blinking like someone abruptly turned out of a warm kitchen.

“That was a sound lie,” Harris said, with a laugh that was good-tempered in the way a slammed door is good-tempered. “He doesn’t have it for half an hour. But I’m not having him bothered by that little beast. He only wants money—and he’s Mrs. Quinton’s brother, curse him. She’s as fine a woman as ever walked.”

“Yes,” Father Brown said. “She’s a good woman.”

“So I’ll hang about the garden till he clears off,” Harris went on. “Then I’ll go in with the medicine. He can’t get in; I locked the door.”

“In that case,” Flambeau said, “we might as well go round the back, by the conservatory end. There’s no entrance there, but it’s worth seeing, even from outside.”

“And I might get a squint at my patient,” Harris said. “He likes to lie at the far end among those blood-red poinsettias. It gives me the creeps.” Then he broke off. “What are you doing?”

Father Brown had paused in the long grass. Almost hidden there lay a dagger: Oriental, crooked, inlaid with minute stones and metals that caught the sun in little flashes of green and garnet. It was exquisitely made—like a chef’s knife made for show rather than work, beautiful in a way that made the stomach uneasy. Brown held it as one might hold a fish with unfamiliar spines.

“What is this?” he asked, with a faint distaste.

“Oh, Quinton’s, I suppose,” Harris said carelessly. “He has all sorts of Chinese gimcracks. Or it belongs to that mild Hindoo he keeps on a string.”

“What Hindoo?” Father Brown asked, still staring at the blade.

“Oh, some conjuror,” Harris replied. “A fraud, of course.”

“You don’t believe in magic?” Father Brown asked, not lifting his eyes.

“O crickey. Magic.”

“It’s very beautiful,” Father Brown said softly, as if speaking of a dish whose fragrance was too heavy. “The colours are very beautiful. But it’s the wrong shape.”

“What for?” Flambeau asked, amused.

“For anything. It’s wrong in the abstract. Don’t you ever feel it about Eastern art? Colours that intoxicate—yet shapes that are mean and bad, deliberately mean and bad. I’ve seen wicked things in a Turkey carpet.”

Flambeau laughed under his breath. “Mon Dieu.”

“They are letters and symbols in a language I don’t know,” Father Brown went on, his voice lowering as the light began to lower, “but I know they stand for evil words. The lines go wrong on purpose—like serpents doubling to escape.”

“What the devil are you talking about?” Harris laughed loudly.

Flambeau answered him quietly. “The Father sometimes gets this mystic cloud on him. But I warn you—I have never known him to have it except when evil was quite near.”

“Oh, rats.”

Father Brown held the dagger at arm’s length, as if it were alive. “Don’t you see? It doesn’t point like a spear. It doesn’t sweep like a scythe. It doesn’t look like a weapon. It looks like an instrument of torture.”

“Well, if you dislike it,” Harris said, “take it back to its owner. Haven’t we come to the end of this confounded conservatory yet? This house is the wrong shape, if you like.”

“You don’t understand,” Father Brown said, shaking his head. “The house’s shape is quaint—even laughable. But there is nothing wrong about it.”

They reached the rounded end of the conservatory: a clear curve of glass with no door, no window, nothing but transparency. Inside, the flowers crowded like a riot—cactus and azalea, poinsettias like pooled blood, petals that looked too thick, as if varnished. In that glowing wilderness lay Leonard Quinton on a sofa, in a brown velvet coat, pale and languid, his loose chestnut hair and thin beard making him look less a man than a perpetually tired boy. He seemed half asleep over a book.

But none of them looked long at Quinton. Their eyes fixed on the figure directly outside the glass.

A tall man stood there, drapery falling faultlessly to his feet in white. His bare brown skull and neck shone in the setting sun like bronze. He stood motionless, looking through the glass at Quinton, as still as a monument.

“Who is that?” Father Brown whispered, stepping back as if the air had bitten him.

“Only that Hindoo humbug,” Harris growled. “But what the deuce is he doing here?”

“It looks like hypnotism,” Flambeau said, his moustache tightening.

“Why do you unmedical fellows always talk that bosh?” Harris snapped. “It looks far more like burglary.”

“We will speak to him,” Flambeau said; action was always his answer to discomfort.

He strode forward. Bowing slightly—his height overtopping even the Indian—he said with bland audacity, “Good evening, sir. Do you want anything?”

Slowly, like a ship turning, the great yellow face turned. They were startled: the man’s eyelids were shut tight, as if he were asleep while standing. Yet his voice came in excellent English, very calm.

“Thank you. I want nothing.”

Then the lids half opened, showing a slit of pale, opalescent eyeball.

“I want nothing.”

Then the eyes opened wide in a startling stare.

“I want nothing.”

And with a soft rustle of cloth he moved away into the thickening dusk, as silently as he had stood.

“The Christian is more modest,” Father Brown murmured. “He wants something.”

“What on earth was he doing?” Flambeau asked, watching the white robe vanish among shadows.

“I should like to talk to you later,” Father Brown said.

The sunlight remained, but only as a red residue. Trees and shrubs thickened into black masses. They walked back along the other side of the conservatory toward the front door. In the deep corner between the study and the main building, something seemed to stir—like a bird startled from a hedge. Again the white-robed fakir slid out of shadow and moved toward the front.

And then another figure came toward them: Mrs. Quinton. In the twilight her heavy golden hair had a dull gleam; her pale face was firm, her mouth set with that courtesy that is also command.

“Good evening, Dr. Harris,” she said.

“Good evening, Mrs. Quinton,” Harris replied heartily. “I’m just going to give your husband his sleeping draught.”

“Yes,” she said clearly. “I think it is quite time.”

She smiled at them—an efficient smile—and swept into the house.

“That woman’s over-driven,” Father Brown said. “That’s the kind that does her duty for twenty years—and then does something dreadful.”

Harris looked at him with sudden interest. “Did you ever study medicine?”

“You have to know something of the mind as well as the body,” the priest said. “We have to know something of the body as well as the mind.”

“Well,” Harris said, “I’ll go give him his stuff.”

They approached the front doorway. As they turned into it, they saw the man in white for the third time. He came so straight toward the front door that it was absurd to think he had not just left the study opposite. Yet they knew the study door was locked.

Father Brown and Flambeau exchanged no comment. Harris was not a man who wasted thought on impossibilities; he let the Asiatic pass out, and stepped into the hall.

There, like a bad smell that would not clear, was Atkinson. He lounged and hummed and prodded ornaments with his cane, as if mere objects might be persuaded to give him money. Harris’s face tightened.

“I must lock the door again,” he muttered to his companions. “Or this rat will get in. But I’ll be out in two minutes.”

He unlocked the study door and slipped inside, locking it behind him just as Atkinson made a clumsy rush. The youth flung himself into a chair, scowling. Flambeau studied a Persian illumination on the wall with the detached eye of a man who has known thieves and artists and knows how easily they trade masks. Father Brown, oddly dazed, watched the closed door.

Four minutes later it opened. Atkinson was quicker. He sprang, thrust a foot forward, held the opening for an instant, and shouted into the room, “Oh, I say, Quinton, I want—”

From within came Quinton’s voice, clear, languid, something between a yawn and a laugh.

“Oh, I know what you want. Take it, and leave me in peace. I’m writing a song about peacocks.”

A half-sovereign flicked through the gap before the door closed. Atkinson, lurching forward, caught it with surprising dexterity, as if he practised catching what fell from other people’s hands.

“So that’s settled,” Harris said, and locked the door with a savage click. He led the way out into the garden again.

“Poor Leonard can get peace now,” he added to Father Brown. “He’s locked in all by himself for an hour or two.”

“Yes,” Father Brown said. “And his voice sounded jolly enough when we left him.” Then he turned, sharply. “Where is Mrs. Quinton!”

“She’s gone up to her room,” Harris answered. “That’s her shadow on the blind.”

Father Brown looked up. Against the lit window, a dark outline moved—her silhouette, strong-shouldered, unmistakable.

“Yes,” he said slowly. “That is her shadow.”

He walked a yard or two and dropped onto a garden seat. Flambeau sat beside him. Harris, being made of restlessness, wandered off smoking into the deepening dusk.

“My father,” Flambeau said in French, low, “what is the matter with you?”

Father Brown did not answer at once. The garden was losing colour; the conservatory glass held the last stains of sunset like wine held to light. Farther off, the Indian sat bolt upright on a grassy bank, face toward the dying sun. Atkinson leaned under a tree, jingling his coin and looking as if he resented the world for not being more generous to him.

“Superstition is irreligious,” Father Brown said at last, “but there is something in the air of this place. I think it’s that Indian—at least, partly.”
