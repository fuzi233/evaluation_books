The morning sun broke over Boston, transforming the city into a steaming, hazy dream of midsummer. As Archer stepped off the Fall River train, the air hit him thick with the scent of brewing coffee, stale beer, and rotting fruit. The streets were a chaotic river of shirt-sleeved figures moving with the frantic, intimate abandon of boarders rushing to the bathroom. It was a scene of untidy domesticity that felt alien to the refined order of Europe, where heat rarely degraded the grandeur of the streets.

Archer hailed a cab and drove to the Somerset Club for breakfast. Even here, the fashionable quarters seemed to succumb to the oppressive warmth, losing their polish for a moment. Caretakers lounged in calico on doorsteps, and the Common looked less like a public park and more like the aftermath of a massive picnic. It was a landscape where he could scarcely imagine fitting Ellen Olenska; the heat seemed to prostrate the very spirit of the city, leaving it deserted and weary.

Yet, Archer ate with renewed appetite and method. He sliced melon and studied the morning paper while waiting for his toast, driven by a sudden surge of energy. Since the night before, when he had told May he was heading to Boston and then on to New York, he had felt a strange liberation. His original plan to return early in the week had been effortlessly altered by a single letter from the office, a piece of fate conveniently placed on the hall table. He felt a flicker of shame at how easily he had manipulated the situation, reminiscent of Lawrence Lefferts' masterful schemes, but the feeling passed quickly. He was not in an analytical mood today; he was simply alive.

After breakfast, smoking a cigarette and glancing at the *Commercial Advertiser*, he watched as acquaintances drifted in, exchanging the polite greetings of a world that felt strangely unchanged despite his sense of having slipped through the meshes of time and space. At half-past nine, he retreated to the writing room. He penned a few lines and dispatched a messenger to the Parker House, instructing him to wait for a reply. Sitting behind another newspaper, Archer calculated the travel time to the hotel, his mind racing with anticipation.

"The lady was out, sir," a waiter's voice cut through his thoughts.

"Out?" Archer stammered, the word sounding foreign on his tongue.

A flush of anger rose in his cheeks. How stupid he had been not to send the note immediately upon arrival. He grabbed his hat and stick, stepping out into a city that suddenly felt vast, empty, and strange, as if he were a traveler from a distant land. Hesitating on the doorstep, he resolved to go to the Parker House himself. Perhaps the messenger had been mistaken.

Crossing the Common, he spotted her instantly on the first bench beneath a tree. She held a grey silk sunshade—how could he have ever imagined her with a pink one? Her posture was listless, as if she had nothing else to do. He noted the drooping profile, the knot of hair fastened low under her dark hat, and the long, wrinkled glove holding the shade. As he approached, she turned, her face registering a startled surprise before melting into a slow, wondering smile.

"Oh," she murmured, making space for him on the bench without rising.

"I'm here on business—just got here," Archer explained, feigning astonishment at the sight of her. "But what on earth are you doing in this wilderness?" He felt as though he were shouting across endless distances, fearing she might vanish before he could catch her.

"I? Oh, I'm here on business too," she replied, turning to face him. Her voice was low, with a faint roughness on the consonants that he had somehow forgotten until this moment.

"You do your hair differently," he said, his heart hammering as if he had spoken an irrevocable truth.

"Differently? No—it's only that I do it as best I can when I'm without Nastasia."

"Nastasia? But isn't she with you?"

"No; I'm alone. For two days it was not worth while to bring her."

"You're alone... at the Parker House?"

She flashed him a look of old malice. "Does it strike you as dangerous?"

"No; not dangerous—"

"But unconventional? I see; I suppose it is." She considered him for a moment. "I hadn't thought of it, because I've just done something so much more unconventional." A faint irony lingered in her eyes. "I've just refused to take back a sum of money—that belonged to me."

Archer sprang up, stepping back slightly. She had furled her parasol and sat absently drawing patterns in the gravel. He returned to stand before her.

"Some one... has come here to meet you?"

"Yes."

"With this offer?"

She nodded.

"And you refused—because of the conditions?"

"I refused," she said after a pause.

He sat down beside her. "What were the conditions?"

"Oh, they were not onerous: just to sit at the head of his table now and then."

Silence stretched between them. Archer's heart felt strangely shut, and he groped vainly for words.

"He wants you back—at any price?"

"Well—a considerable price. At least the sum is considerable for me."

He paused, beating around the question he felt compelled to ask. "It was to meet him here that you came?"

She stared, then burst into laughter. "Meet him—my husband? *Here*? At this season he's always at Cowes or Baden."

"He sent someone?"

"Yes."

"With a letter?"

She shook her head. "No; just a message. He never writes. I don't think I've had more than one letter from him." The mention brought color to her cheek, reflecting in Archer's own vivid blush.

"Why does he never write?"

"Why should he? What does one have secretaries for?"

The young man's blush deepened. She had spoken the word as if it held no special weight in her vocabulary. For a moment, the question "Did he send his secretary?" hovered on his lips, but the memory of Count Olenski's solitary letter to his wife silenced him. He plunged forward again.

"And the person?"

"The emissary? The emissary," Madame Olenska rejoined, still smiling, "might, for all I care, have left already; but he has insisted on waiting till this evening... in case... on the chance..."

"And you came out here to think the chance over?"

"I came out to get a breath of air. The hotel's too stifling. I'm taking the afternoon train back to Portsmouth."

They sat in silence, staring ahead at the passing pedestrians. Finally, she turned her eyes to his face. "You're not changed."

He wanted to answer, "I was, till I saw you again," but instead, he stood abruptly and glanced at the sweltering, untidy park. "This is horrible. Why shouldn't we go out a little on the bay? There's a breeze, and it will be cooler. We might take the steamboat down to Point Arley."

She glanced up hesitantly. He pressed on. "On a Monday morning there won't be anybody on the boat. My train doesn't leave till evening: I'm going back to New York. Why shouldn't we?" He looked down at her, his voice breaking. "Haven't we done all we could?"

"Oh," she murmured. She stood up, reopening her sunshade and scanning the scene as if seeking counsel from the surroundings, assuring herself of the impossibility of staying. Then her eyes returned to his. "You mustn't say things like that to me," she said softly.

"I'll say anything you like; or nothing. I won't open my mouth unless you tell me to. What harm can it do to anybody? All I want is to listen to you," he stammered.

She produced a small gold watch on an enamelled chain. "Oh, don't calculate," he broke out, seizing the moment. "Give me the day! I want to get you away from that man. At what time was he coming?"

Her color rose again. "At eleven."

"Then you must come at once."

"You needn't be afraid—if I don't come."

"Nor you either—if you do. I swear I only want to hear about you, to know what you've been doing. It's a hundred years since we've met—it may be another hundred before we meet again."

She wavered, her anxious eyes fixed on his face. "Why didn't you come down to the beach to fetch me, the day I was at Granny's?" she asked.

"Because you didn't look round—because you didn't know I was there. I swore I wouldn't unless you looked round." He laughed as the childishness of the confession struck him.

"But I didn't look round on purpose."

"On purpose?"

"I knew you were there; when you drove in I recognised the ponies. So I went down to the beach."

"To get away from me as far as you could?"

She repeated in a low voice: "To get away from you as far as I could."

He laughed again, this time with boyish satisfaction. "Well, you see it's no use. I may as well tell you," he added, "that the business I came here for was just to find you. But, look here, we must start or we shall miss our boat."

"Our boat?" She frowned perplexedly, then smiled. "Oh, but I must go back to the hotel first: I must leave a note—"

"As many notes as you please. You can write here." He produced a note-case and one of the new stylographic pens. "I've even got an envelope—you see how everything's predestined! There—steady the thing on your knee, and I'll get the pen going in a second. They have to be humoured; wait—" He banged the hand holding the pen against the back of the bench. "It's like jerking down the mercury in a thermometer: just a trick. Now try—"

She laughed, bending over the paper he had laid on his case, and began to write. Archer walked a few steps away, staring with radiant, unseeing eyes at the passersby, who in turn paused to stare at the unusual sight of a fashionably dressed lady writing a note on her knee on a park bench.

Madame Olenska slipped the sheet into the envelope, wrote a name, and tucked it into her pocket. She stood up, and they walked back toward Beacon Street. Near the club, Archer caught sight of the plush-lined carriage that had delivered his note; its driver was resting, wiping his brow at a hydrant.

"I told you everything was predestined! Here's a cab for us. You see!" They laughed, astonished at the miracle of catching a public conveyance at that hour in such an unlikely spot, in a city where cab-stands were still a foreign novelty.

Archer checked his watch; there was time to drive to the Parker House before heading to the steamboat landing. They rattled through the hot streets and pulled up at the hotel door. Archer held out his hand for the letter, asking if he should take it in, but Madame Olenska shook her head, springing out and disappearing through the glazed doors. It was barely half-past ten, but what if the emissary, impatient and idle, was already seated among the travelers with cooling drinks?

Archer paced up and down before the carriage. A Sicilian youth with eyes like Nastasia's offered to shine his boots, and an Irish matron sold him peaches. Every few moments, the doors opened to release hot men in straw hats who glanced at him as they passed. He marveled at the uniformity of their faces, the endless stream of identical travelers passing in and out of hotel doors across the land.

Suddenly, a face appeared that did not belong to the crowd. He caught only a flash of it as he turned back toward the hotel, amidst the lank, weary, round, and surprised countenances. It was a young man, pale and half-extinguished by heat or worry, yet somehow quicker, vivid, more conscious. Archer hung onto a thin thread of memory, but it snapped and floated away with the disappearing figure—apparently some foreign businessman looking doubly foreign in such a setting. He vanished into the stream of passersby, and Archer resumed his patrol.

He did not wish to be seen watching his watch near the hotel. His unaided calculation suggested that if Madame Olenska was delayed, it was only because she had met the emissary and been waylaid. At the thought, his apprehension rose to anguish.

"If she doesn't come soon I'll go in and find her," he muttered.

The doors swung open, and she was at his side. They hurried into the carriage, and as it drove off, he checked his watch: she had been gone just three minutes. Through the clatter of loose windows that made conversation impossible, they bumped over disjointed cobblestones to the wharf.

Seated side by side on the bench of the half-empty boat, they found they had hardly anything to say, or rather, that what they needed to communicate passed best in the blessed silence of their release and isolation. As the paddle-wheels began to turn and the wharves receded through the veil of heat, Archer felt that everything in the old, familiar world of habit was fading away. He longed to ask Madame Olenska if she shared the feeling that they were embarking on a long voyage from which they might never return. But he was afraid to speak, fearing to disturb the delicate balance of her trust. In reality, he had no wish to betray that trust. There had been days and nights when the memory of their kiss burned on his lips; the day before, on the drive to Portsmouth, the thought of her had run through him like fire. But now, with her beside him, drifting into this unknown world, they seemed to have reached a deeper nearness that a mere touch could sunder.
