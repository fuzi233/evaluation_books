How to begin a story decently is a question that can sour a man’s mouth like over-steeped tea. I tried first the old method—scratching my head until the skin warmed under my nails. Nothing came of it. Then I tried a better household remedy: I consulted my daughter Penelope, who keeps her thoughts as carefully as she keeps the linen—folded, aired, and locked away from meddling fingers.

Penelope’s plan was plain, orderly, and (I must admit) deliciously practical. “Set it down day by day,” says she, “beginning with the day we heard Mr. Franklin Blake was coming.” Dates, she added, would pin my wandering memory to the page the way a clove pins itself in a baked apple: once the spice is set, the fragrance rises of its own accord.

The trouble was fetching the dates. Penelope offered to find them in her diary—the little book she learnt to keep at school, and has continued ever since, as regular as salting the ham at the right season. I made an improvement of my own—why shouldn’t she tell the story straight out of that diary? At this she coloured up as if I had accused her of watering the broth, and looked fierce enough to curdle milk.

“My journal is for my own private eye,” says she; “and no living creature shall ever know what is in it but myself.”

“What does that mean?” says I.

“Fiddlesticks!” says Penelope.

“Sweethearts,” says I—because when a man has lived long with women, he learns that one soft word, properly served, will often cool what might otherwise boil over.

So: Penelope’s plan it shall be. And here is the first day, properly labelled, like a jar in the still-room.

On Wednesday morning, the twenty-fourth of May, eighteen hundred and forty-eight, I was specially called into my lady’s own sitting-room. The room had the settled quiet of a house that knows its own dignity—the curtains drawn to the right measure, the air faint with lavender, and (underneath) that clean, cool scent of beeswax that always makes me think of good order and good bread.

“Gabriel,” says my lady, “here is news that will surprise you. Franklin Blake has come back from abroad. He has been staying with his father in London, and he is coming to us tomorrow to stop till next month, and keep Rachel’s birthday.”

If I had had my hat in my hand, nothing but respect would have prevented me from tossing it up to the ceiling. I had not seen Mr. Franklin since he was a boy in this house; and, out of all sight, he was—so I remembered him—the nicest boy that ever spun a top or broke a window.

Miss Rachel was present, and when I said as much, she turned those steady eyes of hers on me and answered as cool as fresh cream:

She remembered him, she said, as the most atrocious tyrant that ever tortured a doll, and the hardest driver of an exhausted little girl in string harness that England could produce. “I burn with indignation, and I ache with fatigue,” was the way she summed it up, “when I think of Franklin Blake.”

You will naturally ask, hearing this, how it happened that Mr. Franklin passed all the years from boy to man out of his own country. The answer is as simple—and as sour—as a lawsuit that has sat too long. His father had the misfortune to be next heir to a Dukedom and not to be able to prove it.

My lady’s eldest sister married the celebrated Mr. Blake—famous alike for his great riches and his great suit at law. How long he worried the tribunals to turn out the Duke in possession and put himself in the Duke’s place; how many lawyers’ purses he filled till they burst; how many harmless people he set by the ears disputing whether he was right or wrong—these are more years and more quarrels than I can count. His wife died, and two of his three children died, before the tribunals could make up their minds to show him the door and take no more of his money. When it was all over, and the Duke in possession was left in possession, Mr. Blake discovered the only way of being even with his country was not to let his country have the honour of educating his son.

“How can I trust my native institutions,” says he, “after the way in which my native institutions have behaved to me?”

Add to this that Mr. Blake disliked all boys, his own included, and you will admit it could only end one way. Master Franklin was taken from us and sent to institutions his father could trust in that superior country, Germany—Mr. Blake himself staying snug in England to improve his fellow-countrymen in Parliament, and to publish a statement on the subject of the Duke in possession, which has remained unfinished from that day to this.

There. Thank God, that’s told. Neither you nor I need trouble our heads any more about Mr. Blake senior. Leave him to his Dukedom; let us stick to the Diamond.

For the Diamond takes us back to Mr. Franklin, who was the innocent means of bringing that unlucky jewel into the house.

Our nice boy did not forget us when he went abroad. He wrote now and then—sometimes to my lady, sometimes to Miss Rachel, and sometimes to me. We had had a little transaction together before he left: he borrowed from me a ball of string, a four-bladed knife, and seven-and-sixpence in money—the colour of which last I have not seen, and never expect to see again. His letters to me chiefly related to borrowing more.

From my lady I heard how he got on as he grew in years and stature. After Germany had taught him what Germany could, he gave the French a turn, and the Italians after that. They made him among them—so far as I could understand—a sort of universal genius. He wrote a little; painted a little; sang and played and composed a little—borrowing, I suspect, in all these cases just as he had borrowed from me. His mother’s fortune—seven hundred a year—fell to him when he came of age, and ran through him as if through a sieve. The more he had, the more he wanted; there was a hole in Mr. Franklin’s pocket that nothing would sew up.

Wherever he went, his lively, easy way made him welcome. He lived here, there, and everywhere; his address (as he used to put it) being “Post Office, Europe—to be left till called for.” Twice he made up his mind to come back to England and see us; twice (saving your presence) some unmentionable woman stood in the way and stopped him. The third attempt succeeded; and on Thursday, the twenty-fifth of May, we were to see what our nice boy had grown to be as a man—good blood, high courage, and five-and-twenty by our reckoning.

Now you know as much of Mr. Franklin Blake as I did—before Mr. Franklin Blake came down to our house.

Thursday was as fine a summer’s day as ever you saw: sunlight like warmed honey on the stone, and the air full of those small countryside sounds that keep a house from feeling lonely—the far-off bleat of sheep, the soft scissor of leaves, a cart somewhere singing on its axles as it went. My lady and Miss Rachel, not expecting Mr. Franklin till dinner-time, drove out to lunch with friends in the neighbourhood.

When they were gone, I went and had a look at the bedroom prepared for our guest, and saw that all was straight—the sheets turned down like a clean page, the water laid ready, the fire-place blacked to a proper sober shine. Then, being butler as well as steward in my lady’s establishment (at my own particular request, mind, because it vexed me to see anybody but myself in possession of the key of the late Sir John’s cellar), I fetched up some of our famous Latour claret and set it in the warm summer air to take off the chill before dinner.

There is an art to wine, as there is to soup: treat it harshly, serve it cold from pride or ignorance, and you kill what time and care have put into it. So I set the bottle where the sun could coax it, not scorch it. Concluding to set myself in the warm summer air next—seeing what is good for old claret is equally good for old age—I took up my beehive chair to go out into the back court.

I had barely got it settled when I heard a sound like the soft beating of a drum from the terrace in front of the house. It was not the friendly country percussion—the thud of a mallet, the rhythm of a pail—no; it was a deliberate tap-tap, as if somebody were trying to write a message on the air.

I went round to the terrace and found three mahogany-coloured Indians in white linen frocks and trousers looking up at the house. Each had a small hand-drum slung in front of him. Behind them stood a little delicate-looking light-haired English boy with a bag in his hand.

I judged them strolling conjurors; the boy with the bag carried the tools. One of the three—who spoke English, and had manners so smooth they might have been polished like silver—presently told me my judgment was right. He requested permission to show his tricks in the presence of the lady of the house.

Now I am not a sour old man. I am generally for amusement, and I am the last person in the world to distrust another because he is a few shades darker than myself. But the best of us have our weaknesses—and my weakness, when I know the family plate-basket is out on a pantry-table, is to be instantly reminded of that basket by the sight of a strolling stranger whose manners are superior to my own. Besides, there was something in their drumming that sat wrong in the day’s clean country quiet; it was like a foreign tongue spoken too loudly in a small room, drowning the familiar speech of the house.

So I told the Indian the lady of the house was out, and I warned him and his party off the premises. He made me a beautiful bow and went off with his companions.

I returned to my beehive chair, chose the sunny side of the court, and fell—if truth must be owned—not exactly into sleep, but into the next best thing to it, when the mind goes soft and the world’s noises arrange themselves into something like music.

I was roused by my daughter Penelope running out at me as if the house was on fire.

What do you think she wanted? She wanted the three Indian jugglers instantly taken up; because they knew who was coming from London to visit us, and meant mischief to Mr. Franklin Blake.

Mr. Franklin’s name woke me to the bone. I opened my eyes and made Penelope explain herself.

It appeared she had just come from our lodge, where she had been gossiping with the lodge-keeper’s daughter. The two girls saw the Indians pass out after I had warned them off, followed by their little boy. The girls took it into their heads the boy was ill-used—no reason that I could discover except that he was pretty and delicate-looking. So they stole along the inner side of the hedge between us and the road and watched the foreigners on the outer side.

And what they saw—if you take my word—was stranger than any kitchen tale ever told over a winter fire.

The Indians first looked up the road and down the road to make sure they were alone. Then they faced about and stared hard in the direction of our house. Then they jabbered and disputed in their own language, casting looks at one another like men at a crossroad uncertain which way leads home. Then they all turned to their little English boy as if they expected him to help them.

Then the chief Indian—the one with the English and the elegant manners—said to the boy, “Hold out your hand.”

At hearing those words, Penelope declared she did not know what prevented her heart from flying straight out of her. I thought privately it might have been her stays. What I said was, “You make my flesh creep.” (Women like these little compliments.)

The boy shrank back, shook his head, and said he didn’t like it. The Indian asked him—not unkindly—whether he would like to be sent back to London and left where they had found him: sleeping in an empty basket in a market, hungry, ragged, and forsaken. That ended it. The little chap unwillingly held out his hand.

The Indian took a bottle from his bosom and poured out some black stuff, like ink, into the hollow of the boy’s palm. He touched the boy’s head, made signs over it in the air, and said, “Look.”

The boy became quite stiff, like a statue, staring into the ink. So far it seemed to me juggling—and a foolish waste of ink, at that. I was beginning to feel the sun tug me back towards drowsiness, when Penelope’s next words stirred me up.

The Indians looked up the road and down the road once more. Then the chief said to the boy, “See the English gentleman from foreign parts.”

The boy answered, “I see him.”

The Indian asked, “Is it on the road to this house, and on no other, that the English gentleman will travel today?”

The boy said, “It is on the road to this house, and on no other, that the English gentleman will travel today.”

The Indian waited a little, as if listening for something beyond human hearing—some change in the air, some shift in the day’s soundscape that would tell him when to strike the next note. Then he asked, “Has the English gentleman got It about him?”

The boy waited too, and then said, “Yes.”

The Indian put a third and last question: “Will the English gentleman come here, as he has promised to come, at the close of day?”

The boy said, “I can’t tell.”

The Indian asked why.

“I am tired,” the boy answered. “The mist rises in my head, and puzzles me. I can see no more today.”

With that the catechism ended. The chief Indian said something in his own language to the other two, pointing to the boy and pointing towards the town where, as we afterwards discovered, they were lodged. Then, after making more signs on the boy’s head, he blew on the boy’s forehead and woke him with a start. After that they all went on their way towards the town, and the girls saw them no more.

Most things have a moral, if you look for it—like a stew that tells you, by its taste, what hand has been in the pot. I found mine at once.

First: the chief juggler had heard Mr. Franklin’s arrival talked of among the servants out-of-doors, and saw his way to making a little money by it.

Second: he and his men and boy meant to hang about till they saw my lady drive home, then come back and foretell Mr. Franklin’s arrival by magic.

Third: Penelope had heard them rehearsing their hocus-pocus, like actors rehearsing a play.

Fourth: I should do well to keep an eye that evening on the plate-basket.

Fifth: Penelope would do well to cool down and leave her father to doze again in the sun.

That was the sensible view.

If you know anything of young women, you won’t be surprised to hear Penelope wouldn’t take it. The moral, according to my daughter, was serious. She fastened particularly on the Indian’s third question.

“Oh, father!” says Penelope, clasping her hands, “don’t joke about this. What does ‘It’ mean?”

“We’ll ask Mr. Franklin, my dear,” says I, “if you can wait till Mr. Franklin comes.” I winked to show I meant it in joke.

Penelope took it as solemn as a prayer. Her earnestness tickled me.

“What on earth should Mr. Franklin know about it?” I asked.

“Ask him,” says Penelope. “And see whether he thinks it a laughing matter, too.”

With that parting shot she left me.

When she was gone, I settled with myself that I really would ask Mr. Franklin—mainly to set Penelope’s mind at rest. What passed between us when I did ask him later that same day will be found set out fully in its proper place. But I warn you here, before we go further: you will not find the ghost of a joke in our conversation about the jugglers.

To my great surprise, Mr. Franklin—like Penelope—took the thing seriously. How seriously you will understand when I tell you that, in his opinion, “It” meant the Moonstone.
