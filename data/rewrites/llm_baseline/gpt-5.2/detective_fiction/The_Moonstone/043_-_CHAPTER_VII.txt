At the instant I showed myself in the doorway, Rachel rose from the piano as if the last chord she had struck had snapped the thread that held her sitting. I shut the door behind me; the click went through the room like a spoon rapped against a crystal glass—small, sharp, and somehow loud enough to change the air.

We faced each other with the long drawing-room between us, and silence between us longer still. She looked at me with all of her—every faculty gathered up into sight alone—so that even her breathing seemed to wait for what her eyes should decide.

A misgiving struck me that I had come too suddenly. I stepped forward, and kept my voice low, as one speaks in a house where someone sleeps.

“Rachel.”

That single sound brought her back to life: colour warmed in her cheeks as if heat had been turned under a pan, and her limbs obeyed her again. She came towards me without a word, slowly—drawn forward as though some influence outside her will pulled her along. With every step her eyes brightened, quick and waking. I forgot why I was there. I forgot the foul suspicion hanging on my name. I saw only the woman I loved, coming nearer and nearer, trembling, wavering—until I could bear no more restraint. I caught her to me and covered her face with kisses.

For a moment—only a moment—I believed she returned them; believed she too had been carried away from everything except the old tenderness. Then memory seized her like a hand at the throat. With a cry that was horror itself, and with a strength that might have flung a heavier man than I, she thrust me back. Her eyes hardened into merciless anger; her mouth took on merciless contempt. She looked me over as if I had been some stranger who had dared to touch her.

“You coward!” she said. “You mean, miserable, heartless coward!”

Those were her first words.

“I remember a time, Rachel,” I answered, “when you could have told me I had offended you in a worthier way than that. I beg your pardon.”

Bitterness crept into my voice in spite of me. Her eyes, which had turned away, came back unwillingly, and she answered low, with a sullen submission I had never heard from her.

“Perhaps there is some excuse for me,” she said. “After what you have done, is it a manly action to find your way to me as you have found it today? It seems a cowardly experiment—to try an experiment on my weakness for you. It seems a cowardly surprise—to surprise me into letting you kiss me. But that is only a woman’s view. I ought to have known it couldn’t be your view. I should have done better if I had controlled myself, and said nothing.”

The apology humiliated me worse than the insult.

“If my honour was not in your hands,” I said, “I would leave you this instant, and never see you again. You speak of what I have done. What have I done?”

“What have you done! You ask that question of me?”

“I ask it.”

“I have kept your infamy a secret,” she said, “and I have suffered the consequences of concealing it. Have I no claim to be spared the insult of your asking me what you have done? Is all sense of gratitude dead in you? You were once a gentleman. You were once dear to my mother, and dearer still to me—”

Her voice broke. She dropped into a chair, turned her back on me, and covered her face.

I waited until I could trust myself. In that pause I scarcely knew which pricked me more—the sting of her contempt, or the proud resolve that shut me out from all fellowship with her distress.

“If you will not speak first,” I said, “I must. I have come here with something serious to say. Will you do me the common justice of listening while I say it?”

She neither moved nor answered. I did not appeal again; I did not step one inch nearer to her chair. Pride met pride. I told her, from beginning to end, what I had discovered at the Shivering Sand, and all that had led to it. It took time; she never looked round; she never spoke.

I held my temper fast, because my whole future depended on it. The time had come to try Mr. Bruff’s theory. In the breathless interest of that last experiment, I moved so as to stand in front of her.

“I have a question to ask you,” I said. “It obliges me to refer again to a painful subject. Did Rosanna Spearman show you the nightgown? Yes, or no?”

She sprang up and came close of her own accord, searching my face as if to read there some line she had missed before.

“Are you mad?” she asked.

I kept my voice quiet. “Rachel, will you answer my question?”

She went on, heedless of it.

“Have you some object to gain that I don’t understand? Some mean fear about the future, in which I am concerned? They say your father’s death has made you a rich man. Have you come here to compensate me for the loss of my Diamond? And have you heart enough left to feel ashamed of your errand? Is that the secret of your pretence of innocence, and your story about Rosanna Spearman? Is there a motive of shame at the bottom of all the falsehood, this time?”

I stopped her. My control snapped.

“You have done me an infamous wrong!” I cried. “You suspect me of stealing your Diamond. I have a right to know—and I will know—the reason why!”

“Suspect you!” she flung back, anger rising to meet mine. “You villain, I saw you take the Diamond with my own eyes!”

In those words my whole position fell in ruins. The very ground Mr. Bruff had made me stand upon gave way. Innocent as I was, I stood mute; and to her eyes—to any eyes—I must have looked like guilt itself, struck dumb.

She drew back from my silence as if it frightened her.

“I spared you, at the time,” she said. “I would have spared you now, if you had not forced me to speak.” She moved as if to go, then hesitated at the door. “Why did you come here to humiliate yourself?” she asked. “Why did you come here to humiliate me?” She took a few steps, stopped again, and burst out, “For God’s sake, say something! If you have any mercy left, don’t let me degrade myself in this way! Say something—and drive me out of the room!”

I went towards her, scarcely knowing what I did—perhaps meaning to detain her until she had told me more. From the moment I understood she condemned me on the evidence of her own sight, nothing—not even my certainty of my own innocence—was clear. I took her hand and tried to speak with steadiness. All that came was:

“Rachel, you once loved me.”

She shuddered and turned her face away. Her hand lay powerless and trembling in mine.

“Let go of it,” she whispered.

Yet my touch had the same effect my voice had had at the first: after “coward,” after “thief,” while her hand remained in mine, I felt her yield—in spite of herself—as if some deeper habit of feeling still answered to me before her pride could interpose. I drew her back gently into the middle of the room, seated her by me, and spoke as plainly as I could.

“Rachel, I can’t explain the contradiction in what I am going to tell you. I can only speak the truth as you have spoken it. You saw me—before God, you saw me take the Diamond. Before God who hears us, I declare I now know I took it for the first time! Do you doubt me still?”

She neither heeded nor heard.

“Let go of my hand,” she repeated faintly.

Her head sank on my shoulder, and her hand, even as she begged release, closed on mine as if it could not help itself.

I did not press that question again. But I could not stop there. My only chance—my one hope—was that in the chain of what she had seen, some small overlooked trifle might exist, some crumb that careful examination could follow to the truth. I kept her hand. I summoned back what I could of old confidence and old tenderness.

“I want to ask you something,” I said. “Tell me everything that happened from the time we wished each other good-night, to the time you saw me take the Diamond.”

She lifted her head and made another effort to free herself.

“Oh, why go back to it!” she said. “Why go back to it!”

“I will tell you why. You are the victim, and I am the victim, of some monstrous delusion that wears the mask of truth. If we look at what happened on the night of your birthday together, we may end in understanding each other yet.”

Her head fell back on my shoulder. Tears gathered and slid slowly down her cheeks.

“Oh!” she said. “Have I never had that hope? Have I not tried to see it, as you are trying now?”

“You have tried by yourself,” I answered. “You have not tried with me.”

That seemed to stir hope in her—hope answering hope, as one voice finds another in a quiet room. She became docile; more than that, she exerted herself, opened her mind, and helped me.

“Let us begin,” I said, “after we had wished each other good-night. Did you go to bed, or sit up?”

“I went to bed.”

“Did you notice the time?”

“Not very late. About twelve, I think.”

“Did you fall asleep?”

“No. I couldn’t sleep that night.”

“You were restless?”

“I was thinking of you.”

The tone of it—more than the words—went through me. I waited before I could go on.

“Had you any light in your room?”

“None—until I got up again and lit my candle.”

“How long after you went to bed?”

“About an hour, I think. About one o’clock.”

“Did you leave your bedroom?”

“I was going to. I had put on my dressing-gown, and I was going into my sitting-room to get a book—”

“Had you opened your bedroom door?”

“I had just opened it.”

“But you had not gone into the sitting-room?”

“No—I was stopped.”

“What stopped you?”

“I saw a light under the door; and I heard footsteps coming towards it.”

“Were you frightened?”

“Not then. I knew my poor mother was a bad sleeper. And I remembered she had tried hard that evening to persuade me to let her take charge of my Diamond. She was anxious about it—unreasonably, as I thought. I fancied she was coming to see if I was in bed, and to speak of it again if she found me up.”

“What did you do?”

“I blew out my candle, so that she might think I was in bed. I was unreasonable too—I was determined to keep my Diamond where I had put it.”

“After you blew out the candle, did you go back to bed?”

“I had no time. At the moment I blew it out, the sitting-room door opened, and I saw—”

“You saw?”

“You.”

“Dressed as usual?”

“No.”

“In my nightgown?”

“In your nightgown—with your bedroom candle in your hand.”

“Alone?”

“Alone.”

“Could you see my face?”

“Yes.”

“Plainly?”

“Quite plainly. The candle showed it to me.”

“Were my eyes open?”

“Yes.”

“Did you notice anything strange? Anything fixed or vacant in them?”

“Nothing. Your eyes were bright—brighter than usual. You looked about as if you knew you were where you ought not to be, and were afraid of being found out.”

“Did you observe how I walked?”

“You walked as you always do. You came in as far as the middle of the room—and stopped and looked about you.”

“What did you do when you first saw me?”

“I could do nothing. I was petrified. I couldn’t speak, I couldn’t call out, I couldn’t even move to shut my door.”

“Could I see you, where you stood?”

“You might certainly have seen me. But you never looked towards me. It’s useless to ask. I am sure you never saw me.”

“How are you sure?”

“Would you have taken the Diamond? Would you have acted as you did afterwards? Would you be here now—if you had seen I was awake and looking at you? Don’t make me talk of that part! I want to answer quietly. Help me keep as calm as I can. Go on to something else.”

She was right; I went on.

“What did I do after I stopped in the middle of the room?”

“You turned away and went straight to the corner near the window—where my Indian cabinet stands.”

“When I was at the cabinet my back must have been to you. How did you see what I did?”

“When you moved, I moved.”

“So as to see my hands?”

“There are three glasses in my sitting-room. As you stood there, I saw all you did reflected in one of them.”

“What did you see?”

“You put your candle on the top of the cabinet. You opened and shut one drawer after another, until you came to the drawer I had put my Diamond in. You looked at the open drawer for a moment. Then you put your hand in and took the Diamond out.”

“How do you know I took it out?”

“I saw your hand go into the drawer. I saw the gleam of the stone between your finger and thumb when you drew your hand out.”

“Did my hand go back to the drawer again—to close it, for instance?”

“No. You had the Diamond in your right hand; you took the candle from the cabinet with your left.”

“Did I look about again after that?”

“No.”

“Did I leave the room immediately?”

“No. You stood quite still for what seemed a long time. I saw your face sideways in the glass. You looked like a man thinking—and dissatisfied with his own thoughts.”

“What happened next?”

“You roused yourself suddenly and went straight out.”

“Did I close the door after me?”

“No. You passed quickly into the passage and left the door open.”

“And then?”

“Then your light disappeared, and the sound of your steps died away, and I was left alone in the dark.”

“Did nothing happen after that—until the whole house knew the Diamond was lost?”

“Nothing.”

“Are you sure? Might you not have slept for part of the time?”

“I never slept. I never went back to my bed. Nothing happened until Penelope came in at the usual time in the morning.”

I let go her hand, rose, and walked once round the room. Every question I could frame had been answered; every detail I could crave lay before me. I had returned—once more—to the notions of sleep-walking and intoxication, and once more they collapsed; the witness who had seen me had, by what she described, destroyed them. What was left to say? What to do? The Theft—only the Theft—stood there like a thing one could touch: the one solid object in the middle of a darkness that had no edges. Not a glimmer from the Shivering Sand; not a glimmer now.

This time she spoke first.

“Well?” she said. “You have asked, and I have answered. You made me hope something from it, because you hoped something. What have you to say now?”

There was warning in her tone: my influence over her was gone again, as if a door had closed.

“We were to look at what happened on my birthday night together,” she went on, “and we were then to understand each other. Have we done that?”

She waited, pitiless. I answered badly; I made the fatal mistake. The helplessness of my situation exasperated me, and I reproached her for the silence that had kept me in ignorance until now.

“If you had spoken when you ought to have spoken,” I began, “if you had done me the common justice to explain yourself—”

Her fury broke out at once, like flame finding air.

“Explain myself!” she repeated. “Is there another man like this in the world? I spare him when my heart is breaking; I screen him when my own character is at stake; and he—of all human beings, he—turns on me now and tells me I ought to have explained myself! After believing in him as I did, after loving him as I did, after thinking of him by day and dreaming of him by night—he wonders I didn’t charge him with his disgrace the first time we met. ‘My heart’s darling, you are a thief! My hero whom I love and honour, you have crept into my room under cover of night and stolen my Diamond!’ That is what I ought to have said. You villain, you mean, mean, mean villain, I would have lost fifty diamonds rather than see your face lying to me as I see it lying now!”

I took up my hat. In mercy to her—yes, in mercy to her—I turned away without a word and opened the door by which I had entered.

She followed, snatched the door from my hand, shut it, and pointed me back.

“No!” she said. “Not yet! It seems I owe a justification of my conduct to you. You shall stay and hear it. Or you shall stoop to the lowest infamy of all, and force your way out.”

It wrung my heart to see her and to hear her. I could only make a sign that I submitted.

As I went back and took my chair, the crimson of her anger began to fade. She waited, steadied herself, and when she spoke again there was but one sign of feeling left: she did not look at me. Her hands were locked together in her lap; her eyes fixed on the floor.

“I ought to have done you the common justice to explain myself,” she said, repeating my words. “You shall see whether I tried, or not. I told you just now I never slept, and never returned to my bed after you left my sitting-room. It’s useless to trouble you by dwelling on what I thought—you would not understand my thoughts. I will only tell you what I did, when time enough had passed to let me recover myself. I refrained from alarming the house, and telling everybody what had happened—as I ought to have done. In spite of what I had seen, I was fond enough of you to believe—no matter what!—any impossibility, rather than admit to myself you were deliberately a thief. I thought and thought—and I ended in writing to you.”

“I never received the letter,” I said.

“I know you never received it. Wait, and you shall hear why. My letter would have told you nothing openly; it would not have ruined you for life if it had fallen into other hands. It would only have said—in a manner you could not possibly have mistaken—that I had reason to know you were in debt, and that it was in my experience and in my mother’s experience of you that you were not very discreet, or very scrupulous, about how you got money when you wanted it. You would have remembered the visit of the French lawyer, and you would have known what I referred to. If you read on after that, you would have come to an offer I had to make—the offer, privately (not a word, mind, openly between us!), of the loan of as large a sum of money as I could get—and I would have got it!” Her colour rose as she lifted her eyes to me. “I would have pledged the Diamond myself, if I could have got the money in no other way! In those words I wrote to you. Wait! I did more than that. I arranged with Penelope to give you the letter when nobody was near. I planned to shut myself into my bedroom, and to have the sitting-room left open and empty all the morning. And I hoped—with all my heart and soul I hoped—that you would take the opportunity and put the Diamond back secretly in the drawer.”

I tried to speak. She lifted her hand and stopped me, impatient; the quick alternation of her temper had brought anger rising again. She stood up and came nearer.

“I know what you are going to say,” she went on. “You are going to remind me again that you never received my letter. I can tell you why. I tore it up.”

“For what reason?” I asked.

“For the best of reasons. I preferred tearing it up to throwing it away upon such a man as you! What was the first news that reached me in the morning? Just as my little plan was complete, what did I hear? I heard that you—you—were the foremost person in the house in fetching the police. You were the active man; you were the leader; you were working harder than any of them to recover the jewel! You even carried your audacity far enough to ask to speak to me about the loss of the Diamond—the Diamond you yourself had stolen; the Diamond that was all the time in your own hands! After that proof of your horrible falseness and cunning, I tore up my letter. But even then—even when I was maddened by the searching and questioning of the policeman whom you had sent in—even then there was some infatuation in my mind which wouldn’t let me give you up. I said to myself, ‘He has played his vile farce before everybody else in the house. Let me try if he can play it before me.’ Somebody told me you were on the terrace. I went down to the terrace. I forced myself to look at you; I forced myself to speak to you. Have you forgotten what I said?”

I might have told her I remembered every word. But of what use was it then?

How could I make her understand that what she had said had astonished and distressed me; that it had made me fear for her in the tremor of her nerves; that it had even cast, for one fleeting moment, a doubt across my mind whether the loss of the jewel was as mysterious to her as to us all—but that it had not given me the faintest glimpse of the truth? Without one shadow of proof to set beside my bare denial, how could I persuade her that I knew no more than any stranger of what was in her thoughts when she spoke to me on the terrace?
