Thirty days after leaving Dawson, the Salt Water Mail reached Skaguay, with Buck and his team leading the way. They arrived in terrible condition—exhausted, broken down, and hollow-eyed. Buck, once a sturdy one hundred and forty pounds, had shriveled to just over a hundred and fifteen. His teammates, though smaller, had lost even more weight. Pike, the chronic malingerer who’d faked injuries before, now limped for real. Sol-leks dragged one leg, and Dub bore a painful wrench in his shoulder blade.

Every paw was raw and sore. There was no spring left in their steps. Each stride thudded heavily on the trail, jolting their bodies and doubling the toll of every mile. They weren’t just tired—they were spent. Not from sudden strain, but from months of relentless labor that had drained them to the last drop of strength. Their muscles, fibers, cells—all were deadened by fatigue. Recovery was impossible; they had nothing left to give. In less than five months, they’d traveled over two thousand five hundred miles, resting only five days during the final eighteen hundred. When they reached Skaguay, they seemed barely alive. The traces hung loose, and on downhill stretches, they could barely keep out of the sled’s way.

“Mush on, poor sore feets,” the driver urged as they stumbled through the main street. “Dis is de las’. Den we get one long res’. Eh? For sure. One bully long res’.”

The drivers expected a long break. They’d covered twelve hundred miles with only two days off—and by rights, they deserved rest. But the Klondike rush had flooded the region with people, and letters from those left behind piled up like snowdrifts. Official orders came down: fresh Hudson Bay dogs would replace the worn-out ones. The old dogs were to be sold—cheaply, quickly, without mercy.

Three days passed. Then, on the fourth morning, two men from the States bought the entire team—harness included—for next to nothing. They called themselves Hal and Charles. Charles was middle-aged, pale, with weak eyes and a mustache that curled upward defiantly, betraying the droop of his lips beneath. Hal was young—nineteen or twenty—with a Colt revolver strapped at his hip and a hunting knife beside it. His belt, crammed with cartridges, was the most striking thing about him—a badge of reckless youth. Both looked out of place. Why such men would venture into the North was a mystery no one could solve.

Buck watched the exchange, saw the money change hands, and knew the Scotch half-breed, the mail drivers, and all who’d come before were gone. When he was driven to their camp, he saw disorder everywhere—half-raised tent, unwashed dishes, chaos. And there was a woman. “Mercedes,” the men called her. Charles’s wife, Hal’s sister. A family unit, ill-suited to the wild.

Buck eyed them warily as they began packing. They moved with effort, but not with purpose. The tent was rolled clumsily, three times too big. Dishes stayed dirty. Mercedes flitted around, interfering, chattering advice. She argued when they placed a clothes-sack on the front of the sled—then insisted it go on the back—then found things that had to go inside it again. They reloaded twice.

Three neighbors from a nearby tent watched, grinning.

“You’ve got a right smart load already,” one said. “And I wouldn’t tote that tent if I were you.”

“Undreamed of!” Mercedes gasped, throwing her hands up. “How could I possibly manage without it?”

“It’s spring,” the man replied. “No more cold weather coming.”

She shook her head firmly. Charles and Hal stacked the final items on top of the mountainous heap.

“Think it’ll ride?” another asked.

“Why shouldn’t it?” Charles snapped.

“Oh, that’s all right,” the man said hastily. “Just wondering. Seems a bit top-heavy.”

Charles turned away and tightened the lashings—poorly.

“And the dogs can haul this thing all day,” a third man said.

“Certainly,” Hal replied, grabbing the gee-pole and cracking his whip. “Mush! Mush on!”

The dogs lunged forward, strained against the breastbands, then collapsed. They couldn *t move the sled.

“The lazy brutes,” Hal yelled, raising the whip.

But Mercedes grabbed it, tears in her eyes. “Oh, Hal, you mustn’t!” she pleaded. “Poor dears! Promise me you won’t hurt them, or I won’t go a step.”

“Precious little you know about dogs,” Hal sneered. “They’re lazy. You have to whip them to get anything out of them. Ask anyone.”

She looked at the onlookers, pleading, horror etched on her face.

“They’re weak as water,” one said. “Plum tuckered out. They need rest.”

“Rest be blanked,” Hal shot back.

Mercedes winced at the curse.

But she stood by her brother. “Don’t listen to him,” she insisted. “You’re driving our dogs. Do what you think best.”

Hal cracked the whip again. The dogs threw themselves forward, dug in, lowered their bodies—everything they had. But the sled didn’t budge. After two tries, they stopped, panting. The whip whistled through the air—then Mercedes dropped to her knees before Buck, arms around his neck.

“You poor, poor dears,” she sobbed. “Why don’t you pull harder? Then you wouldn’t be whipped.” Buck didn’t like her, but he was too weary to resist. He took it as part of the day’s misery.

One of the watchers, clenching his teeth, finally spoke: “I don’t care what happens to you—but for the dogs’ sake, break out that sled. The runners are frozen. Push on the gee-pole, side to side. Break it free.”

Hal tried again. This time, following the advice, he freed the runners. The overloaded sled lurched forward. The dogs struggled under a storm of blows. A hundred yards ahead, the path curved sharply downward into the main street. It would have taken an expert to keep such a top-heavy load upright—and Hal wasn’t one. As they turned, the sled tipped over, spilling half its cargo through loose lashings. The dogs kept running. The lighter sled skidded along on its side. Angry at the abuse and unfair burden, Buck surged forward. The others followed. Hal shouted “Whoa! Whoa!” but they ignored him. He tripped. The overturned sled crushed him. The dogs raced up the street, scattering the rest of the gear across Skaguay like confetti.

Kind-hearted citizens caught the dogs and gathered the mess. They gave advice: “Half the load, twice the dogs—if you ever want to reach Dawson.”

Hal, Charles, and Mercedes listened reluctantly. They pitched a new tent, sorted their gear. Canned goods were pulled out—laughed at. “Blankets for a hotel!” one man joked. “Half as many is too much. Toss the tent. Those dishes—who’s going to wash them? Good Lord, do you think you’re riding a Pullman?”

So it went—the ruthless purge of excess. Mercedes cried as her bags were emptied. She wept over everything, each item discarded. She rocked back and forth, clutching her knees, declaring she wouldn’t move an inch. She appealed to everyone, then wiped her eyes and threw out even essential clothing. Her fury grew—she attacked her men’s belongings next, sifting through them like a storm.

Still, the load remained massive. That evening, Charles and Hal bought six Outside dogs. Added to the original six, plus Teek and Koona—two huskies picked up at Rink Rapids—the team swelled to fourteen. But these new dogs were useless. Three were short-haired pointers, one a Newfoundland, two mongrels. They didn’t know trace or trail. Buck and his mates looked at them with contempt. He taught them what not to do, but not what to do. Most were confused, broken by the harshness of the north. Only the two mongrels had no spirit at all—just bones that could be shattered.

With the newcomers helpless and the old team wrecked by twenty-five hundred miles, the future looked bleak. Yet Charles and Hal were proud. Fourteen dogs! They’d never seen a sled with so many. They didn’t understand that one sled couldn’t carry food for fourteen dogs. They’d figured it out with a pencil—so much per dog, so many days, Q.E.D. Mercedes nodded, convinced it was simple.

The next morning, Buck led the team down the street. No energy. No fire. They started dead-tired. Buck had made the journey four times. Now, battered and spent, he faced it again. His heart wasn’t in it—and neither was any other dog’s. The Outsiders were afraid. The Insiders doubted their masters.

Buck sensed these people couldn’t be relied on. They didn’t know how to do anything. As days passed, it became clear: they couldn’t learn. They were careless, disorganized. It took them half the night to pitch a shabby camp, half the morning to pack it—so poorly that they spent the whole day stopping and rearranging. Some days, they barely made ten miles. Other days, they didn’t start at all. Never did they cover more than half the distance they’d planned.

It was inevitable they’d run out of dog food. But they rushed it by overfeeding. The Outside dogs, unused to famine, ate greedily. When the huskies pulled weakly, Hal doubled the ration. Then, when Mercedes begged for more food—tears in her eyes—he refused. So she stole fish from the sacks and fed the dogs secretly.

But what Buck and the huskies needed wasn’t food—it was rest. And though they moved slowly, the heavy load sapped their strength.

Then came underfeeding. One morning, Hal realized half the dog food was gone, and only a quarter of the trip completed. No more food could be found. He cut the rations—again, below standard. His sister and brother-in-law agreed. But they couldn’t make the dogs go faster. Their own incompetence kept them from starting earlier or traveling longer. They didn’t know how to work dogs—or themselves.

Dub was the first to fall. Poor, clumsy thief though he was, he’d always worked hard. His injured shoulder worsened until Hal shot him with the Colt. The country says an Outside dog dies on a husky’s ration. These six Outside dogs died on half that. The Newfoundland went first. Then the three pointers. The mongrels held on longest—but not forever.

By then, the soft ways of the South were gone. Arctic travel had stripped away romance, revealing a brutal truth. Mercedes stopped crying for the dogs. She wept for herself, quarreled with Charles and Hal. They fought constantly. Their irritation grew with their suffering, feeding on itself. The patience born of hardship—kindness forged in pain—never came to them. They were stiff, aching, bitter. Words were sharp—first thing in the morning, last at night.

Charles and Hal fought whenever Mercedes let them. Each believed he did more than his share—and each said so. Sometimes she sided with her husband, sometimes with her brother. The quarrel grew monstrous—dragging in ancestors, cousins, distant relatives, some long dead. Hal’s taste in art, his aunt’s social habits—anything—could spark a fight. Mercedes vented her rage on her husband’s family, accusing them of gossip and weakness. Meanwhile, the fire stayed unlit, the camp unfinished, the dogs unfed.

Mercedes nursed a deep grievance—of being treated like a woman. She was soft, pretty, used to being protected. Now, her husband and brother treated her roughly. She was used to helplessness. They complained. And so she made life unbearable. She no longer cared for the dogs. She rode the sled, despite her weight—120 pounds—adding the final burden to the starving animals. She rode for days until they collapsed. Charles and Hal begged her to walk. She wept, accused them of cruelty.

Once, they pulled her off by force. They never did it again. She went limp like a spoiled child and sat down on the trail. They walked on. She didn’t move. After three miles, they turned back, unloaded, carried her, and forced her onto the sled.

In their own suffering, they ignored the dogs’ agony. Hal preached that one must harden. He failed to teach his family—so he beat the dogs with a club. At Five Fingers, the food ran out. An old squaw offered frozen horse-hide in trade for Hal’s revolver. It was worse than nothing—stripped from starved horses months before. Frozen, it felt like galvanized iron. Thawed, it became thin, leathery strings and hair—indigestible, irritating.

Through it all, Buck trudged at the front—like a dream. He pulled when he could. When he couldn’t, he fell. Blows from whip or club brought him back to his feet. His beautiful coat was gone—matted, torn, stained with blood. Muscles had wasted to cords. Flesh vanished. Every rib, every bone showed beneath wrinkled skin. It was heartbreaking—except Buck’s heart was unbreakable. The man in the red sweater had proven that.

Same with his mates. Seven dogs remained—walking skeletons. They no longer felt the lash or club. Pain was dull, distant. Sight and sound blurred. They were not half-alive. They were empty shells—bags of bones with faint sparks still flickering. At stops, they dropped like dead dogs. The spark dimmed. Then, when struck, it flared weakly, and they rose, tottered, and staggered on.

One day, Billee—the good-natured one—fell and couldn’t rise. Hal had traded his revolver. He took the axe and killed Billee where he lay. Cut the body from the harness, dragged it aside. Buck saw. His mates saw. They knew it was coming for them.

Next day, Koona fell. Five remained: Joe, too weak for anger; Pike, crippled, half-conscious; Sol-leks, one-eyed, still loyal, mournful; Teek, fresher but beaten harder; and Buck—still at the head, but blind half the time, feeling the trail only by instinct, by the touch of his paws.

Spring was glorious. The sun rose early, set late. Dawn came at three. Twilight lingered till nine. The world blazed in light. Winter’s silence was gone. The land hummed with life waking up. Sap rose in pines. Willows burst into buds. Shrubs turned green. Crickets sang at night. Creeping things stirred in the sun. Partridges boomed. Woodpeckers knocked. Squirrels chattered. Birds sang. Overhead, geese honked in sharp V-formations, slicing the sky.

From every slope, water trickled—music from unseen springs. Everything thawed, bent, cracked. The Yukon strained to break free. Ice melted from below. Sun burned from above. Air holes formed. Fissures spread. Thin ice crashed into the river.

And through it all—under the blazing sun, in soft breezes—like travelers toward death, stumbled the two men, the woman, and the huskies.
