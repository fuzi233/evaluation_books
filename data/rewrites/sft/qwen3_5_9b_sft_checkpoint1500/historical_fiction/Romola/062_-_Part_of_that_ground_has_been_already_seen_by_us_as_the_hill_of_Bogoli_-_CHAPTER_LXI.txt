Eight days had passed since the night that altered everything, and now Romola stood upon the edge of the Mediterranean Sea, gazing at the gentle, rhythmic pulse of the water just beyond the modest fishing hamlet of Viareggio. Once more, she had escaped Florence. This time, however, no commanding voice called her back; the silence around her was absolute. Clad in the grey habit of a religious sister, she wore the disguise not as a shield, but with a chilling indifference, for in the depths of her heart-sickness, she no longer cared for the labels men placed upon her. Within her, a fresh rebellion had ignited, born of a profound despair. She questioned the necessity of clinging to a single identity when every other thread of meaning had unraveled. Where was the consistent duty bound to "Romola"? What power existed to forge the sacred motive men call duty, save through a love that remained steadfast?

Every tie of deep affection had been severed. In her marriage, the most sacred bond of all, she had failed to perceive the mystical union that guarantees permanence, nor the obligation of a voluntary vow. Had she not demonstrated that the causes to which she had pledged her life were ultimately futile? The urge to liberate herself surged again with overwhelming force, yet she knew this freedom would merely trade one catastrophe for another. For a woman who believes her greatest relationship was nothing more than a fatal error, there is no recompense. She had lost her crown. The profound secret of human blessedness had briefly whispered to her lips before slipping forever beyond her grasp.

Her primary anchor amidst this supreme sorrow had vanished. The vision of any lofty purpose, any end to existence capable of sanctifying endurance and elevating mundane labor with divine fire, was now obliterated. All human endeavor appeared to her as dragging at tangled threads; all fellowship, whether in resistance or advocacy, seemed nothing but unfairness and exclusion. Who, after all, was the man who had embodied the highest heroism for her—not the stoic endurance of stone, but the self-offering love of a willing spirit? What was the cause he strove for? Romola had lost her trust in Savonarola, lost the fervent admiration that had previously allowed her to overlook his deviations, focusing only on the grand sweep of his ambition. Now, her sharp sensitivity toward her godfather, which had placed her in opposition to the Friar, revealed the repulsive and contradictory details of his teachings with a painful, hyper-lucid clarity. In the bitterness of her disillusionment, she declared that his quest to renew the Church and the world was a hollow pursuit of a mere title—a name that signified little more than the strategies required to fortify his grip on Florence, often at the cost of questionable acts and words designed to protect his fragile influence from the consequences of his own mistakes. That political reform, once a beacon of new interest, now reduced itself to narrow schemes for the safety of the city, standing in shameful contradiction to his professed blind trust in Divine providence.

It was inevitable that she judge the Friar unjustly regarding a matter of individual suffering, viewed through the lens of personal tenderness, while he viewed it through the rigid prism of theoretical conviction. In his declaration that the cause of his party was the Kingdom of God, she heard only the echo of selfishness. Yet, perhaps such words rarely escape without that baser ring, for they remain the implicit formula of all energetic belief. If such a belief, pursuing a grand and distant ideal, risks descending into a form of demon-worship where devotees send their children through the flames with a readiness that borders on the sacrificial, then tender compassion for the immediate neighbor carries its own perils, tending toward timidity and skepticism regarding the larger aims necessary for life to ascend into true religion. Thus, blinded by her tears, poor Romola drifted further from truth.

No soul who has ever known the shattering loss of faith in a fellow-man whom they have deeply loved and revered will lightly claim that such a shock leaves the faith in the Invisible Goodness unscathed. As high human trust sinks, the dignity of life follows suit; we cease to believe in our own higher selves, for that too is part of the common nature degraded in our thoughts, and all the finer impulses of the soul grow dull. Romola felt the springs of her former pity dry up, leaving her to a barren, egoistic lament. Had not she suffered her own sorrows? And few had cared for her while she had given so freely to others. She had done enough; she had striven for the impossible and grew weary of this suffocating, crowded existence. She longed for a repose in mere sensation, a state she had sometimes dreamed of during the sultry afternoons of her youth, when she imagined herself floating, naive and fluid, within the waters.

The clear waves seemed to beckon. She wished she could lie down upon them and slip from sleep into death. Yet Romola could not directly seek death; the fullness of young life within her forbade such a direct step. She could only wish that death would arrive.

At the point where she paused, the shore curved sharply inward, revealing a small boat with a sail moored nearby. In her longing to glide across the waters turning golden under the level rays of the sun, she recalled a tale from Boccaccio, one she had cherished reading from the *Decamerone* when her father slept. It was the story of Gostanza, a beauty driven by lovesick despair to live no longer, yet lacking the courage to strike down her young life. She had boarded a boat, pushed off into the sea, and wrapped her mantle around her head, hoping to be wrecked, trusting that her terror would render flight from death impossible. The memory had lingered in Romola's mind as a mere thought, failing to blossom into distinct desire, until now. As she paced back and forth, she saw another vessel, dark against the red-gold light, approach the bend where the first boat lay. A single man rowed it toward the shore. Watching him land, unload cargo, and prepare to depart, she realized her window of opportunity was closing—he would leave soon, taking the chance to acquire the smaller boat. Though she had not yet fully admitted to herself that she intended to use it, a sudden, fierce eagerness seized her to secure the possibility, marking the unconscious growth of a thought into a desperate desire.

"Is that little boat yours?" she asked the fisherman, who had looked up, startled by the tall, grey-clad figure, and bowed in reverence to this holy sister wandering alone in the evening solitude.

"It is mine," he replied. "An old craft, hardly seaworthy, though worth repair to any buyer." By the blessing of Saint Anthony, whose chapel stood near the village, his fishing had prospered, and he now possessed a superior vessel, formerly owned by Gianni, who had died. Yet, he had not sold the old boat. Romola inquired of its value. While the fisherman busied himself, she slipped the remaining coins from her meager dinner into a small satchel on the ground. She then watched him furled his sail and asked how to set it if he wished to venture to sea. Pacing up and down again, she waited for his departure.

As the fisherman departed, gathering his few belongings, the image of herself gliding away on those darkening waters grew stronger, transforming into a physical thirst, much like the craving for a cool stream in oppressive heat. To be freed from the burden of choice when every motive was bruised—to commit herself, in a slumber, to destiny which might bring death or awaken a new necessity within her—it was a thought that beckoned her, especially as the soft evening air invited rest in solitude rather than return to the noise and feverish heat of the village.

Finally, the slow fisherman walked away. Soon, the gold of the sun faded into dusk, leaving sea and sky alike devoid of living things, save for the lulling, monotonous lap of waves. Here, the sea held no tide to aid her drifting if she waited for the ebb, but Romola sensed the land breeze rising slightly. She entered the boat, unfurled the sail, and secured it as she had learned in that first brief lesson. Seeing it catch the faint wind, she deemed it sufficient. Loosening the moorings, she tried to urge the craft forward with an oar, pushing it far from the land until the sea turned dark even to the west, and stars began to disclose themselves like a throbbing life over the vast heavens. Finally resting, she threw back her cowl and removed the kerchief beneath it, doubling both under her head for a pillow against one of the boat's ribs. Her youthful head could endure the hard wood.

Thus she lay, the soft night air breathing upon her as she glided silently over the water, watching the deepening quiet of the sky. She was alone now; she had freed herself from all claims, freeing herself even from that heavy burden of choice that presses harder when those claims loosen their guiding hold.

Had she found anything akin to the dreams of her girlhood? No. Memories clung to her like the weight of broken wings that could never again lift flight—memories of human sympathy which, even in pain, left a thirst that the Great Mother had no milk to quench. Romola felt orphaned in these wide expanses of sea and sky. She read no message of love for her in that distant, symbolic script of the heavens, and with a great sob, she wished she were gliding into death.
