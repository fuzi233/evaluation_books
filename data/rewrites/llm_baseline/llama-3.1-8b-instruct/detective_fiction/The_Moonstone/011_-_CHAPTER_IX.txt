The sky above the estate was a deep, foreboding grey on June twenty-first, the day of Miss Rachel's birthday, but by noon, the clouds parted, and the sun broke through, casting a warm glow over the gardens. In the bustling servants' hall, we prepared for the celebration by presenting our little gifts to the birthday girl, accompanied by my annual speech, a tradition we'd adopted from the Queen's Parliament openings. Though the words were familiar, the excitement in the air was palpable as I delivered them, followed by a mixture of polite grumbles and hopeful anticipation for something new next year.

Following breakfast, I joined Mr. Franklin in a private discussion regarding the Moonstone's transfer from the bank at Frizinghall to Miss Rachel's possession. His mood was unpredictable, veering between enthusiasm and uncertainty, and I couldn't help but wonder if his nocturnal restlessness was contributing to his indecisiveness. As we deliberated, I remained resolute, grounded in the facts: nothing had occurred to warrant alarming my lady, and Mr. Franklin's obligation to hand over the Diamond remained unchanged. After some persuasion, he agreed to ride to Frizinghall, accompanied by Mr. Godfrey and the two young ladies, to retrieve the gem and return with it to our estate.

With the arrangements settled, Mr. Franklin departed, leaving behind the chaos of the preparations. Miss Rachel and her companions spent the morning and early afternoon adorning the entrance with a vibrant display of colors, with Penelope carefully mixing the hues according to their instructions. My lady flitted in and out of the room, occasionally covering her nose with her handkerchief due to the strong scent emanating from the decorations. As the lunch hour approached, the group finally completed their masterpiece, releasing Penelope from her duties and sending her off to recover from the effects of the pungent paint.

The door, adorned with intricately designed griffins, cupids, and other motifs, was indeed a breathtaking sight, although its overwhelming complexity left a lasting impression on the senses. Penelope's contribution to the project had been a testament to her dedication, even if it came at a personal cost. As the family gathered for luncheon, Mr. Franklin excused himself, claiming he needed to escort his cousins, though I knew the truth: he was off to collect the Moonstone from Frizinghall.

As the master of ceremonies, I oversaw the preparations for dinner, ensuring the wine was chilled and the staff were aware of their roles.
