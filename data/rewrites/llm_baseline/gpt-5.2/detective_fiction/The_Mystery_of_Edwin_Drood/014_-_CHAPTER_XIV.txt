Christmas Eve laid Cloisterham under its old spell, as if the year turned back upon itself and found the same marks cut in the same stone. Faces drifted in from the outer world—grown faces that had once been the town’s children—and the place seemed to have shrunk around them, like cloth badly washed, drawing tight at the seams. The Cathedral clock struck with the same deliberate authority it had in their nursery-days, and the rooks cawed from the tower in a harsh, homely chorus that belonged to no single season, but to all seasons at once. Even the air carried its own memory: leaf-rustle remembered by people who, in far-off sickrooms, had fancied their floors strewn with the Close’s autumn fall, the circle of their lives almost closed, beginning and end leaning toward each other until they touched.

Holly berries shone in lattices at Minor Canon Corner. Mr. and Mrs. Tope, careful as cooks pinning sprigs into a joint before it goes to table, were sticking bright leaves into the Cathedral carvings and sconces, dressing the stalls as if the wood itself were a dignitary needing his buttonhole. Shop windows made a lavish show of currants and raisins, nut-brown and glossy; of candied peel cut into jewel-like strips; of spices—cinnamon and clove—whose scent, crushed between the fingers, could warm a whole street; of moist sugar packed down like snow that had learned to melt. There was a holiday swagger too: mistletoe hung heavy in a greengrocer’s doorway, and a forlorn little Twelfth Cake—so stinted it looked as if it had been divided and divided again—balanced a Harlequin on its summit, offered for a shilling a chance at sweetness. The wax-work exhibition, once so admired by distant royalty, promised itself “by particular desire” on a bankrupt stable-keeper’s premises; and the Theatre shouted a new pantomime with a postered clown who asked the town how it did to-morrow, as large as life and as pitiable as paint could make him. Yet Cloisterham’s schools were quiet: the High School boys had fled home, each secretly in love with some young lady who knew nothing of it; and Miss Twinkleton’s windows fluttered only with handmaidens, who, left to represent their sex in the concrete, grew skittish within propriety like sparrows permitted a whole granary.

Three were to meet at the gatehouse that night. All day, as if in separate kitchens before a single meal, each made ready in his own fashion.

Neville Landless, though freed from books by Mr. Crisparkle’s holiday indulgence, worked in his quiet room until two hours after noon, reading and writing with a close, intent air. Then, with the thoroughness of a man scrubbing down his board after kneading, he cleared his table, set his books in strict order, and tore up every stray paper. He burned the scraps until no loose leaf remained but what bore directly upon his studies. Drawers were squared, corners made clean; nothing untidy survived.

Next he turned to his wardrobe and selected a few plain necessities: change of stout shoes and socks fit for walking, clothing chosen not for show but for endurance, like provisions chosen for a long road. These he packed into a knapsack—new, bought yesterday in the High Street. At the same time he had bought a heavy walking-stick, iron-shod and thick in the handle, made for a hand to grip as if it meant to hold its ground. He swung it, tried its balance, laid it beside the knapsack on the window-seat, and, his arrangements finished, dressed to go out.

He had left his room and met Mr. Crisparkle on the staircase, the Minor Canon coming out upon the same story, when Neville turned back for the stick, thinking to carry it now. When he reappeared with it, Mr. Crisparkle paused, saw it, and with a smile took it from him.

“How do you choose a stick, Neville?”

“Really I don’t know that I understand the subject,” Neville answered. “I chose it for its weight.”

“Much too heavy, Neville; much too heavy.”

“To rest upon in a long walk, sir?”

“Rest upon?” Mr. Crisparkle repeated, dropping his shoulders into an easy pedestrian poise. “You don’t rest upon it; you merely balance with it.”

“I shall know better with practice, sir. I have not lived in a walking country, you know.”

“True,” said Mr. Crisparkle. “Get into a little training, and we will have a few score miles together. I should leave you nowhere now. Do you come back before dinner?”

“I think not, as we dine early.”

Mr. Crisparkle gave him a bright nod—cheerful in manner, and meaning to be so, as if confidence itself were a remedy—and left him.

Neville went to the Nuns’ House and asked that Miss Landless be told her brother waited by appointment. He stood at the gate and did not cross the threshold; he was on his parole not to put himself in Rosa’s way.

Helena was as mindful as he. She came out at once. They met with quiet affection and, avoiding loitering, walked out toward the upper inland country where the air lay harder and clearer.

“I am not going to tread upon forbidden ground, Helena,” Neville said when they had gone some distance and turned. “You will understand, though, that I cannot help referring to—what shall I say?—my infatuation.”

“Had you not better avoid it, Neville? You know that I can hear nothing.”

“You can hear, my dear, what Mr. Crisparkle has heard, and heard with approval.”

“Yes; I can hear so much.”

“Well, it is this. I am not only unsettled and unhappy myself, but I am conscious of unsettling and interfering with other people. How do I know that, but for my unfortunate presence, you, and—and—the rest of that former party, our engaging guardian excepted, might be dining cheerfully in Minor Canon Corner to-morrow? Indeed it probably would be so. I can see too well that I am not high in the old lady’s opinion, and it is easy to understand what an irksome clog I must be upon the hospitalities of her orderly house—especially at this time of year—when I must be kept asunder from this person, and there is such a reason for my not being brought into contact with that person, and an unfavourable reputation has preceded me with such another person; and so on. I have put this very gently to Mr. Crisparkle, for you know his self-denying ways; but still I have put it. What I have laid much greater stress upon at the same time is, that I am engaged in a miserable struggle with myself, and that a little change and absence may enable me to come through it the better. So, the weather being bright and hard, I am going on a walking expedition, and intend taking myself out of everybody’s way (my own included, I hope) to-morrow morning.”

“When to come back?”

“In a fortnight.”

“And going quite alone?”

“I am much better without company, even if there were any one but you to bear me company, my dear Helena.”

“Mr. Crisparkle entirely agrees, you say?”

“Entirely. I am not sure but that at first he was inclined to think it rather a moody scheme, and one that might do a brooding mind harm. But we took a moonlight walk last Monday night, to talk it over at leisure, and I represented the case to him as it really is. I showed him that I do want to conquer myself, and that, this evening well got over, it is surely better that I should be away from here just now, than here. I could hardly help meeting certain people walking together here, and that could do no good, and is certainly not the way to forget. A fortnight hence, that chance will probably be over, for the time; and when it again arises for the last time, why, I can again go away. Farther, I really do feel hopeful of bracing exercise and wholesome fatigue. You know that Mr. Crisparkle allows such things their full weight in the preservation of his own sound mind in his own sound body, and that his just spirit is not likely to maintain one set of natural laws for himself and another for me. He yielded to my view of the matter, when convinced that I was honestly in earnest; and so, with his full consent, I start to-morrow morning. Early enough to be not only out of the streets, but out of hearing of the bells, when the good people go to church.”

Helena thought it over, and thought well of it—not only because Mr. Crisparkle did, but because she could taste the health in it: the plain discipline of road and cold air, the honest fatigue that empties a troubled mind the way a long simmer empties a pot of its harshness. She pitied him for going alone at Christmas, yet she knew encouragement was the truer charity, and she gave it.

He would write?

Every alternate day, he promised, with all his adventures.

Would he send clothes ahead?

“My dear Helena, no. Travel like a pilgrim, with wallet and staff. My wallet—or my knapsack—is packed, and ready for strapping on; and here is my staff!”

He handed her the stick. She weighed it and, like Mr. Crisparkle, said it was very heavy. What wood? Iron-wood, he told her.

Up to then he had been very cheerful, as if stating his case in its brightest aspect had stirred his spirits, and having won her approval had steadied him. But as the day closed in and the city lights sprang up before them, his mood sank.

“I wish I were not going to this dinner, Helena.”

“Dear Neville, is it worth while to care much about it? Think how soon it will be over.”

“How soon it will be over!” he repeated, gloomily. “Yes. But I don’t like it.”

Perhaps there would be a moment’s awkwardness, Helena suggested, but only a moment. He was quite sure of himself.

“I wish I felt as sure of everything else, as I feel of myself,” he answered.

“How strangely you speak, dear! What do you mean?”

“Helena, I don’t know. I only know that I don’t like it. What a strange dead weight there is in the air!”

She pointed to copper-coloured clouds beyond the river and said the wind was rising. He scarcely spoke again until he left her at the Nuns’ House gate. She did not go in at once, but stayed looking after him along the street. Twice he passed the gatehouse, reluctant. At length, when the Cathedral clock chimed one quarter, he turned sharply and hurried in.

And so he went up the postern stair.

Edwin Drood’s day was solitary too, but in another key: less restless, more regretful. Something of deeper moment than he had expected had gone out of his life, and in the silence of his chamber he had wept for it last night. Though the image of Miss Landless still hovered behind his thoughts—handsome, quick, and a little dangerous to a young man’s vanity—the stronghold of his mind was held by the “pretty little affectionate creature” who had proved firmer and wiser than he had supposed. Thinking of Rosa, he felt a misgiving about his own unworthiness: how they might have been different, if he had been more in earnest sooner; if he had valued her higher; if he had not taken his lot as something inherited and therefore owed, but had learned the right way to appreciate and improve it. The heartache was sharp, yet youth, with its caprice, would not let the handsome background wholly fade.

That look of Rosa’s when they parted at the gate—what had it meant? Had she seen below the surface of his mind, down into its twilight? Hardly; it had been astonished, keenly inquiring, as if she had discovered a new language in him and could not translate it. He decided he did not understand it, though it was remarkably expressive.

He only waited now for Mr. Grewgious; after seeing him, he would depart. To fill the hours he took a lingering leave of the old city and its neighbourhood, recalling the time he and Rosa walked here or there, mere children, solemnly proud of being engaged. Poor children, he thought, with a sadness that softened rather than accused.

His watch had stopped. He turned into the jeweller’s shop to have it wound and set. The jeweller—oiled with holiday insinuation—begged leave to submit, in a general and aimless way, a bracelet: it would suit a young bride to perfection, especially one of a rather diminutive style of beauty. Edwin looked at it coldly. Then the jeweller turned the tray and invited him to rings for gentlemen: a very chaste signet now, much bought by gentlemen when changing their condition; a ring of a responsible appearance. With the wedding-day engraved inside, several gentlemen preferred it to any other memento.

Edwin viewed the rings with the same coolness and said he wore no jewellery but his watch and chain—his father’s—and his shirt-pin.

“That I was aware of,” the jeweller replied, “for Mr. Jasper dropped in for a watch-glass the other day, and, in fact, I showed these articles to him, remarking that if he should wish to make a present to a gentleman relative, on any particular occasion—But he said with a smile that he had an inventory in his mind of all the jewellery his gentleman relative ever wore; namely, his watch and chain, and his shirt-pin.” Still—so the jeweller considered—it might not apply to all times, though applying to the present time. “Twenty minutes past two, Mr. Drood, I set your watch at. Let me recommend you not to let it run down, sir.”

Edwin put it on and went out thinking, half amused and half touched: Dear old Jack! If I made an extra crease in my neckcloth, he would think it worth noticing.

He strolled about to pass time until dinner. Somehow the city reproached him to-day—not angrily, but with a pensive complaint, as if he had not used it well and it was sorry with him rather than against him. His usual carelessness gave way to a lingering attention to old landmarks. He would soon be far away and might never see them again. Poor youth, poor youth.

As dusk thickened, he paced the Monks’ Vineyard, to and fro by the Cathedral chimes. It had closed in dark before he became aware of a woman crouching near a wicket gate in a corner. The gate commanded a cross by-path little used in the gloaming. The figure must have been there all the while, but the dimness had hid her until, slowly, it admitted her shape.

He turned down the path and walked up to the wicket. A lamp near it showed her haggard face: a weazen chin resting on her hands, eyes staring forward with an unwinking, blind steadfastness. Edwin, always kindly and made kinder by his own softened evening, bent and spoke.

“Are you ill?”

“No, deary,” she answered without looking at him, the stare unchanged.

“Are you blind?”

“No, deary.”

“Are you lost, homeless, faint? What is the matter, that you stay here in the cold so long, without moving?”

By slow stiff efforts she contracted her vision until it rested on him. Then a curious film seemed to pass over her, and she began to shake.

He straightened, recoiled a step, and stared down in dread amazement, for he seemed to know her.

Good Heaven! he thought. Like Jack that night!

Looking up at him, she whimpered, “My lungs is weakly; my lungs is dreffle bad. Poor me, poor me, my cough is rattling dry!” And she coughed in confirmation, a harsh tearing sound that seemed to scrape the very air.

“Where do you come from?”

“Come from London, deary.” The cough still rending her.

“Where are you going to?”

“Back to London, deary. I came here, looking for a needle in a haystack, and I ain’t found it. Look’ee, deary; give me three-and-sixpence, and don’t you be afeard for me. I’ll get back to London then, and trouble no one. I’m in a business.—Ah, me! It’s slack, it’s slack, and times is very bad!—but I can make a shift to live by it.”

“Do you eat opium?”

“Smokes it,” she replied with difficulty, still racked by her cough. “Give me three-and-sixpence, and I’ll lay it out well, and get back. If you don’t give me three-and-sixpence, don’t give me a brass farden. And if you do give me three-and-sixpence, deary, I’ll tell you something.”

He counted the money and put it in her hand. She clutched it instantly, and rose with a croaking laugh of satisfaction.

“Bless ye! Hark’ee, dear genl’mn. What’s your Chris’en name?”

“Edwin.”

“Edwin, Edwin, Edwin,” she repeated, the word trailing off into drowsy echo, as if she tasted it and let it dissolve. Then, suddenly: “Is the short of that name Eddy?”

“It is sometimes called so,” he replied, colour starting to his face.

“Don’t sweethearts call it so?” she asked, pondering.

“How should I know?”

“Haven’t you a sweetheart, upon your soul?”

“None.”

She began to move away, murmuring, “Bless ye, and thank’ee, deary!” but Edwin called after her, “You were to tell me something; you may as well do so.”

“So I was, so I was. Well, then. Whisper. You be thankful that your name ain’t Ned.”

He looked at her steadily. “Why?”

“Because it’s a bad name to have just now.”

“How a bad name?”

“A threatened name. A dangerous name.”

“The proverb says that threatened men live long,” he answered lightly, trying to keep the chill from his voice.

“Then Ned—so threatened is he, wherever he may be while I am a-talking to you, deary—should live to all eternity!” she replied.

She leaned in to hiss it close to his ear, forefinger shaking before his eyes. Then she huddled herself together again, and with another “Bless ye, and thank’ee!” went off toward the Travellers’ Lodging House.

It was not an inspiriting end to a dull day. Alone in a sequestered place, hemmed by old stone and old decay, he felt the remark’s power to breed a shudder. He went toward the better-lighted streets and resolved to say nothing of it that night, but to mention it to Jack—to-morrow—as an odd coincidence; only a coincidence, of course, and nothing worth remembering.

Yet it clung to him as many things far more worthy never had. With another mile or so to kill before dinner, he crossed the bridge by the river, and her words were in the rising wind, in the angry sky, in the troubled water, in the flickering lights. Even the Cathedral chime seemed to carry a solemn echo of them, striking him with a sudden surprise to the heart as he turned in under the archway of the gatehouse.

And so he went up the postern stair.

John Jasper’s day, unlike theirs, ran smooth, agreeable, and bright. With no music-lessons in the holiday season, his time was his own except for Cathedral services. He was early among shopkeepers, ordering little table luxuries his nephew liked—as if he were composing a feast note by note: something sweet here, something rich there, touches chosen not for hunger but for pleasure. His nephew would not be with him long, he told the provision-dealers, and so must be petted and made much of.

Out on these hospitable errands, he looked in on Mr. Sapsea and mentioned that dear Ned and that inflammable young spark of Mr. Crisparkle’s were to dine at the gatehouse and make up their difference. Mr. Sapsea was by no means friendly toward the inflammable young spark. His complexion, Mr. Sapsea said, was “Un-English.” And once Mr. Sapsea had pronounced anything Un-English, he considered it sunk for ever in the bottomless pit.

Jasper was truly sorry to hear him speak so, for he knew well that Mr. Sapsea never spoke without meaning, and had a subtle trick of being right. Mr. Sapsea, by a very remarkable coincidence, was exactly of that opinion.

In the Cathedral Jasper was in beautiful voice. In the pathetic supplication to have his heart inclined to keep this law, he astonished his fellows by sheer melodious power. He had never sung difficult music with such skill and harmony as in that day’s Anthem. His nervous temperament sometimes drove him a shade too quickly; to-day his time was perfect. The building held the sound and gave it back, the vast stone moderating each note into a calm that seemed less art than atmosphere, and to those who listened it was as if the place itself had learned to breathe evenly.

These results were probably attained through a grand composure of spirits. The mechanism of his throat was a little tender—he wore, with singing-robe and ordinary dress alike, a large black scarf of strong close-woven silk slung loosely round his neck—but his composure was so noticeable that Mr. Crisparkle spoke of it when they came out from Vespers.

“I must thank you, Jasper, for the pleasure with which I have heard you to-day. Beautiful! Delightful! You could not have so outdone yourself, I hope, without being wonderfully well.”

“I am wonderfully well.”

“Nothing unequal,” said the Minor Canon, smoothing the air with his hand; “nothing unsteady, nothing forced, nothing avoided; all thoroughly done in a masterly manner, with perfect self-command.”

“Thank you. I hope so, if it is not too much to say.”

“One would think, Jasper, you had been trying a new medicine for that occasional indisposition of yours.”

“No, really? That’s well observed; for I have.”

“Then stick to it, my good fellow,” said Mr. Crisparkle, clapping him on the shoulder, “stick to it.”

“I will.”

“I congratulate you,” Mr. Crisparkle went on as they came out of the Cathedral, “on all accounts.”

“Thank you again. I will walk round to the Corner with you, if you don’t object; I have plenty of time before my company come; and I want to say a word to you, which I think you will not be displeased to hear.”

“What is it?”

“Well. We were speaking, the other evening, of my black humours.”

Mr. Crisparkle’s face fell; he shook his head deploringly.

“I said, you know, that I should make you an antidote to those black humours; and you said you hoped I would consign them to the flames.”

“And I still hope so, Jasper.”

“With the best reason in the world! I mean to burn this year’s Diary at the year’s end.”

“Because you—?” Mr. Crisparkle brightened as he began.

“You anticipate me. Because I feel that I have been out of sorts, gloomy, bilious, brain-oppressed, whatever it may be. You said I had been exaggerative. So I have.”

Mr. Crisparkle’s brightened face brightened still more.

“I couldn’t see it then, because I was out of sorts; but I am in a healthier state now, and I acknowledge it with genuine pleasure. I made a great deal of a very little; that’s the fact.”

“It does me good,” cried Mr. Crisparkle, “to hear you say it!”

“A man leading a monotonous life,” Jasper proceeded, “and getting his nerves, or his stomach, out of order, dwells upon an idea until it loses its proportions. That was my case with the idea in question. So I shall burn the evidence of my case, when the book is full, and begin the next volume with a clearer vision.”

“This is better,” said Mr. Crisparkle, stopping at his own doorsteps to shake hands, “than I could have hoped.”
