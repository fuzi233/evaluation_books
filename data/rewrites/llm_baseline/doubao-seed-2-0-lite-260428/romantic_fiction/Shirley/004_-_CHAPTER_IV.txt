MR. YORKE (_continued_)

He was the very paragon of a Yorkshire gentleman, through and through. At fifty-five, silver hair streaked no softer than new-fallen frost, he looked older at first glance still, his broad, low forehead crisscrossed with lines earned from a lifetime of hard work and sharper wit. The harsh, bracing bluntness of the north was written into every line of his face, just as it laced his speech; not a single soft, delicate Norman aristocratic line lingered in his features, his visage carved from unpolished, unclassic, thoroughly English stock. Fancy folk might dismiss it as vulgar, but anyone with half a brain would call it unforgettable, every furrow thrumming with shrewdness, sagacity, and a raw, unapologetic originality no polished town gentleman could ever fake. It was a face set against being led, and utterly impossible to drive — indocile, scornful, sharp-edged with sarcasm, belonging to a man who answered to no one. He stood well over average height, wiry and straight-backed, carrying himself with a quiet, unyielding integrity that banished any hint of the bumpkin anyone might have expected from his rough-hewn features.

Painting his character is far trickier than painting his face, though. If you, reader, are hoping for some perfect, benevolent, wise old philanthropist, you will be disappointed. He spoke with sense and a spark of genuine kindness to Robert Moore a little while back, but that does not mean he was always just, or gentle.

For one thing, he had not a scrap of what phrenologists call the organ of veneration — a gap that warped his judgement on every matter that called for awe. For another, he barely possessed the organ of comparison, that little nudge that lets a man step into another’s shoes, stripping him of easy sympathy. And last, his nature held far too little benevolence and ideality, draining most of the soft, glowing warmth out of his soul, and for him dimming every trace of that soft, divine grace the rest of the world stumbles across now and then.

His total lack of veneration made him unsparingly intolerant of anyone who claimed rank over him: kings, nobles, priests, dynasties, parliaments, every stuffy institution, most of their laws, all their silly forms, their hoary old claims to special privilege — to him they were all rubbish, plain and simple. He saw no use, no joy in any of it, and was fully convinced the world would be far better off if all those lofty high places were razed to the ground, and the men perched on top crushed in the fall. That same missing reverence left him numb to that electric, thrumming delight of admiring something truly beautiful for its own sake; it dried up a hundred quiet, pure sources of joy, withered a thousand small vivid pleasures no one else thinks twice about. He was no atheist, though he belonged to no chapel or church, but his faith could never be the soft, awe-struck kind known to people who know how to venerate what is greater than themselves. He believed in God and a heaven beyond this life, but his God and his heaven belonged to a man who never knew the hush of awe, the flight of imagination, the soft pull of tender affection.

His stunted powers of comparison made him a walking contradiction: he would spout fine, noble speeches about mutual tolerance and forbearance, all the while nurturing a bigoted, unreasoning hatred for entire classes of people. He spoke of “parsons” and everyone connected to them, of “lords” and all their hangers-on, with a harshness, even an insolence, that was as unjust as it was unbearable. He could not imagine what it was like to stand in the shoes of the people he railed against, could not weigh their faults against the temptations they faced, their flaws against the disadvantages they were born into. He could never picture how his own circumstances would warp him if he was placed in their position, and he would often roar out ferocious, tyrannical threats against people he thought were tyrants themselves. If you judged him by his words alone, he would have used arbitrary, even cruel methods to bring about the freedom and equality he ranted about so often. Equality! Yes, Mr. Yorke talked a great deal about equality, but at heart he was proud as Beelzebub — kind as a father to his workpeople, generous to everyone beneath him who never presumed to push for power over him, but haughty and unyielding to any man the rest of the world dared call his superior. Rebellion ran in his blood: he would not be ruled, his father before him would not be ruled, and his children after him would never bear control either.

That thin streak of general benevolence in his nature made him wildly impatient of foolishness, and every small weakness that grated against his sharp, shrewd temperament. There was no check at all on his biting sarcasm; he was no merciful man, and he would cut and cut again, never pausing to notice how deep his thrusts went, never caring how much pain he left in his wake.

As for his near-total lack of poetic imagination? That can barely be called a flaw. He had a fine ear for music, a sharp, true eye for colour and form, so his taste was impeccable — and who, after all, cares much for imagination anyway? Most folk think it a dangerous, silly trait, close to weakness, verging on frenzy, a kind of sickness rather than a gift.

Only the people who have it, or think they do, would ever disagree. Listen to them talk, and you would believe their hearts would turn cold if that sweet elixir of imagination did not flow through their veins, their eyes dim if that inner flame did not light up the world for them, their lives turn barren and lonely if that strange, gentle companion ever abandoned them. You would think it gave spring an extra gilded hope, summer a soft magic, autumn a tranquil warmth, winter a comfort no ordinary man could ever know. It is an illusion, of course, but the people who live by that dream cling to it tighter than they would cling to solid gold.

Since Hiram Yorke had no poetic imagination of his own, he considered it a thoroughly useless quality in anyone else. Painters and musicians he could tolerate, even encourage, since he could enjoy the fruit of their work — he could see the beauty of a great painting, feel the thrill of a fine melody — but a quiet poet, no matter what fire burned in his breast, no matter what genius churned under his quiet exterior? If that poet could not add up a ledger in a counting house, or strike a sharp bargain at the Piece Hall, he could have lived despised and died scorned right under Yorke’s nose.

And since there are a hundred Hiram Yorkes in the world, it is a mercy that the true poet, for all his outward meekness, often hides a tough, unyielding spirit under his placid surface, sharpens his wits even as he cultivates his gentleness, and can measure every inch of the small-minded men who look down on him, and rightly judge how little their fancy worldly achievements are worth. It is a happy thing that he can find his own joy, his own deep communion with the great wild natural world he loves, completely independent of the men who see no value in him, and for whom he feels no fondness at all. It is only just that when the world turns its cold, dark face to him — as it often does, for he gives it that same careless, distant coldness first — he can keep a bright, warm glow burning in his own chest that makes all the world feel soft and genial to him, even while strangers think his life is nothing but a frozen polar winter with no sun to ever warm it. The true poet is never to be pitied, and he often smirks in secret when some misguided do-gooder whines about his supposed wrongs. Even when utilitarians sit in judgement over him, declaring him and his art entirely useless, he hears their sentence with such broad, unyielding, merciless contempt for the self-righteous Pharisees who condemn him, that he deserves to be chided for his arrogance far more than he deserves any sympathy. But none of these thoughts belonged to Mr. Yorke, and it is Mr. Yorke we are concerned with here.

I have laid out his faults for you, reader — but his good points are equally clear. He was one of the most honourable, capable men in the whole of Yorkshire; even the people who disliked him could not help but respect him. The poor adored him, for he was unfailingly kind, fatherly and generous to everyone who lived in his cottages. To his workpeople he was considerate and warm: if he had to lay them off, he would move heaven and earth to find them new work elsewhere, and if he could not, he would help them move their whole families to a district where jobs could be found. It must also be said that for all his hatred of being ruled himself, he knew how to rule with steady, unyielding strength; if any of his hands showed signs of insubordination, he crushed that unrest in the bud, pulled it up like a noxious weed before it could ever spread across his mill. With his own affairs running so smoothly, he saw no reason not to speak with harsh, unvarnished severity of other mill owners who struggled with unrest, dismissing all their troubles as their own fault, and happily disavowing the other masters’ interests to openly advocate for the workers.

His family was the oldest, most established line in the whole district, and while he was not the richest man for miles around, he was one of the most influential. He had a fine education, and as a young man, before the French Revolution broke out, he had travelled all across the Continent. He spoke French and Italian like a native, and during a two-year stay in Italy he collected a trove of fine paintings and beautiful rare artefacts that lined the walls of his home. When he chose to, he carried himself with the polished, easy grace of an old-school gentleman, and when he wanted to please, his conversation was wildly entertaining and utterly original. He deliberately spoke in his thick, rolling Yorkshire burr instead of the more refined standard English, and declared that “A Yorkshire burr is as much better than a Cockney lisp as a bull’s bellow is better than a rat’s squeak.”

He knew everyone for ten miles around, and everyone knew him — but he had almost no close intimates. A man who was utterly original himself, he hated all dull, ordinary, commonplace people; a rough, sharp, vivid character, high or low, would always win his favour, while a refined, insipid person, no matter how grand their title, made him actively averse. He would happily spend an hour chatting freely with a sharp-witted weaver in his mill, or a queer, shrewd old widow living in one of his cottages, but would grudge a single minute of his time to a posh commonplace gentleman, or the most elegant, fashionable frivolous lady. He carried this preference to an extreme, forgetting that there could be amiable, even admirable people who had no gift for being eccentric and original. Still, he made exceptions: he was peculiarly at ease with plain, guileless souls who cared nothing for refinement, barely had a scrap of intellectual curiosity, and could not even begin to understand the sharp, brilliant parts of his own mind. Those people never winced at his rudeness, never got hurt by his sarcasm, never picked apart his words or his choices to judge him. He was the undisputed lord of that kind of company, and while they followed his lead without question, they never even noticed his superiority, so they were tractable without ever being servile. Their unthinking, easy, unselfconscious indifference was as comfortable to him as the solid oak chair he sat on, or the worn stone floor under his boots.

It was clear he was not unkind to Robert Moore, and there were a handful of small reasons he held a faint partiality for the man. It sounds silly, but the first of them was that Moore spoke English with a distinct foreign lilt, and French with a perfect, unaccented purity, his dark, gaunt face with its fine, worn lines looking nothing like a typical British or Yorkshire man’s. Those small, trivial details tugged at old, happy memories: they brought him back to his travelling days, his long-lost youth. He had seen faces just like Moore’s wandering the streets of Italian cities, heard voices just like his in Parisian cafes and theatres. When he looked at the foreign mill owner and listened to him speak, he felt twenty years old again.

Second, he had known Moore’s father, and had done business with him for years — a less happy tie, admittedly, since his own firm had been tied up with the elder Moore’s, and had lost a fair bit of money when that business collapsed. Third, he had seen that Robert Moore was a sharp, shrewd man of business, and he was sure the man would eventually turn his fortunes around, no matter what it took; he respected his grit, his determination, and even his unapologetic hardness. The fourth link between them was even more practical: Mr. Yorke was one of the guardians for the young minor who owned the estate that Hollow’s Mill was built on, so Moore had to consult him constantly for permission for all the alterations and improvements he planned to carry out.

As for the other guest sitting in Yorke’s parlour that night, Mr. Helstone? There was a double, unyielding antipathy between the host and the parson — the natural dislike of two completely opposite characters, stoked by years of old history. The free-thinking, liberty-loving Yorke hated the rigid formalist, the disciplinarian parson. On top of that, rumour said they had once been rivals for the same woman’s hand, long ago.

As a young man, Mr. Yorke had always been drawn to lively, dashing, high-spirited women: a bold, graceful figure, a sharp wit, a quick tongue, that was what had always attracted him before. He never once proposed to any of those brilliant belles he had chased around local balls, though — until the day he fell hard, head over heels, for a girl who was the exact opposite of every woman he had ever admired before. Her name was Mary Cave, and she had the face of a marble Madonna, stillness made flesh. It did not matter that she only answered him in monosyllables when he spoke to her, that his sighs seemed to go unheard, his glances went unreturned, that she never agreed with his opinions, barely smiled at his jokes, and gave him no respect or attention at all. For some unnameable reason that he never could have explained, Mary Cave was perfect to him, and he loved her more than he had ever loved anything.

Mr. Helstone, then the new young curate of Briarfield, loved her too — or at least, he thought he did. He had none of the humble, reverent adoration that subdued every other suitor of hers; he saw her far more clearly, far more plainly, than anyone else did, so he kept control of his own feelings, and of her. She accepted his first proposal, and they were married.

Nature never intended Mr. Helstone to be a good husband, least of all to a quiet, soft, gentle wife like Mary. He thought that if a woman never complained, she could never be unhappy. If she never spoke up to say she was bored, solitude could never irk her. If she never stated a preference for this thing or an aversion to that, she had no preferences or aversions at all, so there was no point in ever asking what she wanted. He made no pretence of understanding women, or seeing them as equal to men; they were a different, almost certainly far inferior order of being. A wife could never be her husband’s companion, let alone his confidante, let alone his support. A year or two into their marriage, she was barely relevant to him at all. So when she faded away and died one day — so gradually that he barely even noticed her decline, while everyone else in the parish saw her wasting away for months — he felt his loss barely at all, or so it seemed. He grieved dry-eyed, sober and unemotional, which scandalized his old housekeeper and Mary’s lady’s maid, who had sat with her through her illness and knew far more of the deep, tender heart hidden under her quiet stillness than her husband ever had. The two gossiped over her corpse, embellishing stories of her slow decline, working themselves up into a fury against the austere little parson sitting in the next room poring over his papers, entirely unaware of how everyone was judging him.

Before Mary was even buried, rumours were flying all across the parish that she had died of a broken heart. Those rumours swelled, growing into tales of cruel mistreatment, into lurid made-up details of harsh abuse at her husband’s hands — lies, all of them, but lies the neighbourhood ate up greedily. Mr. Yorke heard the stories, and he believed every word. He was already no friend to his successful rival, and even though he was married now to a woman who was the complete opposite of Mary in every possible way, he never forgot the great, crushing disappointment of his youth. When he learned that the woman who would have been his whole world had been neglected, even abused, by the man who stole her from him, he conceived a deep, bitter, unshakable hatred for Helstone that would never fade.

Helstone was barely aware of how deep that hatred ran. He never knew how fiercely Yorke had loved Mary Cave, how gutted he had been when she married another man, and he had no idea of the cruel lies about his treatment of her that everyone in the parish knew by heart. He thought his disagreement with Yorke was purely political, purely religious. If he had ever learned the real truth, no force on earth could have convinced him to step across his old rival’s threshold.

Mr. Yorke never went back to lecturing Robert Moore. The conversation soon picked up again, growing more general, but still sharp and disputatious. The unrest spreading across the country, the recent spate of frame-breaking raids on local mills, gave them endless material to bicker over, and all three men in the parlour held wildly different views on the crisis. Mr. Helstone thought the mill owners were the injured party, the workpeople ungrateful and unreasonable. He condemned the growing widespread disaffection to the government, the unwillingness of the poor to patiently bear hardships he saw as unavoidable. His solution? Stronger government interference, stricter vigilance from local magistrates, and if needed, swift, unflinching military coercion.
