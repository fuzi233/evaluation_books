The sanctuary, checkered with shafts of sunlight and crowned by a canopy of celestial purple, acted as a balm to Father Clement, soothing and enchanting him much as it had done for Margaret. More profoundly, it transported his consciousness directly to the Architect of all benevolent purity. His gaze swept across the great nave, crowded with his compatriots—a thousand snow-white caps, intricately filigreed with gold. Though he had traversed hundreds of leagues, he had witnessed nothing comparable to this congregation save for the vast, silent expanses of winter snow. That morning, he had thundered with judgment; yet this sweet afternoon seemed discordant with such threats. His heart ached with compassion for the multitude; indeed, these poor souls heard scarcely more of divine love from the pulpit in those days than the heathens themselves. He proclaimed the glad tidings of salvation, and the people hung upon his gentle, earnest tongue. He was not among those preachers who gyrate in the pulpit like weathercocks atop steeples; rather, he moved the hearts of others far more than his own physical frame allowed. Yet, he did not entirely neglect those dwelling in the shadows. Warm with this theme, determined that not a single soul in that throng should miss the joyful message of Christ's love, he turned his attention toward the south aisle.

There, bathed in a luminous stream from the window, stood the radiant visage of Margaret Brandt. He gazed upon it without initial emotion; it merely numbed him, soul and body alike. Soon, however, the words died in his throat, and he trembled as he fixed his glare upon her. There she stood, her auburn hair washed in sunbeams, glittering like the halo of a saint, her face glowing doubly bright—its own inherent beauty intensified by the golden light framing it. Leaning lightly against a white column, she listened with tender, downcast lashes. He had seen her listen to him so a hundred times before. There was no alteration in her essence; this was the blooming Margaret he had left behind, merely a shade riper, more lovely. He started at her presence, his eyes wide with monstrous astonishment, his cheeks drained of color. The crowd faded from his sight. He heard, as in a dream, a rustling and rising throughout the church, yet he could not tear his prodigy-struck eyes from that face—all life, all bloom, all beauty, and that wondrous auburn hair glistening gloriously in the sun. He gazed, convinced she must vanish into thin air. She remained.

In an instant, she looked at him, fully. Her own violet eyes! At this revelation, he was beside himself, his lips parting to shriek her name, when she turned her head swiftly. Soon after, she vanished, leaving behind only one final glance. Though rapid as lightning, this encounter met his gaze and left her trembling, her mind whirling, while he panted, gripping the pulpit convulsively. For this glance, though devoid of explicit recognition, was that startled, inquiring, nameless, indescribable look that precedes acknowledgment. He made a mighty effort, muttering something incomprehensible to anyone nearby, then feebly resumed his discourse, stammering and babbling for a while. Gradually, forcing himself to view her absence as a vision from another realm, he rose into a state of unnatural excitement, concluding his sermon in a style of eloquence that electrified the simple minds present, bordering on rhapsody.

When the sermon ended, he sat upon the pulpit stool, terribly shaken. Presently, an idea very characteristic of the era seized him. He had sought her grave at Sevenbergen in vain. Now, she had appeared to him, revealing that she was buried here—probably hard by that very pillar where her spirit had manifested. Once adopted, this notion settled upon his mind with the certainty of fact. He felt he needed only to speak to the sexton, whom he saw to his great disgust working during the service, to learn the precise spot of her interment.

The church was now utterly empty. Descending from the pulpit, he stepped through an aperture in the south wall onto the grass and approached the sexton. He recognized him instantly, though Jorian never suspected the poor lad, whose life he had once saved, hidden beneath the guise of this holy friar. The loss of his shapely beard had wonderfully altered the outline of the boy's face. This change affected him even more than his tonsure, his short hair sprinkled with premature grey, and his cheeks thinned and paled by rigorous fasts and vigils.

"My son," said Friar Clement softly, "if you retain any memory of those whom you lay in the earth, pray tell me: is any Christian buried inside the church, near one of the pillars?"

"Nay, father," replied Jorian, "here in the churchyard lie buried all that are buried. Why?"

"No matter. Pray tell me then where lieth Margaret Brandt."

"Margaret Brandt?" Jorian stared stupidly at the speaker. "She died about three years ago, and was buried here."

"Oh, that is another matter," said Jorian. "That was before my time; the vicar could tell you, likely, if so be she was a gentlewoman, or at least rich enough to pay him his fee."

"Alas, my son, she was poor—and paid a heavy penalty for it—but born of decent folk. Her father, Peter, was a learned physician; she came hither from Sevenbergen—to die."

When Clement had uttered these words, his head sank upon his breast, and he seemed to possess neither the power nor the wish to question Jorian further. One doubts even if he knew where he stood; he was lost in the past. Jorian put down his spade, standing upright in the grave with his arms akimbo, saying sulkily, "Are you making a fool of me, holy sir, or has some wag been making a fool of you!" Having thus relieved his mind, he proceeded to dig again with a vigor that showed his somewhat irritable temper was ruffled.

Clement gazed at him with a puzzled but gently reproachful eye, for the tone was rude and the words unintelligible. Good-natured, though crusty, Jorian had not thrown up three spadefuls ere he became ashamed of himself. "Why, what a base churl am I to speak thus to thee, holy father; and thou standing there, looking at me like a lamb. Aha! I have it; 'tis Peter Brandt's grave you would fain see, not Margaret's. He does lie here; hard by the west door. There; I'll show you."

And he laid down his spade, putting on his doublet and jerkin to accompany the friar. He did not know there was anybody sitting on Peter's tomb. Still less did he realize she was watching for this holy friar. Pietro Vanucci and Andrea did not recognize him without his beard. Indeed, the fact remains that a beard which has never known a razor grows in a very picturesque and characteristic form, becoming a defining feature of the face; so that its removal may, in some cases, prove an effectual disguise.
