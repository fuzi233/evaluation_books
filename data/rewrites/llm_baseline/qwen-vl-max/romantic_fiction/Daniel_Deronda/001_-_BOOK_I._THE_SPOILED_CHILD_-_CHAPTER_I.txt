Men cannot begin without pretending a beginning. Even science, the most precise of all disciplines, must invent a starting point—fixing its clock at zero as if time itself had a clean edge. Poetry, less rigid, is said to begin in the middle, but upon closer thought, this is no different from science’s own method: both look backward and forward, dividing their units into infinitesimal parts, setting off not from nothing, but from a chosen moment—a moment already shaped by what came before. No memory can reach the true origin; whether we start in heaven or on earth, our story begins with a fragment, a slice of something vast and unspoken.

Was she beautiful? Or was it not beauty that held Daniel Deronda’s gaze, but something else—the quiet power in her eyes, the way they seemed to move beyond mere sight? Was it the good spirit or the dark one that shone through them? Likely the latter—else why did her presence stir unease rather than peace? Why did the desire to look again feel like obligation, not longing? Why did the soul resist being drawn to her, even as the body could not turn away?

The woman who stirred these thoughts in Daniel Deronda was not gambling in the open air beneath a sun-warmed sky, nor tossing coins among beggars on crumbling walls. She was in one of those grand resorts built by centuries of wealth and indulgence—places where pleasure is wrapped in gold leaf, deep shadows, and statues of half-naked gods, all designed to concentrate the breath of the fashionable few. The air here was thick, brewed into a soft haze by late September heat. Silence reigned, broken only by faint rattles, the delicate clink of chips, a low sweeping sound, and occasional murmurs in French—words that might have come from a machine made to mimic human speech.

Two long tables stood at the center of the room, crowded with players. All faces were turned toward the games—except one. A small boy, bare-kneed and dressed in an absurd costume, stood motionless near the doorway, staring blankly ahead like a puppet placed there for display. Behind him, a woman leaned over a roulette table, deeply absorbed. Around her, fifty or sixty people gathered—some playing, others watching, some merely passing through. Among them, a woman would occasionally place a five-franc note with a smile, just to taste the thrill of gambling, though she never meant to stay long.

The players themselves were a curious mix: Livonian nobility, Spanish merchants, Italian aristocrats, German scholars, English lords and commoners—all united by a single passion. An English countess, her fingers gleaming with jewels, sat beside a gaunt, yellow-skinned hand reaching for coins—its owner a woman whose face looked like a vulture’s, grizzled and sharp-eyed. Nearby, a London tradesman, neat and well-groomed, sat quietly, not driven by greed but by habit—his life a rhythm of earning, spending, and now, playing, all with the same detached calm. He believed Providence had never condemned his pastimes, and so he continued, willing to stop when the joy of winning faded into the pain of losing.

Near him stood a handsome Italian, poised and still, placing a pile of napoleons across the table. His hand moved with precision, as if he followed a system too perfect to fail. Beside him, another man—thin, pale, wearing a monocle—trembled slightly as he asked for change. His play was not governed by logic, but by superstition: perhaps the eighth of the month was lucky, or maybe he dreamed of white crows. Yet each player, though different in appearance and background, shared a strange uniformity—a dullness in expression, as if they had all eaten from the same root that dulled the mind and narrowed the soul.

Deronda watched them and felt a pang of pity. Compared to these people, lost in artificial pleasures, he remembered how much more alive the Spanish shepherd-boys seemed—free, simple, untouched by the poison of excess. Rousseau might have been right after all: civilization had not improved mankind, but trapped it in gilded cages.

Then—suddenly—the scene shifted. His attention was drawn to a young woman standing nearby, speaking softly in English to an older lady seated beside her. In the next instant, she returned to her game, revealing a figure of striking grace. Her face was not one that demanded admiration—but one that refused to be ignored.

A quiet storm began within Deronda. His gaze lingered, not out of desire, but curiosity. He watched her hands—delicate, gloved in pale gray—as she placed her stake with firm confidence. Then he returned to her face, steady and focused, untouched by the crowd’s noise. She won. As she adjusted the coins pushed toward her, she glanced around—her eyes cold, almost aloof, as if masking a secret triumph. But then, her gaze met his.

She froze.

Not because she was seen, but because she *felt* seen—measured, judged, dismissed. She sensed that he saw her not as a person, but as a specimen—something inferior, something to be observed from above. The idea sparked a sudden, fierce resentment. It didn’t flush her cheeks—it drained the color from her lips. Yet she controlled herself, turning back to the game with no outward sign of emotion except that paleness.

But the moment had changed. Her luck turned. Her stake was swept away. Again. And again. Each loss doubled. She played not to win, but to defy. To prove she was not afraid. To show that she could endure.

Many watched her now. But only one mattered—Deronda. She never looked at him, yet she knew he hadn’t moved.

“Faites votre jeu,” the croupier called.

Her arm stretched out—once more. The last of her coins went down.

“Le jeu ne va plus.”

Five seconds later, she turned—and faced him.

A flicker of irony danced in his eyes. But it was better than indifference. At least he had noticed her. And despite his coolness, she couldn’t help wondering—did he admire her courage? Her spirit? She was young, beautiful, bold—not like the others. Not like the dull men who passed by, scowling as if gambling were a sin. He was different. And that difference made her want to know him.

That evening, the room was hotter, brighter. Gaslight sparkled off silks and feathers. Gwendolen Harleth, now dressed in sea-green robes and silver ornaments, with a feather trailing behind her like a serpent’s tail, walked beside her chaperone and a stiff, German-looking gentleman. They moved through the crowd, and Gwendolen was noticed.

“She’s striking,” someone murmured. “Unlike the others.”
“Yes, she’s become a serpent tonight—green and silver, winding her neck like one.”
“Oh, she always does something extraordinary.”
“Do you find her pretty, Mr. Vandernoodt?”
“Very. A man might risk hanging for her—I mean, a fool might.”
“You like a retroussé nose and narrow eyes?”
“When they go with such an *ensemble*.”
“The *ensemble du serpent*?”
“If you insist. Woman was tempted by a serpent—why not man?”

Gwendolen listened, but her thoughts were elsewhere. She was searching—not for compliments, but for him.

At last, she found her chance.

“Mr. Vandernoodt,” she said, her voice light, almost bored, “you know everyone. Who is that man near the door?”

“There are six near the door. Do you mean the old man with the George IV wig?”

“No. The dark-haired young man on the right. With that… dreadful expression.”

“Dreadful?” he laughed. “I think he’s quite remarkable.”

“But who is he?”

“He’s recently arrived with Sir Hugo Mallinger.”

“Sir Hugo Mallinger?”

“Yes. Have you heard of him?”

“No.” (She blushed faintly.)

“He has a house nearby, but never visits it. What was the name of that gentleman?”

“Deronda. Mr. Deronda.”

“What a lovely name! Is he English?”

“Yes. He’s said to be closely related to the baronet. You’re interested in him?”

“Yes. He’s not like other young men.”

“And you don’t admire young men in general?”

“Not at all. I always know what they’ll say. But I can’t guess what this Mr. Deronda would say. What *does* he say?”

“Nothing, mostly. I sat with his party last night. He didn’t speak. Didn’t smoke. Looked bored.”

“Another reason why I’d like to meet him. I’m always bored.”

“I should think he’d welcome an introduction. Shall I arrange it? Will you allow it, baroness?”

“Why not? Since he’s connected to Sir Hugo Mallinger. Though, Gwendolen, this is new for you—to be bored.”

“Exactly. That’s why I need excitement. If I stop gambling, I must break something—my arm, my collarbone. I must make something happen. Unless you’ll take me up the Matterhorn.”

“Perhaps meeting Mr. Deronda will do instead.”

“Perhaps.”

But the meeting never happened. Mr. Vandernoodt failed to bring them together. And when Gwendolen returned to her room, she found a letter—calling her home.

The drama ended not with a kiss, nor a confession, but with silence and distance. And yet, somewhere between the spinning wheel and the flicker of a glance, something had begun.
