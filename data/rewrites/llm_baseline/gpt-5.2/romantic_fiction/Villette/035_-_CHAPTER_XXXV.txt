“Oubliez les Professeurs.” Madame Beck said it as one tells a child to put aside a toy and be quiet; she meant calm, she meant obedience, she meant me to go on with my portioned days as though nothing had been stirred. She was a wise woman; yet she should not have spoken those words. They were a match struck where tinder lay.

Forget him? They took a singular way to teach me forgetting: they praised him into a figure I could not dismiss. They showed me goodness till it shone; they scrubbed his name clean, as a careful cook scours a kettle till it gives back light; and then they spoke—Père Silas with his grave patience, Madame Beck with her composed emphasis—of the manner of his loving. Before, I had only guessed at what he could be; now they set before me, with a priest’s slow exactness, a history made of fidelity and renunciation, watched beside a tomb through twenty years, as steadily as one watches a hearth through a long winter.

It was not the hollow incense people burn for pleasure. It had the weight of deeds. He had tied up his own wishes, portioned out his earnings, and let what might have been vengeance go, that he might carry a cross for those who were once dear to her who was dead. The story did what stories do when they touch the quick: it altered the very air I breathed. The schoolroom smelled the same—chalk, ink, the faint starch of washed linen—but my mind would not keep its former flavour; it had taken a new seasoning, sharp and persistent.

As for Justine Marie, I knew her without seeing her. There were girls like her in Madame Beck’s classes: pale as watered milk, slow, kind by habit rather than ardour—safe, neutral, good enough, and never splendid. If angels’ wings had been fastened on such shoulders, I knew what poet-fancy had glued them there; if a halo had been painted above that forehead, I knew in whose ardent eyes the circle had been lit.

Was I to tremble before a dead nun? Was the image of pale, vowed, buried womanhood to rise and stand for ever between us? And what of those charities that drank up his money? What of that heart, sworn to a kind of virginity?

Madame Beck—Père Silas—you should not have laid those questions in my way. They became, at once, the knotted riddle, the obstacle like a barred door, and also the spur. For a week, night and day, I fell asleep upon them and woke to them. There was no answer in the whole world except where one small, dark man moved about—sat, stood, lectured—under the odd head-piece of a bonnet-grec, and within the poor girth of an ink-stained paletôt, with that look of quick temper and quicker feeling.

After the Rue des Mages, I wanted to see him again. It seemed that, now I had been forced to imagine what lay behind his choler, his face must read differently; I longed to trace there the mark of that old devotedness, the half-knightly, half-saintly courage Père Silas had sketched. They had made him my Christian hero; and I wanted—simply, strongly—to look at him under that name.

Opportunity did not linger. The next day my new impressions were tried, and in a fashion neither heroic nor tender, but lively enough, and humiliating.

About three o’clock, the first class had settled to its usual stillness. Madame Beck herself was teaching—orderly, lucid, competent; her voice, even, trained, went on like the measured beat of a kitchen clock that keeps all labour in rule. Released from responsibility by her presence, I sat bent over my desk drawing—copying, line by line, the elaborate patience of an engraving. I had then a practical notion of art: to imitate until one had produced a Chinese finical facsimile of steel or mezzotint. It was tedious, and I was pleased; the labour soothed me as kneading soothes—press, fold, press again—till the mind grows quiet in the rhythm.

Then, without warning, peace was cleft by the wild inburst of a paletôt.

My pencils, my copy, my careful little progress—crushed together in a fist—vanished from before me. I myself was shaken out of my chair as if some excited cook, impatient for spice, had shaken a withered nutmeg from a box: one sharp jerk and out it drops. Chair and desk were seized—one under each sleeve—and carried away; in a breath I, with my poor furniture, was transported into the grand salle, that vast room used for dancing and chorus lessons. There we were set down with an emphasis that seemed to forbid the smallest hope of retreat.

When my wits came back in broken pieces, I saw, before me, two men—gentlemen, no doubt. One dark, one fair; one stiff, half-military, in a braided surtout; the other with the careless air of student or artist. Both wore moustache, whiskers, imperial—an abundant forest of hair about faces that looked cold through it. Near them stood M. Emanuel, apart, as if refusing fellowship; his countenance was all choler, and he held out his hand with that tribune gesture of his, as if he stood in a court.

“Mademoiselle,” said he, “your business is to prove to these gentlemen that I am no liar. You will answer, to the best of your ability, such questions as they shall put. You will also write on such theme as they shall select. In their eyes, it appears, I hold the position of an unprincipled impostor. I write essays; and, with deliberate forgery, sign to them my pupils’ names, and boast of them as their work. You will disprove this charge.”

Grand ciel! It was the show-trial I had so long evaded, dropped on my head like thunder. These two braided, sneering personages were none other than the college professors—Messieurs Boissec and Rochemorte: pedants, sceptics, fops, with that commercial air of men who think knowledge a stamped coin and would test it with their teeth. It seemed M. Paul had been rash enough to exhibit something I had written—something he had never praised, never even mentioned to me, and which I had supposed forgotten. The essay was not remarkable in itself; it only looked so beside the average work of foreign school-girls. In an English school it might have passed like plain bread on a crowded table: eaten, perhaps approved, and never spoken of again. These two had questioned its genuineness, insinuated a cheat; and I was dragged forward to witness to truth, while they took their pleasure in the torture of examination.

They began with classics. A dead blank. They went to French history. I hardly knew Mérovée from Pharamond. They tried me in various ‘ologies; I could only shake my head and say, always, “Je n’en sais rien.”

After a pause, expressive enough, they ventured on matters of general information—subjects I did know, on which I had reflected. I felt ideas surge up, quick as water in a rising well; but when I tried to draw them out, there were no words. My tongue, which could have served me in solitude, failed me in public. Whether I could not speak or would not, I scarcely knew. My nerves were wrong, and my humour crossed; I stood like a cook with hands full of ingredients and no fire to set them to—everything ready, nothing possible.

I heard the braided-surtout professor whisper to the other, “Est-elle donc idiote?”

“Yes,” I thought, “for such as you, an idiot she is, and will always be.”

But I suffered; I suffered sharply. I saw the damp gather on M. Paul’s brow. In his eye there was reproach—passionate, and sad. He would not believe that I was wholly without that prompt, popular cleverness which makes answers leap; he thought I could, if I would. I could not make him understand that my thoughts, like herbs gathered fresh, needed time to be washed, sorted, and cut; they would not at a word be thrown into boiling speech.

At last, to release us all, I stammered, “Gentlemen, you had better let me go; you will get no good of me; as you say, I am an idiot.”

I wish I could have spoken with calm dignity, or that I had held my tongue. My voice betrayed me—thin, shaken. I saw the judges cast on M. Emanuel a look of triumph, and I heard the tremor of my own words; then, choking, I burst into tears. They were not the tears of grief, but of anger. Had I been a man and strong, I would have challenged that pair where they stood. I would rather have been scourged than thus made to show emotion.

The incapables! Could they not see, in that composition they called a forgery, the crude hand of a novice? The theme had been classical—new to me. When M. Paul dictated it, I heard it for the first time. I had no seasoned store laid by; no knowledge planted in spring, grown in summer, harvested in autumn, and stored through winter. Whatever I needed, I must go out and gather—wild herbs from hedge and bank—bring them home fresh and green, shred them hurriedly into the pot, and hope that the flavour would serve. I read books, searched facts, built a skeleton out of dry bones, and then clothed it, and tried to breathe into it life. The research was anxious; my inward repugnance to flaw or falsity made me cautious; but the material was not mellow in me. These men mistook the patient cookery of a hungry mind for the ease of a rich scholar.

They would not let me go. I must write before them. I sat down; dipped my pen into ink with a shaking hand; looked at the white paper through eyes half blinded with tears. One of the judges began to apologize in that mincing voice that pretends pity while it enjoys power.

“Nous agissons dans l’intérêt de la vérité. Nous ne voulons pas vous blesser.”

Scorn steadied me as salt steadies a trembling hand.

“Dictate, Monsieur,” I answered.

Rochemorte named the theme: “Human Justice.”

Human Justice! A cold abstraction, blank as an empty dish, giving off no savour, suggesting nothing. There stood M. Emanuel—sad as Saul, stern as Joab—while his accusers triumphed in their moustaches and their braid.

I looked at the two faces, and in looking, memory—sharp as the scent of smoke that suddenly recalls a forgotten hearth—darted its light. Those visages, peering out of their forest of hair, were the same I had seen, projected in full gaslight behind the pillars of a portico on the night of my desolate arrival in Villette. These—these were the heroes who had chased a friendless foreigner beyond her strength, hunted her breathless through a quarter of the town.

“Pious mentors,” I thought. “Pure guides for youth. If ‘Human Justice’ were what she ought to be, you would scarce sit where you sit.”

The idea once seized, I fell to work. “Human Justice” no longer stood as a pale phrase: she rushed before me in a new guise—a red, random beldame, arms akimbo, squatting in her own house, a den of confusion. Servants called to her for orders she would not give; beggars waited at her door, starving, unnoticed; children—sick, quarrelsome—crawled about her feet and yelled in her ears for cure, sympathy, redress. She cared for none of them. She had her warm seat by the fire, her short black pipe, her bottle of Mrs. Sweeny’s soothing syrup. She smoked, she sipped, she enjoyed her little paradise; and when the cries of the suffering pierced her ears too keenly, she seized poker or hearth-brush and settled the weak, the wronged, the sickly. But if the offender was strong, lively, violent—she only menaced, then plunged her hand into her deep pouch and flung a liberal shower of sugar-plums.

So I sketched “Human Justice,” hurried and keen, and set it before Messieurs Boissec and Rochemorte. M. Emanuel read it over my shoulder. I waited no comment. I curtsied to the trio and withdrew.

After school, M. Paul and I met again. The meeting did not run smooth; there was a crow to pluck. That forced examination lay in my throat like a bone. A crabbed dialogue ended with his calling me “une petite moqueuse et sans-cœur,” and with his stormy departure.

Yet I did not want him to go quite away. I only wished him to feel that such a transport as he had indulged could not be taken without cost. So I was not sorry, soon after, to see him in the garden, working in the berceau. The place had its own domestic hush: the soft scrape of spade in soil, the whisper of leaves, the faint drip from yesterday’s rain still lingering in the trellis. In that gentler sound-world, the mind breathes more healthfully; it is not stunned by the clang and trumpet of male voices in a hall, but soothed by earth and growth.

He approached the glass door; I drew near. We spoke first of the flowers growing round it—common things, but real. By-and-by he laid down his spade; by-and-by he began again, and passed to other subjects, and at last touched what he knew must be spoken.

Conscious that his proceeding had been extravagant, he half apologized; he half regretted the fitfulness of his moods; yet he hinted that allowance should be made.

“But,” said he, “I can hardly expect it at your hands, Miss Lucy; you know neither me, nor my position, nor my history.”

His history. The word fell like a seed into ground ready.

“No, Monsieur,” I said at once. “Of course, as you say, I know neither your history, nor your position, nor your sacrifices, nor any of your sorrows, or trials, or affections, or fidelities. Oh, no! I know nothing about you; you are for me altogether a stranger.”

“Hein?” he murmured, lifting his brows.

“You know, Monsieur, I only see you in classe—stern, dogmatic, hasty, imperious. I only hear of you in town as active and wilful, quick to originate, hasty to lead, but slow to persuade, and hard to bend. A man like you, without ties, can have no attachments; without dependants, no duties. All we, with whom you come in contact, are machines, which you thrust here and there, inconsiderate of their feelings. You seek your recreations in public, by the light of the evening chandelier: this school and yonder college are your workshops, where you fabricate the ware called pupils. I don’t so much as know where you live; it is natural to take it for granted that you have no home, and need none.”

“I am judged,” said he. “Your opinion of me is just what I thought it was. For you I am neither a man nor a Christian. You see me void of affection and religion, unattached by friend or family, unpiloted by principle or faith. It is well, Mademoiselle; such is our reward in this life.”

“You are a philosopher, Monsieur; a cynic philosopher.” My eye went, in spite of me, to his paletôt, and he, seeing, brushed its dim sleeve with a quick hand. “Despising the foibles of humanity—above its luxuries—independent of its comforts.”

“Et vous, Mademoiselle? vous êtes proprette et douillette, et affreusement insensible, par-dessus le marché.”

“But, in short, Monsieur, now I think of it, you must live somewhere? Do tell me where; and what establishment of servants do you keep?”

His under-lip projected with a scornful force, and he broke out—

“Je vis dans un trou! I inhabit a den, Miss—a cavern, where you would not put your dainty nose. Once, with base shame of speaking the whole truth, I talked about my ‘study’ in that college: know now that this ‘study’ is my whole abode; my chamber is there and my drawing-room. As for my ‘establishment of servants’” (he mimicked my voice cruelly) “they number ten; les voilà.”

And he spread his ten fingers close under my eyes.

“I black my boots,” he pursued savagely. “I brush my paletôt.”

“No, Monsieur, it is too plain; you never do that,” I could not help saying.

“Je fais mon lit et mon ménage; I seek my dinner in a restaurant; my supper takes care, of itself; I pass days laborious and loveless; nights long and lonely; I am ferocious, and bearded and monkish; and nothing now living in this world loves me, except some old hearts worn like my own, and some few beings, impoverished, suffering, poor in purse and in spirit, whom the kingdoms of this world own not, but to whom a will and testament not to be disputed has bequeathed the kingdom of heaven.”

“Ah, Monsieur; but I know!”

“What do you know? many things, I verily believe; yet not me, Lucy!”

“I know that you have a pleasant old house in a pleasant old square of the Basse-Ville—why don’t you go and live there?”

“Hein?” Again that mutter, half astonished, half suspicious.

“I liked it much, Monsieur; with the steps ascending to the door, the grey flags in front, the nodding trees behind—real trees, not shrubs—trees dark, high, and of old growth. And the boudoir-oratoire—you should make that room your study; it is so quiet and solemn.”

He eyed me closely; a half-smile and a half-colour crossed him, as if warmth tried to break through the discipline of his face.

“Where did you pick up all that? Who told you?” he asked.

“Nobody told me. Did I dream it, Monsieur, do you think?”

“Can I enter into your visions? Can I guess a woman’s waking thoughts, much less her sleeping fantasies?”

“If I dreamt it, I saw human beings as well as a house. I saw a priest—old, bent, grey—and a domestic, old too and picturesque; and a lady, splendid but strange; her head would scarce reach to my elbow—her magnificence might ransom a duke. She wore a gown bright as lapis-lazuli—a shawl worth a thousand francs; she was decked with ornaments so brilliant I never saw any with such a beautiful sparkle; but her figure looked as if it had been broken in two and bent double. She seemed to have outlived the common years of humanity, and to have attained those which are only labour and sorrow. She was become morose—almost malevolent; yet somebody, it appears, cared for her in her infirmities—somebody forgave her trespasses, hoping to have his trespasses forgiven. They lived together, these three people—the mistress, the chaplain, the servant—all old, all feeble, all sheltered under one kind wing.”

He covered the upper part of his face with his hand, but did not hide his mouth. About it I saw hovering an expression I liked—softened, reluctant, almost grateful.

“I see you have entered into my secrets,” said he, “but how was it done?”

So I told him: the errand to the Rue des Mages, the storm that had detained me, the abruptness of the lady, the kindness of the priest.

“As I sat waiting for the rain to cease,” I said, “Père Silas whiled away the time with a story.”

“A story! What story? Père Silas is no romancist.”

“Shall I tell Monsieur the tale?”

“Yes: begin at the beginning. Let me hear some of Miss Lucy’s French—her best or her worst—I don’t much care which: let us have a good poignée of barbarisms, and a bounteous dose of the insular accent.”

“Monsieur is not going to be gratified by a tale of ambitious proportions, and the spectacle of the narrator sticking fast in the midst. But I will tell him the title—the ‘Priest’s Pupil.’”

“Bah!” he said, and the swarthy flush dyed his cheek again. “The good old father could not have chosen a worse subject; it is his weak point. But what of the ‘Priest’s Pupil?’”

“Oh! many things.”

“You may as well define what things. I mean to know.”

“There was the pupil’s youth, the pupil’s manhood—his avarice, his ingratitude, his implacability, his inconstancy. Such a bad pupil, Monsieur! so thankless, cold-hearted, unchivalrous, unforgiving!”

“Et puis?” he asked, taking a cigar, as if he needed smoke between himself and what he felt.

“Et puis,” I went on, “he underwent calamities which one did not pity—bore them in a spirit one did not admire—endured wrongs for which one felt no sympathy; finally took the unchristian revenge of heaping coals of fire on his adversary’s head.”

“You have not told me all,” he said.

“Nearly all, I think: I have indicated the heads of Père Silas’s chapters.”

“You have forgotten one—that which touched on the pupil’s lack of affection—on his hard, cold, monkish heart.”

“True; I remember now. Père Silas did say that his vocation was almost that of a priest—that his life was considered consecrated.”

“By what bonds or duties?”

“By the ties of the past and the charities of the present.”

“You have, then, the whole situation?”

“I have now told Monsieur all that was told me.”

Some minutes passed, meditative, while the garden made its small sounds—leaf against leaf, the far muffled murmur of the city beyond the walls, and nearer, our own breathing, which, when one listens, has its separate language, untranslatable into any word.

Then he said, very quietly, yet with an emphasis that compelled truth:

“Now, Mademoiselle Lucy, look at me, and with that truth which I believe you never knowingly violate, answer me one question. Raise your eyes; rest them on mine; have no hesitation; fear not to trust me—I am a man to be trusted.”

I raised my eyes.

“Knowing me thoroughly now—all my antecedents, all my responsibilities—having long known my faults, can you and I still be friends?”

“If Monsieur wants a friend in me,” I answered, “I shall be glad to have a friend in him.”

“But a close friend I mean—intimate and real—kindred in all but blood. Will Miss Lucy be the sister of a very poor, fettered, burdened, encumbered man?”

Words failed me; yet I suppose I answered. He took my hand, and my hand found comfort in the shelter of his. His friendship did not feel like a brittle thing—some delicate sugar-work that breaks under a touch; it came upon me, at once, with the imagined support of rock.

“When I talk of friendship,” he repeated, “I mean true friendship.”

I could hardly believe such earnest words had been spoken to me, that such an anxious kindness looked from his eyes. If he truly wished my confidence and would truly give me his, it seemed life could offer nothing beyond. In a moment I felt made rich—made safe—substantially happy, as one feels when, after long fare of thin bread, a warm dish is set down, honest and sustaining. To fix the reality, I asked:

“Is Monsieur quite serious? Does he really think he needs me, and can take an interest in me as a sister?”

“Surely, surely,” said he. “A lonely man like me, who has no sister, must be but too glad to find in some woman’s heart a sister’s pure affection.”

“And dare I rely on Monsieur’s regard? Dare I speak to him when I am so inclined?”

“My little sister must make her own experiments,” he said. “I will give no promises. She must tease and try her wayward brother till she has drilled him into what she wishes. After all, he is no inductile material in some hands.”

While he spoke, the tone of his voice, the altered light of his eye, gave me a pleasure I had not known before. I envied no girl her lover, no bride her bridegroom, no wife her husband. I was content with this—my voluntary, self-offering friend. If he would prove reliable—and he looked reliable—what beyond his friendship could I ever covet? Yet, even in content, a fear stirred, as it will when one has once been waked from happiness like from a dream: if all should melt again—if this, too, should prove but a vision, gone with the changing of the air?
