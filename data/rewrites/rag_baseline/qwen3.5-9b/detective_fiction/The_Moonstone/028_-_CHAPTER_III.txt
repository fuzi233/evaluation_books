To honor the fragile vessel of Lady Verinder's spirit, I held my tongue, suppressing the crushing weight of the truth I had already uncovered. I waited in the hallowed silence of her presence, mentally rehearsing the sustaining words I intended to offer at the first opportune moment, bracing myself for any duty, however excruciating, that fate might demand.

"I have been grievously ill, Drusilla, for some time past," my aunt began, her voice trembling with a quiet devastation. "And, strangely, without realizing it myself."

My mind immediately conjured the countless souls perishing in spiritual darkness across the globe, unaware of their own damnation. A profound dread seized me; could my poor aunt be among the lost? "Yes, dear," I replied, my throat tight with sorrow. "Yes."

"I brought Rachel to London," she continued, her eyes fixed on a distant point, "seeking medical counsel. I deemed it prudent to consult two physicians."

Two doctors! And, oh, the irony—that not a single clergyman stood beside them! "Yes, dear?" I echoed softly. "Yes?"

"One of the two gentlemen was a stranger to me," she explained, her composure fracturing slightly. "The other had been a close friend of my late husband, a man whose regard for me stemmed solely from his love for him. After attending to Rachel, he requested a private audience with me in another room. Naturally, I anticipated specific directives regarding my daughter's convalescence. Instead, he grasped my hand with grave intensity and declared, 'Lady Verinder, I view you with both professional and personal concern. I fear you are in far more urgent need of healing than your daughter.' He questioned me, initially treating my responses with casual detachment, until I perceived the distress etched upon his countenance. He subsequently scheduled a follow-up visit for the following day, accompanied by a colleague, at a time when Rachel would be absent. The outcome of that consultation—a verdict delivered with tender gentleness—confirmed what we both dreaded: precious time had been squandered, irretrievably lost. Our condition had transcended the reach of their art. For over two years, I have suffered under an insidious malady of the heart, a silent assassin that has gradually dismantled my vitality without alarming symptoms. I may linger for mere months, or I may depart before the sun sets tomorrow; the physicians dare not speak with greater certainty. It would be futile to claim, my dear, that I have not endured agonizing moments since the reality of my plight was revealed. Yet, I am more resigned than before, diligently arranging my worldly affairs. My sole, consuming terror is that Rachel remains ignorant of the truth. Were she to learn it, she would immediately attribute my decline to anxiety over the Diamond, condemning herself with bitter remorse for a sin entirely foreign to her soul. Both doctors concur that the deterioration began two, perhaps three years ago. I trust you will guard my secret, Drusilla—for I perceive genuine sorrow and compassion in your gaze."

Sorrow and sympathy! Oh, what pagan sentiments to expect from a Christian Englishwoman so steadfastly anchored in her faith!

Little did my poor aunt foresee the torrent of devout gratitude surging through my veins as she concluded her tragic narrative. Here lay a mission of salvation! Here stood a beloved kin, a dying sister, on the precipice of eternity, wholly unprepared, divinely led to entrust her soul to *Me*! How shall I articulate the ecstasy that flooded my heart as I recalled the clerical allies upon whom I could depend, not numbered by dozens, but by hundreds? I drew my aunt into an embrace, my overflowing tenderness refusing satisfaction with anything less than physical contact. "Oh!" I cried, my voice thick with fervor, "the ineffable interest you command from me! Oh! the good I intend to accomplish for you, dear, before we part!" Following a brief, earnest preface, I presented her with a triad of holy friends, all engaged in ceaseless ministry within her neighborhood, each inexhaustible in exhortation and eager to wield their gifts at my command. Alas, the response was discouraging. Lady Verinder appeared bewildered and terrified, deflecting every suggestion with the pragmatic objection that she lacked the strength to endure strangers. I yielded—for the moment, at least. My extensive experience, garnered through fourteen years of pastoral visits under the guidance of cherished clerics, taught me that this required preparation through scripture. I possessed a small library tailored to this crisis, volumes designed to awaken, persuade, prepare, illuminate, and fortify. "You will read, dear, won't you?" I urged, employing my most persuasive demeanor. "You will read, if I bring you my own treasured books? Turned down precisely where you must pause to ask yourself, 'Does this apply to me?' Even this simple appeal—an act so thoroughly secularizing in its influence—startled my aunt. She replied, 'I will do what I can, Drusilla, to please you,' with a look of astonishment that was simultaneously instructive and chilling. No moment could be wasted. The clock on the mantelpiece indicated I had just sufficient time to hasten home, procure a curated selection of twelve essential texts, and return promptly to meet the lawyer and witness Lady Verinder's testament. Vowing faithfully to return by five o'clock, I departed on my errand of mercy.

When no interests but my own are at stake, I am contentedly humble in accepting the omnibus. Permit me to demonstrate my unwavering devotion to my aunt's welfare by noting that, on this occasion, I committed the extravagance of hiring a cab.

I drove home, selected and annotated my initial series of readings, and returned to Montagu Square, burdened with a carpet-bag containing a dozen volumes, artifacts unique to the European literary landscape. I paid the cabman his exact fare. He accepted it with a curse; instantly, I handed him a religious tract. Had I brandished a pistol at his head, this forsaken wretch could scarcely have displayed greater consternation. He leaped onto his box, shouting profane exclamations of dismay, and drove off furiously. Fortunately, quite useless! I sowed the good seed regardless, tossing a second tract through the cab window.

The servant who answered the door—not the chambermaid with ribbons, to my immense relief, but the footman—informed me that the physician had arrived and remained sequestered with Lady Verinder. Mr. Bruff, the solicitor, had arrived a moment prior and awaited me in the library. I was ushered inside to join him.

Mr. Bruff regarded me with surprise. As the family attorney, we had crossed paths several times beneath Lady Verinder's roof. He was a man, alas, aged and grayed by the service of the world. In his professional hours, he served as the chosen oracle of Law and Mammon; in his leisure, he was equally adept at devouring novels and shredding tracts.

"Have you come to reside here, Miss Clack?" he asked, his eyes lingering on my carpet-bag.

To disclose the contents of my sacred bag to a man of such repute would have invited an eruption of blasphemy. I stooped to his level, mentioning my business in the house.

"My aunt has informed me she intends to sign her Will," I answered. "She has graciously requested me to serve as one of the witnesses."

"Aye? aye? Well, Miss Clack, you shall suffice. You are over twenty-one, and you hold no pecuniary interest in Lady Verinder's estate."

No pecuniary interest in Lady Verinder's Will. Oh, the relief that washed over me! Should my aunt, possessing thousands, have remembered poor me—to whom five pounds is a mountain—if my name had appeared in the Will with a modest legacy attached, my enemies might have questioned the motives behind loading me with the finest treasures of my library and draining my dwindling resources for the prodigal expense of a cab. Not the cruelest scoffers could doubt my integrity now. Far better thus! Oh, surely, far better!

These consoling reflections were shattered by Mr. Bruff's voice. My meditative silence seemed to oppress his worldly spirit, forcing him to converse against his will.

"Well, Miss Clack, what is the latest news in the charitable circles? How fares your friend Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite, following the beating he received from the ruffians in Northumberland Street? By Jove! They are spinning a rather convincing tale about that charitable gentleman at my club!"

I had overlooked his earlier remark regarding my age and lack of financial stake. But the tone in which he alluded to dear Mr. Godfrey exceeded my patience. Bound, after the events of that afternoon, to assert the innocence of my admirable friend whenever challenged—and feeling compelled to include a stinging rebuke for Mr. Bruff in this righteous endeavor—I spoke up.

"I live largely outside the world," I stated; "and I lack the privilege of belonging to a club. However, I am acquainted with the story you reference, and I know that a more vile falsehood has never been spun."

"Yes, yes, Miss Clack—you believe in your friend. Natural enough. Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite will not find the world at large as easily convinced as a committee of benevolent ladies. Appearance is dead against him. He was in the house when the Diamond vanished. And he was the first to flee to London afterward. These are grim circumstances, ma'am, viewed through the lens of subsequent events."
