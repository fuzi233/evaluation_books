A woman’s sorrows run deeper than a man’s pen can often trace. We sketch in bare lines what her heart must color in full—griefs we scarcely fathom, trials we dimly perceive. So it was with Margaret Brandt. Until that fateful day when she entered Eli’s house as a foe and left a friend, her suffering had been but faintly outlined. Now, as her greatest trial neared, Gerard was far from her side.

She did not often seek Eli’s house, though his favor had warmed toward her. For every refusal, she had a ready excuse—but never spoke the truth: that two men within those walls filled her with a dread she could not name. Their faces bore the stamp of wickedness, their silent exchanges spoke of secrets best left unread. Instead, she welcomed Catherine and her daughter into her own home. Catherine came daily, her heart alight with the coming joy, for she was one of those women who lived to nurture life, to guide young mothers as if the children were her own.

“The poor lass has no one who knows a thing about such matters,” Catherine fretted to Eli. “What good are a pair of soldiers at a time like this? They ought to be turned out at the door!”

And so Catherine stayed, a comfort to Margaret, whose fears grew as the days passed. She would die, she thought, and Gerard would never know. Yet hardship had strengthened her where ease might have weakened another. Poverty and her father’s frailty had forced her to labor ceaselessly, and now, by Catherine’s counsel, she worked until the very end.

Then, at sunset on a quiet evening, her trial was over. Weak but safe, she lay with a tiny face beside her, the world suddenly brighter, wider—a mother now.

“Why do you weep, child?” Catherine murmured, brushing a tear from Margaret’s cheek.

“He is not here to see.”

“Ah, but he will be before the babe is weaned. And think—God has been good to you. It might have been a girl, as my Kate once threatened me! But here lies the finest boy in Holland, stout and sound, praise the saints.”

Margaret smiled through her tears. “I am ungrateful to weep. Only—if Gerard could but see him. I bore his absence in my pain, but now it cuts deeper that he is not here to share my joy.” Her voice broke. “Prithee, come to me, Gerard! Dear, dear Gerard!” Her trembling arms reached for what was not there.

Catherine turned away, her own eyes wet. When she looked back, Margaret’s gaze held something strange.

“Did you hear nothing?” Margaret whispered.

“No, love. What?”

“I called for Gerard just now.”

“Aye, I heard that well enough.”

“And he answered me.”

Catherine shook her head. “Hush, now.”

“But I heard it—clear as day. He spoke my name. ‘Margaret.’ Only… his voice was sad. Though perhaps that was the distance. Voices always sound sad from far away.” She sighed, then brightened. “Why do you fret, mother? I feel no sorrow tonight. Not a pain, not a care.” And her eyes, soft with wonder, returned to the child in her arms.

That same night, in distant Rome, Gerard flung himself into the Tiber. At the very hour she heard his voice, he cried her name in the jaws of death and despair.

How shall this be explained? I cannot.
