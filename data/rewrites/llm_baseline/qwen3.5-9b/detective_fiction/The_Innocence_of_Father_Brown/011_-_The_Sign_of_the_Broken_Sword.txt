The forest stretched out like a thousand grey arms, its million fingers tipped with silver frost. Above, the sky was a bruised slate-green, dotted with stars that burned bleak and brilliant like shards of shattered ice. The countryside, thick with timber and thin with life, was rigid under a bitter, brittle chill. Between the tree trunks lay deep, black hollows resembling the bottomless caverns of a Scandinavian hell—a realm of incalculable cold. Even the square stone tower of the nearby church seemed alien, a barbaric structure rising from the sea rocks of Iceland. It was a strange night for wandering a churchyard, yet perhaps a fitting one for uncovering secrets.

The graveyard rose abruptly from the ash-grey wasteland of the forest, a hump of green turf that appeared grey in the starlight. Most graves sloped downward, and the path to the church climbed as steeply as a staircase. Atop the hill, in the sole flat expanse, stood the monument that made the place famous. It stood in stark contrast to the featureless plots surrounding it, crafted by one of Europe's greatest modern sculptors, yet his fame was instantly eclipsed by the man he immortalized. Under the small, silver pencils of starlight, the massive metal figure of a soldier lay recumbent, his strong hands sealed in eternal worship, his great head pillowed upon a gun. The venerable face bore a beard—or rather, heavy whiskers—in the old style of Colonel Newcome, while the uniform, rendered with simple strokes, depicted the gear of modern warfare. Beside his right hip lay a sword, its tip snapped off; to his left rested a Bible.

On warm summer afternoons, wagonettes arrived full of Americans and cultured suburbanites to view the sepulcher. Even then, they felt the vast, silent forest land, dominated by its single dumpy dome of church and churchyard, as oddly mute and neglected. In this freezing midwinter darkness, one might expect total solitude. Yet, in the stillness of the stiff woods, a wooden gate creaked. Two dim figures, dressed in black, climbed the narrow path toward the tomb.

The frigid starlight was so faint that nothing could be discerned about them save that both wore black; one was enormously broad, while the other, by contrast, was startlingly small. They reached the great graven tomb of the historic warrior and stood for moments staring at it. There was no living soul within a wide circle, and a morbid observer might have wondered if the pair were human at all. Their opening exchange, however, confirmed their humanity, albeit in a peculiar manner.

After a silence, the small man addressed the tall one: "Where does a wise man hide a pebble?"

The tall man replied in a low voice: "On the beach."

The small man nodded. After a pause, he asked, "Where does a wise man hide a leaf?"

"In the forest," came the answer.

Another stillness followed, then the tall man resumed: "Do you mean that when a wise man must hide a real diamond, he conceals it among sham ones?"

"No, no," laughed the little man. "We will let bygones be bygones."

He stamped his cold feet briefly before continuing, "I'm not thinking of that at all, but of something else—something peculiar. Just strike a match, will you?"

The big man fumbled in his pocket. Soon, a scratch and a flare painted the entire flat side of the monument in gold light. Carved in black letters were the familiar words revered by so many Americans: *"Sacred to the Memory of General Sir Arthur St. Clare, Hero and Martyr, who Always Vanquished his Enemies and Always Spared Them, and Was Treacherously Slain by Them At Last. May God in Whom he Trusted both Reward and Revenge him."*

The match singed the big man's fingers, blackened, and fell. He reached for another, but his small companion stopped him. "That's all right, Flambeau, old man; I saw what I wanted. Or, rather, I didn't see what I didn't want. Now we must walk a mile and a half along the road to the next inn, and I will try to tell you all about it. For heaven knows, a man needs a fire and ale when he dares tell such a story."

They descended the precipitous path, re-latched the rusty gate, and set off at a stamping, ringing walk down the frozen forest road. A quarter-mile in, the smaller man spoke again. "Yes; the wise man hides a pebble on the beach. But what does he do if there is no beach? Do you know anything of that great St. Clare trouble?"

"I know nothing about English generals, Father Brown," answered the large man, laughing. "Though a little about English policemen. I only know that you've dragged me a precious long dance to all the shrines of this fellow, whoever he is. One would think he got buried in six different places. I've seen a memorial to General St. Clare in Westminster Abbey. I've seen a ramping equestrian statue on the Embankment. I've seen a medallion in the street he was born in, and another where he lived; and now you drag me after dark to his coffin in a village churchyard. I am beginning to tire of his magnificent personality, especially as I don't in the least know who he was. What are you hunting for in all these crypts and effigies?"

"I am only looking for one word," said Father Brown. "A word that isn't there."

"Well," asked Flambeau, "are you going to tell me anything about it?"

"I must divide it into two parts," remarked the priest. "First, there is what everybody knows; and then, there is what I know. Now, what everybody knows is short and plain enough. It is also entirely wrong."

"Right you are," said Flambeau cheerfully. "Let's begin at the wrong end. Let's start with what everybody knows, which isn't true."

"If not wholly untrue, it is at least very inadequate," continued Brown. "All that the public knows amounts precisely to this: The public believes Arthur St. Clare was a great and successful English general. They know that after splendid campaigns in India and Africa, he commanded against Brazil when the patriot Olivier issued his ultimatum. They know that on that occasion, St. Clare, with a tiny force, attacked Olivier's massive army and was captured after heroic resistance. They know that, to the abhorrence of the civilized world, St. Clare was hanged on the nearest tree. He was found swinging there after the Brazilians withdrew, with his broken sword hung around his neck."

"And that popular story is untrue?" suggested Flambeau.

"No," said his friend quietly. "That story is quite true, so far as it goes."

"Well, I think it goes far enough!" said Flambeau. "But if the popular story is true, what is the mystery?"

Hundreds of grey, ghostly trees passed before the little priest answered. He bit his finger reflectively. "Why, the mystery is one of psychology. Or rather, two psychologies. In that Brazilian business, two of the most famous men of modern history acted flat against their characters. Mind you, Olivier and St. Clare were both heroes—the old stuff, no mistake; it was like the fight between Hector and Achilles. Now, what would you say to an affair where Achilles was timid and Hector was treacherous?"

"Go on," urged the large man impatiently as the priest bit his finger again.

"Sir Arthur St. Clare was a soldier of the old religious type—the type that saved us during the Mutiny," continued Brown. "He was always more for duty than for dash; with all his personal courage, he was a decidedly prudent commander, particularly indignant at needless waste of soldiers. Yet in this last battle, he attempted something a baby could see was absurd. One need not be a strategist to see it was as wild as wind; just as one need not be a strategist to avoid a motor-bus. Well, that is the first mystery: what had become of the English general's head? The second riddle is, what had become of the Brazilian general's heart? President Olivier might be called a visionary or a nuisance, but even his enemies admitted he was magnanimous to the point of chivalry. Almost every prisoner he had taken was freed or loaded with gifts. Men who had truly wronged him departed touched by his simplicity and sweetness. Why should he diabolically exact revenge only once in his life—and that for the one particular blow that could not have hurt him? Well, there you have it. One of the wisest men in the world acted like an idiot for no reason. One of the best men in the world acted like a fiend for no reason. That's the long and the short of it; and I leave it to you, my boy."

"No, you don't," said the other with a snort. "I leave it to you; and you jolly well tell me all about it."

"Well," resumed Father Brown, "it's not fair to say the public impression is just what I've said without adding that two things have happened since. I can't say they threw new light; for nobody can make sense of them. But they threw a new kind of darkness, casting shadows in new directions.
