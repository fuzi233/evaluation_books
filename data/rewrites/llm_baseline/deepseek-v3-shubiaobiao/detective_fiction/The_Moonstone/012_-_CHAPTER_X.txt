The dinner party at Lady Verinder’s should have been a triumph—twenty-four guests, fine wine, and the radiant Miss Rachel presiding as queen of the evening. Yet from the moment the company assembled, an uneasy tension hummed beneath the surface. Perhaps it was the Moonstone itself, glinting coldly from Rachel’s dress like an omen.

Two guests in particular could not take their eyes from it.

To Rachel’s left sat Dr. Candy, our Frizinghall physician—a man whose tongue outpaced his tact. With a wink and a chuckle, he declared the Diamond ought to be sacrificed to science. *"Let me reduce it to smoke, Miss Verinder! A puff of carbon, and your anxieties vanish!"* Lady Verinder, tight-lipped, looked as though she wished he were serious.

To Rachel’s right was the far more unsettling figure of Mr. Murthwaite, the famed Indian traveler. Silent as a shadow, he studied the gem with an intensity that bordered on reverence. When at last he spoke, his voice was cool, detached: *"If you ever set foot in India, Miss Verinder, leave this behind. Some treasures are not merely stolen—they are sacred."* The Bouncers gasped in delight; my lady stiffened.

The dinner itself was a disaster. Conversations sputtered and died. Dr. Candy, oblivious, cheerfully recommended skeleton-viewing to a widow who—unbeknownst to him—had been mourning her husband for a decade. The resulting eruption left the company in stunned silence.

Even Mr. Franklin, usually the life of any gathering, fumbled terribly. His foreign wit misfired—first scandalizing the vicar’s aunt with French philosophy, then enraging our local squire by dismissing his prized bulls as *"products of insufficient introspection."* By the time he provoked Dr. Candy into a full-blown quarrel over medical quackery, the party was beyond saving.

Then came the drumbeat on the terrace.

The Indians had returned.

Before I could intervene, the guests spilled outside, eager for spectacle. The jugglers bowed low—too low, their eyes darting past the crowd to fix on Rachel. On the *Moonstone.* Only Mr. Murthwaite noticed their tension. A few hissed words in their own tongue, and the color drained from their leader’s face. The performance ended abruptly.

Later, among the shadowed trees, Murthwaite delivered his verdict: *"Those men are no jugglers. They are Brahmins—high-caste priests who have defiled themselves by crossing oceans and donning disguises. For them, this is no theft. It is a holy war."*

Franklin blanched. *"You think they’d kill for it?"*

Murthwaite lit a cheroot. *"As casually as you’d knock out a pipe’s ashes."*

The solution was brutal: *"Destroy the Diamond. Cut it into six stones. End its legend."*

As thunder growled in the distance, Murthwaite’s dry voice cut through the gloom: *"The Indians will want umbrellas tonight."*

I retired to my room, my hands unsteady. Opening *Robinson Crusoe* at random, I found a grim comfort:

The danger, however, was all too real.
