That evening, as darkness settled and the sky threatened rain, Romola returned from the hospital of San Matteo, her lantern glowing softly beside her, accompanied by Maso. She had gone there after vespers, drawn by her quiet devotion to the sick and suffering. Just as they neared their home, she encountered Tito emerging from the monastery of San Marco. He had left the house earlier that day and had spent little time with her, his presence marked only by fleeting moments and formal gestures. Now, he offered to walk her home, dismissing Maso without hesitation—his short steps were an annoyance Tito could not bear.

It was customary for him to accompany her only when duty demanded it. Their marriage had long since become a ritual of mutual respect and silence, not intimacy. They spoke of matters great and small, but always with careful detachment, each adhering to a shared pretense of harmony. If Tito wished for supper in the old library—now transformed into a pleasant banqueting hall—Romola would see to it without question. In return, he allowed her full freedom in her daily life, accepting her help in copying manuscripts or organizing notes, and even providing supplies for her charitable works. Yet between them lay a gulf so deep that even glances were avoided. Tito often pretended she was not at home, refusing to seek her in her room, and joked that she preferred solitude to his company.

In the early days after her decision to stay, Romola had tried—tentatively, painfully—to rebuild trust. But such efforts required honesty, clarity, a willingness to confront their differences openly. Tito, however, saw no value in such truths. To him, affection was the only remedy, and affection, in his view, meant forgetting the past. But Romola could not forget. Her tenderness had died with her illusions. She could be gentle, obedient, even kind—but love was beyond her reach. And so, though she remained his wife, she was also a stranger within his walls.

This night, as they walked together, Tito said, “The day is not yet done for me. I’ll escort you home, my Romola, then I must attend to another matter—one that will take an hour, perhaps more. I need rest, and I shall return soon.”

He began to speak lightly of his visit to Pisa, of the sights and people he’d seen, until they approached a loggia near a street lamp that cast its dim light over a painted image of the Virgin. The street was quiet, but now came the sound of approaching footsteps and voices growing louder.

“We’ll be caught in the rain unless we take shelter,” Tito said quickly, guiding Romola up the step of the loggia with a slight urgency.

“Surely this drizzle isn’t worth waiting for,” Romola replied, surprised.

“No,” he answered, “it’s growing heavier. Let us wait a moment.”

But beneath his calm words was a keen alertness. The flicker of the lamp revealed something—a red feather on a hat, a gleaming sword hilt. A man wearing those signs was the last person Tito wanted to meet tonight, especially with Romola beside him.

Earlier that day, he had met Dolfo Spini, captain of the Compagnacci, and the task he now spoke of was a second meeting with him—part of a plan set in motion after his visit to San Marco. Tito had been entrusted with forged letters addressed to Savonarola, one bearing the seal and signature of the Cardinal of Naples, who had long supported the Friar. These letters claimed the Cardinal was traveling from Pisa and, due to pressing reasons, would not enter Florence. Instead, he intended to pause at San Casciano, ten miles out, and ride to meet Savonarola the next morning on the road to Florence, dressed as a simple priest.

The real purpose? A trap. Spini and his men were to lie in ambush five miles from the city gates. When Savonarola emerged, accompanied only by a Dominican brother, they would seize him and hand him over to Milanese horsemen waiting near San Casciano, who would carry him into Roman territory. It was a dangerous gamble—Savonarola was shrewd, cautious, and wary of leaving the city. His friends insisted he travel with armed guards; here, he would be alone. But Tito calculated that the Frate’s passion for reform and his hope of securing papal support might override caution. Time was limited, and the letter was designed to act swiftly.

Tito had personally delivered the letters to Savonarola, declining to let anyone else handle them. With skillful subtlety, he hinted at their contents, suggesting they involved a rendezvous outside the city, and urged the Friar to request an armed escort from the Signoria. He offered to deliver the plea privately. Savonarola had refused: “An armed guard defeats privacy.” His eyes flashed with resolve. Tito knew then—the Frate would go.

Yet Tito cared little about the outcome. He played both sides, always ensuring his own advantage. If Savonarola fell, he stood to gain diamonds and papal favor. If not, he had strengthened his position with both Savonarola and Spini, and any secrets he uncovered made him more valuable to the Medici. But Spini was reckless. He enjoyed intrigue but lacked discretion. He drank too much, laughed too loudly, and sometimes forgot himself. Though they had agreed never to show open familiarity, Spini might still betray them with a sudden embrace or careless word.

Now, Tito hoped Spini hadn’t seen him. But fate was cruel. Hidden in shadow, Spini had glimpsed Tito under the lamplight—clearly visible, unmistakable. Romola, wrapped in black, remained unnoticed, standing behind her husband’s shoulder in the corner of the loggia.

Spini’s voice cut through the air like a blade:
“Ha! My carrier-pigeon!” he rasped, gripping Tito’s shoulder. “What’s this? Hiding from your comrades? Well, I found you. Saves time. How’s the hunt tomorrow? Will the bald-headed bird rise? Are the falcons ready?”

If rage had been in Tito’s nature, he would have felt it. But instead, he faced a crisis of choice. To silence Spini might raise Romola’s suspicion—she was sharp, and once alarmed, impossible to deceive. To push him away might provoke violence. So he chose a third path—one born of fear: the fear of her.

He gently pressed her hand, as if signaling, and smiled easily.
“Yes, my Dolfo,” he said, “you may prepare. But leave the trumpets behind.”

Spini bristled. “Don’t play Ser Saccente with me. I know where the devil keeps his tail. Did he swallow the bait whole? No scent of hook? The prophet’s nose didn’t work?”

Tito thought, *The brute won’t stop till he empties the bag.* But aloud, he said, “Swallowed it all—like you drink Trebbiano. Ha! Look—torches! A dead body must be coming. I hear the pestilence spreads.”

“Santiddio!” Spini muttered, turning abruptly. “I hate those biers. Good-night.”

The torches were real, but they led not to a corpse, but to a church official returning home. Tito’s lie had worked—Spini had left.

Only then did Tito turn to Romola.
“Do not worry, my Romola,” he said calmly. “That beast said nothing of consequence. We can go now. The rain hasn’t worsened.”

But Romola trembled—not with fear, but with fury. His tone was too easy, too cold. She trusted nothing he said.
“I will not move,” she declared. “Not until I know this treachery will not happen.”

“Wait,” he said, control steady, though irritation stirred. “Let these torches pass.”

They passed—Vicario dell’Arcivescovo—and Tito bowed respectfully. But Romola saw nothing. In that moment, she would have leapt upon him, dragged him down a cliff, if it meant stopping the plot. Union with this man? Impossible. The discipline she had mastered over two years crumbled. She felt only separation.

They stood in near darkness, faces barely visible.

“Tell me the truth, Tito,” she whispered, voice trembling. “This time—tell me the truth. It will be safer for you.”

“Why should I lie?” he replied, a hint of contempt in his voice. “The truth is what you should rejoice in—that I know Spini’s plot, and I’ve ensured the Frate won’t fall victim to it.”

“What is the plot?”

“I decline to say,” Tito replied. “But know this—the Frate will not leave the city.”

She was silent for a moment. Then, in anguish:
“Tito, it’s no use. I don’t believe you.”

She saw him shrug, palms raised—silent, cold.

“If the Frate leaves—or if harm comes to him,” she said, voice rising, “I will tell the Signoria. You will be disgraced. What if I am your wife? I will be disgraced with you. If we are united, I am the part of you that will save you from crime. Others shall not be betrayed.”

“I know what you’d do, _anima mia_,” Tito said, coolly. “So think: if you believe me in nothing else, believe this—I will protect myself. I won’t give you the power to ruin me.”

“Then you promise me—he will not go beyond the gates?”

“He shall not.”

A pause. But doubt remained.

“I will go back to San Marco,” Romola said, stepping forward.

“You shall not!” Tito snapped, seizing her wrists with force. “I am master here. You will not oppose me.”

Passers-by approached. That was why he whispered.

Romola did not struggle. She felt mastered—physically, mentally. But she heard the footsteps, and her pride compelled her to yield, to follow him from the loggia.

They walked in silence, the drizzle soaking their cloaks.

At first, her anger had burned bright—opposition seemed noble, even necessary. But now, memories surfaced: the restraint she had learned, the dignity she had preserved. The grip on her wrists had not enflamed her—it had chilled her. This was not a fight of passion, but of power. And she remembered her flight, her return, the self-control she had regained. That memory held her back.

Now, her mind turned to probabilities. Tito would not risk exposure. He knew she knew. And perhaps—yes, she clung to this possibility—he had never meant to betray Savonarola. Perhaps he had only used the plot to secure his own position.

Tito, meanwhile, was calculating. He waited until they neared home before speaking again.

“Well, Romola,” he said, “have you calmed yourself? If so, consider this: if I truly meant to aid Spini, would I now carry a wife who knows the secret? Such a woman would be a liability. You must see that.”

He assumed the tone of reason—cool, persuasive, confident.

“Yes, Tito,” she said quietly. “You believe I would protect the Republic. You are right. If the Frate is harmed, I will denounce you.”

She paused. Then, with effort:
“But it was not so. I may have spoken too hastily. You never meant to betray him. Only—why must you seem so close to that man?”

“Such alliances are inevitable,” Tito replied, pleased to see her struggle. “Practical men must walk with wolves. You fair creatures live in clouds. Go to bed. Rest well.”

He opened the door for her.
