In the hushed, pale hours of winter, as Romola lay spent by her labors, her mind drifted backward through the days and forward into the misty unknown of tomorrow. The world shifted beneath her gaze, viewed from a vantage point entirely new. Since she had awakened in the boat, her experience had struck her with the force of a fresh seal pressing upon dissolving wax. She had felt untethered, motiveless, sinking into a selfish despair that life offered no sustenance, whispering the fatal wish, "I am weary of life; I wish to die." That cry had accompanied her into sleep, yet from the moment she awoke to the sound of the weeping, reflection had ceased. Unlike in Florence, where she had once rationalized her survival as a duty to alleviate sorrow, she now simply lived. An impulsive, fierce energy drove her to share the burdens of others, to answer the urgent cries of need, and to perform the work that demanded to be done, rendering the arguments for endurance unnecessary.

This transformation felt like a second baptism. In Florence, the simple bonds of humanity had been tangled with the heavy chains of marriage, statecraft, and religious devotion; when these structures failed her, the blow had severed her from life and numbed her compassion. Now, she declared, "It was a base thing in me to crave death. If all else is uncertain, this suffering I can alleviate is certain; if the glory of the cross is a phantom, then sorrow is the only truth. While strength remains in my arm, I will extend it to the fainting; while light visits my eyes, let them seek out the forsaken."

Then, the past surged forth with renewed urgency. Her labor in this green valley was concluded, and the emotions released from the people surrounding her flowed back into the deep, familiar channels of use and affection. This rare solitude, born of total separation from her accustomed life, allowed her to judge herself with a severity unknown before. The compunction inherent in a sympathetic soul, keenly aware of the suffering of others, stirred within her with growing intensity. She questioned the justice of her own judgments and actions: she had been rash, arrogant, perpetually dissatisfied that others were not virtuous enough, while she herself had betrayed the highest ideals her soul once recognized. She began to condemn her flight as cowardly self-preservation, realizing that the reasons Savonarola had given for taking her back were truer and deeper than the excuses for her second departure. How could she claim to feel the needs of strangers while ignoring the needs of those nearest to her?

Yet, a reaction against this self-reproach soon arose. The memory of her life with Tito, of the conditions that rendered their true union impossible while forcing her into a role of false duty—a role that concealed and sanctioned what her mind revolted against—told her that flight had been her only recourse. All minds, save those dulled by insensibility, must endure this recurring conflict where life's twisted conditions forbid the fulfillment of a bond. Strictly speaking, no relation can be replaced; the presence of the new does not erase the breach of the old. Life had lost its perfection; it had been maimed. Until the wounds were fully scarred, conscience would continually cast backward, casting doubtful glances.

Romola shrank with dread at the prospect of returning to Tito, yet unease gnawed at her that she had placed herself beyond his reach, fearing a day when he might suffer and require her aid. A thread of pain remained within her, echoing Fra Girolamo's words: she could not cease to be a wife. Could anything truly end for her that had once mingled with the very current of her heart's blood?

Florence, and every moment of her existence there, returned to her like a ravenous hunger. Her feelings could not wander aimlessly after the possible and the vague; their living fiber was fed by the memory of familiar things. The thought that she had severed herself from them forever became increasingly importunate during these idle hours. What if Fra Girolamo had been mistaken? What if the life of Florence was merely a web of inconsistencies? Was she, then, something superior, entitled to shake the dust from her feet and declare, "This world is not good enough for me"? If she were truly higher, she would not have so easily lost all her trust.

Her indignant grief for her godfather no longer held complete sway, and her sense of debt to Savonarola began to regain predominance. Nothing that had occurred or would occur could erase the fact that there had been a great inspiration in him, one that had awakened a new life within her. Who, in all her experience, could demand greater gratitude from her than he? Yet, his errors—could they not bring calamity?

She could not rest. She scarcely knew whether it was her strength returning with the budding leaves that made her active again, or whether it was an eager longing to draw nearer to Florence. She did not imagine herself daring to enter the city, but the desire to be close enough to learn what transpired there urged itself with a power that excluded all other purposes.

On one March morning, the people of the valley gathered to witness the departure of the Blessed Lady. Jacopo had procured a mule for her and was to accompany her over the mountains. The Padre also went with her to the nearest town, intending to assist her in finding the safest route to Pistoja. Her store of trinkets and money, untouched since arriving in the valley, was ample for her needs.

Had Romola been less drawn by the longing pulling her away, it would have been a difficult moment as she walked the village street for the final time, while the Padre and Jacopo awaited her near the well with the mule. Her steps were hindered by the wailing crowd, who knelt to kiss her hands, then clung to her skirts, kissing the grey folds of her habit and crying, "Ah, why will you go, when the good season begins and the crops will be plentiful? Why will you go?"

"Do not be sorry," said Romola. "You are well now, and I shall remember you. I must go to see if my own people need me."

"Ah, yes, if they have the pestilence!"

"Look at us again, Madonna!"

"Yes, yes, we will be good to little Benedetto!"

Finally, Romola mounted her mule, but a vigorous scream from Benedetto as he saw her turn away in this new capacity served as an excuse for the entire populace to follow, insisting that he must ride on the mule's neck to the foot of the slope. The parting must eventually come, but as Romola turned repeatedly before passing out of sight, she saw the little flock lingering to catch the last wave of her hand.
