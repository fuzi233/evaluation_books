The undisputed star of the dinner party guest list was Mr. Murthwaite, the famed Eastern traveller. Fresh off years of trekking through uncharted, danger-ridden territories, he’d become a darling of London society—everyone adored a man who’d cheated death so many times just to come back and tell the tale. Now he’d announced he was setting off again to push deeper into the unmapped wilds of Central Asia, and that bold indifference to his own safety had revived all the fading public fascination around him. The odds were stacked so heavily against him surviving this second journey that every guest at the table treated him like a living ghost: the sort of eminent dinner companion you could enjoy knowing the next news you heard of him would almost certainly be a report of his murder somewhere in the mountains.

As soon as the ladies withdrew to the drawing room, the gentlemen naturally fell to arguing politics, that dreary national pastime all Englishmen are obligated to indulge in the second no women are around to moderate them. I happen to be one of the most un-English Englishmen alive when it comes to politics—all that back-and-forth strikes me as the most tedious, profitless waste of breath imaginable. Glancing down the table after the first bottle of port had made its round, I spotted Murthwaite clearly shared my low opinion: he was leaning back in his chair, very politely and dexterously fighting off a nap, not wanting to offend his host by admitting the political bickering was putting him to sleep.

I decided it was well worth testing if a well-timed reference to the Moonstone would jolt him fully awake, and if it did, hear what this man who knew Indian customs far better than any of us in Gray’s Inn made of the latest twist in the Indian conspiracy that had turned up in my office only the day before.
“If I’m not mistaken, Mr. Murthwaite,” I said, “you were acquainted with the late Lady Verinder, and took some interest in that bizarre string of events that ended with the Moonstone going missing?”

The great traveller snapped wide awake in an instant, and fixed me with a sharp, curious look. “Who are you, sir?”
I told him I was Mr. Bruff of Gray’s Inn Square, the lawyer who’d drawn up Colonel Herncastle’s will, and who’d managed the old soldier’s affairs right up to his death.

Murthwaite shifted his chair sharply, turning his back on every other man at the table—Conservative and Liberal alike—so he could focus all his attention entirely on me.
“Have you heard anything of the three Indian pilgrims lately?” he asked, no preamble.
“I have every reason to believe one of them paid me a visit at my office yesterday,” I replied.

That answer staggered even the unshakable Murthwaite. I laid out every detail of what had happened to Mr. Luker, then what had played out in my own office, exactly as I’ve recorded it here.
“His last question clearly had a purpose,” I added. “Why on earth would he care what exact time a moneylender allows a borrower to pay back their loan and redeem their pledge?”
“Can’t you see his motive, Mr. Bruff?” he asked.
“I’m ashamed to say I don’t,” I admitted.

Murthwaite leaned forward, eager to walk me through it. “Let’s retrace the entire conspiracy from the moment you drew up Herncastle’s will. It’s in your best interest, and in Miss Verinder’s, to understand every thread of this plot, so you can act fast if the Indians make their next move. First off: the three men we’re dealing with now are not the same three Brahmins who followed Colonel Herncastle to England decades ago. They’re all under forty, which makes them the next generation of successors, sent here by their order to pick up where their predecessors left off. And they didn’t just inherit the mission—they inherited the small, tight underground network the first three pilgrims built here: a pool of available cash, a handful of shadowy English fixers who operate in London’s immigrant underbelly, and the quiet loyalty of a small community of Indian expats working menial jobs across the city who share their faith. It’s not a formidable operation by our Western standards, but it’s effective.”

“Now, when did the Indians get their first real shot at the Moonstone?” he asked.
“The day Colonel Herncastle died,” I answered immediately. “They must have known the diamond was no longer locked in the bank vault under his name.”
“Exactly. They got a copy of his will from Doctors’ Commons via one of their fixers, found out the jewel was bequeathed to Rachel Verinder, and figured out the safest way to go after it was to wait until it was brought up to Lady Verinder’s Yorkshire estate, rather than risk a heist on the London bank. That’s why they turned up in Frizinghall disguised as travelling jugglers, laying low to wait for their chance.”

I nodded, but raised the obvious problem. “But Franklin Blake spotted one of their spies following him in London, raced north hours earlier than planned, and stashed the Moonstone in the Frizinghall bank before the Indians even knew he was in the county. For weeks before Rachel’s birthday, they must have thought the diamond was right in Lady Verinder’s house—why didn’t they make any attempt to break in? I don’t buy that story of their clairvoyant little boy, either. That’s nonsense to a practical man like me.”
“To me too,” Murthwaite laughed. “That clairvoyance trick is just romantic theatre for them—they use mild mesmerism on the boy to make him reflect what they’re all already thinking, and call him a seer to stoke their own morale on this long, dangerous errand. No magic involved. The real reason they didn’t storm the house? No Indian will ever take an unnecessary risk. They knew per the will that the Moonstone would be Rachel’s absolute, unchallenged property on her 18th birthday. Why risk tangling with Franklin Blake, who’d already proven he could suspect and outwit them, when they could wait a few more weeks for the diamond to belong to an innocent young girl who would no doubt parade it around her neck for every guest to see? And their patience paid off—they got to the house on her birthday, and saw the Moonstone pinned to the front of her dress, just as they predicted. I told everyone at the party that night they should cut the diamond into smaller, unidentifiable stones to break the curse of its legend. But then it vanished that same night, the Indians got locked up as vagrants the next day, and their entire first plan fell apart.”

He pulled out his pocket book, flipped to a marked page. “Their second chance came while they were still in that Frizinghall jail. I was staying nearby at the time, and the prison governor came to me with a letter delivered to the Indians, postmarked Lambeth, written in Hindustani. I copied the original and my translation down right here.”

He pushed the open book across to me. The letter ran short, no signature, no date:
*In the name of the Regent of the Night, whose seat is on the Antelope, whose arms embrace the four corners of the earth.
Brothers, turn your faces to the south, and come to me in the street of many noises, which leads down to the muddy river.
The reason is this.
My own eyes have seen it.*
“The Regent of the Night is the Moon God in Hindu mythology,” Murthwaite explained. “The man who sent that letter was a foreign workman Mr. Luker had just fired from his shop on Lambeth. He was the Indians’ inside man, who’d gotten the Moonstone after it vanished from Yorkshire, and passed word to the conspirators in jail exactly where it was being kept. As soon as they were released, they caught the first train to London, and camped outside Luker’s house to watch him. Luker got the diamond into his own banker’s strongroom before they could break in, and they ended up with nothing but his bank receipt, proving the Moonstone was locked away there.”

That was when it clicked. I jumped in my seat. “I see now! The Indians know Luker took the diamond as a one-year pledge. They were asking me exactly what hour the loan matures, so they’ll know the very first minute the thief who pledged the diamond can walk into that bank, pay back the money, and take the Moonstone back!”

Murthwaite nodded. “If the diamond was pawned around the end of last June, that date will be the very last week of June, 1849. I’ll be thousands of miles deep in Central Asia then, far away from all English news. But you, Mr. Bruff? You’d be a fool not to mark that date down in your diary, and plan to be in London when it rolls around.”
“You think something serious will happen?” I asked.
“I think I’d be far safer trekking with the most violent fanatics in the mountains than I would be walking through that bank door with the Moonstone in my pocket,” he said gravely. “They’ve been defeated twice already. I would bet every penny I own they will not fail a third time.”

That was the last we spoke of the subject. Coffee was brought in, the rest of the guests rose to stretch their legs, and we all made our way upstairs to join the ladies in the drawing room.
