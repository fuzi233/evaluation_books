It was the night of August the thirtieth.

In Donegal, a month had slid by since the Lennon House ball, and still the countryside chewed upon one flavour as stubbornly as a bit of gristle: Harry Feversham’s disappearance. The climbing street in the town repeated it from doorstep to doorstep; the gentry, behind polished glass and carved sideboards, passed it with the salted butter and the roast, each serving of gossip accompanied by a fresh certainty. Somebody had seen him at dawn, they swore—still in dress clothes, face bleached like curd, moving along the causeway by Lennon Bridge. Mrs. Brien O’Brien corrected the time with the zeal of a woman pinching out scones by the clock: not five minutes to six, but ten past. A drag-net was suggested with the practical relish of men who like an answer that uses rope and muscle. Mr. Dennis Rafferty, on the Rathmullen road, refused salmon on the grounds that he was not a cannibal, and the joke, as jokes do, began to travel farther than the truth.

No one, in their dinner-talk, touched the real cause, because there were only two who knew it; and those two kept the old routine of living—chores, visits, pride held a shade higher—as if catastrophe were merely a weather change. Ethne, perhaps, had softened in small ways, the way a hand grows gentler when it learns what bruises; Dermod, perhaps, had sharpened, quick to anger like a blade honed too often. But that was all the evidence the world got. So the world filled the silence with its own noise.

Harry Feversham, meanwhile, was not in Donegal at all. He was in London, and Lieutenant Sutch learned it in a crowd.

All day the city had been shaken by rumours from Egypt—Kassassin, east of Ismailia—tidings arriving like gusts that turn a street’s head first one way and then another. Men ran messages as if chased; boys shouted victories and disasters with the same hoarse throat. There had been a moonlit charge, Drury-Lowe’s cavalry rolling up Arabi’s flank and taking guns; there were whispers of an English general dead; of the York and Lancaster cut to pieces. By eleven at night Pall Mall was packed beneath the gas-lamps, faces upturned toward the War Office blinds as if light itself might speak. The crowd was unnaturally still; only when a shadow crossed those lit panels did a shiver go through them and the mass sway, edge to edge, like wheat under a passing wind.

Lieutenant Sutch, careful with his wounded leg, stood on the outskirts with his back to the parapet of the Junior Carlton Club. A touch came on his arm—light, urgent, the sort of touch that cannot wait to be asked its name. He turned and saw Harry Feversham beside him.

Feversham’s face worked as if some private fever kneaded it; it was extraordinarily white, and his eyes shone too brightly, like eyes that had stared at a ceiling through long hours. Sutch, for an instant, was not sure whether he knew him—or whether, in that moment’s London exhaustion, he cared who any man was.

“I might have been out there in Egypt to-night,” Feversham said quickly, in a voice that stumbled over itself. “Think of it! I might have been out there, sitting by a camp-fire in the desert, talking over the battle with Jack Durrance; or dead perhaps. What would it have mattered? I might have been in Egypt to-night!”

Sutch read in his unexpected presence—no less than in that wandering tongue—that something had gone wrong beyond ordinary repair. A dozen questions rose in him. He asked none. Instead he took Feversham’s arm and drew him away from the gathered faces.

“I saw you in the crowd,” Feversham went on, as if he had been holding the sentence in his teeth until he found a listener. “I thought I would speak to you because—do you remember? a long time ago you gave me your card. I have always kept it. I have always feared I should have reason to use it. You said that if one was in trouble, the telling might help.”

Sutch stopped him and pointed him toward a doorway. “We will go in here. We can find a quiet corner in the upper smoking-room.”

Harry looked up. The steps belonged to the Army and Navy Club.

“Good God, not there!” he cried in a sharp low voice; and he stepped quickly into the roadway, where the gaslight fell less directly on his face.

Sutch limped after him. “Then where?”

“Not to-night. It is late. To-morrow, if you will—in some quiet place, after nightfall. I do not go out in daylight.”

Again Sutch did not ask why. He simply accepted the rule, as a cook accepts a guest’s aversion and alters the dish without comment.

“I know a quiet restaurant,” he said. “If we dine there at nine, we shall meet no one we know. Meet me just before nine to-morrow night at the corner of Swallow Street.”

So on the following evening they ate together at a small table in a corner of the Criterion grill-room. Feversham came in with a quick, restless glance, measuring the room as though each pair of shoulders might belong to a judge.

“I dine here often when I am in town,” Sutch said, as if to season the place with familiarity. “Listen.”

Under the talk, under the clink of glasses and the soft scuff of waiters’ shoes, there was the deep throbbing of engines feeding the electric light—an industrial pulse, steady, insistent. One could almost feel it through the soles of one’s feet.

“It reminds me of a ship,” Sutch said with a brief smile. “I can almost fancy myself in the gun-room again. We will have dinner. Then you shall tell me your story.”

“You have heard nothing of it?” Feversham asked, suspicion thick in the question.

“Not a word.”

Feversham’s shoulders loosened by a fraction, as if he had been holding them up by force. He had been living as though every passer-by knew his shame, as if contempt were served in the air with the soot and fog. To find one man ignorant of it felt, absurdly, like finding a quiet field after a week of street noise.

The meal came and went—courses placed and cleared with practiced hands. Aromas rose—hot fat and seared meat, the sharpness of mustard, the browned edge of bread—yet Feversham ate as though taste were a rumour, his nerves too tight to let food have its rightful dominion. Sutch watched him in the full light now: thin, haggard, the hollows under his eyes dark as bruises. He moved with a feverish restlessness, drawing breath as if the air were not quite enough.

Once or twice between courses Feversham began, but Sutch held him off with a gesture. “Not yet. Not until the cloth is cleared.”

At last the table was stripped to coffee and cigars. Sutch held out his case.

“Now,” he said. “Take your time, Harry.”

Then Feversham spoke—slowly, carefully, as if he were forcing his words through a narrow opening so that none could be embellished on the way. He told everything without exaggeration, without omission. He began with the Crimean night at Broad Place and ended with the ball at Lennon House. He made his own disgrace plain, not softened by excuses, and in the very steadiness of his recital there was something almost unreal—like listening to a man describe another man’s ruin simply to fill an hour.

“I came back across Lough Swilly early that morning,” he said when he reached the end, “and travelled at once to London. Since then I have stayed in my rooms all day, listening to the bugles calling in the barrack-yard beneath my windows. At night I prowl about the streets or lie in bed waiting for the Westminster clock to sound each new quarter of an hour. On foggy nights I can hear steam-sirens on the river.” He gave a laugh that had no warmth in it. “Do you know when the ducks start quacking in St. James’s Park? At two o’clock to the minute.”

Sutch listened without interrupting. Yet halfway through—at the moment Feversham spoke of concealing the telegram—Sutch’s posture changed. Until then he had sat forward, arms on the table, eyes fixed on Harry. After that he raised a hand to his forehead and kept it there, screening his face while the story continued.

Feversham had no doubt why. He thought Sutch was hiding scorn—hiding the involuntary curl of a lip. But he did not falter. He kept to his truth like a man walking through fire because he has decided to reach the other side.

When he finished, Sutch did not at once move his hand. Silence settled between them, broken only by the far-off clatter of a plate and the unceasing subterranean vibration of the engines. At last Sutch spoke, and the words struck Feversham with surprise.

“I am much to blame,” Sutch said. “I should have spoken that night at Broad Place, and I held my tongue. I shall hardly forgive myself.”

There was no contempt in his voice. It shook, but with contrition.

“You see, I understood,” he went on. “Your father, I am afraid, never would.”

“He never will,” Harry said, bitterly quiet.

“No,” Sutch agreed. “Your mother—had she lived—would have seen clearly. Few women would, I think, except your mother. Brute courage! Women make a god of it. That girl, for instance—”

“You must not blame her,” Harry broke in. “I was defrauding her into marriage.”

Sutch’s hand dropped from his forehead; he looked at Harry directly. “Suppose you had never met her. Would you still have sent in your papers?”

Harry considered, forcing himself to fairness. “I think not. Disgracing my name—and those dead men in the hall—I think I would have risked. I could not risk disgracing her.”

Sutch thumped his fist softly on the table, despairing not of Harry but of all the missed chances that had shaped him. “If only I had spoken at Broad Place. Harry, why didn’t you let me speak? I might have saved you many unnecessary years of torture. Good heavens! what a childhood you must have spent with that fear all alone with you. It makes me shiver to think of it. I might even have saved you from this last catastrophe. For I understood. I understood.”

He spoke as a man who has walked those inner corridors and knows where the mind hides its injuries. Sutch saw—more clearly than Harry could—the long harm done by growing up with an uncomprehending father and the relentless company of dead soldiers staring from the walls; by having no one to whom a boy might say, in his own faltering private language, what he was afraid of becoming. The fear had sapped at Harry incessantly; it had sat beside him at meals, gone to bed with him, haunted his dreams. It had made intimacy with friends dangerous. Sutch did not wonder that at last it had driven him into an irretrievable mistake.

“Did you ever read ‘Hamlet’?” Sutch asked abruptly.

“Of course.”

“But did you consider it? The same disability is clear in that character. The thing he foresaw, thought over, imagined in act and consequence—that he shrank from, upbraiding himself even as you have done. Yet when the moment of action comes—sharp and immediate—does he fail? No. He excels; and just by reason of that foresight. I have seen men in the Crimea tortured by imagination before the fight—once the fight had begun you must search among Oriental fanatics for their match. ‘Am I a coward?’ Do you remember the lines? ‘Am I a coward? Who calls me villain? Breaks my pate across? Plucks off my beard, and blows it in my face?’ There’s the case in a nutshell. If only I had spoken on that night!”

A few people passed their table on the way out. Sutch glanced around; the grill-room was thinning. He looked at his watch—eleven. It was late enough that decisions could not be postponed by politeness.

“You cannot stay in London,” he said, his voice tightening, “hiding by day, slinking about by night. That’s too like—” He stopped.

“That’s too like Wilmington,” Feversham finished quietly, summoning the old story his father had told him long ago—one he had never let himself forget. “But Wilmington’s end will not be mine. Of that I can assure you. I shall not stay in London.”

He spoke with a decision that had been forged in solitude. He had planned already what Sutch feared to ask.

“Who knows of the feathers?” Sutch said. “How many people? Give me their names.”

“Trench, Castleton, Willoughby,” Feversham began.

“All three are in Egypt. Besides, for the credit of their regiment they are likely to hold their tongues when they return. Who else?”

“Dermod Eustace and—Ethne.”

“They will not speak.”

“You,” Harry said, “Durrance perhaps, and my father.”

Sutch leaned back, startled. “Your father! You wrote to him?”

“No. I went into Surrey and told him.”

Remorse seized Sutch again, sharp as the taste of burnt coffee. “Why didn’t I speak that night?” he said helplessly. “A coward—and you go quietly down to Surrey and confront your father with that story to tell him! You do not even write! You stand up and tell it to him face to face! Harry, I reckon myself as good as another when it comes to bravery, but for the life of me I could not have done that.”

“It was not—pleasant,” Harry said simply. That was all he ever gave to any man, but in that one word Sutch could hear the room in Broad Place, the hard silence, the old man’s judgement falling like a blade.

Harry went on. “My father continues my allowance. I shall need it—every penny of it—otherwise I should have taken nothing. But I am not to go home again. I did not mean to go home for a long while in any case, if at all.”

He drew his pocket-book from his breast and took out the four white feathers. In the grimy light of the restaurant they looked both delicate and indecent, like something stolen from a bird and turned into an accusation. He laid them on the table before him.

“You have kept them?” Sutch exclaimed.

“Indeed, I treasure them,” Harry said quietly. “That seems strange to you. To you they are the symbols of my disgrace. To me they are much more. They are my opportunities of retrieving it.”

He separated three of the feathers and pushed them forward a little on the stained tablecloth. Then he leaned toward Sutch, as if lowering his voice could make the plan less mad.

“What if I could compel Trench, Castleton, and Willoughby to take back from me, each in his turn, the feather he sent? I do not say it is likely. I do not say even it is possible. But there is a chance—and I must wait upon that chance. There will be few men leading active lives as these three do who will not at some moment stand in great peril and great need. To be in readiness for that moment is from now my career. All three are in Egypt. I leave for Egypt to-morrow.”

A look of unexpected happiness came over Sutch’s face—quick and bright, like a lamp lit in a long-dark passage. This issue had not occurred to him, and once it appeared it seemed the only issue. His knowledge of prudence—the obstacles, the improbability, the old risk of shrinking at the last—fell away from him as if it belonged to someone else. His eyes shone with pride; in his heart the feathers were already returned.

Harry himself was more cautious. “There are endless difficulties. Just to cite one: I am a civilian; these three are soldiers, surrounded by soldiers—so much the less opportunity therefore for a civilian.”

“But it is not necessary that the three men should be themselves in peril,” Sutch objected. “For you to convince them that the fault is retrieved.”

“Oh, no. There may be other ways,” Harry agreed. “The plan came suddenly into my mind—indeed at the moment when Ethne bade me take up the feathers and added the fourth. I was on the point of tearing them across when this way out of it sprang clearly up. But I have thought it over since, during these last weeks while I sat listening to the bugles in the barrack-yard. And I am sure there is no other way. It is well worth trying.”

He drew a deep breath. His voice dropped. He kept his eyes on the table as though the dirty cloth could absorb what he was about to say.

“You see—if the three take back their feathers,” he murmured, “why, then she perhaps might take hers back too.”

“Will she wait, do you think?” Sutch asked.

Harry’s head snapped up. “Oh, no,” he cried. “I had no thought of that. She has not even a suspicion of what I intend to do. Nor do I wish her to have one until the intention is fulfilled.”

His next words came slowly, with a new hesitation, as if he were moving into ground that could not be crossed without reverence.

“My thought was different. I find it difficult to tell you. Ethne said something to me the day before the feathers came—something rather sacred. I think I will tell you, because what she said is just what sends me out upon this errand. But for her words, I would very likely never have thought of it. I find in them my motive and a great hope. They may seem strange to you, Mr. Sutch; but I ask you to believe they are very real to me.”

He paused, then spoke as though quoting a line he had repeated in the dark until it became part of his breathing.

“She said—it was when she knew no more than that my regiment was ordered to Egypt—she was blaming herself because I had resigned my commission, for which there was no need, because—these were her words—because had I fallen, although she would have felt lonely all her life, she would none the less have surely known that she and I would see much of one another—afterwards.”

He still did not look at Sutch.

“Do you understand?” he asked softly. “I have a hope that if—this fault can be repaired—” and he pointed to the feathers, “—we might still, perhaps, see something of one another—afterwards.”

It was a strange thing to say across a public table, amid the stale smell of smoke and coffee, with engines throbbing underfoot like an uneasy heart. Yet neither man felt it fanciful. They were not dealing in polite conversation now, but in the simple severe matters by which a life is measured. The incongruity of the place could not touch them.

Sutch said nothing for a long moment. When Harry finally looked up—prepared for ridicule—he saw Sutch’s right hand held out to him.

“When I come back,” Harry said, rising.

He gathered the feathers and returned them to his pocket-book with care, as if they were both wounds and instruments. “I have told you everything. You see, I wait upon chance opportunities. The three may not come in Egypt. They may never come at all, and in that case I shall not come back at all. Or they may come only at the very end and after many years. Therefore I thought I would like just one person to know the truth thoroughly in case I do not come back. If you hear definitely that I never can come back, I would be glad if you would tell my father.”

“I understand,” Sutch said.

“But don’t tell him everything,” Harry insisted. “Not the last part—not what I have just said about Ethne and my chief motive, for I do not think he would understand. Otherwise you will keep silence altogether. Promise.”

Sutch promised, but his face was absent—already wrestling with the sorrow of that limit. Even if Harry died without fulfilling his mission, Sutch was to hide from General Feversham what was best in the son, because the son was right: the father could not understand. That, to Sutch, was the most bitter taste of all.

Harry pressed him again. “You will breathe no word of this to man or woman, however hard you may be pressed, except to my father under the circumstances which I have explained.”

Sutch promised a second time, immediately, without hesitation. There was no reason to refuse; disclosure would look like a boast, and Sutch, whatever his odd kindnesses, had no love of cheap display. Yet as the promise bound him, his mind went back to the beginning of this misery—the mother dead too early to interpret the boy in the language of love; the father left to rule with a vocabulary of iron; and his own silence on the Crimean night at Broad Place.

“If only I had spoken,” he said sadly.

He dropped the end of his cigar into his coffee-cup. The little hiss was lost in the room’s greater hum. He stood and reached for his hat.

“Many things are irrevocable, Harry,” he said. “But one never knows whether they are irrevocable or not until one has found out. It is always worth while finding out.”

The next evening Feversham crossed to Calais.

The night was as wild as the one on which Durrance had left England—rain driven hard, wind worrying at the harbour like an animal that will not be turned away. Like Durrance, Feversham did not go alone into that darkness; a friend came to see him off. The last thing his eyes held, as the packet swung out from the pier, was Lieutenant Sutch’s face under a gas-lamp—pale in the hard light, steady against the weather.

Sutch stayed where he was after the boat had slipped into the blackness, until even the throb of the paddles could no longer be heard and the sea took back its own sound. Then he limped through the rain to his hotel, aware—regretfully aware—that he was growing old.

It was long since he had regretted it. Ever since the Crimea he had been, as he once told General Feversham, on the world’s half-pay list; and between that and the memory of a certain magical season before the Crimea, he had looked on old age as an approaching friend. But to-night he wanted time—time enough to hear, one day, a footstep returning; time enough to look into Muriel Graham’s son’s face and see honour redeemed and the great fault atoned.
