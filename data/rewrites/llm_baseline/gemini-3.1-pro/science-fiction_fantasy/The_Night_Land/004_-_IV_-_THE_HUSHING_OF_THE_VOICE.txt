Now, one night, toward the end of the sixteenth hour, I was completing the domestic rituals that preceded my sleep-time. The quiet, repetitive friction of my mortar and pestle, grinding dried kelp-pods and spiced rock-lichen into a fine dust for a winding-down broth, usually provided a rhythmic acoustic anchor for my mind. But then the aether trembled. It was a shift in the acoustic ecology of the Great Redoubt, a sudden, piercing clarity cutting through the ambient hum of our insulated world. Naani’s voice sounded plain, resonating all within and about me.

I did not answer her immediately with words, but sent the sure, deep frequency of the Master-Word rolling out into the night—a stabilizing bass note against the discordant, chaotic noise of the dark world. The Master-Word beat steadily back to me. I questioned Naani why she broadcasted now, disrupting the carefully curated sonic environment of the sleep-time. By the eleventh hour, the Monstruwacans of the Lesser Pyramid were meant to rest; she should not have been awake in her Tower of Observation. Yet she made no logical answer. Instead, she introduced a sequence of untranslatable phonemes into my spirit, a linguistic shock that caused the pestle to slip from my trembling fingers, the scent of crushed, peppery lichen forgotten in the air:

"Dearest, thine own feet tread the world at night--"

The words carried the heavy, phonetic weight of a dead language. As the syllables bloomed in my mind, they awakened a multi-generational memory-dream, intertwining timelines until the present vanished. I had crafted these very words for Mirdath the Beautiful in the long-gone Eternity of our Age, when she had died and left me stranded in a world suddenly drained of all flavor and song. The subjective experience of that ancient grief—an aching, untranslatable void—rushed over me. Trembling with the force of this temporal collapse, I called out eagerly with my brain-elements, demanding how she knew a dialect extinct for millions of years. 

She made no direct answer, but spoke the words again. Suddenly, I understood: the dominant lexicon of our present age had momentarily fractured. It was not Naani speaking, but Mirdath herself, calling from out of the everlasting night. 

"Mirdath! Mirdath," I cried into the aether, and the faint voice echoed back through the darkness of eternity. Yet, as the timbre settled, the voice of Mirdath was also the voice of Naani. The two identities seamlessly integrated. I had been birthed back into this world in the living-time of my only soul-mate. I poured all my strength into a desperate call to her, but my transmissions met only the sterile silence of the void. Exhausted, unable to find the quiet necessary to rest, I eventually succumbed to a turbulent sleep.

When I awoke, I moved directly to the kitchen alcove, desperately needing to ground my racing mind in the physical world. I steeped a dark, earthy root-tea, inhaling the bitter, astringent steam as it rose from the heavy clay mug. The tannins washed over my tongue, sharp and waking, as I cast my mind out to Naani once, and then twice. 

The Master-Word throbbed solemnly in the night, and soon her voice returned, a little weak as it always was without her Instrument. I questioned her eagerly about the ancient dialect she had spoken. She was bewildered, insisting she had slept deeply, though she had experienced a profound, mythic dream.

As I sipped the dark tea, she recounted the vision. She had seen a tall, powerfully built man in a small, lonesome room, steeped in sorrow. He was writing to ease his grief, and in her dream, she could read the script, though her waking mind recognized it as the phonetic ruins of a dead language. She could remember only one short line, and the word *Mirdath* inscribed above it. 

With a tremor in my spirit, I asked her to repeat the line. Her far voice murmured the evocative syllables:

"Dearest, thine own feet tread the world at night--"

She remembered no more. But it was enough. With a mad, ancient triumph, I projected the remainder of the poem into the aether. The raw, native dialect of our shared past struck her spirit with the violence of a physical blow. The collision of timelines shattered her linguistic boundaries, opening the Gates of Memory. I heard the tears of her spirit—a pure, ringing tone of realization that cleansed the bewildered static from her voice. Using my old love-name, she asked if she was truly Mirdath. My throat tight, my ears thudding with the heavy blood of profound fulfillment, I confirmed it. Our spirits communed across the everlasting night, the mythic reality of our past merging seamlessly with the immediate, sensory present of this Age. 

Naturally, this miraculous rediscovery birthed in me a ravenous hunger to cross the Night Land and find her. But the location of the Lesser Redoubt was a casualty of cultural memory erosion. The food historians and agrarian elders of the Mighty Pyramid knew only from crumbling texts that the Builder had come out of the Southward World in the Beginning. 

Naani and her father, the Master Monstruwacan of the Lesser Redoubt, were equally blind. We even consulted the ancient compasses from the Great Museum. But the massive electromagnetic hum of the Earth-Current flowing from the "Crack" beneath the Pyramid disrupted the instruments. The needle spun lazily, entirely useless, pulled off-center by the deep acoustic resonance of our city's power source. We retained the ancient directional names—North, South, East, West—but only as inherited relics of a forgotten linguistic era when the Sun had governed time and space.

Naani, though starving for our reunion, forbade me from attempting the journey. The chaotic, monstrous noise pollution of the Outer Lands was certain death. Yet, during my telepathic communications, the Master Monstruwacan of our Pyramid—an elder who revered the slow, traditional rhythms of cultivation—noticed a physical quirk in me. Just as a master chef instinctively turns toward the heat of the hearth, my body inevitably oriented itself Northward when I reached out to Naani. 

He documented this phenomenon meticulously in the Hour-Slips of the Tower of Observation. We discussed it over shared meals of slow-braised subterranean tubers and salted fungal steaks, the Elder treating my inner compass as a matter of deep, physiological attunement. My Northward instinct became the subject of much chatter and acoustic transmissions across the cities, bringing me an uncomfortable level of public scrutiny.

Then, a terrible disruption occurred. 

At the seventeenth hour, the carefully balanced acoustic sanctuary of the Pyramid was pierced by Naani’s voice, frail and discordant. I sent the Master-Word and begged to know what troubled her. Her reply was faint, the syllables barely cutting through the surrounding static. The Earth-Current of the Lesser Redoubt had failed.

The protective, stabilizing hum of their existence had vanished, plunging them into the maddening, unstructured noise of the Night Land. Without the grounding frequency of the Current, their people were rapidly losing the will to live. Joy and appetite evaporated in hours. Later, she whispered that the chaotic frequencies from the outside monsters had shattered the minds of many; driven mad by the lack of acoustic protection, they had opened the gates and run out to be slaughtered in the dark. 

For a month, Naani’s voice grew weaker, fading like a dying dialect. Every faint word was a treasure, but the impending silence broke me. I lost all sensory appreciation. I stopped eating. The kitchen lay cold; the spices lost their scent, and I viewed my body only as a failing vessel. 

The Master Monstruwacan intervened, refusing to let me wither into an empty husk. He did not offer abstract philosophies. Instead, he brought me a heavy earthenware bowl of steaming marrow-broth, rich with the sharp, peppery tang of deep-earth mycelium and the fat of cultivated cave-beasts. 

"Eat," he commanded, his voice a comforting, resonant baritone. "You cannot hear the subtle tones of the aether if your body is starving. A hollow reed only amplifies the world's static. To hold the Night-Hearing, you must anchor yourself in the sustenance of the earth." I forced the rich, savory broth down, the warmth restringing the loose cords of my spirit. I knew, despite the impossibility of the task, I would have to venture into the dark. Better to end quickly than to endure the slow starvation of the soul.

That same night, in the Eighteenth Hour, a violent acoustic disturbance woke me. The Master Monstruwacan had roused me to listen. Suddenly, the Master-Word pulsed heavily in the night, followed by a chorus of voices slicing through the aether: "We are coming! We are coming!"

The transmission was harsh, lacking the delicate nuance of Naani's mind. My stomach dropped in a sudden, sickening wave of hope—perhaps they were near our Great Redoubt. I cast the Master-Word into the night, waiting until I caught the faint, distant pulse of Naani’s response. She was still trapped in the Lesser Pyramid, terrified by the same jarring broadcast, assuming we had somehow orchestrated a rescue. I gently sent the steady, soothing rhythm of my presence to calm her, warning her not to feast on false hope, and bade her sleep.

Turning to the Master Monstruwacan, who lacked the Night-Hearing but possessed a heart steeped in deep, agrarian patience, I explained what had transpired. The discordant broadcast had not come from the Lesser Redoubt. The transmission had originated close to our own Great Pyramid, projected not by delicate instruments, but by the brute force of many minds shouting in unison.

"We must go to the Tower of Observation," I told him, a cold realization settling over me. 

We aimed the great spy-glass into the Night Lands. There, beyond the Electric Circle that insulated our city, we witnessed a massive throng of men marching outward into the blackness, stepping willingly into the hideous mysteries and chaotic noise of the world.

The Master Monstruwacan immediately sent word to the Master Watchman. The sanctity of the Pyramid’s borders had been violently breached. To leave the Redoubt during the Sleep-Time was an extreme violation of the Law; egress required strict Examination, Preparation, and the full acoustic announcement of the Opening of the Door to all millions within the city, ensuring the collective harmony was preserved. The ancient, flayed skin of a past transgressor still hung on the metal pegs of the Great Gate—a visceral, tactile reminder that cultural memory and Law were strictly maintained.

The Master Watchman rushed to the Watch-Dome with his Central Guard. They found the men of the Sleep-Time Watch bound and gagged, their mouths stopped to prevent any outcry from alerting the acoustic sensors. 

It was discovered that nearly five hundred young men from the Upper Cities—easily identified by the deep, resonant barrel-chests developed to breathe the thin air of the upper tiers—had ambushed the guards. They had slipped out through the Eye-Gate in the top of the Great Gate, plunging into the untranslatable dark. They had even tampered with the Watch House instruments, sabotaging the recorders so that the desperate guards could broadcast nothing but dead, sterile silence.
