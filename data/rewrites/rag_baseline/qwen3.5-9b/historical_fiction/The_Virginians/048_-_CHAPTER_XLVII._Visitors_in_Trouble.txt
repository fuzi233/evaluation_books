The air in the lodging house tasted of stale beer and damp wool. Harry's second night was less violent than the first, a dull ache replacing the sharp sting of humiliation, but the hardness in his chest remained intact. He viewed the world through a lens of absolute rejection. His aunt, his friend March, his cousin Castlewood—they were all failures, selfish creatures who had stripped him bare. To beg them again would be to admit defeat, to acknowledge the lie of their friendship. There was no loyalty in this cold, selfish country. He would leave. He would trust no Englishman, great or small. Perhaps he would march with the King in Germany, or retreat to Virginia, bury himself in the woods until he became indistinguishable from the soil, hunting all day, tending tobacco fields, marrying Polly or Fanny, doing anything to exist outside the orbit of these fine gentlemen.

Morning broke with a cheerfulness that did not reach his eyes. Gumbo stood ready, having traveled from Bond Street to deliver letters. "I sought to bring more clothes," the servant said, his tone honest and deferential, "but Mr. Ruff, the landlord, refused entry."

Harry ignored the letters initially, opening them mechanically. Bills. More bills. The fourth revealed the landlord's ultimatum: no more goods from Mr. Warrington's estate would leave the premises until payment was made. If unpaid, the goods would be sold, and the black man would be expelled. Gumbo hesitated, clutching Harry's own trunk. "I found refuge elsewhere," Gumbo lied smoothly, "at Lord Wrotham's house. With Colonel Lambert's people."

Lambert. The name curdled Harry's blood. Hetty had fainted upon hearing of his arrest; Lambert, a decent man, had offered shelter. Yet Harry knew better. He translated Gumbo's polished narrative with skepticism, aware of the servant's tendency to embellish. But the mention of the Colonel extinguished any remaining warmth. "Send to Lambert?" Harry ground out, his teeth clenched. "The man who spat my gifts back in my face? If I starved, I would not ask him for a crust!"

Dressed, Harry summoned breakfast and dispatched Gumbo with a note to Mr. Draper in the Temple. The instruction was written with the haughty brevity reserved for inferiors, a tone Harry usually reserved for his own servants.

"The note reads as if addressed to a negro, not a freeborn English gentleman," Draper observed later, his voice dripping with suppressed rage. "For a man in a spunging-house! Hang him! I have a mind not to attend." Yet he went. He found Mr. Warrington seated on his bed like a monarch, draped in a splendid gown, his hair meticulously coiffed, commanding a chair for his attendant.

"Excuse me, madam," the outraged attorney muttered, addressing the Baroness who watched with amusement. "Such airs I am not accustomed to."

"Take a seat, Mr. Draper," the Baroness smiled, delighted by her nephew's insolence in adversity.

The arrangement between the Baroness and her lawyer had been set in stone the day prior. Draper was competent, usually effective, but today he was blinded by pride and confusion. How does one offend a non-gentleman without understanding the mechanics of the offense? The creature acts, provoking scorn; the creature feels the scorn and rages; the observer speaks naturally, and the creature interprets it as mockery. Indifference breeds hatred.

"Gumbo, fetch a chair for Mr. Draper!" Warrington commanded, wrapping his brocade robe around his legs. "Sit, if you please. We discuss my business. Thank you for your prompt response. Have you heard of this misfortune?"

Draper nodded. "News travels fast, Mr. Warrington. I was eager to offer my services. Your friends and family will be pained that a gentleman of your rank finds himself in such a position."

"I have been imprudent," Warrington admitted, bowing stiffly. "I lived beyond my means. I gambled with wealthier men, and a cursed streak of bad luck has drained my cash, leaving me liable for five hundred pounds and more."

"Five hundred currently outstanding," Draper noted.

"It is a trifling sum," Warrington insisted, staring intently at the lawyer. "I thought sending to friends yesterday would suffice. I was mistaken. Please assist me in raising the funds."

"Hm," Draper grunted, pulling a long, grave face.

"Why, sir, it can be done!" Warrington challenged.

It could be done. Indeed, Draper had already proposed to the Baroness immediate payment and release. She intended to make him her heir. Assuming, as everyone else did, that Harry's Virginia inheritance matched its geographical extent in monetary value, Draper possessed the necessary notes and the Baroness's conditional order. Yet, when Harry declared, "It can be done!", Draper's expression hardened. "It can be done in time, sir, but it requires duration. To access the property in England belonging to Mr. George Warrington, we must prove the event and release the trustees. Who will do this? Lady Esmond Warrington in Virginia will not permit her son to rot in prison, but we must await six months for correspondence. Does your Bristol agent hold authority to honor your drafts?"

"He is authorized for two hundred pounds annually," Warrington replied. "I suppose I have no recourse but my aunt, Madame de Bernstein. She will guarantee it."

"Your ladyship will do anything," Draper confirmed. "If she gives the word now, you walk out of here."

"Then go to her, Mr. Draper. I wished not to trouble my relations, but rather than endure this horrible, unnecessary imprisonment, I must speak to her. State my location and plight. Disguise nothing. Tell her I confide in her affection to release me from this... disgrace," Warrington's voice trembled, and he wiped his eyes.

"Sir," Draper countered, fixing the young man with a stare, "I was with her ladyship yesterday discussing this most unpleasant affair—I won't say, as you do, disgraceful."

"What do you mean? Does Madame de Bernstein know of my misfortune?" Harry asked.

"Every detail, sir. The pawning of the watches, everything."

Harry flushed crimson. "An unfortunate business," the lawyer continued, "pawning items you never purchased."

Harry sprang from the bed, his eyes fierce enough to alarm Draper. "This may lead to litigation and unpleasant courtroom remarks. Those barristers respect nothing. When they get a fellow in the box—"

"Great Heaven, sir, do you suppose a gentleman of my rank cannot borrow a watch on credit without intending to defraud the tradesman?" Harry cried, agitated.

"Of course you meant honorably," Draper winked, enjoying the sting. ("Hang the supercilious beast; I hit him there.") "Only the law may not perceive it so. Your aunt calls it the most imprudent act she has ever witnessed—to call it by no worse name."

"You call it by no worse name yourself, Mr. Draper?" Harry asked, slowing his speech, attempting to maintain control.

Draper disliked the look. "Heaven forbid I speak thus between gentlemen—but between client and advisor, it is my duty to say, 'Sir, you are in a very unpleasant scrape,' just as a doctor tells a patient, 'Sir, you are very ill.'"

"And you cannot help me pay this debt? You come only to accuse me of roguery?"

"Obtaining goods under false pretenses? Undoubtedly, yes. Do not look as if you intend to strike me. (Curse him, I make him wince.) A young gentleman with two hundred a year from his mother ordering diamonds and watches, taking them to a pawnbroker. What do people think of such behavior? I tell you honestly. Do not be angry, Mr. Warrington."

"Go on, sir!" Harry groaned.

Draper sensed victory. "Can I not help pay the debt? Yes—and here is the money in my pocket to settle it now, if you wish—not mine, sir, but your aunt's, Lady Bernstein's. However, she imposes conditions, which I have brought."

"State them, sir," Harry said.

"They are not harsh, merely for your own good. If you agree, we summon a hackney coach and proceed to Clarges Street, which I promised to visit regardless. Mr. Warrington, I need not name names, but a marriage question existed between you and a certain party."

"Ah!" Harry's face brightened.

"My noble client, the Baroness, is most averse to this union," Draper continued. "She holds other views, believing it your ruin to marry someone of noble birth and title, yet lacking first-rate character and significantly older than yourself. You gave an imprudent promise."

"Yes; and she retains it," Warrington stated.

"It has been recovered. She dropped it accidentally at Tunbridge," Draper explained. "Indeed, her ladyship showed it to me. Written in blue ink—"

"Never mind, sir!" Harry interrupted, turning red as the ink of his own foolish promise, which had smitten him with shame a thousand times.

"At the same time, letters compromising a noble family were recovered," the lawyer pressed. "You lost them. No fault of yours; you were absent when they were found. You may claim that noble family, and yourself, possess a friend rare among young men.
