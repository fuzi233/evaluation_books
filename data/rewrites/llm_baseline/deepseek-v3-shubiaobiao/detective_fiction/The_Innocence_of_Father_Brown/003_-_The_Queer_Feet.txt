If you ever encounter a member of the exclusive *Twelve True Fishermen* entering the Vernon Hotel for their annual dinner, you might notice—as he sheds his overcoat—that his evening coat is green rather than black. Should you dare to ask why (though such audacity is scarcely advisable), he would likely inform you that it prevents him from being mistaken for a waiter. With that, you would retreat, thoroughly humbled—but also leaving behind an unsolved riddle, and a tale most peculiar.

Now, if by some twist of fate you were to cross paths with an unassuming little priest named Father Brown and inquired about the strangest twist of luck in his career, he might reflect a moment before admitting that his finest hour occurred at the Vernon Hotel. There, by merely listening to the rhythm of footsteps in a corridor, he thwarted a crime and possibly salvaged a soul. He is, perhaps, quietly proud of this deduction—wild as it was. But since you are unlikely to ascend high enough to dine with the Fishermen or sink low enough to stumble upon Father Brown in some shadowed alley, you shall hear the story from me alone.

The Vernon Hotel was an establishment that could only flourish in a society teetering on the edge of aristocratic absurdity. It was an anomaly—a business that thrived not by welcoming patrons, but by repelling them. In the heart of London’s elite, its proprietors had mastered the art of being more fastidious than their clientele. They erected obstacles so that the wealthy might expend both coin and cunning to overcome them. Had there existed a hotel that admitted only men over six feet tall, society would have obediently assembled parties of towering gentlemen to dine within. The Vernon was such a place—small, inconvenient, and thus irresistible.

Its most sacred rule was that no more than twenty-four could dine at once. The crown jewel of the hotel was the terrace table, open to the garden—a privilege reserved for clement weather, making it all the more coveted. The owner, a shrewd Jew named Lever, had amassed a fortune by the simple philosophy that exclusivity breeds demand. The waiters were fewer than the guests—fifteen in total, each trained to move with the silent precision of a butler. To become one was harder than winning a seat in Parliament.

The Twelve True Fishermen would accept no lesser venue for their annual gathering. Their rituals were many, their purpose none—which was, of course, the height of aristocracy. One did not *become* a Fisherman; one either was or was not. Their president was Mr. Audley; their vice-president, the Duke of Chester.

Now, you may wonder how a humble priest like Father Brown came to tread the gilded halls of the Vernon. The answer is simple: duty. When a waiter—an Italian—was struck by a sudden paralysis, his employer, though baffled by such superstitious needs, permitted a priest to be summoned. Father Brown, ever unassuming, requested a room to compose a letter related to the man’s confession. Lever, torn between kindness and horror at this breach of decorum, grudgingly conceded—exiling the priest to a small, windowless chamber near the cloakroom.

As evening darkened, Father Brown’s ears caught an oddity—footsteps. Not the measured tread of waiters or the languid stride of gentlemen, but a bizarre alternation: a rapid scurry, then a slow, deliberate stamp, then another sprint. The rhythm nagged at him like a half-remembered tune.

Then, the steps changed. A run—swift, soft, like a panther’s bound—before halting abruptly into that same languid pace.

Curiosity now fully aflame, Father Brown slipped into the adjoining cloakroom. There, beneath the gaslight’s glow, stood a tall, elegant gentleman in plain evening wear. His face was swarthy, his manner amused. He tossed a coin to the priest, demanding his coat.

"I think, sir," said Father Brown mildly, "you have silver in your pocket."

The man stiffened. "What if I choose to give gold?"

"Because silver is sometimes more valuable," the priest replied. "Especially in quantity."

The stranger’s eyes flickered—toward the door, the window, then back to Brown. In an instant, he vaulted the counter, seizing the priest by the collar.

"Stand still," he hissed.

Father Brown did not flinch. "I would sooner threaten *you*," he rumbled, "with the worm that dieth not and the fire unquenchable."

The man froze. "You’re no cloakroom clerk."

"I am a priest," said Brown. "And I am ready to hear your confession, Monsieur Flambeau."

The thief—for it was he—reeled back, stricken.

Meanwhile, the Fishermen dined in blissful ignorance, savoring their sacred fish course—a monstrous, pearl-laden pudding—while outside the door, a far more remarkable catch had already been made.
