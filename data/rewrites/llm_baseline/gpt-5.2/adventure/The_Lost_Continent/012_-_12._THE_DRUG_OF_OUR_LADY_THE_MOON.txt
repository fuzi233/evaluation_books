Our Lord the Sun slid down toward the rim of His day, and the breath of a burning mountain unrolled before Him like a black banner. The air tasted of cinders and salt. Phorenice wrung her soaked tunic with quick, impatient hands; the water ran from the cloth in little streams, and she shivered as the evening cool crept up out of the lagoon.

“Work those paddles, Deucalion,” she said, her voice sharp as the snap of a knife on bone. “Take me in through the water-gate and let me be restored to comfort. That merchant would howl to see what his pretty garments have come to—and I howl too, being a woman and knowing he has no others I may seize in place of these.” She tossed back her short red hair, wet curls clinging to her brow. “What think you of my wisdom in coming where we came without escort?”

“The Empress can do no wrong,” I said, giving her the old answer with a smile.

“At least I have shown you I can fight.” Her eyes cut sidelong to me, bright as a coal in ash. “I caught you looking your approval once or twice. You were a difficult man to thaw, Deucalion, but you warm perceptibly the longer you are near me. La, sir, we shall be rustic sweethearts yet, if this goes on. I am glad I thought of the device of going near those smelly fishers.”

So the excursion, the lack of guards, the very choosing of that reeking strip of shore where fish-guts lay in windrows—this had been her bait, set not for rebels alone but for me: a fight to watch how I stood in it, and a chance to display her own skill as one sets a blade in lamplight to show its temper. If we must live together as husband and wife, it was well each should measure the other’s courage and steadiness.

Yet I could not keep my thoughts from straying to another woman, and to another choice made with a similar opening. Nais, with pity for squalid folk who did not even call her chieftain, had robbed me of struggle. Phorenice here had thrown away two score of hardy subjects for the mere pleasure of proving herself and pricking at my regard.

But I was bound by vow and council, and I forced my mind back to duty. As I rowed us over the heaving swells toward the forts which guarded the harbour mouth, I sent prayers up to the High Gods for dexterity of speech. They granted it. Words came smooth as oil, and Phorenice drank them with glowing eyes, as if my talk were some spiced broth warming her from the inside.

The fishers’ beach was safe from ships beyond, for a chain of jagged rocks ran from harbour side to the city wall like a saw laid flat beneath the waves. The fishers knew the passes; they slipped through, and if they misjudged, they leapt out and bore their light boats over the stones in their hands until the water took them again. But I had neither their knowledge nor their nimbleness. I felt the reef waiting—patient as a trap.

Now, I thought, the High Gods will show plainly whether They mean this woman—who has defied Them—to reign on; and whether They mean me to serve as her husband.

I held the prayer tight in my heart and waited for the omen.

It came without half-measure. A great wave rose behind us in the lagoon, not born of wind but of earth—a surge that could only have been sent by tremor. On its sleek back we were hurled forward, flung clean through the barrier where seaweed licked and combed the stones, and we passed without so much as scraping a plank.

I bowed my head and rowed on. Not yet, then, would vengeance fall.

The sentries in the two forts beat drums at each other in their accustomed calls, a steady pattern that stitched the evening together. In the thickening dusk they would have given little heed to a fishing-boat rattling at the great chain and shouting for passage. But two officers were taking the air atop the nearer fort, in a turret where stones were kept ready to drop on attackers, and they caught the tone of our voices. The drums were silenced; torches dipped down on cords until their smoke and resinous stink came to us with the glow, and faces were made certain. Then with a splash the chain dropped, and we slid through.

Inside lay a galley nosing the harbour wall. A rope ladder was thrown down from the fort roof, and men came clambering to take the oars. The officers sent gifts too—changes of raiment—plain, serviceable cloth that smelled of storage and old cedar rather than any palace perfume. In the cramped cabin we stripped off our sodden finery, the wet cloth sucking at skin like a leech. Fevers breed in damp after sunset; though I knew drugs that could ward them, I noted with grim amusement how the Empress—Goddess in proclamation—feared the small, mortal coughs and chills of common flesh.

The galley cut swiftly across the calm inner water and made fast to the gold rings on the royal quay. We waited for litters. While we waited, I watched a lantern glow in the guard-boat near Phorenice’s mammoth. The huge red beast stood shoulder-deep in the harbour, trunk curled upward, tame now, sending ripples out with each slow tremble. The light made its wet hide shine like fresh blood on stone.

I did not ask mercy for it. If the harbour mud swallowed it, I could have borne that loss with an easy mind.

To tell truth, the ride on its back had left me with a helplessness that scraped at pride. I have walked on my own feet across four continents; on my own feet I can guard my head and neck and set my weight where I choose. On the back of that detestable beast—pah!—I was as nervous as a child, perched high and powerless, tossed by each lurch like a sack of grain.

But there was no leisure for such private loathing. Phorenice, while we waited, turned on the port-captain—who came bustling up with officious salutations—and asked after Nais. He told her the girl had been clapped into a dungeon beneath the royal pyramid, and the officer of the guard had given bond for her safe-keeping.

“It is to be hoped he understands his work,” Phorenice said. “That pretty Nais knows the pyramid better than most. He may find himself sent to the tormentors for choosing a cell with a secret outlet. You would feel pleasure if the girl escaped, Deucalion?”

“Assuredly,” I said, for there was no profit in pretending. “I have no enmity against Nais.”

“But I have,” she snapped, and the small cruelty in her made itself plain, “and I am still minded to lock your faith to me by that wedding gift you know of.”

“The thing shall be done,” I said. “Before all, the Empress of Atlantis.”

“Poof! You are too stiff and formal.” She thrust her hand at me for the litter, and when I helped her in, she tugged me beside her. “Come in, keep me warm. Ho! you guards with the torches—keep farther back against the street walls. The perfume you burn stifles me.”

Again there was a feast in the royal banqueting-hall; again I sat beside Phorenice on the raised dais beneath the snake and the outstretched hand. This time what had been assumed before was proclaimed openly: Phorenice acknowledged me as her husband that was to be. The court—curled, jeweled, glittering like a heap of bright fish in a net—hailed me as greater than themselves by reason of her choosing.

Their homage was not the new, impertinent drinking of wine to my health. Rumour had reached them of my preference for older forms. So each lord and lady approached and, with decorum, spilled a goblet at my feet—wine dark as pomegranate syrup staining the stone. And when I called any up, man or woman, to receive tit-bits from my platter, they ate simply and thankfully instead of kissing the morsels or hiding them away as charms.

The flaring jets of earth-breath, hissing and glaring, showed me something else: a plainer habit of dress and a soberer mien among these thoughtless diners. No heavy clouds of scent fought the air tonight; the very hall sounded different without that languid courtly cooing that perfumes seem to invite. The scrape of sandals, the clink of metal, the low talk—each noise came clean, not muffled under sweet smoke.

Phorenice leaned close until the ruddy curls at her shoulder brushed my face. “Oh, sir,” she whispered, mock-pleading, “do not make your rule over us too ascetic. I have given no orders for this change, but tonight there are no perfumes in the air; the food is so plain I have half a mind to burn the cook; and as for the clothes and gauds of these diners—by my face!—they might have come straight from the old King’s reign before I stepped in here to show how tasteful could be colours on a robe, or how pretty the glint of a jewel. It’s done by no order of mine. They’ve swung round by sheer courtier instinct. Why, look at the beards of the men—there is not half the curl about them to-day that they showed yesterday. By my face! I believe they’d reap their chins as smooth as yours tomorrow if you go on setting fashions at this prodigious rate and I do not interfere.”

“Why hinder them if they feel more cleanly shaven?” I asked.

“No, sir. There shall be only one clean chin where a beard can grow in all Atlantis, and that shall be carried by the man who is husband to the Empress. Would you have no sumptuary laws? Would you have these folk here and the common people outside imitate us in every cut of hair and fold of garment we discover? If you and I chose to mark sovereignty only by superior strength of arm and wit, they would hate us for arrogance; but if we keep apart a few personal decorations, these become objects to admire and envy pleasantly.”

“You show me there is more in rule than meets the eye.”

“And yet they tell me—and show me—you have ruled with some success.”

“I employed the older method. It requires a Phorenice to invent these nicer flights.”

“Flatterer!” She smote my arm with the backs of her fingers. “You are becoming as great a courtier as any of them. You make me blush, Deucalion, and there is no fan-girl here tonight to cool my cheek. I must choose another. But it shall not be Ylga. Ylga seems to have more kindness for you than I like. If she is wise she will go live in her palace at the far side of the city and occupy herself with ordering slaves and making embroideries. I shall not be hard on Ylga unless she forces me, but I will have no woman in this kingdom treat you with undue civility.”

“And how am I to act,” I said, keeping to her mood, “when I see and hear all the men of Atlantis making their protestations before you? By your own confession they all love you as ardently as they loved you hopelessly.”

“You must not ask impossibilities. I have power if you will, but no force that governs hearts. Yet if you choose, make proclamation that I am given now body and inwards to you, and if they continue to offend your pride you may take culprits and give them to the tormentors. Indeed, Deucalion, I think it would be a pretty attention to me if you arranged such a ceremony. It seems to me,” she added with a frown, “that the jealousy is too much on one side.”

“You must not expect a man divorced from love for all a busy life to learn its niceties in an instant. I was proud of my progress. With any other schoolmistress than you, Phorenice, I should not be near so forward. Indeed—if one may judge by my past record—I should not have begun at all.”

“I suppose you think I should be satisfied? Well, I am not. I can be finely greedy over some matters.”

The banquet did not stretch to inordinate length. Phorenice had gone through much since last she slept; though she had declared herself Goddess, her body still dragged like any mortal’s. Black rings lay under her wondrous eyes. She sank back among the cushions, limbs slackened, the edge gone out of her bright cruelty for the moment. When dancers came and postured, she flung them a jewel and sent them away before they had given half their show. A poet—one of those swelling fellows who would have poured out an ocean of words—she would not hear at all.

“To-morrow,” she said. “But now grant me peace. My Lord Deucalion has given me much food for thought, and presently I go to my chamber to muse on the future policies of this State throughout the night. To-morrow come to me again, and if your poetry is good and short I will pay you surprisingly. But if there are superfluous words, I will pay you for those with the stick.”

She rose then. The hall murmured and bowed as one creature. I led her away from the banqueting-hall and down passages where secret doors lay cunningly masked in stone. She clung to my arm; once, while we waited for a great block to swing slowly aside, she drooped her head against my shoulder. Her breath came warm against my cheek. Her face, close at hand, was loveliness beyond the reach of speech.

I think she expected me to kiss her red lips held so near. Willing though I was to play the part appointed, I could not bring myself to it. When the stone at last moved, she drew away with a sigh, and we went on without further word.

“May the High Gods treat you tenderly,” I said when we reached her bed-chamber door.

“I am my own God,” she said, “in all things but one. By my face! you are a tardy wooer, Deucalion. Where go you now?”

“To my own chamber.”

“Oh, go then. Go.”

“Is there anything more I could do?”

“Nothing your wit or your will would prompt you to. Yes, you are finely decorous in your old-fashioned way, but you are a mighty poor wooer. Don’t you know a woman esteems some things more highly if they are taken from her by rude force?”

“It seems I know little enough about women.”

“You never said a truer word. Bah! And I believe your coldness brings you more benefit in a certain matter than any show of passion could earn. There—get you gone, if the atmosphere of a maiden’s bed-chamber hurts your rustic modesty, and your Gods keep you—if that’s the phrase, and if you think They can do it. Get you gone, man, and leave me solitary.”

I had taken the plan of the pyramid out of the archives before the banquet and learned it to the last turn and blind corner. I was wearied as a man is wearied who has been kept taut too long, but I dared not spend time on sleep. Nais must be sacrificed in part for Atlantis; yet a plan had come to me by which she need not be sacrificed wholly. To carry it through needed speed and exactness while night still held.

Help came from a quarter I did not expect.

As I threaded the angular maze between the ponderous stones toward the apartments Phorenice had assigned me, a woman glided from a side passage like smoke slipping from a crack. I lifted my hand-lamp. The light caught her face: Ylga.

She looked at me half-sullenly. “I have lost my place,” she said. “It seems I need never have spoken. She meant to have you all along, and no warning of mine could turn her. And you—you think me officious, if you have given me a thought at all since.”

“I never forget a kindness.”

“Oh, you will learn that trick soon enough. And you are going to marry her—you!” Bitterness pinched her words. “The city is ringing with it. I thought at least you were honest, but when a high place can be got by taking a woman with it, you are like the rest. I thought too you would be one of those men who distrust ruddy hair. And besides—she is little.”

“Ylga,” I said quietly, “you have taught me these walls are full of crannies and ears. I will listen to no word against Phorenice. But I would have further converse with you soon. If you still have a kindness for me, go to the chamber that is mine and wait there. I will join you shortly.”

Her eyes dropped. “What do you want of me, Deucalion?”

“I want to say something. You will learn who it concerns later.”

“But is it—is it fitting for a maiden to come to a man’s room at this hour?”

“I know little of your conventions in this new Atlantis. I am Deucalion, girl, and if you still have qualms remembering that, do not come.”

She looked up with a sneer. “I was foolish. My lord’s coldness has grown into a proverb, and I should have remembered it. Yes. I will come.”

“Go now, then,” I said.

I waited until her steps had faded and the passages held only the low, living sounds of stone—distant water seeping, a faint draught moaning through unseen joints. With Ylga’s help my tasks would be lightened, but first I must do the work that required no witness.

I made my way, with as little delay and show as possible, toward a certain sanctuary within the temple of our Lady the Moon. Here my knowledge as one of the Seven served me well.

All the temples of Atlantis lay in immediate and secret connection with the royal pyramid. The ways were little used; only the Seven knew them, and the Three above them—if, indeed, there lived at one time three men learned enough in the highest mysteries to stand in that sublime degree. Even then the secret alleys might be trodden only at moments of greatest stress, so that a full generation might pass without a human foot stirring the dust there.

With no little groping and experiment I found the hidden entrance; but once inside, memory guided me. Though I had never walked it, its windings had been taught at my initiation. I went quickly. The air in those deep cracks of stone held lurking evils—stale damp, a sourness like old breath trapped in cloth. And the hours were fleeing. Much must be done before our Lord the Sun rose again.

I came to the spy-place that commanded the temple. The holy space beyond lay empty, dust-covered, as if the very sounds of prayer had died out and left only silence to settle. A vast stone in the wall swung outward and gave me entrance. After the solemn prayer required before such deeds, I took the metal stair from where it is kept, set it firm, and climbed to the lap of the Goddess. Then, pulling the stair after me, I climbed again until my length lay against her calm, mysterious face.

A shiver seized me. Even a warrior hardened to ugly sights may quail when called to juggle with life and death, with years and history, holding the welfare of a country in one hand and the future of a woman who is as life to him in the other. But the hours flew. I laid hold of the jewel studded in the forehead of the image with one hand, and with the other thrust at a corner of the eyebrow.

With a faint creak the massive eyeball below—a stone I could barely have covered with my back—swung inward. I stepped off the stair, climbed into the gap, and found myself in the chamber hollowed from the head of the Goddess.

It was the first time my eyes had seen this most secret place. Yet its aspect was familiar from teaching, and I knew where to find what I needed. Even so, I could not help a moment’s wonder at the craft of its concealment.

High as I was in priestly learning, the structure of what I had come to fetch was hidden from me. I knew only power and effect. There, in the hidden niche, lay roughly rounded balls like nut-kernels, grass-green, hard as bees’ wax. Three of them. I took the one I needed and left the others concealed as I found them. Whether it was drug or something more, I did not know. But I was sure of what it would do, for that was set forth plainly in the teaching. I slipped it into a pouch of my garment, returned by the way I had come, and replaced all things in due order.

Before leaving the temple I looked once more at the image of the Goddess. The jet of earth-breath burned eternally from the central altar, hissing as it flared, lighting her from head to toe and throwing sparks from the great jewel in her forehead. Vast she was, calm beyond human imagining—a symbol of rest and quietness, that deep hush people hunger for and rarely find in this rude world, unless by piety they earn a place where our Lady the Moon and the High Ones reign in eternal majesty.

With tired, dragging limbs I made my way back to the royal pyramid and at last reached my own private chamber.

Ylga awaited me there, though at first I did not see her. The suspicions of these days had bitten deep; she crouched in hiding until she was sure it was I who entered, and that I came alone.

“Oh, frown at me if you choose,” she said sullenly, coming out at last. “I am past caring for your good opinion. I had heard so much of Deucalion, and thought I read honesty in you when first you came ashore. But now I know you are no better than the rest. Phorenice offers you a high place, and you marry her blithely to get it. And why should you not? People say she is pretty, and I know she can be warm. I have seen her warm and languishing to scores of men. She is clever too with her eyes, our great Empress. And as for you—it tickles you to be courted.”

“I think you are a very silly woman,” I said.

“If you flatter yourself it matters a rap to me whom you marry, you are letting conceit run away with you.”

“Listen,” I said. “I did not ask you here to make foolish speeches which are largely beyond my comprehension. I asked you to help me do a service to one of your own blood-kin.”

She stared, the sulk in her face shifting into wonder. “I do not understand.”

“It rests largely with you,” I said, “whether Nais dies to-morrow, or whether she is thrown into a sleep from which she may waken on some later and more happy day.”
