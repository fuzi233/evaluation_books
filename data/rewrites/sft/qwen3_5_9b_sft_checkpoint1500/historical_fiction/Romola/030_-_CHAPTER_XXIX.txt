"The old fellow has vanished," Nello observed, his tone casual yet tinged with curiosity, "went on towards Arezzo the next morning. I suspect he couldn't stand the stench of the French after being their prisoner. I stopped by the hospital to ask if those monkish cooks had discovered whether he'd lost his wits. They said he was perfectly sane, merely aloof, staring at a vineyard twenty miles away as if it grew on his wall. He was a mysterious old tiger. I've always wished to know more about him."

These words were spoken in Nello's shop on the twenty-fourth of November, a mere week after the French entry. The air hummed with anticipation; Florence braced itself for a decisive political shift. Every public square teemed with idlers, and every shopkeeper without a deputy stood guard at his threshold, thumbs hooked in belts, watching the street like cats waiting for a mouse. Artisans drifted past like fallen leaves, ready to bolt at the slightest stir.

Inside, Nello strummed a lute, seated casually on a wooden board near the window, his gaze fixed on the piazza.

"Ah," he declared, setting down his instrument with a flourish, "I would not trade the sight of those French soldiers lumbering in their broad boots after their fleeing prisoners for a gold florin. It happens whenever I leave this central hub to shave some magnificently chin-furnished gentleman. If I step out, chaos ensues in my piazza."

"You should have been there," Piero di Cosimo interjected, his voice sharp as a scalpel, "to witness your favorite Greek trembling as if Satanasso had seized him by the throat. I delight in seeing your perpetually smiling Messeri stripped bare by a sudden gale. Consider the color of a man's liver when he resembles a bleached deer the moment a stranger grabs him."

"Piero, save your vinegar for your own omelets!" Nello retorted warmly. "What fault does my *bel erudito* bear for starting when he felt claws upon him and saw a raving madman beside him? Your scholar is not like those brutish Swiss and Germans, whose minds are built for battering-rams and stomachs big enough to swallow a cannonball before dawn. We Florentines value far more than that crude notion of bravery, which amounts to hiring dullards by the dozen. Once men realized they possessed more brains than oxen, they yoked the beasts to pull for them; and when we Florentines discovered our superior intellects, we employed them to fight for us."

"Treason, Nello!" a voice cried from within the shop, where Francesco Cei leaned forward eagerly. "That contradicts the State's doctrine! Florence is forging weapons, and the last authenticated vision from the Friar depicted Mars standing atop the Palazzo Vecchio, resting his hand on Saint John the Baptist's shoulder, who offered him honeycomb."

"It is well, Francesco," Nello replied smoothly. "Florence possesses several thick-skulled individuals capable of bombarding Pisa, while the finer spirits remain at home to think and shave. As for our Piero, if he prizes valor so highly, let him take his largest brush as a spear and his palette as a shield, challenging the widest-mouthed Swiss he encounters in the Prato to single combat."

"*Va*, Nello," Piero grumbled, "your tongue rolls like a mill driven by a swollen Arno, regardless of whether there is grain to grind."

"Plentiful grain, I assure thee," Nello countered. "It would be as absurd to expect a weather-beaten painter like thee to crave a javelin thrust as it is to anticipate a man whose wit has been honed by classical studies enjoying his handsome visage torn by a wild beast."

"There you go again, assuming folks will march into sacks because you label them 'hosen'," Piero shot back. "Who mentioned a wild beast or an unarmed man charging into battle? Fighting is a trade, not mine. I might avoid danger, but I could face it if it sought me out."

"Why do you fear the thunder, then, my Piero?" Nello pressed, eager to corner the accuser. "One understands why a man trembles at trifles that shake others less—you hide with the rats the moment a storm brews."

"That stems from a specific sensitivity to loud noises; it reflects neither my courage nor my conscience."

"And Tito Melema may share a peculiar sensitivity to being seized unexpectedly by prisoners escaping French guards. Men are born with aversions; I cannot abide the scent of mint. Tito was born with an antipathy toward elderly prisoners who stumble and clutch. *Ecco!*"

A ripple of laughter swept through the room, revealing that Piero's dislike for Tito held no sway among his companions. The painter, contorting his features into an unreadable grimace, stuffed tow into his ears in a gesture of indignant disdain, while Nello continued triumphantly.

"No, my Piero, I cannot afford to disparage my *bel erudito*; nor can Florence, with her scholars shedding feathers at the tender age of forty. Our Phoenix Pico ascended straight to Paradise, as the Friar informs us; and the incomparable Poliziano departed but two months ago—well, let us hope he joins the eminent scholars of the Malebolge rather than descending into hell."

"Intriguingly," interjected Francesco Cei, "have you heard that Camilla Rucellai has surpassed the Friar in prophecy? Two years ago she foretold Pico would die in the season of lilies. He passed in November. 'Not the time of lilies,' scoffed the critics. 'Go to!' replies Camilla; 'I meant the lilies of France, and lo, they lie close beneath your noses.' I say, *Euge, Camilla!* If the Friar can verify that any vision of his has been fulfilled with such precision, I shall declare myself a Piagnone tomorrow."

"You are somewhat too flippant regarding the Friar, Francesco," observed Pietro Cennini, the scholar. "In these turbulent weeks, we are all indebted to him for preaching peace and quelling factional strife. Those who wish to see the populace slip the Friar's leash now possess little discernment. Should the Most Christian King stubbornly refuse to sign a fair and honorable treaty today, Fra Girolamo is the man we must trust to bring him to reason."

"You speak truly, Messer Pietro," Nello agreed. "The Friar stands as one of Florence's firmest anchors—at least, according to the most respectable chins I have the honor of shaving. Yet, young Messer Niccolò remarked not long ago, and perhaps Francesco implies the same, that meanings in visions stretch as magically as Dido's bull-hide. To me, a dream signifies whatever follows it. As Franco Sacchetti noted, a woman dreams of a serpent biting her, smashes a drinking cup the next day, and cries, 'Lo! I thought something would happen—it is plain now what the serpent meant.'"

"But the Friar's visions differ from that sort," corrected Cronaca. "He does not merely predict what will occur—that the Church will be scourged and renewed, and the heathens converted—but declares it shall happen swiftly. He is no slippery charmer crafting loopholes; he is—"

"What is this? What is this?" Nello shouted, leaping from his perch and thrusting his head through the doorway. "People are flooding the piazza, screaming. Some catastrophe must have unfolded in the Via Larga."

"Aha!" Nello burst out, stepping outside laughing and waving his cap. Delight surged through him.

The entire company rushed to the door. News from the Via Larga was exactly what they awaited, though the manner of its arrival left them stunned. Seated above the shoulders of the crowd on a bench seemingly plucked from the street sat Tito Melema, smiling with amused detachment at the coercion he endured. His cap had slid off, dangling by the *becchetto* loosely wound around his neck. Upon spotting the group at Nello's door, he raised his fingers in beckoning recognition. Within a heartbeat, he leaped from the bench onto a cart laden with bales stationed in the open space between the Baptistery and the Duomo's steps, while the populace swarmed around him with the noisy fervor of chickens awaiting feed.

Yet, silence fell instantly when he began to speak in his clear, mellifluous voice.

"Citizens of Florence! I possess no warrant to proclaim news save your own will. But the tidings are good and shall harm none in recounting them. The Most Christian King is signing a treaty honorable to Florence. Yet you owe this boon to one of your citizens, who uttered a phrase worthy of ancient Rome—you owe it to Piero Capponi!"

Instantly, a roar of voices erupted. "Capponi! Capponi! What did our Piero say?" "Ah! He refused to be sent from Herod to Pilate!" "We knew Piero!" "*Orsù*! Tell us, what did he say?"

As the clamor subsided slightly, Tito resumed.

"The Most Christian King demanded a trifle too much—was obstinate—and finally declared, 'I shall order my trumpets to sound.' Then, Florentine citizens! Your Piero Capponi, speaking with the voice of a free city, proclaimed, 'If you sound your trumpets, we will ring our bells!' He snatched the parchment detailing dishonorable conditions from the secretary's hand, tore it into fragments, and turned to depart the royal presence."
