Mrs. Dashwood listened with deep emotion as the truth about Willoughby was revealed. She was relieved to learn that some of the accusations against him were not entirely true—she felt sorrow for the man who had once been her daughter’s beloved, and she hoped he might find peace in the future. But no amount of explanation could undo the past. The trust between Marianne and Willoughby was gone, shattered beyond repair. His actions—especially toward Eliza Williams—had left wounds too deep to heal. No words could erase what Marianne had suffered, nor could they restore the purity of his character in her eyes. And so, despite any sympathy she might feel, Mrs. Dashwood knew that Willoughby could never regain Marianne’s love or threaten Colonel Brandon’s place in her heart.

Had Mrs. Dashwood heard Willoughby’s story directly from him—had she seen the pain in his face, felt the weight of his remorse—her compassion might have been stronger. But Elinor, though moved by the tale, had chosen not to dramatize it. Her own judgment, shaped by time and reflection, had cooled the passion of earlier belief. She wanted only to speak plainly, without embellishment, to give an honest account of what had happened—not to stir emotions that might mislead.

That evening, when the three women sat together, Marianne spoke of Willoughby again—though it was clear she struggled to do so. Her hands trembled, her voice faltered, and a flush crept across her cheeks. Yet she pressed on.

“I wish to assure you both,” she said, “that I understand everything—as fully as you could desire.”

Mrs. Dashwood reached out instinctively to comfort her, but Elinor silently stopped her with a glance. She wanted to hear her sister’s thoughts unfiltered, untouched by maternal tenderness.

Marianne continued slowly, “It is a relief—to know what Elinor told me this morning. I’ve heard exactly what I needed to hear.” For a moment, she paused, as if overwhelmed. Then, steadier than before, she added, “I am now perfectly satisfied. I want nothing to change. I could never have been happy with him—after knowing what I now know. I would have lacked confidence, respect… nothing could have erased that.”

“Oh, I know it!” Mrs. Dashwood exclaimed. “Happy with a man whose conduct has brought such pain? A man who harmed one of our dearest friends—a noble, gentle soul like Colonel Brandon? No, my dear Marianne—your tender heart would never have found joy in such a union. Your conscience would have known every wrong he committed, just as it should.”

Marianne sighed softly. “I wish for no change.”

Elinor stepped in gently. “You see the matter clearly—just as reason and integrity must lead one to see it. And I believe you can see, as I do, that marrying him would have brought endless troubles. His affection, even if real at first, was far from certain. And his lifestyle—he spends freely, even admitting it himself. Self-denial is foreign to him. With your innocence and a small income, such a marriage would have meant constant hardship. You would have tried to live frugally, to manage wisely—but how much could one woman do to stop ruin already set in motion?”

Her lips quivered. “Selfish?” she whispered, almost in disbelief.

“His entire behavior,” Elinor said quietly, “has been driven by selfishness. It began when he toyed with your affections. Then, when he truly loved you, he delayed telling you. And finally, he left Barton without a word—because it suited him. His own comfort, his own ease—those were always his priorities.”

“It is true,” Marianne murmured. “My happiness was never his concern.”

“And now,” Elinor continued, “he regrets what he’s done. But why? Because it hasn’t made him happy. He’s free of financial worries now, and he finds himself alone—yet he blames only the temper of his wife, not the choices that led him here. But does that mean he would have been happy with you? No. He would have faced poverty, constant strain. He’d have had a kind wife—no complaints there—but he’d have lived in want. And soon enough, he’d realize that a secure home, a good fortune, matters more to lasting contentment than even the most gentle disposition.”

“I believe it,” Marianne said. “And I have nothing to regret—only my own foolishness.”

“Rather say your mother’s imprudence,” Mrs. Dashwood replied gently. “She bears the blame.”

But Marianne would not let her go further. Elinor, seeing that both women recognized their own mistakes, chose not to dwell on the past. Instead, she returned to her original thought: “One truth stands out above all—the root of Willoughby’s downfall was his first sin: his treatment of Eliza Williams. That act poisoned everything after. Every mistake, every regret, stems from that beginning.”

Marianne nodded, deeply moved. And Mrs. Dashwood, stirred by the insight, turned to speak warmly of Colonel Brandon—his kindness, his patience, his quiet dignity. But Marianne seemed distant, lost in her own thoughts.

In the days that followed, Elinor noticed that Marianne did not improve as expected. Though she still tried to appear cheerful, her strength waned. Yet her spirit remained firm, and Elinor trusted that time would bring healing.

Margaret returned, and the family was reunited once more, settling back into the quiet rhythm of life at the cottage. Their studies were not yet as intense as before, but they planned to renew them soon.

But Elinor’s mind wandered often to Edward. Since leaving London, she had heard nothing of him—no word of his plans, no clue to where he might be. Only one letter from her brother mentioned him, briefly: “We know nothing of our unfortunate Edward, and can make no enquiries on so prohibited a subject, but conclude him to be still at Oxford.” That was all.

Then, one morning, the family’s servant returned from Exeter. After answering his mistress’s questions, he offered a startling piece of news:

“I suppose you know, ma'am, that Mr. Ferrars is married.”

Marianne gasped, her eyes flying to Elinor’s face. She saw her sister turn pale—and then collapsed into hysterics.

Mrs. Dashwood, who had glanced over at the same moment, saw the depth of Elinor’s distress. She was torn between the two daughters—one broken with grief, the other consumed by silent agony.

The servant, sensing trouble, called a maid. Together, they helped Marianne to another room. By then, she was slightly calmer. Mrs. Dashwood stayed with her, leaving Elinor to question Thomas.

“Who told you Mr. Ferrars was married?” she asked.

“I saw him myself this morning in Exeter—with his lady, Miss Steele,” he replied. “They were stopping at the New London Inn. I was going there with a message from Sally at the Park to her brother, who works as a post-boy. As I passed, I looked up and saw her—Miss Steele, I mean. I took off my hat, and she recognized me. She called out, asked after you, especially Miss Marianne, and said Mr. Ferrars and she sent their best wishes. They’re sorry they couldn’t come to visit, but they’re in a hurry to go farther down. They’ll return soon, and then they’ll definitely call.”

“Did she say she was married?” Elinor asked.

“Yes, ma'am. She smiled and said she’d changed her name since last being here. Always a pleasant girl, very polite. So I wished her joy.”

“Was Mr. Ferrars in the carriage?”

“Yes, ma'am. I saw him leaning back, but he didn’t look up. He never was much for talking.”

“Was anyone else with them?”

“No, ma'am—just the two.”

“Where did they come from?”

“They came straight from town,” Thomas said. “Mrs. Ferrars told me.”

“And are they going westward?”

“Yes, ma'am—but not staying long. They’ll be back soon, and then they’ll surely visit.”

Mrs. Dashwood looked at Elinor. But Elinor knew better than to hope. She recognized Lucy’s voice in every detail—the careful wording, the false cheer. She was confident Edward would never come near them. In a low voice, she told her mother, “They’re likely going to Mr. Pratt’s near Plymouth.”

Thomas had finished speaking. Elinor looked as if she wanted more.

“Did you see them leave?” she asked.

“No, ma'am. The horses were coming out, but I had to hurry back—I didn’t want to be late.”

“Did Mrs. Ferrars look well?”

“Yes, ma'am. She said she was fine. And to my mind, she’s always been a handsome young lady. She seemed very happy.”

Mrs. Dashwood had no more questions. Thomas was dismissed, and the table cleared.

Marianne had already sent word she wouldn’t eat. Mrs. Dashwood and Elinor had no appetite either. Margaret, watching them, felt fortunate that, despite all the recent trials, she had never been forced to skip dinner before.

After dessert and wine were arranged, the two older sisters sat in silence, each lost in thought. Mrs. Dashwood dared not speak. She realized now that she had misunderstood Elinor’s feelings. She had believed her daughter’s attachment to Edward was weaker—because Elinor had kept her pain hidden, guarded by duty and compassion for Marianne. Now she understood: Elinor had suffered deeply, quietly, with greater courage than she had ever imagined.

And Mrs. Dashwood felt a pang of guilt. She had focused so completely on Marianne’s sorrow that she had neglected her other daughter—whose heart had been breaking just as much, though in silence.
