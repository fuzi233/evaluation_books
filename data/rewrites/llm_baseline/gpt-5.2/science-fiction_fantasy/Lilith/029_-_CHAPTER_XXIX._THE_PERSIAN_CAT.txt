I sat with my hands fallen useless in my lap, as if shame had weight enough to pin my wrists to my knees. The room was still, yet not quiet: the house had its own breath, a low timber-creak, the faint chime of cooling coals, and beyond the walls the wind worrying at leaves like fingers testing old paper. Silence, I found, is not the absence of sound, but a kind of listening that admits everything.

Mr. Raven sat opposite—no, the librarian sat opposite, lean and dark-eyed, with that severe patience of one who has watched too many creatures make the same error and call it new. When he spoke, his voice did not trample the air; it went through it as a well-tuned bow through string.

“What I said is true,” he resumed. “You have not been a wise neighbour to the Little Ones.”

It was exactly the sort of rebuke that does not flare and die like anger, but stays, like salt in a cut, and will not let you forget the shape of the wound. I could not deny it.

“You wronged,” he went on, “at the same time the stupid creatures themselves. For them slavery would have been progress. To them a few such lessons as you could have given them—with a stick from one of their own trees—would have been invaluable.”

A gust breathed down the chimney. The fire answered in soft clicks and a brief, irritated hiss, like fat meeting a hot pan. I thought of the Little Ones—how they had cowered and giggled and run, and how I had taken their fear for harmlessness, their laughter for safety.

“I did not know they were cowards,” I muttered.

“What difference does that make? The man who grounds his action on another’s cowardice, is essentially a coward himself. I fear worse will come of it. By this time the Little Ones might have been able to protect themselves from the princess—not to say the giants; they were always fit enough for that. As it was they laughed at them. But now, through your relations with her—”

“I hate her!” The words broke out of me, raw as a bitten tongue.

“Did you let her know you hated her?”

The question found the exact place I had tried to mortar over with excuses. I made no answer.

“Not even to her have you been faithful.” He lifted a hand as if to close a book on the matter, then stopped, head tilted. “But hush! We were followed from the fountain, I fear!”

“No living creature did I see—except a disreputable-looking cat that bolted into the shrubbery.”

“It was a magnificent Persian—so wet and draggled, though, as to look what she was—worse than disreputable!”

His words caught my mind as a hook catches cloth. A new horror tightened, not in the chest alone but in the throat, as if my own breath were suddenly a stranger.

“What do you mean, Mr. Raven?” I whispered. “There was a beautiful blue Persian about the house, but she fled at the very sound of water. Could she have been after the goldfish?”

“We shall see,” he returned. “I know a little about cats of several sorts, and there is that in the room which will unmask this one, or I am mistaken in her.”

He rose with the deliberate economy of an old man who has learned to conserve strength for the necessary moments. From the closet he brought the mutilated volume I had seen before—yet as he turned and came back to his chair, it was no mutilation he carried, but a whole book, entire and sound, the vellum cover unbroken, the pages thick as dried leaves.

I stared until my eyes ached.

“Where was the other half of it?” I managed.

“Sticking through into my library,” he answered, as if the passing of a book through worlds were no stranger than a ladle through steam. He sat down beside me again. I had questions—an ocean of them—but the edge of something nearer than curiosity pressed against my skin, and I held my peace. One more question might have been a step into a dark place with no time left to climb out.

“Listen,” he said. “I am going to read a stanza or two. There is one present who, I imagine, will hardly enjoy the reading!”

He opened the vellum, turned leaves with a care that reminded me of handling saffron threads—touch too roughly and you lose what you meant to use. The parchment smelled faintly of animal skin and dust, but also of something stranger, like rain caught in wool and kept too long in a chest. One leaf showed a dark stain, old as dried blood in a seam. He turned it and searched as if following a remembered path through a forest.

He began to read.

What follows is not what he read—how could it be?—but what it did to my mind. The sounds of the words were unlike any tongue I had known, and yet their meaning slid into me with the certainty of hunger recognizing bread. It was as if some buried dialect of the soul, long outlawed by the dominant speech of waking life, was suddenly spoken aloud again—untranslatable, and therefore true.

“But if I found a man that could believe
In what he saw not, felt not, and yet knew,
From him I should take substance, and receive
Firmness and form relate to touch and view;
Then should I clothe me in the likeness true
Of that idea where his soul did cleave!”

He turned a leaf. The house creaked softly, a settling of beams like an old throat swallowing.

“In me was every woman. I had power
Over the soul of every living man,
Such as no woman ever had in dower—
Could what no woman ever could, or can;
All women, I, the woman, still outran,
Outsoared, outsank, outreigned, in hall or bower.

“For I, though me he neither saw nor heard,
Nor with his hand could touch finger of mine,
Although not once my breath had ever stirred
A hair of him, could trammel brain and spine
With rooted bonds which Death could not untwine—
Or life, though hope were evermore deferred.”

The air grew tight. I became aware, painfully, of the soundscape of the room: the minute rush in my own ears, the fire’s cautious crackle, the far-off drip of water somewhere in the house as if a tap were not fully shut. In that listening, a new sound rose—thin, wrong, and bestial: a feline wail, not the healthy complaint of a cat shut out from the dairy, but the cry of something flayed by its own skin.

I started up on my elbow and stared about me. Nothing moved. No tail flicked, no shadow crossed the floor.

Mr. Raven turned several leaves and went on as if the book were a bell and he were ringing it for a creature that could not bear its note.

“For by his side I lay, a bodiless thing;
I breathed not, saw not, felt not, only thought,
And made him love me—with a hungering
After he knew not what—if it was aught
Or but a nameless something that was wrought
By him out of himself; for I did sing

“A song that had no sound into his soul;
I lay a heartless thing against his heart,
Giving him nothing where he gave his whole
Being to clothe me human, every part:
That I at last into his sense might dart,
Thus first into his living mind I stole.

“Ah, who was ever conquering Love but I!
Who else did ever throne in heart of man!
To visible being, with a gladsome cry
Waking, life’s tremor through me throbbing ran!”

Again the room answered with that repulsive cry, nearer now, as if it had been under the very chair. The sound made my teeth ache. I searched the carpet, the curtains, the shadow beneath the table where a cat might crouch—nothing. Yet my skin knew it was not alone.

Mr. Raven paused, listened for the space of two heartbeats, then turned pages and read on:

“Sudden I woke, nor knew the ghastly fear
That held me—not like serpent coiled about,
But like a vapour moist, corrupt, and drear,
Filling heart, soul, and breast and brain throughout;
My being lay motionless in sickening doubt,
Nor dared to ask how came the horror here.

“My past entire I knew, but not my now;
I understood nor what I was, nor where;
I knew what I had been: still on my brow
I felt the touch of what no more was there!
I was a fainting, dead, yet live Despair;
A life that flouted life with mop and mow!

“That I was a queen I knew right well,
And sometimes wore a splendour on my head
Whose flashing even dead darkness could not quell—
The like on neck and arms and girdle-stead;
And men declared a light my closed eyes shed
That killed the diamond in its silver cell.”

The feline cry came again—uglier, as if the throat that made it were clogged with water and mud. It slid through the room’s ordinary noises like oil on broth, spoiling everything.

Mr. Raven’s gaze swept once about the room, quick and assessing; then he turned several pages and resumed.

“Hideously wet, my hair of golden hue
Fouled my fair hands: to have it swiftly shorn
I had given my rubies, all for me dug new—
No eyes had seen, and such no waist had worn!
For a draught of water from a drinking horn,
For one blue breath, I had given my sapphires blue!

“Nay, I had given my opals for a smock,
A peasant-maiden’s garment, coarse and clean:
My shroud was rotting! Once I heard a cock
Lustily crow upon the hillock green
Over my coffin. Dulled by space between,
Came back an answer like a ghostly mock.”

The cry rose again, and with it I smelled something—wet fur, rank as a cloth left in a pail overnight, and underneath that a metallic taint, like the bottom of a copper pot scoured too hard. My stomach turned, and not from fear alone: the senses do not like being lied to, and the room was full of a lie that had begun to stink.

“I thought some foul thing was in the room!” said the librarian, and there was an almost weary disgust in him, like one who has heard too many imitations of music blasted from cheap horns in the street. But he did not stop. He turned a leaf or two and read again:

“For I had bathed in milk and honey-dew,
In rain from roses shook, that ne’er touched earth,
And ointed me with nard of amber hue;
Never had spot me spotted from my birth,
Or mole, or scar of hurt, or fret of dearth;
Never one hair superfluous on me grew.

“Fleeing cold whiteness, I would sit alone—
Not in the sun—I feared his bronzing light,
But in his radiance back around me thrown
By fulgent mirrors tempering his might;
Thus bathing in a moon-bath not too bright,
My skin I tinted slow to ivory tone.

“But now, all round was dark, dark all within!
My eyes not even gave out a phantom-flash;
My fingers sank in pulp through pulpy skin;
My body lay death-weltered in a mash
Of slimy horrors—”

With a fearsome yell, she burst into sight as if spat out by the room itself. Her fur clung in clammy clumps; water streamed from her like tears from a rag; her tail was thick as a cable, and her eyes flared green as chrysoprase in firelight. Her claws, distended and entangled, made her flounder across the carpet as though the very tools of her violence hindered her. Huge and white, she rushed for the chimney.

Quick as thought Mr. Raven flung the manuscript between her and the hearth.

She stopped as if she had struck an invisible wall. She crouched, her eyes fastened on the book with a hatred that was almost hunger—yet not hunger for food, but the hunger that wants to unmake what it cannot digest.

His voice went on as if he still read, and his eyes, too, seemed fixed on the pages:

“Ah, the two worlds! so strangely are they one,
And yet so measurelessly wide apart!
Oh, had I lived the bodiless alone
And from defiling sense held safe my heart,
Then had I scaped the canker and the smart,
Scaped life-in-death, scaped misery’s endless moan!”

At those words the cat loosed such a howl—so prolonged, so loaded with agony—that the sound battered the room. It was not merely loud; it was corrupting, like a factory’s unending shriek drowning out birdsong, until you forget there were ever birds. We both clapped our hands to our ears.

When the howl broke at last, Mr. Raven walked to the fireplace, took up the book, and stood between the creature and the chimney. He pointed one finger at her, and for a moment everything held—fire, air, beast, man—as if the world waited for a verdict.

She lay perfectly still.

He took a half-burnt stick from the hearth and drew with it a sign upon the floor. The charcoal whispered against the boards. He set the manuscript back in its place with a look that said plainly, Now we have her, I think! Then he returned to the cat, stood over her, and spoke in a voice so still and solemn it seemed to quiet even the ticking of ash falling.

“Lilith, when you came here on the way to your evil will, you little thought into whose hands you were delivering yourself! Mr. Vane, when God created me—not out of Nothing, as say the unwise, but out of His own endless glory—He brought me an angelic splendour to be my wife: there she lies!”

The cat’s beryl eyes burned up at him; his gaze held hers as a hand holds a lid down on a boiling pot.

“For her first thought was POWER,” he continued. “She counted it slavery to be one with me, and bear children for Him who gave her being. One child, indeed, she bore; then, puffed with the fancy that she had created her, would have me fall down and worship her! Finding, however, that I would but love and honour, never obey and worship her, she poured out her blood to escape me, fled to the army of the aliens, and soon had so ensnared the heart of the great Shadow, that he became her slave, wrought her will, and made her queen of Hell.

“How it is with her now, she best knows, but I know also. The one child of her body she fears and hates, and would kill, asserting a right, which is a lie, over what God sent through her into His new world. Of creating, she knows no more than the crystal that takes its allotted shape, or the worm that makes two worms when it is cloven asunder. Vilest of God’s creatures, she lives by the blood and lives and souls of men. She consumes and slays, but is powerless to destroy as to create.”

The words had the force of an old field-curse spoken in the dialect that names every tool and furrow exactly. I felt, with a shudder of disgust, how power that cannot make bread will always try to drink blood instead.

“Then God gave me another wife,” he said, and his voice altered—there came into it the note of a remembered hearth, the sound of a song hummed while hands knead dough: “not an angel but a woman—who is to this as light is to darkness.”

The cat gave a screech that seemed to tear at the boards under her. Then she began to grow.

She went on growing and growing, as if the room itself lent her substance. Her body swelled; the white fur mottled; the spots came as if ink were spilled beneath skin. At last, the spotted leopardess uttered a roar that made the house tremble. The very air shook, and in the far distance I thought I heard, beyond this unnatural din, the faintest echo of a bird startled from sleep—an innocent sound driven off by terror.

I sprang to my feet. Mr. Raven did not so much as blink.

“It is but her jealousy that speaks,” he said, “jealousy self-kindled, foiled and fruitless; for here I am, her master now whom she would not have for her husband! while my beautiful Eve yet lives, hoping immortally! Her hated daughter lives also, but beyond her evil ken, one day to be what she counts her destruction—for even Lilith shall be saved by her childbearing.

“Meanwhile she exults that my human wife plunged herself and me in despair, and has borne me a countless race of miserables; but my Eve repented, and is now beautiful as never was woman or angel, while her groaning, travailing world is the nursery of our Father’s children. I too have repented, and am blessed.—Thou, Lilith, hast not yet repented; but thou must.—Tell me, is the great Shadow beautiful? Knowest thou how long thou wilt thyself remain beautiful? Answer me, if thou knowest.”

As he spoke, something in my mind clicked into place with dreadful simplicity: this was no mere librarian with sharp speech and stranger books. The steadiness, the authority that did not need to shout, the sorrow that did not curdle—these were older than any library. I understood then that Mr. Raven was Adam, the old and the new man; and that his wife, ministering in the house of the dead, was Eve—the mother of us all, the lady of the New Jerusalem.

The leopardess reared. Her spots flickered and fled like a mask losing its hold. In their place stood the princess at last, radiant in her perfect shape, as if beauty were a weapon she could draw and brandish.

“I AM beautiful—and immortal!” she said.

“As a bush that burns, and is consumed,” answered he who had been her husband. “What is that under thy right hand?”

Her arm lay across her bosom; her hand was pressed to her side, as if to keep something in place. A swift pang contorted her face—so quick it might have been denied, had it not been written in the muscles like truth in a scar.

“It is but a leopard-spot that lingers! It will quickly follow those I have dismissed,” she answered, and tried to make the words ring careless.

“Thou art beautiful because God created thee,” Adam said, “but thou art the slave of sin: take thy hand from thy side.”

Her hand sank away. She looked him in the eyes with a fierceness that quailed even as it threatened—no surrender in it, only the panic of a tyrant asked to lay down her crown. He gazed a moment at the place she hid.

“It is not on the leopard; it is in the woman!” he said. “Nor will it leave thee until it hath eaten to thy heart, and thy beauty hath flowed from thee through the open wound!”

She glanced downward and shivered, as if she had smelled rot under perfume.

“Lilith,” Adam said, and his tone changed. The iron in it softened into a beseeching that was more terrible than wrath, as when an elder speaks in the old tongue to a child about to forget it forever. “Hear me, and repent, and He who made thee will cleanse thee!”

Her hand returned, quivering, to her side. Her face grew dark, as if a lamp were smoked over from within. She gave the cry of one from whom hope is vanishing; the cry passed into a howl. She fell writhing on the floor—a leopardess again, covered with spots, as if her own corruption broke through every disguise.

“The evil thou meditatest,” Adam resumed, “thou shalt never compass, Lilith, for Good and not Evil is the Universe. The battle between them may last for countless ages, but it must end: how will it fare with thee when Time hath vanished in the dawn of the eternal morn? Repent, I beseech thee; repent, and be again an angel of God!”

She rose. She stood upright, a woman once more, and said, with the cold relish of one naming a dish she intends to devour, “I will not repent. I will drink the blood of thy child.”

My eyes had been fastened on her as one watches a knife-hand. But when Adam spoke again, I turned—because the air changed. The sound changed. The room, which had endured her howls, seemed suddenly to brace itself against something greater.

He stood towering above her. The form of his visage was altered; his voice was terrible, not with loudness but with law.

“Down!” he cried; “or by the power given me I will melt thy very bones.”

She flung herself on the floor. She dwindled and dwindled, the proud shape collapsing as a fire collapses when its fuel is snatched away. She was again a gray cat, small enough to be carried, hateful enough to poison a house.

Adam caught her up by the skin of her neck, bore her to the closet, and threw her in as one casts a verminous thing into a bin. He described a strange figure on the threshold—charcoal again, a sign that seemed to bite into the air as well as the wood. Then, closing the door, he locked it.

When he returned to my side he was once more the old librarian, looking sad and worn, and furtively wiping tears from his eyes—tears that fell without sound, as if even grief here had learned to walk softly, so as not to feed the wrong listener.
