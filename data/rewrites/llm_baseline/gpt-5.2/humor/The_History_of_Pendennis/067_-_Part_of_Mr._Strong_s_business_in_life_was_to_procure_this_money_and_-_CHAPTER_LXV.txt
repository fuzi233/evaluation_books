Easy as Arthur Pendennis was with Warrington—easy as a man can be who will tell you, over a chop, the entire history of his hat—how did it come to pass that he could not tell George the trifling occurrences at that villa near Tunbridge Wells? He spoke freely enough of meeting old Smirke again (as a man tells of stumbling on a forgotten spice in the back of a cupboard), and of Mrs. Smirke, and of the church with its Anglo-Norman arches that looked, to his fancy, like ribs of some stone beast; he told of the plan of leaving Clapham, and of Rome, and of pictures, and of all manner of things that could be talked of without committing the soul. But when Warrington, with that quiet drawl of his, let Blanche’s name fall—just so, as you might drop a bay-leaf into a pot to see whether it floats—Pen’s answers became thin broth. He said she was good-natured and clever; that, rightly guided, she might make no such bad wife after all; that for the present he had no intention of marrying; that his romance was done; that he was content with his lot; and the like—phrases that fill the mouth and starve the meaning.

Yet to Lamb Court in the Temple there came, now and again, little satin envelopes, pretty enough to be sugared almonds, superscribed in the neatest hand, and sealed with one of those admirable ciphers which say, “This is nobody’s business,” and thereby proclaim it to all the world. Had Warrington been curious enough to watch his friend’s correspondence (which he was not), or had the cipher been made for human eyesight, it might have told him that Mr. Arthur was writing to a young lady whose initials were B. A. Pen answered these dainty missives in his best manner: news of the town, little jokes, small points of wit, and, no doubt, little verses; for Blanche had a Muse who wrote “Mes Larmes,” and a man of Pen’s ingenuity will never refuse a rhyme any more than a cook refuses a truffle. Blanche, you perceive, offers “branch,” “stanch,” “launch,” and other convenient syllables—like a well-stocked larder. Indeed, it was about this time, we incline to believe, that those love-verses of Mr. Pen’s appeared which afterwards made such a pleasing figure in the “Roseleaves,” that charming annual edited by Lady Violet Lebas, and decorated with portraits of female nobility by Mr. Pinkney; and that the lines, before they were pinned like paper roses into print, were first sent, warm from the pen, to Blanche by post—cornets, as it were, blown before Pinkney’s garland.

“Verses are all very well,” said Major Pendennis, finding Pen one day scratching down an artless effusion at the Club while he waited for his dinner—an hour sacred, in a well-ordered life, to the contemplation of sauces. “And letter-writing, if mamma allows it, and between such old country friends there may be a correspondence, and that sort of thing. But mind, Pen—don’t commit yourself, my boy. For who knows what the doose may happen? Make your letters safe. I never wrote a letter in all my life that would commit me; and demmy, sir, I have had some experience of women.”

And the Major—growing more confidential with age, and as fond of a cautionary tale as of a well-hung venison—told instances out of “Society” which, to his mind, proved that ardour is a fine thing in a cutlet and a bad thing in a note. Young Spoony, for example, having addressed the widow Naylor in poetical language, found himself honoured by a remonstrance from her brother Colonel Flint, and compelled into matrimony with a lady old enough to have been his nurse. Miss Louisa Salter, again, having at last secured young Sir John Bird, Hopwood of the Blues, was undone by some letters of her own which Hopwood produced: Sir John withdrew, and afterwards married Miss Stickney of Lyme Regis. The Major might not read much, but he observed; and out of the great world-book—thumbed, dog-eared, and stained with club-gravy—he could quote a multitude of modern instances.

Pen laughed, blushed a little, and promised to be cautious. He blushed, perhaps, because he already was cautious; because, in his letters to Miss Blanche, he had from instinct—or honesty, if you choose—refrained from any avowals that might compromise him. “Don’t you remember the lesson I had in Lady Mirabel’s—Miss Fotheringay’s affair?” he said, with mock humility. “I’m not to be caught again, uncle.” Old Pendennis congratulated himself on the prudence of his nephew, and was pleased to see Arthur taking his place as a man of the world.

Had Warrington been consulted, his opinion would likely have seasoned the dish with other spices. He might have said that a foolish, open letter is more honest than an adroit compliment with a back-door; that only a knave or a coward goes wooing with a retreat prepared. But Pen said nothing to Warrington of Blanche; for he knew pretty well that he was not innocent, and that George’s verdict would have been sharp as vinegar.

While Pen was thus cooking little gallantries for the post, fate began roasting another man at a slow fire. Colonel Altamont had been absent some weeks on his foreign tour, and Sir Francis Clavering had retired into the country, according to his compact with Major Pendennis; when ill-luck fell heavily on the sole remaining partner of the little firm in Shepherd’s Inn. When Strong had refused Altamont’s loan at parting—out of conscience and delicacy—he made a sacrifice which afterwards pinched him like a too-tight boot. Why should a fellow in want refuse a kind offer kindly made? Why should a thirsty man decline a pitcher because the outside of it is a little soiled? Strong’s conscience smote him for having been so scrupulous about money the other had fairly come by and generously proffered; and now, when it was too late, he thought ruefully that Altamont’s cash would have been as well in his pocket as in that of a gaming-house proprietor at Baden or Ems, where his Excellency would be sure to leave his Derby winnings.

It was whispered among tradesmen, bill-discounters, and other gentlefolk of commerce, that Strong and the baronet had parted company, and that the Captain’s “paper” was now no better than pastry—very fine to look at, and eaten in a mouthful. The same tradesmen, who had hitherto put a wonderful confidence in Strong—who could resist his jolly face and honest manner?—now poured in their bills with a unanimity as cowardly as it was punctual. Knocks at the chamber-door were constant. Tailors, bootmakers, pastrycooks who had furnished dinners (and had been praised for their gravies in better days), in their own persons or by their boys, held levees on Strong’s stairs. To these clamorous folk were added a quieter species, more dangerous because less noisy—lawyers’ clerks—who lurked about the Inn, or concerted with Mr. Campion’s young man hard by, carrying dismal pocketbooks in which lay copies of writs to be served on Edward Strong, requiring him to appear, etc. etc.

Strong, with not a guinea in his pocket, had of course no refuge but the Englishman’s castle. Into it he retired, shutting outer and inner door upon the enemy, and not quitting his stronghold till after nightfall. Against that barrier the foe would knock and curse in vain, while the Chevalier peeped from behind the little curtain he had hung over the letter-box orifice, and had the miserable satisfaction of seeing the furious faces of clerk and dun dash up to the door and retreat. But as they could not always keep watch—any more than one can keep a kettle singing forever without coals—the enemies of the Chevalier sometimes left him free.

When pressed, Strong was not quite alone. He had allies, and, like a man besieged, he established signals. His friends kept the garrison from starving by bringing in supplies and by bringing, too, what is more nourishing than bread—cheerfulness. Two faithful allies were Huxter and Miss Fanny Bolton. When hostile visitors prowled about the Inn, Fanny’s little sisters were taught a particular cry or jodel, which they whooped innocently in the court; when Fanny and Huxter came to visit Strong, they sang the same note at his door; the barrier would open, the honest garrison come out smiling, provisions and a pot of porter would be carried in, and the beleaguered one passed a comfortable night. There are men who could not live under this excitement. Strong had seen service. He could stand a siege and keep his temper sweet, as a good cook keeps a stew sweet—by patience, and by not letting it boil over.

Besides allies, Strong had secured—under difficulties—that still more necessary aid, a retreat. It has been mentioned how Messrs. Costigan and Bows lived next door to Captain Strong, and how the window of one of their rooms stood not far from the kitchen-window in the upper story of Strong’s chambers. A leaden water-pipe and gutter served both; and Strong, looking out one day, saw he could spring easily to his neighbour’s sill and clamber by the pipe. He had laughingly shown this refuge to his chum Altamont, and they agreed it would be best not to mention it to Captain Costigan, whose own duns were numerous, and who would be constantly flying down the pipe into Strong’s rooms if he knew of such a roadway.

But now the evil days were come, Strong made use of the passage; and one afternoon burst in upon Bows and Costigan with his jolly face, explaining that the enemy was waiting on his staircase and that he had taken this means of slipping them. So while Mr. Marks’s aides-de-camp were in attendance in the passage of No. 3, Strong walked down the steps of No. 4, dined at the Albion, went to the play, and returned home at midnight—to the astonishment of Mrs. Bolton and Fanny, who had not seen him quit his chambers and could not conceive how he had passed the line of sentries.

He bore this siege for some weeks with admirable spirit; as only an old soldier can, for the pains and privations were enough to depress a man of ordinary courage. What vexed and riled him—his own phrase—was the infernal indifference and cowardly ingratitude of Clavering. Strong wrote letter after letter; the baronet never acknowledged them by a single word, nor sent the smallest remittance; though “a five-pound note,” Strong said, “would have been a fortune.”

Better days, however, were in store for the Chevalier. In the midst of his despondency came a welcome aid.

“Yes—if it hadn’t been for this good fellow here,” Strong said afterwards—“for a good fellow you are, Altamont, my boy, and hang me if I don’t stand by you as long as I live—I think, Pendennis, it would have been all up with Ned Strong. I was the fifth week of being kept a prisoner—couldn’t always be risking my neck across that water-pipe and taking my walks abroad through poor old Cos’s window—and my spirit was quite broken, sir. Dammy, quite beat. I was thinking of putting an end to myself, and should have done it in another week, when who should drop down from heaven but Altamont!”

“Heaven ain’t exactly the place, Ned,” said Altamont. “I came from Baden-Baden; and I’d had a deuced lucky month there, that’s all.”

“Well, sir,” Strong cried, “he took up Marks’s bill, and he paid the other fellows that were upon me, like a man—sir, that he did!”

“And I shall be very happy to stand a bottle of claret for this company,” said Mr. Altamont, blushing, “and as many more as the company chooses. Hallo, waiter! bring us a magnum of the right sort, d’ye hear? We’ll drink our healths all round—may every good fellow like Strong find another good fellow to stand by him at a pinch. That’s my sentiment, Mr. Pendennis, though I don’t like your name.”

“No? And why?” asked Arthur.

Strong pressed the Colonel’s foot under the table here; and Altamont, rather excited, filled another bumper, nodded to Pen, drank, and said that Pen was a gentleman, and that was sufficient, and they were all gentlemen.

This meeting of “all gentlemen” took place at Richmond, whither Pendennis had gone to dinner, and where he found the Chevalier and his friend at table in the coffee-room. Both were hilariously talkative and excited by wine; and Strong, an admirable story-teller, told his siege, his escapes, and his adventures with great liveliness and humour—describing the talk of the sheriff’s officers at his door, the pretty signals of Fanny, the grotesque exclamations of Costigan when the Chevalier burst in at his window, and his final rescue by Altamont—so graphically that his hearers could almost hear the Inn itself: the court’s hollow echo, the stair’s creak, the muffled thud of a dun’s impatient boot, and that childish jodel—bright as a bird-note against the thick, dirty city hum.

“As for me, it’s nothing,” Altamont said. “When a ship’s paid off, a chap spends his money, you know. And it’s the fellers at the black and red at Baden-Baden that did it. I won a good bit of money there, and intend to win a good bit more—don’t I, Strong? I’m going to take him with me. I’ve got a system. I’ll make his fortune, I tell you. I’ll make your fortune, if you like—dammy, everybody’s fortune. But what I’ll do, and no mistake, boys, I promise you: I’ll put in for that little Fanny. Dammy, sir, what d’ye think she did? She had two pound, and I’m blest if she didn’t go and lend it to Ned Strong! Didn’t she, Ned? Let’s drink her health.”

“With all my heart,” said Arthur, and pledged the toast cordially.

Mr. Altamont then began, with extraordinary volubility, to describe his system—how it was infallible if played coolly; how he had it from a chap at Baden who had lost by it, true, but only for want of capital; if he could have stood one more turn of the wheel he would have had all his money back; how he and several more chaps were going to make a bank and try it; how he would put in every shilling he was worth; how he had come back to England expressly to fetch his money and Captain Strong; how Strong should play for him; how he could trust Strong’s temper better than his own, and better than Bloundell-Bloundell or the Italian that “stood in.” As he emptied his bottle he unfolded plans and prospects to Pen, who listened with interest to the daring lawless good-humour of the man, and to the queer music of his talk—half sea-oath, half nursery generosity—beating time, as it were, to the clink of glasses and the scrape of chairs.

“I met that queer fellow Altamont the other day,” Pen said to his uncle, a day or two later.

“Altamont? What Altamont? There’s Lord Westport’s son,” said the Major.

“No, no; the fellow who came tipsy into Clavering’s dining-room when we were there,” said the nephew, laughing. “He said he didn’t like the name of Pendennis, though he did me the honour to think me a good fellow.”

“I don’t know any man of the name of Altamont—I give you my honour,” said the impenetrable Major; “and as for your acquaintance, I think the less you have to do with him the better, Arthur.”

Arthur laughed again. “He’s going to quit the country and make his fortune by a gambling system. He and my amiable college acquaintance, Bloundell, are partners; the Colonel takes out Strong as aide-de-camp. What is it that binds the Chevalier and Clavering, I wonder?”

“I should think,” said the Major, “mind you, Pen—I should think, but of course I have only the idea—that there has been something in Clavering’s previous life which gives these fellows, and some others, a certain power over him. And if there should be no such secret, which affair of ours, my boy—dammy, I say—ought to be a lesson to a man to keep himself straight in life, and not give any man a chance over him.”

“Why, I think you have some means of persuasion over Clavering, uncle, or why should he give me that seat in Parliament?”

“Clavering thinks he ain’t fit for Parliament,” the Major answered. “No more he is. What’s to prevent him putting you or anybody else into his place if he likes? Do you think Government or Opposition would make any bones about accepting the seat if he offered it? Why should you be more squeamish than the first men, and the most honourable men, and men of the highest birth and position—begad?” The Major had an answer of this sort to most of Pen’s objections; and Pen accepted them—not because he believed them, but because he wished to believe them. We do a thing—who of us has not?—not because everybody does it, but because we like it; and our acquiescence proves not that everybody is right, but that we and the world are poor creatures alike.

On his next visit to Tunbridge, Mr. Pen did not forget to amuse Miss Blanche with the history he had learned at Richmond: the Chevalier’s imprisonment, and Altamont’s gallant rescue. And after he had told it in his usual satirical way, he spoke with praise and a certain emotion of little Fanny’s generous behaviour—those two pounds lent out of a small purse, like the last loaf shared in a hard winter—and of Altamont’s enthusiasm on her behalf.

Miss Blanche was somewhat jealous, and a good deal piqued and curious about this Fanny. Among the many confidential little communications which Arthur made to Miss Amory in the course of their delightful rural drives and their sweet evening walks—where the hedges smelled of leaf-mould and last year’s apples, and the gravel underfoot gave that crisp, domestic whisper which is the very opposite of London’s iron roar—he would not omit a story so likely to interest her as that of the poor little Ariadne of Shepherd’s Inn. His own part in the drama he described, to do him justice, with becoming modesty; the moral he wished to draw being one in his satirical key: that women get over their first loves quite as easily as men do; that number one withdrawn, they transfer themselves to number two without much difficulty. And little Fanny was offered as an instance to prove it.

What griefs she had endured and surmounted, what bitter pangs of hopeless attachment she had borne, what time it had taken to heal the wounds of that tender little heart—Pen did not know, or perhaps did not choose to know. For he was at once modest and doubtful about his own power as a conqueror, and averse to believe he had made any dangerous ravage in that particular place. And yet his own argument told against him; for if, as he said, Miss Fanny was by this time in love with her surgical adorer—who had neither good looks, nor good manners, nor wit, nor anything but ardour and fidelity—must she not, in her first sickness of the love-complaint, have had a serious attack indeed, and suffered keenly for a man who certainly had more of the showy qualities that Mr. Huxter wanted?

“You wicked odious creature,” Miss Blanche said, “I believe you are enraged with Fanny for being so impudent as to forget you, and that you are actually jealous of Mr. Huxter.”

Perhaps Miss Amory was right; for a blush came, in spite of him, and tingled on Pen’s cheek—one of those slaps with which vanity keeps a man awake. Superseded! And by such a rival! By such a fellow as that—without any conceivable good quality! O Mr. Pendennis! If Nature had not made that provision for each sex in the credulity of the other—which sees good qualities where none exist, beauty in donkey’s ears, wit in their numskulls, and music in their bray—there would not be near so much marrying and giving in marriage as now obtains, and as is necessary for the continuance of the noble race.

“Jealous or not,” Pen said, “and, Blanche, I don’t say no, I should have liked Fanny to have come to a better end than that. I don’t like histories that end in that cynical way; and when we arrive at the conclusion of a pretty girl’s passion, to find such a figure as Huxter’s at the last page. Is life a compromise, my lady fair, and the end of the battle of love an ignoble surrender? Is the search for the Cupid which my poor little Psyche pursued in the darkness—the god of her soul’s longing—the god of the blooming cheek and rainbow pinions—to result in Huxter smelling of tobacco and gallypots? I wish—though I don’t see it in life—that people could be like Jenny and Jessamy, or my Lord and Lady Clementina in the story-books, and at once under the ceremony, and as it were at the parson’s benediction, become perfectly handsome and good and happy ever after.”

“And don’t you intend to be good and happy, pray, Monsieur le Misanthrope,” asked the author of “Mes Larmes,” with a charming moue, “and are you very discontented with your lot? and will your marriage be a compromise—and is your Psyche an odious vulgar wretch? You wicked satirical creature, I can’t abide you! You take the hearts of young things, play with them, and fling them away with scorn. You ask for love and trample on it. You—you make me cry, that you do, Arthur—and—and don’t—and I won’t be consoled in that way—and I think Fanny was quite right in leaving such a heartless creature.”

“Again, I don’t say no,” said Pen, looking gloomily at Blanche, and not offering to repeat the attempt at consolation which had drawn from her that sweet monosyllable “don’t.” “I don’t think I have much of what people call heart; but I don’t profess it. I made my venture when I was eighteen; I lighted my lamp and went in search of Cupid. And what was my discovery of love?—a vulgar dancing-woman. I failed, as everybody does—almost everybody; only it is luckier to fail before marriage than after.”

“Merci du choix, Monsieur,” said the Sylphide, making him a curtsey.

“Look, my little Blanche,” said Pen, taking her hand, his voice in that sad good-humour which is half confession and half performance; “at least I stoop to no flatteries.”

“Quite the contrary,” said Miss Blanche.

“And tell you no foolish lies, as vulgar men do. Why should you and I, with our experience, ape romance and dissemble passion? I do not believe Miss Blanche Amory to be peerless among the beautiful, nor the greatest poetess, nor the most surpassing musician, any more than I believe you to be the tallest woman in the whole world—like the giantess whose picture we saw as we rode through the fair yesterday. But if I don’t set you up as a heroine, neither do I offer you your very humble servant as a hero. But I think you are—well, there, I think you are very sufficiently good-looking.”

“Merci,” Miss Blanche said, with another curtsey.

“I think you sing charmingly. I’m sure you’re clever. I hope and believe you are good-natured—and that you will be companionable.”
