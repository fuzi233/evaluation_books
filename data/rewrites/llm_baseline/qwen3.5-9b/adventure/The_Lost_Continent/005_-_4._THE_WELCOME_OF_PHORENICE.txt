Now I can speak the absolute truth: until our rival navy intercepted us at the mouth of the gulf, I had dismissed my status as a mere recruit for the Empress. But the ambush laid for us, and the wild ferocity with which those ships fought solely to capture me, were omens that even the blind could not miss. It was evident that I was destined to play a pivotal role in the nation's fate. Yet, if our arrival had been anticipated by enemies, it followed that Phorenice possessed her own scouts. These watchers spotted us from the high mountains, carrying our news swiftly to the capital.

The sea-arm leading to the vast city of Atlantis varies wildly in width. Where the mountains have collapsed, sending molten rock to solidify beneath the waves, the channel narrows to the breadth of a river. Beyond that, for a half-day's sail, it expands into a lake-like expanse where the distant shores are barely visible. Furthermore, the coastline winds unpredictably. A skilled runner, navigating the flats, swamps, and smoking hills along the shore—avoiding fire streams, rising waters, and roaming beasts—could deliver messages from the coast to the capital faster than any galley, however expertly rowed. Naturally, a lone traveler risking everything for speed faced grave dangers. But Phorenice was no miser with her couriers. She dispatched a corps of twenty runners to the headland overlooking the strait's entrance. Each took a separate route, and their speed and cunning were proven when no fewer than seven arrived safely with their tidings; reports suggested three others survived as well. Still, at the time, we knew nothing of this. We pressed onward, convinced our return was entirely unheralded.

The slaves rowing our galley were mostly savages from Europe, their presence emitting an odor so repulsive that the voyage lost all charm. Compounding this misery, the wind carried a constant shower of volcanic grit from an erupting mountain nearby. We were eager to reach land as soon as possible. Admitting this weakness despite my priestly training in stoicism—that the future lies in the hands of the Gods—I confessed to a profound, human curiosity regarding my reception in Atlantis. Would I be taken as a formal prisoner, tried for curing the colony of Yucatan? Or would I step ashore unnoticed, summoned later by the Empress for new duties? The reality of my welcome remained a mystery.

As dawn broke, we caught sight of the sacred mountain's peak, crowned with the eternal fires that guard the city. The boatswains' whips cracked with renewed vigor, driving the exhausted slaves to a final sprint. The wind was dead against us, sails useless, but under oars alone we made steady progress. The slopes of the sacred mountain loomed larger, and soon the pyramids and towering structures of the city emerged from the mist, appearing to float upon the shimmering water. Twenty years had passed since I last beheld Atlantis, and my heart burned with the anticipation of walking her stones again. As we drew closer, the magnificent city seemed to rise from the sea itself. With every stroke of the oar, the shoreline leaped nearer. I recognized the temple where I was admitted to manhood, the pyramid containing the secrets of initiation, and finally, the modest house where a father and mother raised me. Tears blurred my vision as memory flooded back.

We pulled up outside the white harbor walls, adhering to the law. The slaves slumped over their oars, panting and weeping quietly. Usually, vessels anchored here faced delays while a port-captain proved his dignity by making others wait. But today, a boat waited. Signals rang from the twin castles guarding the entrance; the connecting chain dropped, and a ten-oared skiff surged forward, racing alongside us.

"That must be Dason's galley?" the port-captain demanded.

"It was," replied Tob. "I saw Dason's head on your bow."

"You were Captain of Tatho?"

"And remain so. Tatho's fleet was sunk by Dason and his allies to the sea floor, leaving this stinking vessel as our only option to finish the journey."

The captain scanned the figures on our deck. "Captain, I fear your reception will be perilous. I see no sign of my Lord Deucalion. Does he travel with another navy? By the Gods, if you allowed him to perish under your command, the Empress will peel your skin slowly while you live."

"With both Phorenice and Tatho so concerned for his welfare," Tob interjected, "Lord Deucalion seems a hazardous passenger. But I intend to save my hide this voyage." He jabbed a thumb toward me. "He is aboard to vouch for me personally."

The captain paused, disbelief warring with recognition, before bowing deeply, accustomed to ceremony. "I trust my Lord, in his infinite strength, will pardon my ignorance of his noble appearance. Truth be told, I expected attire befitting his station."

"Pish," I said. "If I choose simplicity, I cannot fault being mistaken for a commoner. I have no desire to advertise my rank through gaudy finery. If amends are owed, I beg you end this inquiry."

The captain melted into obsequiousness. "I am the humblest of your Lord's servants. It would be my supreme honor to pilot your galley to the assigned berth."

His boat surged ahead, and our slaves resumed their strokes. Tob watched me with a dry smile as he directed the helm.

"Well," I said, playing along, "what troubles you?"

"I fear," Tob mused, "that when my Lord Deucalion steps among this refined crowd, he'll remember me only as a rude brute."

"Do you truly believe that?"

"Then I must prove my refinement," Tob declared solemnly. He seized my massive hand in his rough grip and squeezed tight. "By the Gods, Deucalion, you may be a great prince, but I've only ever known you as a man. You are the finest fighter of beasts and men alive today. That spear-thrust against the lizard will become a chant in the taverns forever."

We entered the harbor rapidly, soldiers in the entrance castle blowing trumpets as we passed between them. The port-captain hoisted my banner to the masthead of his skiff—a flag seemingly prepared specifically for this announcement. From the quays across the vast basin, the sounds of musicians drifted toward us, accompanied by the pale glow of welcoming fires dancing in the sunlight. I felt a surge of awe; surely an Empress of Atlantis would not trouble herself with such interest in a mere returning subject. Clearly, nothing was to be done halfway.

The port-captain's boat led the way, forcing us to follow. Our galley was run alongside the royal quay and secured to posts and rings of gold, sacred to the reigning house.

"If Dason had foreseen this honor," Tob joked grimly, "he'd have woven silk ropes to tie to the bollards instead of coarse hemp. I'm certain a frown would be etched on his skull this very minute if the sun hadn't baked it rigid. My Lord Deucalion, will you navigate this common ship with care and tread the genteel carpet they've spread for you on the quay?"

The port-captain recoiled at Tob's rudeness, his face contorting in horror. I sighed inwardly, realizing colonial freedom held no place in Atlantis. Once more, I must prepare for the weight of rank and the rigors of grand ceremony. Yet, a self-respecting man must retain his individuality. Though I agreed to enter a crimson pavilion erected to shelter me until the Empress's arrival, my compliance ended there.

Again, the issue of clothing arose. Three gorgeously dressed chamberlains presented me with robes adorned with every conceivable extravagance, urging me to transform into a peacock matching their style. Refusing curtly, I asked if they had received specific orders regarding my attire from Her Mightiness. They confessed they had none.

"This dress suits my temper," I stated firmly. "It protects me from the heat and wind, and it is clean. It seems to me, sirs, that your interference borders on impertinence."

In unison, the chamberlains drew their swords, pushing the hilts toward me.

"It would be a favor," their spokesman pleaded, "if Great Lord Deucalion would take his vengeance now, sparing us the tormentors of the future."

"Nonsense," I dismissed. "The matter is forgotten. You exaggerate a trivial point."

Their action, however, offered clarity. They were deadly serious, offering me weapons to defend their honor. I realized I had returned to a different Atlantis—one where a man feared torture for a disagreement over coat cuts.

Inside the pavilion, I bathed gratefully, though a faint perfume tainted the water's refreshment. Settling myself with composed silence, I waited. The chamberlains, too polite to disturb my calm, stood silent around me. Outside, the sun beat down on the scarlet roof as music blared and the welcoming fires cleared the air of the great square facing the quay.

It is said that the mighty gain dignity by making the lesser wait. Personally, I found this strategy petty. Phorenice evidently shared my disdain. As soon as Tob's galley touched the royal quay, she departed the palace. The magnificent procession, fully arrayed, awaited only its leader. Mounting her steed, trumpets signaled the advance.
