Before the quiet covenant of friendship we had so lately struck could feel truly sealed, there was another power to contend with besides Fifine Beck’s sharp-witted mother: Rome herself, keeping sleepless, jealous watch over her son through the slatted lattice I had once knelt before, the sliding wooden panel of the confessional M. Paul drew closer to month by month.

You might well wonder why I clung so fiercely to that new promise of his steady friendship, when he had shown me proof after proof of his partiality for years. It was the earnest, unreserved way he spoke it, I think: the soft, modest doubt in his own worth as a friend, the tender deference that trusted me enough to rest his weariness in my company. He had called me “sister”, and it felt perfect. Let him call me whatever he pleased, so long as he kept nothing back from me. I would gladly be his sister, so long as he never asked me to fill that role for some future wife of his — and given his long-held tacit vow of celibacy, that danger felt impossibly far off.

I turned that evening’s interview over in my head almost the entire night, too restless to sleep, counting the minutes till the morning bell rang. Prayers dragged, breakfast moved at a snail’s pace, every hour stretching too thin before it was finally time for our literature lesson. I ached to see how he would carry himself as my brother, to test how much of a sister’s gentle courage lived in me, how much of a brother’s unguarded frankness he would show.

When he arrived, the world proved it never could match the soft shape of my expectations. He never once spoke to me that whole day. His lessons were quieter, milder, and far graver than usual; he was kind, fatherly even, to all his other pupils, but not a single brotherly glance or word came my way. Before he left the classroom, I waited for a smile, if nothing else — but all I got was one hurried, shy little nod, gone before I could even react.

I told myself the distance was accidental, involuntary, that patience would make it melt. It did not melt. It stretched on for days, and grew wider. I bit back my surprise, swallowed the hot, stinging feelings that rose unbidden in my throat, and waited.

I had every right to ask him, when he offered me fraternity, “Dare I rely on you?” And he, knowing his own heart’s vulnerabilities better than I, had every right to withhold that pledge. He had bade me tease him, test him, make my own experiments with his good nature — but that privilege was never mine to take. I was the sort of woman who withdrew when repulsed, who stayed passive when left alone, whose lips would never utter a reminder, whose eyes would never dart a pleading glance. Somewhere in my neat calculations, a line had slipped out of place, and I needed time to find where the error lay.

At last the customary evening for our weekly private lesson rolled around, the one he had generously set aside seven days out of seven for months, to go over the week’s work and map out lessons for the days ahead. On those nights, we usually met wherever the other pupils and teachers happened to be, in the big second-floor classroom that emptied out once the day students left, leaving only a handful of boarders clustered around the surveillante’s desk. When the hour struck, I gathered my books, my pen and ink, and made my way there.

The classroom itself was empty, wrapped in cool, soft shadow, but through the open double doors I could see the sunlit common room, full of girls laughing and working, the westering sun blushing crimson over every wall, every dress, every face, fusing all the colours of the room into one warm, honeyed glow. M. Emanuel stood in the middle of their circle, chatting good-humouredly with the other teacher, his dark coat and jet black hair streaked with the sun’s red light, his sharp Spanish face lighting up with a smile when he turned, as if kissing the sun right back. I slipped into an empty desk.

The orange trees, the geraniums, the bright blooming cactuses all basked in that sunset glow, thirsty after a full day of heat. M. Paul had a soft spot for gardening; tending plants with his watering pot always soothed his frayed nerves, and I watched him move from pot to pot, watering each one carefully, his favourite cigar clamped between his lips, its soft blue smoke curling pretty through the blooms in the golden light. He soon stopped talking to the pupils and teachers, and started cooing soft endearments to a tiny silky spaniel that nominally belonged to the school, but loved him far more than any other living soul in the house. She trotted at his heels, her big soft eyes fixed on his face, and whenever he playfully dropped his bonnet or handkerchief, she would crouch over it like a tiny lion guarding a royal flag.

He fetched all the water from the courtyard well himself, so the work stretched on longer than I expected. The big school clock ticked past the hour we usually set for our lesson, and then another hour struck. The sunset glow faded, twilight creeping in fast. My lesson would be very short tonight, I thought — but then the last of the potted plants were watered. Surely it was my turn now?

Alas, he still had his favourite rose bushes and a handful of rare blooms to tend down the garden alley, and the little spaniel yapped gleefully after him, her small paws pattering over the paving stones. I stacked half my books away, no longer needing them, and sat waiting, watching the shadows creep slowly across the floor.

Soon enough the little dog came bounding back, announcing his return. He set the empty watering pot down by the well, washed his hands in the stone basin by the door, and stood slowly adjusting his cuffs, staring up at the thin pale horn of the new moon hanging in the opal sky. Sylvie, the spaniel, grew restless with his quiet contemplation, whined and jumped at his knees till he laughed, and lifted her up in his arms.

“Little greedy thing,” he said to her, “I can’t forget you for a single moment, can I?”

He wandered slowly across the courtyard, no more than a yard from the line of windows where I sat, fondling Sylvie against his chest, calling her all sorts of tender little pet names. On the front steps he paused, looked out over the grey cathedral spires fading into the blue mist of coming night, breathed in the sweet cool dusk air — and then suddenly his sharp gaze swept the length of the classroom windows. I think he bowed; if he did, I had no time to return the courtesy before the door clicked shut behind him, and he was gone.

I gathered up all my things, and carried the unused stack of books back to my own desk in the third classroom. The prayer bell rang, and I went through the motions of the service, my chest heavy.

He would not return to the Rue Fossette the next day, his entire schedule devoted to duties at his college. I dragged through my own teaching, got through the long empty hours, and prepared myself for the heavy loneliness of the evening. When I sat alone at my desk and flipped open its lid, reaching for a book to comfort me, a small lilac-covered pamphlet I had never seen before fell out. I had rearranged my desk only that afternoon, so it must have been slipped there while we were all at dinner.

I opened it, curious. It was no story, no poem, no history: it was a thin, soft-spoken theological tract, full of honeyed, gentle language, meant to persuade its reader to convert to Roman Catholicism. No thundering threats of hellfire, no harsh condemnation — only the soft, coaxing tone of a loving mother, promising the gentle comfort of Holy Church, the great solace of being able to pray your dead loved ones out of purgatory. It was written for tender hearts, not sharp intellects, milk for babes rather than strong meat, and it amused me more than it angered me. On the flyleaf, in his familiar small clear pencil handwriting, it read: From P.C.D.E. to L——y.

I laughed, and all at once the fog of my confusion cleared completely. The riddle was solved. Père Silas, his confessor, had drawn every last detail of our new fraternal covenant out of him, told him that friendship with a Protestant heretic was unholy, dangerous, a threat to his soul. That was the source of that cold, distant reserve, not any change of heart on M. Paul’s part. It was far easier to bear than the fear that he had grown tired of me all on his own.

That night, the sky was completely overcast, a clammy grey fog creeping in from the marshes around Villette, a fine soft rain drizzling steadily down. I heard Sylvie’s sharp happy bark out in the garden, the kind she only ever saved for M. Paul, and looked out to see him digging furiously in the wet dark soil, his coat soaked through, working as if he was digging for his daily bread by the sweat of his brow. I knew that habit of old: he always dug like that for hours, brows knit, teeth set, when his heart was torn apart by painful conflicting feelings, either nervous excitement or sharp stabs of self-reproach.

Sylvie spotted me at the window, and flew barking at the panes, as if to urge me out to join him. He whistled for her to stop, but she only barked louder, till he finally pushed the glass door open and let her burst inside. She jumped straight into my lap, her paws on my neck, her little pink tongue licking my face all over, scattering all my papers and books across the desk.

M. Paul stepped inside to quiet the chaos, gathered up my books, tucked Sylvie gently under his coat where she settled quiet as a mouse, her little peeking over his lapel. His gaze fell straight on the little lilac tract.

“You haven’t read that pamphlet yet, I suppose?” he said, tentative. “It’s not very inviting, is it?”

I told him I had read it. He waited, expecting me to give an opinion unprompted — but I was not going to make the first move, not after two days of cold silence. I deliberately picked up my dull penknife and a handful of quills, pretending to mend them, nicking my finger half on purpose, to make him scold me like he always used to.

“Clumsy thing!” he cried at last, snatching the knife and pens out of my hand. “You’ll cut your whole fingers to ribbons.”

He proceeded to mend the pens with the neat, swift accuracy of a professional, and asked if I had liked the little book. I suppressed a yawn, and said it had made me a little sleepy. He laughed, then chided me gently, insisting that I was far too sensitive to be untouched by its soft, loving appeal. I pulled out a perfectly dry, completely clean handkerchief from my pocket to prove he was wrong, and he launched into a stream of his old familiar sharp, playful criticisms that was sweeter than any music after two days of silence. I shared my chocolates with Sylvie, and he sat there watching, softening, till he leaned over the desk, his voice gentle again.

“Tell me honestly, little sister,” he said, “what did you think of me these past two days?”

I pretended not to hear, my eyes suddenly stinging, and stroked Sylvie’s fur to hide it.

“I called myself your brother,” he went on, his voice low. “I hardly know what I am to you — brother, friend, I can’t tell. I think of you constantly, I wish you every good thing, but I have to check myself. My best friends keep warning me you are dangerous. It’s your religion, that strange, self-reliant Protestant faith of yours. Père Silas says you are good, that he even loves you — but that proud, earnest creed of yours, it’s a threat. Sometimes I see it in your eyes, and my flesh creeps.”

I spoke to him then, soft and steady, no sharp arguments, no heated debate. I told him I believed in the same God, the same Christ, the same Bible that he did, that I would never hurt his faith, that his Church’s slanders about Protestants being irreverent pagans were untrue. He argued with me, pleaded his case for hours, but he could not shake my quiet, gentle certainty. When he left that night, he was not fully satisfied, not fully appeased — but he could no longer doubt that my faith was no threat to him. The rift his confessor had carved between us was not unbreakable, after all.
