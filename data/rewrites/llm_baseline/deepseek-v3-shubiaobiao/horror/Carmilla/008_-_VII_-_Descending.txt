Even now, the memory of that night claws at me with icy fingers. It was no fleeting terror, no phantom of a dream to be shaken off at dawn. The horror seeped into the walls, into the very air I breathed, thickening with each passing hour.

I could not bear solitude the next day. I longed to tell my father, but fear held my tongue. Would he laugh, dismissing it as girlish fancy? Or worse—would he see in my trembling the same cursed affliction creeping through our village? He was already unwell; I would not burden him.

Madame Perrodon and Mademoiselle Lafontaine, ever kind, noticed my unease. Mademoiselle laughed when I confessed, but Madame’s face darkened.

"By the way," Mademoiselle said, grinning, "the lime tree walk behind Carmilla’s window is haunted!"

"Foolishness," Madame chided, though her voice lacked conviction.

"Martin swears he saw a woman walking there—twice—before sunrise," Mademoiselle insisted.

"Cows in the river fields, nothing more," Madame countered, but her fingers tightened around her shawl.

"Don’t speak of it to Carmilla," I pleaded. "She can see that path from her window. She’s frightened enough as it is."

Carmilla appeared late that morning, her pallor more pronounced. "I had such a fright last night," she murmured. "If not for my charm, I’d have seen something dreadful. A black shape hovered near my bed—I felt it. But the charm drove it away."

I told her of my own ordeal. Her eyes widened. "You didn’t have the charm with you?"

"No. But tonight, I will."

Somehow, I slept. The charm, pinned to my pillow, warded off the unseen. For two nights, my rest was deep, untroubled. Yet each morning, a heaviness clung to me, sweet and sorrowful. A languor draped itself over my limbs, a melancholy I welcomed like an old friend.

Carmilla watched me with feverish devotion. Her gaze lingered too long, her touch too tender. It unsettled me—a flicker of something ravenous beneath her affection.

Then the dreams began.

Cold fingers trailed my skin. Lips pressed to my throat, lingering, hungry. My heart hammered, breath hitching as invisible hands tightened. I woke gasping, drenched in sweat, the echo of a woman’s voice in my ears—*"Your mother warns you to beware of the assassin."*

A light flared.

Carmilla stood at the foot of my bed, her white nightgown drenched in blood.

I screamed.

The next I knew, I was in the hallway, shrieking for help. Madame and Mademoiselle rushed out, their faces pale in the lamplight. We pounded on Carmilla’s door. No answer.

The lock splintered under the servants’ hands.

The room stood pristine, untouched.

But Carmilla was gone.
