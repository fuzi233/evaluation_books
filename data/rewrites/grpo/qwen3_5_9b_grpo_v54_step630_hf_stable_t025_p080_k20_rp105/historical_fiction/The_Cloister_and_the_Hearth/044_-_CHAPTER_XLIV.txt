In the quiet chamber, where the air grew thick with the scent of unwashed linen and sudden terror, Catherine did not falter. She was a woman of experience, one who knew that fainting was rarely a ghostly visitation but merely the body's refusal to bear a heavy load. With brisk efficiency, she laid the young girl flat upon the floorboards, her head resting squarely on the wood, and threw open the shutter to invite the cool night air. Only after securing the girl's dress and loosening her bodice did she turn to the door, her voice ringing out with commanding clarity.

"Come here, if you please."

Margaret Van Eyck and Reicht entered to find the scene exactly as described: the girl prone and pale, and Catherine striking her palms together in a rhythm of agitation.

"Oh, my poor girl! What have you done to her?" Margaret cried, her face flushing with a mixture of shock and anger.

"Me?" Catherine snapped, her eyes flashing. "What has happened, then?"

"Nothing, madam," Catherine replied, her tone hardening into stone. "Nothing more than is natural in her situation."

Margaret's cheeks burned hotter, the color rising to her temples in ire. "You do well to speak so coolly," she retorted, pointing an accusing finger. "You that are the cause of her situation."

"That I am not," Catherine declared bluntly, her voice steady despite the turmoil. "Nor is any woman born."

"Was it not you and your husband that kept them apart?" Margaret pressed, her voice trembling with suppressed grief. "And now he has gone to Italy all alone. 'Situation' indeed! You have broken her heart amongst you."

"Why, madam? Who is it then? In Heaven's name!" Catherine exclaimed, genuinely baffled. "To hear you, one would think this was my Gerard's lass. But that can't be. This fur never cost less than five crowns the ell; besides, this young gentlewoman is a wife; or ought to be."

"Of course she ought," Margaret insisted, her composure cracking. "And who is the cause she is none? Who came before them at the very altar?"

"God forgive them, whoever it was," Catherine said gravely, shaking her head. "Me it was not, nor my man."

"Well," the older woman sighed, her anger softening into concern as she noticed the girl's eyelids fluttering. "Now you have seen her, perhaps you will not be quite so bitter against her, madam. She is coming to, thank Heaven."

"Me bitter against her?" Catherine laughed softly, the sound devoid of malice. "No, that is all over. Poor soul! Trouble behind her and trouble afore her; and to think of my setting her, of all living women, to read Gerard's letter to me. Ay, and that was what made her go off, I'll be sworn. She is coming to. What, sweetheart! Be not afeared, none are here but friends."

They lifted the girl into an easy chair. As the rosy hue began to creep back to her cheeks and lips, Catherine drew Margaret Van Eyck aside, her voice dropping to a conspiratorial whisper.

"Is she staying with you, if you please?"

"No, madam."

"I wouldn't let her go back to Sevenbergen to-night, then."

"That is as she pleases. She still refuses to bide the night."

"Ay, but you are older than she is; you can make her. There, she is beginning to notice."

Catherine leaned close, pressing her lips to Margaret Van Eyck's ear for a fleeting moment. It seemed too brief to whisper a full sentence, yet between women, communication often flashes like lightning, bypassing the need for words.

The old lady recoiled slightly, her eyes widening. "It's false! It is a calumny! It is monstrous! Look at her face. It is blasphemy to accuse such a face."

"Tut! Tut! Tut!" Margaret dismissed her with a wave of her hand. "You might as well say this is not my hand. I ought to know; and I tell ye it is so."

Then, to Margaret's utter astonishment, she stepped forward, took the young girl by the neck, and kissed her warmly.

"I suffered for Gerard," Margaret whispered fiercely, tears welling in her eyes. "And you shed your blood for him, I do hear; his own words show me that I have been to blame, the very words you have read to me. Ay, Gerard, my child, I have held aloof from her; but I'll make it up to her once I begin. You are my daughter from this hour."

Another embrace, warm and desperate, sealed this hasty compact, and the impulsive woman departed.

Margaret lay back in the chair, a feeble smile stealing over her face. Gerard's mother had kissed her and claimed her as a daughter; but the next moment, she saw her old friend regarding her with a deeply vexed expression.

"I wonder you let that woman kiss you," the old lady murmured, her tone sharp with disapproval.

"His mother!" Margaret replied, half reproachful, half confused.

"Mother, or no mother, you would not let her touch you if you knew what she whispered in my ear about you."

"About me?" Margaret asked faintly.

"Ay, about you, whom she never saw till to-night." The old lady hesitated, choosing her words carefully as she prepared to share her indignation, when an unexpected interruption silenced her.

The young woman slid from the chair to her knees, bowing her head low. She began to pray piteously for pardon. From the fervor of her words and the anguish in her manner, a bystander would have gathered that she had inflicted some cruel wrong, some intolerable insult, upon her venerable friend.
