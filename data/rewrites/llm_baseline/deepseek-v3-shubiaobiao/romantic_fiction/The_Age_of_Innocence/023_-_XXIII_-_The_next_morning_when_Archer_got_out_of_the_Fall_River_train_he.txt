The morning air clung thick and humid as Archer stepped off the Fall River train into the sweltering heart of Boston. The streets pulsed with the mingled scents of coffee, beer, and overripe fruit, a symphony of summer decadence. Shirt-sleeved men and women moved with the languid familiarity of those too accustomed to the heat to mind it. Archer hailed a cab, retreating into the comparative sanctuary of the Somerset Club, where he ordered breakfast with deliberate calm—melon first, then toast and eggs, the newspaper a shield against his own restless thoughts.

The night before, he had told May he had business in Boston. A lie, of course, but one so easily slipped into place it unsettled him. The letter from his office, left conspicuously on the hall table, had been providence itself. He smoked a cigarette afterward, feigning nonchalance as acquaintances drifted past with nods and pleasantries. The world went on as usual, even as he felt untethered from it.

At half-past nine, he penned a note and sent it to the Parker House. The reply, when it came, was a blade to the ribs: *The lady was out.* Impossible. She couldn’t be. He strode into the street, the city suddenly vast and alien around him.

And then—there she was.

On a bench beneath the trees, her gray silk sunshade tilted just so, as if she had been waiting for him without expectation. The sight of her—the slope of her neck, the quiet drape of her gloves—stopped him mid-step. When she turned, her eyes widened, then softened into a smile that seemed to say, *Of course. You.*

“Business,” he lied again, gesturing vaguely. “But what are *you* doing here?”

She laughed, low and rough at the edges, a sound he realized he had forgotten. “The same, I suppose.”

Words tangled between them. He noticed the way she had pinned her hair—different, or was it?—and she teased him for it. Then, almost absently, she confessed: *I’ve just refused a sum of money. Mine, but with conditions.*

Archer’s pulse spiked. “Your husband sent someone?”

Her smile turned wry. “An emissary. Waiting, even now, at the hotel.”

The thought of that unseen man, lurking with his offer, set Archer’s teeth on edge. Before he could stop himself, the plea was out: “Come away. Just for the day. The bay—it’s cooler there.”

She hesitated, but only for a breath. Then she stood, as if it were the simplest thing in the world to walk away from duty, from consequence.

On the steamboat, the roar of the engine spared them the need to speak. The city shrank behind them, and Archer felt the peculiar thrill of escape—not just from Boston, but from the weight of their own lives. She sat beside him, her face tipped toward the salt wind, utterly at ease.

At the inn, a boisterous party of schoolteachers shattered his hope of solitude. But she merely smiled when he secured a private room, as if she had expected nothing less. The space was plain, the checkered cloth and blueberry pie almost comically innocent. Yet there was something in her quiet composure that stripped the moment of any shame.

She had run away once before. She knew the cost. And yet here she was, unafraid, as if this stolen afternoon were nothing more than two old friends finding their way back to each other.

Archer watched her, the way the light touched her cheek, and wondered if she, too, felt the world tilting beneath them.
