The morning air was sharp with dew, even in the warmth of Grand Canary. My muscles ached from a night spent on the rough ground, and my companion, Dr. Coppinger—ever the health enthusiast—was already stretching with a pair of jagged lava stones in place of dumbbells. With no level ground for a proper run, I followed his lead, though my hands still stung from yesterday’s climb.

We washed in the thin stream at the barranca’s base, the luxury of a toothbrush never more appreciated. Our supplies had dwindled to crumbs and tobacco-tainted scraps, so we finished what little remained. Ten miles lay between us and the fonda at Santa Brigida, and with Coppinger determined to document every inch of these caves, hunger would be our companion long before we tasted the patron’s wine again.

Coppinger was convinced these caves held more than mummies—some lost seat of Guanche knowledge. I was less optimistic. The first few caves yielded nothing but dust and echoes, but he insisted on inspecting each himself, spinning theories about prisons, workshops, and priestly schools. By midday, the sun beat down mercilessly, and even his enthusiasm wavered. Yet he pressed on, until exhaustion forced him to concede.

Three caves remained. Reluctantly, I climbed back, rope burning my raw palms. This one was different—walls smoothed by tools, chambers linked by passages. No markings, no treasures—until my weight cracked a concealed ledge. Beneath the rubble lay sheets of talc, coated in dark wax, etched with strange patterns. Coppinger had sworn the Guanches had no writing, but this was something else.

I pried the cache free, lowering it to Coppinger below. His excitement was instant. "Egyptian!" he declared, though the script defied him. "But how it came here—" The wax, he theorized, was mineral, perhaps asphalt. A marvel lost to time.

By the time we reached Santa Brigida, he was feverish with discovery. I dined and slept; he labored all night. By morning, his eyes were red, but triumphant. "This isn’t just history—it’s a chronicle of Atlantis."

Deucalion, he said, had written it—a name I recognized from myths of the Flood. The pages teemed with beasts and empires lost: cave-tigers, colossal bats, a continent sunk beneath the waves. Phorenice, a ruler as fierce as any legend.

But time severed our collaboration. A summons back to England, a year’s silence—then news that Coppinger had donated "my" find to the British Museum, valuing it at ten thousand pounds. Our correspondence turned legal, cold.

So here it is: Deucalion’s story, as Coppinger translated it. The profits, by his own decree, are mine. Should that change, the world will know. But for now, the past speaks—for those willing to listen.
