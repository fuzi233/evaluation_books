I rose to continue my journey, trudging across miles of barren desert. My heart yearned for a mountain peak, or even a towering rock, from which I might survey the desolate plain and the dried riverbeds, perhaps spotting a sliver of hope on the horizon. Yet, what purpose could such foresight serve? The defining force in any man's destiny is not what lies ahead of his gaze, but what resides within his spirit; the internal operation precedes the external event. To foresee is not to comprehend—otherwise, the prophetic whisper latent in humanity would rise to the surface far more often.

The sun hung halfway to the horizon when a rugged ascent of rock appeared before me. But before I could reach it, the urge to climb evaporated, replaced by a desperate need to lie down. By then, twilight had deepened, and the air grew heavy with approaching night. At my feet spread a carpet of the softest, greenest moss—a royal couch. I collapsed onto it, and instantly, the tide of weariness receded. The moment my head touched the ground, for the third time, I heard the sound of many waters below, playing fractured tunes and ethereal harmonies against the stones of their buried channels. It was the loveliest chaos of musical raw material the Aquarian harp could send to my ears! Who knows what Handel might have crafted from that recurring gurgle and bell-like drip, blending and destroying each other in a shared refrain?

As I lay listening, my eyes wandered up and down the steep rocky slope above me, reading the history carved into its face: ages ago, a mighty cataract had rushed here, filling the channels that had guided me to its base. My heart swelled at the memory of that splendid tumult, where waves danced in helpless descent, massing their power into one thunderous roar below. Soon, however, the hidden brooks lulled me into sleep, their lullabies weaving themselves into my dreams.

I awoke before dawn and climbed eagerly to see what lay beyond. Alas, there was nothing but a desert of fine, powdery sand! No trace remained of the river that had plunged down the rocks; the drift had filled its course to the level of the dreary expanse. Looking back, I saw the river had split into two branches—one leading to the bank where I now stood at the foot of the rocky scarp, and the other crossing to the Evil Wood. I could see the wood between the two streams on the far horizon. To my left, the desert stretched endlessly; to my right, a lift in the skyline offered a glimmer of hope—the forest my hostess had mentioned.

Sitting down, I reached into my pocket for the half-loaf I had carried. Only then did I truly grasp my hostess's warning. The bread was not for tomorrow; it had shrunk and hardened into a stone. I cast it aside and set off again.

By noon, I encountered a few tamarisks and junipers, then some stunted firs. As I progressed, thicker thickets and larger pines greeted me. Eventually, I entered a forest of pines and similar trees, exactly like the one where the Little Ones raise their young. I felt as though I had returned to a further section of the same place. But what difference did *where* make when *everywhere* was identical to *nowhere*? I had not yet transformed any location into a place through action. I was not yet alive; I was merely dreaming that I lived. I was but a consciousness with an outlook. Indeed, in the world I had left, I had been nothing else, and now I understood the truth.

I told myself that if I caught a faint gleam of the Mirror in this forest, I would turn sharply away, lest it trap me unawares and drag me back to my old existence. Here, amidst the unknown, I might finally learn to be someone by doing something. I could not bear the thought of returning with so many beginnings and no ending achieved. The Little Ones would meet their appointed fate; the awful Witch would remain unseen; the dead would ripen and rise without me. I would only wake to realize I had dreamed, and that all my traveling had been futile. Rather than arrive at such a close, I would keep moving forward. I ventured deeper into the wood, weary but determined to rest within it.

The trees were now massive, standing in a regular, almost geometric formation with spacious intervals between them. Undergrowth was sparse, allowing me to see far in every direction. The forest resembled a great cathedral—solemn, silent, and empty. I met no creature on two legs or four that day. Occasionally, a swift thing or a slow one would cross the path where my gaze settled, but always at a distance, enhancing the profound sense of vast emptiness. I heard a few birds and saw countless butterflies, some of marvellous, complex coloring, others of pure, dazzling white.

Reaching a spot where the pines stood farther apart, revealing flowering shrubs, I hoped this signaled nearby habitation. Hungry for the voice and face of my kind—for any living soul, human or otherwise, whom I might understand—I followed the trail of increasingly abundant roses. What a hellish horror it was to wander alone, a bare existence that never expanded outward, never widening its life through connection. Bound by the cords of its own peculiarities, I was an eternal prisoner in the dungeon of my own being! I began to learn that it was impossible to live for oneself unless in the presence of others—and then, alas, fearfully possible! Evil exists only through good; selfishness is but a parasite on the Tree of Life.

In my former world, I had the habit of solitary song. Here, not a crooning murmur ever parted my lips. There, I sang without thinking; here, I thought without singing. There, I had never known a bosom friend; here, the affection of an idiot would be divinely welcome. "If only I had a dog to love!" I sighed, regarding my past self with wonder. That version of me preferred the company of books and pens to that of men and women; if the author of a tale I enjoyed appeared, I would wish him away so I could return to his story. I had chosen the dead over the living, the thing thought over the thing thinking.

"Any man," I declared now, "is more than the greatest of books!" I had neglected my living brothers and sisters, and now I was left without even the dead to comfort me.

The woods thinned further, and the pines grew taller, sending up huge stems like columns eager to support the heavens. Other trees appeared, making the forest richer. The roses had grown into trees, their flowers displaying astonishing splendor. Suddenly, I spied what seemed a great house or castle. Yet its forms were so strangely indistinct that I could not be certain it was anything more than a chance combination of tree shapes. As I drew nearer, the lines held together, but neither they nor the structure grew more definite. Standing before it, I remained as doubtful as before. Was it a habitable house or castle? Perhaps a ruin overgrown with ivy and roses! Yet, hiding within the foliage, I could discern not the poorest remnant of a wall.

Again and again, I seemed to spy what must be a building, only for it to vanish upon closer inspection. Could it be that ivy had embraced a huge edifice and consumed it, its interlaced branches retaining the shapes of the walls it had assimilated? I could be sure of nothing. Before me lay a rectangular vacancy—the ghost of a doorway without a door. I stepped through and found myself in an open space like a great hall, its floor covered with grass and flowers, its walls and roof woven from ivy, vine, and roses. There could be no better place to spend the night!

I gathered a quantity of withered leaves, piled them in a corner, and threw myself upon them. A red sunset filled the hall; the night was warm, and my couch restful. I lay gazing up at the living ceiling, tracing its network of branches and twigs, its clouds of foliage, and the peeping patches of the loftier roof. My eyes waded through the canopy until the sun dipped below the horizon and the sky began to darken.

Then the red roses turned black, leaving only the yellow and white blooms visible. When those vanished, the stars emerged, hanging in the leaves like live topazes, throbbing, sparkling, and flashing with myriad colors. I was canopied by a tree from Aladdin's Cave!

I discovered the hall was full of nests, from which tiny, nearly indistinguishable heads popped out with a chirp or two before disappearing again. For a while, there were rustlings, stirrings, and little prayers. But as darkness deepened, the small heads fell still. Every feathered mother tucked her brood quietly under her wings; the chatter in the little beds ceased, and God's bird-nursery rested beneath the waves of sleep.

Once more, a few flutterings made me look up. An owl sailed across. I caught only a glimpse, yet felt the cool wafture of his silent wings several times. The mother birds did not move; they sensed he sought mice, not children.

About midnight, I woke abruptly, roused by a revelry whose noises were not loud, yet not distant either. They were close, but attenuated.
