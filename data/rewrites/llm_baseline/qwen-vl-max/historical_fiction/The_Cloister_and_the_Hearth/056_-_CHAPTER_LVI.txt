Gerard found modest lodgings on the west bank of the Tiber, where each morning he set out in search of work. Carrying samples of his handwriting—Latin script carefully penned—he visited every shop in Rome that might need such services. But the response was cool and dismissive. “Our letters are finer,” one merchant remarked. “Your ink is too dark,” said another. The common refrain, however, echoed with finality: “What good is this? Latin writing is scarce here now. Can you write Greek?”

“I can,” Gerard replied, “but not as well as Latin.”

“Then you’ll never earn your bread in Rome,” they declared.

Undeterred, he borrowed a beautiful Greek manuscript at great cost, returning home with a lighter purse but unshaken resolve. In just two weeks, he made remarkable progress in mastering the Greek alphabet. To make the most of his time, he worked on his hand until midday, then spent the afternoons seeking clients.

When he returned with a specimen surpassing anything they had seen, the traders were still unimpressed. “Greek and Latin alike are unsaleable,” they told him. “The city swarms with books from across Europe. You should have come last year.”

Still, Gerard refused to give up. He bought a psaltery, hoping music might bring some solace. His landlady, a woman of warm eyes and gentle manners, often spoke kindly to him. One day, she invited him to dine with her, and when he seemed subdued, she asked what weighed so heavily on his heart.

He confessed his struggles.

She listened, then offered her own insight. “Those merchants,” she said firmly, “have their own writers—paid hands who produce work for a noble’s price while receiving barely enough to survive. No wonder they treat you coldly. I think you write too well. How do I know? Because you don’t lock your door like Pietro, that sour man next door. Women are curious, and you’re open—so you must be good.”

Gerard asked for clarification.

“Because,” she explained, “your skill could ruin the work they sell. It would blind them.”

He sighed. “Ah, dear lady, you judge people harshly—yet you are so kind yourself.”

“My dear boy,” she smiled, “Romans are cunning. I am Siennese, thank the Virgin.”

“I regret leaving Augsburg,” Gerard said.

“Augsburg?” she replied, raising an eyebrow. “Is that even worth mentioning beside Rome? I’ve never heard of it.”

Yet she assured him he would succeed, despite the bookshops’ lies. “They deceive strangers without reason or shame. Everyone knows the nobility here are obsessed with writing—spending fortunes on manuscripts, turning homes and lands into parchment to store in chests. God help them through this madness, as He has others. And truthfully, there’s been less bloodshed, fewer factions tearing each other apart, since scribbling became fashionable. Take Cardinal Bassarion and the Pope himself—they both commission endless works. A dozen men like you could write for them night and day. I’ll speak to Teresa; she hears all the court gossip.”

The next day, she returned with news: five more noble patrons had fallen under the “writing spider’s” spell.

Gerard took down their names, purchased parchment, and spent days preparing samples. He left one at each door—complete with name and address—and waited.

No reply came.

Day after day passed, and hope dwindled. Unbeknownst to him, Margaret back in Rotterdam was battling hardship, feeding her dependents and arrested for healing without a license.

Gerard saw ruin ahead. He turned to music, learning canzonets by afternoon. He bought playing cards to color, and reduced his meals to one per day—a desperate move that soon brought new trouble.

In these lodgings, meals were prepared by the hostess, though guests provided the ingredients. When she discovered he was cooking for himself, she confronted him sharply. “Are you ashamed of yourself?” she demanded, making him flush with embarrassment.

But her anger softened instantly. She leaned forward, earnest. “Adversity cannot be overcome on an empty stomach. Patienza, my lad—times will change. For now, I’ll feed you freely.”

“No, hostess,” Gerard insisted. “My purse isn’t empty yet. I’d rather not cause harm by withholding what’s due.”

“You’re as mad as Pietro,” she said, “with his single bad painting.”

“How do you know it’s bad?” he asked.

“Because no one will buy it. There’s a man who has no gift. He’ll have to take up arms and carry his canvas as a shield.”

Gerard perked up. “You know Pietro?”

She nodded. “He came from Florence with money and a half-finished picture. Took one room opposite yours, furnished it neatly. When done, visitors came and offered fair prices—but he refused so proudly he made enemies. Since then, he’s taken it out again and again, always bringing it back. Last month, I saw his furniture vanish one by one. Now he wears only one suit, sleeps on a chest. I learned this by peeking through the keyhole—he locks his door whenever he leaves.”

“Does he fear we’ll steal his chest or his picture?”

“Why, no. He hides his poverty.”

“And the fool! Are all hearts so black as his? At least let us test him first.”

“Yet you speak so harshly. His fate is mine—and he’s a countryman of yours.”

“Oh, we Siennese love strangers. His case is not yours. You’re the handsomest youth ever to stay here—golden hair, bright eyes. He’s dark, sour-faced. And you know how to charm a woman; he does not. Still, I wish he wouldn’t starve in my house—it brings me ill repute. One starving man is enough. You’re far from home. I, as mistress here, shall count your meals—for me and your mother, far away. We two women will manage that. Mind your own business, being a man, and leave cooking to us, who live for little else but roasting fowl, nursing men at birth, and sweeping their grown-up cobwebs.”

“Dear lady,” Gerard said, “you often remind me of my mother.”

“All the better,” she beamed. “I’ll make you *more* like her before I’m done.”

Gerard, neither vain nor blinded by affection, saw his own sorrow mirrored in Pietro’s pride. The more he thought, the more he resolved to share what little he had. Pietro’s dignity, he believed, would repay him.

He tried to speak with him—without success. Then one day, he heard groaning from the room. He knocked. No answer. He knocked again.

A gruff voice barked: “Enter.”

He stepped in timidly.

The garret held little: a chair, a picture facing the wall, an iron basin, an easel, and a long chest upon which coiled a gaunt young man with eyes like fire. Never had Gerard seen anything so like a cobra poised to strike.

“Good Signor Pietro,” he began, “forgive me. I grow weary of solitude and intrude upon yours. I am your nearest neighbor, and perhaps your brother in misfortune. I too am an artist.”

“Are you a painter?” Pietro asked, leaping off the chest and gesturing grandly toward the bed. “Welcome, stranger. Sit.”

Gerard bowed, smiling slightly, but hesitated.

“I cannot call myself a painter,” he said. “I am a scribe, a caligrapher. I copy Greek and Latin manuscripts when I can find them.”

“And you call that art?”

“Without offense to your greater merit, Signor Pietro.”

“None at all. But to me, an artist is one who thinks—and paints his thoughts. A scribe merely traces the words of others.”

“Well said. Yet a writer may record the wisdom of ancient minds, truths beyond paint—yes, even divine thoughts, which no angel could depict. But let that pass. I am a painter too—though a poor one.”

“The better for you. They’ll buy your work in Rome.”

“But to commend myself to one of your eminence, I thought it wiser to call myself a capable writer than a clumsy painter.”

At that moment, footsteps echoed on the stairs.

“Ah!” Gerard exclaimed. “It’s the hostess. Good dame, I am here with Signor Pietro. I trust he’ll allow me to eat my dinner here.”

Pietro bowed gravely.

The landlady entered, carrying a steaming dish. She placed it silently on the chest and left.

Gerard began to eat.

But after a few bites, he paused. “I am a rude fellow, Signor Pietro. I never enjoy food alone. For the love of Heaven, let me share this ragout with you. It’s quite good, I assure you.”

Pietro fixed him with a glittering stare.

“You—a stranger—offer me your dinner?”

“Indeed. There’s more than one person can eat.”

“Very well,” Pietro said calmly, taking the dish. Then, in a sudden burst of fury, he hurled its contents out the window.

Turning, trembling with humiliation and rage, he said: “Let this teach you never to offer alms to an artist you do not know, master writer.”

Gerard flushed with anger. He fought hard not to strike the proud man. And to waste food—his mother’s milk curdled in his veins at such sacrilege.

Yet pity for Pietro’s pride, and a flicker of respect for his stubbornness, won out.

“I suppose,” Gerard said coldly, “what you did might suit a tale by Boccaccio—but it was not honest.”

“Make it good!” Pietro snapped.

“I offered you half my meal. You took it all. You had right to discard your portion—but not mine. Pride is noble, but justice is greater.”

Pietro stared. Then slowly, he reflected.

“You are right. I mistook you for a fool—so transparent was your kindness. Forgive me. And please go. You see how I am. The world has soured me. I hate mankind. I was not always so. Once more, forgive my rudeness. Farewell.”

Gerard sighed and turned toward the door.

But then—an idea struck him.

“Signor Pietro,” he said, “we Dutchmen are famed for bargaining. We are the lads ‘een eij scheeren’—to shave an egg. So for my lost dinner, I claim the right to feast my eyes upon your picture—whose face is turned to the wall.”

“Nay, nay,” Pietro cried, alarmed. “Do not ask that. I’ve already wronged you enough. I would not shed your blood.”

“Saints forbid! My blood?”

“Stranger,” Pietro muttered, “after repeated insults to my picture—my child, my soul—I vowed in anger to drive my dagger into the next who mocked it.”

“You mean to kill anyone who fails to praise it?”

“Not exactly. If you would simply look—and keep silent—I would not turn it to the wall forever. But you will talk. So I hide it. Would I were dead, buried within it!”

Gerard considered. “I accept your condition. Show me the picture. I swear I will hold my peace.”

Pietro turned the canvas, placing it in the best light the room allowed, then coiled himself once more on the chest, stiletto gleaming.

The painting showed the Virgin and Christ soaring through the air, surrounded by shadowy cherubs. Below stretched a vast landscape—forty or fifty miles wide—under a purple sky.

Gerard stood in silence. Then stepped close. Then drew back. He studied it for thirty minutes, wordless.

At last, Pietro snapped: “Well, have you nothing to say?”

Gerard blinked. “Forgive me. I forgot there were three of us. Yes—I have much to say.” And he drew his sword.

“Alas!” Pietro shrieked, leaping from the chest. “What do you mean?”

“To defend myself against your dagger,” Gerard said. “And fairly, as a Dutchman. So stand back while I deliver judgment—or I’ll pin you to the wall like a cockchafer.”

“Oh!” Pietro breathed, relieved. “I feared you’d stab my poor picture—already wounded by so many cruel tongues.”

Gerard pursued criticism under duress—sword point aimed at Pietro, eye darting to the painting.

“First, signor,” he began, “in mixing pigments and preparing oil, your Italians lag behind our Flemings. But let that pass. Though small, I can reveal secrets of the Van Eycks—secrets that would enrich your next work. Meanwhile, in this piece, I see the genius of your nation. Truly, you are sons of the sun. We have color—you have imagination. Mother of Heaven! He has poured his immortal soul onto the panel. One thing I note: this makes other pictures I once admired seem crude, earthbound. The drapery here is stiff—why not let it flow freely? The figures are airborne—why not let the cloth breathe with motion?”

“I will! I will!” Pietro cried. “I’ll do anything for those who truly see!”

“Humph. This landscape enlightens me. From now on, I scorn the tiny, cramped scenes I once praised. Here is nature’s true face—vast plains, distances marked, trees, houses, rivers shrinking in perfect gradation until vision itself dissolves. O, beautiful! And the clever rogue hangs his celestial figures above the world, untouched by earthly chaos. Saints float beneath heaven’s purple veil. Far below, Earth and her busy hives. And they let you carry this painted poem, this blooming hymn, through Rome’s streets—unsold. But I tell you, in Ghent or Bruges—even Rotterdam—they’d tear it from your hands. Yet it’s said a stranger sees clearest. Courage, Pietro Vanucci! I reverence you. Though I am a poor painter, I forgive you—for being a great one. Not forgive—thank God for you, and men like you. I bow in homage. Your picture is immortal. And you, sitting on a chest, are a king in your royal art. Viva, il maestro! Viva!”

At these words, Pietro, overwhelmed, threw himself upon Gerard’s neck.

“They called it a madman’s dream,” he sobbed.

“Madmen themselves!” shouted Gerard. “No—idiots!”

“Generous stranger! I will hate no man again—when the world holds men like you. I was a viper to throw away your dinner—a wretch, a monster!”

“Monster,” Gerard said gently, “will you sup with me now?”

“Oh, yes! Where are you going?”

“To order supper at once. We’ll have the picture as our third guest.”

“I’ll invite it while you’re gone. My poor picture—child of my heart.”

“Oh, master,” Gerard smiled, “it will watch many suppers after worms have eaten us both.”

“I hope so,” Pietro whispered.
