Major Pendennis sauntered home like a man who’d just survived a minor war—possibly against his own conscience, possibly against the weather, but definitely not against any enemy of note. He arrived at the George Inn to find Mr. Morgan, his valet, standing like a sentry outside the door, as if guarding the entrance to some sacred chamber of domesticity.

“Sir,” said Morgan, with all the solemnity of a man announcing a royal visit, “there’s a young gentleman in the coffee-room you might care to see.”

“Is Arthur there?” asked the Major, already bracing himself for a lecture on duty, honor, and the proper way to wear a cravat.

“No, sir,” replied Morgan, “but his great friend, Mr. Foker, is. Lady Agnes Foker’s son. He’s been asleep since dinner, sir—just rang for coffee—and I thought, perhaps, you might fancy a chat.”

With that, he opened the door like a stagehand revealing a scene from *The Comedy of Errors*.

Inside sat Mr. Henry Foker, looking every inch the idle aristocrat who’d just woken up from a nap induced by too much food and too little ambition. His eyes were still half-closed, his legs sprawled across the bench, and the table before him bore the wreckage of a feast: empty decanters, crumbs, and what looked suspiciously like a forgotten slice of plum pudding. The play had clearly been cancelled in favor of a more important performance: the art of dozing off mid-conversation.

The Major hesitated—how does one approach someone who has just emerged from a coma of gastronomic excess? But Foker, ever the social virtuoso, beat him to it.

“Like to look at the evening paper, sir?” he asked, lifting the *Globe* as though offering a peace treaty.

“Thank you,” said the Major, accepting it with a bow that suggested he was receiving an order of chivalry.

“I believe I’m speaking to Mr. Henry Foker,” he continued, “Lady Agnes’s son. I’ve had the pleasure of knowing her ladyship—though I must say, you’ve got quite the Rosherville face about you.”

Foker blinked. “Hullo! I beg your pardon—I took you for a commercial gent.” Then he stopped, realizing how rude that sounded. “To whom have I the pleasure of speaking?”

“To a relative of a friend and schoolfellow of yours—Arthur Pendennis, my nephew. He’s spoken highly of you, and I’m Major Pendennis, whom he may have mentioned.”

“Sir, you do me proud!” said Foker, beaming. “And so you’re Arthur’s uncle?”

“And guardian,” added the Major, puffing out his chest slightly.

“He’s as good a fellow as ever stepped,” said Foker. “Clever too—I was always a bit of a dunce myself, but I know talent when I see it.”

“You show both taste and modesty,” said the Major, flattered.

“Ah, well,” said Foker, wagging his head, “I’m no bookworm. Never could manage Latin or Greek. But Pendennis—he used to write half the class’s poetry. Still—” he paused dramatically—“you’re his guardian, aren’t you? And I hope you won’t mind me saying this: I think he’s what we call… flat.”

The Major nearly dropped the *Globe*. “Flat?”

“About women,” explained Foker, winking as though he were sharing a state secret. “You know—romantic affairs. He’s gone and fallen for Miss Fotheringay—Costigan’s real name, you know—an absolute stunner. I introduced him to the General—her father—a regular old rogue, loves his whisky-and-water—but now they’re thick as thieves. And I’m blessed if he hasn’t proposed.”

He slapped the table so hard the dessert trembled.

“What!” said the Major. “You know about it?”

“Know it? Of course I do. We were talking about it at mess yesterday—chaffing poor Sir Derby Oaks until he was fit to burst. Know him? Dined together. We were smoking outside the theatre when you passed in for dinner.”

“I remember Sir Thomas Oaks,” said the Major, “before he became a baronet. Lived in Cavendish Square, physician to Queen Charlotte.”

“The young one,” said Foker, “is making the money spin. And yes—he’s another suitor.”

“Another what?”

“Another admirer of Miss Fotheringay. We call him Mondays, Wednesdays, and Fridays. Pen’s Tuesdays, Thursdays, and Saturdays. But don’t worry—nothing wrong. Miss F. is far too sharp for that. She plays one off against the other. Two strings to her bow.”

“I think you seem rather wide-awake yourself,” said the Major, laughing.

“Pretty well, thank you, sir. How are you?” replied Foker, utterly unshaken. “I’m not clever, maybe—but I’m downy. And friends say I know what time it is. Can I tell you the hour?”

“Oh, indeed,” said the Major, delighted. “You might be very useful. You’re a man of the world. And as such, I needn’t tell you that our family is most displeased with this absurd affair Arthur’s involved in.”

“Should think not,” said Foker. “Connexion not eligible. Too much beer drunk on the premises. No Irish need apply. That’s what I take to be your meaning.”

The Major nodded, though he wasn’t entirely sure what the meaning was. But he pressed on.

“What can you tell me about the Costigans?”

“Well,” said Foker, “the General enjoys a jovial bowl now and then. If I wanted to invest my money safely, I wouldn’t trust it to him. But he keeps a watchful eye on his daughter. Neither he nor she will stand for anything dishonourable. Pen’s attentions are the talk of the regiment. I hear all about it from Miss Rouncy—used to be close friends with her. She says Sir Derby Oaks has been hanging around Miss Fotheringay ever since his regiment came down. But Pen’s come in and cut him out lately—so mad has the Baronet become, he’s nearly proposed himself. Wish he would. Then we’d see which one Miss F. jumps at.”

“I suspected as much,” said the Major. “You’ve given me great pleasure, Mr. Foker. I wish I’d met you sooner.”

“Didn’t want to put in my oar,” said Foker. “Don’t speak till I’m asked. Then, if there’s no objection, I speak freely. Heard your man was sniffing around my servant—didn’t know what was going on until Miss Fotheringay and Miss Rouncy had a row over ostrich feathers. Then Miss R. told me everything.”

“So Miss Rouncy was the confidante?”

“Confidante? Indeed. Why, she’s twice as clever as Fotheringay—literary, educated. Miss F. can barely read.”

“She can write,” said the Major, remembering the letter in Pen’s pocket.

Foker let out a sardonic laugh. “He, he! Rouncy writes her letters—every single one. Since they quarrelled, she doesn’t know how to get on. Rouncy’s hand is beautiful. Miss F.’s? Well, when Bows isn’t around, it’s a disaster. Rouncy’s been setting her copies lately.”

“I suppose you know it well,” said the Major, archly.

Foker winked. “I’d give a great deal to have a specimen,” said the Major. “Could you spare one?”

“No, no—that’d be too bad,” said Foker. “But let me tell you—Miss F.’s writing ain’t terrible, I suppose. Only she had Rouncy write the first letter, and kept going. Mark my words: until they’re reconciled, the letters stop.”

“I hope they never reconcile,” said the Major, with genuine fervor. “And I can’t tell you how pleased I am to have met you. As a man of the world, you must see how disastrous this engagement is for Arthur’s future. We must free him from these schemers—father and daughter alike. Love on the lady’s side? Out of the question.”

“Love, indeed!” said Foker. “If Pen hadn’t two thousand a year when he came of age—”

“If Pen hadn’t what?” cried the Major, suddenly sitting upright.

“Two thousand a year. Doesn’t he?”

“My dear sir,” shrieked the Major, “thank you! Thank you! I begin to see now. Two thousand a year? Why, his mother has five hundred in the world. She’ll live to eighty, and Arthur hasn’t a shilling beyond what she allows.”

“What! He ain’t rich?”

“Not a penny more than I said.”

“And you’re not leaving him anything?”

The Major sighed. He’d spent every shilling on annuities. He wouldn’t leave Pen a thing. But he didn’t say so.

“How much do you think a major on half-pay can save?” he asked.

“Ah,” said Foker, “then these people have been mistaken. They thought he was wealthy. And you’ve made me the happiest man alive.”

“Sir to you,” said Foker, shaking hands with great cordiality.

As the Major climbed the stairs to his room, and Foker lit a cigar by the inn’s pillars, Arthur—ten miles away—was likely lying in bed, kissing a letter from Emily.

The next morning, before Foker drove off in his drag, the Major had managed to slip a letter from Miss Rouncy into his pocket-book. Let it be a lesson to all women: beware of overconfidence in handwriting.

In high spirits, Major Pendennis visited Doctor Portman at the Deanery and recounted his discoveries. Over breakfast, they could see Captain Costigan’s window—where poor Pen had once been plainly visible.

The Doctor raged against Mrs. Creed, the landlady, for hiding Sir Derby Oaks’s visits. He threatened excommunication.

But the Major, wise in the ways of battle, felt ready.

“I’m going to fight the dragon,” he declared.

“And I shrive you,” said the Doctor, “and bid good fortune go with you.”

Perhaps the Portmans and Miss Myra, seated with the Dean’s lady, glanced up more than once at the enemy’s window, hoping to catch sight of the duel.

The Major walked round, found Mrs. Creed’s door, and ascended to Captain Costigan’s apartment. Inside, he heard stamping and shouts of “Ha, ha!”

“It’s Sir Derby Oaks taking fencing lessons,” said the child guiding him. “Mondays, Wednesdays, Fridays.”

The Major knocked. A tall man emerged, foil in hand, mask dangling, glove on one hand.

Pendennis bowed. “I believe I have the honour of speaking to Captain Costigan. My name is Major Pendennis.”

Captain Costigan saluted with his foil. “Major, the honer is moine; I’m deloighted to see ye.”
