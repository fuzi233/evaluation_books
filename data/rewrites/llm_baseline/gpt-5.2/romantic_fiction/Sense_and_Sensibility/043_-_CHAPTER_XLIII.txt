Marianne rose the next morning at her usual hour, and to every question returned the same faint answer—that she was better—then tried to prove it by taking up the small occupations which had once been her pleasure. But there is a kind of diligence that exposes a body’s falsehood: she sat drawn close to the fire, a book open in her hand, yet her eyes slid over the lines as if the print had lost its language; when she attempted to walk, she shivered as though the very air upon her skin were an unkind draught. By evening she yielded early to bed, more indisposed than she would confess.

Colonel Brandon could not understand how Elinor maintained so even a composure, though she had watched and tended all day in spite of Marianne’s impatience, and at night had insisted—gently, firmly—on the draughts and powders thought proper. Elinor trusted, as Marianne did, to the old household hope that sleep, like a warm broth, might set everything right. But the night was restless, feverish, and thin with waking.

In the morning Marianne persisted in rising, then confessed—at last, and as if to spare them the struggle—she could not sit up, and returned of her own accord to bed. Elinor, now ready to take Mrs. Jennings’s counsel, sent for the Palmers’ apothecary.

Mr. Harris came, examined his patient with practised fingers and a cool attention, and spoke encouragement—only a few days, he said, and she would be restored. Yet he let fall words that turned the room colder than the winter panes: the disorder had a putrid tendency; there might be infection. At that, Mrs. Palmer’s whole mind flew, with the fierce swiftness of maternal terror, to her baby. The house itself seemed suddenly altered to her ear: every footstep in the corridor a threat; every closed door the muffling of danger.

Mrs. Jennings, who had looked on Marianne’s complaint as serious from the first, grew grave indeed under Mr. Harris’s report. She urged Charlotte’s fears, insisted on caution, and pressed that Mrs. Palmer must remove herself and her infant immediately. Mr. Palmer laughed at apprehension as he always did, as if fear were a vulgar noise best silenced by wit, yet his wife’s distress beat upon him without ceasing; and even his obstinacy could not stand against the crying importunity of a young mother with her child in her arms.

So within an hour of the apothecary’s arrival, Mrs. Palmer was gone—her little boy and his nurse carried off to the house of a relation on the far side of Bath. Mr. Palmer promised, under repeated entreaty, to follow in a day or two, and she had been nearly as urgent that Mrs. Jennings should accompany her.

Mrs. Jennings would not stir.

With a kindness that made Elinor truly love her, she declared herself fixed at Cleveland while Marianne remained ill, determined to supply—by sheer constancy and bustling care—the place of the mother they had taken Marianne from. Elinor found her, in every hour, a willing and active helpmate; there was a comfort in the very sound of Mrs. Jennings’s presence—the soft thump of a cushion set right, the quick rustle of skirts, the kettle beginning its low, domestic song—proof that the world still knew how to nurse. Her experience was often of real use; she could fold a warmed flannel with a sure hand, coax a reluctant patient to swallow a draught, and order the sick-room like a well-run kitchen where nothing is wasted and every minute matters.

Poor Marianne, languid and low, feeling herself universally ill, could no longer persuade herself that tomorrow would be recovery. And what tomorrow had been meant to bring—homeward travel, the surprise of their mother—made each ache sharper. They were to have begun their journey that day; they were to have been at Barton the next forenoon, with a servant from Mrs. Jennings to smooth the way. Marianne spoke little, but what she said was lamentation for the delay, for the disappointment of that imagined moment when their mother’s voice would have met them at the door. Elinor tried to rally her, tried to sweeten the thought as one sweetens a bitter cordial—“only a short delay,” she insisted, because she truly believed it.

The next day changed little. Marianne did not appear worse; she was certainly not better. Their party dwindled again. Mr. Palmer, though unwilling to go—unwilling from real humanity, and from a dislike of seeming frightened away by his wife—was at last persuaded by Colonel Brandon to keep his promise and follow Charlotte. While he prepared to depart, Colonel Brandon himself began to speak of going likewise, as though he could not endure the helpless waiting and must find motion, any motion, to keep his mind from dark imaginings.

Here Mrs. Jennings interposed with a shrewd tenderness. To send the Colonel away, she insisted, would be to rob them all of comfort; and besides, she declared, she should want him of an evening to play at piquet while Miss Dashwood sat upstairs. Her plea, half domestic comedy, half true necessity, pressed him to remain; and he, whose heart was gratified to obey, did not long affect reluctance—especially as Mr. Palmer seconded the request warmly, plainly relieved to leave behind someone able to advise in any emergency.

Marianne knew nothing of these arrangements. It never occurred to her that she had driven the owners of Cleveland away within seven days of their arrival. Mrs. Palmer’s absence surprised her not at all; and as it gave her no concern, she never named her.

Two days after Mr. Palmer’s departure, Marianne remained in much the same state. Mr. Harris attended daily, speaking boldly of speedy recovery; and Elinor, clinging to his confidence, was equally sanguine. The others were not. Mrs. Jennings had decided almost from the beginning that Marianne would never get over it, and Colonel Brandon—chiefly useful, now, in listening to Mrs. Jennings’s forebodings—had not strength of mind to resist them. He tried to reason himself out of fear, remembering the apothecary’s assurances; but long hours alone, when the house fell into its quieter sounds and the wind took up the louder ones, admitted every melancholy thought. In the hush between gusts he seemed to hear absence in advance, and could not drive away the persuasion that he should see Marianne no more.

On the morning of the third day, those gloomy anticipations were nearly undone. Mr. Harris arrived and declared his patient materially better—pulse stronger, symptoms more favourable. Elinor’s hope, confirmed, brightened into cheerfulness. She rejoiced that in her letters to her mother she had followed her own judgment rather than Mrs. Jennings’s, making light of the indisposition; she began almost to fix upon the day Marianne might travel.

But evening unmade the promise of the morning. Marianne became ill again—heavier, more restless, more uncomfortable than before. Elinor, still determined to hope, persuaded herself it was only fatigue from having sat up while the bed was made. She administered the cordials prescribed—those sharp, warming drops with their spirituous scent, meant to coax life back to the surface—and watched with a kind of hungry gratitude as Marianne sank at last into slumber.

The sleep was not as quiet as Elinor wished; yet it lasted long enough to tempt belief in its benefit. She resolved to sit by Marianne through it. Mrs. Jennings, knowing nothing of this change, went early to bed; her maid, one of the principal nurses, was downstairs taking her ease in the housekeeper’s room. Elinor was left alone with her sister.

The repose became more disturbed. Marianne shifted continually, her breath catching and slipping, and at intervals little sounds of complaint escaped her—broken, inarticulate, like a language half-forgotten. Elinor, bent over her, watched every movement, listening with the sharpened attention of love in fear; even the house’s smallest noises—the settling of wood, the distant chink of a hearth-tool—seemed too loud, too capable of startling a mind already unsteady.

Then some accidental sound in the house—an indistinct bump, a door somewhere caught by the wind—snapped Marianne awake. She started up with feverish wildness.

“Is mama coming?”

“Not yet,” Elinor answered, hiding her terror as one hides a flame with a hand, and easing Marianne back. “But she will be here, I hope, before it is long. It is a great way, you know, from hence to Barton.”

“But she must not go round by London,” Marianne cried, the words tumbling fast. “I shall never see her, if she goes by London.”

Elinor felt at once that Marianne was not quite herself. She tried to soothe her, and at the same time reached for her pulse. It was lower—and quicker—than ever. Marianne still spoke wildly of their mother; and Elinor’s alarm rose so fast it became decision. Mr. Harris must be sent for instantly; and a messenger dispatched to Barton for Mrs. Dashwood.

To consult Colonel Brandon on how best to effect the latter followed immediately. Elinor rang for the maid to take her place by Marianne, and hurried down to the drawing-room where she knew the Colonel often remained late.

There was no time for hesitation. Her fears and difficulties stood plain before him. He did not attempt to argue away her dread; he listened in silent despondence. But her difficulty—the messenger—was met at once. With a readiness that made it seem as if he had long prepared himself for this service, he offered to go himself and fetch Mrs. Dashwood.

Elinor made no resistance that could not be overcome. She thanked him briefly, fervently; and while he hurried off his servant with a message for Mr. Harris and orders for post-horses, she wrote a few lines to her mother.

The comfort of such a friend—such a companion for her mother—was felt with a gratitude too deep for ornament. His judgment would guide; his attendance would relieve; his steadiness might soften the shock of a summons like this. He, meanwhile, whatever storm was in him, acted with the firmness of a collected mind—arranged everything with swift exactness, calculated the time of his return. No moment was lost. The horses arrived even before they were expected. He pressed Elinor’s hand, looked at her with solemnity, murmured a few words too low to be caught, and hurried into the carriage.

It was about twelve o’clock. Elinor went back to Marianne’s room to wait for the apothecary and watch through the night.

It became a night of almost equal suffering to both. Hour after hour passed in sleepless pain and delirium for Marianne, and cruel anxiety for Elinor. Mr. Harris did not appear for long. Once Elinor’s fears were raised, they made payment—by excess—for all her former security. The servant who sat up with her, since she would not allow Mrs. Jennings to be called, tortured rather than soothed, with hints of what her mistress had always thought.

Marianne’s wandering returned again and again to her mother. Each time the name was spoken it struck Elinor like a blow. She reproached herself for having trifled with days of illness, for trusting too easily, for delaying what might now be irreparable. She pictured their mother arriving too late—too late to see Marianne living, or too late to see her rational.

She was on the point of sending again for Mr. Harris—or for someone else—when at last, not until after five o’clock, he came. His opinion made some amends for his delay. He acknowledged an unexpected and unpleasant alteration, but would not admit the danger to be material. A fresh mode of treatment, he declared, must procure relief; and though his confidence did not wholly enter Elinor’s heart, it steadied her. He promised to call again within three or four hours and left patient and attendant a little more composed than he had found them.

With strong concern and many reproaches for not being called sooner, Mrs. Jennings heard in the morning what had passed. Her old apprehensions returned with new force; she did not doubt the event. She tried to speak comfort, but her conviction would not allow her the comfort of hope. Her grief was real. The rapid decay, the early death of a girl so young and lovely would have struck even a stranger; and Marianne was not a stranger to her. Marianne had been her companion for three months, was under her care, and was known to have been injured and long unhappy. Elinor’s distress—especially dear to her—was before her; and when Mrs. Jennings thought what Marianne might be to her mother, as Charlotte was to herself, her sympathy for Mrs. Dashwood became sincere and aching.

Mr. Harris was punctual in his second visit, but he came to be disappointed. His medicines had failed. The fever was unabated. Marianne was quieter—yet not more herself—sunk in a heavy stupor. Elinor, catching in an instant all that he feared and more, proposed calling in further advice. He judged it unnecessary: he still had something more to try, another fresh application, and spoke of its success with the same confidence as before. His assurances reached Elinor’s ear but could not enter her heart. She remained calm except when she thought of her mother; but she was almost hopeless.

Thus she continued until noon, scarcely stirring from Marianne’s bed. Her thoughts wandered from one suffering friend to another, while Mrs. Jennings’s talk—well-meant, unguarded—pressed upon her the idea that this severity came from the many weeks of prior indisposition brought on by Marianne’s disappointment. Elinor felt the reasonableness of it, and it sharpened her misery.

About noon, however, she began—cautiously, with a dread of being deceived—to fancy a slight amendment in her sister’s pulse. She waited, watched, touched it again and again; and at last, with an agitation she could hardly conceal beneath outward steadiness, ventured to communicate her hope.

Mrs. Jennings, forced to acknowledge a temporary revival, still tried to keep Elinor from indulging any belief in its continuance. Elinor repeated to herself every injunction of distrust, told herself not to hope. But hope had already entered; and with its anxious flutter in her, she bent over Marianne as if watching for some small sign in a saucepan that the simmer had steadied—that the boil of danger had at last broken.

Half an hour passed, and the favourable symptom remained. Others followed: her breath, her skin, her lips all flattered Elinor with signs of amendment. Marianne opened her eyes and fixed them on her with a rational, though languid gaze. Anxiety and hope now weighed on Elinor together and left her no tranquillity until Mr. Harris arrived at four o’clock—when his assurances, his felicitations, his declaration that Marianne’s recovery surpassed even his expectation, gave Elinor confidence, comfort, and tears of joy. Marianne was materially better in every respect; she was entirely out of danger.

Mrs. Jennings, perhaps satisfied that her forebodings had found some justification in the late alarm, allowed herself to trust the apothecary now; she admitted, with unfeigned joy, and soon with plain cheerfulness, the probability of complete recovery.

Elinor could not be cheerful. Her joy was of another kind, leading to anything but gaiety. Marianne restored—to life, to health, to friends, and to her doting mother—filled her heart with exquisite comfort and fervent gratitude; yet it produced no smiles, no words of exultation. It was satisfaction silent and strong.

All the afternoon she remained by her sister with little intermission—calming fears, answering faint inquiries, supplying every small succour, watching almost every look and breath. The thought of relapse would sometimes rise of itself, to remind her what anxiety was; but each minute examination confirmed recovery. When, at six o’clock, Marianne sank into a quiet, steady, comfortable sleep, Elinor silenced every doubt.

The hour approached when Colonel Brandon might be expected back. At ten, she trusted—or not much later—her mother would be relieved from the dreadful suspense in which she must now be travelling. The Colonel too—how slow time moved while they knew nothing! The house’s sounds seemed to mark the delay: the wind in the chimneys, the low creak of a settling stair, each one distinct in the cold air, each one too present.

At seven o’clock, leaving Marianne still sweetly asleep, Elinor joined Mrs. Jennings in the drawing-room for tea. Fear had kept her from breakfast; the sudden reverse from dinner; so the warmth and fragrance of the tray were peculiarly welcome—the faint astringency of tea, the comforting breath of milk, the homely clink of cups that made a kind of peace in the ear. Mrs. Jennings would have persuaded her afterward to rest and let her take Elinor’s place by Marianne; but Elinor had no sense of fatigue, no capability of sleep. She could not be kept away an unnecessary instant.

Mrs. Jennings therefore went upstairs with her to see that all continued right, then left her again to her charge and thoughts, retiring to her own room to write letters and sleep.

The night was cold and stormy. The wind roared round the house; rain beat hard against the windows with a steady drumming that might have been terrible, had Elinor not felt such happiness within. Marianne slept through every blast. The travellers—whoever they were—had a rich reward in store for every present inconvenience.

The clock struck eight. Had it been ten, Elinor would have been convinced she heard a carriage drive up; so strong was the persuasion that, in spite of the almost impossibility of their being already come, she moved into the adjoining dressing-closet and opened a shutter to satisfy herself.

Her ears had not deceived her. The flaring lamps of a carriage were in view. By their uncertain light she thought she could discern four horses; and the excess of her poor mother’s alarm, which such haste proclaimed, explained such unexpected rapidity.

Never had Elinor found it so difficult to be calm. She knew what her mother must be feeling as the carriage stopped—the doubt, the dread, perhaps despair—and what she herself had to tell. With such knowledge, calm was impossible. All that remained was speed. She stayed only long enough to leave Mrs. Jennings’s maid with Marianne, then hurried downstairs.

The bustle in the vestibule, heard as she passed along an inner lobby, assured her they were already in the house. She rushed to the drawing-room—entered it—and saw only Willoughby.
