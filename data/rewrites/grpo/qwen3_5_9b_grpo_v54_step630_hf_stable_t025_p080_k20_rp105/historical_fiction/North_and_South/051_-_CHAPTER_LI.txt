It was a stifling summer evening, the air thick with the weight of the season. Edith entered Margaret's boudoir, the first time in her customary attire, the second time already dressed for dinner. The room was empty at first; upon her return, she found Dixon arranging Margaret's gown upon the bed, but Margaret herself was nowhere to be seen. Edith paced with restless agitation.

"Oh, Dixon! Those horrid blue flowers against that dead gold gown! What taste! Wait a moment, I shall fetch you some pomegranate blossoms instead."

"It is not dead gold, ma'am; it is straw-colour. And blue always suits straw-colour best." But Edith had already placed the brilliant scarlet blooms before Dixon could finish her protest.

"Where is Miss Hale?" Edith asked, once she had inspected the arrangement. "I cannot fathom," she continued, with a petulant air, "how my aunt permitted her to adopt such wandering habits in Milton! I am constantly expecting news of some dreadful accident among those wretched places she wanders into. I should never dare to traverse some of those streets without a servant; they are not fit for ladies."

Dixon remained sulky regarding her dismissed aesthetic judgment and replied curtly:

"It is no wonder, in my opinion, when I hear ladies discourse so extensively about ladies—and when they are such frightfully delicate, dainty creatures too—I say, it is no wonder that there are no longer any saints upon earth."

"Oh, Margaret! Here you are! I have been longing for you. But how flushed your cheeks are with the heat, poor child! Only think what that tiresome Henry has done; truly, he exceeds the bounds of a brother-in-law. Just when my party was assembled so beautifully, fitted with such precision for Mr. Colthurst, Henry arrives with an apology, using your name as an excuse, and asks if he may bring that Mr. Thornton of Milton—your tenant, you know—who is in London attending to some legal business. It will ruin my number entirely."

"I do not mind dinner. I require none," Margaret said in a low voice. "Dixon may bring me a cup of tea here, and I shall be in the drawing-room by the time you ascend. I shall be truly glad to lie down."

"No, no! That will never do. You do look wretchedly pale, to be sure; but that is merely the heat, and we cannot dispense with you. (Those flowers a trifle lower, Dixon. They look like glorious flames, Margaret, in your dark hair.) You know we planned for you to discuss Milton with Mr. Colthurst. Oh! Indeed! And this man comes from Milton. I believe it will prove capital, after all. Mr. Colthurst can interrogate him thoroughly on all subjects that interest him, and it will be great amusement to trace your experiences and this Mr. Thornton's wisdom in Mr. Colthurst's next speech in the House. Truly, I consider it a fortunate stroke of Henry's. I asked him if he was a man one would be ashamed of; he replied, 'Not if you possess any sense, my little sister.' So I suppose he is capable of pronouncing his h's, which is not a common accomplishment in Darkshire—eh, Margaret?"

"Did Mr. Lennox state why Mr. Thornton has come to town? Was it legal business connected with the property?" Margaret asked, her voice constrained.

"Oh! He has failed, or something of the sort; Henry told you of that very day you suffered from such a headache—what was it? (There, that is perfect, Dixon. Miss Hale does us credit, does she not?) I wish I were as tall as a queen and as brown as a gipsy, Margaret."

"But regarding Mr. Thornton?"

"Oh! I truly possess such a terrible head for legal affairs. Henry delights in telling you everything about it. The impression he made upon me was that Mr. Thornton is in dire straits, yet a very respectable man, and that I should treat him with great civility. Since I did not know how to proceed, I came to you to request your assistance. Now, come downstairs with me and rest upon the sofa for a quarter of an hour."

The favored brother-in-law arrived early. Margaret, flushing as she spoke, began to inquire of him the answers she sought concerning Mr. Thornton.

"He ascended regarding the sub-leasing of the property—Marlborough Mills, and the house and premises adjoining, I mean. He is unable to maintain it; there are deeds and leases to be examined and agreements to be drafted. I hope Edith will receive him properly; but she appeared somewhat displeased, as I could perceive, by the liberty I took in soliciting an invitation for him. However, I believed you would appreciate having him attended to; and one must be particularly scrupulous in showing every respect to a man who is descending in the world." He lowered his voice to address Margaret, seated beside him, but as he concluded, he sprang up and introduced Mr. Thornton, who had just entered, to Edith and Captain Lennox.

Margaret watched Mr. Thornton with an anxious gaze while he was thus occupied. It had been considerably more than a year since she had last seen him, and events had altered him significantly in that interval. His fine figure still elevated him above the average height of men, granting him a distinguished appearance derived from the ease of motion natural to him; yet his face appeared older and careworn. Nevertheless, a noble composure rested upon it, impressing those who had just heard of his changed circumstances with a sense of inherent dignity and manly strength. From the first glance he cast around the room, he was aware that Margaret was present; he had observed her intent look of occupation as she listened to Mr. Henry Lennox. He approached her with the perfectly regulated manner of an old friend. With his first calm words, a vivid color flashed into her cheeks, which never faded during the evening. She seemed to have little to say to him. She disappointed him by the quiet manner in which she asked what appeared to him to be merely necessary questions regarding her old acquaintances in Milton. Others entered—more intimate within the household than he—and he fell into the background, where he and Mr. Lennox conversed intermittently.

"You think Miss Hale looks well," said Mr. Lennox, "do you not? Milton did not agree with her, I imagine; for when she first came to London, I thought I had never seen anyone so changed. Tonight she appears radiant. But she is much stronger. Last autumn she was fatigued by a walk of two miles. On Friday evening we walked up to Hampstead and back. Yet on Saturday she looked as well as she does now."

"We!" Who? The two of them alone?

Mr. Colthurst was a very clever man and a rising member of Parliament. He possessed a keen eye for discerning character and was struck by a remark Mr. Thornton made during dinner. He inquired of Edith who that gentleman was; and, to her surprise, she found, from the tone of his "Indeed!", that Mr. Thornton of Milton was not such an unknown name to him as she had imagined. Her dinner proceeded well. Henry was in good spirits and displayed his dry, caustic wit admirably. Mr. Thornton and Mr. Colthurst discovered one or two mutual subjects of interest, which they could only touch upon then, reserving them for more private conversation after dinner. Margaret looked beautiful in the pomegranate flowers; and though she leaned back in her chair and spoke little, Edith was not annoyed, for the conversation flowed smoothly without her. Margaret was studying Mr. Thornton's face. He never looked at her; thus she might observe him unobserved, noting the changes this short time had wrought in him. Only at some unexpected word from Mr. Lennox did his face flash into the old look of intense enjoyment: the merry brightness returned to his eyes, the lips parted slightly to suggest the brilliant smile of former days; and for an instant, his glance instinctively sought hers, as if seeking her sympathy. But when their eyes met, his entire countenance changed; he became grave and anxious once more; and he resolutely avoided looking near her again during dinner.

There were only two ladies besides their own party, and as these were occupied in conversation by her aunt and Edith, when they ascended to the drawing-room, Margaret languidly employed herself with some work. Soon the gentlemen joined them, Mr. Colthurst and Mr. Thornton in close conversation. Mr. Lennox drew near to Margaret and said in a low voice:

"I truly think Edith owes me thanks for my contribution to her party. You have no idea what an agreeable, sensible fellow this tenant of yours is. He has been the very man to provide Mr. Colthurst with all the facts he needed coaching on. I cannot conceive how he managed to mismanage his affairs."

"With his powers and opportunities, you would have succeeded," said Margaret. He did not quite relish the tone in which she spoke, although the words merely expressed a thought that had passed through his own mind. As he remained silent, they caught a swell in the sound of conversation proceeding near the fireplace between Mr. Colthurst and Mr. Thornton.

"I assure you, I heard it spoken of with great interest—curiosity as to its result, perhaps I should rather say. I heard your name frequently mentioned during my short stay in the neighborhood." Then they lost some words; and when next they could hear Mr. Thornton speaking:

"I have not the elements for popularity—if they spoke of me in that way, they were mistaken. I fall slowly into new projects; and I find it difficult to let myself be known, even by those whom I desire to know, and with whom I would fain have no reserve. Yet, even with all these drawbacks, I felt that I was on the right path, and that, starting from a kind of friendship with one, I was becoming acquainted with many. The advantages were mutual: we were both unconsciously and consciously teaching each other."

"You say 'were.' I trust you intend to pursue the same course?"

"I must stop Colthurst," said Henry Lennox, hastily. And by an abrupt, yet appropriate question, he turned the current of the conversation so as not to give Mr. Thornton the mortification of acknowledging his lack of success and consequent change of position. But as soon as the newly-started subject had come to a close, Mr. Thornton resumed the conversation just where it had been interrupted and gave Mr. Colthurst the reply to his inquiry.

"I have been unsuccessful in business and have had to relinquish my position as a master. I am on the lookout for a situation in Milton, where I may find employment under someone willing to allow me to proceed in my own manner regarding such matters. I can rely on myself for having no forward theories that I would rashly bring into practice. My only wish is to have the opportunity of cultivating some intercourse with the hands beyond the mere 'cash nexus.' But it might be the point Archimedes sought from which to move the earth, judging by the importance attached to it by some of our manufacturers, who shake their heads and look grave as soon as I name the one or two experiments I should like to try."

"You call them 'experiments,' I notice," said Mr. Colthurst, with a delicate increase of respect in his manner.

"Because I believe them to be such. I am not sure of the consequences that may result from them. But I am sure they ought to be tried. I have arrived at the conviction that no mere institutions, however wise, and however much thought may have been required to organize and arrange them, can attach class to class as they should be attached, unless the working out of such institutions brings the individuals of the different classes into actual personal contact. Such intercourse is the very breath of life. A working man can hardly be made to feel and know how much his employer may have labored in his study at plans for the benefit of his workpeople. A complete plan emerges like a piece of machinery, apparently fitted for every emergency. But the hands accept it as they do machinery, without understanding the intense mental labor and forethought required to bring it to such perfection. But I would take an idea, the working out of which would necessitate personal intercourse; it might not go well at first, but at every hitch interest would be felt by an increasing number of men, and at last its success in working would come to be desired by all, as all had borne a part in the formation of the plan; and even then I am sure that it would lose its vitality, cease to be living, as soon as it was no longer carried on by that sort of common interest which invariably makes people find means and ways of seeing each other, and becoming acquainted with each others' characters and persons, and even tricks of temper and modes of speech. We should understand each other better, and I'll venture to say we should like each other more."

"And you think they may prevent recurrence of strikes?"

"Not at all. My utmost expectation only goes as far as this—that they may render strikes not the bitter, venomous sources of hatred they have hitherto been. A more hopeful man might imagine that a closer and more genial intercourse between classes might do away with strikes. But I am not a hopeful man."

Suddenly, as if a new idea had struck him, he crossed over to where Margaret was sitting and began, without preface, as if he knew she had been listening to all that had passed:

"Miss Hale, I received a round-robin from some of my men—I suspect in Higgins' handwriting—stating their wish to work for me, if ever I am in a position to employ men again on my own behalf. That was good, was it not?"

"Yes. Just right. I am glad of it," said Margaret, looking up straight into his face with her speaking eyes, and then dropping them under his eloquent glance.
