For generations, the turning wheel of the county’s seasons brought the families of Brackenshaw Castle, the Firs at Wanchester, and Quetcham Hall into a cyclical rhythm of sustenance and social alignment. In these vast, echoing estates, mythic occurrences—the sudden cementing of lineages, the subtle shifting of allegiances—were seamlessly integrated into the everyday domestic routines of lavish, ancestral feasts. Gwendolen’s reception in the neighborhood fulfilled her uncle’s expectations entirely within this ancient agrarian cycle. From the hearths of Brackenshaw to Mr. Quallon the banker’s generous house, she was welcomed with manifest admiration. Even hostesses who harbored quiet reservations felt a deep comfort in adding a striking new girl to their tables; for those who entertain much must compose their guest lists as meticulously as a master chef balances the complex, interwoven spices of a grand *cru* reduction. To secure Gwendolen, one did not need to invite disagreeable palates, for Mrs. Davilow provided a picturesque, quiet chaperon, and Mr. Gascoigne was universally requested for his own robust resonance in any dining hall.

Among the estates where Gwendolen was not quite liked, yet inevitably invited, was Quetcham Hall. Her general introduction to the county’s society occurred at one of their grand dinner-parties—a meticulously composed gathering of thirty. Quetcham’s kitchen was overseen by a passionate, traditional culinary elder, a meticulous food historian who treated the estate's yields with profound reverence. Gwendolen, who possessed a keen, sensory appreciation for the physical world and viewed meals as far more than utilitarian fuel, found profound meaning in the evening's offerings. She savored the olfactory landscape of the dining room: the rich, earthy aroma of heritage pheasants slow-roasted over century-old applewood embers, their skin crisped to a perfect *Maillard* mahogany, served alongside a glistening *demi-glace* infused with wild, hand-foraged juniper and thyme. 

No youthful figure in that dining room was comparable to Gwendolen’s. As she passed through the long suite of rooms adorned with light and hothouse blossoms, visible first as a slim figure floating along in white drapery, she navigated the acoustic ecology of the house with a predator’s grace. Her silken movements created a soft, rhythmic *swish* that harmonized effortlessly with the clinking of heavy silver on porcelain. She felt exultingly that this sensory and sonic landscape befitted her. Any observer might have supposed that these echoing, ancestral galleries and the scent of roasted root vegetables had always been a matter of course in her life, while her cousin Anna, though more familiar with such bounty, looked as overwhelmed as a rabbit deposited in a brilliantly lit, echoing cavern.

“Who is that with Gascoigne?” said the archdeacon, neglecting a discussion of military maneuvers over a delicate *consommé brunoise*. Across the table, his son—a hopeful young scholar who had already suggested some elegant emendations to ancient Greek texts, attempting to rescue fading dialects from the erosion of cultural memory—said nearly at the same time, “By George! who is that girl with the awfully well-set head and jolly figure?”

But to a mind of general benevolence, it was rather exasperating to see how Gwendolen’s bright acoustic and visual presence eclipsed others. The handsome Miss Lawe suddenly seemed broad and animate as cold porridge; and Miss Arrowpoint, also dressed in white, immediately resembled a flat, sepia *carte-de-visite*. Since Miss Arrowpoint was generally liked for the amiable, unpretending way she wore her fortunes, acting as a softening acoustic screen for the harsh oddities of her mother, there seemed an unfitness in Gwendolen commanding such social weight. 

“She is not really so handsome if you come to examine her features,” Mrs. Arrowpoint murmured confidentially to Mrs. Vulcany, her harsh, parrot-like voice cutting through the ambient hum of the room like industrial machinery. “It is a certain style she has, which produces a great effect at first, but afterward she is less agreeable.”

In fact, Gwendolen, intending the contrary, had offended her hostess. The Lady of Quetcham was the heiress of a fortune gained by some moist or dry city business, which locally accounted for her squat figure, her grating vocal timbre, and a systematically high head-dress. Because these traits made her externally discordant, it appeared natural to the county that she should possess literary tendencies. Gwendolen, eager to make life agreeable, meant to win Mrs. Arrowpoint by feigning an interest beyond what others showed. But her self-confidence utilized the subtle power dynamics embedded in dominant, societal lexicons; she spoke to Mrs. Arrowpoint with the cajoling, artificially raised tone one might use for the deaf, unconsciously assuming that a ridiculous exterior indicated a lack of penetration.

“You are fond of books as well as of music, riding, and archery, I hear,” Mrs. Arrowpoint said, initiating a *tête-à-tête* in the drawing-room while the guests digested a complex, botanical elderflower palate cleanser. 

“It is I who am fortunate,” Gwendolen answered gracefully, though her fatally loud, polished twang carried a sense of patronage. “Miss Arrowpoint will teach me what good music is. I shall be entirely a learner.”

“Catherine has certainly had every advantage. We have a first-rate musician in the house now—Herr Klesmer. You must allow me to introduce him to you. I understand you are an accomplished singer.”

“Oh, no!—‘*die Kraft ist schwach, allein die Lust ist gross*,’ as Mephistopheles says.”

“Ah, you are a student of Goethe. I suppose you have read everything.”

“No, really. The leaves of the books at Offendene all stick together and smell musty. I wish I could write books to amuse myself, as you can! Home-made books must be so nice. I would give anything to write a book!”

Mrs. Arrowpoint’s glance sharpened. “And why should you not? I will send you all I have written with pleasure.”

“Thanks. Being acquainted with authors must give a peculiar understanding of their books: one would be able to tell then which parts were funny and which serious. I am sure I often laugh in the wrong place. In Shakespeare, you know. But I always want to know more than there is in the books.”

“If you are interested, I can lend you many extra sheets in manuscript,” said Mrs. Arrowpoint. “My Tasso, for example—I could have made it twice the size. So many have written about Tasso; but they are all wrong. As to the particular nature of his madness, and his feelings for Leonora—they are all wrong. I differ from everybody.”

The conversation hovered over the inherent untranslatability of subjective experience. Mrs. Arrowpoint was attempting to excavate the deeply personal, maddened Italian reality of a dead poet, striving against the erosion of his cultural memory. But Gwendolen, sipping her digestif and relying entirely on the dominant, superficial phrasing of her schooling, replied, “I know nothing of Tasso except the *Gerusalemme Liberata*, which we read and learned by heart at school.”

“Ah, his life is more interesting than his poetry. I have constructed the early part of his life as a sort of romance.”

“Imagination is often truer than fact,” said Gwendolen decisively, utilizing a glib English idiom she understood no better than if it were an extinct Coptic or Etruscan dialect. “I suppose poets are always a little mad.”

“To be sure—‘the poet’s eye in a fine frenzy rolling.’”

“But it was not always found out, was it?” Gwendolen asked innocently. “Mad people are often very cunning.”

A shadow flitted over Mrs. Arrowpoint’s face at this over-acted *naïveté*, but the gentlemen’s entrance prevented further mischief. “Ah, here comes Herr Klesmer,” Mrs. Arrowpoint said, bringing forth a felicitous combination of the German, the Sclave, and the Semite. Herr Klesmer possessed grand features, brown hair floating in artistic fashion, and an alarming cleverness barely masked by a softening air of silliness intended to flatter Beauty. His English flowed perfectly, a dominant lexicon mastered, yet it held the rhythmic undercurrents of his native dialects. 

Music began. Miss Arrowpoint and Herr Klesmer played a four-handed piece on two pianos. Gwendolen recognized immediately that the placid Miss Arrowpoint possessed a mastery of the instrument that eclipsed her own, yet she felt no discouragement regarding her own celebrated touch. Soon, Mr. Arrowpoint, a gentleman known chiefly for importing the finest, most aromatic cigars, led Gwendolen to the piano. 

Gwendolen did not tremble; singing was a profound sensory enjoyment to her, a physical vibration in the chest and throat. She possessed a moderately powerful soprano, a good ear, and perfect pitch. Choosing a favorite aria of Bellini’s, she sang. It was a pretty sound, one that pleased ordinary hearers, but it lacked the deep, root-level resonance of the earth. 

“Charming!” echoed the room, offering the insincere, ambient noise of polite society. 

But Herr Klesmer stood as mute as a stone monolith. The acoustic environment of the drawing-room felt suddenly tense. Gwendolen, pressed to continue, looked at him with a smiling appeal. “It would be too cruel to a great musician. You cannot like to hear poor amateur singing.”

“No, truly; but that makes nothing,” Herr Klesmer said suddenly. The polite veneer of his standard English fractured. His voice took on an odious German fashion with sharp, staccato endings—an eruption of his native identity, surfacing as Irishmen resume their strongest brogue when fervid. “That makes nothing. It is always acceptable to see you sing.”

Gwendolen colored deeply at this unexpected assertion of superiority. Miss Arrowpoint, attuned to the subtle power dynamics and Klesmer’s overly admiring gaze, approached with utmost tact. “Imagine what I have to go through with this professor! He can hardly tolerate anything we English do in music.”

“I should be very much obliged to him for telling me the worst,” Gwendolen said, recovering her poise. “I dare say I have been extremely ill taught.”

“Yes, it is true: you have not been well taught,” Herr Klesmer said quietly. He was a man who understood the profound psychological necessity of pure acoustic ecology, and he viewed superficial commercializations of art as a spiritual pollutant. “Still, you are not quite without gifts. You sing in tune. But you produce your notes badly; and that music which you sing is beneath you. It is a form of melody which expresses a puerile state of culture—a dawdling, canting, see-saw kind of stuff—the passion and thought of people without any breadth of horizon. There is a sort of self-satisfied folly about every phrase of such melody; no cries of deep, mysterious passion—no conflict—no sense of the universal. It makes men small as they listen to it. Sing now something larger. And I shall see.”

“Oh, not now—by-and-by,” Gwendolen said, her heart sinking at the sudden, vast horizon opened around her small, industrialized performance. 

Miss Arrowpoint stepped in. “We will ask him to play to us now: he is bound to show us what is good music.”

Herr Klesmer sat at the moderately responsive instrument and played a composition of his own, a fantasia called *Freudvoll, Leidvoll, Gedankenvoll*. It was a mythic occurrence brought into the domestic space. He possessed an imperious magic in his fingers, sending a nerve-thrill through ivory keys and wooden hammers, compelling the strings to vibrate with the untranslatable subjective experience of the human soul. The room's acoustic ecology was instantly transformed, cleansed of its shallow societal hum. Gwendolen, despite her wounded ego, possessed enough fullness of nature and sensory depth to feel the sheer, unadulterated power of this playing. Her inward sob of mortification transmuted into a flushed, breathless excitement. 

When it ended, young Clintock, the classical scholar, approached her. “I wish you would sing to us again, Miss Harleth. That is the style of music for me. I never can make anything of this tip-top playing. It is like a jar of leeches. I could listen to your singing all day.”

“Yes, another song from you would be a relaxation,” added Mrs. Arrowpoint.

Gwendolen looked up with a bright, mischievous smile, ignoring the hostess. “That must be because you are in a puerile state of culture, and have no breadth of horizon. I have just learned that. I have been taught how bad my taste is, and am feeling growing pains. They are never pleasant.”

Mrs. Arrowpoint, not insensible to this rudeness, retreated to the relief of a hostess whose orchestrated cycle was momentarily self-sustaining. 

“I am glad you like this neighborhood,” young Clintock said.

“Exceedingly. There seems to be a little of everything and not much of anything.”

“That is rather equivocal praise. I think there should be more croquet, for one thing. Depend upon it, croquet is the game of the future. One of our best men has written a poem on it, in four cantos;—as good as Pope. I will send you Jenning’s poem if you like.”

“Is he a great friend of yours?”

“Well, rather.”

“Oh, if he is only rather, I think I will decline,” Gwendolen replied, sipping the last of her botanical cordial, letting the complex, astringent finish ground her against the boy's empty prattle. “Or, if you send it to me, will you promise not to catechise me upon it? Because it is not so easy to know a poem without reading it as to know a sermon without listening.”

*Decidedly,* Mrs. Arrowpoint thought from a distance, *this girl is double and satirical. I shall be on my guard against her.* 

But Gwendolen continued to receive polite attentions from the family at Quetcham, woven inexorably into the county's social fabric not merely by the necessities of grand, ancestral hostessing, but because the trying scene at the piano had awakened a profound, kindly solicitude in the gentle mind of Miss Arrowpoint, who managed the unending cycle of visits and invitations.
