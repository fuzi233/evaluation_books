A half-year had elapsed since the events of Cloisterham, and Mr. Crisparkle found himself seated in a waiting-room within the London headquarters of the "Haven of Philanthropy." He waited, with mounting impatience, for an audience with Mr. Honeythunder.

In his university days, Mr. Crisparkle had encountered professors of the "Noble Art" of boxing. He recalled observing the distinct phrenological contours of their foreheads—the prominent bumps indicative of aggressiveness. Now, standing amidst the bustling lobby of the Philanthropists, he noted a striking resemblance. These self-styled champions possessed the same physical predisposition toward "pitching into" others as the pugilists he had known. Several professors passed through the room with an air ready for a scrap, reminiscent of the fighters of the Fancy. Preparations were underway for a moral exhibition, a kind of intellectual wrestling match, where various professors backed specific speakers as if they were heavyweights entering a ring. An official manager, dressed in black and possessing platform tactics reminiscent of a legendary benefactor known as "Frosty-faced Fogo," oversaw these displays. However, Crisparkle noted a critical difference: whereas real boxers trained rigorously, these philanthropists were out of shape, carrying excessive flesh—"Suet Pudding"—and lacking the good temper of true athletes. Worse still, their "code of honor" allowed for hitting a fallen opponent, kicking, and mauling from behind without mercy. In these brutal aspects, Crisparkle ironically concluded, the philosophers of charity were far less noble than the philosophers of the fist.

Lost in these musings, watching a tide of people seemingly intent on snatching advantages from one another, Crisparkle was called. He was led to Mr. Honeythunder's office by a shabby, underpaid stipendiary whose competence seemed questionable.

Inside, Mr. Honeythumber greeted Crisparkle with a booming, authoritative voice, seating him with the demeanor of a schoolmaster addressing a recalcitrant pupil. While a colleague collected unpaid circulars, Honeythunder turned fully to Crisparkle, his arms squared on his knees, his brow furrowed.

"Now, Mr. Crisparkle," the Philanthropist declared, "we entertain different views regarding the sanctity of human life."

"Do we?" returned the Minor Canon.

"We do, sir."

"Might I inquire," Crisparkle pressed gently, "what are your views on this subject?"

"That human life is sacred," Honeythunder stated flatly.

"And what, sir, do you suppose to be *my* views?"

"They are best known to yourself!" Honeythunder roared, leaning forward.

"Calmly, Mr. Honeythunder," Crisparkle interjected softly. "Since you assert we hold different views, you must have attributed a position to me. What view, pray, have you set up as mine?"

Honeythunder gestured dramatically. "Here is a man—a young man—swept off the face of the earth by a deed of violence. What do you call that?"

"Murder," said Crisparkle.

"What do you call the perpetrator, sir?"

"A murderer," Crisparkle replied.

"I am glad to hear you admit so much," Honeythunder sneered, though he expected resistance. "But I did not expect it."

"Browbeat me no further," Crisparkle said. "Explain your meaning."

"I do not sit here to be bullied," Honeythunder shouted, rising slightly. "Murder! Bloodshed! Abel! Cain! I hold no terms with Cain. I repudiate the red hand when it is offered."

Rather than cheering, Crisparkle crossed his legs calmly. "Do not let me interrupt your explanation before you begin."

"The Commandments say, 'Thou shalt not kill.' NO MURDER, SIR!" Honeythunder bellowed, pausing for dramatic effect, as if implying Crisparkle believed exceptions existed.

"And the Commandments also say, 'Thou shalt not bear false witness,'" Crisparkle countered.

"E-enough!" Honeythunder slammed his fist, a gesture that would have silenced a noisy hall. "My wards are now of age, and I am relieved of a trust that fills me with horror. Here are the accounts you undertook to manage. Take them, or depart!" With a series of emphatic nods, he dismissed Crisparkle, wishing he were "better employed."

Crisparkle rose, his face flushed but his composure intact. "Mr. Honeythunder, regarding your suggestion that I would be better employed joining your Society... I suspect you would have preferred it if I had done so sooner."

"It would have been better!" Honeythunder retorted. "Or, I might prefer a man of your profession devoted to discovering and punishing guilt rather than leaving it to a layman."

"My profession dictates a duty to the destitute and oppressed," Crisparkle argued. "However, I have satisfied myself that it is not my role to make professions. Regarding Mr. Neville: I know I understood his mind and heart perfectly at the time of his tragedy. Without concealing his faults, I am certain his testimony is true. As long as that conviction holds, I will defend him. To betray him would shame me beyond any compensation you could offer."

"So, who do you accuse?" Honeythunder demanded abruptly.

"Heaven forbid," Crisparkle replied, "that in clearing one man I should lightly condemn another. I accuse no one."

"Tcha!" Honeythunder spat in disgust. "And you are not a disinterested witness, we must bear in mind."

"How am I an interested party?" Crisparkle smiled innocently.

"There was a stipend paid for your pupil," Honeythunder growled. "That may have warped your judgment."

"Perhaps I expect to retain it?" Crisparkle teased.

"Well, sir," Honeythunder stood, thrusting his hands into his pockets, "I do not measure people for hats. If you find a hat that fits, wear it. That is your lookout, not mine."

Crisparkle looked at him with indignant disappointment. "Mr. Honeythunder, I hoped to avoid commenting on the intrusion of stagecraft into private affairs. But you have provided such a vivid specimen that I cannot remain silent. These methods are detestable. They violate Christian justice and gentlemanly restraint. You assume a grave crime by someone I believe innocent, and when I disagree, you immediately turn upon me, accusing me of lacking reverence for the crime itself and being its accomplice!"

He continued, listing similar frustrations from previous encounters where Honeythunder would champion a delusion, then mock Crisparkle's skepticism as disbelief in God himself; where Honeythunder would propose absurd solutions to war, then label Crisparkle's opposition as reveling in carnage; where he would punish the sober for the drunken, then paint Crisparkle as wishing to turn humanity into beasts. "In all these cases," Crisparkle concluded, "your supporters run amuck like madmen, attributing base motives with reckless abandon and citing figures as one-sided as a fraudulent ledger. For this, Mr. Honeythunder, I consider the platform a bad school, and bringing it into private life an unendurable nuisance."

"These are strong words, sir!" Honeythunder gasped.

"I hope so," Crisparkle replied. "Good morning."

He departed at a brisk pace, soon finding a smile returning to his face. He wondered what the porcelain shepherdess would have said if she had witnessed his victory over the Philanthropist. He felt a harmless vanity swell within him, believing he had "trimmed the jacket" of the Philanthropic Brotherhood handsomely.

Taking himself to Staple Inn, he bypassed P.J.T. and Mr. Grewgious, climbing many creaking stairs to reach an attic room in a corner. This was Neville Landless's chamber. An air of retreat and solitude hung over the space. The sloping ceilings, rusty locks, and heavy beams gave it a prison-like quality, reflected in Neville's haggard face. Yet, sunlight streamed through the garret window, illuminating a few deluded sparrows hopping on the parapet like feathered cripples, and the soft rustling of nearby leaves.

The room was sparsely furnished but filled with books, evidence of a dedicated student. Crisparkle's warm gaze upon them suggested he had chosen, lent, or donated many of them.

"How goes it, Neville?" Crisparkle asked.

"In good heart, Mr. Crisparkle, and working away," Neville replied.

"Neville, I wish your eyes were not quite so large and bright," Crisparkle observed gently.

"They brighten at the sight of you," Neville smiled. "If you were to leave, they would quickly dim."

"Rally, rally!" urged Crisparkle. "Fight for it!"

"If I were dying, a word from you would rally me," Neville said. "But I have rallied, and I am doing famously."

Crisparkle turned Neville toward the light. "I want to see a ruddier complexion here."
