I scrubbed stinging fountain water from my lids, and there he was: the raven, perched light as a living shadow on the chiselled lip of the massive stone basin I lay half-submerged in. Dawn spilled cold pale silver over his glossy iridescent plumage, and he stood perfectly still, head tilted, staring down at me where I lay propped on my elbows, my face just clear of the rippling surface. I recognized the place at once: this was the great central fountain my father had built decades before, set dead in the heart of the front lawn. High above my head, the thick, gunmetal-sheen stalk of its central jet hurled a roiling column of water a hundred feet skyward, bursting at the peak into a wide frothy blossom that drifted down in fine misty spray.

I was still smarting from his earlier rebuke. “You never told me any of this!” I snapped at him.
“I told you never to do a single thing anyone you distrusted asked you to,” he returned, his voice low and croaky, sharp as a shard of flint.
“Tch! No mortal man could be expected to hold that at the front of his mind every waking moment,” I protested.
“You will not be so quick to forget the cost of forgetting it, I promise you,” replied Mr. Raven, leaning over the basin edge to stretch out a dark clawed hand. I took it, and in a single weightless blink I was standing beside him on the grass, my clothes dripping cold water onto the blades, streaming small puddles at my feet.
“Change your clothes at once,” he commanded, shifting back to his raven form in a ripple of shadow. “Where you come from, a dousing might not matter—but even there this kind of misstep is rare, and here it brings no end of inconveniences.” He stalked toward the open front door of the house, every step stately and deliberate, like a royal procession on scaled feet.
“I don’t have much to change into,” I laughed, remembering I’d stripped off my outer robe entirely to climb the gnarled old tree the night before.
“Feels like centuries since I last moulted a single feather,” the raven rumbled over his shoulder as he crossed the threshold.

The house was wrapped in total quiet, no sound of stirring occupants. I trundled up to my old familiar room, fished out a soft wool dressing gown, and made my way back down to the library. I had barely stepped over the threshold when the librarian stepped out from the back closet, and I threw myself onto the velvet couch near the cold hearth. Mr. Raven dragged an oak chair to sit right at my side, and for two long still minutes neither of us spoke.

I broke the hush first. “What in the name of all that is unearthly does any of this mean?”
“Fair question,” he said, nodding his ebony head. “Nobody on any plane of existence knows what a thing *is*—a man can only ever learn what a thing *means*. And whether he learns that or not depends entirely on what he chooses to do with it.”
“I haven’t done much of anything with anything, yet,” I said.
“Not much, no—but you know that fact, and that is worth more than a lifetime of empty busywork. Most people run through their whole lives without ever figuring out that they’ve learned nothing, and done even less. At the very least, you’ve always wanted to be useful.”
“I did want to help the children—the precious Little Ones, that is,” I amended quickly.
“I know you did. But you set off on the wrong path entirely.”
“I didn’t know what the right path even was!”
“That is true enough—but you are still at fault for not finding it.”
“I’ll believe whatever you tell me, as soon as I can wrap my head around what it means.”
“If you had accepted our invitation when we first extended it, you would have known the right way long ago. When a man refuses to do the work right in front of him where he stands, he will wander half the universe looking for it, and never find it.”
“I certainly have wandered far enough, and got nowhere. I left the Little Ones so I could learn how to serve them better, and all I came back with was the knowledge of just how much danger they are in.”
“When you were with them, you were already exactly where you could help them. You left your work to go looking for it. It takes a very wise man to know when he ought to leave; even a fool can learn to turn back immediately, if he chooses.”
“Do you mean to tell me I could have done something to help the Little Ones, just by staying there with them?”
“Could you have taught them a single thing by running away from them?”
“No—but how was I supposed to teach them? I had no idea where to even start. Besides, they were far, far ahead of me in every way that matters!”
“That is no lie. But you were never meant to be a measuring rod to judge their worth. If they knew even a fraction of what you know, let alone what you could have learned, they would be leagues ahead of you, out of your line of sight entirely. But you saw it yourself, didn’t you? They weren’t growing. They grew so slowly they’d never even conceived of the idea that they *should* grow—they were even afraid of it. You never saw children that clung to childhood like it was a cage they refused to step out of, back in your old world, did you?”
“Wait a minute—surely I had no power to force them to grow?”
“You could have cleared away most of the things that were stopping them from growing, in the first place.”
“What were those barriers? I never figured them out. I thought, once, that maybe they just didn’t have enough water.”
“Of course it is! They don’t even have enough to cry with, poor things.”
“I would have done anything to keep them from ever needing to cry, to need that water for tears!”
“No doubt you would—that’s the exact folly of every well-meaning stupid philanthropist who ever lived. Why, Mr. Vane, if there was no weeping in your old world, it never would have been worth saving at all! You already guessed that water was what they needed. Why didn’t you dig them a well? Two wells, even?”
“It never crossed my mind!”
“Not even when you could hear the rumble of underground rivers, thrumming just under the earth at your feet?”
“I think it did, once. But I was so terrified of the giants, for their sakes. That’s why I put up with so much cruelty from those brutes myself.”
“You almost taught those noble little souls to be afraid of those stupid, greedy Bags. While they fed you, comforted you, worshipped you like some kind of hero, you spent all your time submitting to those bestial men, acting as their slave. You gave those darlings a hero that looked like a coward. You could hardly have done them a worse wrong. They gave you their whole hearts—you owed them your whole soul, in return. You could have turned all those Bags into hewers of wood and drawers of water for the Little Ones by now, if you’d stood your ground.”
“I fear every word you say is true, Mr. Raven. But I swear I served the giants out of love for the Little Ones, not out of cowardice. I was afraid more knowledge would hurt them, make them less innocent, less soft and beautiful.”
“They never gave you any reason to fear that, not for a single second.”
“Isn’t a little knowledge a dangerous thing, though?”
“That is one of your old world’s most beloved, persistent lies. Is all the greatest knowledge any man can ever hold nothing more than a little? And is it dangerous, for that? That silly fantasy that knowledge is some grand, dangerous power in itself makes even the tiniest bit of it far more perilous than a whole lifetime of ignorance. To know every single thing in the universe would not make you a great man.”
“At the very least, I acted out of love for them, not cowardice, when I served the giants.”
“Granted. But you ought to have served the Little Ones, not the giants. You ought to have given them that water they so badly needed. They would have worked out very quickly how to put the giants in their proper place, after that. You could have made those brutes cut down two thirds of their coarse, bitter wild fruit trees right then, to make space for the delicate little fruit trees the Little Ones tended. You lost your chance with the Lovers, Mr. Vane. You spent all your time speculating about them, instead of getting your hands dirty and helping them.”
