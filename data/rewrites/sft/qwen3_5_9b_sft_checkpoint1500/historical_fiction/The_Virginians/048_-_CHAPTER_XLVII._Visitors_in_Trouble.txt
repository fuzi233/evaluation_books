The gentle, generous nature of Mr. Gumbo gradually soothed the storm within his master's soul. By the time the sun rose on Harry Warrington's second night in the spunging-house, the raw edges of his fury had dulled, though the core remained iron-hard. While he accepted the kindness offered by Gumbo, Harry's spirit remained locked against the rest of the world. To him, the English were a breed of self-serving, unfeeling creatures. His pious Aunt Warrington, the haughty March, and the cynical Castlewood—all had failed him. He would not stoop to beg another favor from them, nor would he forgive their broken promises. There was no loyalty in this frozen, selfish nation. His resolve hardened: he would depart, trusting no Englishman, however high-born. Whether he marched with the King in Germany or returned to Virginia to live among the trees, hunt, manage his estate, and wed either Polly Broadbent or Fanny Mountain, he would choose any path over remaining amidst these "fine gentlemen." Thus, he rose with a mask of cheerfulness covering a vengeful heart.

Early in the morning, Gumbo appeared, having traveled from Bond Street. "I sought to bring more garments, sir," the faithful servant explained, "but the landlord, Mr. Ruff, forbade it."

Harry showed no interest in the clothing. Instead, he tore open a stack of envelopes, revealing a mountain of debts. The final letter, from the landlord, was a blunt ultimatum: no more of Mr. Warrington's effects would leave the house until the bill was settled, at which point the landlord would seize the goods and evict the black man. Harry hesitated to let Gumbo remove his own clothes and trunk. However, Gumbo revealed he had already secured shelter at Lord Wrotham's residence. "Colonel Lambert's household," Gumbo noted, glancing sharply at his master. "Indeed, when they learned you were taken, Miss Hetty swooned. Mr. Lambert, a kind soul, instructed me to tell you: if you need him, send word, and he shall come."

A flicker of gratitude touched Harry at the mention of Hetty's distress, though he dismissed Gumbo's account of her fainting as typical exaggeration. Yet, the mention of Colonel Lambert reignited the darkness in his eyes. *Send word to Lambert,* Harry ground out internally, *the man who spat in my face and rejected my gifts? Even if I starved, I would not accept a crust from him!*

Dressed and having eaten a scant breakfast, Harry dispatched Gumbo with a brief, peremptory note to Mr. Draper of the Temple, summoning the attorney immediately.

"The note read like a decree issued to a serf, not a summons to a freeborn English gentleman," Draper observed later, though he had always harbored a deep resentment for Harry's habitual condescension. "For a man in a debtor's prison to act so!" Draper muttered, shaking his head. "Though, hang him, I had a strong inclination to refuse the appointment." Despite his complaints, Draper obeyed. He found Mr. Warrington seated upon his bed, draped in a magnificent brocaded gown, his hair meticulously coiffed, treating his situation with the arrogance of a sovereign. He motioned for Gumbo to retrieve a chair for the lawyer.

"Madam, I confess, I am unaccustomed to such hauteur and airs!" the outraged attorney stammered, addressing the scene through the lens of the narrator's omniscience, who amusedly noted that the Baroness, observing the exchange, delighted in her nephew's insolence in adversity.

The strategy for this meeting had been plotted by the Baroness and Draper the previous day. Draper, usually a capable negotiator, faltered today, blinded by his own irritation and a fundamental inability to comprehend the young Virginian's psyche. As the narrator gently reminds the reader, those who lack gentility often wound others unintentionally, provoking scorn that they cannot decipher, leading to a cycle of rage and misunderstanding.

"Gumbo, bring a chair for Mr. Draper!" commanded Mr. Warrington, wrapping his gown around his legs as he sat upon the dingy mattress. "Sit, if you please, and let us discuss my affairs. I am grateful for your swift response. Have you heard of this calamity?"

Draper, aware of the rumors, replied gravely, "News travels swiftly, sir. I hastened to offer my humble services the moment you required them. Surely, your friends and family are distressed that a gentleman of your standing finds himself in such straits."

"I have been imprudent, Mr. Draper," Harry admitted, bowing slightly. "I lived beyond my means. I gambled with men vastly wealthier than myself, and a string of bad luck has drained my ready funds, leaving me liable for five hundred pounds."

"Five hundred pounds currently outstanding," Draper noted, pulling a long, grave face.

"Why, sir, it is a trifling sum!" Harry exclaimed, staring intently at the lawyer. "I believed that by appealing to one or two friends yesterday, I might settle the debt and return home. I was mistaken. Pray, assist me in raising the necessary funds forthwith."

"Hm!" Draper hummed, maintaining his somber expression. "Why, sir, it *can* be done!" Harry insisted.

In truth, Draper had previously proposed to the Baroness to discharge the debt instantly, securing Harry's release. She had declared her intention to make him her heir, and Draper, like everyone else, assumed Harry's Virginia inheritance was vast. Yet, despite holding the relevant notes and the Baroness's order, the lawyer stalled. "To access the property in England belonging to Mr. George Warrington, we must prove the succession and release the trustees," Draper explained, his tone heavy with bureaucratic obstruction. "Lady Esmond Warrington, your mother-in-law, will not permit her son to remain in prison, yet we must await correspondence from Virginia, a process taking six months. Furthermore, does your agent in Bristol possess authority to honor your drafts?"

"He is authorized only for two hundred pounds annually," Harry stated flatly. "I appear to have no recourse but to appeal to my aunt, Madame de Bernstein. She will stand as my security."

"My lady will do anything for you, sir," Draper assured him, recalling her frequent assurances. "If she gives the word, you may walk free from this place."

"Then proceed to her, Mr. Draper," Harry urged, his voice trembling slightly as he wiped his eyes. "Do not disguise the truth. Tell her where I am and what has happened. Inform her that I entrust myself to her affection and kindness to liberate me from this... this disgrace."

"Sir," Draper interjected, fixing his gaze on the youth, "I was with her ladyship yesterday when we discussed this entire, most unpleasant affair—I hesitate to use your term, 'disgrace.'"

"What do you mean, sir?" Harry asked, his cheeks flushing. "Does Madame de Bernstein know of my misfortune?"

"Every detail, sir," Draper confirmed. "The pawning of the watches, and all."

Harry turned a burning crimson. "It is an unfortunate business," the lawyer continued smoothly, "to pledge timepieces and other items for which you had never paid." Harry sprang from the bed, his eyes fierce enough to alarm the seasoned attorney.

"It may lead to litigation and unpleasant remarks in court, sir. Those barristers respect nothing, and when they secure a defendant in the box—"

"Good heavens, sir, do you truly suppose a gentleman of my rank cannot borrow a watch on credit without intending to defraud the tradesman?" Harry cried, agitated.

"Naturally, you meant every word honorably," Draper winked, masking his triumph. "However, the law may not share your sentiment. Your aunt describes this as the most imprudent action she has ever witnessed—to call it anything less would be unwise."

"Do you describe it otherwise yourself, Mr. Draper?" Harry asked, speaking slowly, striving to maintain composure.

Draper bristled. "Heaven forbid I speak thus between gentlemen—but as between a lawyer and his client, it is my duty to state plainly: 'Sir, you are in a very unpleasant scrape,' just as a physician must tell a patient, 'Sir, you are very ill.'"

"And you cannot assist me in paying this debt? You have come merely to accuse me of potential roguery?"

"Obtaining goods under false pretenses? Undoubtedly, yes. I cannot alter facts, sir. Do not look as though you intend to strike me. A young gentleman, receiving only two hundred pounds a year from his guardian, ordering diamonds and watches, and subsequently pledging them..." Draper paused, letting the weight of the accusation sink in. "What will the public think? I speak honestly. Do not be offended, Mr. Warrington."

"Proceed, sir!" Harry groaned.

Draper felt the momentum shift entirely to his advantage. "Yet, you asked if I could assist in settling this debt? And I say yes—and here lies the money in my pocket to effect payment immediately, if you consent—not my own funds, sir, but those of my honored client, your aunt, Lady Bernstein. She possesses the right to impose conditions, and I have brought them."

"State them, sir," Harry commanded.
