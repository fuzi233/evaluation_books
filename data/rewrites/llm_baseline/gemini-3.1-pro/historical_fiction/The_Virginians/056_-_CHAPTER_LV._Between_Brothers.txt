The rhythmic, syncopated *thwack* of George’s knife against the scarred oak cutting board provided a grounding acoustic anchor, a steady heartbeat that warded off the chaotic, metallic clatter of London’s commercial traffic beyond their window. Down in the street, hawkers peddled mass-produced broadside ballads—a shrill, superficial commodification of culture that grated against George’s ears. But here in the kitchen, the sonic environment was sacred. George was rendering down the fat of a cured pork shoulder, the slow, hissing sizzle releasing a deeply evocative olfactory landscape of hickory smoke, toasted coriander, and the primal warmth of hearth-fire. It was the scent of Virginia, a mythic loop of memory returning them to the soil of their youth.

As the fat rendered, releasing its golden oils, the brothers traded histories, their interwoven timelines circling back to the domestic routines of the estate. Harry spoke of little Dempster, of old Nathan, of Fanny blossoming into a young woman, and of his own youthful infatuation with Parson Broadbent’s daughter. He confessed, with a sheepish grin, to losing pocket-money at cards and enduring the parson’s harsh, medicinal strong-waters—a spirit lacking all nuance or craft—merely for proximity to the girl. But Madam Esmond’s unyielding social lexicon had no room for a Broadbent. 

Then there was the state visit of Colonel Washington’s new wife to Richmond. George, meticulously adjusting the heat beneath the iron pot to ensure a gentle, rolling simmer, described the encounter. Mrs. Washington had been pleasant, her demeanor as rich and approachable as a well-clarified broth, but their mother had deployed a chilling, killing politeness. Madam Esmond’s English was a weaponized dialect of imperial power, each syllable sharply enunciated to establish dominance. Mrs. Mountain, forever stirring the pot of local gossip with a clumsy spoon, had insisted that both brothers had nearly gained the tall, six-foot-two Colonel as a stepfather. 

But Mountain’s idle chatter lacked the harmlessness of a simple kitchen rummage; it was she who had seeded the corrosive rumors regarding the poor Indian woman, the Biche. 

“Our mother repelled the insinuation with scorn,” George said softly, crushing a clove of garlic with the flat of his blade, the sharp, pungent aroma blooming in the warm air. “She told Parson Stack she would sooner fancy me stealing spoons or marrying a woman from the kitchen.” 

Despite recognizing the Biche’s role in nursing George through his fever, Madam Esmond had treated her with an icy haughtiness. Stripped of her native dialect by the dominant colonial lexicon, the Biche possessed no English words to translate the profound, subjective experience of her devotion and subsequent alienation. There is an inherent untranslatability to grief when one’s mother tongue is dying, a cultural memory eroding into silence. Banished to the servant’s quarters, her indigenous sonic landscape replaced by the harsh, utilitarian clatter of a subservient kitchen, the Indian woman had sought refuge in cheap, burning rum, trying to drown a sorrow for which she no longer had a voice. 

“The birthright is mine, Harry,” George murmured, carefully folding a handful of roughly chopped, earthy root vegetables into the simmering stew. “But thou art the favourite. I think my mother almost grudges my return. Had you been the elder, you would have understood the terroir of the estate, ridden the best nag, cultivated the best cellar. You would have been the vibrant life of the county. I am but a glum face, out of rhythm with the world. I should have been the second son, turning parson to say grace at your table. *The time is out of joint, sir. O cursed spite, that ever I was born to set it right!*”

“Why, Georgy, you are talking verses!” Harry cried, tearing off a heel of crusty, hearth-baked bread and dragging it through the rich, mahogany-colored drippings. 

George smiled, the acoustic tension in the room easing. “I think, my dear, someone else talked those verses before me.”

Harry laughed, his mouth full of the savory, fat-soaked crumb, deeply appreciating the sustenance his brother provided. He eagerly recounted his trip to Tunbridge, where he had seen the two authors of those very verses and tipped his hat to them, not for his own literary comprehension, but because he remembered his dear old George speaking of them. The memory caught in Harry’s throat. “It’s like a dream seeing you over again,” he choked out, his voice thick. “Think of that bloody Indian with his knife at my George’s head! I want to give that Monsieur de Florac something for saving you, but I only have my gold knee-buckles, worth a mere two guineas.”

“You have half of what I have, child,” George replied, pouring a splash of dark, fermented ale into the pot to deglaze the caramelized *fond* from the bottom, the hissing steam filling the room with the scent of malt and roasted barley. “We’ll divide it as soon as I have paid the Frenchman.”

At this, Harry broke into a loud, rhythmic litany of blessings and joyous imprecations, his voice a warm, booming baritone of fraternal devotion. For days, Harry had treated his brother as a mythic figure resurrected into the everyday. He would lay down his knife and fork mid-meal just to marvel at George’s living presence, demanding the world recognize the miraculous return of his kin.

But the promise of Museau’s ransom remained a bitter, unresolved flavor on George’s palate. Madam Esmond viewed money as utilitarian fuel, refusing to disburse twelve thousand livres to "Normandy peasants." George, however, understood the profound cultural exchange of the transaction. He remembered the earthy, ash-ripened cheeses and the raw, unfiltered cider of the French farmers—a people deeply connected to their agrarian roots. Madam Esmond dismissed them as priest-ridden papists, furious that George would squander his patrimony on enemies rather than bestow it upon Harry. The dispute had raged until the morning of George’s departure. He vividly recalled the quiet, desolate acoustics of his bedchamber when she came to him after a sleepless night. He told her his honor bound him; she replied she would pray Heaven softened his proud heart. Her final farewell was a silent, tearless departure, a profound failure of emotional translation between mother and son.

“Where didst thou learn the art of winning over everybody, Harry?” George asked, tasting the broth from a wooden spoon and adjusting the salt, the minerals grounding the complex flavors. “At Richmond, I was initially the town favorite, a survivor of Braddock’s field. But the lexicon of the town repulsed me. I could not bear the acoustic violence of it all.”

George described the horrific noise pollution of Richmond society. The officers’ drinking-bouts were a cacophony of rattling dice-boxes and meaningless swearing—a crude, industrial thrum devoid of soul. The ladies’ tea circles were equally abrasive, filled with shrill, staccato gossip that stupefied him. In that jarring sonic environment, George had felt deeply untethered, yearning for the acoustic ecology of Duquesne, where the silent ease of the forest and the rhythmic, purposeful tasks of survival offered genuine psychological peace. 

Because he sought quietude, the dominant social order deemed him supercilious and dull. When the Biche arrived weeks later, the town weaponized their language against him. Boys hooted in the streets; ladies dropped sarcastic half-curtsies. Parson Stack used the pulpit to preach against the "lax principles" learned in Popish countries, an absolute corruption of spiritual language. The officers cracked clumsy, lewd jokes at the coffee-house.

“I should like to see any of the infernal scoundrels who said a word against you, and break their rascally bones!” Harry roared, pacing the floor boards, his heavy footfalls a drumbeat of loyalty. “I had to do something like it for Bob Clubber.”

“That little sneaking, backbiting wretch who sponges off my lord at Greenway Court?” George asked.

“If you whipped him, I hope you whipped him well.” 

George nodded. “We were bound over to keep the peace. The town decided that because I had supposedly broken the seventh commandment, I was eager to break the sixth. I left home with an awful reputation.”

Having plated the rich, steaming stew—a masterful synthesis of memory, technique, and survival—George listened as Harry unbosomed his own affairs. Harry had severed ties with Castlewood, disgusted by my lord’s heartless dismissal of the chaplain and Mr. Will’s treacherous horse-dealing. But regarding Lady Maria, Harry’s devotion remained fierce. Though he acknowledged the rumors of the Countess being a card-playing cynic and Fanny a desperate flirt, he steadfastly defended Maria.

“I know there will be a dreadful piece of work at home, and that Madam and she will fight,” Harry said, the timbre of his voice vibrating with stubborn agrarian resilience. “Well! We must live apart. I’ll make a famous farmer or factor. I can work the soil. I won’t part from Maria. Think of her bringing me every jewel she had, flinging them into my lap with her last guineas!” 

Harry dashed a sleeve across his eyes and stamped his foot—a sharp, definitive sound. “No, brother, I won’t part with her, not to be made Governor of Virginia. And I know you would never advise me to do so.”

“I am sent here to advise you,” George replied slowly, his gaze fixed on the hearty, sustaining meal between them. “I am sent to break the marriage off. But I cannot counsel you to break your word, my boy.”

“What’s said is said. I have made my bed, and must lie on it,” Harry muttered gloomily, finally sitting down to eat. 

George watched his brother consume the meal with honest, unpretentious hunger. Yet, after his private conversations with Aunt Bernstein, who had revealed the subtle, manipulative power dynamics embedded in Maria’s desperate affections, George remained a meticulous historian of human appetite. He knew he could not simply command Harry to abandon his pledge. But as he wiped his blade clean, George began quietly formulating a recipe to test the true depths of Maria’s hunger—to see if her affections were rooted in the enduring sustenance of love, or merely the superficial, utilitarian consumption of his brother's wealth.
