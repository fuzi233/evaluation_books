Speech is the easy, familiar outlet for all the tangled, mundane thoughts humans carry in their heads, but there are emotions so raw, uncomplicated, and overpowering that they burst forth not as formed words, but as rough, wordless cries that carry more weight than any carefully turned speech ever could. At those moments, a man sheds every last trace of the civility that sets him apart from the beasts, and becomes nothing more than a creature of raw, urgent impulse—for even the highest of animals, when pushed to the edge of great triumph or great terror, will cry out without having the words to name what moves them.

That unthinking, almost feral quality hung thick in the forest air that day: it blazed in the roar of brutal triumph that tore from Dierich and his men’s throats the moment they burst from the thicket straight onto the heels of the two fugitives and their mule, and it seared through the sharp, piercing cry of terror that burst from Margaret and Gerard as they darted away so fast their pursuers’ outstretched hands closed on nothing but empty air, scarce two feet behind their backs.

Stunned for a split second, like lions that miss their pounce mid-spring, Dierich and his crew gave Gerard and the mule a full ten yards of head start before they collected themselves, and flew after them with cudgels and daggers raised high over their heads. They were certain they would catch their prey, too—this was not the first time they had raced one another that morning. Out on the open moorland earlier, the hunters had been visibly gaining on the three, and now, at last, there were no more brambles to trip them, no winding tree roots to slow them: it was a fair, unobstructed race, to be decided by nothing but raw speed.

They ate up a hundred yards in the blink of an eye, but that ten-yard gap between pursuers and pursued never shrank. Dierich Brower could not make sense of it—why were the three fugitives suddenly running twice as fast as they had all morning? The answer was simple: when three people run side by side, the whole group can only go as fast as the slowest one. All the way from Peter’s cottage to the edge of the forest, Gerard had held his pace back to match Margaret’s, but now he was running full tilt at his own natural speed: the mule beneath Martin was fleet of foot enough to outrun all of them if she wished, and the young lovers’ unspent youth and sober, unburdened bodies gave them a stamina none of the hard-drinking middle-aged pursuers could match.

Bit by bit, almost imperceptibly, daylight widened between the hunters and the hunted. Dierich threw every last bit of strength he had into one desperate burst, and cut the gap down by two whole yards—but before he could catch his breath, Gerard had quietly pulled that distance back again. The men behind him began to snarl and curse in frustration.

Martin, perched steady on the mule’s back, heard them, and his worn face lit up with hope. “Courage, Gerard! Courage, brave lad! They’re breaking, they’re falling away from each other!”

He was right. Dierich, already winded, was soon overtaken by one of his own men, while another straggled so far behind he was barely visible to the rest. The path ahead sloped up a long, gentle rise, and here the stamina of youth, grit, and a lifetime of clean living told more than ever. Before Dierich even reached the crest of the hill, his forty years of heavy drinking and rough living weighed on him like forty lead bullets. “Our hunt’s done,” he gasped, doubling over, too winded to take another step. “If you can’t take them alive, shoot them dead!” He dropped out of the chase right there, and staggered along after the rest at a slow walking pace.

Jorian Ketel fell out of the run next, then another man, and one by one, Gerard ran every last one of the pursuers to a standstill—all except one, who stuck to the chase as steadfast as a bloodhound, even if he lost a little ground with every stride. If old local tales are to be trusted, his name was Eric Wouverman.

Pursued only by Eric now, the trio rounded a bend in the wood, and came to a second hill, shorter than the last but far steeper. “Hand on her mane!” Martin shouted to Gerard, who was running beside the mule. Gerard obeyed immediately, and the sure-footed creature hauled both him and Martin up the sharp slope faster than they had been running on the flat.

When he saw that trick, the last remaining pursuer lost his nerve. He was now a full eighty yards behind Gerard, and more than that far ahead of his nearest straggling comrade, so he skidded to a sudden halt. Following Dierich’s last order, he unslung his crossbow from his shoulder, drew the bolt back and levelled it slow and deliberate, and just as the three fugitives sank over the crest of the hill, out of sight, he loosed. The bolt whistled through the air, straight at them.

There was a sharp cry of dismay, and the next instant, as if a thunderbolt had struck the whole group, every last one of them—Gerard, Margaret, Martin, and the mule—tumbled together to the ground in a heap.
