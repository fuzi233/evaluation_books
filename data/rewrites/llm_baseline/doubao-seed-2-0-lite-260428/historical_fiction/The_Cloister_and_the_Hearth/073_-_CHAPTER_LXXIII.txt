A Catherine is no unalloyed blessing in a strange house. The urge to order and govern hums strong in her bones. She has scarcely stepped across the threshold before the pewter pots on the dresser shine brighter of their own accord, the hearth sweeps itself free of ash, the cottage windows glow with twice as much light, and the whole little home thrums with the tireless energy of some giant, ever-busy cricket. But this cricket is a relentless meddler, a busybody of the most uncompromising sort, and that is no small character trait. She makes no distinctions: every pot, every chair, every person gets hauled into line to suit her sense of order.
Many things do improve for being tidied. But not everything. Nothing on earth resents being forced into perfect order more than the messy, breathing lives of human beings — save, of course, the quiet, unchanging peace of the grave.
Catherine shifted every chair and table in Margaret’s home, likely for the better, but she would not stop at inanimate furniture. When Margaret’s time to give birth drew near, Catherine decided no bumbling men could be allowed underfoot. She tricked the two good, simple wanderers Denys and Martin, first telling them a short stroll would do them good, then hinting that if their walk stretched on until the middle of the next week, all the better.
The two burly men, who had faced bandits and blizzards without flinching, had no defence against a sharp-tongued woman who ordered them out of their own borrowed home. They crept out at daybreak each morning, slipped back after dark like thieves, and if they ever accidentally lingered indoors, they moved as carefully as cats treading a wall lined with broken glass, their faces wide with awestruck submission, looking as dejected as scolded hounds. Even that was not enough for Catherine. Their very presence jangled on her nerves; surely two big hardy wanderers had no business cluttering up a house where a new life was about to arrive, she thought. No breastplate or sword could deflect the tiny, stinging pinpricks of her sharp, snappish remarks, until at last when Margaret came down the stairs after her confinement, the kitchen was entirely swept clear of heroes.
Martin, stiff with old age, had not wandered far. He dragged out his favourite three-legged stool as his “baggage of war” and perched placidly on it right in the street, baking in the sun like a contented tortoise. Margaret laughed when she spotted him there, not realising he had settled there full-time, and asked where Denys was. “Good, kind Denys,” she said softly, “he must be so pleased to see me up and about again.”
Catherine was wiping an earthen bowl so hard she nearly wore a hole through the wood, and told her dismissively that Denys had set off for his family home in Burgundy. “High time, too — he hasn’t been back to see his folks in three years, by all accounts.”
“What? Gone without saying goodbye to me?” Margaret’s violet-soft eyes filled with tears. Catherine coloured hot, the pricks of conscience stabbing her for the first time. She tossed her head, though, and made excuse that Margaret had been fast asleep when he left, and she had not wanted to wake her.
“Poor Denys,” Margaret whispered, and the tears welled over and rolled down her cheeks. Catherine, seeing that, slipped quietly outside to coddle the remaining displaced wanderer, Martin, with a sudden burst of warmth and hospitality. But old Martin was stubborn, set in his ways. “No thank you, mistress,” he said gruffly. “I’m used to being out of doors now. I never cared for constant changes. I don’t bother anyone here, and no one bothers me.”
“You horrid, grumpy old thing!” Catherine shrieked, flipping instantly from honeyed softness to sharp vinegar, and flounced back into the house.
Later, when she had calmed down, she had a little private cry, then soothed her own guilt by vowing she would be so kind to Margaret and the new baby that the young mother would never miss her two wandering friends. Still a little restive, she washed all her regrets away the simplest, surest way she knew: she gave the new baby a full, proper bath, head to toe, in warm soapy water, and all thoughts of wronged, displaced heroes dissolved into nothing, lost in the lather.
Margaret could not stand still the whole ceremony, hovering round Catherine like an eager little sparrow, desperate to have even a small part in tending her son. She dipped a finger into the bath water first, for form’s sake, not fooling anyone that she was checking the temperature — Catherine would never have let her near it to mess it up anyway. She knelt across from little baby Gerard, chattering to him nonstop, deliberately lisping and using baby talk, for what possible sense could a cherubic newborn make of the ridiculous, formal grown-up way of speaking?
“I wish you could wash that off,” Margaret said suddenly, staring at the baby’s tiny little finger.
“What?” Catherine peered closer.
“Don’t you see? That little brown mole there on his finger.”
Old Catherine leaned in for a good look, and her face lit up. “Why, that’s the strangest thing!” she cried. “Nature is a stubborn, meddlesome old thing, isn’t she? Haven’t you seen that exact same mark on someone else before? Tell me the truth now, lass.”
“On him? No, mother, I never did,” Margaret said, confused.
“Well, it’s there on his father, on the very same spot! You never noticed that, did you? But of course, you never knew your Gerard from a tiny babe up to man’s height the way I did. I had him on my knees a hundred times, just like this little one, washing him top to toe in warm water.” She puffed out her chest a little, proud of her years of knowing the boy Margaret loved, and Margaret looked up at her meekly, as if she were the wisest woman alive.
Catherine paused, reflecting, as busybodies sometimes do (not very deeply, of course, or we would not dare write it down). “Life is a whirligig, spinning round and back on itself,” she mused. “Us women, our lives are all cut from the same cloth, aren’t they? When my Gerard was a tiny babe just like this, I tried to scrub that very mole right off his finger! ‘Fie, what an ugly little blot,’ I said to myself, and scrubbed so hard I made the poor little wail. Only then I stopped, thought I was being silly. And now here you are, telling me to try and wash that same mark off your baby’s finger, too! Think of how the world turns!”
“Wash it off?” Margaret gasped, horrified. “I wouldn’t do that for all the gold in the world. Why, that’s the sweetest, dearest little thing about my baby! I’ll kiss that mole every morning and every night, until the man who first had it comes back to join the three of us.” She leaned down and kissed that tiny brown mark, then before she knew it she had the baby sprawled across her lap, kissing his soft little back over and over, nuzzling him like a mother wolf fawning over her lamb, while Catherine smiled on. She’d seen many a young mother get this same giddy, overjoyed bout of affection in her long days.
For months after that, Margaret’s whole life was summed up in one single, radiant, blissful word: motherhood. You who know all that that word holds can fill in the rest of the picture: the young mother nursing her babe, bathing him, dressing him, singing silly little rhymes, laughing as he coos and gambols on the cottage floor. Now, faster than a lightning strike, dart across the Alps all the way to Italy, to a cold stone cloister. The monks drift past like pale ghosts, their eyes cast down, their hands folded meekly across their chests, their hearts long since dead to all earthly joy and longing. One of those cowled ghosts is Gerard — the man Margaret waits for, who she thinks will one day come back to her, full of youth and love and happiness.
In the Grindelwald valley, the traveller sees on one side the sheer, towering Alps, nothing but rock, ice, and everlasting snow, piercing through the clouds to touch the sky. On the other side are gentle, ordinary green slopes, as bright as emerald, dotted with grazing cows, tiny thatched cottages, and warm, everyday life. Those great mountain peaks are all bare, lifeless, inhuman, and sublime, while the little green lower slopes hum with soft, ordinary joy. Wherever else you go, those soft common things of life pass unnoticed, but set against the grim grandeur of the ice mountains, they soothe the heart, they strike you with their gentle sweetness, all the brighter for the contrast.
That is exactly what these two halves of our story are, seen rightly: on the Italian side, rough adventure, burning passion, despair, vice, penitence, all pure ice and holy snow, soaring straight up towards heaven. On the Dutch side, everything small, soft, womanly, and ever green. And just as a narrow footpath separates those towering ice spires of Grindelwald from the little sunny green hills below, so only this one page lies between the Cloister, and the Hearth.
