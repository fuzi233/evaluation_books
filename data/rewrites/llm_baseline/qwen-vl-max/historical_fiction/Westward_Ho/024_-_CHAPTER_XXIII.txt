“My mariners, Souls that have toil'd, and wrought, and thought with me—Death closes all: but something ere the end,
Some work of noble note, may yet be done,
Not unbecoming men that strove with gods!”

—TENNYSON'S *Ulysses*.

Three years had passed since that small band knelt beneath the great tree of Guayra at evensong—years that seemed blank, marked only by scattered names and misspelled records. They had wandered through untouched hills and dense forests across a region eight hundred miles long and four hundred wide, searching for the Golden City—Manoa—and found nothing. They had followed the banks of the Orinoco, crossed the thunderous foam of Maypures, explored the upper reaches of the mighty Amazon, ventured into Peru itself, and walked among the cinchona groves of Loxa, unaware of their healing powers. They had gazed upon the virgin snows of Chimborazo rising above storm clouds, and watched Cotopaxi darken in fury as rivers of fire poured down its slopes. Defeated by the Andes, they turned east again, plunging from alpine cliffs into “the green and misty ocean of the Montana.” Slowly, painfully, they had made their way north along the eastern foot of the inland Cordillera, and now they rested—bivouacked—on one of the many tributaries of the Meta, flowing from the Suma Paz into the forested plains. There they sat, their watchfires flickering on the water, beneath towering trees, Amyas, Cary, Brimblecombe, Yeo, and the loyal Indian boy who had stayed with them through every trial—alive, yes, but no closer to Manoa than before. Its fairy lake, golden palaces, and wonders remained just out of reach. Again and again, rumors had stirred them—faint whispers of the city’s existence—only to lead to fresh disappointment, and hope deferred, which, as Scripture says, sickens the heart.

There they sat—forty-four men left from the eighty-four who had begun their journey under the tree of Guayra. Where were the others?

“Their bones are scatter'd far and wide,
By mount, by stream, and sea.”

Drew, the master, lies on the banks of the Rio Negro, five brave companions beside him, slain by poisoned arrows in a failed attempt to cross the mountain passes of Parima. Two more perished in the Andean valleys, frozen by icy hail falling from the condor’s nest. Four drowned in the rapids of the Orinoco. Five or six wounded men were left behind among friendly Indians, perhaps never to be recovered. Fever, snakes, jaguars, alligators, cannibal fish, electric eels—each month took its toll. No trace remains of their march through the primeval wilds except those lonely graves.

Yet still they sit, beside the quiet river, beneath the tropical moon—sun-baked and lean, but strong and bold as ever. The quiet flame of English courage burns in every eye; the warm smile of English humor lingers on every lip. Danger is mocked, labor turned to sport, as cheerfully as when they first sailed past the bar of Bideford, in days that feel like a life before time. Their beards hang down to their chests; their long hair is tied back like women’s, to shield against the sun. Their leggings are made from the soft hide of the Guazu-puti deer; their shirts patched with cotton from Indian looms. Jaguar pelts, puma skins, and monkey hides drape their shoulders. Their powder is gone, their muskets ruined by the constant steam of the jungle, abandoned in a cave near an Orinoco cataract. But their swords gleam bright and deadly as ever. They carry bows stronger than any Indian could draw, arrows tipped with fragments of armor. Many wield the pocuna—the Indian blowgun—more silent and deadly than the firearms they’ve left behind. So they have wandered, and so they will wander—lords of the forest and its beasts, feared by hostile tribes, yet kind and just to those who deal fairly. Many a Carib, Ature, Solimo, and Guahiba tell with awe of the bearded strangers who called themselves enemies of the treacherous Spaniards and spoke of a great queen across the seas who would send her warriors to free and avenge the oppressed.

The men sleep among the trees—some on the ground, some in grass hammocks strung between trunks. All is still, save the heavy splash of a tapir feeding in the river, tearing up waterweed for his night’s meal. Occasionally, a jaguar climbs from branch to branch after prey, waking monkeys, who in turn rouse the birds. For ten minutes, the forest erupts in unearthly roars, howls, shrieks, and cackles—like hell itself breaking loose—but it fades quickly. Even during the chaos, the sleepers do not stir, nor does it interrupt the council burning beside the watchfire, where Amyas, Cary, Brimblecombe, and Yeo debate their next move. A hundred such councils have come and gone—always without result. This one may be no different. Yet it feels heavier than most. The two-year agreement to search for Manoa has ended. If they stay here much longer, they’ll spend their lives in this green wilderness.

“Well,” said Will Cary, drawing on his cigar, “at least we’ve learned something from those last Indians. It’s a comfort to smoke again after three weeks without tobacco.”

“For me,” Jack Brimblecombe replied, “Heaven forgive me, but when I get that magical leaf between my teeth, I’m tempted to sit still as a chimney and smoke till I die—no movement, no effort.”

“Then I’ll ban tobacco, Master Parson,” Amyas said. “We must rise and go tomorrow. We’ve wasted three full days here—nothing achieved.”

“Will we ever achieve anything?” Brimblecombe asked. “It seems Manoa’s gold is like the rainbow’s touch—always just beyond reach.”

Amyas was silent. So were the others. There was no denying it: their hopes were fading. Across the vast circuit they’d traveled, only disappointment waited.

“There’s one chance left,” Amyas said finally. “The mountains east of the Orinoco—where we failed first. Perhaps the Incas moved there when they fled.”

“Why not?” said Cary. “They’d have the forests, the Llanos, half a dozen great rivers—all between them and those dogs of Spaniards.”

“Shall we try again?” Amyas asked. “This river feeds into the Orinoco. Once there, we’re at the base of the mountains. What say you, Yeo?”

“I cannot forget,” Yeo said, “how the Indians warned us about those mountains—how vast they are, how hard to cross, with cliffs high above and thick forests below. And we lost five good men there already.”

“What matters?” Amyas replied. “No forest can be thicker than the ones we’ve already cut through. If I’d had a monkey’s tail, I could’ve walked a hundred miles along the treetops—far easier than crawling through vines and being eaten alive by creatures from dawn to dusk.”

“But remember,” Jack added, “they warned us about the Amazons.”

“Jack, afraid of women?”

“Why not?” Jack answered. “I wouldn’t run from a man—I know that. But a woman? It’s unnatural. They must be witches or devils. The Caribs feared them. They said there were men without necks, eyes in their chests. How can a Christian fight such beings?”

“He couldn’t chop off their heads, true,” Amyas said, “but a thrust in the ribs should do as much as for any other.”

“Well,” Jack said, “if I fight, let it be honest flesh and blood. Not these monstrous creatures. How do we know they’re not invulnerable by magic?”

“How do you know they are?” Cary countered. “And as for the Amazons—woman is woman everywhere. I’d wager you could charm them with a few compliments, just like burghers’ wives. Pity I don’t have a court suit and a Spanish hat. I’d go alone as ambassador, orange in one hand, handkerchief in the other, and within a week be as close to Queen Blackfacealinda as Raleigh is to the Queen at Whitehall.”

“Gentlemen,” Yeo said, “where you go, I go—and every man of us, I believe. But we’ve lost half our company. Our ammunition is gone. Without guns, we’re no better than the naked savages around us. And yet—do you recall how Cortez and Pizarro conquered? Not by strength alone, but by the terror of their cannons and horses—beasts I have no faith in. Had we brought such weapons, might we not have stunned the people of Manoa?”

“After we found them,” Amyas laughed. “Like the old fable—every craftsman thinks his trade is the pillar of society.”

“Perhaps,” Yeo said, “as a gunner, I overvalue guns. But need I use slate and pencil to see this truth? Are forty men without shot as strong as eighty with?”

“You’re right, old friend,” Amyas said. “I was only jesting—out of sorrow. I laugh because I cannot cry. Our chance is gone, I think—though I won’t admit it to the men.”

“Sir,” Yeo said, “I feel the Lord’s hand against us in this. Whether He means to keep this wealth for nobler hands, or hide the city in His secret place to spare mankind from greed, and England from the sins of gold—as I’ve seen among the Spaniards—I cannot say. But I’ve long heard a voice within: ‘Salvation Yeo, thou shalt never behold the Golden City on earth, where heathens worship sun and moon. Be content, then, to see the Golden City above, where neither sun nor moon shines, but the Lord God and the Lamb are the light.’”

There was a quiet dignity in Yeo when he spoke like this—a reverence that made even Amyas and Cary look upon him as Muslims regard holy fools, touched by divine insight. Brimblecombe, whose pious soul held Yeo in deep respect despite his Anabaptist beliefs, murmured gently:

“Amen. Amen. My masters all. And it’s been on my mind, too, that providence opposes our going east. See how, in the past two years, whenever we’ve pushed east, we’ve met disaster and lost men. But whenever we’ve gone westward—westward-ho—we’ve prospered. And we prosper still.”

“And what’s more,” Yeo added, “if dreams are from the Lord, as Scripture says, I believe mine last night came from Him. As I lay by the fire, I heard my little maid’s voice calling—clearer than ever I’ve heard. She repeated the words she learned from me and William Penberthy: 'Westward-ho! jolly mariners all!'—a reckless song, my masters, from our wild days. But she called it plain as day, again and again, until I answered, 'Coming, my maid, coming!' After that, she called no more—God grant I find her yet—and I woke.”

Cary had long stopped laughing at Yeo’s talk of the “little maid.” Amyas nodded.

“So be it, Yeo—if the rest agree. But what shall we do westward?”

“Do?” Cary said. “Plenty. Gold, and Spaniards too, they say, lie beyond these mountains. Our swords won’t rust for lack of adventure, my gallant knights-errant.”

They talked on. By midnight, a plan was formed—desperate, yes—but what did such hearts care? They would cross the Cordillera to Santa Fe de Bogotá, famed for its riches. They’d seize either the town or a gold convoy, head to the nearest river—said to flow north—and build canoes to reach the northern sea. From there, if fortune smiled, they’d capture a Spanish ship and sail home—not with Manoa’s treasure, but with a fair haul of Spanish gold. A new dream. Wild, yes—but no wilder than Drake’s fulfilled exploits, or Oxenham’s thwarted ambition.

That night, Amyas sat late, heart heavy. To abandon the dream of years was hard. To face his mother—harder. But it must be done—for the men’s sake. The next day, the plan was proposed—and welcomed. They would climb toward the mountains, rest, retrieve their wounded, then begin anew—with new hopes, new dangers. They were used to danger.

They set off the next morning, spirits lifted. For hours, they paddled easily up the glassy, windless river, flanked by walls of forest blooming with flowers, alive with birds and insects. Vines hung from overhanging branches, dipping into the water, reflecting their own radiant blossoms in the clear mirror. River, trees, flowers, birds—everything was a fairyland, but colossal. Yet they barely noticed. These sights were routine now. Trees two hundred feet tall, covered in yellow or purple blooms from top to bottom. Every branch a hanging garden of crimson orchids and orange vanillas. Strange shapes, colors, creatures—more dazzling than any opium-dream—were common. Monkeys raced along the treetops, whistling and grunting, but no longer amused them. The roar of jaguars, the rustle of boas—no longer frightened them. When a brilliant green-and-rose fish, flat-bodied like a bream, flab-finned like a salmon, saw-toothed like a shark, leapt onto the canoe to escape a charging alligator—whose snout nearly rattled against the hull—Jack didn’t flinch. He simply picked it up.

“He’s four pounds!” he said. “If you can catch ‘pirai’ like that, old fellow, stay in our wake. We’ll give you the scraps for wages.”

Yes. The human mind is not infinite, as people suppose. No matter how hungry the appetite for wonder, once satisfied, it grows dull—like a city diner after a lord mayor’s feast. Only the highest minds—Humboldt, Bonpland, Schomburgk—can endure the endless spectacle of the tropics. Even they tire. Words fail. Superlatives become meaningless. What can one say? “Wonderful, wonderful!” Then, “wonderful, past all whooping!” Humboldt himself could not paint it. Let readers fill in the meaning of “South American forest”—for I cannot.

These adventurers could not. Early explorers rarely described the beauty of the tropics—Spanish or English. Only Columbus (because everything was new) and Raleigh (perhaps the most gifted traveler besides Humboldt) hinted at it. But even they fell short. Their souls were overwhelmed. Like Plato’s men dragged from darkness into sunlight, they saw things too vast, too strange, to speak of. Why talk? Just say, “God is great!”

So it was with them. The grandeur around uplifted their spirits, kept them reverent, heroic. But they knew nothing of the flora and fauna in artistic or scientific terms. One tree made good bows; another, canoes; one bore edible fruit; another yielded caoutchouc; one supplied poison for arrows; ashes from palm nuts made salt; another tree gave milk when tapped. They drank it, thanked God, and felt no surprise. God was great—they had known that before the tropics.

Noble, childlike heroes—romantic enough to avoid the hysterical wonder that often comes from modern skepticism. We don’t trust God enough to expect the impossible. When we see a wonder, we shout and praise ourselves for being “refined.” But they saw it, accepted it, and moved on.

Hour after hour, they paddled, sheltering under the southern bank while the sun blazed on the northern wall of mimosas, figs, laurels, bamboo tufts, and parasitic blooms. Layer upon layer of color rose to the sky, where the outline blurred into a quivering rainbow of hues, mingling with the blue mist.

As the sun climbed, silence fell. Jaguars and monkeys hid. Birds fell silent. Butterflies slept on leaves, indistinguishable from flowers. A colibri hummed down to drink, vanished into the dark woods. A parrot screamed from a limb. A monkey slid down a liana, dipped water, chattered back—then froze, seeing an alligator lurking below. Capybaras, rabbit-sized sheep, paddled slowly among blue water-lilies. Water-hens ran on floating leaves. A freshwater dolphin surfaced, spouted spray, arched a rainbow, and sank again. Flamingoes stood knee-deep, dreaming. Cranes pranced. Ibises and egrets probed the water.

By noon, even they had vanished. Silence reigned—so deep it could be heard. Like the quiet of Devon woods or Exmoor’s heather fields. Humboldt wrote: “If we listen closely beyond the silence, we hear a stifled, continuous hum—the swarm of insects in the air, in bark, in soil, in the holes of lizards, millepedes, bees. A voice telling us that all nature breathes, that life swarms in the earth as in water and air.”

At last, a soft murmur grew into a roar. They neared a cataract. Turning a bend, where the soil rose into a low cliff fringed with ferns, they saw a scene that silenced them—not with wonder, but with something like disgust.

“Rapids again!” someone muttered. “I thought we’d had enough on the Orinoco.”

“We’ll have to drag the canoes overland. Three hours lost—in the hottest part of the day.”

“There’s worse behind—see the spray beyond the palms?”

“Stop grumbling,” Amyas said. “Don’t cry out before you’re hurt. Paddle to the largest island. Let’s look.”
