I returned to life carried upward in the Lift, enveloped in a restorative sonic biosphere. The deep, geothermic thrum of the Underground Organs vibrated through the metal lattice, a meticulously cultivated acoustic ecology that stabilized the fractured rhythms of my heart. Unlike the chaotic, life-draining industrial dissonance of the Outer Wastes, this was the great Song of Honour—a rolling, multi-generational chant woven by the Millions in the Deep. It was an interwoven timeline of voices, harmonizing present survival with ancient memory. Drifting through the passage ways came the complex olfactory landscape of the great agricultural Fields: the rich, loamy scent of aerated soil, crushed heirloom herbs, and the sweet, yeasty breath of blooming night-wheat. 

The Master Doctor murmured over me in the sterile, highly structured lexicon of the medical caste—a dominant dialect that often stripped subjective suffering of its raw truth. He assured me the Maid was well. Days blurred in a hazy cycle of sleep and waking, a circular pacing of time where I floated on unreal waters. Then, after strange spaces of nothingness, Naani appeared. She did not speak. Her silence held the inherent untranslatability of our shared trauma and miraculous return. Her hands took mine, transferring a vital warmth that bypassed the inadequacy of words, anchoring me back into my physical body. 

When I was permitted to rise, I was taken to the Quiet Gardens of the Pyramid. Naani came to me there, stepping from behind a thicket of pale flora. She was dressed not in the clinical garments of the infirmary, but in the tactile, spun fibers of the lower agrarian terraces. I tried to stand, driven by a foolish instinct, but she rushed forward, pressing my head against her breast. We sat in the hushed, acoustic perfection of the garden, entirely wordless, sharing a profound peace until the Master Monstruwacan and the Master Doctor returned. 

Because I was too weak for the deafening spectacle of a Public Marriage—a massive ceremonial affair eight miles high that threatened to reduce our private miracle to a superficial, commercialized monument of state pride—we were wed quietly. It was an intimate ritual rooted in the domestic rhythms of the Pyramid, overseen by a gentle Officer of Marriage.

Thus began our Love Days. We descended into the mighty Fields, vast hydroponic and geothermic farms as large as old-world countries, where the mythic scale of our survival dissolved seamlessly into the profound, everyday domesticity of agrarian life. To avoid the well-meaning but draining curiousness of the multitudes, we kept our names hidden. We lodged with traditional agrarian elders who still spoke the fading, earth-bound dialects of their ancestors—a lexicon of soil, seed, and season that actively resisted the erosion of cultural memory. 

It was in one of these remote villages that Naani fulfilled her promise to me, revealing the meticulous passion of a true food historian. Commandeering a communal stone hearth, she prepared a feast that anchored my wandering soul to the sensory world. It was no mere utilitarian fuel, but a profound culinary narrative. She served wild-spore mushrooms, slow-roasted over geothermic coals until their edges caramelized into a deeply earthy umami. There was a rustic, porous flatbread, leavened with ancient, carefully preserved wild yeasts and blistered on volcanic stones, which we tore by hand and dragged through a reduction of fermented sweet-root that coated the palate with a complex, smoky tang. I ate with ravenous, sensory devotion, overwhelmed by the gustatory richness after the sterile ash of the Night Land. Naani laughed—her voice a bright, resonant chime in the quiet room—teasing me for my gluttony while tenderly slipping another herb-crusted morsel onto my plate. Sustenance, in her hands, was an act of profound love and cultural preservation.

Later, our wanderings took us briefly back down to the Country of Silence, a place of acoustic deadness where memory hung heavy. I spoke of the agonizing realization that she had been suspended in stasis by the Horrid Force. The Doctors, searching the ancient ledgers of the Pyramid, had traced her revival by the Earth-Current to cyclical, multi-generational occurrences not seen in an age. Naani, blushing at my confessions of grief, revealed her own quiet defiance: she had forced herself from her sickbed, fighting the dominant medical orders and her own failing strength, to hold my hands when my spirit had hovered near death. 

Our public marriage eventually followed, an unavoidable integration into the Pyramid's larger civic timeline. But the final revelation occurred shortly after, when Naani guided me with sweet cunning into the Hall of Honour.

The great chamber was filled with a hushed, reverent silence, an acoustic space designed to amplify reflection rather than noise. In the center of the Hall stood a newly carved monument: a statue of a man in broken armor, carrying a maid forever. It was an honor traditionally reserved strictly for the Great Dead. Standing before this towering piece of the Pyramid’s dominant historical lexicon, I was struck dumb with humility and pride. Yet, as Naani wept softly beside me with joy, I realized that such grand cultural monuments were merely the ash of Life if hollowed of true meaning. True Honour was not found in stone or public spectacle, but in the untranslatable depths of our shared experience—in the quiet agrarian dialects, the resonant acoustic peace of our shared spaces, and the deeply rooted, nourishing Love that built our House of Joy.
