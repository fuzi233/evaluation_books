Captain Willoughby had acquired a reputation at his club as the ultimate bore. He was a relentless storyteller, regaling audiences with tedious tales of strangers none of whom they knew, and he possessed an unshakeable immunity to interruption. He neither listened nor paused; he simply spoke, wrapping himself in a fog of self-importance that flattened conversation around him like a steamroller on fresh asphalt. Whether delivering trite anecdotes or profound aphorisms, he did so with the air of someone having just discovered a new truth. Those unfamiliar with him might sit through his monologues, hoping for redemption, but his regular members, upon spotting him, would rise en masse and flee. Consequently, within thirty minutes of entering, Willoughby invariably claimed the largest empty corner of the room, having driven everyone else out.

One crisp afternoon in early October, while seated in this solitary domain, the waiter approached with a calling card. Willoughby accepted it eagerly; he craved company, yet his usual circle had vanished to attend urgent engagements. However, as he scanned the card, his expression darkened.

"Colonel Durrance!" he muttered, scratching his head.

Durrance had never visited him cordially before, and surely he wouldn't make an exception now. It appeared Durrance had somehow deduced Willoughby's recent trip to Kingsbridge.

"Does Colonel Durrance know I'm here?" Willoughby asked.

"Yes, sir," the waiter confirmed.

"Very well. Show him in."

Willoughby braced himself. No doubt Durrance intended to interrogate him regarding Miss Ethne Eustace. Diplomacy would be required to deflect such inquiries, but Willoughby had no desire to intervene further in the feud between Feversham and Durrance. He trusted his social skills but wished to avoid exercising them in this instance. When Durrance entered, Willoughby regarded him with deep suspicion.

Surprisingly, Durrance offered no immediate questions. Willoughby stood, gestured his guest toward his isolated corner, and asked, "Will you smoke?" He paused, realizing the implication of his own habit. "I beg your pardon."

"Oh, I'll smoke," Durrance replied smoothly. "It isn't strictly true that a man cannot enjoy tobacco without seeing the smoke. If I let my cigar go out, I'd know instantly. But don't worry; I won't let it out."

With deliberate precision, Durrance lit his cigar and reclined comfortably. "I'm fortunate to catch you, Willoughby," he continued. "I'm only in town today. I visit Devonshire periodically to see my eye doctor, and I was eager to meet you. My friend Mather mentioned you were away in the countryside during my last visit. Do you recall Mather? We served together in Suakin."

"Of course," Willoughby said warmly. He was relieved to pivot the conversation to Mather, hoping the distraction would obscure the elephant in the room.

"We're both curious about one thing," Durrance pressed. "Have you ever encountered an Arab named Abou Fatma?"

"Abou Fatma," Willoughby drawled. "One of the Hadendoas?"

"No, a member of the Kabbabish tribe."

"Abou Fatma?" Willoughby repeated, feigning ignorance. "No, I've never met him." Then he stopped abruptly.

Durrance noted the unnatural pause. One would expect a follow-up like, "Why do you ask?" Yet Willoughby remained silent. Internally, Willoughby marveled at how Durrance could possibly know of Abou Fatma—a name he himself had heard only once a year prior on the veranda of the Palace in Suakin. He had told the truth; he truly hadn't met the man, despite Feversham mentioning him.

"That makes me even more curious," Durrance persisted. "Mather and I were on the final reconnaissance in '84. We found Abou Fatma hiding in the bushes near Sinkat Fort. He revealed that he had concealed the Gordon letters in Berber. Ah! Now the name rings a bell."

"I was just retrieving my pipe," Willoughby lied, pulling it from his pocket. "But I do remember the name now that you mention the letters."

"They were delivered to you in Suakin roughly fifteen months ago," Durrance explained. "Mather showed me the report in the *Evening Standard*. I'm wondering if Abou Fatma returned to Berber to retrieve them. But since you claim never to have seen him, it implies he didn't recover them."

Willoughby felt a pang of regret for his hasty denial. "No," he admitted with hesitant awkwardness. "It wasn't Abou Fatma."

He dreaded the inevitable next question. Filling his pipe, he pondered his response, but Durrance offered none immediately.

"I wondered," Durrance mused slowly. "I suspected Abou Fatma wouldn't return to Berber. After all, whoever undertook that mission did so at the risk of their life. With Gordon dead, there was no obvious incentive to finish the job."

"Quite so," Willoughby breathed, feeling a wave of relief.

"For no obvious reason," Durrance corrected calmly.

Willoughby glanced sharply at his companion, fearing Durrance knew about his Kingsbridge excursion. Durrance, however, leaned back, tilting his face upward, seemingly losing interest in the Gordon letters. He posed no further questions on the subject.

While Willoughby considered how Feversham might redeem himself, Durrance formulated his own theory. The only plausible explanation for Feversham's recovery of the letters lay in the ruins of Berber. There had been no threat to Suakin since the last reconnaissance; the great troopships sailed safely toward Suez. Willoughby had risked nothing but his health in that white palace on the Red Sea. There was no moment where Feversham could have cried out, *"Your life was forfeit but for me, whom you call a coward."*

Durrance recalled the rumors from Wadi Halfa and his furloughs. Could the fugitive from Khartum, who had confessed his story to him in the silent ruins of Sinkat, have repeated it at Suakin within Feversham's hearing? He was convinced he was right. Willoughby's evasiveness confirmed it. Willoughby had undoubtedly been instructed by Ethne to hold his tongue. Durrance, accustomed to reticence, viewed it as proof of his hypothesis. Furthermore, Willoughby's voice carried a note of wariness that was painfully familiar—it sounded exactly like Ethne speaking.

Durrance had set traps earlier, specifically his comment about "no obvious reason," and Willoughby's slight start and hurried movements proved he had triggered them. Still, Durrance hoped Willoughby wouldn't write to Ethne warning her of his investigation.

"I wanted to explain why I sought you out," Durrance said. "It concerns Harry Feversham."

Willoughby, believing he had escaped the Abou Fatma trap, nearly jumped in his chair. Durrance ignored his agitation.

"Something happened to Feversham over five years ago. He either did something wrong or failed to act, keeping the secret close, until he simply disappeared. Are you returning to the Sudan, Willoughby?"

"In a week," Willoughby replied.

"Well, Harry Feversham is in the Sudan," Durrance stated, leaning forward. "Did you know that?"

"You know?" Willoughby exclaimed.

"Yes. I ran into him this spring at Wadi Halfa," Durrance continued. "He had fallen quite low. I told you about our meeting outside the Café Tewfikieh."

"It's strange, isn't it? A man you knew well vanishing so completely, dropping out of sight as if falling down an oubliette in an old French castle. I want you to watch for him, Willoughby. Try to get him back on his feet. Let me know if you spot him. Harry Feversham was a true friend—one of my few."

"All right," Willoughby said cheerfully, sensing the tension dissolve. "I'll look out for Feversham. I remember he was a great friend of yours."

He reached for the matches. Durrance heard the scratch of the phosphorus and the flare. Willoughby lit his pipe—a well-used briar that bubbled as he drew on it.

"Yes, a great friend," Durrance agreed. "You and I dined with him in his apartment overlooking St. James's Park just before we left England."

At these words, Willoughby's pipe sputtered and died. Silence hung heavy for a moment, then Willoughby swore violently and stomped his foot on the carpet.

Durrance's mind raced, constructing a vivid scene: himself smoking, a round table between them, and Willoughby frozen mid-light, staring suspiciously at the blind man until the burning match singed his fingers, causing him to curse and stamp it out. Durrance realized he had never truly considered that dinner until now.

"There were the three of us—Feversham, you, and I," Durrance continued. "Feversham invited us to announce his engagement to Miss Eustace. He had just returned from Dublin. That was the last time we saw him clearly."
