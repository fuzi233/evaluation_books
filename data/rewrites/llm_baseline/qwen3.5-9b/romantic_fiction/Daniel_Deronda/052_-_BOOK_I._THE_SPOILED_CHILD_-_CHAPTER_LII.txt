The next morning, among the pile of correspondence awaiting Daniel Deronda, lay a letter from Hans Meyrick. It spanned four quarto pages, penned in the small, exquisite handwriting characteristic of the Meyrick family.

"My Dear Deronda," the letter began, "in return for your sketch of Italian movements and your broader views on worldly affairs, I must report that here at home, the most prudent opinion regarding the effects of current events is simply that 'time will show.' Regarding the causes of past effects, it is becoming clear that the recent fraudulent telegrams explain last year's cattle plague—a refutation of false philosophy and a justification for compensating the farmers. My own theory that a murrain will soon strike the commercial class, revealing itself later in the sudden sale of rejected paintings, has been dismissed as an unsound use of analogy. Yet, there are minds unwilling to deprive even the neglected painter of his solace. To my sensibility, there is great beauty in the notion that a bad judge might pay a high price for my *Berenice* series, thereby punishing the city men for my ill-deserved luck.

"Meanwhile, I console myself for your absence by discovering advantages within it. I shine like Hesperus when Hyperion has departed; I sit with our Hebrew prophet and study his head during the hours he formerly spent with you, earning credit with him as a learned young Gentile who would have been a Jew if circumstances allowed. We agree on the general principle that whatever is best is therefore Jewish. Though I have never considered myself a severe reasoner, I see the logic: if whatever is best is A, and B happens to be best, then B must be A, regardless of prior expectations. On this principle, I could understand a pamphlet proving all good art is Protestant. However, our prophet is an uncommonly interesting sitter—a superior model to Rembrandt's Rabbi—and I depart from him with fresh discoveries daily. Constantly, I marvel that despite his fiery passion for his race and traditions, he is not a rigid Jew, spitting at the word 'Christian' and rejoicing in the prospect of the Gentile mouth watering in vain for a slice of roasted Leviathan, while Israel sends up plates for more *ad libitum*. (You perceive my studies prepared me for this expectation of the orthodox Jew.) I confess I have always viewed your account of Mordecai as somewhat apologetic, merely part of your tendency to adopt an antediluvian perspective to avoid injustice to the megatherium. But now, hearing him in his proper person, I find him a philosophical, allegorical, mystical believer nonetheless, possessing a sharp dialectic point capable of silencing any argumentative rattler of peas in a bladder. This mixture may be a Jewish prerogative. Indeed, his mind is so broad that my own correct opinions lie comfortably within it; how they align with the vast remainder is his affair, not mine. I leave the foundation to him, never having seen a basis that isn't a world-supporting elephant, however powerful or expensive to maintain. My means cannot support a private elephant, so I retreat into mystery, a cheaper and more lasting gas supplied by the decomposition of elephants. If an opinion pleases me, I treat it civilly, without suspicious inquiry. I hold a friendly feeling toward Mordecai's notion that a whole Christian is three-quarters a Jew, and that since Alexandria, the most comprehensive minds have been Jewish. I intend to point out to Mirah that, aside from Arabic and other life-incidents, there is little difference between me and Maimonides.

"But recently, I've realized that your shallow lover cannot help making declarations. If Mirah's ways were less distracting, and her presence less of a heaven to watch, I would long ago have thrown myself at her feet, requesting her to state directly whether she wished me to blow my brains out. I possess a knack of hoping, equivalent to an estate in reversion, provided one resists the temptation to convert it into certainty, which may ruin everything. My Hope wanders among orchard blossoms, feels warm snow falling on it through sunshine, and doubts nothing. But catching sight of Certainty in the distance, it sees an ugly, Janus-faced deity winking dubiously on the near side, and turns away quickly. You, with your supreme reasonableness, self-nullification, and preparation for the worst, know nothing of Hope, that immortal, delicious maiden forever courted and forever propitious, whom fools call deceitful as if Hope carried the cup of disappointment. No, it is her deadly enemy, Certainty, whom she escapes only by transformation. (Do you observe my new vein of allegory?) Seriously, however, I must allege that truth prevails, prejudice melts before it, diversity accompanied by merit becomes fascinating, and no virtuous aspiration is frustrated—all doctrines of the schools implying that the Jewess I prefer will prefer me. Any blockhead cites generalities, but the mind-master discerns the particular cases they represent.

"I am less convinced that my society compensates Mordecai for your absence, though another substitute occasionally arrives in the form of Jacob Cohen. It is worthwhile to catch our prophet's expression when he has that remarkable young Israel on his knee, pouring forth Semitic inspiration with a sublime look of melancholy patience and devoutness. Sometimes Jacob thinks Hebrew would be more edifying if he stopped his ears with his palms and imitated the venerable sounds heard through that muffled medium. When Mordecai gently draws down the little fists and holds them fast, Jacob's features assume extraordinary activity, as if walking through a menagerie, succeeding best with the owl and the peccary. You have surely seen this. He treats me with familiar ease, generally looking at me as a second-hand Christian commodity likely to depreciate, remarking on my disadvantages with a frankness implying thoughts of future purchase. It is pleasant, however, to see the change in him if Mirah enters. He turns childlike instantly—his age usually striking one as resembling Israelitish garments in the desert, perhaps near forty, yet with an air of recent production. With Mirah, he reminds me of dogs brought up by women, remaining manageable only by them. Still, the dog is fond of Mordecai, bringing sugar-plums to share, filling his own mouth to an embarrassing extent while watching how Mordecai handles a smaller supply. Judging from this modern Jacob at six years old, I am astonished that his race hasn't bought us all up long ago, pocketing our feebler generations as stock and scrip, mere slave property. There is one Jewess I wouldn't mind being a slave to. But I wish I didn't imagine Mirah grows slightly sadder, trying constantly to hide it. It is natural, of course, while she watches the slow death of this brother, whom she worships with looks of loving devoutness that make me wish to be in his place.

"For the rest, we are a little merrier than usual. Rex Gascoigne—you recall the head you admired among my sketches, a fellow with a good upper lip studying law—now has rooms in town nearby and has had a neat sister (also with a good upper lip) staying with him for two weeks. I introduced them to my mother and the girls, who learned from Miss Gascoigne that she is a cousin to your Vandyke Duchess!!! I added exclamation marks to denote the surprise this information initially produced on my feeble understanding. Upon reflection, I found no ground for surprise unless I had previously believed nobody could be anyone's cousin without my knowledge. This kind of surprise, I take it, depends on a lively spine coupled with a constant nullity of brain. There was a fellow I met in Rome who effervesced with surprise at the simplest information. Tell him you loved easy boots; he would always reply, 'No! Are you?' with the same energetic wonder: the very fellow Pastoral Browne prophesied about—"A wretch so empty that if e'er there be / In nature found the least vacuity / 'Twill be in him." I have accounted for it all—he possessed a lively spine.

"However, this cousinship with the Duchess emerged by chance one day Mirah was visiting, and they discussed the Mallingers. *Apropos*; I am becoming so important that I have rival invitations. Gascoigne invites me to his father's rectory in August to see the countryside. But I think self-interest dictates I go to Topping Abbey, for Sir Hugo has invited me and proposed—God bless him for his rashness!—that I paint a picture of his three daughters sitting on a bank, in the Gainsborough style. He visited my studio recently, recommending I focus on portraiture. Of course, I know what that means: 'My good fellow, your attempts at the historic and poetic are simply pitiable. Your brush is that of a successful portrait-painter—it has a little truth and great facility in falsehood—your idealism will never suffice for gods and heroes, but it may fetch a high price as flattery. Fate, my friend, has made you the hind wheel—*rota posterior curras, et in axe secundo*—run behind because you can't help it.' What great effort it costs our friends to offer such candid opinions! I have even known a man call specifically to tell me I had irretrievably exposed my lack of judgment in treating my subject, adding that if I had asked, he would have lent his own judgment.
