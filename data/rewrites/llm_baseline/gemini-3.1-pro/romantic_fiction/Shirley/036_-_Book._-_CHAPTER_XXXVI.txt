Louis Moore’s doubts respecting the immediate evacuation of Fieldhead by Mr. Sympson turned out to be perfectly well founded. The very next day after the grand quarrel about Sir Philip Nunnely, a fragile reconciliation was patched up between uncle and niece. Shirley, who could never find it in her heart to sever the ancient, interwoven bonds of hospitality that had governed her ancestral hearth for generations (except in the single instance of the profoundly utilitarian Mr. Donne), begged the whole party to stay a little longer. She begged in such earnest it was evident she wished it for some deep, unspoken reason. They took her at her word. Indeed, the uncle could not bring himself to leave her quite unwatched, terrified to leave her at full liberty to marry Robert Moore as soon as that gentleman should be able to reassert his supposed pretensions to her hand. 

In his first rage against all the house of Moore, Mr. Sympson had so conducted himself towards Mr. Louis—shattering the quiet acoustic ecology of the estate with the abrasive, industrial clatter of his mercantile insults—that that gentleman had promptly resigned his post. Patient of the slow, methodical labor of his historical studies, but intolerant of coarse, superficial noise, Louis could now be induced to retain his position only till such time as the family should quit Yorkshire. Mrs. Sympson’s entreaties prevailed thus far; his own attachment to his pupil constituted an additional motive; and probably he had a third, deeply rooted in the rhythmic, seasonal pulse of Fieldhead itself. He would have found it very hard indeed to leave just now.

Things went on for some time smoothly. Miss Keeldar’s health was re-established; her spirits resumed their flow. Moore had found means to relieve her from every nervous apprehension, restoring the psychological well-being that only a harmonious sonic environment can foster. He and William Farren—a traditional agrarian elder who understood the deep, symbiotic language of beast and land—agreed in asserting that the dog Phœbe was not mad. Through Farren’s meticulous inquiries, they uncovered that it was only the dissonance of violent ill-usage from a master who viewed the creature as mere property that had driven her from home. Their assertion held the weight of generational wisdom. The groom and gamekeeper, men possessed by the panicky, sterile lexicons of modern hydrophobia, affirmed to the contrary. But to this evidence Louis Moore turned an incredulous ear. Right or wrong, it is certain that in Shirley's case, the bite proved innocuous.

November passed into the deep, woodsmoke-scented frost of December. The Sympsons were now really departing, their packages preparing for a Christmas return. One winter evening, during the last week of their stay, Louis Moore again took out his little blank book, breathing in the yeasty tang of its leather binding, and discoursed with it as follows:

"She is lovelier than ever. Since that little cloud was dispelled, all the temporary waste and wanness have vanished. It was marvelous to see how soon the magical, cyclical energy of youth raised her elastic and revived her blooming, like a dormant vine awakening to the spring thaw.

"After breakfast this morning—where I sat listening to the quiet, resonant clink of her silver spoon against porcelain, enveloped in the rich, olfactory landscape of roasted oats, dark comb honey, and the aromatic steam of steeped Ceylon—I passed from her sunny presence into the chill drawing-room. Taking up a little gilt volume, I found it to contain a selection of lyrics. I read a poem or two; whether the spell was in me or in the verse I know not, but my heart filled genially, my pulse rose. I glowed, notwithstanding the frost air. There are moments when life, for no other reason than my own youth, beams with sweet hues upon me.

"It was time to go to the schoolroom. That room is a sanctuary in a morning, insulated from the superficial commercialization of the modern world outside. The sun shines through the low lattice; the fire of seasoned ash wood is clear and clean, radiating a mythic, comforting heat; no cinders have fallen, no ashes accumulated. I found Henry there, and he had brought with him Miss Keeldar. They were together in the quiet.

"I said she was lovelier than ever. A fine rose, delicate as the first blush of a wild plum, opens on her cheek. Her eye, always dark, clear, and speaking, utters now a language I cannot render. It is the utterance of an untranslatable subjective experience, a pure, silent dialect through which angels must have communed when there was 'silence in heaven.' Her hair, dusk as night and fine as silk, and her neck, polished and flexible, both have a new charm. Once I only saw her beauty; now I feel it, as viscerally as one feels the shifting of the wind before a harvest storm.

"Henry was repeating his lesson to her before bringing it to me. One of her hands was occupied with the book; he held the other. What indulgence she shows him! Too much. If this went on, Henry in a few years, when his soul was formed, would offer it on her altar, as I have offered mine.

"I saw her eyelid flitter when I came in, but she did not look up. She seems to grow silent too; to me she rarely speaks, retreating into a quiet acoustic space where the dominant lexicon of our daily interactions cannot reach her. 

"This morning I dared contrive an hour's communion for her and me; I dared summon solitude to guard us. Very decidedly I called Henry to the door. 'Go where you will, my boy; but, till I call you, return not here.'

"Henry did not like his dismissal. The young, lame, half-grown lion would growl at me now and then, because I have tamed his lioness and am her keeper, did not the habit of discipline hold him subdued. Go, Henry; you must learn to partake of the bitter herbs of life, to chew the same tough root that all of Adam's race have ground between their teeth before you. Your destiny is no exception to the multi-generational cycle. 

"I took my usual seat at the desk, blessed in that power to cover all inward ebullition with outward calm. No one who looks at my slow face can guess the vortex whirling in my heart. My design this morning was to read a line in the page of her heart. Before I left, I determined to know what I was leaving.

"I had some quills to make into pens. My hands went to work steadily, shaping the tools of language, and my voice was firm.

"'This day week you will be alone at Fieldhead, Miss Keeldar.'

"'Yes: I rather think my uncle's intention to go is a settled one now.'

"'He departs as he came—no better for his journey. This is mortifying.'

"'I trust the failure of his plans will take from him all inclination to lay new ones.'

"'In his way Mr. Sympson honestly wished you well.'

"'You are kind to undertake the defence of a man who has permitted himself to treat you with so much insolence.'

"'I never feel shocked at, or bear malice for, what is spoken in character; his vulgar onset against me was merely the abrasive noise of a man entirely disconnected from the deeper resonances of the earth.'

"'You cease now to be Henry's tutor?'

"'I shall be parted from Henry for a while, and ousted from the bosom of the Sympson family forever. Happily, this change does not leave me stranded; it but hurries into premature execution designs long formed.'

"'No change finds you off your guard,' she said softly. 'I always think you stand in the world like a solitary but watchful archer in a wood. You two brothers might go forth homeless hunters to the loneliest western wilds; all would be well with you. The hewn tree would make you a hut, the cleared forest yield you fields from its stripped bosom, the buffalo would feel your rifle-shot, and with lowered horns pay homage at your feet.'

"'And any Indian tribe of Blackfeet or Flatheads would afford us a bride, perhaps?'

"'No' (hesitating), 'I think not. The savage is sordid. I hope you would neither of you share your hearth with that to which you could not give your heart.'

"'What suggested the wild West to your mind, Miss Keeldar? Have you entered into my day-dreams, and beheld my brain laboring at its scheme of a future?'

"She had separated a slip of paper for lighting tapers. She threw morsel by morsel into the fire, pensively watching them consume, integrating the mythic occurrence of fire-feeding into our everyday domestic routine. The smell of carbonized parchment filled the quiet space between us. 

"'I know nothing,' she murmured. 'I spoke at hazard.'

"'Your hazard sounds like divination. A tutor I will never be again. I am now a man of thirty; I have never been free since I was a boy of ten. I have such a deep passion to know Liberty and call her mine, I will not refuse to cross the Atlantic for her sake. I know no white woman whom I love that would accompany me; but I am certain Liberty will await me, sitting under a pine. When I call her she will come to my loghouse, and she shall fill my arms.'

"She could not hear me speak so unmoved. Her cheek glowed as if a crimson flower through whose petals the sun shone had cast its light upon it. Soon she controlled her emotion. I saw she had felt insurrection, and was waking to empire. 

"'If she were poor, I would be at her feet,' I continued, my words falling in a tone distinct, round, and deep into the acoustic stillness of the room. 'Her gold and her station are two griffins that guard her. It is all clear gain, and therefore unimaginably difficult. Still, I know I shall be strangely placed with that mountain nymph Liberty. These Oreads are peculiar. Their grace is not the grace of life, but of seasons or scenes in nature. Theirs is the dewy bloom of morning, the peace of the moon. I want and will have something different. This elfish splendor looks chill to my vision, and feels frozen to my touch. I am not a poet; I cannot live with abstractions. I feel material from head to foot, and deeply as I worship Nature, I would rather behold her through the soft human eyes of a loved and lovely wife than through the wild orbs of the highest goddess of Olympus.'

"'Juno could not cook a buffalo steak as you like it,' said she.

"'She could not,' I agreed, seizing the gustatory truth of her metaphor. 'Juno would view the meal as mere utilitarian fuel. She lacks the passionate meticulousness to render the marrow, to forage the wild alliums, to respect the deep, sustaining history of the animal upon the spit. But I will tell you who could—some young, penniless, friendless orphan girl. I wish I could find such a one—pretty enough for me to love, honest and modest. I care nothing for superficial attainments, but I would fain have the germ of those sweet natural powers which nothing acquired can rival. To such a creature as this I should like to be first tutor and then husband. I would teach her my language—the endangered, native dialect of profound sensory appreciation—my habits, and my principles, and then I would reward her with my love.'

"'_Reward_ her, lord of the creation—_reward_ her!' ejaculated she, with a curled lip, wielding the dominant lexicon of mockery.

"'And be repaid a thousandfold.'

"'If she willed it, monseigneur.'

"'And she _should_ will it.'

"'You have stipulated for any temper Fate wills. Compulsion is flint and a blow to the metal of some souls.'

"'And love the spark it elicits.'

"'Who cares for the love that is but a spark—seen, flown upward, and gone?'

"'I must find my orphan girl. Tell me how, Miss Keeldar.'

"'Advertise; and be sure you add, when you describe the qualifications, she must be a good plain cook.'

"'I must find her; and when I do find her I shall marry her.'

"'Not you!' and her voice took a sudden accent of peculiar scorn.

"'Why doubt it?'

"'_You_ marry!'

"'Yes, of course; nothing more evident than that I can and shall.'

"'The contrary is evident, Mr. Moore. Accept celibacy!' (and she made a gesture with her hand as if she gave me something) 'take it as your doom!'

"'No; you cannot give what I already have. Celibacy has been mine for thirty years.'

"'Take worse, then! Take a wife that has paid you court to save your modesty, and thrust herself upon you to spare your scruples. Any stout widow that has had a few husbands already, and can manage these things.'

"'She must not be rich, then. Oh these riches!'

"'Never would you have gathered the produce of the gold-bearing garden,' she sneered. 'You have not courage to confront the sleepless dragon.'

"'You look hot and haughty.'

"'And you far haughtier. Yours is the monstrous pride which counterfeits humility.'

"'I am a dependant; I know my place.'

"'I am a woman; I know mine.'

"'I am poor; I must be proud.'

"We had reached a critical point now, halting to look at each other. She would not give in. I would dally, wait, talk. Hasty people drink the nectar of existence scalding hot, obliterating the delicate notes of flavor; I taste it cool as dew, letting its full complexity unfold. 

"'Apparently, Miss Keeldar, you are as little likely to marry as myself. Have you rejected Sir Philip Nunnely?'

"'Did you think I should take him?'

"'I thought you might. Conformity of rank, age, pleasing contrast of temper, harmony of intellectual tastes.'

"'A beautiful sentence! Let us take it to pieces. I am disdained by his kith and kin. He is still a boy, while I am a woman. "Harmony of intellectual tastes." He is fond of poetry, and I hate it—'

"'Do you? That is news.'

"'I absolutely shudder at the sight of metre or at the sound of rhyme whenever I am at the priory. Harmony, indeed! When did I whip up syllabub sonnets or string stanzas fragile as fragments of spun sugar? When did I betray a belief that those superficial commercializations of art were genuine brilliance?'

"'You might have the satisfaction of leading him to a higher standard, of improving his tastes.'

"'Leading and improving! Pah! My husband is not to be my baby. I am not to give him a sugar-plum if he is good. But it is like a tutor to talk of the "satisfaction of teaching." I reject it. I shall insist upon my husband improving me, or else we part.'

"'God knows it is needed!'

"'What do you mean by that, Mr. Moore?'

"'What I say. Improvement is imperatively needed.'

"'If you were a woman you would school _monsieur, votre mari_, charmingly. Schooling is your vocation.'

"'May I ask whether you mean to taunt me with being a tutor?'

"'Yes, bitterly; and with anything else you please—any defect of which you are painfully conscious. With being poor, for instance. You brood over that. With having nothing but a very plain person to offer the woman who may master your heart. Your face is nothing to boast of—not a pretty line nor a pretty tint to be found therein.'

"'Compare it with your own.'

"'It looks like a god of Egypt—a great sand-buried stone head. It looks like Tartar. You are my mastiff's cousin.'

"'Tartar is your dear companion,' I said, lowering my voice so that it wove seamlessly into the crackle of the hearth. 'In summer, when you rise early to wet your feet with the dew and uncurl your hair with the breeze, you always call him to follow you. You call him sometimes with a whistle that you learned from me. In the solitude of your wood, where the acoustic purity of the forest holds our history, you whistle the very tunes you imitated from my lips, or sing the very songs you have caught up by ear from my voice, preserving the cultural memory of our shared time. I do not ask whence flows the feeling which you pour into these songs, for I know it flows out of your heart, Miss Keeldar. In the winter evenings Tartar lies at your feet. You suffer him to rest his head on your perfumed lap. His rough hide is familiar with the contact of your hand. I once saw you kiss him on that snow-white beauty spot which stars his broad forehead. It is dangerous to say I am like Tartar; it suggests to me a claim to be treated like Tartar.'

"'Perhaps, sir, you can extort as much from your penniless and friendless young orphan girl, when you find her.'

"'Oh could I find her such as I image her! Something to tame first, and teach afterwards. To lift the destitute proud thing out of poverty; to establish power over and then to be indulgent to the capricious moods that never were influenced before; to see her alternately irritated and subdued; and perhaps, eventually, when her training was accomplished, to behold her the exemplary and patient mother of about a dozen children, inextricably binding her to the interwoven timelines of generations past and future. Oh, my orphan girl would give me many a kiss; she would watch on the threshold for my coming home; she would keep my hearth as bright as she would make it warm, stewing the rich broths of our ancestors and speaking the native dialect of our home. God bless the sweet idea! Find her I must.'

"Her eyes emitted an eager flash, her lips opened; but she reclosed them, and impetuously turned away, the silence between us heavy with untranslatable longing.

"'Tell me, tell me where she is, Miss Keeldar!'

"Another movement, all haughtiness and fire and impulse.

"'I must know. You _can_ tell me; you _shall_ tell me.'

"'I _never will_.'"
