As Lady Castlewood and her children passed through one door of the drawing-room, Lord Castlewood withdrew by another. Then, from beneath the delicate lace of her tambour frame, where she had been quietly stitching violets and jonquils, Lady Maria lifted her eyes—soft, steady, and full of quiet observation—to where Harry Warrington stood before an ancestral portrait, framed in the shadow of the great fireplace.

He was a sight to behold: flushed, trembling, his cheeks like rosebuds, his forehead glowing, even his ears tinged with crimson. A storm of emotion seemed to have gathered within him, and now it burst forth.

“Why did you refuse to go with our aunt, cousin?” she asked, her voice gentle but probing.

“Because your ladyship bid me stay,” he replied, his voice low but firm.

“I bid you stay? La, child! What one says in jest, you take as law! Are all Virginian gentlemen so dutiful that they believe every word a lady utters is a command? If so, Virginia must be a most delightful land for women!”

Harry’s breath caught. “You said—when we walked on the terrace two nights past—O heaven!” he cried, his voice breaking.

Ah, that sweet night!” murmured Maria, a softness coming into her gaze.

“When you gave me this rose from your own neck,” he blurted out, pulling a crumpled, withered bloom from his waistcoat pocket, “a rose I will never part with—no, by heavens, while this heart beats!”

“You said,” he went on, his voice rising, “‘Harry, if your aunt asks you to go, you will go—and if you go, you will forget me.’ Did you not say so?”

“All men forget,” sighed Maria, her tone distant, almost melancholy.

“In this cold, selfish country, perhaps,” Harry answered, his passion undimmed, “but not in ours. I would have lost an arm rather than deny the old lady. It tore my heart to say no—she has been so kind to me, so generous. But when she introduced me to—O heaven!” He kicked at a spaniel that had wandered too near the fire, sending it yelping away, then stepped forward toward her.

“Look here, cousin—if you bade me leap from that window, I would do it. If you asked me to commit murder, I would do it.”

“La! You need not squeeze my hand so tightly, you silly boy!” she said, though her pulse quickened beneath his grasp.

“I can’t help it—we are so different in the South. Where my heart lies, I cannot keep my tongue still. And you know where that heart is.” He paused, breathing hard. “Ever since that evening—since that moment—I’ve scarcely slept. I want to do something great, to distinguish myself, to become worthy of you. I wish there were giants in the world, as in the books I read, so I could fight them. I wish you were in danger, so I might save you. I wish you needed my blood—then I would give every drop. And when you told me not to go with Madame Bernstein…”

“I never told you such a thing,” she interrupted, her voice sharp.

“You did,” he insisted. “You said you knew I preferred my aunt to my cousin. And I said then, as I say now: ‘Incomparable Maria! I prefer you above all women and all angels in Paradise. I would go to dungeons, to death itself, if you commanded it!’ And do you think I would not stay anywhere—anywhere—so long as you wished me near?”

“Men always talk like that,” she said, hesitating, then correcting herself. “That is… I’ve heard so. What should a woman from the country know about such creatures? When you’re near us, you burn with promises and rapture; when you’re gone, you forget us entirely.”

“But I don’t want to go away,” he groaned. “I’ve grown tired of many things—books, I never cared for study much; games and sports I once loved, but no longer. Before I saw you, I dreamed only of being a soldier. I raged when my dear brother took my place on that ill-fated expedition—when we lost him. But now… now I care for nothing but one thing. And you know what that is.”

“You silly boy!” she said, half laughing, half chiding. “Don’t you know I am nearly old enough to be…”

“I know,” he cut in, “but what does that matter to me? Hasn’t your brother—or someone—told me tales against you? Didn’t they show me the Family Bible, with all your names and dates inscribed?”

“The cowards!” Maria exclaimed, her face flushing. “Who did that? Was it my mother-in-law—the grasping, odious, brazen harpy? Do you know all about her? How she married my father in his cups—the horrid hussy! And…”

“It wasn’t Lady Castlewood,” Harry interjected, bewildered.

“Then it was my aunt,” she continued, her voice rising. “A pretty moralist, indeed! A bishop’s widow, they say—but whose widow before and after? She intrigued with the Pretender, with the Hanoverian Court, and I dare say would have plotted with Rome or the Sultan of Turkey if she’d had the means. Do you know who her second husband was? A creature who…”

“Our aunt never spoke a word against you,” Harry broke in, more amazed than ever by her sudden fury.

She faltered. In his wide, earnest eyes, she saw alarm—not admiration. Her temper cooled.

“Well, well,” she said softly, “I am a fool. I only want you to think well of me, Harry.”

Her hand reached out, and without thought, he seized it, pressing it to his lips.

“Angel,” he whispered, gazing into her face with eyes that burned like stars. The warmth of her presence lit him from within. Two fish-pools under moonlight could not glow more brightly than those blue eyes, into which he looked as if seeking heaven itself. Yet, like the foolish dog who sees his own bone reflected in water and leaps in, losing the real one, he drowned himself in illusion. The surface was calm, but beneath lay depths—hidden currents, secrets, treasures dropped and forgotten.

What lies beneath some quiet faces? What golden dishes, what jewels of love, what bones and flesh have we cast into these still waters? And how many faithful dogs—how many hearts—have jumped in whole, only to be swallowed up?

When a woman is dragged down, what marvels may be found in her depths? *Cavete, canes!* Beware how you drink that water. What do they want of us, these siren sluts? A green-eyed Naiad sings, dances, winds around, whispers secrets, kisses feet, leers from the rushes—“Come, sweet youth! Hither, rosy Hylas!” Pop goes Hylas.

Surely the fable repeats itself endlessly.

Did the Siren enjoy her prey? Did she care for the man she lured? No more than a fisherman at Brighton cares for one herring among ten thousand.

Ulysses rowed past their shore, deaf and blind—he and his men. Young Telemachus wanted to jump overboard, but the tough old crew held him fast. They could not hear the song, nor see the beauty. The witches were stale, leering, old. Away with them!

I dare say they paint their cheeks by now. Their songs are as outdated as Mozart. Their hair is false. But Harry Warrington does not see that. To him, the song is fresh, the voice pure. He hears no warning.

And yet—Brother Boatswain, do you remember how lovely the opera was the first time we heard it? *Cosi fan tutti*—Mozart’s music. Now, they sing it again, with new words, new singers, new crowds. Still, it remains on the bills. Men and women with any sense, or experience, or who’ve read a novel, must know—when Harry pulled out that faded rose just now—that the story is familiar.

We all fall into the same patterns. A pair of lovers whisper in a garden alley, or glance across a room—Jenny to Jessamy—and we muse on the past. These things follow each other by a natural law, not older than the hills, but older than the people walking upon them.

When a young man pulls a decayed flower from his breast and kisses it—what more is there to say? As well tell the gardener’s name, the watering, the pruning, the soil—than explain how Harry came by it.

The rose lived its life—planted, watered, potted, pruned, bloomed, given, transferred to a boy’s pocket. It followed the fate of all roses. And why did Maria give it to him? Why did he treasure it so fiercely?

Is one story less stale than another? Are they not all alike? What good is there in retelling them?

Harry valued that rose because Maria looked at him with old eyes, met him in the garden in the old way, took his hand, whispered behind the old curtain (the ragged one, everyone could see through!), walked early in the park on a fine morning, visited Goody Jenkins with her jelly, and—of course—someone had to carry the basket.

Whole chapters might be written about such details. But *à quoi bon?*

Love-making, especially, repeats itself. I wonder, dear readers, why you still read novels.

Psha! That rose in Harry’s pocket had grown, budded, bloomed, and now rotted—like all others. You’ll want me to say he kissed it? Of course he did. What are lips for, if not to smile, to kiss, to humbug, to eat mutton-chops and cigars?

But I cannot write this part of the story. Harry never dared write it home. His letters to Maria—written, no doubt, since they were in the same house—were destroyed. Later, he remained silent. And we cannot ask her—she never told the truth about anything.

So again—*cui bono?* What good is it?

Take your story, gentle reader. Take mine. Tomorrow it will be Miss Fanny’s, walking off with her doll to the schoolroom. Next day, it will be Baby’s, screaming for his bottle.

Maria might have liked to rule Harry’s heart—but she didn’t want him to quarrel with his aunt for her sake, nor anger Madame de Bernstein. Harry was not yet Lord of Virginia—he was only the Prince. The Queen might marry, bear other princes, and primogeniture might not hold in Virginia. What did she know?

Lord Castlewood and his sister had exchanged no words about the matter. Yet they understood each other perfectly. The Earl had known Maria all her life—her ways, her tricks, her faults—through years spent in her company. He guided her schemes silently, without speech.

Three days earlier, as she walked to visit poor Goody Jenkins, ill with a sore knee, Harry happened to be strolling beneath the elms. Lord Castlewood, with his dogs and gardener, crossed the courtyard just then. He called to his sister.

“Molly, you’re going to see Goody Jenkins. A charitable soul, my dear. Give her this half-crown for me—unless Cousin Warrington has already done so. A pleasant walk to you. Let her want for nothing.”

At supper, he questioned Harry about poverty in Virginia, the care of the poor. Harry answered wisely. The Earl wished England had fewer poor than America. He advised Harry to visit all classes—farmers, artisans, city folk—to learn agriculture, trade, institutions. Harry nodded modestly. Madame Bernstein approved, nodding over her piquet game with the chaplain.

The next day, Harry sat in the Earl’s justice room. The day after, he rode with him across the estate. On their return, the Earl stopped to visit a sick tenant. Lady Maria was out that day, reading to Goody Jenkins—though Madame Bernstein likely never asked about her niece.

At first, I did not care for Castlewood, nor my cousins. Now, I grow accustomed. We understand each other better. With my duty to my mother, please tell her that, considering Lady Castlewood’s kindness, Madam Esmond may be reconciled to her half-sister, the Baroness de Bernstein. The Baroness was my grandmother’s daughter by her first husband, Lord Castlewood (though Grandpapa was the true lord). But that was not his fault, and he was kind to Grandpapa, who always spoke of him kindly.

Madame de Bernstein first married Reverend Mr. Tusher, a learned and good man, beloved by His Majesty—as was my aunt. He became a bishop. After his death, the King honored her, and she married a Hanoverian nobleman, holding office at Court. I believe she left him very rich. My cousin, Lord Castlewood, told me this. I have found her most kind and affectionate.

Lady Castlewood, Will, and Fanny have been described in my last letter, sent by the Falmouth packet on the 20th. The ladies remain unchanged. Will and I are close friends. We ride often. We’ve had excellent cockfights at Hampton and Winton. My cousin is sharp, but I think I’ve shown him that Virginians know their sport.

Reverend Mr. Sampson, the family chaplain, is an excellent preacher—without pretension.

My cousin the Earl grows ever more dear. By next year’s ship, I hope my mother sends him some of our best tobacco and hams. He is most charitable to the poor. So is his sister, Lady Maria. She spends hours reading good books to the sick. She is beloved in the village.

“Nonsense!” said a lady to whom Harry showed his letter. “Why flatter me, cousin?”

“You are beloved in the village—and beyond,” Harry replied, with a knowing glance. “And I’ve flattered you a little more, further on.”

“There is a sick old woman there, whom Madam Esmond would like—a most religious, good old lady. Lady Maria visits her often. It gives her comfort, she says. But though her voice is sweet—both speaking and singing—she plays the organ beautifully—I doubt Gammer Jenkins gains comfort from it. She is very deaf, due to age. But her memory is perfect. She remembers when my honored Grandmother Rachel, Lady Castlewood, lived here. She says my grandmother was the best woman in the world—gave her a cow when she married, cured Gaffer Jenkins of the colics—likely with the Pills and Drops my mother packed in my boxes when I left Virginia. I’ve never been ill since—no use for the pills. Gumbo has, eating and drinking too much in the servants’ hall.

The next angel to my grandmother,” she adds, “Gammer Jenkins says, is Lady Maria. She sends her duty to her Aunt in Virginia, remembers my Grandpapa and Grandmamma when they were in Europe, and she was a child. You know they have Grandpapa’s portrait here. I live in the rooms he occupied—rooms that are to be mine, my Lord Castlewood says.”

Having no more to say, I close with best love and duty to my honored mother, respects to Mr. Dempster, a kiss for Fanny, kind remembrances to Old Gumbo, Nathan, Old and Young Dinah, the pointer dog and Slut, and all friends, from their well-wisher,

“I have written and sent my duty to my Uncle Warrington in Norfolk. No answer yet.”

“I hope the spelling is right, cousin?” Harry asked, showing the letter to Lady Maria.

“Quite well enough for any person of fashion,” she replied, avoiding closer scrutiny.

“One word—‘angel’—I know I spelled wrong in my letter to my mother. But I’ve learned how to spell it right now.”

“And how is that, sir?”

“I think it’s by looking at you, cousin,” he said, bowing low, adding one of his finest blushes—as if offering both a bow and a bouquet.
