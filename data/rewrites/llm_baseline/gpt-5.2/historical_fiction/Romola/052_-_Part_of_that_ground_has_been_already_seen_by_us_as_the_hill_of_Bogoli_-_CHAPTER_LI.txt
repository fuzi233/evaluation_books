When Romola had said that someone else was waiting for her, she meant her cousin Brigida; yet she had not guessed how urgently that good woman needed shelter—not from rain or cold, but from the sharp, clean wind of righteousness that had begun to sweep Florence, stripping the city of its bright excess as one strips rosemary from a twig for the pot.

They were walking back toward the Piazza together, and from a distance they had already seen the white-robed youths collect and halt before Tessa. Romola, drawing nearer, caught the contadina’s frightened face, the poor girl’s helplessness set out plainly as bread on a bare board. “Wait for me a moment, cousin,” Romola said, turning.

Monna Brigida’s hand tightened on Romola’s sleeve, then fell away as if scorched. “Ah—I will not go on,” she answered hastily. “Come for me to Boni’s shop. I shall go back there.”

There were reasons for this sudden retreat, as palpable to Brigida as the feel of a corset-lace pulled too tight. She knew what she carried on her person—those little vanities that were like sweetmeats for the eyes and bitter in the conscience: rouge rubbed into the cheeks, false braids heavy as ripened grapes, pearl embroidery that caught the light and returned it with a cold, delighted glitter. And now, with the Piagnoni abroad, she had begun to fear—truly fear—that paint and purchased hair and ornament did not merely change the face: they endangered the soul.

It was as if the city’s very air had been seasoned with a new herb—sharp, medicinal, hard to disguise. What used to taste harmless had acquired an after-flavor of reproach. Even the familiar pleasures of dressing—pins, ribbons, the pleasing tug of braids made to sit properly—had turned uncertain, as if someone had swapped the sugar for salt. And there was Romola herself, young, grave, beautiful, moving through the world with a steadiness that made Brigida’s own life—once so full of mirrors and small triumphs—feel suddenly frivolous, even culpable.

A widow of five-and-fifty whose contentment has long been kneaded from what she thinks of her own appearance, and what she believes others think of it, does not easily learn to feed on penitence. Brigida’s mornings at her toilet had become skirmishes. If the soul prospered better without these trappings, was it worth the trouble to put on rouge and braids at all?

Yet when she lifted the hand-mirror and saw the sallow skin, the baggy cheeks, the crow’s-feet that no simpering could smooth away—when she parted the grey hair and tried to let it lie in the plain Piagnone fashion, meekly framing her face—courage curdled. Monna Berta would certainly laugh and call her an old hag; and as Monna Berta was only fifty-two, her cruelty had the edge of that small superiority. Every woman who was not of the Piagnoni would shrug. The men would address her like their grandmother. Whereas a woman of five-and-fifty was not so very old—she only required a little making up, a little arranging, like yesterday’s stew warmed and improved with a pinch of spice.

So the rouge went on again, the braids were pinned, the embroidered berretta settled in its pride. The mirror returned the accustomed effect, and Brigida breathed easier, as if she had found the familiar savor again. And her neck—if she covered it, people might suppose it too old to show; but with necklaces lying upon it, it looked better than Monna Berta’s. Even this day, preparing for the Piagnone Carnival, she had fought that battle, and the mixture of fear and longing that had set her hands trembling now drove her backward, toward the safety of Boni’s shop, rather than forward into the streets where collectors of the Anathema might seize on her when Romola was not beside her.

But Brigida was not quick enough.

The white-robed boys in the rear of the troop that had turned toward Tessa had already seen her before she changed direction. Perhaps it even made their surrender of Tessa easier—there was a larger, richer target now, bearing on her head and cheeks the dangerous weight of forbidden finery. Several of the youths were very young, trained early to a zeal that had the fervor of a flame and the heedlessness of a flame too, consuming whatever lay nearest.

A small fellow of ten—olive wreath above cherubic cheeks, wide brown eyes that looked as if they truly expected heaven and hell to lean close at any moment—was the first to reach Brigida and plant himself across her way. His face held an odd mixture: awe at existence, longing to be good, and beneath it the restless itch to command and meddle.

Brigida felt anger rise like pepper in the throat. Her eyes searched for an open door, a threshold to slip across; but there was none near, and flight now would only sharpen pursuit. It was not the cherubic child who spoke first, however, but a youth of fifteen carrying one handle of a broad basket.

“Venerable mother!” he began, with the practiced voice of someone reciting a formula that had been applauded. “The blessed Jesus commands you to give up the Anathema which you carry upon you. That cap embroidered with pearls, those jewels that fasten up your false hair—let them be given up and sold for the poor; and cast the hair itself away from you, as a lie that is only fit for burning. Doubtless, too, you have other jewels under your silk mantle.”

Another youth, holding the other handle, chimed in quickly, his tongue full of Fra Girolamo’s turns of speech. “Yes, lady, they are too heavy for you: heavier than a millstone, and weighting you for perdition. Will you adorn yourself with the hunger of the poor, and be proud to carry God’s curse upon your head?”

Then the child spoke in a sweet soprano that made the words more terrible, as when a clear flute plays a lament over a funeral procession. “In truth you are old, buona madre. You look very ugly with the red on your cheeks and that black glistening hair, and those fine things. It is only Satan who can like to see you. Your Angel is sorry. He wants you to rub away the red.”

He snatched a soft silk scarf from the basket and held it out—like a napkin offered at table, except it was meant to wipe away not sauce but identity. Brigida’s anger and mortification sank fast into a deeper alarm. Monna Berta and the gay world were not there to witness and rescue her pride. Instead she was ringed by young monitors in white robes with wreaths and red crosses—faces bright with a candor that had no mercy in it, like light on a bare plate that shows every stain.

Her Franciscan confessor, Fra Cristoforo of Santa Croce, was not at hand to stiffen her distrust of Dominican severity. She felt herself helplessly taken, shaken by the vague sense that a supreme warning had come—come with feet and voices, come in a rush of white sleeves and basket-handles. No other course suggested itself. She took the scarf and rubbed her cheeks, trembling, obedient as a child made to wash.

“It is well, madonna,” said the second youth. “It is a holy beginning. And when you have taken those vanities from your head, the dew of heavenly grace will descend on it.”

The mischief in the troop—always mingled with sincerity—grew stronger. The cherubic boy put his hand to one of the jewelled pins fastening the braids to the berretta and drew it out. The heavy black plait fell down over Brigida’s face like a curtain dropped without warning; it dragged her head-gear forward. Shamed into haste, she lifted both hands, undid the remaining fastenings, and flung into the basket of doom her beloved crimson-velvet berretta with its unsurpassed embroidery of seed-pearls.

There she stood unrouged: grey hair pushed back from a face where certain deep lines of age had triumphed over fullness. It was as if a rich pastry had been scraped bare to show the honest, darker crumb beneath.

But the berretta was not left quietly in the basket. With impish zeal the youngsters lifted it up and displayed it pitilessly, false hair dangling like a caught fish.

“See, venerable mother,” said the taller youth, “what ugly lies you have delivered yourself from! And now you look like the blessed Saint Anna, the mother of the Holy Virgin.”

In Brigida’s mind, thoughts tumbled—going into a convent at once, never showing herself in the world again, shutting the door on every mirror. There was nothing possible, she told herself, but to take care of her soul. Of course people were laughing; she did not need to turn her head to taste that bitterness. Perhaps it would be better, truly, to be forced to think more of Paradise.

But when the thought came—sharp as vinegar—that the dear accustomed world was no longer hers to choose, hard tears gathered, those scant tears that only moisten elderly eyes. Through the blur she saw a large rough hand holding a red cross thrust suddenly before her over the boys’ shoulders, and heard a strong guttural voice that cut across the chanting zeal like a hawker’s cry across church music:

“Only four quattrini, madonna, blessing and all! Buy it. You’ll find a comfort in it now your wig’s gone. Deh! what are we sinners doing all our lives? Making soup in a basket, and getting nothing but the scum for our stomachs. Better buy a blessing, madonna! Only four quattrini; the profit is not so much as the smell of a danaro, and it goes to the poor.”

In dim-eyed confusion Brigida was already fumbling toward her embroidered scarsella hidden beneath her silk mantle, ready for yet another submission, when the group opened—parted as if a stronger current had entered—and a figure appeared that felt to Brigida like an angel loosening prison-bolts.

“Romola, look at me!” she cried, piteously, thrusting out both hands.

The white troop began to move away, with a faint sense, perhaps, that their zeal about head-gear had been excessive enough to excuse them from pressing her further for offerings. Romola, smitten with pity, could hardly keep from smiling at the sudden apparition of her cousin in a genuine, unadorned guise so unlike every remembered image. She took the black drapery from her own head and threw it over Brigida’s, covering the grey hair like a cloth drawn over a cooling loaf.

“There,” Romola said, softly, as if quieting a frightened animal. “No one will remark you now. We will turn down the Via del Palagio and go straight to our house.”

They hastened away. Brigida clung to Romola’s hand tightly, as if touch were proof that she had not been abandoned to the street and its judgments. The soundscape around them—boys’ voices fading, laughter pricking, footsteps scraping stone—seemed to Brigida like a hostile chorus, while Romola’s steady pace and low words made a small refuge of rhythm.

“Ah, my Romola, my dear child!” Brigida sobbed, the short fat woman taking quick little steps to keep up with the tall young figure beside her. “What an old scarecrow I am! I must be good—I mean to be good!”

“Yes, yes; buy a cross!” the guttural voice pursued, as persistent as a fly that has scented sweet must. The rough hand thrust itself forward again with the red cross: for Bratti would not be abashed by Romola’s presence into abandoning a likely customer, and he had followed their retreat as quietly as a man carrying a tray follows the hunger in a crowd. “Only four quattrini, blessing and all—and if there was any profit, it would all go to the poor.”

Brigida would have had to pause even in a less submissive mood. She lifted one hand, deprecating, to stop Romola’s remonstrance; with the other she reached out a grosso—worth many white quattrini—and said pleadingly, “Take it, good man, and begone.”

“You’re in the right, madonna,” Bratti said, snatching the coin with the quickness of trade and pushing the cross into her hand. “I’ll not offer you change, for I might as well rob you of a mass. What! we must all be scorched a little, but you’ll come off the easier; better fall from the window than the roof. A good Easter and a good year to you!”

When he turned away at last, Brigida’s voice broke with a kind of comic despair that was half sincerity, half the habit of appealing to an audience. “Well, Romola, if I’m to be a Piagnone it’s no matter how I look!”

“Dear cousin,” Romola answered, smiling at her with an affection that made the words warm instead of sharp, “you don’t know how much better you look than you ever did before. I see now how good-natured your face is, like yourself. That red and finery seemed to thrust themselves forward and hide expression. Ask our Piero or any other painter if he would not rather paint your portrait now than before. I think all lines of the human face have something either touching or grand, unless they seem to come from low passions. How fine old men are, like my godfather! Why should not old women look grand and simple?”

“Yes—when one gets to be sixty, my Romola,” Brigida said, relapsing a little into her old calculations, as if counting years like measures of flour. “But I’m only fifty-five, and Monna Berta, and everybody—” She checked herself, breath catching; then, as if the day’s stripping had left her with only the essential, she added, “But it’s no use: I will be good, like you. Your mother—if she’d been alive—would have been as old as I am; we were cousins together. One must either die or get old. But it doesn’t matter about being old, if one’s a Piagnone.”
