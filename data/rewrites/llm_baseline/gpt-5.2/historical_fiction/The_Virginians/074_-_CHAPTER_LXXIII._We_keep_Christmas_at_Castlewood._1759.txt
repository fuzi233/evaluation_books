In all the old tales, at every christening and wedding, somebody is left out in the cold, and goes away nursing a grievance as if it were a coal meant to be kept alive. So it was at Castlewood when the match was made between my lord and the colonial heiress. Cousin Will, who had long counted the girl as his own prize—won, as he swore, by hazard of life and limb—took the news like a man struck across the mouth. He stormed from the house, calling down ruin on all heads, and proclaimed he would never cross the threshold again.

No one, least of all Will himself, put faith in those grand departures. Castlewood had too good a kitchen to make exile long. The house breathed roast and broth, cider and warm spice; the very chimneys seemed to send after him a ribbon of smoke as a lure. After two days’ sulk he came back, not humbled, but hungry; and in token of revenge he sat down to his father’s board, ate his fill, drank with the others, and with every mouthful seemed to swear he was swallowing the insult whole.

At the feast he raised his glass to the bride with a loud enough compliment, and under the rim of the bumper murmured perdition. In chapel, when her father gave her away and my lord vowed to love, honour, and cherish, Will made responses as if each “Amen” were a smothered curse. He was not the only malcontent. Mr. Warrington heard it at the time and afterwards till his ears ached with it: abuse of my lady and of her grandfather poured from many mouths, each speaker eager to prove he had been wronged first.

The City friends of the old gentleman cried out upon the treachery of the little American; the lawyer, who had once displayed a list of her properties as if he were laying out joints on a butcher’s block—estates, moneys, slaves, ships, expectations—now swore the whole inventory was a cheat; there was no such place as New York or Virginia; or, if there were, Van den Bosch owned no land there; there was no Guinea trade; the negroes were a bundle of black falsehoods; the old planter had salted his tale like a ham to make it keep. A Dissenting minister mourned over his stray lamb—if lamb was the name for a creature so quick-eyed and wilful. Poor Jack Lambert, the orthodox young divine, confessed to his mamma that he still kept a lock of her black hair; he kissed it, he wept upon it, and addressed it in a language that would have made his surplice start from shame. As for William Esmond, he called on Acheron and all the heathen rivers to bear witness that out of the pit itself there never crawled such an artful young devil as Miss Lydia. She was Cerberus in a petticoat; and the brother who had stolen her from him was a swindling rascal who had cajoled him with fair words and filched his prize.

“Why,” said Mr. Warrington, when Will poured out this torrent to him, “if she is the she-devil you paint, you are the better for losing her. If she means to poison her husband, as you vow, you should thank the gods you’re not the man. You cannot be better revenged on Castlewood than by letting him keep her all to himself.”

Will admitted the argument might look pretty on paper, but persisted—perhaps not unjustly—that his brother was none the less a scoundrel for cheating him of the fortune he had expected, and for which he had risked his skin.

George Warrington, who had never been able to understand what had driven cousin Will into so perilous a point of honour, pressed him; and Will, with the utmost candour, explained how the little Cerbera had contrived the meeting between them in Tottenham Court Fields—how she had pricked them both with her tongue, calling George a coward for sneering at Will, and Will a poltroon for letting taunts go unanswered; how she had flung Braddock’s expedition at George as a taunt, and boasted Will up by insulting him.

“Deuce take me,” Will said, “I never was more surprised, cousin, than when you stood your ground so coolly there. Me and my second would have laid a wager you’d never come!”

George laughed. “It was lucky for me the constables came up,” he said, “or you would have slipped your sword through me in another minute. Didn’t you see how awkward I was? And you turned white and shook with anger!”

“Yes, curse me,” Will said, colouring now till the red ran up to his forehead, “that’s my way when I’m in a rage. I was confoundedly angry with you. But now ’tis my brother I hate—and that little devil of a Countess. A countess! A pretty countess indeed!”

He discharged another broadside of oaths, and George watched, as he often did, with a kind of dry amusement, the man choking on his own fury and yet too cowardly to bite where he most wished.

“You should moderate your expressions, cousin,” George said, “regarding the dear Countess and my lord your brother. Of you they always speak most tenderly. Her ladyship has told me everything.”

“What everything?” Will cried, suddenly paling.

“As much as women ever tell. She owned she thought you had been a little épris with her. What woman can help liking a man who has admired her?”

“Why, she hates you, and says you were wild about her!” Will retorted, half incredulous, half eager to find George caught.

“Spretae injuria formae, cousin.”

“For me—what’s for me?” Will demanded.

“I never cared for her,” George said; “and hence, perhaps, she does not love me. Don’t you remember the case of the wife of the Captain of the Guard?”

“Which Guard?” Will asked, honestly puzzled.

“My Lord Potiphar,” George answered.

“Lord who?” Will cried. “My Lord Falmouth is Captain of the Yeomen of the Guard, and my Lord Berkeley of the Pensioners. My Lord Hobart had ’em before. Suppose you haven’t been long enough in England to know who’s who, cousin!”

George explained he meant the Captain of the Guard to the King of Egypt, and the wife who persecuted Joseph for not returning her affection. Will said Egypt was a confounded long way off; and if my lord what-d’ye-call’s wife lied, it was like women, who were the same everywhere, he supposed.

When Mr. Warrington paid his first bridal visit to Castlewood, the little Countess herself gave him her version of the quarrel. It differed in some points from Will’s plain-spoken narrative; but George, who had heard both, would not pretend to choose which was the more authentic. She professed herself grieved, even wounded, that she had been deceived in her brother-in-law. Court and town, she feared, had injured those high principles which all the Esmonds were born with; Will’s words were not to be trusted; a loose life and pecuniary difficulties had blunted his honour and perhaps impaired his chivalrous courage.

“Which we Esmonds, cousin,” she said, tossing her head till the light caught her bright eyes, “which we Esmonds must always possess—leastways you and me, and my lord, and my cousin Harry have it, I know!” She drew herself up as if the very syllables of that name were a coronet. “Oh, cousin George! and must I confess I was led to doubt of yours? Without courage a man of ancient and noble family like ours isn’t worthy to be called a man. I shall try, as a Christian lady, and the head of one of the first families in this kingdom and the whole world, to forgive my brother William for speaking ill of a member of our family—though a younger branch and by the female side—and making me for a moment doubt of you.”

“I, my dear lady!” George cried.

“Which he said you said of me,” she continued, her words running like a brook over stones, “and I hope you didn’t, and heartily pray you didn’t; and I can afford to despise ’em. And he paid me his court—that’s a fact; and so have others, and that I’m used to. And he might have prospered better than he did perhaps, for I did not know my dear lord then, nor come to vally his great and eminent qualities as I do out of the fulness of this grateful heart now!—but, oh! I found William was deficient in courage. And no man as wants that can ever have the esteem of Lydia, Countess of Castlewood, no more he can!”

She snapped her little fingers, as if to flick a crumb of him from her glove.

“He said ’twas you that wanted spirit, cousin, and angered me by telling me you was always abusing of me. But I forgive you, George, that I do! And when I tell you it was he was afraid—the mean skunk!—and actually sent for them constables to prevent the match between you and he, you won’t wonder I wouldn’t vally a feller like that—no, not that much!” Again the fingers snapped. “Noblesse oblige; and a man of our family who hasn’t got courage, I don’t care not this pinch of snuff for him—there, now, I don’t! Look at our ancestors, George, round these walls! Haven’t the Esmonds always fought for their country and king? Is there one of us that, when the moment arrives, ain’t ready to show he’s an Esmond and a nobleman? If my eldest son was to show the white feather—‘My Lord Esmond!’ I would say to him, for that’s the second title in our family, ‘I disown your lordship!’”

As she spoke, she looked round at the portraits by Lely and Kneller that glimmered upon the walls of the Castlewood drawing-room—faces whose mouths were set as if they still commanded men, whose eyes, though painted, seemed to listen for drum and trumpet. Even in that quiet room you could hear the house’s winter sound: the fire’s steady eating of logs, the faint crack in the wainscot as the frost worked outside, the hush of thick carpets drinking footsteps, and far off, from the courtyard, the occasional ring of a bit or stamp of a horse, like punctuation to her speech.

Over that room, and over the whole house, domain, and village, the new Countess began very speedily to rule with an unlimited sway. It was astonishing how quickly she learned the ways of command. If she did not adopt the established English methods of precedence, she invented regulations for herself, published them, and made others obey. She had been bred a Dissenter and at first made a blunder or two in the Established service—not knowing when to turn eastward, for instance, a movement not practised, I believe, in other reformed churches besides the English. But between Warrington’s first bridal visit and his second, my lady had become quite perfect; she sailed into chapel upon her cousin’s arm, her two footmen bearing her great Prayer-book behind her, as demurely as that ancient devotee in Mr. Hogarth’s “Morning,” and as if she had lived all her life with a chaplain at her elbow. She patronised the new chaplain, the service, and the church itself, as though she had never in her own country heard a Ranter thunder in a barn. The neighbouring families—grave baronets and their wives, squires of twenty descents who rode over to pay their respects—were made to know their distance and give her the pas.

She procured an old book of heraldry, and engaged a surprising old maiden lady from Winton, learned in genealogies and politeness, from whom she gleaned court etiquette as it had been known in Queen Anne’s time. Soon my lady jabbered of gules and sables, bends and saltires—not always with correctness, but with an extraordinary volubility and perseverance. She made little progresses to neighbouring towns in her gilt coach-and-six, or to the village in her chair, and asserted a quasi-regal right to homage from tenants and other clodpoles. She lectured the parson upon divinity; the bailiff upon farming; and in the stillroom would have had the housekeeper believe she had been born among pickles and preserves.

Here, at least, my lady’s genius was at home, for she loved to put her small hands to the empire of jars and kettles. She inspected the shelves where quinces lay like little suns in syrup, where damsons bled purple into glass, where walnuts, green as unripe secrets, were steeped for cordials; she would sniff at a vinegar, tap a pot of potted beef, and declare it wanted pepper or bay. The stillroom, with its warm breath of mace and clove, its beeswax and dried herbs, received her like a chapel of domestic worship. She gave directions how to salt a side of bacon so the fat should turn pearl and not rancid; how to scald a chine of pork before smoking; how to boil sugar to the feather, and when to lift it so it would not grain; how to blanch almonds, how to steep orange peel till the bitterness was tamed. The old housekeeper, who had made my lord and the dowager wary in former days, now found herself astonished into obedience by a mistress who could scold and praise in one breath, and who listened with her whole sharp body, as if a recipe were a campaign-plan.

As for physic, Madam Esmond in Virginia had not been more resolute about pills and draughts than Miss Lydia, the earl’s new bride. And if the household’s peace depended in part upon quiet sounds—the soft scurry of servants, the muffled closing of doors, the distant clink of plate that told of order—my lady altered the music of the place. Under her, the great London footmen who used to quarrel over cards and grumble as they swilled endless beer, now stepped nimbly when they heard her call; even their footsteps changed, less thudding, more careful, as if the very floors had learned fear. The house’s acoustics became a kind of discipline: a bell rung sharply; a voice that could cut through passages; the hush after a rebuke, in which you might hear the coals settle in the grate like a sigh.

She would have taught the great London fellows to jump behind the carriage too, only it was too high for her little ladyship to mount. She gave the village gossips instructions how to nurse and tend their children long before she had one herself. And she was brisk with her own people. Dinah, her black attendant, complained to Gumbo (who carried complaints, as servants do, from ear to ear) that her ladyship pinched her great ears and pulled at her woolly pate upon offence given.

Old age, in particular, had small sanctity with my lady. Once the settlements were made in her behalf, she treated the ancient Van den Bosch and his large periwig with scarcely more ceremony than Dinah. All trembled before her. Even old Lockwood, who had been gate-porter for half a century, and whose wits wandered as winter birds do, tried to rally them when she came into his lodge to open his window, inspect his wood-closet, and turn his old dogs out of doors.

Lockwood bared his bald head to her new authority, glanced piteously toward his niece, and shook in his chair. The lodge had its own poor homely smells—damp wood, old leather, a dog’s coat dried too near the fire—and its own small sounds, comforting to the old man: the scrape of a paw, the creak of his chair, the wind worrying the latch. My lady’s entrance was like a new instrument introduced into that little orchestra—too bright, too sharp, drowning the rest.

Gumbo, dressing his master for dinner, spoke of Elisha, of whom he had heard the chaplain read that morning—“and his bald head, and de boys who call um names, and de bars eat em up, and serve um right,” said Gumbo, grinning, as he tied a cravat. But my lady, speaking to her cousin of the porter, dismissed him with a wave.

“Pooh, pooh! Stupid old man!” she cried. “Past his work, he and his dirty old dogs! They are as old and ugly as those old fish in the pond!”

She meant the two ancient carp that had lived for centuries in the garden pond, according to tradition; their backs were coated with a hideous grey mould, and they moved slowly under the ice like legends that refused to die.

“Lockwood must pack off,” my lady continued. “The workhouse is the place for him; and I shall have a smart, good-looking, tall fellow in the lodge that will do credit to our livery.”

“He was my grandfather’s man,” Mr. Warrington said, “and served him in the wars of Queen Anne.”

On which my lady, petulant as a child tired of a story, cried, “O Lord! Queen Anne’s dead, I suppose, and we ain’t a-going into mourning for her.”

This question was brought to the family table. The dinner that day had the solid winter comforts of an old English house: a haunch that sent up a rich steam when carved, potatoes mealy and hot, cabbage softened with butter, sauces dark and shining, and a pudding that smelt of suet, lemon, and brandy. The clink of knives on plates, the murmur of servants at the sideboard, the crackle of the fire, were part of the argument, as if the house itself took a side.

My lady announced, between mouthfuls, her intention of getting rid of Lockwood.

“I am told,” Mr. Van den Bosch remarked demurely, “that by the laws poor servants and poor folks of all kinds are admirably provided for in their old age here in England. I’m sure I wish we had such an asylum for our folks at home, and that we were eased of the expense of keeping our old hands.”

“If a man can’t work he ought to go!” cried her ladyship.

“Yes, indeed, and that’s a fact!” said grandpapa.

“What! an old servant?” asked my lord.

“Mr. Van den Bosch possibly was independent of servants when he was young,” Mr. Warrington observed.

“Greased my own boots, opened my own shutters, sanded and watered my own—”

“Sugar, sir?” my lord asked, with languid politeness.

“No; floor, son-in-law!” the old man said, laughing; “though there is such tricks in grocery stores, saving your ladyship’s presence.”

“La, pa! what should I know about stores and groceries?” cried her ladyship, tossing her head.

“He! Remember stealing the sugar, and what came on it, my dear ladyship?” grandpapa chuckled.

“At any rate,” my lady persisted, “a handsome, well-grown man in our livery will look better than that shrivelled old porter creature!”

“No livery is so becoming as old age, madam,” Mr. Warrington said, “and no lace as handsome as silver hairs. What will the county say if you banish old Lockwood?”

“Oh! if you plead for him, sir, I suppose he must stay. Hadn’t I better order a couch for him out of my drawing-room, and send him some of the best wine from the cellar?”

“Indeed your ladyship couldn’t do better,” Mr. Warrington replied, very gravely.

And my lord, yawning, said, “Cousin George is perfectly right, my dear. To turn away such an old servant as Lockwood would have an ill look.”

“You see those mouldy old carps are, after all, a curiosity and attract visitors,” continued Mr. Warrington, still with that same gravity. “Your ladyship must allow this old wretch to remain. It won’t be for long. And you may then engage the tall porter. It is very hard on us, Mr. Van den Bosch, that we are obliged to keep our old negroes when they are past work. I shall sell that rascal Gumbo in eight or ten years.”

“Don’t tink you will, master!” Gumbo said, grinning broadly, his teeth flashing in the candlelight.

“Hold your tongue, sir! He doesn’t know English ways, you see,” Mr. Warrington went on, “and perhaps thinks an old servant has a claim on his master’s kindness.”

The next day, to Warrington’s surprise, my lady did indeed send a basket of good wine to Lockwood, and a cushion for his chair. The bottles clinked softly as they were carried over the gravel; that small music—glass against glass—went into the lodge like an unlikely benediction.

“I thought of what you said yesterday, at night when I went to bed,” she told George. “And guess you know the world better than I do, cousin; and that it’s best to keep the old man, as you say.”

So ended the affair of the porter’s lodge, Mr. Warrington marvelling inwardly at this strange little creature out of the West—naïve and simple in one breath, hard-hearted in the next, with a sudden capacity to mimic English mercies when she saw their use. She could adopt a phrase, a posture, an entire tone, as one might borrow a dish’s name from a foreign tongue—pronouncing it with confidence, yet not tasting all that lived in it.

“You tell me to respect old people,” she said to him another time. “Why? I don’t see nothin’ to respect in the old people I know. They ain’t so funny, and I’m sure they ain’t so handsome. Look at grandfather; look at Aunt Bernstein. They say she was a beauty once! That picture painted from her! I don’t believe it, nohow. No one shall tell me I shall ever be as bad as that! When they come to that, people oughtn’t to live. No, that they oughtn’t.”

At Christmas Aunt Bernstein came to pay her nephew and niece a visit, travelling in company with Mr. Warrington. They went at their leisure in the Baroness’s own landau. The old lady was in particular good health and spirits; the air was fresh without cruelty, and the roads rang pleasantly beneath the wheels. The hedges were bare; the fields had that winter look of rest after labour; and in the pauses of the journey, when the horses’ breath steamed and the postilion’s horn sounded from a distance like a remembered tune, Aunt Beatrice told a hundred stories of Castlewood and the old days.

Though often lethargic and not seldom out of temper, the old lady would light up at times; her conversation became wonderfully lively; her wit and malice flashed; and her memory supplied a hundred anecdotes of a bygone age and society. It is a hard condition of beauty that the possessor should not even have the long enjoyment of it, but must resign it—at most—a forty years’ lease. As she prattled of former lovers and admirers, her auditor knowing more of her past than she supposed, George would look into her face and try, from the ruins, to rebuild an image of her beauty in its prime. It was like standing in a vast abandoned house and imagining it in its season—courts where grass now grew, towers broken, doors ajar, gilt saloons tarnished, tapestries torn and cobwebbed; and yet, in the mind, chandeliers kindling, music rising, laughter running through galleries. Once there had been feasts and balls there; the clatter of fans, the rustle of silks, the bright shocks of applause. Now the same mouth that once gave orders to a roomful of men pursed itself over small irritations; the same eyes that once summoned attention narrowed in complaint. The untranslatability of it—the impossibility of carrying back into the present the exact flavour of what had been—sat in George’s mind like a word remembered from a childhood dialect that no longer had speakers.

When Aunt Bernstein came to Castlewood, her relatives—more on account of her own force of character, imperiousness, and sarcastic wit, than from any desire to possess her money—were accustomed to pay her much deference, which she accepted as her due. She expected the same from the new Countess, whom she meant to greet with especial good humour. The match had been of her making.

“As you, you silly creature, would not have the heiress,” she told George, “I was determined she should not go out of the family,” and she laughed as she spoke of the little schemes by which she had brought the matter about. She had given the girl a coronet and her nephew a hundred thousand pounds; of course she should be welcome to both.

She was delighted with the little Countess’s courage and spirit in routing the Dowager and Lady Fanny. Almost always pleased with pretty people at first sight, Madame Bernstein rattled on about Lydia’s bright eyes and lovely little figure. The marriage was altogether desirable. The old man, Van den Bosch, was an obstacle, to be sure, and his talk and appearance somewhat too homely.

“But he will be got rid of,” the Baroness said, with a shrug, as though she were speaking of a dish that had stood too long and must be removed. “He is old, and in delicate health. He will want to go to America—or perhaps farther.”

“The child has great fire and liveliness,” she continued, “and a Cherokee manner which is not without its charm. Your brother had it—so have you, Master George! Nous la formerons, cette petite. Eugene wants character and vigour, but he is a finished gentleman; and between us we shall make the little savage perfectly presentable.”

So they discoursed on the second afternoon as they journeyed toward Castlewood. The first night they lay at the King’s Arms at Bagshot, where the Baroness was received with profound respect; and thence drove post to Hexton, where she had written to have my lord’s horses in waiting. But the Castlewood horses were not forthcoming at the inn; for a couple of hours they waited, with the sound of stable-boys calling and boots on straw, and the wind fussing at shutters like an impatient servant. At last they were obliged to proceed with their Bagshot horses to Castlewood, the landau rolling on through the winter dusk, and the old lady’s voice, now sharp with recollection, now softened by fatigue, filling the carriage as steadily as the scent of the fur rugs and the faint lingering perfume from her gloves.
