Colonel James Wolfe, newly arrived from America following Harry's enlistment, stood at the center of a whirlwind of expectation. Having risen from Lieutenant-Colonel of Kingsley's to command a fresh battalion awarded for his gallantry at Cape Breton, Wolfe was the darling of the public imagination. The nation, weary of failures like Marlborough's lineage and Sackville's conduct at Minden, hung upon his lips, hoping he was the general who would restore military glory. While Mr. Lambert's duties kept him anchored in London, the bond between the older officer and his younger friend, now a Colonel, flourished with renewed vigor. Lambert delighted in his friend's ascent, and the two shared long, open evenings where Wolfe, possessing a heroic simplicity reminiscent of Nelson, spilled his heart without reserve. To the cynical circles of St. James's, he might seem naive, yet within their intimate circle, he was a beacon of frankness.

There was a boyish romance to Wolfe's spirit; he cherished music and poetry above the carnage of battle, confessing on his final day that he would trade a victory for Gray's *Elegy*. This duality drew him inevitably to George Warrington. Both men, swept by passion and eager for happiness, found solace in sentiment. As Warrington later recalled to Harry, "I admired Mr. Wolfe's simplicity... He was much in love, and he wanted heaps and heaps of laurels to take to his mistress." Wolfe, convinced that honor was a sin worth committing, hungered for glory with the fervor of a knight seeking dragons. Standing in contrast to Wolfe's chivalrous ardour was the Virginian's own steady, almost Roman constancy—a tension that fascinated observers.

While Lambert's family settled comfortably in Southampton Row, Warrington's worldly pursuits drew him deeper into London's theatrical heart. Though he visited Castlewood briefly, finding solace in the memories of his grandfather and Colonel Esmond, his true sanctuary lay in the vibrant social life of the capital. It was here, amidst the company of the elegant and the literati, that his tragedy, *Carpezan*, found its path to the stage. Through the influence of the enthusiastic Chaplain Sampson rather than Mr. Johnson, the play secured acceptance at Covent Garden under Mr. Rich's management, bypassing Garrick who had just engaged the author of *Douglas*.

The night of the premiere was a triumph of camaraderie and art. Following an elegant supper at the Bedford Head, the group adjourned to the theatre. The cast was formidable: Barry as the terrifying Carpezan, Shuter as the old nobleman, Reddish as Ulric, and the young Dublin actor Hagan, playing the King of Bohemia with such grace and elegance that he won universal acclaim. Mrs. Woffington, though appearing aged in the first act, delivered a chilling murder scene that terrified and delighted the audience, while Miss Wayn's singing added a haunting beauty to the climax. Yet, the play's heartbeat was the execution of Sybilla. When her head fell, the tension broke, and the house erupted. Friends rushed to the coffee-house to confirm the total success. Barry announced the repetition immediately, declaring it the debut work of a young gentleman from Virginia.

In the boxes, the atmosphere was electric with emotion. Theo Lambert fluttered with anxiety as the verdict hung in the balance, her eyes sparkling with relief when the victory was sealed. Harry, however, bore the weight of the performance physically; his pallor, remarked upon by Mrs. Lambert, exceeded even Barry's chalk-like whiteness. Yet, when the curtain fell, Harry roared with the enthusiasm of a man who had seen his creation breathe life. Wolfe and General Lambert cheered wildly, while Mrs. Lambert wept, a display Hetty dismissed with sharp wit, though deep down she shared the joy, alongside the Chartreux's little Charley and Lucy from boarding school. Even Lord Castlewood and Lady Maria were present, the latter enchanted by the tragedy and the Chaplain's fervor.

A riotous supper followed, surpassing even Garrick's recent failure with *Agis*. Toasts flowed freely, and the spirits soared. Lady Maria raised "The King of Hungary"—played by Mr. Hagan—who, drunk on wine and role-playing, vowed eternal servitude to the Countess despite having suffered a "death" on stage hours prior. His excessive gallantry, attributed partly to the punch, unsettled the sober-minded Esmonds and Lords, yet the enchantment of the evening held sway. Later, as coaches departed, the King of Hungary retreated with a throbbing headache, while the poet Warrington, astounded by the sheer volume of his own speech-making, found himself besieged by guests the next morning who had been invited to dine with him again.

The aftermath of the play shifted focus to domestic matters. As Mrs. Lambert prepared her daughters for bed, the air crackled with excitement. George Warrington, radiant with news, revealed his secret weapon against the temptations of the greenroom: the eyes of Miss Theo. He declared himself free from fear, for in her gaze lay an amulet against danger. The mother, amused yet stern, chided Hetty for her blunt comparison of the actress's age to George's mother, reminding her of the Fifth Commandment and the propriety of their youth. Yet, beneath the surface, a strange infatuation simmered. Mrs. Lambert doted on Theo, dressing her elaborately and constantly consulting her about George's appearance and health, creating a dynamic where George often stood at a distance, save for one brief, natural moment when he stooped to pick up her dropped scissors, causing a flush that neither parent seemed to notice.

Then came the letter from Virginia, penned in Madam Esmond's neat hand. George trembled as he broke the seal. His mother approved of his attachment to Miss Lambert and his intentions regarding Harry, offering a warm welcome to the young couple. She spoke of her lack of ready cash but promised abundance for future generations, expressing a maternal desire to see her grandchildren near her in her twilight years. Though the letter lacked a formal financial settlement, it conveyed a profound, if somewhat vague, consent. George, ecstatic, fled to Soho to share the tidings. The Lambert household erupted in celebration; General Lambert embraced his son-in-law, calling him a treasure, while Mrs. Lambert wept copiously. Hetty, overhearing the scene, feigned indifference with a biting laugh, only to embrace her sister passionately in private, vowing to protect Theo's heart forever.

However, the reality of George's situation soon surfaced. Upon reading Madam Esmond's letter in full, General Lambert's countenance darkened. There was no dowry, no substantial provision—only a promise of hospitality. The truth of George's depleted patrimony, squandered on bills, commissions, and his own ransom, hit hard. He confessed his inability to support a family, having exhausted his inheritance. The prospect of returning to Virginia to be dependent on his mother repelled him; he envisioned a life of "slavery" and sought to remain in England, perhaps studying law or writing more tragedies. A new plan formed instantly: another tragedy. He began drafting works immediately, showing them to Theo, who found them superior to *Carpezan*, while Hetty yawned over the ink-stained pages.

The news of the engagement rippled through the families, reaching Sir Miles Warrington via a ceremonious letter. Initially, Sir Miles assumed his nephew was financially secure, perhaps even wealthy. But when the truth emerged—that George was penniless, dependent on his mother, and marrying a girl of similar means—the old man's indignation knew no bounds. He viewed the union as a scandal, a product of "respectable" people inveigling a prodigal into ruin. He wrote to his sister in Virginia, condemning the Lamberths' judgment and George's behavior, treating his nephew with icy disdain, denying him invitations to family gatherings, and serving him nothing but small beer. To Harry, however, Sir Miles showed a modicum of leniency, acknowledging his service at Braddock, though he still regarded him as a boy who needed to amend his ways.

Despite the storm brewing with the Warrington relatives, George found solace in the company of the Esmond cousins. Lord Castlewood, a man of wit and questionable morals, maintained a friendship with the Virginians, though his advice was often tinged with cynicism. Lady Maria, though her romance with Harry had ended, retained a warm affection for the brothers, delighting in their stories of valor and theatrical success. One afternoon in Kensington Gardens, George and Theo encountered Lady Maria accompanied by a gentleman in a smart suit and handsome lace hat. It was the King of Hungary, played by Mr. Hagan, who claimed he frequented the gardens to study his parts. They strolled together to Lord Castlewood's door, exchanging a thousand compliments on Theo's beauty and virtue, a moment of lightness that masked the underlying tensions of their lives.

That evening, Harry returned to London and found George at breakfast. A letter from Lady Maria lay on the table, addressed to "Common Garden." Harry teased George about potential love letters, but George, with a peculiar expression, denied any romance, dismissing it as merely a discussion about the play. Later, at dinner, Mrs. Lambert pressed about George's meeting with the actor portraying the King in Kensington Gardens. George deflected, describing it as a casual encounter where the actor studied his lines and the actress crossed paths to visit a colleague. The matter was laid to rest, at least for the moment.
