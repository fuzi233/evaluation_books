Mr. Gryce was a man who understood the value of subtlety. Though he had announced his intention to follow Mr. Adams, he did so with such immediacy that he not only boarded the same train but chose a seat in the same car. His purpose was clear: to observe the young man’s demeanor when he believed himself unwatched.

What he saw intrigued him. The man who had earlier been wracked with visible anguish now sat motionless, his face a mask of unnatural calm. His eyes, though open, seemed to look inward, as if lost in some private torment. Was it the stillness of despair, or simply the detachment of a mind too burdened to register the world around him? Gryce couldn’t decide.

When they reached Brooklyn, he rejoined his prisoner, who barely acknowledged his presence. Within the hour, they stood in Police Headquarters, and the interrogation began.

To Gryce’s surprise, Adams—or rather, Cadwalader—was not reticent. He spoke with a grim urgency, as though eager to unburden himself of a truth too heavy to carry any longer. But the confession that followed was not what the detective had expected.

***

"My name," the man began, "is Thomas Cadwalader. Adams was an alias my brother Felix adopted—for reasons of his own. My father was a Scotsman who settled in Pennsylvania years ago. Felix was his son by an earlier marriage; I, the product of a later union. After my mother’s death, I was sent abroad, only returning this year to find my father on his deathbed and Felix a man of wealth—wealth he had no intention of sharing."

His voice grew bitter. "When I pressed him, he offered nothing but empty promises. Then, in Montgomery, I met a woman—Evelyn Poindexter." Here, his tone softened, then faltered. "I loved her. But I had nothing to offer. Still, I dared to hope Felix might relent if he saw her, if he understood the life I wished to build. So I married her in haste, concealing my poverty from her and her father."

He swallowed hard. "Two days after the wedding, I brought her to Felix’s home. I thought—foolishly—that even he could not resist her grace. But he was colder than I imagined. He sneered at love, at marriage, at the very idea of sentiment. And then—"

Thomas Cadwalader’s hands clenched. "We argued. I said things I should not have. He… he seized a dagger from the table. ‘You want my money?’ he snarled. ‘Then take it!’ And before I could stop him, he drove the blade into his own heart."

Silence filled the room. Then, quietly, he finished: "That is the truth. I did not kill my brother. But his blood is on my hands all the same."

The detectives exchanged glances. It was a tale almost too strange to believe. Yet the raw agony in Thomas’s voice left little room for doubt.

The question now was: would anyone else believe it?
