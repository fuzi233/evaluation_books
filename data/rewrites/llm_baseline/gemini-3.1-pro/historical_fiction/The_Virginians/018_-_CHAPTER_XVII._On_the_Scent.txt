Young Harry Warrington’s act of revolt hung in the air, a sudden acoustic rupture that shattered the morning’s carefully cultivated rhythms. Madame de Bernstein possessed no immediate response save the prompt outbreak of anger with which she swept from the room, leaving behind the rich, lingering scent of the breakfast board’s slow-rendered venison sausages—a dish whose precise sage-and-mace aromatics spoke of generations of Esmond estate kitchens, now entirely unappreciated by the current occupants. She darted two fierce glances at Lady Fanny and her mother as she departed. Beneath the heavy oak crossbeams, Lady Maria scarcely lifted her head from her tambour-frame; the metronomic, rhythmic *shhk-shhk* of her embroidery needle anchored the room's turbulent sonic landscape, seamlessly integrating her mythic machinations into the everyday domestic routine. 

“So, in spite of all, you have, madam?” Lady Castlewood’s glaring eyes seemed to project, attempting to force her daughter into the dominant lexicon of guilt. But what good in looking innocent? Fanny looked puzzled, lacking the deep, ancestral composure of Maria. 

“And small blame to our cousin!” interposed my lord, his voice carrying the practiced, resonant timbre of the estate's master. “A young fellow must not have it all idling and holiday. Let him mix up something useful with his pleasures. Mr. Warrington has to conduct a great estate in America: let him see how ours in England are carried on.” My lord spoke of the deep agrarian cycles—the harvest, the precise fowling techniques of the English autumn, the sonorous, barking kennels in winter. “Though there has been a coolness between us and our aunt-kinswoman in Virginia, yet we are of the same blood. Ere we send our cousin back to his mother, let us show him what an English gentleman's life at home is.”

His frankness was a linguistic smokescreen, a subtle power dynamic embedded in his deliberate phrasing. He did not ask for belief; his tone dictated that his words become the established truth. As my lord concluded his sweeping, multi-generational declarations, his mother-in-law, half-brother, and sister retreated into the corridors, their own scheming a shrill counterpoint to his agrarian vision. 

William gnawed thoughtfully on a sliver of dry-cured Hampshire ham he had pinched from the sideboard, its translucent fat singing with the sharp, ferrous tang of saltpetre and long cellar-aging. “What is Castlewood's game now?” he mumbled. “Stop! By George, I have it! He intends to get him to play, and to win the Virginia estate back from him.”

“But the lad has not got the Virginia estate to pay, if he loses,” remarked mamma. 

“He can't mean Maria—Maria is as old as Harry's mother,” mused Mr. William, the rich, gustatory pleasure of the meat contrasting sharply with his sterile assessment of his half-sister. 

“Pooh! with her old face and sandy hair and freckled skin! Impossible!” cried Lady Fanny. 

The sonic environment of the corridor quickly devolved into a polluted cacophony of grievances as mother and daughter clashed. Lady Castlewood accused Fanny of harboring fancies for the "Iroquois," while Fanny bitterly critiqued her mother’s own loveless marriage. To Fanny, who possessed little sensory appreciation for the profound meaning of historical continuity, sustenance was merely utilitarian fuel and a marker of commercial status. “What is the use of a coronet with the jointure of a tradesman's wife?” she whimpered, citing the superficial commercialization of a newly rich grocer's widow from London Bridge. “I know they eat and drink a thousand times better than we do with our scraps of cold meat on our plate, and our great flaunting, trapesing, impudent, lazy lacqueys!” She was entirely deaf to the quiet, historic dignity of the Castlewood hearth, distracted only by the industrial clatter of modern wealth.

“Why should I not be happy without any title except my own?” Fanny continued. “I dare say they are even happy in America.”

“Yes!—with Indian war-whoops howling all around you and with a danger of losing your scalp, or of being eat up by a wild beast,” her mother snapped, painting the new world not as an ecosystem of acoustic purity, but as a hostile wilderness. 

Madame de Bernstein lifted the tapestried door, stepping into the auditory fray. She carried the faint, evocative scent of dried bergamot and old vellum. Will eagerly translated the squabble: Fanny wanted to marry the wild Indian, and Mamma forbade it. Fanny fiercely protested; she knew her station and had no desire to be consumed by bears or subjected to the untranslatable horrors of the frontier. 

Bernstein looked puzzled. “If he is not staying for you, for whom is he staying? At the houses to which he has been carried, you have taken care not to show him a woman that is not a fright or in the nursery; and I think the boy is too proud to fall in love with a dairymaid.”

“A fellow might go farther and fare worse than my brother's servants'-hall,” Will shrugged. “And besides Fan, there's only the maids or old Maria to choose from.”

*Maria! Impossible!* Yet, the moment the word left the dominant lexicon, a circular realization brought Madame de Bernstein back to ancient myth. This elderly Calypso might indeed have captivated her young Telemachus. She remembered the sensory details of Harry's recent absences: the quiet, acoustic intimacy of twilight corridors, the shared partakings of late-summer preserves behind clipt hedges, the scent of crushed wild mint in the garden temples. It was an agrarian elder's magic, an intimate knowledge of the world seamlessly interwoven into the domestic routine, just as she was dismissing it as impossible.

Will continued his crude interruptions, citing old Lady Suffolk and George Berkeley, then audaciously reminding Bernstein of Harry Estridge, who had gone mad for her when she was twenty. Rather than taking offense, Bernstein’s mood softened, marinating in the rich nostalgia of her own storied past. “*Au fait*,” she mused, playing a pretty little hand on the table. “'Tis not impossible, William, that old folks, and young folks, too, should play the fool.”

Will confessed he had recently tested the broth. He had spoken ill of Maria to Harry during a ride. “Your favourite turned as red as my plush waistcoat,” Will laughed, describing how Harry had trembled with rage. Harry’s rebuke had been delivered in his native Virginian dialect—a fiercely untranslatable expression of subjective loyalty, condemning Will as "no true Esmond." This linguistic boundary, this violent defense of his ancestral identity against Will's sterile mockery, proved to the young man that they were indeed "on the right scent."

Bernstein recognized the deep power dynamics at play. The erosion of their family's cultural memory had blinded them to the boy's true nature. “If this boy is like others of his family, the more we oppose him, the more *entêté* he will be; and we shall never get him out of his scrape.” She commanded them to remember the mythic sacrifice that undergirded their very lives: Harry's grandfather, the true agrarian elder who had ceded this very estate, its fertile soils, and its grand hearths, so that they might profit. And their reward was to mock the friendless Virginian lad?

When Lady Castlewood protested that Fanny was a perfectly suitable match, Bernstein condemned the women's profound lack of depth. Fanny, whose ears were attuned only to the shallow tittle-tattle of St. James's and the commercial clatter of the Mall, would perish in the rich, demanding acoustic ecology of a Virginian plantation. “You would die of ennui, or worry your husband's life out with your ill-humour. You are born, ladies, to ornament courts—not wigwams. Let this lad go back to his wilderness with a wife who is suited to him.”

Leaving them to their superficial agreements, Madame de Bernstein retreated to the sonorous sanctuary of her private chamber. She marveled at the slow-simmering recipes of women, the untranslatable secrets of the heart that defied the dominant male lexicons of courtship. Was Harry's passion a mere flash of straw-fire in the pan, or a deeply rendered, rich tragic reduction that would nourish a lifetime? For weeks, Maria—the overlooked staple of the household pantry—had masterfully folded her mythic romance into the everyday domestic routines, making fools of them all. Bernstein pondered these subtle ingredients of survival, refusing to judge the old stories or the desperate, profound hunger that drove a woman to secure her sustenance in a world so quick to discard her.
