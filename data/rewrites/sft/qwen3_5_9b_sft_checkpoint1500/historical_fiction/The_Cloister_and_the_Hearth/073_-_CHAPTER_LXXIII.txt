A woman named Catherine proves a hazardous guest within a stranger's home, for the spirit of governance resides heavily within her. Before she has fully stepped across the threshold, the utensils gleam with a sudden polish, the hearth sweeps itself clean, the windows flood with light, and the soul of a massive cricket animates the dwelling. Yet, this cricket is a "Busy Body"—a formidable entity lacking discernment. It seeks to right all things and set all people straight. While many things improve under such correction, not everything does; indeed, everything resists having its order imposed upon it, save for the grave where silence reigns eternal.

Consequently, Catherine rearranged every chair and table in Margaret's house, perhaps improving the layout, though she felt compelled to disturb the living furniture as well. As Margaret's confinement approached, Catherine treacherously enlisted the aid of Denys and Martin. When these poor, simple men earnestly asked what service they might render, she feigned benevolence, suggesting a short walk. To extend this stroll into the following week, however, she hinted, was ungracious. Under the influence of a weak master, the strong men behaved with a servile timidity that might have appeased a she-bear with three sickly cubs. They slipped out at dawn like thieves and returned at night with the stealth of burglars; if caught at home, they moved like cats traversing walls of broken glass, wearing expressions of awe and a general aura of subjugation.

Yet, this arrangement failed entirely. Their mere presence jarred Catherine's nerves. Did instinct whisper that a pair of depopulators had no place in a house housing two multipliers? The breastplate offers no defense against a woman's tongue, and Catherine inflicted infinite pricks and needles of speech upon them. By the time Margaret descended the stairs, she found the kitchen cleared of its heroic defenders. Old Martin, stiff and weary, had retreated no further than the street, returning with the honors of war: he carried off his baggage, a single stool, and sat upon it suspended in the air.

Margaret, seeing him bask in the sun, was unaware he had become a fixture in that luminary. Seeking Denys, she spoke tenderly, expecting him to greet her warmly after three years apart. "Good, kind Denys; he will be right pleased to see me about again."

Catherine, wiping a bowl with vigorous, now superfluous force, lied smoothly. "Denys is gone to his friends in Burgundy. And high time, hasn't he? He hasn't been near them for three years, by all accounts."

"What?" Margaret exclaimed, lifting eyes like full-blown violets. "Gone without bidding me farewell?"

Catherine flushed. This new angle pricked her conscience. With a dismissive toss, she offered a falsehood: "Oh, you were asleep at the time, and I would not have you woken."

"Poor Denys," Margaret sighed, and visible dew gathered on her violet eyes.

Watching from the corner of her eye, Catherine, without overt acknowledgment, turned her attention to the surviving depopulator. Suddenly, Martin, old and stiff in many ways, stood before her.

"No, thank you, dame," he murmured. "I have grown accustomed to being outdoors. And I love not changing and changing. I meddle with nobody here, and nobody meddles with me."

"Oh, you nasty, cross old wretch!" Catherine shrieked, shifting instantly from honey to sharpest vinegar, and flounced back into the house.

On calm reflection, she let out a small cry. Half-reconciling herself to her conduct, she vowed that Margaret should never miss her "plagues of soldiers" again. Still feeling uneasy, she dispelled her regrets with a process both simple and sovereign. She took the child and washed him.

From head to foot, she bathed him in tepid water; heroes, their grievances, and wrongs dissolved like dust in an ocean of soap and suds. As this celestial ceremony unfolded, Margaret could not remain silent. Hovering around the fortunate performer, she sought an apparent hand in the act, if not a real one. Dipping her finger into the water—perhaps to pave the way for her son, though she could not deceive herself enough to believe Catherine would let her control the temperature—she knelt opposite little Gerard. Prattling with amazing fluency, yet careful not to articulate like adults lest a cherub find their pronunciation ridiculous, she addressed the infant.

"I wish you could wash out *that*," she said, fixing her gaze on the child's hand.

"What?"

"What? Have you not noticed? Upon his little finger."

Granny peered, and there lay a tiny brown mole.

"Eh, but this is wonderful!" she cried. "Nature, my lass, you are strong—and meddling to boot! Have you noticed such a mark on anyone else? Tell the truth, girl!"

"On him? Nay, mother, not I."

"Well, then he has, and on the very spot. And you never noticed that much. But, dear heart, I forgot; you haven't known him from childhood to manhood as I have. I've had him hundreds of times on my knees, just as this, and washed him from top to toe in lukewarm water." Swelling with conscious superiority, she regarded Margaret as a woman beyond competition.

Catherine looked down from her dizzy height and moralized. She differed from other busybodies in that she occasionally reflected—not deeply, nor would she dare print it.

"It is strange," she mused, "how things come round and about. Life is but a whirligig. At least, we poor women, our lives are all cut upon one pattern. Was I not, in my Gerard's young days, washing out his mole? "'Oh, fie! Here's a foul blot,'" I quoth, and scrubbed away until the poor wight cried, so then I thought 'twas time to desist. And now you say to me, 'Mother,' do try and wash out of my Gerard's finger.' Think on't!"

"Wash it out?" cried Margaret. "I would not for all the world. Why, it is the sweetest bit in his little darling body. I'll kiss it morning and night till he who owned it first returns to us three. Oh, bless you, my jewel of gold and silver, for being marked like your own daddy, to comfort me."

Kissing little Gerard's mole, she could not stop there. Soon, he sprawled on her lap, receiving kisses over his entire back again and again, seeming to worry the lamb as a wolf would, while Catherine watched with a smile. She had witnessed many such savage onslaughts in her day.

This little sketch indicates the tenor of Margaret's life for several months. A few small occurrences happened during that time which must be told, yet I reserve them, since one string serves for many glass beads. While her boy's father passed through those fearful tempests of the soul, ending in the dead calm of the monastery, her life might fairly be summed in one great, blissful word: Maternity.

You, who know what lies within that word, may enlarge my little sketch, seeing the young mother nursing, washing, dressing, undressing, crowing, and gamboling with her first-born. Then, swift as lightning, dart your eye into Italy and behold the cold cloister; see the monks passing like ghosts, eyes lowered, hands meekly crossed over breasts dead to earthly feeling.

One of these cowled ghosts is he whose return, filled with love, youth, and joy, that radiant young mother awaits.

In the valley of Grindelwald, the traveler finds on one side the perpendicular Alps—all rock, ice, and everlasting snow, towering above the clouds and piercing to the sky. On the other hand lie little everyday slopes, green as emeralds, studded with cows and pretty cottages, teeming with life. Whereas those lofty neighbors stand leafless, lifeless, inhuman, and sublime. Elsewhere, sweet commonplaces of nature often pass unnoticed; but facing the grim Alps, they soothe, and even gently strike, the mind by contrast with their tremendous opposites. Such, in their way, are the two halves of this story, rightly viewed: on the Italian side, rugged adventure, strong passion, blasphemy, vice, penitence, pure ice, holy snow, soaring directly at heaven. On the Dutch side, all on a humble scale and womanish, yet ever green. And as a pathway parts the ice towers of Grindelwald, aspiring to the sky, from its little sunny braes, so here is but a page between "The Cloister and the Hearth."
