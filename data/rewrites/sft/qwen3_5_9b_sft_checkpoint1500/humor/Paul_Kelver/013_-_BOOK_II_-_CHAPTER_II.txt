PAUL, ESCAPING FROM HIS SOLITUDE, FALLS INTO STRANGE COMPANY. AND BECOMES CAPTIVE TO ONE OF HAUGHTY MIEN.

All things pass, even the self-imposed misery of the shy young man condemned to solitude by nature's decree. Winter evenings came, and I turned to work; in the glow of a handful of fire and two flickering candles, my "apartment" became a haven. I procured paper, pens, and ink. What more does a writer require? He is merely a vessel waiting to be filled by spirits who may speak or remain silent.

Before me lay London, teeming with a million characters—grave and gay—and ten thousand romances, mysteries, and humors. It stretched out, begging only for intelligent observation. Yet, I could never conceive of weaving a story from the things I truly knew. My tales were populated by cottage maids and bucolic yeomen; my scenes unfolded in windmills, amidst mountains, or beside moated granges. I suspect this specific brand of folly is common to most youthful fictionists.

A trail of gentle melancholy draped over these creations. Sentiment was then the reigning fashion, and as a beginner, I scrupulously adhered to trends. Generally speaking, to be a heroine in my stories was a death sentence. Regardless of how naturally her hair might curl—or how curly hair, I was convinced, signified vitality—whatever signs of robust health she displayed in Chapter One ("dancing eyes," "colour that came and went"), she was tolerably certain to meet an untimely grave. Snowdrops and early primroses (botanical knowledge gleaned from *Our Garden Favourites, Illustrated*) bloomed there like flowers in a greenhouse. Near the coast, sea breezes would whimsically select that particular churchyard as their playground. Years later, a white-haired man would lead little children there to retell the tale, a dismal entertainment indeed.

Occasionally, the gentleman fell victim to my literature's deadly atmosphere. It mattered little, for in his final soliloquy, he would usually conclude, "It was better so." Snowdrops and primroses offered no consolation, and his grave, marked by a rude cross, typically sat in an unfrequented corner of the African veldt or amidst burning sands. Describing such final scenery required a trip to the British Museum reading-room.

Dismal little fledglings! Again and again I drove them from the nest, only to watch them flutter back, soiled, crumpled, and physically damaged. Yet, one person had admired them, cried over them—myself.

I exhausted every method. Sometimes I dispatched them with curt, take-it-or-leave-it business notes. Other times, I attached pathetic pleas for consideration. I declared prices of five guineas, requesting crossed cheques, or I tickled editorial greed by offering my first contribution free—a sample packet, so to speak, with one trial supposedly sufficient. At other junctures, I wrote sarcastically, enclosing a brutally penned rejection note. Or I wrote frankly, explaining I was a novice and asking to be told my faults, if any.

None found a resting place for their feet. A month, a week, a couple of days, they would vanish, only to return. I never lost a single one. I wished I had; it would have varied the monotony.

I detested the poor little slavey who, bursting joyously into the room, would hold them out to me from between her apron-hidden thumb and finger. Her chronic sniff I translated into contempt. If I flew down the stairs at the sound of the postman's knock and seized the letter from his hands, it seemed to me he smiled. Tearing them from their envelopes, I would curse and abuse them, sometimes flinging them into the fire. But before they were scorched, I would snatch them out, smooth them, and reread them. The editor himself could never have seen them; it was impossible. Some jealous underling must have intercepted them. I had sent them to the wrong paper, or they had arrived at an inopportune moment. Their triumph would come. Rewriting the first and last sheets, I would send them forth again with fresh hope.

Understanding that the would-be happy warrior must shine in camp as well as in the field, I sought to fit myself for the social side of life. Smoking and drinking were the twin sins I found most difficult to acquire. I am not claiming mental excellence, but confessing a bodily infirmity. The spirit had always been willing, but my flesh was weak. Fired by emulation, I had occasionally essayed a cigarette at school. The result had been distinctly unsatisfactory, and after two or three attempts, I abandoned the endeavor, excusing my faint-heartedness with sanctimonious airs that smoking was bad for growing boys. In the presence of contemporaries with stronger stomachs, I assumed a fine pose of disapproval, yet in my heart, I knew myself a young hypocrite, disguising physical cowardice in the robes of moral courage—a self-deception to which human nature is prone.

Similarly, I had tasted the red wine that stood year in, year out, decanted on our sideboard. The true inwardness of St. Paul's prescription had been revealed to me; the attitude of those who drink it under a doctor's orders, regarding it purely as medicine, appeared reasonable. I noticed others, some grown men, making wry faces when drinking my mother's claret, concluding that a taste for strong liquor was an accomplishment less easily acquired than generally supposed. The lack thereof in a young man could be no disgrace, and accordingly, I weakly postponed effort in that direction.

But now, a gentleman at large, my education could no longer be delayed. Training, particularly severe training, was an absolute necessity for the artist. A quarter of a century ago, a genius who did not smoke and drink—indeed, who drank more than was good for him—would have been dismissed as an impostor. About the genius I was hopeful, but at no time positively certain. Regarding smoking and drinking, however, I could ensure results. I set to work methodically. Experience taught me that smoking was better practiced on Saturday nights, leaving Sunday for walking off the effects. Patience and determination eventually crowned with success: I learned to smoke a cigarette to all appearances as though I were enjoying it. Young men of lesser character might have rested content, but attainment of the highest has always been my motive force. The cigarette conquered, I next attacked the cigar.

My first cigar remains vivid in memory. It was at a smoking concert in the Islington Drill Hall, to which Minikin had invited me. Feeling unsure whether my growing dizziness stemmed solely from the cigar or partly from the hot, overcrowded room, I made my excuses and slipped out. I found myself in a small courtyard, divided from a neighboring garden by a low wall. Clearly, the cigar was the culprit. My inclination was to remove it and toss it, but conscience urged perseverance. I reasoned that if I climbed onto the wall and walked from end to end, there would be no excuse for ignoring the counsels of perfection. Conversely, if the wall proved too narrow, I would be entitled to discard the beastly thing and make my way home. Attaining the wall with some difficulty, I commenced my self-inflicted ordeal. Two yards further, I found myself lying across the wall, legs hanging down one side, head overhanging the other. The position suited my requirements, so I maintained it. Inclination seized its opportunity, urging me to take a solemn vow never to smoke again. I am proud to write that through that hour of temptation, I remained firm, strengthening myself by whispering, "Never despair. What others can do, so can you. Is not all victory won through suffering?"

A liking for drink proved, if possible, even more difficult to achieve. Spirits almost caused me to despair. Once, confusing bottles, I drank hair oil in mistake for whiskey and found it decidedly less nauseous. Twice a week, I forced myself to swallow a glass of beer, standing over myself to insist on draining it to the bitter dregs. As a reward, I treated myself to chocolates, comforting myself by assuring myself it was for my good, promising a day when I would truly like it and be grateful for my severity.

In other, more sensible directions, I sought progress. Gradually, I was overcoming my shyness. It was a slow process. I found the best plan was not to mind being shy, to accept it as part of my temperament, and to laugh at it with others. The coldness of an indifferent world serves to harden a too-sensitive skin. The gradual rubbings of existence rounded off my many corners. I became possible to my fellow creatures, and they to me. I began to take pleasure in their company.

By directing me to this particular house in Nelson Square, Fate had done me a kindness. I flatter myself we were an interesting menagerie gathered under its leaky roof. Mrs. Peedles, our landlady, who slept in the basement with the slavey, had been an actress in Charles Keane's company at the old Princess's. True, she had played only insignificant parts there. London, as she explained, was a poor judge of art, plagued by prejudices. Previously, in the Provinces, she had enjoyed a career of glory: Juliet, Amy Robsart, Mrs. Haller in *The Stranger*—almost the entire roll of the "Legitimates."
