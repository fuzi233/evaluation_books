I cannot describe the turmoil within me, for the shock I suffered froze both my faculties and my emotions. By the time Betteredge reached me, I was unaware of my actions; according to him, I laughed at his inquiries and handed him my nightgown, urging him to solve the mystery inscribed upon it.

Our conversation on the shore has slipped from my memory entirely. The first image I can clearly recall is returning to the fir plantation with Betteredge, where he promised that a glass of grog would restore us both to composure.

From the plantation, we moved to Betteredge's small sitting-room. My resolve never to step foot in Rachel's house again dissolved. I welcomed the cool shade and silence of the room, sipping a grog mixed with icy well-water—a rare luxury for me at that hour. Usually, such a drink would stupefy me, but today it sharpened my nerves. I began, as predicted, to "face" the ordeal, and so did Betteredge.

To the uninitiated eye, my behavior must seem absurd. Placed in a situation without parallel, why did I not retreat from humanity, analyze the impossible facts, or rush to London for counsel? Instead, I sat at ten in the morning, drinking spirits and water with an old servant in a house I had vowed never to debase myself by entering again. Yet, the sight of Betteredge's familiar face offered an incomparable comfort, and his grog sustained me when I lay prostrate in body and mind. I can only plead for my own sanity and admire the unwavering dignity and logic displayed by every reader of these pages throughout their lives.

"Now, Mr. Franklin," Betteredge declared, tossing the nightgown onto the table as if addressing a living beast, "there is one certainty, at least. *He* is a liar."

His reassurance did not align with my thoughts.

"I am as innocent of knowing the diamond's whereabouts as you are," I replied. "Yet there is the evidence! The paint on the nightgown and the name embroidered on it are undeniable facts."

Betteredge lifted my glass and pressed it into my hand. "Facts?" he murmured. "Drink more, Mr. Franklin, and you'll overcome the delusion that facts are absolute! It was foul play, sir!" he continued, lowering his voice. "Somehow, someone tampered with things, and you and I must uncover it. Did you find anything else in the tin box when you searched it?"

The question jolted me. I reached into my pocket and retrieved a letter. It was lengthy and densely written. Scanning the bottom, I found the signature: "Rosanna Spearman."

A sudden illumination struck me, followed by a rising suspicion. "Stop!" I cried. "Rosanna Spearman was rescued from a reformatory by Aunt Rachel. She was a former thief, yes, but..."

"There is no denying it, Mr. Franklin. So what of it?"

"So what of it? Could she not have stolen the diamond afterward? Could she not have deliberately stained my nightgown?"

Betteredge placed a hand on my arm, halting me. "You will be acquitted of this charge, Mr. Franklin, beyond shadow of a doubt. But I pray you do not acquit yourself *so*. Read the letter, sir. For the sake of the girl's soul, read it."

I felt the weight of his earnest gaze, a silent rebuke. "I will judge her for myself," I said, "but I shall read it aloud."

I began, and the words unfolded before me:

*"Sir—I have something to confess to you. A confession born of terrible misery may be brief. These truths can be spoken in three words. I love you."*

The letter fell from my hand. I stared at Betteredge. "By Heaven," I stammered, "what does this mean?"

He recoiled slightly from the question. "You and Limping Lucy were alone this morning, sir. Did she not speak of Rosanna?"

"She never mentioned her name."

"Return to the letter, Mr. Franklin. I cannot bear to distress you further after what you have endured. Let her speak. And finish your grog, for your own sake."

I resumed reading.

*"It would be shameful for me to tell you this if I were alive when you read it. I shall be dead and buried, sir, when you find this letter. That gives me courage. Even my grave will not remain to identify me. I may speak the truth, though the quicksand waits to swallow me the moment the ink dries.*

*"You will find your nightgown hidden in my secret place, bearing the smear of paint. You will wonder why I hid it and remained silent during my lifetime. I have but one reason: I did these strange things because I loved you.*

*"I need not recount my early life. Lady Verinder took me from a reformatory, where I had been sent from prison. I was imprisoned because I stole. I stole because my mother lived on the streets when I was a child, and she did so because my father abandoned us. This is a tale common enough in the papers.*

*"Lady Verinder and Mr. Betteredge were the only kind souls I ever met. I might have survived, unhappily but safely, if you had not visited. I blame no one, sir. It is all my fault.*

*"Do you remember when you emerged from the sandhills that morning, seeking Mr. Betteredge? You appeared like a prince from a fairy-tale, a lover from a dream. You were the most adorable creature I had ever seen. A spark of happiness, a life I had never known, leaped within me the instant I saw you. Do not mock me if you can; if only you could understand how seriously I take this!*

*"I returned to the house and wrote your names in my work-box, tying a true lovers' knot beneath them. Then, some angel—or perhaps a devil—whispered, 'Look in the glass.' The mirror revealed... I cannot say. I was too foolish to heed the warning. I grew deeper in love with you, imagining myself a lady of your station, the fairest flower in your garden. I tried, oh, how I tried, to catch your eye. If you knew how I wept at night, mourning your ignorance of me, you might have pitied me, offering a glance to sustain me.*

*"It would have been a cruel kindness if you had known how I hated Miss Rachel. I believe I realized you loved her before you knew it yourself. She gave you roses for your buttonhole. Alas, Mr. Franklin, you wore *my* roses far more often than either of you suspected! My only solace was slipping my rose secretly into your glass, replacing hers, then discarding hers.*

*"Had she been truly beautiful as you claimed, I might have borne it better. No, I became more spiteful. Imagine placing Miss Rachel in a maid's dress, stripping her jewels. Her figure was poor, too thin. Who can dictate what men desire? Young ladies may act with impropriety. It is none of my business, yet hearing Miss Rachel praised as 'pretty' stirs one, knowing it is merely her dress and her vanity.*

*"One truth I must share first. My life, though hard as a thief, was bearable. It was only when the reformatory taught me to feel my degradation and strive for virtue that the days stretched endlessly. The prospect of the future haunted me. Honest people, even the kindest, seemed like reproaches. A crushing loneliness followed me everywhere. I knew I must make peace with my fellow servants, yet I could not. They seemed to suspect my past. I do not regret becoming a reformed woman, yet it was a weary existence. You arrived like a beam of sunlight, only to fail me. I was mad enough to love you, yet unable to attract your notice. There was profound misery there.*

*"In those days of bitterness, I visited my favorite haunt twice or thrice—the beach above the Shivering Sands. I told myself, 'Perhaps it ends here. When I can endure no more, I shall end it here.' You understand, sir, that the sands held a spell over me before you arrived. I always felt something awaited me at the quicksand, but I never considered suicide until now. Then, I thought this place would end my troubles instantly and hide me forever.*

*"Aggravated by the gossip among the maids, wondering who to suspect, and angered by your frantic search and call for the police, I secluded myself until late afternoon, when Officer Seegrave arrived.*

*"Mr. Seegrave stationed a guard on the women's bedrooms. Enraged, we followed him upstairs to demand the insult. I accompanied them, for if I had acted differently, Seegrave would have immediately suspected me. We found him in Miss Rachel's room. He barred us from staying, pointing to the smear on the painted door, claiming some of our petticoats caused the damage, and ordered us downstairs.*
