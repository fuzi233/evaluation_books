The two young men lingered outside the Nuns’ House courtyard, the brass doorplate glinting coldly under the moonlight as if mocking their presence. With a shared glance, they turned and ambled down the silent street, the weight of unspoken friction already settling between them.

“How long do you remain in Cloisterham, Mr. Drood?” Neville asked, his tone measured.

“Not long,” Edwin replied, dismissive. “London calls me back tomorrow. Though I’ll return now and then—until Midsummer. After that, I bid farewell to England altogether for some time.”

“Traveling?”

“Waking up Egypt,” Edwin said with a condescending smirk. “You’re not one for books, I take it?”

“Reading?” Edwin echoed, a thread of derision in his voice. “Hardly. I’ve better things to do—engineering, work. My father left me a share in his firm. Until I come of age, I’m under the guardianship of Jack Jasper. You met him at dinner.”

Neville’s gaze sharpened. “Mr. Crisparkle mentioned your… other fortunate prospects.”

Edwin stiffened. “What prospects?”

The air between them grew taut. Neville hesitated, then pressed on, cautious but deliberate. “I meant no offense in referring to your engagement, Mr. Drood.”

Edwin barked a laugh, quickening his pace. “Is there a soul in Cloisterham who *hasn’t* chattered about it? I’m surprised the taverns haven’t hung my face as their sign: *The Betrothed’s Head*—or hers.”

“Mr. Crisparkle spoke of it openly,” Neville defended.

“And so did you,” Edwin countered. “Why?”

Neville’s jaw tightened. “I assumed you’d take pride in it.”

A brittle silence followed. Edwin’s indifference scraped against Neville’s simmering disdain—for him, for the careless way he held what Neville already sensed Rosebud deserved. And Edwin, in turn, bristled at this stranger’s presumption, this brother of Helena’s who dared judge him.

“Pride isn’t always loud, Mr. Neville,” Edwin said at last, feigning nonchalance. “But then, I’m a busy man. You scholars know everything, don’t you?”

Neville’s restraint frayed. “You mock me for lacking your advantages. But my upbringing wasn’t among *civilized* men.”

Edwin’s smile was razor-thin. “Perhaps civility is simply minding one’s own affairs. Try it.”

“You overstep,” Neville snapped. “Where I come from, a man answers for such arrogance.”

“With what?” Edwin halted, eyes gleaming with challenge. “Words? Or something sharper?”

A hand clamped onto Edwin’s shoulder. Jasper materialized from the shadows, his voice smooth as oil. “Ned. Neville. Must we quarrel?” His grip was firm, steering them apart. “You’re the host here, Ned. Hospitality, remember?” Then, to Neville: “And temper, my friend. Let’s have no more of this.”

Grudgingly, the two muttered assent, though Neville’s jaw remained clenched. Jasper, ever the mediator, herded them toward his gatehouse, where wine waited.

The room’s warmth did little to thaw the tension. Edwin sprawled in his chair, lazily defiant; Neville stood rigid, the portrait of Helena glowering above the hearth. Jasper, observant, raised his glass.

“To Ned—may his path be bright.”

They drank. The wine burned, and so did the silence.

Edwin, flushed, smirked at Neville. “If I painted your sister—”

“You won’t.”

“But if I *did*,” Edwin pressed, “you’d see real skill.”

Neville’s fingers whitened around his glass. “You’ve never known hardship. It might have humbled you.”

Edwin’s laugh was a spark to tinder. “Hardship? What, like yours? Enlighten me.”

“You’d not last a day in my world,” Neville shot back.

“Your world?” Edwin sneered. “A distant, *savage* place, then?”

Neville lunged. Wine splattered Edwin’s shirt as Jasper intercepted the thrown goblet, shattering it against the grate.

“Enough!” Jasper’s voice cut like a whip.

Neville stormed out, the night air doing nothing to cool his fury. The moon watched, indifferent, as he staggered toward Minor Canon Corner.

Mr. Crisparkle answered his knock, dismayed. “Mr. Neville! This state—”

“I’ve disgraced myself,” Neville choked.

Crisparkle steadied him. “You’re not sober.”

“I drank little—it took me like a fever. And Edwin—he provoked me. I nearly struck him.”

Crisparkle’s gaze was heavy. “Rest. We’ll speak tomorrow.”

As Neville slumped at his desk, a final knock came. Jasper stood at the door, holding Neville’s forgotten hat.

“It was murderous,” Jasper murmured. “Had I not intervened—”

Crisparkle paled. “Strong words.”

“There’s a darkness in him,” Jasper insisted. “A tiger’s instinct. You’ve taken on a perilous charge, my friend.”

Crisparkle met his gaze steadily. “I fear nothing.”

Jasper’s smile didn’t reach his eyes. “No. But you *should*.”

With that, he was gone. Crisparkle hung the hat, the weight of the night settling over him like a shroud.
