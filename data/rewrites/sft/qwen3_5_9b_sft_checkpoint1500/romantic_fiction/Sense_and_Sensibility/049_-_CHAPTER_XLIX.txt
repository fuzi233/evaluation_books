Edward was free. Whether the circumstances of his liberation appeared unaccountable to the rest of the family was irrelevant; what mattered was the certainty of his freedom, and the inevitable conclusion everyone quickly drew regarding how he would employ it. Having survived the agony of a single, imprudent engagement contracted without maternal consent—a mistake he had endured for over four years—their collective wisdom dictated that the failure of *that* union must inevitably lead to the immediate formation of another.

His mission at Barton was singular and direct: he intended to ask Elinor to marry him. Yet, despite possessing ample experience in matters of courtship, Edward felt a profound, almost paralyzing discomfort. He craved encouragement, fresh air, and the steady hand of a guide. However, destiny moved swiftly. Within moments, he forged his resolution; shortly thereafter, an opportunity presented itself. By the time the family gathered around the table at four o'clock—some three hours after his arrival—he had not only secured his lady's hand but also obtained her mother's blessing. He emerged from the room not merely as a lover in a rapture of sentiment, but as a man grounded in the solid, joyful reality of truth.

His situation was a tapestry woven from threads of unparalleled joy. Beyond the standard triumph of accepted love, he possessed the relief of being absolved from a long, miserable entanglement with a woman he no longer loved. Elevated at once to a security he had once deemed impossible, he was transported from the abyss of doubt to the heights of happiness. The transformation was evident in a cheerfulness so genuine and flowing that his friends had never before witnessed it.

With his heart now fully open to Elinor, Edward confessed his weaknesses and errors, treating his youthful infatuation with Lucy Steele with the philosophical detachment appropriate to a man of twenty-four.

"It was a foolish, idle inclination on my side," he admitted, "born of ignorance and a lack of occupation. If my mother had provided me with an active profession when I left Mr. Pratt's care at eighteen, I am certain this would never have happened. Although I departed Longstaple with what I then considered an unconquerable preference for his niece, I would have outgrown that fancied attachment in a short time had I been occupied with pursuits and mixed more freely with the world. Instead, I returned home to complete idleness. For the first twelve months following, I lacked even the nominal employment that university attendance would have afforded, for I was not entered at Oxford until I was nineteen. With nothing in the world to do but to imagine myself in love, and with no comfort at home, no brother-companion, and a distaste for new acquaintances, it was natural for me to spend nearly all my time at Longstaple, where I always felt at home. There, Lucy appeared everything amiable and obliging. She was pretty—at least I thought so then—and having seen so little of other women, I could make no comparisons to reveal defects. Considering everything, I hope that, however foolish our engagement was, however disastrous it proved, it was not at the time an unnatural or inexcusable piece of folly."

The shift in the Dashwoods' fortunes was so profound it promised them a sleepless night. Mrs. Dashwood, overwhelmed by happiness, struggled to express her gratitude without wounding Edward's delicacy, torn between praising Elinor and ensuring her son had space to speak freely. Marianne, conversely, spoke her joy only through tears; comparisons and regrets mingled in her mind, rendering her spirit heavy yet sincere in her love for her sister.

But how to describe Elinor's feelings? From the moment she learned Lucy was married and Edward was free, she had been a whirlwind of emotion. Yet, when the second moment arrived, when every doubt vanished, when she saw him honorably released from his former bond and instantly turning to declare an affection as tender and constant as she had ever imagined, she was overcome. Her felicity was so intense that it took several hours to settle her spirits into tranquility.

Edward planned to stay at the cottage for at least a week, for the subjects of the past, present, and future demanded endless discussion. While lovers often exhaust topics rapidly, between Edward and Elinor, no subject was ever truly finished; every communication required repetition, a dozen times over, to feel complete.

Lucy's marriage became the first topic of inquiry, a source of bewildering wonder. How could Robert, who had spoken of Lucy's beauty without admiration, be drawn to her? How could a girl already engaged to his brother—who had consequently been rejected by his family—be united with him? To Elinor, it was a delightful absurdity, a puzzle that baffled her reason.

Edward offered an explanation rooted in vanity and flattery. Perhaps, he suggested, an accidental meeting had ignited a chain reaction where Robert's ego and Lucy's desire for influence converged. Elinor recalled Robert's words in Harley Street regarding how his mediation might have altered things if applied sooner.

"That was exactly like Robert," Edward observed. "And that, perhaps, was in his mind when the acquaintance first began. And Lucy, initially, may have sought only his good offices in my favor. Other designs likely arose later."

Yet, the duration of this secret conspiracy remained unclear to both. At Oxford, Edward had relied solely on Elinor for news of Lucy, and her letters to the very end had been neither less frequent nor less affectionate than usual. No suspicion had prepared him for the storm that awaited. When the letter finally burst upon him, he had been half-stupified by the shock, the horror, and the sheer relief of his deliverance.

He handed the letter to Elinor.

*Being very sure I have long lost your affections, I have thought myself at liberty to bestow my own on another, and have no doubt of being as happy with him as I once used to think I might be with you; but I scorn to accept a hand while the heart was another's.*
*Sincerely wish you happy in your choice, and it shall not be my fault if we are not always good friends, as our near relationship now makes proper. I can safely say I owe you no ill-will, and am sure you will be too generous to do us any ill offices. Your brother has gained my affections entirely, and as we could not live without one another, we are just returned from the altar, and are now on our way to Dawlish for a few weeks, which place your dear brother has great curiosity to see, but thought I would first trouble you with these few lines, and shall always remain,*

*"I have burnt all your letters, and will return your picture the first opportunity. Please to destroy my scrawls—but the ring with my hair you are very welcome to keep."*

Elinor read and returned it without comment.

"I will not ask your opinion of it as a composition," Edward remarked. "For worlds would not I have had a letter of hers seen by you in former days. In a sister, it is bad enough, but in a wife! How I have blushed over the pages of her writing! And I believe I may say that since the first half-year of our foolish business, this is the only letter I ever received from her of which the substance made me any amends for the defect of the style."

"However it may have come about," Elinor said after a pause, "they are certainly married. And your mother has brought on herself a most appropriate punishment. The independence she settled on Robert, born of resentment against you, has empowered him to make his own choice. She has effectively bribed one son with a thousand-a-year to perform the very deed for which she disinherited the other. She will hardly be less hurt by Robert's marrying Lucy than she would have been by your marrying her."

"She will be more hurt by it," Edward corrected gently. "Robert was always her favorite. She will be more hurt, and on the same principle, she will forgive him much sooner."

Regarding his current standing with his family, Edward knew little, having left Oxford barely twenty-four hours after receiving Lucy's letter. His sole objective was the road to Barton, leaving no room for schemes beyond reaching Elinor. Confident in her reception, despite his past jealousy of Colonel Brandon and his own modesty, he proceeded with a charming assurance.

To Elinor, Lucy's deception was crystal clear. Edward, now fully enlightened regarding her character, saw no scruple in believing her capable of the utmost meanness and wanton ill-nature. Though he had long recognized her lack of liberality in some opinions, attributing it to a want of education, he had always maintained she was a well-disposed, good-hearted girl deeply attached to him. Only the firm belief in this innocence could have prevented him from ending an engagement that had long been a source of disquiet, especially after it exposed him to his mother's fury.

"I thought it my duty," Edward explained, "independent of my feelings, to give her the option of continuing the engagement or not when I was renounced by my mother and stood alone. In such a desperate situation, where there seemed nothing to tempt the avarice or vanity of any living creature, how could I suppose, when she so earnestly insisted on sharing my fate, whatever it might be, that anything but disinterested affection was her inducement?
