Some little while before Mr. Foker packed up his portmanteau and his cautious conscience and quitted Oxbridge, Boniface College received—after an amount of refusal elsewhere that would have cooled a lesser vanity—a gentleman who came like a late frost upon a tender garden-bed.

His name was Horace Bloundell; and because his family had been ancient long enough to have forgotten what youth looks like, he was, by universal consent of the young wags, “Bloundell-Bloundell, of Bloundell-Bloundell Hall, in Bloundell-Bloundellshire,” as though the very syllables must be paid for by the acre. He had belonged once to Camford, the other university; but had quitted it in consequence of “differences” with tutors and authorities—differences, that is to say, of such a nature that the authorities were of one opinion and Mr. Bloundell of another, and the authorities possessed power.

There was a living in the family; and it was thought important that Mr. Bloundell should hold it. While he wore crimson pantaloons and a sable shako in a dragoon regiment, he was not designed for the pulpit; but when his third brother—who was—to whom the benefice had been as naturally destined as the best goose to the Christmas table—sickened and died, Horace, with the adaptability of a man who can change uniforms without changing habits, resolved to exchange spurs for a white neckcloth. Camford, in its prudish way, would not have him: but Dr. Donne, Master of Boniface, was a Suffolk man; and Suffolk, like a big kitchen, will sometimes take in a questionable joint because the label on it is venerable, and because it once lay in the same larder as the cook’s own.

So Mr. Bloundell came up, and, without the least delay, sat down among the “select” as though he had been born at the High Table.

In Pen’s second year, Major Pendennis paid one of those brisk visits which are meant to admonish youth without interrupting it. He was introduced to Pen’s chosen companions: to gentle Lord Plinlimmon, whose politeness seemed hereditary; to gallant Magnus Charters, open as a door in summer; to sly Harland, who fired off Greek quotations as other men flick crumbs; to intrepid Ringwood—“Rupert” in the Union, because his opinions rode hard and his blunders rode harder; to Broadbent, dubbed “Barebones,” because he was republican and wore his dissenting shoes as if they, too, protested; and to Mr. Bloundell-Bloundell, who managed in five minutes to look as if the whole university had been waiting his arrival in order to become fashionable.

Major Pendennis did not understand Harland’s Greek, nor did he quite approve the grimy sincerity of Broadbent’s hands; but he was delighted with the circle round his nephew, and approved of everybody—except precisely the person who most approved of himself.

The morning after the party in Pen’s rooms, uncle and nephew sat at breakfast. The Major was in that fine humour which follows upon having told old stories to a young audience. In London, anecdotes are like yesterday’s fish—people sniff and push the plate away: at Oxbridge they come on like steaming new rolls, and the lads butter them with laughter. The Major had been listened to with that honest roar, that eager pleasure, that respectful gravity, which is the very music a practiced raconteur lives upon. Only once or twice, while the tale was being carved and handed round, Mr. Bloundell’s face took on a look as if the dish were beneath him and he had dined on it before.

Indeed, he had the audacity to interrupt; to correct a particular; and to give his own version, which, he said, he knew to be right because it had been “talked of openly at the Club” by persons of the vaguest and grandest description—So-and-so and T’other—who were present at the business. The young gentlemen stared. They did not yet understand that melancholy grace with which Major Pendennis could accept correction from a cub and thank him for it, as if receiving a favour instead of an impertinence.

When next they met, Bloundell spoke contemptuously of “old Pen;” said everybody knew old Pen; regular old trencherman at Gaunt House; notorious old bore; regular old fogey.

Major Pendennis, on his side, liked Mr. Bloundell not one whit; and these antipathies, like the smell of certain cheeses or the taste of certain wines, are commonly mutual. You may prove by every rule of logic that a dish is wholesome; but if the stomach turns, it turns. All the reasons in the world will not make us love Dr. Fell; and if we dislike him, we may take it as certain he is not composing sonnets upon our virtues.

So the Major, tapping his egg with that air of judicial delicacy with which he could condemn a man while praising a sauce, said:—

“Pen, my boy, your dinner went off a merveille; you did the honours very nicely; you carved well. I am glad you learned to carve. In most good houses it’s done on the sideboard now, but it remains an important point, and may aid you in middle life. Lord Plinlimmon is a very amiable young man; quite the image of his dear mother, Lady Aquila Brownbill, whom I knew. Lord Magnus’s republicanism will wear off: it sits prettily on a young patrician, though nothing is so loathsome among persons of our rank. Mr. Broadbent seems to have much eloquence and considerable reading. Your friend Foker is always delightful. But your acquaintance, Mr. Bloundell”—and here the Major’s spoon paused, as if it did not choose to travel further in such company—“struck me as in all respects a most ineligible young man.”

“Bless my soul, sir—Bloundell-Bloundell!” cried Pen, laughing, for names that repeat themselves always seemed to him to be twice as good as other names. “Why, he’s the most popular man of the university. We elected him of the Barmecides the first week he came up—had a special meeting on purpose. He’s of an excellent family—Suffolk Bloundells—descended from Richard’s Blondel; bears a harp in chief—and motto O Mong Roy.”

“A man may have a very good coat-of-arms and be a tiger, my boy,” the Major said, chipping his egg with a firmness that suggested he would gladly have chipped the subject of discourse instead. “That man is a tiger. A low man. I will lay a wager he left his regiment, which was a good one (for a more respectable man than my friend Lord Martingale never sat in a saddle), in bad odour. There is the unmistakable look of slang and bad habits about him. He frequents low gambling-houses and billiard-hells—haunts third-rate clubs. I know it by his style. I never was mistaken in my man yet. Did you remark the quantity of rings and jewellery he wore? That person has Scamp written on his countenance, if any man ever had. Mark my words and avoid him. Let us turn the conversation. The dinner was a leetle too fine—but I don’t object to your making a few extra frais when you receive friends. Of course you don’t do it often, and only those whom it is your interest to fêter. The cutlets were excellent; and the soufflé uncommonly light and good. The third bottle of champagne was not necessary; but you have a good income, and as long as you keep within it I shall not quarrel with you, my dear boy.”

Poor Pen! The worthy uncle little knew how often those dinners occurred—how frequently the young Amphitryon, eager to be thought a judge of everything that ever simmered or sparkled, delighted to show hospitality and skill in gourmandise. There is no art (so long to learn, so difficult to acquire, so impossible to practice in perfection without a purse as well as a palate) about which boys are more anxious to wear an air of knowingness. They will sniff a claret as if it were a cousin, and discuss a salmi as if they had raised the bird and written the recipe in their own dialect. The cookery and the wine are to them what a moustache is to a raw ensign—a sign of manhood, whether earned or glued on.

Pen, in his character of Admirable Crichton, felt it necessary to be great in dinners; and the old college-cook (who could talk of capons and gravies with the same tenderness with which an agrarian elder speaks of weather and wheat) respected him for it, and trusted him—blindly, fatally—because Pen praised the crispness of the skin, the perfume of the herbs, the clean crack of the crust, with such honest delight. The kitchen loved a young gentleman who could hear, even through the din of plates, the soft approving murmur a good sauce makes when it is properly finished with butter, and who could smell, in imagination, the thyme on the hill and the cellar’s cool corked darkness in a bottle of wine.

But appetite is a creditor that never signs a receipt. In the third year of the lad’s residence at Oxbridge, his staircase was no longer besieged by dish-covers and desserts and cheerful waiters bearing in triumph hot metal and cold glass. Instead, crowds of different sorts of attendants—messengers with faces sulky or piteous—hung about the outer oak, and assailed the unfortunate lad when he issued from his den, as if he were a rare bird to be plucked for payment.

Nor did his guardian’s advice take any effect, or induce Mr. Pen to avoid the society of the disreputable Mr. Bloundell. Young men love in companions what got Pen himself half his reputation—a real or supposed knowledge of life. A man who can speak of the world with a knowing air; a Lovelace with adventures; a roue with a story ready at the end of his cigar—is sure of an admiring audience among boys. It is hard to confess; but so it is. We are taught early—by books, by banter, by the very language of our praises—to admire that sort of prowess; and the lexicon of a school is often a tyrant, crowning the wrong kings. The thought should make the man tremble who holds the pen, lest untruth, impurity, unjust anger, or unjust praise escape it; for words travel further than men, and outlive them; and a fashionable phrase may do more mischief than a fashionable vice.

One such diseased creature is enough to infect a whole colony. The tutors of Boniface began to find the moral tone of their college lowered and their young men growing unruly—almost ungentlemanlike—soon after Mr. Bloundell’s arrival. The young magnates of neighbouring St. George’s, in whose society Pen lived, were not taken in by Bloundell’s flashy graces and rakish airs of fashion. Broadbent called him Captain Macheath, and said he would live to be hanged. Foker, during his brief stay at the university with Macheath, declined, with characteristic caution, to say anything openly in the Captain’s disfavour; but hinted to Pen that it was better to have him for a partner at whist than play against him, and better to back him at écarté than bet on the other side.

“You see, he plays better than you do, Pen,” was Foker’s astute remark. “He plays uncommon well, the Captain does; and Pen, I wouldn’t take the odds too freely from him, if I was you. I don’t think he’s too flush of money, the Captain ain’t.”

Beyond these dark suggestions and generalities, the cautious Foker could not be got to speak.

Not that his advice would have weighed much with a headstrong young man. Pen’s appetite for pleasure was insatiable; he rushed at it wherever it presented itself with an eagerness that bespoke his fiery constitution and youthful health. He called it “seeing life,” and quoted Terence and Horace and Shakspeare to show that one should do all that might become a man—taking the maxims, as hungry lads take mustard, not because it is nourishing, but because it bites.

One night, after a supper-party in college at which Pen and Macheath had been present, and at which a little quiet vingt-et-un had been played—an amusement much pleasanter to men in their second and third year than the old boisterous custom of singing songs and bringing the proctors thumping to the door, every man having expended his budget of choruses long ago—when the caps were being taken up and the guests were going, after no great losses or winnings, Mr. Bloundell playfully picked up a green wine-glass from the supper-table. It had been destined for iced cup—that innocent, fragrant compromise of lemon-peel and sugar and chill. But into it he inserted something far more pernicious, namely a pair of dice, which he took from his waistcoat-pocket as neatly as if producing a lozenge.

Then, giving the glass a graceful wave that showed his hand was well acquainted with the profession of throwing, he called sevens the main, whisked the ivory cubes gently on the table, swept them up lightly again, and repeated the process two or three times. The other men looked on—Pen among them—who had never rattled dice yet, except in the humdrum domestic piety of backgammon at home, with the fire singing and the kettle keeping time.

Mr. Bloundell, who had a good voice, began to troll out the chorus from Robert the Devil, then in great vogue; many joined, and especially Pen, who was in very high spirits, having won a good handful of shillings and half-crowns at the vingt-et-un. Presently, instead of going home, most of the party were seated round the table playing at dice, the green glass going round from hand to hand—until Pen, after throwing six mains, finally shivered it.

From that night Pen plunged into hazard as eagerly as it was his custom to pursue any new pleasure. Dice can be played in the morning as well as after dinner or supper; sin is not a bird that only sings at dusk. Bloundell would come into Pen’s rooms after breakfast, and it was astonishing how quick the time passed while the bones were rattling, or rather, while they ought to have rattled. For they had little quiet parties with the doors closed, and Bloundell devised a box lined with felt, so that the dice should make no noise and their tell-tale music not bring the sharp-eared tutors padding up the staircase.

Once, indeed, Bloundell, Ringwood, and Pen were very nearly caught by Mr. Buck, who, passing in the quadrangle, thought he heard through Pen’s open window the words “Two to one on the caster.” But when the tutor got into Arthur’s rooms, he found the lads with three Homers before them, and Pen, with great gravity, said he was trying to coach the other two, and asked Mr. Buck what was the present condition of the River Scamander, and whether it was navigable or no. Thus did scholarship become a felt box to smother noise; and the old languages—those precious dialects that carry whole worlds of feeling—were used as a screen for modern folly, the meaning of Homer turned into mere vocabulary for evasion.

Mr. Arthur Pendennis did not win much money in these transactions with Mr. Bloundell, nor indeed gain good of any kind except a knowledge of the odds at hazard, which he might have learned out of books without losing his character into the bargain.

Captain Macheath had other accomplishments which he exercised for Pen’s benefit. His stories had a great and unfortunate charm for Arthur, who was never tired of hearing histories of garrison conquests and feats in country-quarters—tales delivered with that easy club-air by which the cheapened word “world” is made to seem a passport. He had been at Paris, and had plenty of legends about the Palais Royal and the Salon and Frascati’s.

“I went to the Salon one night,” said he, after a dinner at the Café de Paris, “when we were all devilishly cut, by Jove; and on waking in the morning in my own rooms, I found myself with twelve thousand francs under my pillow, and a hundred and forty-nine Napoleons in one of my boots. Wasn’t that a coup, hay?”

Pen’s eyes glistened as he heard. He respected the man who could win such a sum; and sighed that it would set him all right. Macheath laughed, and told him to drink another drop of Maraschino—that syrupy cherry cordial, perfumed and treacherous, which makes virtue seem a little dull and debt a little distant.

“I could tell you stories much more wonderful than that,” he added; and so indeed the Captain could have done, without further trouble than invention, with which portion of the poetic faculty Nature had copiously endowed him.

He laughed to scorn Pen’s love for Miss Fotheringay, when he came to hear of that amour from Arthur—pretty soon, for Pen was not averse to telling the story now to confidential friends, and he and they were rather proud of the transaction. But Macheath took away all Pen’s conceit on this head—not by showing the folly of the lad’s passion for an uneducated woman much his senior, but by exposing, with a sneer, Pen’s absurd desire of gratifying his passion in a legitimate way.

“Marry her?” said he. “You might as well marry ——,” and he named one of the most notorious actresses on the stage.

“She hadn’t a shred of a character.” He knew twenty men openly her admirers; named them; named the sums each had spent.

No kind of calumny is more frightful or frequent than this which takes away the character of women; no men more reckless and mischievous than those who lightly use it; and no cowards more despicable than the people who invent such slanders. They coin a word and pass it; and the world, with its lazy ear, hears the jingle and calls it truth. So the dominant lexicon—those ready-made labels of “pure” and “lost,” so convenient to men—does the work of power without a blow.

Is it, or is it not, a misfortune that a man of candid disposition, disposed like Pen to blurt out the truth, begins life by believing all that is said to him? Would it be better for a lad to be less trustful and so less honest? It requires no small experience to know that a man, without especial reason, is telling you lies. At all events, Pen had a natural credulity which enabled him to accept all statements made to him; and he took every one of Captain Macheath’s figments as if they had been unquestioned history.

So Bloundell’s account about Miss Fotheringay pained and mortified Pen exceedingly. If he had been ashamed of his passion before, what were his feelings now, when the object of so much pure flame and adoration turned out, by this fellow’s easy assertion, to be only a worthless impostor—an impostor detected by all but him? It never occurred to Pen to doubt the fact, or to question whether the stories of a man who never spoke well of any woman were likely to be true.

One Easter vacation, when Pen announced to his mother and uncle his intention not to go down, but stay at Oxbridge and read, Mr. Pen was nevertheless induced to take a brief visit to London in company with his friend Mr. Bloundell. They put up at a hotel in Covent Garden, where Bloundell had a “tick,” as he called it; and took the pleasures of the town very freely after the wont of young university men.

Bloundell still belonged to a military club, whither he took Pen to dine once or twice. The young men would drive thither in a cab, trembling lest they should meet Major Pendennis on his beat in Pall Mall, as if the Major were a moral constable and they were smuggling a barrel of contraband champagne. At the club Pen was introduced to a number of gallant young fellows with spurs and mustachios, who drank pale-ale of mornings and beat the town of nights; and here he saw “life” indeed. The roaring places they frequented—theatres and singing-houses—were of a sort where a guardian with the Major’s sense of decorum was not likely to appear.

One night, nevertheless, they were very near. A plank only separated Pen, who was in the boxes of the Museum Theatre, from the Major, who was in Lord Steyne’s box with that venerated nobleman. On the stage, Miss Fotheringay was in the pride of her glory. She had made a hit: had drawn good houses for nearly a year; had starred the provinces with great éclat; had returned to London with somewhat diminished lustre; and now was acting with “ever increasing attraction,” and “triumph of the good old British drama,” as the play-bills vowed, to houses where there was plenty of room for anybody who wanted to see her.

It was not the first time Pen had seen her since that memorable day at Chatteris when they parted. In the previous year, when the town made much of her and the press lauded her beauty, Pen had found a pretext for coming up in term-time, and rushed to the theatre to see his old flame. He recollected it rather than renewed it. He remembered how ardently he used to watch at Chatteris for the speech before Ophelia’s entrance—how the mere preparation on the actor’s lips used to set his heart listening, as if love were an acoustic creature that lived in the pause before applause.

Now, as the actor spoke, he had but a feeble thrill. As the house thundered, and Ophelia entered with her old bow and sweeping curtsey, Pen felt a slight shock and blushed very much as he looked at her, and could not help thinking the whole house regarded him. He hardly heard her in the first part of the play; he thought with rage of the humiliation she had caused him; and began to fancy he was jealous and in love still.

But that illusion did not last. He ran round to the stage-door, eager to see her if possible, and did not succeed. She passed indeed under his nose with a female companion; but he did not know her—nor did she recognise him. The next night he came in late and stayed quietly for the afterpiece. And on the third and last night of his stay in London—why, Taglioni was to dance at the Opera—Taglioni!—and there was to be Don Giovanni, which he admired of all things in the world: so Mr. Pen went to Don Giovanni and Taglioni.

This time the illusion about her was quite gone. She was not less handsome, but she was not the same, somehow. The light was gone out of her eyes—or else Pen’s no longer were dazzled by it. The rich voice spoke as of old, yet did not make his bosom thrill as formerly. He thought he could recognise the brogue underneath; the accents seemed coarse and false. It annoyed him to hear the same emphasis on the same words, only uttered louder: worse than that, it annoyed him to think he should ever have mistaken loud imitation for genius, or melted at mechanical sobs and sighs. He felt it was another life, another man who had loved her so madly. He was ashamed and bitterly humiliated, and very lonely. Fine dreams are sometimes better than dismal waking; the imagination seasons the plainest fare, and truth can taste stale.

They went and had an uproarious supper that night—hot meats, sharp pickles, the greasy comfort of late-hour dishes, and wine that talked louder and louder as the candles burned down; and Mr. Pen had a fine headache next morning, with which he went back to Oxbridge, having spent all his ready money.

As all this narrative is taken from Pen’s own confessions, the reader may be assured of the truth of every word of it; and as Pen himself never had any accurate notion of the manner in which he spent his money, and plunged into deeper pecuniary difficulties during his luckless residence at the University, it is impossible to give any precise account of his involvements beyond the general notion already sketched.

He does not speak too hardly of the roguery of university tradesmen, or of those in London whom he honoured with his patronage at the outset of his career. Even Finch, the money-lender to whom Bloundell introduced him, and with whom he had various transactions in which the young rascal’s signature appeared upon stamped paper, treated him, according to Pen’s own account, with forbearance, and never mulcted him of more than a hundred per cent. The old college-cook—his fervent admirer—made him a private bill, offered to send him dinners up to the very last, and never would have pressed his account to his dying day. There was a kindness and frankness about Arthur Pendennis which won most people he came near; and which, if it rendered him an easy prey to rogues, got him perhaps more goodwill than he merited from many honest men. It was impossible to resist his good nature; or, even in his worst moments, not to hope for his rescue from utter ruin.

At the very height of his university pleasure, he would leave the gayest party to go sit with a sick friend. He never knew the difference between small and great in the treatment of acquaintances; however much his sumptuous tastes led him to prefer good society, he was only too ready to share his guinea with a poor friend; and when he got money had an irresistible propensity for paying, which he never could conquer through life.
