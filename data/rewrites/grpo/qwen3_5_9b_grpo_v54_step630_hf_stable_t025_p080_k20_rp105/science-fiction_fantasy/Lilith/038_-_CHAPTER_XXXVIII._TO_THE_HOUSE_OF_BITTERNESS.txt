The sun rose pale and thin over the wastes, and we pressed forward toward the forest with desperate speed. I rode Lona's horse, the beast I had stolen from her, while I carried her corpse wrapped in her own cloak. My mind was a storm of conflicting duties. I intended to deliver Lona's body to her father, hoping he would grant her a proper resting place in a chamber of the dead. If he refused, or if he deemed her unworthy since she had not come willingly, I would leave her to rot in the desert sands, a final testament to her rebellion. But I dared not hope for refusal; she had died long ago, and the weight of her death demanded a humble penance from me.

And then there was Lilith. To Adam, I owed the return of his wife. I possessed no power to force her repentance, no moral authority to slay her, and certainly no right to remain her eternal jailer. Yet, here she was, bound and broken, trailing behind us.

On the road, I offered her food again and again, but she met my kindness only with a glare of hungry hatred. Her eyes, fiery and unblinking, rolled restlessly until we crossed the scorching stream. Afterward, they remained sealed shut until we finally approached the House of Bitterness.

That evening, as we camped, a small child wandered toward Lilith. Instinctively, I lunged to intervene, but it was too late. The child had pressed something to the princess's lips and recoiled with a scream of agony.

"Please, king," the child whimpered, holding up a tiny, bleeding finger. "Suck finger. Bad giantess make hole in it!"

I leaned down and sucked the wound clean. The child's name was Luva. A moment later, she offered another fruit to a mouth that craved sustenance, but Lilith snatched her hand away, dropping the offering to the dust.

The following day, we crossed the hot stream once more. On their own territory, the Little Ones—the children of the forest—were jubilant. Their nests lay far ahead, so we halted at the Ivy-Hall, drawn by the scent of ripe grapes. Seeing the great clusters, the children rushed forward, feasting eagerly until exhaustion claimed them. They slept soundly on the green floor and scattered through the surrounding woods. Hoping to witness the nightly dance of the spirits, I cleared a wide space in the center of the hall. I lay down among the sleeping children, Lona's body beside me, though sleep eluded me.

Night fell, and suddenly the company arrived. I wondered if these spectral dancers would perform eternally, perhaps forcing me to join them in my stubbornness, when the children's eyes snapped open. They leaped to their feet, grabbing the dancers' hands and pulling them into a chaotic, joyful chase. The spectres seemed to welcome the interruption; perhaps they remembered their own lost childhoods. The children's innocent gambols brought a strange refreshment to weary souls who possessed neither present nor future, living only on the shadow of a vanished past. Their pranks were never rude, and though they occasionally disrupted the rhythm, the ghosts showed no annoyance, for they had nothing left to smile with.

Just as dawn began to bleed into the sky, I saw the skeleton-princess standing in the doorway. Her eyes were open and glowing, the black spot of decay marking her side. She hesitated, then glided inward as if to join the dance. I sprang up, and a cry of repugnant fear erupted from the children, causing the lights to vanish. The moon peered in, revealing the children clinging to one another in terror. The ghosts were gone, or at least invisible. The princess had disappeared too. I darted to where she had stood; she lay motionless, eyes closed, as if she had never moved. Returning to the hall, I found the Little Ones already settling down for sleep.

The next morning, we spotted two skeletons moving through a nearby thicket. The children broke formation and ran to them. Even though I walked freely now, without splints or ligatures, I recognized the pair. The children made friends instantly, stroking the long bones of their fingers. The skeletons seemed to welcome the touch, drawn together by their shared deprivation. Seeing them gather herbs to rub onto their bones, the Little Ones collected similar plants and filled the outstretched skeletal hands. They promised to visit again before resuming their journey, marveling that such kind people lived in the forest.

At the nest-village, I stayed the night to ensure the children were settled safely. Lona still appeared lifeless, and there was no rush. But the princess had eaten nothing, her eyes remaining shut. Fearing she might die before we reached our destination, I woke in the night and placed my bare arm against her lips. She bit down with ferocious strength, and I cried out in pain. I barely escaped her grasp, waking to find myself lying beyond her reach. Morning broke, and I immediately prepared to depart.

I selected twelve of the sweetest, merriest Little Ones, mounting them on six elephants, and assigned two of the wise Clumsies to carry the princess. I rode Lona's horse, dragging her body before me. I chose the direct route across the left branch of the riverbed to the House of Bitterness, hoping to learn how to navigate the broader, rougher branch and avoid the basin of monsters. I dreaded the terrain for the elephants and the danger for the children.

The third night in the desert proved terrible. We stopped the elephants in a sheltered spot, letting the princess slip down between them to lie on the sand. She seemed dead, yet I suspected otherwise. I lay a short distance away, keeping watch over both the corpse and the dangerous spirit. The moon hung half-way down the west, a pale, thoughtful orb mottling the desert with shadows. Suddenly, it was eclipsed, sending forth no light despite remaining visible. A thick, diaphanous film covered its beauty, looking troubled. As the film swept aside, I saw the jagged outline of a bat-like wing, torn and hooked.

A cold wind with a burning sting swept over us, and Lilith was upon me. Her hands were still bound, but with her teeth, she tore the cloak from my shoulder and drove her nails into my flesh. I lay paralyzed as life seemed to drain from me into her. Remembering my resolve, I struck her hand. She raised her head with a gurgling shriek, shivering violently. I flung her off and sprang to my feet.

She knelt, rocking back and forth. A second blast of hot-stinging cold enveloped us, and the moon shone clear again. Her face was gaunt and ghastly, smeared with blood.

"Down, devil!" I cried.

"Where are you taking me?" she asked, her voice a dull echo from a sepulchre.

"To your first husband," I answered.

"He will kill me!" she moaned.

"At least he will take you off my hands!"

"Give me my daughter!" she screamed, grinding her teeth.

"Never! Your doom is upon you at last!"

"Loose my hands for pity's sake! I am in torture. The cords are sunk in my flesh."

"I dare not. Lie down!" I commanded.

She threw herself on the ground like a log. The rest of the night passed in silence, and by morning, she appeared dead once more.

By evening, we saw the House of Bitterness. Immediately, one of the elephants approached my horse.

"Please, king, you are not going to that place?" whispered the Little One riding on its neck.

"Indeed I am! We are going to stay the night there," I replied.

"Oh, please, don't! That must be where the cat-woman lives!"

"If you had ever seen her, you would not call her by that name!"

"Nobody ever sees her: she has lost her face! Her head is back and side all round."

"She hides her face from dull, discontented people! Who taught you to call her the cat-woman?"

"I heard the bad giants call her so."

"What did they say about her?"

"That she had claws to her toes."

"It is not true. I know the lady. I spent a night at her house."

"But she MAY have claws to her toes! You might see her feet, and her claws be folded up inside their cushions!"

"Then perhaps you think that I have claws to my toes?"

"Oh, no; that can't be! You are good!"

"The giants might have told you so!" I pursued.

"We shouldn't believe them about you!"

"Are the giants good?"

"No; they love lying."

"Then why do you believe them about her? I know the lady is good; she cannot have claws."

"Please how do you know she is good?"

"How do you know I am good?"

I rode on, telling the others what I had said. When they caught up, I explained further.

"I would not take you to her house if I did not believe her good," I said.

"We know you would not," they answered.

"If I were to do something that frightened you--what would you say?"

"The beasts frightened us sometimes at first, but they never hurt us!" answered one.

"That was before we knew them!" added another.

"Just so!" I answered. "When you see the woman in that cottage, you will know that she is good. You may wonder at what she does, but she will always be good. I know her better than you know me. She will not hurt you,--or if she does,----"

"Ah, you are not sure about it, king dear! You think she MAY hurt us!"

"I am sure she will never be unkind to you, even if she do hurt you!"

They fell silent.

"I'm not afraid of being hurt--a little!--a good deal!" cried Odu. "But I should not like scratches in the dark! The giants say the cat-woman has claw-feet all over her house!"

"I am taking the princess to her," I said.

"Why?"

"Because she is her friend."

"How can she be good then?"

"Little Tumbledown is a friend of the princess," I answered, "so is Luva: I saw them both, more than once, trying to feed her with grapes!"

"Little Tumbledown is good! Luva is very good!"

"That is why they are her friends."

"Will the cat-woman--I mean the woman that isn't the cat-woman, and has no claws to her toes--give her grapes?"

"She is more likely to give her scratches!"

"Why?--You say she is her friend!"

"That is just why.--A friend is one who gives us what we need, and the princess is sorely in need of a terrible scratching."

They were silent again.

"If any of you are afraid," I said, "you may go home; I shall not prevent you. But I cannot take one with me who believes the giants rather than me, or one who will call a good lady the cat-woman!"

"Please, king," said one, "I'm so afraid of being afraid!"

"My boy," I answered, "there is no harm in being afraid. The only harm is in doing what Fear tells you. Fear is not your master! Laugh in his face and he will run away."

"There she is--in the door waiting for us!" cried one, covering his eyes.

"How ugly she is!" cried another, doing the same.

"You do not see her," I said; "her face is covered!"

"She has no face!" they answered.

"She has a very beautiful face. I saw it once.--It is indeed as beautiful as Lona's!" I added with a sigh.

"Then what makes her hide it?"

"I think I know:--anyhow, she has some good reason for it!"

"I don't like the cat-woman! she is frightful!"

"You cannot like, and you ought not to dislike what you have never seen.--Once more, you must not call her the cat-woman!"

"What are we to call her then, please?"

"Lady Mara."

"That is a pretty name!" said a girl; "I will call her 'lady Mara'; then perhaps she will show me her beautiful face!"

Mara, dressed and muffled in white, stood in the doorway to receive us.

"At last!" she said. "Lilith's hour has been long on the way, but it is come! Everything comes. Thousands of years have I waited--and not in vain!"

She came to me, took the princess from my arms, carried her into the house, and returned to take the bound woman. Lilith shuddered but made no resistance. The beasts lay down by the door. We followed our hostess, the Little Ones looking very grave. She laid the princess on a rough settle at one side of the room, unbound her, and turned to us.

"Mr. Vane," she said, "and you, Little Ones, I thank you! This woman would not yield to gentler measures; harder must have their turn. I must do what I can to make her repent!"

The pitiful-hearted Little Ones began to sob sorely.

"Will you hurt her very much, lady Mara?" said the girl who had spoken first, putting her warm little hand in mine.

"Yes; I am afraid I must; I fear she will make me!" answered Mara. "It would be cruel to hurt her too little. It would have all to be done again, only worse."

"May I stop with her?"

"No, my child. She loves no one, therefore she cannot be WITH any one. There is One who will be with her, but she will not be with Him."

"Will the shadow that came down the hill be with her?"

"The great Shadow will be in her, I fear, but he cannot be WITH her, or with any one. She will know I am beside her, but that will not comfort her."

"Will you scratch her very deep?" asked Odu, going near and putting his hand in hers. "Please, don't make the red juice come!"
