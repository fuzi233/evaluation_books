The sea crashed violently against the base of the cliff, driven by a brisk breeze, while the sun poured life onto the landscape beneath a mottled blue sky. In this peak of English weather, Trent—who had suffered a restless night—descended before eight o'clock to a secluded pool among the rocks, following directions previously given to him. He dove deep into the crystal-clear water. Swimming between massive grey boulders, he pushed outward into the churning surf, fighting briefly against a coastal current before returning to his rocky shelter, battered yet revitalized.

Ten minutes later, he was scaling the cliff once more. His mind, momentarily cleared of the heavy disgust he felt for the case at hand, began to organize his plans for the morning. Today was the day of the inquest, the day after his arrival. Since leaving the American on the road to Bishopsbridge, he had accomplished little more. In the afternoon, accompanied by Mr. Cupples, he had walked into town, purchased items at a chemist's, consulted privately with a photographer, dispatched a reply-paid telegram, and made inquiries at the telephone exchange. He had spoken sparingly to Mr. Cupples, who displayed no curiosity whatsoever, revealing nothing of his investigative findings or intended actions. Afterward, Trent wrote a lengthy dispatch for *The Record* and sent it via the newspaper's local representative. He dined with Mr. Cupples and spent the remainder of the evening in solitary meditation on the veranda.

As he climbed the cliff this morning, Trent reflected that he had never undertaken a case he disliked so profoundly, yet one that consumed him so completely. Contemplating it in the golden sunlight, the affair appeared increasingly malevolent and defiant. Every suspicion and fragment of knowledge that haunted his questing brain for hours, excluding sleep, now stood out sharply against the glorious light and air. Washed in body and spirit by the sea's fierce purity, he perceived more clearly the darkness of the guilt he believed existed and was more bitterly repelled by the motive he suspected. Yet, his zeal had awoken; the instinct of the hunter sharpened. He would neither slacken nor spare mercy; here, no compunction was needed. By the end of the day, he hoped, his net would be closed. He had work to do in the morning and awaited the answer to the telegram sent the previous day with vivid expectation, though little serious hope.

The path back to the hotel wound along the cliff's summit. Near a spot he had marked from the sea level, where the cliff face had long since crumbled away, Trent approached the edge. He looked down, hoping to witness the delicate dance of water washing over broken rock. But there was no rock below. Instead, a few feet down, a broad ledge emerged—a rough platform the size of a large room, thick with wiry grass and steep-walled on three sides. Close to the precipice, where the drop became sheer, sat a woman. Her arms encircled her drawn-up knees, her eyes fixed on the trailing smoke of a distant liner, her face lost in reverie.

To Trent, whose training demanded he live entirely through his eyes, she constituted the most beautiful picture he had ever witnessed. Her face possessed a southern pallor, kissed by the wind with color on her cheeks, presenting a profile of delicate regularity devoid of hardness. Nevertheless, her black brows bent downward toward a meeting point, giving her a look of severity in repose, strangely redeemed by the open curve of her mouth. She told herself that the merit of a lover writing sonnets to a mistress's eyebrow ultimately depended on the quality of that eyebrow. Her nose was straight and fine, exquisitely avoiding the fault of excessive length that often forces a conscientious mind to admire the tip-tilt. Her hat lay pinned to the grass beside her, while the lively breeze played with her thick dark hair, blowing back the two broad bandeaux that should have covered her forehead and agitating a hundred tiny curls from the mass gathered at her nape.

Everything about this lady was black—from her suede shoes to the discarded hat; lustreless black covered her to her bare throat. All she wore was fine and impeccably arranged. Despite the dreamy delicacy of her spirit, it was evident she was long-practiced in the oldest of arts: dressing well, possessing a primal joy in the excellence of a body so admirably curved in her pose of embraced knees. With a suggestion of French taste in her attire, she made a very modern figure seated there, until one looked at her face and saw the glow and triumph inherent in all vigorous beings facing sun, wind, and sea in the prime of youth. One sensed a womanhood so unmixed and vigorous, so unconsciously assured of itself, that it seemed scarcely English, still less American.

Trent, halting only a moment in surprise at seeing the woman in black, passed by on the cliff above her, perceiving and feeling the details set down before him. At all times, his keen vision and active brain absorbed details with a marvelous swiftness that baffled men of slower perception; he held that the need to stare was evidence of blindness. Now, the feeling of beauty was awakened and exultant, doubling the power of his senses. In these instants, a picture was imprinted on his memory that would never fade.

As he passed unheard on the turf, the woman, still alone with her thoughts, suddenly moved. She unclasped her long hands from her knees, stretching her limbs and body with feline grace, then slowly raised her head and extended her arms with open, curving fingers, as if to gather to her all the glory and overwhelming sanity of the morning. This was a gesture not to be mistaken: a gesture of freedom, the movement of a soul resolving to exist, to possess, to move forward, perhaps to enjoy. So he saw her for an instant as he passed, and he did not turn. He knew suddenly who the woman must be, and it was as if a curtain of gloom were drawn between him and the splendor of the day.

At breakfast, Mr. Cupples found Trent disinclined to speak, offering an excuse of a restless night. Conversely, Mr. Cupples was in a state of bird-like alertness. The prospect of the inquest enlivened him. He entertained Trent with a discourse on the history of the coroner's court, noting its enviable freedom from rigid rules and precedents. Transitioning to the upcoming case, he remarked, "Young Bunner mentioned to me last night the hypothesis he puts forward regarding the crime. A very remarkable young man, Trent. His meaning is occasionally obscure, but in my opinion, he possesses a clear-headed knowledge of the world quite unusual for one of his apparent age. Indeed, his promotion by Manderson to the position of his principal lieutenant speaks for itself. He seems to have assumed with perfect confidence the control, as he expresses it, of the complicated business situation caused by the death of his principal. He has advised wisely regarding the steps I should take on Mabel's behalf and the best course for her to pursue until the will is executed. Consequently, I was less disposed than I might otherwise have been to regard his suggestion of an industrial vendetta as far-fetched. When I questioned him, he described numerous cases where attacks of one sort or another—often successful—had been made upon individuals who incurred the hostility of powerful labor organizations. This is a terrible time in which we live, my dear boy. There is none recorded in history where the disproportion between the material and moral constituents of society has been so great or so menacing to the permanence of the social fabric. But nowhere, in my judgment, is the prospect darker than in the United States."

"I thought," said Trent listlessly, "that Puritanism was about as strong there as the money-getting craze."

"Your remark," answered Mr. Cupples, with as near an approach to humor as was possible, "is not a testimonial to what you call Puritanism—a convenient rather than accurate term; for I need not remind you that it was invented to describe an Anglican party aiming to purge the services and ritual of their Church from elements repugnant to them. The sense of your observation, however, remains sound, and its truth is extremely well illustrated by the case of Manderson himself, who, I believe, possessed the virtues of purity, abstinence, and self-restraint in their strongest forms. No, Trent, there are other and more worthy things among the moral constituents I spoke of; and in our finite nature, the more we preoccupy ourselves with the bewildering complexity of external apparatus which science places in our hands, the less vigor we have left for the development of the holier purposes of humanity within us. Agricultural machinery has abolished the festival of the Harvest Home. Mechanical travel has abolished the inn, or all that was best in it. I need not multiply instances. The view I am expressing to you," pursued Mr. Cupples, placidly buttering a piece of toast, "is regarded as fundamentally erroneous by many who think generally as I do about the deeper concerns of life, but I am nevertheless firmly persuaded of its truth."

"It needs epigrammatic expression," said Trent, rising from the table. "If only it could be crystallized into some handy formula, like 'No Popery' or 'Tax the Foreigner', you would find multitudes willing to go to the stake for it. But you were planning to go to White Gables before the inquest, I think. You ought to be off if you are to get back to the court in time. I have something to attend to there myself, so we might walk up together. I will just go and get my camera."
