It is not my purpose to delay the reader with the whole history of how these papers came to me. That point, if it matters at all, is the last I learned myself, and it may lie quietly until the close. Yet a word must be said at the outset, because the shape of what I possess explains the shape of what follows.

The bundle is, in part, what one would expect from a gentleman of the eighteen-forties who had conceived the project of “a volume of travel.” There are the beginnings of that agreeable, gossipy kind of book—half itinerary, half anecdote—in which the inn-parlour and the post-chaise are as important as the cathedral. There are also, as the papers advance, signs that the writer’s hand was drawn away from mere description and towards something narrower and more personal, until the record ceases to be a guide and becomes an account of a single experience, set down with an exactness which has the look of necessity.

The writer names himself: Mr Wraxall.

From his pages I collect that he was past middle age; that he had private means, though the management of them made demands upon him; that he lived for long stretches in hotels and boarding-houses, always on the brink of “settling,” never settled; and that he was alone in the world in the particular way in which a man may be surrounded by waiters and fellow-guests and yet have no one to speak to whose silence would matter. He had been, he hints, near a Fellowship at Oxford (Brasenose, if the scraps of allusion are to be trusted). He had once published a book on Brittany—very likely anonymously, for I have searched in the catalogues and cannot bring it to light. His fault, if it may be called so in a traveller, was curiosity; and it is a fault which in his case took on the force of appetite: once he had tasted a subject, he must needs have the whole dish, and would go on seeking flavour long after prudence would have put down the spoon.

In the early summer of 1863 he set out for Sweden, with the notion of writing another book—travel enlivened by episodes from the history of great families. In Stockholm he remained some weeks, and there, by the help of a learned resident, he was directed to a collection of family papers belonging to the owners of an old manor-house in Westergötland. Permission was obtained; introductions were written; and so, after the long and light days had established themselves, he came to the district and to the house which, for the sake of convenience, I shall call Råbäck.

It is not its true name. But any one who knows Dahlenberg’s great volume—_Suecia antiqua et moderna_—will recognize the place in the engraving of 1694, for the building stands now much as it stood then: red brick with stone facings, broad and sober in the manner of our own early seventeenth-century houses. It was built soon after 1600 by a member of the De la Gardie family, and his descendants possess it still. De la Gardie is the name I shall keep to.

They received Mr Wraxall with a courtesy which, even on paper, seems to have had warmth in it: that effortless, domestic kindness by which a stranger is made at once an expectation and an obligation. They pressed him to stay under their roof while he worked. But he preferred to be “independent”; he distrusted his Swedish in long conversation; and he installed himself at the village inn, a mile or so away, which proved comfortable enough during the summer.

That daily walk, repeated there and back, drew him into the country’s small and intimate geography. The manor-house stood in a park grown up with great old timber. Near it lay the walled garden, where the air was close with leaf-mould and the warm, bruised green smell of stems snapped for no reason but idleness. Beyond the garden one went into a strip of wood which fringed a little lake—one of many, for the land is pitted with them like a loaf with holes. Then came the demesne wall, and after it a steep rocky knoll lightly clothed in soil. On the top of this rose the church, fenced in with tall, dark trees whose branches took and held the sound: the place had that curious hush that comes when needles and leaves drink the wind, so that even your own footfall seems impertinent.

The church itself struck English eyes as strange. The nave and aisles were low, packed with pews and galleries. In the western gallery stood a handsome old organ, gaily painted, its silver pipes catching what light there was and giving it back coldly. The ceiling was flat and had been painted in the seventeenth century with a Last Judgement that was less theology than nightmare: lurid flames, falling cities, burning ships, brown demons smiling with an almost domestic satisfaction at the souls that cried and twisted. Brass coronae hung from the roof; the pulpit was like a carved doll’s-house overrun with little painted cherubs and saints; a threefold hour-glass stood hinged to the preacher’s desk, as if time itself were measured out in sermon-lengths.

But what distinguished the church at Råbäck was an addition to it. At the eastern end of the north aisle the builder of the manor-house had raised a mausoleum for himself and his family: an eight-sided building with oval windows, a domed roof, and above that a pumpkin-like swelling tapering into a spire—one of those shapes in which Swedish taste seems to have delighted. The copper roof was painted black; the walls, like the church’s, were startlingly white; and there was no access to it from within. Its portal, steps, and door were on the north side.

Past the churchyard the path ran down to the village, and within three or four minutes one stood at the inn door and smelled, under whatever cooking there was, the steady odour of old boards, tarred rope, and ale. There is a kind of life that repeats itself in those places—bread cut, plates scraped, shutters put up and down—so that a man may almost forget what day it is, and the house will still remember for him by habit.

On his first day, finding the church open, Mr Wraxall went in and made notes such as I have condensed. Into the mausoleum he could not force his way. He put his eye to the keyhole and could just descry the pale gleam of marble and the duller sheen of copper sarcophagi, with a lavishness of armorial ornament which made him eager to spend time there.

The family papers at the manor-house were precisely of the kind to feed his project. There were journals and correspondence and account-books from the earliest owners—neatly kept, clearly written, full of those small picturesque particulars which make a dead time seem as busy as one’s own. The first De la Gardie who appears in them—Count Magnus—stands out as a strong and capable man; and not a lenient one. Shortly after the building of the house there had been distress in the district, and peasants had risen and attacked châteaux. The owner of Råbäck had taken a leading part in suppressing the trouble, and the papers refer to executions of ringleaders, to severe punishments inflicted in the manor-yard with no sparing hand.

In the hall of the house hung a portrait of this Magnus de la Gardie, which Mr Wraxall studied after his day’s work. He does not describe it in detail, but the impression is clear: a face remarkable not for beauty, still less for goodness, but for force—indeed he notes that Count Magnus was almost phenomenally ugly.

That evening Mr Wraxall supped with the family and walked back late—late by the clock, though the northern summer still kept the light. He notes, as a man notes some small errand connected with household comfort:

“I must remember to ask the sexton if he can let me into the mausoleum at the church. He evidently has access to it himself, for I saw him tonight standing on the steps, and, as I thought, locking or unlocking the door.”

Early next morning he had talk with his landlord. The conversation is written down at length, and at first the minuteness surprised me; but it soon becomes plain that, in those opening pages, he is still gathering the chatty stuff of the travel book—innkeeper’s talk, peasants’ tales, those local judgements which can be set beside the description of a church ceiling or an organ-case. His purpose was to discover whether any tradition of Count Magnus lingered, and whether the popular estimate of him was favourable.

It was not.

If tenants came late to work on the days owed to their lord, they were set on the wooden horse, or flogged and branded in the manor-yard. There were stories—told with that careful vagueness which makes them difficult to contradict—of men who had occupied land encroaching on the demesne, and whose houses were mysteriously burnt on winter nights with the whole family inside.

Yet what most dwelt on the innkeeper’s mind—he returned to it, as a tongue returns to a sore tooth—was that Count Magnus had been on the Black Pilgrimage and had brought something, or someone, back with him.

Mr Wraxall asked, as any one would, what the Black Pilgrimage was. The landlord grew unwilling—held back, fenced, looked about as if the very boards had ears. Then he was called out for a moment and went with a too-ready alacrity, putting his head back in only to say he must go to Skara and would not return till evening.

So Mr Wraxall went to his work at the manor-house unsatisfied, and occupied himself with other matter. He was set that day among the letters of Sophia Albertina in Stockholm to her married cousin Ulrica Leonora at Råbäck (1705–1710), letters of exceptional interest for the light they throw upon the culture of the time. When he had done with these and returned their boxes to the shelf, he took down other volumes near them to choose his next day’s labour.

The shelf held mostly account-books in the first Count Magnus’s writing. But one volume among them was different: a compilation of alchemical and other tracts in a sixteenth-century hand. Mr Wraxall, not being deeply read in that literature, takes pains to set down names and beginnings—book of the Phoenix, book of the Thirty Words, book of the Toad, book of Miriam, _Turba philosophorum_, and so on—and then comes to the point which quickened him. On a leaf left blank near the middle, he found some writing of Count Magnus himself, headed:

_Liber nigræ peregrinationis._

Only a few lines had been copied; but enough to show that the landlord’s phrase was no new coinage, and that the belief was at least as old as Count Magnus, and very likely shared by him. Mr Wraxall translates thus:

“If any man desires to obtain a long life, if he would obtain a faithful messenger and see the blood of his enemies, it is necessary that he should first go into the city of Chorazin, and there salute the prince….”

Here one word had been erased, not very thoroughly, so that Mr Wraxall felt sure he read it as _aëris_—“of the air.” There was no more of the text, only the note:

_Quære reliqua hujus materiei inter secretiora._  
(Seek the rest of this matter among the more private things.)

It threw, he admits, a lurid light upon the Count’s tastes and beliefs. Yet to him, divided from Magnus by nearly three centuries, it served rather as spice than poison: it made the grim noble more picturesque. He spent some time in the hall looking up at the portrait, and then set off toward the inn with his mind full of Count Magnus, as if he had been reading a recipe and could still taste it on the tongue. Of the country he saw nothing: no evening sheen on the lake, no resinous scent of the woods, no shift in the wind among the treetops.

All at once he pulled up short and found himself at the churchyard gate, within minutes of his dinner. His eyes fell on the mausoleum—white walls, black roof, oval windows catching what remained of day like blind eyes.

“Ah,” he said aloud, “Count Magnus, there you are. I should dearly like to see you.”

“Like many solitary men,” he writes, “I have a habit of talking to myself aloud; and, unlike some of the Greek and Latin particles, I do not expect an answer. Certainly, and perhaps fortunately in this case, there was neither voice nor any that regarded: only the woman who, I suppose, was cleaning up the church, dropped some metallic object on the floor, whose clang startled me. Count Magnus, I think, sleeps sound enough.”

That evening, the landlord—who had heard Mr Wraxall say he wished to see the parish clerk or deacon—introduced him to that official in the inn parlour. The room had its accustomed domestic soundscape: the soft, repetitive scrape of a knife on wood, a chair-leg complaining, a fly rattling at the pane, and beneath all that the steady tick of a clock that seemed to insist on the ordinary passage of minutes even while strange subjects were spoken of. A visit to the tomb-house was arranged for the next day, and then there was a little general talk.

Mr Wraxall, remembering that Scandinavian deacons often teach candidates for Confirmation, chose to refresh his own memory on a Biblical point, as a man might turn to a familiar flavour to steady himself.

“Can you tell me,” he said, “anything about Chorazin?”

The deacon started—so Mr Wraxall notes the movement of his head and the quick change in his look—and then, with readiness, reminded him of the denunciation pronounced upon that village.

“To be sure,” said Mr Wraxall; “it is, I suppose, quite a ruin now?”

“So I expect,” replied the deacon. “I have heard some of our old priests say that Antichrist is to be born there; and there are tales—”

“Ah! what tales are those?” Mr Wraxall put in at once.

“Tales, I was going to say, which I have forgotten,” said the deacon; and soon after that he said good night.

Then the landlord was alone with Mr Wraxall; and Mr Wraxall’s curiosity, once roused, was not a polite thing.

“Herr Nielsen,” he said, “I have found out something about the Black Pilgrimage. You may as well tell me what you know. What did the Count bring back with him?”

Swedes are slow, perhaps; or perhaps the man’s reluctance made him slower. Mr Wraxall notes that the landlord looked at him at least a full minute before he spoke. Then he came close—as if the words could not cross even the short breadth of a table safely—and with effort said:

“Mr Wraxall, I can tell you this one little tale, and no more—not any more. You must not ask anything when I have done.

“In my grandfather’s time—that is, ninety-two years ago—there were two men who said: ‘The Count is dead; we do not care for him. We will go tonight and have a free hunt in his wood’—the long wood on the hill that you have seen behind Råbäck. Well, those that heard them say this, they said: ‘No, do not go; we are sure you will meet with persons walking who should not be walking. They should be resting, not walking.’ These men laughed. There were no forestmen to keep the wood, because no one wished to live there. The family were not here at the house. These men could do what they wished.

“Very well, they go to the wood that night. My grandfather was sitting here in this room. It was the summer, and a light night. With the window open, he could see out to the wood, and hear.

“So he sat there, and two or three men with him, and they listened. At first they hear nothing at all; then they hear someone—you know how far away it is—they hear someone scream, just as if the most inside part of his soul was twisted out of him. All of them in the room caught hold of each other, and they sat so for three-quarters of an hour. Then they hear someone else, only about three hundred ells off. They hear him laugh out loud: it was not one of those two men that laughed, and, indeed, they have all of them said that it was not any man at all. After that they hear a great door shut.

“Then, when it was just light with the sun, they all went to the priest. They said to him:

“‘Father, put on your gown and your ruff, and come to bury these men, Anders Bjornsen and Hans Thorbjorn.’

“You understand that they were sure these men were dead. So they went to the wood—my grandfather never forgot this. He said they were all like so many dead men themselves. The priest, too, he was in a white fear. He said when they came to him:

“‘I heard one cry in the night, and I heard one laugh afterwards. If I cannot forget that, I shall not be able to sleep again.’

“So they went to the wood, and they found these men on the edge of the wood. Hans Thorbjorn was standing with his back against a tree, and all the time he was pushing with his hands—pushing something away from him which was not there. So he was not dead. And they led him away, and took him to the house at Nyköping, and he died before the winter; but he went on pushing with his hands.

“Also Anders Bjornsen was there; but he was dead. And I tell you this about Anders Bjornsen, that he was once a beautiful man, but now his face was not there, because the flesh of it was sucked away off the bones. You understand that? My grandfather did not forget that.

“And they laid him on the bier which they brought, and they put a cloth over his head, and the priest walked before; and they began to sing the psalm for the dead as well as they could. So, as they were singing the end of the first verse, one fell down, who was carrying the head of the bier, and the others looked back, and they saw that the cloth had fallen off, and the eyes of Anders Bjornsen were looking up, because there was nothing to close over them. And this they could not bear. Therefore the priest laid the cloth upon him, and sent for a spade, and they buried him in that place.”

The tale, written down in Mr Wraxall’s plain hand, has a peculiar effect: it is made of sounds—scream, laughter, the shutting of a door—and it is the sounds, more than the sight of the poor man’s face, that seem to have gnawed at the hearers and to have been passed on like a family taste for bitterness. The wood on the hill becomes, in the telling, not merely a place but an instrument: a dark body that takes in human noise and gives back something else.

The next day the deacon called soon after breakfast and took Mr Wraxall to the church and the mausoleum. Mr Wraxall noticed that the key of the latter hung on a nail just by the pulpit. The church door, he had observed already, was commonly left unfastened; and it occurred to him at once how easy it would be to return alone if there proved to be more among the monuments than could be digested at a first visit.

The tomb-house, when he entered it, was not unimposing. The air had the dry, contained smell of stone shut away from weather, with a faint metallic coldness from the copper. The monuments—large erections of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries—were dignified if luxuriant. Heraldry and epitaphs crowded the walls in abundance. In the central space under the dome stood three copper sarcophagi, their surfaces engraved with fine ornament. Two had, as is common in those countries, a great metal crucifix upon the lid. The third—the Count Magnus, as it appeared—had instead a full-length effigy engraved upon it; and round the edge ran several bands showing scenes.

One band was a battle: cannon belching smoke, walled towns, troops of pikemen. Another showed an execution. In a third, among trees, a man was pictured running at full speed, hair flying, hands outstretched. After him followed a strange form. It was hard to tell whether the artist had meant a man and failed, or whether he had meant a monstrosity and succeeded. Considering the skill of the rest, Mr Wraxall inclined to the latter. The figure was unduly short, muffled in a hooded garment sweeping the ground; and from within that shelter there projected something not shaped like any hand or arm. Mr Wraxall compares it to the tentacle of a devil-fish.

“On seeing this,” he writes, “I said to myself, ‘This, then, which is evidently an allegorical representation of some kind—a fiend pursuing a hunted soul—may be the origin of the story of Count Magnus and his mysterious companion. Let us see how the huntsman is pictured: doubtless it will be a demon blowing his horn.’”

But there was no such sensational figure. Only the semblance of a cloaked man on a hillock, leaning on a stick, watching the hunt with an interest the engraver had tried to express in his posture.

Mr Wraxall also noted the three steel padlocks—massive and finely worked—which secured the Count’s sarcophagus. One of them lay detached on the pavement. Then, unwilling to delay the deacon or to waste his own working time, he went on to the manor-house.

“It is curious,” he wrote that evening, “how, on retracing a familiar path, one’s thoughts engross one to the absolute exclusion of surrounding objects. Tonight, for the second time, I had entirely failed to notice where I was going (I had planned a private visit to the tomb-house to copy the epitaphs), when I suddenly, as it were, awoke to consciousness, and found myself (as before) turning in at the churchyard gate, and, I believe, singing or chanting some such words as, ‘Are you awake, Count Magnus? Are you asleep, Count Magnus?’ and then something more which I have failed to recollect. It seemed to me that I must have been behaving in this nonsensical way for some time.”

He found the key where he expected it and returned alone to the mausoleum. He copied the greater part of what he wanted, staying until the light began to fail him at the windows. He notes, in the same matter-of-fact tone in which a man might remark that a latch does not catch:

“I must have been wrong in saying that one of the padlocks of my Count’s sarcophagus was unfastened; I see tonight that two are loose. I picked both up, and laid them carefully on the window-ledge, after trying unsuccessfully to close them. The remaining one is still firm, and, though I take it to be a spring lock, I cannot guess how it is opened. Had I succeeded in undoing it, I am almost afraid I should have taken the liberty of opening the sarcophagus. It is strange, the interest I feel in the personality of this, I fear, somewhat ferocious and grim old noble.”

The day following was, as it turned out, the last of Mr Wraxall’s stay at Råbäck. Letters reached him—those practical, unpoetic papers by which money makes itself felt—that made it desirable he should return to England. His work among the manuscripts was, in the main, done; travelling was slow; and he decided therefore to take his leave, set a few finishing touches to his notes, and be off.
