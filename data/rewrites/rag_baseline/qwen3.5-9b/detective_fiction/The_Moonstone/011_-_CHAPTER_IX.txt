June twenty-first dawned under a canopy of brooding clouds, the air thick with the unsettled promise of storm, yet by midday the heavens had cleared with a defiant brilliance. In the servants' hall, we commenced this annual rite of passage, presenting our modest tributes to Miss Rachel with the ceremonial gravity due a queen's court. Once more, I assumed the role of chief herald, delivering the speech mandated by tradition—a verbatim recitation of the Queen's parliamentary address, adapted for the kitchen. Like Her Majesty's proclamation, my annual oration is awaited with feverish anticipation, as if it were a revelation of unprecedented magnitude. Yet, once uttered, it reveals itself merely as a familiar refrain; the household grumbles briefly at the lack of novelty, only to pivot hopefully toward the prospect of innovation in the coming year. Such is the nature of an easily governed populace, whether in the halls of Westminster or the scullery.

Following breakfast, Mr. Franklin and I convened in private to discuss the Moonstone, for the appointed hour had arrived to transfer the gem from the safety of the Frizinghall bank into Miss Rachel's direct custody. Whether Mr. Franklin's agitation stemmed from a renewed, albeit rejected, suit of affection for his cousin, or whether his sleepless nights had exacerbated the erratic fissures in his psyche, remains unclear. Certain, however, is that on this auspicious morning, he displayed none of his customary composure. Within twenty minutes, he oscillated through a dozen conflicting resolutions regarding the Diamond. I, conversely, adhered rigidly to the empirical facts: no incident warranted alarming my lady concerning the jewel, and the legal imperative binding Mr. Franklin to deliver it to his cousin remained unalterable. Though he twisted and turned in his deliberations, logic eventually compelled him to align with my assessment. We finalized the arrangement for him to ride to Frizinghall post-luncheon, retrieving the Diamond accompanied by Mr. Godfrey and the two young ladies, presumably to ensure his safe return.

With this settled, Mr. Franklin departed for Miss Rachel. The remainder of the morning and a portion of the afternoon were consumed by the Sisyphean task of adorning the front door. Penelope assisted, mixing pigments according to strict direction, while my lady paced the periphery, handkerchief pressed to her nose against the acrid fumes of Mr. Franklin's paint, vainly attempting to dislodge the artists from their canvas. It was not until three o'clock that they removed their smocks, releasing Penelope—who bore the brunt of the chemical assault—and scrubbing the mess from their garments. Their labor was complete; the door, festooned with griffins, cupids, and convoluted floral devices, stood proudly transformed. Though the figures were undeniably beautiful, their sheer multitude, tangled entanglements, and topsy-turvy postures rendered them mentally exhausting, lingering in the observer's mind long after the visual pleasure had faded. Indeed, Penelope concluded her shift by vomiting in the back kitchen—a sacrifice I deem necessary for Art, even if the victim is my own daughter. Let creativity demand its toll!

Mr. Franklin seized a hurried bite from the luncheon table before mounting his horse to Frizinghall, ostensibly to escort his cousins, though privately known to himself and me, his mission was singular: to retrieve the Moonstone. Meanwhile, I assumed my station at the sideboard, commanding the dining service. With the wine secured and the staff reviewed, I retired to compose myself. A scent of lavender and a perusal of a favored volume steadied my body and mind. I was awakened from a reverie—not a nap, but a profound introspection—by the thunderous clatter of hooves outside. Stepping to the threshold, I welcomed a cavalcade comprising Mr. Franklin and his three cousins, attended by one of Old Mr. Ablewhite's grooms. Strikingly, Mr. Godfrey mirrored his cousin's demeanor; he lacked his usual buoyancy. He extended a polite handshake and expressed delight at finding me in robust health, yet a palpable shadow hung over him. When I inquired about his father's condition, he replied curtly, "Much as usual." Conversely, the two Miss Ablewhites radiated vitality sufficient to lift the spirits of twenty men. Nearly as tall as their brother, these spanking, golden-haired, rosy-cheeked creatures overflowed with superabundant flesh and blood, bursting from head to toe with vigor. The horses beneath them trembled under the weight, and when they dismounted without assistance, they bounced upon the earth as if sculpted from India rubber. Every utterance began with a resonant "O"; every action executed with a dramatic flourish; every slight provoked a giggle or scream. "Bouncers," I dubbed them.

Exploiting the cacophony of the young ladies, I approached Mr. Franklin in the hall. "Have you secured the Diamond, sir?" he nodded, tapping the breast pocket of his coat. "Have you sighted the Indians?" "Not a glimpse." Satisfied, he requested my lady, proceeding immediately to the small drawing-room. Moments later, the bell chimed, summoning Penelope to inform Miss Rachel that Mr. Franklin Blake desired her presence. Half an hour elapsed before I crossed the hall, only to be halted abruptly by a fresh eruption of screams from the drawing-room. Unalarmed, recognizing the signature "O" of the Miss Ablewhites, I entered ostensibly to inquire about dinner preparations.

There stood Miss Rachel at the table, transfixed, holding the Colonel's ill-fated Diamond. Flanking her, the two Bouncers knelt, devouring the gem with their eyes, shrieking with ecstatic delight at every new angle of reflected light. Opposite them, Mr. Godfrey clapped his hands like a delighted child, murmuring, "Exquisite! Exquisite!" Seated nearby, Mr. Franklin plucked nervously at his beard, casting anxious glances toward the window. There, framed by the glass, stood the object of his scrutiny: my lady, clutching the extract from the Colonel's Will, her back turned to the assembly. She faced me when I sought instructions, her brow furrowed in a familial frown, the corners of her mouth twitching with suppressed tension. "Come to my room in half an hour," she commanded. "I shall have something to say to you then." Departing, she carried the weight of a dilemma identical to that which had paralyzed Mr. Franklin and me at the Shivering Sand: Did the legacy of the Moonstone prove her brother's cruel injustice, or confirm his depravity beyond her darkest imaginings? These were grave questions for my lady to resolve, while her daughter, innocent of the Colonel's character, held the birthday gift in trembling hands.

Before I could withdraw, Miss Rachel, ever mindful of the old servant who witnessed her birth, detained me. "Look, Gabriel!" she cried, flashing the jewel before my eyes in a shaft of sunlight piercing the window. Good heavens! It *was* a diamond! Of size comparable to a plover's egg! The light streaming from it mimicked the glow of the harvest moon. Gazing downward into the stone, one descended into a yellow abyss that swallowed all peripheral vision, leaving only the gem's depth visible. It appeared unfathomable; this jewel, graspable between thumb and forefinger, seemed infinite as the heavens themselves. We positioned it in the sun, then extinguished the lights, and it blazed forth from its own luminosity, casting a spectral, moonlit gleam in the darkness. No wonder Miss Rachel was mesmerized; no wonder her cousins screamed. The Diamond claimed me utterly, and I erupted in a roar of "O" rivaling the Bouncers themselves. The sole exception was Mr. Godfrey. Placing an arm around each sister's waist, he surveyed the scene with compassionate detachment, remarking, "Carbon, Betteredge! Mere carbon, my good friend, after all!" Presumably aiming to instruct me, he merely reminded me of the impending dinner. I shuffled downstairs to my army of waiters. As I exited, Mr. Godfrey declared, "Dear old Betteredge, I have the truest regard for him!" While embracing his sisters and ogling Miss Rachel, he bestowed this token of affection upon me. A reservoir of love indeed to draw from! By comparison, Mr. Franklin resembled a savage.

Half an hour later, I presented myself in my lady's chamber. Our exchange largely mirrored the earlier conversation at the Shivering Sand, save that I carefully withheld information regarding the jugglers, deeming no justification present to alarm my lady. Upon receiving my dismissal, I observed she viewed the Colonel's motives in the darkest light possible, resolved to reclaim the Moonstone from her daughter at the earliest opportunity. Returning to my quarters, I encountered Mr. Franklin, who queried if I had seen his cousin Rachel. I reported seeing nothing. Could I locate cousin Godfrey? I admitted ignorance, though suspicion gnawed that Godfrey was likely near Rachel. Mr. Franklin's doubts aligned with mine; he pulled hard at his beard and retreated to the library, slamming the door with a resonance laden with ominous significance.

My preparations for the birthday dinner proceeded uninterrupted until the time arrived for me to groom myself for the reception. Just as I donned my white waistcoat, Penelope appeared at my dressing table, feigning to brush my sparse locks and adjust my cravat. High-spirited, she whispered, "News for you, father!
