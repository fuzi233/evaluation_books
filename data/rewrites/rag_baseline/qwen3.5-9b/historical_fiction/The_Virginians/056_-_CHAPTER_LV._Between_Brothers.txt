The air in the room was thick with the scent of tobacco and unresolved business. Harry and George sat facing one another, the silence between them filled only by the rustle of papers and the occasional clink of a glass. They had spoken of home, dissecting the landscape of their childhoods with the clinical precision of surgeons examining a corpse. Harry detailed the condition of his dogs, the health of little Dempster, and the enduring presence of Nathan. He spoke of Mountain, whose well-being seemed to hinge entirely on the weather, and Fanny, who had blossomed into a girl of considerable pretension.

Parson Broadbent's daughter, Polly, was engaged to Tom Barker of Savannah, a union destined to carry them to Georgia. Harry confessed, with a flush of shame, that he had once been infatuated with her, squandering pocket money at cards and consuming strong waters with the parson solely to secure proximity to the girl. Yet, the barrier of class remained impregnable; Madam Esmond would never permit him to throw himself away on Polly Broadbent.

George, conversely, described his wife, the widow of Colonel Washington. She was a woman of pleasing features and ample fortune, a prize he had successfully paraded before Madam Esmond in a display of rigid ceremonial courtesy. His mother had greeted the new wife with a politeness so stiff it bordered on the hostile. "Never mind, George," Mrs. Mountain had remarked, her mind already racing toward the next matrimonial arrangement. "The Colonel has taken another wife, but I feel certain that at one time two young gentlemen I know of ran a very near chance of having a tall stepfather six feet two in his boots." It was she, the relentless matchmaker, who had circulated the rumor concerning the Indian woman. Madam Esmond had dismissed the insinuation with scornful hauteur, comparing the idea of Mr. Esmond marrying a negro woman to the theft of silverware. Though she spared the woman, Biche, from direct accusation, her demeanor was so chillingly aloof that the Indian woman retreated to the servants' quarters, drowning her disappointment in alcohol.

George's account of his two months at home painted a portrait of isolation. "The birthright is mine, Harry," he said, his voice dropping. "But thou art the favourite, and God help me! I think my mother almost grudges it to me. Why should I have taken the pas, and preceded your worship into the world? Had you been the elder, you would have had the best cellar, and ridden the best nag, and been the most popular man in the country, whereas I have not a word to say for myself, and frighten people by my glum face." He lamented the inversion of natural order, invoking Shakespeare's curse: "O cursed spite, that ever I was born to set it right!"

Harry laughed, finding the sentiment theatrical. "Georgy, you are talking verses," he protested. "Somebody else talked those verses before me." George smiled, acknowledging the borrowed nature of his despair. Harry then recounted his pilgrimage to Tunbridge, where he had bowed to the authors of the books George revered, admitting he cared little for the literature itself but cherished the memory of his brother's admiration. "I say, dear, it's like a dream seeing you over again," Harry whispered, his voice catching. "Think of that bloody Indian with his knife at my George's head! I should like to give that Monsieur de Florac something for saving you—but I haven't got much now, only my little gold knee-buckles, and they ain't worth two guineas."

"You have got the half of what I have, child," George replied calmly. "And we'll divide as soon as I have paid the Frenchman."

At this, Harry erupted into a torrent of blessing and imprecation, his love manifesting as a violent need to assert his bond. He swore that no brother in the world compared to George. For days following his arrival, Harry's eyes tracked his sibling's movements. He would pause mid-meal, drop his knife, and laugh silently at the sight of George. Walking together on the Mall or in Hyde Park, Harry would gesture broadly at the passersby, presenting George as a miracle: "Look here, gentlemen! This is he. This is my brother, that was dead and is alive again! Can any man in Christendom produce such a brother as this?"

Harry insisted that George fulfill his promise to pay Museau's heirs. This demand had been a source of deep friction at home. With Museau dead, Madam Esmond had eagerly seized the opportunity to void the bargain, arguing that paying twelve thousand livres to peasant priests in Normandy—who would undoubtedly funnel the funds to Rome—was an act of wickedness. She refused to subsidize the wages of iniquity. While she acknowledged George's age and independence, she viewed the expenditure as a dangerous extravagance. Instead, she urged him to give the money to Harry, who had been stripped of his inheritance. The dispute had raged throughout George's final days in Virginia, remaining unresolved until the morning of his departure. Madam Esmond had visited his bedside, her face pale and tearless, asking if he still intended to waste his father's property. George, overwhelmed by grief and perplexity, had affirmed his oath of honor. Her final image of him was her standing by his bed, turning away slowly, leaving the room in silence.

"Where didst thou learn the art of winning over everybody to thy side, Harry?" George asked, shifting the focus to his own social failures. "How is it that you and all the world begin by being friends? Teach me a few lessons in popularity."

He recalled his initial reception in Richmond. As the sole survivor of Braddock's defeat, he had been a favorite, receiving private and public kindness. The parson had preached his escape; neighbors had visited; the family coach had been deployed. But his mother had routed off the admirers with prodigious haughtiness, extinguishing his popularity before the scandal of the Indian woman could fully take root. He was too somber for the officers' drinking bouts and dice games; too sarcastic for the ladies' tea and gossip. He felt profoundly lonely amidst the scandal, regretting his imprisonment and longing for the silent ease of Duquesne. He was shy, speaking unreservedly to few, sitting in utter silence before most. At home, Harry had been the talker, drawing smiles from their mother; with George, they shared no common ground save for disputes over law and divinity.

The gentlemen deemed him supercilious and dull; the ladies found him cold and enigmatic, unable to discern his sincerity. Their verdict was unanimous: he was a disagreeable fellow. This judgment hardened when the appearance of the Indian woman confirmed their suspicions. Boys hooted her in the streets; ladies dropped half-curtseys and crossed to the opposite side of the road. A clergyman preached sermons on the horrors of seduction and the lax principles imported from papist lands. Even the return of Fawn was interpreted as a calculated scheme between her and George. Officers bantered at the coffee house, cracking clumsy jokes about the woman he had chosen. Enraged, George considered retreating to Castlewood to live alone, seeking refuge in his aunt's ministry, until news of Harry's distress provided a pretext for his journey to Europe.

"I should like to see any of the infernal scoundrels who said word against you," Harry roared, pacing the room. "I had to do something similar for Bob Clubber."

"That little sneaking, backbiting, toad-eating wretch," Harry spat, describing the man who hung around Lord Greenway, subsisting on the charity of every gentleman in the country. "If you whipped him, I hope you whipped him well, George?"

"We were bound over to keep the peace," George explained. "I offered to go to Maryland with him to settle our differences, and the good folk remarked that, having made free with the seventh commandment, I was inclined to break the sixth. By this logic—and being as innocent of the crime imputed to me as you are—I left home with a reputation worse than any young gentleman ever earned."

There was a temptation here to moralize, to compile a memoir of the lies that surrounded them. The world knew everything about them, and far more besides. A recent critique had corrected the date of Mulligan's birth; another warned against mentioning New South Wales due to a brother residing there. These falsehoods formed a vast, noble tapestry of misinformation. Perhaps, George mused, a society of literati should collaborate on a series of biographies written by friends who did not know the subjects.

Having aired his grievances as a champion and martyr, Harry opened his own chest. He revealed the collapse of the Castlewood family dynamics. He had refused to aid the chaplain in his necessity, dismissing him with false pretexts. Regarding Mr. Will, he had made his judgment clear after the horse-dealing incident, marking his displeasure on the man's eyes and nose. His comments on the Countess and Lady Fanny were guarded but unfavorable; he had heard tales of a card-playing old cat and a desperate flirt, stories relayed by Maria herself, who had freely confided anecdotes about her stepmother and half-sister that were hardly flattering.

Yet, regarding Lady Maria, Harry was staunch and hearty. "It may be imprudent," he admitted, his voice trembling. "I may be a fool. I know there will be a dreadful piece of work at home, and that Madam and she will fight. Well! We must live apart.
