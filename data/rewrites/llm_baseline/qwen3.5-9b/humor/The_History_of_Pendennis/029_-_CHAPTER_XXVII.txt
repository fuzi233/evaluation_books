Arthur Pendennis decided to adopt the most grumpy expression possible, hiding behind the shadowy alcove of a window draped in cheap calico. From this vantage point, he watched Miss Bell attempt her maiden quadrille with Mr. Pynsent as her partner. Mr. Pynsent was a study in rigid propriety; he moved through the dance with the same solemn gravity one might expect of a man walking directly to his church pew, his face devoid of smiles and utterly immune to the festive atmosphere. Yet, Miss Laura Bell was a different story entirely. Beaming with joy, she seemed intoxicated by the lights, the crowd, and the music. As she swept across the floor in her white gown, her brown curls cascading over her rosy cheeks, she radiated such infectious happiness that several gentlemen couldn't help but stare. Even Lady Fogey, who treated the countryside as merely a temporary stopover before returning to her London mansion, leaned over to Lady Rockminster. "Do you know that young lady?" she asked, comparing Laura favorably to a reigning London beauty and declaring, "She'll do."

Lady Rockminster, armed with her magnifying glass, surveyed Laura with majestic indifference. She approved of the girl's natural charm and innocent demeanor, noting only that her arms were slightly red—a youthful imperfection easily overlooked. She contrasted this with Miss Blanche Amory, dancing opposite, whose manner was far too "pert" for her liking. Indeed, while Laura danced with genuine enthusiasm, Miss Blanche executed her moves with calculated flair. Her shoulders were impossibly white, her posture perpetually stiff, and her eyes darting around the room with predatory intent. Every movement seemed to scream: *Look at me! Not at that healthy country bumpkin, Miss Bell, who barely knows how to move without my instruction. This is the true Parisian style. Notice my feet, Mr. Pynsent. Notice them, Mr. Pendennis, lurking behind the curtain. I know you're dying to dance with me.*

Laura, meanwhile, kept a sharp eye on Pen in the window embrasure. He didn't leave his post until the second quadrille, when the obliging Lady Clavering summoned him to the dais of honor reserved for the dowagers. Pen approached, blushing furiously and acting as awkward as any vain young man, offering a haughty bow to Lady Rockminster, who barely acknowledged him, before paying his respects to the Widow Amory, resplendent in diamonds, velvet, and excessive millinery.

Next came Young Mr. Fogey, currently in the fifth form at Eton and eagerly anticipating his beard and a commission in the dragoons. He was captivated by Miss Bell. "I prefer you immensely to the French girl," he admitted candidly, referring to his previous partner, Miss Amory. Laura laughed, looking even more cheerful, and caught sight of Pen watching her with an absurdly pompous scowl. They shared a silent, mutual amusement.

The music shifted to a waltz. Mr. Fogey sighed, admitting he didn't know how to waltz and vowing to hire a teacher for the holidays. Then, Mr. Pynsent claimed Miss Bell's hand again. Pen watched in fury as she twirled around the room, her waist encircled by the stiff arm of the serious gentleman. This was a stark contrast to their summers at Clavering Park, where Pen, the Chevalier Strong, and the two young ladies often improvised lively balls. Back then, Laura's animation could even lift Mr. Pynsent's spirits.

However, tonight belonged to Miss Blanche, who was paired with Captain Broadfoot of the Dragoons. Despite his energetic efforts, the Captain lacked timing and musicality, moving too slowly. Consequently, Miss Blanche grew increasingly jealous of Laura's success. After a few turns with the heavy dragoon, she feigned exhaustion and returned to her mother's side, complaining to Pen about being abandoned to the "odious man in spurs and a red coat."

"I assumed spurs and scarlet were the most captivating sights for young ladies," Pen replied dryly. "I never dared compete my black coat with that magnificent red jacket."

"You are unkind, cruel, and sulky!" Miss Amory retorted, shrugging her shoulders. "Go away. Your cousin is glaring at us over Mr. Pynsent's shoulder."

When Pen finally asked if she would waltz, she declined, citing her current partner. "I'm promised, but I'll tell Mr. Poole I've forgotten our engagement," she offered, leaving Pen to mutter that women forget readily but always return with regret.

As the dances concluded, Laura approached Pen, extending her hand while Mr. Pynsent offered a stiff bow. "So glad to see you, dear Pen," she said kindly. "Three dances are over; I'm yours for the next."

"I've just engaged myself to Miss Amory," Pen announced. Miss Amory nodded politely. "Then he shall waltz with me, dear Blanche," Laura countered. "Won't you, Pen?"

"I promised Miss Amory," Pen stated firmly.

"Provoking!" Laura exclaimed, curtsying before slipping under the protective wing of Lady Rockminster. Pen, delighted by the chaos, leaned casually against the wall, chatting mockingly with Blanche. He ridiculed the heavy dragoons, the eccentric country dandies, and the bizarre hairstyles of the ladies—one resembling a bird's nest, another adorned with six pounds of grapes. "A coiffure of almonds and raisins," he quipped, suggesting it would serve better as dessert. His wit was biting and vivacious, keeping Blanche in stitches while ensuring Laura could clearly see and hear their intimate banter.

Across the room, separated only by Cornet Perch's shell-jacket, Blanche whispered to Laura, "Arthur is charming tonight."

"Who?" Laura asked.

"Arthur," Blanche replied in French. "Such a lovely name!"

During the interval, Pen asked Laura if she found Cornet Perch amusing, effectively planting two daggers in her gentle heart before returning to his teasing with Blanche. Laura, confused by Pen's sudden sulkiness but naturally forgiving, chose to enjoy herself with the talented Chevalier Strong instead. Meanwhile, Captain Broadfoot struggled with a large partner, Miss Roundle, a stout young woman in a strawberry-colored dress.

Enter Mr. Arthur Pendennis. Feeling triumphant, he whirled with Blanche, hoping to catch Laura's approving gaze, but she was too busy with Strong to notice. Their brilliance was short-lived. As they spun lightly, they collided violently with the lumbering Captain Broadfoot and Miss Roundle. The center of gravity vanished for all four dancers. Captain Broadfoot and Miss Roundle were toppled, and so was Pen. Unlike his partner, who landed safely on a bench, Pen sprawled flat on the floor amidst the general wreckage.

Captain Broadfoot, good-natured despite his weight, burst into laughter at his own misfortune. Miss Amory, however, was furious about her fall. Miss Roundle sat up, presenting a sight that drew inevitable giggles from the crowd. Pen, unable to tolerate ridicule, especially directed at himself, boiled with rage. He stormed away, clumsily apologizing to Miss Amory, blaming the "woman in pink," and asking if she was injured. Miss Amory, petulant, refused further dancing and graciously accepted a glass of water from a stranger wearing a blue ribbon and a three-pointed star. She thanked him, turned her shoulder haughtily toward Pen, and allowed the stranger to lead her away.

To Pen's astonishment, the man was none other than Mirobolant, the kitchen chef. Pen shouted, "By Jove, it's the cook!" Immediately realizing the insult, he wished he hadn't spoken. Blanche, oblivious, had simply invited the distinguished-looking foreigner to escort her. Mirobolant, hearing Pen's exclamation, glared back with a furious intensity that amused Pen. The chef, a proud Gascon accustomed to serving fine ladies in his homeland, felt deeply humiliated by the implication that he was beneath a lady's notice.

Chevalier Strong, aware of the chef's precarious mental state, stepped in. Speaking rapidly in Spanish, he convinced Mirobolant to step aside, promising the chef he would lose his job if he continued to walk with Blanche. The chef bowed profoundly and retreated, grinding a Gascon oath between his teeth.

Just then, a commotion erupted in the ballroom. Strong rushed out to investigate, leaving Blanche alone with the lingering mystery of the chef's reaction. The scene shifted to the refreshment room, a select area reserved for the elite, where Colonel Altamont, an officer in the service of the Nawab of Lucknow, had already commandeered a bottle of champagne. Dressed in a black wig and sporting military orders, the Colonel had charmed the staff and guests alike with his confident demeanor and constant jingling of sovereigns.

When Sir Francis Clavering and Mr. Wagg entered, the Colonel's odd behavior raised suspicions of drunkenness among the locals. Mr. Pynsent, ever eager to converse with everyone, approached the Colonel with grave deference, tasting a glass of "Sham" and praising its excellence. This exaggerated politeness baffled the Colonel, who muttered that Pynsent was a "rum one."
