"The human nature unto which I felt
That I belonged, and reverenced with love,
Was not a punctual presence, but a spirit
Diffused through time and space, with aid derived
Of evidence from monuments, erect,
Prostrate, or leaning toward their common rest
In earth, the widely scattered wreck sublime
Of vanished nations."
—WORDSWORTH: _The Prelude_.

Sir Hugo fulfilled his plan to spend the autumn at Diplow, and by early October his presence had injected a fresh cheer into the neighborhood, reaching from the stately halls of Brackenshaw and Quetcham down to the respectable shop-parlors of Wanchester. Sir Hugo was a man of affable display, a Liberal of noble lineage who trusted in reform without fearing it would disrupt the cherished English habit of adorning society with hereditary rank. Consequently, he transformed Diplow into a delightful gathering place, carefully curating his guest list to avoid mixing disparate social strata. He invited old solicitors and young curates alike, yet ensured they did not dine together as equals, lest the younger ones feel slighted. Easy-going Lord Brackenshaw might have welcomed the company of Robinson the attorney, but Robinson would have been naturally offended to be asked to mingle with those he considered his peers. Sir Hugo, keenly aware of these nuances, secured popularity for himself and pleasure for others—a dual result that perfectly suited his nature. The rector of Pennicote now found a reception at Diplow vastly different from the haughty tolerance he endured under Grandcourt. It was not that the baronet liked Mr. Gascoigne; rather, he sought a marked cordiality with him on behalf of Mrs. Grandcourt, for whom Sir Hugo's chivalry had grown increasingly devoted. Why? The chief reason remained a secret, one he would not share even with Lady Mallinger, demonstrating that his chivalry included a rare reticence regarding a woman's secrets.

After becoming engaged to Mirah, Deronda felt compelled to declare his position and purposes to Sir Hugo, choosing the letter as his medium. He sensed that his fatherly friend would feel some dissatisfaction, perhaps pain, at this turn of fate. Reading unwelcome news offered the advantage of avoiding a hasty, regrettable outburst; Deronda dreaded the verbal collision that could make pardonable feelings lastingly offensive.

Sir Hugo, though not entirely surprised, was thoroughly vexed. His immediate recourse was to show the letter to Lady Mallinger, expecting her astonishment to serve as a counterweight to his discontent. Indeed, when she expressed shock and distress that Daniel's wonderful talents and the comfort of his presence should end in such madness over the Jews, the baronet could dismiss her concerns. "Oh, nonsense, my dear! Depend upon it, Dan will not make a fool of himself. He has large notions about Judaism—political views which you can't understand. No fear but Dan will keep himself head uppermost." Yet regarding the prospective marriage, she offered no solace. The gentle lady admitted, without rancor, that she had little dreamed of the coming storm when Mirah sang at her musical parties. After hesitation, she confessed that it had crossed her mind that Daniel might eventually marry Mrs. Grandcourt, given his presence in Genoa at that precise time. Though she was not fond of widows, she conceded such a union might have been preferable to his total departure with the Jews. Sir Hugo, sharing this opinion so strongly, could not correct it as a feminine error; his ill-humor at the disproof of his conclusions regarding Gwendolen remained unventilated. He instructed Lady Mallinger to say nothing until further notice, reasoning that if it were an unkind blow to Gwendolen, the delay would be kinder in her nervous state, and she would learn the truth best from Daniel himself. Sir Hugo's conjectures, fueled by his knowledge, led him to believe he understood the entire situation.

Meanwhile, his residence at Diplow allowed him to continue his fatherly attentions to Gwendolen, a role Lady Mallinger was willing to support despite her reservations about widows. The move to Offendene had been executed, and Gwendolen settled there with a calm surpassing her mother's hopes. She experienced a peaceful melancholy born of renouncing demands for self and accepting the ordinary good of existence—even kindness from a dog—as a gift beyond expectation. Does one who has nearly perished in a pit of darkness complain of the sweet air and daylight? One learns to view daily life as an escape, taking the quiet return of dawn and evening, or the star-like glow of pure fellowship, as a salvation that reconciles us to hardship. Those possessing a self-knowledge prompting self-accusation, like Hamlet's, understand this habitual feeling of rescue. Gwendolen dwelt on every word of Deronda's that pointed to her past deliverance from the worst evil within herself and the infliction of it upon others, clinging to words that offered resistance to self-despair.

Yet she was also buoyed by the prospect of seeing him again. She did not imagine him otherwise than always within her reach; her supreme need blinded her to the separateness of his life, filling the entire scene of his existence with her own relation to him. This was not unique to Gwendolen, for we are all prone to this passionate egoism of imagination, extending even toward God. The future she faced with a willing step was one where she would continually assimilate herself to a type he would hold before her. Had he not first risen in her vision as a corrective presence, initially resented but finally loved and trusted? She could not spontaneously conceive of an end to that reliance, which had become to her imagination the firmness of the earth, the only condition of her walking.

Deronda was not long in arriving at Diplow, a more convenient distance from town than the Abbey. He had planned to take Ezra and Mirah to a mild coastal spot while preparing a new home for Mirah as his bride, where they might jointly watch over her brother. But Ezra begged not to be removed unless it were to accompany them to the East. Outward solicitations became a burden to him, yet his mind dwelt on the possibility of this voyage with visionary joy. In his preparations for a marriage he hoped to conclude within two months, Deronda wished to consult Sir Hugo regarding his resources and affairs, providing a reason for his visit. But he valued another reason more: his promise to Gwendolen. The sense of blessedness in his own lot still held an aching anxiety at his heart—a paradox, for the beloved lover is always called happy, and happiness is often viewed as indifference to sorrow outside it. Yet human experience is frequently paradoxical. It was no treason to Mirah, but part of his full nature, that his joy in her could hold beside it the care for another. For what is love itself, for the one we love best? An enfolding of immeasurable cares which yet are better than any joys outside our love.

Deronda visited Diplow twice and saw Gwendolen twice, yet he returned to town without telling her of the change in his lot and prospects. He blamed himself, but in all momentous communications likely to cause pain, one feels dependent on some preparatory turn of words or associations, some agreement of the other's mood with the probable effect of what is to be imparted. In the first interview, Gwendolen was so absorbed in what she had to say—questions about arranging her life, how to reduce her ignorance, how to be kindest to everyone and amend her selfishness—that Deronda utterly shrank from waiving her immediate needs to speak of himself, or inflicting a wound when she leaned on him for help. In the second interview, when he went with new resolve to steer the conversation, he found her in deep depression, overpowered by distasteful, miserable memories that seemed more real than any new material for her future. She cried hysterically, claiming he would always despise her. He could only offer soothing words, and when she gradually revived with that pathetic look of renewed childlike interest, it was impossible to lay another burden on her.

Time passed, and he felt a pressing duty to make the difficult disclosure. Gwendolen never recognized his having other affairs, nor had she ever asked why he happened to be in Genoa. This unconsciousness would make a sudden revelation of affairs determining his life course a heavier blow; if he left the revelation to others, she would feel he had treated her with cruel inconsiderateness. He could not communicate in writing; his tenderness could not bear the thought of her reading his virtual farewell in solitude, perhaps feeling his words full of hard gladness for himself and indifference for her. He descended to Diplow again, feeling that every other peril was preferable to returning and leaving her in ignorance.

On this third visit, Deronda found Hans Meyrick installed with his easel at Diplow, beginning a picture of three daughters on a bank in the "Gainsborough style," and varying his work by sketching village children at Pennicote to improve his acquaintance with the Gascoignes. Hans appeared to have recovered his vivacity, yet Deronda detected feigning in it, much like the artificiality of a lady's bloom that is too high-toned and persistently steady. Despite his grateful friendship and admiration for Deronda, Hans could not help a certain irritation, such as incautious, open natures feel when a friend's reserve breaks to reveal a state not merely unsuspected but the reverse of what had been hoped. Poor Hans had always cared chiefly to confide in Deronda, remaining incurious about any confidence given in return, yet what outpourer of his own affairs is not tempted to think any hint of his friend's affairs is an egotistic irrelevance? It was a sore reflection to Hans that while he had naively opened his heart about Mirah, Deronda had kept secret a feeling of rivalry that now revealed itself as the important determining fact. Friends turning out to be something more than we are aware of is always at our peril. Hans must be excused for these promptings of bruised sensibility, since he had not allowed them to govern his substantial conduct; he possessed the consciousness of having done right by his fortunate friend, or as he told himself, "his metal had given a better ring than he would have sworn to beforehand." Hans had always declared himself a _dilettante_ in virtue: very fond of it in others, but cutting a poor figure if he meddled with it himself. Perhaps in reward for his good behavior, he gave his tongue more freedom, too fully possessed by the notion of Deronda's happiness to conceive what Deronda was feeling about Gwendolen, so he spoke of her without hesitation.

"When did you come down, Hans?" Deronda asked, joining him in the grounds where he studied the requisite bank and trees.

"Oh, ten days ago; before the time Sir Hugo fixed. I ran down with Rex Gascoigne and stayed at the rectory a day or two. I'm up in all the gossip of these parts; I know the state of the wheelwright's interior, and assisted at an infant school examination. Sister Anna, with the good upper lip, escorted me, else I should have been mobbed by three urchins and an idiot, because of my long hair and a general appearance which departs from the Pennicote type of the beautiful. Altogether, the village is idyllic. Its only fault is a dark curate with broad shoulders and broad trousers who ought to have gone into the heavy drapery line. The Gascoignes are perfect—besides being related to the Vandyke duchess. I caught a glimpse of her in her black robes at a distance, though she doesn't show to visitors."

"She was not staying at the rectory?" Deronda asked.

"No; but I was taken to Offendene to see the old house, and as a consequence I saw the duchess' family. I suppose you have been there and know all about them?"

"Yes, I have been there," Deronda said quietly.

"A fine old place. An excellent setting for a widow with romantic fortunes. And she seems to have had several romances. I think I have found out that there was one between her and my friend Rex."

"Not long before her marriage, then?" Deronda asked, really interested, "for they had only been a year at Offendene. How came you to know anything of it?"

"Oh—not ignorant of what it is to be a miserable devil, I learn to gloat on the signs of misery in others. I found out that Rex never goes to Offendene, and has never seen the duchess since she came back; and Miss Gascoigne let fall something in our talk about charade-acting—for I went through some of my nonsense to please the young ones—something that proved to me that Rex was once hovering about his fair cousin close enough to get singed. I don't know what was her part in the affair. Perhaps the duke came in and carried her off. That is always the way when an exceptionally worthy young man forms an attachment. I understand now why Gascoigne talks of making the law his mistress and remaining a bachelor. But these are green resolves. Since the duke did not get himself drowned for your sake, it may turn out to be for my friend Rex's sake. Who knows?"

"Is it absolutely necessary that Mrs. Grandcourt should marry again?" Deronda asked, ready to add that Hans's success in constructing her fortunes hitherto had not warranted a new attempt.

"You monster!" retorted Hans, "do you want her to wear weeds for _you_ all her life—burn herself in perpetual suttee while you are alive and merry?"

Deronda could say nothing, but he looked so annoyed that Hans turned the current of his chat, and when alone, shrugged his shoulders slightly over the thought that there really had been some stronger feeling between Deronda and the duchess than Mirah would like to know. "Why didn't she fall in love with me?" Hans thought, laughing at himself. "She would have had no rivals. No woman ever wanted to discuss theology with me."

No wonder Deronda winced under that sort of joking with a whip-lash. It touched sensibilities already quivering with the anticipation of witnessing pain to which even Hans's light words seemed to give more reality: any recognition by another giving emphasis to the subject of our anxiety.
