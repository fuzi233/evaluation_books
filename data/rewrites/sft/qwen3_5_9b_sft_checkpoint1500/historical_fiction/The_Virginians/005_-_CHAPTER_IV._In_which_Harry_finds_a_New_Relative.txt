If one possessed kind neighbors, a respectable lineage, ample wealth, a comfortable home, a loyal household of servants, robust health, affectionate offspring, and a competent staff, surely one ought to be deemed happily blessed? Madam Esmond-Warrington, later known as Madam Esmond, possessed every blessing save contentment. She recited them in prayer twice daily, yet remained perpetually dissatisfied. To the country gentry of Westmoreland County, Virginia, she appeared as "Princess Pocahontas," reigning from her castle of Castlewood with a whimsical despotism. While the local poor bowed to her charity and the lesser gentlemen lost games of backgammon to her wit, the reality behind the throne was far starker. Having lost her husband early, she ruled alone, dominating her household with a rigid, uncompromising will. She believed obedience was a virtue and resistance a sin, often engaging in fierce battles with anyone who dared question her authority. Though her father, the Colonel, had once amused himself with her caprices, his passing left her unchecked, transforming her into a tyrant over her own domain.

Her reign was marked by a shift from the modesty of her youth to a flamboyant assertion of status. She discarded her maiden name, embracing "Madam Esmond" with pride, frequently invoking the lost titles of her ancestors bestowed by King James. While her half-brother, Lord Castlewood, might have claimed precedence, she reserved it solely for Lord Fairfax, the colony's highest civilian authority, and yielded gracefully only to the wife of the Governor, representing the Sovereign. Her relations with the English relatives of her husband, the Warringtons, were strained to the point of rupture. A single encounter in London, where gossip regarding her treatment of her kin reached the ears of the English aristocracy, caused correspondence to cease entirely. Letters sent to the Warringtons and the Hampshire Esmonds lay unanswered, a silence that fueled her fury and further isolated her within Virginia society.

The internal fractures at Castlewood ran deep, particularly concerning her two sons, George and Harry. The first rift opened over the dismissal of their former tutor, Mr. Dempster. Madam Esmond, suspicious of anything intellectual, viewed him as a dangerous Jesuit in disguise. Where George stood firmly by his old master, Harry, who preferred sport and nature to books, maintained a distance. However, the true storm broke over the inheritance of six thousand pounds due to Harry upon the death of his uncle. As the sole beneficiary, Harry was entitled to the sum, yet Madam Esmond, convinced she was its rightful owner, refused to release it. She raged against the London lawyers, calling them insolent pettifoggers, and threatened to buy estates and slaves to provide for her younger son, despite the legal impossibility.

When pressed, George sided with reason and the law, refusing to violate his oath as executor, while Madam Esmond saw only betrayal. Tears, accusations, and bitter pleas tore through the household. She lamented Harry's "slender younger brother's pittance," accusing George of selfishness, though he offered no defense other than love. This tragedy shifted the family's focus; the construction of grand halls ceased, imports of luxury furniture halted, and the family carriage, once a symbol of pride, became a vehicle for sorrow. During Sunday rides, the scene was one of poignant division. Madam Esmond, seated opposite her sons, alternated between groans for Harry's lack of fortune and grim glances at George's apparent indifference. Her companion, Mrs. Mountain, a pragmatic widow, gently challenged the mother's obsession, noting that the estate's needs did not require such frantic hoarding.

"You will give me plenty while you live, and George will give me plenty when you die," Harry declared cheerfully, attempting to lighten the mood.

"Not unless he changes in spirit, my dear!" the mother snapped, her gaze hardening on George. "Unless Heaven softens his heart and teaches him charity!"

Mrs. Mountain interjected with a knowing chuckle, suggesting the mother was already disturbing herself unnecessarily. "Humph! I know you are always disturbing yourself..." she began, but the tension escalated. When George, overwhelmed by his mother's tears, begged for a blessing, Harry stepped forward, wrapping his arms around his older brother's neck and kissing him repeatedly. "Never mind, George. I know whether you are a good brother or not," Harry assured him. "Don't mind what she says. She doesn't mean it."

"And so it is, Harry," Mrs. Mountain agreed, shaking the boy's hand. "You never said a truer word."

Enraged, Madam Esmond accused her companion of setting her children against one another, threatening to turn them out. Mrs. Mountain, undeterred, retorted that she would pack immediately rather than endure false flattery, declaring she brought little to Castlewood and would take little away. Before the argument could escalate further, the church bells rang. The widow, softened by the sight of her children sitting close together during the sermon, attempted to compose herself, looking with profound affection at both boys.

Later, the narrator recounted these tales to an elderly listener, Madam Bernstein, who found infinite amusement in the stories. They conversed in a wainscoted parlor adorned with portraits of the Esmond line. Above Madam Bernstein's chair hung a vibrant painting by Sir Godfrey Kneller of a young lady from Queen Anne's time—a vision of beauty with auburn hair and fair skin.

"Are you my cousin?" the boy asked, noticing the resemblance.

"That picture is by Sir Godfrey," Madam Bernstein explained, pointing to the canvas. "He thought himself the greatest painter, but he was surpassed by Lely, who painted your grandmother—my Lady Castlewood—and by Van Dyck, who painted your great-grandfather. Some of us are painted darker than we are. Does that portrait remind you of anyone?"

"No, indeed," Harry replied.

"Ahem!" sighed the lady. "Here is a sermon! Harry, that face was mine once. My name was Beatrix Esmond. And your mother is my half-sister, child, and she has never even mentioned my name!"
