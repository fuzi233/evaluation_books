The air in the dining room was still, a vacuum created by the absence of Lieutenant-Colonel James Wolfe. He had declined the invitation, preferring the company of Miss Lowther to the finest provisions prepared by Lord Wrotham's household. In his stead, the reader occupies the sixth chair. Across the table, the women appear radiant, their happiness a stark contrast to the recent turmoil involving Harry Warrington. Mrs. Lambert, having secured the release of her son through the intervention of her husband, Major-General Martin Lambert, sits with a fervor that borders on religious ecstasy. Her gratitude is not merely spoken; it is performed. When her husband enters late, bearing news of Harry's freedom and Theo's recovery, the domestic sphere erupts into a chaotic liturgy of tears and embraces. Mrs. Quiggett, the housekeeper, observes this display with a mixture of astonishment and amusement, her laughter sharp and metallic, echoing like the jingle of keys against old ribs. She notes the absurdity of the situation—a quarrel resolved instantly, a husband elevated to sainthood overnight. Yet, beneath the surface of this domestic bliss, a darker current flows.

After supper, the family gathers in prayer, a moment of solemn unity broken only by the arrival of George and Harry Warrington. They arrive from Aunt Bernstein's, unable to sleep without offering thanks. The atmosphere shifts from familial warmth to something more tense, more expectant. The General dismisses the trivialities of the evening meal, signaling that the purpose of this gathering is not sustenance but testimony. The cloth is removed. The servants withdraw. The room falls silent, save for the rustle of fabric and the shifting weight of bodies leaning forward.

George Esmond Warrington begins his statement. His voice carries the weight of memory, recounting a scene far removed from the cozy parlor where they sit. He speaks of the table of the General, of the Philadelphia agents who dismissed the dangers of the campaign with arrogant confidence. He describes the terrain: woods almost untrodden, roads forced through dense forests, a line of troops stretched thin and vulnerable. The General had scoffed at the threat of the savages, confident in the superiority of regular troops. But the reality of the battlefield was different. On that fatal ninth of July, the arrogance of command collided with the ferocity of ambush.

George recounts the chaos of the engagement. The initial cheer of the advancing column quickly curdled into terror as hidden rifles tore through the ranks. Men dropped faster than they could reload. The orderly formation disintegrated into a panicked retreat. Amidst the carnage, George, wounded in the leg, lay exposed. A hybrid figure, half-French, half-Indian, approached him with a clubbed musket, intent on killing and plundering. With a desperate grasp, George seized a fallen fusil and felled the attacker. But he was not safe. An Indian warrior, scalp in hand, advanced upon him, knife dripping. George was paralyzed, helpless, until a French officer emerged from the shadows.

It was Florac, his dueling partner from Quebec. Recognizing George amidst the blood and confusion, Florac intervened. He killed the Indian with a swift thrust of his sword, saving George's life. With pragmatic cruelty, Florac bound George's leg with his sash, disguised him in the clothes of a French volunteer, and urged him to feign allegiance to survive the rout. Before departing to face the collapsing British lines, Florac revealed a personal connection: a gambling debt owed by George, settled long ago. He stayed briefly, ensuring George was hidden among the trees, before rushing forward to join the flight.

As dusk fell, the pursuit ended. Florac returned with two Indians, claiming George was a wounded comrade. They carried him back to the fort, placing him in a blanket beside the officer's log-house. George lay on Florac's own bed, the pain of his wound acute, his consciousness fading in and out. The narrative hangs suspended here, the story of survival cut short by the darkness of the night, leaving the listener in the parlor to wonder at the cost of glory and the fragility of life in the shadow of empire.
