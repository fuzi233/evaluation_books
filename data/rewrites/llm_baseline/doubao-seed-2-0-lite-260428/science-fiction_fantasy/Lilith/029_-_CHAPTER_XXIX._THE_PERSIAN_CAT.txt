I sat hunched, heavy with cold, crawling shame. Every word Mr. Raven had spoken was true: I had been no wise guardian, no kind neighbour to the gentle Little Ones.

The raven-faced librarian leaned forward, his voice sharp but not unkind. “You did the very creatures you sought to spare a deeper wrong. Slavery would have been progress for them—firm, gentle lessons, meted out with a switch hewn from their own fruit trees, would have taught them courage they never could have learned left unprotected. It would have been priceless.”

“I did not know they were all cowards!” I burst out.

“What difference does their cowardice make? Any man who builds his choices around exploiting another’s fear is a coward in his own core.” His brows drew tight, shadowed over old, deep worry. “I fear worse harm will come of your choices. At this rate, the Little Ones might have grown strong enough to fend off the princess on their own, never mind the giants—they laughed at the giants, for heaven’s sake! But now, through the tangled mess you wove with her—”

“I hate her!” I cried, the words tearing out of me.

“Did you ever let her know that?”

Silence swallowed me whole. I could not answer.

“Not even to her were you honest, unflinching. But hush—we were followed from the fountain, I am certain.”

“I saw no living thing,” I frowned, “except a scruffy, disreputable cat that bolted straight into the shrubbery when it spotted us.”

“It was a magnificent Persian—so soaked, draggled, and foul matted that it looked as wretched as it truly is, far worse than disreputable. There was a beautiful blue Persian that haunted my house long ago, that would flee screaming at the very sound of running water. Could it have been creeping after the goldfish in the fountain pool?”

“We shall find out,” the librarian said, rising to his feet. “I know cats of every breed that walks all the worlds, and there is a book in that closet that will unmask this creature once and for all, if I am not mistaken.”

He crossed to the closet door, pulled out the half-mutilated volume I had seen broken apart months before, and set it down on the table between us. I gaped: it was whole, entire, the vellum cover smooth, every page intact.

“Where was the other half?” I gasped.

“Sticking through the thin membrane of this world into my library, on the other side.”

I held my tongue. One more question would plunge me into a bottomless sea of unknowing, and I could tell there was no time to wander.

“Listen,” he said, opening the cover. “I am going to read a few stanzas. There is someone hidden in this room, I think, who will not enjoy a single line of what follows.”

He turned the aged parchment pages, stained dark with time, one sheet blotched two-thirds over with a dark, old fluid. He paused, scanning the pages for the right passage, and when he began to read, the language was one I had never heard before—yet every word sank into my skull, clear as water, impossible to write down in my own tongue, only capable of being approximated in the fragments that clung to my memory later:
> *But if I found a man that could believe
> In what he saw not, felt not, and yet knew,
> From him I should take substance, and receive
> Firmness and form relate to touch and view;
> Then should I clothe me in the likeness true
> Of that idea where his soul did cleave!*

He turned a leaf, and read again:
> *In me was every woman. I had power
> Over the soul of every living man,
> Such as no woman ever had in dower—
> Could what no woman ever could, or can;
> All women, I, the woman, still outran,
> Outsoared, outsank, outreigned, in hall or bower.*
> *For I, though me he neither saw nor heard,
> Nor with his hand could touch finger of mine,
> Although not once my breath had ever stirred
> A hair of him, could trammel brain and spine
> With rooted bonds which Death could not untwine—
> Or life, though hope were evermore deferred.*

He paused, flipped another page, and began once more:
> *For by his side I lay, a bodiless thing;
> I breathed not, saw not, felt not, only thought,
> And made him love me—with a hungering
> After he knew not what—if it was aught
> Or but a nameless something that was wrought
> By him out of himself; for I did sing
> A song that had no sound into his soul;
> I lay a heartless thing against his heart,
> Giving him nothing where he gave his whole
> Being to clothe me human, every part:
> That I at last into his sense might dart,
> Thus first into his living mind I stole.
> Ah, who was ever conquering Love but I!
> Who else did ever throne in heart of man!
> To visible being, with a gladsome cry
> Waking, life’s tremor through me throbbing ran!*

A sharp, repulsive feline wail tore through the still air of the room. I jolted upright, staring all around me, but no shape met my eyes. Mr. Raven flipped several more pages, and his voice went on, unshaken:
> *Sudden I woke, nor knew the ghastly fear
> That held me—not like serpent coiled about,
> But like a vapour moist, corrupt, and drear,
> Filling heart, soul, and breast and brain throughout;
> My being lay motionless in sickening doubt,
> Nor dared to ask how came the horror here.
> My past entire I knew, but not my now;
> I understood nor what I was, nor where;
> I knew what I had been: still on my brow
> I felt the touch of what no more was there!
> I was a fainting, dead, yet live Despair;
> A life that flouted life with mop and mow!
> That I was a queen I knew right well,
> And sometimes wore a splendour on my head
> Whose flashing even dead darkness could not quell—
> The like on neck and arms and girdle-stead;
> And men declared a light my closed eyes shed
> That killed the diamond in its silver cell.*

Again that ugly cry of feline pain sliced through the quiet. I scanned every corner, saw no movement, no form. Mr. Raven listened for a heartbeat, then turned more pages and resumed:
> *Hideously wet, my hair of golden hue
> Fouled my fair hands: to have it swiftly shorn
> I had given my rubies, all for me dug new—
> No eyes had seen, and such no waist had worn!
> For a draught of water from a drinking horn,
> For one blue breath, I had given my sapphires blue!
> Nay, I had given my opals for a smock,
> A peasant-maiden’s garment, coarse and clean:
> My shroud was rotting! Once I heard a cock
> Lustily crow upon the hillock green
> Over my coffin. Dulled by space between,
> Came back an answer like a ghostly mock.*

That bestial, guttural wail burst out a third time.
“Thought I heard some foul skulking thing in the room,” the librarian said, glancing casually around, before he turned more pages and kept reading:
> *For I had bathed in milk and honey-dew,
> In rain from roses shook, that ne’er touched earth,
> And ointed me with nard of amber hue;
> Never had spot me spotted from my birth,
> Or mole, or scar of hurt, or fret of dearth;
> Never one hair superfluous on me grew.
> Fleeing cold whiteness, I would sit alone—
> Not in the sun—I feared his bronzing light,
> But in his radiance back around me thrown
> By fulgent mirrors tempering his might;
> Thus bathing in a moon-bath not too bright,
> My skin I tinted slow to ivory tone.
> But now, all round was dark, dark all within!
> My eyes not even gave out a phantom-flash;
> My fingers sank in pulp through pulpy skin;
> My body lay death-weltered in a mash
> Of slimy horrors—*

A fearsome, unearthly yell shattered the verse. A huge white cat burst from the shadows, its fur clumped and clammy, its tail thick as an anchor cable, its eyes flaring green as polished chrysoprase, its claws tangling in the carpet so it stumbled and floundered straight for the chimney to escape. Quick as a striking bird, the librarian flung the vellum manuscript down between her and the hearth. She froze instantly, crouched low, eyes fixed fast on the open book. His voice continued, steady, as if he still read from the pages, his own gaze fixed unblinking on the creature:
> *Ah, the two worlds! so strangely are they one,
> And yet so measurelessly wide apart!
> Oh, had I lived the bodiless alone
> And from defiling sense held safe my heart,
> Then had I scaped the canker and the smart,
> Scaped life-in-death, scaped misery’s endless moan!*

At those words, a prolonged, shrieked howl of pure agony burst from the cat, so loud we both clapped our hands over our ears to muffle it. When the noise died away, Mr. Raven walked to the fireplace, picked up the manuscript, and stood between the creature and the chimney, pointing one finger at her for a long quiet moment. She lay perfectly motionless, pinned. He took a half-burned stick from the hearth, drew a strange glowing sigil on the floor, tucked the manuscript back in its closet shelf with a small, grim nod that seemed to say *we have you now*, and returned to stand over the cat, his voice low, solemn, no trace of the bumbling old librarian left.

“Lilith, when you crept here on your way to work your evil, you never dreamed you would walk right into my hands. Mr. Vane, when God created me, he did not make me from nothing, as the foolish say—he made me from his own endless, uncreated glory. He gave me an angel of splendour to be my wife. There she lies.” He nodded at the motionless cat. “Her first thought was power. She called being one with me, bearing children for the God that gave us life, slavery. She bore one child, then, swollen with the lie that she had created that child, she demanded I fall down and worship it. When she found I would love and honour her always, but never kneel to her, she spilled her own blood to escape me, fled to the horde of the aliens, and ensnared the great Shadow’s heart so thoroughly he became her slave, did every bidding, and crowned her queen of Hell. She knows her own state now, and I know it too. That one child of hers she fears and hates, and would kill, claiming a false right over the life God sent through her into the new world. She knows no more of true creation than a crystal that grows to its set shape, or a worm that splits in two to make two worms. She is the vilest of God’s creatures, feeding on the blood, lives, and souls of men. She consumes and slays, but she has no more power to destroy than she does to create.”

The cat lay still, its beryl eyes flaming, fixed on his gaze so it could not look away.
“Then God gave me a second wife—not an angel, but a woman—who is to her what pure light is to utter darkness.”

The cat let out a horrible screech, and began to swell, growing larger and larger by the second. At last, a spotted leopardess reared up, and her roar shook the very foundations of the house. I jolted to my feet. Mr. Raven did not twitch a single eyelid.
“That is only her jealousy speaking,” he said, calm as ever. “Jealousy of her own making, fruitless, foiled. Now she is my subject, my creature to command, the same woman who once would not have me for her husband. My beautiful Eve still lives, hoping immortally. The daughter she hates lives too, hidden far past her reach, and one day she will be the very thing Lilith counts as her destruction—for even Lilith shall be saved by the child she bore. She exults that my human wife once dragged both of us into despair, and bore a whole race of the wretched and lost. But my Eve repented, and is now more beautiful than any woman or angel that ever was, and her groaning, travailing world is the nursery of our Father’s children. I repented, too, and I am blessed. But you, Lilith, you have not repented yet. But you must. Tell me: is the great Shadow beautiful? Do you know how long your own beauty will last? Answer me, if you know.”

In that moment I knew. Mr. Raven was Adam—the old man, the new man, the first father of us all. The wife who tended the house of the dead was Eve, mother of every living soul, the lady of the New Jerusalem.

The leopardess reared, her spots flickering and shifting, fading away one by one until the princess stood before us, radiant, in her perfect, human form.
“I am beautiful. And I am immortal,” she said, the look of a goddess in her eyes.
“Like a bush that burns, and is entirely consumed,” Adam answered. “What is that under your right hand? Your arm is crossed over your bosom, your hand pressed hard to your side.”

A sharp, swift pain twisted her beautiful face for a heartbeat, and vanished.
“It is just a leftover leopard spot,” she said. “It will fade away with all the rest.”
“You are beautiful because God made you, but you are the slave of your own sin. Take your hand away from your side.”

Her hand fell limply to her side, and she stared him down, fierce and quailing, no surrender in her gaze. He looked at the faint dark mark on her flesh. “That spot is not on the leopard. It is inside the woman. It will not leave you until it has eaten all the way to your heart, and all your beauty flows out of you through that open wound.”

She glanced down, and shivered from head to toe.
“Lilith,” Adam said, his voice softening to tender, pleading, “hear me. Repent, and he who made you will wash you clean.”

Her hand flew back to her side, shaking. Her face went dark as storm clouds. The cry that tore out of her was the cry of a soul whose last spark of hope was dying, curdling into a howl. She writhed on the floor, shifting back into a spotted leopardess.
“The evil you planned to do, you will never accomplish, Lilith. Good, not evil, is the core of the universe. The war between them may stretch across uncounted ages, but it will end. What will become of you when Time dissolves into the dawn of the eternal morning? Repent, I beg of you. Repent, and be an angel of God once more.”

She stood upright again, a woman once more, and her voice was cold, hard as flint. “I will never repent. I will drink the blood of your child.”

My eyes were fixed on her, but when Adam spoke I turned to him. He towered over her, the line of his face transfigured, his voice thunder, terrible to hear. “Down! Or by the power given me, I will melt your very bones to ash.”

She flung herself to the floor, dwindling, shrinking, until she was once more a scruffy gray cat. Adam caught her up by the scruff of her neck, carried her to the closet, and tossed her inside. He drew the glowing sigil he had marked on the floor across the threshold of the door, locked it tight, and came back to my side once more, the worn old librarian, wiping furtive tears from his eyes.
