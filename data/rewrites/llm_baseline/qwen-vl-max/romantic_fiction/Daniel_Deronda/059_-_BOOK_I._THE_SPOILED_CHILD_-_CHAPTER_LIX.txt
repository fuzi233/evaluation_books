CHAPTER LIX. “I count myself in nothing else so happy / As in a soul remembering my good friends.” —SHAKESPEARE.

Sir Hugo Mallinger did not set out for Genoa as quickly as Mr. Gascoigne had, and Daniel Deronda, for reasons both personal and profound, waited until he could meet the baronet in person. Grandcourt’s death was still fresh—shattering, irrevocable—and then there was the deeper upheaval within Deronda himself, the revelation of his origins, which he had not dared to unfold fully in writing. The truth about his mother—her fleeting presence, her sorrowful departure—had left him with questions too tender for letters. So he stayed, waiting at the station on the fifth evening, as promised by telegram, to greet Sir Hugo, whose arrival was expected between eight and nine.

As he stood beneath the pale twilight, anticipation stirred in him. The sight of that familiar face—the kind, steady features etched into the earliest memories of his life—would bring comfort. And yet, even now, amidst grief and uncertainty, a quiet smile touched his lips. He imagined Sir Hugo’s joy at finally being master of his own estate, free to pass it on to his daughters, or perhaps—according to a vision recently awakened in Deronda’s mind—to accept a future heir through a daughter’s child. Such thoughts were not without warmth, though they carried their own weight of irony.

We are not meant to be entirely hard-hearted, after all. To feel no pleasure in another’s happiness unless it aligns perfectly with our own ideals would make us strangers to kindness. Our faces might grow sour, our eyes cold, while our own lives remained imperfectly aligned with what we believed should be. But human connection thrives not on perfection, but on understanding. It is possible—even necessary—to be grateful for love, even when it is misdirected, when its intentions are pure, however flawed the means.

Deronda knew this well. He could not condone Sir Hugo’s role in concealing his true parentage. That was not merely silence—it was a lie, a veil drawn over the truth of his birth. Yet he also understood that Sir Hugo had likely never known the full depth of the mother’s betrayal. With his easygoing nature, the baronet may have simply believed it reasonable—indeed generous—that a woman who refused to part from her son should see him raised as an English gentleman, despite her own unorthodox ways. She had chosen secrecy, and Sir Hugo had honored it, not out of malice, but out of affection and duty.

And so, though Deronda’s judgment was firm, his heart remained tender. He wished to forgive rather than condemn. There is a grace in holding fast to principle while softening the edge of blame—like seeing a crooked frame hung askew and feeling pity for the hand that tried to hang it straight. Perhaps, after the past ten days—days that had stripped away illusions and revealed hidden truths—Deronda was more inclined than ever to resist the urge to punish, to resent, to condemn. He had seen too much suffering, too many lives shaped by unseen forces, to let anger rule him now.

When Sir Hugo stepped down from the train, the old bond surged forward—stronger than memory, deeper than words. The years melted away in the grip of their hands, the look in their eyes speaking volumes. No greeting was needed. They walked slowly toward the hotel, letting the evening air carry them, the stars beginning to glimmer above the sea.

“I’ve taken my journey easily,” said Sir Hugo, “and I’m in excellent spirits.”

Deronda nodded. “I’m glad.”

“I didn’t rush off,” continued Sir Hugo, “because I wanted to look into things a little. I saw your letter to Lady Mallinger before I left. How is the widow?”

“Getting calmer,” Deronda replied. “She’s recovering from the shock and the physical strain. Her uncle and mother arrived two days ago, and she’s well cared for.”

“And any chance of an heir?” Sir Hugo asked, his tone light but probing.

“From what Mr. Gascoigne told me,” Deronda said carefully, “I don’t think so. He seemed to suggest it’s uncertain whether she’ll inherit the estates at all.”

Sir Hugo gave a short laugh. “Then it won’t be much of a blow to her affections, I suppose, losing her husband?”

Deronda hesitated. “The suddenness of his death has been a great blow,” he said quietly, avoiding the question.

“Did Grandcourt give her any hint about the terms of his will?” Sir Hugo pressed.

Deronda looked at him. “Do you know what they are, sir?”

“Yes,” Sir Hugo answered swiftly. “Gad! If there’s no legitimate heir, he’s left everything to a boy he had with Mrs. Glasher—a woman who lived with him for years, practically a wife. Three older daughters, and now this boy, who’s already called Henleigh. He’ll take the name Henleigh Mallinger Grandcourt. The Mallinger part? Bah—I’m glad that won’t matter to him. He’ll have more than enough with fourteen years’ minority. No need for me to have wasted fifty thousand on Diplow, which he had no right to claim. Meanwhile, the young widow gets two thousand a year and the house at Gadsmere. A nice little exile, if she chooses to shut herself up there—which I doubt she will. Mrs. Glasher has been living there lately. I’m utterly disgusted with Grandcourt. I don’t feel obliged to respect him just because he’s dead. Though, frankly, nothing in his life suited him like leaving it.”

Deronda paused. “In my view, he was wrong to marry her—not in leaving the estate to the other son,” he said, “but in not providing for her properly. She was penniless, raised to a rank far beyond her background. She deserved a decent income—four or five thousand a year, and the London house for life. That’s what I would have done.”

Sir Hugo nodded. “I agree. Even a wise man lets folly slip into his will. My father did. And if a man has bitterness in him, he often hides it in such documents. Clearly, Grandcourt intended his death to end his wife’s hopes—if she bore no heir.”

“And if she had?” Deronda asked, his voice edged with scorn.

“Precisely,” Sir Hugo said. “Gadsmere and two thousand pounds. Odd, isn’t it? One nuisance is that he made me executor. But since he was my brother’s only son, I can’t refuse. Still, I’ll do what I can to help the widow. Lush thinks she knew about the family secrets—about the will, too. He hints there wasn’t much harmony between them. But I imagine you’re the one who truly knows what she felt—or didn’t feel—eh, Dan?”

His tone was serious now, not playful. Deronda sensed the gravity behind the question. He could not dodge it.

“She was not happy,” he said. “They were ill-matched. But regarding the estate… based on what I’ve seen of her, I believe she will accept whatever comes.”

Sir Hugo shrugged slightly. “Then she’s not like most women. But she must be something extraordinary—there must be some strange connection between your fate and hers. When the telegram came, Lady Mallinger said, ‘How very strange that it should be Daniel who sends it!’ And I’ve had something similar in my own life. Once, abroad, I met a woman left destitute by her husband. I helped her—only to discover she was an old flame, foolish enough to marry an Austrian baron with a long mustache and a short temper. But it was my own business that brought me there—not knight-errantry. Just as your coming here wasn’t about the Grandcourts.”

They walked in silence for a moment. The port slipped behind them, the sea calm under the stars. Sir Hugo had begun with the easier topic—the Grandcourts—but both men knew the real conversation lay ahead.

Deronda had written to Sir Hugo after meeting his mother, but the letter had only deepened the distance between them. He felt it was better to wait for the baronet to lead.

Then, just as they turned toward the *Italia*, Sir Hugo stopped, gazing back at the harbor. He turned, his voice softer now.

“And about your reason for coming to Genoa, Dan? Has anything you’ve learned deeply wounded you? Is there anything that changes your place in life? You know—whatever happens to you, it matters to me.”

Deronda took a breath. “I want to repay your kindness with complete honesty, sir. But I cannot answer those questions with yes or no. Much of what I’ve learned has hurt me. Meeting and saying farewell to my mother—seeing her in pain—was difficult. But knowing my parentage… that is not a wound. It is a relief. A clearing of doubt. And as for my position—my gratitude to you remains unchanged. Your care, your love, have always been a father’s to me. But knowing I am Jewish… that may shape my life in ways I cannot yet explain.”

He spoke with quiet resolve, though fear lingered beneath the words. He knew their worlds were different, and soon, inevitably, those differences would become harder to hide.

Sir Hugo looked at him sharply, then turned away. After a pause, he said, “I’ve always expected something remarkable from you, Dan. But for God’s sake—don’t go chasing eccentricities! I tolerate anyone’s beliefs, but not melodrama. At this stage of the world, if you want to be taken seriously, keep clear of theatrics. Don’t misunderstand me—I don’t think you’re mad. But you’re drawn to the persecuted, Dan. I feel for them too. But choosing company based on suffering… that’s risky ground. Still, I won’t pressure you. When you decide on a path that needs money, I’ve got sixteen thousand pounds saved for you—above what you’ve already received. And now that I’m here… do you want to return to England soon?”

“I must go first to Mainz,” Deronda said. “To retrieve a chest belonging to my grandfather. And perhaps visit a friend of his. Though it’s been twenty years, I feel a strange urgency to get it myself—like something might happen to it now. And I lingered after my mother left, instead of departing immediately. But I don’t regret it. If I hadn’t been here, Mrs. Grandcourt would have had only servants to assist her.”

Sir Hugo smiled wryly. “Yes, yes. I hope you’re not going to elevate a dead Jew above a living Christian.”

Deronda flushed, biting back a retort.

They stepped into the *Italia*, the night quiet around them, the sea whispering beneath the stars.
