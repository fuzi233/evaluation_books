As Jorian donned his doublet and jerkin, preparing to depart for Peter's tomb, his tongue remained ceaselessly active. "They used to call him a magician out toward Sevenbergen," he began. "And they say he bestowed a final touch of his craft upon them before leaving. He claimed to see Margaret's son descending the Rhine in splendid attire, laden with wealth, yet his face marred by a foreign blade, lacking neither entirely his arms nor his legs. But, dear heart, nothing came of it. Margaret still languishes for her boy; and Peter lies as silent as his neighbors—though she has placed a stone slab over him to keep him exactly where he rests, as you shall soon witness."

He pressed both hands against the grave's edge, poised to rise, when the friar laid a trembling hand upon his shoulder, whispering strangely, "How long since Peter Brandt passed?"

"About two months. Why ask?"

"And his daughter buried him, you say?"

"Nay, I performed the burial rites, though she paid the fee and commissioned the stone."

"Then—but he had but one daughter, Margaret?"

"No more, at least, that he acknowledged."

"Then you believe Margaret is... is alive?"

"Believe? Why, I should be dead otherwise. Puzzle that out for yourself."

"Alas, how can I? You love her!"

"Not more than reason demands, being a married man and father of four sturdy lads like myself. No, the truth is, she saved my life scarcely six weeks past. Had she been dead, she could not have sustained me. Bless your heart, I could barely keep food in my stomach; physicians could do naught for me. My Joan declared, 'It is time to purchase a shroud for thee.' 'I dare say so,' I replied, 'yet try borrowing one first.' Enter my lady, this Margaret—who, by your account, died three years ago—opens the windows, shifts me from my bed, and cures me in the blink of an eye. With what? Prickles the shoe; with the vilest herb, plucked from my own garden, sweet feverfew. An herb, quotha, 'tis a weed; leastways, it was a weed until it healed me. Now, whenever I pass my hunch, I lower my cap and cry, 'Fly service to thee!' Why, Father, why do you grow so pale, then so red, then so white? What troubles you? What is the matter?"

"The surprise—the joy—the wonder—the fear," gasped Clement.

"Why does it concern thee? Are you of kin to Margaret Brandt?"

"Nay; but I knew one who loved her dearly, so deeply her death nearly destroyed him, body and soul. Yet you claim she lives. And I begin to believe you."

Jorian stared, and after a prolonged silence, spoke very gravely, "Father, you have asked many questions, and I have answered them truthfully. Now, for our Lady's sake, answer me but two. Did you truly know one who loved this poor maiden? Where?"

Clement stood on the brink of revelation but recalled Jerome's letter and recoiled from using the name he had once borne in the world. "I knew him in Italy," he said.

"If you knew him, you can tell me his name," Jorian urged cautiously.

"His name was Gerard Eliassoen."

"Oh, but this is strange. Wait, what led you to say Margaret Brandt was dead?"

"I was with Gerard when a letter arrived from Margaret Van Eyck. It informed him that the one he loved was dead and buried. Let me sit down, for my strength fails me. Foul play! Foul play!"

"Father," Jorian interjected, "I thank Heaven for sending you to me. Yes, sit; you look like a specter. You fast too much to remain strong. My mind misgives me; I seem to hold the key to this riddle. If I do, there are two knaves in this town whose heads I would gladly crush as easily as this mold." He clenched his teeth and hoisted his long spade high, bringing it down furiously upon the earth several times.

"Foul play? You have never spoken a truer word in your life. If you know where Gerard is now, lose no time in showing him the trap they have set. Mine is but a dull head, but sometimes the slow hound discerns the scent. Go on. I believe you and I have seized two ends of the same stick, and a most foul one at that."

After some typical preliminary chatter common among men of his station, Jorian came directly to the crux of the matter. He had been hired by the burgomaster of Tergou to repair the floor of an upper chamber. As he finished and gathered his tools, curiosity sparked by loud voices below. He lay prone on the floor and overheard the burgomaster discussing a letter Cornelis and Sybrandt intended to deliver to replace one that Hans Memling was carrying to Gerard. "'Their will was good, but their stomach was small,'" the old man had remarked. "'To give them courage, I showed them a drawer full of silver, and if they complete the task, they may take as much as fits in their grasp.'"

"Well, Father," Jorian continued, "I paid little heed then, save for the bargain itself, which kept me awake most of the night. Consider this: next morning, at dawn, who should I see emerging from their homes but Cornelis and Sybrandt, each sporting a black eye? 'Oho,' I muttered, 'what mark has Hans left upon you? Well, I hope that is all you gained for your pains.' Did they not rush straight to the burgomaster's house? I hid myself."

At this revelation, the monk's nostrils flared, and his restless eyes betrayed intense suspense.

"Well, Father," Jorian proceeded, "the burgomaster summoned them to that very room. He held a letter in his hand. Though I am no scholar, I possess as many eyes as the Pope, and I saw the drawer open. Those two rogues plunged a hand in each, drawing it out brimming. Saints in glory, how they struggled to fit more and more of that stuff! Sybrandt had smeared his hand in something sticky—I think glue—and managed to carry one or two pieces away adhering to his palm. Ha, ha, ha! 'Tis a sin to laugh. See, fortune favored the wrong side as usual; they completed the trick, yet how they did it remains unknown until Doomsday. Off they went, leaving their immortal treasures in that drawer. They received a handful of silver; the Devil won that bargain. There, Father, that clears my mind. Often I longed to confide in someone, but dared not speak to the women, lest Margaret lose her sole friend in the world. Those two black-hearted villains are the favorites. 'Tis always so. Have not the elders recently secured a grand new shop for them right here in this town, on Hoog Straet? There you may see their sign: a gilt sheep and a lambkin. A pair of wolves suckling their dam would be closer to the mark. There the entire family feasts today; oh, 'tis a fine world. Not a word, holy father? You sit like stone, offering not even a curse upon them, the stony-hearted wretches. Was it not enough that the poor lad stood alone in a strange land? Must his own flesh and blood go and bury the one blessing his enemies left him? And consider her pining, pining all these years, seated at the window gazing down the street for Gerard! So constant, so tender, so true: my wife insists no woman ever loved a man truer than she loves the boy those villains tore from her side. Not a single day passes without her weeping salt tears for him. And when I reflect that, but for those two greedy, lying knaves, that winsome lad—whose life I saved—might be by her side today, the happiest man in Holland; and that sweet girl, who saved my life, might be resting her cheek upon her sweetheart's shoulder, the happiest woman in Holland instead of the saddest; oh, I thirst for their blood! The nasty, sneaking, lying, cheating, cowardly, heartless, bowels-less—how now?"

The monk leaped up wildly, his face livid with fury and despair, rushing headlong from the spot with both hands clenched aloft. Such was the terrifying, inarticulate eruption of rage that Jorian's petty anger instantly evaporated at the sight. He stood transfixed, dismayed, watching the human tempest he had unleashed.

While lost in this reverie, he felt his arm grasped by a small, trembling hand. It was Margaret Brandt. He started; her presence there seemed utterly inexplicable. She had waited long upon Peter's tombstone, but the friar never appeared. She entered the church to search for him but found him absent. Soon, ascending the south aisle, the gigantic shadow of a friar swept rapidly across the floor and vanished behind a pillar, seeming to pass right through her. She nearly screamed, but remembering Jorian's shadow had emerged so abruptly from the churchyard, she attempted to exit via the nearest path. She succeeded, albeit with difficulty, by which time Clement was just disappearing down the street. Yet, so expressive can the body be as well as the face, she perceived he was greatly agitated.
