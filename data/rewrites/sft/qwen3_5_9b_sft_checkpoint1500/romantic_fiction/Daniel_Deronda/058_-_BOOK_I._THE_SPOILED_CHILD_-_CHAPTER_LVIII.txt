Time, it seems, measures itself by things far too imperfect to gauge the true depth of life. The vast distance the sun travels tells us nothing of how our hearts have aged, just as the acres of a field reveal nothing of the seeds quietly sprouting beneath the soil. A man might march south and stumble over a bone, meditating on it until he uncovers a new path for anatomy; or turn east and find a key to language that unlocks a forgotten saga of races. He might lead an expedition that carves new continents, suffer a maiming, and endure a whole epic of heroism and grit. Yet, when he returns after a few short months, he finds his neighbors still grumbling over the same parish grievances, sees the same elderly gentleman pacing the pavement, shaking his head at the same butcher's boy, and lingering at the same shop window to gaze at the same prints. If the sharpest thought moves with the speed of a greyhound, the slowest creeps along like a limpet, appearing stuck until, over a long while, it is revealed to be a steady, if invisible, advance. These disparities define the variable rhythm of human existence—from the thunderous surge of revolution that births a new soul, to the quiet, recurring hum of the familiar, marked only by hunger and the turning sky.

A stark reflection of this duality was visible in the year's passage. The brilliant, self-assured Gwendolen Harleth of the Archery Meeting had metamorphosed into a crushed penitent, driven to confess her unworthiness where, once, it would have been her greatest joy to be deemed worthy. Meanwhile, her family at Pennicote Rectory remained largely unchanged, save for outward habits and a recalibration of hopes to fit a reduced income, fewer visitors, and paler compliments. The rectory retained its former charm; the red and pink peonies on the lawn and the rows of hollyhocks by the hedges bloomed as vibrantly this season as last. The rector, Mr. Gascoigne, kept his cheerful faith in the goodwill of his patrons and his determination to earn it through diligent duty, regardless of whether anyone heard of it. He wrote two ecclesiastical articles annually, unsigned, attributing them to anonymous sages, though the patrons who received private copies surely knew his hand. Yet, he chewed no bitter cud of suspicion regarding their silence; instead, he added marginal notes to his own drafts to make them engaging loans, finding solace in the fact that the Archdeacon and other authorities took no issue with his general argument. There was a peaceful quality to his authorship—an existence in the air of the fields and downs, untouched by the "thrice-breathed breath" of criticism. It brought no Dante-like emaciation but rather assisted his nutrition with complacency, offering perhaps a more pervasive sense of accomplishment than the creation of a *Divina Commedia*.

Then there was the father's recovered delight in his favorite son, a happiness that outweighed the loss of eighteen hundred pounds a year. Whatever hidden shift Rex had undergone following the disappointment of his first love, it appeared secondary to the evidence of serious ambition sparked by the family's misfortune. Indeed, Mr. Gascoigne tended to view the previous year's distress—the "little affair"—as merely an evaporation of superfluous moisture, akin to the final stage of baking bread where the dough demands a specific finish. Rex had recently returned for a summer visit, bringing Anna home. While he displayed the old liveness with his siblings, he maintained his holiday habits of an eager student, rising early and closing himself away late to pursue a rigorous course of study.

"Do you regret choosing the law as your profession, Rex?" his father asked.

"There is no profession I would prefer," Rex replied. "I wish to spend my life as a first-rate judge and help draft a code. I reverse the famous saying. 'Give me something to do with making the laws, and let whoever wishes compose the songs.'"

"You'll have to stuff yourself with an immense amount of rubbish, I suppose—that's the downside," the rector noted.

"I don't see that legal rubbish is worse than any other kind. It isn't as bad as the trivial literature that clogs the mind. It doesn't dull one as much. Our wisest men have often been lawyers. Any methodical approach to viewing things as cases and evidence seems superior to a constant deluge of disjointed fragments leading nowhere. From a higher perspective, the foundations and evolution of law offer the most compelling insights into philosophy and history. Of course, there will be much drudgery, perhaps exasperation. But the greatest prizes in life cannot be won easily—I have learned that."

"Well, my boy, the finest omen of a man's success in his vocation is that he believes it to be the finest in the world. But I suspect most workers feel this when they enter with a will. Brewitt, the blacksmith, told me the other day that his apprentice lacked a mind for the trade. 'Yet, sir,' said Brewitt, 'what would a young fellow have if he didn't like blacksmithing?'

The rector treasured a paternal delight, allowing it to surface only in moderation. He had easily endured the departure of Warham, who had gone to India, but Rex represented the romance of later life—a son he recognized as superior to himself, envisioning a future eminence modeled on famous examples. To his wife, he declared with conviction, "Rex will be a distinguished man, Nancy, I am sure of it—as sure as Paley's father was about his son."

"Was Paley an old bachelor?" asked Mrs. Gascoigne.

"That is hardly relevant, my dear," the rector replied, forgetting the irrelevant detail. Mrs. Gascoigne felt her question had sounded somewhat weak.

This tranquil progression of time at the rectory was mirrored in the household across the road, the modest white house nestled among evergreens, known to villagers as "Jodson's." Mrs. Davilow's delicate face bore only a slight deepening of her mild melancholy, her hair sporting just a few additional silver strands due to the trials of the last year. Her four daughters had blossomed slightly, emerging from the shadows, while the good Jocosa maintained her neutral stance toward the world's pleasures and glories, reserving them for those not "in a situation."

In the low, narrow drawing room, expanded by two quaint projecting windows with lattices wide open to the scent of monthly roses on a July afternoon, the air was thick with the faint murmur of the garden and the occasional, clarifying ring of hooves and wheels. The scene was lively and crowded, Rex and Anna joining the usual group of six. Anna, always a favorite with her younger cousins, delighted them with tales of her London experiences and the acquaintances she'd made. On her first solo visit, countless inquiries had been directed at her regarding Gwendolen's residence in Grosvenor Square, what Gwendolen herself had said, and the gossip surrounding her. Had Anna visited Gwendolen after learning about the yacht? No. An answer that left speculation wild regarding the mysterious vessel, beyond the fact that Gwendolen had written just prior to departure stating she and Mr. Grandcourt were yachting in the Mediterranean, and again from Marseilles, expressing certainty that she would enjoy the trip, the cabins were elegant, and she wouldn't write again until she had filled pages with *ditto*s. Furthermore, this movement of Mr. and Mrs. Grandcourt had been mentioned in "the newspapers," adding a striking dimension to the sisters' romantic narratives, with the book-devouring Isabel tossing in a corsair or two to ensure a happy ending.

However, when Rex was present, the girls, adhering to instructions, avoided this captivating topic. Today, conversation focused on the Meyricks and their extraordinary Jewish friends, eliciting astonished questions from minds for whom the idea of living Jews, outside of books, suggested a difference almost zoological—as if a strange race from Pliny's *Natural History* sleeping under the shade of its own ears. Bertha could not conceive what Jews believed now, harboring a vague notion that they rejected the Old Testament since it validated the New. Miss Merry thought Mirah and her brother "could never have been properly argued with," while the amiable Alice expressed indifference to Jewish beliefs, simply stating she "couldn't bear them." Mrs. Davilow gently corrected her, noting that the great Jewish families in society were perfectly respectable in both London and Paris, though admitting that lesser, unconverted Jews were objectionable. Isabel asked if Mirah spoke as they did, or if one could be with her without realizing she was a Jewess.

Rex, possessing no partisanship for the Israelites and having acquired a troubling familiarity with the minutiae of their ancient history through "cramming," amused himself by playfully exaggerating each speaker's notion, until Anna interceded, begging them to understand he was merely joking. Their laughter was cut short by the delivery of a letter for Mrs. Davilow. A messenger had brought it in great haste from the rectory. Inside lay a telegram. As Mrs. Davilow read and reread it in silence and agitation, all eyes turned to her with anxiety, yet no one dared speak. Finally looking up, seeing the young faces "painted with fear," she realized they might be imagining horrors worse than the truth, recalling her own first dread that rendered the words unintelligible.
