"Good day, Messer Domenico," Nello announced to the first visitor entering the shop, nodding a silent greeting to the second. "You arrive with the precision of cheese upon macaroni. Ah! You are in a hurry—desiring a shave without delay—ecco! And today is a morning where every soul carries a burden of grave import. Florence orphaned—the very pivot of Italy snatched away—heaven itself bewildered as to its next move. *Oimè*! Yet, well, well; the sun continues its march toward dinner-time, and as I stated, you arrive like cheese already grated."

For this young stranger sought an honorable merchant to advance funds against a specific ring of value. Even if Nello had enumerated every goldsmith and moneylender in Florence upon his fingers, he could not have conceived a finer name than Menico Cennini. Furthermore, the youth possessed other wares relevant to the trade: Greek learning and youthful eyes—a dual asset printers invariably require.

The grave elderly man, son of Bernardo Cennini, who two decades prior had witnessed the German printing press and subsequently cast his own typefaces in Florence, remained submerged in a lathered silence and passive observation while Nello bombarded him with commentary. He cast a slow, sideways glance at the stranger.

"This fine young man offers unlimited proficiency in Greek, Latin, or Italian at your disposal," Nello continued, indulging in expansive paraphrase. "He stands as a marvel of juvenile intellect comparable to Francesco Filelfo or our own unparalleled Poliziano. Indeed, a second Guarino, though fate dealt him a cruel blow in the form of shipwreck, presumably costing him a treasury of precious manuscripts that might have corrected even your most rigorous editions, Messer Domenico. Fortunately, he salvaged several gems of rare worth. His name is—you mentioned yours, Messer, was—"

"Tito Melema," the stranger replied, sliding the ring from his finger and presenting it to Cennini. Nello, equally swift with his razor as with his tongue, had by then removed the shaving cloth.

Meanwhile, the companion who had entered with the goldsmith—a tall figure of approximately fifty, sporting a short-trimmed beard, an old felt hat, and a threadbare mantle—had maintained a fixed gaze upon the Greek. Now, he spoke abruptly.

"Young man, I am painting a depiction of Sinon deceiving old Priam, and I would be grateful for your likeness to serve as my Sinon, provided you grant me a sitting."

Tito Melema recoiled slightly, his face flushing with pale astonishment as if struck by a sudden accusation. However, Nello afforded him no moment to formulate a defense.

"Piero," the barber declared, "you are the most extraordinary amalgamation of humors and fantasies ever compressed into human skin. What stratagem will you employ with this distinguished visage of the young scholar to fashion it into a suitable traitor? Instead, bid him raise his eyes upward; thus, you might craft a Saint Sebastian capable of drawing legions of devout women. Or, if you prefer a classical register, drape myrtle about his curls to transform him into a young Bacchus—or rather, a Phoebus Apollo—for his countenance burns with the warmth and brilliance of a summer morning. It earned me his friendship within the span of a single 'credo.'"

"Aye, Nello," the painter responded, his speech marked by abrupt halts. "If your tongue could cease its incessant chirping long enough for your understanding to engage with the matter, you would discern precisely why this face suits my traitor. A perfect traitor requires a countenance untouched by the markings of vice—lips capable of lying with a dimpled smile, eyes of such agate-like luminosity and depth that no infamy can dull them, cheeks that rise after murder yet remain free of haggardness. I assert not that this young man is a traitor; I mean only that he possesses the visage of a perfect traitor should he harbor the requisite heart. Which is to say neither more nor less that he bears a beautiful face, informed by rich young blood, sufficiently nourished by sustenance to retain its color without substantial aid from virtue. He may possess the heart of a hero alongside it; I claim nothing to the contrary. Ask Domenico there if lapidaries can always identify a gem by sight alone. And now, I shall stuff tow into my ears, for your chatter combined with the bells exceeds my endurance. Say no further to me, but trim my beard."

With these final words, Piero, known as "di Cosimo" from his master Cosimo Rosselli, extracted two pieces of tow, stuffed them firmly into his ears, and settled into the chair before Nello. The barber shrugged, casting a grimacing look of complicit intelligence at the Greek, signaling: *A whimsical fellow, you perceive! Everyone treats his pronouncements as mere jests.*

Tito, who had stood transfixed, his long dark eyes resting on the enigmatic man who had addressed him so ambiguously, appeared to recall his self-command only with Piero's shift in posture. Satisfied with the explanation, he redirected his attention to Cennini, who remarked shortly thereafter:

"This is a curious and valuable ring, young man. This intaglio of the fish bearing the crested serpent above it, set within the black stratum of the onyx—or rather, nicolo—is excellently rendered by the surrounding blue of the upper layer. Does the ring, doubtless, carry a history?" Cennini added, regarding the young stranger with keen intensity.

"Yes, indeed," Tito replied, meeting the scrutiny with frank openness. "The ring was discovered in Sicily, and I have learned from those devoted to gems and sigils that both the stone and the carving possess virtues to render the wearer fortunate, particularly at sea, and to restore to him whatever he may have lost. But," he continued, smiling, "although I have worn it constantly since departing Greece, it has not entirely ensured fortune at sea, you perceive, unless I count escape from drowning as sufficient proof of its efficacy. It remains to be seen whether my lost chests will resurface; however, to forfeit no opportunity for such a result, Messer, I implore you to hold the ring briefly as pledge for a modest sum far below its intrinsic value. I shall redeem it as soon as I can liquidate certain other gems secured within my doublet, or indeed as soon as I can earn something through any scholarly employment, should I be so fortunate as to encounter such opportunities."

"That may be verified, young man, if you will accompany me," Cennini stated. "My brother Pietro, who judges scholarship with greater acuity than I, may well provide a task to test your capabilities. In the interim, retrieve your ring until I can deliver the necessary florins. If it pleases you, proceed with me."

"Yes, yes," Nello interjected. "Go with Messer Domenico; you could not find better company. He was born under the constellation that bestows skill, riches, and integrity, whatever that celestial configuration may be—a distinction of lesser consequence since infants cannot select their own horoscopes, and indeed, if they could, there might arise an inconvenient surge of births during specific epochs. Moreover, our Phoenix, the incomparable Pico, has demonstrated that your horoscopes are merely nonsensical dreams—an opinion all the less troublesome because it spares us anxiety. *Addio! bel giovane!* Do not fail to return to me."

"No fear of that," Tito replied, offering a beckoning farewell as he turned his radiant face toward the threshold. "You are to perform me a great service: that constitutes the most positive guarantee of your seeing me again."

"Say what thou wilt, Piero," Nello muttered as the young stranger vanished. "I shall never view such an exterior without interpreting it as a sign of a lovable nature. Why, thou wilt next argue that Leonardo, whom thou dost rave about perpetually, ought to have fashioned his Judas as beautiful as Saint John! But thou art as deaf as the summit of Mount Morello with that accursed tow in thy ears. Well, well: I shall extract a bit more of this young man's history before escorting him to Bardo Bardi."
