M. Paul's demeanor remained unchanged, his mercurial nature still prone to flashes of irritability when his emotions ran high. His veins pulsed with a deep-seated jealousy, a sentiment that resided more in the cerebral realm than the heart. This was not the tender, all-consuming passion that often accompanies romantic love, but a cold, calculating force that governed his interactions with others.

As I observed him, I couldn't help but draw parallels between M. Paul and Napoleon Bonaparte. Like the great Emperor, M. Paul had a propensity for quarrels and a willingness to engage in petty bickering, unencumbered by concerns for dignity or loss. He would have delighted in banishing multiple Madame de Staëls from his presence, should they dare to cross him.

A particular incident involving Madame Panache, a history instructor temporarily employed by Madame Beck, stands out in my memory. This woman was intelligent and adept at presenting her knowledge, possessing an uncanny ability to wield words and confidence with ease. Her physical appearance was undeniably striking, and many would have deemed her a fine woman. However, M. Paul found her robust and effusive nature to be off-putting, and the sound of her voice, echoing through the corridors, would send him into a state of agitation.

One day, he decided to infiltrate her class, swiftly grasping her instructional method, which diverged from his own preferred approach. Without ceremony or courtesy, he pointed out what he deemed her errors. Madame Panache, however, refused to back down, delivering a scathing rebuke for his unwarranted interference. Rather than retreating with dignity, M. Paul chose to escalate the situation, issuing a challenge that Madame Panache accepted with alacrity. The ensuing battle of wits was fierce, with both parties exchanging verbal barbs.

As the conflict unfolded, I expected M. Paul to maintain a detached, amused perspective, but instead, he became consumed by a profound animosity towards Madame Panache. He pursued her with unyielding fervor, refusing to rest or find solace until she was eliminated from the establishment. Though he ultimately emerged victorious, his triumph was not marked by the elegance one might expect. I ventured to suggest as much, and to my surprise, he acknowledged the possibility, attributing his loss of composure to the presence of individuals like Madame Panache, who embodied a coarse, self-satisfied quality that triggered an uncontrollable aversion within him.
