On the feast day of San Giovanni, three weeks had passed since Tito handed his florins to Cennini. As he strode toward the Via de’ Bardi, his demeanor betrayed no hint of unease—only the careless grace of a man untroubled by shadows. The sun beat down as he turned into a narrow street, pushing back his black beretta to let the breeze cool his brow. His face, smooth and unmarked by care, held neither deceit nor candor, only the effortless charm of youth.

The festive crowds thickened as he neared San Martino. Outside the tavern of the *Bertucce*, a raucous knot of onlookers had gathered. Tito might have passed by, but a child’s pleading cry—*"Loose me! Holy Virgin, help me!"*—pierced the laughter. Pushing through, he found a young *contadina* struggling against a grinning *cerretano*, his fingers clamped around her wrists. The girl’s hood had slipped, revealing Tessa’s tear-streaked face, her blue eyes wide with fear.

At the sight of Tito, hope flashed across her features. Without hesitation, he stepped forward.

"Release her," he demanded. "What right have you to hold her against her will?"

The conjuror—a sharp-featured man with a mouth perpetually twisted in amusement—shrugged and let go. "I meant no harm, *Messere*! Only sought a willing assistant for my tricks. But it seems she prefers your company."

A ripple of laughter followed them as Tito guided Tessa away, her small hand clutching his sleeve. "Where were you going?" he asked, his voice low.

"I lost my mother in the crowd," she stammered. "If I return alone, my father-in-law will beat me."

Tito sighed. Duty warred with pity. He had no time for stray *contadine*, yet her trembling grip gave him pause. "Come, then," he relented. "I’ll take you as far as the Prato."

As they walked, Tessa’s tears dried like morning dew. She chattered of her home—the garden, the cows, the black-faced kid she adored—while Tito half-listened, his mind already leaping ahead to the obligations awaiting him. Yet her artless trust was disarming. When they reached the quiet stretch of the Prato, he found himself lingering beneath a broad plane tree, sharing apricots from her apron as she beamed up at him.

"You should be my little servant," he teased, brushing crumbs from her lap. "Would you like that?"

Her face lit with sudden joy. "Take me with you now! Then I wouldn’t have to go back."

But duty reclaimed him. With a laugh, he pressed a coral charm into her palm—a talisman against evil spirits—and kissed her brow. "Be brave, *piccina*. I’ll come see you another day."

As he walked away, Tessa sat motionless, clutching the charm. The world had shifted; the future shimmered with possibilities. For Tito, the interlude was already fading—a pleasant diversion, soon eclipsed by the demands of his ambitions.

Yet as he crossed the Arno, a stray thought flickered: *Would Romola ever look at me with such simple devotion?*

He quickened his pace. The Via de’ Bardi awaited.
