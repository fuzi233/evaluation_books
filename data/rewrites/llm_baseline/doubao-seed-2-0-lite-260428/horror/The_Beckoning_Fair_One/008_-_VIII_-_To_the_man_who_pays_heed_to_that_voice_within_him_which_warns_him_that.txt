To the man who heeds that small, shrill voice under his skin warning him that twilight and rot are creeping over the edges of his soul, terror feels like something absolute: a chasm yawning at his feet, that will swallow him whole in a blink if he does not throw every possible barrier between it and the soft, unprotected core of who he is. Mercifully, the materials to build those barriers are never far to hand. He piles up tiny, ordinary things against the dark: the exact time he brews his morning tea, the familiar nod to the baker on the corner, the old rituals, unspoken conventions, small unexamined habits that shore up the line between the world of the living and the hungry things that lurk just outside it. He is even willing to box joy and terror both as absolute, uncrossable forces, for to blur those lines, to tear down the landmarks that tell you what is real and what is not, is the worst treason a man can commit—not just against himself, but against the safety of every soul that still walks in the light.

It was that treason Oleron began to commit, step by unthinking step. He did it insensibly, brushing off the safeguards Elsie Bengough had tried to lay down for him as nothing more than impertinent overcaution. Two months prior, the phrase “haunted house” applied to his beloved, bewitching dwelling would have turned his marrow to slush; now, his sense of scale warping thin, he only found himself asking Haunted by what?—blind to the truth that horror loses none of its awful, unyielding power just because you convince yourself it can be measured, classified, held in the palm of your hand. The landmarks blurred. Mists of confusion wrapped around him thicker by the day, and the only thing he felt sharp and bright beneath it all was a ravenous, gnawing curiosity. He had to know. He would know. Nothing short of unravelling the secret of the house would sate him.

He need not have schemed for it. Answers came easier than breath. The same way the words of his old writing once used to pour out of him fully formed, no edits needed, before he could even fully form the thought behind them, now the questions he posed to the dark empty rooms were answered in the exact same moment they crossed his mind. There was a giddy, heady exhilaration to the speed of it, a thrill he had not felt since the first days his writing flowed fresh and unforced, as if some hidden guide was leaning over his shoulder, dictating every step he needed to take.

First, define the problem, he thought, and his mind sorted it into cold, clear math. He was not alone in the house. The old place had already sunk its teeth deep into his spirit. That shared bond, the common denominator of the four walls, tied whatever his unknown co-tenant was directly to him. All he needed to do was identify the other half of the equation. The answer was obvious: the key lay in his half-written novel, not just the new Romilly he had been drafting, but the old, discarded one he had abandoned the day he first walked through the front door. A month prior he would have thought himself mad to entertain such an idea; now he accepted the dizzying hypothesis without a single tremor.

He reviewed the history of the first, fifteen-chapter *Romilly* in a rush. He remembered now, clear as day, that he had found her stiff, lifeless, wrong on the very first morning he sat down to work at his new desk. He remembered the night he had almost hurled the entire manuscript into the kitchen fire, and the morning after that when he had begun to sketch out the new *Romilly* from scratch. That was the very same morning Mrs. Barrett, overhearing him hum a stray little melody he had picked up from the dripping kitchen tap the night before, had told him he was singing an old air he had never heard of in his life: *The Beckoning Fair One*.

The name hung in the air of the silent room like cobwebs strung between rafters. Oleron did not pause. The first Romilly, cast off entirely, the new one had latched onto him immediately, screaming to be born out of his skull. Now, looking back, he could almost feel the hate, the ravenous passion that had fuelled the supplanting of the old character. More than once, a stray, lazy thought directed at his discarded creation had not just felt wrong—it had felt like an insult, directed at the new thing that had taken her place. The idea that this jealous, almost homicidal malice could extend past fictional characters to Elsie, his old friend, made a strangled “Good God!” tear out of his throat, loud in the hush. The thought of Elsie derailed his cold abstract inquiry entirely, made the back of his neck prickle.

He still could not think of the fight he’d had with Barrett the landlord a few days prior without his face flushing hot and his jaw tightening. He’d marched over to the man’s doorstep first thing the next morning, ready to beard him and demand he stop spreading his garbage rumours, but the man had been nothing but vague, blustering, repeating scraps of lines over and over: “Certain things… Mrs. Barrett… respectable house… if the cap fits… proceedings that shall be nameless.” Oleron had shouted, warned him if a single breath of the slander reached Elsie he would make him pay, but he’d left the square knowing he had only made matters far worse. Now he kept catching glimpses of Barrett’s face pressed to windows across the way, peeking around curtains, lifting blinds, always watching, as if waiting to catch him in some unnameable act to use against him. The neighbours stared at him when he walked past. He started doing his grocery shopping on the far side of town, avoiding the corner shops near the square entirely. He told himself he was fine living alone, bachelor habits were nothing new, that he was deep in research he did not want disturbed.

He was staring out the window one raw, midday, tired and faintly feverish, glad he did not have to go out, when he saw Elsie crossing the square straight for his front door. The wind whipped her skirts out like sails, her veil streaming behind her, rain spitting at her cheeks.

Panic seized Oleron so hard he did not even grab his overcoat. He grabbed his hat, sprinted down the stairs three at a time, his eyes darting up to the sharp slate edges over the eaves unbidden, as if he already knew if she stepped over his threshold, something would slip and fall, something would break. He met her at the gate, words pouring out of him fast, desperate.
“This is terrible timing, Elsie! I’ve been called away urgently, I’m so sorry, you must think me the worst host alive.”
He lied that he was heading to town to see a man named Chambers, that Chambers was leaving for Brighton that afternoon, that he’d just got the letter that morning. He even led her the wrong direction away from the bus stop first, before she gently reminded him the route to town ran the other way. He laughed too loud at his own stupidity, and walked her towards the terminus, shivering so hard his bones ached, all the while thanking every god he could name that he had stopped her before she crossed the line of the house gate.

No one on the top of the open bus would have guessed that the pale, coatless man sitting beside Elsie was fighting not to pass out from relief that he had kept her out of the danger waiting inside his four walls. He even insisted the raw, gusty wind was too warm, that he was not cold at all.
He tried to ease the conversation to the lie he had been turning over in his head, that he was leaving town the next day for a rest, that he would send for her when he got back.
She only looked straight ahead, past the bus driver, and said, “I know.”
He jolted. “You know what?”
“You’re not going anywhere.” Her voice was even, steady. “You never were planning to go out this morning. You only ran out the second you saw me turn into the square. Don’t act like we’re strangers, Paul.”
He had no words. He noticed the wind had stained her cheeks the bright pink of forced rhubarb, and he could not meet her eyes.
“You ought to leave, you know,” she went on, quiet. “You don’t look in the mirror much these days, do you? People keep turning to stare at you in the street. You look half gone. But you won’t go. I know why.”
He shivered, coughed to hide the tremor in his chest, snapped that there was no use discussing it if she already thought she knew everything.
“You’re right about the gossip, about Barrett,” she said, “but you could handle Barrett. This is bigger than him. I promised you I wouldn’t name it, so I won’t. That’s why I came out today, though. To force your hand. We can’t keep going on like this. It’s over now.”
“Over?” he repeated stupidly.
“I’m not going to pester you. I won’t show up uninvited, I won’t hover, I won’t make you uncomfortable with whatever expectations you think I might have.” She held out a cold hand to him, as the bus slowed to his stop. “I only have one rule. If I need you, you don’t have to spare me a single thought. But if you need me, Paul? You will need me, sooner than you think. I will know. And I will come for you, no matter what it costs.”
He could only groan, numb, as he climbed down onto the kerb. The bus pulled away a second later. He watched Elsie’s figure shrink into the distance through the smudged window, and for the first time in all the years he had known her, she did not smile, did not wave her long arm at him as she left. He stood in the gusting wind, shivering, and turned back towards the square. The empty old house was waiting for him, hungry.
