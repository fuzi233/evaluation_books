Mr. Godfrey arrived with the punctuality of a clockwork mechanism, entering the room at the precise moment his name was announced. He was neither so close behind the servant as to startle us, nor so distant as to force an awkward pause and the opening of the door. In the seamless integration of his daily routine, the true Christian reveals himself; and this dear man was remarkably complete in his execution.

"Go to Miss Verinder," my aunt instructed the servant, "and inform her that Mr. Ablewhite has arrived."

Both of us immediately inquired after his health, asking in unison if he had recovered from the terrifying ordeal of the previous week. With impeccable tact, he managed to answer us simultaneously. Lady Verinder received his verbal response, while I was granted the benefit of his charming smile.

"What," he cried with infinite tenderness, "have I done to merit all this sympathy? My dear aunt! My dear Miss Clack! I have simply been mistaken for someone else. I was merely blindfolded, strangled, and thrown flat upon a thin carpet over a hard floor. Consider how much worse it could have been! I might have been murdered or robbed. What have I lost? Only 'Nervous Force'—a concept the law does not recognize as property. Strictly speaking, I have lost nothing. Had it been my choice, I would have kept this adventure to myself; I shrink from all this fuss and publicity. However, since Mr. Luker made his injuries public, mine have inevitably followed suit. I have become the property of the newspapers until the gentle reader grows weary of the subject. I am thoroughly sick of it myself. May the gentle reader soon share my sentiment! And how is dear Rachel? Still enjoying the gaieties of London? I am delighted to hear it. Miss Clack, I require all your indulgence. I am sadly behindhand with my Committee Work and my dear Ladies. Yet, I sincerely hope to visit the Mothers'-Small-Clothes next week. Did you make cheerful progress at Monday's Committee? Was the Board optimistic about future prospects? And are we adequately supplied with Trousers?"

The heavenly gentleness of his smile rendered his apologies irresistible, while the richness of his deep voice added an indescribable charm to the mundane inquiry regarding our trousers. In truth, we were far too well-provided; we were completely overwhelmed. I was on the verge of confirming this when the door opened again, introducing an element of worldly disturbance in the person of Miss Verinder.

She approached Mr. Godfrey with a speed that was decidedly unladylike, her hair shockingly disheveled and her face flushed in a manner I would find unbecoming.

"I am charmed to see you, Godfrey," she said, addressing him with the casual familiarity of one young man speaking to another. "I wish you had brought Mr. Luker along. You and he are, as long as our present excitement lasts, the two most fascinating men in all London. It is morbid to say so; it is unhealthy; it is precisely the sort of thing a well-regulated mind like Miss Clack's instinctively shudders at. Never mind that. Tell me the entire story of Northumberland Street directly. I suspect the newspapers have omitted details."

Even the angelic Mr. Godfrey shares the fallen nature inherited from Adam—it is a small portion of our human legacy, alas, but he possesses it. I confess it pained me to see him take Rachel's hand in both of his own and lay it softly against the left side of his waistcoat. It was a direct encouragement to her reckless manner of speech and her insolent reference to me.

"Dearest Rachel," he replied in the same voice that had previously thrilled me when discussing our prospects and trousers, "the newspapers have informed you of everything—and they have done so far better than I could."

"Godfrey thinks we all make too much of the matter," my aunt interjected. "He has just stated that he cares not to speak of it."

"Why?"

She posed the question with a sudden flash in her eyes and a sharp upward glance at Mr. Godfrey. On his side, he looked down at her with an indulgence so injudicious and undeserved that I felt compelled to intervene.

"Rachel, darling!" I remonstrated gently, "true greatness and true courage are ever modest."

"You are a very good fellow in your way, Godfrey," she said, ignoring me entirely and continuing to address her cousin as if they were peers. "But I am quite certain you are not great; I do not believe you possess any extraordinary courage. And I am firmly persuaded that if you ever possessed any modesty, your lady-worshippers relieved you of that virtue many years ago. You have some private reason for avoiding discussion of your Northumberland Street adventure, and I intend to discover it."

"My reason is the simplest imaginable and the most easily acknowledged," he answered, enduring her with patience. "I am tired of the subject."

"You are tired of the subject? My dear Godfrey, I am about to make a remark."

"What is it?"

"You live too much in the company of women. Consequently, you have contracted two very bad habits. You have learned to speak nonsense seriously, and you have developed a habit of telling fibs for the sheer pleasure of it. You cannot walk straight with your lady-worshippers. I intend to make you walk straight with *me*. Come, sit down. I am brimful of direct questions, and I expect you to be brimful of direct answers."

She physically dragged him across the room to a chair by the window, ensuring the light would illuminate his face. I deeply regret the necessity of reporting such language and describing such conduct. Yet, hemmed in as I am between Mr. Franklin Blake's cheque on one side and my own sacred regard for truth on the other, what am I to do? I glanced at my aunt. She sat unmoved, seemingly devoid of any inclination to interfere. I had never witnessed such torpor in her before. Perhaps it was the reaction following the trying time she endured in the country. Regardless of the cause, it was an unpleasant symptom to note in dear Lady Verinder, considering her age and her autumnal exuberance of figure.

Meanwhile, Rachel had settled herself at the window with our amiable, forbearing—and too forbearing—Mr. Godfrey. She launched into her string of questions, paying no more attention to her mother or myself than if we were not present.

"Have the police done anything, Godfrey?"

"Nothing whatever."

"It is certain, I suppose, that the three men who laid the trap for you were the same three who later trapped Mr. Luker?"

"Humanly speaking, my dear Rachel, there can be no doubt of it."

"And not a trace of them has been discovered?"

"Not a trace."

"It is thought—is it not?—that these three men are the three Indians who visited our house in the country."

"Some people think so."

"Do you think so?"

"My dear Rachel, they blindfolded me before I could see their faces. I know nothing whatsoever of the matter. How can I offer an opinion?"

Even the angelic gentleness of Mr. Godfrey was beginning to falter under the relentless persecution he faced. Whether unbridled curiosity or ungovernable dread drove Miss Verinder's questions, I do not presume to inquire. I merely report that when Mr. Godfrey attempted to rise after giving his answer, she seized him by the shoulders and pushed him back into his chair. Oh, do not claim this was immodest! Do not even suggest that the recklessness of guilty terror alone accounts for such behavior! We must not judge others. My Christian friends, indeed, indeed, indeed, we must not judge others!

She continued her interrogation, unabashed. Earnest biblical students might recall, as I did, the blinded children of the devil who proceeded with their orgies unabashed in the days before the Flood.

"I want to know something about Mr. Luker, Godfrey."

"I am again unfortunate, Rachel. No man knows less of Mr. Luker than I do."

"You never saw him before you met him accidentally at the bank?"

"Never."

"You have seen him since?"

"Yes. We have been examined together, as well as separately, to assist the police."

"Mr. Luker was robbed of a receipt from his banker—was he not? What was the receipt for?"

"For a valuable gem he had placed in the bank's safekeeping."

"That is what the newspapers say. It may suffice for the general reader, but it is insufficient for me. The banker's receipt must have specified what the gem was?"

"The banker's receipt, Rachel—as I have heard it described—mentioned nothing of the kind. A valuable gem belonging to Mr. Luker; deposited by him; sealed with his seal; and to be released only upon his personal application. That was the form, and that is all I know."

She paused after he finished, looking at her mother and sighing, then returning her gaze to Mr. Godfrey.

"Some of our private affairs at home," she said, "appear to have leaked into the newspapers?"

"I grieve to say, it is so."

"And some idle strangers are attempting to trace a connection between the events at our Yorkshire house and what has occurred since in London?"

"The public curiosity, in certain quarters, is, I fear, taking that turn."

"The people who claim the three men who ill-treated you and Mr. Luker were the three Indians also assert that the valuable gem—"

She stopped abruptly. Gradually, over the last few moments, her face had grown whiter and whiter. The extraordinary blackness of her hair made this pallor so ghastly that we all feared she would faint at the moment she checked herself mid-sentence. Dear Mr. Godfrey made a second attempt to leave his chair. My aunt entreated her to say no more. I followed suit with a modest medicinal peace-offering in the form of a bottle of salts. None of us achieved the slightest effect on her.

"Godfrey, stay where you are," she commanded. "Mamma, there is no reason to be alarmed about me. Clack, you're dying to hear the end of it—I won't faint, expressly to oblige *you*."

These were the exact words she used, recorded in my diary the moment I returned home. But, oh, let us not judge! My Christian friends, let us not judge!

She turned once more to Mr. Godfrey. With an obstinacy dreadful to witness, she returned to the point where she had halted and completed her question:

"I spoke to you a minute ago about rumors in certain quarters. Tell me plainly, Godfrey, do any of them say that Mr. Luker's valuable gem is—the Moonstone?"

As the name of the Indian Diamond passed her lips, I perceived a transformation in my admirable friend. His complexion darkened. He lost the genial suavity of manner that was one of his greatest charms. A noble indignation animated his reply.

"They *do* say it," he answered. "There are people who do not hesitate to accuse Mr. Luker of lying to serve some private interest. He has repeatedly declared that, until this scandal assailed him, he had never even heard of the Moonstone. And these vile people reply, without a shadow of proof, that he has reasons for concealment; we refuse to believe him on his oath. Shameful! Shameful!"

Rachel looked at him strangely—I cannot adequately describe the expression—while he spoke. When he finished, she said, "Considering that Mr. Luker is merely a chance acquaintance of yours, you take up his cause, Godfrey, rather warmly."

My gifted friend offered one of the most truly evangelical responses I have ever heard.

"I hope, Rachel, I take up the cause of all oppressed people rather warmly," he said.

The tone in which those words were spoken might have melted a stone. But, oh dear, what is the hardness of stone? Nothing compared to the hardness of the unregenerate human heart! She sneered. I blush to record it—she sneered at him in his face.

"Keep your noble sentiments for your Ladies' Committees, Godfrey. I am certain the scandal that has assailed Mr. Luker has not spared you."

Even my aunt's torpor was roused by those words.

"My dear Rachel," she remonstrated, "you have really no right to say that!"

"I mean no harm, mamma—I mean good. Have a moment's patience, and you will see."

She looked back at Mr. Godfrey with what appeared to be sudden pity. She went the very unladylike length of taking his hand.

"I am certain," she said, "that I have discovered the true reason for your unwillingness to speak of this matter before my mother and before me. An unlucky accident has associated you in people's minds with Mr. Luker. You have told me what scandal says of *him*. What does scandal say of *you*?"

Even at the eleventh hour, dear Mr. Godfrey—always ready to return good for evil—tried to spare her.

"Don't ask me!" he said. "It's better forgotten, Rachel—it is, indeed."

"I *will* hear it!" she cried fiercely, at the top of her voice.

"Tell her, Godfrey!" my aunt entreated. "Nothing can do her such harm as your silence is doing now!"

Mr. Godfrey's fine eyes filled with tears. He cast one last appealing look at her—and then spoke the fatal words:

"If you insist, Rachel—scandal says that the Moonstone is in pledge to Mr. Luker, and that I am the man who pawned it."

She sprang to her feet with a scream. She looked backward and forward from Mr. Godfrey to my aunt, and from my aunt to Mr. Godfrey, in such a frantic manner that I genuinely believed she had gone mad.

"Don't speak to me! Don't touch me!" she exclaimed, shrinking back from all of us (I declare like a hunted animal!) into a corner of the room. "This is my fault! I must set it right.
