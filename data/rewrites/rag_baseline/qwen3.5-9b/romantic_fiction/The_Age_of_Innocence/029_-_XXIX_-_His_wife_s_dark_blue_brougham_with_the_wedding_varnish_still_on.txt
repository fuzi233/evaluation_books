The dark blue brougham, still bearing the glossy sheen of its wedding varnish, waited at the ferry landing. It carried Archer to the Pennsylvania terminus in Jersey City, a vehicle of luxury amidst a sombre, snowy afternoon. Gas lamps flickered inside the cavernous station, casting long shadows against the reverberating walls. On the platform, pacing before the arrival of the Washington Express, Archer recalled the brotherhood of visionaries who prophesied a tunnel beneath the Hudson, allowing trains to surge directly into New York. They also foresaw transatlantic crossings in five days, flying machines, electric light, and wireless telephony—Arabian Nights made manifest.

"I do not care which of these fantasies becomes real," Archer mused, "provided the tunnel remains unbuilt."

In a childish ecstasy, he visualized Madame Olenska's emergence from the train. He imagined spotting her distant among the sea of indifferent faces, her hand finding his as he guided her toward the carriage. Their journey would proceed slowly past swaying horses, laden carts, and shouting teamsters, culminating in the stark silence of the ferry boat. There, seated side by side in the motionless carriage, they would watch the earth glide away beneath them, rolling toward the other side of the sun.

The number of things he needed to say to her multiplied in his mind, arranging themselves in an eloquent, desperate order. Then, the clanging and groaning of the approaching train grew louder. It lumbered into the station like a beast burdened with prey entering its den. Archer pushed forward, elbowing through the crowd, peering blindly into the windows of the high-hung cars.

Suddenly, he saw her. Madame Olenska's pale, surprised face filled his view, close enough to trigger the mortifying realization that he had forgotten what she truly looked like. They met; their hands joined, and he drew her arm through his.

"This way—I have the carriage," he said.

Everything unfolded exactly as he had dreamed. He assisted her into the brougham with her luggage, retaining only a vague memory of reassuring her regarding her grandmother and summarizing the Beaufort situation. Struck by the softness of her presence, he thought, *Poor Regina!*

Meanwhile, the carriage navigated its way out of the station's chaotic coil, crawling down the slippery incline toward the wharf. It was menaced by swaying coal-carts, bewildered horses, disheveled express-wagons, and an empty hearse looming nearby.

"Oh, that hearse!" she whispered, shutting her eyes as it passed and clutching Archer's hand. "If only it does not mean... poor Granny!"

"Oh, no, no—she is much better. She is quite alright, truly. There—we have passed it!" he exclaimed, as though the statement alone could alter reality.

Her hand remained in his. As the carriage lurched across the gangplank onto the ferry, Archer bent forward, unbuttoned her tight brown glove, and kissed her palm with the reverence due a relic. She withdrew with a faint smile.

"You did not expect me today?"

"Oh, no."

"I intended to go to Washington to see you. I had made all my arrangements—I nearly missed you on the train."

"Oh—" she exclaimed, as if terrified by the narrowness of their escape. "Do you know—I hardly remembered you?"

"Hardly remembered me?"

"I mean: how shall I explain? I—it happens every time. Each time you happen to me all over again."

"Oh, yes: I know! I know!"

"Does it—to you?" he insisted.

She nodded, gazing out the window.

"Ellen—Ellen—Ellen!"

She offered no response. He sat in silence, watching her profile blur against the snow-streaked dusk beyond the glass. What had she done in those four long months? How little they truly knew of one another! Precious moments slipped away, yet he had forgotten everything he meant to say, helpless to brood over the mystery of their simultaneous proximity and remoteness—a paradox symbolized by their closeness in the carriage and their inability to see each other's faces clearly.

"What a lovely carriage! Is it May's?" she asked abruptly, turning from the window.

"Yes."

"It was May who sent you to fetch me, then? How kind of her!"

Archer paused, then spoke explosively: "Your husband's secretary visited me the day after we met in Boston."

In his brief letter, he had omitted mention of M. Rivière's visit, intending to bury the incident within himself. Yet, the knowledge that they occupied his wife's carriage provoked an impulse of retaliation. He would test whether she preferred his reference to Rivière as much as she had preferred hers to May.

As on previous occasions when he sought to disrupt her composure, she showed no sign of surprise. Immediately, he concluded: "He writes to her, then."

"M. Rivière went to see you?"

"Yes: did you not know?"

"No," she answered simply.

"And you are not surprised?"

She hesitated. "Why should I be? He told me in Boston that he knew you; that he had met you in England, I think."

"Ellen—I must ask you one thing."

"Yes."

"I wanted to ask it after seeing him, but I could not put it in a letter. Was it Rivière who helped you escape when you left your husband?"

His heart beat with suffocating intensity. Would she meet this question with the same calm?

"Yes: I owe him a great debt," she replied, her quiet voice devoid of tremor.

Her tone was so natural, so almost indifferent, that Archer's turmoil subsided. Once more, her sheer simplicity had rendered him stupidly conventional just as he believed he was discarding convention.

"I think you are the most honest woman I have ever met!" he exclaimed.

"Oh, no—but perhaps one of the least fussy," she answered, a smile in her voice. "Call it what you like: you look at things as they are."

"Ah—I have had to. I have had to look at the Gorgon."

"Well—it has not blinded you! You have seen that she is merely an old bogey like all the others."

"She does not blind one; but she dries up one's tears."

The answer halted the pleading rising in Archer's throat; it seemed to emanate from depths of experience beyond his reach. The ferry boat's slow advance ceased, and her bow bumped violently against the piles of the slip, causing the brougham to stagger and throw Archer and Madame Olenska against one another.

Trembling, the young man felt the pressure of her shoulder and passed his arm around her.

"If you are not blind, then you must see that this cannot last."

"What cannot?"

"Our being together—and not together."

"No. You ought not to have come today," she said, her voice altered. Suddenly, she turned, threw her arms around him, and pressed her lips to his.

At that exact moment, the carriage began to move, and a gas lamp at the head of the slip flashed its light into the window. She drew away, and they sat silent and motionless while the brougham struggled through the congestion of vehicles at the ferry landing.

As they gained the street, Archer began to speak hurriedly. "Do not be afraid of me: you need not squeeze yourself back into your corner like that. A stolen kiss is not what I desire. Look: I am not even attempting to touch the sleeve of your jacket. Do not suppose that I do not understand your reasons for not wishing to let this feeling between us dwindle into an ordinary, clandestine affair. I could not have spoken thus yesterday, because when we were apart, and I looked forward to seeing you, every thought burned in a great flame. But then you arrive; and you are so much more than I remembered, and what I want of you is so much more than an hour or two every now and then, separated by wastes of thirsty waiting, that I can sit perfectly still beside you, like this, with that other vision in my mind, quietly trusting it to come true."

For a moment, she made no reply, then asked, barely above a whisper: "What do you mean by trusting it to come true?"

"Why—you know it will, don't you?"

"Your vision of you and me together?"

She burst into a sudden, hard laugh. "You choose your place well to put it to me!"

"Do you mean because we are in my wife's brougham? Shall we step out and walk, then? I do not suppose you mind a little snow?"

She laughed again, more gently. "No; I shall not step out and walk, because my business is to reach Granny as quickly as possible. And you will sit beside me, and we will look, not at visions, but at realities."

"I do not know what you mean by realities. The only reality to me is this."

She met his words with a long silence. During this pause, the carriage rolled down an obscure side street and turned into the searching illumination of Fifth Avenue.

"Is it your idea, then, that I should live with you as your mistress—since I cannot be your wife?" she asked.

The crudeness of the question startled him; the word was one women of his class avoided, even when their conversation drifted closest to the subject.
