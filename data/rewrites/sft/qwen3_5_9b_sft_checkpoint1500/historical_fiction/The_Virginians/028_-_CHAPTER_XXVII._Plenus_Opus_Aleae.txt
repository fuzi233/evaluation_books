"Tell me about those children, Harry—the ones I saw darting about the house where they sheltered you after your terrible fall?" Maria asked, pacing alongside him along the common. "Oh, that fall! "The people are the kindest souls I've ever encountered," Mr. Warrington exclaimed, his voice brimming with emotion. "Mrs. Lambert was a childhood friend of my mother's, and Colonel Lambert is a man of immense distinction, a veteran of Scotland, Flanders, and Minorca. Harry shoved his hand deep into his pocket, grasping the crisp, heavy folds of cash earned from his recent gambling sprees with Morris and March.

"We could visit the toy shop, my dear, and purchase dolls for the children," Lady Maria suggested gently. "Offering coin for their charity would surely insult their generosity."

"Dolls for Hester and Theo!" Harry scoffed, a flicker of irritation crossing his features. "Do you truly believe a woman ceases to be a woman at forty? Miss Hester is hardly a child, and Miss Theo is years older. They speak multiple languages fluently. Their libraries are vast, filled with volumes upon volumes. They master the harpsichord and sing with exquisite harmony, with Theo composing her own melodies."

"I admit, I barely noticed them. I assumed they were mere children. I had no notion they possessed such refinement or were anything less than ordinary."

"That is precisely why women are so tedious!" Harry declared, laughing. "Back home, if George or I praise a woman, Mrs. Esmond or Mountain will immediately find a flaw."

"I would never fault anyone who treats you with such kindness, Mr. Warrington," Maria sighed, her tone softening. "Though I envy them deeply—for they cared for you when you were broken and ill, while I..." She trailed off, looking away. "...while I had to abandon you."

"You dear, good Maria!" he said, reaching out to comfort her.

"No, Harry! Stop it. You need not be so persistent. Look!" She pointed toward the street. "There goes your black man, striding among a dozen others in liveried servitude. These wretched creatures are heading to the tea-garden to become as drunk as their masters. That dreadful Mr. Morris was already staggering when I left you, frightening you half to death."

"I had just secured massive wagers against both of them," Harry announced, his mood instantly lifting. "What shall I buy you, my dear cousin?" He recounted his recent triumphs, his laughter ringing out, a hint of boastfulness coloring his speech. "For the honor of Virginia, I resolved to demonstrate what true leaping entails. Maria, delighted by her young champion's success, leaned in. "But beware, child. Be cautious about play. It has ruined our entire family line. My brother Castlewood, Will, our poor father, even Lady Castlewood herself—all fell victim to it. And as for my Lord March, he is the most voracious gambler and the most successful player of all the nobility."

"I have no intention of fearing him, nor his companion, Jack Morris," Harry replied, thumbing the gold in his palm. "What games do you enjoy at Aunt Bernstein's? Cribbage, all-fours, brag, whist, commerce, piquet, quadrille? I am proficient at any of them. Does that clock strike seven?"

"And you wish to commence immediately?" Maria's voice held a note of gentle reproach. "You seem to have forgotten your poor cousin. You used to enjoy strolling with me not long ago."

"Hah! Youth *is* youth, cousin!" Harry tossed his head back, strutting confidently beside her. "A young man must have his fling!" He moved with an air of absolute certainty, buoyant and eager. Just recently, he had relished walking with her; the day prior, he had preferred the company of Theo, Hester, and Mrs. Lambert. Yet, the allure of pleasure, life, gaiety, the burning desire to shine and conquer, exerted a powerful pull on the lad. Like so many boys, he seized the cup without a thought for the migraine awaiting him at dawn.

While they conversed, the violins from the open orchestra on the Parade began a raucous tuning, a series of screeches preparing for the evening's concert. Maria sensed her aunt had awakened again; she knew she must return to her duties. Harry never inquired about this "slavery," though he likely understood its nature if he had paused to reflect. He offered no pity for his cousin. His mind was entirely elsewhere. Yet, when disaster had struck him, she had suffered a wound far deeper and more cruel. She had scarcely lost sight of him, a fact she buried beneath layers of smiling hypocrisy, a necessary armor for a woman of her station. *I know,* thought the narrator, addressing the reader, *that you consider her an old fool?* Indeed, do you suppose fools' caps fail to conceal gray hair as effectively as jet or auburn? Treat our elderly *fredaines* gently, O Minerva of a woman! Perhaps you are so wise you avoid novels entirely. But observe this: late crops of wild oats grow as readily as early harvests, and from personal experience, I posit that *avena fatua* reaches maturity right up until the final days of the year.

Much like worldly parents eager to shed a troublesome child to attend their own social engagements, Madame Bernstein and her staff had prematurely laid the sun to sleep, drawing the curtains shut while daylight lingered. They busied themselves with cards, candles, tea, and negus. Ladies arrived one by one, seated in chairs that landed them at the Baroness's door, adorned in paint, patches, and brocade. Gentlemen followed in gala attire. Mr. Poellnitz boasted the largest star and the most embroidered coat of the assembly.

Lord March and Ruglen, however, were transformed. Gone was the disheveled figure Harry had met at the White Horse; now he wore a neatly curled feather top, powdered hair, and a sumptuous French suit, replacing his jockey's garb. Jack Morris mirrored this transformation with a similar wig and a suit of fine stuff that closely resembled his lordship's. Mr. Wolfe attended his beautiful mistress, Miss Lowther, alongside her aunt, a card-loving matron beloved by all.

When Lady Maria Esmond entered, her appearance defied Madame Bernstein's previous descriptions. Her form was striking, her dress revealing much of her figure. Nature had gifted her with an exceeding fair complexion, accentuated by a dark, frilled ribbon jeweled around her neck, enhancing its snow-like whiteness. Her cheeks lacked the flush common to the other ladies, whose roses were openly purchased from perfumeries. Artful patches, it was whispered, augmented her charm. Her hoops were modest, no larger than the iron contrivances fashionable today; while absurd in some aspects, the costume was, in general, pleasing.

As for the Baroness de Bernstein, despite her grand toilette, she appeared magnificent yet melancholy, bordering on terrifying. One reads the past in her aged visage. The fire had gone out of her eyes, replaced by crow's-feet that puckered around them. They flashed no longer with scorn, anger, or love; they merely gazed, unmelting, undazzling. To the young reader who finds herself outnumbered at the ball by peers like the peerless Lindamira, console yourself: at fifty, you will look as kind and pleasant as she appears now at eighteen. You need not relinquish your carriage of beauty nor endure the depreciation of your smiles. You will not see fashion abandon your quarter, leaving your saloons dusty and your windows plastered with "to Let" signs. You may lack grandeur, but you will avoid bankruptcy.

"Our hostess," Lord Chesterfield whispered confidentially to a friend, unaware of the volume of his words, "reminds me of Covent Garden in my youth. Then it was the court end of town, inhabited by high fashion. Now, a nobleman's house is nothing more than a gaming-house, or you may enter with a friend to order a bottle."

"A bottle and a tavern are virtues in their own right," Lord March shrugged. "I was not born before the Georges ascended, yet I intend to live to a hundred. I knew the Bernstein only as an old woman; if she ever possessed beauty, hang me if I know how she squandered it."

"How did she spend it?" Jack Morris laughed.

"Here is a table! Sit down and play. Do not let the Frenchman join; he will not pay. Mr. Warrington, will you take a card?"
