I write these lines—from India, in the heat and dust of war—to my family back in England. My purpose is simple: to explain why I have refused John Herncastle’s hand in friendship. The silence I’ve kept until now has been mistaken for coldness or pride by those whose judgment I value. I ask them—respectfully, urgently—to withhold their conclusions until they have read what follows. And I swear upon my honour that every word I am about to set down is true, as strictly and literally as truth can be told.

The rift between my cousin and me began not in private quarrel, but in the midst of one of history’s most dramatic military campaigns—the storming of Seringapatam on May 4th, 1799, under General Baird. To understand what happened, I must take you back to the days before the assault, when our camp was alive with stories of hidden treasure buried within the Sultan’s palace.

One tale, more fantastical than the rest, spoke of a Yellow Diamond—no mere jewel, but a relic of ancient Indian legend. According to tradition, it once adorned the forehead of a four-armed deity representing the Moon. Its colour alone marked it as extraordinary; but more than that, it was said to respond to the phases of the moon—brightening when the moon waxed, dimming when it waned. From this belief came its name: **the Moonstone**.

Such superstitions were not unknown in antiquity. In ancient Greece and Rome, similar myths surrounded a translucent stone believed to reflect lunar power—though never one consecrated to a god, as this diamond was. Still, the idea endured, and so did the name, carried forward into modern times by collectors who still speak of “moonstones” today.

But the story of this particular gem goes much deeper. It begins in the eleventh century, when Mahmoud of Ghizni invaded India, sacked the sacred temple at Somnauth, and stripped it bare. All gods were desecrated—except one. The moon-god, protected by three devoted Brahmins, escaped with the Yellow Diamond still embedded in his brow. By night, they carried him across the land to Benares, the second holiest city in India. There, in a new shrine of gold and gems, the deity was reinstalled.

On the night the shrine was completed, Vishnu—the Preserver—appeared to the Brahmins in a dream. He breathed divine breath upon the Diamond, and commanded that it be guarded without cease, day and night, by three priests in turn, generation after generation. He warned that whoever stole the stone would bring ruin upon themselves and their descendants. The prophecy was inscribed in golden letters above the shrine’s gates.

And so it was. For centuries, the guardians watched. Until Aurungzebe, Emperor of the Moguls, ordered the temples destroyed. The shrine was defiled, the images shattered, and the Moonstone seized by a high-ranking officer. But the priests followed in secret, watching through disguise and danger. The stone passed from hand to hand among lawless men, each bearing its curse. Yet the descendants of the original three remained vigilant, waiting for the moment when fate would restore the gem.

Time passed. The Diamond came into the hands of Tippoo Sultan of Seringapatam. He had it set into the hilt of a dagger, which he placed among his most prized weapons. Even then, in the heart of the Sultan’s palace, the three guardian priests still watched—in secret, disguised as members of Tippoo’s household. Three trusted servants, strangers to the others, who had won favour by pretending to convert to Islam—these were the ones the people whispered about.

That was the tale circulating in our camp—wild, improbable, yet deeply compelling to some. Most dismissed it as myth. But not my cousin. John Herncastle, ever drawn to the marvellous, believed it wholeheartedly. On the night before the attack, he flew into a rage when we laughed at the story. We called it fiction. He insisted it was real. And in his arrogance, he declared—before all of us—that if the English took Seringapatam, he would wear the Moonstone on his finger.

We roared with laughter. We thought nothing more of it. We were wrong.

At dawn, the assault began. I lost sight of Herncastle early. I crossed the river, planted the flag in the first breach, fought through the ditch, and entered the town. But I did not see him again until dusk, when the city lay in ruins and Tippoo’s body had been found beneath a pile of corpses.

General Baird had sent detachments to prevent looting. I was assigned to one such party. So was Herncastle. We met in the courtyard outside the royal treasury, where chaos reigned. Soldiers had broken in through an unguarded door and filled their packs with gold and jewels. The camp followers were worse—drunk, reckless, out of control.

It was there, trying to restore order, that I heard a scream—a blood-curdling yell—from the other side of the courtyard. I ran toward it, fearing another outbreak of violence. I reached an open doorway and saw two dead Indians—by their uniforms, palace officers—lying across the threshold.

Then came another cry from inside. I burst into a room that served as an armoury. A third Indian, mortally wounded, was collapsing at the feet of a man with his back turned. The man turned just as I entered.

It was John Herncastle.

In one hand, a torch. In the other, a dagger dripping with blood. At the end of the hilt, a stone—set like a pommel—flashed in the firelight, glowing like molten flame.

The dying man raised a trembling finger toward the dagger and spoke in Hindi:

Then he fell dead.

Before I could react, the soldiers behind me surged in. Herncastle, wild-eyed, bellowed at them:
“Clear the room! Set a guard on the door!”

I stationed two of my own men to watch the entrance. Then I waited—watching, listening, wondering.

Later, when the plunder continued unchecked, General Baird issued a public decree—beat of drum—declaring that any soldier caught stealing would be hanged. The provost-marshal stood ready to enforce it.

That was when I saw Herncastle again. He offered his hand as usual. “Good morning,” he said.

I hesitated.
“Tell me,” I said, “how the Indian died. What did those last words mean—when he pointed to your dagger?”

He replied calmly:
“The man died from a mortal wound. As for his words—I don’t know more than you do.”

I studied him closely. The fury of the previous day had vanished. He seemed composed, even detached. But I knew better.

“I suppose that’s all?” I asked.

“That’s all,” he said.

I turned away. And since that moment, I have not spoken to him.

Let it be understood: what I write here is meant only for the family. I do not intend to accuse Herncastle publicly—there is no proof. I cannot say I saw him kill the two men at the door. I cannot prove he killed the third. I only heard the dying man’s words—and even if I believe them, they could be interpreted as delirium.

Still, I cannot forget what I saw. And though I reject the Indian legends, I confess—I feel something else. A conviction, perhaps a superstition: that crime brings its own doom. I believe Herncastle is guilty. And I fear—deeply—that if he keeps the Moonstone, he will live to regret it. If he gives it away, others will suffer for taking it.

Let my relatives judge for themselves. Whether I am right or wrong, I stand by my decision. The past cannot be undone—but the future may yet be shaped by truth.
