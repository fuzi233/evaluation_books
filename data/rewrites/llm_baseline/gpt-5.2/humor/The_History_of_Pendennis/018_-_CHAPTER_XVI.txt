Pen’s escapade—his furious notions of honour, his challenge, his whip-hand heroics—could no more be kept private than the smell of onions in a snug parlour. The whole neighbourhood had it in its nostrils by breakfast. Doctor Portman took the news as if Pen had flung a loaded sauce-boat at the Ten Commandments. Major Pendennis took it as if it were a little after-dinner story—highly improper, certainly, but seasoned and therefore amusing. Mrs. Pendennis took it as if the roof had fallen into the pudding.

To hear that her boy had been “violent,” “unchristian,” “ferocious in intention,” was to poor Helen like hearing that the bread had been dropped in the dirt and then served up with a smile. Every consequence she could imagine—discomfort, disgrace, crime, all the black kitchen-smoke of calamity—seemed to roll out of that one foolish squabble; and, above all things, she hungered (with a mother’s desperate appetite) to have him out of Chatteris, away from the actress who had brought so much fever into their quiet house.

Pen, when remonstrated with—when his mother pleaded and the Doctor stormed—assumed that grave, inflated earnestness which youth wears as solemnly as a new coat.

He was “very sorry,” he said, with the air of a man signing a treaty; but the insult, he protested, had come upon him “without provocation.” He would permit no man to insult him “upon this head” without vindicating his honour; and then, appealing to his uncle, demanded with great dignity whether he could possibly have acted otherwise “as a gentleman” than by resenting the outrage and offering satisfaction.

“Vous allez trop vite, my good sir,” said Major Pendennis, who had been sprinkling the lad with his own old campaigning notions—pistols and punctilio, a little gunpowder in the gravy. The Major was puzzled now to find the dish served back to him, piping hot. “Between men of the world, I don’t say. But between two schoolboys—ridiculous, my dear boy—perfectly ridiculous.”

“It is extremely wicked,” sobbed Mrs. Pendennis, “and unlike my son.” Her eyes filled, and her voice had the bewildered tremble of one who has tasted salt where she expected sugar.

Pen kissed her, and answered with pompous tenderness, “Women, dear mother, don’t understand these matters. I put myself into Foker’s hands. I had no other course.”

Major Pendennis shrugged and grinned as if he had just heard a clever epigram at his club. The young ones were certainly making progress.

Mrs. Pendennis, however, declared Mr. Foker a wicked little wretch, and was sure he would lead her dear boy into mischief if Pen went to the same college with him. She even threatened, in that impulsive maternal fashion, that she had “a great mind” not to let him go at all—until she remembered, like a hand laid on hers, that Pen’s father had always destined the boy for the very college where he himself had once had his brief education. That recollection, like an old family recipe, had authority; and the veto melted.

Go he was to go, at the October term. The authorities who presided over Arthur’s welfare had settled it as firmly as if they were salting down a ham for winter. Foker promised introductions to the right set; and Major Pendennis laid store on those introductions as a careful housekeeper lays store on knowing which butcher supplies the best joints. The Major, who could recite noble names as some men recite sauces, ran them off with relish.

“Mr. Foker knows the very best young men,” he said. “Pen will form acquaintances of the greatest advantage through life. The young Marquis of Plinlimmon is there—eldest son of the Duke of Saint David’s. Lord Magnus Charters is there—Lord Runnymede’s son, and first cousin of Mr. Foker. Lady Runnymede—Lady Agatha Milton—you remember. Lady Agnes will certainly invite him to Logwood. And far from being alarmed at his intimacy with her son—a singular and humorous, but most prudent and amiable young man—to whom we are under every obligation for his admirable conduct in the affair of the Fotheringay marriage—I look upon it as one of the luckiest things possible that Pen should have formed an intimacy with this most amusing young gentleman.”

Helen sighed. She supposed the Major knew best; he spoke of society as if it were a grand dinner at which one must be properly placed. Mr. Foker had been kind in the wretched Miss Costigan business; for that she was grateful. Yet she could not help a dim presentiment of evil, as one hears a far-off cart-wheel on a night road and knows it will soon be at the door. Quarrels, riots, “worldliness”—those harsh sounds, those jangling modern noises of the soul—frightened her for her boy.

Doctor Portman, for his part, was decidedly of opinion that Pen should go. He hoped the lad would read; he allowed a moderate indulgence in the best society too (as if prescribing wine by the glass). He expected Pen would distinguish himself; Smirke spoke very highly of his proficiency. The Doctor had heard him construe, and thought he acquitted himself remarkably well. Anyhow, to get him out of Chatteris was a great point; and Pen, distracted from private grief by public rows, said gloomily that he would obey.

Meanwhile, August and September brought assizes, races, dinners, visits—the whole clatter of county life, with its carriages and its clinking glasses and its perpetual talk, as if talk were the only harvest the gentry reaped. Miss Fotheringay continued to act and to take her farewells at the Chatteris Theatre. Her announced departure moved nobody very much except the few persons already named; and the polite county folks, who had London houses and could admire her there—when Fashion had first told them to—found nothing remarkable in the actress on the little Chatteris boards. Many a genius and many a quack have been chilled by that same provincial draught before and since.

Miss Costigan bore public neglect, and any other crosses, with a comfortable equanimity. She ate, drank, acted, slept, with the regularity of a person whose nature is like a well-managed kitchen range: always warm, always ready, never much disturbed by storms outside. What grief and harmful excitement a healthy dulness and cheerful insensibility save! One does not say Virtue is not Virtue because it was never tempted; only that dulness is a finer gift than we credit, and some are lucky whom Nature has endowed with a good store of that great anodyne.

Pen went drearily in and out of the play according to his fancy. His doings tortured his mother; her anxiety would often have led her to interfere, but Major Pendennis checked and encouraged her by turns, for the wily man fancied he saw a favourable change in the lad’s malady. One symptom pleased him exceedingly: an efflux of versification, violent as a boil-over.

Pen might be heard spouting lines in the shrubbery, or muttering them between his teeth at night among the home party, as if he were chewing rhymes like tough meat. One day, prowling about the house in Pen’s absence, Major Pendennis found a great book full of verses in the lad’s study—English and Latin, quotations set out in scholarly foot-notes, like little garnishes placed with care around the main dish.

He can’t be very bad, thought the Pall-Mall Philosopher; and he made Pen’s mother remark—perhaps not without a secret disappointment, for she loved romance like other soft women—that during the last fortnight the young gentleman came home quite hungry to dinner and showed a very decent appetite at breakfast.

“Gad, I wish I could,” said the Major, thinking ruefully of his dinner pills. “The boy begins to sleep well, depend upon that.” Cruel—true.

Pen had no one to confide in. He could not speak to his mother of his loves and mortifications; his uncle treated such matters with a guarded, worldly scorn that jarred on the lad’s sentiment; Foker was much too coarse for refined secrets. So Pen’s friendship for the Curate redoubled: he was never tired of having Smirke for a listener on that one subject. What is a lover without a confidant? He employed Mr. Smirke as Corydon does the elm-tree—to cut the name upon, to make it echo. He made Smirke ring continually with the name of the beautiful Amaryllis. When men have left off playing the tune, they care little for the pipe; but Pen fancied he had a great friendship for Smirke because he could pour his sighs into the Curate’s ears—and Smirke had his own reasons for being always ready.

Pen’s affection gushed into sonnets to the “friend of his heart.” He plied Smirke with Latin Sapphics and Alcaics—hot little dishes of scholarship, served up with love-sauce. The songs multiplied; Smirke declared them beautiful and believed it too. Pen, in return, expressed boundless gratitude that Heaven had sent him such a friend. He presented his tutor with his best-bound books, with his gold guard-chain, and even wished him to take his double-barrelled gun. He went into Chatteris and got a gold pencil-case on credit—he had no money, and was still in debt to Smirke for some of the Fotheringay presents—and gave it to the Curate with an inscription proclaiming unalterable and eternal regard. Smirke, of course, was pleased.

But Smirke was naturally dismayed at the contemplated departure of his pupil. When Arthur should go, his occupation and delight would go too. What pretext would remain for daily visits to Fairoaks, for a kind word or glance from the lady there—those small mouthfuls of comfort which nourished him better than Madame Fribsby’s frugal dinners? Arthur gone, Smirke could only visit like any other acquaintance; little Laura could not learn the Catechism more than once a week. He had curled himself like ivy round Fairoaks, and now felt the gardener’s hand approaching.

Should he speak his mind to the widow—go down on his knees? He turned over her words and looks as a cook turns over remembered flavours. She had praised his sermons three weeks ago; she had thanked him exceedingly for his present of a melon for a little dinner-party Mrs. Pendennis gave—oh, a melon properly chosen, with its cool scent and honeyed heart, is no mean ambassador. She had said she should always be grateful for his kindness to Arthur; and when he declared there were “no bounds” to his love for that dear boy, she had replied in a romantic manner, indicating her strong gratitude and regard for all her son’s friends. Should he speak out—or delay? If he spoke and she refused him—awful!—the gate of Fairoaks might be shut for ever; and within that door lay all the world for Mr. Smirke.

Thus we see how every soul carries its own private business, more pressing than another’s sorrow. While Mrs. Pendennis fretted at losing her boy—her anxious hold upon him in the mother’s nest whence he was to fly—while the Major’s great soul chafed inwardly, thinking of London parties and dukes and duchesses, and of his being marooned in a country hole by “cursed affairs”—while Pen tossed between passion and another sensation, unacknowledged yet but swaying him: a longing to see the world—Mr. Smirke lay awake with his own care, like a ticking clock in a silent room, hearing every sound of his hope and fear. How lonely we are; how secret. Two people may press the same pillow forty years and still each has a world under their own hat—each hears different music in the same room, each tastes a different taste from the same dish, because experience has its own dialect and no dictionary can translate it cleanly. But let us return to the solitary Smirke.

Smirke had one confidante: that most injudicious woman, Madame Fribsby. How she became “Madame,” nobody knew. She had left Clavering as Miss Fribsby for a London milliner’s; she pretended she acquired her title in Paris. How the French king, were he ever so obliging, could have made her “Madame,” need not detain us. She went away a bouncing young lass; she returned rather elderly, with a Madonna front and melancholy countenance, bought Mrs. Harbottle’s business for a song, took her elderly mother to live with her, was good to the poor, constant at church, and had the best of characters. But no one in Clavering—not even Mrs. Portman—read so many novels. She had time for it (few besides the Rectory and Fairoaks employed her), and perpetual perusal of works not so moral then as now had made her sentimentally absurd. In her eyes life was one immense love-match; she could not see two people together without fancying they were dying for each other.

The day after Mrs. Pendennis’s visit to the Curate—long ago recorded—Madame Fribsby settled it in her mind that Mr. Smirke must be in love with the widow, and she set to encourage the passion on both sides, as if she were basting a roast and keeping the fire up.

Mrs. Pendennis she rarely saw except in public and in her pew. The widow needed little millinery; she made much of her own. But on the rare occasions when Madame Fribsby received a visit from her, or paid respects at Fairoaks, she never failed to praise the Curate: what an angelical man; how gentle, studious, lonely; and she would wonder, with a sigh ready-made from her novels, that no lady took pity on him.

Helen laughed at such remarks and wondered why Madame herself did not pity and console her lodger. Madame Fribsby shook her Madonna front. “Mong cure a boco souffare,” she said, laying her hand where she indicated her “cure.” “It est more en Espang,” she added with a sigh, proud of her French, which flowed more readily than it meant. Mrs. Pendennis had no desire to penetrate the secrets of this wounded heart. Reserved, perhaps proud, she considered her son’s tutor merely an attendant on that young prince—respectable as a clergyman, certainly, but still a dependant. Madame’s constant allusions were not agreeable; yet Madame persisted in the pernicious error that the widow had a secret regard for Mr. Smirke.

Her lodger, however, was much more willing to talk. Every time after Madame praised the Curate to Mrs. Pendennis, she came away with the notion that Mrs. Pendennis had been praising him in return.

“Etre soul au monde est bien ouneeyoung,” she would say, glancing up at a print of a French carbineer in green coat and brass cuirass that decorated her room, like an old label from her Paris fable. “Depend upon it, when Master Pendennis goes to College, his Ma will find herself very lonely. She is quite young yet—you wouldn’t suppose her five-and-twenty. Monsieur le Cury—song cure est touchy—j’ang suis sure—Je conny cela biang—Ally, Monsieur Smirke.”

He blushed softly; sighed; hoped; feared; doubted; sometimes yielded to the delicious idea. His pleasure was to sit in Madame Fribsby’s little apartment and talk it over, while the milliner ran on in French, and her old mother—deaf as an old kettle at the back of the stove—understood not one syllable. This secrecy, made by accident and bad pronunciation, was to Smirke like a hedge around a forbidden garden.

So when Major Pendennis at last announced to the Curate that his nephew would go to college in October, and that Mr. Smirke’s valuable services would no longer be needed—at which the Major, speaking as grandly as a lord, professed himself exceedingly grateful and besought the clergyman to command his interests in any way—Smirke felt the critical moment had come. The thought racked him like toothache in a quiet night.

Madame Fribsby, naturally, had taken the strongest interest in Master Pen’s love affair with Miss Fotheringay. She went to Chatteris, saw the actress perform, and pronounced her old and overrated; and then talked Master Pen’s passion to death in her shop with the half-dozen old maids and old women in male clothes who form the genteel population of little towns. Captain Glanders, H.P., declared Pen was going to be a devil of a fellow and had begun early. Mrs. Glanders bade him check his horrid observations and respect his own wife, if he pleased. She said it would be a lesson to Helen for her pride and absurd infatuation about that boy. Mrs. Pybus observed that many were proud of very small things, and for her part she did not know why an apothecary’s wife should give herself such airs. Mrs. Wapshot called her daughters away from that side of the street one day when Pen, mounted on Rebecca, was stopped at the saddler’s for a new lash to his whip. One and all made visits of curiosity to Fairoaks, tried to condole or to bring the Fotheringay affair on the tapis, and were checked by Mrs. Pendennis’s haughty reserve and the Major’s frigid politeness.

Rebuffs do not put an end to gossip; they only make it louder, like a door slammed on a noisy crowd. Slander increased about the unlucky family at Fairoaks. Glanders—half-pay and a large family—fuddled on brandy-and-water instead of claret since leaving the Dragoons, had occasional entrée at Fairoaks and kept the Major informed of every story in Clavering. Mrs. Pybus took an inside place by the coach to Chatteris and went to the George on purpose to get particulars. Mrs. Speers’s man treated Mr. Foker’s servant to drink at Baymouth for a similar purpose. It was said Pen had hanged himself in despair in the orchard and his uncle had cut him down; or, on the contrary, that Miss Costigan had been jilted and not young Arthur; and that the whole affair had been hushed up by payment of a large sum—the exact amount of which several people could testify, varying beautifully with the imagination and spite of the teller.

Pen shook his mane and raged when these scandals—touching Miss Costigan’s honour and his own—reached him. Why was not Pybus a man (she had whiskers enough), that he might call her out and shoot her? Seeing Simcoe pass by, Pen glared so from Rebecca and clutched his whip so menacingly that the clergyman went home and composed—or thought over, for he delivered oral testimony at great length—a sermon upon Jezebel, theatrical entertainments (a double cut, for Doctor Portman was known to frequent such), and youth going to perdition; and it was clear to every capacity that Pen was meant, and the road alluded to.

After the affair with Hobnell, Pen was pronounced a murderer as well as a profligate; his name became a byword and terror in Clavering. Nor was he the only one talked of. The village began to chatter about his unlucky mother.

“It is all settled,” said Mrs. Pybus to Mrs. Speers; “the boy is to go to college, and then the widow is to console herself.”

“He’s been there every day, in the most open manner, my dear,” continued Mrs. Speers.

“Enough to make poor Mr. Pendennis turn in his grave,” said Mrs. Wapshot.

“She never liked him, that we know,” says one.

“Married him for his money. Everybody knows that: was a penniless hanger-on of Lady Pontypool’s,” says another.

“It’s rather too open, though, to encourage a lover under pretence of having a tutor for your son,” cried a third.

“Hush! here comes Mrs. Portman,” said someone, as the Rector’s wife entered Madame Fribsby’s shop to inspect the monthly book of fashions from London—fresh printed commandments of cap and trimming, which the country obeyed as devoutly as any creed.

And indeed Madame Fribsby could hold out no longer. One day, after she and her lodger had been talking of Pen’s approaching departure and the Curate had gone off to give one of his last lessons, Madame communicated to Mrs. Pybus—who stepped in with Mrs. Speers—her strong suspicion, her certainty almost, that an attachment existed between a certain clerical gentleman and a certain lady whose naughty son was growing quite unmanageable, and that a certain marriage would take place pretty soon.

Mrs. Portman saw it all, of course, the instant it was mentioned. What a sly fox that Curate was! Low-church too; she never liked him. And to think of Mrs. Pendennis taking a fancy to him after being married to such a man as Mr. Pendennis! Mrs. Portman could hardly stay five minutes at Madame Fribsby’s, so eager was she to run to the Rectory and tell Doctor Portman.

When Doctor Portman heard the intelligence, he flew into such a rage that his first movement was to break with his curate and beg him to transfer his services to some other parish.

“That milksop of a creature pretend to be worthy of such a woman as Mrs. Pendennis!” broke out the Doctor. “Where will impudence stop next!”

“She is much too old for Mr. Smirke,” Mrs. Portman remarked. “Why, poor dear Mrs. Pendennis might be his mother almost.”
