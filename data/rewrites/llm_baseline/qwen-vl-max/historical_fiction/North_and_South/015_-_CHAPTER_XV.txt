“Thought fights with thought; out springs a spark of truth
From the collision of the sword and shield.”

“Margaret,” her father said the next day, “we must return Mrs. Thornton’s call. Your mother isn’t feeling well, and she thinks she can’t walk so far. But you and I will go this afternoon.”

As they walked, Mr. Hale began to speak of his wife’s health, his voice tinged with a quiet worry that Margaret was glad to see had finally stirred him.

“Did you consult the doctor, Margaret? Did you send for him?”

“No, Papa,” she replied. “You mentioned he’d come to see me. But I was well then. Still, if I could find a good doctor, I’d go today and ask him to visit Mama. I’m certain she’s more unwell than she lets on.”

She spoke plainly, driven by the urgency she’d felt before, when her father had dismissed her concerns with such firmness. Now, however, he answered in a tone heavy with doubt.

“Do you think there’s something serious? Is she truly ill? Has Dixon said anything? Oh, Margaret! I can’t shake the idea that coming to Milton has harmed her. My poor Maria!”

“Oh, Papa, don’t say such things!” Margaret exclaimed, alarmed. “She’s not well—that’s all. Many people feel unwell for a time, but with proper care, they grow stronger than ever.”

“But has Dixon said anything about her?”

“No. You know how Dixon loves turning small matters into mysteries. She’s been unusually vague about Mama’s condition, which has only made me anxious—though perhaps without reason. After all, you did say the other day I was getting fanciful.”

“I hope I am,” he said softly. “But don’t mind what I said then. I’d rather you were fanciful about your mother’s health. Don’t hesitate to tell me your thoughts. I want to hear them—even if I sounded annoyed. We’ll ask Mrs. Thornton if she knows of a good doctor. We won’t waste money on anyone but someone truly skilled. Wait—this is the street we’re looking for.”

The street looked unlikely to hold a house grand enough for Mrs. Thornton. Her son’s presence had never given any clue as to where he lived, but Margaret had unconsciously imagined that such a tall, dignified woman would live in a home matching her stature. Yet Marlborough Street stretched on with rows of modest houses, some broken by blank walls—nothing from their vantage point suggested grandeur.

“He told me he lived in Marlborough Street,” Mr. Hale said, puzzled.

“Perhaps it’s one of the savings he still practices—living in a small house,” Margaret mused. “But there are people around. Let me ask.”

She approached a passerby, who pointed out the factory lodge door at the end of the long wall they’d noticed. The lodge gate was no different from a garden entrance. On one side stood massive iron gates for wagons and lorries. The keeper admitted them into a vast rectangular yard. On one side were offices for business; opposite, a towering mill, its windows countless, humming with machinery and the deep groan of steam engines—a sound so constant it seemed to threaten to drown out human voices.

At the end of the yard, facing the street, stood a stone house—blackened by smoke, yet kept immaculate: steps, windows, paint all gleaming. It had clearly been built fifty or sixty years ago—the long narrow windows, the stone trim, the double flights of stairs flanking the front door—all spoke of age. Margaret wondered why those who could afford such a house didn’t choose a quieter life in the country or suburbs, away from the ceaseless din of industry. Her ears strained to catch her father’s words as they waited on the steps.

Inside, the drawing-room felt untouched. No one sat there. It seemed abandoned since the moment the furniture had been wrapped up, as though expecting an earthquake. The walls were pink and gold; the carpet bore floral patterns, but its center was covered by a plain linen rug. Lace curtains hung at the windows; every chair and sofa wore a delicate veil of netting. Alabaster figures stood under glass domes. In the center, beneath a chandelier also wrapped in cloth, stood a circular table, its polished surface lined with neatly bound books arranged like spokes of a wheel. Everything reflected light; nothing absorbed it. The room shimmered with a cold, glittering sterility, so starkly clean it felt uncomfortable. Margaret noticed not the effort to keep it spotless, but the absence of warmth—the labor here was not for comfort, but for preservation, for beauty untouched by use.

They had time to observe, to whisper, before Mrs. Thornton entered, rustling in black silk, as always. Her lace and muslin matched the purity of the room. Margaret explained why her mother couldn’t join them, careful not to deepen her father’s fears. But her explanation was clumsy, leaving Mrs. Thornton with the impression that Mrs. Hale’s illness was merely a temporary, high-society fancy—one that might have been ignored had the occasion warranted it. Remembering the horses hired for her own visit, and how Fanny had been sent ahead by Mr. Thornton to show respect, Mrs. Thornton stiffened slightly, offering neither sympathy nor belief.

“How is Mr. Thornton?” asked Mr. Hale. “I was worried by his hurried note yesterday.”

“My son rarely falls ill. When he does, he doesn’t complain. He told me he couldn’t spare time to read with you last night, sir. He regretted it—I’m sure he values your company.”

“It’s a pleasure to me as well,” Mr. Hale replied. “Seeing his delight in classical literature makes me feel young again.”

“I suppose classics are useful for those with leisure,” Mrs. Thornton said. “But I opposed my son returning to them. His life demands full attention. Classics may suit men who idle in colleges or country estates—but Milton men should focus entirely on the work of today. That’s my opinion.”

“But surely,” Margaret interjected, “if the mind focuses too long on one thing, it becomes rigid, unable to take in new ideas.”

“I don’t understand what you mean by a mind becoming stiff,” Mrs. Thornton replied sharply. “And I don’t admire those who jump from interest to interest, forgetting everything the next day. A Milton manufacturer must have one great purpose—and all his life should serve it.”

“And that purpose is—?” Mr. Hale asked.

Her cheeks flushed, her eyes brightened.

“To hold a respected, honorable place among the merchants of the land—the men of the town. My son has earned that. Go anywhere—England, Europe—the name John Thornton of Milton is known and respected. Of course, it’s unknown in fashionable circles,” she added with disdain. “Idle gentlemen and ladies don’t concern themselves with a factory owner unless he enters politics or marries nobility.”

Both Mr. Hale and Margaret felt a quiet discomfort. They had never heard of this famous name—until Mr. Bell had written, recommending Mr. Thornton as a valuable ally in Milton. This proud woman’s world was alien to theirs—neither Harley Street society nor Hampshire gentry. Margaret’s expression, though she tried to hide it, revealed her disconnection. Mrs. Thornton noticed.

“You think you’ve never heard of my wonderful son, Miss Hale. You think I’m an old woman whose pride is rooted in Milton, whose crow is the whitest ever seen.”

“No,” Margaret said, with quiet strength. “It may be true I hadn’t heard of Mr. Thornton before coming here. But since I’ve arrived, I’ve heard enough to respect and admire him—and to believe your pride is well placed.”

“Who told you this?” Mrs. Thornton asked, softened a little, yet wary that another’s words might not do him justice.

Margaret hesitated. She disliked being questioned so directly. Mr. Hale stepped in.

“It was what Mr. Thornton himself said,” he offered. “Wasn’t it, Margaret?”

Mrs. Thornton straightened.

“My son doesn’t boast of his own deeds. May I ask again, Miss Hale—whose words shaped your opinion of him? A mother is naturally eager to hear praise of her child.”

“It was more what Mr. Thornton chose not to say,” Margaret replied. “What he withheld, compared to what Mr. Bell told us about his past—that made us appreciate him more. And it gave me reason to see why you’re proud.”

“Mr. Bell!” Mrs. Thornton scoffed. “What does he know of John? He lives in a sleepy college, doing nothing. But I thank you, Miss Hale. Many young ladies would shy away from giving an old woman such pleasure.”

“Why?” Margaret asked, staring at her.

“Because,” Mrs. Thornton said with a grim smile, “they might fear their conscience telling them they’re making me an advocate—should they have designs on my son.”

Margaret laughed—out loud, brightly. It startled Mrs. Thornton, who found the laughter almost offensive, as if the words behind it must be utterly absurd.

She stopped herself immediately, seeing the irritation.

“I beg your pardon, madam. But I truly thank you for clearing me of any plans on Mr. Thornton’s heart.”

“Young ladies have done so before,” Mrs. Thornton said stiffly.

“I hope Miss Thornton is well,” Mr. Hale said, wishing to shift the topic.

“She is as well as she ever is. Not strong,” Mrs. Thornton replied curtly.

“And Mr. Thornton? I trust I may expect to see him on Thursday?”

“I cannot guarantee my son’s schedule. There’s unrest in town—a threat of a strike. If it comes, his judgment will be sought. But I expect he can come. At the very least, he’ll let you know if he can’t.”

“A strike?” Margaret asked. “For what?”

“For the mastery of other people’s property,” Mrs. Thornton snapped. “That’s always their goal. If my son’s workers strike, I’ll call them ungrateful hounds. But I suspect they will.”

“They want higher wages, I suppose?” Mr. Hale asked.

“That’s the surface. But the truth is, they want to be masters, to turn the masters into slaves. They try this every five or six years. This time, they’ll learn their mistake. If they walk out, they may not get back in so easily. I believe the masters have plans ready to teach them not to strike again soon.”

“Does it make the town dangerous?” Margaret asked.

“Of course. But surely you’re not afraid? Milton isn’t for cowards. I’ve walked through crowds of angry men swearing they’d kill Makinson if he showed his face. He knew nothing. Someone had to warn him—or he’d have died. And I had to go. Once inside, I couldn’t leave. So I climbed to the roof, where stones were piled to drop on the crowd if they forced the doors. I would have thrown them myself—if I hadn’t fainted from the heat. If you live here, you must learn courage, Miss Hale.”

“I would try,” Margaret said, pale. “I don’t know if I’m brave until I’m tested. But I fear I might be a coward.”

“Southern people often fear what we Darkshire folk call living and struggling. But after ten years among people who harbor grudges and wait to repay them, you’ll know whether you’re brave or not.”

That evening, Mr. Thornton came to call. He was shown into the drawing-room, where Mr. Hale read aloud to his wife and daughter.

“I’ve come partly to deliver a note from my mother, and partly to apologize for missing our appointment yesterday. The note contains the address you requested—Dr. Donaldson.”

“Thank you,” Margaret said quickly, taking the note. She didn’t want her mother to know they’d been seeking a doctor. She was pleased when Mr. Thornton handed it over without comment.

Mr. Hale turned to the strike.

“Yes,” Mr. Thornton said, his face hardening into an expression like his mother’s worst. “The fools will strike. Let them. It suits us. We gave them a chance. They think trade is as strong as last year. We see the storm coming and are pulling in our sails. But because we don’t explain, they refuse to believe we’re acting wisely. They demand reasons for every decision. Henderson tried a trick at Ashley—he wanted a strike. So when the men asked for a five percent raise, he said he’d consider it and reply on pay day, knowing full well what he’d decide. But they guessed the truth, heard about declining trade, and withdrew their claim. Now he’s stuck working. But today, the Milton masters have decided—we won’t raise wages. We may even lower them. So we stand waiting for their next move.”

“And what will that be?” Mr. Hale asked.

“I expect a general strike. Soon, Miss Hale, you’ll see Milton without smoke.”

“But why,” Margaret asked, “couldn’t you explain your reasons? I don’t know the right words, but I think you understand.”

“Do you give your servants reasons for your spending or saving?” Mr. Thornton replied. “We, the owners of capital, have the right to decide how to use it.”

“A human right,” Margaret murmured, so low he barely heard.

“I beg your pardon?” he said.

“I’d rather not repeat it,” she said. “It was a personal feeling—I don’t think you’d agree.”

“Won’t you try me?” he asked, suddenly intense.

She was irritated by his persistence but didn:’t wish to dwell on it.

“I said you had a human right. I meant—there seems no reason but religious ones why you shouldn’t do as you please with your own.”

“I know we differ in religion,” he said quietly, “but don’t you grant me some beliefs, even if they’re not yours?”

He spoke softly, as if addressing only her. She didn’t want the conversation to become private.

“I don’t need to consider your particular views,” she said evenly. “All I meant is, while no human law stops employers from wasting their wealth, Scripture suggests stewards have a duty not to squander it. But I know little about strikes, wages, capital, or labor—so I’d better not talk to a political economist like you.”

“On the contrary,” he said eagerly, “I’d be glad to explain anything that seems strange. Especially now, when everyone will be talking.”

“Thank you,” she said coldly. “But I’ll first ask my father for guidance. I don’t want to misunderstand this new world.”

“You think it strange. Why?”

“I don’t know—perhaps because I see two groups dependent on each other, yet treating each other as enemies. I’ve never lived where people constantly tear each other down.”

“Who have you heard speaking against the masters?” he asked. “I don’t ask who criticized the workers—I see you still misunderstand me. But who has slandered the masters?”

Margaret blushed, then smiled.

“I don’t enjoy being questioned. I won’t answer. Besides, it’s irrelevant. I’ll only say I’ve heard someone—perhaps a worker—say it’s in the employers’ interest to keep their workers poor, so they stay dependent.”

“I dare say it was Higgins,” Mrs. Hale remarked. Mr. Thornton didn’t seem to notice, but he caught it.

“And I heard it’s better for the masters to have ignorant workers—not hedge-lawyers,” Margaret added, addressing her father more than him.

“Who is Captain Lennox?” Mr. Thornton thought to himself, a strange displeasure rising. He didn’t reply.

Her father took up the thread.

“You’ve never cared much for schools, Margaret. You wouldn’t have known how much education is being provided in Milton.”

“No,” she said gently. “I admit I don’t care enough. But the ignorance I meant wasn’t about reading or writing. It was about wisdom—the kind that guides people. I don’t know what that is. But my informant said the masters prefer their hands to be large children—living only in the moment, obeying blindly.”

“In short,” Mr. Thornton said, offended, “your informant found a willing listener for every slander he chose to spread.”

Margaret didn’t reply. She resented the personal attack.

Mr. Hale spoke next.

“I confess, though I haven’t spoken with many workers as Margaret has, I’m struck by the hostility between employer and employee—on the surface, at least. Even your own words suggest it.”

Mr. Thornton paused. Margaret had just left the room. He was annoyed by the tension between them. But the frustration cooled his temper, lending dignity to his reply.

“My theory is simple: my interests are the same as my workers’. And theirs are mine. Miss Hale dislikes the word ‘hands,’ so I won’t use it—though it’s the natural term, born long before me. Someday—in some perfect future—this unity might exist. Just as I can imagine a republic as the most ideal form of government.”

“We’ll read Plato’s Republic once we finish Homer,” Mr. Hale said.
