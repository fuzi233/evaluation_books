The heat of July and August pressed down upon the Salcombe estuary, breeding a feverish tension in Ethne's blood that eventually forced its way past her lips. One morning, standing in the blinding sunlight at an open window of Mrs. Adair's cottage, she voiced the dread that had taken root in her chest. Behind her, Mrs. Adair offered a quiet, unreadable smile.

"Of what?" she asked, her eyes fixed on the page of a book she pretended to read. "That some accident befell Colonel Durrance yesterday in London?"

"No," Ethne replied, her voice slow and heavy. "Not of that. For he is at this moment crossing the lawn towards us."

Mrs. Adair's smile widened, though she did not lift her head from her book, masking her amusement behind the cover of fiction. "I thought so," she murmured, the words barely carrying across the distance. They failed to pierce Ethne's mind, for as she scanned the stone-flagged terrace and the broad, shallow steps leading to the green expanse below, a sharper fear seized her.

"Do you think he has any hope whatsoever that he will recover his sight?"

The question caught Mrs. Adair off guard; she dismissed it immediately, offering a flippant rebuttal. "Would he travel up to town so frequently to consult his oculist if he held none?" she asked, as if the logic were self-evident. "Of course he hopes."

"I am afraid," Ethne said, turning abruptly toward her friend. "Have you not noticed how rapidly he grows? How quickly he learns to interpret your silences, to deduce your unspoken thoughts from your gestures, to complete your sentences before you finish them? Laura, have you not seen it? At times, I feel the very corners of my mind laid bare to him. He reads me like a child's lesson book."

"Yes," Mrs. Adair conceded, "you are at a disadvantage. You no longer possess the shield of your face to hide your thoughts."

"And his eyes no longer offer me any protection," Ethne added.

Truth lay in both assertions. While Durrance had once relied on Ethne's vibrant complexion and her steady, frank gray gaze to anchor his understanding, his blindness had inverted their dynamic. Now, without visual cues, he weighed her pauses and movements with a detached precision that terrified her, leaving her perpetually in the dark regarding his intentions. Conversely, she could no longer gauge his reactions, trapped in a new kind of opacity.

Yet, Mrs. Adair cared little for this psychological reversal; she was consumed by Ethne's sudden vulnerability. The girl, formerly defined by a quiet candor, was unraveling into a creature of shifting anxieties and agitation.

"There is something, then, to be concealed from him?" Mrs. Adair asked softly.

"Yes."

"Something of consequence?"

"Something which, at all costs, I must conceal," Ethne exclaimed, unsure even as she spoke whether Durrance had already uncovered the secret. Stepping from the window onto the terrace, she surveyed the landscape: the lawn stretching to a hedge, the rolling grass fields beyond, and the chimney smoke rising from Colonel Durrance's house amidst the trees. She hesitated, gathering her strength, composing her defense for the man approaching steadily across the grass. Yet, as she observed him, she noted a stark contrast: while she trembled, Durrance moved with the certainty of one who sees perfectly. His cane tapped rhythmically against the turf, a habitual gesture rather than a tool of navigation, suggesting his internal map was flawless despite the darkness outside his eyes.

As Ethne descended the steps, preparing for a difficult confrontation, Mrs. Adair abandoned her pretense. Dropping her book, she rushed to the window, hidden by the falling drapes. Her face, previously serene, transformed into a mask of hungry anticipation, her eyes blazing with a fierce, predatory light.

"She must conceal it," Mrs. Adair whispered to herself, her voice trembling with exultation. Contempt colored her tone—a disdain for Ethne Eustace, the woman of the open air who feared marriage to a blind man and dreaded the loss of her liberty. It was precisely this shrinking spirit that Ethne sought to hide. "For my part, I am glad," she thought, fiercely rejoicing that blindness had disabled Durrance. If her chance ever arrived—and it seemed increasingly imminent—blindness would reserve him exclusively for her, a possession denied to any other woman. Jealousy burned in her heart; she coveted his every word and glance, eager to savor the ultimate pleasure of his total dependence. Watching Ethne and Durrance meet at the foot of the steps, she saw the younger woman pleading, and in her heart, she longed desperately to overhear.

And Ethne *was* pleading.

"You saw your oculist yesterday?" she asked urgently as soon as they joined him. "Well, what did he say?"

Durrance shrugged. "One must wait. Only time can reveal whether a cure is possible."

Ethne leaned forward, scrutinizing his face with suspicion. "But must *we* wait?"

"Surely," he returned. "It would be wiser on all counts." Then, unexpectedly, he posed a question whose intent she missed. "It was Mrs. Adair, I imagine, who proposed this plan—that I return to Guessens while you remain with her here across the fields?"

Puzzled, Ethne answered directly. "I was in great distress upon hearing of your accident. Overwhelmed, I could not think what to do until I came to London and confided in Laura. Since she is my friend, this was her suggestion. Naturally, I welcomed it with all my heart," she said, her voice ringing with a note of desperate entreaty. She sought his confirmation, and he understood. Turning to her with a gentle smile, he said, "I know that very well, Ethne."

Relief washed over her face, the anxiety receding momentarily. "It was kind of Mrs. Adair," Durrance resumed, "though it is rather hard on you, who would prefer to be back in your own country. I recall a sentence spoken by Harry Feversham a long time ago, in London just before I departed for Egypt. Speaking of you, he said: 'She is of her country and more of her county. I do not think she could be happy in any place which was not within reach of Donegal.' Remembering that, it seems selfish of me to claim your presence here at such a cost to you."

"I was not thinking of that," Ethne cried out. "That makes me seem most selfish. I was merely wondering why you prefer to wait, why you insist upon it. For, of course, although one hopes and prays with all one's soul for your recovery, the mere fact of a cure cannot alter the situation."

She spoke slowly, her voice laced with pleading. This time, Durrance did not affirm her words. Instead, she repeated them with greater force: "It can make no difference."

Suddenly, Durrance started as if awakened from a trance. "I beg your pardon, Ethne," he said. "I was thinking of Harry Feversham. There is something I wish you to tell me. You stated a long time ago at Glenalla that you might one day bring yourself to share it with me, and I would very much like to know now. You see, Harry Feversham was my friend. Tell me what happened that night at Lennon House—the event that severed your engagement and cast him out as an outcast."

Ethne fell silent for a moment before answering gently. "I would rather not. It is all over and done with. I do not wish for you to ask me ever."

Durrance did not press. "Very well," he said cheerfully. "I will not ask. It might hurt you to answer, and I certainly do not wish to cause you pain."

"It is not because I fear pain," Ethne explained earnestly, choosing her words carefully. "What took place was so long ago... I regard Mr. Feversham as a man I knew well, who is now dead."

Walking toward the wide gap in the tree line along the creek bank, Ethne raised her eyes from the ground. She spotted the small boat she had earlier seen tacking up the creek; now, it had grounded itself against the shore. Its sail was furled, the pole-mast sticking upright above the grass verge, and standing upon the bank was a figure staring vaguely toward the house, uncertain of his footing.

"A stranger has landed from the creek," she announced. "He appears to have lost his way. I shall go and guide him."

Seizing the intruder's arrival as a lifeline, even if only temporary, she ran forward. The interruption provided a fleeting respite, akin to a witness in a courtroom relieved when the judge rises for lunch. The interview with Durrance had left her feeling as though she had just exited a witness box, having endured a cross-examination so skillful that its precise aim remained elusive, though she suspected it from the start.

The stranger approached her. He was of medium build, possessing a short, snub nose, vacant protruding brown eyes, and a moustache of aggressive ferocity. Lifting his hat, he revealed a round, balding forehead.

"I have sailed down from Kingsbridge," he declared. "But I have never ventured into this part of the world before. Can you tell me if this house is called The Pool?"
