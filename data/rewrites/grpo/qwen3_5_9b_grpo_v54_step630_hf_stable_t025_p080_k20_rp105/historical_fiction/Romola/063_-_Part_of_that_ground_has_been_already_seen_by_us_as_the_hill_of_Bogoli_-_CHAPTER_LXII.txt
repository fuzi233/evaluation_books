February 27th dawned with a peculiar gravity, the air thick with the final breath of Carnival. By ten o'clock, the usual currents of Florentine life had shifted decisively toward San Marco. The populace knew the second Bonfire of Vanities awaited them before the Old Palace, yet the true magnet drew them all to the square. The Piazza di San Marco was a sea of humanity, a surging tide of new arrivals pressing against the rigid front ranks that had already formed a dense, immovable wall around the semicircular barrier guarding the church entrance. Within this enclosure, the Dominican Brethren assembled in silence, their black mantles absorbing the morning light.

Yet, the temporary wooden pulpit erected above the church door remained starkly empty. It was destined for the man the Pope had exiled from the Duomo, the cleric shunned by the ecclesiastical hierarchy and forbidden to the citizens under threat of excommunication. This man, Savonarola, had dared declare that a wicked, bribed Pontiff was no Vicar of Christ, that his curses were broken swords and his commands antithetical to the Christian life. He had proclaimed it lawful not merely to disobey, but to refuse obedience entirely. Despite the Pope's terrifying threats of interdict and ruin, the people still flocked to hear him, still accepted Communion from his hands. They wondered: if this friar truly held sway over divine judgment, why wait for a sign? If God had chosen him to rebuke the High Priest, surely the choice would be attested by unmistakable power.

For months, the demand for a miracle had swelled in Savonarola's ear, a fatal question gnawing at his resolve. As long as his preaching offered hope without immediate peril, no sign was needed; his words were a music to which the city marched willingly. But now, with the threat of commercial collapse and political isolation looming, the crowd demanded proof. "What miracle showest thou?" the question echoed in his mind. His faith wavered, though his speech remained unbroken—a necessary performance for any man speaking to a hungry multitude, relying on yesterday's conviction to fuel tomorrow's hope.

The wooden pulpit had been raised specifically to answer this popular impatience for a supernatural guarantee. As the ordinary friars in black entered, arranging themselves and chanting, the multitude's gaze did not yet fix upon the empty platform. They watched the monks, noting the familiar faces of high-born families mingling with the sons of artisans and shopkeepers who formed the bulk of the crowd. Only when the choir was complete, their voices rising in psalmody, did a hush fall, heavy with anticipation. "Fra Girolamo must be coming now," someone whispered, and the silence deepened, spreading like a pale solemn light over thousands of upturned faces.

In an instant, the pulpit was no longer empty. A figure, cloaked entirely in black from head to foot, stepped inside and knelt, head bowed, face turned away. To the eager crowd, the time seemed interminable as the black figure knelt and the monks continued their chant. Yet the stillness held; the friar's communion with Heaven carried an electric awe that unnerved even those ready to stone him.

Finally, a vibration rippled through the multitude, a silent passing of hands like a shared breath as the expected presence revealed itself. Savonarola rose, turned toward the people, and pushed back his cowl. The monotonous wail of the choir ceased, merging instantly into the force of his flashing glance as he looked out upon the silence. He stretched forth his hands—exquisite, delicate instruments transformed from tools of grasping into vessels of acute sensibility. They seemed to speak from the soul masked by his strong, passionate face, now etched with deeper lines than forty-four years of ordinary life could produce.

At the first extension of his hands, some in the front ranks fell to their knees, and scattered devout disciples followed suit. But the majority stood firm. Some resisted the impulse to kneel before an excommunicated man, fearing judgment upon the act itself; others viewed it with scorn, seeing the ambitious deceiver staging a new comedy, a fashion they felt powerless to resist.

Then came the voice, clear and low, uttering the words of absolution: "*Misereatur vestri*." More fell to their knees. As the voice rose, clearer and stronger, the number of erect heads dwindled until, at the climax "*Benedicat vos omnipotens Deus*," it erupted into a masculine cry, a trumpet blast protesting the demon's clutch that sought to stifle it. The sound rang to the extremities of the Piazza, and under its resonance, every head bowed.

After the blessing, Savonarola himself fell to his knees, hiding his face in temporary exhaustion. Such great jets of emotion were necessary to his existence; he had told the people long ago, "Without preaching I cannot live." But it was a life that shattered him.

Minutes passed. Some rose, but a larger number remained kneeling, all faces fixed intently on him. He held a crystal vessel containing the consecrated Host and addressed the crowd.

"You remember," he said, his voice carrying in the vast stillness, "three days ago I besought you. When I held this Sacrament in my hand before you all, I asked you to pray fervently to the Most High: if this work of mine does not come from Him, let Him send a fire to consume me, that I may vanish into eternal darkness, away from His light which I have hidden with my falsity. Again I beseech you to make that prayer, and to make it *now*."

It was a breathless moment. Perhaps no one truly prayed, save those in devout obedience making the effort. Every consciousness was dominated by the sense that Savonarola was praying, his voice distinct and audible in the wide silence.

"Lord, if I have not wrought in sincerity of soul, if my word cometh not from Thee, strike me in this moment with Thy thunder, and let the fires of Thy wrath enclose me."

He ceased speaking, standing motionless with the consecrated Mystery in his hand, eyes uplifted, his whole aspect quivering with excitement. All else was motionless and silent. Sunlight, which had pierced the greyness in fitful streaks for the last quarter-hour, suddenly parted wider. With a gentle quickness, like a smile, a stream of brightness poured onto the crystal vase and spread over Savonarola's face with mild glorification.

An instantaneous shout tore through the Piazza: "Behold the answer!"

The warm radiance thrilled through Savonarola's frame, and so did the shout. It was his last moment of untroubled triumph. In rapturous confidence, he felt carried to a grander scene, before an audience representing all Christendom, where he would be sealed as the messenger of supreme righteousness, charged with Divine strength. It was but a moment that expanded into a vision. While the shout still rang in his ears, he turned away within the church, feeling the strain too great to bear longer.

When the friar disappeared and the sunlight returned to its impartial, ordinary spreading over clean and unclean alike, a confusion of voices arose alongside the crowd's movement. Strong discords and varying scales of laughter revealed that the previous silence and universal kneeling had been a submission to a momentary spell, hostility and scorn merely holding their ground.

"It seems to me the plaudits are giving way to criticism," observed Tito, watching from an upper loggia opposite the church. "Nevertheless, it was a striking moment, was it not, Messer Pietro? Fra Girolamo is a man to make one understand that there was a time when the monk's frock was a symbol of power over men's minds rather than over the keys of women's cupboards."

"Assuredly," replied Pietro Cennini. "And until I have seen proof that Fra Girolamo has much less faith in God's judgments than the common run of men, instead of having considerably more, I shall not believe that he would brave Heaven in this way if his soul were laden with a conscious lie."
