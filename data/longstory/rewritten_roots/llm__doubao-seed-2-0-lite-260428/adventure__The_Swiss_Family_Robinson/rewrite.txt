For seven days straight, the storm had not let up. Six sunsets bled over frothing, mountain-high waves, six dawns broke to show the gale raging harder than before, and we were blown so far off our charted course none of us had the faintest clue what stretch of ocean we drifted across. The crew was broken, exhausted to the bone from bailing nonstop, our masts split clean through and swept overboard, and leaks sprung in every seam of the hull that the water kept rising, faster than we could bucket it back out.

No one shouted curses anymore. The seamen screamed prayers to God for mercy, spouting wild, half-crazed promises of good deeds they’d do if He spared them, every one of us alternating between whispering our souls into the hands of our Creator and scrambling for any last trick that might keep us alive a little longer.

My chest felt tight as I looked at my family huddled together amid the wreckage: our four young sons, white-faced and shaking with terror. “Boys,” I called over the roar of the waves, “God can pull us out of this peril if it is His will. If not, we can meet it calmly, knowing we will all be reunited forever in that peaceful home above. Death holds no sting if it does not tear the people who love each other apart away from one another.”

My wife, who had been sobbing into her apron, lifted her head at that, her jaw setting soft and brave, and she wrapped her arms around the boys as they clustered around her, murmuring quiet, steady words to calm their panic. My own heart ached so bad I thought it would burst, but seeing her fortitude steadied me, and we all knelt, one by one praying raw, earnest prayers. Fritz, our oldest, 14 years old and already fierce with love for his family, begged for deliverance for his parents and little brothers first, never even mentioning his own fear. The storm still howled outside, but the weight on our shoulders lightened, just a little.

Then, above the thunder of the surf, a shout cut through the noise: “LAND! LAND!”

The next second, the whole ship lurched so hard every single one of us was thrown flat onto the deck. The hull shrieked like a dying beast, splitting open with cracks that echoed all through the wreckage, and seawater poured in in torrents. “Lower the boats! We’re lost!” the captain screamed over the chaos.

“Lost?” the word hit me like a dagger, but when I saw the boys’ faces go white again, I forced cheer into my voice. “Courage, lads! We’re all still above water, aren’t we? The shore isn’t far. We just have to fight to reach it. God helps those who help themselves. Stay with your mother, I’m going up on deck to see what we can do.”

I stumbled topside, waves crashing over me one after another, and I saw the full wreckage: the bow of the ship was already ground to splinters against sharp rocks, but the stern section that held our cabin was jammed wedged between two tall boulders, hoisted half out of the breakers. And the entire crew? They’d forgotten we even existed, piling into the lifeboats in a panic, the last man off hacking through the davit ropes to cast them adrift. I screamed and waved for them to wait, to let my family climb aboard, but the howling wind swallowed my voice whole, and the mountainous waves would have sunk the boats for sure if they’d tried to turn back. Through the spray, I could just make out the dark line of a rocky coastline, and suddenly, our situation didn’t feel completely hopeless.

I climbed back down to the cabin, forcing a smile for my family. “Don’t look so glum, all of you. Our ship will never sail again, but our cabin is sitting high and dry right now. Once the wind dies down tomorrow, there’s no reason we can’t make it to shore.” The boys’ faces lit up instantly — they’d always trusted my judgment, and what had looked like a death sentence just a minute before felt like a sure, good chance. My wife saw the tight edge under my forced smile, though, and I told her the full, grim truth: no crew, no rescue, just us and a broken wreck and an unknown shore. She didn’t flinch for a second, her faith unshaken, and her courage seeped right back into me.

“Then we need a good, hot supper,” she said firmly. “Fasting won’t do us any good. We’ll need all our strength tomorrow.”

Night fell fast, the storm still raging, every few minutes a loud crash echoing through the wreck as more of the bow section tore apart. None of us thought the lifeboats could have survived that sea. “God is going to save us soon, right Papa?” little Franz, our youngest, asked, his voice small. “Don’t be silly,” Fritz snapped. “We don’t get to tell God what to do. We just have to be patient.” “That’s the right thought,” I told him, “but you need to speak kinder to your brothers, even if you’re trying to be sensible.”

We ate heartily, and the three youngest boys fell asleep almost instantly. Fritz stayed up on watch with us, and after a long quiet he spoke up: “Papa — what if we make float belts for Mom and the little ones? You and I can swim. If the ship breaks apart tonight, we could all make it to shore, right?” It was a brilliant idea. We hunted through the wreck and found stacks of empty glass flasks and sealed tin canisters, tied them two and two together to make buoyant flotation belts, and every one of us strapped one on, just in case. I stuffed my pockets full of dry tinder, matches, knives, coils of strong cord, any small useful thing we could grab, so if we had to jump for it we wouldn’t hit the shore completely empty handed.

The rest of the night my wife and I kept watch, praying, every new creak of the wreck making our hearts jump. By dawn, the gale finally softened. Patches of blue sky peeked through the clouds, the sunrise painting the horizon pink and gold, and I woke the boys up to climb up on deck.

They froze when they saw no other soul was left on the wreck. “Where did all the sailors go? They left us here alone? What are we going to do by ourselves?” “We are not alone,” I told them. “The men we trusted to keep us safe ran away, but God will never leave us. Let’s think — who has a plan to get to shore?”

Fritz said we could swim once the sea calmed. Ernest, 12, bookish and a little lazy, huffed and said that was fine for people who knew how to swim, and we ought to build a raft that could carry all of us at once. Jack, our wild, fearless 10 year old, had already run off to the captain’s cabin, and when he wrenched the door open two huge mastiffs sprang out, so excited to see a human after days locked up alone that they bowled Jack right over, licking his face all over. He didn’t even cry — he grabbed the bigger dog by the ears, hopped on his back, and rode him right up to me at the hatchway, making me laugh so hard my sides hurt.

When we gathered back in the cabin to show off the treasures we’d scavenged, Fritz had two full hunting guns, a powder horn, shot, and a pouch full of bullets. Ernest had his cap stuffed full of nails, a pair of heavy scissors, an axe and a hammer, pincers, chisels and augers sticking out of every pocket. Even little Franz had a big box, full of tiny, sharp fishhooks, that his older brothers teased him for — until I told him those hooks might very well keep us all fed once we got to shore. My wife had the best news of all: she’d gone down to the livestock hold, and had saved most of the animals: a donkey, two goats, six sheep, a ram, and a pregnant cow and sow, all still alive after days of neglect, and she’d milked the goats so we had fresh milk to drink. “And Papa,” Jack cut in, grinning, “the dogs! They can help us hunt! They’re not useless!”

“Oh, I have an even better idea than swimming or a raft,” Jack announced. “We could each sit in a big wooden tub and float! I did that all the time at the pond back home.” He was right — it was genius. We dragged four huge sound, iron-hooped casks out of the flooded hold, sawed every single one clean in half, and suddenly we had eight sturdy little one-person tubs. We nailed them all along a long, flexible plank that curved up at both ends to make a keel, added side planks to hold them steady, and we had a weird, narrow homemade craft with eight separate compartments. It was so heavy we almost couldn’t move it, but we used a long capstan bar as a lever, slid it over rollers made from a cut-up spar, and launched it into the water. It tipped hard to one side at first, until we added ballast, then nailed long poles out on both sides as outriggers, with empty brandy casks tied to the ends to keep it perfectly balanced.

We spent that night on the wreck again, strapping our float belts back on for safety, and my wife traded her heavy long skirts for a midshipman’s old canvas uniform, which was far more practical for scrambling around shore. We all slept in hammocks, more peaceful than the night before, and woke up before the sun the next morning to pray, then pack our craft full of all the supplies we could carry: canvas for a tent, more carpentry tools, fishing rods, an iron pot, portable soup, hard biscuits, even ten hens and two roosters we tucked into one spare tub with wire netting over the top. The ducks and geese we set free to swim beside us, the pigeons took off flying straight for the shore. My wife slung a big pillow-sized bag of her own supplies over her shoulder, refusing to say what was in it.

We cast off, the older two boys taking the oars, the tide pushing us gently towards the land. We’d left the two mastiffs Turk and Juno back on the wreck to save weight, but when they saw us leaving they howled piteously and jumped straight overboard, swimming after us, resting their front paws on the outriggers now and then to catch their breath. I wouldn’t let Jack shoo them away — the dogs were loyal, and they’d earned their spot with us. The rocky shore looked barren and harsh at first, until we spotted green grass and tall palm trees peeking through the cliffs. Jack pulled a tiny stolen spyglass out of his pocket, and with it I spotted a narrow creek opening up into a sheltered, calm little cove, right where the geese and ducks were swimming. I steered straight for it, and we glided into shallow, smooth water, the bank sloping soft and easy up to a small green plain tucked between the cliffs.

The dogs scrambled onto the shore first, barking wildly to announce our arrival, the chickens squawking, the wild flamingoes and penguins that had claimed the cove as their home screaming in surprise at the noise. We all stumbled out of our tub craft — even little Franz, who’d been packed tight in his compartment like a potted shrimp — and the first thing we did was kneel down on the warm, dry dirt, to say a prayer of thanks for our miraculous escape. We unloaded all our supplies, stretched the canvas sail over a spar propped against the rocks to make a temporary tent, stacked our chests around the edges to hold the tent pegged down, and gathered dry wood to build a stone hearth right next to the little brook that babbled down from the cliffs. My wife heated water in the iron pot, dropping in squares of portable soup to make our first shore dinner. Little Franz leaned over the pot, squinting at the melting pale blocks. “Mama?” he asked, confused. “That looks just like the glue Papa uses for carpentry. You aren’t making us eat glue soup, are you? Where’s the beef?”

We would have been utterly exposed that night without our tent’s shelter—for the dark hours turned as bitterly cold as the day had been sweltering hot. But every one of us was so worn thin from backbreaking shipwreck labors that we slept like logs, warm and safe under our canvas.

The last sound I heard before drifting off was our rooster’s loud, proud crow, saluting the rising moon. At the first streak of dawn, that same rooster woke me. I nudged my wife awake, and while all four of our boys still slept, we huddled together to hash out our plans for survival. Two priorities stood clear first: we had to search for any trace of our shipmates who’d abandoned us on the wreck, then map every resource this strange, wild island had to offer.

By the time we’d agreed on our course, we called the boys up cheerfully: “Rouse up, lads! Go help your mother get breakfast going!”

She smiled and shook her head at me. “All we’ve got is that soup pot—what else is there to make?”

“Nonsense, you forgot Jack’s famous lobster!” I said. “Where’d you stow that prize, boy?”

“Tucked it in that little hollow in the rock last night, Father,” Jack piped up. “Figured the dogs like tasty treats just as much as we do, and I didn’t want them stealing it like they did the agouti earlier.”

“Smart thinking,” I told him, grinning. “Even my wild, impulsive Jack is learning good sense fast. That lobster’s huge, too—perfect, we can take half of it out with us on our expedition today.”

The word “expedition” sent all four boys leaping in the air, clapping and cheering so loud the birds in the nearby trees took flight.

“Easy, now,” I laughed, holding up a hand to calm them. “Not all of us can go. This trip will be long, rough, and far too dangerous for the little ones. Our landing spot’s perfectly safe here, so Fritz and I will go alone. We’ll take one dog with us, leave the other two to guard you all. Fritz, go prep our guns, and tie Flora up so she doesn’t try to follow us out.”

At the word “guns,” Fritz’s face flushed bright red with shame. The rifle he’d fumbled and damaged the day before was still crumpled beyond repair, no matter how hard he tried to straighten the barrel. I let him fumble with it for a few minutes to let his embarrassment sink in, then handed him a spare working rifle from our stores—glad to see the mistake had taught him real care, not just frustration.

A minute later he reached for Flora to tie her up, but the dog remembered the hard blows she’d gotten from him the day before, snarled, and backed away, refusing to let him touch her. Turk, our bigger, fiercer hound, did the exact same. I had to call their names myself, soft and steady, to get them to creep over to us. Fritz’s eyes welled up with tears, and he begged his mother for a handful of biscuit, swearing he’d skip the rest of his breakfast if it meant he could make the dogs forgive him. He carried the biscuits over, fed them piece by piece, stroked their fur, and every move he made looked like he was begging for their pardon. Dogs, after all, are less prone to holding grudges than almost any other creature, human included: Flora softened right away, licking his hands. Turk, stubborn and independent, hung back still, refusing to trust him.

“Give him a claw of my lobster!” Jack yelled. “I was gonna save that for your trip, anyway.”

That tasty treat was all it took. Turk wagged his tail, and the rift was mended. We geared up then: each of us slung a rifle over our shoulder, strapped a game bag to our belts. Fritz tucked a pair of pistols into his waistband, I slipped a small hatchet into mine. Once breakfast was done, we stowed the leftover half lobster, a stack of biscuits, and a full flask of water in our bags, ready to head out.

“Wait a second,” I said, stopping him. “We forgot something far more important than supplies.”

Fritz blinked, confused. “What could we have missed?”

“Morning prayer,” I said. “It’s far too easy, scrambling to survive day to day, to forget the God who gave us all we have.” We knelt together, commended our family and our journey to his care, then said our goodbyes to my wife and the boys, warning them not to wander far from the wreck and the tent. None of us said it out loud, but we all felt that little tight twist of anxiety in our chests: we had no idea what dangerous things waited for us in this uncharted land.

We soon spotted that our camp was far safer than we’d guessed: the banks of the nearby stream were lined with sharp, jagged rocks, with only one narrow little path leading down to the water, and no path at all cut across to the opposite bank. The other side of our tent was backed by sheer, unclimbable cliffs—no predator could sneak up on the family unannounced.

Fritz and I followed the stream upstream until we reached a small waterfall, the water tumbling from a good ten feet up over half-submerged boulders. We hopped across the rocks safely, and found ourselves with the bright blue sea on our left, and a long line of rocky, tree-dotted hills stretching inland to our right.

We’d barely pushed fifty yards through the thick, tangled, sun-withered grass when we heard a loud rustle behind us. The grass swayed wildly, like some large animal was barreling through it. Fritz spun on his heel, lifted his rifle, and had it aimed dead at the source of the noise before I could even blink. My chest swelled with pride at his cool, quick thinking—proof I could rely on him completely when real danger struck. No savage beast leaped out at us, though: it was just Turk. My thoughtful wife had snuck him off after us before we left, to give us an extra guard. I praised Fritz for his quick reflexes and his good sense, waiting to make sure he saw the animal before he pulled the trigger.

The little incident was a sharp reminder of how dangerous the wild grass could be, so we hurried out onto the open sand of the shore. The view there was breathtaking: soft rolling green hills behind us, grass swaying in the breeze, little groves of trees dotted right down to the edge of the water. We combed the whole stretch of beach for footprints of our lost shipmates, and found not a single mark.

“Should I fire a couple of shots?” Fritz asked. “If they’re within earshot, that’ll draw them to us.”

“It would draw them,” I said, “but it’d also draw any band of hostile natives that might live on this island. No—we search quiet, no loud noise, no risk.”

Fritz huffed, kicking sand at his feet. “Why do we even bother looking for them? They left us to fend for ourselves. I don’t care if I ever see any of them again.”

“You’re wrong, lad,” I told him. “First off, we don’t repay cruelty with cruelty. Second, if we find them alive, they can help us build a proper permanent shelter, far faster than just the two of us. Last, they left the wreck with no food at all. They could be starving out there right now.”

“Then why don’t we head back to the wreck first, instead of wasting time hunting for people who don’t care about us?” he argued. “We could save all the farm animals trapped on board before the ship breaks apart.”

“When you’ve got two choices of good deeds to do, you always pick the one that saves more lives,” I said. “Saving a human being is more important than making sure a handful of farm animals are comfortable, especially since we already left enough food on the wreck to feed them for days. The sea’s dead calm right now, too—there’s no way the ship will sink or shatter to bits before we get back to it.”

We kept walking, chatting, until we reached a thick, cool grove that ran right down to the water. We stopped to rest under the shade of a huge spreading tree, next to a tiny rivulet that babbled over pebbles on its way to the ocean. Hundreds of bright, rainbow-plumed birds twittered and darted above our heads. Fritz suddenly jumped to his feet.

“A monkey!” he yelped. “I swear I just saw a monkey!”

He spun around the other side of the tree to chase the sight, and tripped over a big round, fuzzy object on the ground. He picked it up, grinning. “Is this a bird’s nest? I’ve read about these round hanging nests before!”

“Nice guess,” I laughed, taking it from him. “But every round fuzzy thing isn’t a bird’s nest. This is a coconut. See the thick fibrous outer husk? The outer green shell rotted away after it fell off the tree, that’s why the stringy fibers are sticking out so much. Let’s crack it open, and you’ll see the hard shell and nut inside.”

It took us a while to split it open, and we groaned in disappointment when we did: the coconut was so old, the meat inside was completely dried out, tough and inedible.

“Wait,” Fritz said, confused. “I thought coconuts were full of that sweet, creamy almond milk stuff everyone talks about?”

“They are, when they’re young and fresh,” I explained. “As they ripen, the milk thickens, then hardens completely into the white kernel you see here. If a coconut falls on good soil, the little germ inside pushes through three tiny holes near the stalk, bursts the shell, and grows a whole new palm tree.”

We hunted for half an hour to find a fresh coconut, since the ones that fall naturally off the tree are always overripe. We found one that was a little oily and slightly rancid, but it was still perfectly edible, and the sweet fruit refreshed us so much we decided to skip our midday dinner to save our store of supplies.

We pushed on through a thick thicket tangled so full of hanging lianas that we had to chop our way through with our hatchets, and emerged back on the shore, where a line of strange trees with huge, knobby bumps all up their trunks stood scattered across the sand.

“Those are the silliest trees I’ve ever seen!” Fritz exclaimed, pointing. “Look at all those weird bulges on their trunks.”

We walked over to get a better look, and I recognized them immediately: calabash gourd trees. The gourds grew right out of the trunk, hard-shelled, perfect for making bowls, spoons, and bottles. “Indigenous people carve these all the time,” I told him. “You can even cook food in them without burning the shell.”

“That can’t be right,” Fritz said, frowning. “If you set that hard rind over a fire, it’d burn right through.”

“I never said you set it on the fire,” I laughed. “You fill the hollowed-out gourd full of water, drop your meat or vegetables inside, then toss in red-hot stones until the water boils. The gourd never even gets hot enough to char, and your food cooks perfectly.”

“ That’s genius,” he said. “I probably would have figured that out myself, if I’d thought about it long enough.”

“Just like Columbus’s egg trick,” I said. “Everyone thinks a trick’s obvious once someone shows you how. Let’s harvest a few of these gourds, we can prep them to take back home.”

Fritz grabbed one, tried to saw it in half evenly with his knife, and the blade slipped, leaving a ragged, jagged cut. “Useless!” he complained, flinging it down. “It looked so easy, and I messed it all up.”

“Patience,” I told him. “Those broken bits aren’t trash—you can carve spoons out of them. Watch what I do for the perfect bowl.” I pulled a length of strong twine out of my pocket, tied it as tight as I could around the middle of a whole uncut gourd, then tapped the string repeatedly with the back of my knife. The string slowly sank into the hard rind, and when I pulled the two ends of the string as hard as I could, the gourd split perfectly, clean down the middle, into two identical bowl halves.

“Cheater!” Fritz cackled. “Where’d you learn that trick?”

“From old travel books,” I said. “That’s how people living in remote villages split their calabashes, no saw required.” We filled our new gourd bowls with damp sand to help them dry out into hard, rigid vessels, marked the spot with a pile of stones so we’d find them on our way back, and kept walking.

Three hours later, we reached a steep, rocky promontory that jutted far out into the ocean. We climbed to its summit, and the view stretched for miles in every direction: a wide, sweeping golden-sand bay below us, the water glinting bright blue under the sun, two shadowy headlands at either end that nearly hid the far edges of the shore. Not a single footstep, not a single wisp of smoke, no sign of human life at all. We both went quiet for a minute, hit by the strange, heavy loneliness of being the only people for hundreds of miles.

“Cheer up, lad,” I said, clapping him on the shoulder. “Remember, before we left home, we talked about wanting to build a new settler life somewhere wild. We didn’t plan on being quite this alone, but look at this paradise—we’re not stuck on some bare, rock desert island. We have everything we need here.” We climbed back down the peak, heading for a small cluster of palm trees we’d spotted in the distance.

To get to them, we had to cut through a thick wall of tall, sharp reeds. I sent Turk trotting ahead of us first, to chase out any venomous snakes that might be hiding in the undergrowth. I chopped one of the reeds off at the base, and noticed thick, sweet-smelling sap oozing out of the cut end. I tasted it, and grinned—we were standing in a whole field of sugar cane.

I told Fritz to cut himself a reed to use as a walking stick. He chopped one, and when he beat the ground in front of him to clear the way, the cane split open, covering his hand in that same sweet sap. He touched a drop to his tongue, then yelped in delight. “Sugar cane! It’s sugar cane! Let’s cut dozens of them to take home for Mother and the boys!” Before I could warn him not to overpack, he’d chopped a dozen of the biggest, thickest canes, stripped all the leaves off, and slung them under his arm.

We pushed through the cane brake into the palm grove, and a whole troop of monkeys, who’d been playing on the ground, leaped straight up to the top of the nearest trees, chattering, grimacing, taunting us. Fritz’s face flushed in frustration, he lifted his rifle, ready to shoot the sassy creatures.

“Stop!” I called out. “You don’t waste a life for no reason. A dozen live monkeys are far more useful to us right now than dead ones.” I grabbed a handful of small pebbles off the ground, and tossed them up at the monkeys. Apes are natural mimics, and they reacted instantly: they grabbed every fresh, ripe coconut within reach, and hurled them down at our feet in a perfect hail of fruit.

Fritz whooped in delight, grabbing up the biggest, juiciest coconuts. We poked holes in the shells, drank the refreshing coconut milk, scraped the thick creamy meat off the inside of the shells with our new rough spoons. The half lobster we’d been carrying to save for later suddenly seemed totally unappetizing, so we tossed it to Turk. He ate it in two bites, then immediately started gnawing on the ends of Fritz’s sugar canes, begging for more treats. We slung a couple whole coconuts over our shoulders by their fibrous stalks, and turned for home.

Fritz was huffing within ten minutes, shifting the heavy bundle of sugar canes from shoulder to shoulder, until he finally stopped, sighing. “I had no idea reeds would be this heavy. I feel terrible for the enslaved people who have to carry loads of these all day for no pay—though I can’t wait to see my little brothers’ faces when they taste how sweet this is.”

We rigged a simple carrying frame with two extra sticks, strapping the bundle of canes crosswise to Fritz’s rifle to distribute the weight better, and set off again. Fritz noticed me sucking on the end of my cut sugar cane, and tried to do the same—couldn’t get a drop of juice out. “Why isn’t it working for me?” he complained.

“Think,” I said. “You’re smart enough to figure it out.”

“Ah!” he yelled. “It’s like trying to suck marrow out of a bone without poking a hole in the other end! No air can get in, so there’s no vacuum to pull the juice up the cane.” We both laughed, and soon he had the trick of it down, chewing the soft sweet pulp to get every bit of sugary sap. He’d filled his flask earlier with fresh coconut milk, and when he unscrewed the cork to show me, the fermented liquid exploded out in a foamy pop, fizzing like champagne, sweet and tangy and bubbly in the hot sun. We sipped it, grinning, invigorated by the unexpected treat.

We collected our fully dried calabash bowls from our marked spot, ready to head the last stretch of the way home, when Turk suddenly bolted. He lunged at the pack of monkeys that had taunted us earlier, and before we could call him off, he’d seized a mother monkey, tearing at her. A tiny baby ape that had been clinging to her back scrambled away through the grass, watching the whole scene shaking.

Fritz dropped all his supplies, sprinting to stop Turk—but he was too late. The little baby monkey spotted Fritz, and in one single leap, bounded straight up his shoulders, wrapped its tiny little hands tight in his curly hair, and clung to his neck so hard he couldn’t pry it off. Fritz yelped, squirming, jumping up and down, screaming that the little creature was hurting his scalp, and the harder he tried to pull it off, the tighter it clung. “I won’t drag you through every tedious detail of our first day alone on the rocks,” my wife Elizabeth laughed as we huddled around the campfire that evening, brushing fine sand off her sleeves. “I spent almost all of it too anxious to rest, staring out at your signals from the shore, and nearly burst with relief when I spotted your ‘all safe’ flag flying at first light. I shot my reply signal straight back, and while the boys still snored, I sat down and thought hard: this bare, sun-scorched rock camp we’re stuck in? We can’t last here much longer. The midday sun beats so hot the tent canvas turns into an oven, and there’s not a scrap of shade to escape to. Why shouldn’t I and the younger boys pull our weight, same as you and Fritz? I was determined to track down that pretty, shady grove you two explored yesterday, no question.”

By the time the three younger boys stumbled awake, Elizabeth already had their small expedition mapped out. Jack, ever the busy little tinkerer, had snuck over to the dead jackal carcass Fritz had left by the tent, slicing long narrow strips of its hide, scraping them clean, pushing heavy iron nails through the soft raw skin with the points facing out, then covering the flat nail heads with doubled scrap sail canvas. He trotted over begging her to stitch the canvas down to line the collars so the sharp nail points wouldn’t chafe our dogs’ necks; she let him fumble clumsily at the work for ten minutes before taking pity on his uncalloused little fingers and finishing the job, plus a thick stitched hide belt he’d designed to tuck his tiny pistols into. Ernest mocked the whole project for all of five minutes before suggesting they nail all the wet hides flat to a board to stretch them tight in the sun so they wouldn’t shrink as they dried – a trick Jack immediately put to use, tucking the board under his arm for the entire hike so the sun could hit it at every step.

The little party of four, plus our two dogs Turk and Juno, set off at once armed with two small rifles, a water flask, a tiny hatchet, and an empty foraging sack. Turk, who’d come with us on our first inland hike, recognized the trail instantly and bounded ahead as their proud guide. Elizabeth later told me she’d never been more grateful for the hunting training I’d given the boys since toddlerhood, knowing her whole group’s safety rested in their steady little hands.

They refilled their water jars at the stream, climbed the low ridge we’d spotted the day before with the sweeping view of the whole bay, and spotted a lovely, leafy grove in the distance. Dense grass that grew higher than the boys’ heads blocked their direct path, so they detoured along the beach, then cut back through tall rustling reeds – and nearly stumbled right on top of two massive, heavy birds that exploded into the air with a roar of beating wings before the kids could even lift their rifles to aim.
“Pity that wasn’t an eagle!” Franz gawked, staring at the vanishing speck in the sky.
“Eagles don’t nest on the ground, dolt,” Ernest huffed, already launching into a full lecture on gallinaceous bird traits, identifying them as great bustards from the ruffled red feathers on their wings and the fine moustache-like tuft over the largest male’s beak they’d both spotted mid-flight. They found the messy grass nest half-hidden in the reeds, spotted the fuzzy baby bustards darting away through the grass, and decided not to chase them, too busy marveling at the strange little grove they’d just reached.

The patch of “wood” was only a dozen trees total, but they were unlike anything any of them had ever seen: thick, massive trunks perched on wide arched stilt roots that lifted the whole tree six feet off the ground, soft thick green grass carpeting the floor beneath the dense canopy, not a single thorn or bramble in sight. Jack measured the biggest trunk with a length of twine, and it wrapped all the way around a full 18 yards. It was cool, shaded, perfectly sheltered, and they ate their midday meal there to the sound of singing birds, the dogs curling up to doze happily instead of begging for scraps. By the time they packed up to leave, Elizabeth was completely sold: this was the perfect spot to build a home, high off the ground, safe from jackals and snakes, no more baking in the shore tent.

On their walk back along the beach they found the tide had washed up piles of wreck cargo: heavy casks, a few waterlogged chests, all too big for them to carry, but they dragged what they could past the high-tide line. They noticed the dogs were busy digging furiously in the sand, and soon found out why: Juno had uncovered a nest of turtle eggs, which they stowed in their foraging sack, before spotting our raft rounding the point back from the wreck. They hurried across the stream to the landing spot, where we met up at last.

“So your grand master plan is for all five of us to perch in the treetops like roosting birds, little wife?” I teased her that night over supper, grinning.
“Laugh all you want,” she shot back, “I saw a lovely little treehouse arbour built in a lime tree back home in Switzerland, with a proper wooden floor and a staircase to climb up. We’d be completely safe from jackals, from storm surges, no more baking on these hot rocks. You’ll see, it’s not nearly as silly as you make it sound.”

I promised to take the idea seriously, and we all tumbled into our tents to sleep like hibernating marmots, waking at dawn to hash out our next steps. I was hesitant to abandon our rocky camp right away: the high cliffs around it made it a natural fortress, only accessible via the narrow stream ford, and it was the perfect base to make trips back to the wreck to salvage more supplies. Elizabeth pushed back, frustrated by the unbearable heat on the rocks while I took Fritz out on the raft all day in the cool breeze, begging me not to make any more risky trips out on the rough open water to the half-sunken wreck. We struck a deal: we’d move to her perfect shady grove, turn this rocky camp into a locked, fortified storehouse for our gunpowder and extra heavy supplies, and first build a proper bridge across the stream so we could move all our bags, animals, and supplies back and forth without wading through the often-swelling current.

The boys erupted in cheers when they heard the news, immediately naming the new grove “The Promised Land”, and begging to start work at once. After a breakfast of biscuit boiled in goat and cow milk, Fritz, Ernest, and I set off on the raft to salvage planks from the wreck for the bridge – but as we rounded the point of the little islet at the bay entrance, thousands of seabirds exploded into the air like a screaming grey cloud, all swarming a massive shape washed up on the sand.

It was the shark! The exact one Fritz had shot in the head the day before, so huge it took all three of us to drag a little ways up the beach, the seagulls tearing ravenously at its thick flesh. We chased the birds off with our gun ramrods, sliced off thick sheets of the rough shark skin, which Ernest helpfully pointed out worked just like fine sandpaper – the same material as the fancy shagreen leather used by carpenters to polish fine wood. Even better: the storm had washed piles of perfect, long, dry planks right up on the islet beach, exactly the length we needed for the bridge, so we didn’t even have to sail all the way out to the wreck. We tied the planks to a small makeshift raft we towed behind us, and sailed back home to shore far earlier than expected.

When we shouted our hello as we moored the raft, Elizabeth, Jack, and little Franz came running around the rocks, their handkerchief bundles bursting with wriggling crawfish. They’d found a whole swarm of the fat little crustaceans feasting on the jackal carcass they’d left near the stream earlier that day, two hundred of them at least, squirming everywhere as the boys chased the escaping ones around the sand.

After we laughed over the chaotic crawfish hunt, we all herded the planks up to the edge of the stream, and Ernest came up with a clever trick to measure the exact width of the water: tie a rock to the end of a ball of string, throw it across, pull it tight, and measure the line. It was 18 feet across, so we had more than enough 24-foot planks to span the banks. We sat down for a quick dinner of boiled crawfish, where Elizabeth showed off the two huge canvas saddlebags she’d stitched by hand, punching every stitch hole with a nail because she had no large sail needle left, before we headed back out to the bank to test the plan I’d worked out for hoisting the heavy planks across the stream.

The whole family was up before the sun peeked over the island’s palm treetops, and the four boys scrambled across the wide branches of their giant tree castle like a troop of overexcited young monkeys.
“Dad! What do we get to do first today?” they whooped, thumping down the ladder one after another. “Give us jobs, we’re ready!”
“Today, we rest,” I told them, grinning at their confused, surprised faces.
“Rest?” Jack echoed, bouncing on the balls of his feet. “Why would we rest? There’s a hundred cool things to build and explore!”
“Remember the rule we followed at home: six days for work, and the seventh for honoring the Creator who’s kept us safe so far. That’s today—Sunday,” I explained.
“Wait, it’s actually Sunday?” Jack’s face lit up. “That’s fun! I won’t do boring work, I promise. But can I bring my bow and shoot, and climb around the trees all afternoon?”
“That isn’t quite the gentle rest we’re supposed to keep for the Lord’s day,” I laughed. “You’re used to worshipping in a stone church back home, but we’ve got something far more beautiful here: the whole island’s green shade, the birds singing, the fresh grass under our feet. We can worship here just as well.”
“But there’s no minister, no organ, no hymn books!” Franz piped up, pouting a little.
“Then we’ll sing the hymns we remember, right here under the leaves. C’mon, boys, down the ladder—let’s turn our tree landing into our little worship space for the morning.”
As the last boy slid down to the soft grass, I leaned over to my wife Elizabeth. “I’ll walk them through a short service with a little allegory story instead of a long sermon, to keep their attention. Once that’s done, they can have whatever quiet, innocent fun they like, and we’ll all take a slow sunset walk later to enjoy the island.”
Elizabeth agreed wholeheartedly. After a quick breakfast, we all sat in the dappled shade, sang the old hymns we could recite from memory, and said prayers of thanks that we’d all survived the shipwreck. When I told the boys I’d tell them a special parable instead of a regular sermon, Franz’s eyes went wide.
“I love parables best of all!” he said. “When can we read from our Bible again, Dad? I miss our old story readings.”
“Your words are such a good reminder, little one,” I admitted, my cheeks warm. “I’ve been so focused on hauling supplies from the wreck to feed our bodies that I completely forgot to grab a copy of the Bible. I’ll make it a top priority on my next trip back to the ship.”
At that, Elizabeth jumped up, rummaged in her little “magic bag” of supplies she’d grabbed from the wreck, and pulled out a full, dry copy of the Holy Scriptures. My chest filled with relief as I took it, read a few beloved passages out loud, and told the boys the story of the Great King who tended a desolate island until it bloomed for his colonists, who fell into rebellion only to be offered free pardon and a chance to return to his perfect, bright kingdom. The boys sat completely silent, rapt, until I finished tying the parable’s lessons to our little new life on the island, and closed the service with a short, quiet prayer.
Once the service ended, everyone drifted off to their own small projects. I grabbed a handful of arrows and set to work tipping them with sharp porcupine quills. Franz hung around begging for his own tiny kid-sized bow and set of arrows, while Fritz asked for help turning a wild tiger-cat hide into waterproof carrying cases. Jack helped me fix the quills to the arrow shafts, and mumbled that he wished he had glue to hold the spines extra tight.
“Mamma’s soup is super sticky!” Franz blurted out. “I can go ask for a chunk of it right now!”
“Nah, silly, that’s not glue,” Jack snickered. “We should look for real glue in the toolbox we hauled off the wreck.”
“Hey, don’t tease your brother,” I told Jack gently. “Some of the most famous, world-changing inventions started out as ideas that sounded just as silly as Franz’s soup glue thought. You never know what random thought might work.”
Just then a crack rang out right over our heads, and two small birds dropped dead at our feet. We looked up to see Ernest grinning from a high tree branch, still holding his smoking gun.
“Did you see that? Perfect shot, right?” he cheered, slipping down the ladder to grab the birds. One was a plump thrush, the other a tiny, sweet ortolan that we knew was a famous delicacy. We realized the fig trees they’d been feeding on would soon be full of flocks of the little birds—if we caught enough of them and preserved them in barrels of melted butter, we’d have piles of ready food for the rainy season ahead.
By the time we finished talking, Jack had made a whole pile of sharp quill arrows, I’d finished Franz’s little bow, and the tiny hunter begged for a quiver to hold all his new gear. I stripped a long sheet of bark off a small sapling, fitted it with a flat bottom and a shoulder strap, and soon Franz was trotting around the clearing, quiver slung over his back, looking exactly like a tiny, cheerful Cupid his mom had laughed at.
We all gathered for lunch soon after, and I pitched a new, fun idea to the boys: let’s name all the different spots we’d explored on the island, so we wouldn’t have to say long, clunky descriptions every time we wanted to talk about a place.
“Oyster Bay!” Fritz shouted first.
“No, Lobster Bay!” Jack yelled over him. “To remember that giant lobster that tried to bite my leg our first day on the beach!”
“I think we should name it Safety Bay,” Elizabeth said softly, “to give thanks that we all made it here alive when the rest of the ship was lost.”
Everyone agreed immediately, and the names came fast after that. Our first old campsite got named Tentholm, the tiny islet in the bay where we found the dead shark became Shark’s Island, the reedy wetland full of flamingos we spotted was Flamingo Marsh. Our tree castle? After a whole minute of arguing, we named it Falconhurst, then quickly ticked off the rest: Prospect Hill for the first high hill we climbed to scan the horizon, Cape Disappointment for the rocky headland where we’d strained our eyes for hours looking for the rest of the ship’s crew and found no one, and Jackal River for the big stream that ran past our landing spot.
The rest of the afternoon was full of silly fun. Fritz finished his hide cases, and he and Jack talked me into helping make a spiky porcupine-skin cuirass for our dog Turk. We cleaned the hide, sewed it to fit snugly around his chest, and soon the big dog was trotting around like a weird, spiky little tank. Juno, our other dog, gave him the widest possible berth, and we all agreed no jackal or wild cat would ever dare take a bite out of him. I used the leftover scrap of porcupine hide to make a spiky little helmet for Jack, who paraded around the clearing so proud he could barely stop grinning.
When the sun started to dip low, we took a slow walk through the flower-dusted glades near Falconhurst, sang another hymn, and climbed up to our tree bunks for a peaceful, restful Sabbath night.
The next morning, I announced we’d be making a trip back to Tentholm—but we’d take a brand new route no one had explored before. We all trundled out fully armed: me and the three older boys carried guns and game bags, Franz had his tiny bow and full quiver. We must have looked hilarious: Fritz was wearing his margay-skin belt, Jack had his spiky porcupine helmet on, and the two of them looked like tiny, friendly young savages. Elizabeth walked beside me, the only unarmed one, carrying an empty jar to bring back a fresh supply of butter from our stores at Tentholm. Turk trotted ahead in his spiky armor, with Juno trailing a few steps behind, still too weirded out by his new look to get too close.
Knips, our pet monkey, immediately tried to hop up on Turk’s back for his usual ride—but one touch of his paw on the spiky porcupine hide made him make the most dramatic, disgusted face we’d ever seen. He chattered furiously, bounded over to Juno, hopped on her back instead, and settled in like he’d never had a better steed. Our pet flamingo, who’d gotten spoiled from all the pets we’d given him, decided he wanted to come too, and walked right at my heel the whole way down the stream bank.
The boys were running ahead, exploring the edges of the treeline, when we heard Ernest yell at the top of his lungs. He came sprinting back to us, red in the face, clutching a leafy green plant in his fist.
“Potatoes! Dad, that’s a potato plant!” he gasped.
“Acres of them, acres!” Jack yelled right behind him. “Come look!”
We all ran to the clearing, and sure enough, a huge stretch of rich soil was thick with the green leaves and little knobbly potato roots we all recognized right away. It was one of the best discoveries we could possibly have made—we’d never go hungry here, now that we had a steady crop of potatoes that would grow year round.
We dug and dug for so long that every game bag, every pocket, every pouch we carried was stuffed full of rough, brown potatoes. The boys begged to turn right back for Falconhurst immediately to cook our first harvest, but I reminded them we still had supplies to pick up at Tentholm, and we kept trundling along, heavy but giddy.
When we got to the stream’s rocky waterfall, we waded across, and stepped out into the most gorgeous tropical grove we’d ever seen: tall palms towered over thick ferns, jasmine and sweet-smelling vanilla blooms perfumed the air, and clusters of ripe pineapples grew all around. The boys immediately started tearing into the sweet, tangy fruit, until Elizabeth warned them there were no doctors on the island if they ate too much and got a stomach ache. We sent Knips scrambling up to the top of the pineapple patches to fetch us all the ripest fruit hidden high in the leaves, and I pointed out the valuable karatas plant nearby—its long fibrous leaves made perfect thread, the soft dry pith was great tinder for starting fires, and the bruised sap from the leaves made a great healing salve for cuts and scrapes. I broke off a dry piece of the karatas pith, struck two pebbles together over it, and a tiny spark caught the tinder immediately. The boys cheered, and I handed Elizabeth a handful of the soft, strong fibers to use for sewing.
Jack spotted an Indian fig tree covered in sharp thorns a minute later, and tried to climb it to grab the sweet fruit, only to come back with pricked, bleeding hands. I showed him how to skewer the fruit off the branches with a sharp wooden stick, no need to touch the thorns at all, and we all sat around eating the sweet, pulpy fruit until our bellies were full. Ernest found tiny bright red bugs clinging to the fruit, and realized immediately they were cochineal—the rare insects that make expensive scarlet dye back in Europe. We all laughed when Jack said we didn’t need fancy red clothes, but we’d definitely bring a bunch of the bugs back home with us when we were rescued.
By the time we finished exploring the grove, we’d reached Tentholm. All our old supplies were still perfectly safe, Elizabeth filled her jar with fresh butter, Fritz checked our stock of ammunition, and Jack and Ernest ran down to the beach to catch the half-wild ducks and geese we’d left penned there. The birds were too shy to come near the shore, so Ernest came up with a genius plan: he tied chunks of cheese to long pieces of string, tossed them out into the water, and gently reeled the hungry ducks back onto the sand one by one.
We packed a big sack of salt on Turk’s back (after taking off his spiky armor so he’d be more comfortable), tied the ducks and geese to our game bags, locked up our Tentholm tent, and started the walk home along the beach. By the time we trundled back up to Falconhurst, we clipped the wings of our new waterfowl to stop them from flying away, and set them free on the little stream near our tree. We had a delicious dinner of boiled new potatoes, fresh milk, and butter that night, then climbed up to our tree bunks full and happy.
I woke up at dawn the next morning, roused Ernest up early with me to pull driftwood off the beach. I wanted to break him of his lazy streak, and he groaned a little at first, but soon perked up, excited to have a secret little adventure before the rest of the boys woke up. We brought our donkey with us, and dragged a big wide palm bough behind him to carry our driftwood load. We pulled armfuls of heavy, smooth driftwood off the sand, stacked it on the palm bough for the donkey to drag home, and I spotted a half-buried old sailor’s chest sticking out of the dunes half-hidden by sand. We dragged that back too.
By the time we got back to Falconhurst, the other boys were blasting away at the ortolan birds in the fig trees, just like Ernest had predicted—they’d been wasting shots, missing most of their targets because they were firing straight up through the thick leaves. Elizabeth pointed out we’d run out of all our gunpowder soon if we kept that up, so we split up work: the younger boys wove simple bird snares out of the tough karatas fibers, while Fritz and Ernest helped me nail the driftwood together into a big, sturdy sledge that we could use to haul heavy loads of supplies back from Tentholm without straining our backs.
We were mid-build when we heard a huge squawking fuss coming from the chicken coop. The hens were flapping and cackling, and Ernest spotted Knips the monkey sneaking off into the grass with a stolen warm egg in his paw. He’d snuck into the coop and eaten half a dozen new laid eggs, and we found the rest hidden under a tuft of grass. Elizabeth decided she’d lock Knips up in the tree every morning until she’d collected all the new eggs, so the little thief couldn’t steal any more.
A little later, Jack was setting his new bird snares high in the fig tree branches, and came running back down to tell us he’d spotted a pair of our pet pigeons building a nest in the branches right above our landing. We all agreed no one would fire a gun near the nest, and the snares would only be set for the wild fruit-eating birds, so the little pigeon family would stay safe. We were building a home, piece by piece, and every new discovery, every new little addition to our little community, made it feel more and more like we belonged right there on our little island.

The next morning, as our breakfast cooked over the campfire, I tended to the glossy kangaroo pelt we’d gotten the day before, working to cure it whole before it could spoil. By the time I was done, Fritz had our gear fully prepped for our return trip to the stranded wreck, so I called over Ernest and Jack to give them clear rules for watching over their mother and little Franz while we were gone.

To my surprise, the two boys had vanished the second they’d swallowed their last bite of porridge. Their mother could only guess they’d run off to dig up more potatoes, since we were running low on our store. I asked her to scold them for leaving without asking permission, but since they’d taken our loyal dog Turk with them, I knew they weren’t in any real danger. It was with real reluctance that I said goodbye to my family, cheering my wife up with promises of all the useful new supplies we’d haul back soon.

We crossed the narrow log bridge over Jackal River, and before we could go another step, Ernest and Jack burst out of the bushes where they’d been hiding to jump-scare us. They cackled so hard they could barely speak, thrilled that their little prank had made both Fritz and I yelp in surprise. It was obvious they fully expected we’d let them tag along to the wreck.

I turned them down flat, but I couldn’t find it in me to yell at the two merry rascals. In fact, their unexpected detour gave me the perfect chance to send a message I’d been too nervous to say out loud before we left: we wouldn’t be back that night. Building a proper, heavy-duty raft to haul all the wreck’s best supplies in one trip would take all day, and heading back at dark would be far too dangerous. I told them to hurry home so their mother wouldn’t panic, and to collect a bag of sea salt on their walk back to make the chore worth their while.

“Goodbye lads, we’re off!” Fritz called as we climbed back into our sturdy tub boat, shoving off from the shore. The current swept us fast out of the bay, and in no time we were tied safely to the wreck’s splintered hull, clambering up onto what little remained of the main deck to make our plans.

Our project took shape fast. A stack of empty, sealed water casks made for the perfect buoyant base: we pushed a dozen of them over the side, lashed them side by side to thick wooden spars in three neat rows of four, then laid a solid plank floor across the whole thing, adding a low bulwark around the edges to keep cargo from slipping overboard. The raft was rock solid, exactly what we needed to haul all the heavy, bulky gear our tiny tub boat could never carry.

By sundown we were both exhausted, having worked almost nonstop all day with nothing but a few small snacks from our packs to fuel us. We couldn’t have made it back to shore even if we’d tried. Delighted that we’d already warned the family to not wait up, we feasted on the wreck’s stored ship’s rations, then crashed on proper spring mattresses — an absolute luxury after months of sleeping on thin, lumpy hammocks.

The next morning we hauled nonstop to load the raft and our tub boat. First we cleared out our old cabins, then stripped the captain’s quarters of furniture, doors, window frames, and all their iron locks and hinges. We dragged up the officers’ sea chests, the carpenter’s heavy tool box, and the gunsmith’s crate of supplies, hauling their contents out armful by armful because they were far too heavy to move whole.

We found one chest stuffed so full of glittering trinkets it looked like a jeweller’s stall: gold and silver watches, snuff boxes, polished buckles, chains, rings, every kind of fancy useless bauble you could imagine. We paused only to glance at them, far more excited to find a crate of plain, sturdy knives and forks far more practical for our wild island life than the fancy silverware we’d dragged ashore on our first trip.

The best discovery of all, though, was a crate of carefully wrapped baby fruit saplings, their labels still intact: apple, pear, chestnut, orange, almond, peach, apricot, plum, cherry, and even young grape vines, all the familiar European produce we’d missed so badly. The wreck had been stocked to supply a brand new far-off colony, and it held every supply we could possibly dream of: ironmongery, plumber’s tools, lead sheets, paint, grindstones, cart wheels, a full portable forge set, spades, plowshares, sacks of maize, peas, oats, wheat, a hand mill, and even a pre-numbered half-assembled saw mill that we could put together easily once we had extra hands.

We were so overwhelmed by all the treasure around us that we almost froze, unsure what to take first. We knew the next big storm would tear the wreck completely apart, sinking everything left behind, so we prioritized the most vital supplies: all the grain, the young fruit trees, fishing line, reels, extra rope, two heavy harpoons, and a working mariner’s compass. Fritz, still giddy about our recent shark run-in, waved one of the harpoons around like a professional whaler, posing dramatically over the edge of the boat until I laughed and told him to save the show for when we were back on shore.

By early afternoon both the raft and our tub boat were groaning under their heavy loads, and we cast off for home, nervous about towing the bulky raft through open water. Luckily the sea was glass calm, the wind at our backs, and we made great speed. Fritz grabbed the telescope and squinted at a dark shape bobbing far out in the water, then passed it to me: it was a massive sea turtle, fast asleep on the surface, completely unaware we were approaching.

“Steer for it, father!” he yelped. I turned the boat toward it, not paying attention as Fritz stepped behind me, hidden by the sail. A second later there was a sharp thud, and the whole boat jolted hard, rope zinging through the air. Before I could yell at him to stop, the turtle bolted, dragging our full tub boat and the heavy loaded raft behind it like it was a steam tug.

“Fritz, what have you done? You’ll sink all our supplies!” I shouted, grabbing my hatchet to chop the harpoon line loose.

“I got him! I got him!” he whooped, bouncing with excitement. “Wait, don’t cut it! I swear I’ll snip the line the second it gets dangerous! Let us catch him!”

I hesitated, then agreed, watching carefully for any sign the turtle was going to yank us out into open sea. When it tried to bolt for deep water, I hoisted the sail to pull against it, and the startled turtle swerved hard for the nearest shallow beach. The shore there was a long, gradual shelving sandbar, and we ran aground hard with a sharp jolt, no tipping, no damage. The turtle was exhausted — no wonder, it had dragged two fully loaded vessels at full speed for nearly a mile. I waded through the shallow water to it and dispatched it with my hatchet, only cutting its head off after it fought hard, the tenacious creature refusing to go down easy.

Fritz fired his gun and yelled at the top of his lungs to signal our family, and soon the whole crew — my wife, Ernest, Jack, and little Franz — came sprinting down the beach to meet us. My wife was overjoyed we were unhurt, but horrified when we told her the story of the runaway turtle that had almost dragged us out to sea. It took all four of us to heave the giant turtle onto our sledge, its massive weight taking up almost the entire bed alongside our young fruit saplings.

We trundled back to our treehouse at Falconhurst, chattering the whole way about our wild adventures. The first thing we did when we got home was prep the turtle for supper. My wife turned up her nose at the thick, bright green fat clinging to the meat, saying no one could possibly eat something so disgusting. I laughed and told her that was the most prized, delicious part of the turtle, the treat fancy epicures back home would pay huge money for, and that any extra could be rendered down for lard. Fritz immediately suggested we turn the huge, empty turtle shell into a water trough to set by the nearby brook, and Jack spoke right up that he’d found a huge bed of soft clay nearby when he’d slipped and fallen face first in the mud that morning, perfect for making a stable, level base for it.

Then Ernest pulled a handful of weird, knobby roots out of his pocket, saying he’d found them in the woods that morning, rooting around where our old sow had been gorging herself on them. I lit up the second I held one: it was manioc, the starchy root that made cassava bread. “Even if every other crop we have failed,” I told the boys, “these roots will keep us from starving. We just have to squeeze all the poisonous juice out first, and we can make flour.”

We headed back out for one more sledge run before dark, and Fritz puzzled over why we weren’t cutting the turtle’s shell up for pretty trinkets. I explained this was a common edible sea turtle — the pretty, polished tortoiseshell used for combs and boxes came from a different rare species that was completely inedible, so we weren’t wasting a single thing by using the shell as a water trough.

When we got back to the treehouse, my wife had a huge surprise waiting for us: she’d found a half-buried small cask of canary wine washed up on the beach while we were gone, half sunk in the sand, and she’d dragged it back home for us. We all took turns sipping it through straws, though I had to stop the greedy boys before they drank too much and got tipsy. Our supper of tender, savory turtle meat was the best meal we’d eaten since we’d wrecked on the island, and that night we hauled all the new spring mattresses up to our treehouse bedrooms, sleeping deeper and better than we had in months.

I woke up early the next morning to check on our unguarded boats and cargo out on the open beach, harnessed our stubborn donkey to the sledge, and hauled a light load of supplies back home. To my shock, the whole family was still fast asleep, the soft lulling mattresses and the sweet canary wine making them oversleep well past sunrise. The boys stumbled down the ladder yawning, rubbing their eyes, and I made a mental note we’d only be cracking that wine on very rare, special occasions from then on.

We worked through the rest of the morning to haul all the remaining cargo off the raft and boat, and before the tide pulled out, Fritz and I climbed back aboard to sail to Safety Bay, so we could anchor the vessels safe out of reach of any storm surges. Jack begged so hard to come with us, his big eyes pleading, that I couldn’t say no. The calm, perfect weather tempted us to make one last quick run back to the wreck to grab more small supplies. Jack went rummaging in the dark hold and yelled so loud he made us jump: he’d found a perfect wheelbarrow, which he immediately claimed as his own to haul potatoes. Even better, Fritz found a whole pre-fabricated pinnace sailboat, all its parts carefully packed and sectioned, even with full rigging and two tiny brass cannons, just waiting to be assembled once we had the time to put it all together.

We loaded up our small tub boat with the new treasure: three more wheelbarrows, a big copper boiler, iron plates for baking, grindstones, a small keg of gunpowder, and two tobacco graters, then headed back for shore. As we got close, we saw a whole line of small dark shapes standing perfectly still on the beach, dark coats, white bellies, their little wings hanging at their sides.
“Lilliputians!” Jack shrieked, bouncing in his seat. I laughed and told him no, those were penguins, the flightless diving birds we’d first spotted the day we wrecked. Jack jumped out of the boat as soon as the water was shallow enough, waded ashore, and whacked six of the unsuspecting birds with his stick before they could dive into the water. We tied the stunned live ones up by their feet with long grass, and Jack insisted on trundling them all the way home in his personal new wheelbarrow. Our two dogs were so overexcited to see us that they came bounding full speed down the path, knocking Jack’s wheelbarrow right over. Penguins went rolling everywhere, and a very cross Jack stood up and cuffed both dogs soundly for ruining his perfect parade.

Back at the treehouse, we tied each live penguin to one of our domestic geese or ducks, hoping they’d integrate with the flock and make a new self-sustaining colony for us. Little Franz accidentally spilled his mother’s secret: she’d been planting seeds from her “magic bag” of saved garden seeds in every patch of ground we dug up for potatoes, and they already had sprouting rows of Indian corn, melons, pumpkins, and cucumbers. When my wife saw the tobacco graters I’d hauled ashore, she groaned, saying we had no business messing around with snuff before we had enough food to feed everyone. I laughed and told her the graters weren’t for snuff at all — they were for shredding our manioc roots, so we could bake hot, fresh flat bread on the new iron plates we’d brought back, no oven required. She was still skeptical, and set a huge pot of potatoes to boil just in case my bread plan flopped, but happily set to sewing the thick strong canvas bag I needed to squeeze all the poisonous juice out of our manioc roots. The whole island was ours to build, supply by supply, root by root, and we were finally making it feel like home.

Now that we’d figured out how to bake steady supplies of bread for the whole family, my thoughts immediately drifted back to the wreck, and all the precious gear still locked inside its hull. More than anything, I was determined to get my hands on the beautiful little sailing pinnace stowed deep in its hold — and I knew we’d need every strong pair of hands we had to pull it off. I coaxed my wife for days to let me take all the boys except little Franz on a dedicated wreck expedition, until she finally, reluctantly agreed, only after I swore a solemn promise never to spend a single night sleeping on the half-sunken hulk. Neither of us felt totally settled with the arrangement, but the boys were over the moon at the chance for a big adventure, and happily stuffed their provision bags full of cassava bread and roasted potatoes before we set off.

We reached Safety Bay without a single mishap, first stopping to feed the tame geese and ducks that were thriving in the marsh, make sure they were well settled, then strap on our cork floatation belts, climb into our tub boat, and tow our spare raft straight out for the wreck. Once we clambered on board, I sent the boys scrambling to grab whatever useful supplies they stumbled on and load the raft for our return trip, while I went to inspect the pinnace properly. I quickly realized the job was way harder than I’d thought: the pretty little craft was wedged all the way at the far end of the hold, in a space so cramped there was no way we could assemble it there on the spot. All its numbered heavy parts were far too big to haul piece by piece to a more open work site, too.

I sent the boys off to rummage for treasures while I sat down to puzzle the problem out. As my eyes adjusted to the dim light seeping through cracks in the hull, I spotted that every single plank, fitting and bolt of the pinnace was marked with a clear number — the makers had planned for it to be assembled somewhere else, and if I could just carve out enough open work space, we could absolutely put it together.
“Space! That’s all we need, lads!” I yelled when the boys trotted over to hear my plan. “Grab your axes, we’re smashing this whole cluttered compartment apart to clear room!”
We hacked away for hours, but by sunset we’d barely made a dent in the thick surrounding timbers. It was clear we’d need days of steady, stubborn work to get that tiny ship out. We dragged ourselves back to shore ready to trudge the long walk all the way to Falconhurst, only to be shocked and overjoyed to find my wife and little Franz camped out waiting for us at Tentholm. She’d decided to move their base there for the whole time we worked on the wreck, so we wouldn’t have to travel miles every night, and she could keep half an eye on us to make sure we were safe.
The days that followed were filled with nonstop backbreaking work: we hacked away at the hold timbers every morning, cleared more and more space, and slowly, piece by piece, put the whole pinnace together until it stood completely finished, ready to launch — trapped behind a wall of solid wreck wood that no amount of levering or pulling could shift. I was this close to giving up in despair, when a wild, perfect idea hit me.
Without explaining a thing to the boys, I built a giant homemade petard: a big cast iron mortar packed full of gunpowder, with a drilled oak block fixed to the top for a slow-burning match. I chained it firmly in place right against the wall of wreck timber that was trapping the pinnace, so the explosion would blow that whole side of the hull out without damaging the boat. I lit the slow match, herded the boys back into the tub boat, and we rowed fast for shore, my heart hammering in my chest as I strained to listen for the boom.
It came at last: a flash, a deafening roar, a great billowing cloud of smoke that blotted out the sky. My wife and kids stared at the sea terrified, until I casually said it was probably an accidental fire near the gunpowder stores down in the hold, and called for volunteers to come check the damage. The boys piled straight into the boat, I whispered a quick explanation to my wife to set her mind at rest, and we raced back to the wreck faster than we ever had before.
The side we usually boarded on looked totally normal, but when we rounded to the far side, our jaws dropped. A huge, ragged gap had been blown right through the hull, floating wreckage bobbed everywhere in the water, and our beautiful pinnace sat dead center, totally unharmed, glinting in the sun. “Hurrah! She’s ours!” I yelled, and the boys whooped so loud they nearly split their throats, guessing my secret trick right away. We dragged the rollers I’d stashed under her keel earlier into place, hefted with all our might using levers and pulleys, and the pinnace slid smooth and steady right into the open water, floating like it had been born to be there. We cheered so loud the surrounding cliffs echoed the sound back at us.
We tied our new prize up in the most sheltered nook of the bay, then headed back to Tentholm to finish rigging her sails, mount her two tiny brass cannons, and get her fully ready to sail. The boys went wild with martial excitement, chattering nonstop about chasing off pirate canoes and fighting off savage invaders, though I reminded them we’d be far happier if they never had to test that brand-new valour at all.
We planned a huge surprise for my wife. We cast off from the wreck, spread the sails, and glided across the bay straight for Tentholm, Fritz barking the order, “Fire!” The two cannons went off with a thunderous bang. My wife and little Franz came sprinting out of the tent, half scared out of their wits, until they spotted us on the deck and realized what we’d done. Fritz pulled his mom on board, and she stammered, “You dear, horrid, wonderful lot! I thought a whole crew of pirates was sailing up to get us!” She ran a hand over the polished wood of the pinnace and laughed, “I’d happily sail around the whole island in this, no problem at all.”
Then she dragged all of us away to show us her own secret project: a huge, neatly laid out garden by the cascade, full of neat beds of lettuce, cabbages, beans and peas, with rows of potatoes and cassava roots planted further back. She’d sown maize seeds all around the beds of tender European greens, so the tall plants would cast shade over them and protect them from the scorching tropical sun, and she planned to run hollow bamboo pipes from the cascade to irrigate everything, with melons and pineapples planted in the warm, sheltered nooks by the rocks.
That night we feasted happily, talking over all our new projects, and I told the boys that real, lasting happiness always came from building something good for the people you loved, not just from chasing your own fun. The next Sunday we rested properly, read from the Bible, sang our favourite hymns, and I taught the boys running, jumping, wrestling and climbing drills, telling them that strong bodies made you far braver and more self-reliant — strong enough to help people, defend people, or run fast if you ever had to.
To practice their aim and dexterity, I made a set of tiny homemade lassos, weighted with lead bullets on each end, and told them how South American hunters used much longer versions of the same rope to lasso wild horses and buffalo at full gallop. The boys went mad for it, and soon all of them were spending every free minute practicing, with fast, strong Fritz quickly becoming the most skilled of the lot.
The next morning a heavy gale blew up, churning the sea white, and I breathed a huge sigh of relief that our hard-won pinnace was safely moored in the sheltered bay. We decided it was the perfect day to set off on a big family expedition to the Calabash Wood to carve new bowls and utensils out of gourds. We harnessed the donkey to our sledge, loaded it with supplies, brought all the dogs — even little Knips the pet monkey, who insisted on riding on Juno’s back — and set off.
Halfway through the trek, Fritz and Jack spotted movement in the bush, fired their guns, and a huge bustard bird fell to the ground, only to leap back up and sprint away at top speed, the whole pack of dogs hot on its heels. We finally caught it, though my wife felt terrible for its supposed baby chicks — until I reminded her that the chicks were fully grown long ago, and if the bustard’s wounds healed, we could keep her as a new breeding bird for our poultry yard.
When we reached the familiar Monkey Grove, where Fritz had rescued little Knips months before, Ernest stopped under a giant coconut palm, sighed, and mumbled, “It’s so tall. I wish a coconut would just fall down.”
As if by magic, a huge, heavy coconut plopped right at his feet. He jumped back, staring up at the fronds, before a second coconut came crashing down right beside him.
“Hey! Look up there!” Fritz yelled suddenly. “There’s something in the tree! Flat, round, as big as a dinner plate — and it’s got horrible big claws! It’s creeping down the trunk right now!”

The first pink streaks of sunrise had barely gilded the island’s palm tops the next morning before we had our day’s plan locked in: we were heading straight back to the Calabash Wood to retrieve the sledge we’d left loaded with all the hand-thrown dishes, carved bowls, and woven baskets we’d built the week before. Only Fritz came with me. I left his three brothers behind to keep their mother company, since I intended to push past the line of jagged rocky hills that curved along the coast, and a big party would only slow us down on untested ground.

Halfway through the evergreen oak grove, we spotted our pet sow gorging herself on fallen acorns, completely unharmed by the feral dog scare we’d pulled her from the day before. If anything, she seemed friendlier than usual, snuffling up to nudge our boots like she thought we were her personal saviors. The grove hummed with wild birds, unphased by our quiet footsteps until Fritz lifted his gun and dropped a brilliant blue jay and a pair of parakeets, one scarlet plumaged, the other streaked green and gold. He was reaching to reload when a low, thudding noise drifted through the trees – the dull, heavy thump of a muffled drum. Our skin prickled. Could there be hostile savages hidden somewhere on the island?

We ducked low, creeping through bushes and thick tangled creepers until we reached a sunlit glade, and the source of the noise revealed itself. Perched on a rotting fallen log was a bird the size of a farm rooster, its feathers rich chestnut brown mottled with dark grey, with tufts of iridescent green-tinged black velvet feathers puffed out on its shoulders. It strutted and circled, ruffling its wings, lifting its tail, putting on a whole stately performance for a flock of plainer, similar-looking birds gathered around the stump watching it. After parading for several minutes, it fanned its tail wide like a feather fan, stiffened its wings, and began beating them faster and faster, until a low rumble like distant thunder rolled out across the glade – the drumming we’d heard, amplified by the hollow rotting wood of the log under its feet.

I was transfixed, but before I could stop him, Fritz fired a shot. The beautiful bird crumpled dead off its perch, its audience of admirers scattering in panic. I was furious at him for spoiling such a rare, magical sight with an unnecessary shot – it was a perfect specimen of a ruffed grouse, and we’d ruined one of the island’s most charming little secrets for no good reason. We tucked the bird on the back of our waiting ass and pushed on, finding our sledge completely untouched where we’d left it. It was still early enough that I stuck to my original plan: I was going to trace that whole line of coastal cliffs, find out if there was any break that let us push inland, or confirm once and for all we were walled off on this stretch of shore.

Past Calabash Wood, the ground was thick with wild manioc, sweet potatoes, and dozens of unknown leafy plants we’d never spotted before, crisscrossed by tiny gurgling streamlets that watered the rich, fertile soil. A patch of bushes heavy with tiny, waxy white sticky berries caught my eye, and I grinned so wide my cheeks ached. This was Myrica cerifera, the wax myrtle! I explained to Fritz that if we melted and strained the berries, we could make our own candles, so his mother wouldn’t have to stop all her work the second the sun dipped below the horizon and fumble around in the dark. The green wax would burn sweet-smelling, even if it was a little more brittle than beeswax, so we filled an entire large canvas bag slung on the ass’s back with berries in no time and kept moving.

A little further on, we stumbled on one of the strangest natural wonders I’d ever seen: a single lone tree covered in a massive, shared nest the size of a small hut, built entirely out of grass, straw, and plant fiber, that housed an entire colony of tiny brown grosbeaks the size of yellowhammers. Thousands of the little birds swarmed in and out of tiny doors cut into the spongy, sponge-like mass of nests, and we spotted dozens of tiny bright green parrots squabbling with the rightful owners over the best nest spots. Fritz, a fearless climber, scaled the tree to get a better look, only to yelp loud when he stuck his finger deep in one hole and got bitten hard by a strong parrot beak. He shook his hand to throw the attacker off, then reached back in, seized the squawking little parrot, stuffed it in his coat pocket, and slid back down the tree, chased by a whole flock of screeching birds that pecked at his hair and ears the whole way down. He was delighted with his new pet, already making plans to tame it and teach it to talk.

As we trundled onwards, we chatted about the incredible instinct that made animals work together for the common good: beavers building shared dams, bees and ants running entire organized colonies, coral insects building entire reefs under the waves. I told Fritz stories of the giant ant hills in South America that were six feet tall, strong enough to withstand rain and sun so well you could turn an empty one into a working baking oven, and of the great toothless ant-eaters that used their massive curved claws to tear the hills apart and lap up thousands of the insects with their sticky worm-like tongues. He was so engrossed in the stories he almost missed the grove of tall broad-leafed trees growing ahead of us, dripping sticky gummy resin from cracks in their bark. Fritz scraped a handful off the bark, and yelped when he realized it stretched elastic and bounced right back to its original shape when he pulled it. It was caoutchouc – india-rubber! We were giddy with excitement, knowing we could tap the milky sap to make waterproof boots, watertight containers, dozens of useful things far beyond just erasing pencil marks.

Before long we reached the coconut grove that marked the edge of Cape Disappointment, the furthest point we’d ever explored on the coast, and spotted a small wind-broken palm tree I instantly recognized as the sago palm, its pith full of mealy starch dotted with plump little fat grubs. I remembered that in the West Indies those grubs were considered a delicacy, so I lit a small fire, speared half a dozen on a stick sprinkled with salt, and roasted them. They oozed rich savory fat that smelled incredible, and when I tried one on a piece of baked potato it was buttery and delicious, no trace of the ick factor I’d expected. Fritz worked up the courage to try one too, and we both agreed it was the best wild snack we’d found on the island. We cut a huge bundle of sweet sugarcane from the nearby sugar brake, loaded everything onto our sledge, and headed for home, with no more adventures slowing us down until we trundled into Falconhurst at dusk. The whole family crowded around us, oohing over our haul, the little green parrot instantly becoming the favorite toy of the two younger boys. We ate a hearty supper by firelight, climbed up into our tree-castle, hauled the ladder up after us, and crashed asleep after the long, exhausting, perfect day.

The next morning, all the boys woke up chattering about nothing but candles. Over breakfast they badgered me until I agreed we could start our chandlery experiment right away, even if I privately had small doubts we could get the wax to burn bright without extra animal fat. Under my direction, they dumped all the wax berries into a big shallow iron pot over the fire, skimming off the sweet-smelling green wax that rose to the top, while my wife twisted cotton cloth into wicks for us. We dipped the wick over and over in the melted wax, hanging them on a nearby bush to harden, until we had a full set of thick, sturdy candles. That very night, we stayed up three whole hours after dark, sitting in the warm glow of our homemade candles, no more fumbling around by firelight. My wife laughed and said if I was so clever, I could figure out how to make butter too, because all her extra cream was going to waste with no way to churn it.

I remembered the old Hottentot trick for making butter without a churn. I grabbed a hollowed out gourd bottle, filled it half full with cream, corked the hole tight, and told the boys to lash it in a sling of sailcloth strung between four posts, then roll it back and forth nonstop for half an hour. When they pried the cork open, they pulled out a solid lump of perfect fresh golden butter, and my wife could not stop grinning.

We spent the next six weeks busy from dawn to dusk. We built a sturdier, heavier wooden cart to replace the old sledge that was falling apart, laid a smooth gravel path from Falconhurst all the way across Family Bridge to Tentholm, so we could reach our secondary shelter even in the worst rainstorms, and lined both sides of the path with walnut, cherry, and chestnut saplings to grow into a shady avenue. We planted grapevines winding around the great mangrove’s arched roots, and fortified Tentholm for emergencies first: we grew a thick, impenetrable hedge of prickly thorn bushes all around its perimeter, dragged two old ship’s cannons from the wreck to mount on the small hillocks overlooking the bridge, so we could defend ourselves from any wild animal or unexpected stranger that might stumble onto the island. The hard work made the boys strong and fast, so I had them practice running, leaping, climbing, and swimming every day – though the downside was their clothes, once neat and well-mended, were now ragged tattered messes held together with dozens of patches.

I decided it was time to make one last trip out to the wreck to salvage every last useful thing, before the rotting hull broke apart completely. I took the three boys out in the pinnace, and we rummaged every inch of the ship’s hold: sailors’ chests, bales of linen and cloth, extra small guns, barrels of pitch, window shutters, locks and bolts, even spare benches. We made trip after trip, towing our tub boat behind us, until not a single useful scrap was left on the hulk. Then I told the family we were going to blow the old wreck up, so its sharp splintered planks wouldn’t tear our boats to shreds on the rocks. We tied a huge heavy copper cauldron to a pair of empty casks so it would float, lit the fuse, and retreated to the shore to watch. Darkness fell, and suddenly a vivid pillar of fire burst from the black waves, a low deep boom rolling across the bay. We all felt a sharp little twinge of sadness, like we’d lost an old friend that had carried us all the way from our home in Switzerland.

The next morning, all that sadness melted away when we saw the shore strewn with floating planks and beams from the wreck, and the copper cauldron bobbing right where we’d expected it. My wife ran over to us with even better news: two of our domestic ducks and a goose had snuck off to nest in the river reeds, and were now parading past us with huge fluffy broods of ducklings and goslings. Ernest whooped that soon we’d be feasting on roast duck and green peas, almost civilized again. I noticed all the young saplings in our new shady avenue were bent almost double by the wind, so I announced we’d make a trip to harvest bamboo stakes to prop them up. Everyone, my wife included, begged to come along for the multi-day adventure, so we hitched the cow and our ass to our new cart, loaded it down with a tent, provisions, extra ammo, and all our tools, and set off to collect every wild fruit and useful plant the unexplored part of the island had to offer. The kids laughed and cheered as we passed the grosbeak tree they’d never seen, stopped to harvest a fresh sack of wax berries, and then turned our steps straight for the caoutchouc grove, ready to tap the milky rubber sap to make the waterproof boots we’d all been daydreaming of.

Dawn broke bright over our island camp, and my four boys and I hauled our bamboo-laden cart out at first light, bound for the half-planted avenue of fruit saplings we’d laid out months prior. We left the buffalo behind: the gash in his nostril still had not fully scabbed over, and I wanted no risk of reopening it before he was fit for hard work.

We could not have arrived a minute later. The last unseasonal gale had torn through the grove overnight: half our young trees lay flat on the mud, roots half ripped out, the rest bowed so far over their crowns nearly brushed the ground, with only a handful still standing straight. We knelt in the muck, dug deep around each toppled root, and drove stout bamboo stakes deep into the earth, lashing every wobbly sapling firm to its new support with tough, broad island fibre.

“Papa,” small Franz said, passing me a fresh length of twine as I worked, “are these wild trees, or tame?”
“These are the fiercest, most ferocious trees in the whole forest,” Jack cackled, not missing a chance to tease his little brother. “We’re tying them up so they don’t run off. Soon we’ll loosen the ropes a little, and they’ll trot after us like pets, dropping peaches and pears right at our feet! We’ll even put a nose ring through their trunks, just like the buffalo!”
“That’s silly,” Franz said, frowning very seriously. “I know wild trees grow in the woods, the sour little crabapples, and tame ones grow in gardens, like the pear and peach trees we had back in Switzerland. So which are these?”

“Neither fully wild, nor naturally tame,” I told him, wiping mud off my hands. “They’re grafted trees. No European fruit tree will grow good, sweet fruit on its own, not until you graft it.” When he blinked, confused at the new word, I hurried to explain: “Grafting means you snip a small living twig from a good fruit tree, and tuck it into a little notch, or eye, cut into the branch of a hardier wild tree. The two grow together, and the branch that sprouts bears fruit from the twig you brought, not the sour wild stock. If we took a twig from a fine sweet Ribston pippin apple tree, and tucked it into a wild crabapple’s notch, in two years that branch would hang heavy with perfect, juicy apples.”

“But if no good fruit trees grow on their own where we live,” Ernest put in, curious as always, “where did the very first slips of good fruit come from?”
“From far away,” I smiled. “Only the cold, temperate parts of the world require that trick. South of us, in warm tropical lands, luscious fruit grows wild all on its own, no tending needed. Back when the Romans invaded Britain, all they found growing wild was sour crabapples, hazelnuts, and brambles. They brought grafting slips from across the empire, and suddenly Britain had sweet apples, filberts, raspberries. Even our own Switzerland! Not a single one of our native fruit trees grew there naturally. Cherries? They come from Cerasus, a land in old Asia Minor, brought to Europe by the Roman general Lucullus 70 years before the birth of Christ. Walnuts came from Persia, grapes are even older—you remember the Bible tells us Noah planted the very first vineyard after the flood.”

We talked as we worked, until the last wobbly sapling was lashed tight to its bamboo stake, and by the time we trundled back to Falconhurst, our appetites were so ravenous even the fussiest gourmet would have stared. My wife nearly gasped at the way we fell on the plates of corned beef and buttery boiled palm cabbage she set out, and one by one we collapsed back on our mats to digest, brainstorming our next round of big camp projects.

“I have one very specific request,” she said, once we were all full. “That treehouse nest up in the great banyan is perfect, I wouldn’t change a thing about it. But I am sick of scaling that rickety wobbly rope ladder every single time to get up there. Can we build proper stairs instead?”

I thought hard for a minute. “Building stairs bolted to the outside of the trunk would be impossible, but—what if we built them inside the trunk? I swear that massive old banyan is hollow, at least part of the way. Didn’t you tell me a few days ago you saw honeybees buzzing in and out of a small hole halfway up?”
“I saw them!” Franz piped up. “I climbed up to look, and one flew right in my face and stung my cheek. I almost cried, but I didn’t.”
“Brave boy,” I said. “If there’s enough room for an entire swarm of bees inside that trunk, it might be hollow for almost its whole height, just like the willow trees back home that grow thick and strong through their bark even when their whole inner core rots away.”

Jack was already on his feet before I finished speaking, scrambling up the lower branches to tap at the trunk with his knuckles, the rest of the boys hot on his heels, peeking in the bee hole and rapping at the wood to listen for the hollow thud. They forgot all about the bees living inside that trunk—until a low angry buzz swelled out of the hole, and a whole swarm of infuriated insects burst out to chase them. They swarmed the boys, stinging their faces, hands, necks, tangling in their hair, and the four of them came scrambling back to me yelling and swatting. It took us ten minutes to wave the bees off, and Jack, who’d been the first one to lean over the hole, looked the worst: his face was puffed up so round and swollen he could barely see, and we had to slather cool damp mud over all their stings to dull the throbbing pain.

While they recovered, I got to work. I carved a perfect little beehive out of a large hollow calabash gourd: I flattened the bottom, cut an arched little doorway in the front, and wove a thatched straw roof over the top to keep the rain and hot sun out. We couldn’t do anything else that day, the bees were still too riled up, so after full dark fell, when every last bee had crawled back inside the trunk, Fritz and I crept over and sealed every single crack and hole in the wood tight with wet clay, trapping them safely inside.

We were up before sunrise the next day. I pushed a hollow reed cane through the clay plug over the main bee hole, lit my pipe full of strong tobacco, and blew thick billowing smoke down into the trunk. The roar of angry buzzing inside was deafening at first, then it softened to a low murmur, and after I’d smoked through two full pipes, it went dead quiet. The bees were completely stupefied.

“Fritz, quick, chisel and hammer,” I called. We cut a small removable door into the trunk right next to the old bee hole, propped it open, and carefully scooped every cluster of drowsy bees we could find out into the waiting gourd hive. Then we scraped every last drop of golden honey and beeswax out of their old nest, stowing all of it in a sealed cask I’d set aside. To make sure the bees didn’t try to wander back to their old hollow once they woke up, I set a small smouldering pile of tobacco leaves on a ledge nailed inside the trunk, so the whole cavity reeked of acrid smoke. It worked perfectly: when the bees woke up, they buzzed in circles around the smoky trunk for ten minutes, then finally settled back into their clean, sweet new gourd hive, and by sunset they were quiet and working as normal. That night we strained all the honey out of the comb, poured it into earthen jars, melted down the leftover wax, and pressed it clean through a canvas sack to store for candle making.

The next morning we started the big project: hollowing out the entire banyan trunk for the spiral staircase. We cut a proper man-sized doorway right at the base, just big enough to fit the spare heavy oak door we’d salvaged from the wrecked captain’s cabin, hung it on its hinges, then scraped out all the crumbly rotten inner wood until we could stand at the bottom of the trunk and look straight up through the open hollow all the way to the sky. We set a thick young sapling dead in the centre as a central axle, cut notches all up its length to hold the steps, matching notches in the inner wall of the trunk, then cut each step smooth from planks salvaged off the wreck, nailing every one firm into place. We spiralled the stairs up and up, cutting small glass-paned windows into the trunk wall every ten feet to let in light and fresh air, running smooth handrails along the central axle and the outer trunk wall the whole way. It took us a full month of steady, easy work to finish the whole flight, all the way up to the floor of our treehouse nest.

We didn’t waste a minute of that month on idle work. We tended the whole camp: the goats and the sheep both had frisky new baby kids and lambs, and we tied tiny little cast brass bells we’d fished off the wreck’s cargo hold around their necks, so we could hear them tinkle through the trees if they wandered off. Juno our mastiff had a litter of fluffy new puppies; we kept two, and Jack tucked his tiny adopted jackal cub Fangs right in with the puppies, and Juno accepted the strange little wild pup as her own without a fuss. Our buffalo’s nose wound healed completely, and we broke him to saddle and pack: we started by tying sacks and bundles to his hide girth to get him used to carrying weight, then let our little monkey Knips ride on his back to get him used to the feel of a passenger, before the boys climbed on to ride him bareback through the fields. Fritz trained his pet eagle to hunt: he left bits of raw small game on the buffalo’s horns and the back of our great tame bustard, until the eagle learned to swoop down and strike prey on the wing, perfectly obeying Fritz’s whistle, and we kept him far away from the poultry yard so he never got a taste for our tame chickens. Ernest wove a tiny little rush basket with straps for Knips the monkey, and trained him to scamper up tall coconut palms to pick ripe fruit and stuff it in the basket to bring back down. Even my attempt to make new, better candles worked out: we mixed half beeswax, half waxy candleberry sap, split our bamboo moulds down the middle to slide the finished candles out easily, and tested two types of wick: the karata fibre my wife spun, and long thin splints of flammable native Antilles wood. The wood wicks burned bright and steady, even if they flared a little, and beat the dim sputtering karata wicks by a mile—no more wasting our soft cotton neck sweatbands for wicks. I even made the whole family a set of waterproof boots: I moulded clay over sand-filled socks to make a perfect foot-shaped form, brushed on layer after layer of gummy caoutchouc rubber, nailed a tough slab of buffalo hide to the sole, and coated that in rubber too, making boots that kept out every drop of mud and rain.

The last big convenience we added that month was a gravity-fed water system. We built a small low dam across Jackal River a ways upstream, raised the water level just high enough that it would flow downhill all the way to Falconhurst, then strung hollow bamboo pipes straight from the new reservoir to a huge empty tortoise shell we set right outside our front door, to use as a trough. No more hauling heavy buckets of water from the river every single day. We celebrated the grand opening by dumping a full sack of dirty potatoes straight into the flowing trough to wash them clean.

Then one morning, halfway through adding the final smooth handrail to the top of our spiral stairs, we heard it: a terrible, unearthly roar, echoing through the forest, so loud and strange none of us could place it. Jack yelped it was a lion, Fritz guessed a gorilla, and Ernest said, very reasonably, that it sounded most like a hyena. “Whatever it is,” I said, grabbing my rifle, “everyone up the stairs to the treehouse right now, I’ll lock the base door. We sent the dogs out to patrol the pastures, they’ll warn us if it comes closer.”

Fritz and I crept back down the stairs a few minutes later, guns cocked, to scout the treeline. The roar boomed out again right beside us, and Fritz immediately dropped his rifle, snorting so hard he nearly doubled over laughing. The weird sound was hee-haw, hee-haw, hee-haw: our old stray jackass, who’d wandered off weeks before, braying at the top of his lungs. And he wasn’t alone. Trotting right behind him, slim, graceful, and the colour of pale sand, was a wild onager—the rare wild Asiatic ass no European had ever tamed on this side of the world.

I sent Fritz quietly back to the house for a length of strong rope, a handful of oats, and a pinch of salt, while I split a bamboo stalk halfway down to make a set of soft pincers for the onager’s nose. We tied one end of the rope to a thick tree, made a tight running noose at the other, and crept out to hide in the brush. The old tame jackass saw the oats in Fritz’s hand and trotted right over to nibble from his palm, and the shy onager, seeing her new friend unafraid, stepped forward to sniff at the salt in his other hand. Fritz flicked the noose out in one smooth motion, and it settled right around her neck. She reared back so hard the noose pulled tight, and she stumbled to the ground half choked. I ran forward immediately, loosened the rope, replaced it with a soft leather halter, slipped the bamboo pincers over her nose, and tied her secure between two strong trees while she recovered. She lashed out with her hooves, snorting and stamping for an hour before she finally tired and went still, quivering. By dusk, I was able to lead her gently to the stables we’d built under the banyan tree, and tie her up right next to her old new jackass friend. We had a new addition to our island family, and not a single one of us could wait to break her to saddle.

At last, the gale died away. Bright, golden sunbeams burst through the riven storm clouds, the last of the rain drizzled to a stop, and soft, sweet spring settled over our island. No prisoner freed from a dark cell could have felt half as giddy with joy as our whole family did when we stumbled out of our cramped winter quarters, blinking at the lush new green grass under our feet, listening to a thousand birds trilling their mating songs, and breathing in the warm, flower-scented air that stung our cheeks in the best possible way.

The crops we’d sown before the storm were thriving vigorously, tender green shoots poking through the damp, rich earth, and every tree and bush was bursting with new leaves. Our first priority was Falconhurst, our beloved tree-top nest: the gale had torn half our thatch, scattered leaves all through the living space, and left the whole structure lopsided. We worked nonstop for three days patching it up, until it was as cozy and safe as ever. My wife was the first to badger me to get their flax-processing setup up and running, so I built a sturdily lined drying oven, fumbled my way through carving a spinning wheel and a beetle-reel out of hardwood, and soon she and little Franz were hard at work, the boy grinning as he reeled off the smooth linen thread she spun.

I’d been fretting for weeks about our stores stashed at Tentholm, so as soon as the weather cleared, Fritz and I headed over to check on them. The damage to Falconhurst was nothing compared to the ruin we found there: the canvas tent had been ripped to shreds and torn away by the wind, all our loose provisions were soaked through, and two whole casks of our precious gunpowder were ruined beyond saving. We dragged out every salvageable item and spread them across the rocks to dry in the sun. Our seaworthy pinnace was still tied up safe at the shore, but the little homemade tub boat we’d used for short trips had been dashed against the rocks into splinters. Staring at the wreckage, I made a firm promise to myself: before the next rainy season hit, we would have winter quarters far sturdier than any tent or tree house could ever be. That was when Fritz had his wild, brilliant idea: why not carve a cave straight into the solid face of the cliff above Tentholm?

The work sounded impossibly hard, but I agreed to try. Even if we couldn’t carve a full room, we could at least make a locked, dry cellar for our most valuable, perishable supplies. A few days later, we loaded the cart high with pickaxes, chisels, sledgehammers, crowbars, and shovels, trundled back to the cliff face, and chalked the outline of our entrance straight onto the smooth, perpendicular rock. For six straight days, we swung our tools nonstop, and barely made a dent in the stone. The little hole we’d chipped out wasn’t even big enough to make a decent kennel for our pet monkey Master Knips. We refused to quit, though, and soon we hit a layer of far softer rock, and our work sped up fast.

On the tenth day of digging, Jack was heaving on his crowbar with all his might when he yelled at the top of his lungs: “Father! Fritz! My bar went right through the mountain!”

Fritz snickered. “Go run around the other side and grab it! It probably fell all the way to Europe, you can’t lose a good crowbar.”

“No, I’m serious!” Jack waved us over, bright red and excited. “It went right through! I heard it crash down somewhere inside, come look!”

We crowded over to the tiny hole he’d made. I pushed the handle of my hammer into it, and it slid right through no resistance at all. Fritz handed me a long bamboo pole, and I poked it in as far as it would go, and never hit the other side. There was only a thin sheet of rock standing between us and a huge, hidden natural cavern on the other side.

The boys whooped and started hacking at the rock as fast as they could swing their tools, and soon the opening was big enough to squeeze through. I leaned in to take a look, and a thick, stale gust of stagnant air hit me square in the face, making my head spin so bad I stumbled back against the cliff, shouting at the boys to stay back. When I got my breath back, I explained the danger: trapped air in unventilated caves is full of noxious fumes and no oxygen, and you can pass out and die before you know what hit you. The old trick to flush it out, I told them, was to toss blazing hay inside to get the air moving. We tried that, but the flame went out the second it touched the opening.

That’s when I got a better idea. We dragged over the chest of signal rockets we’d salvaged from the wreck, and fired dozens of them straight into the hole. The fiery little rockets whizzed off deep into the dark cave, streaking past walls we couldn’t see, lighting up the whole massive space like magic, bouncing off a glittering, crystal-covered ceiling that glinted like thousands of stars. We waited ten minutes, then tossed in another armful of burning hay—this time it burned bright, steady, no flicker. The air was safe now.

Fritz and I widened the hole until it was big enough to walk through easily, and Jack jumped straight on the back of his buffalo Storm and galloped all the way back to Falconhurst to tell the rest of the family the wild news. He made such a big, dramatic fuss that ten minutes later he was thundering back across the log bridge, with my wife, Ernest, Franz, and all the dogs piled in the cart right behind him, every single one of them bouncing with excitement. Jack had stuffed every candle we owned into the cart, so we lit them all, grabbed our rifles, and stepped single-file into the cave, me leading the way and poking the ground ahead of us with a long pole to make sure we didn’t step into a hidden chasm.

The sight that met us took our breath away. It was a palace made of crystal, thousands of glistening stalactites dripping from the ceiling, great thick crystal pillars rising from the sandy floor like giant marble trees, every surface catching the candlelight and glinting every color of the rainbow. The floor was made of fine, perfectly dry sand, no damp anywhere. I scraped a little shard of the crystal off the wall, tasted it, and laughed out loud: this was a massive, pure rock salt cave. We had an unlimited, never-ending supply of the finest, cleanest salt right there, no more rationing the stuff we’d hauled off the wreck.

The only thing that gave me pause was a few loose big salt crystals scattered on the floor, that had fallen from the ceiling recently. We fired our rifles off at the ceiling from the entrance, to make sure no loose chunks were teetering up there waiting to fall on our heads—and nothing else shifted. Our new cave home was perfectly safe. We trundled back to Falconhurst that night chattering nonstop about what we’d found, already making plans to turn it into our main winter home, a totally impregnable shelter no storm, wild animal, or roving pirate could ever break into. We decided we’d keep old Falconhurst as a cozy summer hangout, and make the salt cave our proper Rock House fortress.

We went to work immediately. We hewed a line of proper windows into the cliff face, and fitted them with the window frames we’d salvaged from the officers’ cabins on the wreck, hung a heavy solid wooden door on the entrance, and covered up the old tree-hole entrance at Falconhurst with bark so no stray predators would ever spot it. We split the huge cavern into four distinct sections: a big main front room divided into a sitting area, dining space, and sleeping quarters; a right-hand wing that was our kitchen and workshop; a left-hand wing that housed all our stables for the livestock; and a dark, locked back recess that was our dry storehouse and gunpowder magazine. We built a proper stone fireplace and a carved chimney running up through the rock, so we’d never again be choked with smoke all winter long.

While we were working out of Tentholm every day, we stumbled on all kinds of new unexpected bounty. Every night, giant sea turtles would crawl up onto the beach to lay their eggs, their meat making for the richest, tastiest feasts we’d had since we shipwrecked. We started turning them on their backs, drilling a small hole through the edge of their shells, and tying them to stakes stuck in the sand right at the tide line, so we had an endless supply of fresh turtle meat any time we wanted it, no need to go hunting every day. Lobsters, crabs, and plump mussels were so thick on the shore we could scoop them up by the bucketful.

One morning, we spotted a strange glowing phenomenon out at sea: the water was churning and boiling, catching the sunrise so it looked like it was full of dancing fire, hundreds of seabirds screaming and diving into the surface over and over. When we ran down to the shore, I realized it was a massive, miles-wide shoal of herrings, fleeing the big predatory fish chasing them out in deep water, pushing right into our shallow bay. The boys waded right in with wicker baskets, scooping the silvery fish out by the armful as fast as they could, tossing them up onto the shore. We layered them in casks with salt, stowed the sealed casks in the cool back of our cave, and smoked the rest in a little reed hut we built out of brushwood, to make cured smoked herring that would keep for months.

Right after the herring shoal left, dozens of seals showed up off the shore, attracted by the scraps of fish we’d tossed back into the water. We caught two dozen of them, skinned their thick, tough hides to make new harness and strong work clothing, and boiled their blubber down into gallons of oil for our lamps, for soap making, and for tanning leather. When we were clearing up the gypsum deposits mixed in with the base of the salt crystals, we burned it down to make smooth, white plaster, that we used to seal the airtight lids of all our fish casks.

A month later, Jack spotted another huge school of fish swimming up to the mouth of Jackal River, and yelled that it was a bunch of baby whales. When we got down to the shore, we saw it was a massive run of huge sturgeon, salmon, and trout, all swimming upriver to lay their eggs in the stony shallows. Jack grabbed his homemade bow and arrow, tied a barbed spike to the arrowhead with a long length of twine, and waded right up to the bank. He shot the biggest sturgeon in the side, and the fish bolted so fast it almost dragged him into the river with it. I ran to help, and we both hauled the 6-foot giant ashore, laughing so hard our sides hurt. Soon all of us were in on the fun: Fritz had a harpoon, Ernest had a fishing rod, I had a big iron pitchfork, and even my wife suggested we harness Storm the buffalo to the rope when Fritz harpooned an 8-foot sturgeon that was too big for all of us to drag ashore alone. We salted some of the fish, smoked some, preserved some in oil, turned the sturgeon roe into caviar, and washed and dried their air bladders to make clear, transparent isinglass that worked perfectly as a replacement for glass in our new cave windows.

When we took a break from the cave work to check on our old fields back near Falconhurst, we found that little Franz and my wife had scattered handfuls of extra grain seed from the wreck in the tilled earth, and now the whole field was bursting with a bumper crop of wheat, barley, rye, peas, and Indian corn, all ripening at once. A huge flock of birds was trying to steal our corn, so Fritz unhooded his trained eagle, who chased down a fat ruffed grouse, and our pet jackal Fangs rounded up a dozen plump quails for our dinner. We sipped a sweet, cool drink my wife made from crushed young corn stalks, sugar cane juice, and water, and made plans for a new, outlying farm far away from our main camp, to raise our rapidly growing flock of chickens, pigs, sheep, and goats that we no longer had space to feed near the tree house.

We harnessed the buffalo, the cow, and the ass to the big cart, loaded up the animals, and tramped through the woods all the way out towards Cape Disappointment. When we stepped out of the forest onto a wide open plain, little Franz screamed that it was snow, and he wanted to make snowballs. The ground and the low bushes really were covered in soft white fluffy flakes—but they weren’t snow. It was a whole field of wild dwarf cotton bushes, bursting full of soft, fine cotton wool. My wife stuffed three full sacks of it, over the moon, because now we could spin all the cloth we needed for blankets and clothing.

We climbed a grassy, wooded hill just past the cotton field, and the view from the top was perfect: acres of rich green pasture, a sparkling little brook gushing down the slope, plenty of shade trees, shelter from the wind, and a clear view of the ocean. We couldn’t have picked a better spot for our new farm. We built a sturdy two-story shed using the existing thick tree trunks as pre-cut posts, laid a waterproof bark roof nailed down with acacia thorns, and while we were gathering firewood, we stumbled on even more treasures: wild fir trees that would give us tar and turpentine to seal our boats, terebinth trees that oozed clear amber resin we could turn into varnish, and a whole pile of loose, aromatic wild cinnamon sticks half-buried in the dirt. As we sat around our campfire that night, the soft cotton blankets under us, staring out at the sunset over the sea, we realized we weren’t just surviving on this island anymore. We were building a home.

We were up before the sun the next morning, the tang of wood smoke and wild honey still hanging in the air as we scraped our last bites of breakfast from our tin plates and set straight to work on the bird-lime we’d mixed the day before. The thick, stretchy adhesive, made of caoutchouc oil and turpentine, came out perfectly – sticky enough to catch even the most skittish, feather-light bird if it so much as brushed against it.

The boys fetched armfuls of thin, light rods from the underbrush, which I slathered thick with the goop, then positioned high in the boughs of the evergreen oaks, where sweet acorns hung heavy and flocks of pigeons congregated by the hundreds. These numbers dwarfed the straggling small group we’d spotted the year before; branches creaked and bowed under the weight of thousands of roosting birds, and the ground under the oaks was thick with their droppings, proof they returned there to sleep every night. That gave me an idea: we’d stage a torchlight raid after dark, the same trick Virginia colonists used to catch whole roosts at once.

For hours the bird-lime worked like a charm. Pigeons landed on the smeared rods without a second thought, and the more they fluttered and squawked in panic, the more they coated themselves in the sticky stuff, until they tumbled to the ground rods and all, crying out in bewildered distress. The boys soon got the hang of retrieving the captives, re-smudging the rods, and resetting the snares without any help from me, so I left them to it and headed off to chop pinewood and mix extra turpentine to make sturdy torches for our night expedition.

Half an hour later Jack came trotting over, clutching a trembling pretty pigeon unlike all the dark wild ones we’d caught, begging not to kill it. I recognized it as one of the European domestic breed we’d set free after our shipwreck, now gone feral, and carefully wiped its sticky wings and feet clean with a mix of oil and ash before tucking it into a wicker cage. Jack soon brought me several more just like it, a whole handful of breeding pairs, and I was delighted – instead of chasing feral pigeons through the trees for meat, we’d soon have a permanent flock of our own, with fresh pigeon-pie whenever we wanted.

When dusk bled over the treetops we set off for the acorn wood, armed only with long bamboo canes, our tarry torches, and empty canvas sacks. The boys grumbled that the gear looked far too light to catch so many birds, but they ate their words the second we stepped under the oak canopy after full dark. I lit our torches, and the golden flare cut straight through the black to reveal every branch crammed full of roosting ortolans and wild pigeons, dazzled and stunned by the sudden light. They fluttered helplessly through the boughs, half-blinded, and dozens fell to the grass before we even lifted our canes, easy to scoop up and tuck into the sacks. By the time we started thumping the branches with the bamboo sticks, my wife and Franz were scrambling to keep up, piling so many plump birds into the sacks they grew heavy enough to drag on the path by the time we turned for home. We stumbled back to Falconhurst long after midnight, stowed our game, and collapsed straight into our bunches too tired to do more than kick off our boots.

The whole next day was a whirlwind of plucking, boiling, roasting, and stewing pigeon, the whole homestead smelling of savory meat and spiced gravy, and we barely found time to sit down. The morning after that, though, we loaded up for a major expedition to Woodlands – our goal, to make sure the mischievous monkey raiders that had torn up our cottage and eaten our crops would never dare come near our farm again. My wife packed us three days’ worth of provisions, then stayed behind with Franz and our mastiff Turk to hold down the homestead, while we set off loaded with extra-strong, extra-sticky birdlime far thicker than the stuff we’d used on the pigeons, dozens of short sharp stakes, spools of twine, and a pile of hollow coconut shells and dried gourds. Our buffalo hauled all the gear plus Ernest riding on his back, and I rode our ass, until we reached a hidden patch of thick underbrush a short walk from Woodlands. We pitched our small tent, tethered our grazing animals, and tied up our hunting dogs tight so they wouldn’t roam and bark and blow the whole ambush.

The little cottage at Woodlands was quiet and empty, so we lost no time prepping for our monkey visitors. We drove the stakes lightly into the grass all around the cottage to make a ramshackle irregular paling, then strung twine crisscrossing every gap between them to make a tangled labyrinth no monkey could pass through without brushing against string or wood. We slathered every single inch of the stakes and twine thick with our heavy birdlime, then set basins of the goop everywhere, sprinkled with rice, maize, and dried fruit as bait. We finished the whole trap before sundown, and crawled into our tent to sleep, hidden away from sight.

We woke at dawn to the familiar chattering, rustling racket of a huge horde of apes heading straight for us. We grabbed our heavy clubs, held the straining dogs back by their leashes, and crept behind the thick bushes to watch the show. The branches creaked and crackled overhead, the apes screeching and howling so loud our ears rang, before the whole disorderly rabble came pouring out of the trees, leaping, tumbling, and racing straight for the cottage. Drawn by the strange new bowls of food, they swarmed every inch of the clearing, some scrambling for the bait, others clambering onto the cottage roof to yank at the wooden pegs trying to force their way inside.

One by one, they all got stuck. Apes wandered away from the bait, suddenly finding their paws, heads, backs, or tails coated in thick sticky lime. Some sat down making the most ridiculous faces, scrubbing futilely at their fur trying to clean themselves. Others got tangled in the twine, trailing stakes and string behind them as they hopped around in bewildered despair. Groups of monkeys that tried to help each other out only stuck fast to each other, the more they pulled and kicked the more hopelessly trapped they got. Apes that dove for the coconut shells of bait got their paws stuck inside, the shells clattering and banging around their wrists as they ran. The silliest sight of all was an old big male ape who’d found a gourd full of leftover palm wine: he’d drunk deep, and the sticky rim of the gourd had glued right to his forehead and whiskers, covering his eyes completely. He blundered around the clearing doing the wildest somersaults trying to shake it off, squawking at the top of his lungs.

Hundreds of the apes tried to flee for the treetops, but we’d slathered lime on the trunks of all the nearby trees, so many got stuck to the bark, hanging from branches squawking and writhing. With the whole horde panicking, I loosed our three straining hunting dogs, who charged straight into the fray like they were eager to serve up justice to the raiding thieves. We joined in with our clubs, and the clearing was soon full of barking, howling, and shrieking, until at last the whole noise died away into heavy silence. We stood staring at the carnage, at least forty dead apes littering the grass, and the boys went quiet and sad, their faces fallen. I felt the same heavy twist in my chest, and hurried to set them to work burying the bodies, burning all the stakes, string, and lime bowls, wiping the whole clearing clean of our gruesome trap. After that we fixed up the damaged cottage, rounded up our scattered sheep, goats, and poultry, and herded them safely back into their pens.

As we worked we kept hearing heavy thuds from the trees nearby, and when we went to investigate we found three stunning birds stuck to the limed twigs we’d strung in the branches. Two were rare Blue Molucca pigeons, and the third was a resplendent Nicobar pigeon, its feathers shimmering iridescent green, bronze, and steely blue in the sunlight. I told Fritz we’d be the first to settle them in a new dove-cot near our cave, and teased him that I’d use a little “magic” to keep them around, refusing to explain more.

In a few short days Woodlands was fully repaired and everything was settled, so we headed back to Falconhurst to a raucous welcome from my wife and Franz. We all agreed next we’d head for Tentholm, to carve a proper pigeon loft right out of the rock cliff facing Jackal River. We spent weeks hollowing out a wide, spacious cavity in the stone, fitting it with wooden perches and nesting nooks, then nailing a big sturdy wooden facade over the entrance, cut with little pigeon-sized entry holes, sliding shutters, and a wide flat wooden platform out front where the birds could walk and rest.

When it was all finished, I sent the rest of the boys off to work at a distance, then pulled Fritz aside to share my “magic” trick. I mixed up a special bait of soft clay, aniseed, and salt that the birds would go crazy for, then pounded aniseed seeds into oil, strained it through a scrap of cloth, and slathered the sweet-smelling stuff all over the entry holes of the loft, so every bird that flew in or out would get the scent on their feathers to attract every wild pigeon for miles. We put our tamed domestic pigeons and the three rare new foreign pigeons inside, and shut the sliding door. When the rest of the boys climbed the ladder to peek through the little glass window I’d set in the wooden facade, they found all the birds peacefully pecking at the sweet scented bait, and Fritz kept my secret, calling it “magic food” to his brothers.

On the third morning I woke Fritz before dawn, and he climbed the rope ladder to tie a long cord to the sliding panel, so we could open and close the door from the ground below, then doused the entrance with more aniseed oil. We woke the rest of the family, and gathered around the base of the cliff, where I waved a stick with over-the-top mock solemnity, mumbled a fake wizard’s incantation, and nodded at Fritz to yank the cord.

The pigeons poked their heads out of the holes one by one, glanced around, then hopped out onto the wooden platform. With a sharp whir of wings the whole flock took off, circling high over our heads, before sweeping out of sight. A few minutes later they all circled back and settled on the loft’s platform, except for the three rare blue foreign pigeons, who streaked straight off through the trees in the direction of Falconhurst, flying fast like they were never coming back. We were all gutted, convinced we’d lost our beautiful new birds forever, and checked the loft every hour all day waiting to see if they’d return.

Mid-afternoon the next day Jack came sprinting into the cave, red-faced and yelling at the top of his lungs that one of the blue pigeons was back. We all poured outside, and sure enough the first Molucca pigeon was strutting on the loft platform, cooing and bobbing his head at a pretty wild female of his own breed, popping in and out of the entry hole to show off the nest he’d found until the shy little hen followed him inside. Before the boys could rush to pull the shutter closed, Fritz pointed at the sky, and the second blue pigeon came flying in, also leading a new pretty mate behind him. Last of all, late that evening, Franz and his wife came running to get us, and we saw the handsome Nicobar pigeon had returned too, escorting a shimmering hen just as colorful as he was.

Soon they were all building nests, and we noticed them carrying long strands of soft grey lichen back with them for nesting material – the exact same fibrous West Indian moss people used to stuff mattresses instead of horsehair, my wife immediately decided, already planning dozens of new pillows, bolsters, saddle cushions, and soft padding for all our furniture. We even found whole nutmegs mixed in with the seeds the pigeons dropped on the loft floor, which we planted carefully in our orchard.

Things stayed quiet for a stretch, until Jack snuck off on one of his secret private expeditions, and came back hours later covered head to toe in mud and green slime, one shoe missing, hauling a huge bundle of fine Spanish reeds over his back that were just as caked in muck as he was. He’d waded into the swamp behind the old powder magazine to get the reeds to weave new baskets and hen coops, wandered too far out, and sunk deep into the soft black mud, stuck up to his knees screaming for help until his faithful dog Fangs had bounded out to find him. Trapped and desperate, Jack had cut all the reeds he could reach around him, tied them together into a makeshift raft, grabbed hold of Fangs’ tail, and the dog had dragged him inch by inch all the way back to solid ground. We fussed over him, praised his quick thinking, and cleaned him up, then I used his perfect reeds to secretly build a loom for my wife – teasing the other boys that it was a “strange outlandish musical instrument” their mother knew how to play, until the day I presented it to her, and her delight was only matched by the boys’ endless amusement watching her “play the loom” weaving cloth for our family.

Not long after, a beautiful strong little foal was born to our wild onager, we named him Swift, and began training him to be my personal riding mount. With the rainy season fast approaching, I rigged a simple aqueduct made of hollow bamboo poles, leading fresh running water straight from Jackal River right into our cave, connected to a large wooden cask as a reservoir. No more hauling heavy buckets half a mile through the mud, my wife declared, and she was prouder of that simple bamboo pipe than she would have been of a fancy marble fountain carved with mermaids and dolphins.

We spent the rest of the dry season stockpiling every bit of food we could harvest: roots, sweet potatoes, rice, guavas, sweet acorns, pinecones, every sack and cask we had stuffed full, even tearing apart our old raft to repurpose its big wooden tubs for extra storage space. The weather turned wild and unsettled, heavy storm clouds piling on the horizon, thunder rolling, and huge foaming groundswells crashing against the cliff faces. June was almost here, and twelve long weeks of nonstop rain were coming. We moved the cow, the ass, the two light-footed riding goats, all our dogs, our pet monkey Knips, and our tamed eagle into the salt cave with us to ride out the storms. We rigged a tall bamboo pole all the way up to the cave’s high vaulted ceiling, fixed a pulley in a crack in the rock, and hoisted a big four-wick ship’s lantern high up to light the dark inner cavern. We split up chores to make the cave cozy for the long rainy months: Ernest and Franz built and arranged shelves for our little library, Jack and my wife set up the kitchen and sitting room, while Fritz and I dragged our carpenter’s bench, lathe, and big tool chest into the workshop, hanging every last tool on the walls where we could reach it easily, ready for the long months of work and warmth inside while the storms raged outside.

Dawn broke pink over Safety Bay, and I whooped to my family as we stumbled out of our makeshift shelters: “Right, lads and lasses, time to finish this stinky, greasy whale job for good!”

We set straight to work first thing, rigging a rough wooden scaffold to stack the tubs of blubber on. Heavy stones piled on top of the slabs squeezed every last drop of glistening, pure train oil out of the lumps of fat, dripping down into clean clay pots we’d set underneath. A little later, we dragged a big iron cauldron far away from Rockburg to boil down the remaining blubber, skimming off gunk and straining every batch through coarse linen until we had barrels and stitched intestine-sacks brim full of good, useful oil, all stowed away in our roughest store nook the boys had nicknamed “the cellar.”

The stench was miserable all day long, my poor wife paling more than once even as she kept her good humor through the whole mess. But she was quick to point out a brilliant fix. “We should turn that little sandbar where the whale washed up into a second outpost,” she said. “Our goats and chickens we leave there will be safe from thieving apes, and we can do all our smelly oil-boiling and rendering work far away from the cave where we sleep and eat!”

The boys cheered so loud the seagulls took off, all of them bouncing at the chance to explore a new settlement right away—until I reminded them the rotting whale carcass was still lying there reeking to high heaven. They conceded pretty quick it made more sense to let wind, storms, seabirds, and insects pick the skeleton clean first, and let the air clear for a few weeks before we went back.

That night, a silly idea that had been bouncing around my head for days stuck: I was going to build a mechanical rowing machine. I took a thick iron bar, laid it across the middle of our small boat so it jutted a foot out over each side, fixed ridged gear teeth right in the center of the bar, and fastened four flat, angled paddle spokes to each end of the bar like the paddles on a water wheel. Then I rigged our heavy ship’s jack to lock its big cogwheel right into the ridged gear on the iron bar. Wind the jack, and the whole bar spun fast, the paddles churning through the water to push the boat along without a single oar to pull.

It was clunky, rough around the edges, far from perfect—but when Fritz and I wound the mechanism up and took it for a test run across the bay, we splashed along so fast the whole family was cheering and clapping so loud the coves rang. Everyone piled down to the shore begging for a ride, but the sun was dipping low by then, so I promised them a full day cruise the next morning to Prospect Hill and Cape Disappointment, to collect plants to start our new Whale Island farm.

We packed all our gear that night: spades, mattocks, food, hunting knives, extra salt, and even a slab of cooked whale tongue the boys had insisted we save for a special treat at the farm. We left Rockburg neat and locked up, pushed off at sunrise, and the whole ride was smooth as glass. Our little rowing machine hummed along no trouble, leaving Safety Bay and Shark Island far behind, as we all leaned back to stare at the green, cliff-fringed coast rolling past us.

When we landed at Prospect Hill, we moored the boat and tramped through the woods to our little remote farm. Halfway there, we heard a rooster crow, and my chest ached so sharp I almost stopped walking—for a second I was back on our old family farm in Switzerland, tramping up the lane to a neighbor’s house at sunrise, that familiar, silly farm sound greeting us. It hit hard that here, the only living things that recognized us were the wild animals we’d reared ourselves, out in the middle of nowhere. Our sheep, goats, and chickens had forgotten us while we were gone, and their lambs, kids, and chicks scattered like we were a pack of wolves. The boys had to lasso the goats one by one, tie their legs gently, and bribe them with salt licks to get a pail of fresh goat’s milk. My wife lured the hens over with handfuls of rice, catching a dozen young ones to take back with us if we wanted.

We laid out our cold picnic, and the boys all grabbed for the famous whale tongue first—only for every single face to twist up in disgust. It tasted like nothing but strong train oil! Not a single person would take a second bite, and we all dumped the whole slab straight to our big shaggy dog Fangs, who gulped it down like it was the best meal he’d ever had. Fresh coconut and cold goat’s milk fixed our moods fast, though, and soon Fritz and I were digging up sugar cane shoots and young coconut saplings to plant.

We pushed off again after lunch, deciding to round Cape Disappointment first to scout the coast beyond—only to hit a stretch of hidden reefs and a huge, miles-long sandbar sticking out from the headland, the waves crashing white over the hidden rocks. No way we could sail past there safely, so we turned the boat straight for Whale Island instead.

We set to planting the saplings right away, but the boys soon got bored of digging, wandering off to hunt for shells and coral along the shore. A few minutes later, Jack came sprinting back yelling at the top of his lungs. “Father! Mother! Come quick! There’s a giant skeleton here! A mammoth! An antediluvian monster, for sure!”

I laughed and told him he was being silly—of course it was the skeleton of our whale. But Jack shook his head so hard his hair flew. “No! These are beast bones, not fish bones! The whale’s probably floated out to sea! This thing is bigger than our whole boat!” Before I could follow him, Ernest’s voice cut across the island from the other direction. “Father! Hurry! A huge turtle! It’s waddling back to the sea right now, and I’m hanging onto its back leg but I can’t stop it!”

That was far more urgent, so I grabbed an oar and sprinted over. Sure enough, there was a giant sea turtle a full eight feet long, scrambling for the waves, Ernest hanging on for dear life to its hind leg. I jammed the oar under its shell as a lever, and together we tipped it right onto its back, helpless and kicking.

We walked over to check Jack’s “mammoth” then, and he soon saw he’d been wrong. I pointed out the footprints we’d left all over the sand the week before, and the hacked open whale jawbones where we’d cut out the baleen plates. “You boys thought whales were fish, didn’t you?” I teased. “They’re mammals, same as us, same as cows, same as bats and sloths. They have the same mammary glands that make milk for their babies as every other land warm-blooded creature. Their bones are hollow and filled with air, same as bird bones, to make their heavy bodies buoyant in the water. You can see the wisdom of the Creator in that design, if you stop to look.”

Fritz pointed at the mountain of bone and asked if we could put it to use. “Not today,” I said. “Leave it to bleach in the sun a few more months, and we’ll saw it up later to make chairs, or a reading desk for our little museum. But now we need to get this turtle home.”

We rigged a long rope, tied one end to our boat’s bow, tied the other tight around the turtle’s neck and front flippers, and fastened a big empty water cask to the line so it couldn’t dive deep and drag us under. We tipped the turtle back right side up, and it bolted straight for the water, plunging in and swimming as hard as it could. It couldn’t dive, the cask bobbing along behind it, so it towed our whole boat across the bay back to Rockburg! The boys were whooping so hard they could barely breathe, teasing me that I was Neptune riding a seaturtle chariot instead of dolphins. We landed safe at our usual cove, and when we cleaned the turtle out, its eight-foot long shell was perfect to turn into a giant water trough for the cave’s kitchen, and we salted down all the meat to feed us for weeks.

We spent the next weeks busy with small, satisfying projects. I gave up on trying to plow a new field before the rains came—we had no oxen trained to the plow yet anyway—and finished a rough, clumsy but perfectly functional hand loom, using old skills I’d picked up as a boy hanging around weavers’ workshops in Switzerland. Instead of wasting flour to paste the threads to keep them from drying out, I used clear isinglass that worked perfectly, and even cut thin, transparent isinglass plates to fit into the little windows cut high in the cave rock, just as clear as horn, keeping rain out and letting sunlight in. Then I taught the boys to make harnesses out of thick cured leather, stuffing the saddles and collars with soft hairy moss, so our horses, donkeys, and oxen all had proper stirrups, bridles, and straps for the boys to ride them, much to all their delight.

When the annual herring shoals arrived, followed by swarms of other fish and seals, we caught dozens of them, curing the meat and storing the oil for winter use. We wove baskets of all shapes and sizes, since our old sacks were wearing out—even a giant two-man carry basket, that the boys immediately plopped little Franz inside and paraded him around the farm like a royal prince.

That gave Fritz a brilliant, very silly idea. “We can hang that big basket between our two oxen, Storm and Grumble!” he said. “Make a soft, swinging litter for mother, so she doesn’t have to bounce around in our bumpy old ox cart!” Before we could stop them, the boys had the basket slung between the two docile oxen, and plopped Ernest inside to test it. At first it swayed slow and smooth, and Ernest was cheering that it was more comfortable than any carriage he’d ever ridden—until his brothers decided to have fun, and made the oxen trot faster and faster, bouncing poor Ernest around in the basket like a pea in a pod. By the time they pulled up in front of the cave, Ernest was red-faced and furious, yelling at his brothers for teasing him, and a fight was about to break out. I stepped in quick, reminding them that our family’s good humor and kindness to each other was the only real safety we had on this wild island, and the boys soon made up, Ernest even going to get salt and barley treats for the two oxen to reward them for the ride.

We were all sitting in the shade of our verandah later that afternoon weaving wicker, when Fritz jumped to his feet, staring down the avenue leading to Jackal River. “Father! There’s something so weird out there! It’s coiled on the ground like a big ship’s cable, then it rears up like a little mast, then sinks back down and the coils move towards our bridge!”

My wife grabbed the younger boys and hurried them into the cave, and I told them to bar all the lower entrances, and take up their guns at the high rock windows. I lifted my spyglass to my eye, and my blood ran cold. “It’s a boa constrictor,” I said, “over thirty feet long. It’s heading straight for our bridge, no doubt.” We all knew better than to rush it: giant snakes like that are almost impossible to kill with a single bullet, too strong, too fast, too hard to hurt unless you hit them right in the head. I sent Fritz up to the rest of the family to help load guns, and kept watching the giant serpent slither closer, rising its head fifteen feet in the air every few seconds to sniff the wind and look for prey.

It slithered slowly across the wooden bridge, and I ran back up to the cave opening to join the rest of my family, all of us hidden behind the high rock slits, guns loaded. We waited, our hearts pounding, as the snake slithered into the clearing outside our cave. The boys could not hold their fire any longer: Fritz, Jack, Ernest, even my wife all pulled their triggers. The bullets only startled it, making it move faster. Fritz and I fired next, aiming for the wide, raised head—but we missed, and the great serpent slid away, vanishing straight into the thick reedy marsh to our left.

For three whole days we did not dare step more than a few hundred feet from the cave door, all of us on edge. Our geese and ducks were so spooked they kept cackling and flapping their wings all night, too scared to roost in the reeds—until they finally took flight and flew all the way over to Shark Island to sleep. We were running low on hay for our animals, and I decided the next morning that Fritz would lead all the livestock across the river at the shallow ford to graze on the far bank, where the snake could not get them.

But as we were tying the animals in a line to lead them over, our silly old donkey Grizzle, who’d been cooped up for three days, got frisky, broke free of his halter, and bolted straight for the marsh before any of us could stop him. We called and yelled, but he kept galloping—right into the reeds. We watched, horrified, as the great boa reared up out of the underbrush, jaws open wide, fangs glinting. Grizzle froze, braying so loud the whole valley echoed, but it was too late. The serpent coiled around him in a second, squeezing tight, wrapping itself over and over the donkey’s struggling body, dodging his kicking hooves.

“Shoot it, father! Save Grizzle!” the boys screamed. But I shook my head. We could not risk shooting the snake now: we would hit the donkey, and the wounded snake would slip away into the reeds even more angry and dangerous. “Once it gorges on Grizzle, it will be slow, heavy, and we can kill it for sure then,” I told them.

“But it can’t swallow the whole donkey at once, can it?” Jack gasped.

“Snakes have no chewing teeth,” I explained. “They swallow every prey animal whole, no matter how big, bones, hooves, hair, and all, and digest it over weeks.”

Fritz stared at the squirming mass of snake and donkey in the reeds, his eyes wide. “That can’t be possible,” he said. “There’s no way the donkey’s big ribs and thick hooves could ever fit down that serpent’s throat.”

The life-or-death battle with the giant boa was over, but a cold knot of anxiety stayed tight in my chest long after we’d dragged its massive carcass clear of our farm paths. I couldn’t stop wondering if another serpent had slipped unseen into the swamp thickets, or might slither through the only unblocked pass at the Gap from the wild, unexplored country beyond Falconhurst.

I mapped out two critical trips first: the first a full, exhaustive sweep of the marsh to root out any hidden threats, the second a push all the way to the Gap to seal that entry point tight before any more predators found their way into our territory. When I called my sons to join me for the marsh survey, both Ernest and Jack dragged their feet, Jack swearing he still got cold shivers thinking about how that snake’s last tail swipe could have caved his ribs right in. I didn’t let their reluctance win out, though, and we set out laying planks and woven wicker hurdles across the bog, shifting them forward step by step to keep from sinking waist-deep in the black muck.

All we found at first were the boa’s broad, flattened tracks winding through the reeds, and its trampled lair where the rushes and bog grass lay crushed flat. When we pushed past the thicket onto firm ground near the steep cliff face, we spotted a sparkling clear brook trickling out of a low overhang that turned out to be the mouth of a huge natural grotto. Its vaulted ceiling dripped with glistening stalactites, some fused into thick, stately stone pillars that looked strong enough to hold the whole mountain roof up. The floor was dusted with fine, snow-white earth that felt smooth and soapy under our fingers—fuller’s earth, I realized immediately.

“Now that’s a lucky find!” I grinned. “This works just as well as store-bought soap, saves me the trouble of messing around with lye and boiling fat to make our own.”

The stream wound deeper into the cave through a wide gap in the inner rock, and Fritz squeezed through to trace its source. A minute later he was shouting back that the cavern opened up far further in, and waving me to follow. I left the other boys in the outer grotto, fired a pistol shot into the dark to test the space, and the echoing boom bounced off the walls so many times it rattled in our teeth. I lit the stub of candle I always kept in my pocket, and we pressed onward, the tiny flame barely cutting through the vast dark around us.

“Father! I think this is a second salt cave!” Fritz cried out suddenly. “Look how the walls glint! The light’s bouncing off every surface of the roof!”

“These aren’t salt crystals,” I told him. “The stream running over them tastes perfectly sweet, and leaves no crust on the rock. I’d wager we just stumbled into a cave lined with rock crystal.”

“Treasure, then!” he whooped.

“Only if we can find a use for it,” I laughed. “Stranded here, it’s worth about as much as the lump of gold Robinson Crusoe found on his island—pretty, but useless for keeping us fed or warm.”

Fritz still insisted on breaking off a specimen chunk, though, and we spent almost an hour wandering the glittering cavern until our candle sputtered low. He stuffed a large, perfect hunk of crystal with multiple six-sided pyramid facets into his pack, and we hurried back to the cave mouth, Fritz firing a final farewell shot just to hear the thunder of the echoes bounce around the walls.

When we stepped back out into the sunlight, we found Jack sobbing his eyes out by the rock wall. He ran straight into my arms the second he saw us, snot and tears streaming down his cheeks. “I thought you were dead! I heard two loud booms, I thought the whole cave collapsed on top of you and Fritz! I was sure you were crushed!” I calmed him down and explained the pistol shots, and he told us Ernest had wandered off into the reeds a little ways back. We found him there, hunched over a clever funnel fish trap he’d woven out of willow, designed so fish could swim in but never find their way back out the narrow opening.

“I shot a serpent while you were gone, father!” he announced proudly, pointing at a bulge half-covered in rushes. “Nearly four feet long, thick as my arm!” I rushed over, heart in my throat, expecting a whole nest of boas, and burst out laughing. That “serpent” was just a huge, fat eel. “You’ve got us a wonderful supper tonight, son,” I told him. The eel was so tough and full of life it was still wriggling even after Ernest had shattered its skull with a rock.

We tramped back to the homestead along the firm cliff path, and found my wife washing clothes at the fountain. She was overjoyed to see us safe, and absolutely delighted with our haul of fuller’s earth, since our store of soap was almost gone. We feasted on fried eel that night, and told the whole story of the crystal grotto late into the candlelight.

It was clear we needed to make a full, weeks-long expedition to sweep all the land between our settlement and the Gap, to make sure no more big serpents were hiding out in the woods. The whole family would come, I decided, and no one complained. We packed our small tent, sacks of rice and dried meat, every firearm we owned, axes, torches, cooking pots, and loaded everything onto our big cart pulled by our two draft animals, Storm and Grumble. Jack and Franz rode the animals double, yelling and whipping them playfully like proper cowboys, my wife sat comfortably on a pile of blankets in the cart bed, Fritz rode scouting ahead, Ernest and I walked beside the wheels, and our two hunting dogs plus Fangs our tame jackal patrolled the flanks.

We passed through Woodlands first, and found the deep, broad furrow the boa had dragged through the loose soil on its way to our homestead, so wide you could have driven a small cart down it. We halted at Falconhurst for a quick rest, and our goats, sheep, and free-range poultry all trotted over to greet us, bleating and clucking for treats. By nightfall we reached the little farm at Woodlands, which was totally undisturbed—no boa tracks, no mischievous apes tearing up the crops, everything lush and peaceful.

The next day we spent surveying the surrounding woods, harvesting armfuls of wild cotton to stuff new pillows and cushions. In the afternoon I took Franz out with me, carrying the tiny light hunting gun we’d just let him use for the very first time, with Bruno the dog and Fangs trailing behind. We followed the left bank of the lake, winding through thick reed beds that kept us far from the water, and the dogs flushed out flocks of ducks, snipe, and heron that all flew straight across the lake out of Franz’s range. He got frustrated fast, and begged to take a shot at the pair of sleek black swans floating on the glassy water.

That’s when we heard it: a deep, booming, lowing roar rolling out of the reeds. Franz’s eyes went wide. “Father! Is that Swift, our young onager that wandered off?”

“No, that’s not him,” I said, listening closely. “That’s a bittern— a big wading bird related to the heron, famous for that weird, deep roar that sounds more like an ox than something with feathers.” Franz begged for the chance to make his first ever trophy a rare bittern, so we sent the dogs beating through the reeds to flush it out, and stood ready with our guns. A second later there was a huge rustle in the thicket, Franz fired, and he was screaming in delight. “I hit him! I hit him! It’s a wild pig, bigger than the one Fritz shot last month!”

I ran over to find him dancing with joy over a stout, bristly, tailless yellow-grey animal half as big as a dog. Its snout was broad and blunt, it had webbed feet, and I realized at once it was a capybara, the giant water rodent native to South America. Franz was over the moon to have “discovered a whole new creature” for our collection. We cleaned the heavy carcass out to make it lighter, he carried it until he was exhausted, then strapped it to Bruno’s back, and trundled back to Woodlands as the sun went down.

We walked in to find Ernest surrounded by a pile of dead, furry large rats spread out on the grass. “You all went rat hunting instead of harvesting rice?” I asked, bewildered.

“We stumbled on them totally by accident,” Ernest said. “We were in the rice swamp with Knips the monkey, and he bounded over to this big dome-shaped mud mound in the reeds, and grabbed a rat by the tail before it could dart back into its hole. It hissed and squeaked, I pulled it out and killed it with my stick, and then I got curious and broke the whole mound open. Dozens of them poured out! Some swam away, but I killed a handful right there, until the whole colony swarmed me. I started panicking, thought I was Bishop Hatto trapped in the mouse tower on the Rhine, and Knips was leaping around screaming too, until Juno came crashing through the reeds and chased all the rest off.”

I went to look at the mounds, and realized they were the lodges of muskrat, or musquash, the little semi-aquatic creatures that build dome homes like tiny beavers. Their pelts would make perfect warm winter caps, I told the boys, and we set them aside to skin that night. Fritz and Jack trundled back from their own scouting trip soon after, reporting no sign of serpents of any size anywhere near the western woods. Jack had a dozen wild bird eggs tucked safely in his hat, and Fritz had shot a pair of heath fowl.

We sat down to supper, and Franz insisted on trying the capybara he’d hunted, only making a face at the strong, musky, gamey taste. I told them all about the different animals that produced that strong scent, from the musk deer to the civet cat whose sweet-smelling pouch the Dutch harvested by locking the civet in a narrow cage that kept it from turning, so they could scrape the oily perfume out with a horn spoon without getting bitten. Then Jack pulled a handful of pale green, round wild apples out of his game bag. “We didn’t eat them, we were scared they were poisonous manchineel fruit,” he said. “We brought them for Knips to test, he’s the expert on what’s safe to eat.” Knips snatched one half the second I set it down, and crunched it happily, and the boys scrambled for the rest. They were sweet, spiced cinnamon apples native to the Antilles, I realized.

The next morning we packed up camp, and marched toward the stretch of woods right between the sugar-cane brake and the Gap, where we’d built a rough bough arbour months before. It was still standing, so we stretched a sailcloth over the top instead of setting up our tent, and made a cozy little base camp. We were all in the sugar cane patch sucking sweet fresh cane juice later that day, when the dogs started barking like mad, and a herd of peccaries—small, fierce wild grey hogs with sharp little tusks—burst out of the thick cane, trotting single file in a straight line right past us. We brought down almost a dozen of them in minutes, and I had the boys cut out the strong-smelling scent gland on their backs immediately before it could taint the meat, so we could cure a whole winter’s worth of hams and bacon.

Jack ran back to get the cart to haul the meat, and came back with Ernest, who said the peccary herd had run right past our base camp, and he and Juno had killed three more of them. We strung the meat up in a new smokehouse we built out of branches, stoking a low smouldering fire of green wood that filled the whole hut with thick, fragrant smoke to cure the hams. The boys dug a Tahitian-style underground oven for the smallest whole peccary, lining the pit with hot stones, stuffing the pig full of potatoes, onions, and herbs, wrapping it all in big scented wild leaves, and burying it under hot ashes and dirt. A few hours later when they dug it up, the whole campsite smelled of roast pork spiced with nutmeg, clove, and cinnamon from the rare ravensara leaves we’d wrapped it in. It was the best meal we’d eaten in weeks.

Over the next few days while the meat smoked, we roamed all the surrounding woods, found no trace of any serpents, and harvested huge, fifty-foot tall giant bamboos that made perfect waterproof buckets, barrels, and storage pots, their hard sharp thorns working as well as iron nails. We made a quick detour to check on the Prospect Hill farm, and were furious to find our ape pests had torn the whole place apart, scattered the poultry and livestock, ripped the cottage bedding to shreds, and covered everything in mud and filth. There was no fixing it that day, so we locked the gates and vowed to come back with extra hands to clean it later.

When all the hams were cured and stored safely in our locked camp hut, we packed the cart back up and set out for the Gap, the narrow mountain pass that was the only way into the wild unknown lands beyond our territory. A few hours’ march brought us to the very edge of our coastal territory, right on the lip of the pass: to our right, sheer, frowning cliff faces rose hundreds of feet straight up, to our left, a roaring white torrent raced down the mountain to spill into the sea, and the narrow strip of rough ground between them was the Gap, the only entry point for miles. We set up camp in the shelter of a small wood within gunshot of the pass mouth, scouted the surrounding trees and found nothing more dangerous than a few skittish wild cats, and settled in for the night.

By sunrise the next morning, we were all packed and ready to push through the Gap. My brave wife agreed to stay back at the camp with little Franz for company, while the three older boys, all the hunting dogs except Juno, and I headed out to explore the vast savannah on the other side of the mountains, further than any of us had ever gone before.

As dusk bled pink over the island’s ridge, the rich, steamy scent of bear-paw stew coiled out of the fire pot, thick and savory enough to make even our usually patient stomachs growl. We huddled around the crackling flames, my wife and I, craning our ears for the first sound of our four young explorers returning from their dawn ride. We’d fussed over them all morning, and every distant rustle made us lean forward, half expecting to spot them trotting across the clearing.

At last, the sharp thud of hooves on packed earth hit our ears. A tiny troop of riders burst over the far rise at a gallop, and a ringing, whooping cheer split the air. We sprang to our feet to meet them, and they tumbled laughing off their steeds, turning the horses loose to graze on the sweet grass before crowding around us, practically bursting to show off their plunder and tell their tales.

They looked like absolute rapscallions: Franz and Jack each had a fuzzy young fawn slung over their back, the four tied-together little legs sticking straight out under their chins. Fritz’s game bag bulged oddly, round lumps and sharp bumps shifting under the canvas, and every few seconds a tiny twitch proved there was living cargo inside.

“Hurrah for the hunt, father!” Jack yelled, bouncing so hard his fawn shifted. “There’s nothing like real wild chase! Storm and Grumble flew across the grass plain like they had wings! We ran those little antelopes right off their feet before we could catch them!”

“Wait till you hear the rest!” Franz put in, grinning. “Fritz’s got two fluffy angora rabbits in his bag, and we found wild honey! And a clever cuckoo – a honeyguide, Fritz says – led us straight to the hive!”

“My brothers forgot the biggest win,” Fritz cut in, puffing out his chest proudly. “We herded an entire wild herd of antelopes right through the Gap into our protected valley! They’re all shut in there, ours to hunt or breed whenever we want!”

“Well done, lads!” I cheered, clapping him on the back. “But far more precious than all your game is the fact that you’re all back safe and sound. Now sit, and tell us the whole story from the start.”

Fitz launched into his tale: they’d galloped hard down Glen Verdant to the rocky barrier pass, the morning air cool on their faces, the horses running flat out most of the way. Once through the Gap, they slowed, scanning the ground for game, and crested a grassy hill to spot two herds of sleek, horned antelope grazing beside the stream below. He’d had the bright idea not to waste a shot, but to drive the whole herd back through the Gap into our lands, where they’d be a permanent food source. The boys and the two dogs had formed a semicircle behind the biggest herd, yelling and waving their hats to chase the antelope toward the narrow pass. The herd had tried to turn and bolt a dozen times, but they’d blocked every escape route until the lead antelope darted through the Gap mouth, and the rest had followed right after.

“Brilliant!” I said. “But how are you going to stop them wandering back out the Gap whenever they feel like it?”

“We thought of that!” Fritz laughed. “We strung a rope all the way across the defile, strung it with rags, feathers, and shiny bits of shell that dance and flutter in the wind. I read in that Cape travel book how the Hottentots use that exact trick to pen antelopes – those skittish things will never dare cross that spooky fluttering line.”

“Clever of you to remember what you read,” I said, then shook my head. “Though I will warn you about those rabbits in your bag – they breed so fast you’ll have an island overrun with the little pests in six months if you’re not careful.”

“That’s why I’m putting them on Whale Island!” Fritz explained. “There’s tons of clover and grass there, and they’ll never swim all the way back here. It’ll be a perfect little warren, no trouble at all. And these two? My eagle caught them for me, unhurt. I rewarded him with the third one he brought down, dead, for a job well done.”

“Father, listen to my part now!” Jack interrupted, shoving Fritz playfully aside. “He’ll go on for hours if you let him. We were chasing the fawns, right? Fritz had run off after the rabbits, the dogs flushed these two little fawns out of the grass, and off they went, bounding like they were flying. We outran the dogs in ten minutes, chased them till they dropped, completely worn out and too scared to move. That’s when the stupid cuckoo started teasing us, hopping just out of reach and calling at us like it was making faces. Franz thought it was an enchanted princess, I was gonna shoot it to break the spell, but Fritz stopped me, said it was a honeyguide leading us to honey. Sure enough, it stopped right at a hollow tree full of bees! I was in such a hurry to get the honey I tossed a handful of lit matches right into the hole, and –”

He paused, rubbing his puffy, red, bee-stung face. “It didn’t kill them. It made them furious. They swarmed out all over me, stung me every place they could reach. I galloped off on Storm as fast as I could, and it felt like hours before I shook them off. My face is burning – I’m going to make mother slather it with that aloe salve right now!” He darted off before I could tease him about his very bad honey-hunting plan.

I decided then to go along with Fritz’s rabbit plan: Whale Island was remote, it would make a perfect safe little farm for rabbits, and if we ever got trapped there in an emergency, we’d have a steady source of fresh meat waiting for us. The rest of the night passed easy: we feasted on the rich, tender bear paws, their odd vague resemblance to human hands didn’t stop a single one of us from devouring the meal, even when Jack cackled out his “Fee-fi-fo-fum” giant joke right in the middle of dinner. We smoked the rest of the bear meat, rendered down all the fat to store for cooking and lamp oil, and collected a huge pile of sturdy bamboo poles.

At dawn the next day, I roused the boys early. Ernest chose to stay back to help my wife and pore over his natural history books, so the rest of us – Fritz on the mule, me on Lightfoot, Jack and Franz on their usual steeds, the two hunting dogs trotting at our heels – galloped off to harvest the toxic red gum from the euphorbia trees, and check on the ostrich nest we’d spotted half-buried in the sand dunes days earlier.

We made fast time across the Green Valley, passed Turtle Marsh, and filled our water flasks before reaching the sandy rise. I let Jack and Franz gallop ahead to stretch their horses’ legs, while Fritz and I stopped to collect the coagulated red euphorbia gum from the incisions I’d cut in the tree trunks weeks earlier. I rolled the sticky stuff into balls and stuffed it into a sealed bamboo jar.

“Father, didn’t you say this sap is deadly poison?” Fritz asked. “Why collect so much of it?”

“It is – the Cape natives use it to poison water holes to hunt big game,” I told him. “I’m going to use it to drive off any roving apes that try to raid our stores, and to tan our animal skins so insects won’t eat through them.”

Just then, a great flapping of wings burst out from the sand ahead. Four giant ostriches, a glossy black-plumed male leading three ashen grey females, exploded out of their hidden nests and ran straight toward us, so fast their long legs blurred.

Jack and Franz whooped and charged to herd them right into our line. Fritz had already wrapped soft cloth around his eagle’s beak the night before, so the bird couldn’t injure a captured ostrich, and he unhooded it now. The eagle soared high, then dove straight for the lead male ostrich’s head, talons brushing his eyes, panicking the big bird so bad he lost all his speed, stumbling in the sand. Jack hurled his lasso, and the coiled rope wrapped tight around the ostrich’s wings and legs, dragging him crashing to the ground. The rest of the flock fled out of sight over the dunes, and we leaped off our horses to secure our prize.

The ostrich thrashed so hard his powerful legs kicked craters in the sand, one blow nearly grazing my thigh hard enough to shatter bone. I grabbed my heavy wool coat and my hunting bag, and tossed them both over his head, blocking out all the light. As soon as the dark fell over his eyes, he went completely still, all the fight draining out of him. We strapped a wide sealskin harness around his body, tied long stout cords to either side, then looped a loose cord around his legs to stop him from bolting at full gallop.

“Remember how they tame wild elephants in India?” Fritz said, grinning. “They tie the new elephant between two already tame ones. We don’t have two tame ostriches, but Storm and Grumble will do the job just fine!”

We led the two horses up on either side of the now-sitting ostrich, tied the ostrich’s harness cords tight to their saddles. Jack and Franz mounted, whips in hand, and we pulled the cloths off the ostrich’s eyes. He blinked, got to his feet with a bound, and tried to bolt forward – but the stout cords pulled him up sharp, bringing him to his knees. He struggled a few more times, then seemed to accept his fate, falling into a steady fast trot between the two horses, the boys yelling and laughing beside him.

Fritz and I left the boys to lead the ostrich home slow, and we headed back to the ostrich nest. The eggs were still warm, the mother only just having left them. We left half the eggs buried to hatch wild, packed the rest carefully in a lined sack, and slung it across my saddle. We caught up to the rest of the group soon after, and made it back to camp without further incident.

My wife stared at the giant ostrich when we came into view, hands on her hips, half amused and half exasperated. “My dearest husband,” she said, “do you think our stores are so overflowing that we need to bring home a seven-foot bird that eats pebbles and grass and devours whole handfuls of nails? We already have a whole farm full of goats, chickens, antelope, dogs, monkeys – you’ve added an ostrich. Next I suppose you’ll dig up an iron mine to feed it!”

“Useless? No way, mother!” Jack yelled immediately, throwing his arm around the ostrich’s long neck. “He’s going to be my new steed! Faster than any horse on the whole island! I’ll make a saddle for him, and I’ll ride him everywhere.”

The rest of the boys started bickering over who got to claim what part of the ostrich, until I laughed and made a ruling: Jack had been the one to lasso him and bring him down, so if he took full charge of training and taming the bird, the ostrich was his, no arguments.

By dawn the next day, our whole little caravan was packed and heading back to Rockburg. We couldn’t risk the ostrich bolting, so we kept him tied between Storm and Grumble, harnessed the cow to the supply cart so the two horses could stay at their posts, and loaded the cart with all our plunder, plus space for my wife to ride when she grew tired. At the mouth of the Gap, we tore down the fluttering feather line the boys had strung, and built a tall, thick palisade of bamboo poles all the way across the pass, so no antelope could ever escape our valley. We stopped at the sugarcane grove to pick up the peccary hams we’d left smoking, gathered a sack full of sweet-smelling vanilla seeds my wife had spotted growing wild, and paused for the night at our Woodlands farm rest stop.

We woke the next morning to find our little farmyard in perfect condition: the chickens had multiplied, the goats were fat, and we spotted the herd of antelope the boys had herded through the Gap grazing in the trees nearby, completely settled in their new home. We repaired the stalls and the little farm cottage, then pushed on at dawn the next day, reaching Rockburg by midday. We threw open the windows, got all the animals settled in their stalls, and unloaded all our treasures from the cart. For the time being, we tied the ostrich between two stout bamboo posts right outside the front door.

Fritz and I took the boat out the next morning: first to Whale Island, where we turned the two angora rabbits loose to make their new warren, then to Shark Island, where we released the two fuzzy young fawns to graze safe from predators. We got back just in time to find Jack trotting up the path, grinning ear to ear, carrying a wicker basket holding an eel almost as long as he was. He and Ernest had set fishing lines the night before, caught the huge eel, and it turned out so tender and delicious we ate half for dinner, and salted the rest away to store in our root cellar.

We spent the next few days working around the homestead: we planted wheat, barley and maize in a new cleared field, the horses pulling the plow while we dug and hoed the soil, our arms sore and aching by the end of each day, fully living the old scripture of earning your bread with the sweat of your brow. We sowed vanilla, pepper and flowering creeper seeds all around the pillars of our veranda to give us shade and scent in the summer months, built enclosed hen coops to keep the new chicks safe from our curious pet monkey Knips, and hung Ernest’s stinky rat-skins outside the house where the smell wouldn’t make anyone gag – though Jack still pretended to faint every time he walked past them to tease his brother.

The ostrich still refused to tame, though. No amount of gentle petting would calm him down, so I used the same trick that had tamed our wild eagle: I held his head over a pot of smoking tobacco fumes. He fell over completely motionless, and when he woke he was quiet – but he refused to eat for three whole days, growing weaker and thinner by the hour. We all panicked, until my wife stepped in, rolling soft balls of maize meal mixed with melted butter, and slipping them one by one into the ostrich’s beak. He swallowed the first, then the second, then the third, and soon his appetite was back, and all his wild, skittish fight was gone.

After a month of gentle training, the ostrich was completely docile: he’d trot, gallop, stop on command, eat grain right out of our hands. I got to work building a custom rig for him: I made a soft leather hood that fit over his head, with eye holes fitted with spring-loaded blinkers tied to the reins. Pull the right rein, the right blinker shut and the ostrich turned left. Pull the left rein, the left blinker shut and he turned right. Pull both reins, both blinkers slammed shut and he stood stock still instantly. I built him a sturdy, high-backed saddle modeled after an old cavalry trooper’s saddle, fastened tight across his wings and breast so it could never slip off his sloped back, perfect for Jack to ride without ever falling off. By the time we were done, Jack had a steed faster than any horse on the entire island, ready for a hundred new adventures.

I had barely set the last piece of my hand-thrown earthenware to cool when roiling black storm clouds rolled over the treetops, and gale-force winds screamed across the island, heralding the start of the brutal rainy winter season. The daily expeditions we had grown to love were immediately put on hold, and even inside our spacious, snug rock house, with all our crafts and projects to keep us occupied, the days stretched long and heavy. All of our moods sank under the constant drumming of rain on the rocks, and even the occasional wild gallop on horseback across half-dry stretches of savanna when the storm lulled could not lift our spirits for long.

Fritz noticed the slump as clearly as I did. “Father,” he said one evening, leaning in by the fire, “why can’t we build a canoe faster and lighter than the heavy, clunky craft we’ve got now? I keep daydreaming of a little skiff I can skim across the bay in like a seabird.”

The whole family lit up at the idea—all save my wife, who had never liked any trip out on the unpredictable water, and protested at once. “We already have a pinnace and two canoes, and more than enough risk of drowning when we take them out!” she said. “Why build another tiny, flimsy boat that will tempt you all to sail farther out than you should, chasing adventure?”

Her fears were easy to soothe. I told her we would not be building some thin, fragile cockleshell: I planned to copy the traditional Greenland kayak, a craft so sturdily framed and watertight it could survive rough open sea, and it would keep the boys busy and engaged for weeks. The work was underway before the week was out. We built a light, strong skeleton from scrap whalebone, reinforced the sides with split bamboo canes, and ran a full bamboo deck the full length of the craft, leaving only a single square hatch just big enough for one person to sit inside.

The work absorbed every last bit of our restless energy, so that by the time we sanded the last edge smooth, the rains had cleared completely, and bright, warm sunlight poured down over the island again. We hauled the finished frame out the front door (it was just narrow enough to squeeze through) and stretched cured seal skin tight over every inch of the sides and deck, sealing every seam with rubber sap to make it completely waterproof. The finished kayak was a strange, sleek little thing, so light a single person could lift it one-handed. When we launched it at the beach, it bounced on the waves like an inflatable rubber ball.

Fritz was unanimously declared its rightful owner—but Mother refused to let him climb into the tiny craft until she had finished a custom flotation suit for him. “If that little boat gets punctured by a sharp rock or the fin of a big fish and sinks out from under you,” she said, “you need something to keep you afloat.” The odd garment she sewed was a double-layer waistcoat, coated all over in rubber solution, every seam sealed tight, with one small valve to blow air between the two layers and inflate it. I thought the kayak was far sturdier than she feared, but I agreed to help her finish it anyway.

On the first calm afternoon, we all gathered on the beach to test it. Fritz pulled on the puffy suit, inflated it until he looked round as a balloon, and waded into the water to the raucous laughter of his younger brothers. He paddled so fast he crossed the whole bay to Shark Island before the rest of us could even climb into our larger boat to follow. The test was a complete success, and Ernest, Jack and Franz immediately stopped teasing him to beg Mother for their own matching inflatable suits.

While we were on Shark Island, we checked on the small animal outpost we had built there the previous autumn. All our domestic creatures were thriving, antelope tracks crisscrossed the ground around the shelter we had built for them, and after scattering handfuls of maize and salt for the herd, we wandered across to the far side of the island. The shore was strewn with bright, beautiful shells and delicate pieces of coral, which the boys stuffed straight into their museum sacks. Mother had them gather huge piles of colorful seaweed too, refusing to explain what she planned to do with the odd serrated-edged seaweed leaves she picked out, and drying them carefully in the oven when we got home, grinning and dodging my questions when I asked what they were for.

A few days later, when the boys and I stumbled back to the verandah exhausted after a long hike, Mother called us over holding a porcelain dish heaped with quivering, crystal-clear jelly that glittered in the sunlight. “A little treat for you,” she said. Fritz took the first bite and whooped that it was ambrosia. It was sweet, sharp with citrus, perfectly delicious, and Mother finally laughed and admitted it was the secret from her seaweed: she had learned to make it when she lived with Dutch settlers at the Cape, soaking the serrated seaweed leaves in fresh water for a week, then boiling them with orange juice, citron and sugar. The boys were so eager for more we set out for Shark Island the very next day.

All our plantations there were flourishing, the palms and mangroves had shot up at a fantastic rate, and random seeds we had tucked into rock crevices were already sprouting green over the bare stone. We even found a new sparkling fresh-water spring hidden in the rocks, its muddy edges crisscrossed with antelope footprints. Satisfied, we sailed over to Whale Island to check on our plantations there, and groaned when we saw the damage: the rabbit population had exploded, and they had gnawed every tender new tree shoot down to nubs, leaving only the coconut palms untouched. We quickly built little prickly thorn fences around every remaining tree trunk to keep the rabbits away, then paused by the great bleached whale skeleton we had found earlier, picked clean by seabirds and sun-bleached to brilliant white. The big, round whale vertebrae gave me a brilliant idea: they would make perfect heavy rollers for a grain crushing machine, no heavy stone required. I rolled a dozen of the big bones down to the boat to take home.

The next morning I set out alone to find a hardwood tree to cut the wooden support blocks for my new crusher, riding our stubborn buffalo Storm, since the boys had already snuck off on a hunting trip with their guns and traps. Halfway to the forest I found our entire cassava field completely trampled and eaten down by wild pigs. I tracked the herd for miles, until Floss and Bruno, our two hunting dogs, rounded them up and sent the whole pack of feral hogs grunting and snarling at bay. I fired twice, dropping two fat young porkers, and the rest of the herd fled. I slung the two pigs over Storm’s back, found the perfect hardwood tree, marked it, and headed for home.

Ernest, who had stayed behind at the house, helped me skin the young porkers, and by the time Mother was roasting them for supper, the rest of the boys came thundering back. Jack rode in first on their tamed ostrich, galloping at full tilt, with Fritz and Franz right behind him on their horses, their game bags bulging. They unloaded four wild birds, a full kangaroo, twenty musk-rats, a monkey, two hares, half a dozen beaver rats, and a duck-billed platypus. Jack was bouncing off the walls with excitement, ranting about how fast the ostrich ran, how the wind stung his eyes so bad he had to shut them half the time, begging me to make him a set of glass goggles so he could ride faster. I praised them for the great haul, but reminded them never to run off on a multi-hour expedition without letting us know, so we wouldn’t worry. They also brought back cuttings of cinnamon and sweet-apple trees for Mother’s garden, and a bundle of spiky thistles Fritz had found: they were fuller’s teazle, their sharp hooked thorns perfect for raising the nap on woven cloth.

We feasted on roast pork and spiced mead that night, but the next morning, staring at the huge pile of game hides waiting to be skinned, I decided to find a faster way than scraping every hide off by hand. I rummaged out a large surgeon’s syringe from the old ship’s medical chest, fitted two small valves inside the tube, and turned it into a powerful air pump. The boys howled with laughter when I told them I was going to skin the kangaroo with a “squirt”. I hung the kangaroo by its hind legs, made a tiny slit in the skin of one leg, pushed the nozzle of the pump inside, and forced air in between the skin and the flesh. The hide puffed up and separated completely from the meat in minutes, and I peeled it off clean in one go, with barely a knife cut. The boys were stunned, and we skinned every single one of the animals in a couple of hours flat, a job that would have taken us two full days by hand.

A few days later we felled our huge hardwood tree, sawed it into thick four-foot blocks, and built the whole grain crusher in no time at all using the whale vertebrae as heavy rollers. That evening we noticed all our chickens trotting home full to the brim, completely uninterested in their supper feed—we ran to the wheat field, and realized our grain had ripened completely, a hundredfold more than we had sown. Mother panicked at the sudden mountain of work: we had to reap the grain, thresh it, dig up all the potatoes and manioc, on top of all our usual fishing and preserving. I reassured her we’d use the fast, low-labor Italian farming method, no fancy work required. We flattened a stretch of hard clay into a smooth, rock-solid threshing floor, and went out to reap the wheat without stooping, just grabbing the grain heads in big handfuls and tossing them straight into the basket, wasting no time cutting long straw. Mother was horrified when she saw the tall stubble left in the field, but I told her we’d let the cattle graze on the leftover grain, then mow the regrowing grass and stalks for winter fodder.

We laid the small sheaves of wheat in a big circle on the threshing floor, and the boys mounted our four horses to ride them in circles over the grain, trampling all the kernels loose under their hooves. We didn’t even bother to stop the animals from nibbling at the grain as they worked, remembering the old rule: never muzzle the ox that treads out the corn. When all the grain was loosened, we tossed it high in the air with shovels when the sea breeze blew, and the wind carried all the light chaff away, leaving the heavy clean grain to fall on the floor. By the time we were done, we had harvested more than sixty, even a hundred times the amount of grain we had sown, our storehouse overflowing. We planted rye, barley and oats in the cleared field for our second harvest of the year, right before the next rainy season.

As we wrapped up all our farm work, the annual herring shoals arrived off the coast, and we pickled two full barrels of the fish for winter stores. Right after that, the seal herds turned up on the beaches, and we hunted a large number of them for their thick, durable skins to finish rigging out Fritz’s kayak. We added a small watertight storage compartment on the deck, big enough to hold pistols, ammunition, fresh water and trail rations, and hung two harpoons tied to seal-skin float bladders alongside the little craft, ready for all the adventures we had planned out on the open water.

The long-awaited test day for Fritz’s hand-built Inuit-style cajack finally dawned bright and warm over Safety Bay. We all gathered on the shingle shore to laugh at his ridiculous getup: his mother’s custom-designed full-body inflatable flotation suit, thick as a quilted mattress, puffed him up the second he blew up its air chambers until he looked exactly like the frog from the old fable, squeezed into the kayak’s tiny cockpit like a overstuffed sausage. Even his mother, who’d stitched every seam of the suit, couldn’t hold back giggles at the sight of him.
I hauled our sturdier support rowboat down to the water right after, Ernest and Jack scrambling aboard with me, ready to dart in the second anything went wrong. The cajack glided light as a leaf across the glassy green bay, and Fritz grabbed his double paddle to show off: darting arrow-fast across the water, wheeling sharp left and right, then tipping the craft almost fully on its side to prove it wouldn’t capsize. His mother shrieked at the sight, but the little skin boat never dipped even a drop of water, and he righted himself whooping, before he drifted straight into the fast, unmarked current of Jackal River. Before we could call a warning, he was being swept fast out past the bay’s headlands toward the open ocean.
My heart jumped. We all hauled on the oars at top speed, his mother yelling after us to hurry, calling the tiny kayak “a horrid soap bubble that’s bound to pop any second.” We rounded the old shipwreck rocks and scanned the empty waves for ten long, panicky minutes before we spotted a faint puff of smoke, followed by the crack of Fritz’s hunting pistol. We fired a signal shot in reply, and within seconds his cheery halloo drifted over the water, the cajack darting out from behind a rocky headland.
“Come ashore!” he waved wildly. “I’ve got something to show you!”
We pulled up on the beach to stare in shock at a full 14-foot young walrus, harpooned clean through and dead at the tide line.
“You killed this?” I asked, half-expecting a whole tribe of Inuit hunters to step out of the trees to claim it.
“Of course I did!” Fritz laughed. “Once the current dragged me out I spotted a whole herd of them, and forgot every rule I ever learned chasing after this one. I harpooned him twice before he dragged himself up on these rocks to die, and I shot him in the head before I got close — remembered that big serpent that almost got Jack, didn’t I?”
I scolded him gently for scaring his half-mother out of her wits, explaining that even the gentle walrus could easily smash a full-sized whale boat to splinters with its tusks if wounded, but we were all far more relieved to see him unhurt than upset. We set to work chopping off the walrus’s massive skull to get at its perfect ivory tusks, and stripping long tough strips of its hide to tan later for tool straps and harnesses. It was then that I noticed the air had gone thick and still, heavy with the scent of coming storm. We rushed to pack up, and Fritz begged to head back alone in the cajack to break the good news to his mother, rather than crowding into our slow rowboat. I let him go.
By the time we’d pushed off from the rocky beach, the sky had turned black as ink. Gales tore over the waves, tossing 10-foot breakers over the gunwales of our little boat, and we lashed ourselves tight to the seats with our swimming belts buckled on, riding out the tempest as rain lashed us half-blind and lightning split the dark all around us. I barely worried about our own boat — it was sturdy enough to weather the storm — every last one of my prayers was for Fritz, who’d long vanished out of sight ahead of us. I’d fully given him up for lost by the time we rounded the headland and glided into Safety Bay.
Then we saw them: his wife and Fritz, kneeling side by side on the shore, praying so hard for our safe return they never noticed we’d pulled up until we called their names. The whole family collapsed in tears of relief, stumbling back to our Rockburg home to peel off our soaked clothes, huddle by the fire, and tuck into a hot supper while we traded tales of the storm.
Later, we cleaned, embalmed, and mounted the walrus’s full skull on the prow of Fritz’s cajack, naming the craft *Sea-Horse*, and the tanned hide made some of the toughest, most useful leather we’d ever had. But the storm had done widespread damage: it washed out half the bridge over Jackal River, flooded half our unharvested crops, and cracked all our rainwater tanks, so we spent weeks repairing everything. We found a thick patch of wild clove berries growing under the cascade that same week, planting saplings in our nursery garden so his wife could flavor their meals with spice instead of their usual overused black pepper, and we laid new clay aqueducts to irrigate our corn fields with runoff from the grain mill. The fishing runs that season were bountiful, the larder stuffed full of salmon, sturgeon, and herring — even if we had to laugh Jack’s ridiculous plan of tethering live salmon to the bay floor by their gills, like we did our turtles, straight out the door.
After weeks of monotonous farm work, the boys grew restless. Fritz put forward a plan: they were almost out of bamboo for making new farm hurdles, and we also needed to restock our supply of fine pottery clay up at the Gap. We agreed on a quick trip: Fritz, Jack, and Franz would take the cart pulled by our faithful oxen Storm and Grumble, plus our ostrich Hurry and the two hunting dogs Floss and Bruno, while Ernest stayed behind with us to help build the new sugar mill I’d been planning for months. Fritz made a whole stock of North American trapper’s pemmican — pounded, dried, cooked bear meat, compressed into dense portable bars — for the trip, though his wife teased him that it would probably taste like sawdust. I spotted Jack sneaking a basket of our homing pigeons under the supplies in the cart, and grinned — the little rascal didn’t trust the pemmican either.
The first evening the boys were gone, we sat on Rockburg’s porch resting after a long day on the sugar mill, when one of the homing pigeons fluttered onto our dovecot. Ernest ran to fetch it, pulling a tiny scrap of paper tied to its leg:
> Dearest Parents and Ernest,
> A brute of a hyena killed our ram and two lambs. The dogs seized it. Franz shot it. It is dead and skinned. The pemmican isn’t worth much, but we are all right. Love to all.
> Fritz
> Woodlands, 15th instant
We laughed at the no-nonsense hunter’s note, even as his mother fretted about the danger they were running. The pigeon post kept us updated the whole trip: we heard how the boys arrived at Woodlands to hear uncanny cackling human laughter drifting from the bushes, the ostrich Hurry panicking and bolting straight into the rice swamp. Franz crept through the thicket with the dogs to find the massive hyena dancing and whirling in circles around its fresh kill, letting loose that ghoulish hysterical cackle. Franz fired both barrels of his gun, breaking its foreleg and wounding its chest, and even though the hyena badly bit both Floss and Bruno before it died, the boys patched up the dogs, skinned the hyena, and moved on.
They spent the next few days mapping the shoreline of Wood Lake, driving tall bamboo marker poles in the ground at every safe, firm crossing spot in the marsh. They fought off angry parent swans to catch three fuzzy baby black swans to bring back to live on Safety Bay, Fritz used his trained eagle to capture a stunning long-necked wild heron, and a huge snuffling tapir burst right through the reed beds next to them before anyone could blink, diving into the lake and vanishing before they could even call the dogs. Franz and Jack even invented a clever looped birdlime arrow to catch live birds without harming them, netting five cranes, two of them the rare, delicate Numidian demoiselle cranes. They tried the pemmican exactly once, then fed the whole batch to the dogs, feasting instead on peccary ham, cassava bread, and roasted sweet potatoes.
When they trekked up to Prospect Hill, a horde of screaming wild monkeys swarmed them, pelting them hard in the head with pinecones. They fired a few warning shots to scatter the apes, only to find our little summer cottage there completely torn apart, wrecked and trashed by the monkey horde. Fritz found a bottle of toxic euphorbia tree sap in the old tool chest, mixing it with sweet milk and crushed millet to make bait, hanging the poisoned coconut shell treats high in the treetops out of reach of their farm animals. That night the whole forest came alive: jackals howled in the distance, they heard deep distant roars they swore were lions, and the weird rumbling snorts of hippos from the far swamp — before the monkeys started screaming, the poison kicking in. The racket lasted until dawn, and when the sun came up, the ground under the trees was littered with dead apes. Fritz felt sick with guilt, and they worked for hours to bury every body deep or dump them far out at sea, scrubbing every last trace of the poison away before they set out for the Gap.
The last pigeon arrived at Rockburg late that same night, the note scrawled in a panic:
> The barricade at the Gap is broken down. Everything laid waste as far as the sugar-brake, where the hut is knocked to pieces, and the fields trampled over by huge footmarks. Come to us, father — we are safe, but feel we are no match for this unknown danger.
I saddled Swift immediately, riding hard under the bright full moon all the way to the Gap, surprising the three boys who’d expected me at dawn the next day. We inspected the damage at first light: thick, heavy barricade posts had been snapped like dry twigs, every single young sugarcane plant eaten or trampled flat, massive footprints of every size scattered everywhere. It was a full herd of elephants, the whole lot of them, that had wandered through. We traced the tracks out of the Gap back to the uninhabited wild forest, so the herd had moved on, no lingering threat left on our land. We sat up that whole night around a roaring bonfire, the boys spilling every last detail of their wild adventures, making plans to build a new, massive, reinforced barricade at the Gap — strong enough to keep even the largest elephant from ever breaking through again.

As I flipped through the water-stained pages of my journal one evening, the old verse from King David looped in my head: We spend our years as a tale that is told. Ten full years had slipped past since we were wrecked on the shores we’d named New Switzerland, no calendars, no distant news, just sunrises and harvests and small, brave adventures stacking one on top of the next.
The soft sadness of that quiet retrospect dissolved fast in a rush of gratitude. Not one of my family had come to harm in all that time, and our four boys had grown straight and strong, tempered by hard work, long walks, and the steady affection of our little household. My greatest hope, that any young person reading our story would see how a peaceful, loving, industrious family shapes good, brave men, felt closer than ever to coming true.
I didn’t see any point in boring our readers with ten years’ worth of trivial hunting trips, clunky homemade tool experiments, or encyclopedia-style lists of every plant and animal we’d discovered. But before we moved on to the biggest, most life-altering stretch of our story, it was worth pausing to lay out exactly how far we’d come.
Rockburg, our cliffside winter home, was almost unrecognizable from the sweltering, bare hollow my wife Elizabeth had once hated so much. Fountains splashed in its central courtyard, trellised verandahs draped in flowering creepers wrapped around its balconies, and native and European saplings we’d planted had grown into shady groves heavy with jasmine and orange blossom. Out in Safety Bay, Shark Island stood guard, its slopes thick with graceful palms, a small battery and flagstaff visible atop its highest rock.
The swamp we’d spent months clearing and draining had turned into a wide, glassy lake, with just enough reedy marsh at its far end to make perfect cover for waterfowl. Black swans sailed its surface alongside white geese and iridescent wild ducks; flamingos waded in the shallows alongside soft blue-grey cranes and crested herons, none of them afraid of us at all. Jack’s two giant pet frogs, Grace and Beauty, had grown as big as small rabbits, and would waddle all the way from the marsh at his call to gobble up a juicy grasshopper. Old Hurry, our stately pet ostrich, paced the paths under the shade trees like he was king of the whole island, and every kind of bright, soft-cooing pigeon nested in our cliffs and dovecots.
Rockburg was only accessible by one narrow bridge: the stretch of river above the waterfall that had once been fordable was now completely blocked by a dense, impenetrable thicket of thorn trees, lemon bushes, and prickly pears we’d planted ourselves. Shark Island’s rabbit warren supplied us with all the meat and soft fur we needed, and when we found antelopes didn’t thrive on nearby Whale Isle, we moved them there too, turning Whale Isle over to all our messy, smelly work: candle making, tanning hides, cleaning wool. The Woodlands farm flourished, our flocks and herds gave us all the mutton, beef, and milk we could use, and Elizabeth’s dairy grew so busy she could barely keep up.
The boys still loved naming every animal they owned: they had a creamy white cow named Blanche, a bull with a roar so loud you could hear it half a mile away named Stentor, two fleet young wild donagers called Arrow and Dart, and Jack kept a descendent of his old favorite jackal Fangs, which he named Coco, and babbled about how every good name needed an “o” in it until our ears ached.
We’d all stayed in excellent health those ten years, only suffering from occasional mild bouts of fever for Elizabeth, and tiny scrapes and tumbles for the boys, who were all now fine, strapping young men:
Fritz, 24, was average height, broad-shouldered, absurdly strong, and full of high spirit. Ernest, two years younger, was tall and slight, gentle, quiet, and studious, the old lazy streak he’d had as a boy almost entirely gone, with a sharp, curious intellect and refined taste for all things beautiful. Jack, 20, looked almost as tall as Fritz but was lighter built, famous for being impossibly graceful and quick rather than bulkily strong. Franz, the youngest at 17, had a little bit of every one of his brothers in him – sharp, witty, and kind, if not quite as silly and prankish as Jack. All four were honorable, God-fearing boys, unfailingly dutiful to Elizabeth and me, and fiercely loyal to each other.
Even after ten full years of total seclusion, I still clung to the quiet hope that one day we’d be reunited with other human beings. But time that made our boys men was also creeping up on Elizabeth and me, and I sometimes lay awake at night with a cold, heavy dread of what would happen to them if we died and left them all alone. I never told the family about those dark spells, though. I would just pray it out, and the strength and hope would always come back.
The older boys often took off on all-day expeditions without a heads up, and my unease always melted away the second they came tramping back, grinning and loaded down with treasure. One day, Fritz didn’t come home at all. By sunset, we realized his cajack – his small, fast sailing skiff – was gone from the harbor, and he’d headed out to open sea.
Worried he’d get lost after dark, I took Ernest and Jack out to Shark Island to climb the watchtower, hoist our signal flag, and load the old cannon we kept there to warn him we were watching. We stared out across the glittering, sunlit water for ages before we spotted a tiny black dot on the horizon, which our telescope confirmed was Fritz’s skiff. He was moving far slower than usual, and I could see he was towing a huge bulging sack behind the cajack, on top of all the gear piled on his deck. We fired the cannon, then hurried back to the harbor to meet him.
“Welcome home, Fritz!” I called as he pulled up to the dock. “Looks like you’ve got a whole cargo there!”
“More than cargo – I made discoveries that’ll make all of us want to head straight back out there,” he laughed. “C’mon boys, haul the stuff up, I’ll tell you the whole story while I rest.”
We all clustered around the campfire while he apologized for leaving without permission, and launched into his tale. He’d been plotting a full coastal exploration voyage for ages, ever since he’d killed the walrus years before, mapping stretches of shore we’d never seen. He’d pre-packed the cajack with provisions, a compass, his rifle, harpoon, axe, boat hook, net, and brought his pet eagle Pounce along for the trip. The morning had dawned calm and bright, and he’d slipped out of the harbor before anyone woke, letting the current carry him out past the point where our old wreck had gone down. He could even see the rusted cannons and iron debris scattered across the flat, clear seabed under the water.
He sailed past cliffs crowded with thousands of seabirds, and low rock shelves where walruses and seals lazed in the sun, lumbering over the rocks on their tusks. He hurried past that stretch, nervous of the huge tusked beasts, and an hour and a half later he rounded a tall, steep headland jutting far out to sea – and spotted a massive natural arch carved straight into the rock face, like the grand entrance to a giant stone cathedral.
He sailed right under it, and found the huge cavern inside was full of tiny swallows barely bigger than wrens, their nests plastered all over the walls. The nests weren’t made of twigs – they looked fibrous, gelatinous, almost like sponges or fungi, and Fritz had stuffed a whole net full of them.
I laughed when I saw them. “Those are edible bird nests! The Chinese pay their weight in silver for the fresh white ones, they make a strengthening soup out of them. Java has whole industries built around harvesting these things from caves just like this, people risk their lives climbing for them. Why don’t you try boiling a few, Elizabeth, just for curiosity?”
“Ugh, they look disgusting,” she said, grinning. “But I’ll try, if you all really want to.”
“Please please please!” Jack cackled. “I want to taste bird soup even if it feels like I’m swallowing a mouthful of feathers!”
Fritz kept going with his story. He’d sailed all the way through the long, tunnel-like cavern, and emerged into a completely hidden, almost entirely landlocked bay so calm it looked like a lake. The shores beyond the rocks stretched out into fertile, grassy plains, leading to a river mouth and a dense cedar forest. He’d looked down through the crystal clear water and spotted huge clusters of giant oysters stuck to the rocks by hairy little threads. He hooked a few up with his boat hook, left them on the sand while he explored a little, and when he came back the hot sun had made all the shells gape open. He cut one open with his knife – and found three tiny, round, pearly balls rolling around inside.
The boys whooped when he pulled the little box of gleaming pearls out for them to see. “Treasure!” I exclaimed. “Right now they’re just pretty trinkets, but if we ever meet another civilized ship again, that oyster bed will make us all rich. We have to go explore that Pearl Bay as soon as possible.”
Fritz told us he’d sailed all the way across the new bay, found it fully sheltered from the open sea by a line of reefs with only one narrow channel out. When the tide came in strong, trapping him on the wrong side of the channel, he’d climbed the headland cliffs – and hundreds of seabirds, from gulls to giant albatrosses, had mobbed him, screaming and diving at his head. He’d swung his boat hook at them in frustration, and knocked one huge albatross clean unconscious into the water. He’d hauled it onto his cajack, nursed it back to life, and then sailed home through the channel once the tide calmed.
That night, after the rest of the boys had run off to start pripping open more oysters, Fritz pulled me aside, his face tight with excitement. “I didn’t tell everyone the whole truth about that albatross, father. Tied around its leg was a scrap of old rag, with English writing scrawled on it. It said: Save an unfortunate Englishwoman from the smoking rock. I was so shocked I could barely breathe. I tore a strip off my handkerchief, wrote Do not despair! Help is near! tied it to the bird’s other leg, and let it fly west once it woke up. We have to find her. We have to rescue her.”
I was stunned. I told him it was wisest not to tell the rest of the family yet – the note could have been written years ago, on some distant shore, and the poor woman could be long dead. The “smoking rock” he referenced had to be an active volcano, and there were none on our island. But Fritz was sure the rag wasn’t that old, and that the woman was still alive somewhere nearby. After a long quiet talk, we agreed we’d use the planned trip to Pearl Bay as cover. We’d modify Fritz’s cajack to fit two people, and load it with enough provisions for a long, multi-day search voyage to look for her.
Back at the house, the boys were half-crazy with excitement prying oysters open, yelling every time they found a new pearl. They begged to start a full pearl fishery right away, building a hut on the bay shore to harvest the oysters full time. I announced to the whole family that Fritz was now old enough to take full control of his own voyages, that none of the rest of us would panic if he stayed away for a few days, and that we trusted his judgement completely. His mother hugged him tight, teary, and blessed him.
It took us a little while to build a set of special scraping tools I’d designed to pry the oysters off the bay rocks, which gave Fritz time to refit the cajack with a second extra seat for a passenger. His brothers assumed he’d take one of them as his first mate, and he let them think that.
Finally, the day of our big excursion came. We loaded up our main yacht, took the dogs and Jack’s jackal Coco along, and set out. Jack rode in the new second seat in Fritz’s cajack, proudly leading the way as our pilot. We sailed past the walrus rookery, through a stretch of calm mirror-smooth water where tiny, fairy-like paper nautilus shells bobbed on the waves. The boys chased them down and caught half a dozen for our natural history collection, and we named that spot Nautilus Creek. Further on we rounded a flat little point with a sharp rock at the tip, named Cape Pug-Nose, and soon spotted the huge stone headland Fritz had told us about. We sailed right through that grand archway, which was so tall and intricately shaped with stone pillars and spires it looked exactly like the entrance to a gothic cathedral, so we named the whole place Cape Minster.
A whole cloud of the tiny edible swallows burst out of the arch as we sailed in, their nests strung all along the walls of the long, light-dappled stone tunnel like decorations. The tide carried us smoothly all the way through, past stone pillars that looked like cathedral columns and shadowed side caves that looked like side chapels, until we burst out into the bright sun, right in the middle of Pearl Bay. It was even more beautiful than Fritz had described, dotted with tiny green islets, its shores lined with grassy meadows and murmuring brooks and shady groves.
We found a perfect landing spot next to a sparkling little stream, built a big watch fire as the sun went down, ate a quick supper, left the dogs and Coco on shore to keep watch, and anchored the yacht a short way out from the bank for the night. The boys were chattering nonstop about diving for oysters and hunting for pearls at dawn, and no one but Fritz and I knew that somewhere out west, past the bay, past the cliffs, a stranger was waiting, hopefully, for the help we’d promised.

My wife and young Franz had been half wild with worry when we stumbled back to the homestead, missing Fritz entirely, but the pair sighed in huge relief the second we hauled ourselves through the door safe and sound. They hunched around us, wide-eyed, as we recounted every twist of our journey: my wife nearly screamed out loud when we described the narrow escape from the pair of man-eating lions, and poor Jack still looked so pale from his near miss falling off the cliff that she kept prodding his arms to make sure he was really unhurt.

Franz burst into tears the second he heard how loyal old Juno died fighting the boar to protect them, and begged us to dig up her body so she could be buried right outside the house she’d called home for so many years. We laid her to rest the next morning, and Ernest carved a silly, sweet epitaph on a flat limestone slab set above her grave:
> A servant true lies here
> A faithful friend, a Dog,
> To all most dear;
> Who met her end
> Fighting right bravely in her master's cause.

We handed over the wild boar meat and sacks of truffles to my wife, who clapped her hands giddy at the thought of all the rich, savory stews and roasts she’d cook from them. She tried very hard to convince me to let her bake the boar’s head in the traditional Hottentot style, crispy and stuffed with herbs, but I’d already promised Ernest we’d add it to his growing natural history museum, and I wouldn’t break my word. We hauled the prize boar head, plus the two cured lion skins, over to our tanning shed on Whale Island to work them soft.

Five whole days dragged by, and Fritz never came back. I couldn’t hide my mounting anxiety any longer, and announced I was going out in our little pinnace yacht to track him down. To my surprise, even my wife insisted on coming along, and at dawn the next day, with a sweet offshore breeze at our backs, all five of us plus our pack of dogs climbed aboard and set sail for Cape Minster.

Our tiny bright boat bounded across the sparkling blue water, all of us giddy with the warm sun and crisp sea spray, and I was steering straight for the familiar rock archway entrance to the cape’s hidden coves when I spotted a huge dark shadow floating just under the surface right dead ahead. “Sunken rock!” I thought, and wrenched the helm hard over — but we still scraped the top of it with a soft thud before we glided past, no harm done. When I glanced back, though, the shadow was gone. There was no rock there at all.

Before we could gape long enough to make sense of it, Jack shrieked, “Another one! Starboard side!” Sure enough, another dark mass lurked just below the waves — and then Franz yelled, “It’s moving!” A massive black body surged out of the ocean, and a thick spout of water shot thirty feet into the air with a thunderous roar before raining back down on us. It was a gigantic cachalot sperm whale, and he was furious we’d scraped his back. He backed off a few yards, and we could see him coiling to charge straight for our tiny craft.

Stories of whales ramming and sinking full-sized ships flashed through my head, and I grabbed a loaded rifle off the gunwale. Jack grabbed the other, and we fired almost at the exact same time. Both bullets found their mark. The whale thrashed the water into a froth of white foam for a few seconds, then dove under the surface and vanished. We reloaded quickly, following the bright red trail of his blood out toward the shallow shore, and spotted him rolling and lashing his tail in the shallows. We fired two more shots, and the huge beast gave a final full-body shudder before going completely still: dead.

The boys were just about to let out a victory cheer when they froze. Peeking out from behind a jagged rock, a lone dark-skinned man paddled a small canoe, staring right at us. A cold dread curled in my gut. I was certain this was a scout for a band of Malay pirates, and that Fritz was already their prisoner. We all grabbed our guns and loaded them, I tied a white linen flag to the end of an oar, and yelled out the few lines of broken Malay I knew to signal we wanted peace. The man just kept paddling away, like he was going to run back to warn his raiding party.

Jack lost his patience, grabbed the speaking trumpet, and yelled, “Hey you! Get over here and make friends, or we’ll blow you out of the water!”

But instead of running, the man turned the canoe and started paddling straight for us. Then Franz screamed, “Wait! That’s Fritz’s kayak! I see the walrus head he mounted on the bow!”

Ernest didn’t even hesitate. He grabbed the trumpet and yelled, “Fritz! You old rascal, come on up!”

Sure enough, it was Fritz. He’d rubbed dark ash all over his face and skin to disguise himself as a native scout. He clambered aboard cackling, and explained that when he’d heard our gunshots a little while before, he’d been convinced a gang of pirates had invaded the coast, so he’d dressed up to sneak and spy on us instead of walking right into an ambush. When I yelled at him in Malay earlier, he’d been 100% sure we were raiders.

Once we’d wiped the ash off his face and heard our full story of the whale fight, he winked and said he knew a perfect hidden anchorage nearby, on a little island full of all sorts of wild, unexpected sights. That’s when I pulled my wife aside and finally told her the secret I’d been keeping: Fritz hadn’t just gone off exploring. He’d found proof there was a lone shipwreck survivor stranded somewhere along the coast, and he’d set out to rescue them. She burst into happy tears, almost overwhelmed at the thought of meeting another person who hadn’t grown up with our little ragtag crew.

We sailed to the small island he led us to, dropped anchor, and tramped silently through the thick palm thicket until we stepped out into a clearing. There, a neat leaf-and-bough hut sat with a cheerful fire crackling right outside the door. Fritz darted inside, and a second later came back out holding hands with a slim, sharp-dressed young man in a faded English naval officer’s uniform, introduced as Edward Montrose. He asked us to welcome the stranger as a new brother to our family.

The boys were over the moon to have a new adventure companion. They ran back to the yacht to haul up food, mead, blankets and supplies for a big bonfire feast, and by the time everyone sat down to eat, “Edward” was laughing and chatting as gaily as the rest of us, clearly already at home. We all raised a toast to our new friend, drinking spiced mead to loud cheers.

When Edward had headed back to the yacht for the night, the boys piled around the bonfire, pestering Fritz to tell the full story of his secret voyage. He sighed, and began: “You all remember that time I shot a wounded albatross out on the water? Well, I found a scrap of a stitched lady’s handkerchief tied to its leg. I realized some castaway had sent the bird out to find help, so I modified my kayak to fit two people, brought my falcon Pounce with me, and paddled out to track the signal. I got caught in a brutal storm halfway out, barely made it to a small cove to ride it out. The next day, I paddled for hours, hearing tigers roaring and hyaenas cackling from the jungle, and I felt more alone than I ever have in my life. I passed flocks of toucans with ridiculous giant rainbow-colored beaks, tossing pepper berries in the air and catching them in their beaks for fun.

That night I slept in my kayak, too nervous to camp on the shore. The next morning I was roasting a parrot over my fire for breakfast, and a full-grown tiger stepped right out of the long grass behind me. I couldn’t reach my gun fast enough to stop him from pouncing. But Pounce, my brave falcon, dove straight for the tiger’s eyes, flapping and pecking so hard he stopped the big cat mid-leap. I grabbed my rifle and fired, killing the tiger instantly — but one swipe of his paw had crushed Pounce. I buried him right there, and I was so heartbroken I almost turned for home. But then I rounded a big rocky headland, and I saw a steady curl of smoke rising from a reef out in the bay. It was the castaway’s beacon fire. I paddled as fast as I could, and there on the rocks, waiting for me, was not a man — but a young woman named Jenny Montrose. She’d dressed as a male naval officer for safety, three years after her ship, the *Dorcas*, was wrecked in a storm that blew all the way off course. She was the only survivor, and she’d built that hut all by herself, made fishbone needles, mother-of-pearl hooks, even trained a wild cormorant to hunt fish and small game for her.”

“Miss Montrose!” the boys yelled, all jumping up in shock. Ernest laughed, nodding, and said he’d figured it out the second he laid eyes on her, that none of them had been fooled for a second by the “Edward” disguise. We all cheered, raising our mugs to our new sister Jenny, as the firelight glowed bright over the little hidden island cove.

The short, storm-lashed winter months flew by faster than any of us could have guessed. Curled up in our rock-walled study by the crackling hearth, we passed the long dark evenings trading wild adventure tales, patching nets, weaving palm mats, and mending harness, the rosy glow of the fire gilding every face. By the time the last rain clouds blew off and the warm spring sun broke through to gild the cliffs, we’d almost forgotten what it felt like to walk outside without huddling against a gale.

Every corner of our little island homestead hummed with new energy. The boys threw themselves into tending the fields, the orchards, and the vegetable plots we’d planted the previous fall, while Elizabeth and Jenny kept the poultry yards and larders running smoothly. It was past time to check in on all our far-flung outposts: Falconhurst, Woodlands, Prospect Hill, Shark Island and Whale Island all got a full tour and tune-up, and we assigned Jack and Franz the job of servicing the old coastal battery on Shark Island.

They worked from dawn till dusk: patching the broken flagstaff, running the Union Jack back up the mast, oiling the two rusted old guns until they were ready to fire. By the time the sun started dipping low, the rest of us were strolling the main beach enjoying the cool ocean breeze, watching them wrap up the job. They loaded the guns, trundled them out to the firing ledge, then paddled out in their cajack canoe with an empty wooden tub to set it bobbing a mile out as a practice target. They fired from the shore, and the tub blew apart in a shower of splinters. Whooping in triumph, they cleaned the guns and rolled them back under cover.

They’d barely latched the battery shed door when three deep, thunderous booms rolled across the water from the west.

Every one of us froze, dead silent. No one had fired those shots. The two boys were already halfway across the cove in their canoe, paddling so hard the water foamed at the bow, eyes wide as saucers. They’d heard the guns too.

Our heads spun with a dozen conflicting feelings at once: wild, bubbling hope that it was a European rescue ship, cold spike of fear that it was a crew of Malay pirates here to raid our stores and harm our family, confusion over what strangers could possibly be doing this far off all known shipping lanes. Would these unknowns be friends? Enemies? Or a crew of shipwrecked survivors who needed our help? There was no way to know.

Jack and Franz hit the beach gasping. “Did you hear that? What do we do? Where do we hide?” Franz panted, grabbing Fritz’s hands tight. “It has to be a European ship. We’ll see Germany again, we’ll see home!” It was only then I realized how badly the two of them had ached for news of the old world ever since Jenny, their new long-lost Swiss-English sister, had joined our family.

Every eye turned to me. “Night’s falling fast, we can’t do a thing right now,” I told them. “We’ll make what preparations we can, and wait for daylight.” No one slept that night. The boys and I took turns keeping watch from the verandah, scanning the dark western horizon for lights. But around midnight, a hurricane blasted out of nowhere. The wind howled so loud we couldn’t hear anything ten feet away, and the waves smashed against the reef with such force I knew no boat could survive out on the open sea that night.

The storm raged for two full days and two nights before the wind died and the sun broke through over a calm, glistening ocean. The boys begged me to head straight for Shark Island and fire the guns—maybe the ship had survived, maybe it was waiting for a sign we were here. I went with them immediately. One crack of the old cannon, two. We waited.

Minutes later, a faint answering boom rolled over the water. The strangers were still there. They’d heard us. We ran the flag up the mast to signal to the rest of the family we were unhurt, then paddled straight back to Rockburg, where everyone was nearly bursting with excitement. I calmed them as best I could, warning them not to get their hopes too high—we still had no idea if these visitors were friendly or not.

Fritz and I grabbed our guns, our cutlasses, a spyglass, and set off in the cajack to scout. Elizabeth made us promise to be careful as we paddled out of Safety Bay, rounding the jagged western cliffs. We sailed for almost an hour in the direction the gunshots had come from, seeing nothing but sheer rock faces and waves crashing against their bases, until we rounded Cape Pug-Nose.

There she was, anchored in the sheltered little cove on the other side: a full-rigged British man-o’-war brig, sails furled, the Union Jack flying bright at her masthead. Through the spyglass I spotted crewmen on the spotless deck, half a dozen tents pitched under the trees on the shore nearby, campfire smoke curling up between the branches. For a split second a jolt of misgiving hit me: what if this was a mutinous pirate crew, flying a false flag to lure unwary travelers in? But when I passed the glass to Fritz, he whooped. “Look at the captain! He’s wearing a proper Royal Navy uniform! The decks are polished so bright they shine! No pirates keep a ship this tidy!” He was right. The burnished brass, the orderly patrols along the rail, the perfectly arranged camp on shore all spoke of strict, regular naval discipline, no lawlessness at all.

We decided not to reveal ourselves yet. We paddled quietly back to Rockburg, and laid out our plan to the family: we wouldn’t go visit them looking like ragged castaways begging for help. We’d meet them as the lords and masters of New Switzerland, to ask what business they had visiting our shores.

We spent the rest of the day prepping our little sailing yacht. We scrubbed her decks until they were white, polished her tiny brass cannons until they gleamed, hauled all the extra junk off her onto the shore, and ran our own Union Jack up to her peak. Elizabeth dug out the proper tailored wool uniforms we’d salvaged from the original wreck years before—we’d gotten so used to wearing wide shapeless hats, linen work clothes, and goatskin buskins for trekking through the bush, we would have looked ridiculous showing up in front of a smart navy crew in our rough work rags.

At dawn the next day, we ate a quick, quiet breakfast, our hearts too full of feeling to chatter. Fritz and Jack snuck out to the orchard and came back with armfuls of the juiciest, ripest mangoes, pineapples, and guavas we could find, as welcome gifts for the strangers. We climbed aboard the yacht, raised the sails, and glided merrily out of Safety Bay, skirting the frothing reef, and rounded Cape Pug-Nose straight for the brig.

The entire crew, both on the ship and in the shore camp, froze and stared. They’d assumed this stretch of coast was totally uninhabited, home only to wild beasts and savages. No one expected a trim little pleasure yacht, full of ragged, bearded settlers, to come sailing out of the wilderness.

Fritz and I climbed into the small dinghy and pulled for the brig’s deck. The captain, a jovial, no-nonsense British seaman named Captain Littlestone, welcomed us warmly, then led us to his cabin to ask how on earth we’d ended up on this forgotten shore. I told him the whole story of our original wreck off the coast, our years building up our little colony, and how we’d rescued Jenny Montrose three years prior from her own shipwrecked boat.

The captain’s face lit up. He jumped up and shook Fritz’s hand so hard his arm ached. “That’s exactly why I’m here! Jenny’s father, Colonel Montrose, hasn’t stopped looking for her ever since her ship the *Dorcas* vanished three years ago. I ran into three surviving sailors from the *Dorcas* in Sydney a few weeks back, they told me they’d spotted land somewhere in these latitudes. I applied for permission to cruise out here to look for any trace of the lost crew. I never dreamed I’d find the girl herself, alive and well.”

He immediately sent a boat over to our yacht to fetch the rest of the family. He greeted Elizabeth, Jenny, and the other boys like old friends, and his officers vied to make us feel at home. Over lunch he explained that he’d been sailing from Sydney to the Cape of Good Hope with an invalid passenger named Mr. Wolston, his wife, and their two young daughters. The recent hurricane that had blown the *Unicorn* into our cove for repairs had left Mr. Wolston so weak he’d begged to stay on shore to rest and recover for a while.

We went to visit their camp that afternoon, and the Wolstons were absolutely delighted to meet us. We chatted so late that night there was no point paddling back to Rockburg, so the captain lent us spare tents to camp on the shore. That night, Elizabeth and I had a long quiet talk, debating if we really wanted to take the chance to sail back to Europe. After years of building our home here, planting our crops, raising our animals, making every part of this island ours, neither of us wanted to leave. New Switzerland was our home, now and forever. We only wanted our sons to get to choose their own paths, whatever they might be. We agreed we’d ask Captain Littlestone to help us formally place New Switzerland under British protection, to set the stage for a proper, peaceful colony of good, hardworking settlers to join us one day.

The next morning, we invited Captain Littlestone and his entire crew to sail back with us to Rockburg, hoping the peaceful air of our settlement would help Mr. Wolston recover his strength. When they rounded the headland into Safety Bay, their jaws dropped. A full salute of eleven guns boomed out from the Shark Island battery, the royal standard flapping bright in the breeze, and the wide, fertile fields of our homestead stretched out before them. Mr. Wolston’s face lit up, and he said at once that he, his wife, and his elder delicate daughter wanted to stay here permanently, to be part of our new colony, while his younger daughter sailed on to the Cape to join her brother, who would come join them later once he’d wrapped up his work there.

Then we asked the boys what they wanted to do. Fritz announced he’d sail back to England immediately, to deliver Jenny home safely to her grieving father. Young Franz said he wanted to go with Fritz, to go to university and get an education he could never get on the island, then come back later to bring more settlers and new knowledge to New Switzerland. Ernest decided he’d stay behind with us, to catalogue all the island’s plants and animals and serve as the colony’s official naturalist. Jack laughed and said he wasn’t going anywhere near European schools—he wanted to stay right here, to be the best rider and the best shot on the whole island. The whole company cheered, raising their glasses in a toast to New Switzerland.

Captain Littlestone smiled and said it all felt like divine providence. His mission had been to find survivors of the *Dorcas*, and he’d found a whole new thriving settlement, plus a family of new settlers eager to stay, and three young people eager for passage back to Europe. He’d take all of us on board, no extra charge.

For the next week, the whole settlement was a buzz of activity. We packed trunks of pearls, rare spices, furs, and corals for Fritz, Franz, and Jenny to take with them to England, to help them make their way in the world. We loaded the *Unicorn* down with fresh fruit, vegetables, fish, and salted meat to feed the crew on the long voyage. I gave my sons all the old papers and personal effects that had belonged to the captain of our original wrecked ship, to hand over to his living relatives, and charged them both to stay true to the faith we’d raised them in, no matter what adventures they found across the ocean.

By the time the sails of the *Unicorn* dipped over the western horizon, I knew our little island adventure was entering a whole new chapter. We weren’t isolated castaways anymore. We were citizens of a brand new nation, and the best days of New Switzerland were still ahead.
