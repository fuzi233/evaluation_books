When Baldassarre, his wrists knotted together so that the cords bit the old flesh, the rope looped about his neck and girding his body like a rough harness, shouldered his way behind the hanging curtain, the cool breath of the Duomo struck him—stone-chill, faintly damp, and steeped in that mingled scent of beeswax, tallow, and old incense which clings to a church as smoke clings to rafters. He had expected emptiness: a cavern of marble where the silence was dead and the images—pale saints, rigid limbs, dim panels—stood like lifeless tokens. He had expected, at most, a thin trickle of worshippers somewhere in the choir, their chant a monotonous humming as indifferent as a mill-wheel heard far off.

Instead, the nave was alive.

It was a sea of faces—warm with breath, shadowed with lashes, mouths held still as if the whole multitude had been commanded to keep the very air unmoving. The ranks were mixed as grain and chaff are mixed on a threshing-floor: magistrates with grave brows; women in rich hoods; artisans with hands that bore the stain of metal and dye; country folk whose coarse cloaks carried the honest smell of wet wool and field-earth. All were turned toward the same angle where nave and choir met, toward a pulpit that seemed, in that instant, the hearthstone of the whole city.

In it stood a Dominican, dark-haired, strong-featured, a crucifix caught in his hand as a cook catches up a knife—no ornament, but an instrument. His voice had been shaping words before Baldassarre saw him, but for the first minutes the old man heard only the blood in his own ears, the rush and crash of remembered things. His entrance, though he had tried to make it as noiseless as an animal slipping into a stall, drew a brief pricking of eyes. Near the doorway, a few heads turned; suspicion flared and then faltered. The rope told its own tale—escaped prisoner. Yet the church was sanctuary; and the grey hair, the hollow cheeks, the wild misery stamped on his face, took the edge from fear and left pity behind, like the softened scrape after a blunt blade is removed.

He stood pressed against the stone by the doorway, motionless, his gaze wandering down to the pavement as if the pattern of worn flags might give him counsel. Soon, as when a new sound in a forest is judged harmless and the birds go back to feeding, attention flowed away from him and returned to the stronger current—the sermon.

Among the eyes that had marked him were Romola’s. She had entered late by a side door and stood where the main entrance lay full in her view. Grey hairs drew her as surely as the smell of bread draws the hungry. There was something in her that answered to age’s sorrows—an instinct trained by a life of tending and being tended, by the daily knowledge that the old carry their pains as quietly as they carry their years. The rope round the stranger’s neck, the look of suffering that seemed not of today only but of a long yesteryear, moved her with a swift, domestic compassion, as if she had seen an elder at her own table sit down with an unspoken wound.

She fancied, when his eyes roved outward for a moment, that they met hers. But Baldassarre saw nothing distinctly—not her, not any single face. The cathedral, the multitude, the lifted pulpit were only a flash of scene, like a sudden opening in cloud, and then the fiercer inner storm closed again. Tito’s image—his smile, his very movements—kept thrusting itself upon him with the heat of fever, as though memory were a brand pressed against his mind. The future could not shape itself; he had only the bitter sense of his failing strength and a hardening resolve—no trust, nowhere.

Then the sound found him.

It was as if the great domed space had been a brazen vessel struck with a hammer, and the blow sent a vibration through his marrow. A voice, loud and full, rang out like thunder returned from stone to stone—so sure, so exultant in its own truth, that Baldassarre’s heart leaped to it as a parched mouth leaps at the thought of water.

“The day of vengeance is at hand!”

He quivered and lifted his head. He was too far to see more than the preacher’s general shape—arm outstretched, crucifix lifted; but he strained for the voice again, panting for it as one pants for the next draught.

A pause came. The friar’s arm lowered. He set the crucifix on the pulpit’s edge and crossed his arms upon his breast, looking over the multitude as if he would meet each gaze, as if his eyes would name every soul.

“All ye in Florence are my witnesses,” he said, and his words, though they carried, had a steadiness like a knife laid flat. “For I spoke not in a corner. Ye are my witnesses that four years ago, when there were yet no signs of war and tribulation, I preached the coming of the scourge. I lifted up my voice as a trumpet to the prelates and princes and people of Italy and said, The cup of your iniquity is full.”

The phrases came with the force of things flung down—vivid, weighty. He spoke of pleasant wine turned to molten lead; of priests who said there was no Presence in the sanctuary, and who thought they might sin behind the veil; of sacred garments becoming winding-sheets of flame. He painted music changed to shrieks and hissing, couches to thorns, the breath of wantons to pestilence. Even the air seemed to change with it, as if the cathedral’s hush became a listening skin. Sound here was not mere sound: it was atmosphere, the city’s shared breath held in a single chest.

“Trust not in your gold and silver,” he cried, “trust not in your high fortresses… though the walls were of iron… the Most High shall put terror into your hearts and weakness into your councils… He shall break in pieces mighty men without number, and put others in their stead.”

And then, with the same certainty that a man has when he names his own hands, he spoke of his mission—chosen, unworthy servant; the Scriptures alive in him; visions; angels. Not as one selling an art in the market, but as one possessed by something too urgent for barter.

“For four years I have preached,” he said, “three things… that in these times God will regenerate his Church, and that before the regeneration must come the scourge over all Italy, and that these things will come quickly.”

He turned on those who wanted morality without prophecy—virtue as a thin, tidy dish served without fire. He would not have it. God, he said, was not far off; judgment was not a dead parchment. Storm, fire, pestilence—armies—were ministers, driven by angels’ breath. And Italy, chosen land with sanctuary within her, had polluted it.

“Behold,” his voice rose, and the stones seemed to throw it back doubled, “the ministers of his wrath are upon thee—they are at thy very doors!”

At the height of passion he fell suddenly silent. His hands dropped and then clasped before him. Yet the silence did not loosen the crowd; it bound it. Through the vastness of the Duomo, faces stayed lifted, eyes fixed, bodies still—breathing statues, held by a spell made not of incense but of sound withheld.

Then, in low clear tones, he began again.

“Yet there is a pause… a stillness before the storm… the winds are stayed, that the voice of God’s warning may be heard. Hear it now, O Florence… Repent and forsake evil: do justice: love mercy… that the spirit of truth and holiness may fill your souls and breathe through all your streets and habitations…”

The word “breathe” seemed to pass through the building like a soft draught, and Romola, listening, felt it as one feels the difference between a courtyard where water trickles and birds answer, and a street where iron wheels grind and men shout for gain—one nourishes, one wears away. Here, the voice made a kind of acoustic shelter: no clatter of commerce, no huckster’s cry, no polished flatteries of art sold for applause—only the grave music of warning, and the answering hush.

“For the sword is hanging from the sky,” he cried now, and the hush itself seemed to sharpen. “It is quivering… The sword of God upon the earth, swift and sudden!”

He spoke of the French king at the gates; of horses, cannon wheels, the earth shaking; of a multitude that could shave the land bare. The foreign army, he said, was God’s sickle; the wicked would melt and be mown like stubble. He named tyrants and unbelieving priests who trafficked in souls, who made a throne out of the multitude’s vices, who brought fornication into the sanctuary. The punishment he promised was not a distant metaphor: it was heat, stench, burning—so tangible that one could almost taste bitter smoke at the back of the throat.

But then he turned, and the hardness became command and promise.

“Thou, O Florence, take the offered mercy. See! the Cross is held out to you: come and be healed. The tyrant is driven out… and no blood has been spilled. And now put away every other abomination… wash yourselves from the black pitch of your vices… put away the envy and hatred that have made your city as a nest of wolves.”

His words had the bite of lye on stained cloth, as if he would scrub the city clean down to its weave. He promised that armies might pass like a flight of birds, Pisa might be restored, famine and pestilence kept far from the gates—if Florence would not keep the accursed thing lying in the camp.

Then, in the next breath, his tone altered. Authority melted into entreaty; the harsh edge softened like a simmering broth turning mellow after long fire. His voice took on the yearning cadence of a mother calling children in from danger.

“Listen, O people, over whom my heart yearns… For you I toil, for you I languish… Take me, stretch me on thy cross… let the thorns press upon my brow… I desire to be made like thee in thy great love. But let me see the fruit of my travail—let this people be saved!”

He lifted his arms, his eyes to heaven. The great voice trembled, swelled, broke, rose again. He spoke of concord as of angelic song—voices rising together, not in the jangling contest of the streets but in one harmony, a sound-world where the spirit could live. He spoke of a witness remembered among men, of iniquity not prospering forever.

At last the passion overcame speech. He ended in a sob and fell on his knees, burying his face in his mantle.

The change rippled outward like a struck chord in a vast instrument. The audience, carried not only by belief but by deeper sympathies than theory, answered at once: a loud sob rose from the multitude, rolling under the dome as if the whole city had exhaled grief and longing in one breath.

In that great sob Baldassarre’s voice mingled.

No one there vibrated more strongly to the tones than he did; but it was as if all the strings of his inner harp had been wrenched away except one. Threat—vengeance—pursuit beyond death, an avenger holding the hated sinner in an eternal grapple: that alone rang clear to him. The wisdom he had once prized, the old contempt for priestly things, slipped from his soul like a tongue he had forgotten how to speak—lost words, dead sounds. Even had he remembered them, what could they answer to the thirst in him? The denunciation fell on his nerves with the force of self-evidence. He did not ask; he was seized, as a war-horse is seized by the clangor that promises battle.

No word that was not a threat reached him. The mercy, the cleansing, the call to love—these passed like wind over stone. But the fierce delight that vengeance might be endless rose higher when the preacher offered himself as victim. To Baldassarre, that offering meant only a dark triumph: that he too was giving himself—signing with his own blood—into an unending fire that would be coolness compared with his hatred’s burning.

“I rescued him—I cherished him,” the old thought surged in him with the bitter taste of betrayal, as if he were chewing ash. “If I might clutch his heart-strings for ever! Come, O blessed promise! Let my blood flow; let the fire consume me!”

He clenched his bound hands, driving his long nails into his palms until pain made its own sharp, clean note within the roar; and with the rest—under the same vast dome, in the same shared hush broken by one great human sound—he burst into a sob.
