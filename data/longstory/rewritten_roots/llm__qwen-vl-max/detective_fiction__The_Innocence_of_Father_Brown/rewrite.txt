The Blue Cross

Between the pale silver ribbon of dawn and the shimmering green edge of the sea, a ferry slipped into Harwich, disgorging its passengers like a swarm of bees. Among them was a man who stood out not at all—unless you looked closely. He wore a light grey jacket, a crisp white waistcoat, and a straw hat trimmed with a blue-grey ribbon. His clothes were holiday bright, but his face was stern, almost severe. Lean and dark, with a sharp black beard that gave him a faintly Spanish air, he smoked a cigarette as if it were a serious business. There was nothing remarkable about him—except what lay beneath. Under the jacket, a revolver. Under the waistcoat, a police badge. And beneath the hat, one of the most brilliant minds in Europe.

This was Valentin—the head of the Paris police, the world’s greatest detective—and he had just crossed from Brussels to London on a mission: to capture Flambeau.

Flambeau was no ordinary criminal. He was a legend—a titan of crime whose name sent shivers through law enforcement across three continents. For years, he’d roamed freely, committing crimes so bold and clever they defied logic. The world trembled when he moved. Then, without warning, he vanished. Silence fell. But now, after years of quiet, the trail had led to London—possibly hiding among the crowds attending the Eucharistic Congress.

Valentin knew Flambeau would disguise himself. Probably as a minor clerk or secretary, blending into the chaos of the event. But there was one thing Flambeau could never hide: his height. Over six feet four inches tall, he was impossible to miss—if you saw him.

But Valentin hadn’t seen him yet.

He had boarded the boat at the Hook of Holland, scanned every passenger, and found nothing. At Harwich, only a few more joined the train: a railway official, three market gardeners, a short widow, and a tiny priest from Essex. The priest, especially, amused Valentin. He was barely five feet tall, round-faced, dull-eyed, and carried several brown-paper parcels. He couldn’t manage his umbrella, didn’t know which end of his ticket was up, and told everyone he had something precious—real silver with blue stones—in one of his packages. He was harmless, almost absurd. A man like this could never be Flambeau.

Still, Valentin watched carefully. He kept his eyes open for anyone over six feet—anyone who might be hiding behind a false identity. But none matched the description.

At Liverpool Street, Valentin felt confident. He had not missed the criminal. He went to Scotland Yard to register his presence and secure backup. Then, lighting a cigarette, he set off on foot through London, walking past parks and squares, thinking of Flambeau.

And then—something stopped him.

It was a quiet square, typical of London: peaceful, slightly neglected. Tall houses surrounded a central patch of shrubbery, empty and still. One side rose higher than the rest, like a dais, and there stood an odd restaurant—out of place, charming, with striped yellow-and-white blinds and potted plants. It was perched high above the street, accessible by a narrow flight of steps that seemed to lead nowhere.

Valentin paused. Something about the place tugged at him—not logic, but instinct. He walked up the steps and sat at a table by the window. He ordered coffee and added a poached egg. As he stirred sugar into his cup, he paused.

It tasted salty.

He looked at the sugar bowl. It was clearly labeled, meant for sugar. Yet it contained salt. He checked the salt cellar—it held sugar. A strange switch. He rang for the waiter.

“Is this your usual joke?” Valentin asked, raising an eyebrow. “Switching salt and sugar?”

The waiter blinked, confused. “No, sir,” he stammered. “That’s odd.” He examined both containers, then hurried away. Moments later, he returned with the owner.

They both stared, baffled. Then the waiter remembered.

“I zink… I zink it was those two clergymen.”

“Clergymen?”

“Yes,” he said, pointing to a dark stain on the wall. “They threw soup at it.”

Valentin frowned. “Threw soup at the wall?”

“Yes, sir. They came in early, had soup. One paid and left. The other stayed longer. Just before he left, he picked up his half-empty cup and splashed it right onto the wall. I rushed out—but they were gone. Into Carstairs Street.”

Valentin stood up instantly. This was a clue—strange, yes, but a clue. He paid his bill and stepped out into the street.

As he passed a greengrocer’s, something caught his eye. On a pile of oranges sat a sign reading: “Best tangerine oranges, two a penny.” On the nuts: “Finest Brazil nuts, 4d. a lb.” The signs were swapped.

He approached the shopkeeper. “Why are these labels reversed?”

The man glared. “If you’re one of their friends, tell ‘em I’ll break their heads if they mess up my apples again.”

“They did?”

“One rolled ‘em all over the street. I’d’ve caught him if I hadn’t had to pick ‘em up.”

“Which way did they go?”

“Up the second road on the left, then across the square.”

Valentin vanished into the crowd.

On the other side of the square, he found a policeman. “Have you seen two clergymen in shovel hats?”

The officer chuckled. “I have, sir. One was drunk—standing in the middle of the road, bewildered…”

“Which way did they go?”

“They took a yellow bus to Hampstead.”

Valentin flashed his badge. “Call two men. We’re chasing them.”

Within minutes, two officers joined him. They climbed onto the top deck of the crowded omnibus.

“What’s going on?” the inspector asked.

“We follow,” Valentin said simply. “We don’t know where they’re going. So we stay behind. Watch everything. Look for anything odd.”

The inspector sighed. “What kind of odd thing?”

“Any kind.”

They rode in silence, through endless suburbs, past drab taverns and flashy hotels, until twilight began to fall. Then, near Camden Town, Valentin leapt to his feet.

“Stop!” he shouted.

They tumbled down. He pointed to a window—large, frosted glass, part of a grand pub’s dining room. In the center: a shattered pane, like a starburst.

“Our cue,” he declared.

“What cue? What proof?” the inspector asked.

“Proof?” Valentin snapped. “There is no proof. But we must act. Either we follow a wild guess—or go home.”

Inside, they ordered food. Valentin asked the waiter about the broken glass.

“Oh, that,” the man said. “Two foreign parsons came in. Had lunch. One paid and left. The other—well, I realized he’d overpaid. Three times too much. I called him back. He smiled. Said, ‘Sorry to confuse your accounts, but it’ll pay for the window.’ Then—he smashed it with his umbrella.”

All three stared.

“And then?”

“They ran down Bullock Street. I chased them, but they were too fast.”

Valentin didn’t hesitate. He sprinted toward Bullock Street, his mind racing.

He wasn’t chasing a criminal. He was chasing a pattern.

A pattern of absurdity. Of reversals. Of defiance. Of a man who turned reality upside down.

And somewhere ahead, in the dimming light, the tallest man in London was walking—unseen, unguessable, and utterly unforgettable.

The Secret Garden

Aristide Valentin, Chief of the Paris Police, was running late for dinner. His guests had begun to arrive before him, but their anxiety melted away when Ivan, his trusted servant, reassured them. Ivan was an old man with a scarred face and a grey moustache, who sat silently at a table in the entrance hall—a space lined with ancient weapons. Valentin’s home was as unusual as its owner: a grand, centuries-old house perched beside the Seine, shaded by towering poplars. But its most striking feature was that it had no exit other than through the front door, guarded by Ivan and the armory. The garden was expansive and ornate, with multiple doors leading from the house into it. Yet there was no way out from the garden—its perimeter was sealed by a high, smooth wall topped with sharp spikes. It was, perhaps, a fitting refuge for a man whose life had earned him the sworn hatred of a hundred criminals.

Ivan informed the guests that Valentin had called to say he’d be delayed ten minutes. In truth, he was finalizing grim matters—executions and other affairs that repulsed him deeply, though he handled them with cold precision. Though ruthless in tracking down lawbreakers, he was merciful in sentencing. As head of French—and much of European—police reform, he had championed lighter punishments and prison reforms. He was one of France’s great humanist freethinkers, though such men often mistake kindness for indifference, making mercy colder than justice itself.

When Valentin finally arrived, he was already dressed in formal black and wearing the red rosette—an elegant figure, his dark beard streaked with silver. He moved directly to his study, which opened onto the garden behind the house. The garden door stood open, and after locking his official box in its usual place, he paused at the threshold, gazing out. A sharp moon pierced through the tattered clouds of a storm, and Valentin watched it with a rare wistfulness—unusual for a man of such scientific rigor. Perhaps such minds carry within them a silent intuition of life’s greatest mysteries. But soon enough, he shook off the mood. He was late, and his guests were arriving.

A quick glance into the drawing room confirmed what he already suspected: his most important guest had not yet arrived. All the others were present—Lord Galloway, the English Ambassador, a choleric old man with a russet face like a ripe apple, adorned with the blue ribbon of the Garter; Lady Galloway, slim and delicate, with silver hair and a refined, sensitive expression; her daughter, Lady Margaret Graham, pale and elfin with copper-colored hair; the Duchess of Mont St. Michel, dark-eyed and regal, accompanied by her two daughters, equally striking; Dr. Simon, a typical French intellectual, with glasses, a pointed beard, and a forehead furrowed by constant skepticism; and Father Brown, a priest from Cobhole, Essex, whom Valentin had met recently in England.

But it was Commandant O’Brien, a tall man in Foreign Legion uniform, who drew Valentin’s attention more than any. O’Brien had bowed stiffly to the Galloways, receiving only cold acknowledgment. Now he approached Valentin alone, his demeanor both bold and melancholic. Clean-shaven, dark-haired, and blue-eyed, he carried himself with the swagger of a soldier who had seen too much—victories and defeats alike. Born an Irish gentleman, he had fled his homeland after financial ruin and now lived freely under foreign banners, spurs jingling and sabre at his side. When he bowed to the Ambassador’s family, Lord and Lady Galloway remained rigid, while Lady Margaret turned her gaze away.

Yet none of these figures held true significance for Valentin. None were the guest he awaited. He was expecting Julius K. Brayne, a man of global fame, whose wealth and eccentric philanthropy had made headlines across America and Europe. Brayne had funded obscure religions, funded unknown poets, and once claimed Luke P. Tanner of Paris, Pennsylvania, was “more progressive” than Walt Whitman. He believed in progress, whatever form it took—even if it meant supporting causes few could understand. He admired Valentin, calling him “progressive,” though this flattery was misplaced.

Then, as if summoned by fate, Brayne entered the room. His presence was immense—like a physical force. He was enormous, fat as he was tall, clad entirely in black evening wear, without so much as a watch chain or ring. His white hair was swept back like a German general’s, his face red and round, with a single dark tuft beneath his lip that lent his cherubic visage a sinister, almost demonic air. But his arrival wasn’t merely dramatic—it was urgent. He was late, and the dinner had already begun. He was quickly ushered into the dining room with Lady Galloway on his arm.

The Galloways were generally cordial, except for Lord Galloway, who grew increasingly irritable. He was fine during dinner, but as cigars were passed, three younger men—Simon, Brown, and O’Brien—drifted away to mingle with the ladies or smoke in the conservatory. Then the diplomat became restless. Every minute brought fresh dread: was O’Brien signaling to his daughter? How? He couldn’t imagine, but the suspicion gnawed at him. Left alone with Brayne and Valentin over coffee, he found himself trapped between two men who spoke of ideas he didn’t care to hear. Their debate on “science and religion” reached a dull climax, and Lord Galloway rose abruptly, seeking the drawing room.

He wandered through long corridors, lost for several minutes, until he heard voices—the doctor’s high-pitched lecture, then the priest’s quiet tone, followed by laughter. With a curse, he thought they must be arguing again. But when he pushed open the salon door, he saw only absence: Commandant O’Brien was gone. Lady Margaret was gone too.

Frustrated, he stormed back down the hall. His obsession with protecting his daughter from the reckless Irishman had become all-consuming. As he headed toward the back of the house, where Valentin’s study lay, he unexpectedly encountered his daughter—her face pale, her expression scornful. If she had been with O’Brien, where was he? If not, where had she been?

Driven by irrational suspicion, he pushed deeper into the shadowed corners of the mansion, eventually finding a servants’ entrance leading to the garden. The storm had cleared. The moon, sharp as a scimitar, lit every corner of the garden. A tall figure in blue—O’Brien—was walking briskly across the lawn toward the study door. Moonlight glinted off his shoulder epaulettes, confirming his identity.

He vanished through the French windows, leaving Lord Galloway furious and bewildered. The garden seemed like a stage set for romance—moonlit, serene, mocking his authority. The grace of O’Brien’s stride enraged him as if the man were a rival. He felt trapped in a dream of troubadours and fairy tales. To shake off the madness, he stepped forward—then tripped over something in the grass. Irritated, he looked down. Then, curiously, he looked again.

And suddenly, the moon and the poplars witnessed something extraordinary: an elderly English diplomat sprinting across the lawn, shouting at the top of his lungs.

His cries brought Dr. Simon to the study door. The doctor, pale and anxious, listened as Lord Galloway gasped, “A corpse in the grass—a bloodied corpse! O’Brien has gone mad!”

“We must tell Valentin at once,” Simon said, once the nobleman had described what he’d seen. “It’s fortunate he’s here.” And just then, the detective entered, drawn by the commotion. At first, he came as a host might—concerned, courteous. But when told of the body, his expression shifted instantly. This was no longer social duty; this was his work.

“Strange,” he said, as they hurried outside, “that I’ve hunted mysteries across the globe, only to find one in my own backyard. But where is the body?”

They crossed the lawn, now veiled in a light mist rising from the river. Guided by Galloway, they found the corpse half-buried in deep grass. It was a large man, broad-shouldered, lying face down. Only his back was visible—black cloth, bald head, a few strands of wet brown hair clinging like seaweed. From beneath his face, a crimson trail of blood seeped slowly into the grass.

“At least,” Simon said, voice low and solemn, “he isn’t one of us.”

“Examine him, doctor,” Valentin said sharply. “He may not be dead.”

Simon knelt. “Not quite cold,” he murmured, “but dead enough. Help me lift him.”

They raised the body slightly. The moment they did, the horror became undeniable: the head had been completely severed. The neck was cleanly cut, as if by a blade of divine precision. Even Valentin flinched. “He must have been as strong as a gorilla,” he muttered.

Simon lifted the head. The neck was slashed, but the face was intact—pale yellow, sunken yet swollen, with a hawk-like nose and heavy lids. It resembled a wicked Roman emperor, perhaps with a hint of Chinese imperial lineage. No one recognized it. Nothing else marked the man—except the faint white gleam of a shirtfront beneath the body, stained with blood. He had been dressed for a formal occasion.

Valentin dropped to his hands and knees, scanning the ground around the body. Simon helped, though clumsily. Galloway joined in, barely knowing what to do. They found nothing but broken twigs—snapped short, as if chopped. Valentin picked one up, studied it briefly, then tossed it aside.

“Twigs,” he said flatly. “Twigs. And a stranger, headless. That’s all we have.”

Silence fell. Then Galloway snapped, “Who’s that? Over by the garden wall!”

A small figure emerged from the haze—Father Brown, the priest, looking confused. “I say,” he said quietly, “there are no gates to this garden, do you know?”

Valentin frowned—his instinctive irritation flaring at the sight of the cassock. But he was too fair-minded to dismiss the point. “You’re right,” he admitted. “Before we solve how he died, we must ask how he got in.”

He turned to the group. “If possible, let’s keep this discreet. We have ladies, a foreign ambassador. If we must treat this as a crime, we will. But until then, I’ll handle it myself. I’m the head of the police—I can afford privacy. I swear I’ll clear each of you before I call my men. Gentlemen, upon your honor, none of you leave this house until noon tomorrow. There are rooms for all. Simon, go fetch Ivan—the man in the front hall. Tell him to send another servant to guard the door and come to me immediately. Lord Galloway, you’ll inform the ladies. They must stay. Father Brown and I will remain with the body.”

His command was obeyed without hesitation. Simon went to the armoury, roused Ivan, the detective’s private man. Galloway returned to the drawing room, delivering the news with tact—by the time the company gathered, the women were shocked but calm. Meanwhile, Father Brown and Valentin stood beside the corpse, silent under the moonlight—two figures representing opposing views of life and death.

Ivan burst from the house like a cannonball, racing across the lawn. His face was alive with excitement. “Sir!” he exclaimed. “May I examine the remains?”

“Yes,” Valentin said. “But briefly. We must discuss this indoors.”

Ivan lifted the head—then nearly dropped it.

“Why,” he gasped, “it’s… no, it can’t be. Do you recognize this man, sir?”

“No,” Valentin replied coolly. “Let’s go inside.”

Together, they carried the body to a sofa in the study, then moved to the drawing room.

Valentin sat at his desk, writing rapidly. His eyes were sharp, unyielding. “Is everyone here?” he asked.

“Mr. Brayne is missing,” said the Duchess.

“And Mr. O’Brien,” added Galloway, voice hoarse. “I saw him walking in the garden when the body was still warm.”

“Ivan,” Valentin ordered, “go fetch Commandant O’Brien and Mr. Brayne. Brayne is finishing a cigar in the dining room. O’Brien, I believe, is pacing the conservatory. I’m not certain.”

Ivan vanished. Before anyone could speak, Valentin continued, swift and steady.

“A dead man has been found in the garden—beheaded. Dr. Simon, you examined it. Could such a wound be made with a knife?”

“Impossible,” the doctor said. “Not even clumsily. This was clean. A battle-axe, an old headsman’s axe, or a two-handed sword.”

“But there aren’t any swords here!” the Duchess cried.

Valentin kept writing. “Could it be done with a French cavalry sabre?”

A knock came at the door—soft, yet chilling. Everyone froze.

“Possibly,” Simon whispered.

“Thank you,” Valentin said. “Come in, Ivan.”

The servant reappeared, bringing Commandant O’Brien, disheveled and defiant.

“What do you want?” O’Brien demanded.

“Sit down,” Valentin said calmly. “Why aren’t you wearing your sword?”

“I left it on the library table,” O’Brien replied, his accent thickening. “It was in the way.”

“Ivan,” Valentin said, “fetch the Commandant’s sword from the library.”

As the servant left, Valentin asked, “Lord Galloway says he saw you in the garden before discovering the body. What were you doing?”

O’Brien threw himself into a chair. “Admiring the moon. Communing with Nature, me bhoy.”

Silence. Then another knock.

Ivan returned, holding an empty steel scabbard. “This is all I found.”

“Place it on the table,” Valentin said, without looking up.

The silence was suffocating—like the quiet before a verdict. The Duchess’s sobs had ceased. Galloway’s rage had cooled. Then a voice broke the stillness.

“I can tell you,” Lady Margaret said, her voice clear and trembling with courage. “I know what O’Brien was doing in the garden. He asked me to marry him. I refused. I told him, given our circumstances, I could give him nothing but respect. He was angry. But I swear—he never touched that man. I offer him my respect now. I’ll swear it anywhere.”

Galloway stepped toward her, his voice low and threatening. “Hold your tongue, Maggie. Why defend him? Where’s his sword? Where’s—”

He stopped. Her eyes locked onto his—cold, fierce, revealing a truth far deeper than murder.

“You old fool,” she whispered. “What are you trying to prove? I tell you—he was innocent while with me. But if he wasn’t, he was still with me. If he killed someone in the garden, who saw it? Who knew? Do you hate Neil so much you’d accuse your own daughter—”

Lady Galloway screamed.

Everyone sat frozen, sensing the hidden tragedies between lovers—murdered husbands, poisoned hearts, betrayals older than memory.

Then, from the corner, a gentle voice interrupted: “Was it a very long cigar?”

They turned. Father Brown, still in his corner, had spoken.

“I mean,” he said, “the cigar Mr. Brayne is finishing. It looks nearly as long as a walking stick.”

Valentin glanced up—surprised, then thoughtful.

“Quite right,” he said. “Ivan, go check on Mr. Brayne. Bring him here at once.”

The servant left. Valentin turned to Margaret.

“Lady Margaret,” he said, “we’re grateful for your honesty. But there’s a gap. You were seen leaving the study, and Galloway found the garden minutes later. O’Brien was still walking it.”

Margaret smiled faintly. “I just refused him. We wouldn’t return arm in arm. He’s a gentleman. He lingered behind. So he’s accused.”

“In those moments,” Valentin said, “he might have—”

The knock came again.

Ivan poked his head in. “Sir, Mr. Brayne has left the house.”

“Left?” Valentin rose.

“Gone,” Ivan said, grinning. “Scooted. Evaporated. Hat and coat are gone. And here’s something to cap it all—I ran outside and found a trace. A big one.”

“What?” Valentin asked.

“I’ll show you.”

Ivan returned, brandishing a naked cavalry sabre—bloodstained near the tip and edge.

The room fell silent. The weapon gleamed like a bolt of lightning.

“I found this,” Ivan said calmly, “flung among the bushes fifty yards up the road to Paris. Right where Mr. Brayne threw it when he ran.”

The Queer Feet

If you happen upon a member of the exclusive "Twelve True Fishermen" entering the Vernon Hotel for their annual dinner, you’ll notice—when he removes his overcoat—that his evening coat is green, not black. Should you, in a moment of reckless boldness, ask why, he’ll likely reply that it’s to avoid being mistaken for a waiter. You’d then retreat, humbled and bewildered. But behind this absurdity lies a mystery—one worth telling.

Now, suppose you were to meet a quiet, unassuming priest named Father Brown, and asked him what the most remarkable stroke of luck in his life had been. He might say, without hesitation, that it was at the Vernon Hotel, where he prevented a crime and perhaps saved a soul—all by listening to footsteps echoing down a dim corridor. He’s fond of this tale, though he rarely tells it. And since it's highly improbable that you’ll ever rise high enough to dine with the Twelve True Fishermen, or sink low enough to cross paths with Father Brown, you may never hear the story unless I tell it.

The Vernon Hotel, where the Fishermen held their dinners, was an oddity born of a society obsessed with exclusivity. It wasn’t built to attract guests—it was built to exclude them. A paradoxical business: an “exclusive” enterprise that made profit not by welcoming people, but by making entry difficult. In a world ruled by wealth and pretense, even service staff became more refined than their patrons. The hotel prided itself on its inconveniences—each one a barrier protecting its elite clientele. For instance, only twenty-four people could dine there at once. The main table, known as the terrace table, stood open to the air on a veranda overlooking one of London’s most beautiful old gardens. But because it was exposed to the elements, it could only be used in warm weather—making it all the rarer and more coveted.

The owner, a Jewish man named Lever, earned nearly a million pounds from this carefully curated scarcity. Yet he ensured every detail was flawless. The wine and food rivaled any in Europe; the waiters moved with silent precision, trained like gentleman’s servants. There were only fifteen of them—more difficult to become than a Member of Parliament. Each guest had a waiter assigned to them, almost like a personal attendant.

The Twelve True Fishermen would not have dined anywhere else. They demanded privacy and luxury. Their rituals were elaborate—their fish knives and forks, each shaped like a fish and adorned with a large pearl at the hilt, were laid out for the fish course, which was always the centerpiece of the meal. The club had no real purpose, no history—just tradition. To belong, you didn’t need talent or fortune—you simply had to already be the kind of person who belonged. It had existed for twelve years. Its president was Mr. Audley; its vice-president, the Duke of Chester.

So how did I come to know such intimate details? And how did Father Brown—a humble priest—end up in this world of polished silences and hidden snobbery?

It was simple, really. There’s a certain kind of man—aged, rebellious, preaching brotherhood—who storms into the most refined places, spreading radical ideas. Wherever he goes, Father Brown follows. That day, one of the hotel’s waiters—an Italian—had suffered a paralytic stroke. Mr. Lever, mildly amused by such superstitions, agreed to send for the nearest Catholic priest. What the waiter confessed to Father Brown remains unknown—he kept it to himself. But it required him to write a note, perhaps to correct a wrong or deliver a message. So Father Brown, with his usual modest audacity, requested a room and writing materials.

Mr. Lever hesitated. He was kind, yet deeply afraid of disruption. The presence of a stranger—even a priest—was like a stain on a perfectly cleaned surface. The hotel operated like a private house: no strangers, no interruptions. Fifteen waiters. Twelve guests. No one extra. The idea of a new face was unthinkable. And Father Brown looked… ordinary. His clothes were muddy, his appearance plain. One glance might trigger panic among the guests.

In the end, Lever devised a compromise. Behind the main lounge, near the cloakroom and the office, was a small private room—used only for delicate matters, like lending a duke money or refusing him sixpence. It was a sacred space. Yet for half an hour, it was occupied by a priest, scribbling in silence.

What Father Brown wrote is lost to us. Perhaps it was a better story than this one. But we know it was long, and the final paragraphs were dull. It was during these last lines that his mind began to wander. Darkness fell. The light in his room failed. And in the growing gloom, his senses sharpened.

As he wrote, he noticed a rhythm—something outside his door. Not music, but sound: footsteps. At first, they seemed normal—just someone walking past. But then he listened closer. The pattern was strange.

First came a burst of quick, light steps—like a man racing. Then, abruptly, they slowed into a heavy, dragging stamp. Then again, the rapid pace returned. This cycle repeated. The same boots—identifiable by a faint creak—were responsible. No other footsteps disturbed the silence. The hotel was still. Guests had retreated to their rooms. Waiters vanished into invisibility.

Yet this rhythm defied logic. Why run to walk? Why walk to run? Father Brown traced the pattern on the table edge, like a musician learning a tune. His mind raced. Was it a ritual? A dance? A secret signal?

Then he realized something: the slow step didn’t belong to the proprietor—too fast, too waddling. Not a servant—too careless. Not drunk—too controlled. It was a gentleman. A man of leisure. Western European. Someone who had never worked for a living.

Just as he reached this conclusion, the footsteps changed again. This time, the running was faster—so swift, so soft, it sounded like tiptoeing. Yet it wasn’t secretive. It felt charged with urgency. He couldn’t place it. It was familiar—almost haunting. Then, suddenly, it hit him.

He sprang up, walked to the door. His room opened into two directions: the glass office and the cloakroom. The office door was locked. He turned to the window—now filled with purple clouds and the afterglow of sunset—and for a moment, he sensed danger. Like a dog smelling rats.

But reason returned. The proprietor had told him to lock the door. He reminded himself that many explanations might account for the sounds. He picked up his paper, brought it to the window to catch the fading light, and resumed writing.

For twenty minutes, he bent over his work. Then—again—the footsteps returned.

This time, there was a third element. Before, the man had walked or run. Now, he *leapt*. The sound was like a panther bounding through the corridor—swift, powerful, desperate. Then, just as it passed the office, it reverted to the old slow, swaggering stamp.

Father Brown dropped his paper. He knew the office door was locked. So he went to the cloakroom.

The attendant was absent. Only coats hung in the dimness. He found the counter—half-door—leading to the corridor. Above it, a light glowed. It illuminated the hallway, but left him in shadow against the sunset-stained window.

And there, standing in the light, was a man.

Tall, elegant, dressed plainly in evening wear. He wore no hat, yet carried himself with ease—like a shadow slipping through a crowd. His face was swarthy, lively—foreign. His figure was strong, his manner confident. But his coat—though fine—bulged oddly, as if concealing something.

He glanced at Brown’s silhouette, tossed down a scrap of paper, and said cheerfully: “I want my hat and coat—I must leave at once.”

Brown took the slip silently and retrieved the coat. As he placed it on the counter, the man laughed: “I haven’t got any silver. Keep this.” He dropped half a sovereign and grabbed his coat.

Brown remained still—dark, motionless. But inside, his mind exploded. In those moments, when reason fled, inspiration struck. He saw connections no one else could see. He had lost his head—but in doing so, he had gained clarity.

“I think, sir,” he said calmly, “that you have some silver in your pocket.”

The man blinked. “Hang it—if I choose to give gold, why should you complain?”

“Because silver,” said Brown gently, “is sometimes more valuable than gold. Especially in large quantities.”

The man stared. Then he looked up the corridor. Then back at Brown. Then at the window, still glowing with the storm’s afterimage. Finally, he made a decision.

He placed a hand on the counter, vaulted over it like an acrobat, and loomed above the priest, gripping his collar.

“Stand still,” he whispered, voice tight. “I don’t want to threaten you, but—”

“I do want to threaten you,” said Father Brown, voice deep and thunderous. “I threaten you with the worm that dieth not, and the fire that is not quenched.”

“You’re a queer cloak-room clerk,” said the man.

“I am a priest,” said Brown, “and I am ready to hear your confession.”

The man staggered back, gasping, collapsing into a chair.

Meanwhile, in the dining room, the Twelve True Fishermen enjoyed their first courses in serene silence. The menu was written in a French so elevated it made real Frenchmen laugh. The hors d’œuvres were endless, absurd—deliberately pointless, like the club itself. The soup was light, austere—a prelude to the grand finale: the fish course.

The talk was polite, trivial, yet deeply influential. Cabinet ministers were mentioned by first names, praised for poetry or riding skills, not politics. The Radical Chancellor was admired for his hunting saddle; the Tory leader, for his wit. No one spoke of ideology—only of style.

Mr. Audley, the chairman, was a relic of a bygone era—mild, unremarkable, yet indispensable. He wore Gladstone collars, had grey hair combed over his neck, and looked like the empire’s ideal statesman from behind. From the front, he was just a gentle bachelor with rooms in the Albany.

The Duke of Chester, young and rising, was pleasant, freckled, and slightly foolish. He was brilliant when he joked, wise when he solemnly declared “this is no time for trifling.” In private, he was just a boy playing at power.

They sat along the inner side of the terrace table, facing the garden. No one sat opposite. The view was uninterrupted. The fish course arrived—what looked like a monstrous wedding cake, filled with fish reduced to indistinguishable shapes. The Fishermen picked up their silver fish knives and forks—each a masterpiece—and approached it with solemn reverence.

They ate in silence, save for the Duke’s ritual comment, once his plate was nearly empty: “They can’t do this anywhere but here.”

“Nowhere,” said Mr. Audley, nodding gravely. “Nowhere, assuredly. I was told it could be done at the Café Anglais—”

He paused as a waiter removed his plate, then continued: “Nothing like it, sir. Nothing like it.”

“Overrated place,” muttered Colonel Pound.

“Oh, I don’t know,” said the Duke, optimistically. “It’s jolly good for some things. You can’t beat it at—”

But the sentence was cut short by the sudden silence of the hall—and the distant echo of a priest’s voice, calling out from the shadows.

The Flying Stars

“*The most beautiful crime I ever committed,*” Flambeau would say in his later years, when morality had finally caught up with him, “*was also, by a strange twist of fate, my last. It happened at Christmas.*” As an artist of villainy, he had always believed that a crime should be tailored to its setting—like a painter choosing the right landscape for his masterpiece. A nobleman’s fraud deserved a long oak-paneled hall; a Jewish merchant’s downfall, the glittering confusion of Café Riche. In England, if he were to rob a dean, he’d do it amid cathedral lawns and grey towers. In France, stealing from a wealthy peasant meant placing the scene against a row of clipped poplars and the vast, silent fields where Millet’s spirits wandered.

“So,” he’d continue, “my final act was a Christmas crime—a cozy, middle-class English affair, straight out of Charles Dickens. I did it in a fine old house near Putney: crescent drive, stable beside it, gates bearing the family name, and a monkey tree in the garden. You know the type. I think I captured the Dickensian spirit quite well—almost too well. It’s a shame I repented the same night.”

And then, with quiet pride, he’d recount the tale—not as a confession, but as a performance. Even from within, the story was odd. From without, it made no sense at all. And so, let us begin not from the inside, but from the outside—the only place where truth can be seen clearly.

It began on Boxing Day, as the front doors of the Putney house opened onto the garden, where the monkey tree stood like a sentinel. A young girl stepped out, wrapped in thick brown furs, carrying bread for the birds. Her face was lovely—bright eyes, warm cheeks—but her body was almost hidden beneath layers of fur, making her look less like a girl and more like a small, amiable bear.

The afternoon light was deepening into dusk, painting the bare flowerbeds in crimson shadows. On one side of the house stood the stable; on the other, a laurel-lined path led to the back garden. The girl scattered crumbs—again, perhaps for the fourth or fifth time that day, since the dog kept eating them—and then slipped through the laurels into the evergreen grove behind.

There, she stopped short.

Above the high garden wall, silhouetted against the fading sky, sat a figure—perched like a rider on an invisible horse.

“Oh, don’t jump, Mr. Crook!” she called, startled. “It’s much too high!”

The man leapt down lightly, landing beside her as if it were nothing. He was tall and angular, with dark hair standing up like bristles, sharp features, and a sallow complexion that seemed foreign. But it was the red tie he wore—the only part of his clothes that seemed cared for—that drew attention. Perhaps it was a statement.

“I think I was born to be a burglar,” he said calmly. “I’d have been one, too, if I hadn’t been born next door. I can’t see any harm in it.”

“How can you say such things?” she protested.

“Well,” he replied, “if you’re born on the wrong side of the wall, why shouldn’t you climb over?”

“I never know what you’ll do next,” she said, smiling.

“I don’t often know myself,” he answered. “But now I’m on the right side.”

“And which side is the right one?” she asked.

“The side you’re on,” he said.

They walked together toward the front garden when suddenly, three sharp blasts echoed from a motor horn. A sleek, pale-green car sped up the drive, stopping with a hum before the front doors.

“Hullo, hullo!” said the man in the red tie. “Here’s someone born on the right side after all. Didn’t know, Miss Adams, that your Santa Claus was this modern.”

“That’s my godfather, Sir Leopold Fischer,” she said. “He always comes on Boxing Day.”

After a brief pause—too long to be natural—she added, “He’s very kind.”

John Crook, journalist and occasional revolutionary, had heard of Sir Leopold Fischer, the powerful City financier. But Fischer had never heard of him—except perhaps in *The Clarion* or *The New Age*, where Crook had once criticized him sharply. Yet Crook said nothing. Instead, he watched as the chauffeur and manservant helped their master from the car, carefully unwrapping him like a priceless parcel.

Rugs, furs, scarves—layer after layer fell away, revealing a large, friendly-looking man with a grey goat-beard and a broad smile. He rubbed his fur gloves together, still adjusting to the cold.

Before the process ended, Colonel Adams—father of the girl—emerged from the house, wearing a red smoking cap that gave him the air of a colonial governor. With him was James Blount, his brother-in-law, recently returned from Canada—a big, boisterous farmer with a yellow beard. Also present was Father Brown, the quiet priest from the nearby Catholic church. The colonel’s late wife had been Catholic, and the children had been raised in the faith. Though unassuming—his name was Brown, and he looked it—he had become a trusted guest.

Inside, the entrance hall was spacious enough to hold the entire party. The porch and vestibule were unusually large, forming a sort of grand room between the front door and the staircase. Over the fireplace hung the colonel’s sword. There, Sir Leopold finished removing his wraps and presented his gift to Ruby.

From a deep pocket, he pulled a black oval case. With a flourish, he opened it—and the room seemed to catch fire.

Three white diamonds, larger than eggs, lay nestled in orange velvet. They sparkled like stars, casting light across every face. The “Flying Stars”—the three great African diamonds, stolen so many times they were legendary. Fischer smiled proudly.

“I’ll put them back now,” he said, tucking the case into his coat. “Had to be careful coming down. They’re worth a fortune—and dangerous. Any thief might have taken them on the way. Could’ve lost them.”

“Quite natural,” muttered Crook. “If people ask for bread and you give them nothing, I wouldn’t blame them for taking the stone.”

“You mustn’t talk like that,” Ruby said, flushing.

“You’ve only started since you became a Socialist,” she added, half-joking.

“A saint,” said Father Brown quietly.

Sir Leopold frowned. “Ruby means a Socialist.”

“A radical isn’t a man who eats radishes,” Crook retorted. “A Conservative isn’t one who makes jam. And a Socialist isn’t someone who wants tea with a chimney-sweep. A Socialist wants all chimneys swept—and the sweeps paid.”

“But won’t let you own your own soot,” murmured the priest.

Crook looked at him with interest. “Does one want to own soot?”

“One might,” said Brown. “Gardeners use it. And once, when the conjuror didn’t come, I made six children happy at Christmas—using soot. Applied externally.”

“Oh, splendid!” Ruby laughed. “Do it for us now!”

Blount roared with laughter. Fischer looked mildly offended. Then came a knock at the front door.

Father Brown opened it. Outside, the garden glowed under a violet sunset. A dusty messenger stood there, holding a letter.

“Any of you gentlemen Mr. Blount?” he asked.

Blount took the letter, tore it open, and read. His face darkened slightly—then cleared.

“I’m sorry to be a nuisance, colonel,” he said cheerfully. “But an old friend of mine is coming tonight—Florian, the French acrobat and comic actor. I knew him in the West. He says he has business with me.”

“Of course,” said the colonel. “Any friend of yours.”

“He’ll black his face,” Blount joked. “Or everyone else’s eyes. I don’t care—I like pantomime. Man sits on his top hat.”

“Not on mine,” Fischer said firmly.

“Well,” said Crook, “don’t let’s argue. There are lower jokes than sitting on a hat.”

Fischer, annoyed by Crook’s closeness to Ruby, sneered, “No doubt you’ve found something lower. What is it?”

“Letting a top hat sit on you,” said Crook.

Blount laughed. “Now, now, don’t spoil the evening. Let’s do something fun. A proper old English pantomime—clown, columbine, harlequin. I saw one at twelve. It burned in my mind like a bonfire. I came back last year and found it gone—only fairy tales now. I want a hot poker and a policeman turned into sausages. They give me princesses moonlighting. Blue Beard’s more my style.”

“I’m all for turning a policeman into sausages,” said Crook. “That’s better than some recent definitions of Socialism. But costumes? I haven’t killed a policeman lately.”

Blount slapped his thigh. “We can! I’ve got Florian’s number. He knows every costumier in London. I’ll phone him to bring a police outfit.”

He dashed to the telephone.

“Oh, glorious!” Ruby cried. “I’ll be columbine, and you’ll be pantaloon, godfather.”

Fischer stiffened. “I think, my dear, you should find another pantaloon.”

“I’ll be pantaloon,” said Colonel Adams, taking his cigar out of his mouth—for the first and last time.

“We need a statue,” Blount said, returning. “Mr. Crook will be clown—he knows all the old jokes. I’ll be harlequin. Florian’s bringing the costume. We can perform here. Audience on the stairs. Doors for the backdrop. Open, it’s a moonlit garden. Closed, it’s a drawing room. Magic.” He drew a line across the floor with billiard chalk—footlights.

How they managed it in minutes remains a mystery. But youth, reckless and energetic, took over. The columbine wore a lampshade-like skirt. The clown and pantaloon painted themselves white with flour, red with rouge. Harlequin, already covered in silver paper from cigar boxes, nearly shattered the chandelier until Ruby produced old paste jewels from a fancy dress party.

Blount, wild with excitement, placed a paper donkey’s head on Father Brown, who bore it patiently. He even tried to attach a tail to Fischer’s coat—until Ruby stopped him.

“Uncle’s too absurd,” she said to Crook, dangling a string of sausages around his neck.

“He’s harlequin to your columbine,” Crook said. “I’m just the clown who tells the old jokes.”

“I wish you were the harlequin,” she said, leaving the sausages swinging.

Father Brown, though he had helped make a pillow into a pantomime baby, sat among the audience, solemn as a child at a first matinee. Sir Leopold sat in front, his massive fur-collared figure blocking the view—but whether that mattered, no one knows.

The pantomime was chaotic, yet strangely alive. Crook, as clown, became everything: author, prompter, musician. At key moments, he bounded to the piano and pounded out ridiculous tunes—“The Pirates of Penzance,” “Where Did You Get That Hat?”—each note matching the madness.

Then, the moment arrived. The front doors flew open, revealing the moonlit garden—and the professional guest: Florian, dressed as a policeman.

Crook played the constabulary chorus. The crowd erupted. Florian mimed the perfect officer—then was hit by the harlequin. He turned, astonished. Hit again. Then, the harlequin tackled him, and Florian collapsed—perfectly limp.

He was swung like a sack, twisted like a club, lifted off the ground—while Crook played “I Arise from Dreams of Thee.” When the harlequin shuffled him across his back, the music changed to “With My Bundle on My Shoulder.” Finally, the policeman was dropped with a convincing thud—and the piano burst into jingles: “I sent a letter to my love, and on the way I dropped it.”

At that point, Father Brown’s view was blocked. Sir Leopold rose abruptly, rummaging in his pockets. He sat, fumbled, stood again. For a moment, it seemed he would cross the stage. Then he turned a glare at Crook—and left the room silently.

Brown watched the harlequin dance slowly backward into the garden, the silver costume glowing under the moon. The applause was thunderous—until someone touched his arm.

“Come to the study,” a voice whispered.

He followed, unsure. Inside, Colonel Adams sat in pantaloon costume, eyes sad. Fischer leaned against the mantelpiece, breathing hard.

“This is painful, Father Brown,” said Adams. “The diamonds—those we saw earlier—are gone. And since you—”

“As I,” Brown interrupted, grinning, “was sitting directly behind him—”

“Nothing like that shall be suggested,” Adams said firmly. “I only ask for your help—as a gentleman.”

“Which is turning out his pockets,” said Brown, and did so. Seven and sixpence, a ticket, a crucifix, a breviary, a chocolate bar.

Adams stared. “I’d rather see the inside of your head than your pockets. My daughter is one of your people. She has lately—”

“She has lately,” Fischer snapped, “opened her father’s house to a cut-throat Socialist who admits he’d steal from the rich. Now look—the rich man is poorer.”

“If you want my head,” said Brown, weary, “you can have it. But first, I notice: men who plan to steal diamonds don’t talk about Socialism. They denounce it.”

Both men shifted. Brown continued: “We know these people. That Socialist wouldn’t steal a diamond—or a pyramid. We must look at the one man we don’t know—the policeman. Where is he now?”

Adams strode out. A silence followed. Fischer stared at Brown. Brown read his breviary.

Adams returned. “The policeman is still on stage. The curtain has risen and fallen six times. He hasn’t moved.”

Brown dropped the book. His eyes went wide. Then, slowly, understanding dawned.

“Colonel,” he said, “when did your wife die?”

“Two months ago,” Adams replied. “Her brother James arrived a week too late.”

Brown jumped. “Come on! We must go to the stage!”

They rushed past the actors, who were whispering quietly. Brown knelt beside the prostrate policeman.

“Chloroform,” he said, rising. “I just realized it.”

And in that moment, the true crime revealed itself—not in theft, but in betrayal.

The Invisible Man

In the cool blue twilight of two steep streets in Camden Town, the corner shop—a confectioner’s—glowed like the end of a lit firework. Its light, fractured by mirrors and dancing across gilt cakes and brightly wrapped sweets, cast a rainbow glow that drew children like moths to flame. The chocolates were wrapped in shimmering red, gold, and green foil, almost more tempting than the chocolate itself. And looming in the window, a giant white wedding cake seemed both distant and desirable—like the North Pole made edible. Such dazzling displays naturally attracted the neighborhood’s younger set, but this spot also held appeal for older youths.

One such young man, no less than twenty-four, stood transfixed before the same window. He was tall, broad-shouldered, with fiery red hair and a face of quiet determination beneath a relaxed demeanor. Under his arm he carried a flat, grey portfolio filled with black-and-white sketches—drawings he’d sold off and on to publishers ever since his uncle, an admiral, cut him off for delivering a lecture against capitalism. His name was John Turnbull Angus.

At last, he stepped inside, passing through the confectioner’s into the back room, which doubled as a modest pastry restaurant. He tipped his hat to the young woman behind the counter, a dark-haired, elegant girl in black, her cheeks flushed and her eyes sharp and quick. After a brief pause, she followed him into the inner room to take his order.

His request was familiar: “One halfpenny bun and a small cup of black coffee,” he said precisely. Just as she turned away, he added, “And I want you to marry me.”

She froze. “I don’t allow jokes like that,” she said firmly.

Angus lifted his grey eyes—unexpectedly serious.
“Really and truly,” he said, “it’s as serious as the bun. It costs money, like the bun. It’s indigestible. It hurts.”

She hadn’t taken her eyes off him. Now, after a long look, she gave the faintest hint of a smile and sat down.

“Don’t you think,” he mused, “it’s cruel to eat these buns? They might grow up to be penny buns. I’ll give up this brutality when we’re married.”

She rose, walked to the window, clearly deep in thought. When she turned again, she found him carefully arranging items from the display—pyramids of candy, plates of sandwiches, decanters of port and sherry, and at the center, the enormous sugar-coated cake that had adorned the window.

“What on earth are you doing?” she asked.

“Duty, my dear Laura,” he began.

“Oh, for heaven’s sake,” she interrupted, “don’t talk like that. What is all this?”

“A ceremonial meal, Miss Hope.”

“And that?” she asked, pointing to the mountain of sugar.

“The wedding cake, Mrs. Angus.”

She marched over, removed it with a clatter, and returned it to the window. Then she leaned on the table, elbows out, studying him with irritation tinged with something softer.

“You don’t give me time to think,” she said.

“I’m not such a fool,” he replied. “That’s my Christian humility.”

She looked at him, her expression softening slightly, then hardening again.
“Mr. Angus,” she said steadily, “before we go any further, I must tell you something about myself—briefly.”

“Delighted,” he said gravely. “You might tell me something about myself while you’re at it.”

“Oh, shut up and listen,” she snapped. “It’s nothing I’m ashamed of. Not even sorry about. But what if there’s something that isn’t my business—and yet haunts me?”

“In that case,” he said seriously, “I’d suggest bringing back the cake.”

“Well, listen first,” she insisted. “My father owned the ‘Red Fish’ inn in Ludbury—a sleepy little place in the Eastern Counties. I used to serve in the bar.”

“I’ve always wondered,” he said, “why this shop feels so… well, Christian.”

“Ludbury’s dull and green. The only people who came were occasional salesmen or the worst kind of idle men—men with just enough money to live on, nothing better to do than lounge in bars and bet on horses. We didn’t see many of them. But two did come too often—too often in every way. Both had their own money, both were idle and overdressed. But I felt sorry for them. I think they came to our bar because each had a slight oddity—one that some country folk would laugh at.”

“One was very small—almost a dwarf, though not quite. He had a round black head, a neat black beard, bright bird-like eyes. He jingled coins in his pockets, wore a thick gold watch chain, and always dressed like a gentleman who wasn’t one. He wasn’t stupid—just useless. He could do clever little tricks: make matches ignite like fireworks, carve a dancing doll from a banana. His name was Isidore Smythe. I can still see him, barely reaching the counter, making a kangaroo leap from five cigars.”

“The other was taller, thinner, fair-haired, with a high nose bridge—almost handsome, in a ghostly way. But he had the most terrible squint. When he looked at you, you couldn’t tell where you were. I think it made him bitter. While Smythe liked showing off, James Welkin never did anything but sit in the bar or walk alone through the flat, grey countryside. Still, I think Smythe was sensitive about his size, though he hid it better. So when both offered to marry me in the same week, I was stunned—and deeply sorry.”

“I did something I now think was foolish. But they were friends, and I feared they’d think I refused them because they were ugly. So I lied—I said I wouldn’t marry anyone who hadn’t earned their way. That I wouldn’t live off inherited wealth, like theirs. Two days later, both left—to seek their fortunes, like characters in a fairy tale.”

“I’ve never seen either since. But I’ve received two letters from Smythe—exciting ones.”

“Have you heard from the other?” Angus asked.

“No,” she said after a pause. “He never wrote. Smythe’s first letter said he’d started walking to London with Welkin. But Welkin was such a fast walker, Smythe dropped behind and rested by the roadside. There, he was picked up by a traveling show. Because he was nearly a dwarf and clever, he did well—soon sent to the Aquarium to perform tricks. That was the first letter. The second came last week—it shook me.”

Angus finished his coffee, watching her with calm patience. A faint smile touched her lips. “You’ve seen the posters for ‘Smythe’s Silent Service,’ haven’t you? Clockwork servants—‘Press a Button—A Butler Who Never Drinks.’ ‘Turn a Handle—Ten Housemaids Who Never Flirt.’ You’ve seen them, I suppose.”

“Of course,” he said.

“Well, those machines are making him rich. And I’m glad—though terrified. He’s carved his way in the world, just as he promised. But I fear he’ll come back to claim me.”

“And the other man?” Angus repeated quietly.

Laura stood abruptly. “My friend,” she said, “you’re a witch. You’re right. I’ve never seen a word from Welkin. I have no idea where he is. But it’s *him* I’m afraid of. He’s everywhere. He’s driven me near madness. I’ve felt him where he couldn’t be. Heard his voice when he couldn’t speak.”

“If you were truly mad,” Angus said cheerfully, “you’d think you were sane. But yes, something’s odd about this unseen man. Two heads are better than one. If you’ll let me—”

Just then, a sharp metallic shriek echoed from outside. A small car, speeding wildly, skidded to a halt at the shop door. In the next instant, a tiny man in a shiny top hat stamped into the outer room.

Angus, who had been keeping his composure for mental balance, suddenly surged forward, confronting the newcomer. One glance confirmed his suspicion: this dapper, dwarfish figure—with his black beard, restless eyes, nervous fingers—was none other than Isidore Smythe, the inventor of the silent service, the man who turned bananas into dolls and matchboxes into fireworks.

For a moment, the two men locked eyes—each sensing the other’s claim, each recognizing the cold camaraderie of rivalry.

Smythe didn’t mention the woman. Instead, he exploded: “Has Miss Hope seen that thing on the window?”

“On the window?” Angus repeated, startled.

“There’s no time for explanations,” Smythe snapped. “There’s tomfoolery here that needs investigating.”

He pointed his polished cane at the window, recently stripped of its bridal display. Angus stared—and saw a strip of paper pasted along the glass, not there moments before. Outside, a yard and a half of stamp paper had been glued to the pane, bearing a jagged message: *If you marry Smythe, he will die.*

“Laura,” Angus called into the shop, “you’re not mad.”

“It’s Welkin’s writing,” Smythe growled. “I haven’t seen him in years, but he’s always bothering me. Five times in two weeks, threatening letters have appeared at my flat. I can’t find who leaves them. And now—this.” He gestured at the window. “While people in the shop were having tea.”

“Quite so,” Angus said modestly. “But I assure you, I appreciate your directness. This fellow can’t be far—he wasn’t there ten minutes ago. But he’s too far to chase. We don’t know which direction. My advice, Mr. Smythe: put this in the hands of a private investigator. I know someone—Flambeau. He’s brilliant, honest now, though his youth was stormy. Lives in Lucknow Mansions, Hampstead.”

“That’s odd,” Smythe said, raising an eyebrow. “I live in Himylaya Mansions, just around the corner. Perhaps you’d care to come with me? I can sort through these Welkin documents while you fetch your detective.”

“Very kind,” Angus said. “Let’s move quickly.”

Both men bowed formally to Laura, then jumped into Smythe’s compact car. As they turned the corner, Angus noticed a massive billboard for “Smythe’s Silent Service”—a headless iron doll carrying a saucepan, labeled: *A Cook Who is Never Cross.*

“I use them in my flat,” Smythe laughed. “Good for ads and real convenience. Honestly, they’re faster than any servant—press the right knob. But…” He paused. “They can’t tell me who left those letters.”

The car was small, swift—like its owner. It was one of his inventions. If he was a quack, he believed in his product. They sped up long white curves under the fading evening light. Soon, the road spiraled upward, rising above London like a hillside fortress. Terrace after terrace climbed toward the sky. At the top, the tower block of Himylaya Mansions loomed, gilded by sunset.

As they entered the crescent, Angus saw a chestnut seller at one end, a dim blue policeman at the other—the only human figures in the quiet solitude. Yet they seemed to belong to a story.

The car shot up to the building, and Smythe burst out like a bomb. He questioned the commissionaire and porter—both swore no one had approached since his last inquiry. Then, with Angus following, they took the lift to the top floor.

“Just a moment,” Smythe panted. “I’ll show you the letters. Then you can fetch Flambeau.” He pressed a hidden button—door opened automatically.

Inside was a spacious anteroom lined with towering mechanical figures—headless, painted green, red, or black, with hook-like arms for trays. They stood like silent guards. But between them lay something more startling: a torn scrap of paper, scrawled in wet red ink: *If you have been to see her today, I shall kill you.*

Smythe handed it to Angus without a word.

“Would you like whiskey?” he asked quietly. “I feel I need one.”

“Thank you,” Angus said grimly. “I’d prefer Flambeau. This is getting serious. I’ll go get him.”

“Right,” Smythe said cheerfully. “Bring him as fast as you can.”

As Angus closed the door, he saw Smythe press another button. A clockwork figure glided forward, carrying a tray with siphon and decanter. Leaving the inventor alone among the dead servants—who now moved—felt strangely eerie.

Six steps down, the porter was handling a bucket. Angus stopped him, promising a bribe to stay and watch the stairs. Then he rushed to the front hall, instructing the commissionaire to guard the entrance—no back door existed. He even pulled the policeman aside, persuading him to stand opposite the house. Finally, he bought chestnuts and asked how long the vendor would stay.

The man shrugged, pulling up his collar. “Probably soon. It’s going to snow.”

Indeed, the sky grew grey and cold. But Angus insisted: “Stay warm on your chestnuts. Eat them all. I’ll give you a sovereign if you wait until I return—and tell me if anyone enters that house.”

Then he walked briskly away, pausing only to glance back at the isolated tower.

“I’ve made a ring around that room,” he muttered. “They can’t all four be Welkin’s accomplices.”

Lucknow Mansions sat lower on the hill. Flambeau’s flat was on the ground floor—utterly different from Smythe’s sterile, mechanical world. It was a cluttered, artistic den: sabres, old guns, Eastern curios, wine flasks, a Persian cat, and a small, dusty-looking Roman Catholic priest sitting quietly in a corner.

“This is Father Brown,” Flambeau said. “I’ve wanted you to meet him. Splendid weather—though cold for Southerners.”

“Yes,” Angus said, sinking onto a violet-striped ottoman. “It should clear.”

“No,” the priest said calmly. “It has begun to snow.”

And indeed, as he spoke, the first flakes drifted across the darkening windowpane.

The Honour of Israel Gow

A stormy evening, painted in shades of olive and silver, settled over the Scottish Highlands as Father Brown, wrapped in a grey Scotch plaid, approached the end of a narrow valley. Before him stood Glengyle Castle—imposing, isolated, and utterly mysterious. It blocked the glen like a dead-end street, and seemed to mark the edge of the world. Its steep roofs and spires, clad in seagreen slate, resembled the pointed hats of witches from old fairy tales. The surrounding pine woods, dark and restless, looked like flocks of ravens gathered in mourning. This was no mere landscape—it carried an atmosphere of quiet dread, a sense of ancient pride and sorrow that clung to the noble houses of Scotland more fiercely than anywhere else. For Scotland bore a double burden: the weight of bloodlines in its aristocracy and the shadow of doom in its Calvinist soul.

Father Brown had taken a day off from his duties in Glasgow to meet Flambeau, the amateur detective, who was at Glengyle with Inspector Craven of Scotland Yard, investigating the strange life and death of the late Earl of Glengyle. The earl was the last of a long line known for their fierce courage, erratic minds, and cunning schemes—men so entangled in intrigue that they had once played a deadly role in the court of Mary Queen of Scots.

An old rhyme echoed through the countryside:

*As green sap to the simmer trees
Is red gold to the Ogilvies.*

For centuries, no decent lord had ruled Glengyle. When the Victorian age arrived, one might have expected such eccentricities to fade. But the final Earl of Glengyle defied expectations—he simply vanished. Not abroad, not in exile. He remained within the castle, if he was anywhere at all. His name appeared in church records and the Peerage, but no living soul had seen him under the sun.

Only one man claimed to have seen him: Israel Gow, a solitary servant—part gardener, part groom, part cook. Deaf, perhaps dumb, certainly slow-witted, Gow was a gaunt, red-haired man with a stubborn jaw and blank blue eyes. He worked tirelessly in the garden, dug potatoes with grim precision, and disappeared into the kitchen regularly. People assumed he was feeding someone hidden inside the castle—the earl himself. And when the provost and minister were called to the castle one morning, they found Gow had taken on another role: undertaker. He had placed the body of Lord Glengyle—whether it was truly his or not—into a coffin and buried it in the hillside churchyard.

No formal investigation had been conducted until Flambeau arrived a few days earlier. By then, the body had lain unexamined in the graveyard for some time.

As Father Brown stepped into the dim garden beneath the looming chateau, clouds thickened and the air grew damp and electric. Against the fading golden light, he saw a black silhouette—a man in a chimney-pot hat, carrying a large spade. The image struck him as oddly familiar, like a sexton preparing for burial. Then he remembered: it was Gow, the deaf gardener. He understood the man’s habits—his respectability, his frugality, his need to wear “blacks” for official business, yet still work the land. Even the way Gow flinched at the priest’s approach made sense: the kind of guarded caution common among rural Scots.

The great door opened to reveal Flambeau and Inspector Craven, a lean man with iron-grey hair and papers in hand. The entrance hall was stripped bare, but portraits of past Ogilvies—pale, sneering faces in black wigs—watched from the walls like silent judges.

In the inner room, Flambeau and Craven sat at a long oak table littered with scribbled notes, whisky bottles, and cigars. Along the length of the table lay a series of strange objects: a pile of glittering shards, a mound of brown dust, a plain wooden stick.

“You’ve got something of a geological museum here,” said Father Brown, nodding toward the dust and crystals.

“Not geological,” replied Flambeau. “Call it psychological.”

“Oh, for heaven’s sake,” laughed Craven, “let’s not start with big words.”

“Don’t you know what psychology means?” asked Flambeau. “It means being off your head.”

“I’m still not following,” said Craven.

“Well,” said Flambeau firmly, “we’ve only learned one thing about Lord Glengyle: he was mad.”

Outside, the silhouette of Gow passed the window again, dark against the sky. Brown watched silently.

“I can see he must have been odd,” said the priest. “Otherwise, why would he bury himself alive—and so quickly after death? But why do you think madness?”

“We’ve found things,” said Flambeau. “Things that don’t make sense.”

“A storm is coming,” said Craven suddenly. “We need a candle. It’s too dark to read.”

“Have you found any candles among your curiosities?” asked Brown, smiling.

Flambeau looked up sharply.

“That’s curious,” he said. “Twenty-five candles, but not a single candlestick.”

Brown walked along the table, stopping beside the heap of reddish-brown dust. As he bent down, a sharp sneeze broke the silence.

“Hullo!” he said. “Snuff!”

He took a candle, lit it carefully, and stuck it in the neck of a whisky bottle. The flame flickered wildly in the gusting wind. Outside, the pine forests roared like a sea around a lonely rock.

“Let me read the inventory,” began Craven, picking up a paper. “These are items found loose and unexplained in the castle. The house was mostly empty and neglected, but certain rooms showed signs of recent, simple use—by someone other than Gow. Here’s what we found:

“First: A large collection of loose diamonds—no settings, no jewelry. Odd, since family jewels are usually worn or displayed. Yet the Ogilvies seem to have kept theirs like pocket change.

“Second: Piles of snuff, scattered everywhere—on mantels, sideboards, pianos. No horn, no pouch. As if the earl couldn’t be bothered to keep it contained.

“Third: Tiny metal fragments—steel springs, microscopic gears—like parts from a broken clockwork toy.

“Fourth: Candles, used without candlesticks. They’re stuck in bottles because there’s nothing else to hold them.

“This is stranger than we expected,” continued Craven. “We came to solve the mystery of whether the earl lived here, died here, or was replaced by Gow. But even the most dramatic theories—murder, impersonation, secret burial—don’t explain why an elderly nobleman would spill snuff on the piano or carry candles without sticks. The core of the story we could guess. But the details… they defy reason. How does one connect snuff, diamonds, wax, and clockwork?”

“I see the connection,” said Father Brown calmly.

Both men stared.

“What a wild idea!” exclaimed Flambeau.

“It’s not true,” said Brown. “But you said no one could link those things. I just gave you a plausible link. The real truth is deeper.”

He paused, listening to the wind howling through the turrets.

“The late Earl of Glengyle was a thief. He lived a secret life as a burglar. He didn’t use candlesticks because he carried a lantern with short candles. The snuff? He used it like pepper—thrown in the face of pursuers to blind them. The diamonds and tiny steel wheels? Those are tools for cutting glass. Diamonds and fine steel wheels are the only instruments that can slice through pane glass.”

A broken pine branch slammed against the window behind them, like a mocking intruder. But no one turned.

“Diamonds and wheels,” murmured Craven. “Is that really the answer?”

“No,” said Brown. “I only offered it because you said no one could connect them. Ten false theories fit this place. But we want the truth. Are there more exhibits?”

Craven laughed. Flambeau rose and walked down the table.

“Fifth: A collection of pencil lead—not pencils, just the cores. Sixth: A bamboo stick, splintered at the top. Might be a weapon—but there’s no crime. Seventh: Old missals and Catholic images, defaced. The Ogilvies kept them from medieval times—family pride outweighed Puritanism. We included them because they’re strangely damaged.”

As the storm surged outside, plunging the room into darkness, Brown picked up one of the illuminated pages. He spoke before the light returned—his voice transformed, younger, sharper.

“Mr. Craven,” he said, “you have a warrant, don’t you? To examine that grave? Let’s go now. The sooner we get to the bottom of this, the better.”

“Now?” asked Craven, stunned.

“Yes. Because this isn’t snuff or stones. This is serious. These religious images weren’t just torn or scratched. They were carefully altered. Every instance of the name of God has been removed. Only the halo of the Child Jesus has been erased. That’s not vandalism. That’s ritual. I say we go to the grave. Now.”

“What do you mean?” demanded Craven.

“I mean,” said Brown, his voice rising above the gale, “that the devil himself may be sitting atop this castle, roaring like the Apocalypse. There’s black magic here.”

“Black magic?” whispered Flambeau, though he knew such things existed.

“Something damnable,” replied Brown impatiently. “How should I know? Perhaps snuff and bamboo are torture tools. Perhaps lunatics crave wax and filings. Maybe lead pencils contain a poison. Our best path to truth is up the hill—to the grave.”

His companions followed him without thinking. Craven held a hatchet, Flambeau the heavy spade, Brown the small gilt book from which God’s name had been torn.

The path to the churchyard was short but treacherous in the wind. The pines stretched endlessly, bending in unison, like a planet without purpose. Through the endless forest sang a high, ancient sorrow—the cry of forgotten gods lost in a pagan wilderness.

“You see,” said Brown quietly, “the early Scots worshipped demons. That’s why they embraced Puritanism so eagerly.”

“Then what about the snuff?” asked Flambeau, turning sharply.

“There’s one mark of all genuine religions,” replied Brown. “Materialism. Devil-worship is a real religion.”

They reached the grassy hilltop, where a shaky wire and timber fence marked the graveyard. At the foot of the grave grew tall thistles, silver in decay. Craven jumped when a seedball drifted past him.

Flambeau drove his spade into the wet earth. He paused, leaning on it.

“Go on,” said Brown gently. “We’re only seeking truth. What are you afraid of?”

“I’m afraid of finding it,” said Flambeau.

Craven spoke suddenly, trying to sound cheerful. “Why did he hide like that? Was he a leper?”

“Worse,” said Flambeau.

“And what could be worse than a leper?” asked Craven.

“I don’t imagine it,” said Flambeau.

He dug in silence for minutes. Then, in a choked voice: “I’m afraid he’s not the right shape.”

“Nor was that piece of paper,” said Brown calmly. “And we survived that.”

Flambeau dug harder, blind with effort. The storm cleared the clouds, revealing starlight. Finally, they unearthed a crude wooden coffin. Craven raised his axe, flinched at a thistle, then hacked open the lid.

“Bones,” he said. “But it’s a man.”

“Is he… all right?” asked Flambeau, his voice wavering.

“Seems so,” said Craven, bending over the skeleton. “Wait a minute.”

A tremor shook Flambeau. “And now I realize—why shouldn’t he be all right? What gets hold of a man on these cold mountains? It’s the repetition—forests upon forests, endless pine trees. Like the dream of an atheist. Trees and trees and more trees…”

“God!” cried Craven. “He hasn’t got a head!”

The others froze. Brown, for the first time, showed surprise.

“No head?” he repeated. “No head?”

Images flashed—headless babies, headless youths, a ghostly figure wandering the halls. But the thought dissolved. They stood numb, listening to the wind and the trees, like animals drained of thought.

“There are three headless men,” said Brown, “standing around this grave.”

Craven opened his mouth, then closed it, staring at the axe in his hand. He dropped it.

“Father,” said Flambeau, his voice childlike, “what do we do?”

Brown answered instantly, like a trigger pulled.

“Sleep!” he said. “We’ve come to the end. Do you know what sleep is? Every man who sleeps believes in God. It’s a sacrament—an act of faith, a food. We need one. Something terrible has fallen upon us—perhaps the worst thing possible.”

“What do you mean?” asked Craven.

Brown turned toward the castle.

“We’ve found the truth,” he said. “And the truth makes no sense.”

He walked ahead, stumbling slightly, and when they reached the castle, he collapsed onto his bed like a dog, exhausted.

Despite his praise of sleep, Brown woke before anyone else—except Gow. He was smoking a pipe, watching the gardener work in the kitchen garden. The storm had ended in rain; dawn brought a fresh, clear light. Gow seemed to be speaking, but when the detectives approached, he planted his spade sullenly, muttered about breakfast, and retreated into the kitchen.

“He’s a valuable man,” said Brown. “He grows excellent potatoes. Still…” He added, with calm charity, “…he has flaws. Look.” He stamped on a patch of soil. “I’m doubtful about this potato.”

“And why?” asked Craven, amused.

“Because Gow was doubtful too. He dug everywhere except here. There must be a fine potato beneath.”

Flambeau grabbed the spade and thrust it into the ground. It hit something hard—cold, round, and grinning.

“The Earl of Glengyle,” said Brown, sadly. He looked down at the skull.

And in that moment, the truth was laid bare—not in logic, but in silence.

The Wrong Shape

Certain roads leading north from London stretch far into the countryside, thinning out like ghostly echoes of city streets. They are dotted with shops, fields, inns, and private homes, arranged in a loose, irregular pattern. Amid this patchwork stands a house that draws the eye—though no one can quite say why. It’s long and low, painted white and pale green, with a veranda shaded by sun-blinds and small cupola-topped porches reminiscent of old-fashioned English country houses. Yet it feels oddly suited to a hotter climate—its whitewashed walls and broad awnings evoke visions of pugarees and palm trees. Perhaps it was built by someone who once lived in India.

Anyone passing this house might feel an inexplicable pull toward it—a sense that something significant has happened here, or is about to. And they would be right. This is the story of what really occurred during Whitsuntide in the year 18--, when the quiet façade of this peculiar house gave way to events both strange and terrible.

On the Thursday before Whit-Sunday, at half-past four in the afternoon, the front door opened, and two men stepped out: Father Brown, a priest from the modest parish of St. Mungo, and his tall French friend, Flambeau. The former smoked a large pipe; the latter, a tiny cigarette. Neither man was particularly striking, but their presence marked the beginning of something unusual.

Yet the house itself held more secrets than either of them could know. Its layout was shaped like a T—with a long horizontal arm facing the road and a short vertical tail extending behind. The main part of the house, two stories high, contained most of the rooms. The rear section, just one story, consisted of two long chambers: first, the study where Leonard Quinton, the celebrated poet, composed wild Oriental epics; and beyond it, a glass conservatory glowing with tropical blooms so vivid they seemed almost unreal. When the front door opened, passersby often paused, captivated by the view down the hall—like stepping into a dream scene of crimson stars and golden suns, alive yet distant.

Quinton had designed this effect carefully. He was a man obsessed with color—so much so that he often sacrificed form for sensation. His poetry reflected this: tales of burning skies, elephants painted purple, and jewels that blazed with ancient fire. He wrote not of truth or meaning, but of spectacle—of beauty so intense it verged on horror. His life mirrored his work: weak in temperament, addicted to opium, and haunted by Eastern mysticism. His wife, a strong, hard-working woman, disapproved of both his habits and his latest guest—an Indian fakir in white robes whom Quinton insisted on hosting as a spiritual guide.

It was from this household that Father Brown and Flambeau emerged, clearly relieved to be outside. Flambeau had known Quinton in their younger days in Paris, but their friendship had grown strained. He found the poet's lifestyle—opium, erotic verse, and spiritual pretensions—unworthy of a gentleman. As they stood on the doorstep, preparing to walk through the garden, the front gate burst open violently.

A young man tumbled up the steps, hat askew, necktie askew, cane clattering. He was flushed, unsteady, and desperate.

“I say,” he panted, “I must see Quinton. Has he gone?”

“Mr. Quinton is in,” said Father Brown calmly, cleaning his pipe. “But I don’t know if you can see him. The doctor is with him.”

The youth staggered into the hall just as the doctor emerged from the study, closing the door behind him and adjusting his gloves.

“See Mr. Quinton?” the doctor said coolly. “No, you can’t. In fact, you mustn’t. I’ve just given him his sleeping draught.”

“No, but look here,” the young man pleaded, grabbing the doctor’s coat. “I’m absolutely ruined. I need help—”

“It’s no good,” the doctor replied firmly, pushing him back. “When you can reverse the effects of a drug, then I’ll reconsider.” With that, he stepped out into the sunlight, followed by Father Brown and Flambeau.

The young man, Atkinson—Mrs. Quinton’s brother—stood frozen, dazed, watching them go.

“That was a solid lie,” the doctor chuckled. “Quinton doesn’t get his draught for another half-hour. But I’m not letting that little scoundrel bother him. He only wants money he’ll never repay. Still, she’s a fine woman—Mrs. Quinton. One of the best.”

“Yes,” said Father Brown quietly. “She’s a good woman.”

“I’ll wait in the garden until he leaves,” the doctor decided. “Then I’ll go in with the medicine. I locked the study door.”

“In that case,” said Flambeau, “let’s walk around the back by the end of the conservatory. There’s no entrance there, but it’s worth seeing.”

“True,” laughed the doctor. “And I might sneak a peek at my patient. He likes to lie on an ottoman at the far end, surrounded by those red poinsettias. Gives me the creeps.”

As they walked, Father Brown stopped abruptly and bent down. From the long grass, nearly hidden, he picked up a curious knife—crooked, ornately inlaid with colored stones and metals, its blade twisted like a serpent’s coil.

“What is this?” he asked, holding it with distaste.

“Oh, Quinton’s,” said the doctor casually. “He collects Chinese trinkets. Or maybe it belongs to that Hindoo he keeps around.”

“What Hindoo?” asked Father Brown, still studying the dagger.

“Oh, some Indian conjuror,” the doctor shrugged. “A fraud, of course.”

“You don’t believe in magic?” Father Brown asked without looking up.

“O crickey! Magic!” the doctor scoffed.

“It’s beautiful,” the priest murmured, “the colors are exquisite. But it’s the wrong shape.”

“For what?” Flambeau asked.

“For anything. It’s the wrong shape in the abstract. Don’t you ever feel that about Eastern art? The colors dazzle—but the forms are broken, deliberate. Like evil made visible. I’ve seen wickedness in a carpet.”

“Mon Dieu!” Flambeau laughed.

“The lines twist on purpose,” Father Brown continued, voice dropping. “Like serpents trying to escape. These are symbols in a language I don’t understand—but I know they mean harm.”

“What nonsense are you spouting?” the doctor laughed.

Flambeau spoke quietly. “The Father sometimes gets lost in mysticism. But I’ve never known him do so unless something dark is near.”

“Oh, rats,” said the doctor.

Father Brown held the knife out like a dangerous thing. “Look at it. It doesn’t point like a spear. It doesn’t sweep like a scythe. It doesn’t look like a weapon. It looks like an instrument of torture.”

“Well, since you hate it,” said Harris cheerfully, “we’ll return it. Have we reached the end of the conservatory yet?”

“This house is the wrong shape, if you like,” he added.

“You don’t understand,” said Father Brown. “The house is odd—almost laughable. But it’s not wrong. This knife… it’s *wrong*.”

They rounded the curved glass end of the conservatory—no doors, no windows—only clear glass. Inside, they saw Quinton lying on a sofa, half-asleep, book in hand. Pale, slight, with chestnut hair and a beard that softened his features. A familiar figure.

But their attention was drawn elsewhere.

Just outside the glass, standing motionless as stone, was a tall man in flowing white robes. His bare head and face glowed bronze in the setting sun. He stared through the glass at the sleeping poet.

“Who is that?” Father Brown breathed, stepping back.

“Oh, that Hindoo humbug,” growled the doctor. “What’s he doing here?”

“It looks like hypnotism,” said Flambeau, biting his moustache.

“Hypnotism?” the doctor scoffed. “Looks more like burglary.”

“We should speak to him,” Flambeau said, stepping forward. He approached the figure, towering over him, and bowed slightly. “Good evening, sir. Do you want anything?”

Slowly, like a ship turning, the man turned his head. His eyelids were closed—sealed shut as if in sleep.

“Thank you,” he said in perfect English. “I want nothing.”

Then, barely opening his eyes, he repeated, “I want nothing.” Then fully opened them—glowing, startling—and said again, “I want nothing.” Then he turned and drifted away into the gathering dusk.

“The Christian,” muttered Father Brown, “wants something.”

“What was he doing?” Flambeau asked, frowning.

“I’d like to talk later,” said Father Brown.

The light faded to deep red, shadows thickening. They walked along the side of the conservatory, and as they passed the corner between the study and the main house, they saw the white-robed figure slip from the shadows—this time, not alone.

Mrs. Quinton stepped into view—golden hair, square face, calm but stern.

“Good evening, Dr. Harris,” she said.

“Good evening, Mrs. Quinton,” the doctor replied warmly. “I’m going to give your husband his sleeping draught.”

“Yes,” she said clearly. “I think it’s time.” She smiled faintly and went inside.

“That woman is overworked,” said Father Brown. “The kind who does her duty for twenty years, then does something terrible.”

The doctor looked at him with new interest. “Did you study medicine?”

“You have to know the mind as well as the body,” the priest replied. “We priests know the body too.”

“Well,” said the doctor, “I’ll go give Quinton his dose.”

They reached the front door. As they turned into it, they saw the Indian for the third time—walking straight toward the front entrance, as if he had just come from the study. But the study door was locked.

They said nothing. The doctor stepped in, unlocked the door, then relocked it—just as Atkinson lunged forward.

The young man sat down, humming, poking things with his cane. Flambeau studied a Persian painting. Father Brown stared blankly at the door.

Four minutes passed. The door opened again. Atkinson sprang forward.

“Oh, I say, Quinton, I want—”

From the other end of the study came Quinton’s voice—half yawn, half laugh.

“Oh, I know what you want. Take it, and leave me alone. I’m writing a song about peacocks.”

A half-sovereign flew through the gap. Atkinson caught it deftly.

“So that’s settled,” said the doctor, locking the door again. “Poor Leonard can rest now.”

“He sounded cheerful,” said Father Brown.

He glanced around. Atkinson stood jingling his coin. In the twilight, the Indian sat rigid on a grassy bank, face toward the sunset.

“Where is Mrs. Quinton?” Father Brown asked suddenly.

“She’s upstairs,” said the doctor. “That’s her shadow on the blind.”

Father Brown looked up. Yes—it was her silhouette. He walked to a bench and sat.

Flambeau joined him. The doctor wandered off into the twilight.

“My father,” Flambeau said in French, “what is wrong?”

Father Brown was silent for a moment. Then: “Superstition is irreligious. But there’s something in the air here. That Indian—at least, partly.”

He watched the fakir, who swayed gently, like a tree in the wind. The storm was coming fast.

“When he spoke,” said Father Brown softly, “I saw his world. Three times he said, ‘I want nothing.’ First, it meant he was impenetrable—Asia does not reveal itself. Second, he meant he was self-sufficient—no God, no sin. Third, he said it with blazing eyes. Then I understood—he truly wanted nothing. Nothing was his desire. Annihilation… destruction… the end of all things…”

Two drops of rain fell. Flambeau jumped.

At the same moment, the doctor ran toward them, shouting.

He grabbed Atkinson by the collar. “Foul play! What did you do to him, you dog?”

Father Brown rose instantly, voice sharp and commanding.

“No fighting. We’re enough to hold anyone. What’s wrong, doctor?”

“Quinton—something’s wrong,” the doctor gasped, pale. “I saw him through the glass. He’s not as I left him.”

“Let us go in,” said Father Brown. “Leave Atkinson. I’ve watched him since Quinton spoke.”

“I’ll stay,” said Flambeau. “You go.”

They rushed to the study, unlocked the door, and entered—nearly tripping over the central table. On it lay a single sheet of paper. The doctor snatched it up, read it, handed it to Father Brown, and cried: “Good God—look at this!”

Father Brown read it three times.

The words: “I die by my own hand; yet I die murdered!”

In Quinton’s unmistakable handwriting.

Then Father Brown strode toward the conservatory, only to meet the doctor returning, face grim.

“He’s done it,” said Harris.

They crossed the garden of cactus and azalea, and found Quinton slumped over the ottoman, head hanging, curls brushing the floor. The crooked dagger was thrust into his side. His hand still gripped the hilt.

Outside, the storm broke—rain lashing the glass roof, darkness swallowing everything.

Father Brown held the paper close, reading it in the dim light. Then he raised it against the fading glow. Lightning flashed—white and sudden—the paper black against the brilliance.

Darkness returned, thunder rolling.

Then, from the dark, Father Brown’s voice: “Doctor, this paper is the wrong shape.”

“What do you mean?” the doctor demanded.

“It isn’t square,” said Brown. “One corner is cut off. What does it mean?”

“How should I know?” the doctor growled. “Shall we move him?”

“No,” said the priest. “Leave him as he lies. Send for the police.” He still studied the paper.

Back in the study, he paused by the table and picked up a small pair of nail scissors.

“Ah,” he said, with relief. “This is what he used. But yet—” He frowned, knitted his brows.

And the mystery deepened.

The Sins of Prince Saradine

When Flambeau took his month-long break from his office in Westminster, he did so not in a grand hotel or a seaside resort, but aboard a small sailing boat—so modest in size that it often served more as a rowboat than a vessel for the open water. He navigated the quiet rivers of eastern England, waters so narrow and winding that the boat seemed to glide through meadows and cornfields as if sailing across land. It was just large enough for two people, with only room for essentials. Flambeau had stocked it carefully, according to his own philosophy: tins of salmon for sustenance, loaded revolvers for protection, a bottle of brandy for emergencies—perhaps fainting—and a priest, in case death came uninvited. With this sparse cargo, he drifted down the gentle Norfolk streams, aiming eventually for the Broads, but enjoying every moment along the way—the overhanging gardens, the mirrored reflections of manor houses and villages, the quiet pools where he paused to fish, and the peaceful closeness of the riverbank.

Flambeau, like any true philosopher, had no real goal for his holiday—only an excuse. He carried a half-formed intention, one he treated lightly enough that failure wouldn’t spoil the trip, yet seriously enough that success would make it memorable. Years before, during his days as the most notorious thief in Paris, he had received many strange letters—some admiring, some accusing, even one expressing love. But one message stood out: a visiting card, postmarked from England, with a note written in French and green ink on the back: “If you ever retire and become respectable, come and see me. I want to meet you, for I have met all the other great men of my time. That trick of yours—getting one detective to arrest the other—was the most splendid scene in French history.” On the front, engraved in formal script: “Prince Saradine, Reed House, Reed Island, Norfolk.”

At the time, Flambeau had paid little attention to the prince beyond confirming that he had once been a dazzling figure in southern Italy—known for eloping with a married woman of high rank. Though such affairs were common among the elite, this one had taken a tragic turn: the husband, insulted, supposedly threw himself off a cliff in Sicily. The prince later lived in Vienna, then vanished into years of restless travel. When Flambeau himself left the world of crime and settled in England, he thought of reaching out to this forgotten exile. He didn’t expect to find him—or even know where Reed Island might be—but fate brought them together sooner than expected.

One night, they moored their boat under a bank thick with tall grass and short pollarded trees. After a long day of rowing, sleep came early, and by chance, they woke before dawn. Not quite dawn, though—a large, pale moon hung low in the sky, just beginning to set behind the reeds above them. The sky was a vivid violet-blue, bright but still dark. Both men suddenly remembered childhood dreams: the feeling of being surrounded by towering weeds like ancient woods. Standing upright, the daisies looked giant, the dandelions enormous. The riverbed dipped slightly beneath them, making them feel as though they were looking up from underground at a forest of flowers. “By Jove!” Flambeau exclaimed. “It’s like being in fairyland.”

Father Brown sat up sharply and made the sign of the cross. His sudden movement startled his companion, who asked, mildly, what was wrong.

“The people who wrote the medieval ballads,” replied the priest, “knew more about fairies than you do. Fairyland isn’t just full of nice things.”

“Oh, nonsense,” said Flambeau. “Only good things could happen under such a gentle moon. I say we push on and see what happens. We may never again see such a moon or such a mood.”

“All right,” said Father Brown. “I’m not saying it’s always wrong to enter fairyland. Just that it’s always dangerous.”

They moved slowly upstream as the sky lightened. The violet and gold of the moon faded into the colorless haze that precedes sunrise. By the time the first streaks of red and gold split the horizon, they were nearing a village or town perched on the riverbank ahead. Twilight had fallen, but everything was visible. The houses, with their long, sloping roofs, leaned over the water like cattle drinking. The dawn turned to daylight before they saw anyone. Then, finally, a calm, prosperous man in shirt sleeves appeared—his face round as the moon, his whiskers red and radiating like sunbeams—leaning on a post above the slow tide. Without thinking, Flambeau rose in the swaying boat and called out, asking if he knew Reed Island or Reed House.

The man smiled wider and pointed upstream. Flambeau nodded and steered onward without another word.

The boat wound through grassy bends and silent reaches until, around a sharp corner, they entered a quiet pool or lake, ringed with rushes. In the center floated a long, low island, upon which stood a house built of bamboo or tropical cane. Its walls were pale yellow, its roof darker brown or red, and the structure repeated itself in simple, almost monotonous lines. The morning breeze rustled the reeds and hummed through the ribbed walls like wind through a giant panpipe.

“By George!” shouted Flambeau. “Here it is! This must be Reed Island. And that’s Reed House. I swear that fat fellow with the whiskers was a fairy.”

“Perhaps,” said Father Brown quietly. “If he was, he was a bad one.”

Even as he spoke, Flambeau had already run the boat ashore through the rattling reeds. They stepped onto the island beside the strange, silent house.

The house faced away from the river and landing stage; the main entrance opened toward the long garden. The visitors walked along a narrow path under the low eaves, circling nearly three sides of the building. Through windows on different angles, they glimpsed the same long, well-lit room—panelled in light wood, filled with mirrors, and set for an elegant lunch. At last, they reached the front door, flanked by two turquoise flowerpots. It was opened by a butler—tall, thin, grey, and listless—who murmured that Prince Saradine was away but expected soon. The house was kept ready for him and his guests. When Flambeau showed the visiting card with the green ink message, the butler’s face flickered with life. He offered them a seat with stiff courtesy. “His Highness may arrive any minute,” he said. “He’d be distressed to miss anyone he invited. We always keep cold lunch ready. I’m sure he’d want you offered it.”

Curious, Flambeau accepted. The butler led them into the mirror-lined room. Nothing was remarkable except the unusual pattern of long windows alternating with long oblong mirrors, giving the space a dreamlike, airy quality—almost as if dining outdoors. A few quiet pictures hung in the corners: a large grey photograph of a young man in uniform, and a red chalk sketch of two boys with long hair. When Flambeau asked if the soldier was the prince, the butler replied curtly: “No. That’s Captain Stephen Saradine, the prince’s younger brother.” Then he fell silent.

After lunch, with coffee and liqueurs, they were shown the garden, library, and the housekeeper—a dark, dignified woman with a presence like a noble Madonna. She and the butler were the only remnants of the prince’s original household; the others were new, hired locally. She went by Mrs. Anthony, but her Italian accent hinted she’d adopted a British name. Mr. Paul, the butler, also had a faint foreign air, though he spoke English with polished precision—common among servants of cosmopolitan nobility.

Though beautiful, the place felt strangely luminous and sad. Hours stretched like days. The rooms were flooded with daylight, but it felt dead. Over everything, the sound of the river flowed—melancholy, constant, like a distant lament.

“We’ve taken a wrong turn,” said Father Brown, staring out at the silver flood and grey-green reeds. “But sometimes, being the right person in the wrong place can do good.”

Father Brown, usually quiet, had a gift for listening. In those endless hours, he drew out secrets from the household more deeply than Flambeau, despite saying little. The butler, Paul, was sullen and loyal to the prince, whom he claimed had been cruelly treated. The chief offender, he insisted, was the prince’s brother, Captain Stephen—a ne’er-do-well who had drained his brother of fortune and forced him into exile. Paul refused to say more, clearly biased.

Mrs. Anthony, however, was more revealing—though not without bitterness. As the men studied the red sketch of the two boys, she swept in on business. The mirrors reflected her image four or five times. Father Brown stopped mid-sentence. Flambeau, close to the drawing, said aloud, “The brothers Saradine, I suppose. Both look innocent enough. Hard to tell which is good and which is bad.” Then, realizing she was there, he changed the subject and stepped outside.

But Father Brown remained, gazing at the sketch. Mrs. Anthony stared at him.

She had large, sorrowful eyes and a face glowing with painful curiosity—as if questioning his identity. Perhaps his clerical clothes stirred memories of confession, or perhaps she sensed he knew more than he let on. In a low voice, like a conspirator, she whispered, “Your friend is right in one way. It would be hard to pick the good brother. Oh, it would be hard—very hard—to pick the good one.”

“I don’t understand,” said Brown, turning to leave.

She stepped closer, brows furrowed, body tensed like a bull lowering its head.

“There isn’t a good one,” she hissed. “Captain Stephen took money, yes—that’s bad. But I don’t think the prince was much better. Giving it away? That wasn’t goodness. The captain’s not the only one with something against him.”

A light sparked in the priest’s mind. He silently formed the word *blackmail*. Before he could speak, she whirled around, pale as death. The door opened silently. Paul stood there, ghost-like. The mirrors multiplied him—five Pauls entering at once.

“His Highness has arrived,” he said.

Outside the first window, a man passed, crossing the glass like a figure on stage. Then the second window—again, the same profile, the same stride. The man was tall and alert, but his hair was white, his skin an odd ivory yellow. He had a Roman nose, long lean cheeks masked by moustache and beard—the moustache darker, giving a theatrical effect. He wore a white top hat, an orchid, a yellow waistcoat, yellow gloves, which he swung as he walked. At the front door, Paul opened it, and the newcomer said cheerfully, “Well, you see I’ve come.” Paul bowed, spoke softly, then announced, “Everything is at your disposal.”

The glove-flapping Prince Saradine entered, laughing. Five princes seemed to walk in from five doors.

He placed his hat and gloves on the table and shook hands warmly.

“Delighted to see you here, Mr. Flambeau,” he said. “I know you by reputation—if that’s not too bold.”

“Not at all,” answered Flambeau, smiling. “I’m not easily offended. Very few reputations are built on pure virtue.”

The prince glanced sharply at him, then laughed and offered chairs.

“Pleasant little place,” he said, coolly. “Not much to do, I fear—but the fishing is excellent.”

Father Brown watched him with a baby’s solemn gaze. He studied the white curls, yellow face, slim figure—foppish, perhaps, like a stage actor. But something else nagged at him—the shape of the face. It felt familiar. Then he recalled the mirrors and dismissed it as illusion.

Prince Saradine charmed both guests. He guided Flambeau to the best fishing spot, returning swiftly to join Brown in the library. He spoke several languages—mostly slang—and told stories of opium dens, bushrangers, brigands. He seemed worldly, brilliant, yet restless. His eye was wild, his manner nervous. He relied entirely on Paul, who managed the household like a steward or chamberlain. Paul dined privately, consulted with the prince formally, and commanded the staff. The housekeeper was a shadow compared to him. Brown heard nothing more of the blackmail rumors. But the prince’s secrecy made the story plausible.

Later, as evening fell, they returned to the mirror-room. Yellow light touched the water and willow banks. A bittern called in the distance. The same sense of eerie sadness returned. “I wish Flambeau were back,” muttered Brown.

“Do you believe in doom?” asked the prince suddenly.

“No,” said Brown. “I believe in Doomsday.”

The prince turned, his face in shadow against the sunset. “What do you mean?”

“I mean we’re on the wrong side of the tapestry,” said Brown. “The events here don’t seem to mean anything. But somewhere else—somewhere else—retribution will fall on the true offender. Here, it often falls on the wrong person.”

The prince made a strange, animal-like noise. His eyes shone oddly in the dimness. A new thought struck Brown: Was the prince truly sane? He repeated, “The wrong person… the wrong person…” far too many times for mere conversation.

Then Brown noticed the mirror: the door stood open, and Paul stood there, silent and pale.

“I thought it better to announce at once,” said Paul, with the stiffness of a family lawyer. “A boat with six men has arrived at the landing-stage. A gentleman sits in the stern.”

“A boat?” repeated the prince. “A gentleman?” He rose.

Silence followed, broken only by the bird’s call. Then, as before, a new figure passed the windows—taller, sharper, with an aquiline profile. But unlike Saradine’s flashy style, this man wore an old-fashioned black hat, a shabby blue coat, a red waistcoat, coarse white trousers—clothes from another age. His face was olive, young, clean-shaven, with a chin like Napoleon’s. He looked sincere, almost terrifyingly so.

“The deuce!” said the prince. He grabbed his white hat and rushed to the door.

On the lawn stood the newcomer and his six men, arranged like soldiers. The boatmen had pulled the craft high onto shore, oars held upright like spears. Swarthy, some with earrings. One stood forward, carrying a large black case.

“Your name,” said the young man, “is Saradine?”

The prince nodded, casually.

“My name is Antonelli,” said the young man.

“Antonelli,” repeated the prince. “Somehow I remember that name.”

“Allow me to introduce myself,” said the Italian.

With his left hand, he removed his hat politely. With his right, he struck the prince across the face with a force that sent the white hat rolling down the steps and rocked one of the blue pots.

The prince lunged, but the young man twisted away with a strange politeness.

“That is all right,” he panted, in halting English. “I have insulted. I will give satisfaction. Marco, open the case.”

The Hammer of God

Perched atop a steep hill, the village of Bohun Beacon seemed to rise like a solitary mountain, its church spire piercing the sky like a crown. At the base of the church stood the smithy—always glowing with fire, littered with hammers and iron scraps—and across a rough crossroads lay “The Blue Boar,” the village’s only inn. It was here, at dawn’s first silver light, that two brothers met on the street—one beginning his day, the other ending it.

The Reverend and Honorable Wilfred Bohun, devout and solemn, walked toward the church for early prayer. His elder brother, Colonel Norman Bohun, sat outside the inn in evening dress, sipping what could be called either his last drink of Tuesday or his first of Wednesday—no one cared much which. The colonel didn’t care much either.

The Bohuns were among the few aristocratic families whose lineage stretched back into the Middle Ages; their banner had once flown over the Holy Land. But noble blood did not guarantee noble character. Aristocrats, more often than not, followed fashion rather than tradition. The Bohuns had been rakes under Queen Anne, dandies under Victoria. By the modern age, they’d decayed into drunkenness and vanity, and whispers of madness began to follow them. Colonel Bohun embodied this decline—tall, striking, with startling yellow hair and deep-set blue eyes that seemed almost black. His face bore a permanent sneer, etched by furrows from nose to jaw, and he wore a pale yellow coat so odd it looked like a dressing gown. On his head perched a wide-brimmed green hat, likely some exotic souvenir picked up without thought. He took pride in wearing such absurd clothes—and in making them look stylish.

His brother, the curate, shared the same golden hair and refined features, but dressed in plain black, clean-shaven, and deeply serious. Wilfred lived for religion—or so it seemed. Yet some, especially the blacksmith (a Presbyterian), suspected it was less devotion and more a love of Gothic beauty that drew him to the church. They said he haunted its crypts, galleries, even the belfry—not to worship, but to commune with silence and shadow. Still, his piety was real enough. And now, as he approached the church through the smithy yard, he paused when he saw his brother’s intense gaze fixed in the same direction.

He assumed the colonel was drawn to the church—but then realized the only alternative was the smithy. The blacksmith, Barnes, was known to have a beautiful wife, and rumors had circulated about her. Wilfred cast a suspicious glance toward the shop. The colonel rose, laughing.

“Good morning, Wilfred,” he said. “Like a good landlord, I’m watching over my people. I’m going to call on the blacksmith.”

“The blacksmith is out,” Wilfred replied flatly. “He’s at Greenford.”

“I know,” the colonel said, still grinning. “That’s why I’m calling on him.”

Wilfred frowned. “Norman, are you ever afraid of thunderbolts?”

“What do you mean?” asked the colonel. “Is your hobby meteorology?”

“I mean,” Wilfred said, not looking up, “do you ever think God might strike you down in the street?”

“Oh,” the colonel replied, “your hobby’s folk-lore.”

“And yours,” Wilfred shot back, stung, “is blasphemy. But if you donー’t fear God, you should fear man.”

The colonel raised an eyebrow. “Fear man?”

“Yes,” Wilfred said firmly. “Barnes is the strongest man within forty miles. You’re no coward, but he could throw you over that wall.”

The colonel’s expression darkened for a moment—then broke into laughter, revealing sharp, dog-like teeth beneath his thick mustache. “Then,” he said lightly, “it was wise for the last of the Bohuns to come out partly armored.”

With that, he removed the green hat, revealing it was lined with steel—a light helmet torn from a war trophy in the family hall. Wilfred recognized it instantly.

“It was the nearest hat,” the colonel said airily. “Always the nearest hat—and the nearest woman.”

“The blacksmith is away,” Wilfred said quietly, turning to enter the church, crossing himself as if casting off something foul. He sought refuge in the cool shadows of the Gothic nave, hoping to cleanse his mind. But fate had other plans.

As he stepped inside, he saw a figure rise from prayer near the altar. It was Mad Joe—the village idiot, nephew of the blacksmith. A dark, slouching young man with a pale face, dark hair, and mouth perpetually open, Joe was known for nothing except his silence. Yet here he was, kneeling in the church—something no one had ever seen before. What prayers had he offered? Wilfred couldn’t imagine.

He watched as Joe left the church, only to see his brother laugh and toss coins into Joe’s open mouth, aiming carefully as if playing a game.

The sight—this grotesque blend of cruelty and idiocy—shook Wilfred deeply. He climbed to the gallery, seeking solace beneath a stained-glass window depicting an angel holding lilies. There, amid the soft blues and silvers, he tried to forget the world below.

Half an hour later, Gibbs, the village cobbler, found him. Gibbs was an atheist, and his presence in church was unusual. He came breathless, urgent.

“You must excuse me, sir,” he whispered hoarsely, “but we thought you should know at once. Something dreadful has happened. Your brother—”

Wilfred clenched his hands. “What devilry has he done now?”

“Sir,” Gibbs coughed, “I’m afraid he’s done nothing. He won’t do anything. He’s dead.”

The curate rushed downstairs, following Gibbs through a winding stairway to the yard. There, beneath the morning sun, lay a scene of horror. A man in evening dress sprawled face-down in the dirt, skull shattered like glass. Blood fanned out in a starburst pattern. Wilfred knew it was his brother—down to the rings on his fingers.

“My brother is dead,” he gasped. “What does this mean? What is this horror?”

Silence fell. Then Gibbs spoke: “Plenty of horror, sir. But not much mystery.”

“What do you mean?” Wilfred demanded.

“Only one man in forty miles could have struck such a blow,” Gibbs said. “And he had the most reason to.”

The doctor, a tall man with a black beard, interrupted nervously. “We mustn’t jump to conclusions. But I can confirm what Mr. Gibbs says. That blow—no ordinary man could have done it. Fragments of bone were driven into the body and ground like bullets into mud. It was the hand of a giant.”

He paused, adjusting his spectacles. “The good news is—it clears most suspects. If any normal man were accused, he’d be acquitted as easily as a child accused of stealing Nelson’s column.”

“That’s what I say,” Gibbs insisted. “Only one man could’ve done it—and he’d have done it. Where’s Simeon Barnes?”

“He’s at Greenford,” Wilfred said.

“More likely in France,” muttered Gibbs.

“No,” said a quiet voice. “He’s coming up the road now.”

All turned. From the distant path, walking slowly with a hammer on his shoulder, came Simeon Barnes, the blacksmith. A massive man with deep, dark eyes and a thick beard, he moved with calm authority. Behind him walked two men from Greenford.

“My God!” exclaimed Gibbs. “There’s the hammer he used!”

“No,” said the inspector, a sensible-looking man with a sandy mustache. “That hammer is over there, by the church wall. We’ve left everything as it was.”

They all turned. The hammer was small, nearly invisible among the others. But on its edge—blood and yellow hair.

The short priest, Father Brown, stepped forward and studied it silently.

“Mr. Gibbs was wrong,” he said quietly. “There *is* a mystery. Why would such a man use such a small hammer for such a blow?”

Gibbs waved it off. “Forget the hammer! What do we do with Barnes?”

“Leave him alone,” Father Brown said calmly. “He’s coming of his own accord. Those two men with him are honest folks from Greenford—they’re here about the revival meeting.”

Indeed, the smith rounded the corner and entered his yard. He stopped. The hammer dropped from his hand.

The inspector stepped forward. “I won’t ask if you know anything. But I must arrest you in the King’s name—for the murder of Colonel Norman Bohun.”

“Not bound to say anything,” Gibbs chimed in, thrilled by the legal formality. “They have to prove it. They haven’t even proven it’s the colonel.”

“That won’t wash,” the doctor murmured to Father Brown. “I was his physician. I know his hands—he had identical second and third fingers. That’s the colonel.”

The blacksmith stared at the corpse, then said calmly: “If Colonel Bohun is dead… then he’s damned.”

“Don’t say anything!” Gibbs cried, dancing with glee. “You’re free to stay silent!”

The smith turned, his voice low and fierce. “It’s well for you infidels to dodge like foxes—because the world’s law favors you. But God guards His own. You’ll see today.”

He pointed at the body. “When did this dog die in his sins?”

“Moderate your language,” the doctor said.

“Moderate the Bible’s language,” the smith replied, “and I’ll moderate mine. When did he die?”

“I saw him alive at six this morning,” Wilfred said.

“God is good,” the smith said. “I have no objection to being arrested. But you may regret arresting me. I’ll leave court without stain. You may not leave with your career intact.”

The inspector looked at him anew. The smith continued: “Two men from Greenford will swear I was in the mission committee room all night—from midnight to dawn. Twenty people in Greenford saw me. If I were a heathen, I’d let you fail. But as a Christian, I give you your chance—will you hear my alibi now or in court?”

The inspector hesitated. “Of course—I’d be glad to clear you now.”

The smith returned to his friends. Each gave testimony that no one doubted. The evidence was solid. Simeon Barnes was innocent.

A heavy silence settled. Wilfred broke it, turning to Father Brown: “You seem very interested in that hammer, Father.”

“Yes,” said Father Brown. “Why is it so small?”

The doctor spun around. “By George—that’s true! Who would pick a tiny hammer when ten larger ones are nearby?”

He leaned close to Wilfred. “Only someone who can’t lift a big one. It’s not courage—it’s strength. A bold woman could kill with a light hammer. She couldn’t lift a heavy one.”

Wilfred stared. Father Brown listened, head tilted slightly, truly curious.

The doctor went on: “These fools always assume the husband hates the lover. But nine times out of ten, it’s the wife. Look at her!”

He gestured toward the red-haired woman on the bench. Her tears had dried. Her eyes glowed with a strange, electric fury.

Wilfred shuddered. Then Father Brown, brushing ash from his sleeve, said: “You’re like many doctors—your mental science is clever. But your physical science is impossible. No woman could smash a skull like that. And notice—there’s an iron helmet scattered like glass. Look at her arms.”

Silence again.

The doctor sighed. “Well, maybe I’m wrong. But no man would pick a small hammer if he could use a big one.”

Wilfred clutched his head. Then dropped his hands. “That’s it—the word I needed. ‘No man but an idiot would pick the small hammer.’”

The doctor blinked. “Well?”

“Well,” Wilfred said, trembling, “no man but an idiot did.”

Everyone froze.

“The lunatic,” Wilfred said, voice rising. “Mad Joe. He was praying in the church this morning. A madman’s prayers—maybe upside down. Maybe he prayed before killing. I saw him with my brother. My brother was mocking him.”

“By Jove!” the doctor said. “This makes sense. But how—?”

Wilfred was feverish. “Don’t you see? It explains both riddles—the small hammer and the great blow. The smith could’ve made the blow, but wouldn’t choose the small hammer. The wife would pick the small hammer, but couldn’t deliver the blow. But a madman—yes. He might pick any hammer. And in a fit of rage, a lunatic can have the strength of ten men.”

The doctor exhaled. “By golly—you might be right.”

Father Brown had watched him closely. Now, with quiet respect, he said: “Mr. Bohun, your theory holds water. It’s unassailable. But I must tell you—on my knowledge—it’s not the truth.”

And with that, he walked away, staring again at the hammer.

The doctor muttered, “That fellow knows more than he lets on. Popish priests are deucedly sly.”

“No,” Wilfred said, exhausted. “It was the lunatic. It was the lunatic.”

The group drifted apart. Then voices came from the other side. The blacksmith was speaking loudly:

“I hope I’ve convinced you, Mr. Inspector. I’m strong, yes—but I couldn’t have thrown my hammer from Greenford. It hasn’t wings to fly half a mile over hedges.”

The inspector laughed. “No, you’re cleared. Though it’s one of the oddest coincidences I’ve seen. Now, help us find a man as big and strong as you. You might be useful—just to hold him. Any idea who it could be?”

And the silence returned—thick, expectant, waiting for the next clue.

The Eye of Apollo

The Thames, ever shifting in mood and hue, shimmered under the midday sun as it climbed above Westminster. Two men crossed Westminster Bridge: one tall, one short—so different in stature they might have been likened to the proud clock tower of Parliament and the humble, hunched roofline of Westminster Abbey. The shorter man wore clerical robes; he was Father Brown, a priest from St. Francis Xavier’s Church in Camberwell, returning from a visit to a dying parishioner. The taller was M. Hercule Flambeau, private detective, on his way to his new office in a modern apartment building facing the Abbey.

The building was a stark, American-style skyscraper—imposing, sleek, and still incomplete. Its elevators and telephones were polished and efficient, but few tenants had moved in. Only three offices were occupied: one just above Flambeau’s, one just below, and the rest remained empty. Yet what caught the eye more than any architectural detail was a massive golden sculpture affixed to the outside of the floor directly above Flambeau’s office—a giant, radiant human eye, glowing with rays of gold, taking up the space of two or three windows.

“What on earth is that?” asked Father Brown, halting in surprise.

“Oh, a new religion,” Flambeau replied, chuckling. “One of those movements that claim you’re not really sick because you never were ill to begin with. Sounds like Christian Science, perhaps. A fellow calling himself Kalon—though I doubt that’s his real name—has taken the flat above me. He calls himself the New Priest of Apollo and worships the sun.”

“Let him be careful,” said Father Brown. “The sun was the cruelest of all gods. But what does that monstrous eye mean?”

“It’s their philosophy,” Flambeau explained. “They believe a person can endure anything if their mind is perfectly steady. Their symbols are the sun and the open eye—the idea being that a truly healthy person could look straight into the sun without blinking.”

“If a man were truly healthy,” Father Brown mused, “he wouldn’t feel the need to stare at it.”

“Well, that’s about all I know,” Flambeau said, shrugging. “They claim their faith can cure every physical illness.”

“And what about the one spiritual disease?” Father Brown asked, his voice thoughtful.

“What’s that?” Flambeau smiled.

“Oh,” said the priest, “the belief that one is completely well.”

Flambeau was far more intrigued by the office beneath him than the flamboyant temple above. He was a clear-minded Southerner, skeptical of religious fads—especially ones wrapped in bright, hollow ideals. But people fascinated him, especially when they were striking. And the women downstairs were certainly that.

The office belonged to two sisters—Pauline and Joan Stacey. Both were slight and dark-haired, but Pauline stood out: tall, sharp-featured, with an aquiline profile and eyes like steel—bright, intense, unyielding. She seemed carved from some hard, clean metal, cutting through life with precision. Her younger sister, Joan, was quieter, paler, almost shadow-like—like a faint echo of her elder sibling. They both dressed in practical black, with stiff collars and cuffs, typical of the many brisk, business-minded women of London. But their outward appearance masked something deeper.

Pauline Stacey was, in truth, an heiress—possessor of ancient lands, a family crest, and immense wealth. She’d grown up in grand country houses, but a fierce modern spirit had driven her to reject aristocratic idleness. She didn’t renounce her fortune—she kept it, insisting it should be used for practical social good. Part went into her typewriting business, which she envisioned as a model for women’s work. The rest funded various causes promoting female advancement. Whether Joan shared her sister’s ideals was uncertain—but she followed Pauline with quiet devotion, a loyalty tinged with sorrow, more touching than her sister’s rigid confidence. For Pauline denied tragedy altogether; she saw no room for weakness in the world.

Her manner was sharp, impatient, and exacting. Once, Flambeau entered her office to find her having thrown her sister’s spectacles onto the floor and crushed them underfoot. She raged against “sickly medical notions” and the very idea of needing artificial aids. “Do we wear wooden legs or false hair?” she snapped, her eyes blazing. “No! We are born whole! People only think they need help because they’ve been taught fear, not strength. Just as nurses tell children not to look at the sun, so they grow afraid to see it. But why should there be one star among the stars I cannot gaze upon? The sun is not my master. I will look at it whenever I please!”

Flambeau, bewildered, asked bluntly: “But isn’t a lift also a machine? Why is using a lift noble, but wearing glasses weak?”

“That’s entirely different!” Pauline declared, scornful. “Engines and motors show human power—our mastery over distance and time. That’s science, true science. But these doctor-made props? They’re signs of cowardice. Doctors attach limbs as if we were born broken. But I was born free! We only need such things because we’ve been trained to fear, not to conquer. And just as children blink at the sun, so we blink at our own strength.”

Flambeau bowed slightly. “Then your eyes,” he said, “will blind the sun.” He found her intensity amusing, even admirable—and her defiance oddly thrilling. But as he walked up to his own floor, he sighed and whistled softly. “So she’s fallen into the hands of that conjurer upstairs with his golden eye.” Though he cared little for Kalon’s cult, he knew of the man’s obsession with staring at the sun.

Soon, Flambeau realized the connection between the floors above and below was growing stronger. Kalon, the man who claimed to be the new priest of Apollo, was a magnificent figure—tall, broad-shouldered, with a golden beard and piercing blue eyes. His presence was commanding, even regal. He looked like a Norse king, yet there was a depth of intellect and spirituality behind his strength. Despite his surroundings—a plain office in a modern building, a clerk in a cheap suit, a brass nameplate, and the gilded eye displayed like a signboard—he radiated an undeniable force. When he stepped into the room, one felt the presence of greatness—even if it was a charlatan’s greatness.

He wore simple linen clothes during the day, but when he performed his daily rituals, he donned white robes and a golden circlet. Three times a day, he stepped onto his balcony to greet the sun: at dawn, at noon, and at dusk. It was at noon, when the bells of Parliament rang and the city hummed, that Father Brown first saw him.

Flambeau, already tired of the spectacle, hurried into the building without waiting for his friend. But Father Brown stopped, drawn by ritual—or perhaps by absurdity. He stared upward as Kalon stood on the balcony, arms raised, reciting his solar litany. His voice carried across the street, deep and resonant. His eyes were fixed on the sun, unblinking, unwavering. It was impossible to say whether he saw anything else—certainly not the small, round-faced priest below, watching with squinting eyes.

“O Sun,” Kalon called, “O star too great to dwell among the stars! O fountain flowing quietly in the void called space. White Father of all pure things—white flames, white flowers, white peaks. Father, purer than your purest child, primal innocence, into whose peace—”

A sudden crash shattered the silence—like a rocket exploding backward—followed by shrieking voices. Five figures burst into the gate, three rushed out. For a moment, the air crackled with shock. No one knew what had happened, but it felt like disaster.

Two figures remained motionless: the fair priest of Apollo on his balcony, and the unassuming priest of Christ below.

Flambeau emerged from the building, towering over the crowd, shouting orders. “Someone get a surgeon!” Then, turning back, he led Father Brown into the dark entrance. Even as they passed through the throng, the prophet’s voice still echoed from above, praising the sun.

Inside, they found the elevator shaft—empty. But something had fallen down it. Something that shouldn’t have.

For four minutes, Flambeau had watched. Now he saw the body of Pauline Stacey—brained, bleeding, dead. There was no doubt in his mind. He hadn’t liked her exactly, nor disliked her—not fully. But she had been real. And now, suddenly, she was gone. The details—the way she’d spoken, the way she’d walked—now stung with unbearable poignancy. A beautiful, defiant woman, dashed to death in a lift shaft. Suicide? Impossible. Murder? But who could have done it? In this half-empty building?

He turned sharply. “Where’s Kalon?”

A calm voice answered: “He’s been on his balcony for fifteen minutes. Worshipping the sun.”

Flambeau froze. Then, turning to Father Brown, he asked, “If he’s been up there all along… who could have done it?”

Father Brown nodded slowly. “Perhaps we should go upstairs and check. We’ve half an hour before the police arrive.”

Leaving the body to the surgeons, Flambeau raced up to the Staceys’ office—empty. Then to his own—also empty. He returned, pale.

“Her sister,” he said grimly, “seems to have gone out for a walk.”

Father Brown smiled faintly. “Or perhaps she went up to Kalon’s office. Let’s check. Then we’ll talk in your office—no, wait,” he added, as if recalling something. “In theirs. Downstairs.”

Flambeau blinked but followed. They descended to the Staceys’ flat, where Father Brown took a large red leather chair near the entrance, facing the stairs. He waited.

Four minutes passed. Then three figures appeared—solemn, silent.

First came Joan Stacey, her face drawn, her hair streaked with grey. She walked straight to her desk, arranging papers with brisk efficiency. The act alone restored a sense of order. If she were guilty, she was coldly composed.

Father Brown watched her for a moment, then turned to Kalon.

“Prophet,” he said, “I’d like to hear about your religion.”

Kalon inclined his head. “I’d be honored. But I’m not sure I understand.”

“It’s like this,” Father Brown said, candidly. “We believe that if a man has flawed principles, it’s partly his fault. But we also distinguish between someone who knowingly disrespects conscience and someone whose conscience is clouded by lies. Now—do you believe murder is wrong at all?”

“Is this an accusation?” Kalon asked, calm.

“No,” Father Brown replied gently. “It’s the defense.”

Silence fell. Then Kalon rose—slowly, deliberately—like the sun itself rising. His presence filled the room. His robes seemed to drape the walls in classical splendor; his gesture expanded the space. The small, dark figure of the priest seemed almost an intrusion.

“We meet at last, Caiaphas,” Kalon said. “Your church and mine are the only truths on earth. I worship the sun—you worship its dimming. You serve the dying God; I serve the living. Your work is suspicion, slander—your church is a black police force, hunting confessions through treachery or torture. You convict men of guilt; I convict them of innocence. You preach sin; I preach virtue.”

He paused, then continued, voice firm and commanding. “Reader of evil, let me finish. Not even your judgment matters to me. The things you call disgrace—hanging, prison—are no more than a monster in a child’s book to a man grown. You offered the defense—I’ll offer the prosecution. One thing can be said against me—and I’ll say it myself. Pauline Stacey was my love, my bride—not in your narrow sense, but by a law purer than yours. We walked a world beyond yours, treading crystal palaces while you crawled through brick tunnels. Yes, I admit: love often breeds hatred. So there’s your first charge. But the second is stronger—and I give it freely. Not only did Pauline love me, but this morning, before she died, she wrote a will leaving me and my church half a million pounds. Where are the handcuffs? Do you think I care what you do to me? Prison? It’s just waiting for her at a station. The gallows? It’s rushing to her in a car.”

His words struck with the force of thunder. Flambeau and Joan stared, awestruck. Father Brown sat with his eyes downcast, a deep crease across his brow—as if wounded.

Kalon leaned against the mantel. “That is the full case against me. Now, let me destroy it. I did not commit this crime. Pauline Stacey fell from this floor at 12:05. A hundred witnesses will testify I was on my balcony from just before noon until 12:15—my usual prayer time. My clerk, a respectable young man from Clapham, will swear he sat in my outer office all morning, no one passing through. I arrived ten minutes early—fifteen before the accident. I did not leave. My alibi is perfect. You can subpoena half Westminster. Put away the handcuffs. The case is closed.”

Then, with a final, sweeping gesture, he added: “And to clear the air forever—let me tell you how I believe she died. You may blame me, or my beliefs—but not me personally. It is known that certain adepts throughout history have mastered levitation—floating above the ground by willpower alone. This is part of our occult wisdom. Pauline was impulsive, ambitious. She believed herself deeply initiated. She often told me, as we rode the lift, that if one’s will were strong enough, one could float down like a feather. I believe she tried it—in an ecstasy of noble thought. Her will failed. Gravity took its revenge. That is the truth—sad, perhaps presumptuous, but not criminal. Call it suicide. I will always call it heroic failure—advancing science, climbing toward heaven.”

Flambeau had never seen Father Brown so defeated. He sat, eyes lowered, brow furrowed, as if ashamed. The prophet’s words had stirred something profound—a sense that here stood a man of bold freedom, confronting a world of petty suspicion.

At last, Father Brown spoke, blinking as if in pain. “Well, if that’s so, sir, then all you need do is take that testamentary paper and go. I wonder—where did the poor lady leave it?”

The Sign of the Broken Sword

The forest stood like a thousand grey arms reaching into the sky, each branch a silver finger against a slate-blue night. Stars glittered cold and sharp, like shards of ice scattered across the heavens. A brittle frost clung to every surface, turning the thick woods into a silent, frozen world. The hollows between the trees were deep and black, resembling the gaping mouths of some northern hell—cold beyond measure. Even the square stone church tower seemed out of place, more like a relic of ancient heathenism than a house of worship, as if it had been carved from the rocks of Iceland itself. It was not a night for wandering, especially not through a graveyard. Yet there was something about this place that demanded attention.

Rising abruptly from the ash-colored expanse of woodland was a small hill, crowned with a patch of turf that looked ghostly pale under the starlight. Most of the graves sloped gently, and the path leading to the church climbed steeply, like a staircase carved into the earth. At the summit, in the only flat space, stood the monument that gave the place its name. It stood apart from the plain, unmarked stones around it—a masterpiece by one of Europe’s greatest sculptors. But no one remembered the artist; all eyes rested on the man he had immortalized.

By the faint glow of the stars, the figure could be seen: a soldier lying at rest, hands folded in eternal reverence, his head resting upon a rifle. His face, weathered and noble, bore a beard in the old-fashioned style of Colonel Newcome. Though the uniform was rendered in just a few bold strokes, it was unmistakably modern. At his side lay a sword—its tip broken off—and beside it, a Bible. In summer, tourists from America and London would come in carriages to admire the tomb, but even then they felt uneasy beneath the vast silence of the forest, as though the land itself refused to speak. Now, in the dead of winter, with the air sharp and still, it felt as if the stars alone witnessed whatever might happen here.

Yet, in that quiet, a wooden gate groaned open. Two figures, both dressed in black, climbed the narrow path toward the grave. The light was so weak that nothing could be made out except their silhouettes—one tall and broad-shouldered, the other small and slight. They approached the tomb and stood before it in silence. No living thing stirred within miles. One might have wondered, in that stillness, whether they were men at all.

Then, after a long pause, the smaller man spoke.

“Where does a wise man hide a pebble?”

The taller man answered softly: “On the beach.”

The little man nodded. After a moment, he asked again: “Where does a wise man hide a leaf?”

“In the forest,” came the reply.

Another silence fell. Then the tall man said: “So when a wise man has a real diamond to hide, he hides it among false ones?”

“No, no,” laughed the small man. “Let bygones be bygones.”

He stamped his feet to warm them and then said: “I’m not thinking of that. I’m thinking of something else—something odd. Strike a match, will you?”

The big man fumbled in his pocket. A spark flew, and a sudden flare lit the entire face of the monument. Carved in dark letters were the words many had read with reverence:

*“Sacred to the Memory of General Sir Arthur St. Clare, Hero and Martyr, who Always Vanquished his Enemies and Always Spared Them, and Was Treacherously Slain by Them At Last. May God in Whom he Trusted both Reward and Revenge him.”*

The match burned his fingers, sputtered, and fell. He reached for another, but the small man stopped him.

“That’s enough, Flambeau,” he said. “I saw what I needed—or rather, I didn’t see what I didn’t want. Now we must walk a mile and a half to the next inn. I’ll tell you everything then. Heaven knows, a man needs fire and ale to tell such a tale.”

They descended the path, closed the gate behind them, and began walking down the frozen road through the forest. For a quarter-mile, neither spoke. Then the small man broke the quiet.

“Yes,” he said. “A wise man hides a pebble on the beach. But what does he do if there is no beach? Do you know anything about General St. Clare?”

“I know nothing of English generals,” replied the large man, laughing. “Though I know a bit about English policemen. Still, you’ve dragged me to every shrine of this fellow. One would think he was buried in six places. I’ve seen a memorial in Westminster Abbey. There’s a statue of him on the Embankment. Medallions in the streets where he was born and lived. And now you bring me here, after dark, to his actual tomb. I’m beginning to tire of his grandeur—especially since I don’t know who he was. What are you searching for in all these tombs and statues?”

“I’m looking for one word,” said the priest. “A word that isn’t there.”

“Well?” asked Flambeau. “Are you going to tell me?”

“I must divide it into two parts,” said Father Brown. “First, what everyone knows. Then, what I know. What everyone knows is short and simple. And entirely wrong.”

“Right you are,” said Flambeau cheerfully. “Let’s start with the wrong end. Let’s begin with what everyone thinks—but isn’t true.”

“If not wholly untrue,” continued Brown, “it’s at least incomplete. The public knows that Arthur St. Clare was a great British general. That he fought bravely in India and Africa. That he led a small force against President Olivier of Brazil, who issued an ultimatum. That St. Clare attacked with a tiny army against a much larger one, was captured after fierce resistance, and—horror of horrors—was hanged on the nearest tree. When the Brazilians left, they found him swinging, with his broken sword hanging round his neck.”

“And that story is false?” asked Flambeau.

“No,” said the priest quietly. “It’s true—as far as it goes.”

“Then why is it a mystery?” asked Flambeau.

They walked past hundreds of ghostly trees before Brown answered. He bit his finger thoughtfully.

“The mystery lies in psychology. Or rather, in two psychologies. In that Brazilian conflict, two of the most famous men of the age acted completely against their nature. Both were heroes—the kind of heroes history remembers. It was like Hector fighting Achilles. So imagine: what if Achilles were timid, and Hector treacherous?”

“Go on,” said Flambeau impatiently.

“Sir Arthur St. Clare was a soldier of the old school—the kind who saved us during the Mutiny. He valued duty over glory, and was cautious, even prickly, about wasting lives. Yet in this final battle, he did something any child could see was madness. You don’t need to be a general to know it was reckless—like charging a motor-bus. That’s the first puzzle: what happened to the general’s judgment?

“The second is worse. President Olivier—called a visionary, a nuisance, but always generous—had never treated a prisoner harshly. Most were freed, some honored. Even those who had wronged him left moved by his kindness. Why, then, would he act like a monster—once, and only once—against a man whose defeat couldn’t possibly have hurt him? One of the wisest men behaved like a fool. One of the noblest, like a fiend. That’s the heart of it. And I leave it to you.”

“No,” said Flambeau sharply. “You leave it to me. You’re going to tell me.”

“All right,” said Brown. “But first, let me add two things that happened later. Not because they explain the mystery—but because they deepen the darkness.

“The family doctor of the St. Clares quarreled with them and wrote articles accusing the general of religious madness. But that went nowhere. Everyone knew St. Clare had quirks—puritanical, yes—but not insane.

“The second event was more telling. Captain Keith, who was engaged to St. Clare’s daughter and later married her, was part of the regiment that attacked the Black River. He was captured, but unlike the general, was released and treated well. Twenty years later, now a lieutenant-colonel, he published an autobiography titled *A British Officer in Burmah and Brazil*. Where one expects details of the disaster, he writes only this:

*‘Everywhere else in this book I have told the truth. I hold the belief that England’s honor is strong enough to stand on its own. But in this matter—I make an exception. My reasons are private, but honorable. I will say only this: General St. Clare has been accused of incompetence. I can testify that this action, properly understood, was one of the most brilliant and intelligent of his life. President Olivier has been called cruel. I believe it is fair to say he acted with even greater generosity than usual. To put it plainly: St.5 Clare was not a fool, and Olivier was not a brute.’*

“And that’s all. He refuses to say more.”

As he spoke, a large moon, pale and luminous, broke through the branches above them. By its light, Brown had recalled the passage from a scrap of paper in his pocket. As he folded it away, Flambeau raised his hand in a sudden gesture.

“Wait!” he exclaimed. “I think I’ve got it!”

He strode ahead, breathing hard, his head low, neck taut, like a man racing against time. The priest, amused and intrigued, struggled to keep pace. Ahead, the trees parted slightly, revealing a wide, moonlit valley. The road dipped down into it, then curved back into another wall of woods. The entrance to the far forest looked like a black tunnel mouth, distant yet looming.

“I’ve got it!” Flambeau shouted, slapping his thigh. “Four minutes’ thought—and I’ve solved it.”

“All right,” said Brown. “Tell it.”

Flambeau paused, then lowered his voice.

“General St. Clare,” he said, “came from a family cursed with madness. He feared it would pass to his daughter, or worse, to his future son-in-law. He believed the end was near. So he decided to die—by his own hand. But suicide would expose the very secret he dreaded. So instead, he sacrificed duty to privacy. He charged into battle recklessly, hoping to die instantly. When he was captured instead, the final blow came. The madness broke. He broke his sword and hanged himself.”

He stared ahead at the forest, at the dark opening that swallowed the road—like a grave’s mouth. Something in the scene intensified his vision. He shuddered.

“A terrible story,” he said.

“A terrible story,” echoed Brown, bowing his head. “But not the true one.”

Then, with a sort of sorrow, he added: “Oh, I wish it had been.”

Flambeau turned sharply.

“Yours is a clean story,” said the priest, deeply moved. “A pure, honest one—clear as that moon. Madness and despair are innocent enough. But there are worse things, Flambeau.”

Flambeau looked up, startled. From his position, a single black bough crossed the moon like a devil’s horn.

“Father—father,” he cried, stepping forward urgently, “do you mean it was worse than that?”

“Worse than that,” said Brown, his voice like a grave echo.

And they plunged into the dark wood, which flowed around them like a tapestry of shadows—like a dream corridor with no exit.

Soon they were deep in the heart of the forest, surrounded by unseen foliage. Then Brown spoke again.

“Where does a wise man hide a leaf? In the forest. But what does he do if there is no forest?”

“Well?” snapped Flambeau, growing restless. “What does he do?”

“He grows a forest to hide it in,” said the priest, his voice low and heavy. “A fearful sin.”

“Look here,” said Flambeau, irritated. “Will you tell me this story or not? What proof do you have?”

“There are three more pieces of evidence,” said Brown. “I’ve gathered them from corners and forgotten places. I’ll give them in order of logic, not time.

“First, the official account comes from Olivier’s dispatches—clear and precise. He was entrenched on high ground overlooking the Black River. On the opposite bank was marshy land, and beyond that, rising terrain, where the English outpost stood. The British had more troops overall, but this regiment was isolated. Olivier considered crossing the river to cut them off. But by sunset, he decided to stay put—his position was too strong.

“At dawn, he was stunned. The English had crossed the river—half by bridge, half by ford—and were massed on the marshy shore below him.

“That they would attack such a position with such odds was absurd. But what shocked Olivier more was their behavior. Instead of advancing, they stayed stuck in the mud—like flies in glue. The Brazilians opened fire with artillery. The English returned shots, bravely but steadily weakening. Yet they held. Olivier ended his report with praise: *‘Our line advanced and drove them into the river. We captured General St. Clare and several officers. The colonel and major fell. I cannot resist saying that few finer sights in history could have been seen than the last stand of this extraordinary regiment—wounded officers picking up rifles, the general facing us bareheaded, sword broken.’* He says nothing of what happened to St. Clare afterward.”

“Well,” grunted Flambeau. “Next?”

“The next clue took time to find,” said Brown. “In an almshouse in Lincolnshire, I met an old soldier who was wounded at the Black River. He knelt beside Colonel Clancy when he died. Clancy was a big Irishman—fierce and proud. He died of rage as much as bullets. He wasn’t responsible for the raid. It was ordered by the general. His last words, according to the man: *‘And there goes the damned old donkey with the end of his sword knocked off. I wish it was his head.’* Notice—everyone sees the broken sword. But not reverently.”

“Now the third piece,” said Brown.

Their path began to rise. He paused for breath.

“Only a few months ago, a Brazilian official died in England—Espado, a Spaniard, a yellow-faced dandy with a hooked nose. I knew him. He was Catholic, and I visited him at the end. Among his papers, I found five exercise books—diaries of an English soldier, likely taken from one of the dead. It ends the night before the battle.

“But the last entry is worth reading. It begins with jokes about someone called ‘the Vulture.’ Not one of the soldiers. Not English. Not enemy. Maybe a guide or journalist. He talks to Major Murray—lean, dark-haired, Ulster-born, a Puritan. The men joke about Murray’s seriousness versus Clancy’s drinking. They also mention the Vulture wearing bright clothes.

“But then—everything changes. A road ran behind the camp, parallel to the river. Westward, it curved toward the bridge. Eastward, it led to the next outpost. That evening, cavalry appeared—glittering, clattering. The general rode in, on his white horse, famous in pictures. The salute was not ceremonial—he dismounted at once, joined the officers, and began talking intensely. What struck the diarist was how often he spoke to Major Murray. Not unusual—both were devout Evangelicals. But when the general remounted, he was still talking to Murray. They walked slowly down the road, the major holding the horse’s bridle.

“The soldiers watched until they vanished behind a thicket.

“Then—miraculous sight—the white horse galloped back, faster than wind. They thought it had run wild. But no—the general was urging it on, riding like lightning. He reined in, face like flame, and roared for the colonel—like a trumpet waking the dead.

“The men scrambled into ranks. An attack was coming. The general and major had discovered something at the bridge. Time was short. The major raced back to call the reserve. Help might not arrive in time. But they must cross the river tonight and seize the heights by morning.

“And then—silence. The diary ends.”

Brown had walked ahead. The path twisted upward, narrow and steep, like a spiral stair. His voice came from above, dimmed by darkness.

“Now,” he said, “you know the facts. But the truth—what really happened—lies not in the battlefield, but in the hearts of men. And that is the story I must now tell.”

The Three Tools of Death

Father Brown, both by calling and conviction, understood better than most that every man is dignified in death. Yet even he felt a flicker of unease when, at dawn, he was roused from sleep with news that Sir Aaron Armstrong had been murdered. There was something jarringly absurd about such violence befalling a man so universally beloved and cheerfully eccentric. Sir Aaron Armstrong was not merely popular—he was a national figure, a comic force in the world of philanthropy. To hear of his murder was like learning that Mr. Pickwick had died in an asylum or that Sunny Jim had taken his own life.

Though he championed temperance—his favorite cause—Sir Aaron approached it with such relentless gaiety that it seemed almost a performance. His speeches were floods of anecdote and laughter; his health was robust to excess; his ethics radiated optimism. He spoke of drink as if it were a villain in a farce, which only made him more endearing to those who’d never touched a drop. His conversion story was well known: once a boy drawn to Scotch whisky rather than Scotch theology, he had risen above both and become—modestly enough—the man he now was. But his white beard, cherubic face, and sparkling spectacles made it hard to believe he’d ever been anything so somber as a Calvinist or a drunkard. He was, one felt, the most earnestly joyful man alive.

He lived on the edge of Hampstead, in a tall, narrow house perched high above the railway line. The house was modern, plain, and slightly ominous—a tower of glass and stone. Its narrow side overhung the steep grassy bank beside the tracks, trembling each time a train passed. Sir Aaron claimed he had no nerves. But this morning, the tables had turned: it was the house that gave the shock—not the train.

The engine slowed just beyond the point where the house’s corner jutted onto the slope. Most mechanical stops are gradual, but this one was sudden. A man dressed entirely in black—black coat, black trousers, even black gloves—appeared atop the bank, waving his arms like a windmill of mourning. Alone, this would not have halted a train. But then came a cry—something so unnatural, so piercing, that even the driver said he would have stopped regardless of what words were shouted. The word was “Murder!”

Once the train halted, the scene unfolded swiftly. The man in black was Magnus, Sir Aaron’s manservant. The baronet had often laughed at Magnus’s gloomy attire, but no one was laughing now.

A few moments later, the first onlookers crossed the smoky hedge and saw the body of an old man in a yellow dressing gown with a vivid red lining, rolled down nearly to the bottom of the bank. A scrap of rope was caught around one leg, likely from a struggle. Blood was present but minimal. The body was bent in a way no living thing could endure. It was Sir Aaron Armstrong.

Then came Patrick Royce, the secretary—a large, fair-bearded man once famous in Bohemian circles. He emerged in a daze, echoing Magnus’s horror. By the time Alice Armstrong, the dead man’s daughter, appeared, tottering into the garden, the train had resumed its journey, bound for help.

Father Brown had been summoned at the request of Royce, an Irishman and a Catholic by birth, though one who rarely remembered his faith until trouble struck. The summons might not have been obeyed so promptly had it not been for the young detective Merton, whose friendliness with Flambeau had led him to hear many stories of the priest. As they walked across the fields toward the house, their conversation grew more personal.

“As far as I can tell,” Merton said, “there’s no sense to any of this. No one we can suspect. Magnus is a solemn fool—too much of a fool to be a killer. Royce has been with the baronet for years. And Alice clearly adored him. Besides, it’s all too absurd. Who would kill such a cheerful old man? It’s like murdering Father Christmas.”

“Cheerful, yes,” Father Brown replied quietly. “But did he make others cheerful? Was anyone else in the house truly happy?”

Merton paused. He had visited the Armstrong home often on minor police matters. Now, recalling it, he realized how bleak it really was. The rooms were high and cold, poorly decorated, lit by harsh electric bulbs that cast a ghostly glow. Though Sir Aaron’s presence blazed like a fire, it left no warmth behind. His vitality seemed to drain the air. The others in the house were shadows: Magnus, morose and gloved; Royce, big and solid, but with a face worn by sorrow; and Alice, pale and delicate, her frame trembling like a leaf in the wind. She seemed to flinch at the thunder of passing trains.

“You see,” said Father Brown, blinking modestly, “I’m not sure Armstrong’s cheerfulness was so cheerful for others. You say no one would kill such a happy man. But I wonder… *ne nos inducas in tentationem*. If I ever killed someone, it might be an optimist.”

“Why?” Merton asked, amused.

“People like laughter,” Father Brown said, “but not a permanent smile. Cheerfulness without humor is exhausting.”

They walked in silence along the grassy bank until Father Brown suddenly said, “Of course, drink itself isn’t good or bad. But sometimes I think men like Armstrong need a glass of wine now and then—to soften them.”

At the house, Gilder, a grizzled senior detective, stood waiting with Royce, whose broad shoulders and bristling hair made him seem larger than life—even though he walked with a heavy stoop, like a buffalo pulling a cart.

Royce greeted Father Brown warmly and stepped aside to speak privately. Merton addressed Gilder respectfully, though with a hint of impatience.

“Well, sir, have you made any progress?”

“There’s no mystery,” Gilder replied, staring at the rooks. “Three minutes after you left to fetch the priest, everything came out. You know the servant in black gloves who stopped the train?”

“I’d recognize him anywhere. He gave me the creeps.”

“After the train left,” Gilder drawled, “he vanished. Rather cool of a criminal to escape on the very train sent for the police.”

“You’re certain he killed the baronet?” Merton asked.

“Quite certain,” Gilder said dryly. “He took twenty thousand pounds from the master’s desk. The only puzzle is how he killed him. The skull appears broken by a heavy blow, but there’s no weapon. And if he carried it away, it must have been small—or perhaps too big to notice.”

“Perhaps the weapon was too big to notice,” Father Brown said, giggling faintly.

Gilder turned sharply. “What do you mean?”

“Silly phrasing, I know,” Brown apologized. “Sounds like a fairy tale. But poor Armstrong was killed by a giant’s club—a great green club, too large to see. We call it the earth. He was thrown against this very bank.”

Gilder frowned, then looked up. Following Brown’s gaze, they saw an attic window open at the top of the house’s blind rear wall.

“Don’t you see?” Brown pointed awkwardly. “He was thrown from there.”

Gilder examined the window. “Possible. But why are you so sure?”

Brown blinked. “Look at the rope around the corpse’s leg. See the other piece caught at the window’s corner?”

From below, it looked like a speck. But Gilder nodded. “You’re right, sir. That’s one to you.”

Just then, a special train arrived, disgorging more police—and Magnus, the missing servant.

“By Jove!” Gilder exclaimed. “They’ve got him.” He stepped forward. “Have you recovered the money?”

“No,” the policeman replied. “Not here.”

“Which is the inspector?” Magnus asked.

His voice—clear, sharp, unnaturally precise—made everyone jump. He was a dull man, with flat black hair, a colorless face, and eyes that slanted slightly, suggesting Eastern ancestry. His past was murky—rescued by Sir Aaron from a London restaurant, some said from worse things. But his voice was alive where his face was dead.

“I always knew this would happen,” he said calmly. “My master mocked my black clothes, but I always said I’d be ready for his funeral.”

He raised his gloved hands.

“Sergeant,” Gilder snapped, “put the bracelets on him—he looks dangerous.”

“Actually, sir,” the sergeant said, “we can’t.”

“What do you mean?”

“We arrested him,” the sergeant said gravely, “just as he was leaving the Highgate police station—where he’d deposited the money in Inspector Robinson’s care.”

Gilder stared. “Why would he do that?”

“To keep it safe from the criminal,” Magnus replied placidly.

“But surely,” Gilder began, “it could have stayed with the family.”

The train roared past, drowning the rest—but through the noise, Magnus’s voice rang clear: “I have no reason to trust Sir Aaron’s family.”

Everyone froze. Then Alice Armstrong appeared behind Father Brown—pale, beautiful, yet with hair so faded it seemed gray in shadow.

“Be careful what you say,” Royce muttered. “You’ll frighten Miss Armstrong.”

“I hope so,” Magnus said.

She winced. He continued: “I’ve seen her tremble for years. Some thought it was fear or cold. I know it was hatred—wicked anger. She would have fled with her lover and the money, but for me. Ever since her father stopped her marriage to that drunken scoundrel—”

“Stop!” Gilder commanded. “We don’t care about your family quarrels. Unless you have evidence—”

“Oh, I’ll give you evidence,” Magnus cut in. “You’ll have to subpoena me. And I’ll tell the truth. After the old man was thrown from the window, I ran into the attic and found her unconscious—fainting—on the floor, with a red dagger still in her hand. Allow me to hand it over.” He pulled a horn-handled knife from his pocket, smeared with blood, and handed it to the sergeant.

He stepped back, his eyes narrowing into a sneer.

Merton felt sick. “Surely you’d take her word over his?”

Father Brown lifted his face—so fresh, so innocent, it seemed he’d just washed it. “Yes,” he said gently. “But is Miss Armstrong’s word against his?”

Alice gasped—a small, startled sound. Her body stiffened. Only her face moved—alive with horror.

“This man claims,” Gilder said slowly, “that you were found with a knife, unconscious, after the murder.”

“He speaks the truth,” she whispered.

Then Royce strode forward, his massive head bowed. “Well, if I’ve got to go,” he said, “I’ll have a bit of pleasure first.”

He swung a fist—crashing into Magnus’s face. The servant fell like a sack of stones. Police rushed forward.

“None of that, Mr. Royce,” Gilder said firmly. “I arrest you for assault.”

“No,” Royce replied, voice like iron. “You’ll arrest me for murder.”

Gilder glanced at Magnus, who was already sitting up, wiping blood from his unharmed face.

“What do you mean?”

“It’s true,” Royce said. “Miss Armstrong fainted with a knife in her hand. But she didn’t use it to attack her father. She used it to defend him.”

“Against whom?” Gilder asked.

“Against me.”

Alice looked at him—her expression tangled with grief and something else. Then softly: “After all, I am still glad you are brave.”

“Come upstairs,” Royce said heavily. “I’ll show you the whole cursed thing.”

The attic was Royce’s private room—small, cramped, filled with signs of violence. A revolver lay near the center, as if tossed aside. A whisky bottle, half-empty, sat nearby. The tablecloth was torn, a length of rope sprawled across the windowsill. Two vases were shattered on the mantel, one on the floor.

“I was drunk,” Royce said simply. “And this is the beginning of the end.”

He continued, voice thick with shame: “You know my story. I was once called clever. Armstrong saved what was left of me from the taverns. He was kind. But he wouldn’t let me marry Alice. And maybe he was right. But you don’t need me to explain. That’s my whisky. That’s my gun—empty. That’s my rope. That’s my window. I confess—I killed him. I give myself to the gallows. And by God—that’s enough.”

The police moved to escort him. But Father Brown was kneeling on the carpet, examining the floor as if praying.

“I say,” he said cheerfully, “this won’t do at all. At first, you said there was no weapon. Now we’ve found three: a knife, a rope, a pistol—and yet he broke his neck falling! It’s not economical.”

He shook his head like a grazing horse.

“And now three impossible things. First, these bullet holes in the carpet. Why shoot at the floor? A drunk man shoots at the enemy’s head—what’s grinning at him. Not at the feet. Then the rope—why would a drunk man try to strangle someone, then wrap it around their leg? Royce wasn’t that drunk. And finally—the whisky bottle. You say a dipsomaniac fought for it, then rolled it away, spilling half? That’s the last thing a drunk would do.”

He rose awkwardly. “I’m terribly sorry, sir, but your story is rubbish.”

Alice approached Brown. “Can I speak to you alone?”

They stepped into the next room.

“You’re trying to save Patrick,” she said. “But it’s no use. The truth is black. The more you learn, the worse it gets for the man I love.”

“Why?” Brown asked.

“Because,” she said steadily, “I saw him commit the crime.”

“Ah,” he said, unmoved. “And what did he do?”

“I was in the next room. Both doors were closed. Then I heard a voice—like nothing on earth—roaring ‘Hell, hell, hell!’ Again and again. Then the doors shook with the first gunshot. Three more times it fired before I could open them. Smoke filled the room. The pistol was smoking in Patrick’s hand. I saw him fire the final shots. Then he lunged at my father, who clung to the window-sill. He threw the rope over his head, but it slipped to his shoulders, then his legs. It tightened around one leg, and Patrick dragged him down like a madman. I grabbed a knife from the mat and cut the rope—before I fainted.”

“I see,” Father Brown said, equally calm. “Thank you.”

And he returned to the attic—quietly, as if nothing had changed.
