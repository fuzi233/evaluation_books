We all know, dear children, from our favourite fairy tales, how in every wedding or christening there is always someone left out—some poor soul who feels slighted and swears revenge. So it’s no wonder that good cousin Will should rage and curse when he heard the news of his brother’s engagement to the colonial heiress. At first, Will stormed out of the house, swearing he would never return. But no one truly believed him—not even he himself. The prodigal son, though unrepentant, came back after a day or two, drawn by the scent of the wedding feast. He couldn’t bring himself to give up his place at Castlewood’s table. He returned, ate, drank, and toasted the young bride with a glass of wine, while muttering curses under his breath. When her father gave her away in the chapel, and my lord vowed to love, honour, and cherish her, Will responded with muffled imprecations. He was not alone in his grumbling; Mr. Warrington knew well that many had spoken ill of the new Countess and her grandfather.

The old gentleman’s City friends, his legal adviser, the Dissenting clergyman who had led their services upon arrival in England, and poor Jack Lambert—the orthodox young minister whose eloquence the old man had hoped might have won the girl over in private—all resented her perceived betrayal. Each had a tale of being deceived, encouraged, then jilted by the clever American. The lawyer, who had compiled such an exact list of her properties, estates, slaves, ships, and expectations, now declared it must be false—there was no such place as New York or Virginia, or at least Mr. Van den Bosch owned nothing there. There was no Guinea trade, and the negroes were mere fabrications conjured by the cunning planter. The Dissenting pastor lamented his lost lamb—though such a wily, mischievous creature could hardly be called a lamb at all. Poor Jack Lambert confessed to his mother that he still kept a lock of her black hair, which he wept over and addressed in language quite unfit for a clergyman. As for Mr. William Esmond, he unleashed his usual torrent of shrieks and oaths—even in the presence of his sister-in-law—swearing before Acheron that no devil more artful than Miss Lydia existed. He called her an infernal Cerberus and invoked every wrath of heaven and earth upon his brother, whom he accused of deceiving him with sweet words and stealing his prize.

“Why,” said Mr. Warrington, when Will ranted on about these matters, “if she’s truly the kind of woman you describe, then you’re better off without her. If she intends to poison her husband, as you say, how fortunate for you that you’re not the one! You ought to thank the gods, Will, instead of cursing them, for sparing you such a fury. And what greater revenge could you have on Castlewood than letting him have her all to himself?”

“All very well,” Will replied, perhaps not unfairly, “but my brother remains a scoundrel for cheating me out of the fortune I expected—and which I risked my life to win.”

George Warrington wondered how Will had ever come to risk his precious life—perhaps, he thought, a rope’s end would have been a fitting end for it. But Will, with full candour, told him how the little Cerbera had actually brought them together, only for Sir John Fielding’s men to interrupt their meeting. She constantly mocked George for sneering at Will, and Will for ignoring the taunts. She claimed George was a coward, and Will a poltroon. Then, during Braddock’s expedition, George had fled in terror and nearly died of fright. “Deuce take me,” said Will, “I was never more surprised, cousin, than when you stood your ground so coolly in Tottenham Court Fields. My second and I wagered you wouldn’t show up!”

Mr. Warrington laughed and thanked him for the compliment.

“Though,” he added, “it was lucky for me the constables arrived, or you’d have run your sword through me in another minute. Didn’t you see how clumsy I looked standing before you? And you turned pale and trembled with anger!”

“Yes, curse me,” said Will, now turning red, “that’s how I show rage. And I was furious with you, cousin! But now it’s my brother I hate—and that little devil of a Countess—a countess! A pretty countess, indeed!” And with another thunderous volley of oaths, he saluted the reigning member of the family.

“Well, cousin,” said George, looking him sharply in the eye, “you let me off easily. I dare say I owe my life to you—or at least a whole waistcoat. I admire your restraint and courage. What a pity such bravery should be wasted as a mere court usher! You’re a loss to His Majesty’s army. You truly are!”

“I never know whether you’re joking or serious, Mr. Warrington,” growled Will.

“I should think few gentlemen would dare joke with you, cousin, if they cared for their lives or ears!” retorted Mr. Warrington, who delighted in teasing his noble kinsman and watching him choke back his curses, grind his teeth in helpless fury, and smother his cowardly rage.

“And you should moderate your language, cousin, regarding the dear Countess and my lord your brother,” Mr. Warrington continued. “They speak of you most kindly. Her ladyship has told me everything.”

“What everything?” asked Will, aghast.

“As much as women ever do tell, cousin. She admitted she thought you had been a little enamoured of her. What woman can help liking a man who admires her?”

“She hates you, and says you were wild about her, Mr. Warrington!” exclaimed Mr. Esmond.

“Spretae injuria formae, cousin!”

“For me—what’s for me?” asked the other.

“I never cared for her, and thus perhaps she does not love me. Don’t you remember the case of the wife of the Captain of the Guard?”

“Which guard?” asked Will.

“My Lord Potiphar,” said Mr. Warrington.

“Lord Who? My Lord Falmouth is Captain of the Yeomen of the Guard, and my Lord Berkeley of the Pensioners. My Lord Hobart held it before. Suppose you haven’t been long enough in England to know who’s who, cousin!” remarked Mr. William.

But Mr. Warrington explained he meant the Captain of the King of Egypt’s Guard, whose wife persecuted Joseph for refusing her advances. Will responded that Egypt, he believed, was a confounded long way off, and if Lord What-d’ye-call’s wife lied about him, it was typical of her sex—women, he supposed, were the same everywhere.

In truth, when Mr. Warrington visited Castlewood for his marriage, he had heard from the Countess herself her version of the quarrel between Will and her. This account differed in some ways, though he did not claim it was more truthful than Will’s straightforward tale. The lady regretted how she had been misled by her brother-in-law. She feared his time at court had corrupted the noble principles all Esmonds were born with. She doubted Will’s word, believing his loose living and financial troubles had made him mercenary, dulled his honour, perhaps even weakened the chivalrous courage “which we Esmonds, cousin,” she declared, tossing her head, “must always possess—at least you, me, my lord, and my cousin Harry do, I know!”

“Oh, cousin George!” she went on, “and must I confess I began to doubt yours—without which a man of ancient and noble lineage like ours isn’t worthy to be called a man! I shall try, George, as a Christian lady and head of one of the first families in this kingdom and the world, to forgive my brother William for speaking ill of a family member—even if a younger branch by the female side—and making me doubt you. He did. Perhaps he told me many bad things you had said about me.”

“I, my dear lady!” cried Mr. Warrington.

“Which he said you said of me, cousin, and I hope you didn’t, and pray you didn’t. I can afford to despise them. And he paid me court—that’s true—and others have done so too. I’m used to it. He might have prospered better than he did (for I didn, at the time, know my dear lord nor value his great qualities as I do now, out of the fulness of my grateful heart!). But oh! I found William lacked courage, and no man without it can ever earn Lydia, Countess of Castlewood’s esteem—no, not that much!” And she snapped her fingers. “Noblesse oblige. A man of our blood who lacks courage—well, I don’t care a pinch of snuff for him—there, now, I don’t! Look at our ancestors, George, around these walls! Haven’t the Esmonds always fought for country and king? Is there one of us who, when the moment comes, isn’t ready to prove he’s an Esmond and a nobleman? If my eldest son showed the white feather, ‘My Lord Esmond!’ I would say to him (for that’s the second title in our family), ‘I disown your lordship!’” And with that, the intrepid little woman surveyed the portraits of her forebears—painted by Lely and Kneller—adorned the walls of her drawing-room.

Over that room, and indeed over the entire house, estate, and village, the new Countess soon established absolute rule. It was astonishing how quickly she learned command. Though she didn’t follow English customs of precedence, she invented her own rules, announced them, and enforced them. Having grown up a Dissenter and unfamiliar with the Established Church rites, Mr. Warrington noted she made a few mistakes—such as not knowing when to face east during service, a practice not observed in most Reformed churches. But between his first and second visits to Castlewood, she mastered her duties perfectly. She entered chapel on her cousin’s arm, her two footmen bearing her great Prayer-book behind her, as demurely as the pious old devotee in Hogarth’s famous painting *Morning*, as if she had always had a chaplain at her side.

She patronized not only the new chaplain but the church itself, as if she had never heard a Ranter preach in a barn. She made the oldest families in the land—grave baronets and their wives, worthy squires of twenty generations—know their place, and grant her the pas. She acquired an ancient heraldry book and sought instruction from a remarkable old maid from Winton, renowned for etiquette and genealogy, who had known Queen Anne’s court. Soon, she prattled fluently about gules and sables, bends and saltires—sometimes inaccurately, but with relentless energy. She made small progresses to nearby towns in her gilt coach-and-six, or to the village in her chair, asserting a quasi-regal right to homage from tenants and villagers alike.

She lectured the parson on theology, the bailiff on farming, instructed the astonished housekeeper in preserving and pickling, and would have taught the London footmen how to leap behind the carriage—had her little frame not been too small to mount it. She gave the village gossips advice on nursing and child-rearing before she had any children of her own. And when it came to medicine, Madam Esmond of Virginia was no more stubborn about her remedies than Miss Lydia, the earl’s new bride. One wondered, recalling the tale of the Fisherman and the Genie in the *Arabian Nights*, how such a vast spirit could be contained in such a tiny body.

When Mr. Warrington returned to London after his first visit, she sent gifts to his young friends in Dean Street—Theo, older, and Hetty, barely younger than herself. She sent a trinket to one and a book to the other—G. Warrington insisting Theo’s was a doll, and Hetty’s a nursery book with single-syllable words. As for Mr. Will, her younger brother-in-law, she treated him with maternal gravity and tenderness, often speaking to him with a protective air that amused Warrington, though it only intensified Will’s oaths and blasphemies.

As for old age, my Lady Lydia had little patience for it. Once the settlements were secured, she treated the elderly Van den Bosch and his large periwig with no more ceremony than Dinah, her black attendant, whose ears she would pinch and woolly head pull without hesitation when offended—so at least Dinah told Gumbo, who told his master. The entire household quivered before the Countess: the housekeeper, once feared even by my lord and the dowager; the pampered London footmen, who once quarreled over cards and complained loudly as they drank endless beer, now moved swiftly at her call. Even old Lockwood, gate-porter for half a century or more, tried to gather his failing wits when she entered his lodge to inspect his window, wood-closet, and order his dogs outside. Lockwood bowed his bald head before his new mistress, glanced pleadingly at his niece, and trembled before her authority.

Gumbo, dressing his master for dinner, spoke of Elisha—whom the chaplain had read about that morning—“and his bald head and de boys who call um names, and de bars eat em up, and serve um right,” said Gumbo. But the Countess, discussing the old porter with her cousin, dismissed him with a wave. “Pooh, pooh! Stupid old man! Past his work, he and his dirty dogs! They’re as old and ugly as those old fish in the pond!” She pointed to two ancient carp in the garden pond—rumoured to have lived for centuries, their backs covered in grey mould. “Lockwood must go. The workhouse is where he belongs. I’ll have a smart, tall fellow in the lodge, one who’ll do credit to our livery.”

“He was my grandfather’s man, served him in Queen Anne’s wars,” interposed Mr. Warrington.

“Oh, Lord! Queen Anne’s dead, I suppose, and we’re not going into mourning for her,” snapped the Countess.

This matter was debated at dinner, when she announced her intention to dismiss the old man.

“I am told,” said Mr. Van den Bosch gravely, “that English law provides admirably for poor servants and the aged. I wish we had such an asylum at home, and were spared the cost of keeping our old hands.”

“If a man can’t work, he ought to go!” declared her ladyship.

“Yes, indeed, that’s a fact!” agreed grandpapa.

“What! An old servant?” asked my lord.

“Mr. Van den Bosch may have been independent when young,” remarked Mr. Warrington.

“Greased my own boots, opened my own shutters, sanded and watered my own——”

“Sugar, sir?” asked my lord.

“No—floor, son-in-law!” chuckled the old man. “Though there are tricks in grocery stores, saving your ladyship’s presence.”

“La, pa! What should I know about stores and groceries?” said her ladyship.

“He! Remember stealing the sugar, and what came of it, my dear ladyship?” said grandpapa.

“At any rate, a handsome, well-grown man in our livery will look better than that shrivelled old porter!” insisted the Countess.

“No livery is so becoming as old age, madam, and no lace as handsome as silver hairs,” said Mr. Warrington. “What will the county say if you banish old Lockwood?”

“Oh! If you plead for him, sir, I suppose he must stay. Hadn’t I better send him a couch from my drawing-room and some of the best wine from the cellar?”

“Indeed, your ladyship couldn’t do better,” Mr. Warrington replied, very seriously.

And my lord yawned, “Cousin George is perfectly right, my dear. To turn away such an old servant as Lockwood would look ill.”

“You see, those mouldy old carps are a curiosity, and attract visitors,” Mr. Warrington continued gravely. “Your ladyship must allow this old man to remain. It won’t be long. Then you may hire the tall porter. It’s hard on us, Mr. Van den Bosch, that we must keep our old negroes when they’re past work. I shall sell that rascal Gumbo in eight or ten years.”

“Don’t tink you will, master!” grinned Gumbo.

“Hold your tongue, sir! He doesn’t understand English ways,” said Mr. Warrington. “He thinks an old servant has a claim on kindness.”

The next day, to Warrington’s surprise, the Countess actually sent a basket of fine wine and a cushion for Lockwood’s armchair.

“I thought of what you said last night, when I went to bed. I guess you know the world better than I do, cousin. It’s best to keep the old man, as you say.”

And so the affair ended, Mr. Warrington wondering inwardly at this strange little character from the West—her naivety, simplicity, and heartlessness that would have shamed the most hardened dowager in St. James’s.

“You tell me to respect old people. Why? I don’t see anything to respect in the old ones I know,” she said to Warrington. “They aren’t funny, and they certainly aren’t handsome. Look at grandfather; look at Aunt Bernstein. They say she was a beauty once! That portrait painted from her! I don’t believe it. No one shall tell me I’ll ever be as bad as that! When they reach that state, people shouldn’t live. No, they shouldn’t.”

At Christmas, Aunt Bernstein came to visit her nephew and niece, accompanied by Mr. Warrington. They travelled slowly in the Baroness’s own landau, the old lady in excellent health and spirits, the weather crisp but not too cold. As they neared Castlewood, Aunt Beatrice regaled her companion with stories of the house and bygone days. Though often sluggish and irritable, she could suddenly blaze with liveliness, her wit sharp, her memory rich with anecdotes of a vanished age and society. How cruel it is, I thought, that beauty should not enjoy its own span, but be forced to surrender it after forty years at most. As she spoke of her former lovers and admirers—of whom I knew far more than she suspected—I studied her face, trying to reconstruct her beauty in youth. What a sermon I read there! The courts overgrown with grass, towers crumbled, doors ajar, gilded halls tarnished, tapestries cobwebbed and torn. That ruined palace had once thrived with music and splendour, its windows blazing with light. What balls and feasts had taken place here, what laughter and passion! I could imagine secret meetings, hidden intrigues, though the curtains were drawn tight. I often felt like saying to her, “Madam, I know the story wasn’t as you tell it—but so and so.” (I had read at home the history of her life, written by my dear old grandfather.) My mind wandered through her, as I had walked through our father’s house at Castlewood, meditating on vanished glories, imagining ancient times.

When Aunt Bernstein arrived at Castlewood, her relatives respected her not for her wealth, but for her commanding presence, sharp wit, and imperious nature. She expected the same deference from the new Countess, whom she welcomed with special warmth. After all, she had orchestrated the match. “Since you, you silly creature, refused the heiress,” she said, “I was determined she wouldn’t leave the family.” She chuckled over her schemes to bring the marriage about. She had given the girl a coronet and her nephew a hundred thousand pounds. Naturally, she deserved a warm welcome. She admired the Countess’s boldness in overcoming the Dowager and Lady Fanny. Always fond of attractive people, Madame Bernstein praised her niece Lydia’s bright eyes and lovely figure. The marriage was ideal. The old man was an obstacle, yes, and rather homely in speech and appearance. But he would soon be gone. “He’ll want to go to America, or perhaps farther,” she shrugged. “As for the child, she has fire and vivacity, a Cherokee manner that has charm,” said the pleased Baroness. “Your brother had it—so do you, Master George! Nous la formerons, cette petite. Eugene lacks character, but he’s a finished gentleman. Between us, we’ll make the little savage perfectly presentable.”

Thus we talked on the second afternoon as we journeyed toward Castlewood. We stayed the first night at the King’s Arms in Bagshot, where the Baroness was always received with deep respect, then drove post to Hexton, where she had arranged for my lord’s horses to wait. But they were not there, and after two hours we continued with our Bagshot horses to Castlewood.
