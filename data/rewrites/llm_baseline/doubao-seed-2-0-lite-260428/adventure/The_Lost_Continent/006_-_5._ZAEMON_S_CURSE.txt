It looked, for the time being, like I was to take up residence inside the royal pyramid. The glittering, gold-cloaked cavalcade drew up in the vast paved square before the monolith, hooves stamping, trumpets blaring, and massed into clusters that glinted like scattered coins under the midday sun. The mammoth carrying us rumbled to a halt directly before the pyramid’s grand entrance, attendants hauled a broad oak stair up to its howdah, the horns wailed again, and the three of us who’d ridden beneath the emerald-encrusted canopy of carved snakes – Phorenice, her fan-girl, and I – stepped down to the stone.

Before I even thought to check myself, the decades-deep muscle memory of my priestly upbringing kicked in. I turned unthinking toward the craggy, smoke-wreathed heights of the Sacred Mountain that loomed over the city, dropped to my knees, pressed my forehead to the paving stones, and whispered the age-old prescribed prayer of thanks to the Sun and the old Gods for the safe air that let men draw breath. It was nothing but habit, a ritual I’d performed every day for forty years. But when I stood back up, I could have sworn I heard a snickering titter ripple through the ranks of the courtiers watching us.

I scowled in the direction of the scoffers, half-ready to demand Phorenice punish them for that open disrespect, and that was when I spotted the oddity. I’d expected to see her kneeling right there beside me, bowing to the Mountain as every rightful ruler of Atlantis had done for a thousand years. Instead she was standing perfectly straight, not a crease in her silk robes, never having bent her head at all. She was staring at me, that half-smirk on her face I couldn’t quite parse, like I’d just done something ridiculous.

Whatever she was thinking, she didn’t air it in front of the crowd. “Come,” she said, and turned to the great stone doorway, calling out the secret daily passphrase that swung the hundred-ton granite portal open on hidden, greased hinges. She swept past into the cool, dim interior, her fan-girl gliding demurely at her heels, and a cold heaviness settled in my chest as I stepped after them. The doors thudded shut sullenly behind us, sealing the three of us inside the maze of solid stone.

We didn’t go far at first. Phorenice stopped in the Hall of Waiting, the same hall I remembered so clearly from twenty years prior, when the old king had bade me farewell before I sailed off to take up my viceroyalty in distant Yucatan. Red walls plastered with carvings of long-dead kings lined the space, and the central jet of burning earth-breath blazed on its bronze stand, casting amber light over everything. The air here was nothing like the crisp, clean air I remembered, though: it was thick with cloying, sweet perfumes, heavy enough to make my head swim.

“Deucalion, my old minister,” Phorenice said, “I do not think you meant any insult. But two decades of that colonial life have turned you dreadfully simple. That obeisance you just made to the Mountain? No one has done that since I took the throne.”

“Your Majesty,” I said, “I was raised from boyhood in the Priests’ Clan, taught to revere the Gods. I’ve been chased through volcanic passes by searing fire flows and choking toxic fumes three separate times, in the old days patrolling my Yucatan territory. That prayer isn’t empty ritual to me – it comes straight from the gratitude of a man who knows how lucky he is just to be breathing.”

“Atlantis changed while you were gone,” she said. “When we give thanks now, we do not waste them on those old, forgotten Gods.”

The impiety of it hit me like a blow to the gut. If that was the new order, I wanted no part of it. I’d sworn to serve my rightful monarch faithfully, but I would not bow to sacrilege, would not treat a swineherd’s daughter who’d clawed her way to the throne as a living goddess worthy of prayers. I chose to call that bluff right then.

“Phorenice,” I said, “I’ve been a priest my whole life. Until I see proof that the old ways are false, I will hold to that oath. If that faith costs me, I will pay the price.”

She smiled slow, showing no anger. “You are a stubborn, strong man, Deucalion.”

I bowed.

“I’ve known plenty of stubborn men,” she said, shaking out her full mass of ruddy hair so the earth-breath light glowed over the curves of her face. “They all converted, in the end. I never even had to burn the hard ones. They threw themselves at my feet, begging to worship me. But it seems my charms fall short today.”

“You are Empress,” I said, stolid. “I am your servant. Give me leave tomorrow, and I will march out and crush the rebel rabble camped outside your walls, prove my worth to you that way.”

“They say you are one of the best fighters in the kingdom,” she said. “I hold a little skill with a sword myself, I know a good warrior when I see one. Tomorrow we will ride out and taste battle side by side. But today, the welcome feast I planned for you will be ready soon. There are private chambers set aside for your use, stocked with everything you could need. Ylga, my fan-girl here, will show you the way.”

We waited until Phorenice vanished down a shadowed passage at the far end of the hall, then Ylga picked up an oil lamp and led me off through the twisting, narrow stone corridors. The passages doubled back on themselves over and over, built so deliberately convoluted a stranger could get lost for days in the maze, no intruder ever reaching the royal quarters unannounced. Old tales said the pyramid’s builder had killed every last mason who’d laid its inner stones, to make sure no one but the royal line ever learned the layout.

Ylga moved like she’d walked those halls a thousand times, no hesitation. She turned sharp corners, pushed a hidden indent in a blank wall to swing a secret stone door open, even stamped her foot on a loose flagstone that reared up to reveal a steep, narrow hidden stair that led down, then up again through another inclined tunnel. Finally she stopped before a heavy granite slab, pushed it open, and gestured me inside a broad, richly furnished chamber.

“All the chests along the walls hold fine new robes,” she said, “jewels and gold trinkets in the bronze coffer there. Phorenice sent them as her first gift, she says, and there will be far more if you play along. You can drop that old colonial simplicity now, dress in the finery the rest of the court wears.”

“Watch your tongue, girl,” I snapped. “I do not need advice on how to dress.”

“If you think that rude, you can tell the Empress, and she’ll have me whipped,” she shot back, her dark eyes glowering up at me from under her thick brows. “You could even ask to see it done yourself. Phorenice would grant you almost anything right now.”

“You are closer to that whipping than you think,” I said.

“I have a name,” she retorted. “Ylga. You’d have heard it on the mammoth ride here if you hadn’t been so wrapped up in staring at the Sacred Mountain.”

I stared at her, curious. “You’ve never met me before. Why speak to me like that? There’s some reason for it.”

She slid the heavy stone door shut behind us, then grabbed my wrist, pulling me away from the airshaft and into the far, shadowed corner of the room, where no hidden listener could hear. “I am Zaemon’s daughter,” she said, quiet. “The Zaemon you called your oldest friend, back before you sailed to Yucatan.”

“Did he send you with a message?” I whispered, shocked.

“How could he? He lives up on the Sacred Mountain, in the priests’ compound. I haven’t seen him in two years, not since Phorenice dragged me down to the court to be her personal attendant. But the second I saw you step off that red pavilion at the harbour, I felt sorry for you, Deucalion. I knew exactly what she’s been plotting these last two months.”

“I cannot hear you speak ill of the Empress,” I said, automatically.

“Even when she is planning to destroy you?” she snapped, stamping her sandal on the stone floor. “You are either very blind or very brave. I won’t interfere more right now, but I’ll be watching. If you ever need a friend, I will help you. I am second highest ranking lady in Atlantis now, do not forget that. Women hold power here, under Phorenice. My friendship is a thing men have begged for, for years.”

“Thank you,” I said, and she left, after showing me how to call the chamber slaves. I stood there for a full minute, still reeling, realizing I’d just promised my gratitude to the daughter of the man who’d taught me my first priestly rites decades before.

The slaves came running when I called, immediately trying to smother me in thick perfumes, lace my hair with gold dust, dress me in layers of frilly silk finery I had no use for. Irritated by their fussy impertinence, I set them to beating each other with their own palms, promising if they didn’t do it hard enough I’d send them to the brander to mark their skins with permanent stripes. Even lowly servants can get under your skin, when they’re fawning over you like you’re some pretty toy.

But nothing I saw that day shocked me half as much as how Phorenice summoned the feast. I sat waiting in my chamber, when a low, rumbling hum started, growing so slowly I barely noticed it at first, until it swelled into a clangorous, ear-splitting din that made the whole massive pyramid shake like a child’s wooden toy, groaning and trembling under my feet.

It was the sacred Earth-Roar, the ritual the Priests’ Clan only performed once a year on the peak of the Sacred Mountain, to remind all the people that fire and tremor had birthed them, and fire and tremor would swallow them in the end. And Phorenice was prostituting that most holy of signs, turning it into nothing more than a dinner bell to call her guests to gluttony and debauchery.

How had she gotten control of that secret power? Had some disloyal priest on the Mountain betrayed the most sacred mysteries of our order to her? Or had her sharp, ungodly mind cracked the secrets of the earth’s fire all on her own? If she’d worked it out herself, I could still serve her faithfully, as my Empress. But if a priest had betrayed the order? That was a crime we hounded to the end of the earth, the traitor and anyone he’d told.

My head spinning, I followed the slaves through the maze of passages to the great banqueting hall. The sight was dazzling: the vast carved chamber was packed with courtiers draped in every possible shade of silk and gem, all painted and perfumed to within an inch of their lives. No one spared a thought for the sacred roar that had just summoned them, no one cared that rebels besieged the city walls outside, no one mourned the starving poor dying in the streets, no one thought of the old Gods on the Mountain. They only cared about the next sip of wine, the next bite of spiced meat.

The burning earth-breath jets lit the hall as bright as midday, and trumpets blared the second I stepped through the doors. No one roared out a hearty welcome, though. They all lisped my name, bowing mincingly to the floor so their silks swished and their jewels jangled, no heart to it at all. When Phorenice stepped out onto the dais even their cheers were fake and soft, hearty loud welcome was considered barbaric, by this new shallow culture. Two smirking chamberlains with gilded wands led me to the dais, to seat me right beside her on her own private divan, eat from her own plate.

“Honour could not run higher than this,” I said, as I knelt to sit.

“Deucalion could get far more than that, if he asked nicely,” she said, that sidelong smirk back on her face. “All the men in the known world have dreamed of favours I could give you, favours no one else will ever get. But you have to ask for them warmly.”

“First favour,” I said, “give me leave to ride out and wipe that rebel rabble from the walls tomorrow.”

“Pah,” she said, frowning. “Are you only interested in grim, blood-drenched fun? They must have been terribly rustic in that Yucatan colony of yours. For now, eat. Tell me if you like the feast.”

The dishes were piled high, over-spiced, over-sweet, wines so thick they clung to the glass. I had no appetite for any of it, but the rest of the court revelled in every bite, treating each sip and morsel like a gift from the gods, caressing their palates like it was sacred. Phorenice smiled, calling favoured courtiers up to her to give them a scrap of meat from her own plate, which the lucky recipients ate with over-the-top dramatic gestures, and two of them even tucked the bits of food into their clothing to keep as relics. I kept catching glances at Ylga, standing behind Phorenice’s divan, her face sharp and honest, no simpering fake grace like the rest of the court. She was a thousand times easier to look at than even Phorenice, for all the Empress’s legendary beauty.

Halfway through the feast, a foppish outlying governor who’d fled his rebellious province for the capital stumbled up to the dais, drunk, bowing low. “Greatest of all Empresses, fairest of Goddesses, queen of my adoring heart!” he cried, and before anyone could stop him he grabbed Phorenice’s full cup, drained every last drop of wine, and yelled “May all your troubles pass as easily, and leave as sweet a taste!”

Phorenice looked over at me, amused. “You don’t approve of this new custom?”

“It was once the highest mark of respect to pour a libation of wine at a ruler’s feet,” I said blunt. “Drinking it yourself is just greedy self-indulgence, you can do that anywhere.”

“You still cling to those old, austere rules,” she said. “Our new code says enjoy every pleasure first, and don’t let anything get in the way of that.”

The feast dragged on, the courtiers growing more and more ridiculous, the guards lining the walls standing as still and stern as the stone statues carved into the outer walls. Then, all of a sudden, a shout went up at the far doorway. Two guards crossed their spears to block a ragged intruder, but he shoved right past them, unharmed, and walked straight up the centre of the banqueting hall between the rows of feasters. The crowd jeered at him, then went quiet, the women shuddering like a cold winter wind had blown through the room. He stopped right in front of Phorenice’s divan, stared her dead in the eye, and did not bow at all.

He was a frail old wanderer, wild white hair tumbling over his shoulders, ragged white beard caked in volcanic dust, mud clotted on his bare feet. A single rough cloth was wrapped around his waist and shoulders, and in his hand he carried a priest’s sun-wand, the symbol of the Sun glowing bright red at its tip. I recognized him instantly as one of the wandering ascetics, priests who’d renounced the comforts of the Mountain to live out in the dangerous volcanic badlands, facing fire flows and venomous lizards and toxic fumes every day, gaining mastery over earth mysteries no normal priest could ever touch.

The whole hall went dead silent. Phorenice spoke first, her voice sharp and clear. “Those two guards at the door failed me. Remove them.”

The next rank of guards drove their spears straight through the two men’s chests, so fast one didn’t even make a sound as he fell dead to the floor.

The old priest’s voice was thin, high, and cracked, but every word carried to the far corners of the hall. “You have added another sin to your tally, Phorenice. Not half your army could have stopped me entering if I’d wanted to come. I bring you your final warning. The Gods favoured you, gave you enough power and cunning to climb above every other mortal, take the throne of Atlantis for your own. We on the Sacred Mountain even let you have that last ambition, thought you might rule well. But you have failed.

“Undo all the evil you have done. Give bread to the starving people of the city, even if you take it out of the gluttons sitting in this very hall. Restore Atlantis to the honourable, orderly state the old kings built it to be. If you do not? You will be removed. We will not let this country sink back into barbarism. Give me your answer, true.”

“Speak him fair! For your own sake, speak him fair!” Ylga hissed in a desperate, urgent whisper from behind us. Phorenice did not turn to her. She leaned forward on the cushions of the divan, her brows knitted in cold, furious anger.

“Do you dare to threaten me, old man?” she snarled. “Knowing exactly who I am?”
