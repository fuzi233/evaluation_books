For centuries, the canons of Steinfeld have whispered of a hidden hoard belonging to Abbot Thomas, a fortune in gold buried beneath the monastery grounds. Though they have searched in vain, the legend persists: Thomas, in the prime of his life, concealed a massive pile of wealth nearby. When pressed for its location, he would laugh and reply, "Job, John, and Zechariah shall reveal it to you or your descendants," adding that he bore no ill will toward those who might eventually claim it. Among his many works, the Abbot is remembered for filling the great eastern window of the south aisle with exquisite stained glass, a testament to his piety and artistry, and for restoring the Abbot's lodging with a marble-adorned well in the courtyard. He died suddenly in 1529, at the age of seventy-two.

"I suppose I must translate this," the antiquary murmured to himself, finishing his copy of the *Sertum Steinfeldense Norbertinum*. "Better to do it now than later." He rendered the Latin into English, noting the gossip of the treasure and the details of the Abbot's final years.

The antiquary's true quest, however, was to locate the lost stained glass of Steinfeld Abbey. Following the dissolution of German monasteries, vast quantities of such glass had found their way to England, adorning parish churches and private chapels. Steinfeld's contribution was significant, identifiable by inscriptions and narrative cycles. Yet, a specific passage in the *Sertum* had led him to a different conclusion. In a private chapel, he had encountered three large figures—Job, John, and Zechariah—each holding a scroll with a peculiar inscription. Job's scroll read, "There is a place for gold where it is hidden"; John's, "They have on their raiment a writing which no man knoweth"; and Zechariah's, "Upon one stone are seven eyes." These texts deviated strangely from standard scripture, hinting at a coded message. The antiquary, Mr. Somerton, deduced that these figures were part of Abbot Thomas's original window, and that the Abbot's cryptic answer regarding the treasure pointed directly to the glass itself.

Convinced that the secret lay within the painted figures, Somerton traveled to the chapel. His hypothesis was confirmed: the style matched the era, and another pane displayed the arms of Abbot Thomas von Eschenhausen. The connection was undeniable. The antiquary spent weeks poring over the glass, tracing every mark and inscription, convinced that the Abbot had set a riddle for posterity. The first clue, Job's altered text, clearly referenced hidden wealth. The others remained enigmatic until Somerton returned to his Berkshire manor, determined to crack the code.

On a crisp autumn morning, Mr. Gregory, the Rector of Parsbury, stepped out to greet the postman. Among the mail was a letter bearing a foreign stamp, written in a clumsy but distinctly English hand. It was from William Brown, the confidential valet of Mr. Somerton. The missive was a frantic plea for help: Somerton had suffered a "naasty shock" and was confined to bed. He requested that Gregory travel immediately to Coblentz, then to Steinfeld, to assist him. The letter ended with a postscript correcting the destination: "The Village for Town I will not Turm. It is name Steenfeld."

The arrival of the letter plunged the quiet parsonage into chaos. By evening, Mr. Gregory was aboard a train to Antwerp and then to Coblentz, securing passage to Steinfeld. The town itself was a melancholy place, a small settlement surrounded by ruined abbey buildings, its ancient grandeur stripped away by time and revolution. The inn where Gregory and Brown were lodged was the only option, and Brown was a study in nervous energy, far removed from his usual composure.

Upon entering Somerton's room, they found the antiquary pale and trembling, sitting up in bed. He was relieved to see Gregory but immediately became agitated. "Don't ask me what it is," Somerton urged, gripping Gregory's hand. "I'm not fit to explain yet. It involves keeping something... putting it back. It is perfectly safe in the daytime. Brown will show you tomorrow." He instructed Brown to take Gregory to the spot where the object lay, warning them to keep the matter quiet and to lock the door, as "they" liked it that way. That night, Gregory slept fitfully, convinced he heard fumbling at his locked door, a sensation that haunted him until dawn.

The next morning, Gregory accompanied Brown to the designated location. The task was brief and uneventful, though Gregory was forbidden from asking what they had retrieved. Later that evening, as Somerton recovered his strength, he finally offered the explanation.

Somerton recounted his journey to Steinfeld, driven by the desire to solve the mystery of the painted glass. On his second visit to the chapel, he had meticulously examined the inscribed scrolls. The text on Job's robe, "There is a place for the gold where it is hidden," was the key. However, the other scrolls contained only jumbled letters. A stroke of luck occurred when Lord D——, the chapel's owner, sent a servant to clean the dusty window. As the broom scraped the black borders of the figures' robes, Somerton noticed yellow stains appearing where the pigment had been removed. Beneath the black layer, he discovered clear, yellow capital letters forming a cryptogram.

Each figure had a count of letters scratched on the border: thirty-eight for Job, thirty-nine for John, and forty for Zechariah. Realizing this was a numerical cipher, Somerton worked tirelessly. He noticed that Job held one finger up, John two, and Zechariah three. This was the key: skip one letter, then two, then three. Decoding the jumble revealed the first part of the message: "Decem millia auri reposita sunt in puteo in atrio..." (Ten thousand pieces of gold are laid up in a well in the court...). The remaining letters, when decoded using alternating skips, completed the phrase: "...domus abbatialis de Steinfeld a me, Thoma, qui posui custodem super ea. Gare à qui la touche." (By me, Thomas, who have set a guardian over them. Beware who touches it).

Armed with this knowledge, Somerton and Brown traveled to Steinfeld. They located the Abbot's house and the courtyard well, a structure adorned with Italian marble and biblical carvings. The well was deep, with a spiral staircase descending into the darkness. Somerton descended cautiously, counting the steps. At the thirty-eighth step, he found a patch of masonry that felt smoother than the rest. Striking it with a crowbar, he revealed a slab engraved with a cross composed of seven eyes—the mark of Zechariah.

Removing the slab, Somerton peered into the cavity. The air was foul, but a candle burned steadily. Inside, he felt a heavy, leather-like bundle. As he pulled it toward the entrance, the candle flickered out. Suddenly, Brown screamed and fled up the stairs, leaving Somerton alone in the dark.

In the silence, a terrible form emerged from the shadows. It was not a man, but a grotesque, humanoid entity with a sunken, grinning face and limbs that clung to Somerton's body. The creature smelled of rot and mold, pressing its cold face against Somerton's as it dragged him down into the well. Somerton screamed, clawing at the rope that bound him, until Brown, brave and quick, hauled him back to safety.

Brown later described seeing a "holdish man" with a fallen face laughing at them before vanishing, though no one else was there. The creature had been a guardian, a manifestation of the Abbot's curse, active only in the dark. Somerton spent a nightmare-filled night recovering, plagued by the smell of mold that seemed to emanate from outside his door, confirming that the guardians were creatures of the night.

With the dawn breaking, the horror receded. Gregory and Brown returned to the well, replacing the stone slab and sealing it with mud to restore the facade. Gregory noted a grotesque carving on the well-head labeled "Depositum custodi"—"Keep that which is committed to thee." The treasure remained, guarded by the unseen, waiting for a successor to the riddle of Abbot Thomas.
