Mrs. Lambert's fleeting reverie evaporated as quickly as it had formed. Both Miss Theo and her mother were forced to admit, in the privacy of their hearts, that their idealized hero was merely a fallible human being. They exchanged few words on the matter, yet they understood each other's thoughts with the intuitive bond of deep affection. With an extra tenderness and a specially caressing demeanor toward her daughter, the mother sought to soothe her disappointment. "Never mind, my dear," she whispered, pressing a maternal kiss to her filial cheek. "Our hero has proven to be but an ordinary mortal, and surely no such common man is worthy of my Theo. Thou shalt find a true husband ere long, if fortune permits in England. Why, I was scarce fifteen when your father first beheld me at the Bury Assembly, yet while I was still at school, I vowed I would accept no other suitor. If Heaven grants me such a husband—the finest man in the entire kingdom—surely it will bless my child equally, for she deserves a king if she dares to imagine one!" Indeed, it is possible that Mrs. Lambert, who undoubtedly knew the Prince of Wales's age and recognized his handsome, virtuous nature, harbored a secret fantasy: that he too would ride past her gate, tumble from his steed, be ushered into the house, be cured, and thereby earn a regiment for Martin Lambert from his royal grandfather, all while falling hopelessly in love with Theo.

For his part, the Colonel, along with his second daughter, Miss Hetty, sat firmly on the side of laughter, scorn, and disbelief. Mamma was perpetually engaged in matchmaking. Mrs. Lambert was notably addicted to novels, often reducing her eyes to tears with great diligence over their contents. Not a single coach passed the gate without her expecting a husband to alight, ring the bell, and commence courtship. As for Miss Hetty, her tongue wagged with a sauciness rarely seen; she peppered their conversations with a hundred sly allusions to their unexpected guest. She introduced Prussia and Persia into their discourse with abominable pertness and frequency. She inquired whether the current King of Prussia was known as the Shaw or the Sophy, calculated the distance from Ispahan to Saxony—which His Majesty was supposedly invading—and questioned why Papa was so preoccupied with maps and newspapers regarding such wars? She retrieved the *Persian Tales* from her mother's closet and laid them slyly upon the parlor table where the family sat. "I would not marry a Persian prince for myself," she declared, preferring a gentleman who possessed only one wife at a time. She dubbed our young Virginian, Theo's companion, "Theo's prince." She then asked her mother if she, Hetty, wished to claim the other visitor, the "black prince," for herself. Thus, she relentlessly rallied her sister and mother against their sentimentalities, refusing to cease until she had provoked them into anger; only then would she burst into tears, kiss them violently one after another, and coax them back into good humor.

Simple Harry Warrington, meanwhile, remained oblivious to the jokes, tears, quarrels, reconciliations, and matrimonial schemes for which he served as the innocent catalyst. A hundred allusions to Prussians and Persians were hurled at him, yet those Parthian arrows failed to penetrate his skin at all. Was he a Shaw? A Sophy? He likely believed a Sophy to be a lady and would have deemed it the height of absurdity that a man with a magnificent black beard should bear such a name. We stumble into the midst of a quiet family, dropping like a stone into a still pool; we are perfectly compact and cool, unaware of the flutter and excitement we generate, disturbing the fish, frightening the ducks, and agitating the entire surface of the water. How could Harry know the profound effect his sudden appearance produced in this little, quiet, sentimental circle? He thought quite well of himself on many points, yet he was diffident regarding women, being of that tender age when young gentlemen require encouragement and advancement, having been raised in very modest and primitive relations with the opposite sex. Consequently, Miss Hetty's jokes circled around the lad like summer gnats, which he minded no more. It was not that he was stupid, as she certainly assumed; he was simply simple, too engrossed with himself and his own honest affairs to concern himself with others. Why, what tragedies, comedies, interludes, intrigues, farces, and pantomimes unfold beneath our noses in friends' drawing-rooms where we visit daily, leaving us utterly ignorant, self-satisfied, and blind!

As these sisters sat combing their flowing ringlets of night or conversed within the great bed—a custom of the day wherein they lay together—how could Harry know that he held such a significant share in their thoughts, jokes, and conversation? Three days after his arrival, his new and hospitable friends walked with him through Lord Wrotham's magnificent park, where they were free to roam. Upon reaching a piece of water, they encountered some swans, which the young ladies were accustomed to feeding with bread. As the birds approached the young women, Hetty cast a queer look at her mother and sister, followed by a glance at her father, who stood nearby, honest and happy in a red waistcoat. Then Hetty spoke: "Mamma's swans are something like these, papa."

"What swans, my dear?" asked mamma.

"Something like, but not quite," continued Miss Hetty. "They have shorter necks than these, and there are scores of them on our common. I saw Betty plucking one in the kitchen this morning. We shall have it for dinner, with apple-sauce and—"

"Don't be a little goose!" chided Miss Theo.

"And sage and onions. Do you love swan, Mr. Warrington?"

"I shot three last winter on our river," replied the Virginian gentleman. "Ours are not such white birds as these—they eat very well, though."

The simple youth had the slightest notion that he himself was an allegory at that precise moment, and that Miss Hetty was narrating a fable concerning him. In some exceedingly obscure Latin work I have read, it is noted that long before Virginia was discovered, other peoples were equally dull of comprehension.

Thus, it was a premature sentiment on the part of Miss Theo—that little, tender flutter of the bosom we have acknowledged she felt upon first beholding the Virginian, so handsome, pale, and bleeding. This was not the great passion which she knew her heart was capable of feeling. Like the birds, it had awakened and begun to sing at a false dawn. "Hop back to thy perch, and cover thy head with thy wing, thou tremulous little fluttering creature!" For it is not yet light, and roosting is preferable to singing. Soon, however, morning will arrive, the whole sky will redden, and thou shalt soar up into it to salute the sun with thy music.

One little phrase, some thirty-three lines back, perhaps the fair and suspicious reader noticed: "Three days after his arrival, Harry was walking with," etc. If he could walk—as it appeared he did perfectly well—what business had he to be walking with anyone but Lady Maria Esmond on the Pantiles at Tunbridge Wells? His shoulder was set, his health entirely restored; he lacked even a change of coats, owing to the Colonel's generosity, and was obliged to wear borrowed raiment. Surely a young man in such a condition had no right to linger at Oakhurst and was bound by every tie of duty, convenience, love, and relationship, alongside a gentle heart waiting for him and a washerwoman's final entreaty, to depart for Tunbridge. Why did he remain behind, unless he was in love with either of the young ladies (and we assert he was not)? Could it be that he did not wish to go? Hath the gracious reader understood the meaning of the mystic 'S' with which the last chapter commenced, in which the designer feebly endeavored to depict the notorious Sinbad the Sailor, surmounted by that odious old man of the sea? What if Harry Warrington should be that sailor, and his fate that choking, deadening, inevitable old man? What if, for the past two days, he has felt those knees throttling him round the neck? If his fell aunt's purpose is answered, and if his late love is killed as dead by her poisonous communications as fair Rosamond was by her royal and legitimate rival? Is Hero then lighting the lamp and preparing supper, whilst Leander sits comfortably with some other party, never thinking of taking to the water? Ever since that coward's blow was struck in Lady Maria's back by her own relative, surely kind hearts must pity her ladyship. I know she has faults—yes, and wears false hair and false jewels—but a woman in distress, shall we not pity her? A lady of a certain age, are we to laugh at her because of her years? Between her old aunt and her unhappy delusion, be sure my Lady Maria Esmond is having no very pleasant time at Tunbridge Wells. There is no one to protect her. Madam Beatrix has her all to herself. Lady Maria is poor, hoping for money from her aunt. Lady Maria possesses secrets which the old woman knows and brandishes over her. I, for one, am quite melted and grown soft-hearted as I think of her. Imagine her alone, a victim to that old woman!
