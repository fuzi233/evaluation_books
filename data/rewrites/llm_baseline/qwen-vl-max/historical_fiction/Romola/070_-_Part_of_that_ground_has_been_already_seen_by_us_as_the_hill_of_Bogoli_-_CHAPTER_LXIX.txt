In the quiet stillness of winter’s early hours, Romola lay at rest, her body freed from exhaustion, her mind wandering through memory and into the uncertain reaches of what was to come. From this new vantage point, the world appeared transformed—its shapes altered, its meanings deepened. The journey she had just completed, since awakening in that small boat drifting on unknown waters, had left an imprint as sharp and enduring as a seal pressed into wax. Once, she had felt unmoored—no purpose, no tether, only a hollow resentment toward life itself. She had whispered to herself, “I am weary of living; I wish only to die.” That plea had echoed softly in her sleep. But the moment she awoke and heard the cry that drew her, something shifted within her. She did not pause to reflect, as she once had in Florence, that life was worth keeping because it allowed her to ease another’s pain. No—she simply *lived*, driven by an urgent need to respond, to act, to reach out where help was called for. The reasons for enduring, for laboring, for caring, no longer needed justification—they were instinct, necessity, truth.

It was as though she had been reborn.

In Florence, human bonds had always been entangled with complex obligations—marriage, civic duty, religious devotion. When those ties failed, when trust dissolved, the blow had seemed so great that it severed her from the world, numbing her compassion. But now she saw clearly: “How could I have ever wished death? If all else is uncertain, suffering is real—and I can ease it. If the cross holds no divine glory, then sorrow is all the more genuine. While my arm has strength, I will stretch it to the fainting. While my eyes can see, they will seek the forgotten.”

And with that realization, the past surged forward—not as a burden, but as a summons. Her work in this green valley was finished. The love and gratitude she had given here, once scattered among the people, now flowed back into the deeper currents of her own history—into the memories of Florence, of home, of those who had shaped her soul. For the first time, she stood apart from her old life, seeing it whole and unfiltered. In that rare clarity, self-examination came like a flood. A conscience attuned to others’ pain began to question her own actions. Had she been hasty? Proud? Always demanding virtue from others while failing to uphold the ideals she once believed in?

She reproached herself for fleeing—her second departure from Florence. Was it not cowardice, disguised as principle? Savonarola had once welcomed her back, not out of sentiment, but because he saw in her a deeper truth than she herself had recognized. His grounds were stronger, rooted in a faith that endured beyond personal comfort. How could she claim to feel the needs of strangers and yet ignore the deepest need—the one closest to her heart?

Yet even as guilt stirred, it was met with resistance. The memory of Tito returned—of their marriage, of the impossibility of true union beneath the façade of shared life. The duties imposed upon her were not love, but lies. She had been trapped between loyalty and integrity, between affection and moral revolt. And flight had been the only way out. All minds, unless dulled by indifference, must wrestle with such contradictions. Life does not allow easy substitutions. The presence of new bonds does not erase the brokenness of old ones. The wound remains—until healed, it calls again and again. Conscience never forgets.

Romola dreaded returning near Tito, yet she also feared being too far away to know his fate. What if he suffered? What if he needed her? A quiet ache remained—a remnant of Fra Girolamo’s words: “You cannot cease to be a wife.” Could anything truly be erased from the blood, from the heart? Once woven into the fabric of her being, could love or obligation ever be wholly undone?

Florence rose in her thoughts like hunger. It was not the future she craved, nor the unknown—it was the familiar. Her emotions fed on memory, on place, on people known and loved. The idea that she might never return grew heavier with each silent hour. What if Fra Girolamo had erred? What if Florence, with all its flaws and contradictions, was not so corrupt after all? Was she, then, elevated above it, entitled to turn her back and declare it unworthy? But if she were truly higher, would she not have held fast to her faith, instead of letting it falter?

Her grief for her godfather softened, replaced by a renewed sense of debt. Nothing could undo the fact that Savonarola had inspired her, awakened a new life within her. Who else had touched her soul so deeply? His mistakes might bring disaster—but could she abandon him because of them? She could not rest.

She scarcely knew whether it was spring’s return stirring her energy, or the burning desire to draw nearer to Florence. She did not imagine entering the city—yet the longing to be close enough to learn its fate consumed her. It drove her forward, eclipsing every other thought.

Then, on a March morning, the villagers gathered to bid farewell to the blessed Lady. Jacopo had brought a mule for her journey, and he would accompany her over the mountains. The Padre, too, would walk with her to the nearest town, guiding her toward the safest path to Pistoja. Her trinkets and money, untouched during her stay, were more than sufficient for her needs.

Had her heart not been pulled so strongly toward the distant city, this moment would have been unbearable. As she walked down the village street for the last time, the Padre and Jacopo waited near the well with the mule. People knelt before her, weeping, kissing her hands, clinging to her robe, begging her not to go.

“Ah, why leave us now, when the season turns fair and the fields will bloom?” they cried.
“Do not grieve,” Romola said gently. “You are well now. I shall remember you always. I must go—to see if my own people need me.”
“But if they suffer pestilence?”
“Look at us again, Madonna!”
“Yes, yes—we will care for little Benedetto!”

At last, she mounted the mule. But as she turned away, Benedetto let out a piercing cry—his small voice breaking the silence. It became the signal for everyone to follow, insisting he ride on the mule’s neck until the foot of the slope. The parting could not be delayed. Yet as Romola rode onward, she turned again and again, watching the little group linger, their faces raised, their hands waving—each gesture a final thread of connection, slowly unraveling into the distance.
