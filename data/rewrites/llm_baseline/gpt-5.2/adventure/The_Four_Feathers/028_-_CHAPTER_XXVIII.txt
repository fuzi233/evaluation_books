For three days Harry Feversham’s mind ran like water spilled on hot stone—hissing, vanishing, returning in sudden pools of sense and then scattering again. Through those days, and through three nights when the House of Stone coughed out its hoarse cries and clanking chains, Trench kept him alive with the stubborn patience of a man basting a joint through a long, mean fire: fetching Nile water in an earthen bowl, breaking his own scant ration, dampening Feversham’s cracked lips, and, when the gate shut and the roof of horror pressed down, standing with Ibrahim to take blows meant for the new-comer.

On the fourth morning Feversham woke cleanly, as if the fever had at last boiled down and left only a bitter crust behind. He opened his eyes and saw Trench’s face bending over him.

At first it seemed part of the sickness—one of those nightmare masks that swell in a child’s dark until they fill the world. Feversham lifted a weak arm and thrust at it, half in fear, half in anger. But his hand met warm flesh; the face did not dissolve. He blinked, and the hard blue sky steadied into place above him; the brown, trampled soil, peppered with droppings and dried scraps, took its true shape; the prisoners dragging chains or lying as still as empty sacks regained their terrible meaning.

He turned sharply, caught at Trench’s sleeve as if the next blink might snatch him away, and then—astonishingly—smiled.

“I am in the prison at Omdurman,” he said. “Actually in the prison. Umm Hagar—the House of Stone. It seems too good to be true.”

He leaned back against the wall with an exhale of relief so deep it sounded like gratitude.

To Trench, the satisfaction rang at first like sarcasm. It was a tone a man might use who prided himself on being past pain and pleasure, who wore indifference as another wears a well-cut coat. Yet Feversham was no such man; his delirium had proved it. The relief was genuine: Dongola’s danger behind him, Trench found, Omdurman reached. Better this enclosure than a gibbet far down the Nile with vultures balancing on his shoulders and the one purpose of his life left undone.

“You have been here a long while,” Feversham said, and his eyes searched Trench’s face the way a cook reads a bruise on fruit.

“Three years.”

Feversham looked round the thorn zareeba, the wedge of shade, the bodies in rags, the dust that never settled.

“Three years of it,” he murmured. “I was afraid I might not find you alive.”

Trench nodded without pride. “The nights are the worst. The nights in there.” He jerked his head towards the squat building of stone. “It’s a wonder any man lives through a week, and yet I have lived through a thousand nights.” Even to him, saying it made endurance seem like something unreal. “A thousand nights of the House of Stone.”

“But we may go down to the Nile by daytime,” Feversham said, and tried to lift himself with sudden alarm, as though liberty might be a rumour that could evaporate. His eyes went to the hedge of thorns. “Surely we are allowed so much. An Arab at Wadi Halfa told me.”

“And it’s true.” Trench pointed to the earthen bowl by his side, damp at the rim. “I filled that this morning.”

“I must go,” Feversham insisted, and forced himself upright. “I must go this morning.”

His voice rose, sharp with the kind of eagerness that makes men careless. Trench’s hand clamped his arm.

“Hush. There are many prisoners here,” he whispered, “and among them many tale-bearers.”

Feversham sank back, partly from weakness, partly from the warning.

“But they cannot understand what we say.”

“They can see that we talk together—earnestly. Idris would know within the hour, and the Khalifa before sunset. Then there would be heavier fetters, and the courbatch.” Trench’s eyes went briefly to the gaolers’ whips, rhinoceros-hide, coiled like snakes. “Lie still. You are weak. I am tired. We will sleep. Later we go together.”

Trench lay down beside him and was asleep almost at once, as if his body had been waiting for permission to drop. Feversham watched him. In the loosened lines of the sleeping face he read the three years plainly: the cheeks sharpened, the mouth set as though it had long chewed grit, the skin drawn tight as dried hide. Noon was creeping near when Trench woke.

“There is no one to bring you food?” he asked.

“A boy should come,” Feversham answered. “He should bring news as well.”

They waited. When the gate opened and the friends and wives of prisoners crowded in, the enclosure turned at once into a feeding-pen without grass. The gaolers took their share first, fingers quick as rats. What remained was mostly aseeda—the town’s staple: dhurra pounded to a heavy paste, shaped into damp, yielding lumps, bland as mud and as necessary as breath. Its smell—sour grain and human hands—rose at once; even hunger has an odour, and here it was everywhere.

Men thin as stripped reeds hopped and lurched towards the entrance as fast as chains allowed. Groups fought groups—four against three, strong against weak—with a blind, animal purpose. A man stumbled, went down hard, and, once fallen, lay flat in a dull surrender, knowing the day’s portion had passed him by. Others grabbed at the messengers and tore the food away even as the courbatch cracked on their backs. There were thirty gaolers, each with his whip, but this was the single moment when the whip seemed neither feared nor, for sheer starvation, felt.

Among the food-bearers, a boy tried to hide behind taller bodies and looked about with the uncertainty of someone carrying contraband. He was spotted. A fist knocked him down; his bundle was snatched; his hands were suddenly empty. But the boy had lungs, and his screams dragged Idris-es-Saier himself through the press.

“For whom do you come?” Idris demanded, shoving prisoners aside with the impatience of a man separating dogs from a carcass.

“For Joseppi, the Greek,” the boy cried.

Idris’s eyes flicked to the corner where Feversham lay, and he pointed. The boy crawled and then rose, pushing forward with palms held out, empty, as if to show he had been robbed. He came close enough to Feversham to seem merely continuing his explanation—lips moving, voice low—then, with a small, practised motion, loosened the gazelle-skin pouch hidden beneath his jibbeh and let it slip to the ground beside Feversham’s hip.

The pouch was warm. Inside, folded tight as a secret, was a chicken.

In that place, where dhurra paste kept men alive like a grudged ration of air, the smell of meat was like a memory made physical. Trench and Feversham ate in quick, careful bites, tearing flesh from bone with the neatness of men who know how much noise hunger makes. The meat tasted of dust and smoke and the animal’s own sweetness; it tasted, above all, of fat—of life stored up—and it struck their mouths with a richness so sudden it was almost painful, like music heard after long silence.

That chicken served them for breakfast and dinner and supper.

An hour later permission came for them to pass out of the zareeba and go down to the Nile. They walked slowly, halting often. Feversham’s legs shook under him; Trench’s strength, worn into stubborn cord, dragged him onward.

During one halt Trench said, “We can talk here.”

Below them, at the water’s edge, prisoners unloaded dhows; others waded knee-deep, paddling in mud the colour of old broth. The shore was crowded with men shouting and yelling—sound without meaning, a constant tearing at the ears. The gaolers were within sight, but the noise and distance kept words from carrying.

“Yes,” Feversham said. “We can talk here.”

Trench looked at him. “Why have you come?”

“I was captured in the desert, on the Arbain road,” Feversham answered slowly, choosing each word as though it might be weighed.

Trench’s mouth twitched. “Yes—masquerading as a lunatic musician who wandered out of Wadi Halfa with a zither. I know. But you were captured by your own deliberate wish. You came to join me in Omdurman.”

“How do you know?”

“You told me.” Trench’s voice stayed low, but there was an odd strain in it, as if he could not decide whether to be grateful or offended. “During the last three days you have told me much.”

Feversham’s head turned sharply. Alarm ran across his face. “Very much?”

“Very much.” Trench paused. “You came because five years ago I sent you a white feather.”

Feversham’s throat tightened. “And was that all I told you?”

“No.” Trench dragged the word out like a chain. He sat up, clasped his head between his hands, and stared at the river so that he did not have to see Feversham’s eyes. “You spoke of a girl—the same girl you spoke of in London, when Willoughby and Durrance and I dined with you. I know her name now—her Christian name. She was with you when the feathers came. I had not thought of that. She gave you a fourth feather.” His voice sank. “I am sorry.”

Silence lay between them, thick and hot as the still air before a storm. At last Feversham answered, slowly, as if tasting the truth before speaking it.

“For my part, I am not sorry. I mean—I am not sorry she was present. I think, on the whole, I am rather glad. She gave me the fourth feather, yes, and I am glad of that too. Without her presence, without that fourth feather snapped from her fan, I might have given up there and then. Who knows? I doubt if I could have stood up to the three years in Suakin.”

Trench turned his head a fraction, listening like a man hearing a tune he once despised and now cannot forget.

“I used to see you and Durrance and Willoughby,” Feversham went on, “and men who had once been my friends. You were all going about the work I was used to. You cannot think how the routine of a regiment—routine I cursed when it belonged to me—came to seem like bread in a famine. I could have slipped on a boat to Suez. I could have run away easily enough.” His mouth tightened. “But the chance I waited for never came—for three years.”

“You saw us?” Trench asked, and the question held both wonder and hurt. “And you gave no sign?”

“How would you have taken it if I had?” Feversham asked quietly.

Trench was silent.

“No,” Feversham continued. “I saw you, but I was careful you should not see me. And I doubt I could have endured it without remembering that night at Ramelton—without the feel of the fourth feather to keep the memory sharp. Without it I should never have gone down from Obak into Berber. I should never have joined you in Omdurman.”

“She would be glad to hear you say that,” Trench said, and the words came out before he could soften them. “I have no doubt she is sorry about her fourth feather—sorry as I am about the other three.”

“There is no reason she should be sorry,” Feversham replied. “Nor you either. I do not blame you—or her.”

They both looked towards the river. The air was shrill with cries. The shore swarmed with Arabs and negroes in robes of blue, yellow, and dirty brown; dhows bumped and scraped; loads thudded; men cursed and laughed; a constant racket rose and fell like waves. Across the water, beyond the fork, the palms of Khartum stood black against the cloudless sky. The sun was moving down behind them, and with it crept the knowledge that in a few hours the House of Stone would take them back.

Yet their minds, at the same time, were far from mud and shouting. They were under elms by the Lennon River; they were in a hall where a door stood open to the cool night; they heard the soft echo of a waltz; they saw three white feathers on the floor between a man and a girl. One of them remembered it. The other imagined it. To both, it was equally vivid—and in that vividness lay a kind of hunger too, not for meat but for home, for the old language of familiar places.

Feversham smiled at last. “Perhaps she has now seen Willoughby. Perhaps she has taken his feather.”

Trench held out his hand. “I will take mine back now.”

Feversham shook his head. “No—not yet.”

Trench’s face brightened, abruptly, like a lamp sheltered from wind. A hope that had struggled in him through three days and nights—fought down, for fear it should be false—sprang up alive.

“Not yet—then you have a plan.”

Feversham’s own face tightened with a new fear. “I said nothing of it,” he pleaded. “Tell me that. When I was delirious in the prison there—did I say nothing? I told you of the four feathers, I told you of Ethne, but of the plan—did I breathe a word?”

“Not a single word,” Trench replied. “So I doubted, and did not dare to believe.”

Feversham’s breath left him with relief so sharp it was almost a laugh. His hand trembled on Trench’s arm.

“You see, if I spoke of that in the House of Stone,” he said, “I might have spoken of it in Dongola. For in Dongola as well I was delirious. But I didn’t, you say—not here, at all events. So perhaps not there either.” He swallowed. “I was afraid I should. There was a woman in Dongola who spoke some English—very little, but enough. She had been in the kauneesa of Khartum when Gordon ruled there. She was sent to question me. I had unhappy times in Dongola.”

“I know,” Trench murmured. “You told me things which made me shiver.” He caught Feversham’s arm, shoved back the loose sleeve. The wrists were scarred, the skin ridged and shiny where the bonds and the lash had done their work.

Feversham pulled the sleeve down. “I felt myself getting light-headed there,” he went on. “I made up my mind I must let no hint slip of your escape. So when I felt my mind going, I tried to think of something else—with all my might.” He gave a small, strained laugh. “That was why you heard me talk of Ethne.”

But Trench had barely listened to the last. He was staring out at the water, as if already he could see it turn into a road.

“So it is true,” he said in a quiet, wondering voice. “There will be a morning when we shall not drag ourselves out of the House of Stone. There will be nights when we shall sleep in beds—actually in beds.” He hesitated, shy as a man confessing a tenderness. “There will be—something more.”

He stood up abruptly. “We have sat here too long. Let us go forward.”

They moved nearer to the river and sat again, closer to the crowd so their low talk might vanish into the general roar.

“You have more than hope,” Trench pressed. “You have a plan?”

“More than a plan,” Feversham said. “The preparations are made. There are camels waiting in the desert—ten miles west of Omdurman.”

“Now?” Trench’s whisper cracked with disbelief. “Now?”

“Yes—now. There are rifles and ammunition buried near them. Provisions. Water. We travel by Metemneh, where fresh camels wait; from Metemneh to Berber. There we cross the Nile; camels are waiting five miles from Berber. From Berber we ride in over the Kokreb pass to Suakin.”

“When?” Trench demanded, and the single word held three years’ thirst in it.

“When I have strength enough to sit a horse for ten miles,” Feversham answered, “and a camel for a week.”

“How soon?”

“Not long,” Feversham said. “Not long, Trench. I promise you.” He rose with care, and as he did he murmured, “As you get up, glance round.”

Trench did, casually, as if following a movement in the crowd.

“You will see a man in a blue linen dress,” Feversham said, “loitering between us and the gaol. As we came past, he made me a sign. I did not return it. I shall return it on the day we escape.”

“He will wait?”

“For a month. We must manage, on one night during that month, to escape from the House of Stone. We can signal him to bring help. A passage might be made through the wall in one night; the stones are loosely built.”

They walked to the water’s edge. Among the shouting, shoulder to shoulder with labouring men, they spoke again of escape with faces composed into the expression of idle amusement, as though their words were no more dangerous than a jest.

“There is a better way than breaking through the wall,” Trench said, and he let out a laugh to cover the sentence. He pointed, as though entertained, at a prisoner with a load on his back who had fallen face-first into the river. Fetters and weight pinned him; he half rose, then soused down again, splashing mud and water, struggling like a man drowning in shallow soup.

“There is a better way,” Trench repeated. “You have money?”

“Ai, ai!” Feversham shouted, roaring with laughter at the man’s second collapse, playing his part in the noisy theatre. “I have some concealed on me. Idris took what I did not conceal.”

“Good,” Trench said. “Idris will come to you to-day or to-morrow. He will talk of Allah’s goodness in bringing you out of the wicked world to the holy city of Omdurman. He will tell you at length about the peril of your soul and the only means of averting it. Then he will finish with a few significant sentences about his starving family. If you come to the aid of his starving family—and bid him keep fifteen dollars out of what he took—you may get permission to sleep in the zareeba outside the prison. Be content with that for a night or two. Then he will come again. Again you will assist his starving family, and this time you will ask permission for me to sleep in the open too.” Trench’s laugh rose again, but his eyes had gone hard with calculation. “Come—there’s Idris shepherding us home.”

It happened as Trench had foretold, as if Idris were following a recipe learned by heart. That afternoon Idris gave Feversham a lecture of uncommon length. Feversham learned that now God loved him. He heard, for the first time, how Hicks Pasha’s army had been destroyed—by holy angels, so the story ran, without a single shot fired, without a single spear thrown by the Mahdi’s soldiers. The spears, it was said, had flown from their hands by angelic guidance and pierced the unbelievers. He heard of a most convenient spirit, Nebbi Khiddr, who was the Khalifa’s eyes and ears and reported all that went on in the gaol. Idris made sure Feversham understood that if Nebbi Khiddr reported against him, heavier shackles would be riveted on, and other unpleasant things would follow.

At last came the familiar turn to the starving children. Feversham listened with the meekness expected of him and then begged Idris to take fifteen dollars.

That night Feversham slept in the open zareeba outside the prison walls. Two nights later Trench lay down beside him. Overhead the sky was clear and the stars burned with a fierce steadiness, as if the desert had scrubbed the darkness clean. After the stone-roofed stench of Umm Hagar, the night air felt almost like food: cool, sharp, filling the lungs with something that tasted of distance.

“Only three more days,” Feversham whispered.

He heard Trench draw in a long breath beside him, slow and hungry, like a man smelling bread from an oven far down a street.

For a while they lay without speaking. From within the House of Stone came muffled shouts and yells—sound trapped by walls, turned dull and savage. Here, outside, the world’s noises changed character. The Nile’s presence could be felt even when it could not be seen; the night wind moved in the thorn hedge with a faint hiss like dry grain poured into a sack. In a place where the day was one continuous clamour of commerce and cruelty, those small natural sounds were mercy, and they soothed something in the mind that chains could not reach.

Trench spoke at last.

“Are you awake?”

“Yes.”

“Well—” He hesitated. “Each man has his weak spot of sentiment, I suppose. I have mine. I am not a marrying man, so it’s not sentiment of that kind. Perhaps you will laugh.” His voice tightened. “It isn’t merely that I loathe this squalid, shadeless, vile town, or the horrors of its prison. It isn’t merely that I hate the emptiness of the desert. It isn’t merely that I am sick of the palms of Khartum, or these chains, or the whips. It’s something more.”

He drew breath again, and the confession came out as simply as a truth in an old dialect, unpolished but exact.

“I want to die at home. I have been desperately afraid so often that I should die here. I want to die at home—not merely in my own country, but in my own village—and be buried under the trees I know, in sight of the church and the houses I know, and the trout stream where I fished when I was a boy.” He gave a small, self-mocking sound. “You’ll laugh, no doubt.”

Feversham did not laugh. The words struck him with a strange familiarity—like a phrase once heard, half forgotten, and now returning with its whole weight. He knew why: it was the kind of longing Ethne Eustace might have spoken, though she had never said it to him in those exact syllables. It was not a longing you could translate into the loud, thin talk of Omdurman without losing what made it human.

“No,” Feversham answered quietly. “I am not laughing. I understand.”

There was warmth in his tone that surprised Trench. From that night a real friendship began between them—something made not of shared jokes or old club manners, but of shared deprivation and the unguarded naming of what mattered.

They lay side by side, speaking in low voices. Inside the prison the muffled uproar went on, and, by contrast, the open air seemed almost comfortable. They could breathe; they could see; no low roof pressed on their faces. Before morning the cold would sharpen and wake them to shiver in their rags, and they would huddle together in their corner like men guarding a last ember. But for now they lay on their backs, hands clasped behind their heads, and watched the great stars and planets burn in the blue dome.

“It will be strange to find them dim and small again,” Trench said.

“There will be compensations,” Feversham answered with a brief laugh, and they began to make plans—of what they would do after the desert, after the Mediterranean, after Europe, back in their own country of softer skies. The simplest phrases conjured whole worlds: hedgerows, wet grass, the smell of smoke from a kitchen chimney, the particular hush that falls on a village at dusk when the loudest sound is water over stones.

They were no longer prisoners in that barbarous town that lay like a murky stain upon the wide spaces of sand. In their minds they were at home, following old pursuits. They stood outside clumps of trees with guns in their hands while the sharp cry “Mark! Mark!” rang out—clean, precise sound, not the senseless yelling of the riverbank. Trench heard again the unmistakable rattle of the reel of his fishing-rod as he wound in line upon the bank of his trout stream. They talked of theatres in London and the last plays they had seen and the last books they had read—six years ago, as if six years were only a pause between pages.

“There goes the Great Bear,” Trench said suddenly. “It is late.”

The tail of the constellation dipped behind the thorn hedge.

They turned over on their sides.

“Three more days,” Trench whispered.

“Only three more days,” Feversham replied.

And in a minute they were neither in England nor the Soudan. The stars marched on towards morning unnoticed above their heads, and the two men, at last, slipped into the pleasant countries of sleep.
