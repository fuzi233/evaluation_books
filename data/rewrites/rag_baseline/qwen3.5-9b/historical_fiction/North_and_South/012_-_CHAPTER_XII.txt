The morning light filtered through the high windows of the Thornton parlor, illuminating dust motes that danced in the stagnant air, much like the unresolved tension between mother and son. Mr. Thornton had spent considerable effort attempting to elevate his mother to a plane of mere civility, a task akin to polishing a stone that naturally preferred to remain rough. Mrs. Thornton rarely ventured beyond the sanctuary of her own home, and when she did, she approached social obligations with the gravity of a military campaign. Her son had provided a carriage, yet she had steadfastly refused to allow him to maintain horses for it; for solemn occasions alone warranted the hiring of vehicles, ensuring that every step of her departure and arrival was marked by a deliberate, temporary expenditure.

For three days, indeed nearly a fortnight prior, she had ridden out in borrowed steeds, systematically exhausting her circle of acquaintances until they were left breathless, forcing them to incur similar expenses in their attempts to reciprocate. Yet, Crampton lay too distant for her weary feet, prompting repeated inquiries from her regarding whether her desire to visit the Hales was robust enough to justify the cost of a cab. She harbored a secret relief that it might not be; as she reasoned aloud, "Why should I cultivate friendships and intimacies with every teacher and master in Milton? Next, he will expect me to call upon Fanny's dancing-master's wife!"

"And so I would, mother," John countered gently, "were Mr. Mason and his wife friendless in this strange land, much as the Hales are."

"Oh! Do not speak so hastily," she retorted, her composure cracking slightly. "I am departing tomorrow. My sole intention was to ensure you understood the logistics."

"If you are going tomorrow, I shall order the horses."

"Nonsense, John. One would think you were carved from pure gold."

"Not quite, yet. However, regarding the horses, I am resolved. Recall the last time you rode in a cab; you returned home suffering from a severe headache caused by the violent jolting."

"I never complained of it, I assure you."

"No! My mother is not a creature of complaint," he declared, a flicker of pride brightening his features. "Precisely because you do not complain, I have all the greater responsibility to safeguard your health. Consider Fanny there; a measure of hardship would prove beneficial to her constitution."

"She is not forged from the same iron as you, John. She could not withstand such rigors."

Mrs. Thornton fell silent, her previous words striking a chord that mortified her. She possessed an unconscious disdain for weakness, particularly in those areas where both she and her son displayed formidable strength. Mrs. Thornton was not a woman inclined toward prolonged introspection or debate; her rapid judgment and unwavering resolve substituted for endless internal deliberation. Instinctively, she perceived that nothing could fortify Fanny to endure trials with patience or confront obstacles with courage. Though she winced inwardly at this admission concerning her daughter, it transformed into a specific, pitying tenderness in her demeanor—the exact manner in which mothers treat their frail, sickly offspring.

To a casual observer, Mrs. Thornton's behavior might have suggested a far deeper reservoir of love for Fanny than for John. Such a conclusion, however, would have been profoundly erroneous. The boldness with which mother and son articulated unpalatable truths to one another demonstrated a reliance on the solid bedrock of their shared souls. In contrast, the uneasy tenderness Mrs. Thornton displayed toward her daughter, coupled with the shameful attempt to conceal Fanny's poverty beneath the grandeur of her own innate qualities—which she held in such high esteem in others—betrayed a lack of a secure foundation for her affection. She never addressed her son by any name other than "John"; terms of endearment like "love" and "dear" were exclusively reserved for Fanny. Yet, her heart gave thanks for her son day and night, and she strode proudly among the women of Milton solely for his sake.

"Fanny dear! I shall arrange for horses to the carriage today to visit these Hales. Pray, go and see Nurse; it lies in the same direction, and she is invariably delighted to see you. You might proceed there while I attend to Mrs. Hale."

"Oh! Mamma, it is such a long way, and I am so exhausted."

"With what?" Mrs. Thornton queried, her brow furrowing slightly.

"I do not know—the weather, I suppose. It feels so... relaxing."

"Could you not request that Nurse come here? The carriage could fetch her, allowing her to spend the remainder of the day here, which I am certain she would appreciate."

Mrs. Thornton remained silent, laying her embroidery down upon the table as if contemplating a difficult equation. "It will be a considerable distance for her to walk back at night," she finally remarked.

"Oh, but I shall send her home in a cab. I never considered her walking."

At that precise moment, Mr. Thornton entered, just before his departure for the mill. "Mother! I scarcely need remind you that if there is any trivial matter that could serve Mrs. Hale as an invalid, you will certainly offer it, I am sure."

"If I can ascertain what it is, I will. However, having never been ill myself, I am not particularly versed in the whims of invalids."

"Well! Here stands Fanny, who is seldom free from some ailment. She ought to be able to suggest something, perhaps—will you not, Fan?"

"I do not always suffer from ailments," Fanny replied pettishly. "And I am not accompanying Mother. I have a headache today, and I shall not go out."

Mr. Thornton appeared annoyed. His mother's gaze was fixed intently on her needlework, where she stitched away with industrious fervor. "Fanny! I require you to go," he stated authoritatively. "It will benefit you rather than harm you. You will oblige me by complying, without further discussion."

He departed abruptly from the room immediately after issuing this command. Had he lingered even a moment longer, Fanny would have burst into tears at his tone of authority, even when he utilized the phrase, "You will oblige me." As it stood, she merely grumbled. "John always speaks as though I imagine I am ill, and I am certain I never indulge in such fantasies. Who are these Hales that he makes such a fuss about?"

"Fanny, do not speak disparagingly of your brother. He possesses valid reasons of some kind, or he would not wish us to visit. Hasten and prepare your things."

Yet, the brief altercation between her son and her daughter failed to soften Mrs. Thornton's disposition toward "these Hales." Her jealous heart replayed her daughter's query, "Who are they, that he is so eager for us to pay them such attention?" It resurfaced like a persistent refrain in a song, long after Fanny had forgotten the incident amidst the pleasant excitement of admiring a new bonnet in the mirror.

Mrs. Thornton was inherently shy. Only in recent years had she possessed sufficient leisure to engage with society, and even then, she found little joy in it. She derived satisfaction from hosting dinners and critiquing the culinary efforts of others. However, the act of initiating acquaintance with strangers was a vastly different endeavor. She felt profoundly ill-at-ease, appearing more stern and forbidding than usual as she crossed the threshold into the Hales' modest drawing-room.

Margaret was diligently embroidering a small piece of cambric intended for an article of dress for Edith's anticipated baby—"Flimsy, useless work," Mrs. Thornton chided silently. She preferred Mrs. Hale's double knitting far more; that craft possessed a sensible utility. The room was cluttered with knick-knacks that demanded extensive dusting, and for people of limited means, time was synonymous with currency. These observations swirled through her mind as she engaged in her stately discourse with Mrs. Hale, uttering the stereotypical platitudes that most people recite with their senses effectively blindfolded.

Mrs. Hale, however, exerted considerably more energy in her responses, captivated by a genuine piece of antique lace Mrs. Thornton wore. "Lace," she later noted to Dixon, "of that old English point which has not been manufactured for seventy years, nor can it be purchased. It must be an heirloom, proving she possessed ancestors." Consequently, the owner of this ancestral treasure deemed herself worthy of more than the languid exertion required to be agreeable to a visitor, which would otherwise have been the limit of Mrs. Hale's conversational efforts.

Presently, Margaret, straining her brain to converse with Fanny, overheard her mother and Mrs. Hale descending into the interminable topic of domestic service.

"I presume you are not musical," Fanny remarked, noting the absence of a piano.

"I have a fondness for hearing fine music; I cannot play well myself. Furthermore, Papa and Mama do not hold it in high regard, so we sold our old instrument upon arriving here."

"I wonder how you manage to exist without one. It almost seems a necessity of life."

"Fifteen shillings a week, with three saved from the proceeds!" Margaret thought bitterly. "She must have been very young. She likely has forgotten her own personal experience, yet she must recall those days."

A distinct chill permeated Margaret's manner when she responded next. "You host excellent concerts here, I believe."
