“Within the gentle heart Love shelters him,
As birds within the green shade of the grove.
Before the gentle heart, in Nature’s scheme,
Love was not, nor the gentle heart ere Love.”
—GUIDO GUINICELLI (Rossetti’s Translation)

The small, sun-dappled Meyrick parlor hummed with quiet routine that Wednesday afternoon: Mab fumbled her way through a wobbly rendition of *Lascia ch’io pianga*, Mirah’s soft fingers guiding the accompaniment from the piano bench beside her, and Mrs. Meyrick twisted her knitting needles while she waited for her son to arrive. Hans Meyrick never missed bringing the *Times* on Wednesdays — he would joke that it was for his mother’s news habit, that she devoured everything from Continental politics to the marriage notices, claiming the latter let her finish all the fashionable novels without wading through three hundred pages of silly drama to see the happy ending. But everyone who knew him well guessed the real reason: he timed his arrival exactly for the end of Mirah’s weekly music lesson with Mab, so he could linger and listen to her sing.

That day, though, he burst through the door on his latchkey far louder than usual, shaking the crackling newspaper high above his head, interrupting Mab’s wobbly aria mid-phrase. The piano cut off abruptly. Mirah jumped as if the sharp rustle of the paper had been thunder, turning wide eyes toward him, while Mab groaned.
“O-o-o, Hans! You’re making a worse racket than my bad singing!”
Mrs. Meyrick set her knitting down, leaning forward. “What is it? News from Italy? Have the Austrians given up Venice at last?”
“Nothing about Italian independence — but news *from* Italy,” Hans said, his tone thick with the kind of giddy, secretive certainty that came when a long-dormant private suspicion was suddenly proven true. “Not bad news for anyone we care about, I’d say. Uncommonly lucky, in fact. I’ve never heard of anyone dying so conveniently before.”
“Not bad news about Deronda?” Mrs. Meyrick asked, instantly anxious, and Mirah’s heart seized tight in her chest, the same fear coiling cold in her bones.
“Hardly,” Hans laughed, tapping a paragraph on the front page. “Duke Alfonso — Grandcourt, that is — drowned off the coast of Genoa. The Duchess is alive. It seems she jumped right in after him, and the fishermen pulled her out in time, no harm done. And Deronda? He was staying in the very same hotel. Saw them drag her out of the water, was there to take care of her the whole time. What a stroke of luck for the man.”
Mirah sank back onto the piano stool, her eyelids fluttering shut, her hands clasped so tight her knuckles went white.
“Poor thing,” Mrs. Meyrick murmured, soft with sympathy. “She must have loved her husband terribly, to risk her own life like that.”
“More of an absent-minded lapse, if you ask me,” Hans grinned, flopping into the chair nearest Mirah, his tone still teasing. “Who could ever grow fond of a man like that? A jealous, glowering brute, always throwing icy asides like a baritone villain in some bad opera. He got exactly what was coming to him, and now the Duchess is free to marry a man with a full head of hair and a gaze that melts instead of freezes. I’ll put money on an invitation to their wedding before the year’s out.”

Mirah shot to her feet then, her face deathly pale — the usual rose bloom of her lips gone stark white — and her eyes blazed at Hans with a fury none of them had ever seen on her gentle face.
“Mr. Hans, you ought not to speak that way,” she said, her voice shaking with suppressed rage. “Mr. Deronda would never approve of such jokes. Why do you call this lucky? Why do you toss words about life and death around as if they are nothing, when what is deliverance to one person is ruin to another? How do you know it would be a blessing for him to love Mrs. Grandcourt? It could be a terrible harm. She would take him away from my brother. I know she would. Do you think Mr. Deronda would count it luck to break Ezra’s heart?”

The whole room went silent. Hans stared at her, his face flushing bright crimson as if he were a shy girl caught in a misdeed, and he stammered, “I’m a fool. A brute. I take every single word back. I’ll go hang myself like Judas, if that’s what it takes to make amends.” Even in his flustered remorse, he could not keep a flicker of drollery from his words, but Mirah’s anger did not soften. She had snapped the way wounded creatures bite through their own flesh to make agony bearable, and no one dared speak until she turned back to the piano, her shoulders set, and smoothed out the sheet music in front of her.
“Mirah is perfectly right to scold you,” Mab said, tossing a pillow at her brother. “You’re always taking Mr. Deronda’s name in vain with those silly jokes. It’s horrible to speak of him marrying that woman like it’s some trivial amusement.”
“Quite right,” Hans mumbled, turning to stare out the back window, not daring to meet his mother’s eyes.

Mirah lifted her voice, steady now, and turned to Mab. “We still have time for your lesson. Would you like to try that aria again, or shall I sing it for you?”
“Please sing it,” Mab said, eager to let the tension dissolve.

Mirah sang *Lascia ch’io pianga* then, pouring every unspoken, unnameable ache into the notes, the melody swelling with sobs she would never let fall, her voice fuller and more raw than Mab had ever heard it. When the last chord rang out, she stood up quickly. “I must go home now. Ezra is waiting for me.”
She pressed Mrs. Meyrick’s hand, not daring to lean in for her usual goodnight kiss, too conscious that she had been too sharp, too ungrateful in her anger. But the little mother pulled her face down to press a soft kiss to her forehead. “God bless you, my dear.”

Hans had already grabbed his hat, holding the front door open for her before anyone could stop him.
“Hans, don’t you dare walk her home,” Mab called after him, teasing, sharp with sisterly fondness. “I’m sure she’d rather not. You’re dreadfully disagreeable today.”
“I’m going to see she gets there safe,” Hans said, stepping out beside her, and Mirah did not protest. They walked the quiet London streets side by side, neither speaking, each lost in their own whirring thoughts.

Hans’ earlier mischief had curdled into a sharp, sober realization. Mirah was not just fond of Deronda as a friend, as the savior who had reunited her with her brother — she loved him. The revelation stung, not just for his own unspoken, long-nursed fondness for Mirah, but for the raw grief he saw in her. He had long been certain Deronda cared deeply for Gwendolen Grandcourt, that their quiet, charged chemistry would end in marriage once she was free, and he had never once imagined Mirah could be anything more than a beloved little sister to Deronda, not when Deronda was widely known to be Sir Hugo Mallinger’s heir, a man outside her Jewish faith. For the first time, his usual flighty optimism wavered. He longed to say something soft, apologetic, to wrap his silent companionship in tender penitence, to soothe the bruise he had accidentally exposed.

When they reached the door of her lodgings, Mirah paused, looking up at him with eyes full of quiet, sad gentleness. “Will you not come in and see Ezra?”
Hans followed her in, half his melancholy melting away at the invitation. If Deronda was going to marry Gwendolen, he told himself, then surely there was space for him, for his own devotion, to grow, to mend the crack in Mirah’s heart.

They found Mordecai glowing, his emaciated face lit up with quiet, triumphant joy, a closed letter in his hand.
“A letter from Daniel Deronda,” he said, answering Mirah’s unspoken question before she could ask. “He writes only that unexpected claims have detained him in Genoa, but he will be home soon. The promise of his return is like the bow in the cloud to me. To you both, it must be gladness too. Who else has two friends half as good as him?”

While Hans answered him, Mirah slipped away to her tiny bedroom. She did not scream, she did not sob. She had spent years of her girlhood hiding every last flicker of pain from her cruel, selfish father, performing and singing and smiling on stage even when her heart was breaking. She took off her hat, splashed cold water on her face until the dewy curls at her nape glistened, and sat still on the edge of her bed, breathing deep. The quiet old familiar sorrow she had known all her life had returned to sit with her, and she did not fight it. She knew now that the quiet, secret love she had never dared name to herself, that soft reverent fondness for Deronda she had mistaken for only gratitude, was jealousy, sharp and hot as a pincer in her chest. She would never speak of it. She would hide it, bear it alone, no burden to anyone else.

Downstairs that evening, Mordecai spoke to her at length of his faith, of the Shemah that taught the divine Unity, and how that unity promised a future where all of mankind would be one. He told her an old midrash story of a Jewish maiden who loved a Gentile king so deeply, she traded places with the woman he was fated to marry, dying in her stead to give him the happiness he deserved.
“No, Ezra,” Mirah said, her voice soft but firm. “That was not selfless. She would have died so that the king would remember her, so she could hold the first place in his heart, even in death. There was jealousy in it. You imagine only the greatest, most selfless love in your stories. But I do not think it works that way.”
Mordecai was quiet for a long moment, smiling faintly. “You have spent too long watching the passions portrayed in plays, little sister. You judge by the stage, and not by your own soft, good heart, the one that is so like our mother’s.”
Mirah said nothing. She sat quiet, stirring his tea, and tucked that sharp, secret, shameful love of hers deeper into the dark, hidden part of her heart, where no one would ever see it.
