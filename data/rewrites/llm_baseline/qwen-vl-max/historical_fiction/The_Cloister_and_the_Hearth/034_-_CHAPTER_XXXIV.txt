“Where be the true men?”
“Here be we. God bless you all! God bless you!”

A rush of movement filled the air as half a dozen strong hands reached out, gripping those of the rescued pair with rough warmth. The room blazed with torchlight, casting flickering shadows across polished breastplates and weathered faces. Archers stood tall, their bronze skins glowing in the fire’s glow, while bound thieves slumped against the walls, pale and defeated. At the center lay the lifeless form of the giant, sprawled in his own blood, unattended—his end a grim testament to the night’s violence.

Gerard moved slowly among the archers, his eyes glistening as he clasped each hand in turn. One by one, they returned the gesture with a kiss—a ritual of gratitude and camaraderie. This time, Gerard kissed them back, his voice soft but steady. Then, turning to a young archer who matched him in age and bearing, he said, “Prithee, good soldier, keep watch over me. A strange drowsiness grips me. Let no man cut my throat while I sleep—for pity’s sake.”

The archer laughed, thinking it jest. But no sooner had the words left Gerard’s lips than he sank into a deep, unbroken slumber. Denys watched, surprised—but did not disturb him. It suited his purpose well enough.

Nearby, two archers were examining the Abbot’s corpse, rolling it gently with their boots. “Which of you flung this great rogue from above like that?” one asked. “We’d fain learn the trick of your arm.”

Denys hesitated at first, puffing his cheeks in mock indignation. But then he saw Gerard asleep—and knew the moment was ripe. “Well,” he declared, stepping forward, “I ran my sword through him up to the hilt. Others swarmed around me—so I had either my blade or death. So I planted my foot on his belly, tugged with my hand, sprang with my leg—and sent him flying to his doom! He died mid-air, and his carcass rolled among you—made you jump, I warrant. But pikestaves and pillage! What matter now? These things are past. *Buvons*, comrades, *buvons*!”

The archers exchanged glances. “Easy to say *buvons* when there’s no wine,” one muttered.

“Nay,” replied Denys, “I’ll find you wine. My nose knows where to seek it. Follow me—and I’ll follow my nose. Bring a torch!”

They descended a narrow stone stairway into a low, damp cellar. The air was thick with moisture, the walls coated in crystallized saltpetre that glittered faintly in the torchlight. “Ah,” said Denys, inhaling deeply, “the sweet scent of old wine lingers here. Advance thy light.”

Then, suddenly: “Diable! What is that in the corner? A pile of rags?”

No—it was a figure, crouched in shadow, pale as ash, trembling violently.

“It is the landlord,” Denys said flatly.

One archer bellowed, “Get up, thou craven heart! The thieves are bound, and we’re dry from binding them. Show us thy wine—no bottles here!”

The landlord stammered, “Be the rascals bound? Good news… Wine? That will I, honest sirs.”

He rose shakily, joints weak, offering to lead them to the wine cellar. But Denys stepped between them. “You’re all in the dark, comrades. He’s in league with the thieves.”

“Alack, good soldier!” the landlord protested. “Me? With the accursed robbers? Is that reasonable?”

“The girl said so,” Denys replied coldly.

“The girl? What girl?” the landlord whispered. “Ah—curse her, traitress!”

Another archer spoke up. “The girl is gone to the bailiff. Let the burghers judge if he’s guilty. We caught him not in act.”

Denys paused. “One moment. Why cursed he the girl? If he’s innocent, he should bless her—as we do.”

“Alas, sir,” the landlord pleaded, “I have only my name to live by. I cursed her because you said she lied about me.”

“Humph,” said Denys. “I suspect thou art a thief—and what thief cannot lie smoothly? Hold him, comrades. A prisoner can draw wine even if his hands are bound.”

The landlord made no protest. Instead, he offered to show them where the wine was kept, adding, “But I pray ye pay for what ye drink. My rent is due these two months.”

The archers smiled grimly, seeing through his pretense. One placed a firm hand on his shoulder; another led on with the torch. They reached the threshold when Denys called sharply, “Halt!”

“What now?”

“There be bottles in this corner. Advance thy light.”

The torch-bearer approached. Denys had just removed his scabbard and was probing the heap where the landlord had crouched.

“Nay, nay!” the landlord cried. “The wine is in the next cellar. Nothing here.”

“Nothing is mighty hard,” said Denys, pulling something from the pile. It was a bone. He tossed it aside. It rattled on stone.

“There is naught here but the bones of the house,” the landlord insisted.

“Just now ‘twas nothing,” said Denys. “Now that we’ve found something, ‘tis nothing but bones. Here’s another. Look at this one, comrade. And you—come too. Bring the knave along.”

Philippe, the torch-bearer, held the bone to the flame, turning it slowly.

“Well?” Denys asked.

“Well,” Philippe said, “if this were a battlefield, I’d say it was a man’s shankbone. No more, no less. But this is no field of battle—nor churchyard. This is an inn.”

“True,” said the other archer. “Yet yon man’s face—ashen, soulless—is as good a sign as any. When the chine is missing, and the dog won’t look at you without his tail between his legs—what thief could hide such a truth? Good brothers, my mind misgives me. The deeper I dig, the more I find. Mayhap if these bones could speak, they would make true men’s flesh creep.”

“Alas, young man!” the landlord cried. “What dreadful fancies! These are bones of beeves, sheep, kids—not men or women. Holy saints preserve us!”

“Hold thy peace!” Denys snapped. “Thy words are wind. Thou hast not deceived burghers—only fools. We are soldiers who have sought our comrades and found their bones picked clean by crows. Men and women, saidst thou? Yet prithee—when spake I of women’s bones? Wouldst make a child suspect thee. Field of battle, comrade? Was not this house a battlefield but half an hour ago?”

He thrust the landlord forward, forcing him close. Then, with sudden fury, he pulled from the heap a small object and held it before the man’s eyes.

“Alas! I know not,” the landlord whimpered.

“Nor I,” Denys admitted, “but it is too like the thumb bone of a man’s hand. Mates, my flesh creeps. Churchyard? How know I this is not one?”

Drawing his sword, he began to rake the earth and broken crockery from the pile. The landlord protested again, pleading innocence.

“We poor innkeepers are sinners,” he said, “we give short measure, we water the wine—we do these things. The laws are unjust to us. But we are not assassins. How could we afford to kill our guests? May Heaven strike me dead if there be any bones here but those of meat. ’Tis the kitchen wench who throws them here. I swear by God’s holy mother, by Saint Paul, by Saint Dominic, and by Denys, my patron saint—ah!”

Denys held up a bone—silent, deadly. It was unmistakable. Not ox, not sheep, not goat. A human bone.

The liar’s mouth closed. His breath stopped. His hair stood on end like bristles. His knees gave way.

The archers dragged him upright, forcing him to stand under the torchlight. There, staring into the hollow sockets of a skull—white as his own cheek, yet colder—his lips parted, but no sound came.

“Ah,” Denys said, voice low and trembling with rage, “look upon the sockets from which thou didst pluck the eyes. Let them blast thine own—so that crows shall pick them out ere this week ends. Now, hold that while I search on. Hold it, I say—or I’ll rob the gallows myself!”

He raised his naked sword, threatening the quaking wretch until, groaning, the man took the skull and clutched it, nearly fainting. Oh! That every murderer might see himself thus—sick, terrified, holding the cold relic of his own crime, knowing his head would soon match it.

Soon, the heap was scattered. And not one, nor two, but many skulls emerged. Each discovery brought a fresh moan from the culprit.

Then Denys let out a cry—strange, sharp, coming from a man so bold. In his hand, he held a mass of long, glossy, golden hair. A woman’s hair.

At once, the archers shook the wretch violently. He whined.

“I have a sister,” Denys choked. “Her hair is just so fair and shining.”

“Jesu!” he gasped. “If it should be hers! Take my sword and dagger—keep them from my hand, lest I strike him down and wrong the gibbet.”

Then, on bended knee, he swore aloud: “Poor innocent victim—whose lovely hair this was—I swear by God, never to leave this man till I see him broken upon the wheel—for thy sake.”

He rose. “Ay,” he roared, “had he as many lives as there are hairs, I’d take them all. By God!”

And he tucked the hair into his bosom. Then, seized by fury, he grabbed the landlord by the neck, forced him to his knees, and ground his face into the bones of his victims—where they lay thickest. The assassin screamed—then whined, like a dog whose nose is pressed into the body of its prey.

“Now lend me thy bowstring, Philippe!”

He threaded it through the eye sockets of a skull and hung it round the man’s neck. Then pulled him up, kicked him fiercely into the kitchen—where the alderman had arrived with constables, already taking a deposition.

The grave magistrate stared in horror at the landlord, bleeding from a dozen scratches, wearing the ghastly crown of skulls. But Denys came panting after, and in a few fierce words explained all.

“Bind him like the rest,” said the alderman sternly. “I count him the blackest of them all.”

As the wretch begged piteously for the skull to be removed, the alderman replied: “Humph! I had not ordered such a thing to be worn by mortal man. Yet being there, I will not lift finger nor voice to take it off. Methinks it fits thee truly, thou bloody dog. ’Tis thy ensign—and hangs well above a heart so foul as thine.”

Then he turned to Denys. “Do you think we have secured the whole gang?”

“Your worship,” Denys answered, “there are seven thieves and this landlord. One we slew upstairs. One we cast down dead. The rest are bound before you.”

“Good,” said the alderman. “Go fetch the dead one from upstairs and lay him beside the one I caused to be removed.”

Suddenly, a shrill voice broke in—like a guinea-fowl peevishly squawking.

“Now, now, now—where is the hand? That is what I want to see!”

It was a petty clerk, eager for evidence.

“You’ll find it above,” someone said. “Nailed to the door-post by a crossbow bolt.”

“Good!” squeaked the clerk. He whispered to his master, “What a fine display the *pièces de conviction* will make!” And he began writing, listing them one by one:
Skulls—Bones—A woman’s hair—A thief’s hands—1 axe—2 carcasses—1 crossbow bolt.

Done, he itched to search the cellar himself—perhaps find an ear, or an earring. The alderman nodded, and the clerk snatched a torch and hurried toward the stairs—when disaster struck.

The constables ascended in single file. But the head constable, seeing the phosphorescent corpse seated by the bed, froze. Then trembled. Then howled.

In his recoil, he stumbled backward—knocking into the next man. That man, shaken by the cry and glimpsing something terrible, staggered back—unable to support the heavy head constable. The two toppled onto the third. The three carried the fourth. They tumbled into the kitchen, falling in a heap like cards laid out on a table.

The clerk, rushing forward with torch, collided with the last man—who, carrying the momentum of the pile, knocked him violently to the ground—the ace of the quint. He lay there kicking, torch still aloft, seemingly triumphant—but actually convulsed, breath and sense driven out by the blow.

“What is to do now, in Heaven’s name?” the alderman cried, leaping up in alarm.

Denys explained, and offered to accompany him.

“So be it,” said the alder man.

His men picked themselves up ruefully. He led the inspection—above and below.

As for the prisoners, their questioning was delayed until they could be confronted with the servant.

Before dawn, the thieves—alive and dead—along with every relic of crime and retribution—were swept away into the law’s net. The inn fell silent, nearly empty. Only one constable remained, and Denys—along with Gerard, still sleeping heavily.
