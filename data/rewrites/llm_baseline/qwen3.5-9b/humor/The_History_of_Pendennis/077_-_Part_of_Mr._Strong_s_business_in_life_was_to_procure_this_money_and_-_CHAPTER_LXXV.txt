Part of Mr. Strong's business in life was to procure this money, and the rest was to ensure it reached the right hands without scandal. But tonight, the business belonged to Pen, whose sole objective was to secure a certain fortune for a specific purpose: the procurement of a bride.

Pen fumed and fretted until the arrival of the evening train to London, a journey that felt like a full half-hour—or perhaps six hours, depending on one's mood. Even this immense interval was finally conquered. The train arrived, sped on, and the London lights came into view. Just as the carriage was settling, a gentleman who had forgotten his carpet-bag in the train rushed out, hailed a cab, and commanded, "Drive as hard as you can go to Jermyn Street." The cabman, though a respectable hansom driver, graciously accepted the gratuity placed in his hand. Meanwhile, Pen sprinted up the stairs to Lady Rockminster's apartments.

Laura was alone in the drawing-room, reading by the lamp with a pale face. That pale complexion lifted slightly when Pen burst through the door.

"May we follow him?" someone whispered.

Great moments in life are often indistinguishable from ordinary ones; your fate is frequently sealed in a word or two. A single glance from the eyes, a mere pressure of the hand, or a quiver of the lips can decide destiny, even when silence reigns supreme.

When Lady Rockminster, having completed her post-dinner nap, rose to enter her sitting-room, we may proceed with her ladyship.

"Upon my word, young people!" were the first words she uttered, while her attendant cast wondering glances over her shoulder. And well might she exclaim so, and well might the attendant stare; for the young people were in an attitude. Pen occupied a position familiar to every young lady who has heard tell of it, seen it, hoped for it, or at the very least deserves to witness it.

In short, the moment Pen entered, he approached Laura, whose pale face showed no time to utter, "What, back so soon?" Seizing her outstretched, trembling hand just as she rose from her chair, he fell to his knees before her and declared quickly, "I have seen her. She has engaged herself to Harry Foker—and—and... Now, Laura?"

The hand pressed tight; the eyes beamed a reply; the quivering lips answered, though speechless. Pen's head sank into the girl's lap as he sobbed, "Come and bless us, dear mother," while arms as tender as Helen's once more enveloped him.

It was precisely at this juncture that Lady Rockminster entered, declaring, "Upon my word, young people! Beck! Leave the room. What do you want poking your nose in here?"

Pen sprang up with looks of triumph, still clutching Laura's hand. "She is consoling me for my misfortune, ma'am," he explained.

"What do you mean by kissing her hand? I don't know what you'll be doing next." Pen promptly kissed her Ladyship's hand.

"I have been to Tunbridge," he continued, adopting a tragic air, "and seen Miss Amory. Upon my return, I discovered that a villain has transplanted me in her affections."

"Is that all? Is that what you were whimpering on your knees about?" the old lady demanded, growing angry. "You might have kept the news until tomorrow."

"Yes—another has superseded me," Pen lamented. "But why call him a villain? He is brave, constant, young, wealthy, and beautiful."

"What stuff are you talking, sir?" cried the old lady. "What has happened?"

"Miss Amory has jilted me and accepted Henry Foker, Esq.," Pen reported. "I found her warbling ditties to him as he lay at her feet. Presents had been accepted, vows exchanged—all within the last ten days. Harry was merely old Mrs. Planter's rheumatism, which kept dearest Laura out of the house. He is the most constant and generous of men. He promised the living of Logwood to Lady Ann's husband and gave her a splendid present upon her marriage; then he rushed to fling himself at Blanche's feet the instant he found himself free."

"And so, as you can't get Blanche, you put up with Laura; is that it, sir?" asked the old lady.

"He acted nobly," Laura said softly.

"I acted as she bade me," Pen replied. "Never mind *how*, Lady Rockminster; but to the best of my knowledge and power. And if you mean that I am not worthy of Laura, I know it, and pray Heaven to better me. If the love and company of the best and purest creature in the world can aid me, at least I shall have these to help me."

"Hm, hm," replied the old lady, looking with a somewhat appeased air at the young couple. "It is all very well; but I should have preferred Bluebeard."

Now, Pen, wishing to divert the conversation from a theme growing painful to those present, recalled his interview with Huxter in the morning and Fanny Bolton's affairs, which he had momentarily forgotten amidst the excitement of his own crisis. He recounted how Huxter had elevated Fanny to the rank of wife and the terrors regarding the arrival of his father. He described the scene with considerable humor, dwelling especially on Fanny's coquetry and her irrepressible desire to captivate mankind. His underlying message was clear: "See, Laura, I was not so guilty in that little affair; it was the girl who made love to me, and I who resisted. Since I am no longer present, the little siren practices her arts upon others. Let that transaction be forgotten, if you please; or visit me with a very gentle punishment for my error."

Laura understood his meaning beneath the eagerness of his explanations. "If you did any wrong, you repented, dear Pen," she said. "And you know," she added, with meaningful eyes and blushing cheeks, "that I have no right to reproach you."

"Hm!" grumbled the old lady. "I should have preferred Bluebeard."

"The past is broken away. The morrow is before us. I will do my best to make your morrow happy, dear Laura," Pen vowed. His heart was humbled by the prospect of his happiness; it stood awestruck in the contemplation of her sweet goodness and purity. He liked his wife better because she had admitted to that passing feeling for Warrington and laid bare her generous heart to him.

And she? Very likely she was thinking, *"How strange it is that I ever should have cared for another! I am vexed almost to think I care for him so little, am so little sorry that he is gone. Oh, in these past two months, how I have learned to love Arthur! I care about nothing but Arthur; my waking and sleeping thoughts are of him; he is never absent. And to think that he is to be mine, mine! That I am to marry him, and not serve as his servant as I expected only this morning—I would have gone down on my knees to Blanche to beg permission to live with him. And now—Oh, it is too much. Oh, mother! Mother, that you were here!"*

Indeed, she felt as if Helen were there—physically present, though invisible. A halo of happiness beamed from her. She moved with a different step, blooming with new beauty. Arthur noticed the change, and the sharp-eyed old Lady Rockminster remarked upon it.

"What a sly demure little wretch you have been," she whispered to Laura, while Pen laughed loudly, recounting his story about Huxter. "And how you have kept your secret!"

"How are we to help the young couple?" asked Laura. Of course, Miss Laura felt an interest in all young couples, for generous lovers always cherish other lovers.

"We must go and see them," said Pen.

"Of course we must go and see them," agreed Laura. "I intend to be very fond of Fanny. Let us go this instant. Lady Rockminster, may I have the carriage?"

"Go now! Why, you stupid creature, it is eleven o'clock at night. Mr. and Mrs. Huxter have their nightcaps on, I dare say. And it is time for you to go. Good night, Mr. Pendennis."

Arthur and Laura begged for ten more minutes. "We will go to-morrow morning, then. I will come and fetch you with Martha," Lady Rockminster decided.

"An earl's coronet," said Pen, clearly pleased with the prospect, "will have a great effect in Lamb Court and Smithfield. Stay—Lady Rockminster, will you join us in a little conspiracy?"

"How do you mean, conspiracy, young man?"

"Will you please feign illness to-morrow? And when old Mr. Huxter arrives, will you let me call him in? If he is put into a good humor by the notion of attending a baronet in the country, what influence won't a countess have on him? When he is softened, when he is quite ripe, we will break the secret upon him, bring in the young people, extort the paternal benediction, and finish the comedy."

"A parcel of stuff," said the old lady. "Take your hat, sir. Come away, miss. There—my head is turned another way. Good night, young people."
