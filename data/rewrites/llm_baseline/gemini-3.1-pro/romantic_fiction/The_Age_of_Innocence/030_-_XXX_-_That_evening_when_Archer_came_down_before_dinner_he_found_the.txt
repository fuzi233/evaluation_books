That evening when Archer came down before dinner, he found the drawing-room empty. He and May were dining alone, all the family engagements having been postponed since Mrs. Manson Mingott's illness. As May was the more punctual of the two, he was surprised she had not preceded him. He knew she was at home; while dressing, the acoustic landscape of the house—a sterile, heavily carpeted vacuum designed to smother the robust clamor of the modern industrial city—had permitted the faint, rhythmic creak of her floorboards to drift down to him. He wondered what delayed her, letting his mind circle back through the generations, tracing how his father-in-law had similarly retreated into the domestic quietude of trifles, erecting walls of silence to ward off the vibrant, terrifying noise of true passion. 

When May finally appeared, she looked drained. She had donned the low-necked, tightly laced dinner-dress exacted by Mingott ceremonial even for informal evenings, her fair hair built into its accumulated coils. Her face was wan, almost faded, yet she shone on him with a utilitarian tenderness, her eyes keeping the blue dazzle of the day before. 

"What became of you, dear?" she asked. The cadence of her voice was flat, adhering strictly to the dominant lexicon of their class, devoid of any raw emotional dialect. "I was waiting at Granny's, and Ellen came alone, and said she had dropped you on the way because you had to rush off on business. There's nothing wrong?"

"Only some letters I'd forgotten, and wanted to get off before dinner." 

"Ah—" she said. The silence that followed was oppressive, a heavy sonic deadness that made Archer's ears ring. "I'm sorry you didn't come to Granny's—unless the letters were urgent."

"They were," he rejoined, surprised at her insistence. "Besides, I don't see why I should have gone to your grandmother's. I didn't know you were there."

She turned to the looking-glass above the mantel, lifting her long arm to fasten a slipped puff of hair. Archer, observing the languid, inelastic line of her body, felt the sudden weight of their deadly monotony. Then he remembered his forgotten promise from that morning, shouted over the stairs, to meet her. He was smitten with compunction, yet irritated that such a trifling omission should be stored up against him. He was weary of living in a perpetual tepid honeymoon, a state of being that lacked the rich, simmering heat of a long-braised passion, yet demanded all the exactitude of a delicate reduction. May was trained to conceal imaginary wounds under a Spartan smile; her subjective experience was entirely untranslatable, locked behind a native dialect of repression he could no longer interpret. 

To disguise his annoyance, he asked after her grandmother. Mrs. Mingott was improving, May answered, but disturbed by news of the Beauforts. 

"What news?"

"It seems they're going to stay in New York. I believe he's going into an insurance business, or something. They're looking about for a small house."

The preposterousness of the case was beyond discussion, and the chime of the dining bell called them to the table. Dinner was an exercise in sensory erosion. For Archer, whose soul hungered for the mythic resonance of ancestral feasts and the precise, terroir-driven flavors of Old New York, sitting across from May was an act of mourning. She ate with the mechanical efficiency of one who views meals solely as utilitarian fuel. A clear soup, boiled down to a tasteless, golden astringency, was followed by a roast of mutton that had been stripped of its feral, gamey heritage, served alongside winter vegetables bleached of their earthy vitality. During the meal, their talk moved in its limited, circular orbit. Archer listened to the sterile clink of silver against porcelain—a sharp, inorganic sound that grated against his nerves. May made no allusion to Madame Olenska, nor to old Catherine's reception of her. Her omission was an acoustic void, a deliberate silencing of Ellen's name that felt profoundly ominous.

They went up to the library for coffee. The room smelled of old leather and roasting beans, a slight comfort. Archer lit a cigar, drawing the thick, aromatic smoke over his palate, and took down a volume of Michelet. He had retreated into history—into the study of lost civilizations and the tragedy of language death—because May had developed a habit of asking him to read poetry aloud, only to offer comments that severed the evocative roots of the verse. Seeing his choice, she fetched her workbasket and drew up an armchair to the green-shaded student lamp. 

She was not a clever needlewoman. Her large, capable hands were made for the tactile grip of oars and reins, yet she labored over a sofa cushion for him, her right hand rhythmically stabbing the canvas. *Thwack, pull. Thwack, pull.* The sonic repetition was maddening. As the lamplight hit her clear brow, Archer realized with a suffocating dismay that he would always know her thoughts. The rich, spontaneous poetry of their short courting had evaporated, the function exhausted. She was ripening into a copy of her mother, enforcing a culinary and emotional blandness upon his life. 

He stood up impatiently. "The room is stifling: I want a little air."

He had insisted the library curtains be movable, refusing the immovable loops of lace that choked the drawing-room's windows. He pulled them back and shoved up the sash, thrusting his face into the icy night. The sudden rush of the acoustic environment was a shock of cold water: the distant, rhythmic clatter of carriage wheels on cobblestones, the low, mechanical hum of the expanding metropolis, the faint scent of coal smoke and roasted chestnuts rising from the avenues. It was a polluted, industrial roar, but it broke the stifling silence of his home, reminding him of a whole world beyond his world. It cleared his brain.

After a few minutes, her voice cut through the cold air. "Newland! Do shut the window. You'll catch your death."

He pulled the sash down, cutting off the city's chaotic music. "Catch my death!" he echoed. He wanted to scream: *I am already dead. Starved and silenced for months.* 

And suddenly, the dark play of the word flared up into a wild, intoxicating recipe for salvation. What if it were *she* who was dead? If she were to die soon, and leave him free! The sensation of standing in that warm room, smelling the faint starch of her dress and the lingering cigar smoke, and wishing her dead, was so overmastering it left him breathless. It was a new possibility, a sudden influx of oxygen to a dying fire. 

She glanced up, her eyes widening at the strangeness in his. "Newland! Are you ill?"

He shook his head and turned to his armchair. As he passed, he laid his hand on her hair. "Poor May!" he said.

"Poor? Why poor?" she echoed with a strained laugh. 

"Because I shall never be able to open a window without worrying you," he said, laughing too. 

Her head bowed over her work. "I shall never worry if you're happy."

"Ah, my dear; and I shall never be happy unless I can open the windows!"

"In *this* weather?" she remonstrated, and with a sigh, he buried his head in his book.

Six or seven days turned in their slow, circular rhythm. Archer heard nothing from Madame Olenska, her name effectively eradicated from the family's lexicon. He let himself drift, anchored only by the secret, dark resolve born in the icy draft of the library window. Then, one day, May delivered a message: Mrs. Manson Mingott wished to see him.

"Shall we go together this afternoon?" Archer asked, feeling the violent flutter of his pulse.

"Oh, you'd much better go alone," May smiled, her tone perfectly manicured. "It bores Granny to see the same people too often."

Archer's heart beat a wild tattoo as he stood on old Mrs. Mingott's doorstep. He was desperate to see Ellen, to ask the singular, untranslatable question of when she would return to Washington. But inside the yellow damask sitting-room, redolent of ancient beeswax and heavy silk, only the mulatto maid awaited him. Her teeth shone like a keyboard, and she ushered him into old Catherine's presence with a melodic murmur that carried the echoes of an older, uncolonized dialect. 

The old woman sat in a vast, throne-like armchair near her bed. Beside her, a cast bronze lamp warmed the room, filling the space with the comforting, restorative scent of hot oil and starched muslin. Conversation was Mrs. Mingott's sole pursuit; there was no sterile fancywork here. She looked paler, the shadows in the recesses of her obesity darker, yet she sat like a traditional agrarian elder, a shrewd and kindly ancestress who had yielded joyously to the physical pleasures of the table, her body a living testament to generations of rich broths, roasted meats, and sweet, fermented wines. 

"Don't let in any one else," she ordered the maid, her voice a deep, resonant rumble that anchored the room's acoustics. "If my daughters call, say I'm asleep." She turned to Archer. "My dear, am I perfectly hideous?" she asked gaily, her little hand searching the folds of her billowing purple dressing-gown. 

"My dear, you're handsomer than ever!" Archer rejoined, stepping into her sphere of grounded, earthly warmth.

She threw back her head and laughed, a hearty, untamed sound. "Ah, but not as handsome as Ellen! Was she so awfully handsome the day you drove her up from the ferry? Was it because you told her so that she had to put you out on the way?" She chuckled again, a deep, mythic rumbling in her chest. "It's a pity she didn't marry you. I always told her so. But who ever thought of sparing their grandmother worry?" 

Suddenly, she broke out: "Well, it's settled, anyhow: she's going to stay with me, whatever the rest of the family say! They'd talked me over... persuaded me, Lovell, and Letterblair, and Augusta, that I must hold out and cut off her allowance, till she was made to see her duty to go back to Olenski. Handsome proposals they were. Marriage is marriage, and money's money..." 

She paused, breathing heavily, before striking down the dominant lexicon of the family's control. "But the minute I laid eyes on her, I said: 'You sweet bird, you! Shut you up in that cage again? Never!' It's settled. She's to stay here and nurse her Granny. And I've told Letterblair she's to be given her proper allowance."

Archer's veins glowed. The news washed over him like a miraculous, restorative broth. If Ellen had consented to stay, she had recognized the impossibility of giving him up. It was a half-measure, but it was a reprieve, a dangerous sweetness on his tongue. 

"She couldn't have gone back—it was impossible!" he exclaimed. 

"Ah, my dear, I always knew you were on her side. That's why I sent for you. For you see, my dear—" she looked him full in the eyes—"we shall have a fight yet. The family don't want her here. I'm not well enough to fight them one by one, and you've got to do it for me. You're Letterblair's partner, ain't you?" Her round eyes sharpened, her pale nails clutching his hand like bird-claws. 

"Oh, my dear, I back you to hold your own without my help; but you shall have it if you need it," he reassured her. 

"Then we're safe!" she sighed, settling into her cushions. "I always knew you'd back us up, because they never quote you when they talk about its being her duty to go home."

He winced at her terrifying perspicacity. "And Madame Olenska? When am I to see her?"

The old lady crumpled her lids, going through a pantomime of archness. "Not today. She's gone out in my carriage to see Regina Beaufort." She let the announcement steep in the quiet room. "The day after she got here, she told me she was going to call on Regina. 'She's the wife of a scoundrel,' I answered. 'Well,' she says, 'and so am I, and yet all my family want me to go back to him.' That floored me. After all, Regina's a brave woman, and so is she; and I've always liked courage above everything."

Archer bent down, moved by the sheer, unpretentious humanity of her worldview, and pressed his lips to the little hand that tasted faintly of lavender and age. 

"Eh—eh—eh! Whose hand did you think you were kissing, young man—your wife's, I hope?" she snapped with a mocking cackle that shattered the room's reverence with brilliant, lively sound. "Give her her Granny's love; but you'd better not say anything about our talk."
