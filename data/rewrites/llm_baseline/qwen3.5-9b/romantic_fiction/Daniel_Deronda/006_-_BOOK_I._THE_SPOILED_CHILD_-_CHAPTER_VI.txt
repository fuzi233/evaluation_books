"Do you think I've humiliated myself by telling you that the earth revolves around the sun?" Fontenelle once asked, adding a note of defiant pride. "I swear I hold myself no less esteemed."

That very sentiment echoed in Gwendolen's mind, sparking a fresh, peculiar ache within her. She would never willingly admit that she felt ill-equipped compared to Miss Arrowpoint, lacking the musical training necessary to critique Herr Klesmer with authority. Even less could she confess to herself that meeting Miss Arrowpoint invariably stirred a sharp, unwonted jealousy. It wasn't merely because the rival was an heiress; it was because it was genuinely infuriating that a girl of slight build, average height, small features, tolerable eyes, and a sallow complexion possessed a mental superiority that defied explanation. Miss Arrowpoint displayed an exasperating thoroughness in her musical accomplishments and a fastidious discernment in her tastes that commanded awe rather than admiration. This seemingly insignificant twenty-four-year-old, whom anyone might glance over if not for her name, seemed to harbor a quiet conviction that Miss Harleth's talents were merely common. Such a thought was unbearable, especially when cloaked beneath Miss Arrowpoint's perpetual, perfect kindness.

Gwendolen refused to dwell on facts that cast such an unfavorable light on her own character. Since the Musical Magus who had so dramatically expanded her horizons was frequently traveling between London and Quetcham, she seized upon his absences as opportunities to coax him into a more admiring frame of mind. In the meantime, the evident pleasure her singing brought to Brackenshaw Castle, the Firs, and other gatherings restored her composure. She preferred to believe that approval was more reliable than objection, and unlike those few individuals who crave a perfection their neighbors do not demand, she was content. Perhaps it would have been rash to claim she was exceptionally complex inwardly; her uniqueness lay mostly in her rare grace of movement, her bearing, and a certain daring that added spice to a very common, egoistic ambition. Many hide such ambitions behind clumsy exteriors, unnoticed by the world. After all, the tilt of one's head does not dictate the hunger of the inner self for supremacy, only perhaps *how* that supremacy is pursued and to what degree it can be achieved. This was particularly true for a girl whose passion for distinction is bounded by ideals of breeding and free from the sordid need for income.

Gwendolen was internally rebellious against the constraints of her family's circumstances, ready to look past obligations to her own fundamental lack of feeling for them, as if fueled by bold speculation. Yet, she possessed no such theories. She would have immediately distanced herself from any theoretical or practical reformer by mocking them. She delighted in feeling exceptional, yet her horizon remained confined to the realm of genteel romance: a heroine whose soul pours out in journals filled with vague power and rebellion, while her actual life moves strictly within the sphere of fashion. Should she wander into a swamp, the tragedy lies partly in the fact that she wears satin shoes. Nature and society provide this restraint on the pursuit of striking adventure, holding a soul burning with a sense of what the universe is not—and eager to consume all existence as fuel—captive within the ordinary wirework of social forms, leaving her to achieve nothing particular.

This mundane fate was what Gwendolen feared even amidst the novelty of her first winter at Offendene. She was certain she did not wish to live the life of ordinary young ladies, yet she was unclear on how to begin living differently or what specific acts would assert her freedom. Offendene served as a promising backdrop if events were to unfold, but largely, the neighborhood failed to inspire.

Beyond the initial impact of her beauty, her early invitations offered little excitement. Returning home after brief sallies of satire and knowingness—which had previously offended Mrs. Arrowpoint—she filled the idle days with childish schemes. Her strongest assertion of individuality involved skipping Alice's lessons (operating on the principle that ignorance bred excellence) to spend time with Miss Merry and the maid rumored to serve all the ladies. Together, they arranged dramatic costumes Gwendolen had prepared for future charades or theatrical pieces, projects she intended to launch through sheer will or contrivance. Though she had never truly acted beyond posing in *tableaux vivants* at school, she was convinced of her ability. Having visited the Théâtre Français and heard her mother speak of Rachel, her reverie often turned to becoming an actress like the famous Jewish tragedienne, reasoning that she was far more beautiful than the "thin Jewess."

The wet days preceding Christmas were spent pleasantly preparing Greek, Oriental, and composite costumes. Gwendolen posed and delivered lines before a domestic audience, even pressuring the housekeeper to swell the applause. However, when the housekeeper pointed out that Miss Harleth looked far more regal in her own attire than in the baggy costume exposing her arms, she was politely dismissed from further participation.

"Do I look as well as Rachel, mamma?" Gwendolen asked one day, displaying her Greek garb to Anna and enacting fragments of scenes with heavy tragic intent.

"You have better arms than Rachel," Mrs. Davilow replied lazily. "Your arms would suffice for anything, Gwen. But your voice lacks her tragic depth; it isn't deep enough."

"I can deepen it if I wish," Gwendolen said provisionally, then decided firmly, "I believe a higher voice is more tragic. It is more feminine. And the more feminine a woman is, the more tragic she appears when performing desperate deeds."

"There may be truth in that," Mrs. Davilow murmured. "But I don't see the benefit in making one's blood crawl. If anything terrible must be done, I'd prefer it left to the men."

"Oh, mamma, you are dreadfully prosaic!" Gwendolen retorted. "As if all great poetic criminals weren't women! Men are poor, cautious creatures."

"Well, dear, and you—who are afraid to be alone at night—I don't think you'd be very bold in committing crimes, thank God."

"I'm not speaking of reality, mamma," Gwendolen snapped impatiently. Once her mother left the room, she turned quickly to her cousin. "Anna, please ask Uncle to organize some charades at the rectory. Mr. Middleton and Warham could act with us, just for practice. Mamma says it's improper for Mr. Middleton to consult and rehearse here. He is stiff, but we could give him suitable roles. Please ask, or I will."

"Oh, not until Rex arrives," Anna interjected. "He is so clever and dear; he'll act as Napoleon gazing over the sea. He looks exactly like Napoleon. Rex can do anything."

"I don't believe in your Rex, Anna," Gwendolen laughed. "He'll turn out like those wretched blue-and-yellow watercolors you hang in your bedroom and worship."

"Very well, you'll see," Anna countered. "It's not that I know what constitutes cleverness, but he has a scholarship already, and Papa says he'll get a fellowship. Nobody is better at games. He is cleverer than Mr. Middleton, and everyone but you calls Mr. Middleton clever."

"So he may shine in a dim-lantern sort of way. But he *is* stiff. If he had to say, 'Perdition catch my soul, but I do love her,' he'd deliver it in the exact same tone as, 'Here endeth the second lesson.'"

"Oh, Gwendolen!" Anna exclaimed, shocked by these casual insults. "It's unkind to speak so of him, for he admires you greatly. I heard Warham tell Mama, 'Middleton is regularly spoony upon Gwendolen.' She was furious, but I know what it means—it's the college term for being in love."

"How can I help it?" Gwendolen said with disdain. "Perdition catch my soul if I loved *him*."

"No, of course; Papa wouldn't wish it. And he leaves soon. But it saddens me when you ridicule him."

"What will you do to me when I ridicule Rex?" Gwendolen teased wickedly.

"Now, Gwendolen, dear, you *won't*?" Anna pleaded, her eyes filling with tears. "I couldn't bear it. But there's really nothing to ridicule. Only you might discover things. No one ever laughed at Mr. Middleton before you. Everyone said he was good-looking with perfect manners. I was always frightened by his learning, his square-cut coat, and his status as the Bishop's nephew. But you won't ridicule Rex—promise me." Anna finished with a beseeching look that touched Gwendolen.

"You're a dear little cousin," Gwendolen said softly, tapping the tip of Anna's chin. "I never want to do anything that vexes you. Especially if Rex is to make everything succeed—the charades and all."

When Rex finally arrived, the animation he brought to Offendene and the rectory, along with his enthusiastic partnership in Gwendolen's plans, erased any inclination for ridicule unless it was open and flattering. He was a fine, open-hearted youth with a handsome face resembling both his father and Anna's, yet softer in expression and larger in scale. His nature was bright, healthy, and loving, finding joy in innocent pursuits so readily that vice held no temptation for him. Any knowledge he possessed of darker matters resided in the remote corners of his mind, too distant to cause repulsion.
