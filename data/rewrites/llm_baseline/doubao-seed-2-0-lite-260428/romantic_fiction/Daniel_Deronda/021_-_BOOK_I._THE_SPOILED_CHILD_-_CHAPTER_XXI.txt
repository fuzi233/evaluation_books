We all repeat the old adage that Knowledge is power—but who ever stops to reckon with the far greater, far crueler power of Ignorance? Knowledge spends patient, frugal centuries building up, documenting, refining, making life bright and layered with every small, hard-won discovery. Ignorance? It will burn every last page of that recorded progress in an hour, just to roast its single dinner, to flavor its one small pleasure with the toil and lost dreams of a hundred generations. Knowledge is reined in by scruple, by a quiet sense of what ought to be. Ignorance is a blind, roving giant: unshackle it, and it will gleefully rip out the pillars holding up all the hard-won good of human life, smother every joy under darkness as thick as the dust of buried Babylon. That same ruinous force works on a single life, too. How many of us, blind to the slow, fragile chain of cause and effect, chase a foolish fantasy a single reckless step too far, and send our whole carefully curated world crashing down?

It was half past ten on a crisp, blustery autumn morning when Gwendolen Harleth stepped off the train fresh from her gloomy, sleepless journey from Leubronn, at the sort of country station that never seemed built to be near anywhere at all, just equally far from every neighboring village. No carriage was waiting for her: she’d told her mother in her Dover telegram to expect her on a later train, and her own impatience to escape the chaos of London’s terminals had made her rush off without picturing how miserable it would be to arrive unannounced, alone, with all her trunks, thirty minutes’ drive from the home she’d always ruled over like a little queen.

The waiting room was a grubby, paint-peeling hovel, the water in the glass decanter stale and flat, the walls plastered with loud, screaming religious texts that felt like a personal taunt. Every detail felt like a preview of the life of humiliation that had just opened up before her. She fled out to the platform, but even the sunbeams over the fields felt thin and sad, the wind tangling the autumnal leaves, flaring the feathers of a scruffy old rooster and two croaking hens pecking aimlessly at the grass, left behind by their grown chicks with nothing to do all day. The station master was a new man, didn’t know her, and stared at her with his cross-eyed, innocent curiosity like he was wondering what sort of unimportant stray she was. The dirty, dented old barouche hired from the nearby railway inn trundled up at last, and as the old farm laborer strapped her overstuffed trunks to the seats, Gwendolen felt the last of her old bright, defiant spirit fraying at the edges. She was used to being admired, waited on, never having to beg anyone for anything—and now here she was, dumped at a hick station, broke, with no one to run to. What was the point of being charming, clever, sharp, if all the world had to offer her was ugliness, petty indignity, and no money? This was only the start of coming home.

She stewed all through the slow, jolting drive down the rutted lanes, one great trunk squishing the meek old driver next to him. She’d clung to the hope all the way from the continent that the family would just move abroad, settle in some quiet cheap coastal town, avoid all the pitying stares of their old neighbors. She’d seen plenty of poor English expats scraping by in France and Italy, and she’d dreaded the thought of wasting away there, watching her little half-sisters Alice, Bertha, Fanny and Isabel grow up dull and tedious, while she crept closer and closer to thirty, no prospects, nothing to make her stand out. But even that miserable future was better than the one she was driving back to, she soon realized. She’d lost almost two hundred pounds at the roulette table in Leubronn that last night—if only her mother had told her the bad news earlier, that money could have kept them afloat for months. And the man who’d stepped between her and the big win she’d chased? The stranger Deronda? She found herself seething at Grandcourt, too, the wealthy suitor who’d been almost hers, whose dark secret had stopped her from marrying into the fortune she’d thought was hers, all of them robbing her of the life she was owed.

The barouche rumbled up the long avenue to Offendene, and she spotted her mother waiting on the portico, that beautiful soft face lined with new grief, and all her petty anger dissolved for a second. She sprang out of the carriage and threw her arms around her mother’s neck, and for a minute she only felt sorrow for the woman she loved, not for herself. The four little girls crowded behind, their faces bright with relief—wherever Gwendolen was, something interesting always happened. Even poor meek Miss Merry the governess scurried around, paying the driver and seeing to the trunks, not expecting a greeting. Gwendolen dragged her mother up to their shared favorite bedroom, the black and yellow one with the soft velvet curtains, and kissed the tears off her cheeks. “Never mind, mamma,” she whispered. “I’m here now. I’ll do something. I’ll make things right.” For a few bright minutes, she meant it completely, brimming over with generous, fierce resolve.

But the hope ebbed as the day wore on, when the full weight of their ruin settled over her. Her mother broke the news slow, gentle as she could: they had to move out of Offendene at once, there was no money to pay for the servants or the fuel to heat the big drafty house. They couldn’t afford to travel abroad at all. The only cheap cottage they could find, the only place they could possibly live? Sawyer’s Cottage. The tiny two-parlor, four-bedroom hovel that had once been home to the village exciseman.

Gwendolen went white with rage. “That’s impossible. Uncle mustn’t let that happen. I won’t submit to that.” Her mother tried to explain: every last penny of their money was gone, lost in a reckless mine speculation, they had nothing at all except the small charity her uncle and aunt were willing to give them. The only prospects anyone had thought up for her? Governess jobs, one in a bishop’s household with three awkward daughters, another at a fancy girls’ school, paying a paltry hundred pounds a year.

“Be a governess?” Gwendolen laughed, sharp and bitter. “Shut up tutoring three girls as dull as Alice, getting looked down on by every rich family that thinks they’re doing me a favor? I’d rather emigrate than do that.”

She was already reaching for the desk by the window, her mind flying back to the brilliant, sharp-witted musician Herr Klesmer, who was staying with the wealthy Arrowpoint family at their house in Quetcham. She knew he’d seen her sing once, had told her she had a rare, wonderful gift for performance. Maybe that gift could be her ticket, not just out of Sawyer’s Cottage, but to fame, to a life worthy of her. She scribbled a quick, urgent note: begging him to call on her the very next day, to give her advice on a matter of life or death, that her family’s ruin had forced her to make her way in the world using her talent, and she trusted no one but his great judgment to guide her.

She sent the note off with a farm boy from nearby Leek’s, told him to wait for a reply. As she paced the big quiet drawing room, her old confidence coming back, she told herself that if anyone could help her make a name for herself, escape the petty drudgery everyone else was pushing her toward, it was Klesmer. For the first time since the ruin broke, she dared to hope that she was still that remarkable girl, that her charm and her wit and her talent would not let her be dragged down into the small, miserable life everyone else was planning for her.
