Fear not, gentle reader, for the fate of young Mr. Harry Warrington. Though his ill-fated mount—that wretched, broken-kneed beast of Mr. Will’s—sent him sprawling upon the road, rest assured our hero is not so easily dispatched from this tale. We have already mourned one Virginian; must we now bury the other before he has scarcely tasted manhood? No indeed. Heroes are not so hastily swept from the stage unless the plot demands it, and ours has only just begun to unfold.

Harry, though bruised and shaken, is very much alive. He has been carried into a welcoming household, where the bell was rung by servants of Madame de Bernstein, and there he now lies, tended with care. To prove his survival beyond doubt, here follows a letter from the lady who has taken him in—a letter addressed to none other than his mother, Mrs. Esmond Warrington, of Castlewood, Virginia.

---

If memory serves you kindly, you may recall a girl named Molly Benson, your schoolmate at Kensington some three-and-twenty years ago. We quarreled often—usually, I confess, through my own fault—but I remember still the bright, spirited Rachel Esmond, the “Princess Pocahontas” whom so many of us admired.

Time has softened old grievances, Rachel. I still treasure the gold *etui* your father gave me after our performance of *Julius Caesar* on speech-day. Only yesterday, I dreamed we were girls again, reciting lessons before stern Miss Hardwood, with you, as ever, surpassing me. How vividly those days return! The walks in Kensington Gardens, the scent of lavender in our chests of drawers (yours always neater than mine), the whispered stories in the dormitory—do you remember?

But I digress. The true purpose of my writing is to set your mind at ease regarding your son, Mr. Henry Warrington. He is here, under my roof, and though he suffered a fall from his horse, he is in no mortal danger. My husband, Colonel Lambert (a skilled hand in such matters, thanks to his army service), has set his dislocated shoulder and bled him, and the doctor pronounces him well on the mend.

Indeed, I almost wish his recovery were slower, for his presence has been a delight. Last evening, as we sat at tea, the gate-bell clanged urgently—a rare sound in our quiet home. Our servants rushed out and returned bearing a young gentleman, pale and unconscious, who had tumbled from his saddle upon the road. My husband, ever the Good Samaritan, brought him inside, where we have since tended him.

Your cousin, Lady Maria Esmond, displayed the tenderest concern for him, though the Baroness de Bernstein, being of more advanced years, was eager to continue her journey to Tunbridge Wells once assured of his safety. My husband, with a physician’s confidence, declared he would sooner trust his own treatment than that of any local doctor, and so the ladies departed, leaving Harry in our care.

He is a most charming young man, Rachel. Handsome, with a noble bearing—my own boys, fine as they are, cannot compare. When he spoke of his late brother, my heart ached for you. A mother’s grief is a sorrow no time truly mends.

Fear not; he shall soon be strong enough to travel. My husband will escort him as far as Westerham, where an old comrade resides. And since this letter will not be posted until he is fully recovered, you may trust he is in safe hands.

*P.S.* How strange fate is! That your son should stumble upon our very gate—that I, of all people, should be the one to nurse him! I often think of our youthful quarrels now with a smile. Time teaches us forgiveness, does it not?

---

Harry Warrington possessed one of those faces which inspire instant trust—bright-eyed, ruddy-cheeked, with a look so frank it disarmed even the wary. Yet let it not be said he was a mere innocent. Youth may wear the guise of artlessness, but beneath it often lurks a shrewdness keener than age’s seasoned cunning.

As he lay abed in the Lamberts’ finest chamber, Mrs. Mary Lambert gazed upon him with maternal admiration. "What a fine figure he has!" she murmured to her husband. "Our Jack might have been as handsome, had not the smallpox marked him, and Charles—well, Charles takes after you, my dear."

The Colonel chuckled. "You need not say it aloud, Mrs. Lambert. I know well enough you measure every young man against our own boys."

She flushed. "And if I do? A mother cannot help but wonder which of her daughters might suit him."

"Ah! So I have guessed it. Already you are scheming to marry him to Theo or Hester—or even little Lucy, though she is still at school."

"You know me too well," she sighed.

"Because you wear your thoughts plain as print, my love," said the Colonel, kissing her hand.

They stole from the room, leaving Harry to his rest, and descended to the hall where their daughters awaited their French lesson. The Colonel, ever the jester, chose a scene from Molière’s *Tartuffe*, playing Orgon with exaggerated solemnity while Theo blushed and giggled over her lines.

The girls adored their father’s theatrics, and their mother beamed at the sight. There is no purer joy than that of a united family—no treasure greater than the love of faithful hearts.

That night, as Harry drifted between sleep and fevered dreams, he fancied he heard the Evening Hymn, sung as if by his brother George’s voice. The illusion comforted him, and he slept deeply, wrapped in the peace of a household where kindness reigned.
