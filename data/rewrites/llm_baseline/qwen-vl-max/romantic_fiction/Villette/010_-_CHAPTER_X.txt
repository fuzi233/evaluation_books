Madame Beck was a woman of unshakable composure—gentle with the world, yet untouched by affection for any part of it. Her own children did not stir her from the quiet discipline of her nature. She cared deeply for their welfare, watched over their health and future with vigilant attention, but never once did she show the warmth that might have softened her sternness. I’ve seen her seated in the garden, watching Trinette walk the little ones along a distant path, her face calm, thoughtful, composed. She often pondered what she called “leur avenir”—their future—but when the youngest, a frail and charming child, spotted her and broke free from the nurse, toddling eagerly toward her with laughter and breathless excitement, Madame would simply extend one hand to stop the sudden rush. “Prends garde, mon enfant!” she’d say, without a trace of emotion. The child would stand near her for a moment, then be led back to Trinette—no smile, no embrace, no tender word.

Her treatment of the eldest daughter, Désirée, was equally revealing, though in a different way. This girl was wilful, sharp-tongued, and cruelly clever at provoking others. “Quelle peste que cette Désirée! Quel poison que cet enfant là!” she was called alike in kitchen and classroom. She delighted in mischief—sneaking into servants’ rooms, tearing caps, soiling shawls, smashing porcelain in the dining room or raiding the storeroom for preserves and sweet wine, always leaving behind clues that pointed to the staff. When Madame learned of these acts, her only response was: “Désirée a besoin d’une surveillance toute particulière.” And so she kept her close, watching her constantly—not because she sought to correct her, but because she lacked the courage to confront her openly. She never explained the harm in such behavior, nor warned of its consequences. Instead, she relied on silence and scrutiny.

But this strategy failed. Désirée turned her attentions from the servants to her mother. Anything belonging to Madame’s dressing table or work desk that she could reach, she stole and hid. Madame saw it all, yet pretended not to. She had no strength of spirit to face her daughter’s faults directly. When something valuable disappeared, she would pretend she thought Désirée had taken it playfully, and ask her to return it. But Désirée had long since learned to lie as easily as she stole. She would deny having touched the brooch, the ring, or the scissors. Then Madame would feign belief, quietly watch her every move until she discovered where the stolen items were hidden—a crevice in the garden wall, a corner of the attic, an outbuilding. Once found, Madame would send Désirée out with Trinette, and while she was gone, retrieve the things herself.

Désirée, true to her cunning parentage, showed no shame when confronted with her loss. She remained perfectly unruffled.

The second child, Fifine, was said to resemble her father, who had died young. Though she carried her mother’s strong frame, blue eyes, and rosy cheeks, her spirit came from elsewhere. She was honest, joyful, impulsive—warm-hearted and energetic, though prone to accidents. One day, she tumbled headlong down a flight of stone steps. Madame, hearing the crash (she heard everything), hurried from the dining room, picked her up, and said calmly, “Cet enfant a un os de cassé.”

At first we hoped it was not serious. But it was. One arm hung limp and lifeless. “Let Meess take her,” Madame said, “et qu’on aille tout de suite chercher un fiacre.” Without hesitation, she left the house, cool and composed, to find a surgeon. The family doctor wasn’t home, but that didn’t matter. She searched until she found someone suitable, brought him back with her.

By then, I had already cut off Fifine’s sleeve, undressed her, and laid her gently in bed. We all gathered around—Trinette, the cook, the portress, and myself—watching anxiously as the new doctor entered. I focused on calming Fifine, whose cries, loud and desperate, filled the small, overheated room. They grew louder still as the stranger approached her bed.

“Let alone!” she cried fiercely in broken English. “I will not you—I want Dr. Pillule!”

“And Dr. Pillule is my very good friend,” replied the man in fluent English, “but he is busy three leagues away. So now, when we’re a little calmer, we’ll begin. Soon your arm will be bandaged and mended.”

He called for a glass of *eau sucrée*, gave her a few spoonfuls—Fifine loved sweets, and anyone could win her favor through food—and promised more after the procedure. Then he began his work.

He needed help, and asked the cook, a strong, sturdy woman, to assist. But she, along with the portress and nurse, fled. I hesitated to touch the child’s injured limb—my hands trembled—but before I could act, Madame stepped forward. Her hand was steady, mine shook.

“Ca vaudra mieux,” the doctor said, turning from me to her.

He made the right choice. My stoicism would have been forced; hers, genuine. When he finished, he nodded. “Merci, Madame; très bien, fort bien!” He admired her composure. She, in turn, appreciated his praise.

And perhaps it was not just her strength that impressed him. As the evening deepened and a lamp was lit, I noticed that unless Madame Beck were less than a woman, she could not help being drawn to him. This young doctor—though young—had a presence unlike any other. He stood tall in the cramped chamber, towering over the Dutch-made women around him. His profile was sharp and noble, his eyes quick and bright, though they darted too swiftly from face to face. Yet there was a kindness in them, and in his mouth, a charm. His chin was full, well-formed, almost Grecian. And his smile—oh, his smile—was hard to define. It pleased, yes, but also stirred something deeper, something private and unsettling, like a mirror held up to one’s own hidden flaws.

Yet Fifine liked it. Despite the pain, she reached out her hand at the end, saying goodnight with a friendly wave. He patted her hand gently, and then he and Madame descended together, talking animatedly, she in high spirits, he listening with a mild, amused air—just a hint of playful mischief beneath the surface.

I noticed that though he spoke French well, his English was flawless. His complexion, eyes, and build were distinctly English. But more than that—when he passed me on his way out, turning briefly to speak to Madame, I caught sight of him clearly. A memory surged, sudden and complete. This was the same man I had spoken to at the bureau—the one who helped me with my trunk, who guided me through the dark, rain-soaked park. And now, as he walked down the long vestibule, I recognized his step—the same firm, even stride I had followed under the dripping trees.

It seemed likely that this visit would be his last. Dr. Pillule was expected home the next day, so there was no reason for the substitute to return. But fate had other plans. Dr. Pillule had been called to attend a wealthy, anxious old man in the ancient university town of Bouquin-Moisi. After prescribing travel and change of air, he was asked to accompany the patient on a journey lasting several weeks. Thus, the young doctor remained.

I saw him often. Madame refused to leave Fifine in Trinette’s care; she insisted I spend much time in the nursery. I believed he was skilled. Fifine recovered quickly under his care. But even as she healed, he stayed. Destiny and Madame Beck seemed aligned—both determined he should become familiar with every corner of the Rue Fossette.

No sooner had Fifine left his care than Désirée claimed to be ill. The girl had a gift for pretense. Captivated by the attention and indulgence of sickness, she decided illness suited her perfectly and took to her bed. She performed admirably, and Madame played her part with equal skill—though she saw through the deception completely, she treated it with solemn gravity and false concern.

What surprised me most was that Dr. John—so named by Fifine, and soon adopted by all of us—agreed to go along with the charade. At first, he looked uncertain, glanced between mother and daughter, paused as if weighing the truth. But then he smiled faintly, shrugged, and joined in the farce.

Désirée ate heartily, jumped about in bed, built tents from sheets, lounged among pillows, threw shoes at her nurse, mocked her sisters—overflowing with health and mischief. She only appeared weak when her mother and doctor arrived.

I knew Madame was glad to have Désirée out of the way, but I wondered why Dr. John continued. Every day, he came punctually, received the same warm welcome, the same exaggerated concern from Madame. He wrote harmless prescriptions and watched her with a sharp, amused eye. She met his glances without anger—she had too much sense for that.

Though flexible in manner, he was not subservient. He enjoyed his role at the pensionnat, lingered strangely around the Rue Fossette, yet carried himself with independence. Still, he often seemed lost in thought, distant.

It was not my place to question his demeanor, yet I could not help observing. He paid me little notice—only the kind of attention one gives to a chair or a carpet, something unremarkable. Often, while waiting for Madame, he would gaze out the window, smile to himself, listen, as if believing he were alone. And I, meanwhile, studied him—his face, his movements, the peculiar blend of interest, attachment, and mystery that bound him to this secluded household.

He never seemed aware that I observed him. Nor would he have known, had it not been for one afternoon, when I sat near him, studying the golden hue of his hair, his beard, his skin—the way light caught him, making him glow like a statue from legend. I compared him silently to Nebuchadnezzar’s golden image, shining under the sun.

Then, suddenly, a new idea struck me—sharp, vivid, undeniable. I don’t know how I looked at him. Surprise and certainty overwhelmed me. I forgot myself entirely—until I saw his reflection in the oval mirror beside the window, catching my movement.

Though naturally cheerful, he was sensitive to direct gazes. Startled, he turned and said, his tone polite but dry, edged with mild rebuke: “Mademoiselle does not spare me. I am not vain enough to believe it is my merits that draw her attention. It must be some defect. Dare I ask—what?”

I was stunned—yet not utterly undone. I had not stared from admiration or curiosity, but from a quiet, unspoken awareness. I could have defended myself, but chose not to. I remained silent. I rarely spoke to him. Let him think what he wished. I returned to my sewing, keeping my head bent low for the rest of his visit.

There is a strange comfort in being misunderstood—especially when truth cannot be known. In places where one is never truly seen, it is oddly satisfying to be ignored completely. Who among us has not, when mistaken for a thief, felt a flicker of amusement rather than outrage?
