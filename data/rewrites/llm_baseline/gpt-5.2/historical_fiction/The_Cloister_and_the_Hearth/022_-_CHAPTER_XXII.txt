The change came so swift it seemed witch-work: one instant the bolt sang through the air, the next there was a stunned hush, as if the very birds had swallowed their notes. Even the man who had loosed the shot stood gaping for a heartbeat—then his voice cracked out, summoning the rest to help net the quarry, and he himself began to scramble up the rise.

He had not won halfway when a shape rose against the pale strip of sky: Martin Wittenhaagen, bow bent, the cord drawn to the cheek, the whole man turned into a single tense line—wood, sinew, and will. Eric Wouverman, who had the stomach to hunt lovers with iron and rope, had not the stomach to face that bow. He slipped behind a tree as a cook slips behind a hanging of herbs when a master’s eye is sharp, pressing himself flat to bark and shadow.

Martin’s name had long been salted with stories—how he could send a shaft so clean it seemed to part the air without disturbing it. The dead dog, still warm in memory, was proof enough. Wouverman craned out, cautious as a man lifting a lid from a pot he fears has boiled over; there—glinting, unwavering—was the arrowhead, fixed on him like a skewer poised above meat. He dared not stir. The forest held its own acoustics: the rasp of his breath against his teeth, the minute tapping of sap on leaf, a far-off jay’s complaint—each sound suddenly loud, each silence a threat.

He played that fearful little game of peeping round the trunk for minutes that felt like hours, until boots and voices came swelling up behind him—his companions arriving in a press. Only then did Martin let his scorn show. A short laugh—dry as old crust—broke the tension; and he vanished, as if the wood had folded him into itself. Almost at once the muffled drumming of hooves told the rest: he was off, riding the mule hard.

The men surged to the top together. From the high ground they looked down into a narrow glade that ran on and on, a green corridor seemingly without end. Far away—so far the eye could barely name them—Gerard and Margaret were fleeing, two dark motes on the bright strip, like gnats dancing at the edge of sight. And behind them, stretched low over the beast’s neck, Martin galloped ventre à terre. The hunters had been beaten twice over: outwitted, and outrun.

To understand Martin’s craft, one must be careful with “coincidences.” They are like a hare’s stumble at the very crack of a gun: it makes men cry, “Hit!” and then, with a contemptuous flick, the creature is off untouched, and their hopes lie flat in the dust.

So it had been with the burgomaster’s mule. At the very moment the crossbow-bolt hissed over her head—harmless as a curse that misses its mark—her hoof found a rabbit-hole. Down she went, sudden as a sack dropped from a cart, and threw both riders. Gerard caught Margaret as instinctively as hands catch a loaf slipping from the peel, but her weight and the impetus of the fall carried him under; and for an instant the earth was strewn with actors in the play—lover, maid, mule—tangled together.

The mule, docile as ever, was on her feet again almost at once, trembling all over, ears laid back, nostrils flaring at the smell of churned soil and fear. Martin rose next. A quick look told him the truth: only one pursuer had gained on them. In that instant he made his choice. He sent the young pair forward on foot—lightened of the beast—and stayed to hold the line, doing precisely what had just been seen: pinning Wouverman to a tree with the mere promise of a shaft.

Then Martin wheeled and drove after them. It was a long chase, the kind that is measured not by distance but by breath: the rasp in the throat, the sting of cold air in the lungs, the drum of blood in the ears louder than any shouting left behind. When he came up at last, he wasted no time in tenderness or talk. He hoisted Gerard and Margaret onto the mule, then ran at her side until his own breathing went ragged; when his wind failed, he took the saddle, and one of the lovers ran, and so they changed in turn—rider and runner alternating like hands at a kneading-trough, so the work never stopped and no one tired beyond recovery.

In that steady rotation—horseflesh, human sinew, and a grim intelligence that understood the value of rhythm—they slipped their enemies. Long before they dared to ease their pace, every sound of pursuit had thinned into nothing; no crack of twig, no clink of tackle, no far halloo came riding the air. The forest’s own music returned—wind in needles, leaf-talk, the distant chuckle of water—and with it the knowledge that the hunters were left with only their humiliation.

Dierich and his men, beaten and empty-handed, went trudging back—crestfallen—to seek their chief and the dog that had run on so bravely and so fatally, as if it had been given wings only to fly into death.
