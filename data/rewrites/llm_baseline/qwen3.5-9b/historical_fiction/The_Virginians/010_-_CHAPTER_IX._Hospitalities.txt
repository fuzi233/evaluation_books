His Excellency, the Commander-in-Chief, departed for a visit to Madam Esmond with a splendor befitting the highest authority in His Majesty's vast dominions across North America. A detachment of dragoons marched ahead, leading the procession out of Williamsburg amidst the raucous cheers of a devoted, predominantly Black populace. The General traveled in his own carriage, attended at the threshold by Captain Talmadge, his Master of the Horse, who rode alongside the heavy, ornately decorated vehicle throughout the journey to Castlewood. Seated within the carriage were Major Danvers, the aide-de-camp, and the postmaster from Philadelphia, Mr. Benjamin Franklin. Once merely a printer's apprentice, Franklin had evolved into a remarkably astute individual, possessing a wealth of knowledge concerning the colony and England itself—where he had previously resided—that commanded the respect of the General and the entire Esmond family.

"'Tis truly remarkable how someone of such humble origins could acquire such diverse learning and refinement," His Excellency remarked graciously, tipping his hat to the postmaster.

Franklin bowed politely, attributing his polish to the fortunate chance of associating with gentlemen like the General, which allowed him to study their manners and adapt accordingly. Regarding formal education, he modestly noted his limitations, citing his father's financial struggles and the sparse educational resources available in his native New England. "However, I have exerted myself to the utmost," Franklin explained. "I possess nothing comparable to what exists in England."

General Braddock erupted in laughter. "Education? By George, there are officers in the army who cannot decide whether to spell 'bull' with one or two 'b's! I've heard the Duke of Marlborough was hardly a proficient penman either. Though I never had the honor of serving under that noble commander—he predated my time—I thoroughly thrashed the French despite his lack of scholarship."

"I am fully aware of both instances," Franklin replied calmly.

"Nor is my Duke a scholar," Braddock continued, noticing Franklin's knowing glance. "Aha, Mr. Postmaster, you've heard that tale too—I see the twinkle in your eye."

Instantly, Franklin withdrew his mischievous expression, replacing it with the wide-eyed innocence of a child. "He lacks scholarly pursuits, true, but he matches any French general in combat. He secured the crown for the best of kings, our Most Gracious Majesty, King George."

Franklin removed his hat, sending a cloud of powdered hair drifting from his large wig. "He is the soldier's greatest ally and the unyielding foe of those beggarly red-shanked Scottish rebels and cunning Roman Catholic Jesuits seeking to strip us of our liberties and faith. His Royal Highness, my gracious master, may not be a scholar, yet he ranks among the finest gentlemen in the world."

"I have witnessed His Royal Highness on horseback during a review of the Guards in Hyde Park," Franklin added. "The Duke is indeed a magnificent rider."

"You shall propose a toast to him today, Postmaster. He is the ideal master, friend, and son; the finest gentleman who has ever worn an epaulet."

"Epaulets are entirely outside my sphere of interest, sir," Franklin laughed. "I reside in a Quaker city."

"Naturally, they suit you less," the General agreed warmly. "Every man to his trade. You and your class belong to your studies, and we welcome you. Our role is to fight the enemy and govern the nation. Come, gentlemen! Look at these roads in this colony; how this infernal carriage bounces!"

Turning his gaze outward, he spotted two Negro boys in livery. "Who rides that fine gelding?"

"It is Mr. Washington," Major Danvers identified.

"I'd make him a corporal in the Horse Grenadiers," the General mused. "He carries himself well on horseback and clearly knows this terrain, Mr. Franklin."

"Yes, indeed," Franklin confirmed.

"And he is a remarkably genteel young man, considering his background. One might expect the polish of Europe given his opportunities."

"He strives his best," Franklin observed, casting an innocent look toward the stout Chief. The General sat swaying heavily in the carriage, his face flushed crimson matching his coat, swearing profusely while remaining ignorant of everything except the virtues of alcohol and women. Not of noble birth yet absurdly proud of his lineageless status, he was brave as a bulldog, savage yet generous, gentle in softer moments, easily amused, dull-witted, and completely uneducated. Yet, he believed his country supreme and himself equal to any gentleman in it.

"Yes, he is remarkably accomplished for a provincial," Franklin admitted. "Though he was defeated at Fort What-d'ye-call-it last year near the Thing-a-ma-jig River. What was the name of that place, Captain Talmadge?"

"The Lord knows, sir," Talmadge replied, adding dryly, "and I suspect the Postmaster is mocking us both."

"Oh, Captain!"

"A classic trap. He had only militia and Indians. Good day, Mr. Washington. Fine steed, sir. Was that your first engagement last year?"

"That at Fort Necessity? Yes, sir," Washington responded solemnly as he approached, flanked by two neatly dressed Negro grooms in velvet caps. "I began poorly, sir, having never seen action until that unfortunate day."

"You were all raw recruits, my good fellow. Imagine our militia fleeing from the Scots and cursing them. You needed proper troops."

"Your Excellency knows it is my fervent desire to serve alongside them," Washington stated earnestly.

"By George, we shall endeavor to grant that wish, sir," the General boomed, using one of his customary oaths. As the heavy carriage rolled toward Castlewood, Washington requested permission to ride ahead, announcing the General's imminent arrival to the hostess.

The Commander-in-Chief's progress was so glacial that several lesser invitees, eager to greet him, caught up to form a procession trailing the dust of his wheels. Leading this rear guard was Mr. Dinwiddie, the Lieutenant-Governor, accompanied by his Negro servants and Parson Broadbent, the jovial Williamsburg chaplain. Soon joined was Mr. Dempster, the schoolmaster, sporting a grand Ramillies wig reserved for state occasions. Appearing next was Mr. Laws, the judge, riding with Madam Laws on a pillion behind him, while a Negro servant carried her cap atop a mule. The spectacle was so comical that Major Danvers and Mr. Franklin burst into laughter, though quietly enough not to disturb the sleeping General. They signaled the quirky rearguard to proceed, leaving the Commander-in-Chief and his dragoon escort to arrive at their leisure. At Castlewood, there was ample space for everyone. Meat, drink, and the finest tobacco awaited His Majesty's soldiers; joy and celebration were prepared for the Negroes; and a hearty welcome extended to their masters.

The honest General required assistance reaching nearly every dish and constantly demanded refills. He praised Nathan's *sangaree* highly, having consumed it liberally upon arrival. The table groaned with cider, ale, brandy, and abundant Bordeaux wine—some brought personally by Colonel Esmond from England, deemed worthy even of pontiffs by the diminutive Mr. Dempster, who winked knowingly at Parson Broadbent. The clergyman reciprocated the gesture, drinking without concern for the Latin phrase, for he had never troubled himself with the language. Broadbent was a notorious gambler, drinker, and cockfighter who had spent considerable time in Fleet Prison and various London taverns. Sent to Virginia to manage a church living, he had become fast friends with young George Esmond, engaging in countless duels of cocks, hunts, fishing expeditions, and the slaughter of myriad birds. Rumors circulated that Broadbent was the midnight poacher pursued by Mr. Washington's dogs at Mount Vernon, resulting in a bruised face and torn coat after narrowly escaping capture. George and Broadbent shared all games and pastimes, while Harry Esmond reportedly stayed behind with Mr. Dempster, leaving George to read his own books and keep silent about his brother's exploits.

Every bird of Virginia's sky and every seasonal fish graced Madam Esmond's table to nourish the General and the gathering of English and American gentlemen. The gumbo was hailed as perfection—a dish so renowned that young George's Black servant, discovered hiding his head in a bowl of it, was christened Gumbo upon the spot. The shad were rich and fresh; the stewed terrapins were deemed worthy of London aldermen, and indeed, any duke or emperor. The Negro women possessed a natural genius for cooking, nurtured under the strict supervision of the late and current Madam Esmond. Certain delicacies, particularly sweets and tarts, were prepared by Madam Esmond herself with exquisite neatness. Following the kindly, cumbersome customs of the era, she rolled up her lace-sleeved cuffs, revealing charming arms and slender wrists as she carved the principal meats, demonstrating a hospitality far more vigorous than contemporary standards. The traditional law of the table dictated that the mistress press her guests with eager warmth, encouraging further enjoyment, mastering culinary arts, and executing carvings of poultry, fish, game, and joints. She cheered her guests to new efforts, whispering to Mr. Braddock, "I have reserved the salmon's cheek for Your Excellency—I accept no refusal!"
