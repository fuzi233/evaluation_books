The brothers’ parting being once accomplished, George had taken his place in General Braddock’s household and on the line of march, and Harry was left at Castlewood to the duties of the quiet roof. He did them as he must; but there was no savour in them. The very passages of the old house had altered with George’s absence, as a kitchen seems when the chief hand is away and the accustomed fire burns without its master. Harry went by his brother’s empty chamber with his eyes averted, as a man passes a cold hearth. At table he sat where George had sat, and drank from the silver tankard that had so often rung against his brother’s teeth; and the bright metal gave back no warmth, only a thin, accusing glimmer.

Yet the household would not let the vacancy sit in silence. Madam Warrington, stiff as ever in her chair (she never deigned the mercy of a back), called “The King!” with a soldierly stoutness every day; and on Sundays, when Harry read the service and came to the prayer for travellers by land and water, her “We beseech Thee to hear us” had a weight that made even the old boards seem to listen. She spoke of George constantly, not with sighs, but with a kind of drilled cheerfulness, as if his return were a date already written in an almanack. She walked into his room with her head upright and her face composed; saw his linen laid straight, his books squared, his papers kept as though he might enter at any moment and touch them; and at meals she appealed, with a particular respect, to the old servants about what must be done “when Mr. George comes home.”

Mrs. Mountain could not keep from whimpering whenever his name was spoken; and little Fanny, prim at her sampler, pricked her finger oftener than the pattern required. Harry, who had been so quick of laughter, wore now a look as if he were perpetually startled by some sound half-heard. Madam, outwardly grave, was changed in smaller ways: she made blunders at piquet and backgammon beyond anything her pride would formerly have pardoned; and if the servants rose early, they found her already awake and dressed, as if sleep itself had become an enemy to be met standing. She had even prayed Mr. Dempster to return to residence at Castlewood; and the little Jacobite, with his sour mouth and faithful heart, came back under her roof. She was gentler now in assertion and reply, less haughty with any of the party; and she spoke often of her own father and his campaigns—how he had come out of them all with no very severe wounds to cripple him—and so, she said, she hoped and trusted would her eldest son.

George, for his part, wrote often; and when the army took the woods, he began a rough journal, sending it by such chances as offered. That packet, when it arrived, was treated like a loaf newly drawn from the oven: carried reverently, broken open with eager fingers, and made to feed the whole house. More than once on long July evenings Harry read it aloud in council, while Madam sat bolt upright at her tea-table, the scent of her strong bohea rising with the steam; while little Fanny stitched, the thread whispering through linen; while Mr. Dempster and Mrs. Mountain sat at their cards; while the old servants moved softly in the dusk and paused, as though the very air of the room were a thing to be handled with care. Outside, the summer had its own music—the far frogs in the marsh, the cicadas’ incessant rasp, the clap of a shutter now and then; and between those natural sounds, George’s words came with a sharpness that made him seem bodily present.

Hear Harry reading his brother’s page, the yellow paper crackling as he turned it:

“It must be owned the provinces act scurvily by his Majesty King George II., and his representative here is in a flame of fury. Virginia is bad enough, and poor Maryland not much better, but Pennsylvania worst of all. We pray them to send us troops from home to fight the French; we promise to maintain the troops when they come. We not only don’t keep our promise and make scarce any provision for our defenders, but our people insist upon the most exorbitant prices for their cattle and stores, and actually cheat the soldiers who are come to fight their battles. No wonder the General swears, and the troops are sulky. The delays have been endless. Owing to the failure of the several provinces to provide their promised stores and means of locomotion, weeks and months have elapsed, during which time, no doubt, the French have been strengthening themselves on our frontier and in the forts they have turned us out of. Though there never will be any love lost between me and Colonel Washington, it must be owned that your favourite (I am not jealous, Hal) is a brave man and a good officer. The family respect him very much, and the General is always asking his opinion. Indeed, he is almost the only man who has seen the Indians in their war-paint; and I own I think he was right in firing upon Mons. Jumonville last year.

“There is to be no more suite to that other quarrel at Benson’s Tavern than there was to the proposed battle between Colonel W. and a certain young gentleman who shall be nameless. Captain Waring wished to pursue it on coming into camp, and brought the message from Captain Grace, which your friend, who is as bold as Hector, was for taking up, and employed a brother aide-de-camp, Colonel Wingfield, on his side. But when Wingfield heard the circumstances of the quarrel, how it had arisen from Grace being drunk, and was fomented by Waring being tipsy, and how the two 44th gentlemen had chosen to insult a militia officer, he swore that Colonel Washington should not meet the 44th men; that he would carry the matter straightway to his Excellency, who would bring the two captains to a court-martial for brawling with the militia, and drunkenness, and indecent behaviour; and the captains were fain to put up their toasting-irons, and swallow their wrath. They were good-natured enough out of their cups, and ate their humble-pie with very good appetites at a reconciliation dinner which Colonel W. had with the 44th, and where he was as perfectly stupid and correct as Prince Prettyman need be. Hang him! He has no faults, and that’s why I dislike him. When he marries that widow—ah me! what a dreary life she will have of it.”

“I wonder at the taste of some men, and the effrontery of some women,” Madam said, setting down her teacup so that the china rang—small, clear, and commanding in the room’s hush.

“I wonder at any woman who has been married once, so forgetting herself as to marry again! Don’t you, Mountain?”

“Monstrous!” Mrs. Mountain answered, with a queer look that did not prevent her from drawing little Fanny closer.

Mr. Dempster kept his eyes on his glass of punch as if the amber surface held a battle-map. Harry’s mouth twitched, as though laughter and some other hidden thing strove together in him; but Madam, unmoved, said, “Go on, Harry. Continue with your brother’s journal. He writes well; but, ah—will he ever be able to write like my papa?”

Harry read on, the paper soft under his thumb:

“We keep the strictest order here in camp, and the orders against drunkenness and ill-behaviour on the part of the men are very severe. The roll of each company is called at morning, noon, and night, and a return of the absent and disorderly is given in by the officer to the commanding officer of the regiment, who has to see that they are properly punished. The men are punished, and the drummers are always at work. Oh, Harry, but it made one sick to see the first blood drawn from a great strong white back, and to hear the piteous yell of the poor fellow.”

“Oh, horrid!” Madam said. “I think I should have murdered Ward if he had flogged me. Thank Heaven he got off with only a crack of the ruler!”

Harry’s voice dipped as he came upon a sentence he did not care to carry, like a dish too highly seasoned for a child’s palate. “The Indians have just broken up their camp, and retired in dudgeon, because the young officers were for ever drinking with the squaws—and—and—hum—ha—”

He stopped; and little Fanny bent lower over her stitches, listening with the fierce attention of innocence.

“Pass over that about the odious tipsy creatures,” Madam commanded; and Harry, grateful for the order, lifted his tone again.

“Each regiment has Divine Service performed at the head of its colours every Sunday. The General does everything in the power of mortal man to prevent plundering, and to encourage the people round about to bring in provisions. He has declared soldiers shall be shot who dare to interrupt or molest the market-people. He has ordered the price of provisions to be raised a penny a pound, and has lent money out of his own pocket to provide the camp. Altogether, he is a strange compound, this General. He flogs his men without mercy, but he gives without stint. He swears most tremendous oaths in conversation, and tells stories which Mountain would be shocked to hear—”

“Why me?” cried Mrs. Mountain. “And what have I to do with the General’s silly stories?”

“Never mind the stories; and go on, Harry,” Madam said.

“—would be shocked to hear after dinner; but he never misses service. He adores his Great Duke, and has his name constantly on his lips. Our two regiments both served in Scotland, where I dare say Mr. Dempster knew the colour of their facings.”

“We saw the tails of their coats, as well as their facings,” growled the little Jacobite, and there was in his tone the old rasp of Culloden—an accent that carried a whole lost cause inside a few words, and made plain how a man’s native turn of speech can preserve what his rulers would gladly have him forget.

“Colonel Washington has had the fever very smartly, and has hardly been well enough to keep up with the march. Had he not better go home and be nursed by his widow? When either of us is ill, we are almost as good friends again as ever. But I feel somehow as if I can’t forgive him for having wronged him. Good Powers! How I have been hating him for these months past! Oh, Harry! I was in a fury at the tavern the other day, because Mountain came up so soon, and put an end to our difference. We ought to have burned a little gunpowder between us, and cleared the air. But though I don’t love him, as you do, I know he is a good soldier, a good officer, and a brave, honest man; and, at any rate, shall love him none the worse for not wanting to be our stepfather.”

“A stepfather, indeed!” cried Harry’s mother. “Why, jealousy and prejudice have perfectly maddened the poor child! Do you suppose the Marquis of Esmond’s daughter and heiress could not have found other stepfathers for her sons than a mere provincial surveyor? If there are any more such allusions in George’s journal, I beg you skip ’em, Harry, my dear. About this piece of folly and blundering there hath been quite talk enough already.”

Harry went on—glad, perhaps, to escape from that nettle of words into the safer brushwood of camp detail:

“’Tis a pretty sight to see a long line of redcoats threading through the woods or taking their ground after the march. The care against surprise is so great and constant, that we defy prowling Indians to come unawares upon us, and our advanced sentries and savages have on the contrary fallen in with the enemy and taken a scalp or two from them. They are such cruel villains, these French and their painted allies, that we do not think of showing them mercy. Only think, we found but yesterday a little boy scalped but yet alive in a lone house, where his parents had been attacked and murdered by the savage enemy, of whom—so great is his indignation at their cruelty—our General has offered a reward of five pounds for all the Indian scalps brought in.

“When our march is over, you should see our camp, and all the care bestowed on it. Our baggage and our General’s tents and guard are placed quite in the centre of the camp. We have outlying sentries by twos, by threes, by tens, by whole companies. At the least surprise, they are instructed to run in on the main body and rally round the tents and baggage, which are so arranged themselves as to be a strong fortification. Sady and I, you must know, are marching on foot now, and my horses are carrying baggage. The Pennsylvanians sent such rascally animals into camp that they speedily gave in. What good horses were left, ’twas our duty to give up; and Roxana has a couple of packs upon her back instead of her young master. She knows me right well, and whinnies when she sees me, and I walk by her side, and we have many a talk together on the march.

“July 4. To guard against surprises, we are all warned to pay especial attention to the beat of the drum; always halting when they hear the long roll beat, and marching at the beat of the long march. We are more on the alert regarding the enemy now. We have our advanced pickets doubled, and two sentries at every post. The men on the advanced pickets are constantly under arms, with fixed bayonets, all through the night, and relieved every two hours. The half that are relieved lie down by their arms, but are not suffered to leave their pickets. ’Tis evident that we are drawing very near to the enemy now. This packet goes out with the General’s to Colonel Dunbar’s camp, who is thirty miles behind us; and will be carried thence to Frederick, and thence to my honoured mother’s house at Castlewood, to whom I send my duty, with kindest remembrances, as to all friends there, and how much love I need not say to my dearest brother from his affectionate—

GEORGE E. WARRINGTON.”

The page ended; the room breathed again. Outside, the July heat lay over the land like a lid upon a pot, pressing down all scent and sound into a heavy closeness. For ten days thereafter no news came from the column pushing toward the Ohio. It was told and believed, in parlours and at tavern bars, that their slow labour through the forest must soon bring them up with the enemy; that the troops, led by consummate captains, were now seasoned to the wilderness and not to be surprised; that every precaution had been taken against ambuscade. It was always the outlying enemy, so the talk ran, who were discovered, pursued, destroyed by vigilant scouts and skirmishers. The last certain word was that the army had advanced beyond the ground of Mr. Washington’s former discomfiture, and in two days more must be within a day’s march of the French fort. Of taking it no fears were entertained. The numbers of French reinforcements from Montreal were known; and Mr. Braddock, with his veteran regiments from Britain and their colonial allies, was judged more than a match for any troops that could be gathered under the white flag.

So men spoke, as men will speak when they season danger with confidence, like over-salting a thin broth to persuade themselves it is rich. At Castlewood, too, at their family repasts, they kept their tone cheerful—never naming dread, never admitting uneasiness. Madam vowed George should have the best horse in Virginia or Carolina in place of Roxana; horses enough could be had for money, she said; it was only for the King’s service they were not forthcoming. Yet the house, for all its brave talk, could not sit still. They were forever on the trot from one neighbour’s to another, sniffing for news as a cook sniffs the air for a scent of burning. Reports ran with prodigious speed. When the noted border warrior called Colonel Jack offered himself and his huntsmen and the General declined his terms and his service, the defection of Jack and his men became the talk of thousands of tongues at once.

The very servants carried and scattered tidings. The house negroes, in their midnight gallops about the country in search of junketing or sweethearts, brought back scraps and shreds of intelligence from astonishing distances, and flung them abroad. They knew, and laughed, at the cheats practised on the army for horses, provisions, and the like—how bacon was salted too hard and sold too dear, how flour was cut with what it should never have met, how cattle were driven in lean and priced as fat; and there was in that laughter a sharp truth about power and words: the same bargain called “patriot thrift” in one mouth was “rascality” in another, and the name a man gave his act could change its moral weight like a spice altering a dish.

For three weeks after the army’s departure, the thousand reports were cheerful; and at supper the Castlewood news was still pleasant. But on the 10th of July a vast and sudden gloom spread over the province, as if a storm-cloud had fallen without wind. Terror and doubt seemed to settle on every face. The affrighted servants eyed their masters and withdrew, humming and whispering together. The fiddles ceased in the quarters; the song and laugh of those cheery black folk were hushed, and the night, which had been full of life, lay listening to itself. Right and left, everybody’s servants were on the gallop for tidings. The country taverns were thronged with horsemen, who drank and cursed and brawled at the bars, each bringing his gloomy tale, as though the land had but one dreadful story and could not decide which tongue must tell it.

The army had been surprised. The troops had fallen into an ambuscade and had been cut up almost to a man. The officers were picked off by French marksmen and savage allies. The General had been wounded and carried off the field in his sash. Four days afterward, the report grew worse: the General was dead—and scalped by a French Indian.

Ah, what a scream poor Mrs. Mountain gave when Gumbo brought that news from across the James River; and little Fanny sprang, crying, to her mother’s arms, her thread dangling like a broken line of prayer.

“Lord God Almighty, watch over us, and defend my boy!” Madam Warrington cried; and for once her pride gave way to nakedness of need, so that she sank on her knees and lifted her rigid hands to Heaven. The room’s sounds—the child’s sob, the mother’s gasp, the tea-spoon’s faint chink—seemed suddenly immense, as if the world had been emptied of all else.

The gentlemen were not at home when the rumour arrived; but within an hour or two they came in, each from his hunt for news. Mr. Dempster did not dare to look up and meet the widow’s agonised gaze. Harry Warrington was as pale as his mother. It might not be true about the manner of the General’s death—but he was dead. The army had been surprised by Indians, and had fled, and been killed without seeing the enemy. An express had arrived from Dunbar’s camp; fugitives were pouring in there.

Should he go and see? He must go and see.

He and stout little Dempster armed themselves and mounted, taking a couple of mounted servants with them. They followed the northward track which the expeditionary force had hewn out for itself; and at every mile that brought them nearer, the disaster of that fearful day seemed to magnify, as a sound swells when one walks toward its source. The land itself appeared to have changed its voice: fewer birds; the wind more restless in the leaves; the hoofbeats too loud on the hard road. It was as if Nature, which had sung so steadily through the summer nights at Castlewood, now held its breath and listened to the far-off cracking of muskets that still seemed to echo between the trees.

The day after the defeat, a number of the miserable fugitives from the fatal battle of the 9th July had reached Dunbar’s camp, fifty miles from the field. Thither poor Harry and his companions rode, stopping stragglers, asking news, giving money, getting from one and all the same dreadful reckoning: a thousand men slain; two-thirds of the officers down; all the General’s aides-de-camp hit.

Hit? But were they killed?

Those who fell never rose again, said the broken men. The tomahawk did its work upon them.

O brother, brother! All the fond memories of their youth—shared bread at the schoolroom table, the taste of apples stolen together and eaten breathless behind a hedge, the laughter that had rung along the river-bank, the romantic vows pledged in boyhood as though spoken in some private dialect only they two understood—came back upon Harry with pangs that no words could fully carry. Wounded men looked up and were softened by his grief. Rough women melted when they saw the woe written on the handsome young face. The hardy old tutor could scarcely look at him for tears, and grieved for him even more than for the dear pupil whom he believed lay dead under the savage knife.
