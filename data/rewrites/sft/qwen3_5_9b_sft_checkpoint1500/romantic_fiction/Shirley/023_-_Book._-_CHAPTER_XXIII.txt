The heat of a fine summer day had pressed down upon Caroline, sealing her within the solitude of the Rectory. With her uncle away at Whinbury, the long, bright, and utterly noiseless hours that followed sunrise stretched before her like a desert journey across shadowless sands, devoid of track or companion. Though surrounded by the blooming gardens of an English home, she felt as isolated as if wandering the wastes of Sahara. Sitting in her alcove, the needle moved steadily in her fingers, yet her mind worked with restless agitation.

"Fanny!" a low voice called from the depths of the room.

At the threshold, Fanny paused, peering out over the lawn before glancing back into the shadows. Seeing no sign of her mistress, she cried out softly, "Miss Caroline!"

From the alcove, a faint "Fanny!" answered. Hastening forward, Fanny placed a note into trembling fingers. Miss Helstone did not inquire of its origin nor inspect it immediately; instead, she allowed it to slip from her grasp, landing silently upon the folds of her embroidery.

"Joe Scott's boy, Harry, brought it," Fanny whispered.

Though Fanny possessed no magic, her words cast a spell upon her young mistress. Caroline raised her head, her lethargy vanishing instantly into a sharp, questioning alertness.

"Harry Scott! Who sent him?"

"He came from the Hollow."

The note was snatched up, the seal torn away, and read with eager intensity. It was a letter from Hortense, announcing her return from Wormwood Wells. Robert had gone to Whinbury market, leaving Hortense alone. She expressed a profound desire for Caroline's company at tea, noting that such a change would be most beneficial to Caroline, who currently lacked both safe guidance and improving society due to the estrangement caused by the dispute between Robert and Mr. Helstone. The letter signed off with Hortense's title, "meilleure amie," and ended with a postscript urging Caroline to put on her bonnet and run down immediately.

Caroline needed no further urging. She laid aside the brown holland child's slip she had been trimming, rising swiftly to cover her curls with her straw bonnet and wrap her shoulders in a black silk scarf. The simple drapery suited her slender frame, contrasting beautifully with the purity of her dress and the fairness of her face. She fled the solitude, the sadness, and the nightmare of her daily existence, running down the green lane that sloped toward the Hollow. Even though she knew Robert was not at the cottage, the mere act of returning to his home, to the room where he had sat that morning, felt like a reunion. As she walked, illusion danced before her in a peri-mask; the soft agitation of invisible wings caressed her cheek, and the warm air seemed to whisper, *"Robert may come home while you are in his house. At least you may look in his face—give him your hand. Perhaps, for a minute, you may sit beside him."*

"Silence!" she responded internally, yet she clung to the comfort of the vision.

Through the branches of the garden shrubs, Miss Moore spotted the gleam of Caroline's white attire. Emerging from the cottage porch, she greeted her visitor. Usually straight, unbending, and phlegmatic, Hortense softened as she marked the delight in Caroline's eyes. She embraced her tenderly, leading her inside with a mixture of genuine pleasure and calculated flattery. Hortense was half-deceived by Caroline's childish joy and wholly flattered by it. Had she known clearly to whom this affection was truly directed, Hortense might have felt shocked, for sisters rarely approve when their younger siblings fall in love with their brothers. To them, such feelings appear presumptuous, silly, or a delusion—a crude romance that repels them.

"You would expect to find me alone, from what I wrote," observed Miss Moore, conducting Caroline toward the parlour. "But this was written this morning. Since dinner, company has arrived."

As she opened the door, it revealed an ample spread of crimson skirts overflowing an elbow-chair at the fireside, presided over by a cap more formidable than a crown. This hat, a massive balloon of black silk held wide with whalebone, had never worn a bonnet; it was brought in a vast bag. Its frill stood a quarter of a yard broad around the face, adorned with ribbons of the "love-kind," flourishing in puffs and bows. This was Mrs. Yorke, who had visited in a friendly manner to take tea, a favor as rare and grand as if a queen had shared pot-luck with a subject.

Though Mrs. Yorke generally scorned visiting and labeled local matrons as "gossips," she was a favorite with Miss Moore. She claimed to prefer Miss Moore's steady deportment, lack of levity, and foreign status, which required a friend. In reality, Mrs. Yorke admired plainness, homeliness, and unattractiveness, condemning beauty and elegance.

Caroline approached the mighty matron with diffidence, knowing little of her. She received a cool reception and quickly hid her discomfort by removing her bonnet. Immediately, a little personage in a blue frock and sash darted from behind her mother's chair, throwing her arms around Caroline's neck.

"My mother is not civil to you," the child, Jessie, declared as she received a smiling salute. "Rose there takes no notice of you. It is their way. If a white angel with a crown of stars had come, mother would nod stiffly. But I will be your friend—I have always liked you."

"Curb that tongue of yours, Jessie!" scolded Mrs. Yorke.

"But, mother, you are so frozen!" Jessie protested. "Miss Helstone has never harmed you. Why can't you be kind? You sit so stiff and look so cold... Rose is such an aut—machine in the shape of a human being. You will drive every soul away."

"I am an automaton? Good! Let me alone," Rose spoke from a corner, seated on the carpet with a volume of Mrs. Radcliffe's *The Italian* spread on her knees. She offered Caroline a brief, unreadable glance before returning to her study.

Caroline, possessing both tact and fine instinct, sensed Rose was peculiar, one of the unique. Kneeling quietly beside her, she looked over the child's shoulder. Rose pointed to the page, asking if Caroline was ready.

"Do you like it?" Rose inquired later.

"Long since, when I read it as a child, I was wonderfully taken with it," Caroline replied. "It seemed to open with promise, such foreboding of a strange tale."

"And reading it feels as if you were far away from England—in Italy, under a southern sky," Rose observed. "It makes me long to travel."

"When you are a woman, perhaps you may gratify your wish."

"I mean to make a way, if one is not made for me. I cannot live always in Briarfield. I must see the outside of our round planet."

"How much?"

"First this hemisphere, then the other. I am resolved that my life shall be a life—not a black trance like a toad's buried in marble, nor a slow death like yours in this rectory."

"Like yours!" Caroline asked, startled. "What do you mean?"

"Might you not as well be tediously dying as forever shut up in that glebe-house, a place that reminds me of a windowed grave? I never see movement, never hear a sound. Smoke never issues from the chimneys. What do you do there?"

"I sew, I read, I learn lessons."

"Are you happy?"

"Would I be happy wandering alone as you wish?"

"Much happier, even if you did nothing but wander. In a day, you pass many hills and woods, each altering as the sun shifts. Nothing changes in Briarfield. The plaster, the paper, the curtains—they remain the same."

"Is change necessary to happiness?"

"Yes."

"Is it synonymous with it?"

"I don't know, but I feel monotony and death are almost the same."

Just then, Jessie interjected, "Isn't she mad?"

"But, Rose," Caroline countered, "I fear a wanderer's life would end like the tale you read—in disappointment, vanity, and vexation."

"Does *The Italian* so end?"

"I thought so."

"Better to try all things and find them empty than to try nothing and leave life a blank. To bury one's talent is despicable sluggishness!"

"Rose," Mrs. Yorke intervened, "solid satisfaction is only realized by doing one's duty."

"Right, Mother! And if the Master gave me ten talents, my duty is to trade them and multiply them. Not in the dust of household drawers, nor in a broken teapot, nor hidden beneath piles of woolen hose. Least of all will I hide them in a tureen of cold potatoes." Rising from the floor, Rose continued, "The Lord will demand an account. These vessels will yield up their barren deposits. Suffer your daughters to put their money to the exchangers!"

Mrs. Yorke dismissed this with a request for Rose to sit and do a line of marking. After a pause, she asked if Rose felt oppressed.

"No, Mother."
