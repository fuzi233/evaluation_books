I rose, determined to continue my journey, and walked on through the endless desert miles. How I longed for a mountain, or even a towering rock, from which I might gaze beyond the desolate plain and the cracked, lifeless channels—any vantage point that might reveal a glimmer of hope on the horizon. But what good would sight ever do me? It is not what lies outside a man that shapes his fate, but what stirs within him. The event is not in the vision, but in the transformation it brings. To foresee is not to understand—otherwise, prophecy would bloom more often from the soul’s hidden depths.

The sun was halfway to the edge of the sky when I saw before me a jagged, rocky rise. Yet by the time I reached it, the desire to climb had vanished. Instead, I craved only stillness, rest. The sun dipped low, casting its final light, and the air began to darken. At my feet lay a soft, emerald moss—a royal bed beneath a sky of fading fire. I collapsed onto it, and as soon as my head touched the earth, I heard again the distant murmur of water: broken songs rising from buried streams, echoing through stone-choked channels. The music was wild, yet sublime—an ethereal chaos played by the aquarian harp, sending ripples of sound into my ears. What wonders Händel might have woven from such a refrain—the gurgle, the bell-like drip, the melodies that intertwined and then dissolved into one another! As I listened, my eyes wandered up the steep slope above me, tracing the ancient scars where once a cataract thundered down, carving its path through the stone. My heart swelled at the memory of that great tumult—waves dancing in their reckless fall, merging into a single, roaring organ of sound below.

But soon the hidden brooks lulled me into sleep, their lullabies weaving through my dreams.

I woke before dawn, eager to see what lay beyond the ridge. Alas, nothing but a vast expanse of fine, shifting sand stretched before me. No trace remained of the river that had once plunged over the rocks. The wind had filled its course with dust, leveling it into an empty plain. Looking back, I realized the river had split into two branches—one I’d followed to this spot, the other leading toward the Evil Wood. The wood now loomed faintly on the far horizon between them. Before me, the desert stretched endlessly. To the right, however, I saw a subtle rise in the skyline—a hint of forest, perhaps, the one my hostess had spoken of. I sat down and fumbled in my pocket for the half-loaf I had brought. Only then did I grasp her meaning. The bread was no longer food—it had shrunk, hardened, turned to stone. I tossed it aside and set off again.

By noon, I passed a few tamarisk and juniper trees, then stunted firs. As I pressed onward, thickets grew denser, pines taller, until I stood beneath a forest much like the one where the Little Ones found their children—perhaps the same woods, extended across time and space. But what difference did *where* matter, if *everywhere* was indistinguishable from *nowhere*? I had not yet done anything to make any place real. I was not truly alive—I was merely dreaming of living. A consciousness with a view, but no purpose. In my old world, I had been nothing more than this; now I knew it. I told myself: if I glimpsed the mirror here, I would turn away. I would not let it lure me back into the illusion of my former self. Here, I could become something—not just a ghost of memory, but a being shaped by action.

I could not bear the thought of returning—of having begun so many times, yet never completing anything. The Little Ones would face their destinies. The witch I feared, I would never meet. The dead would awaken without me. And I? I would wake to realize I had dreamed—and that all my wandering had led nowhere. I would rather walk forever than end like that.

I ventured deeper into the forest, weary, seeking shelter. The trees were large, standing in orderly rows, almost geometric in their spacing. Between them, open spaces allowed clear sight in every direction. There was little undergrowth. The silence was profound—like a cathedral emptied of worshipers. Occasionally, a swift shadow darted across my line of sight, or a slow-moving creature crept through the distance. Always far away, always unseen. They only deepened the emptiness.

I heard birdsong, saw butterflies—some dazzling in color, others pure white, radiant as snow. When I came upon a clearing where roses bloomed among the pines, I hoped it meant a dwelling nearby. I followed the trail of blossoms, hungry for the voice, the face, the presence of kindred life—any living soul, human or otherwise, whom I might begin to know.

What a hell it must be, I thought, to wander alone—trapped in oneself, unable to expand into another’s existence, bound by the narrow cords of one’s own nature. An eternal prisoner in the dungeon of the self.

I began to understand: one cannot truly live alone—even for oneself—unless in the company of others. And even then, it becomes terrifyingly possible. Evil exists only through good. Selfishness is merely a parasite feeding on the tree of life.

In my old world, I sang alone, often without thinking. Here, no song escaped my lips. There, I sang freely; here, I thought deeply—but never sang. There, I had no close friend; here, even the affection of an idiot would feel divine.

“If only I had a dog to love,” I sighed, staring at the man I once was—the man who chose books over people, pens over faces, stories over lives. If an author appeared while I read his tale, I wished him gone so I could return to the fiction. I preferred the dead to the living, thoughts to thinkers.

“Any man,” I said now, “is worth more than the greatest book.”

I had neglected my brothers and sisters. Now, even the dead were gone—no comfort left.

The forest thinned further. The pines grew immense, their trunks like columns reaching for the heavens. Other trees joined them—richer, more varied. The roses became trees, their blooms astonishing in splendor.

Then I saw it—a shape that seemed a great house or castle. But its form was blurred, uncertain. Was it a trick of shadows and foliage? As I approached, the lines held, yet never clarified. When I stood before it, I still could not say what it was. Not a habitable structure—more likely a ruin swallowed by ivy and roses. Yet I found no trace of masonry, no wall, no foundation. Again and again, I thought I saw architecture—but each time, it dissolved upon closer look.

Could it be that the ivy had consumed a grand edifice, preserving its shape in tangled roots and vines? I could not be sure.

Before me was a rectangular void—a doorway without a door. I stepped through, and found myself in a vast open space—like a hall, but covered in grass and flowers. The walls and roof were made of ivy, vine, and roses. No better place to spend the night.

I gathered dry leaves, piled them in a corner, and lay down. A crimson sunset painted the hall. The night was warm. I stared upward at the living ceiling—branches crisscrossing, leaves forming clouds, patches of higher canopy peering through. My eyes drifted, entangled in the greenery, until the sun sank and darkness crept in.

The red roses turned black. Then only yellow and white remained. When they too faded, stars emerged—hanging in the leaves like live topazes, pulsing with color, flashing, shimmering. I was beneath a tree from Aladdin’s cave.

Then I noticed the nests—tiny heads popping out, chirping softly, vanishing again. Rustlings, whispers, small prayers. As night deepened, the heads stilled. Every mother bird nestled her young. God’s nursery rested beneath the waves of sleep.

A flutter drew my gaze—then an owl swept silently across the sky. I caught only a glimpse, but felt the cool breath of his wings several times. The birds didn’t stir. They knew he sought mice, not chicks.

At midnight, I awoke—roused by a quiet revelry. The sounds were near, yet subdued, almost delicate. My eyes were dazzled at first, seeing nothing. Then clarity returned.

I was lying on my leaves in the corner of a magnificent hall. Before me danced a crowd—men in rich garments, women in flowing robes. None seemed to notice me. Dance after dance unfolded—each movement a story of life: meetings, passions, partings. As a student of Shakespeare, I recognized some—the minuet, the pavin, the hey, the coranto, the lavolta. Their costumes matched the age of their dances.

A moon had risen, casting light through the leafy roof. But so many shadows crossed it that I could barely distinguish faces. Yet something was wrong. I sat up to see better.

Heavens—could those be faces?

They were skulls—bone gleaming, jaws bare, noses truncated, teeth lipless, fixed in grimaces that could never smile. Some teeth flashed white and sharp—murderous. Others were stained, broken, colored by the earth they seemed to have lain in for centuries.

And the eye sockets—were not empty. Each held a lidless, living eye!

In those ruined faces, eyes burned—glowing, flashing, sparkling—every color, every shape, every expression. A proud, dark eye, lustrous and condescending—more terrible for its beauty. A languishing, lovely eye—more repulsive for its longing. And the dim, sad eyes—less incongruous, yet infinitely sorrowful, pulling at the heart despite the horror.

I rose, stepping among them, desperate to understand. Were they souls? Or mere phantasms of the past? Not a gesture, not a glance, betrayed awareness of me. I was invisible to them. Were they aware of each other? Did they see one another as I saw them? Or were they each trapped in their own dream?

Did they know how they appeared—to each other, to themselves? Death with living eyes.

Had they used their faces not to communicate, not to express thought or feeling, but to conceal? Had they made their faces masks—and now, stripped of them, were condemned to walk without faces until they repented?

“How long,” I wondered, “must they flaunt their facelessness before faceless eyes?”

How long would the punishment last? Had they finally begun to love? To wise? To feel shame?

I heard no words. Saw no movement of mouth. Were they silenced because of lying? With their eyes, they spoke—pleading, yearning to be understood. Was it truth—or falsehood—that flickered there?

They seemed to know each other. Did they see one skull beautiful, another plain? They had studied skulls long enough to tell.

My body passed through theirs unimpeded. Was I flesh, and they spirit? Or was I spirit, and they flesh? When a dancer brushed against me, I felt them pass through—man or woman, I sensed their passage, though I could not see.

On some skulls, hair clung—beautifully styled, golden or black, yet horrifying on bone. Often, the outer ear remained, and at its tip, the jewel Sidney called “the pearl of the ear”—a diamond, opal, or pearl—glimmering beneath the dark locks or pale curls, like sunrise on ivory or moonlight on cloud.

I looked down—daintily arched instep. Up—plump shoulders supporting a full neck—only to see it wither mid-way, becoming the fluted shaft of a gibbous skull.

The music grew wilder. The dance faster. Eyes blazed. Jewels sparkled. Color and fire lit the pallid grins, weaving a ghastly tapestry of motion—when suddenly, silence.

Every eye turned to the doorway.

There stood a woman—perfect in form, in posture, in hue—gazing upon them as from a pedestal. The dancers froze—“like one forbid”—struck dead by the vision of life that killed.

“Dead things,” her scornful glance declared, “I live!”

Then, as if stirred by a sudden wind, they turned to one another, broke into new motion, their eyes now glowing with shared triumph.

“You too,” they seemed to say, “will soon grow weak. You will become like us.”

I turned back to the woman—and saw a small dark shadow at her side.

She noticed the change in the dead stares. She looked down. Understood. Pressed her hands to the shadow, gave a stifled cry—and fled.

The birds rustled in their nests. A flash of joy lit the dancers’ eyes.

Then—suddenly—a warm wind swept through, growing stronger. It blew out every light.

Yet the low moon still glimmered on the horizon, sickly trying to shine. And the eyes—so many eyes—still glowed with a turbid radiance. I could still see clearly.

As if each figure were a snowman, they began to collapse. Flesh peeled away in papery flakes, falling like dirty snow beneath their clothes. Garments fluttered down in rags. Skeletons emerged—white, bare, lank—standing amid the ruins.

A faint rattling shiver ran through the naked forms. One by one, the eyes went out.

Darkness closed around me—complete, lonely.

For a moment, the leaves fluttered in unison. Then the wind ceased. The owl floated silently through the silent night.

Not once had I been afraid.

True, anyone crossing into a new world must leave fear behind. But I did not claim courage. I simply was not afraid. I did not know why. Nor did I know why I might have been. I feared not even fear—the most dangerous danger of all.

I stepped out into the wood, ready to continue my journey. Another moon was rising. I turned my face toward it.
