Xodar sat with his long legs folded beneath him, the pale sheen of the phosphorescent dome washing his sharp features in an underwater light that made him look carved from old bone. While I told him what had passed in the arena at the rites—Issus upon her throne, the ceremonial blades, my own point at her throat—he listened as though I were recounting some impossible recipe: a man seasoning the gods and finding they tasted of mere flesh.

When I finished he let out a breath that was half laugh, half sob.

“It is the final proof,” he said. “No more is needed. She is no goddess—only a wicked old woman. She has fattened herself on lies, and kept Barsoom hungry for truth.”

“She is still all-powerful here,” I answered. “A cook may know the king is mortal and still be hanged for serving him a bitter dish. We leave at the first moment that offers itself.”

He tipped his head, listening, as though even in the stone cell he could hear the broad breath of the Lost Sea beyond the walls. “A propitious moment,” he repeated, amused. “In all my life I have never seen one in which a prisoner of the First Born might escape.”

“To-night will do as well as any,” I said.

His eyes narrowed with the practical hunger of a man who has decided. “It will soon be night. How may I aid?”

“Can you swim?” I asked.

“No slimy silian that haunts the depths of Korus is more at home in water than is Xodar.”

“Good. The red one likely cannot. There is scarce water enough in his world to teach it to him. One of us will have to bear him through. I had thought we might keep the whole distance below the surface, but he will not do it.”

“The red one is to go with us?” Xodar’s tone held both surprise and calculation.

“Yes.”

“It is well. Three blades are better than two.” His gaze flicked to me, then away, as if he were tasting an old idea and finding it changed. “I have watched him in the arena at the rites of Issus many times. Never, until I saw you fight, had I seen one so unconquerable when odds were thick. When you two move, one might swear you master and pupil—or father and son. There is the same grim smile, the same contempt that maddens an enemy. Even your faces—there is a resemblance.”

“Be that as it may,” I said, “he is a great fighter. With him we will be hard to overcome. And if my friend Tars Tarkas, Jeddak of Thark, were but here—” I let the thought hang. There are names that are like old household spices; say them, and the mouth remembers feasts and firesides that are far away.

Xodar’s laugh came short. “It will be the whole world against you when they learn from whence you came. That, too, is one of Issus’ superstitions—another taste she has trained tongues to crave. She works through the Holy Therns, and those white fools are as ignorant of her true self as the outer world. Her decrees come to them written in blood upon strange parchment. They think a goddess’ hand lays them on guarded altars—altars no one may touch without being seen.”

His voice fell into the cadence of confession, and as he spoke I heard beneath his words the distant, ceaseless lap of Omean against stone—water worrying rock with patient insistence, the oldest kind of argument.

“I myself have borne these messages for years. There is a tunnel from Issus’ temple to Matai Shang’s principal temple, dug ages ago by slaves in such secrecy no thern guessed its existence. The therns, for their part, have temples dotted across the civilized world. Priests the people never see teach of the Mysterious River Iss, the Valley Dor, the Lost Sea of Korus—so that the deluded will take the voluntary pilgrimage. It swells thern wealth and adds to their slaves.

“Thus the therns gather riches and labor, and the First Born take what they need. Sometimes we raid the outer world ourselves—capturing females of royal houses, the newest battleships, and the artisans who build them, that we may copy what we cannot create.

“We are a non-productive race,” he added with bitter pride, as if repeating a proverb he had once been forced to honor. “We boast of it. A First Born does not labor or invent. Lower orders exist to feed our idleness. Fighting is all that counts. Were it not for that, there would be more of us than Barsoom could support—for none of us, so far as I know, dies a natural death. Our females would live forever but that we tire of them and remove them to make room for others. Issus alone is protected against death. She has lived for countless ages.”

“Would not the other Barsoomians live forever,” I asked, “but for the doctrine that drives them down Iss at or before their thousandth year?”

“I feel now,” he said slowly, “there is no doubt they are the same species as we. I hope I shall live to fight for them—in atonement for what I have done through ignorance, as generations were taught.”

A weird call rang across the waters then—thin and carrying, as a conch note carries over a shore. I had heard it the evening before and knew it marked the ending of the day, when the men of Omean spread their silks upon battleship and cruiser and surrender to that dreamless Martian sleep.

Our guard came to inspect us one last time. His sandals whispered; the metal of his accoutrements made the small, habitual sounds of routine, the complacent clink of a system that has never been challenged. He looked, grunted, went. The heavy door closed, sealing us again in stone.

We waited until he had time to return to his quarters. Then I sprang to the grated window and surveyed the near waters. A monster battleship lay perhaps a quarter mile from the island, hulking and dark against the glow. Between her and the shore floated smaller cruisers and one-man scouts. Only the battleship carried a watch. I saw him plainly, a solitary figure in the upper works, and watched him spread his sleeping silks upon his tiny platform. He lay down like a man who trusts the world to stay as it is.

Lax discipline, but why not? No enemy guessed that such a fleet existed—nor even the First Born, nor the Sea of Omean. Why keep ears tuned for a sound that has never been heard?

I dropped to the floor again and described the craft to Xodar.

“There is one there,” he said, and for an instant the old pride warmed his voice, “my personal property, built to carry five men. The swiftest of the swift. If we can board her, we can at least make a memorable run for liberty.” He spoke of her engines and fittings with a lover’s attention, naming each feature as a cook names his tools—the right blade for a clean cut, the right fire for a quick sear.

In his explanation I recognized a trick of gearing Kantos Kan had shown me once, when we sailed under false names in the navy of Zodanga beneath Sab Than. Only Helium gears thus. So the First Born had stolen it; they were thieves even in their vaunted idleness. And I knew Xodar spoke truth of her speed. Nothing that cleaves the thin air of Mars matches the ships of Helium.

We agreed to wait an hour, until stragglers had sought their silks. Meanwhile I was to fetch the red youth to our cell, so we would be ready to move together.

I sprang to the top of the partition wall and pulled myself up. Along the flat surface, scarce a foot wide, I walked to the neighboring cell. The boy sat upon his bench, leaning back and gazing up at the glowing dome as though it were a hearth. When he saw me balancing above him his eyes widened, then a grin broke across his face—quick, bright, full of that reckless appreciation a fighter has for any bold stroke.

As I stooped to drop to the floor beside him he motioned me to wait and whispered up, “Catch my hand. I can almost leap to the top of that wall myself. I have tried it many times. Each day I come a little closer. Some day I should have made it.”

I lay flat across the wall and reached down. With a short run from the center of his cell he sprang. His fingers met mine; I locked my grip and hauled, and he came up beside me, breathing quietly through his teeth—controlled, practiced.

“You are the first jumper I ever saw among the red men,” I told him.

He smiled. “It is not strange. I will tell you why when we have more time.”

Together we returned to Xodar. We descended and talked in low voices until the hour had passed, setting each step of our plan like measures of grain—this much risk, this much hope, nothing wasted. We bound ourselves by solemn oath to fight to the death for one another against whatever enemies should confront us. We knew that even if we slipped the First Born, the outer world might still rise against us—the power of superstition is like a dominant tongue: it commands mouths that never chose it, and punishes those who speak otherwise.

It was agreed that I should navigate once we reached the craft, and that if we made the outer world we should attempt to reach Helium without a stop.

“Why Helium?” the boy asked.

“I am a prince of Helium,” I said.

He gave me a peculiar look—measuring, intent, as though my words carried a flavor he recognized but could not name in his own speech. He said nothing more, and in the press of the moment I let it go.

“Come,” I said at last. “Now is as good a time as any. Let us go.”

In a moment I was atop the partition wall again with the boy beside me. I unbuckled my harness, snapped it into a single long strap, and lowered it to Xodar. He grasped it and climbed swiftly, sitting beside us with a soft laugh.

“How simple,” he murmured.

“The balance should be even simpler,” I replied.

I raised myself to the top of the outer wall so I could peer over and find the passing sentry. For five minutes I waited, muscles quiet, hearing my own blood and the distant water, and then he came on his slow beat around the structure.

I watched him make the turn at the end of the building, carrying him out of sight of the side that would witness our dash. The instant he disappeared I drew Xodar up, placed the strap in his hands, and lowered him quickly to the ground. The boy slid down after him.

As arranged, they did not wait for me. They walked slowly toward the water—perhaps a hundred yards—directly past the guard-house where sleeping soldiers lay.

When they had taken a dozen steps I dropped too and followed at a leisurely pace. Passing the guard-house I felt the pull of the blades within. Swords are more than metal here; they are language, status, survival—the lexicon of force the First Born have made everyone speak. If ever men would need that vocabulary, it was we three on the path we’d chosen.

I glanced toward the shore. Xodar and the boy had slipped over the dock’s edge into the water. They clung to metal rings set into the concrete-like substance at water level, with only mouths and nostrils above the surface, as planned.

The lure of the weapons was strong. I hesitated—just a breath, just the blink between impulse and wisdom—and in that blink I betrayed us. Another moment found me creeping toward the guard-house door.

Gently I pressed it open a crack. Inside, a dozen blacks lay stretched upon their silks, sunk in profound slumber. At the far side stood a rack holding swords and firearms. I pushed the door wider, easing my body through. A hinge gave out a resentful groan—old metal complaining like a harsh instrument in a quiet room. One man stirred. My heart stopped. The world narrowed to the sound of his breath.

With a noiseless spring I lit beside him, hands poised near his throat, waiting for the opening of his eyes. Time dragged, thick as cold paste. Then he turned, sighing, and resumed the even respiration of deep sleep.

I stepped between and over the sprawled bodies until I reached the rack. All were quiet. Their breathing rose and fell in a rhythm so steady it was almost musical—soothing, domestic, the sound of men who have never had to keep watch over their own power.

I drew a long-sword. The scabbard scraped its holder with a sound to my strained nerves like iron being rasped. I waited for the eruption of alarm. None came.

The second sword slid free without sound. The third clanked with a frightful din. I braced to charge for the doorway, to take my chance in open fight. But again, to my intense surprise, not a black moved. Either they slept like the dead, or fear had made every sound inside my skull larger than it truly was.

I turned to go. My eye fell upon the revolvers. I could not carry more than one; I was already laden. As I lifted a revolver from its pin, I noticed an open window beside the rack—an easy exit onto the dock, scarcely twenty feet from the water. Relief surged.

Then, from the opposite door, a figure stepped in. The officer of the guard stood looking straight at me. He took in the scene at a glance—my arms full of swords, the revolver in my hand—and his face hardened with the quick comprehension of a man whose world is about to be shamed.

Our revolvers rose together. The two reports were so nearly one that they sounded as a single clap. I felt the wind of his bullet brush past my ear. At the same instant he crumpled. Where I hit him I did not know—nor whether I killed him—for even as he fell I was through the window.

Water swallowed me. The sea of Omean closed above my head, and the three of us struck out for the little flier a hundred yards away.

Xodar bore the boy. I carried three long-swords, and the revolver I had dropped. The weight made my arms feel bound in wet cloth; though we were strong swimmers our progress seemed slow, as if the sea itself wished to keep its secret. I swam entirely beneath the surface; Xodar had to rise often so the youth could breathe. It was a wonder we were not discovered.

We reached the boat and clambered aboard before the watch on the battleship, roused by the shots, detected us. Then an alarm gun bellowed—deep, brutal—and its boom reverberated under the rocky dome until the air itself seemed to vibrate like a struck kettle.

Instantly the sleeping thousands were awake. Decks filled with fighting-men. An alarm on Omean was rare; it tasted of panic.

We cast away before the first report died. A heartbeat later we were rising from the sea. I lay full length along the deck at the controls. Xodar and the boy stretched behind me, prone to offer as little resistance to the air as possible.

“Rise high,” Xodar whispered close to my ear. “They dare not fire their heavy guns toward the dome—the fragments would rain among their own craft. If we are high enough our keel plates will protect us from rifle fire.”

I lifted her as he bade. Below, men leaped into the water by hundreds, striking out for small cruisers and scouts moored about the big ships. Larger craft were getting under way but stayed on the surface, their propellers raising a constant purr and churn that, mingled with shouted orders and cracking rifles, became a kind of poisoned storm-song. In that clamor I heard what all tyrannies breed: not music, but noise—the harsh ecology of domination, where every sound is command or fear and none is peace.

“A little to your right!” Xodar cried—there are no true compass points in Omean, where every direction feels due north.

I dared not throw the speed lever to its utmost for fear of overrunning the mouth of the shaft to the world above, but even so we were moving at a clip few craft could match on this windless sea.

Smaller fliers began to rise toward us. Then Xodar shouted, sharp as a snapped cord: “The shaft! Dead ahead!”

I saw it: a black, yawning opening in the glowing dome, like a wound in the underworld.

A ten-man cruiser rose directly before us to cut off our escape. It was the only vessel that truly barred the way, but at her angle and speed she would be between us and the shaft in time. She climbed at about forty-five degrees, intent on skimming above our deck and combing us with grapples.

There was one forlorn hope, and I took it.

To try to pass over her would let her force us against the rocks above—we were already too near. To dive beneath would put us precisely where she wanted us, helpless under her bulk. On either side other craft converged. The alternative I chose was all risk with a thin thread of chance.

As we neared I rose as though to pass above, tempting her to do what she did: climb steeper to drive me higher. At the last instant I yelled to my companions to hold tight, shoved the speed lever to its final notch, and deflected our bows until we ran horizontal at terrific velocity—straight for the cruiser’s keel.

Her commander may have understood then; if so, he had only time to taste fear before it swallowed him. Almost at impact I turned our bows upward. With a shattering jolt we struck. What I had gambled on occurred: already tilted, the cruiser was carried completely backward. Her crew fell twisting and screaming through the air to the water far below, while the cruiser, propellers still madly churning, dived headfirst after them to the bottom of the Sea of Omean.

Our own bows were crushed. The collision nearly hurled us from the deck. We landed in a wildly clutching heap at the very extremity of the flier. Xodar and I caught the hand-rail. The boy would have plunged over had I not snatched his ankle as it swung out into emptiness.

Unguided, our vessel careened in its mad flight, rising ever nearer the rock above. For an instant the ceiling of stone filled my vision, close enough to make the skin crawl. Then I regained the levers. With barely fifty feet to spare I leveled her again and drove toward the black mouth of the shaft.

The collision had slowed us. Now a hundred swift scouts were close. Xodar had warned me: if we ascended the shaft by repulsive rays alone, with propellers idle, craft with greater buoyancy would overtake us easily. The fastest boats rarely carry large buoyancy tanks; they trade lift for speed.

As many vessels were already near, it seemed inevitable we would be overhauled in the shaft—captured or killed in short order.

But there is always another way. When you cannot go over, under, or around, there remains the oldest, hardest choice: through.

“Reverse?” Xodar screamed behind me. “For the love of your first ancestor, reverse. We are at the shaft!”

“Hold tight!” I shouted. “Grasp the boy—hold tight. We are going straight up the shaft.”

The words were hardly out before we swept beneath the pitch-black opening. I threw the bow hard up, dragged the speed lever to its last notch, and with one hand on a stanchion and the other on the steering-wheel, hung on like a man who has put his soul into a single violent motion.

I heard Xodar’s sharp intake of breath—then a grim laugh. The boy laughed too and said something I could not catch for the shriek of wind as speed tore sound into a single continuous blade.

I looked above, hoping for stars to guide me, to keep the hurtling craft centered. To touch the shaft’s side at this speed would mean instant death. But not a star showed—only utter, impenetrable darkness.

Then I glanced down and saw a circle of light diminishing rapidly: the mouth of the opening above the phosphorescent radiance of Omean. By that bright ring I steered, keeping it as perfect as I could. It was a slender cord holding us from destruction, and I think I steered that night less by skill than by the blind instinct that has saved me too often to doubt.

We were not long within. Perhaps speed itself saved us; we had entered true and passed through before there was time to drift. Omean lies perhaps two miles below the surface crust of Mars. We must have been traveling near two hundred miles an hour—Martian fliers are swift—so at most we were in the shaft under forty seconds.

We had been out for some moments before I realized we had done the impossible.

Black darkness wrapped us. There were neither moons nor stars. Never before had I seen such a sky on Mars, and for an instant I was wholly at sea, robbed of the familiar celestial landmarks. Then understanding came: it was summer at the south pole. The ice cap was melting, and those meteoric phenomena—clouds, unknown upon most of Barsoom—had rolled over this portion of the planet, shutting out the light of heaven.
