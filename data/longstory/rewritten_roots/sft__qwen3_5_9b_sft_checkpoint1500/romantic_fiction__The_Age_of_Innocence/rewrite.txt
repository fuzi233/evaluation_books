January evenings in the early seventies brought a certain hush to New York, a suspended breath before the storm of change that lay just beyond the horizon. Inside the Academy of Music, the air was thick with the perfume of fresh lilies and the electric anticipation surrounding Christine Nilsson. Though whispers circulated of a magnificent new Opera House rising in the distant north—a temple of marble and gold intended to rival the grandest stages of Europe—the city's heart still beat within the shabby, intimate embrace of the old Academy. Its red and gold boxes, worn by time yet polished by tradition, remained the sanctuary of the faithful. Conservatives loved its cramped exclusivity, a fortress keeping the "new people" at bay; sentimentalists wept over its ghosts; and musicians, ever practical, relied on its notoriously difficult acoustics.

Madame Nilsson's return marked the season's crowning event. A galaxy of the elite had traveled through snow-drifted streets in private carriages, their breath misting in the chill, united by a singular purpose: to witness the Swedish soprano. To arrive in a humble *Brown coupe* was considered a badge of honor, a democratic nod to the art of escaping quickly as soon as the curtain fell, avoiding the clogged lines and the weary, gin-laced faces of one's own coachmen waiting outside the portico. It was a masterstroke of urban logistics, born from the American desire for instant gratification and immediate departure.

Newland Archer stood at the rear of the club box, the heavy velvet curtains framing the stage just as they parted for the Garden Scene. There was no necessity for haste; he had dined alone with his mother and sister hours prior, lingering over a solitary cigar in the Gothic library, a room dedicated to silence and smoke. Yet, New York possessed a peculiar morality, a code where arriving early to an opera was a breach of etiquette as serious as any ancient taboo. Furthermore, Newland was a dilettante by nature, deriving a sweeter, more profound pleasure from the anticipation of beauty than from its realization. His mind drifted, savoring the exquisite moment he sought, until the Prima Donna's voice pierced the air.

The words, translated from the original German into Italian for the sake of clarity, hung in the air like dew on a summer morning. Christine Nilsson, dressed in white cashmere slashed with pale blue satin, pressed the disheveled daisy to her lips. Her large eyes swept across the stage, locking onto the diminutive, purple-clad figure of M. Capoul, who strained to appear innocent beside his artful victim.

Across the aisle, Newland shifted his gaze from the stage to the opposite boxes. Dominating the view was the suite of Old Mrs. Manson Mingott, though the matriarch herself was absent, confined to her chair by a monstrous obesity that had long barred her from the theater. Instead, her daughter-in-law, Mrs. Lovell Mingott, and her daughter, Mrs. Welland, occupied the front seats. Behind them sat a young girl in white, her eyes wide and ecstatic, fixed upon the lovers. As Nilsson's final *"M'ama!"* soared, washing over the silent hall, a flush of warm pink climbed the girl's cheeks, coloring her brow and suffusing the slope of her chest, hidden beneath a modest tulle tucker fastened with a single gardenia. She lowered her gaze to the immense bouquet of lilies-of-the-valley resting on her lap, her gloved fingertips gently touching the stems.

A surge of satisfied vanity swelled within Newland. He watched her, feeling a possessive thrill mixed with a tender reverence for her abysmal purity. *The darling,* he thought, *she has no idea what this all means.* His imagination leaped ahead, conjuring scenes of old European witchery by the Italian lakes, where he would reveal the secrets of the world to her. It was only that afternoon that May Welland had hinted, in the soft, consecrated language of maidens, that she "cared." Already, his mind raced past the engagement ring and the march from *Lohengrin*, picturing a future where she was not a simpleton, but a partner capable of holding her own among the witty, worldly women of the "younger set"—women who could charm men while playfully discouraging them.

Yet, Newland knew better than to wish for a wife who mirrored the dangerous allure of the married woman whose charms had recently disrupted his winter. That woman, a creature of fire and ice, had nearly ruined everything. How to create a miracle of balance, a union that could survive the harsh realities of the world, remained an unsolved equation for him. He contented himself with the unspoken doctrine of the "carefully brushed, white-waistcoated gentlemen" sitting around him. They were intellectually superior, perhaps, to the average man of the box, having read more and seen further, but collectively they formed "New York." Their solidarity was a chain, and striking out on one's own path was not merely troublesome; it was bad form.

Suddenly, Lawrence Lefferts, the arbiter of all things proper, broke the spell. With a sharp exclamation, *"Well—upon my soul!"* he turned his opera glasses away from the stage, scanning the box across from them. Lefferts was the foremost authority on form in the city, his judgment as innate as the curve of his fair mustache or the careless elegance of his posture. From the slant of his bald forehead to the lean grace of his legs, he embodied the knowledge of how to wear clothes and conduct oneself.

"My God!" Lefferts muttered, silently passing his glasses to Old Sillerton Jackson.

Newland followed the direction of Lefferts's gaze, surprised to see why. Standing in the center of Mrs. Mingott's box was a new figure, a slim young woman slightly shorter than May. Her dark hair curled tightly around her temples, bound by a narrow band of diamonds that gave her the fashionable "Josephine look." Her gown, a deep blue velvet, was theatricaly caught up under her bosom by a girdle with a large, antique clasp. She stood motionless, seemingly unconscious of the attention her unusual attire attracted, briefly consulting with Mrs. Welland regarding seating arrangements before yielding with a slight smile to sit alongside Mrs. Lovell Mingott.

Sillerton Jackson, the patriarch of the box, returned the glasses. The entire company turned instinctively, waiting for his verdict. Jackson was the keeper of New York's genealogy, a living map of cousinships and forbidden alliances. He knew the tangled web of the Mingotts, the Dallases, the Thorleys, and the Chiverses, reciting the leading characteristics of each lineage with the precision of a botanist describing a rare flower. He spoke of the stinginess of the Leffertses, the foolish matches of the Rushworths, and the recurring insanity of the Albany Chiverses, which had historically prevented intermarriage with their New York cousins—with the disastrous exception of Medora Manson, whose mother, a Rushworth, had doomed her to tragedy.

Beyond the forest of family trees, Jackson carried a secret register of the scandals and mysteries that simmered beneath the polite surface of society. Few knew the true identity of Julius Beaufort, the banker, or the fate of handsome Bob Spicer, Old Mrs. Mingott's mysterious husband who vanished with a fortune shortly after marrying a Spanish dancer. These truths were locked safely within Jackson's breast, guarded by a keen sense of honor and a reputation for discretion that ensured he never revealed what he had heard unless it served a purpose.

For a long moment, the box held its breath. Jackson scrutinized the new arrivals with his filmy blue eyes, his expression unreadable beneath the veil of age. Then, slowly, he twisted his silver mustache, a gesture that signaled deep thought, and delivered his sentence simply and quietly: "I didn't think the Mingotts would have tried it on."

Newland Archer found himself in a peculiar and humiliating predicament. It was mortifying that the box commanding the exclusive attention of the male guests in New York was occupied by his fiancée, flanked by her mother and aunt. For a fleeting moment, he failed to recognize the lady in the empire-waisted gown, unable to fathom why her presence sparked such a ripple of curiosity among the cognoscenti. Then, clarity struck, followed by a sudden surge of indignant pride.

Certainly, no one would ever suspect the Mingotts of playing such a game! Yet, they had. The hushed whispers circulating behind him confirmed beyond doubt that the young woman was May Welland's cousin—the one invariably dismissed within the family as "poor Ellen Olenska." Newland knew she had arrived from Europe just days prior; he had even received a note from Miss Welland, devoid of reproach, confirming that she had visited Ellen, who was currently residing with the formidable old Mrs. Mingott. While Newland held family loyalty in the highest esteem—admiring the Mingotts' fierce defense of their few black sheep—he drew a sharp line between private charity and public spectacle. To welcome Countess Olenska into the domestic fold was one thing; to parade her before the entire opera house, specifically in the box of the girl whose union with Newland Archer was imminent, was quite another. In truth, he shared the sentiments of Old Sillerton Jackson: the Mingotts simply wouldn't dare attempt something so audacious.

Of course, Newland knew that within the bounds of Fifth Avenue, if anyone possessed the temerity to defy convention, it was Old Mrs. Manson Mingott, the matriarch of the lineage. He had long admired her towering stature, despite her origins as Catherine Spicer of Staten Island—a girl of mysterious parentage, lacking both fortune and standing sufficient to erase those shadows. Yet, she had allied herself with the head of the wealthy Mingott line, wedded two daughters to "foreigners"—an Italian marquis and an English banker—and cemented her legacy of audacity by constructing a vast mansion of pale cream-colored stone. In an era where brown sandstone was the unfailing standard for afternoon wear, her house stood isolated in an impenetrable wilderness near Central Park, a physical testament to her moral fortitude.

Throned within this sanctuary, amidst pre-Revolutionary furnishings and mementoes of the Tuileries under Louis Napoleon, she sat as calmly as if residing above Thirty-fourth Street or opening French windows like doors rather than sash windows were utterly normal. Every soul, including Mr. Sillerton Jackson, concurred that Old Catherine lacked conventional beauty—a divine gift that, in New York's eyes, legitimized every triumph and absolved numerous shortcomings. The uncharitable whispered that she resembled her imperial namesake, conquering success through iron will, a hardened heart, and a haughty impudence sanctified by the profound decency and dignity of her private life. Her husband, Mr. Manson Mingott, had perished at twenty-eight, leaving a fortune "tied up" with caution born of the Spicer family's notorious distrust. But his bold widow forged ahead fearlessly, mixing freely in international circles, marrying off her daughters to dubious aristocrats, socializing with dukes and ambassadors, associating intimately with papists, entertaining opera singers, and befriending Mme. Taglioni. And yet, as Sillerton Jackson was quick to declare, not a whisper tainted her reputation; the sole distinction lay in her unblemished conduct compared to the earlier Catherine.

Though Old Mrs. Mingott had successfully untied her husband's estate and lived in opulence for half a century, the ghosts of her early poverty haunted her. She practiced an excessive thriftiness, ensuring that every garment or piece of furniture purchased was of the utmost quality, yet refusing to lavish funds on the ephemeral pleasures of the table. Consequently, her fare mirrored that of the Archers—plain and unadorned—and her wines offered no redemption. Her relatives believed this culinary penury tarnished the Mingott name, traditionally synonymous with gastronomic excellence, prompting her son Lovell to hire the city's finest chef in an attempt to restore the family honor. To these protests, the old lady laughed lightly: "What's the use of employing two good cooks in one family when I've married the girls and can't enjoy sauces?"

As Newland Archer contemplated these matters, he cast his gaze back toward the Mingott box. He observed that Mrs. Welland and her sister-in-law faced their semicircle of critics with the *aplomb* instilled in them by Old Catherine, save for May Welland, whose cheeks flushed slightly—perhaps because she sensed his scrutiny—and who clearly grasped the gravity of the moment. As for the source of the uproar, she sat gracefully in her corner, eyes fixed upon the stage, revealing a hint of shoulder and bosom that New York usually expected to remain concealed from view on ladies intent on passing unnoticed.

To Newland Archer, nothing seemed more abhorrent than an offense against "Taste," that distant deity for whom "Form" served merely as a visible vicar. Countess Olenska's pale, serious countenance seemed appropriate for her tragic circumstances, yet the manner in which her dress, devoid of a tucker, draped away from her slender shoulders disturbed and troubled him deeply. He loathed the idea of exposing May Welland to the influence of a young woman so indifferent to the dictates of Taste.

Then, he heard a younger voice behind him inquire, "After all, just *what* happened?"
"Well," replied a companion, "she left him. Nobody dares deny that."
"He's an awful brute, isn't he?" pressed a young man named Thorley, eager to champion the lady's cause.
"The very worst," interjected Lawrence Lefferts with authority. "I knew him at Nice. A half-paralyzed white sneerer, rather handsome in head, but with eyes full of lashes. When he wasn't surrounded by women, he was collecting china. He pays any price for either."
A murmur of laughter rippled through the crowd, and Thorley ventured, "Well, then..."
"Then she bolted with his secretary."
"Oh, I see." Thorley's face fell.
"It didn't endure long, however. I heard of her a few months later, living alone in Venice. I believe Lovell Mingott traveled abroad to find her; he claimed she was desolately wretched. That is acceptable—but parading her at the Opera is a different matter."
"Perhaps," suggested young Thorley tentatively, "she is too miserable to remain at home."
This elicited an irreverent chuckle, causing the youth to blush deeply as he attempted to feign innocence regarding what the astute termed a "double entendre."
"Well—it is strange to have brought Miss Welland, anyhow," someone murmured softly, casting a sidelong glance at Archer.
"Oh, that is merely part of the campaign: Granny's orders, surely," Lefferts laughed. "When the old lady undertakes a venture, she executes it thoroughly."

As the curtain descended and the box erupted in a general stir, Newland Archer felt an irresistible urge to take decisive action. The desire to be the first to step into Mrs. Mingott's box, to announce publicly his engagement to May Welland, and to stand united against whatever tribulations her cousin's unconventional existence might impose, suddenly silenced all his scruples and hesitations. Hurried through the crimson corridors to the farther side of the theater, he entered the box. His eyes met Miss Welland's, and he realized instantly that she comprehended his motive, though the rigid family dignity they both revered prevented her from voicing it. In their world, lives were lived in an atmosphere of faint implications and pale delicacies; the silent understanding between them, needing no words, bridged a distance deeper than any explanation could have achieved. Her eyes spoke, *"You see why Mother brought me,"* and his replied, *"I would not, for the world, have had you absent."*

"My niece, Countess Olenska?" Mrs. Welland asked as she extended her hand to her future son-in-law. Archer bowed respectfully, refraining from a handshake as per custom when introducing oneself to a lady. Ellen Olenska inclined her head slightly, her pale-gloved hands clasped over a massive fan of eagle feathers. On the night of her annual ball, Mrs. Julius Beaufort never missed a performance at the Opera. In fact, she deliberately scheduled her grandest evening parties to coincide with an Opera night, sending a clear, unspoken message to her circle: she was above domestic drudgery, entirely liberated from household cares, and surrounded by a retinue of servants so capable they could orchestrate a feast of epic proportions while she remained unseen.

Their townhouse was a fortress of status, one of the few remaining in New York to boast a true ballroom—a space that predated the opulent halls of the Mansons and the Headlys. While the fashion was shifting toward "provincial" pragmatism, where carpets covered drawing-room floors and furniture was shoved upstairs, the Beauforts stood defiant. Their ballroom, shut tight for three hundred and sixty-five days a year, sat in a silence broken only by the dust motes dancing in the dark. Gilt chairs were stacked like soldiers in a corner, and the massive chandelier slept inside its protective sack. Yet, this sheer, undeniable excess compensated perfectly for any shadow in the Beaufort past.

Mrs. Archer, a woman who loved distilling social truths into sharp axioms, once mused, "We all have our pet common people." Though the phrase was bold, it was whispered in hushed tones within many exclusive hearts. And yet, the Beauforts were not merely common; some whispered they were worse. Mrs. Beaufort, however, hailed from one of America's most venerated lines. She was the former Regina Dallas, a Southern beauty of legendary charm, introduced to the city by her cousin, Medora Manson—a woman whose motives were noble, but whose actions were consistently disastrous. One born into the Mansons and the Rushworths held a *droit de cité*, a birthright to the social stage, as noted by Mr. Sillerton Jackson, who had known the Tuileries intimately. But did one truly forfeit that crown by marrying Julius Beaufort?

Who, exactly, was Mr. Beaufort? To the world, he was an English gentleman, agreeable, handsome, witty, and hospitable. He had arrived in America with glowing recommendations from Old Mrs. Manson Mingott's son-in-law, a banker, and swiftly carved out a formidable position in the world of affairs. But behind the veneer lay a man of dissipated habits, a bitter tongue, and a history shrouded in mystery. When Medora announced her cousin's engagement to him, it was dismissed as yet another reckless stumble in her tragic chronicle.

Yet, folly often births miracles as surely as wisdom does. Two years after their marriage, it became undeniable that Mrs. Beaufort presided over the most distinguished house in New York. The mechanics of this transformation remained a closely guarded secret. She was indolent, passive, and occasionally described as dull, even by those who called themselves caustic. Yet, draped in pearls, growing younger, blonder, and more radiant with each passing season, she sat enthroned in Mr. Beaufort's heavy brownstone palace. She commanded the entire galaxy of society without lifting a jeweled finger.

Those who read between the lines claimed it was Mr. Beaufort himself who ran the show. Perhaps he instructed the staff, taught the chef new recipes, directed the gardeners on which exotic blooms to cultivate for the dinner table, curated the guest list, mixed the punch, and penned the intimate notes his wife sent to her friends. If this were true, these tasks were performed with invisible hands. To the public eye, he remained the picture of a careless, wealthy bachelor, strolling into his drawing room with the detached amusement of a host who happens to be visiting his own home, exclaiming, "My wife's gloxinias are a marvel, aren't they? I believe she sourced them directly from Kew."

The consensus was absolute: Mr. Beaufort's genius lay in execution. It was easy to whisper rumors about his origins, suggesting he had been aided to escape England by a powerful international bank, but he swept those whispers aside as lightly as the others. Though New York's business conscience was as prickly as its moral code, Mr. Beaufort moved forward unimpeded. He dragged the entire city into his drawing rooms, and for over twenty years, visitors flocked to the Beauforts with the same unshakeable confidence they reserved for the Mansons, enjoying the promise of roasted canvas-back ducks and vintage wines rather than lukewarm champagne and reheated croquettes from the city.

Thus, as expected, Mrs. Beaufort took her seat in her box moments before the Jewel Song. And when, as tradition dictated, she rose at the close of the third act, wrapped her opera cloak around her shoulders, and vanished, New York knew the signal: the ball would commence within the hour.

The Beaufort mansion was a shrine that New Yorkers proudly displayed to foreigners, particularly on ball nights. They had been pioneers in owning their own scarlet velvet carpet, having it unrolled down the steps by their own lacquered-footed servants beneath their own awnings, rather than renting it as part of a package deal. They had also innovated the custom of allowing ladies to remove their wraps in the foyer, sparing them the indignity of scurrying to the hostess's private chambers to curl their hair under the gaslight; Mr. Beaufort was rumored to have remarked that he assumed all his wife's acquaintances had maids to ensure their hair was perfect before they left home.

Furthermore, the house itself was boldly designed around the concept of the ball. Instead of navigating a cramped hallway to reach the festivities, as at the Chiverses', guests marched solemnly down a vista of enfiladed drawing rooms—the sea-green salon, the crimson chamber, the *bouton d'or*—gazing from afar at the reflection of many-candle chandeliers in the polished parquet, and beyond that, the lush, shadowed depth of a conservatory where camellias and tree ferns arched their expensive greenery over seats of black and gold bamboo.

Newland Archer, fitting the role of a young man of his station, strolled in somewhat late. He had left his coat with the silk-stockinged footmen—a peculiar vanity of Mr. Beaufort's—and dawdled in the library, a cavern hung with Spanish leather and furnished with Buhl and malachite, where a few men were chatting and donning their dancing gloves. Finally, he joined the line of guests awaiting reception on the threshold of the crimson drawing room.

Archer was distinctly nervous. Unlike the carefree "young bloods" who typically retreated to their clubs after the Opera, he had walked several miles up Fifth Avenue on the cool night air before turning back toward the Beauforts'. His anxiety stemmed from a singular fear: that the Mingotts might be pushing too far. There was a chilling possibility that Granny Mingott had given orders to escort the Countess Olenska to the ball.

Recalling the gravity of the situation in the club box, he realized how terrible a mistake that would be. Although he was more determined than ever to "see the thing through," his chivalrous eagerness to champion his betrothed's cousin had dimmed since their brief exchange at the Opera.

Drifting into the *bouton d'or* drawing room, where Mr. Beaufort had the audacity to hang Bouguereau's controversial nude, *Love Victorious*, Archer found Mrs. Welland and her daughter standing near the entrance to the ballroom. Beyond the open doors, couples were already gliding across the floor. The light of wax candles illuminated swirling tulle skirts, girlish heads wreathed in modest blossoms, the dashing plumes (*aigrettes*) and ornaments of the young wives' coiffures, and the gleam of highly glazed shirt fronts and fresh *glacé* gloves.

Miss Welland, poised to enter the dance, lingered on the threshold, holding a sprig of lilies-of-the-valley—she carried no other bouquet. Her face was faintly pale, but her eyes burned with a candid excitement. A cluster of young men and girls swirled around her, exchanging handshakes, laughter, and pleasantries. Mrs. Welland, standing slightly apart, cast a beam of qualified approval upon the scene. It was evident that Miss Welland was announcing her engagement, while her mother maintained an air of the parental reluctance deemed appropriate for such occasions.

Archer paused. This announcement had been his express wish, yet it was not the manner in which he would have preferred to unveil his happiness. To declare it amidst the clamor and heat of a crowded ballroom robbed it of the delicate bloom of privacy that things nearest the heart deserved. His joy was so profound that this blurring of the surface left its essence untouched, but he had wanted the surface, too, to remain pure. He found a small comfort in realizing that May Welland shared this sentiment. Her eyes fled to his with a beseeching look, conveying a silent plea: *Remember, we are doing this because it is right.*

No appeal could have found a quicker resonance in Archer's breast. He wished, however, that the necessity of their action had been driven by some ideal principle, rather than simply by the plight of poor Ellen Olenska. The following morning, the formalities of courtship unfolded with the precision of a well-oiled machine. New York society operated on a schedule as rigid as its calendar, and Newland Archer moved through the rituals with practiced ease. First, accompanied by his mother and sister, he presented himself to Mrs. Welland. Then, the trio—Archer, his mother, and May—departed for the mansion of Old Mrs. Manson Mingott to secure the blessing of the clan's matriarch.

For a young man of Newland's standing, a visit to Mrs. Mingott was less a social obligation than a theatrical diversion. Her residence on Fifth Avenue stood as a monument to a specific, flamboyant era, though it lacked the stolid, Gothic grandeur of the older homes nearby. Where those neighbors clung to the somber dignity of 1830s design—with heavy rosewood consoles, cabbage-rose garlands, and massive mahogany bookcases—Mrs. Mingott had swept the past aside. She had traded her ancestral heaviness for the frivolous opulence of the Second Empire, filling her space with upholstery that spoke of Parisian cafes rather than provincial parlor rooms.

Her daily routine was equally performative. She preferred to sit in a window of her ground-floor salon, observing the world pass by with the calm detachment of a queen watching ants march across a chessboard. There was no urgency in her posture, nor any anxiety about the changing times. To Mrs. Mingott, the chaotic sprawl of tenements, brick factories, and ragged gardens surrounding her estate was merely a temporary phase. She was convinced that soon, the asphalt would swallow the cobblestones, the sleek new mansions would eclipse the crumbling facades, and the world would finally look like the elegant capital of France she imagined it to be. For now, however, she remained the undisputed center of gravity, a sovereign who required no subjects to validate her reign.

Time and nature had transformed her physical form into something majestic and overwhelming. Once a plump, active woman with a neat ankle, she had succumbed to a deluge of flesh that now draped her like a living landscape. Yet, she wore this transformation with a philosopher's grace. In the mirror, she revealed an expanse of firm, unlined skin, pale as snow, where the faint contours of her youthful features lingered like ruins waiting to be excavated. Double chins cascaded smoothly toward a bosom swathed in snowy muslins, pinned by a miniature portrait of her late husband. Around her, waves of black silk spilled from a chair so vast it seemed to dwarf the occupant, leaving two tiny, porcelain hands resting atop the fabric like gulls perched on a storm-tossed sea.

Her mobility was limited by this accumulation of weight, rendering stairs impossible. With a characteristic disregard for convention, she had relocated her receiving rooms to the ground floor, defying the strictures of New York propriety that dictated a lady's reception areas be elevated above the domestic sphere. Consequently, guests seated in her salon often found themselves with a startling view into her private chambers through an open door and a looping curtain. They beheld a bedroom furnished with the audacity of a European novel: a low bed upholstered like a sofa, a vanity table fringed with lace, and a gilt mirror reflecting a space designed for intimacy rather than modesty.

Visitors were invariably struck by this arrangement, which felt dangerously akin to the illicit apartments of French literature. It suggested a freedom from restraint that the average American mind struggled to comprehend. To Newland Archer, who had once privately placed the romantic scenes of *Monsieur de Camors* within these walls, it was a fascinating paradox. He marveled at how this blameless woman conducted a life that mimicked the stage-set of adultery, yet he silently conceded that if a lover truly desired her, the intrepid Mrs. Mingott would surely have claimed him.

Fortunately, Countess Olenska was absent from the drawing room during the betrothed couple's visit. Mrs. Mingott noted casually that the Countess had gone out—a statement that, given the blazing sun and the shopping hour, carried a subtle sting of judgment regarding a "compromised" woman's conduct. However, her absence served a practical purpose: it spared the newly engaged pair the awkward shadow of Ellen's unhappy past, allowing the focus to remain squarely on their radiant future.

The reception proceeded smoothly. Mrs. Mingott, whose foresight had long predicted this union, expressed her delight. Her attention then turned to the engagement ring, a substantial sapphire set in invisible claws.

"The setting is quite modern," Mrs. Welland offered gently, glancing sideways at her future son-in-law. "Perhaps it appears a bit stark to traditional tastes."

"Stark? My dear, I embrace every novelty," Mrs. Mingott declared, lifting the gemstone to inspect it with bright, unaided eyes. "It is a masterpiece. Very liberal indeed. In my day, a cameo in pearls sufficed. But look at the hand holding it, Mr. Archer." She waved a slender finger, the wrist encircled by rolls of aged flesh that resembled ivory bracelets. "That hand was modeled in Rome by Ferrigiani yourself. You should commission May's hand similarly. No doubt he intends to. It is larger, of course—the modern sport spreads the joints—but the complexion is exquisite." She paused, her gaze sharpening as it fixed on Archer. "And when does the wedding take place?"

"Oh—" Mrs. Welland murmured softly. Archer, offering a warm smile to his fiancée, replied, "As soon as possible, if you will support me, Mrs. Mingott."

"Nonsense about taking time to know one another," the old lady interjected, dismissing the notion with a wave of her hand. "Everyone in New York knows everyone. Do not wait until the wine loses its bubbles. Marry them before Lent; I might catch pneumonia in this winter, and I intend to host the breakfast."

These declarations were met with the customary mixture of feigned surprise, amusement, and gratitude. As the visit began to wind down in a light-hearted tone, the front door swung open.

Countess Olenska entered, wrapped in a bonnet and mantle, followed closely by Julius Beaufort. A ripple of pleasurable murmurs ran through the female guests. To the carnivorous old lady, the prospect of fresh influence—and presumably, fresh beauty—was a delicious treat.

While the others drifted into the hallway, Newland Archer watched from the entrance as Countess Olenska regarded him. Her expression held a faint, searching smile.

"You must already know," he said, answering her look with a shy laugh. "About May and me."

The smile softened, transforming her features into the likeness of the bold, brown-haired girl he remembered from childhood. "Of course I know," she said. "And I am so pleased. Though, naturally, one does not announce matters of such magnitude amidst a throng."

The ladies were moving toward the threshold, and she stepped forward to offer her hand. "Farewell. Come and see me sometime." Her eyes remained locked on his as she spoke.

Back in the carriage, rolling down Fifth Avenue, the conversation focused pointedly on Mrs. Mingott—her age, her indomitable spirit, and her extraordinary flair. Not a word was spoken of Ellen Olenska, yet the silence was palpable. Archer sensed clearly that Mrs. Welland was thinking: *It was a grave error for Ellen to appear today, parading up Fifth Avenue at the height of the shopping hour alongside Julius Beaufort.*

Newland echoed the thought internally, though perhaps with a touch more justification. *And she ought to understand that a man who has just become engaged does not waste his time calling upon married women.* Yet, he acknowledged the truth in the counter-argument: *But in the circle she inhabits, they do nothing else.* Despite his pride in his cosmopolitan outlook, he found himself thanking heaven that he was a New Yorker, about to unite himself with someone of his own kind, bound by the same unwritten laws that governed their world.

The following evening, Old Mr. Sillerton Jackson descended upon the Archers' household for dinner, a ritual as cherished by Mrs. Archer as any social gala. To Mrs. Archer, a woman of quiet temperament who nonetheless hungered for the currents of the city's tide, Mr. Jackson was the ultimate cartographer of the local landscape. With the patience of a fossil hunter and the taxonomic rigor of a naturalist, he cataloged the lives of his neighbors, piecing together a mosaic of the season from the fragments gathered by his sister, Miss Sophy Jackson. While Sophy, the perpetual guest of those who could not secure her formidable brother, supplied the minor gossip, Mr. Jackson reserved his visits for moments when specific knowledge was required. Thus, whenever Mrs. Archer desired to understand the shifting tides of society, she extended her invitation, honoring so few others that Mr. Jackson invariably arrived in person rather than dispatching his sister.

If Mr. Jackson could have engineered the universe, he would have orchestrated dinners where Newland was absent. Though the two men shared a genuine camaraderie at their club, the old anecdotist sensed a subtle, unspoken calculation in Newland's demeanor—a tendency to weigh his words with a gravity that the ladies of the family, in their innocent gossips, rarely displayed. Furthermore, he harbored a secret grievance regarding the quality of Mrs. Archer's cuisine. Yet, New York itself was divided into two distinct tribes: the Mingotts and Mansons, who venerated gastronomy, fashion, and fortune, and the Archer-Newland-van der Luyden clan, devotees of travel, horticulture, and high literature, who viewed the pleasures of the table as somewhat vulgar. One could not feast on canvas-backed ducks and vintage wine at the Lovell Mingotts' and simultaneously discuss Alpine vistas and *The Marble Faun* at Adeline Archer's, especially since the Archer Madeira had circled the Cape and returned transformed. Mrs. Archer, a widow long past, resided with her son and daughter on West Twenty-eighth Street. An entire floor was dedicated to Newland, leaving the mother and daughter to squeeze into the narrower quarters below. In this unclouded harmony, they cultivated ferns within Wardian cases, crafted macramé lace and wool embroideries, collected American Revolutionary glassware, subscribed to *Good Words*, and devoured Ouida's novels for the sheer atmospheric beauty of their Italian settings. They preferred tales of peasant life for the lush descriptions of nature and the wholesome sentiments, yet generally favored stories of society, finding the motives of the genteel more comprehensible. They spoke sharply of Dickens, whom they accused of failing to draw a gentleman, and regarded Thackeray as less worldly-wise than Bulwer-Lytton, who, however, was already beginning to be deemed old-fashioned. Both Mrs. and Miss Archer were ardent lovers of scenery, the primary pursuit of their occasional travels abroad, regarding architecture and painting as subjects reserved for men, or perhaps for scholars who read Ruskin.

Born a Newland, Mrs. Archer and her daughter Janey were, as the town whispered, "true Newlands." Mentally, however, the likeness was less complete than their identical mannerisms suggested. Yet, beneath the surface, while Mrs. Archer's serene unimaginativeness found comfort in the familiar and accepted, Janey was prone to sudden aberrations of fancy, wellsprings of suppressed romance bubbling up from depths unknown to her mother.

Mother and daughter adored each other and held their son and brother in reverential esteem. Archer, in return, loved them with a tenderness rendered compunctious and uncritical by the weight of their exaggerated admiration, bolstered by a secret satisfaction in their reliance. He believed it essential for a man to have his authority respected within his own home, even if his sense of humor occasionally led him to question the rigidity of his mandates.

That evening, following Mr. Jackson's departure and the ladies' retreat to their curtained sanctuary, Newland Archer ascended the stairs to his study with a thoughtful, heavy gait. As always, a watchful hand had tended the fire and trimmed the lamp, rendering the room—a cavern lined with volumes, adorned with bronze fencers on the mantel, and populated by photographs of masterpieces—singularly domestic and inviting.

As he sank into his leather armchair before the blaze, his gaze landed upon a large portrait of May Welland. It was a gift from the early, electric days of their courtship, and now it dominated the space where lesser likenesses had once resided. With a sudden, profound sense of reverence, Archer studied the frank brow, the serious eyes, and the playfully innocent mouth of the creature destined to be his soul's keeper. Yet, staring back at him through the guise of this familiar face, he saw something chilling: the terrifying product of the social machinery he worshipped. She was a young girl who knew nothing and expected everything, looking at him like a stranger. In that glance, the illusion shattered for him anew; marriage was not the safe harbor he had been taught to believe in, but a voyage across uncharted, storm-tossed seas.

The shadow of the Countess Olenska had already disturbed the sediment of his convictions, setting them adrift in dangerous currents. His own words, "Women should be free—as free as we are," echoed in his mind, striking at the root of a problem his world conveniently declared nonexistent. "Nice" women, no matter how wronged, never claimed the liberty he espoused, allowing generous men like himself to feign chivalry while secretly upholding the iron shackles of convention. This verbal generosity was merely a humbug masking the inexorable laws binding everyone to the old patterns. Here lay the terrible paradox: he was pledged to defend, on behalf of his betrothed's cousin, a conduct that, if committed by his future wife, would invite the thunders of Church and State.

Of course, the dilemma was purely hypothetical. Since he was not a blackguard Polish nobleman, it was absurd to speculate on his wife's rights if he *were*. But Newland Archer was too imaginative not to feel the chill of what might be. Could he and May truly know one another? Was it not his duty, as a "decent" gentleman, to conceal his past from her, and hers, as a marriageable girl, to possess no past to conceal? What if, driven by subtler, unseen forces, they tired of each other? Reviewing the marriages of his peers—the supposedly happy unions—he found none that resembled the passionate comradeship he envisioned. They were dull associations of material and social interest, cemented by ignorance on one side and hypocrisy on the other. Lawrence Lefferts, the high priest of form, had realized this enviable ideal most completely. He had crafted a wife so entirely suited to his convenience that, amidst his frequent adulteries, she wandered in smiling unconsciousness, claiming, "Lawrence was so frightfully strict," and blushing indignantly when anyone hinted at Julius Beaufort's "other establishment."

Archer tried to console himself with the thought that he was not quite such an ass as Larry Lefferts, nor May such a simpleton as poor Gertrude. But the distinction was one of intelligence, not of moral standards. In truth, they all inhabited a world of hieroglyphics, where the real thing was never spoken, acted, or thought, but only represented by arbitrary signs. Just as Mrs. Welland, knowing precisely why Archer had pressured her to announce May's engagement at the Beaufort ball, felt compelled to simulate reluctance and act as though her hand had been forced—much like the savage bride dragged from her parents' tent in accounts of primitive peoples—so too did the young girl remain the most inscrutable figure in this elaborate system of mystification. She was frank, poor darling, because she had nothing to hide; she was assured because she knew of nothing to fear. And with no better preparation, she was about to be plunged overnight into what people euphemistically termed "the facts of life."

The young man was sincerely, placidly in love. He delighted in her radiant health, her horsemanship, her grace, and the shy intellectual curiosity blooming within her under his guidance. She had progressed far enough to join him in mocking the *Idylls of the King*, though not yet to grasp the tragic beauty of *Ulysses* or the seductive danger of the *Lotus Eaters*. She was straightforward, loyal, and brave, possessing a humor proved chiefly by her laughter at *his* jokes. He suspected, deep within her innocently gazing soul, a warm glow of feeling waiting to be awakened. Yet, when he had completed his brief survey of her virtues, discouragement overtook him. All this frankness and innocence were merely artificial products. Untrained human nature was not naturally open; it was bristling with the twists and defenses of instinctive guile. He felt oppressed by this creation of factitious purity, so cunningly manufactured by a conspiracy of mothers, aunts, and grandmothers, simply because it was what he wanted—and what he felt entitled to exercise his lordly pleasure in shattering like an image made of snow.

These reflections carried a certain triteness, habitual to young men approaching their wedding day. Usually, they were accompanied by a sense of compunction and self-abasement, which Newland Archer felt no trace of. He could not deplore, as Thackeray's heroes so often did, that he lacked a blank page to offer his bride in exchange for her unblemished one. He could not escape the fact that, had he been raised as she had, neither of them would have been fit to navigate the world without being lost like the Babes in the Wood. Nor could he, despite his anxious cogitations, discern any honest reason—any reason unconnected with his own momentary pleasure or the passion of masculine vanity—for why his bride should not have been granted the same freedom of experience as himself.

Such questions drifted through his mind at such an hour, but he was acutely conscious that their uncomfortable persistence and precision stemmed from the inopportune arrival of the Countess Olenska. Here he was, at the zenith of his betrothal, a moment for pure thoughts and cloudless hopes, when he had been pitchforked into a coil of scandal that raised every problem he had wished to leave undisturbed. "Hang Ellen Olenska!" he grumbled, covering the fire and beginning to undress. He could not fully comprehend why her fate should bear so heavily on his own, yet he dimly sensed that he had only just begun to measure the risks of the championship his engagement had forced upon him.

A few days later, the bolt fell.

The Lovell Mingotts issued cards for what was known as "a formal dinner"—three extra footmen, two dishes per course, and a Roman punch served midway—headlined with the words "To meet the Countess Olenska." In accordance with the hospitable American fashion, which treated strangers as if they were royalty or their ambassadors, they extended the invitation.

Guest selection displayed a boldness and discrimination that the initiated recognized as the firm hand of Catherine the Great. Alongside immemorial standbys like the Selfridge Merrys, who were invited everywhere because they always had been, and the Beauforts, with whom there was a tenuous claim of relationship, sat Mr. Sillerton Jackson and his sister Sophy, who followed her brother's dictates. There were the Lawrence Leffertses, Mrs. Lefferts Rushworth (the lovely widow), the Harry Thorleys, the Reggie Chiverses, and young Morris Dagonet and his wife, a van der Luyden. Indeed, the company was perfectly assorted, comprising the little inner group that, throughout the long New York season, disported itself together daily and nightly with apparently undiminished zest.

Forty-eight hours later, the unbelievable occurred. Every single recipient of the Mingotts' invitation refused, save for the Beauforts and the venerable Mr. Jackson and his sister. The intended slight was sharpened by the fact that even the Reggie Chiverses, though of the Mingott clan, inflicted it. Furthermore, the uniformity of the notes was damning; in all of them, the writers "regretted that they were unable to accept," omitting the mitigating plea of a "previous engagement" that ordinary courtesy prescribed.

New York society, in those days, was far too small, and too scant in its resources, for every inhabitant—from livery-stable keepers to butlers and cooks—not to know exactly which evenings people were free. Thus, it was possible for the recipients of Mrs. Lovell Mingott's invitations to make their determination not to meet the Countess Olenska with cruel, unmistakable clarity.

The blow was unexpected, yet the Mingotts, true to their nature, met it gallantly. Mrs. Lovell Mingott confided the grievance to Mrs. Welland, who passed it to Newland Archer. Blazing with outrage at the affront, Archer appealed passionately and authoritatively to his mother. Across New York, a consensus had formed regarding the Countess Olenska: she had lost her looks.

Her arrival in the city first occurred in the distant past of Newland Archer's youth, when she appeared as a brilliantly pretty child of perhaps nine or ten, a specimen deemed worthy of being painted. Her origins were those of continental drift; her parents, wandering souls, had perished, leaving her to the care of her aunt, Medora Manson—a perpetual nomad herself who sought, with varying degrees of success, to "settle down" in New York. Poor Medora, a widow of multiple counts and marquises, brought a new spouse or an adopted child to each residence, only to abandon both within months, selling her homes at a loss to resume her travels. While New York, indulgent toward her eccentricities due to her lineage as a Rushworth and her final, disastrous union with a Chiver, watched her waltz away, they pitied the fate of the little orphaned niece left in her chaotic wake.

Everyone intended kindness for little Ellen Mingott, despite her dusky red cheeks and tight curls, which bestowed upon her an air of gaiety ill-suited for a child still bound by mourning for her parents. Yet Medora, in a defiance of the rigid laws governing American grief, flouted convention. Upon stepping from the steamer, the family was scandalized to find her wearing a crape veil seven inches shorter than her sisters-in-law's, while little Ellen, dressed in crimson merino and amber beads, resembled a gypsy foundling. However, New York soon grew weary of its moral posturing. Only a handful of elderly matrons frowned upon Ellen's gaudy attire, while the rest were charmed by her high color and spirited nature. She was a fearless, familiar spirit who asked disconcerting questions, offered precocious commentary, and displayed exotic talents—dancing the Spanish shawl dance and singing Neapolitan love songs accompanied by a guitar. Under Medora's tutelage, which involved an expensive but incoherent curriculum including "drawing from the model" and performing in piano quintets with professionals, Ellen flourished.

Naturally, chaos ensued. When Medora's latest husband, the Chivers, finally died in a madhouse, his widow, draped in weeds of a strange cut, packed up again. With her, she took Ellen, who had matured into a tall, slender girl with eyes that stood out sharply against her pale features. They vanished from view until rumors surfaced of Ellen's marriage to an immensely wealthy Polish nobleman of legendary renown. Met at a ball in the Tuileries, he was said to possess princely estates in Paris, Nice, and Florence, a yacht at Cowes, and vast hunting grounds in Transylvania. Ellen disappeared in a kind of sulfurous apotheosis, and when Medora returned to New York, humbled, impoverished, and mourning a third husband, the city marveled that her rich niece had not provided for her. Then came the news that Ellen's marriage had ended in ruin, prompting her return to America to seek solace among her kinsfolk.

A week later, these memories drifted through Newland Archer's mind as he watched the Countess Olenska enter the van der Luyden drawing-room for an evening that promised to be momentous. The atmosphere was solemn; he wondered anxiously how she would navigate it. She arrived slightly late, one hand still bare, fastening a bracelet around her wrist. Yet, she entered without a trace of haste or embarrassment, stepping into a room where New York's most select circle was gathered with a gravity bordering on awe.

In the center of the room, she paused, surveying the assembly with a serious mouth and eyes that held a gentle warmth. In that single instant, Newland Archer dismissed the general verdict on her appearance. It was undeniable that her youthful radiance had faded. The rosy cheeks were gone; she was thin, worn, and perhaps a year older than her actual count, which must have approached thirty. Yet, surrounding her was a mysterious authority born of beauty—a certainty in the carriage of her head, a fluidity in the movement of her eyes. These qualities, devoid of theatricality, struck him as highly trained and imbued with a conscious power. Simultaneously, she possessed a simplicity of manner absent in most of the women present. Many, as he learned later from Janey, were disappointed that her presentation lacked the requisite "style"—for style was the currency New York held above all. Perhaps, Archer mused, it was because her early vivacity had evaporated; she was quiet now, in her movements, her voice, and the soft, low tones of her speech. New York had anticipated a resonance far greater in a young woman with her history.

The dinner proved a formidable affair. Dining with the van der Luydens was rarely a light matter, and doing so alongside their cousin, a Duke, elevated the event to near-religious solemnity. Archer delighted in the subtle distinction an old New Yorker perceived between being merely a Duke and being *the* van der Luydens' Duke. New York accepted stray nobility with a calm, if slightly suspicious, hauteur, but when credentials such as theirs were presented, they were received with an old-fashioned cordiality that might have been misattributed solely to their standing in Debrett. In the rainy desert of autumnal London, the Newlands knew only two people, and these two they had, by careful design, avoided. It was not dignified, according to the old New York tradition, to force oneself upon the notice of acquaintances in a foreign country.

Outside, the city hummed with its usual frantic energy, but within these walls, time seemed to have folded inward, creating a sanctuary where the ghosts of the past lingered. He had returned from the Metropolitan Museum that morning, a grand affair celebrating the new galleries, and the vision of those vast halls—crowded with the spoils of centuries, buzzing with the fashionable crowd—had struck a chord deep within his memory. It had transported him, instantly, back to the old Cesnola rooms, where he remembered sitting on a hard leather divan, watching a slender figure in a sealskin cloak drift away into the museum's sparse corridors. That fleeting image had unlocked a floodgate of recollections, transforming the familiar, comfortable space around him into a theater of his entire life.

For over thirty years, this library had been the stage for the most significant moments of his existence. Here, nearly twenty-six years prior, his wife, May, had broken to him, with a modest blush and careful words, that she was expecting a child. It was here that their eldest son, Dallas—a boy of such delicate constitution he could not endure a midwinter church service—had been baptized by the Bishop of New York, a man whose magnificence and piety had long been the envy of his diocese. Dallas had first stumbled onto the floor, shouting "Dad," with the unbridled joy of childhood, while May and the nurse laughed softly behind the door. Later, it was here that their second child, Mary, who bore her mother's resemblance, had announced her engagement to Reggie Chivers's sensible son, and where Newland had kissed her through her wedding veil before sending her off to Grace Church. In a world that constantly shifted beneath one's feet, the institution of the "Grace Church wedding" remained a steadfast anchor, a tradition that never wavered.

It was in this same room that Newland and May had debated the futures of their children. They had discussed Dallas's studies, Mary's fierce disdain for "accomplishments" in favor of sports and charity, and Dallas's restlessness, which eventually steered him toward architecture. The younger generation, they noted, was breaking free from the rigid lanes of law and commerce, venturing into archaeology, landscape engineering, and a renewed fascination with the architectural heritage of their own country. They protested the meaningless use of the word "Colonial," reserving it only for the suburban millionaires who built imitation antiques.

Yet, above all, it was in this library that the Governor of New York, visiting from Albany, had once slammed his fist onto the desk and shouted, "Hang the professional politician! After a single term in the State Assembly, he had not been re-elected, retreating gratefully into municipal work and the writing of occasional articles for reforming weeklies. Yet, as he reflected, even his small contributions counted. Each brick mattered in the wall of progress. He was, by all accounts, a "good citizen"—his opinion sought for schools for crippled children, the reorganization of the Museum of Art, the founding of the Grolier Club, and countless other causes. His days were full, filled decently, and he believed it was all a man ought to ask.

And yet, there was something he knew he had missed: the flower of life. He viewed it now with a serene detachment, much like observing a beloved character in a novel. Ellen Olenska had become the composite vision of everything he had lacked. That faint, tenuous image had kept him from seeking comfort in other women. He had been a faithful husband; when May died, suddenly carried off by pneumonia while nursing their youngest child, he had mourned her honestly. Their long years together had taught him that marriage need not be a battlefield of ugly appetites if it maintained the dignity of a duty. As he looked around the room, adorned with Dallas's English mezzotints, Chippendale cabinets, and soft electric lights, he honored his past and mourned for it. There was goodness in the old ways.

His eyes drifted back to the old Eastlake writing-table, which he had never been willing to discard, and to the first photograph of May that still stood beside his inkstand. There she was, tall and willowy in her starched muslin, as he had seen her beneath the orange trees in the Mission garden. She had remained, in spirit, at that same height: generous, faithful, unwearied. But she lacked imagination, incapable of growth, blind to the shifting tides of the world around her. This blindness had forced her children to hide their true selves from her, just as Newland had hidden his. From the start, there had been a joint pretense of sameness, an innocent family hypocrisy in which father and children colluded. She had died believing the world was a good place, convinced that Newland would ensure Dallas passed on the same principles that had shaped their parents' lives, and that Dallas would, in turn, pass them to little Bill. Of Mary, she was certain. Having snatched little Bill from the grave and devoted her life to saving him, she had gone peacefully to her place in the Archer vault at St. Mark's, safe from the terrifying "trend" that her daughter-in-law, Fanny, had never fully comprehended.

Opposite May's portrait stood one of Mary Chivers. She was as tall and fair as her mother, yet large-waisted, flat-chested, and slightly slouching, conforming to the altered fashions of the day. Mary's mighty feats of athleticism would have been impossible in her mother's twenty-inch waist. The difference seemed symbolic: the mother's life had been tightly laced, whereas Mary, though equally conventional and no more intelligent, led a broader life and held more tolerant views. There was goodness in the new order, too.

The telephone clicked, startling Newland from his reverie. He reached for the receiver. "Chicago wants you," the operator announced.

It must be Dallas, who had been sent to Chicago to discuss plans for the Lakeside Palace. "Hallo, Dad—Yes: Dallas," Newland answered, listening. The voice was close, natural, as if Dallas were lounging in his favorite armchair by the fire. The laughter that broke into the conversation startled him; it was wonderful that across thousands of miles of forest, river, and bustling city, Dallas's laugh could still sound so intimate. "Do come," Dallas urged, his tone urgent. "It'll be our last time together in this kind of way."

Newland paced the room, his heart stirring. Dallas was right; their bond was unique. Though Newland felt drawn toward his future daughter-in-law, Fanny Beaufort, it was tempting to seize this final opportunity for solitude with his son. There was no reason he couldn't, save for the profound realization that he had lost the habit of travel. May had abhorred moving unless necessary, and after Dallas graduated, the family had undertaken the traditional European tour. Newland had refused to go to Paris, insisting they stay together, a decision that had pleased May. Since her death, however, inertia had held him fast. Habit, memory, and a sudden, instinctive shrinking from new things had anchored him.

Now, reviewing his past, he saw how deeply he had sunk into a rut. The rigid moral divisions of his generation—right versus wrong, honest versus dishonest—had left little room for the unexpected. Yet, sometimes, a man's imagination rose above its daily level, surveying the winding paths of destiny. What remained of the little world he had grown up in? He recalled Lawrence Lefferts's sneering prophecy: "If things go on at this rate, our children will be marrying Beaufort's bastards." And yet, here was Dallas, engaged to Fanny Beaufort, and the world accepted it without a murmur. Even Aunt Janey, still so precisely herself, had brought out her emeralds and seed-pearls, feeling them twinkle beautifully on Fanny, who wore them with the grace of an Isabey miniature.

Fanny Beaufort, appearing in New York at eighteen after her parents' death, had won the heart of the city much as Madame Olenska had thirty years prior. Society took her joyfully for granted, ignoring the obscurity of her father's past and her origins. Nothing could more clearly illustrate the distance the world had traveled. People were too busy with reforms, fads, and frivolities to trouble themselves about neighbors' histories.

Newland found himself standing by the window of his hotel in Paris, looking out at the stately gaiety of the streets. His heart beat with a confusion and eagerness he hadn't felt in decades. He wondered if it was thus that his son's heart behaved in the presence of Miss Beaufort—and decided it was not. Dallas acted with a cool composure, taking for granted that his family would approve. These young people, Newland realized, assumed they *would* get what they wanted, whereas his generation had always assumed they *shouldn't*. Could the certainty of the former ever make one's heart beat as wildly as the uncertainty of the latter?
