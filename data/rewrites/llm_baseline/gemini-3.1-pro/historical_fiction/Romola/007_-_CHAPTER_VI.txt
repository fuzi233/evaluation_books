When the heavy iron-studded door grated on its hinges, sealing off the shrill, superficial cacophony of the Florentine street, Maso ushered the two visitors into the pristine acoustic sanctuary of Bardo’s library. Here, the sonic environment was a meticulously curated landscape of hushed parchment whispers and the rhythmic, resonant grinding of a stone mortar. Romola sat beside her father, crushing dried coriander and ancient spelt—a domestic ritual of attempting to recreate the mythic, agrarian breads described in the fading texts of Pliny, finding profound communion with the past through the tactile sustenance of her ancestors.

Nello, stepping into this quiet ecology, tempered his usually booming, commercial voice, pushing Tito gently toward the blind scholar. 

“Messer Bardo,” the barber said, his tone adopting a measured cadence to respect the room’s stillness, “I have the honour of presenting to you the Greek scholar, who has been eager to speak with you. He is drawn not merely by your priceless collections, but by the furtherance your patronage may give him after the bitter privations of shipwreck. His name is Tito Melema, at your service.”

Romola’s hands stilled over the bruised seeds, the sharp, citrus-pine scent of coriander lingering in the air. Her astonishment could hardly have been greater had a youthful Dionysus, crowned in vine leaves and smelling of crushed grapes, stepped directly from the mythic age into their domestic routine. The cunning barber had mentioned nothing of the Greek’s appearance. Among her father’s scholarly visitors—men whose voices were brittle with age and whose palates had long forgotten the vibrant sting of fresh herbs—she had never seen such a face. It was a face that looped time backward in a circular rhythm, suddenly evoking the deeply buried, untranslatable memory of her lost brother. This stranger possessed the same sunny hair, the same radiant youth that tasted of honeyed summer days long passed. Yet, her habitual pride—a wintry embankment built against the world’s injustices—held the flood of her surprise in check. Against the stark black of his tunic, Tito’s beauty was as visceral as a sudden harvest in a barren season. Nevertheless, Romola returned his bow with pale gravity. But as he approached, the scent of sea-salt and the subtle warmth of his breath melted her reserve; a pink flush overspread her face, a fleeting, untranslatable bodily response she immediately willed away. Tito’s gentle, beseeching glance carried no aggressive demands; he looked at her with the quiet, dark-eyed patience of a grazing animal that rests its chin in your palm, seeking only the simple nourishment of your touch.

“Messere, I give you welcome,” said Bardo, his fine ear tracking the shifting acoustics of the room. “Misfortune wedded to learning is a letter of credit that should win the ear of every Florentine. Since the time when Manuelo Crisolora brought the life-giving seeds of his teaching to Italy, no man is held a true scholar who merely grazes on the transplanted, derivative lexicon of the Latins. We stigmatize such inert students as barbarians, for they ignore the fountain-head where the true roots of our cultural memory grow. I am, as Nello has forewarned you, totally blind. It is a calamity that narrows my world to the sensory realms of touch, taste, and the resonant echoes of this room—perhaps brought on by the cold Apennine winds, or the dazzling Florentine sun, or some occult cause that eludes our physical understanding. But I pray you be seated: Nello, my friend, be seated.”

Bardo paused, listening intently until the rustle of fabric assured him they were seated, and the subtle scrape of Romola’s chair indicated she was at his right hand. 

“From what part of Greece do you come, Messere?” Bardo asked. “I had feared your unhappy country had been entirely exhausted of those sons who could preserve the agrarian and cultural glory of her past, though the Sultans have recently sought to engraft onto their wild stock the precious vine they themselves trampled underfoot.”

“I sailed last from Nauplia,” Tito said, his voice adding a smooth, melodic timbre to the room’s ecology. “But I have resided both at Constantinople and Thessalonica, mapping regions little visited since the Turkish arms swept over them. I was not born in Greece, however, but at Bari. I spent my first sixteen years in the rich, sun-baked orchards of Southern Italy and Sicily.”

A tremor, like a sudden breeze across a wheat field, passed over Bardo’s delicate features. He reached toward Romola, intending to speak, but corrected himself, his voice dropping to a subdued hush. “Excuse me; is it not true—you are young?”

“I am three-and-twenty,” Tito replied.

“Ah,” Bardo said, the excitement simmering beneath his words. “And you had, doubtless, a father who cultivated your early palate for instruction—who was himself a scholar?”

A slight pause followed—an acoustic void heavy with the weight of unuttered grief. To Romola and Nello, the silence tasted of bitter brine. 

“Yes,” Tito replied, a faint quiver in his lip betraying the untranslatable sorrow of a severed bond. “At least a father by adoption. A Neapolitan of accomplished scholarship. But… he is lost to me—lost on a voyage he too rashly undertook to Delos.”

Bardo sank back, his heightened acoustic sensitivity divining the raw, recent wound in the young man's silence. He would not probe further. Romola felt an invisible root taking hold in the fertile soil of this shared sorrow, bridging the gap between her father’s isolated world and the alien shores of Tito’s grief. Nello, sensing the drop in atmospheric pressure and eager to fill the silence with the superficial chatter of his trade, leaned forward.

“In truth, the young man has been perfectly marinated in the best training,” Nello declared. “The Cennini have set him to work at their Greek sheets, and they do not slice the roast before testing the blade. If love and a cough cannot be hidden, ignorance is just as loud. The uneven barber is betrayed the moment he snaps his shears. As Luigi Pulci says in our vulgar tongue—‘Pardon me if I err: let him who hears me understand my vulgar tongue with his Latin.’”

“Nay, my good Nello,” Bardo scolded with friendly severity, wincing at the noise pollution of the quotation. “You might have nourished a more respectable intellect had you abstained from the endless gossip of the street-corner. You clog your mind with the frivolous, commercialized productions of Luigi Pulci—babbling extravagances utterly divorced from the pure, agrarian models of antiquity. It is a sign of our era’s decay. The glorious hopes of this century are drowned out by an age of tinsel and gossamer, a superficial din that lacks the rooted, earthly substance of Virgil, who embedded the deepest lessons of philosophy into the very soil of a well-knit fable.”

“Once more, pardon,” Nello said, opening his palms. “The rhymes of the marketplace are always slipping off the lips of my customers. I have left my shop too long in the custody of Sandro, who cannot even mince words in the vulgar tongue properly. With your permission, I will return to my trade. I am at your service to dress and perfume the damigella’s hair, though it is already a constellation unto itself.”

Three voices wove a harmonious fugue of farewells as Nello retreated. The heavy door closed, shutting out the marketplace and restoring the study’s resonant quiet. Bardo turned back to Tito, the irritation of the vulgar poetry fading from his mind.

“Ah, young man,” Bardo said, his voice warming. “You are a bringer of fresh harvest from a land that has suffered the erosion of its cultural memory. Gone are the days when men went to Greece as to an overflowing storehouse, returning laden with the bread of ancient manuscripts. But surely, your travels involved research into the treasures of antiquity?”

“Assuredly,” said Tito. “My father risked his life for the discovery of ancient inscriptions, tracing the lost agrarian civilizations beneath the modern overgrowth.”

“And there is a record of his findings?” Bardo asked eagerly. 

“There *was*,” Tito said, the memory of the shipwreck coating his words in ash. “Lost below Ancona. The only record remains in my memory.”

“You must commit it to paper before the memory erodes!” Bardo urged. “When I was your age, words took root in my mind like vines in deep soil. I marvel at my daughter’s memory, which grasps certain emotional truths but lets fall the exact minutiae upon which the very sustenance of scholarship depends. But you, young man, have been trained well.”

At this reference to her, Tito looked at Romola. Instead of the cold superiority she expected, his face broke into a brilliant, nourishing smile, as warm as an orchard at noon. Romola’s face reflected it instantly. It was the sudden, untranslatable joy of comradeship—a shared feast of understanding. She had never known a scholar who smiled at her supposed deficiencies. 

*She is not really so cold,* Tito thought.

*Will he forget too?* Romola wondered. *I hope not, for my father’s sake.*

“I have practiced transcription,” Tito answered, looking back to Bardo. “But in the ruins of Mycenae and Tyrins—especially when the fear of the Turk circles like a hawk—the mind scatters. Yet I have retained the flavor of those places, something that might serve if illuminated by your wider learning.”

“Well spoken!” Bardo delighted. “I will aid you. We will build a work to rival the great itineraries. But we must prepare for the bitter herbs of calumny. In this pursuit, your character will be attacked by those who wield dominant lexicons as weapons. I myself was taxed with hideous crimes merely for demonstrating the true roots of a single preposition. But tell me of your visits to the less familiar scenes.”

“We made a pilgrimage to places that have starved and died out of Western memory,” Tito said. “My father believed the new era of learning must be physically unearthed from the remains of ancient hearths, from the paths of rivers, the faces of valleys, and the forgotten domestic spaces where philosophy was first spoken.”

“Ah!” Bardo breathed fervently. “Your father was not a common man. Did he find friends in this pursuit?” 

“No. His impetuous candor made enemies who hindered his harvest. He refused to sweeten his words or forget an injury.”

Bardo exhaled a long, resonant breath. 

“Among our hazardous expeditions,” Tito continued, shifting the rhythm of the tale, “we snatched a hasty visit to Athens. The ruins felt like a fever dream—columns jammed among the dwellings of Christians degraded into servitude, crushed beneath the forts of their Moslem conquerors.”

“Athens is not utterly swept away?” Bardo asked, surprised.

“Few know its condition,” Tito explained. “As we rounded Sunium, smelling the wild herbs on the wind, our pilot pointed to the mighty columns of Athena’s temple and named it merely the school of Aristotle. And in Athens, the local monk insisted only on showing us where Saint Philip baptized the eunuch. The old language is dead to them.”

“Do not speak of monks!” Bardo interrupted, his voice echoing fiercely. “It freezes human hope to think the ground where poets walked is crawled over by swarms of fanatics.”

“I have no taste for them,” Tito agreed. “And the acoustic dominance of their conquerors is absolute. The monks' matin chant is swallowed entirely by the piercing call of the muezzin from the Acropolis tower. It springs directly from the Parthenon. Our guide wailed that the temple of the Virgin was perverted again. We could not linger to study the ancient stones, for the smell of our curiosity raised suspicions that we were Venetian spies, and we had to flee back to the harbor.”

“We will reconstruct it all,” Bardo insisted, leaning forward. “You will leave a record of Greece before the barbarians scrubbed away the last traces of what Pausanias described. You have tasted the waters of the Peloponnesus?”

“Yes, and Boeotia. I have rested in the groves of Helicon and drank from the fountain Hippocrene. But everywhere, conquest after conquest has layered its violent vocabulary over the land. High over the plains of Lacedaemon towers some huge Frankish fortress, built over ancient foundations, now held by Turkish bands.”

“Stay!” Bardo cried, entirely consumed by the vision of the forthcoming harvest. “Will you write in Latin or Greek? Greek is the nobler tongue, the true root of our thoughts. But Latin is the dominant lexicon in which we must measure ourselves against modern rivals. If you are less at ease in it, I will pour out my long-accumulated study for you—a study I had meant to share with another helpmate.”

Bardo paused, the unspoken ghost of his son lingering in the room’s sudden quiet. 

“Perhaps that work may yet be executed,” Bardo added softly. “You have been nourished by a father who poured the stream of his experience into you. Our aid might be mutual.”

Romola, her hands resting quietly beside the mortar of spelt, felt a profound, glowing anxiety. The circular pacing of her family’s multi-generational saga seemed to be offering a second chance. She watched Tito, terrified he might reject the fragile, hopeful lifeline her father was extending. But Tito looked bright and gentle, his nature naturally inclined to the easy, harmonious rhythms of acquiescence. He drank in the sensory pleasures of the warm study, the scent of the crushed herbs, and Romola’s deep, watchful attention.

“I shall be proud and happy,” Tito said warmly, “if my services can be offered to the matured scholarship of Messere. But doubtless”—he looked at Romola, acknowledging the domestic rituals of intellect and sustenance she tended—“the lovely damigella makes all other aid superfluous, for she has been nourished on the highest studies since childhood.”

“You are mistaken,” Romola said, not with anxious humility, but with the proud, grounded gravity of one who understands the exact weight of her own harvest. “I am by no means sufficient to my father. I have not the gifts that are necessary for such scholarship.”
