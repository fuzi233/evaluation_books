As Mr. Warrington entered the White Horse ordinary, the warm glow of the fire and the murmur of conversation enveloped him like a comforting shawl. He was greeted by the familiar, jolly countenance of Parson Sampson, who was regaling the company with tales of London's latest gossip. The parson's words danced with wit, drawing laughter from the assembly as he spun a web of stories that wove together the threads of high society and low life. News of the royal court, the dukes' households, and the latest scandals at Newmarket were all woven into the tapestry of the parson's narrative, though accuracy was hardly a concern for the rustic gentlemen who hung on his every word.

A century ago, such a chaplain would have been a common sight in English society – a man of the cloth who reveled in the company of actors, musicians, and gamblers, whose voice could be heard ringing out across the hunting fields and whose song could charm the crowds at the Rose or the Bedford Head. And Parson Sampson, with his rosy face and easy smile, was a modern-day embodiment of this tradition. As he spotted Mr. Warrington, he rose hastily from his chair, nearly stumbling in his eagerness to greet the Virginian. "My dear sir, my very dear sir, I am delighted to see you looking so fresh and well!" he exclaimed, his words tumbling forth in a joyful torrent.

Harry returned the chaplain's greeting with equal warmth, pleased to see his old friend and happy to offer his own compliments on the parson's ruddy complexion and robust health.
