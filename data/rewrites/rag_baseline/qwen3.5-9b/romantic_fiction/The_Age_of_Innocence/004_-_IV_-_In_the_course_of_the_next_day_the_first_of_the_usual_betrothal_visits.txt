The next day, the ritual unfolded with the rigid precision of a clockwork mechanism. Newland Archer accompanied his mother and sister to pay their respects to Mrs. Welland, and subsequently, the trio drove to the ancestral estate of Mrs. Manson Mingott. For Archer, this visit was a familiar performance, a scripted encounter with history itself. The Mingott house was a document of its era, distinct from the grim, harmonious architecture of University Place—a deliberate rejection of the heavy mahogany and cabbage-rose garlands of the 1830s in favor of the frivolous, Second Empire upholstery that defined her taste.

Mrs. Mingott presided over the ground floor, seated in a window as if observing the slow, inevitable march of civilization toward her solitary doors. She possessed a patience matched only by her certainty that the chaotic sprawl of the city—the hoardings, the quarries, the ragged gardens—would eventually yield to the stately order of her own domain. She viewed her geographic isolation not as a deficit, but as a strategic advantage; she filled her rooms with visitors without expanding her household budget. Time had transformed her physically, burying her former vitality beneath an immense accumulation of flesh, turning her into a vast, natural phenomenon. Yet, she accepted this metamorphosis with philosophical indifference, presenting to the mirror an expanse of firm, pink skin where only the ghost of a small face awaited excavation. Her reception rooms, located upstairs, were accessible only through a breach in propriety: a door left ajar revealed a bedroom with a low, sofa-like bed and a vanity table, a layout that suggested the architectural incentives to immorality found in French fiction rather than American convention.

Archer found amusement in imagining her blameless life staged within a set designed for adultery, convinced that if a lover had existed to match her desires, the intrepid woman would have claimed him. Fortunately, the Countess Olenska was absent during the betrothal visit. Mrs. Mingott dismissed her absence lightly, noting she had gone out shopping—a detail that seemed indelicate for a woman of her reputation, yet conveniently spared the guests the shadow of her unhappy past.

The engagement proceeded smoothly. Mrs. Mingott delighted in the union, which had been vetted by family councils long before the announcement. She admired the engagement ring, a large sapphire set in invisible claws. "It's the new setting," Mrs. Welland explained, offering a conciliatory glance at her future son-in-law. "Old-fashioned eyes? I hope you don't mean mine, my dear? I like all the novelties." The ancestress lifted the stone to her clear, unglassesed eyes. "Very handsome," she declared, returning the jewel. "Very liberal. In my time, a cameo set in pearls sufficed. But it's the hand that sets off the ring, isn't it, my dear Mr. Archer?" She waved a tiny hand, encircled by rolls of aged fat like ivory bracelets. "Mine was modeled in Rome by the great Ferrigiani. You should have May's done... Her hand is large—it's these modern sports that spread the joints—but the skin is white." Fixing her gaze on Archer, she asked when the wedding would be.

"As soon as ever it can, if only you'll back me up, Mrs. Mingott," Archer replied, smiling at his betrothed.

"We must give them time to get to know each other a little better, mamma," Mrs. Welland interposed, feigning reluctance.

"Know each other? Fiddlesticks! Everybody in New York has always known everybody. Let the young man have his way; don't wait till the bubble's off the wine. Marry them before Lent; I may catch pneumonia any winter now, and I want to give the wedding-breakfast."

These declarations were met with the requisite expressions of amusement and gratitude as the visit dissolved into mild pleasantry. Just then, the door opened to admit the Countess Olenska, wearing a bonnet and mantle, followed by the unexpected figure of Julius Beaufort. A cousinly murmur rippled among the ladies. Mrs. Mingott extended Ferrigiani's model to the banker. "Ha! Beaufort, this is a rare favour!" she exclaimed, using the foreign custom of addressing men by surname. "Thanks. I wish it might happen oftener," the visitor replied in his easy arrogance. "I'm generally so tied down; but I met the Countess Ellen in Madison Square, and she was good enough to let me walk home with her."

"Oh—I hope the house will be gayer, now that Ellen's here!" Mrs. Mingott cried with glorious effrontery. "Sit down—push up the yellow armchair; now I've got you I want a good gossip. I hear your ball was magnificent; and I understand you invited Mrs. Lemuel Struthers? Well—I've a curiosity to see the woman myself." She had forgotten her relatives, who drifted out into the hall under Ellen's guidance. Mrs. Mingott admired Julius Beaufort for their shared cool dominion over conventions. She was eager to learn why the Beauforts had invited Mrs. Lemuel Struthers, the widow of the shoe-polish magnate, back from her European sojourn to besiege New York's tight citadel. "Well, we need new blood and new money—and I hear she's still very good-looking," the carnivorous old lady declared.

In the hall, as Mrs. Welland and May donned their furs, Archer observed the Countess looking at him with a faintly questioning smile. "Of course you know already—about May and me," he said, answering her look with a shy laugh. "She scolded me for not giving you the news last night at the Opera: I had her orders to tell you that we were engaged—but I couldn't, in that crowd."

The smile traveled from her eyes to her lips; she looked younger, more like the bold brown Ellen Mingott of his boyhood. "Of course I know; yes. And I'm so glad. But one doesn't tell such things first in a crowd."

The ladies stepped onto the threshold, and she held out her hand. "Good-bye; come and see me some day," she said, her gaze lingering on Archer.

In the carriage, descending Fifth Avenue, they spoke pointedly of Mrs. Mingott's age, her spirit, and her wonderful attributes. No mention was made of Ellen Olenska. Yet Archer knew Mrs. Welland was thinking: *It's a mistake for Ellen to be seen, the very day after her arrival, parading up Fifth Avenue at the crowded hour with Julius Beaufort.* And the young man himself mentally added: *And she ought to know that a man who's just engaged doesn't spend his time calling on married women. But I daresay in the set she's lived in they do—they never do anything else.* Despite his pride in his cosmopolitan views, he thanked heaven that he was a New Yorker, about to ally himself with one of his own kind.
