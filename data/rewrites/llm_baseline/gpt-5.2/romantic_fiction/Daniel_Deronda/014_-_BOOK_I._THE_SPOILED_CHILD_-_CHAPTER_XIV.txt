Gwendolen came down late to breakfast the next morning with that fresh firmness which sometimes follows a night’s tossing—as if sleep had kneaded yesterday’s misgiving into something smooth, and she could rise again, tall and white and vigorous, like a lily opened by the first heat. The day promised Cardell Chase, roving archery, shifting shade, voices among the leaves; and she had already dressed her fancy in green boughs and sunlight, moving through the beeches as if she belonged to them. Grandcourt’s attentions, too, fell into their places in her mind with an air of being manageable—no lyric shepherd, certainly, but that suited her. She had foreseen him edging, by those slow approaches he made as if he were feeling the weight of his own words, toward what must end in a declaration; and she had foreseen herself receiving it with the composed encouragement she had resolved on with her uncle.

Only Mrs. Davilow remained at the table when Gwendolen entered. Letters lay on her plate. She broke one open, and her smile gathered as she read—one of those smiles that take the mother’s heart by surprise and make it believe in ease again. She handed the note across.

Mrs. Davilow read it and smiled too, as if her daughter’s brightness were a coal warmed into life.

“You don’t feel inclined to go a thousand miles away?” she said, folding the paper back.

“Not exactly so far.”

“It was a sad omission not to have written again before this. Can’t you write now—before we set out this morning?”

“It is not so pressing. To-morrow will do. They leave town to-day. I must write to Dover. They will be there till Monday.”

“Shall I write for you, dear—if it teases you?”

Gwendolen’s cup touched her lip; she sipped, set it down, and answered with a brusqueness that was like a little slap given to prevent tenderness from clinging. “Oh no. Let it be; I will write to-morrow.” Then, as if she disliked even her own harshness, she looked up with sudden playful softness. “Dear, old, beautiful mamma!”

“Old, child, truly.”

“Please don’t, mamma! I meant old for darling. You are hardly twenty-five years older than I am. When you talk in that way my life shrivels up before me.”

“One can have a great deal of happiness in twenty-five years, my dear.”

“I must lose no time in beginning,” said Gwendolen, merrily. “The sooner I get my palaces and coaches the better.”

“And a good husband who adores you, Gwen,” Mrs. Davilow added, as if to coax Fate into kindness by naming it.

Gwendolen only pushed out her lips in saucy silence.

One small cloud lay on the morning: the rector was detained by magistrate’s business and would likely not reach Cardell Chase. She did not greatly care that Mrs. Gascoigne and Anna stayed behind, but she missed her uncle’s presence as a kind of seal upon the decision she had almost taken. To decide had begun to look like stepping into a mould—this unhoped-for fullness rounding itself too neatly. When one begins to wish largely for one’s self, whatever arrives soon seems a fence, shutting out all the other imaginable lives. Still, she told herself, marriage was a gate into a larger freedom.

They met at Green Arbor—a grassy hollow where a hanging wood made an amphitheatre, the leaves roofing it so that the light fell in sifted coins. A coachful of servants had gone before with provisions; the warden of the Chase was to guide the archers along a curve marked by familiar points—the double Oak, the Whispering Stones, the High Cross—so that the roving should not become mere wandering. They would stroll first, and keep the longer expedition for the afternoon when shadows lengthened and the forest put on its richest colour.

The assembling was quick enough to prevent dulness. Soon groups scattered through beech shade and open oak-light, and the whole scene had that composed gayety in which it seems as if even footsteps take care not to jar. Roving archery, prettiest of sports, is also a capricious one: the marks shift, distances deceive, the bow answers differently when the air cools under thicker leaves. Gwendolen, though graceful as ever, did not win great credit in those first tries—perhaps because her mind was not wholly in the aim, and because she wished her mind not to be seen elsewhere. Yet she made even her failures look like choice, laughing them off with a charm that asked pardon of no one.

She wore white and green as she had worn it on the former day—the day that had become an epoch because it had brought her Grandcourt. He was continually beside her now; but to an eye watching only looks and manners, their relation might have seemed scarcely changed. Still, people read what they want to read; and there were enough glances, enough nearness and quiet understanding, to make most conclude that if they were not engaged already, they stood on the lip of it. Gwendolen believed it herself.

As they returned toward Green Arbor in divergent little knots, talking more than aiming, words passed that seemed to be the first clear turning of the lock.

“Do you know how long it is since I first saw you in this dress?” Grandcourt said.

“The archery meeting was on the 25th, and this is the 13th,” Gwendolen answered with a light laugh. “I am not good at calculating, but I will venture to say it must be nearly three weeks.”

A pause. Then, in his slow way, “That is a great loss of time.”

“That your knowing me has caused you? Pray don’t be uncomplimentary; I don’t like it.”

Pause again. “It is because of the gain that I feel the loss.”

Gwendolen herself let silence stand, amused and watchful. She thought, with a kind of complacent surprise, He is really very ingenious. He never speaks stupidly. Her unusual quietness seemed to him a yielding, and he went on.

“The gain of knowing you makes me feel the time I lose in uncertainty. Do you like uncertainty?”

“I think I do, rather,” she said, suddenly beaming on him, playful. “There is more in it.”

He met her laughing eyes with that steady, slow gaze of his, as if he looked, not at her, but through her—as if the act of seeing were separate from any warmth. “Do you mean more torment for me?”

Something in the moment—his look, his tone, the sudden closeness of his meaning—shook her out of her usual self-possession. She blushed, turned her eyes away, and said, “No—that would make me sorry.”

He might have pressed on; her changed manner seemed decisive. But they were now descending into Green Arbor where the slope became inconveniently steep. The very ground offered him an excuse; he put out his hand to help her. She took it, and they came down in silence, plainly observed by those already below—Mrs. Arrowpoint among them, standing near Mrs. Davilow.

That lady, having long settled that Grandcourt’s merits would never have satisfied Catherine, looked on him without romance. She spoke with a clear voice that carried farther than she meant.

“Mr. Grandcourt is not equal as a man to his uncle, Sir Hugo Mallinger—too languid. To be sure, Mr. Grandcourt is much younger; but I shouldn’t wonder if Sir Hugo outlived him, notwithstanding the difference of years. It is ill calculating on successions,” she concluded.

“It is indeed,” said Mrs. Davilow; and she could say it with quiet cheerfulness, so content had she become with the visible promise of things.

There was a feast laid in that green refectory—cold meats, pastries, fruit with bloom still on it, bottles drawn from cool shade, baskets opened and closed with brisk hands. The talk and laughter, too, were of the well-bred kind that takes its good fortune as if it were a matter of course. The forest beyond made large pictures—glades opening like windows, distant trunks standing in ordered ranks, the light changing without hurry, as if time itself had softened in the leaves. Yet for Gwendolen the beauty entered only by narrow oblique inlets; her mind was busy with its small drama, as self-absorbed as a puppet-show.

After luncheon some gentlemen strolled and smoked. Strange to say, Grandcourt was among them; but Lush was not. Lush, in fact, threw himself into service—ordering, fetching, attending—so active and obliging that he became more than ever a blot in Gwendolen’s sense of the scene, though he kept himself amiably at a distance and never stared.

When the general move began for setting out again, it appeared that the bows were all under the charge of Lord Brackenshaw’s valet, and that Lush was saving ladies the trouble of fetching theirs from the carriage where they were propped. He did not mean to bring Gwendolen’s; but she, fearing even the approach of that officious figure, hurried to fetch it herself.

The valet, seeing her, met her bow—and with it handed her a letter addressed to her. She asked no question. At once she saw the handwriting was a lady’s: delicate, formed for small confidences, of a kind now less common. She moved away with her bow in hand, and as she did so she saw Lush coming to fetch other bows. To avoid him she turned so that her back was toward the line of carriages and opened the letter as she walked.

It said:

If Miss Harleth is in doubt whether she should accept Mr. Grandcourt, let her break from her party after they have passed the Whispering Stones and return to that spot. She will then hear something to decide her; but she can only hear it by keeping this letter a strict secret from every one. If she does not act according to this letter, she will repent, as the woman who writes it has repented. The secrecy Miss Harleth will feel herself bound in honor to guard.

A shock went through her, swift and cold; yet her first thought was: It is come in time. Youth, eager for revelations, made her mind rush to the imagined disclosure. She had not a moment’s suspicion of contrivance that might justify showing the letter. At once she resolved she would manage to reach the Whispering Stones unseen. She thrust the paper into her pocket with that bracing sense of concealment which, for her nature, strengthened rather than weakened self-command.

It surprised everyone that Grandcourt was not, like the other smokers, ready to set out roving with the rest.

“We shall alight on him by-and-by,” Lord Brackenshaw said. “He can’t be gone far. At any rate, no man can be waited for.”

His apparent forgetfulness might have been read as the lover’s distraction; and the Earl, good-natured, threw Gwendolen a distant hint to that effect. She received it with fitting quiet. But she thought: Can he too be starting away from a decision? The thought was not pleasant—yet it was near the truth. Not “starting away” by any energetic flight; rather that numb languor which sometimes seized Grandcourt when an end came within easy reach: to desist at the very moment when all expected him to advance became another gratification of mere will, independent of motive. He had begun a second large cigar with a vague obstinacy that, if any mortal whom he could insult with impunity had tried to coax him back, would have been met only with a slow removal of the cigar and an undertone: “You’ll be kind enough to go to the devil, will you?”

No one interrupted him. The rovers set out without visible dampening of spirits, leaving behind a few less vigorous ladies—Mrs. Davilow among them—who preferred a quiet stroll free from the obligation of keeping pace. Soon the day rose to its highest pitch: the sport grew livelier; the forest opened and closed around them in changing rooms of shade; and the lengthening afternoon mellowed everything, making voices softer, footsteps more distinct, and the small sounds of the Chase—bird-calls, creaking leather, the thud of arrows, faint laughter carried and lost—compose themselves into a kind of music. Someone said they were acting an extemporized As You Like It; and when a compliment was turned on Gwendolen, naming her Rosalind, she felt the more obliged to be surpassing in loveliness. Her excitement helped her: what had happened to-day had set a restless energy in her, not fear exactly but adventure, and a strained attention to the instant when she might slip away without being hindered.

Nearly an hour had passed when they reached the Whispering Stones—two tall conical blocks leaning toward each other like gigantic figures wrapped in grey mantles. They were glanced at, admired, passed; someone said they would make good ghosts on a starlit night. But soft sunlight lay on them now, and Gwendolen felt bold.

They stood near a fine grove of beeches where the archers found plenty of marks. Gwendolen got in front for a moment beside the warden.

“How far are we from Green Arbor now?” she asked.

“Oh, not more than half a mile, taking along the avenue we’re going to cross up there; but I shall take round a couple of miles, by the High Cross.”

She began to fall back among the others. Then she noticed that the whole party seemed to hurry obliquely forward under Mr. Lush’s guidance. Linger a little—only a little—and the chance would be hers. She let them go on. In a moment she was out of sight.

She did not run; yet she seemed to herself to fly, the ground sliding under her feet like something unreal. She counted the moments as nothing, except for one sharp dread: if there were nothing after all—nothing behind the stones but her own foolishness, and she must go back puzzled. That was her only fear now. Coming close, she walked straight round the right-hand stone; and there, at a foot’s distance, she found herself fronting a woman.

Large dark eyes met hers. For all her expectation she started and shrank back; but in that recoil she took in the stranger’s whole figure—unmistakably a lady, and one who must once have been strikingly handsome. A few yards away two children sat on the grass.

“Miss Harleth?” the lady said.

“Yes.” Wonder filled Gwendolen’s consciousness like a sudden fog.

“Have you accepted Mr. Grandcourt?”

“No.”

“I promised to tell you something. And you will promise to keep my secret. However you may decide, you will not tell Mr. Grandcourt, or any one else, that you have seen me?”

“I promise.”

“My name is Lydia Glasher. Mr. Grandcourt ought not to marry any one but me. I left my husband and child for him nine years ago. Those two children are his, and we have two others—girls—who are older. My husband is dead now, and Mr. Grandcourt ought to marry me. He ought to make that boy his heir.”

As she spoke she looked at the boy, and Gwendolen’s eyes followed. The handsome little fellow puffed out his cheeks, trying to blow a tiny trumpet which remained dumb. His hat hung backward by a string; brown curls caught the sunlight like fine threads. He was as pretty as a painted cherub, and the silence of his toy made the scene more uncanny—as if even play had been deprived of its proper voice.

The two women looked at each other again. Gwendolen, pale-lipped, shivering as if cold had seized her under the warm leaves, said with pride, “I will not interfere with your wishes.”

“You are very attractive, Miss Harleth. But when he first knew me, I too was young. Since then my life has been broken up and embittered. It is not fair that he should be happy and I miserable, and my boy thrust out of sight for another.”

The words came with a biting accent, yet she held herself—no violence, no theatrics—only a determined restraint that made the threat of passion more dreadful. Watching her face, Gwendolen felt a terror that was like seeing, in waking daylight, a vision one expects only in nightmare: I am a woman’s life.

“Have you anything more to say to me?” Gwendolen asked in a low voice, still proud, still cold. The revulsion within her did not soften her; it made everything hateful.

“Nothing. You know what I wished you to know. You can inquire about me if you like. My husband was Colonel Glasher.”

“Then I will go,” said Gwendolen, moving away with a ceremonious inclination. The other returned it with equal grace.

In a few minutes Gwendolen was back among the beeches; but her party had gone out of sight, and no one seemed to have sought her. Solitude lay thick, broken only by distant, muffled cries of sport. She reached the avenue the warden had pointed out and took it toward Green Arbor. Rapid movement was, just then, her only way of keeping thought from rising up and choking her. And as she went she had already made up her mind what step she would take.

At Green Arbor, Mrs. Davilow was astonished to see her return alone, and uneasiness pressed hard—yet other ladies stood near, and she could not show it. Gwendolen answered her mother’s surprised questions with an ease that was almost too ready.

“Oh, I have been rather silly. I lingered behind to look at the Whispering Stones, and the rest hurried on after something, so I lost sight of them. I thought it best to come home by the short way—the avenue the warden told me of. I’m not sorry after all. I had had enough walking.”

“Your party did not meet Mr. Grandcourt, I presume,” said Mrs. Arrowpoint, with intention under her smoothness.

“No,” said Gwendolen, with a flash of defiance and a light laugh. “And we didn’t see any carvings on the trees, either. Where can he be? I should think he has fallen into the pool or had an apoplectic fit.”

She meant not to betray agitation, yet her tone rang too high and hard, like metal struck. Her mother felt at once that something unpropitious had happened.

Mrs. Arrowpoint, thinking only of ordinary pique, concluded that the self-confident young lady was smarting, and that Mr. Grandcourt might be cooling into change.

“If you have no objection, mamma, I will order the carriage,” Gwendolen said. “I am tired. And every one will be going soon.”

Mrs. Davilow assented. There was delay—horses had to be fetched from the warden’s stables—and by the time the carriage was announced ready, the roving party reappeared. With them was Mr. Grandcourt.

“Ah, there you are!” Lord Brackenshaw said to Gwendolen, who was arranging her mother’s shawl for the drive. “We thought at first you had alighted on Grandcourt and he had taken you home. Lush said so. But after that we met Grandcourt. However, we didn’t suppose you could be in any danger. The warden said he had told you a near way back.”

“You are going?” Grandcourt said, coming up with his usual air, as if he could not imagine any omission on his own part. Lord Brackenshaw gave place and moved away.

“Yes, we are going,” Gwendolen answered, looking busily at her scarf as she arranged it across her shoulders Scotch fashion.

“May I call at Offendene to-morrow?”

“Oh yes, if you like,” she said, sweeping him at a distance with her eyelashes. Her voice had the light sharpness of the first touch of frost.

Mrs. Davilow took his arm to be led to the carriage. But while that happened, Gwendolen, with incredible swiftness, got in advance of them and sprang into the carriage alone.

“I got in, mamma, because I wished to be on this side,” she said apologetically when her mother followed. But she had avoided Grandcourt’s touch. He only lifted his hat and walked away, with the not unsatisfactory impression that she meant to show herself offended by his neglect.

For five minutes mother and daughter drove in silence. Then Gwendolen spoke, her voice set hard as if she were pressing it into a mould.

“I intend to join the Langens at Dover, mamma. I shall pack up immediately on getting home, and set off by the early train. I shall be at Dover almost as soon as they are. We can let them know by telegraph.”

“Good heavens, child! what can be your reason for saying so?”

“My reason for saying it, mamma, is that I mean to do it.”

“But why do you mean to do it?”

“I wish to go away.”

“Is it because you are offended with Mr. Grandcourt’s odd behavior in walking off to-day?”

“It is useless to enter into such questions. I am not going in any case to marry Mr. Grandcourt. Don’t interest yourself further about it.”

“What can I say to your uncle, Gwendolen? Consider the position you place me in. You led him to believe only last night that you had made up your mind in favor of Mr. Grandcourt.”

“I am very sorry to cause you annoyance, mamma, dear, but I can’t help it,” Gwendolen said; and the tenderness of the words only made the resistance in her tone more stubborn. “Whatever you or my uncle may think or do, I shall not alter my resolve, and I shall not tell my reason. I don’t care what comes of it. I don’t care if I never marry any one. There is nothing worth caring for. I believe all men are bad, and I hate them.”

“But need you set off in this way, Gwendolen?” Mrs. Davilow pleaded, miserable and helpless.

“Now mamma, don’t interfere with me. If you have ever had any trouble in your own life, remember it and don’t interfere with me. If I am to be miserable, let it be by my own choice.”

Mrs. Davilow could only tremble into silence, feeling dimly that, whatever this trouble was, it might indeed be easier borne—less made a public spectacle—if Gwendolen went away.
