Somewhere in the dusty archives of Arthur Pendennis’s youth—tucked between an old Oxbridge waistcoat and a cravat that had seen better days—lay the manuscript of a novel. Not just any novel, mind you, but *the* novel. The kind of novel written by a young man who had just discovered three things:

1. German poetry.
2. That love hurts (especially when the object of affection prefers someone richer).
3. Ink is cheaper than therapy.

Titled *Leaves from the Life-book of Walter Lorraine*, it was, in Pen’s own later estimation, "a spectacular display of youthful despair, with bonus philosophical brooding." The hero, Walter, was a masterpiece of self-inflicted torment—a Byronic figure who scowled magnificently, sighed artistically, and suffered in iambic pentameter. Passages were underlined with the fervor of a man who believed his heartbreak was the first of its kind. Tears had once blotted the pages—though whether from genuine emotion or the strain of trying to rhyme "despair" with "fair" was now unclear.

Years later, when Pen rediscovered this relic of his literary growing pains, he did what any sensible man would: he laughed until his ribs ached. Then he read passages aloud to his long-suffering friend Warrington, who was busy trying to light his pipe with the fire-tongs.

"*False as thou art beautiful! Heartless as thou art fair!*" Warrington intoned, waving the manuscript like a bad actor in a melodrama. "*O Leonora! What evil spirit hath sent thee to torture me so?*"

"Good heavens," Pen groaned. "I wrote that after Lady Mirabel—then plain Miss Fotheringay—smiled at someone else at a ball. I believe I wore a black armband for a week in mourning for my own dignity."

Warrington, ever the critic, flicked ash onto the page. "It’s dreadful. Also, I suspect half of it’s plagiarized."

"*Inspired*," Pen corrected. "All artists borrow. The great ones steal outright."

"Then you, my friend, are a highwayman of the highest order."

Still, beneath the purple prose and adolescent angst, there were flickers of something worthwhile—a turn of phrase here, a genuine ache there. Warrington, despite himself, admitted it might be salvageable with ruthless editing and perhaps less use of the word "alas."

Thus began the great resurrection of *Walter Lorraine*. Pen trimmed, tweaked, and (reluctantly) deleted entire soliloquies about the cruelty of fate. Warrington contributed scathing commentary and the occasional helpful suggestion, like "burn it" and "start over."

News of Pen’s literary endeavors soon reached the ears of Bacon and Bungay, rival publishers whose enthusiasm for books was matched only by their enthusiasm for undercutting each other.

Bungay, a man who judged manuscripts by their weight, was initially skeptical until Warrington casually mentioned that Pen moved in *fashionable circles*. (This was technically true, if one counted being glared at by dowagers at parties as "circulating.")

Bacon, not to be outdone, arrived at their shabby chambers, tripped over a laundry bucket, and nearly impaled himself on a collapsing chair. "Charming," he muttered, eyeing the peeling wallpaper.

Warrington, ever the showman, gestured to a pile of society invitations on the table. "Pendennis dines with lords and ladies, yet deigns to share mutton with us. Such humility!"

Bacon, now convinced Pen was a literary genius in disguise, made increasingly extravagant offers. Bungay, hearing rumors of this, panicked and upped his bid. Pen, watching this dance of desperation, felt like a debutante at her first ball—flattered, bewildered, and mildly concerned he’d trip over his own feet.

The final sum Pen received for *Walter Lorraine* shall remain a mystery, lest every young scribbler in England abandon honest work for the fickle muse of fiction. Suffice it to say, it was enough to:

- Startle Major Pendennis, who muttered, "People *pay* for this?"
- Prompt Warrington to suggest a celebratory binge at the nearest tavern.
- Allow Pen to repay Laura—a gesture that moved her to tears and Dr. Portman to an impromptu kiss on Mrs. Pendennis’s hand (a scandalous moment everyone agreed never to mention again).

As for the novel itself? It sold. Not brilliantly, not disastrously, but enough to confirm two universal truths:

1. No one knows what the public will like.
2. If you must write a novel, try not to weep directly onto the manuscript. Blotting paper exists for a reason.

And so Pen, richer in coin and (marginally) humility, moved forward—still a fool, but now a *published* fool.

Which, in literary circles, is practically a virtue.
