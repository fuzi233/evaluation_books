Between what truly matters and what merely seems to, how can the world ever judge wisely? When Sigsbee Manderson’s cunning, relentless mind was shattered by a single shot from an unseen hand, the world lost nothing worth mourning—yet gained something unforgettable: a brutal lesson in the emptiness of wealth amassed without loyalty, without kindness, without a single deed that might earn lasting honor. He left no friend to weep for him, no legacy to stir memory beyond the cold tally of his fortune.

Yet when news of his death reached the centers of commerce, it felt as though the earth itself had trembled. In the flamboyant history of American finance, no figure had ever loomed so large over the trading world. Manderson occupied a unique place—a monument in the temple of money. There had been other financial titans before him, men who wielded capital like armies and reaped millions in return. But Manderson was different. Around him clung a strange, almost romantic aura—something akin to piracy, beloved by his countrymen—despite his image as the unshakable guardian of stability, the crusher of speculative crises, the rival of Wall Street’s raiding lords.

His wealth began with his grandfather, a lesser chieftain of the frontier financial wars, and passed through his father, a man of quiet discipline who lent money but never speculated. Manderson, born into affluence, should have embodied the new American plutocracy—rooted in tradition, shaped by inherited power. But he did not. While his upbringing gave him European tastes in elegance and restraint—quiet grandeur, luxury without ostentation—he also carried forward the spirit of the Forty-Niner, the financier-buccaneer of old.

In his youth, during what was called his “early bad manner,” Manderson was little more than a genius gambler—every man’s enemy, a prodigy of speculation whose mind outmatched all others. At St Helena, they said war was *une belle occupation*; for young Manderson, the Stock Exchange was his battlefield. The market’s chaos thrilled him, its intricate struggles a game of life and death.

Then came the change. At thirty, on the death of his father, something shifted within him. As if struck by revelation, he turned from gambling to steady work in his father’s banking firm. He silenced the noise of the Street, closed his ears to the clamor of speculation. Within years, he took full control of the great house of Manderson, Colefax & Company—once known for its conservative strength, now standing like a cliff above the turbulent sea of markets. Doubts about his past vanished. He became another man—sober, disciplined, commanding.

How this transformation happened remained a mystery. But there was talk of words spoken by his father—the only man Manderson truly respected, perhaps even loved.

Soon, Manderson rose above mere finance. His name echoed across global bourses. To speak of Manderson was to conjure an image of America’s solid, enduring wealth. He orchestrated vast mergers, unified industries across continents, financed state and private ventures with flawless judgment. Many times, when he stepped in to break a strike or unify labor under one ownership, he brought ruin to countless small homes. If miners or steelworkers defied him, he could be fiercer than they—lawless, ruthless—but always in pursuit of business ends. Thousands cursed his name, yet financiers and speculators alike feared him no more. He shaped the flow of wealth across the nation, coldly, efficiently, serving the American hunger for scale and power. A grateful public dubbed him the Colossus.

But few outside his inner circle knew of Manderson’s secret longing. Beneath the surface of calm authority, there lived a ghost of the gambler. A few trusted aides, former allies from his reckless days, understood: sometimes, the pirate stirred. It was as if Blackbeard had settled in Bristol, rich from plunder, only to flash his knife at sudden moments.

In those rare hours, Manderson would sketch bold raids on paper—plans to shake markets, to crush rivals, to seize advantage. But they were never executed. The pirate would retreat. He’d return to his desk, humming a tune—perhaps a verse of “Spanish Ladies”—and resume his role as the pillar of order.

Afterward, he’d casually mention to a colleague how a million could have been made if only he’d acted. “Seems tos me,” he’d say wistfully, “the Street’s getting dull since I quit.”

This hidden weakness slowly leaked into the financial world—and people delighted in it. They found comfort in knowing the Colossus had a human side.

Then came the news of his death.

Panic swept through the markets like a hurricane. It struck at the worst possible moment. Prices collapsed like towers in an earthquake. For two days, Wall Street roared with despair—pale faces, frantic voices, deals unraveling. Across America, where speculation held sway, ruin spread like plague. Suicides followed in waves. In Europe, too, lives ended—men who had never seen Manderson, yet whose futures were tied to his fate.

In Paris, a banker walked from the Bourse and fell dead on the steps, a crushed vial in his hand. In Frankfurt, another leapt from the cathedral, leaving a crimson stain on the red stone. Men stabbed themselves, shot themselves, hanged themselves, drank poison, breathed gas—as if the world had ended because one cold heart in England had stopped beating.

The timing couldn’t have been worse. Wall Street had already been trembling under the fallout from the arrest of Lucas Hahn and the exposure of his thefts from the Hahn banks. The market had been artificially inflated—overheated, ready to crash. Reports from farms were poor; railway earnings disappointed. Yet for a week, the “Manderson crowd” had held the line. Speculators, quick-witted and sentimental, believed the giant’s hand still guided them from afar.

The newspapers echoed: Manderson was in constant contact with his lieutenants. One claimed to reveal the exact cost of cables between New York and Marlstone—thousands of dollars spent in twenty-four hours. Another reported that Manderson had planned to return by the *Lusitania*, but changed his mind once he had things under control.

All lies—fabricated or encouraged by the very men who needed the myth. In truth, no word had come from Manderson. Howard B. Jeffrey, the real architect behind the market’s stabilization, had been working quietly, but the illusion helped keep panic at bay.

For four feverish days, tension eased. By Saturday, Jeffrey believed victory was near. The market stood firm, even rising slightly. Wall Street drifted into Sunday rest—exhausted, but hopeful.

Then, Monday morning. A rumor exploded across the financial district—like lightning, sudden and silent. It began somewhere—perhaps whispered over a phone line—alongside a rush order to sell. Within minutes, the stock list shuddered. The low hum of Broad Street’s curb market climbed into shrieking panic. From inside the Exchange, a drone of fear grew louder. Men ran in and out, hats off, breathless.

“Is it true?” everyone asked.

“It’s a lie,” others replied, shaking, “a trick by some short seller trying to cover.”

But then came confirmation: a catastrophic collapse of “Yankees” in London. Enough.

New York had four hours left. The strategy of relying on Manderson’s presence had backfired catastrophically. Jeffrey, listening at his private phone, heard the disaster unfold. He saw the financial world crumbling. The new Napoleon had lost Marengo.

Within half an hour, reports of Manderson’s body surfaced—accompanied by rumors of suicide. Before any newspaper reached Wall Street, the storm was already raging. Jeffrey and his team were swept away like leaves in a gale.

And yet—nothing had actually changed. The corn ripened under the sun. Rivers carried barges and powered engines. Flocks grazed, herds multiplied. Workers labored as always, bound in their usual chains. War rumbled in the distance, but slept. To nearly all of humanity, Manderson’s death meant nothing. Life went on.

Weeks before, strong hands had already controlled every wire in the network he oversaw. Before his body was buried, his countrymen discovered a startling truth: the existence of Sigsbee Manderson—this engine of monopoly—had not been essential to material prosperity after all.

The panic burned out in two days. Markets recovered. Bankrupts vanished. Order returned.

But just as the delirium faded, a scandal erupted in England—one that seized the attention of two continents. The next day, the Chicago Limited derailed. In New Orleans, a prominent politician was gunned down in cold blood by his sister-in-law. Within a week, “the Manderson story” was cold to editors across America.

The flood of American tourists through Europe swirled around memorials to men who died in poverty—yet none paused for the nation’s most famous millionaire. Like the poet who died young and poor in Rome a century before, Manderson was buried far from home. But while thousands visit Keats’s grave beneath Monte Testaccio, not one—nor ever will—stand in reverence beside the rich man’s tomb beside the little church of Marlstone.

The only comfortably furnished room in the offices of the *Record* was quiet except for the low hum of the city outside and the steady scratch of pens at desks. Then, without warning, the telephone on Sir James Molloy’s desk buzzed sharply.

Sir James didn’t look up. He merely raised a hand with his pen poised, and Mr. Silver, his secretary, immediately rose from his chair and crossed to the instrument.

“Who is that?” he asked, speaking into the receiver.

“Who?... I can’t hear you.... Oh, it’s Mr. Bunner, is it?... Yes, but—” A pause. “I know, but he’s dreadfully busy this afternoon. Can’t you—? Oh, really? Well, in that case—just hold on, will you?”

He placed the receiver gently before Sir James.

“It’s Calvin Bunner,” he said, matter-of-fact. “Sigsbee Manderson’s right-hand man. He insists on speaking to you personally. Says it’s the gravest news. He’s calling from the house down by Bishopsbridge, so you’ll need to speak clearly.”

Sir James glanced at the phone—not fondly—and picked up the receiver. His voice, when it came, was deep and commanding.

“Well?”

He listened.

Then: “Yes.”

A moment passed. Mr. Silver watched closely. And then—suddenly—Sir James’s face changed. The calm vanished. His eyes widened. His mouth opened slightly. A low murmur escaped him.

“Good God.”

He held the phone tightly, rising slowly to his feet, still listening intently. At intervals, he repeated, “Yes.” Once, he glanced at the clock, then turned sharply to Mr. Silver.

“Go and find Figgis and young Williams. Hurry.”

Mr. Silver darted out.

Sir James was a man of fifty, tall, strong, and sharp-featured, with a thick black mustache and dark, piercing eyes. An Irishman of immense energy and keen intelligence, he had built the *Record* into one of the most powerful morning papers in the country—and its evening counterpart, the *Sun*, across the street. He was managing director and editor-in-chief, known not just for his influence, but for his rare ability to see through pretense. He had no illusions, made no false claims, and trusted only solid merit. His staff respected him deeply, not because he demanded worship, but because he earned it through fairness, insight, and relentless drive.

Yet beneath the polished exterior, there was a darkness—a tension that showed in his brow when pressure mounted. But when relaxed, he was warm, generous, and utterly engaging.

Now, as he listened, his expression remained rigid. After a few minutes of questioning, he spoke again.

“You’re sure that’s all?... And how long has this been known?... Yes, of course, the police are informed—but the servants? Surely it’s all over the place by now.... Well, we’ll have a try.... Look here, Bunner—I’m infinitely grateful for this. You’ve done me a good turn. You know I mean it. Come see me the first day you get back to town.... All right, understood. Now I must act. Goodbye.”

He hung up.

Without hesitation, he grabbed a railway timetable from the rack and flipped through it. A word escaped him—sharp, decisive—as Mr. Silver returned, followed by two others: a hard-faced man with glasses, and a young reporter with bright, alert eyes.

“I want you to jot down some facts, Figgis,” Sir James said, banishing any trace of emotion. “As soon as you have them, shape them into a lead for a special edition of the *Sun*.”

Figgis nodded, pulled out a notebook, and sat at the writing table. The clock read three minutes past three.

“Silver,” Sir James continued, “go tell Jones to wire our local correspondent urgently. Tell him to drop everything and get to Marlstone at once. No explanation in the telegram. Not a word about this until the *Sun* hits the streets—you understand. Williams, cross the street and tell Mr. Anthony to prepare for a two-column front-page story that’ll knock the town endways. Tell him to take every precaution for a scoop. Say Figgis will be over in five minutes with the facts—and that he should let Figgish write it in his private room. On your way, ask Miss Morgan to come here at once. And tell the switchboard to try to reach Mr. Trent on the wire. After seeing Anthony, return here and stand by.”

The youth vanished like a shadow.

Sir James turned to Figgis, who had already begun scribbling shorthand.

“Sigsbee Manderson has been murdered,” he began, pacing slowly with hands behind his back. “He and his wife and two secretaries have been staying at White Gables, near Bishopsbridge, for the past fortnight. He bought the place four years ago. They spend part of each summer there. Last night, he went to bed around half-past eleven—routine. No one knows when he left. He wasn’t missed until this morning. About ten o’clock, a gardener found the body lying beside a shed in the grounds. Shot through the left eye—death instantaneous. No robbery. But there were marks on the wrists—signs of a struggle. Dr. Stock of Marlstone has been called. He’ll conduct the post-mortem. The police from Bishopsbridge arrived quickly but are tight-lipped. They admit they have no clue to the killer’s identity. That’s it, Figgis. Mr. Anthony’s waiting. I’ll call him now.”

Figgis looked up.

“One of Scotland Yard’s best detectives is already on the case,” he offered. “That’s a safe statement.”

“If you like,” Sir James replied.

“And Mrs. Manderson?” Figgis asked. “Was she there?”

“Yes. What about her?”

“Prostrated by shock,” suggested the reporter, “and refuses to see anyone. Human interest.”

“I wouldn’t put that in,” said a quiet voice.

It was Miss Morgan—pale, composed, graceful—who had entered silently during the dictation.

“I’ve seen Mrs. Manderson,” she said, turning to Sir James. “She looks perfectly healthy, quite together. If her husband has been murdered, I doubt the shock would prostrate her. More likely, she’s helping the police.”

“Something in your own style, then, Miss Morgan,” Sir James said, smiling faintly. Her calm efficiency was legendary in the office.

“Cut it out, Figgis. Off you go.”

He turned to her. “Madam, I expect you know what I need.”

“Our Manderson biography is up to date,” she replied, lowering her lashes thoughtfully. “I reviewed it recently. It’s practically ready for tomorrow. I’d suggest the *Sun* use their old sketch from two years ago—the one about his Berlin mission and the potash settlement. It was well-written, and they won’t have room for much more. As for us—we’ve got plenty of cuttings, mostly junk. The sub-editors can sort through them as they come in. We also have two excellent portraits—one a drawing by Mr. Trent, made when they were both on the same ship. It’s superior to any photograph, though I suppose the public prefers a bad photo to a good drawing. I’ll send both down immediately. You can choose. As far as I can see, the *Record* is ahead of the game—except we can’t get a special man down in time for tomorrow’s paper.”

Sir James sighed.

“What are we good for, anyhow?” he asked Mr. Silver, who had resumed his seat.

“She even knows Bradshaw by heart,” he muttered.

Miss Morgan adjusted her cuffs with serene patience.

“Is there anything else?” she asked, as the telephone rang.

“Yes, one thing,” Sir James said, picking up the receiver.

“I want you to make a terrible mistake someday, Miss Morgan—an everlasting bloomer—just to bring us down to earth.”

She gave the faintest hint of a smile before leaving.

“Anthony?” Sir James asked, and plunged into immediate consultation with the editor across the street.

He rarely visited the *Sun* building. He claimed the atmosphere of an evening paper was too chaotic for his taste. Mr. Anthony, the Murat of Fleet Street, would say the same of a morning paper—though he thrived on chaos.

Five minutes later, a uniformed boy entered.

“Mr. Trent is on the line.”

Sir James ended his conversation abruptly.

“Put him through at once.”

He waited a moment, then said:

“Hullo!”

A voice answered: “Hullo be blowed! What do you want?”

“This is Molloy.”

“I know it is,” the voice replied. “This is Trent. I’m in the middle of painting—a critical moment. I hope it’s important.”

“It is,” Sir James said firmly. “I want you to do some work for us.”

“Some play, you mean,” Trent retorted. “Believe me, I don’t want a holiday. The working fit is strong. I’m doing something decent. Why can’t you leave a man alone?”

“Something very serious has happened.”

“What?”

“Sigsbee Manderson has been murdered—shot through the brain. Body found this morning at his estate near Bishopsbridge. They don’t know who did it.”

Sir James relayed the facts briefly and clearly—the same ones he’d given Figgis.

“What do you think of it?” he concluded.

A long silence. Then a grunt.

“Come now,” Sir James pressed. “Tempter!”

“You’ll go down?”

A pause.

“Are you there?” Sir James asked.

“Look here, Molloy,” Trent snapped, “this might be a case for me—or it might not. We can’t tell yet. It could be a mystery—or as simple as bread and cheese. The body wasn’t robbed—that’s interesting. But maybe he caught some tramp sleeping in the grounds and tried to throw him out. That kind of thing would suit him. Such a man might know better than to steal—leave the valuables behind. Honestly, I wouldn’t help hang a poor devil who took daylight into a man like Sig Manderson as a protest.”

Sir James smiled into the phone—a smile of triumph.

“Come, my boy,” he said. “You’re getting feeble. Admit you want to go and look at it. You know you do. If it’s not your kind of case, you’re free to walk away. By the way—where are you?”

“I am blown along a wandering wind,” Trent replied, sounding half-exasperated, half-poetic. “And hollow, hollow, hollow—all delight.”

“Can you get here within an hour?”

“I suppose I can,” he grumbled. “How much time have I?”

“Good man. There’s time enough—that’s just the worst of it. I’ll depend on our local man tonight. The best train left half an hour ago. The next is slow—leaving Paddington at midnight. You could take the Buster”—Sir James referred to his fast motor car—“but you wouldn’t get down in time to do anything tonight.”

“And I’d miss my sleep. No, thanks. Train for me. I’m quite fond of railway travel, you know. I have a gift for it. I am the stoker and the stoked. I am the song the porter sings.”

“What’s that you say?”

“It doesn’t matter,” Trent said sadly. “Say—will your people find a hotel near the scene and telegraph for a room?”

“At once,” Sir James replied. “Come here as soon as you can.”

He replaced the receiver.

As he turned back to his papers, a sudden, shrill cry erupted below.

He walked to the open window.

A mob of excited boys poured from the *Sun* building, racing down the narrow street toward Fleet Street. Each carried a bundle of newspapers and a large broadsheet bearing a single headline:

Sir James smiled and rattled the coins in his pocket.

“It makes a good bill,” he observed to Mr. Silver, standing beside him.

Such was Manderson’s epitaph.

At around eight in the morning, Mr. Nathaniel Burton Cupples stood on the veranda of the Marlstone hotel, lost in thought—not about the day ahead, but about breakfast. For him, the word carried literal weight: he considered every meal with the same care and precision he applied to all deliberate actions. The previous day’s chaos—following the discovery of a dead man—had unsettled his appetite, leaving him undernourished. Now, after an hour awake and active, he was ravenous. He resolved to allow himself an extra slice of toast and a second egg, keeping everything else as usual. The rest would be made up at lunch—a matter to be addressed later.

With that decision settled, he turned his attention to the view. The rugged coastline stretched before him, where a towering sea stack rose from the glassy water. Beyond it, rolling pastures, cultivated fields, and woodlands climbed gently toward the distant moor. It was a landscape of quiet grandeur, and Mr. Cupples, a connoisseur of such scenes, took pleasure in its order and beauty.

He was a man of average height, slender build, and nearing sixty. Though frail by nature, he remained alert and energetic. His thin beard and mustache were sparse, framing a gentle mouth and sharp, kind eyes. His narrow jaw and prominent nose gave him a clerical appearance, reinforced by his plain dark clothes and soft black hat. In sum, he looked like a man of the cloth—though he was not one. Rather, he was a retired banker, widower, and member of the London Positivist Society, known for his meticulous mind, moral seriousness, and deep, if dry, intellect. His life had been spent among books and museums, where his encyclopedic knowledge of obscure subjects earned him respect among scholars and curators. He read Montaigne often, finding solace in his reflective essays. He possessed a rare blend of kindness and innocent joy, unmarred by irony or cynicism.

Just as Mr. Cupples finished his meal at a small table on the veranda, a large motorcar pulled into the hotel drive. “Who is that?” he asked the waiter.

“Id is der manager,” the young man replied listlessly. “He have been to meed a gendleman by der train.”

The car stopped. The porter hurried forward. Mr. Cupples let out a surprised exclamation as a tall, loosely built man—much younger than himself—stepped out and strode onto the veranda, tossing his hat onto a chair. His high-cheekboned face wore a warm smile; his tweed clothes, hair, and short mustache were slightly disheveled.

“Cupples, by all that’s miraculous!” the man exclaimed, lunging forward before Mr. Cupples could rise, seizing his hand in a firm grip. “My luck is serving me today. This is the second slice within an hour. How are you, my best of friends? And why are you here? Why sit’st thou by that ruined breakfast? Dost thou recall its former pride, or ponder how it passed away? I _am_ glad to see you!”

“I was half expecting you, Trent,” Mr. Cupples replied, smiling broadly. “You look splendid, my dear fellow. I’ll tell you all about it. But you haven’t eaten yet. Will you join me here?”

“Rather!” said the newcomer. “An enormous breakfast—with refined conversation and tears of recognition never dry. Ask young Siegfried to lay a place while I go wash. Won’t take three minutes.” He vanished into the hotel, and Mr. Cupples, after a moment’s hesitation, walked to the telephone in the porter’s office.

When he returned, Trent was already seated, pouring tea, clearly engaged in selecting his food with genuine interest. “I expect this to be a hard day,” he said, speaking in his characteristic jerky rhythm. “I shan’t eat again until evening. You guess why I’m here?”

“Undoubtedly,” said Mr. Cupples. “You’ve come to write about the murder.”

“That’s rather dull phrasing,” Trent replied, slicing into a sole. “I’d say I’ve come as avenger of blood—to hunt down the guilty and vindicate society’s honor. That’s my line. Families waited on at their private residences. Say, Cupples, I’ve made a good start already. Wait a bit—I’ll tell you.”

There was a pause. Trent ate swiftly, distractedly, while Mr. Cupples watched, pleased.

“Your manager,” Trent began, “is a remarkable judge of character. He admires me. Knows more about my best cases than I do. The _Record_ wired last night that I was coming. When I stepped off the train at seven, there he was, waiting with a car the size of a haystack. He’s beside himself with joy. It’s fame.”

He sipped tea. “His first words? ‘Would you like to see the body?’ He thought he could arrange it. The corpse lies in Dr. Stock’s surgery in the village, just as it was found. They’re doing the post-mortem today—so I arrived just in time. He drove me straight there, filling me in along the way. By the time we got there, I was pretty well informed. I suppose the manager has influence with the doctor. Anyway, no objections from him—or the constable, though he insisted I not expose him in print.”

“I saw the body before it was moved,” Mr. Cupples remarked. “Nothing remarkable, except that the bullet wound in the eye barely marred the face and caused little bleeding. The wrists were scratched and bruised. With your trained eye, I imagine you noticed other details.”

“Other details, yes—but they don’t suggest much. Just oddities. Take the wrists. How did you see the bruises?”

Mr. Cupples paused. “Now that you mention it—I remember Manderson was wearing stiff cuffs, covering his hands when I saw him earlier.”

“He always did,” Trent said. “The manager confirmed it. I pointed out to him what you missed: no cuffs were visible. They’d been pulled up inside the sleeves—like someone hurrying into a coat without adjusting them. That’s why you saw the wrists.”

“Well, that’s suggestive,” Mr. Cupples observed mildly. “Perhaps he rushed through dressing.”

“But did he?” Trent countered. “The manager said exactly what you did—‘He was always a bit of a swell.’ Then he added: ‘Look at his shoes. Mr. Manderson was particular about footwear. But those laces were tied in a hurry.’ I agreed. ‘And he left his false teeth in his room,’ he said. ‘Doesn’t that prove he was flustered?’ I allowed it looked that way. But then I asked: ‘If he was so pressed, why part his hair so carefully? That parting is a work of art. Why wear so much? He had full underclothes, studs, suspenders, watch chain, keys—everything. What’s the logic?’ The manager couldn’t explain. Can you?”

Mr. Cupples reflected. “Maybe he was only hurried at the end—coat and shoes last.”

“But not false teeth,” Trent said. “Ask anyone who wears them. And he didn’t wash at all—unusual for a neat man. That suggests haste from the start. Then there’s this: one waistcoat pocket was lined for his gold watch. But he put the watch in the other. Anyone with habits knows how strange that is. So—signs of panic, signs of calm. For now, I’m not guessing. I need to reconnoiter. If I can get on the right side of the household.”

Trent resumed eating, thoughtful.

Mr. Cupples smiled. “That’s precisely where I can help.”

Trent looked up, surprised. “You told me you half expected me. Go on.”

“Mrs. Manderson,” Mr. Cupples said, “is my niece.”

“What!” Trent dropped knife and fork with a clatter. “Cupples, you’re joking.”

“I’m serious,” Mr. Cupples replied earnestly. “Her father, John Peter Domecq, was my wife’s brother. I’ve never mentioned her marriage before. It’s been painful. But to return—last night, when I was at the house—by the way, you can see it from here. You passed it in the car.” He pointed to a red-roofed house among poplars, three hundred yards away, isolated from the village below.

“I did,” Trent said. “The manager told me about it on the way from Bishopsbridge.”

“Others here know of you,” Mr. Cupples continued. “Last night, Mr. Bunner—one of Manderson’s secretaries—hoped the _Record_ would send you. He mentioned your past cases. Mabel—my niece—was interested when I told her. She’s holding up remarkably well. She remembers reading your articles on the Abinger case. She hates the press involvement and begged me to keep journalists away. But she said you seemed capable—so if you came, she wouldn’t block your inquiries.”

Trent leaned across the table and shook his hand silently. Mr. Cupples, delighted, went on.

“I called her just now. She’s glad you’re here. She asks me to say you may ask any questions, and the house and grounds are yours. She prefers not to see you—she’s staying in her sitting room. A detective already questioned her, and she feels unable to endure more. She says she can’t offer anything useful. The two secretaries and Martin, the butler—an intelligent man—can answer your questions.”

Trent finished his breakfast, brow furrowed. He lit a pipe slowly and sat on the veranda rail. “Cupples,” he said quietly, “is there something about this you know—and would rather not tell me?”

Mr. Cupples started, staring. “What do you mean?”

“I mean about the Mandersons. Here’s a man murdered violently, and no one seems to care. The manager spoke of him as coolly as if he’d never met him—though they’ve been neighbors for years. You talk about it coldly. Mrs. Manderson—well, I’ve heard of women more devastated by their husband’s death. Is there something wrong here? Was Manderson… peculiar? I traveled with him once, but never spoke. I only know his public image—repulsive enough. This might matter. That’s why I ask.”

Mr. Cupples paused. He touched his beard, gazed at the sea. Finally, he turned.

“I see no reason not to tell you—between us. But this must never be mentioned. The truth is, no one really liked Manderson. Those closest to him disliked him most.”

“Why?”

“Most couldn’t explain. I tried to understand my own feelings. I sensed a total absence of empathy. He wasn’t rude, vicious, or dull—he could be fascinating. But I felt he’d sacrifice anyone for his goals, for imposing his will. Perhaps I’m imagining it. But Mabel—my niece—was deeply unhappy. I’m nearly twice your age, though you make me feel younger. I’ve heard many marital troubles, but none like hers. I’ve known her since childhood. I know—without exaggeration—that she is one of the most amiable, honorable women alive. But Manderson had made her miserable.”

“What did he do?”

“She said he nursed a perpetual grievance. Kept distance. Said nothing. I don’t know how it started. All she’d say was he had no cause. She knew what was in his mind, but she’s proud. This went on for months. Then, a week ago, she wrote to me. I’m her only close relative. Her mother died young. After John Peter passed, I was like a father to her—until her marriage five years ago. She asked me to come. I came at once. That’s why I’m here.”

Mr. Cupples paused, sipping tea. Trent smoked, watching the June landscape.

“I wouldn’t go to White Gables,” Mr. Cupples continued. “You know my views on capitalism, labor, and power. You know the abuses Manderson committed—especially in the Pennsylvania coal-fields three years ago. I saw him not just as a man I disliked, but as a criminal, a disgrace. I came here instead. I saw Mabel. She told me what I’ve said. The strain, the humiliation, the pretense—she was breaking. She asked for advice. I said she should confront him. But she refused. She’d always ignored his change, and nothing would make her admit injury once pride set in. Life is full of stubborn silences,” he sighed, “and cultivated misunderstandings.”

“Did she love him?” Trent asked sharply.

Mr. Cupples hesitated. “Had she any love left?”

“She didn’t,” he said slowly. “But don’t misjudge her. No force on earth would make her admit it—even to herself—while she felt bound to him. And apart from this recent coldness, he’d always been considerate, generous.”

“You said she refused to speak to him.”

“She did. And I knew from experience—Domecqs don’t budge when dignity’s involved. So I thought carefully. Next day, I met Manderson passing the hotel. I asked for a few minutes. He stepped inside the gate. We hadn’t spoken since her marriage, but he remembered me. I told him what Mabel had confided. I said I wouldn’t approve or condemn her bringing me in—but she was suffering, and I had a right to know how he justified placing her in such pain.”

“And how did he react?” Trent asked, smiling faintly at the scene.

“Not well. Not at all. He said, ‘See here, Cupples, you don’t want to butt in. My wife can look after herself. I’ve found that out, along with other things.’ He stayed calm—though I saw a light in his eyes that would scare a guilty man. But I was furious—his tone, his words—so I snapped back. I reminded him the law protects wives from unbearable treatment. I brought up his public record, said men like him weren’t fit to live. I said these things—foolish things—under the eyes of half a dozen people on this very veranda. I noticed them watching me as I walked back.”

“And Manderson?”

“Not a word. He listened, eyes fixed on mine. When I stopped, he smiled slightly and walked away.”

“When was this?”

“The Sunday morning.”

“So you never saw him alive again?”

“No. Or—once. Later that day, on the golf course. I didn’t speak. And next morning, he was found dead.”

They sat in silence. Guests returning from the sea chattered nearby. The waiter approached. Mr. Cupples rose, took Trent’s arm, and led him to the long tennis lawn beside the hotel.

“I have a reason for telling you all this,” he began.

“Trust you for that,” Trent replied, refilling his pipe. He lit it, smoked, then said, “Let me guess your reason—if you like.”

Mr. Cupples’s solemn face softened into a slight smile. He said nothing.

“You thought,” Trent mused, “that I might discover the depth of the conflict between the Mandersons. You feared I’d suspect Mrs. Manderson—let my imagination run wild. So you told me the truth, to prevent speculation, and to affirm your trust in her. Is that it?”

Mr. Cupples nodded.

Philip Trent, painter and son of a painter, had by his early thirties earned a name for himself in the world of English art. His work sold—solidly, steadily—and not just because of family connections or inherited wealth, though both helped. What truly set him apart was a rare combination: an original, forceful talent, matched with a disciplined yet passionate approach to creation. He painted not in bursts of frenzy, but in long, quiet stretches of concentration, punctuated by sudden surges of inspiration. More than that, he had a gift—unconscious, perhaps—for making people like him. He was good-humored, quick-witted, and genuinely interested in others. People felt at ease around him, never self-conscious, never under scrutiny. Whether laughing through absurdity or deep in thought, his face retained a spark of alertness, as if life itself were always a fascinating puzzle.

His education extended beyond painting. He read widely, though without pretension, and poetry was his true passion. At thirty-two, he still lived with the energy of youth, unburdened by routine, unafraid of adventure.

It was only by chance that he stepped into another world—one where logic and imagination became tools of detection.

One day, flipping through a newspaper, he came across a report of a murder committed on a train—a rare event in England. The case was baffling; two men were held under suspicion. Intrigued, Trent began reading every account he could find. He found himself reconstructing the facts in his mind, rearranging them, testing possibilities. A strange excitement took hold—something akin to the thrill of artistic creation, or of a daring escapade. That evening, he wrote a long letter to the editor of *The Record*, chosen simply because it had offered the most thorough coverage.

In it, he did what Edgar Allan Poe once did in the Mary Rogers case: he pointed out overlooked details, reorganized the evidence, and cast doubt on a man who had presented himself as a witness. Sir James Molloy, the paper’s editor, printed the letter in bold type. Later that same night, the *Sun* announced the arrest and confession of the man Trent had named.

Sir James, well-connected in London society, quickly sought out Trent. The two hit it off immediately—Trent possessed a natural charm that erased differences in age and status. He even painted a scene of the *Record*'s printing presses, which Sir James bought sight unseen, calling it a “machinery-scape” in the style of Heinrich Kley.

Then came the Ilkley mystery. Sir James invited Trent to dinner, then offered him a staggering sum to serve as the paper’s special correspondent. “You can write,” he said. “You know how to talk to people. And you’ve got a mind for puzzles. Think how it would feel if you cracked it.”

Trent hesitated. It was unfamiliar territory. But fear had long been something he fought against—not with courage, but with habit. He accepted.

He cracked it. Again, he gave the authorities a surprise, and again, his name spread across the country. Afterward, he returned to painting. He had no desire for journalism. Sir James, respectful of art and artists, left him alone. Yet over the years, he’d called upon Trent nearly thirty times—sometimes to solve mysteries abroad, sometimes in England. Sometimes Trent declined; sometimes the truth was already uncovered. But each time, his involvement added to his growing fame.

And now, walking briskly up the hill toward White Gables, Trent mused that the Manderson case might be simpler than it seemed. Cupples, the hotel manager, was wise—but likely biased when it came to his niece. Still, the man had spoken passionately of her beauty, but even more so of her character. “There isn’t a child here who doesn’t brighten at the sound of her voice,” he’d said. “Not a grown-up either. Everyone looked forward to her coming in summer. She’s not just kind—she’s got backbone. Pluck. Any amount of go.” And then, quietly: “Nobody in Marlstone isn’t sorry for her—though some may think she’s lucky, after all.”

Trent wanted to meet her.

Ahead, through a wide lawn and shrubbery, stood the house: a modern two-story structure of dull-red brick, its twin gables giving it its name. Ten years old, perhaps. Well-maintained, peaceful, serene. Behind it stretched woodlands and valleys; before it, meadowland sloped down to cliffs. It was hard to imagine violence here—so orderly, so rich in quiet dignity. Yet there, near the garden hedge and the gardener’s toolshed, the body had been found—tumbled against the wooden wall.

Trent walked past the gate, along the road until he reached the shed. Forty yards farther, the road turned sharply into thick plantations. At the corner, a small white gate marked the boundary. He approached it, opened it gently, and stepped onto a path between a high hedge and a wall of rhododendrons. Through a gap, he saw the wooden shed nestled among trees, facing a corner of the house.

He examined the area carefully—searching the ground, crouching low, fingers probing, eyes scanning. But nothing remained except the flattened grass where the body had lain. The search yielded nothing.

Then came a sound—the first from the house: the click of a front door closing.

Trent straightened, stepping to the edge of the drive. A man emerged from the house, walking quickly toward the main gate.

At the crunch of gravel, the man turned sharply, eyes wide. His face was pale, drawn—yet young. No wrinkles marred the hollows beneath his blue eyes, though they spoke of exhaustion, of sleepless nights. He was broad-shouldered, lithe, strong—his carriage stiff with fatigue, but his bearing unmistakable. His short, smooth blond hair, regular features, and calm voice betrayed a certain discipline. Trent smiled inwardly. “Oxford was your playground, I think,” he murmured.

“If you’re Mr. Trent,” the young man said, “you’re expected. Mr. Cupples called from the hotel. My name is Marlowe.”

“You were secretary to Mr. Manderson?” Trent asked.

“Yes,” Marlowe replied. “I’ve been driving all night and most of yesterday. Didn’t sleep after hearing the news. But I have an appointment now—down at the doctor’s. The inquest will probably be tomorrow. If you’ll go up to the house and ask for Mr. Bunner, he’ll expect you. He’s the other secretary—an American, a fine fellow. He’ll look after you. There’s a detective here too—Inspector Murch, from Scotland Yard. Came yesterday.”

“Murch!” Trent exclaimed. “But we’re old friends. How on earth did he get here so fast?”

Marlowe shrugged. “No idea. But he was here last night, before I returned from Southampton. Interviewing everyone. He’s been here since eight this morning. He’s in the library—by the open French window at the end of the house. You might want to speak with him.”

“I think I will,” Trent said. Marlowe nodded and continued on his way.

Trent moved silently across the thick turf, his footsteps as quiet as a cat’s. In moments, he was peering through the open leaves of the south-facing window. Inside, a large man bent over papers on a table—back broad, head bent, grizzled hair cut short.

“’Twas ever thus,” Trent said softly. “From childhood’s hour I’ve seen my fondest hopes decay. I thought I’d beat Scotland Yard this time—only to find the biggest officer in the entire Metropolitan force already in position.”

The man turned abruptly.

The inspector smiled grimly and stepped to the window. “I was expecting you, Mr. Trent. This is the sort of case you enjoy.”

“Since my tastes are being considered,” Trent replied, stepping inside, “I wish they’d kept my hated rival out of it. You’ve got a head start—I know all about it.” His eyes scanned the room. “How did you manage? You’re fast, I know—but how did you get here in time to be working last night? Did Scotland Yard start an aviation corps? Or are you in league with the devil? Either way, the Home Secretary should make a statement.”

“It’s simpler,” Murch said, matter-of-fact. “I was on leave with my wife at Havley—twelve miles down the coast. As soon as word came, I wired the Chief and was put in charge. I bicycled over last night. Been at it ever since.”

“Ah,” Trent said, distracted. “And how is Mrs. Inspector Murch?”

“Never better,” Murch replied. “She often speaks of you and the games you used to play with our kids. But I must say, Mr. Trent, you don’t need to talk nonsense while using your eyes. I know your ways by now. I understand you’ve secured permission to investigate. You’re going to cut me out again, aren’t you? I owe you one for beating me on the Abinger case. But if you’re not in the mood for pleasantries, let’s skip the compliments and get to business.”

Trent moved to the table, glanced over the papers, then opened the roll-top desk. He checked the drawers swiftly.

“Cleared out,” he noted. “Well then, inspector—let’s play the game as usual.”

Trent had met Inspector Murch many times before. He was a quiet, shrewd man—highly respected in the Criminal Investigation Department. Courageous, methodical, with a history of dealing with dangerous criminals. Broad in frame and broader in humanity. Their relationship was unusual: a mutual respect born of rivalry and shared curiosity. They had developed a kind of unspoken code—what Trent called “detective sportsmanship.” Neither used information gained from the other for journalistic purposes. Each reserved the right to withhold key insights. Yet they discussed cases freely, often learning from one another. Victory alternated between them—sometimes Murch’s experience, sometimes Trent’s intuition.

Now, leaning beside the French window, they reviewed the case.

Trent pulled out a thin notebook and began sketching a rough plan of the room—a habit he cultivated. It was usually idle, but occasionally useful.

This was a large, light room at the corner of the house. Two walls had generous windows. A broad table stood in the center. To the left, near the entrance, was the roll-top desk. The inner door was at the far end of the left wall, opposite a wide casement window. A carved corner cupboard rose high against the wall beyond the door. Another cupboard filled a recess beside the fireplace. Colored prints by Harunobu hung on the remaining walls. Books lined the shelves—bound in sober elegance, arranged like soldiers in formation. Most hadn’t been touched. Furniture was sparse: carved oak chairs, a modern armchair, a swivel office chair. The room was expensive but cold. The only movable items: a porcelain bowl of vivid blue on the table, a clock and cigar boxes on the mantel, and a telephone stand on the desk.

“Seen the body?” Murch asked.

Trent nodded. “And the spot where it lay.”

“The case puzzles me,” Murch said. “At first, I thought it might be a tramp—common robbery, though rare here. But no signs of burglary. No theft. The body wasn’t robbed. If it weren’t for a few facts, it’d look like suicide.”

“And why isn’t it suicide?” Trent asked, sitting on the window threshold, knees clasped.

“For one thing—no weapon. We’ve searched everywhere. Nothing within a stone’s throw. Second—the wrists. Fresh scratches, bruises—clear signs of a struggle. Third—who shoots themselves in the eye? And then there’s this: Manderson dressed fully before going out. But he forgot his false teeth. How does a man planning suicide, dressing neatly for his own corpse, forget such a thing?”

“That hadn’t occurred to me,” Murch admitted. “But it has weight. Still, I’m not considering suicide. I’ve been looking around the house. I expect you’re doing the same.”

“I am,” Trent said. “This is a case for ideas. Let’s begin with suspicion. Let’s suspect everyone. I suspect Mrs. Manderson, of course. Both secretaries—there are two, and I can’t decide which is more suspicious. The butler, the lady’s maid, the other servants—especially the boot-boy. By the way—what domestics do you have?”

“All very well to laugh,” Murch replied. “But at this stage, suspicion is the only safe principle. As for the staff—there’s the butler, lady’s maid, cook, three maids (one young), one chauffeur—away with a broken wrist. No boy.”

“What about the gardener?” Trent asked. “You’re keeping him hidden. Play the game—out with him—or I’ll report you to the Rules Committee.”

“The garden is cared for by a man from the village—he comes twice a week. I spoke to him. Last visit was Friday.”

“Then I suspect him all the more,” Trent said. “Now—about the house. I’ll start with Manderson’s room and bedroom. But since we’re here—what have you found?”

Murch nodded. “I’ve been through both bedrooms. Nothing significant. Manderson’s room is bare—simple, almost like a cell. No valet. Clothes and shoes—nothing unusual. Exactly as he left it, they say. Opens into Mrs. Manderson’s room—which is anything but a cell. She likes pretty things. But she cleared out after the discovery—said she couldn’t sleep in a room opening into her husband’s. Understandable. She’s staying in a spare bedroom now.”

Trent scribbled notes. “Have you got your eye on her?” he muttered. “That tone—you’re either hiding something, or you’re sure she’s innocent. Either way, it’s part of the game. And the game is getting interesting.”

To Murch, he said aloud: “I’ll draw the bedroom later. What about this?”

There are moments in life—few, but unforgettable—when the quiet machinery within us suddenly whispers a truth we cannot explain. A sense of certainty, unbidden and profound, rises like a bird from hidden heather: not the reckless assurance of a gambler, nor the blind optimism of the hopeful, but a deep, wordless knowing that something important is about to unfold. The general feels it at dawn, sensing victory; the golfer knows, before the putt is struck, that the green will yield. As Trent climbed the stairs outside the library door, such a feeling washed over him—a clear, unwavering conviction that the mystery was nearing its end. His mind swirled with guesses and half-formed theories, observations too fragmented to fit into any coherent picture. Yet, as he ascended, he felt certain that light would soon break through.

The bedrooms lined a wide, carpeted corridor, lit by a tall window at one end. It ran the full length of the house, turning sharply into a narrower passage where the servants’ rooms opened. Martin’s room was an exception—it branched off from a small landing halfway up. As Trent passed, he glanced inside. A modest, tidy space, nothing remarkable. He continued upward, stepping carefully, his body close to the wall, each footfall measured and soft. Still, the old floor groaned beneath him, betraying his presence with a series of audible creaks.

He knew Manderson’s room was the first on the right when he reached the bedroom floor. He approached it immediately, testing the latch and lock—both functioning normally—and examined the key’s wards. Then he turned to the room itself.

It was small, oddly bare. The man’s toiletries were minimal, almost austere. Everything remained exactly as it had been the morning the body was found in the grounds. The bed was unmade, sheets and blankets tangled over a narrow wooden frame. Sunlight streamed through the window, illuminating the mess. On a plain table beside the bed sat a shallow glass bowl filled with water—inside, the delicate metalwork of dentures gleamed. A wrought-iron candlestick stood nearby. Clothes lay carelessly draped over one of two rush-bottomed chairs. On the chest of drawers, used as a dressing table, objects were scattered haphazardly—evidence of haste. Trent scanned them with a keen eye. He noted that the occupant had neither washed nor shaved. With a fingertip, he lifted the dental plate from the bowl, frowning at its inexplicable presence.

The emptiness of the room, drenched in sunlight, stirred a sense of unease in Trent. His imagination conjured a vision: a pale, anxious man rising before dawn, dressing in silence, eyes darting toward the inner door where his wife slept, consumed by dread.

Trent shivered. To steady himself, he opened the two tall wall cupboards flanking the bed. They held clothing—plenty of it, clearly a necessity for comfort. And shoes. An extraordinary collection of polished footwear lined long low shelves against the wall. No boots among them. Trent, a connoisseur of fine leather, studied them with interest. The shoes were narrow, round-toed, impeccably crafted—all from the same last, suggesting a man who took pride in his well-shaped feet.

Then his gaze fixed on a pair of patent-leather shoes on the upper shelf.

These were the ones the inspector had described—the ones Manderson wore the night before his death. They were worn, but recently polished. Something about their uppers caught Trent’s attention. He leaned closer, comparing them to the others. Then he picked them up, examining the seam where the upper met the sole.

As he did so, a faint, precise melody began to hum from his lips—an air Inspector Murch, if he’d been there, would have recognized instantly.

Many men who master self-control have a tell—a small, involuntary habit that reveals suppressed emotion. Murch had once observed that when Trent sensed a breakthrough, he whistled softly, almost imperceptibly, a tune from Mendelssohn’s *Lied ohne Worte* in A Major.

Trent turned the shoes over, measured them with a marked tape, and scrutinized the soles. In the angle between heel and instep, he detected a faint trace of red gravel.

He placed the shoes back on the floor and walked to the window, hands behind his back, still whistling faintly. He stared out, though his eyes saw nothing. Once, his lips parted, releasing the Englishman’s classic expletive of sudden insight. After a moment, he returned to the shelves and examined every shoe with swift, careful precision.

Next, he collected the clothes from the chair, inspected them thoroughly, and set them down again. He searched the wardrobes, then revisited the cluttered dressing table. Finally, he sat on the empty chair, head in hands, staring at the carpet. Minutes passed. Then he rose, opened the inner door leading to Mrs. Manderson’s room.

At once, it was clear the room had been hastily cleared. The usual accoutrements of a woman’s boudoir—dressing table, hats, bags, ribbons, gloves—were gone. The bed, chairs, tables—nothing bore the signs of daily use. It looked like a guest chamber left vacant. Yet every detail spoke of refined taste—unconventional, yes, but meticulous. Trent, trained in aesthetics, noted the harmony of color and form. He realized this woman, however ill-matched her husband, possessed artistic sensibility. His curiosity deepened. He thought of the burdens she carried, and the unfolding story now taking shape in his mind.

He went to the tall French window facing the door, opened it, and stepped onto a small iron-railed balcony. Below stretched a broad lawn, beginning just beneath the house, separated only by a narrow flowerbed. It sloped gently toward the orchard. Another window opened above the library’s garden entrance. In the far corner, a second door led to the passage—the one the maid used in the mornings, the one Mrs. Manderson might take when leaving.

Trent sat on the bed and quickly sketched a plan in his notebook. The bed stood in the angle between the connecting door and the sash window, its head against the wall dividing the rooms. He stared at the pillows, then lay down deliberately, looking through the open doorway into Manderson’s room.

Satisfied, he rose and added details to his sketch: two small tables with covers, one farther from the door holding a copper electric lamp connected by a free wire to the wall. He studied the switches—on the wall near the door, out of reach from the bed. He tested the lights, confirmed they worked, then turned abruptly and walked into Manderson’s room.

He rang the bell.

“I need your help again, Martin,” he said as the butler appeared, upright and impassive. “I want you to speak to Mrs. Manderson’s maid. I need to talk to her.”

“Certainly, sir,” Martin replied.

“What’s she like? Is she level-headed?”

“She’s French, sir,” Martin said, pausing. “Not long with us, but I’ve noticed she knows more of the world than is good for her—since you asked.”

“You think butter might melt in her mouth?” Trent asked with a smile. “Well, I’m not afraid. I want to ask her some questions.”

“I’ll send her up at once, sir.” The butler withdrew.

Trent paced the room, hands behind his back. Sooner than expected, a small, neat figure in black appeared.

The maid, Célestine, had seen Trent earlier across the lawn and hoped he would summon her. She needed to vent—her nerves were frayed. But the other servants dismissed her outbursts, and Inspector Murch had silenced her with cold authority. Trent, from afar, had seemed different—not like a policeman, but *sympathique*.

Yet as she entered, instinct told her coquetry would be misplaced. Instead, she offered an amiable, candid smile. “Monsieur desires to speak with me,” she said. “I am called Célestine.”

“Naturally,” Trent replied, calm and businesslike. “Now, Célestine, I need to know: When you brought tea to your mistress yesterday at seven o’clock, was the door between these two rooms open?”

Célestine became animated. “Oh yes!” she exclaimed, using her favorite English phrase. “Always open, monsieur. I shut it as always. But let me explain. When I enter madame’s room from the other door—ah! If monsieur will step in there, all becomes clear.” She guided him into the larger bedroom. “See! I come in with the tea. I approach the bed. Before I reach it, here is the door—always open—to my right. But monsieur can see I cannot see into Mr. Manderson’s room. The door opens toward the bed, not toward me. I shut it without seeing inside. That is the routine. Yesterday was no different. I see nothing of the next room. Madame sleeps like an angel—she sees nothing. I shut the door. I place the tray—I open the curtains—I prepare the toilette—I leave—voilà!”

Trent watched her gestures closely, nodding. “I understand perfectly now. Thank you, Célestine. So Mr. Manderson was assumed to be still in his room while your mistress dressed and ate breakfast?”

“Oui, monsieur.”

“No one missed him,” Trent remarked. “Well, Célestine, thank you very much.” He reopened the door.

“It is nothing, monsieur,” she said, crossing the room. “I hope you catch the assassin of Monsieur Manderson. But”—she paused, hand on the outer door—“I do not regret him at all. Not at all!” Her voice cracked. She clenched her teeth, color rising in her cheeks. English fled. “Je ne le regrette pas du tout, du tout! Madame—ah! je me jetterais au feu pour madame—une femme si charmante, si adorable! Mais un homme comme monsieur—maussade, boudeur, impassible! Ah, non! De ma vie! J’en avais par-dessus la tête! Ah, vrai! Est-ce insupportable, tout de même, qu’il existe des types comme ça? Je vous jure que—”

“Finissez ce chahut, Célestine!” Trent interrupted sharply. Her outburst triggered memories of his student days. “En voilà une scène! C’est rasant, vous savez. Faut rentret ça, mademoiselle. Du reste, c’est bien imprudent, croyez-moi. Hang it! Have some common sense! If the inspector heard you say that, you’d be in trouble. And don’t wave your fists—someone might get hit. You seem,” he added, softer now, “to be even happier than most that Mr. Manderson is gone. I could almost suspect, Célestine, that he didn’t pay you the attention you believed you deserved.”

“A peine s’il m’avait regardé!” she answered simply.

“Ça, c’est un comble!” Trent observed. “You’re quite a character for a small tea-party, I must say. A star burned on your birthday whose fierce, serene, red, pulseless planet never yearned in heaven, Célestine. Mademoiselle, I’m busy. Bon jour. You certainly are a beauty!”

Célestine took it as a compliment—unexpected, perhaps, but welcome. Surprise steadied her. With a flash of her eyes and teeth, she opened the door and vanished.

Left alone, Trent muttered two forceful words in French and returned to his thoughts. He placed the shoes he’d examined on one chair, sat opposite, hands in pockets, eyes fixed on them. Occasionally, he whistled a few bars—almost inaudibly. The room was still. A soft twittering came from the trees beyond the open window. A breeze rustled the thick creeper along the sill. But Trent sat unmoving, face grim, lost in thought.

He remained like that for half an hour. Then he rose, replaced the shoes on the shelf, and stepped onto the landing.

Two doors faced him across the hall. He opened the one directly opposite—a bedroom far from orderly. Fishing rods leaned in a corner, books piled elsewhere. The housemaid hadn’t managed to tame the chaos: pipes, knives, pencils, keys, golf balls, letters, photos, boxes, tins, bottles littered the dressing table and mantelshelf. Two fine etchings and watercolors hung on the walls. Unhung engravings leaned against the wardrobe. A row of shoes and boots stood beneath the window.

Trent crossed the room, studied the footwear, then measured several with his tape, whistling softly. Satisfied, he sat on the edge of the bed, eyes scanning the room.

The photographs on the mantel caught his attention. He rose and examined one—Marlowe and Manderson riding horses. Two others showed Alpine peaks. A faded print depicted three youths—one unmistakably the man with the haggard blue eyes—dressed in sixteenth-century soldier’s garb. Another was a portrait of a stately elderly woman, vaguely resembling Marlowe.

Trent mechanically took a cigarette from an open box, lit it, and stared at the photos. Then he noticed a flat leather case beside the box.

He opened it. Inside lay a small revolver, finely crafted, with a dozen loose cartridges. Engraved on the stock: “J. M.”

A step echoed on the stairs. As Trent opened the breech and peered into the barrel, Inspector Murch appeared in the doorway.

“I was wondering—” he began, then stopped, seeing what Trent was doing. His eyes widened slightly. “Whose revolver is that, Mr. Trent?” he asked casually.

“Clearly the occupant’s,” Trent replied lightly, pointing to the initials. “Mr. Marlowe. I found it on the mantelpiece. Neat little pistol. Looks like it’s been cleaned recently. But I know little about guns.”

“Well, I know a lot,” Murch said quietly, taking the revolver. “Firearms are a specialty of mine, as you know. But even a novice can tell one thing.” He placed the gun back in its case, pulled out a cartridge, and laid it in his palm. Then he took a small object from his pocket—a lead bullet, slightly battered, with fresh scratches.

“That’s the one?” Trent murmured, leaning in.

“That’s him,” Murch said. “Lodged in the bone at the back of the skull. Dr. Stock removed it an hour ago and sent it to me. Those bright scratches? Made by the doctor’s tools. The others? From the rifling of the barrel—just like this one.” He tapped the revolver. “Same make, same caliber. No other weapon marks a bullet quite like this.”

They stood in silence, the revolver between them. Trent broke it first. “This mystery is all wrong,” he said. “It’s madness. The symptoms of delusion are clear. Let’s see where we stand. We agreed, I think, that Manderson killed Marlowe in the car on the way to Southampton, and that Marlowe returned late last night—hours after the murder.”

“There’s no doubt about that,” Murch said, emphasizing the verb.

“And now,” Trent continued, “this clean, elegant firearm invites us to believe this: that Marlowe never went to Southampton; that he returned in the night; that he somehow woke Manderson, made him dress, and lured him into the grounds; that he shot him with this pistol; that he cleaned it, returned it to the mantel—without waking anyone—and hid for the rest of the day—with a large motor car; and then reappeared, pretending ignorance—at what time?”

“A little after nine p.m.,” Murch said, still watching Trent. “Yes, that’s the theory suggested by this find. And it’s wild enough—though it collapses at the start. When the murder happened, Marlowe was fifty to a hundred miles away. He *did* go to Southampton.”

“How do you know?”

“I questioned him last night. Took down his story. He arrived in Southampton around 6:30 Monday morning.”

“Come off it,” Trent snapped. “What do I care about his story? What do you care? How do you *know* he went?”

Murch chuckled. “I thought I’d get a rise out of you, Mr. Trent. Well, no harm in telling. After I arrived, I got the story from Mrs. Manderson and the staff. The first thing I did was wire our people in Southampton. Manderson had told his wife he changed his mind and sent Marlowe to get information from someone on the next boat. Seemed plausible—but Marlowe was the only one not under my watch. He didn’t return until later. So I wired Southampton. This morning I received this.” He handed Trent a series of telegraph slips.

> Person answering description in motor answering description arrived Bedford Hotel here 6.30 this morning gave name Marlowe left car hotel garage told attendant car belonged Manderson had bath and breakfast went out heard of later at docks inquiring for passenger name Harris on Havre boat inquired repeatedly until boat left at noon next heard of at hotel where he lunched about 1.15 left soon afterwards in car company’s agents inform berth was booked name Harris last week but Harris did not travel by boat Burke Inspector.

“Simple and satisfactory,” Murch said, as Trent read it twice. “His story matches every detail. He told me he waited at the docks for half an hour, hoping Harris would arrive late. Then he went back, had lunch, decided to return immediately. He wired Manderson—‘Harris not turned up missed boat returning Marlowe’—which was delivered here in the afternoon and placed among the dead man’s letters. He drove back fast, arrived exhausted. When Martin told him of the murder, he nearly fainted. Sleepless, tired, he was barely coherent when I interviewed him last night—but he was perfectly logical.”

Trent picked up the revolver, twirled the cylinder idly. “It was unfortunate for Manderson that Marlowe left his pistol and cartridges so carelessly,” he said at last, placing it back in the case. “It was like throwing temptation in someone’s path, wouldn’t you say?”

“Calvin C. Bunner, at your service,” said the newcomer with a slight flourish, removing an unlit cigar from his mouth. He was accustomed to Englishmen being slow and formal with strangers, so Trent’s brisk reply caught him off guard. “You’re Mr. Trent, I suppose?” he continued. “Mrs. Manderson mentioned you earlier. Captain, good morning.”
Mr. Murch acknowledged the greeting with a curt nod. “I was heading up to my room when I heard a unfamiliar voice in here. Thought I’d check it out.”
Bunner chuckled easily. “You suspected I might be eavesdropping?”
“No, sir. I overheard a mention of a pistol—this one, I imagine—that’s all.”

Mr. Bunner was a slender, slightly undersized man with a pale, clean-shaven face that seemed almost delicate, framed by dark, wavy hair parted down the middle. His eyes were large and dark, sharp with intelligence, and his lips—usually occupied by a cigar—tended to hang slightly open, giving him a look of perpetual alertness. When he smoked, though, that expression vanished, replaced by the cool, calculating demeanor of a seasoned Yankee. Born in Connecticut, he’d entered a brokerage firm after college and quickly caught the attention of Manderson, whose business dealings he frequently handled. The Colossus had watched him closely before offering him the role of private secretary—a position he filled with precision, discretion, and unwavering loyalty. While many men could match Bunner’s reliability and methodical nature, Manderson valued him for something more: speed, secrecy, and an uncanny instinct for market movements.

Trent and Bunner exchanged glances, each assessing the other. There was no hostility, only mutual respect.

“I’ve just been told,” Trent said smoothly, “that finding this pistol doesn’t mean much. Apparently it’s common among Americans—and gaining popularity here.”

Bunner reached into the case and took the weapon, handling it with practiced ease. “That’s right, sir. We call it a Little Arthur back home. I’ll bet there are a hundred thousand like it tucked into pockets across the country. Personally, I find it too light,” he added, reaching under his jacket and pulling out a heavier, more menacing firearm. “Feel this one, Mr. Trent—it’s loaded, by the way. Now, this Little Arthur—Marlowe bought it last year, just to please the old man. Manderson insisted every man should carry a gun in the twentieth century. So he went out and picked up whatever they handed him—didn’t even ask me. Not that it’s a bad gun,” Bunner conceded, squinting along the barrel. “Marlowe wasn’t great with it at first, but I’ve coached him. He’s improved. Still, he never got used to carrying it around. It’s second nature to me—like wearing trousers. I’ve carried one for years, ever since there was always someone watching Manderson. And now…” He trailed off, voice heavy. “They got him when I wasn’t around.”

“Well, gentlemen,” Bunner said, rising, “excuse me—I’m headed into Bishopsbridge. A lot to do. Got a stack of cables to send—big enough to choke a cow.”

“I must go too,” Trent replied. “Appointment at the ‘Three Tuns’ inn.”

“Let me give you a ride,” Bunner offered. “I pass right by. Captain, you coming? No? Then come on, Mr. Trent—help me get the car out. Chauffeur’s off duty. We do most of the work ourselves, except for cleaning the grime off her.”

Still talking in his calm, measured tone, Bunner led Trent downstairs through the house to the garage at the rear. Set apart from the main building, it offered a welcome respite from the midday heat. Bunner didn’t hurry. He offered Trent a cigar, which was accepted, and lit his own for the first time. Then he sat on the footboard of the car, hands folded between his knees, studying Trent intently.

“Listen, Mr. Trent,” he said after a pause. “There are things I can tell you that might help. I know your reputation—you’re sharp. I like dealing with smart men. That detective fellow? I don’t know if I’ve sized him right, but he strikes me as a fool. I’d answer any question he had the sense to ask—but I won’t volunteer anything unless he asks. Understand?”

Trent nodded. “That’s a feeling many have around our police force,” he said. “It’s the official manner, I suppose. But let me say this—Murch isn’t what you think. He’s one of the sharpest officers in Europe. Not quick-minded, perhaps, but sure. And he’s seen more than most. My strength is imagination, but in real detective work, experience beats it every time.”

“Experience outweighs nothing!” Bunner shot back sharply. “This isn’t an ordinary case, Mr. Trent. Let me tell you why. I believe the old man knew something was coming. And I believe it was something he couldn’t escape.”

Trent pulled a crate opposite Bunner and sat down. “This sounds serious,” he said. “Tell me your thoughts.”

“I say this because of the change in Manderson’s behavior over the past few weeks. You’ve probably heard—he was always in complete control. That’s true. I’ve always thought him the coldest head in business. His composure was terrifying. I’ve never seen anything like it. And I knew him better than anyone else. I worked beside him in the business he lived for. I knew him deeper than his wife did—poor woman. Deeper than Marlowe. He never saw Manderson in the office during big deals. I saw him at his most vulnerable.”

“Did he have friends?” Trent asked.

Bunner glanced at him sharply. “Someone’s been feeding you rumors,” he said. “Not really. He had acquaintances—big men, people he saw daily. They’d go yachting or hunting together. But I don’t think there was ever a soul he opened up to. Not truly.”

He paused, then continued. “A few months ago, he began acting differently—gloomy, irritable, as if he were constantly brooding over something terrible, something he couldn’t fix. It didn’t stop. Same at home, same in the city. Something weighed heavily on him. But it wasn’t until recent weeks that his self-control started slipping. And here’s something no one else knows—only me. With everyone else, he was just morose. But when we were alone—me and him—in his office or working together, if anything went wrong, he’d explode. By God, he’d fly off the handle like a madman. I’ve seen him rip open a letter, read something he didn’t like, and start shouting, ‘I wish I had the man who wrote this here! I wouldn’t do a thing to him!’—all that, till it was heartbreaking. Never saw such a change. And then, for a week before he died, he stopped working altogether—the first time in my memory. Wouldn’t answer letters, wouldn’t respond to cables, even though everything was collapsing. I thought his nerves were shot from constant worry. Once I suggested he see a doctor. He told me to go to hell. But nobody else saw this side of him. If he was having one of these rages in the library and Mrs. Manderson walked in, he’d be calm and cold again in seconds.”

“So you think this was due to a secret fear—fear someone wanted to kill him?” Trent asked.

Bunner nodded.

“Then,” Trent said, “you must have considered the possibility of mental instability—overwork, stress, something like that. That’s what your account suggests. And it’s common enough among American industrialists, isn’t it? That’s what the papers make it sound like.”

“Don’t let them fool you,” Bunner said firmly. “Only the ones who made money too fast and can’t hold onto it go crazy. Think of the real big men—anyone near Manderson’s level. Ever hear of one losing his mind? They don’t. Believe me. Sure, everyone has their quirks—what we call their *loco point*. But that’s not madness. It’s just oddities—like hating cats, or my own weakness: I can’t eat fish.”

“And Manderson’s?”

“He had plenty. He despised unnecessary luxury—fancy trinkets, ornaments. Didn’t want servants doing little things for him. Hated having people follow him around unless he needed them. He was meticulous about clothes and shoes—spent a fortune on them—but never had a valet. Never liked anyone touching him. All his life, no one shaved him.”

“I’ve heard that,” Trent said. “Why?”

“It was part of his nature,” Bunner said slowly. “A deep suspicion. Like a dog guarding a bone, always thinking the world was trying to steal it. He didn’t actually believe the barber would try to cut his head off. But he felt it was possible. So he took no risks. In business, he always believed others were after his success—which often was true. But not always. The result? He became the most cautious, secretive operator in finance. That’s what made him rich. But it’s not madness. Not by a long shot. You asked if he was losing his mind? I think he was worn out—broken by worry. Losing nerve.”

Trent smoked in silence, weighing the implications. He wondered how much Bunner knew about the marital tension in Manderson’s household—and decided to test the waters.

“I understand he had problems with his wife.”

“Sure,” Bunner replied. “But do you think that would shake Sig Manderson? No, sir. He was too big a man to fall apart over something like that.”

Trent studied him, seeing beyond the sharpness to a kind of innocent conviction. Bunner genuinely believed domestic strife was trivial compared to business crises.

“What *was* the problem between them?”

“Can’t say,” Bunner replied. “Marlowe and I talked about it endlessly. Never figured it out. At first, I thought maybe the old man was disappointed—he expected a child. But Marlowe thinks it was the other way around. He heard something from Mrs. Manderson’s French maid.”

Trent looked up sharply. “Célestine!” he said. And inside, he thought: *So that’s what she meant.*

Bunner misread his expression. “Don’t think I’m betraying anyone,” he said. “Marlowe isn’t that sort. Célestine liked him—speaks French like a native, and she’d always pull him aside for gossip. French servants are different that way. And whether servant or not,” he added firmly, “I don’t see how a woman could bring up such a subject with a man. But the French… well, they beat me.”

He shook his head slowly.

“Back to what you were saying,” Trent said. “You believe Manderson was afraid for his life for some time. Who could threaten him? I’m still in the dark.”

“Terror? Maybe not,” Bunner mused. “Anxiety. Suspense—more like it. The old man wasn’t easy to scare. And more than that, he wasn’t taking precautions—he was avoiding them. It looked like he was inviting a quick end—if my theory’s right. Why else would he sit in that library window at night, shirt white as a target, staring into the dark? As for who might want him dead—well, sir,” Bunner said with a faint smile, “you haven’t lived in America. Take the Pennsylvania coal strike alone—thirty thousand men, women, children depending on them, who’d have jumped at the chance to shoot the man who forced them to starve or surrender. Thirty thousand of the toughest outsiders in the country. And there are types—desperadoes—who’ve waited years to kill a man. I’ve heard of dynamiting a man in Idaho for something he did in New Jersey ten years earlier. Do you think the Atlantic Ocean stops them? It takes courage to be a big businessman in our country. No, sir—the old man knew. Always knew. There were dangerous men scattered across the States who hated him. My belief is, he finally learned they were coming after him. What confuses me is why he didn’t run. Why did he walk straight into the garden yesterday morning—knowing he was being shot at?”

Bunner fell silent. For a moment, both men sat in quiet thought, blue smoke curling from their cigars.

Then Trent stood. “Your theory is new to me,” he said. “It makes sense. Only question is whether it fits all the facts. I can’t reveal what I’m doing for my paper, Mr. Bunner—but I can say this: I’ve already concluded this was a premeditated crime. An unusually clever one. Thank you. We’ll talk again.”

He checked his watch. “My friend’s been expecting me. Shall we go?”

“Two o’clock,” Bunner said, looking at his own. “Ten a.m. in little old New York. You don’t know Wall Street, Mr. Trent. Let’s hope we never see anything closer to hell than what’s loose on the Street right now.”

The sea crashed violently against the base of the cliff under a strong breeze, while sunlight poured over the land from a sky streaked with soft blue clouds. In this perfect English weather, Trent—restless after a poor night’s sleep—descended before eight o’clock to a secluded rock pool, guided by directions he had been given. He dove into the clear water and swam out past massive grey boulders into the churning open sea, pushing himself forward against the current before turning back, battered but invigorated. Ten minutes later, he climbed the cliff again, his mind momentarily freed from the grim weight of the case he was investigating. As he ascended, thoughts turned to the morning ahead.

It was the day of the inquest—the first full day since his arrival. Since parting ways with the American on the road to Bishopsbridge, Trent had made little progress. That afternoon, he had walked into town with Mr. Cupples, stopping at a chemist’s to make purchases, speaking privately with a photographer, sending off a reply-paid telegram, and making an inquiry at the telephone exchange. He had said little about the case to Mr. Cupples, who appeared uninterested; nothing at all about what he had discovered or what steps he planned to take. After returning from Bishopsbridge, Trent had written a detailed report for the *Record* and sent it via the local representative. He dined with Mr. Cupples and spent the evening alone on the veranda, lost in quiet contemplation.

Now, as he scaled the cliff once more, Trent reflected that he had never taken on a case he disliked so deeply—and yet one that held him so completely. The more he considered it under the golden light of the new day, the darker and more compelling it seemed. Every suspicion, every half-known truth, had consumed his thoughts through the night, robbing him of sleep. Even now, cleansed by the sea’s fierce purity, he saw more clearly the darkness he believed lay beneath the surface—and felt even stronger repulsion toward the motive he suspected. But his resolve had returned. The hunt stirred within him. He would not hesitate, nor show mercy. There could be no hesitation here. By the end of the day, he hoped, his net would be complete. He had work to do, and he waited with keen anticipation—though not much hope—for the reply to the telegram he had dispatched the previous day.

The path back to the hotel ran along the cliff top. As he neared a spot he had marked earlier from below, where the cliff face had long ago eroded away, he paused at the edge, hoping to catch sight of the most delicate movement of water—a gentle wash over broken rock. But there was no rock. Instead, a broad ledge jutted out, a rough platform the size of a large room, covered in wiry grass and bounded steeply on three sides. On the very edge, where the cliff dropped sheer, sat a woman. Her arms were wrapped around her drawn-up knees, her gaze fixed on the distant smoke trail of a passing liner, her face lost in thought.

To Trent, trained to observe with precision and depth, she formed the most striking image he had ever seen. Her southern pallor was touched with color from the wind, giving her cheeks a soft glow. Her profile was delicately regular, free of harshness, though her dark brows arched downward, almost meeting at the bridge of her nose, lending her a look of quiet severity—yet softened by the gentle curve of her mouth. Trent mused that whether a lover might write sonnets to a woman’s eyebrow depended entirely on the quality of that eyebrow. Hers was straight and fine, perfectly proportioned—neither too long nor too sharp, avoiding the pitfall of the overly prominent tip-tilted nose that sometimes drew admiration despite its excess. Her hat lay pinned to the grass beside her, and the breeze played with her thick, dark hair, lifting the wide bands meant to cover her forehead and stirring tiny curls from the mass gathered at her nape. Everything about her was black—from her suede shoes to the discarded hat—dull black covering her down to her bare throat. Her clothes were finely tailored, impeccably worn. Though her demeanor suggested a dreamy, delicate spirit, there was no mistaking her mastery of dress, a skill honed through years of practice. She wore her body with pride, its elegant curves emphasized by the way she sat, knees clasped close. With touches of French style in her attire, she appeared modern—until one looked at her face and saw the vibrant life, the radiant strength of someone fully alive, facing sun, wind, and sea in their prime. She was neither purely English nor American, but something more vivid, more complete.

Trent, who had stopped only briefly in surprise at seeing her, continued along the path above her, absorbing everything: the grace of her posture, the play of light on her features, the subtle details of her appearance. His vision and mind worked swiftly, effortlessly—his ability to absorb detail without staring a marvel to those less gifted. Now, the sensation of beauty heightened his awareness, doubling his perception. In those fleeting moments, a picture burned into his memory—one that would never fade.

As he passed unseen on the turf, the woman suddenly moved. She released her hands from her knees, stretched her limbs with feline elegance, then slowly raised her head and extended her arms, fingers open and curved, as if reaching to embrace the brilliance of the morning. It was unmistakable—a gesture of freedom, of resolution. A soul choosing to live, to claim, to move forward. For a single instant, Trent saw her—then he did not turn back. In that moment, he knew who she must be. And it felt as though a shadow had fallen across the day.

At breakfast, Mr. Cupples found Trent withdrawn, offering a tired night as an excuse. Cupples, however, was alert, almost animated. The prospect of the inquest excited him. He launched into a lecture on the history of the coroner’s court—its ancient roots, its once-busy role, and its freedom from legal rigidity. Then he turned to the case at hand.

“Young Bunner mentioned to me last night,” he said, “the theory he’s formed about the crime. A remarkable young man, Trent. His ideas are sometimes obscure, but I believe he possesses a rare clarity of mind for one so young. Manderson’s decision to appoint him as his principal lieutenant speaks volumes. He’s taken charge of the business affairs here with confidence, advised me wisely about Mabel’s interests, and outlined the best course for her until the will is executed. That makes me less inclined to dismiss his idea of an industrial vendetta as far-fetched. When I questioned him, he cited several cases where labor organizations had attacked—or successfully killed—individuals who had angered them. We live in terrible times, my dear boy. I can’t think of any period in history when the imbalance between material wealth and moral values has been so great, so threatening to society’s stability. And nowhere, in my view, is the outlook bleaker than in America.”

“I thought,” Trent replied listlessly, “that Puritanism was just as strong there as the pursuit of money.”

“Your remark,” Cupples answered, with a hint of dry humor, “is not exactly a compliment to what you call Puritanism—a term more convenient than accurate. It was originally used to describe Anglicans who sought to purify church rituals. But your point holds merit. Consider Manderson himself—he embodied purity, self-restraint, abstinence. Yet still, we see deeper moral failures. The more we focus on the complex machinery science gives us, the less energy remains for cultivating our inner lives. Harvest festivals have vanished with farm mechanization. Inns have died with rail travel. These are just two examples. My view may be controversial among those who share my concerns, but I stand by it.”

“It needs a pithy phrase,” Trent said, rising. “If only it could be reduced to a slogan—like ‘No Popery’ or ‘Tax the Foreigner’—you’d find crowds willing to die for it. But you’re going to White Gables before the inquest, aren’t you? You should leave soon if you want to make it back in time. I’ve some matters to attend to there myself. Let’s walk together. I’ll just get my camera.”

“By all means,” Cupples replied, and they set off into the warming morning.

The roof of White Gables, a dull red patch against the dark trees, matched Trent’s mood. He felt heavy, uneasy, burdened. If a blow must fall upon the radiant being he had seen that morning, he did not wish it to come from his own hand. An old sense of chivalry, nurtured by his mother, stirred in him—but now, the horror of harming such beauty felt more like an artist’s recoil than a gentleman’s duty. At the same time, could the hunt end in nothing? The nature of the case made inaction unbearable. No other case had ever held such power, and he was certain he alone grasped the truth. He resolved that the day would reveal whether his belief was illusion or fact. He would suppress any compunction until he was absolutely sure it was needed. That morning, he would know.

As they approached the gate, they saw Marlowe and the American standing in conversation near the front door. In the shadow of the porch stood the woman in black. She saw them and stepped forward with calm dignity, moving with the poised balance Trent had expected. When Mr. Cupples introduced her, she welcomed Trent with kind eyes—golden-flecked brown, steady and composed. Her pale face, masked in grief, showed no trace of the emotional glow he had seen on the cliff. She spoke in a low, even voice, exchanging polite remarks. After a few words with Cupples, she turned to Trent.

“I hope you succeed,” she said earnestly.

“Do you think I will?” he asked.

He made up his mind as she spoke. “I believe I shall, Mrs. Manderson. Once I’ve gathered enough evidence, I’ll ask to speak with you—explain what I’ve found. It may be necessary to consult you before the facts are published.”

She looked puzzled, and a flicker of distress crossed her eyes. “If it’s necessary, of course,” she said.

On the verge of speaking, Trent hesitated. He remembered she had refused to repeat her account to him—or to be questioned at all. He wasn’t blind to his own desire to hear her voice, to watch her face a little longer. But the matter he wanted to raise troubled him—it didn’t fit neatly into the pattern he’d begun to piece together. She might explain it instantly; no one else likely could. He summoned his courage.

“You’ve been generous,” he said, “allowing me access to the house and every chance to investigate. So I’ll ask a few questions—nothing you’d rather not answer. May I?”

She glanced at him wearily. “It would be foolish to refuse. Ask your questions, Mr. Trent.”

“It’s this,” he said quickly. “We know your husband recently withdrew a large sum of cash from his London bankers and kept it here. It’s still here. Do you have any idea why?”

Her eyes widened. “I cannot imagine. I didn’t know he’d done so. I’m surprised to hear it.”

“Why is it surprising?”

“I thought he had very little money at home. On Sunday night, just before he left in the car, he came into the drawing-room where I was sitting. He seemed agitated and asked if I had any notes or gold I could lend him until the next day. I was startled—he always carried a hundred pounds or so in his note-case. I unlocked my escritoire and gave him what I had—nearly thirty pounds.”

“And he didn’t tell you why he needed it?”

“No. He put it in his pocket, then said Mr. Marlowe had convinced him to go for a moonlight drive, which might help him sleep. He’d been having trouble sleeping, as you may know. Then he left with Mr. Marlowe. I thought it odd he’d need money that night, but I forgot about it. I didn’t remember until now.”

“That is curious,” Trent murmured, staring into the distance.

Cupples began discussing arrangements with his niece. Trent moved toward Marlowe, who was pacing the lawn. The young man seemed relieved to talk about the day’s events. Though still weary and tense, he displayed a quiet wit in describing the pompousness of the local police and Dr. Stock’s solemn air. Trent steered the conversation toward the crime.

“Bunner told me his theory,” Marlowe said when Trent brought it up. “I’m not convinced. It doesn’t explain some of the strangest facts. But I’ve lived in America long enough to know such acts—secret, dramatic revenge—are not unheard of. They’re characteristic of certain labor factions. Americans have a taste for theatricality in such matters. Have you read *Huckleberry Finn*?”

“Do I know my own name?” Trent retorted.

“Well, the most American thing in that great novel is Tom Sawyer’s elaborate plan—taking days—to rescue Jim, when it could’ve been done in twenty minutes. They love secret societies. College clubs have signs and grips. You’ve heard of the Know-Nothings, the Ku Klux Klan. Look at Brigham Young’s rule in Utah—real bloodshed. The Mormons were pure Yankee stock. That’s all part of the same mindset. Americans mock it themselves. But I take it seriously.”

“It can be horrifying,” Trent agreed, “especially when tied to crime or vice. But I have a grudging respect for the drive to make life exciting despite civilization. Back to the case—has it occurred to you that Manderson might have been affected by this threat Bunner believes in? For instance, sending you off at midnight was unusual.”

“About ten o’clock, actually,” Marlowe corrected. “But if he’d woken me at midnight, I wouldn’t have been surprised. It fits what we’ve discussed. Manderson had a flair for drama. He liked sudden moves, direct actions. He decided he needed word from a man named Harris—”

“Who is Harris?” Trent interrupted.

“Nobody knows. Bunner never heard of him. All I know is that when I went to London last week, I booked a deck cabin for Mr. George Harris on the Monday boat—at Manderson’s request. He suddenly needed news from Harris—something too sensitive for telegraph. No train was available, so he sent me.”

Trent glanced around to ensure they weren’t overheard, then faced Marlowe gravely. “There’s one thing I can tell you,” he said quietly, “that you probably don’t know. Martin the butler caught the end of your conversation with Manderson in the orchard. He heard him say, ‘If Harris is there, every moment is of importance.’ Now, Mr. Marlowe—you know my role here. I’m investigating. Don’t take offense. I need to ask: in light of that, will you still say you know nothing about the purpose of the trip?”

Marlowe shook his head. “I know nothing. I’m not easily offended. Your question is fair. I’ve already told the detective everything. Manderson said plainly he couldn’t tell me. He just wanted me to find Harris, tell him he wanted to know how things stood, and bring back a message. Harris might not appear. If he did, ‘every moment was of importance.’ That’s all I know.”

“The conversation happened *before* he told your wife about the moonlight ride. Why hide it from her?”

Marlowe shrugged. “Why? I can’t guess better than you.”

“Why,” Trent muttered, gazing at the ground, “did he hide it—from Mrs. Manderson?”

“From Martin too,” Marlowe added coolly. “He was told the same.”

With a swift motion, Trent dismissed the subject. He pulled a letter-case from his pocket and took out two small sheets of clean paper. “Look at these, Mr. Marlowe,” he said. “Have you seen them before? Any idea where they came from?”

Marlowe examined them closely. “They seem cut from a small diary—October pages. No writing. Nobody here has such a diary, as far as I know. What are they?”

“There may be nothing,” Trent said doubtfully. “Someone in the house might have one without you knowing. But I didn’t expect you to recognize them. I’d have been surprised if you had.”

He paused as Mrs. Manderson approached.

“My uncle thinks we should go,” she said.

“I’ll walk with Mr. Bunner,” Cupples announced, joining them. “There are urgent business matters. Will you join us, Mabel? We’ll wait.”

Trent turned to her. “Mrs. Manderson, I hope you’ll forgive me. I came up this morning to search for clues. I hadn’t intended to attend the court just yet.”

She looked at him with open honesty. “Of course, Mr. Trent. Please do as you wish. We rely on you. Mr. Marlowe, please wait a moment—I’ll be ready.”

She entered the house. Cupples and the American had already walked toward the gate.

Trent met Marlowe’s gaze. “That is a remarkable woman,” he said softly.

“You say so without knowing her,” Marlowe replied. “She is more than that.”

Trent said nothing. He stared out over the fields toward the sea.

A sound broke the silence—a rapid clatter of hobnailed boots. A boy appeared, running from the direction of the hotel, holding an orange envelope. Trent watched impassively as he passed the others. Then he turned to Marlowe.

“Just wondering,” he said, “were you at Oxford?”

“Yes,” Marlowe replied. “Why?”

“Just checking if I was right. It’s one of those things you can often tell about a man, isn’t it?”

“Suppose so,” Marlowe said. “We’re all marked in some way. I’d have guessed you were an artist—if I hadn’t known.”

“Why? Does my hair need cutting?”

“Oh, no. It’s the way you look—at people, at things. Steady, from detail to detail. Not just looking at them, but *over* them.”

The boy arrived, panting. “Telegram for you, sir,” he said. “Just come.”

Trent tore it open with a brief apology. His eyes lit up visibly as he read. Marlowe’s tired face softened into a smile.

“It must be good news,” he murmured.

Trent looked at him, expression unreadable. “Not exactly news,” he said. “It only confirms another guess of mine was correct.”

The coroner, a man of ample frame and genial disposition, was determined to rise to the occasion. For one day—just one—he would be the center of national attention, and he meant to conduct himself with the dignity befitting such a moment. He had long appreciated the theatrical side of his role, and now that a mysterious death had occurred within his jurisdiction, he felt nothing short of triumphant. His ability to organize facts was matched by a gift for oratory, which could sway juries like puppets on strings. Occasionally, this eloquence veiled a questionable grasp of legal nuance, but today no one seemed to mind.

The inquest took place in a long, bare room appended to the hotel—a space originally intended for balls and concerts. Rows of reporters occupied the front seats, their pens poised, their eyes sharp. Behind the coroner’s table sat the witnesses, awaiting their turn. To either side, the jury stood in two neat lines, their hair carefully combed, their expressions stiffly neutral, as if they’d been cast from the same mold. The rest of the room was filled with townspeople who watched in hushed reverence as the proceedings began. The journalists exchanged murmurs, some confirming among themselves that the famed detective Trent was not yet present.

The first witness called was Mrs. Manderson. She stepped forward with quiet dignity, lifting her veil just before speaking. Her face was pale, but composed—no trace of hysteria, only a deep stillness that spoke of inner strength. There was something profoundly feminine about her, yet she carried herself with an unyielding resolve. She touched her eyes once or twice with a handkerchief, but her voice remained steady throughout.

She described how her husband had returned to their bedroom around his usual bedtime on Sunday night. Their rooms were connected by a door left open during the night, though both had separate entrances from the hall. Mr. Manderson had always favored simplicity—sleeping in a small, unadorned space. Mrs. Manderson had not been fully awake when he came up, but she stirred slightly when the light in his room switched on. She recalled speaking to him, though she couldn’t remember exactly what she said. She did recall that he had gone out for a moonlit drive and she had asked if it had been a good run, and what time it was. She had expected him to stay out late, so she was surprised when he told her it was half-past eleven.

“And did he explain why he hadn:’t gone?” the coroner asked gently.

“Yes,” she replied. “He said he had changed his mind. I remember it clearly because—” She paused, visibly flustered.

“Because?” prompted the coroner.

“Because my husband was not usually inclined to talk about business,” she said, raising her chin slightly. “He thought it wouldn’t interest me, and he rarely mentioned it. That’s why I found it unusual when he told me he had sent Mr. Marlowe to Southampton to retrieve important information from a man leaving for Paris the next day. He said Marlowe could do it easily—if there was no accident. Then he added that he had started in the car, but walked back home a mile or so, and felt better for it.”

“Did he say anything else?”

“Not that I remember. I was very sleepy. I fell asleep again shortly after. I only recall him turning off the light. That was the last time I saw him alive.”

She gave no further details, her tone flat and final. But the coroner wasn’t finished.

“Mrs. Manderson,” he said, his voice soft but firm, “I must ask a question that may be painful under these circumstances. Was there, over the past months, a breakdown in your relationship with your husband? Were you estranged?”

She straightened, color rising in her cheeks.

“If it is necessary,” she answered, her voice cold and clear, “then I will answer plainly. During the last few months, my husband’s behavior toward me caused me great sorrow and anxiety. He became distant, reserved, almost suspicious. I saw less of him than ever before. He preferred solitude. I cannot explain why. I tried to maintain my dignity while reaching out to him, but I never asked directly what troubled him. My pride kept me from doing so. I simply continued being the wife I had always been—so far as he would allow. And now I shall never know what lay between us.”

Her voice trembled slightly at the end. She lowered her veil and stood motionless.

One juror ventured a question, hesitantly.

“Was there ever any kind of… words… between you and your husband, ma’am?”

“Never,” she replied, the word spoken without emotion—but the silence that followed spoke volumes. Everyone sensed the depth of her restraint, and the absurdity of the question.

The coroner then asked if she knew of anything else that might have weighed heavily on her husband’s mind.

“No,” she said simply. “Nothing.”

Her testimony concluded, she withdrew quietly. The room turned its attention to Martin, the next witness.

It was then that Trent entered through the doorway, slipping into the crowd unnoticed. He didn’t look at Martin. Instead, his gaze fixed on a woman moving swiftly through the throng—Mrs. Manderson. His expression darkened. As he stepped aside, bowing slightly, she approached him.

“I wanted to ask you,” she said in a low, fragile voice, “if you would give me your arm for a little way. I can’t see my uncle near the door, and suddenly I feel faint… I’ll be better outside…”

“No,” she insisted when he began to suggest otherwise. “I must go to the house. Please.”

Her hand gripped his arm briefly—tight enough to pull him along, yet weak enough to lean upon him. With bowed head and slow steps, she moved toward the exit, guided by Trent. He walked beside her in silence, thoughts racing like storm-winds behind a calm mask. Every suspicion, every clue he had gathered, surged through his mind. But above all, the sensation of her cool fingers against his skin burned in his memory—both comforting and infuriating.

When they reached White Gables, she sank onto a couch in the morning room. Removing her veil, she looked up at him with genuine gratitude.

“I’m much better now,” she said. “A cup of tea will restore me completely. I’m sorry I took you away from something important.”

She smiled faintly. “I thought I could handle it, but those last questions… I didn’t expect them to shake me so. And all those men staring at the door…” She paused. “Thank you for helping me when I asked.”

Trent nodded, saying nothing. He left soon after, his hand still tingling from her touch.

The remaining testimonies—the servants’, the finder of the body, the police—added little new to the public record. But Bunner’s account, delivered with quiet conviction, became the highlight of the day. He repeated what he had told Trent: the risks taken by industrialists in America, the tensions between labor and capital, and his belief that Manderson had been targeted. The press captured every word, and the story spread across Britain and the United States with startling speed.

Public opinion paid no heed to the coroner’s subtle hint—offered in his closing remarks—that suicide could not be ruled out. He had pointed out that no weapon was found near the body, and that the medical evidence suggested the wound was not self-inflicted due to the lack of powder residue or close-range marks. Dr. Stock had testified that death occurred six to eight hours before discovery, caused by a bullet entering the left eye and shattering the base of the brain. The external wound showed no signs of the gun being pressed against the face, though it was technically possible the victim fired it himself at a slight distance.

Still, the doctor noted that bruises and scratches on the wrists and forearms were recent—clear signs of violence. The body had been found in a collapsed position, consistent with a fatal shot, but the injuries indicated a struggle.

The coroner emphasized Bunner’s testimony, not as proof, but as a point for consideration. “Can we say,” he asked the jury, “that the deceased was under threat? Was he more vulnerable than an ordinary man? And does the change in his behavior—his sudden anxiety—suggest that his final days were marked by fear?”

With that, he left the jury to deliberate.

And thus, the weight of the case shifted—not just to the facts, but to the shadows behind them.

“Come in!” Trent called out, his voice crisp and alert.

Mr. Cupples stepped into the sitting room of the hotel, the evening light casting long shadows across the worn furnishings. It was the same day the coroner’s jury had returned their verdict—without leaving the box—declaring the death a result of “a person or persons unknown.” Trent stood near the window, his eyes fixed on a photographic negative resting in an enamelled dish. He moved it slowly, studying it with intense concentration. His face was pale, and his hands trembled slightly as he worked.

“Sit down,” he said, gesturing toward the sofa. “The chairs are relics—bought at auction after the fall of the Holy Inquisition in Spain. This one’s actually quite comfortable.”

He held up the negative, tilting it to catch the fading daylight. “This is a good one,” he remarked, adjusting his head for better judgment. “Washed enough now. Let’s let it dry and clear this mess away.”

As Trent busied himself clearing the table—basins, bottles, racks, boxes—Mr. Cupples picked up each item, examining them with quiet curiosity.

“That’s hypo-eliminator,” Trent said, catching him sniffing a bottle. “Useful when you’re racing against time. But don’t drink it—I wouldn’t risk it. It removes sodium hypophosphite, but I’d wager it could remove a man too.”

After arranging the last object on the cluttered mantel, Trent sat down on the edge of the table, facing Mr. Cupples.

“The beauty of a hotel room,” he mused, “is that it doesn’t distract. No charm, no comfort—just function. Perfect for work. Have you ever been in this room before?”

“No,” replied Cupples.

“I have—hundreds of times. It follows me around England like a familiar ghost. If they gave me another room in some distant inn, I’d feel lost. Look at this tablecloth—the ink stain from Halifax. That hole in the carpet? Ipswich. And the glass over ‘Silent Sympathy’—they’ve repaired it. I threw a boot at it in Banbury. This is where I do my best thinking. Today, since the inquest, I’ve finished several excellent negatives. There’s a decent darkroom downstairs.”

“The inquest,” said Cupples, suddenly remembering. “I came to thank you for looking after Mabel earlier. I didn’t expect her to be affected. She seemed so composed—so strong. I thought she could manage alone while I listened to the evidence. I’m glad she found someone to help her. She’s back to herself now.”

Trent said nothing at first, his hands tucked into his pockets, a faint frown creasing his brow.

“I was just getting to the really interesting part,” he said finally. “Would you like to see a little high-class detective work? The kind old Murch should be doing right now. Maybe he is. But I hope not.”

He rose abruptly and vanished into his bedroom. Moments later, he returned carrying a large drawing board loaded with odd objects.

“Let me introduce you to these,” he said, placing them carefully on the table. “An ivory paper knife. Two pages torn from my own diary. A bottle of toothpaste. A walnut case. Some of these need to go back to a bedroom at White Gables before nightfall. That’s the sort of man I am—nothing stops me. I took them this morning, while everyone was at the inquest. Some might think it peculiar. Now—there’s one thing left on the board. Can you tell me what it is, without touching it?”

Cupples leaned forward. “It’s a glass bowl. Looks like a finger-bowl. Nothing unusual about it.”

“I can’t see much either,” Trent admitted. “And that’s exactly why it’s interesting.”

He handed Cupples a small fat bottle. “Pull the cork. Do you recognize the powder inside?”

“It’s grey powder,” Cupples said, squinting. “They give it to babies. Mercury and chalk, I suppose?”

“Exactly. Great stuff. Now—while I hold the bowl sideways over this sheet of paper, pour a little of it onto the surface here.”

Cupples did as instructed. The powder fell evenly.

“Perfect,” Trent said. “Sir Edward Henry himself couldn’t have done better. You’ve done this before, haven’t you?”

“I haven’t,” Cupples insisted. “I’m completely baffled. What did I just do?”

Trent returned the excess powder to the bottle. “Now I’ll brush the powdered area gently with this camel-hair brush. Look again.”

Cupples peered closely.

“How curious!” he exclaimed.

“Two large grey fingerprints,” Trent said. “They weren’t there before. The human hand, even when clean, leaves moisture—especially under stress. When it touches cold glass, it leaves traces. This bowl was handled recently by someone with damp fingers.”

He sprinkled more powder. “Here’s the thumb mark—excellent impression. And this—index finger. A single-spiral whorl, symmetrically arranged. The second finger—simple loop, staple core, fifteen ridge counts. I know because I have the exact same prints on this negative.”

He held it up to the dimming sunlight, pointing with a pencil. “See the bifurcation? The scar near the center? They match perfectly. An expert would swear in court these marks came from the same hand.”

“But where did you take the prints?” Cupples asked, wide-eyed.

“From the inside of the left pane of the front window in Mrs. Manderson’s bedroom. I couldn’t bring the window, so I photographed it—using black paper on the other side for contrast. The bowl? It’s from Manderson’s room. That’s where he kept his false teeth at night. I brought that one.”

“So these aren’t Mabel’s prints?”

“Impossible,” Trent said sharply. “They’re twice the size of any print she could make.”

“Then they must belong to her husband.”

“Possibly. But let’s test it again.”

Whistling softly, Trent opened a squat bottle of dense black powder.

“Lamp-black,” he explained. “Hold a piece of paper for a few seconds—this will reveal your fingerprint pattern.”

With tweezers, he lifted one of the diary pages and showed it to Cupples—no marks visible. Then he dusted both sides lightly with the powder, shook off the excess, and held it out.

On one side, two distinct black fingerprints appeared—identical to those on the bowl and the negative.

He turned the paper over. On the reverse, a bold thumbprint matched the grey one on the glass.

“Same hand,” Trent said, laughing shortly. “I knew it. Now I know.”

He walked to the window, staring out.

“I know,” he repeated, quietly, almost to himself. His voice was low, bitter.

Cupples watched him, stunned.

“I still don’t understand,” he said after a moment. “I’ve heard of fingerprinting, but I don’t see how finding Manderson’s prints helps us—”

Trent cut him off, returning quickly to the table.

“I’m sorry, Cupples,” he said, his tone firm. “When I started this, I meant to include you every step of the way. But now I can’t say more. Not yet. I’ve discovered something that could cause terrible consequences if anyone else learns it.”

His face hardened. He struck the table once.

“It’s unbearable. Until now, I hoped I was wrong. I may still be. But there’s only one way to find out—and I must face it.”

He smiled suddenly, easing the tension.

“Don’t worry—I won’t dwell on tragedy. I’ll tell you everything soon. For now—look, I’m not done with the powder bottles.”

He pulled over one of the battered chairs and sat down, picking up the ivory paper knife.

“Let’s test this blade,” he said.

Cupples, still reeling, leaned forward, handing him the lamp-black bottle.

Mrs. Manderson stood at the window of her sitting room at White Gables, staring out at a landscape blurred by fine rain and low-hanging mist. The weather had turned abruptly—unusual for June in that part of the coast. Grey veils of fog drifted up from the restless sea, swallowing the fields and clinging to the horizon. The sky was a solid sheet of dull grey, releasing a drizzle so faint it seemed almost like breath, yet sometimes stinging the glass with a sharp, brittle sound. She watched the scene with a face drawn tight with sorrow. It was a day made for grief—alone, bereaved, and without direction.

A knock came at the door. “Come in,” she called, straightening slightly—an unconscious movement she always made when the weight of the world pressed too close upon her spirit.

The maid announced Mr. Trent. He apologized for arriving so early, but said he hoped she would see him on a matter of urgent importance. Mrs. Manderson nodded. She would see him.

She walked to the mirror, looked at the olive-toned face reflected there, shook her head with a fleeting grimace, then turned as he entered. His appearance struck her immediately—he looked worn, sleepless, his usual warmth replaced by a guarded stillness. His eyes held no trace of their customary ease, only a quiet tension that spoke of something heavy beneath the surface.

“May I come to the point at once?” he asked, taking her hand. “There’s a train I must catch at Bishopsbridge at twelve. But I can’t leave until I’ve settled this—something that concerns you alone. I’ve been working half the night, thinking the rest. Now I know what I must do.”

“You look exhausted,” she said gently. “Won’t you sit down? This chair is very comfortable.” She paused. “Of course, it’s about the case. About your work as a correspondent. Please—ask me anything you believe I can properly tell you. I know you won’t make things worse than necessary. If you feel you must speak with me, it’s because you believe it’s right.”

Trent hesitated, then spoke slowly, deliberately: “Mrs. Manderson, I won’t make things worse than I must. But I’m bound to make them difficult—for you. Only between us, I hope. As to whether you can properly answer my questions, that’s for you to decide. But I swear to you, on my honour: I will ask only what is necessary to determine whether I should publish or withhold certain facts I’ve uncovered about your husband’s death—facts no one else suspects, nor, I think, ever will. What I’ve discovered—what I believe I’ve proven—will shock you deeply. It may hurt you more than that. And if you give me reason to believe it would, then I will not publish my report. I’ll destroy it.”

He laid a long envelope on the table beside him. “This contains a private note to my editor, followed by a dispatch meant for the *Record*. You may refuse to answer. If you do, my duty—to my employers, as I see it—is to take this to London today and leave it to my editor’s judgment. I believe I have no right to suppress it based only on a possibility. But if I learn from you—only from you—that this possibility has substance, then I have no choice as a man of principle, or as someone who... who wishes you well. I will not publish. In some ways, I resist helping the police. Have I made myself clear?”

He waited, his voice carefully neutral, though his eyes betrayed a flicker of anxiety. She stared back, pale but composed, hands clasped before her, shoulders drawn back into a rigid stillness. She looked exactly as she had at the inquest.

“I understand,” she said quietly. She drew a deep breath. “I don’t know what terrible thing you’ve found, or what possible explanation you’re considering. But it was noble of you to come to me about it. Now—please tell me.”

“I cannot,” Trent replied. “The secret belongs to my newspaper—if it doesn’t belong to you. If I find it’s yours, you shall have my manuscript to read and destroy. Believe me,” he added, his voice warming slightly, “I despise secrecy. But it’s not I who created this. This is the most painful hour of my life—and you make it worse by treating me like an animal. The first thing I ask is this: is it true, as you stated at the inquest, that you had no idea why your husband changed toward you—became distant, suspicious—in the months before his death?”

Her dark brows lifted. Her eyes flared. She rose sharply from the chair. Trent stood up at once, retrieving the envelope. His posture suggested the interview was over.

But she raised a hand. A flush crept into her cheeks. Her voice trembled. “Do you know what you’re asking, Mr. Trent? You’re asking if I lied under oath.”

“I do,” he answered calmly. After a pause, he added, “And you already knew I wasn’t here to uphold polite fictions. The idea that any honest person, under oath, could never withhold truth—no matter the cost—is just that: a fiction.”

He remained standing, waiting. She was silent.

Then she walked to the window, and he watched the slight rise and fall of her shoulders, the way they tensed and relaxed. At last, with her face turned away, gazing out at the bleak weather, she spoke clearly.

“Mr. Trent,” she said, “you inspire trust. I feel that whatever I say, even if I don’t want it known, will be safe with you. And I know you wouldn’t do this unless you had a serious reason—though I don’t know what it is. Perhaps telling you the truth now would help justice. To understand it, you need to know about my marriage. Many people could tell you the same—but I’ll tell it.”

She paused, gathering herself. “It wasn’t a successful marriage. I was only twenty. I admired his strength, his courage, his certainty. He was the only powerful man I’d ever known. But soon I realized he cared more for his business than for me. And I saw earlier than I wanted to admit that I’d been deceiving myself—blindly promising myself things I couldn’t have, misreading my own feelings, all because I was dazzled by the idea of wealth beyond anything an English girl dreams of. I’ve despised myself for that for five years.”

She stopped, breathing quickly. Then continued: “My husband’s affection for me—I can’t speak of that. But I must say this: along with it, he always believed I would become a great figure in society. That I would embrace it eagerly, become a name, bring him credit. That belief stayed with him, even after others faded. I was part of his ambition. And that was his deepest disappointment—when I failed him as a social success.”

She paused again, then went on, her voice gaining strength. “He was shrewd enough to know, deep down, that marrying a woman like me—a girl brought up on music, books, impractical ideals, always doing things her own way—was risky. A man like him, twenty years older, burdened with business, devoted to nothing but work—he must have sensed the danger. Yet he counted on me to carry the dignity of his position. And I found I couldn’t.”

Her voice rose, animated now, her hands moving as if to emphasize each word. She turned from the window, facing him fully. Her face was flushed, her eyes bright, alive with emotion.

“The people,” she said. “Oh, those people! Can you imagine stepping from a world where creativity mattered—where men and women had professions, ideals, beliefs—into another where money is everything? Where being rich isn’t enough—you must be shamefully rich just to exist? Where men who earn millions are worn out by their work, so they only care for sport; and those who don’t work are even emptier, more vicious. Women live for display, silly pleasures, petty immorality. Do you know how awful it is? Of course, there are clever people, tasteful ones—but they’re drowned, spoiled. In the end, it’s all empty. Empty!”

She took a breath. “I suppose I exaggerate. I did make friends. I had good times. But that’s how I feel now. The seasons in New York and London—how I hated them! The house parties, the yacht cruises—the same faces, the same emptiness.”

“And you see,” she continued, “my husband couldn’t understand any of this. His life wasn’t empty. He didn’t live for society. When he was in it, he still thought of business. He had no idea what I felt. And I never told him. I couldn’t. It wouldn’t have been fair. I felt I had to do something to justify being his wife—sharing his fortune, his status. So I tried. I tried every year to live up to his idea of me. I played the role. But I wasn’t a popular hostess—how could I be? I was a failure. But I kept trying.”

She lowered her voice. “I used to steal holidays. I’d take an old school friend—who couldn’t afford to travel—away to Italy, and we’d go cheaply, just the two of us. We’d be happy. Or I’d stay in London with quiet people I’d known forever, living as we used to—worrying about theatre seats, talking about affordable dressmakers. Those were my best moments after marriage. They helped me endure the rest. But I knew my husband would hate knowing how much joy I found in returning to that old life.”

She paused, then whispered, “In the end, despite everything, he found out. He could see through anything once he focused. He’d always noticed I wasn’t fulfilling his vision of me—but he thought it was bad luck, not fault. But when he began to see, past my pretense, that I wasn’t even pretending anymore… he understood. He knew how much I loathed the luxury, the brilliance, the sheer waste of money—because of the people it created. It happened last year. I don’t know when or how. Maybe a woman told him—they all understood, of course. He said nothing. I think he tried not to change. But such things hurt. It was killing us both. I knew he knew.”

She looked at him, her eyes filled with quiet pain. “We’d been on a footing of intelligent companionship—talking freely about things that mattered without going too deep. Then that ended. I felt the foundation of our life together slipping away. And it did.”

She sank into the corner of the sofa, as if collapsing after a long effort. Silence fell.

Trent stood, sorting through impressions. He was stunned by her candour, by the force of her words. In this moment, he saw her—not the pale, controlled woman of public view, but a vibrant, passionate soul, unguarded and raw. He saw her beauty intensified by emotion, her darkness lit with fire. And suddenly, a strange fear gripped him—not of her, but of himself. A memory surfaced: beautiful women in England were usually calm; fiery ones lost their grace. But she—she combined both. And that was why she affected him like no other.

“All this is very disputable,” his mind argued.
But instinct whispered, “Yes—except that I am under a spell.”
And deeper still, a voice cried, “Away with it!”

He forced himself back to her words. A conviction grew fast—irresistible. It was all noble. But it wouldn’t hold.

“I feel as if I’ve led you into saying more than you meant,” he said slowly. “But there’s one question—the whole point. Will you assure me that your husband’s change toward you had nothing to do with John Marlowe?”

And then it came.

“Oh!” she gasped, her face lifted, hands thrown out as if pleading. Then she covered her face, buried her head in her arms, and collapsed among the cushions, sobbing violently. Her body shook. Her hair fell forward, hiding her face. One foot turned inward in helpless surrender.

Like a tower crumbling, she had fallen apart.

Trent stood up. His face was white, composed. With mechanical precision, he placed the envelope in the centre of the polished table. He walked to the door, closed it silently, and stepped out into the rain.

Within minutes, he was trudging through the wet, invisible, directionless. His mind was numb. His soul shaken. He fought a raging impulse—one born of pity, of awe, of something deeper—that demanded he throw himself at her feet, beg forgiveness, pour out words he didn’t know but felt burning inside. Words that would ruin his pride, betray his purpose, drown her grief in his own infatuation—speaking to a woman whose husband lay unburied, to a woman who loved another.

That was the magic of her tears—quickening in an instant what his heart had known must never awaken.

For Philip Trent was young—not just in age, but in spirit. A life of sharp edges and volcanic energy had left him ill-prepared for the moment that comes once to most men in youth: the meeting that tests virtue, the encounter that reveals the power of the will—and, as he told himself harshly, serves no purpose but that.

My Dear Molloy:—This is in case I don’t find you at your office. I’ve uncovered who killed Manderson, as this dispatch will show. This was my problem; yours is to decide what to do with it. It directly implicates someone no one suspected, and practically names him as the murderer. So I doubt you’ll publish it before he’s arrested—and I believe it’s illegal to do so afterward until he’s tried and found guilty. You may choose to publish it then, or perhaps use the facts earlier in some way. That’s entirely up to you. In the meantime, would you please contact Scotland Yard and let them see what I’ve written? I’m done with the Manderson mystery, and I wish I’d never touched it. Here follows my dispatch. P.T.

Marlstone, June 16th.

I begin this, my third and likely final dispatch to the *Record* on the Manderson murder, with mixed emotions. I feel a strong sense of relief—because in my two earlier reports, I had to withhold certain facts I’d discovered. Revealing them then might have warned a particular man, possibly allowing him to escape. He’s not just bold—he’s clever, resourceful, and dangerous. Now I can lay those facts bare. But I must admit, I have no desire to tell this story of betrayal and twisted intellect. It leaves a bitter taste, a deep unease about the motive behind the crime—a motive that, I believe, I’ve finally unraveled.

As I mentioned in my first dispatch, I arrived here early Tuesday morning and described the scene: how the body was found, its condition, the utter confusion surrounding the murder, and various local theories. I gave details of Manderson’s household, his habits, and his movements the night before his death. I also noted a detail that may have seemed trivial: a large amount of whisky—more than Manderson ever drank—had vanished from his private decanter since the last time he was seen alive.

The next day, during the inquest, I sent only a summary of the proceedings. A full transcript was prepared by the *Record*’s court stenographers at my request. As I write this, that day is still ongoing. And now, after completing an investigation that has led me straight to the man responsible, I can say with certainty: I know who must answer for Manderson’s death.

Beyond the central puzzle—why Manderson rose so early and left the house to meet his fate—there were two smaller oddities that, I suspect, struck many readers of the press accounts. They were apparent from the start.

First: though the body was found less than thirty yards from the house, no one inside reported hearing any cry or noise during the night. Manderson hadn’t been gagged. The marks on his wrists suggested a struggle. And there had been at least one gunshot. (I say “at least,” because in firearm murders—especially when there’s a fight—the killer often misses once.) This silence was even more puzzling when I learned that Martin, the butler, was a light sleeper with exceptional hearing. His bedroom window faced almost directly toward the shed where the body was found.

Second: Manderson had left his dental plate beside his bed. He had risen, dressed fully—even down to his tie, watch, and chain—and gone outside without remembering to insert the plate he’d worn every day for years. It contained all his upper teeth. There was no sign of panic; even if he had been rushed, he’d be far more likely to forget anything else than this denture. Anyone who uses such a removable plate knows how automatic it is to put it in each morning. Speech, eating, appearance—all depend on it.

Neither of these quirks seemed to lead anywhere at first. But they planted a seed of suspicion in me: something hidden, something deeper, lurking beneath the surface of this already baffling mystery.

With that background, I turn now to the discovery that, within hours of arriving, set me on the right path—one that had been carefully concealed by considerable ingenuity.

I’ve already described Manderson’s bedroom: its sparse furnishings, the contrast with the abundance of clothes and shoes, and the connection between his room and Mrs. Manderson’s. On the upper shelf of the shoe rack, I found the patent leather shoes Manderson had worn the night before. I glanced over them—not expecting a clue, but simply because I happen to be a connoisseur of footwear. All the shoes were impeccably made, of the highest quality. But one pair caught my eye.

They were lightweight dress shoes, thin-soled, without toe caps, beautifully crafted like the rest. But they were old and worn. Still, polished and kept on their trees, they looked neat. What stood out was a tiny split in the leather near the vamp—the area where the laces rise from the upper. This is where stress occurs when a tight shoe is forced onto a foot. Normally, this area is reinforced with strong stitching across the base. In both shoes, that stitching had broken, and the leather underneath had torn. The splits were barely an eighth of an inch long. When the tension was released, the edges closed again, making it invisible to anyone but a trained eye. Even more subtle—only visible under close inspection—was the strain on the stitching joining the upper to the sole. At the toes and outer sides, the stitches had been pulled taut.

These signs could mean only one thing: the shoes had been worn by someone whose feet were too large for them.

It was immediately clear that Manderson always wore well-fitting shoes. He was particular, perhaps even vain, about his narrow, small feet. None of the other shoes in the collection showed similar wear. They belonged to a man who didn’t squeeze into ill-fitting footwear. Someone else had worn these shoes—and recently. The tears were fresh.

The idea that someone had worn them after Manderson’s death was absurd—the body had only been found twenty-six hours earlier. Why would anyone wear them? The idea that someone had borrowed them while Manderson was alive seemed equally unlikely. With so many alternatives, why choose these? Only three men lived at the house: the butler and two secretaries. But I didn’t dwell on these possibilities. My mind was racing. In cases like this, I’ve found it best to let intuition run free. Since stepping off the train in Marlstone, I’d been immersed in every detail of the case. Suddenly, the moment came—the daemon woke.

Let me explain more plainly. It’s a psychological phenomenon familiar to anyone who deals with complex problems. When you grasp the key fact in a tangled situation, your thoughts suddenly reorganize themselves around it—before you’ve even fully understood its significance.

In this case, the moment I thought, “Someone other than Manderson wore these shoes,” a cascade of ideas flooded my mind—each related to that central truth.

Manderson rarely drank much whisky at night. He was never untidy—yet the body was found with cuffs pulled inside the sleeves, shoes unevenly laced. He always washed in the morning—yet he hadn’t. He usually changed into fresh clothing—but wore yesterday’s shirt and collar. His watch was in the wrong pocket, one not lined for it. These points had been noted in my first dispatch, but no one saw their importance.

And then there was the matter of his wife. Manderson had never been open with her about his comings and goings—especially at bedtime, when he rarely spoke to her at all. Yet on this night, he had spoken to her. That was strange. And even stranger: he left his false teeth behind.

All these observations surged into my mind as I examined the shoes. And yet, when I formed the idea—“It wasn’t Manderson in the house that night”—it felt absurd. Of course, Manderson had dined at home and left with Marlowe in the car. People had seen him. But did he return at ten?

That question, too, seemed ridiculous. But I couldn’t dismiss it. It felt like dawn breaking—light slowly spreading across the landscape. I began to analyze each point, trying to understand why a man impersonating Manderson would act so unlike him.

The reason for wearing shoes too small was clear. Footprints are easily analyzed by police. But this man didn’t just avoid leaving his own prints—he ensured Manderson’s were left. His entire plan, if my theory was correct, was to make it seem as if Manderson had been in the house all night. And he succeeded. The shoes were found outside the bedroom door—just as Manderson always left them. The maid polished them and returned them to the shelf later, after the body was discovered.

Then there was the false teeth. If my theory held, the unknown had brought the denture with him and left it in the bedroom—for the same reason: to prove Manderson had been there, had gone to bed. That meant one chilling conclusion: Manderson was already dead before the imposter arrived.

Other evidence supported this. The clothing—trousers, waistcoat, shooting jacket—was in the bedroom. Martin had seen the jacket on the man at the telephone. No one could mistake it. Clearly, the jacket was a crucial part of the plan. The imposter knew Martin would assume it was Manderson at first glance.

But then I realized something I’d missed: **Martin hadn’t seen the man’s face. Nor had Mrs. Manderson.**

Mrs. Manderson, based on her inquest testimony, hadn’t seen him at all. She’d merely spoken to him, half-asleep, continuing a conversation she’d had with her husband an hour earlier. Martin, I realized, had only seen the man’s back—crouched over the phone. The pose was likely mimicked. And the man wore Manderson’s wide-brimmed hat. The shape of the head and neck alone carries a lot of character. If the imposter was roughly Manderson’s build, he needed no further disguise—just the jacket, the hat, and his voice.

I paused, struck by the audacity and precision of the plan. It was nearly foolproof—if the mimicry was good and the nerves steady. With those two things, only a miracle could expose him.

Back to the puzzle. Why enter through the garden window instead of the front door? Because entering the front way would have risked being heard by Martin in the pantry across the hall. A face-to-face encounter was possible.

Then there was the whisky. I hadn’t paid much attention to it at first—whisky vanishes in households with many people. But this disappearance on this night was odd. Martin had been stunned by it. Now it made sense: a man fresh from a violent act—undressing a corpse, preparing for a dangerous deception—might reach for a drink. He likely had one before calling Martin. After succeeding with the trick, he probably had another.

But he knew when to stop. The hardest part was ahead: locking himself in Manderson’s room and staging the scene to look like Manderson had been there. The risk was real—though small, as he likely knew—of Mrs. Manderson waking and seeing him. From her bed, which faced the wall beyond the door, she could only see one of the cupboards near Manderson’s head. And since he knew the household routines, he’d expect her to be asleep. Another advantage: the growing estrangement between the couple. Though they pretended to sleep in connected rooms, everyone knew they were apart. He might hope she’d ignore the presence of her husband.

So, following this hypothesis, I imagined the imposter reaching the bedroom. And then—I gasped—imagined the sound he must have dreaded most: a drowsy voice from the next room.

Mrs. Manderson couldn’t recall exactly what she said at the inquest. She thinks she asked if he’d had a good drive. And what does the imposter do? He doesn’t just reply in Manderson’s voice—he adds a detail. He tells her he’s sent Marlowe to Southampton on an urgent errand—to retrieve information from a man leaving for Paris by steamboat.

Why reveal this? Why mention Marlowe—when Manderson had become increasingly distant from his wife, especially about business?

Now I present my conclusions:

(1) Between ten and eleven o’clock, Manderson was shot—likely far from the house, since no shot was heard.
(2) His body was brought back, stripped, and left by the shed.
(3) Around eleven, a man who wasn’t Manderson—wearing Manderson’s shoes, hat, and jacket—entered the library via the garden window.
(4) He carried Manderson’s trousers, waistcoat, motor coat, the dental plate, and the murder weapon.
(5) He hid these items, rang for Martin, sat at the telephone with his hat on and his back to the door.
(6) He stayed at the phone while Martin was in the room.
(7) Later, he went to Marlowe’s room and placed the revolver—Marlowe’s gun—back in its case.
(8) Then he went to Manderson’s room, left the shoes outside, threw the clothes on a chair, placed the denture by the bed, and chose new clothes, shoes, and a tie from the wardrobe.

Now, the crucial question:

Based on what I knew, I deduced five things about him:

(1) He had been closely associated with Manderson. His behavior with Martin and his conversation with Mrs. Manderson were flawless.
(2) He was physically similar—same height, shoulder width—so his back would look like Manderson’s when seated and hooded. But his feet were larger, though not significantly.
(3) He had talent for mimicry and acting—possibly professional experience.
(4) He knew the household’s routines intimately.
(5) He had a critical need to make it appear that Manderson was alive and in the house past midnight Sunday.

These conclusions were firm. And they pointed to one man.

I now present the facts about John Marlowe:

(1) He had been Manderson’s private secretary for nearly four years, on terms of great intimacy.
(2) Both men were about five feet eleven inches tall, powerfully built. Marlowe, though younger by twenty years, was slightly slimmer. His shoes were roughly one size larger and broader than Manderson’s.
(3) I telegraphed a friend at Oxford—a fellow interested in theater—asking for Marlowe’s record in acting. He replied:
*Marlowe was member O.U.D.S for three years and president 19— played Bardolph, Cleon, and Mercutio. Excelled in character acting and imitations. In great demand at smokers. Hero of some historic hoaxes.*
I’d noticed a photograph on Marlowe’s mantel—himself and two others in Falstaff’s company, inscribed from *The Merry Wives*, stamped by an Oxford photographer.
(4) During his time with Manderson, Marlowe lived as a family member. No one else had such access to the household’s inner workings.
(5) I confirmed beyond doubt that Marlowe arrived in Southampton Monday morning at 6:30, carrying out the mission described by the imposter to Mrs. Manderson. He returned to Marlstone, shocked and horrified by the news.

These facts align perfectly with the five conclusions. The false Manderson was John Marlowe.

“I’m returning the cheque you sent for my work on the Manderson case,” Trent wrote to Sir James Molloy from Munich, where he had arrived immediately after submitting a brief, unremarkable report to the *Record* office. “What I did wasn’t worth a tenth of what you offered. But I wouldn’t hesitate to keep it—if only I hadn’t decided, for reasons I won’t explain, to take no payment at all for this matter. If you don’t mind, please pay for the space at your usual rate and forward the money to a charity that doesn’t thrive on bullying people—assuming such an institution exists. I’ve come here to visit old friends and sort out my thoughts. And the clearest thought that emerges is this: I want something active to do. I can’t paint—not even a fence. Would you consider me as your own correspondent? If you can find me a good assignment, I’ll send you solid reports. After that, I might settle down and work properly.”

Sir James replied by telegram: proceed at once to Kurland and Livonia, where Citizen Browning was stirring up rebellion again, and both town and countryside were ablaze with revolt.

It was a wandering commission, and for two months Trent followed his luck—and it served him well enough. He was the only journalist to witness General Dragilew’s assassination in the streets of Volmar, carried out by an eighteen-year-old girl. He saw buildings burn, men hanged, mobs lynch, and soldiers open fire without mercy. Each day deepened his disgust for the absurdity bred by tyranny. Many nights he slept in danger. Many days he went without food. Yet every morning and evening, he saw her—the woman he loved beyond reason, beyond hope.

He found himself strangely proud of how deeply and stubbornly this feeling held him. It fascinated him as a phenomenon: not merely emotion, but a force that defied logic. It amazed and illuminated him. Nothing like this had ever touched him before. At thirty-two, he was no stranger to passion or longing, but what he’d known came naturally, unforced, untouched by bitterness. He had never been broken by love; yet he remained haunted by its mysteries. He moved through life with quiet respect for certain feminine vulnerabilities, and a profound unease before others’ strength. He had clung to a half-hearted belief that something deeper still waited within him, waiting for the right voice to call it forth—when it would come, if at all, not by seeking, but by fate.

But he hadn’t expected that if the moment came, it might arrive wrapped in darkness.

The two things that stunned him about his feelings for Mabel Manderson were their suddenness and their futility. Before they took hold, he had laughed at the idea of enduring, unrequited love—as if it were some noble boyish fantasy. Now he knew better. And he lived daily in the pain of knowing it.

In his imagination, she always appeared as he first saw her—on the cliffside, unaware, caught in a gesture of wild joy, arms raised in release. That motion had spoken louder than words: her widowhood was freedom, escape from torment. And it confirmed, with terrible clarity, a suspicion already forming in his mind—that her liberation meant happiness with another man. The man she loved.

He couldn’t pinpoint exactly when he’d first suspected this. But the seed must have been planted during his first meeting with Marlowe—his presence, his bearing, the grace and power in him, could easily have captivated any woman not bound by duty. Coupled with Mr. Cupples’ account of the Mandersons’ marriage, the idea had taken root in the silent corners of his mind.

When he began searching for motive after identifying the murderer, he turned away from that grim possibility: that Marlowe—driven by passion, perhaps aware of her suffering—had acted like Bothwell, taking the most damning page from history. But in all his inquiries, in all his later reflections, he found no evidence that could justify such a crime—except one temptation, whose full weight he could not grasp, though he knew it would press hard upon a bold soul whose conscience had somehow been numbed.

If he trusted his judgment, Marlowe was neither mad nor inherently cruel. But that didn’t absolve him. Murder for love was not unknown. Heaven knew! Though modern society, with its comforts and fear of detection, had made such crimes rare among the privileged, they were far from impossible. All it took was a man of courage and intelligence, blinded by obsession, to plan and execute such a deed.

A thousand times, heart heavy with anguish, Trent tried to dismiss the terrifying notion that Mabel Manderson had known of the plot against her husband. That she knew the truth afterward—he could not doubt it. Her collapse when questioned about Marlowe had shattered any lingering hope that there was no bond between them. Worse, it seemed to speak of fear—fear of exposure.

She had read his manuscript, then destroyed it. She had taken his word to protect the secret that threatened her lover’s life.

But the real horror was whether she had known before—whether she had suspected, guessed, even consented. Had she connived?

He could never forget that his first clue to Marlowe’s motive had been his escape route—through her room. At the time, before he’d seen her, he’d considered her complicity likely. He imagined a passionate, unstable woman—ruthless in love and hate—a willing partner, perhaps even the mastermind behind the crime.

Then he met her. Spoke with her. Helped her in her grief. And those suspicions vanished—replaced by shame at their cruelty. He had seen her eyes, heard her voice, felt the atmosphere around her. Trent was one of those who believe they can smell true evil in the air. In her presence, he felt an inner certainty: she was fundamentally good.

That she had briefly given herself over to relief on the cliff—relief from years of stifled sympathy and unfulfilled motherhood—was not a sign of guilt. That she had turned to Marlowe in her loneliness, he believed. But that she had known of his murderous intent—he refused to believe it.

Yet the doubts returned, relentless. He remembered how close Marlowe had been to her—how he prepared in the dead man’s bedroom, just steps from her chamber; how he fled through her window. Had he risked telling her? Or, more likely, played his part while she slept?

He doubted she knew of the disguise when she testified at the inquest—her words sounded honest. But the question refused to die: had she lain awake, listening for footsteps, waiting for a whisper that would tell her it was done?

Could such purity of face and manner hide a heart as black as night—cold, cunning, ruthless?

These thoughts clung to him when he was alone.

Trent worked diligently for Sir James, earning his salary handsomely over six months. Then he returned to Paris, where he resumed his life with renewed vigor. His talents had returned, and he found himself living more contentedly than he had dared expect, surrounded by an odd assortment of friends—French, English, American—artists, poets, journalists, policemen, hoteliers, soldiers, lawyers, merchants, and others. His natural gift for empathy earned him, as it had in his student days, access to circles rarely open to foreigners. He was welcomed into a French family’s home again—just as he had been before. The house was familiar, even down to the wallpaper patterns and furniture. But the young artists of today, _les jeunes_, were different. Shallow, childish, less clever than their predecessors. Their so-called revelations about art and life were trivial compared to the bold truths once claimed by earlier generations.

He believed this until one afternoon, sitting beside a well-fed man at a restaurant. Despite the years of indulgence, Trent recognized him instantly—one of the original _jeunes_, once known as the Hermits of the New Parnassus. They had declared poetry free, talked loudly outside cafés, defied convention. This man now sat in the Ministry of the Interior, decorated and powerful. He told Trent France needed a strong hand. He quoted exact sums paid for betrayals Trent had never heard of.

And then it struck him: it wasn’t _them_ who had changed. It was he. Like his former comrade, he had altered. The world remained the same. Only his vision had dimmed.

Still, he couldn’t say precisely what he had lost—unless it was simply his spirit.

One June morning, descending the Rue des Martyrs, he saw a figure approaching. He glanced around—uncomfortable at the thought of meeting Mr. Bunner again. For some time, he had felt his wound healing under the discipline of work. He thought of Mabel less often, and with less pain. He didn’t want those three days reopened.

But the narrow street offered no escape. Bunner spotted him almost at once.

His easy warmth embarrassed Trent—he had liked the man. They sat long over lunch. Bunner spoke freely, and Trent listened—genuinely pleased, occasionally adding a comment. He enjoyed Bunner’s conversation, with its endless verbal twists, for its own sake.

Bunner was now the chief Continental agent for the Manderson firm, and thoroughly satisfied with his role. After twenty minutes of business talk, he mentioned that Marlowe had joined his father’s company shortly after Manderson’s death. The business was thriving, and Marlowe was already in control. They remained close friends, planning a summer holiday together.

“Jack Marlowe has a natural big head,” Bunner said, “and if he had more experience, I wouldn’t want to face him. He’d beat me every time.”

As Bunner continued, Trent grew increasingly puzzled. Something was wrong. There was no mention of the central figure—Mabel.

Then Bunner added that Marlowe was engaged to an Irish girl, whom he praised enthusiastically.

Trent clenched his hands beneath the table.

What had happened?

His thoughts tumbled. He forced himself to ask directly.

Bunner knew little. Mrs. Manderson had left England soon after settling her husband’s affairs. She’d lived in Italy for a while, then returned recently to London. She had sold the Mayfair house and bought a smaller one in Hampstead, and another in the country. She avoided society.

“And all that money—good hard dollars—just waiting for someone to spend it,” Bunner said, with a touch of sadness. “She’s got money to burn—money to feed birds—and nothing doing. The old man left her more than half his fortune. And think of the impression she could make. She’s beautiful, and the best woman I’ve ever met. But she just can’t seem to learn how to spend money the way it should be spent.”

His words trailed off. Trent was elsewhere—lost in thought.

He excused himself soon after, and they parted cordially.

Half an hour later, Trent was in his studio, mechanically cleaning up. He needed to know. He had to find out what had become of her. He couldn’t approach her—not after their last meeting. He wouldn’t bring back the shame. He doubted he’d even see her again.

But he had to know.

Cupples was in London. Marlowe was there too. And Paris no longer held him.

In twenty-four hours, his ties to the city were severed. He stood at the edge of a leaden sea, gazing toward the gleaming cliffs of Dover.

Though he had instinctively chosen a course from the chaos of his thoughts, he found it stalled at the start. He decided to see Cupples first—he would know more than Bunner. But Cupples was traveling, not due back for a month. Trent had no reason to rush him home.

He wouldn’t confront Marlowe until he’d gathered more information.

He forbade himself to seek out her house in Hampstead. He couldn’t enter it. The mere thought of being seen near it flushed his face.

He stayed at a hotel, rented a studio, and waited—for Cupples, for answers. He tried to lose himself in work, but failed.

After a week, an idea struck him. She had once mentioned a fondness for music.

That evening, he went to the opera. And every night after.

Perhaps he’d see her. And if she noticed him, they could pretend not to recognize each other—anyone might attend the opera.

So he went alone, moving quickly through the crowd in the vestibule. Each night, he left knowing she hadn’t been there.

It became a ritual—both comforting and charged with guilt. He loved music, and in its spell, he found peace.

One night, hurrying through the glittering throng, he felt a light touch on his arm.

An instant certainty flooded through him. He turned.

It was her.

More radiant than ever—free of sorrow, smiling, dressed for the evening. He could not speak.

She breathed quickly too, her eyes bright with daring as she greeted him.

Her words were few.

“I wouldn’t miss a note of _Tristan_,” she said. “Nor should you. Come see me during the interval.” She gave him the number of her box.

The next two months marked a time in Trent’s life he would never recall without a shiver. He crossed paths with Mrs. Manderson half a dozen times, each encounter sharpening his confusion. Her composure—measured, polite, neither cold nor warm—frustrated him. At the opera, he found her seated beside Mrs. Wallace, a lively woman from his childhood, and was stunned to see her woven into his own social world. She’d returned from Italy and somehow drifted into circles that were natural to him by birth and temperament. “I’ve pitched my tent in your hunting-grounds,” she explained lightly. Several of his friends lived nearby.

He remembered little of what he said that evening—only that he had been awkward, flushed, babbling about his travels in the Baltic provinces, speaking more to Mrs. Wallace than to her. The other woman, once agitated when they first met in the vestibule, now appeared perfectly at ease, chatting amiably about her journey, her London life, mutual acquaintances.

But during the final act, as he sat behind them in the box, all thought slipped away. He saw only the curve of her cheek, the sweep of her dark hair, the line of her shoulder, the way her hand rested on the cushion. The black tresses seemed to stretch into an endless forest—deep, untamed, enchanted—a place where danger and desire entwined. When the performance ended, he left pale and quiet, parting with them formally.

Their next meetings—first at a country house, then elsewhere—were more controlled. He matched her reserve, behaving, he believed, with dignity, though inwardly he was consumed by turmoil: bewilderment, regret, longing. He could not fathom her behavior. It was certain she had read his manuscript, understood the implication of his last question at White Gables. Yet she treated him no differently than anyone else—warmly, frankly, as if nothing had ever happened.

And yet, despite her outward calm, he sensed something had been wounded. On rare occasions when they spoke privately, he felt her edge toward the subject—then watched her deftly redirect it. Fear gave her ingenuity. Two decisions took root in him. First, once he finished a commission binding him to London, he would leave—and stay gone. The strain was unbearable. He no longer sought the truth; he craved confirmation that his judgment had been wrong, that he had misread her tears, that he had accused her unjustly, painted himself a fool. He stopped speculating on Marlowe’s motive for killing Manderson. Mr. Cupples returned to London, but Trent asked him nothing. He now knew he had been right in those words—Trent recalled them sharply, spoken with emphasis—“So long as she considered herself bound to him... no power on earth could have persuaded her.” He dined at her uncle’s imposing, somber home in Bloomsbury and spent most of the evening in conversation with a German archaeologist.

His second resolution: never be alone with her again.

Yet when she wrote, asking him to visit her the following afternoon, he did not hesitate. This was an invitation—not a request. A challenge.

She welcomed him with tea, and for a while she joined his slightly fevered talk about current affairs so naturally that he began to hope she had abandoned whatever plan she’d had to confront him. She seemed careless, laughing easily, her smile recalling ancient words about a Princess of Brunswick: “Her mouth has ten thousand charms that touch the soul.” She led him through her beautifully furnished room, pointing out treasures gathered from countless antique shops, chuckling over her bargains. When he asked if she would play one of his favorite pieces—music he’d heard her perform before—she agreed at once.

She played with flawless technique and deep feeling, stirring emotions he hadn’t known he still carried. “You are a musician born,” he said quietly when the final note faded. “I knew that before I ever heard you.”

“I’ve played since I can remember,” she replied simply, turning to him with a smile. “It’s been a great comfort. When did you first sense music in me? Oh, of course—the opera. But that wouldn’t prove much, would it?”

“No,” he murmured, still caught in the echo of the music. “I think I knew the moment I saw you.” Then, startled by his own words, he froze. For the first time, the past had surfaced.

A silence followed. Mrs. Manderson looked at him, then quickly glanced away. Color rose in her cheeks. She pursed her lips as if preparing to whistle. Then, with a defiant lift of her shoulders—one he recognized well—she rose abruptly from the piano and sat across from him.

“That remark,” she began slowly, staring at the tip of her shoe, “will serve as well as anything to bring us to what I wanted to say. I asked you here today because I couldn’t endure it any longer. Ever since you left me at White Gables, I’ve told myself it didn’t matter what you thought of me in that matter—that you wouldn’t speak to others about it, given what you’d told me about suppressing your manuscript. I asked myself how it could possibly matter. But I always knew it did. It mattered terribly. Because what you thought was not true.” She raised her eyes, meeting his gaze steadily. Trent returned her look, his face utterly still.

“Since I began to know you,” he said, “I’ve ceased to believe it.”

“Thank you,” she whispered, blushing deeply. Then, playing with a glove, she added, “But I want you to know what _was_ true.”

“I didn’t know if I’d ever see you again,” she continued, voice softening. “But I knew that if I did, I had to speak to you about this. I thought it wouldn’t be hard—you seemed understanding. And a married woman isn’t expected to struggle with such things as a young girl might. But we met again, and I discovered it was very difficult indeed. You made it difficult.”

“How?” he asked gently.

“I don’t know,” she said. “But yes—I do. It was because you treated me as if you’d never suspected a thing. I expected you to turn on me with that hard, terrible look you wore when you asked me that last question at White Gables—do you remember? Instead, you were just like any other acquaintance. Just”—she hesitated, spreading her hands—“nice. You know. After the first time at the opera, when I spoke to you, I went home wondering if you’d really recognized me. I thought you might have seen my face but not remembered who I was.”

A quiet laugh escaped Trent, though he said nothing.

She smiled apologetically. “Well, I couldn’t recall whether you’d named me. I thought it might be so. But the next time, at the Iretons’, you did say my name—so I knew. And a dozen times in those days, I almost brought myself to tell you—but never quite. I began to feel you wouldn’t let me. That you’d deflect the subject if I approached it. Wasn’t I right? Tell me, please.” He nodded. “But why?” He remained silent.

“Well,” she said, “I’ll finish what I came to say—and then you’ll tell me why you made it so hard. When I realized you wouldn’t let me speak of it, I became more determined than ever. I suppose you didn’t realize I’d insist—even if you discouraged me. I doubt I could have done it if I’d been guilty, as you thought. You walked into my parlor today, thinking I wouldn’t dare. Well, now you see.”

She had shed all hesitation. She had, as she often said, talked herself into enthusiasm, and in her fervor to clear the misunderstanding that had haunted her, she felt in command of the moment.

“I’m going to tell you the story of the mistake you made,” she continued, as Trent, hands clasped between his knees, stared at her enigmatically. “You’ll have to believe it, Mr. Trent—it’s entirely true, with its confusions, hidden motives, misunderstandings, and perfectly ordinary errors people take for facts without blinking. Please understand: I don’t blame you—not at all—for jumping to the conclusion you did. You knew I was estranged from my husband. You knew what that often means. You guessed, before I even told you, that he had taken offense. I foolishly tried to explain it away. I gave you the reason I’d first given myself—before I saw the truth: that he was disappointed in me because I couldn’t lead society brilliantly. That was true—he was. But I could see you weren’t convinced. You’d already guessed what took me much longer to see—because I knew how irrational it sounded. Yes: my husband was jealous of John Marlowe. You saw it.”

Then, when you revealed you’d seen it, I behaved like a fool. It was a blow—when I thought the humiliation was over, that his delusion had died with him. You practically asked me if Marlowe was my lover, Mr. Trent—I *have* to say it, because I want you to understand why I broke down. You took that for a confession. You thought I was guilty—and perhaps even complicit in the murder. That hurt. But maybe you couldn’t have thought otherwise. I don’t know.”

Trent, who had not moved his eyes from her face, lowered his head. He kept it bowed as she spoke.

“But it was shock and grief that made me break down—the memory of all the pain that mad suspicion had caused me. And when I regained control, you were gone.”

She rose and walked to an escritoire by the window, unlocked a drawer, and pulled out a sealed envelope.

“This is the manuscript you left with me,” she said. “I’ve read it over and over. I’ve always wondered, like everyone else, at your skill in these matters.” A faint, teasing smile touched her lips and vanished. “I thought it was splendid, Mr. Trent—I almost forgot it was my own story, I was so absorbed. And now, holding it, I want to thank you—for your generous, chivalrous choice to sacrifice your triumph rather than endanger a woman’s reputation. If all had been as you supposed, the truth would have emerged when the police investigated the case you handed them. Believe me, I understood exactly what you did—and I never stopped being grateful, even when I felt crushed by your suspicion.”

As she spoke, her voice trembled slightly, her eyes bright. Trent noticed nothing. His head remained bent. He seemed not to hear. She placed the envelope in his open palm resting on his knee. There was a tenderness in the gesture that made him look up.

“Can you—” he began slowly.

She raised her hand. “No, Mr. Trent. Let me finish before you speak. It’s such a relief to have broken the ice at last. I want to end this story while I still feel the triumph of beginning it.” She sank onto the sofa. “I’m telling you something no one else knows. Everyone knew something had come between us—I did everything to hide it. But no one guessed what my husband believed. People who know me don’t think such things. And his idea was so absurdly wrong. Let me tell you what really happened.”

Mr. Marlowe and I had been friendly since he came to us. Despite his intelligence—my husband said he had a sharper mind than any man he knew—I saw him as a boy. I’m older than he is, and he had a gentle lack of ambition that made me feel it more. One day, my husband asked what I liked best about Marlowe. Without thinking, I said, ‘His manners.’ He turned very dark at that. After a pause, he said, ‘Yes, Marlowe is a gentleman; that’s so,’ without looking at me.

Nothing was said again until about a year ago, when I discovered Marlowe had fallen desperately in love with an American girl. To my disgust, he chose the most worthless girl among all we knew. She was wealthy, spoiled by her parents, beautiful, educated, athletic—what they call a woman-athlete—and cared only for her own amusement. She was the most unprincipled flirt I’ve ever known—clever too. Everyone knew it, and Marlowe must have heard it. But she made a fool of him—completely. I don’t know how she did it, but I can imagine. She liked him, of course, but she was clearly playing with him. The whole affair was so ridiculous, I grew furious. One day, I asked him to row me on the lake—at our house by Lake George. We’d never been alone together before. In the boat, I spoke to him kindly. He listened well, but didn’t believe me. He had the gall to say I misunderstood Alice’s nature. When I hinted at his prospects—he had little of his own—he said if she loved him, he could make his way in the world. That may have been true, with his talents and connections—he’s well-connected, popular. But his awakening came soon after.

My husband helped me out of the boat. He joked with Marlowe about something—I remember. Throughout what followed, he never changed his manner toward him. That’s why it took me so long to grasp what he thought. But to me, he was distant, silent—not angry. After dinner, he spoke to me only once. Marlowe was describing a horse he’d bought for his Kentucky farm, and my husband looked at me and said, ‘Marlowe may be a gentleman, but he seldom quits a loser in a horse-trade.’ I was surprised, but at the time—and even the next morning when I found them together—I didn’t understand his meaning.

That morning, Marlowe received a sweet note from the girl announcing her engagement. He looked wretched at breakfast. I thought he was ill. Later, I went to his study and asked what was wrong. He said nothing, just handed me the note and turned to the window. I was glad it was over, but sorry for him. I don’t remember what I said, but I remember placing my hand on his arm as he stared at the garden. Then my husband appeared at the door with papers. He glanced at us, then walked quietly back to his study. I thought he might have heard me comforting Marlowe—and that it was kind of him to slip away. Marlowe didn’t see or hear him. My husband left that morning for the West while I was out. Even then, I didn’t understand. He often left suddenly for business.

It wasn’t until he returned a week later that I grasped the truth. He looked pale, strange. As soon as he saw me, he asked where Marlowe was. The tone of his question told me everything instantly.

I nearly gasped. I was furious. You know, Mr. Trent, I don’t think I’d have minded if anyone thought me capable of leaving my husband openly for another man. I might have done it. But this crude suspicion—of a man he trusted—and the idea of secrecy! It enraged me. Every shred of pride in me tightened till I quivered. I swore then and there I would never show, by word or deed, that I knew he thought such a thing. I would behave exactly as I always had—and I did, until the very end. Though I knew a wall had formed between us—one that could never be crossed—even if he begged forgiveness, I never showed I noticed the change.

And so it went. I could never go through such a time again. My husband remained coldly polite when we were alone—only when necessary. He never mentioned what he suspected, but I felt it, and he knew I did. We were both stubborn. He was friendlier than ever to Marlowe—Heaven knows why. I thought he was planning revenge, but that was just a guess. Certainly Marlowe never knew what was suspected of him. We stayed friends, though we never discussed intimate matters again. I made sure to see him as often as before. Then we came to England, to White Gables—and after that—my husband’s tragic end.”

She gestured with her hand as if closing a chapter. “You know the rest—so much more than any other man,” she added, glancing up at him with a curious expression.

Trent was struck by that look, but it passed quickly. Inside, he was overwhelmed with relief. All color had returned to his face. Long before she finished, he had known the truth of her story—had doubted his own earlier assumptions from the start, built on foundations that now seemed fragile.

“I don’t know how to begin my apologies,” he said. “There are no words to express how ashamed and humiliated I feel, realizing how crude and arrogant my suspicion was. Yes, I suspected you. I almost forgot I’d ever been such a fool. Almost—but not quite. Sometimes, alone, I’ve remembered it and despised it. I’ve tried to imagine the real facts. I’ve tried to excuse myself.”

She interrupted sharply. “Nonsense! Be sensible, Mr. Trent. You’d seen me only twice before coming to me with your solution. If you’re going to talk of folly, it’s folly for a man like you to pretend a woman like me is so transparent—so obviously innocent—that you’d ignore strong evidence against her after seeing her only twice.”

“What do you mean by ‘a man like me’?” he demanded, almost fiercely. “Do you think I lack normal instincts? I don’t say you seem simple or open—what Mr. Calvin Bunner calls a case of open-work. I don’t say a stranger wouldn’t suspect you of wrongdoing if the evidence were strong. But a man who, after knowing you, could link you with the horror I imagined—such a man is a fool. A fool afraid to trust his senses. And as for making it hard for you to speak—yes, you’re right. It was moral cowardice. I knew you wanted to clear this up. I recoiled at the idea of discussing my error. I tried to show you, through my actions, that it was as if it had never happened. I hoped you’d forgive me without words. I can’t forgive myself. And yet—if you could know—” He paused. “Will you accept this as an apology? The coarsest sackcloth, the grittiest ashes. I didn’t mean to get emotional,” he finished lamely.

Mrs. Manderson laughed—a sudden, cascading sound, full of joy. He knew that laugh well—had often tried to amuse her just for the pleasure of hearing it.

“But I love to see you worked up,” she said. “The way you always crash down as soon as you realize you’re airborne is delightful. Oh, we’re both laughing. What a triumphant end to our explanations—after all my dread of facing you. Now it’s over. You know. And we’ll never speak of it again.”

“I hope not,” Trent said, relieved. “If you’re willing to be so generous, I’m not noble enough to demand your lightning bolts. And now, Mrs. Manderson, I should go. Changing the subject after this would be like playing puss-in-the-corner after an earthquake.” He stood.

“You’re right,” she said. “But wait. There’s one more thing—part of the same matter. We should pick up all the pieces now.” She took the envelope from the table. “I want to speak about this.”

His brows furrowed. “So do I, if you do,” he said slowly. “I need to know one thing.”

“Tell me.”

“If you insist,” Trent said, “I suppose I’ll do as you ask. But I’d far rather write this letter when I’m alone. Still, if it must be done here—bring me paper whiter than starlight, or the hand of a singing angel. I mean plain note-paper, nothing stamped with your address. Don’t think I’m not making a sacrifice. Honestly, I’ve never felt less inclined to write in my life.”

She smiled at him, amused and patient. “What shall I say?” he asked, pen poised above the blank sheet. “Shall I compare him to a summer’s day? What *shall* I say?”

“Say whatever comes to mind,” she suggested gently.

He shook his head. “What I really want to say—what I’ve wanted to shout to every person I’ve met for the past twenty-four hours—is ‘Mabel and I are engaged, and all is well.’ But that wouldn’t make much sense as the opening line of a letter meant to sound formal, even ominous. I’ve managed ‘Dear Mr. Marlowe’ so far. What now?”

“I’m sending you a manuscript,” she prompted, “one I thought you might find interesting.”

“Do you realize,” he said, “that in that sentence only two words have more than one syllable? This letter is supposed to impress—not comfort. We need long words.”

“I don’t see why,” she replied. “It’s customary, I know, but why? I’ve received countless letters from lawyers and businessmen, always beginning with ‘with reference to our communication’ or something equally ponderous. They go on like that for pages. Yet when I meet them in person, they speak plainly enough. It seems absurd.”

“It’s not absurd to them,” Trent said, putting down his pen as if relieved, then rising. “Let me explain. Our people, generally speaking, aren’t fond of using their minds. We get by with a limited, simple vocabulary. Long words are unusual—and like anything unusual, they’re either hilarious or deeply solemn. Take ‘intelligent anticipation,’ for example. If someone used that phrase in France or Germany, no one would blink. But here? It’s become a national joke. Everyone laughs when they hear it in speeches or newspapers. Why? Because it’s made of two long words. The idea behind it is as dull as cold mutton. Then there’s ‘terminological inexactitude’—how we all roared at that! And the whole humor lies in the length of the words. Same thing when we want to sound serious—we reach for big words. A solicitor who starts a sentence with ‘pursuant to the instructions communicated to our representative’ feels he’s earning his fee. Don’t laugh—it’s true. Continental Europeans don’t feel that way. They care about ideas, and so even shopkeepers and peasants use a vocabulary that would baffle most Britons. I once dined with a Paris cabman friend. We were at a grim little restaurant near the central post office—full of drivers and porters. Conversation was lively, and I noticed how effortlessly words like ‘functionary,’ ‘unforgettable,’ ‘exterminate,’ and ‘independence’ flew across the table. These weren’t intellectuals—just ordinary, cheerful, red-faced men. Mind you,” he added quickly as Mrs. Manderson stepped forward and picked up his pen, “I mention this only to illustrate my point. I’m not suggesting cabmen should be philosophers. I agree with Keats—happy is England, sweet her artless cabmen, enough their simple loveliness for me. But when we talk about the people who shape the country’s industrial mind… well, do you know—”

“Oh no, no, no!” Mrs. Manderson interrupted, stepping away from the desk. “I don’t know anything right now, except that your talking must stop if we’re ever going to finish this letter to Mr. Marlowe. You’re not getting out of it. Come!”

She placed the pen firmly back in his hand. Trent looked at it with distaste. “Don’t discourage me from talking,” he said, sounding defeated. “Believe me, men who don’t talk are worse company than those who do. Beware of silent natures. I confess—I’m avoiding writing this. It feels almost indecent. It’s mixing two moods: writing a letter of grave intent while sitting in the same room with you.”

She guided him back to his chair before the escritoire and gently pushed him down. “Just try,” she said. “I want to see what you write—and I want it sent immediately. I could be content to let things rest, but you say you must uncover the truth. And if you must, I want it settled soon. Do it now—you know you can—and I’ll send it off the moment it’s ready. Don’t you ever feel that urge—the desperate need to seal the envelope, drop it in the post, and be free of it forever? So you can’t recall it, so you can stop worrying?”

“I’ll do as you wish,” he said, turning to the paper and dating it from his hotel.

Mrs. Manderson watched him, her eyes soft with affection. She reached out as if to smooth his unruly hair, but stopped herself. Instead, she walked silently to the piano and began to play softly.

Ten minutes passed before Trent spoke. “If he refuses to reply—says he won’t speak?”

She glanced over her shoulder. “Of course he won’t take that stance. He’ll answer—to keep you from exposing him.”

“But I won’t expose him anyway,” Trent said. “You wouldn’t allow it—you said so. Besides, I won’t, even if you did. The case is too uncertain now.”

“But poor Mr. Marlowe doesn’t know that,” she laughed.

Trent sighed. “What strange codes of honor we have!” he murmured. “There are things I’d do without hesitation—like giving a man who insulted me a black eye, or swearing violently after stubbing my toe in the dark—that would shame you if you did them. And now you’re calmly asking me to bluff Marlowe with a threat I don’t intend—a tactic even the devil himself wouldn’t use in the depths of guilt. No, I won’t do it.”

He returned to writing. The lady, smiling indulgently, continued her quiet music.

A few minutes later, Trent paused. “At last—I’m finished. Want to read it?”

She hurried across the dimly lit room and turned on the reading lamp beside the desk. Leaning over his shoulder, she read:

_You will perhaps remember that we met, under unfortunate circumstances, in June of last year at Marlstone._

_On that occasion it was my duty, as a journalist, to investigate independently the circumstances surrounding the death of Sigsbee Manderson. I conducted my inquiry and reached certain conclusions. You may learn what those conclusions were from the enclosed manuscript—originally written as a dispatch for my newspaper. For reasons I need not explain, I decided at the last moment not to publish them nor inform you directly. Only two people besides myself know the contents._

At this point, Mrs. Manderson lifted her eyes from the page. Her brows furrowed slightly. “Two persons?” she asked, voice low.

“My uncle is the other,” Trent said. “I told him everything last night. Have you any objection? I’ve always felt uneasy keeping it from him—he expected to hear all I discovered, and my silence seemed like secrecy. Now it’s time to clear it up. There’s no question of protecting you. I wanted him to know the full truth. He’s shrewd in his own way, and I’d like him with me when I meet Marlowe. I have a feeling two heads will be better than one on my side.”

She sighed. “Yes, of course—uncle should know. I hope no one else does.” She squeezed his hand. “I just want that horror buried—deep. I’m happy now, dear, but I’ll be happier still when you’ve satisfied your curious mind, uncovered everything, and sealed it beneath the earth.”

She resumed reading.

_Quite recently, however, new facts have come to light which have changed my decision. I do not intend to publish what I found, but I have resolved to approach you directly and request a private statement. If you have anything to say that might alter the understanding of events, I can see no reason why you should withhold it._

_I expect, therefore, to hear from you when and where I may call upon you—unless you prefer the meeting to take place at my hotel. In either case, I insist that Mr. Cupples, whom you will recall and who has read the enclosed document, be present.—Faithfully yours,_

_Philip Trent._

“What a stiff letter!” she remarked. “I’m sure you couldn’t have made it stiffer in your own rooms.”

Trent slipped the letter and manuscript into a long envelope. “Yes,” he said. “I think it’ll make him sit up sharply. And this mustn’t fail. Best to send a special messenger with strict orders to deliver it personally. If he’s not home, it shouldn’t be left.”

She nodded. “I’ll arrange it. Wait here a moment.”

When she returned, Trent was rummaging through the music cabinet. She sank onto the carpet beside him, her dark dress spreading around her like a shadow.

“Tell me something, Philip,” she said softly. “If it’s among the few things I know.”

“When you saw your uncle last night,” she continued, “did you tell him about—about us?”

“No,” he answered. “I remembered you said nothing should be told. It’s for you to decide—when, if ever, we reveal it to the world.”

“Then will you tell him?” she asked, looking down at her clasped hands.

“I wish you to tell him,” she said. “Perhaps you’ll understand why if you think about it... There! That’s settled.”

She raised her eyes to his. For a long moment, they sat in silence.

Eventually, Trent leaned back in the deep chair. “What a world,” he murmured. “Mabel, will you play something on the piano—something that expresses pure joy? Not feverish, not thorny, but real joy—joy that has chosen the universe? It won’t last long, so let’s take all we can from it.”

She rose and went to the piano. After a pause, she struck a few chords, then launched into the final movement of Beethoven’s Ninth Symphony—the theme that sounds like the gates of heaven opening.

A heavy oak desk stood by the window in a spacious room overlooking St. James’s Park from an elevated position. The space was large and tastefully furnished—evidence of refined taste—but carried the unmistakable air of a bachelor’s retreat, untouched by domestic warmth. John Marlowe unlocked the desk and pulled out a thick, sturdy envelope from the back compartment.

“I understand,” he said to Mr. Cupples, “that you’ve read this.”

“I read it for the first time two days ago,” replied Mr. Cupples, seated on a sofa, his face gentle as he scanned the room with quiet interest. “We’ve discussed it thoroughly.”

Marlowe turned to Trent. “Here is your manuscript,” he said, placing the envelope on the table. “I’ve gone over it three times. I don’t believe there’s another man who could have uncovered as much of the truth as you’ve laid bare here.”

Trent ignored the compliment. He sat beside the table, staring into the fire, his long legs crossed beneath him. “You mean,” he said, pulling the envelope closer, “that there’s still more to reveal. We’re ready to hear it whenever you are. I expect it will be a long story—and the longer, the better, so far as I’m concerned. I want to understand everything. What we’d both like, I think, is some background on Manderson—your relationship with him. From the beginning, it seemed to me that the dead man’s character had to be central to what happened.”

“You were right,” Marlowe replied grimly. He crossed the room and settled onto the corner of a tall, cushioned fender. “I’ll begin where you suggest.”

“I should tell you first,” Trent said, meeting his eyes directly, “that while I’m here listening, I haven’t yet abandoned the conclusions I’ve drawn in this report.” He tapped the envelope. “This is a defense you’re about to make—I assume you understand that?”

“Perfectly.” Marlowe remained calm, composed, a man transformed from the worn, nervous figure Trent had known at Marlstone eighteen months earlier. His frame was lean and strong, held with the precision of physical discipline. His brow was open, his blue eyes clear—though when he paused, gathering his thoughts, they still carried the same unsettling look Trent had noticed during their first encounter. Only the lines around his mouth betrayed tension, a sign he was aware of his peril and prepared to face it.

“Sigsbee Manderson was not a man of normal mind,” Marlowe began quietly. “Most of the wealthy Americans I met had risen through abnormal greed, relentless industry, overpowering drive, or sheer luck. None possessed exceptional intellects. Manderson shared those traits—he loved accumulating wealth, worked ceaselessly, wielded a dominant will, and enjoyed his share of fortune. But what made him unique was his mental power. In America, people might say his defining trait was ruthlessness—but many others would have done just as little for others if they’d been capable of plotting such schemes.

“I’m not saying Americans aren’t clever—they’re ten times sharper than we English, as a nation. But I never met anyone else who combined such foresight, memory, and intellectual stamina as Manderson did in his business dealings. They called him the ‘Napoleon of Wall Street’ often enough in the papers. But few understood how accurate the title was. He never forgot a fact that might serve him. He studied business data systematically—just as Napoleon once digested military intelligence—reviewing summaries prepared for him at regular intervals, always within reach. He could assess coal, wheat, railroads, or any market at a moment’s notice. And then he would devise plans bolder and more ingenious than anyone else. People learned not to expect the obvious from Manderson; what he actually did was almost always a surprise. That unpredictability was key to his success. When the Street knew the old man was out with his gun, it shook. Opponents often surrendered without a fight—like Colonel Crockett’s coon in the old tale. The scheme I’m about to describe would take most men weeks. Manderson could plot it, down to the last detail, while shaving.

“I used to wonder if his distant Indian blood—the line so faint it was nearly lost—might explain his cunning and cruelty. Strangely, no one but himself and I knew it existed. It came to light when he asked me to research his family history. In tracing it, I discovered he descended from the Iroquois chief Montour and his French wife—a woman of fierce reputation who ruled tribal politics in the wilderness two centuries ago. The Mandersons had been active in the fur trade along Pennsylvania’s frontier, and several had married Native women. Other strains may have flowed into Manderson’s bloodline through earlier or later unions—some ancestors were untraceable, and generations passed before civilization reached the region. My research led me to believe that American bloodlines contain a great deal of indigenous ancestry, widely dispersed. Newer families intermarried with older ones, and many took pride in their native heritage. But Manderson saw mixed blood as a disgrace—a belief, I suspect, intensified after the Civil War and the rise of racial tensions. He was stunned when I told him, and desperate to hide it. Naturally, I never revealed it while he lived—I don’t think he believed I would. But since then, I’ve wondered if that discovery marked a turning point in his attitude toward me. It happened about a year before his death.”

“Did Manderson,” Mr. Cupples asked suddenly, catching both men off guard, “have any clear religious beliefs?”

Marlowe hesitated. “None I ever heard of,” he said. “Worship or prayer were foreign to him, as far as I could tell. I never heard him mention religion. I doubt he truly sensed God, or could experience Him emotionally. But as a child, he’d received a strong moral upbringing rooted in faith. His private life, in conventional terms, was blameless. He was almost ascetic—except for smoking. I lived with him four years and never heard him utter a direct lie, even though he constantly practiced deception in other ways. Can you imagine someone who freely used every trick of the market to mislead others, yet refused to speak a falsehood—even on trivial matters? Manderson was like that. And he wasn’t alone. You might compare it to a soldier who is personally honest but stops at nothing to deceive the enemy. The rules of war allow it—and so, in their minds, do the rules of business. For them, it’s always wartime.”

“It’s a sad world,” Mr. Cupples observed.

“As you say,” Marlowe agreed. “Now, I was saying that one could trust Manderson’s word when he gave it plainly. The first time I ever heard him lie outright was the night he died—and hearing it, I believe, saved me from being hanged for his murder.”

Marlowe stared at the ceiling light. Trent shifted uneasily in his chair. “Before we get to that,” he said, “will you tell us exactly what your relationship with Manderson was during the years you worked for him?”

“We were on excellent terms throughout,” Marlowe replied. “Not friendship—he wasn’t the kind to form close bonds—but a solid, trusting relationship between employer and secretary. I joined him shortly after graduating from Oxford. I was meant to enter my father’s business, but he suggested I travel for a few years. So I took this post, which promised variety and experience. The year stretched into four before it ended. The job came through something I’d never have thought a qualification: chess.”

At the word, Trent clapped his hands together with a low exclamation. The others looked at him in surprise.

“Chess!” repeated Trent, rising and stepping toward Marlowe. “Do you know what struck me the first time we met? Your eyes, Mr. Marlowe. I couldn’t place them then—but now I know. They were the eyes of Nikolay Korchagin, the great Russian master, whom I once shared a railway carriage with for two days. I thought I’d never forget the look of a chess player’s gaze after that—but I couldn’t name it when I saw it in you. I beg your pardon,” he added abruptly, resuming his reserved posture.

“I’ve played since childhood,” Marlowe said simply. “With good players. It’s hereditary, if you can call it a gift. At Oxford, I was nearly as strong as anyone. I devoted most of my mind to chess, the O.U.D.S., and general mischief. Oxford, as you probably know, offers endless distractions—and authorities encourage them. Near the end of my final term, Dr. Munro of Queen’s—whom I’d never beaten—summoned me. He said I played a decent game. I thanked him. Then he asked, ‘They say you hunt too.’ I said, ‘Now and then.’ He asked, ‘Is there anything else you can do?’ I said, ‘Not really,’ not liking the tone—he had a way of making people defensive. He grunted, then mentioned inquiries were underway for a wealthy American businessman seeking an English secretary. Manderson was the name. He’d never heard it—understandable, since he never read newspapers and hadn’t slept outside the college in thirty years. If I could improve my spelling—‘rub up your spelling,’ he put it—I might have a chance. Chess, riding, and an Oxford education were the only essentials.”

“And so I became Manderson’s secretary. For a long time, I enjoyed it. Serving an energetic American tycoon in his prime meant few dull moments. It also gave me independence. Around that time, my father faced serious financial setbacks, and I was glad to avoid relying on him. After the first year, Manderson doubled my salary. ‘It’s big money,’ he said, ‘but I guess I don’t lose.’ By then, I was doing far more than riding with him in the mornings or playing chess at night. I managed his houses, his Ohio farm, his Maine hunting lodge, his horses, his cars, his yacht. I became a walking railroad guide and a connoisseur of cigars. I was always learning.

“So now you understand my role in the final years of my time with Manderson. It was, overall, a satisfying life. Busy, varied, interesting—time for leisure, and money to spend. Once, I made a fool of myself over a girl. That was unpleasant—but it taught me to appreciate Mrs. Manderson.” He nodded respectfully toward Mr. Cupples. “She may choose to tell you about it. As for her husband, he never changed his stance toward me, despite the shift in his behavior in his final months. He treated me well—generously, though coldly—and I never felt he regretted our arrangement. That consistency made the revelation all the more shocking when, on the night of his death, I realized how deeply Manderson hated me.”

Trent and Mr. Cupples exchanged a glance.

“You never suspected he disliked you before?” Trent asked.

“And to what did you attribute this change?” Mr. Cupples added simultaneously.

“I never guessed until that night,” Marlowe answered. “How long it had existed, I don’t know. I can’t imagine why it was there. After his death, I considered it must have been madness—a delusion where he believed I was plotting against him, as lunatics sometimes do. Some insane conviction must have driven it. But who can grasp the depths of a madman’s mind? Can you picture a man condemning himself to death to deliver someone he hates to the gallows?”

Mr. Cupples stiffened. “So you’re saying Manderson caused his own death?”

Trent glanced at him impatiently and returned his focus to Marlowe, whose face had relaxed slightly in the act of speaking.

“I do,” Marlowe said firmly, meeting the questioner’s gaze. Mr. Cupples nodded slowly.

“Before we go further,” the old gentleman said, adopting a tone of academic detachment, “one might note that the state of mind you attribute to Manderson—”

“Let’s hear the story first,” Trent interrupted gently, laying a hand on Mr. Cupples’s arm. “You were telling us,” he continued, turning to Marlowe, “how things stood between you and Manderson. Now, will you tell us what happened that night?”

Marlowe flushed slightly at the subtle emphasis on “facts.” He straightened.

“Bunner and I dined with Mr. and Mrs. Manderson that Sunday evening,” he began, speaking carefully. “It was like any other dinner among the four of us. Manderson was quiet and dark, as we’d grown accustomed. We kept the conversation going. We left the table around nine. Mrs. Manderson went to the drawing-room, Bunner went to the hotel to visit an acquaintance. Manderson asked me to join him in the orchard behind the house, saying he wanted to talk. We walked the path, out of earshot. As he smoked, he spoke in his usual cool, deliberate manner. He’d never seemed more rational—or more amiable. He said he needed me to do him an important service. A major operation was underway. It was secret. Bunner knew nothing. The less I knew, the better. He wanted me to follow his orders exactly, without questioning.”

“This was typical of Manderson’s method,” Marlowe continued. “When he needed a tool, he’d say so. He’d used me in that way a dozen times. I assured him I could be trusted. ‘Right now?’ he asked. I said, of course.”

“He nodded. ‘There’s a man in England involved in this with me. He was supposed to leave tomorrow for Paris by the noon boat from Southampton to Havre. His name is George Harris—at least, that’s the alias he’s using. Do you remember that name?’ ‘Yes,’ I said. ‘Last week, when I went to London, you asked me to book a cabin under that name. I gave you the ticket.’ ‘Here it is,’ he said, pulling it from his pocket.”

“Then,” Marlowe continued, “he said—word for word, as best I recall—‘George Harris cannot leave England tomorrow. I need him here. I need Bunner here. But someone must go by that boat and carry certain papers to Paris. Otherwise, the whole plan collapses. Will you go?’ I said, ‘Certainly. I’m here to obey orders.’”

“He bit his cigar. ‘That’s good—but these aren’t ordinary orders. Not something you’d ask of a man in routine duty. Here’s the point: the deal I’m working on requires that neither I nor anyone connected to me appear publicly yet. That’s vital. But the people I’m opposing know your face as well as mine. If your identity is linked to crossing to Paris at this time—if it’s known you met certain people—then the game is over.’”

He threw away his cigar and looked at me.

“I didn’t like it, but I liked failing Manderson even less. I said lightly, ‘I suppose I’ll have to disguise myself. I’ll do my best.’ I mentioned I used to be good at makeup.”

“He nodded. ‘Good. I thought you wouldn’t let me down.’ Then he gave instructions. ‘Take the car now and head for Southampton—no train fits. You’ll drive all night. You should arrive by six a.m., barring accidents. But when you do, go straight to the Bedford Hotel and ask for George Harris. If he’s there, tell him you’re replacing him, and ask him to phone me immediately. It’s critical he knows as soon as possible. But if he’s not there, that means he got my wire today and didn’t come to Southampton. In that case, forget him. Just wait for the boat. Leave the car at a garage under a false name—mine mustn’t be used. Change your appearance—however you like, as long as it’s convincing. Travel as George Harris. Pretend to be anything you wish, but stay silent. Upon arrival, check into the Hotel St Petersbourg. You’ll receive a message addressed to George Harris—telling you where to deliver the wallet I’ll give you. The wallet is locked. Guard it closely. Have you got it all?’”

“I repeated it. I asked if I’d return from Paris after delivering the wallet. ‘As soon as you like,’ he said. ‘But whatever happens—don’t contact me at any stage. If you don’t get the message right away, wait—days, if necessary. No communication. Understand? Now get ready. I’ll drive with you partway. Hurry.’”

“That’s the substance of what he said,” Marlowe concluded. “I went to my room, changed clothes, packed a few essentials in a kit-bag. My mind was racing—not from the task itself, but from its suddenness. I remember telling you last time,” he said, turning to Trent, “that Manderson loved theatricality. He enjoyed mystery and drama. I told myself this was pure Manderson.”

“I hurried downstairs, retrieved the car from the garage behind the house. As I brought it round, I remembered something troubling—I had only a few shillings in my pocket.”

“For weeks, I’d been short on cash—living temporarily on borrowed money. While with Manderson, I’d been careless with finances. I was sociable, made many friends—some from a New York set with idle fortunes. Though I was well paid, I hadn’t indulged much. Until I started speculating—out of curiosity. An old story, especially on Wall Street. I thought it easy. I was lucky at first. I’d always be prudent. Then came the week I drowned. In one stretch, I lost everything—as Bunner put it when I confessed. I owed money. I’d learned my lesson. I went to Manderson, told him what I’d done. He listened with a grim smile, then advanced me a sum to cover my debts—his nearest approach to sympathy. ‘Don’t play the markets again,’ he said.”

“So on that Sunday night, Manderson knew I had no money. He knew Bunner knew it too. He may have known I’d borrowed extra from Bunner for pocket money, since my next paycheck would be small due to my advance. Keep that in mind.”

“I returned to the library and told him about the expense issue.”

“What followed gave me my first hint something was wrong. As soon as I mentioned ‘expenses,’ his hand instinctively moved to his left hip-pocket—where he always kept a case with about a hundred pounds in notes. This habit was so ingrained that I was startled when he suddenly stopped himself. Then, to my shock, he swore under his breath. I’d never heard him do that before—but Bunner had told me he’d recently shown irritation like this when alone. I wondered: Had he misplaced his note-case? But it didn’t matter—because the week before, when I’d gone to London, I’d withdrawn a thousand pounds from his bankers—all in small denominations, as he requested. I didn’t know why he needed so much cash, but I knew the packets were in his locked desk in the library—or had been earlier that day, when I saw him handling them.”

“What did you say about our appointment at half-past seven?” Mr. Cupples asked as the two men stepped out of the grand entrance to the apartment building. “Do we actually have one?”

“Absolutely,” Trent replied, his voice bright with anticipation. “You’re dining with me. Only one thing could properly mark this moment—and that’s a dinner I pay for. No, no! I invited you first. I’ve finally cracked a case so extraordinary it’s been haunting my mind for over a year—something so unique, so deeply layered, that if that isn’t reason enough to treat myself and a friend, then nothing is. Cupples, we’re not going to my club. This isn’t just any meal—it’s a celebration. And let’s be honest: being seen in a London club in high spirits is more than enough to ruin any man’s reputation. Besides, their dinners are always the same—same taste, same predictable menu. I’ve watched countless members like myself grow bored by that eternal feast, and I’ll do nothing to prolong the ritual tonight. Tonight, let the table be laid in vain, as far as we’re concerned. We’re not going where the elite gather. We’re going to Sheppard’s.”

“Who is Sheppard?” Mr. Cupples asked mildly as they walked up Victoria Street. His companion moved with an almost unnerving lightness, and a passing policeman, catching sight of his face, offered a small, indulgent smile—surely the only explanation he could imagine was alcohol.

“Who is Sheppard?” Trent echoed, his tone sharp with irony. “That question, if I may say so without offense, Cupples, is perfectly emblematic of the modern spirit—restless, aimless, forever chasing shadows. I suggest we dine at Sheppard’s, and instantly you cross your arms and demand, in a fit of intellectual pride, to know who Sheppard is before stepping inside. I won’t indulge such vices of the age. Sheppard’s is a place where one can eat. I don’t know Sheppard. Never even considered he existed. He might well be a myth born of tribal legend. All I know is that at Sheppard’s, you can find a saddle of mutton so exquisite it has made American tourists curse the very day Columbus set foot in the New World… Taxi!”

A cab glided smoothly to the curb, and the driver nodded solemnly as he received their destination.

“There’s another reason I chose Sheppard’s,” Trent continued, lighting a cigarette with trembling fingers. “I’m going to marry the most extraordinary woman in the world. I trust the connection is clear.”

“You’re marrying Mabel!” Mr. Cupples exclaimed. “My dear fellow, what wonderful news! Shake hands, Trent—this is magnificent! I congratulate you both from the bottom of my heart. And may I say—I don’t mean to interrupt your joy, which is entirely natural, and I remember feeling exactly the same when I was young—but how deeply I’ve hoped for this! Mabel has endured so much sorrow, yet she remains, beyond doubt, one of nature’s finest gifts—a woman meant to uplift and inspire a good man. But I never knew her feelings toward you. _Your_ feelings, however…” Mr. Cupples added, twinkling with a shrewdness that would have startled a seasoned cynic, “…I saw from the start. When you were both at my house, listening to Professor Peppmuller while gazing at her—I saw it then. Some of us older fellows still have our wits about us, my boy.”

“Mabel says she knew long before that,” Trent replied, his voice slightly subdued. “And I thought I was doing a fine job of pretending I wasn’t mad about her. Well, I’ve never been any good at hiding things. I wouldn’t be surprised if old Peppmuller himself noticed something through those thick spectacles of his. But however foolish I may have been as a secret suitor,” he said, regaining his cheer, “I’m going to be far worse now. As for your congratulations—thank you a thousand times, because I know you mean them. You’re the kind of blunt, honest soul who’d scowl like a thundercloud if you thought we were making a mistake. By the way, I can’t help acting like a fool tonight. I’m compelled to babble. Try to bear with me. Perhaps it would be easier if I sang you a song—one of your old favorites. What was that tune you used to hum? Like this, right?” He began tapping out a rhythm on the cab floor:

*There was an old nigger, and he had a wooden leg.
He had no tobacco, no tobacco could he beg.
Another old nigger was as cunning as a fox,
And he always had tobacco in his old tobacco-box.*

Now for the chorus!

But you’re not singing. I thought you’d be filling the air with sound.

“I never sang that song,” Mr. Cupples protested. “Never heard it before.”

“Are you certain?” Trent asked, uncertain. “Well, I suppose I must take your word. Still, it’s a beautiful tune—nothing the entire forest of birds could sing together could match it. Somehow, it captures exactly how I feel right now. It rises unbidden to the lips. Out of the fullness of the heart, the mouth speaks—just as the Bishop of Bath and Wells said when hearing Mr. Balfour speak.”

“When was that?” Mr. Cupples asked.

“On the occasion,” Trent replied, “of the introduction of the Compulsory Notification of Diseases of Poultry Bill—an ill-fated measure you surely remember. Hullo!” he broke off as the cab sped down a side street and turned sharply into a busy avenue. “We’re here already.” The cab stopped.

“Here we are,” Trent said, paying the driver and guiding Mr. Cupples into a long, panelled room filled with tables and low conversation. “This is the temple of appetite, the garden of roses. I see three bookmakers enjoying pork at my usual table. Let’s take the one across the room.”

He conferred earnestly with a waiter, while Mr. Cupples stood quietly by the fire, lost in thought. “The wine here,” Trent said as they sat down, “is almost certainly made from grapes. What shall we drink?”

Mr. Cupples emerged from his reverie. “I think,” he said, “I’ll have milk and soda water.”

“Lower your voice!” Trent urged. “The head-waiter has a weak heart—he might hear you. Milk and soda water? Cupples, you may believe yourself strong, and I won’t argue that, but I warn you—mixing drinks has killed stronger men than you. Be wise now. Fill your cup with Samian wine; leave soda to the Turks. Here comes the food.” He gave another order, and the waiter placed dishes before them before vanishing. Trent clearly knew his way around. “I’ve ordered wine I know,” he said. “Try it. If you’ve taken a vow, then in honor of all teetotal saints, drink the water beside you—but don’t seek cheap fame by asking for milk and soda.”

“I’ve never sworn any oath,” Mr. Cupples said, examining his mutton with interest. “I simply don’t care for wine. Once I bought a bottle to try it, and it made me sick. Though perhaps it was bad wine. I’ll sample yours, since it’s your dinner. And I assure you, my dear Trent, I’d like to do something unusual to show how deeply I feel about this moment. I haven’t been this delighted in years. To think—the Manderson mystery resolved, the innocent cleared, and your happiness with Mabel complete—all coming at once! I raise my glass to you.” Mr. Cupples took a tiny sip.

“You have a noble soul,” Trent said, visibly moved. “Your outward appearance belies the vastness within. I’d expect as little to see an elephant conducting opera as to see you drinking my health. Dear Cupples—may your beak keep its delicate rose hue!—No, curse it all!” he burst out, catching a flicker of discomfort on his companion’s face as he tasted the wine again. “I have no right to meddle with your preferences. I apologize. You may have whatever you want—even if it kills the head-waiter in his pride.”

Once Mr. Cupples had been served his monastic beverage and the waiter had left, Trent looked across the table with intent. “In this sea of talk,” he said, “we can speak freely—as if we were alone on a mountain ridge. The waiter is whispering secrets to the young woman at the desk. We’re alone. What do you make of that meeting this afternoon?”

He began eating with relish.

Without pausing in his careful slicing of mutton, Mr. Cupples answered: “The most striking feature, to me, was the irony. We both held the key to Manderson’s madness—the jealousy Marlowe found so inexplicable. We knew of his obsession, and wisely kept silent, especially for Mabel’s sake. Marlowe will never know what he was suspected of. Strange, isn’t it? Most of us, I suspect, move through life surrounded by invisible networks of assumptions others hold about us. I recall, for instance, discovering years ago that several acquaintances believed I had secretly joined the Church of Rome. The fiction stemmed from my mentioning a preference for weekly meat abstinence—a detail many mistook for conclusive proof. Manderson’s belief about his secretary likely rested on something even slighter. It was Mr. Bunner, I think you said, who told you of his deep-rooted, almost inherited suspicion. As for Marlowe’s story—it seemed straightforward, and once we accept Manderson was mentally unstable, nothing in it seems especially remarkable.”

Trent laughed. “I admit,” he said, “it struck me as a bit odd.”

“Only in the details,” Mr. Cupples argued. “What’s abnormal about the core facts? A madman forms a delusional grudge; he plots against his imagined enemy; it leads to his own destruction. Stated plainly, what would any student of madness call strange? Now consider Marlowe. He finds himself in danger, though innocent. Speaking the truth won’t save him. Is that unheard of? He escapes through clever deception. That happens every day, probably.”

He attacked his nearly unrecognizable mutton.

“I’d like to know,” Trent said after a pause, “if there’s anything in history you couldn’t make seem ordinary with reasoning like that.”

A soft smile touched Mr. Cupples’s lips. “Don’t accuse me of empty paradox,” he said. “Let me mention some things that truly seem remarkable. For example—the life cycle of the liver-fluke, thanks to Poulton’s research.”

“I can’t argue,” Trent replied. “Science may have honored the fluke’s birth, but I’ve never even heard of it.”

“It’s not exactly appetizing,” Mr. Cupples mused. “I won’t pursue it. My point is, my dear Trent, that remarkable things happen constantly—if only we notice. We dishonor perception by calling only sensational events ‘remarkable.’”

Trent clapped his knife handle on the table. “I haven’t heard you like this in years,” he said. “You must be as exalted as I am. It’s a terrible case of the restlessness people call joy. But as much as I enjoy it, I won’t sit idly while you dismiss the Manderson affair as mundane. You may say what you like, but impersonating Manderson under those circumstances was extraordinarily clever.”

“Clever—yes!” Mr. Cupples agreed. “Extraordinarily so? Not really. In those conditions, it was almost inevitable for a clever man. Marlowe was famous for mimicking Manderson’s voice; he had theatrical talent; he thought like a chess master; he knew the household inside out. I grant the execution was brilliant—but everything aligned in his favor. The idea itself doesn’t rank with, say, using recoil from a firearm to reload automatically. Still, I admit—as I did at first—that the case had unusual details. It became complex.”

“Did it really strike you that way?” Trent asked, sarcasm heavy in his voice.

“The complexity arose,” Mr. Cupples continued calmly, “because after Marlowe’s suspicions awakened, a second sharp mind interfered with the first’s plans. Such duels occur often in business or politics, but rarely in crime.”

“I’d say never,” Trent replied. “Because even the smartest criminals rarely use strategic depth. When they do, they usually escape—since clever detectives tend to be less subtle than the average criminal. That depth rarely fits the criminal psyche. Look at Crippen. Clever, yes. He solved the body disposal problem neatly. But how far did he see ahead? Criminals and detectives are often bold tacticians, but neither handles anything beyond simple schemes. After all, it’s rare in any field.”

“One troubling thought remained,” Mr. Cupples said, seeming to tire of abstraction. “If Marlowe hadn’t suspected anything and walked into the trap, he’d likely have been hanged. How often has someone framed an innocent person successfully? I imagine many accused, convicted on circumstantial evidence, died protesting innocence. From now on, I’ll never support a death sentence based on such proof.”

“Neither have I,” Trent said. “Hanging in such cases defies the obvious principle: ‘you never can tell.’ I agree with the American jurist who said we shouldn’t hang a yellow dog for stealing jam on circumstantial evidence—even if he’s covered in jam. And yes, framing innocent people happens constantly. It’s common under coercive regimes—whether in Ireland, Russia, India, Korea. If police can’t get their target fairly, they resort to deceit. But there’s one case in the State Trials that’s particularly relevant: the Campden Case. Probably you’ve heard of it.”

Mr. Cupples confessed ignorance and took another potato.

“John Masefield wrote a powerful play about it,” Trent said. “If it ever returns to London, you should see it—if you enjoy emotional drama. I’ve seen women silently weep over sentimental nonsense in theaters. By George! They ought to be overcome with emotion if they saw that play performed well! The facts: John Perry accused his mother and brother of murdering a man, swearing he helped. His story was detailed, plausible—except the body was missing. The judge, possibly drunk—this was Restoration England—ignored that. The mother and brother denied it. All three were convicted and hanged, solely on John’s testimony. Two years later, the victim returned to Campden—he’d been kidnapped by pirates. His disappearance inspired John’s lie. The twist? John included himself in the accusation—essentially committing suicide—which convinced everyone of its truth. No one believed a man would die to hang another. That’s exactly what the prosecution would have said if Marlowe had told the truth. Not one juryman in a million would have believed the Manderson plot.”

Mr. Cupples fell quiet, pondering. “I know little of that history,” he said at length. “But memories from my childhood come back. We know from Mabel’s stories the spiritual truth behind this—Manderson’s insane jealousy. We understand he could devise such a plan. But usually, justice fails when it comes to uncovering spiritual truths. Sometimes they’re hidden deliberately, as here. Sometimes, people simply can’t express them—and others fail to grasp them. When I was a boy in Edinburgh, the whole country went mad over the Sandyford Place murder.”

Trent nodded. “Mrs. M’Lachlan’s case. She was innocent.”

“My parents believed it,” Mr. Cupples said. “I did too, once I could read the sordid details. But the mystery was so dark, the lies so tangled, that others were equally sure old James Fleming was innocent. Scotland split. Parliament debated it. The press fought fiercely. Yet if the truth about Fleming had been known—what some suspected about his temperament—then the case would have been clear. If he was capable of killing Jessie M’Pherson and blaming the feeble-minded man, then no doubt would remain.”

“Even a dull old man like Fleming can be a mystery to the world,” Trent said. “Especially in court. Law falters when delicacy is needed. It fails easily with people like Fleming. And those caught in legal battles must feel like they’re in a jungle of apes—win or lose. Perhaps it’s good for them to face reality now and then. But what would twelve angry jurors do to Marlowe? His story would be worse than no defense. There’s not a shred of evidence supporting it. Can’t you imagine the prosecution tearing it apart? The judge brushing it aside? The jury, snorting in outrage, telling each other it’s the clearest case they’ve ever seen, that they’d have respected him more if he’d fled with the loot instead of lying. Imagine yourself on that jury, not knowing Marlowe, trembling at the record—greed, murder, robbery, cowardice, shameless lies! Why, you and I believed him guilty until—”

“I beg your pardon!” Mr. Cupples interrupted, laying down his cutlery. “When we discussed it the other night, I was careful not to suggest such a belief. _I_ was always certain he was innocent.”

“You said something similar at Marlowe’s earlier,” Trent said. “I wondered what you meant. Certain he was innocent? How can you be certain? You’re usually more cautious with words than that, Cupples.”

“I said ‘certain,’” Mr. Cupples repeated firmly.

Trent shrugged. “If you truly were, after reading my manuscript and discussing it all, then you’ve abandoned reason altogether—an attitude that’s bad Christianity, bad philosophy, and frankly absurd. Why, man—”

“Let me speak,” Mr. Cupples interjected, folding his hands over his plate. “I assure you, I haven’t abandoned reason. I’m certain he’s innocent—and I always was—because of something I know, something I’ve known from the beginning. You asked me to imagine myself on Marlowe’s jury. That’s pointless. I wouldn’t be a juror. I’d be a witness—giving evidence for the defense. You said just now, ‘If there were a single piece of evidence.’ There is. And it’s mine. And,” he added quietly, “it’s conclusive.” He picked up his knife and fork and resumed eating.

Trent’s face had gone pale with shock. At the final word, blood rushed back into his cheeks, and he slammed his hand on the table, laughing wildly. “It can’t be!” he cried. “It’s a dream, a fantasy conjured from milk and soda. You can’t mean that all along, while I was working on the case, you knew Marlowe was innocent?”

Mr. Cupples, finishing his last bite, nodded cheerfully. He wiped his sparse mustache, then leaned forward. “It’s simple,” he said. “I shot Manderson myself.”
