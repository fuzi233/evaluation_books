The small hosier’s household at Tergou were gathered over their midday rye bread and pottage, the hearth glowing soft against the crisp autumn chill, when Luke Peterson burst through the door, dust caked thick on his boots and wool tunic, his breath ragged from riding nonstop. “Good people, Mistress Catherine is wanted instantly at Rotterdam.”

Eli the hosier paused mid-bite, and his wife Catherine wiped grease from her fingers on her apron, frowning. “My name is Catherine, young man. Kate, that will be Margaret, sure.”

“Ay, dame,” Luke nodded, still catching his breath. “She said to me, ‘Good Luke, hie thee to Tergou, and ask for Eli the hosier, and pray his wife Catherine to come to me, for God His love.’ I didn’t wait for daylight to set out.”

“Holy saints!” Catherine clapped a hand to her chest. “Has Gerard come home, then? Nay, she would have said so at once. What on earth can have befallen her?” She tumbled over wild guesses one after another, till Kate, her stepdaughter, ventured timidly that perhaps the young messenger knew the truth of it.

“That I do,” Luke said gravely. “Her babe is a-dying. And she was so wrapped up in the little lad, it has all but broken her.”

A quick, furtive glint of satisfaction passed between Eli’s two ne’er-do-well sons, Cornelis and Sybrandt. Luckily for them, Catherine never saw it, her face turned straight to her husband. “Now Eli,” she cried fiercely, “if you say a single word against me going to her, we shall quarrel after all these years of peace.”

“Who gainsays thee, foolish woman?” Eli grunted, already pushing back his bench to stand. “Quarrel with your own shadow while I run and borrow Peter’s mule for ye.”

“Bless thee, my good man!” Catherine’s face softened. “Thou never yet failed me at a pinch. Eat your dinners who can, while I go make ready.”

She brought Luke back with her in their cart, cross-questioning him so sharply then wheedling him so gently by turns that before they had even cleared the edge of Tergou, she had every last scrap of information he possessed dragged out into the open. Margaret met her at her Rotterdam cottage door, pale as linen, her eyes hollow with weeping, and threw her arms around Catherine’s neck, her look full of desperate pleading.

“Come, he is alive, thank God,” Catherine said, scanning her face keenly, and stepped past her to the cradle where the babe lay listless, his little cheeks sunken. She looked from the wasting child to the hollow-eyed mother, back and again, and said plainly: “Lucky you sent for me. The child is poisoned.”

“Poisoned! By whom?” Margaret gasped.

“By you. You have been fretting yourself to skin and bone over Gerard.”

“Nay, indeed, mother! How can I help fretting?”

“Don’t tell me that. A nursing mother has no business to grieve so. She must turn her mind away from her sorrow to the comfort that lies in her lap. Know you not the child pines if the mother vexes herself? This comes of all your reading and writing – those idle crafts befit a man, but they drive all useful, plain sense out of a woman’s head. The child must be weaned at once.”

“Oh, you cruel woman!” Margaret burst out vehemently. “I am sorry I sent for you. Would you rob me of the only bit of comfort I have left in all the world? When I nurse my Gerard, I forget I am the most unhappy creature beneath the sun.”

“That you do not,” Catherine retorted staunchly, “or he would not be wasting away this way.”

“Mother!” Margaret begged, tears spilling over.

“’Tis hard, I know,” Catherine relented, softening a little. “But bethink thee: would it not be far harder to look down and see his lovely wee face looking up at you out of a little coffin?”

“Oh Jesu, no!” Margaret shuddered.

“And how could you face all your other troubles with your heart aye full of grief and your lap empty of your boy?”

“Oh, mother, I consent to anything. Only save my son.”

“That is a good lass. Trust to me! I stand outside your grief, and see clearer than you can.”

Unfortunately, there was another consent to be gained: the babe’s, and he was far more refractory than his mother. “There,” Margaret said, trying to sound like she was scolding his misbehavior, “he loves me too well to give up my milk easy.” But Catherine was a match for them both. On her walk through the village that morning, she had spotted a hale, young woman sitting outside her own door with a fat rosy infant of her own, and now she went to her, explained the case, and asked if she would nurse the pining little lad temporarily, till Margaret could wean him proper.

The young woman agreed with a smile, popped her own babe into his cradle, and came straight into Margaret’s house. She dropped a curtsey, and Catherine put the ailing child into her arms. The woman examined him, pitied him, cooed over him, and proceeded to nurse him as naturally as if he had been her own flesh and blood. Margaret, paralyzed at her ease, cast a rueful glance at Catherine and burst out sobbing.

The visitor looked up. “What is to do? Wife, ye told me not the mother was unwilling.”

“She is not,” Catherine said. “She is only a fool. Never heed her; and you, Margaret, I am ashamed of you.”

“You are a cruel, hard-hearted woman,” Margaret sobbed.

“Them as take in hand to guide the weak need be hardish,” Catherine replied bluntly. “And you will excuse me; but you are not my flesh and blood – and your boy is.” She let that plain speech sink in a moment before she added, “Come now, this good wife is robbing her own babe to save yours, and you can think of nothing better than blubbering right in her face. Out fie, for shame!”

“Nay, dame,” the nurse put in gently. “Thank Heaven, I have enough milk for my own and for hers to boot. Prithee wyte not on her! Maybe the troubles o’ life have soured her own milk.”

“And her heart into the bargain,” the remorseless Catherine said, but not unkindly.

Margaret looked her full in the face, then dropped her gaze. “I know I ought to be very grateful to you,” she sobbed to the nurse, then turned her head away over the back of a chair, so she would not have to witness the intolerable sight of another woman nursing her Gerard, and little Gerard making no distinction between this new stranger and the mother he had known his whole short life.

“You are very welcome, my poor woman,” the nurse said softly. “And so are you, Mistress Catherine, which are my townswoman, and know it not.”

“What, are ye from Tergou? All the better, but I cannot call your face to mind.”

“Oh, you know not me: my husband and me, we are very humble folk by your standard. But true Eli and his wife are known and respected all through the town. So I am at your call, dame; at yours, wife; and yours, my pretty poppet, night or day.”

“There’s a woman of the right old sort,” Catherine declared as the door closed behind her.

“I HATE her. I HATE her. I HATE her,” Margaret burst out, fierce with the fervor of raw grief. Catherine only laughed. “That is right. Better say it out loud than sit sly and brood over it. It is very natural, after all. Come, here is your bundle o’ comfort. Take and hate that, if ye can,” and she put the now drowsy little Gerard into her lap.

“No, no,” Margaret said, turning her head half away from him – she could not for her life turn the other half. “He is not my child now; he is hers. I know not why she left him here, for my part. It was very good of her not to take him to her house, cradle and all; oh! oh! oh! Like father, like son – some other woman has got him away from me, oh! oh!” Catherine let her cry it out in peace, sorry for her pain.

After that, whenever Catherine wanted Joan’s help, she would take little Gerard out for a walk in the fresh air. Margaret never objected, never voiced the smallest bit of incredulity, but every time they returned, she was red-eyed and in tears. Before long, she was altogether as eager as Catherine to get the boy weaned. It was done at last, and soon Gerard recovered his health and vigour – only to fall to the next trial of teething. Here Catherine’s long years of motherhood made her wisdom invaluable, and even in the thick of her grief and anxiety over the missing Gerard, Margaret had moments of pure bliss, watching tiny new teeth peep through her son’s soft red gums. “Teeth, mother?” she would cry. “I call them not teeth, but pearls of pearls.” Each little white nub that sparkled into view was to her the greatest feat Nature had ever achieved. Catherine shared the illusion entirely: had a stranger told them that fields of standing corn were equally admirable, Margaret would have stared at him like a wounded gazelle, and Catherine would have marched him straight out the door. Every new pearl’s arrival was announced with a shriek of triumph by whichever of them spotted it first.

Catherine gossiped often with Joan, and learned she was the wife of Jorian Ketel, a man from Tergou who had once been servant to Ghysbrecht Van Swieten, the powerful burgomaster, before he had fallen out of favour and returned to his native Rotterdam. His friends had got him the post of parish sexton, and between that and his carpentering work, he did very well for a humble man. Catherine told Joan in turn whose child she had nursed, all about Margaret and her lost Gerard, and the deep, gnawing anxiety his total silence had plunged them all into. “Ay,” Joan sighed, “the world is full of trouble.”

One day Joan leaned in close to Catherine, and whispered: “It’s my belief my man knows more about your Gerard than anybody in these parts; but he has grown closer than an oyster of late. Drop in some day just afore sunset, and set him talking. And for our Lady’s sake say not I set you on. The only hiding he ever gave me was for babbling his business, and I do not want another. Gramercy! I married a man for the comfort of the thing, not to be beat for every word I say.”

Catherine dropped round to their cottage that evening. Jorian was ready enough to tell her how he had once befriended her son, and even perhaps saved his life – but that was no news to Catherine. The moment she began to cross-question him, to ask if he could guess why her lost boy neither came home nor wrote, he cast a grim glare at his wife, who met it with a look of perfect, stolid innocence, and his answers turned short and sullen. “What should he know more than any other man?” he grunted, and after a long pause added, “Think you the burgomaster takes such as me into his secrets?”

“Oh, then the burgomaster knows something?” Catherine said sharply.

“Likely. Who else should?”

“I’ll go ask him.”

“I would. Go make him mine enemy. That is what a poor fellow always gets if he says a single word to you women.” From that moment Jorian shrunk inwards, as impenetrable as a hedgehog, and almost as prickly.

His conduct left the two poor women writhing in agony of mind, wracked with alarm and irritated curiosity. They knew Ghysbrecht was Gerard’s mortal enemy: he had stopped their marriage, imprisoned him, hunted him across the country. And here his old servant had hinted, almost against his will, that this same enemy held the secret to Gerard’s silence. They sifted every word, every look of Jorian’s, but the truth stayed dark and mysterious. Finally Catherine told Margaret to go and speak to him herself. “You are young, you are fair. You will maybe get more out of him than I could.”

The guess was a reasonable one. Margaret tucked little Gerard into her arms, and tapped timidly at Jorian’s door just before sunset. “Come in,” called a sturdy voice. She stepped inside, and there sat Jorian huddled by the fireside. At the sight of her, he rose, snorted, and stormed straight out of the house.

“Is that for me, wife?” Margaret said, turning bright red.

“You must excuse him,” Joan replied, a little coldly. “He lays it to your door that he is a poor man instead of a rich one. It is something about a piece of parchment that went amissing. He got not a penny from the burgomaster all along of that one.”

“Alas! Gerard took it,” Margaret said, wincing.

“Likely. But my man says you should not have let him – you were pledged to him to keep them all safe. And sooth to say, I blame not my Jorian for being wroth. ‘Tis hard for a poor man to be so near fortune and lose it by those he has befriended. However, I tell him another story. Says I, ‘Folk that are out o’ trouble like you and me didn’t ought to be too hard on folk that are in trouble; and she has plenty.’ Going already? What is all your hurry, mistress?”

“Oh, it is not for me to drive the goodman out of his own house,” Margaret said, already making for the door.

“Well, let me kiss the bairn afore ye go. He is not in fault anyway, poor innocent.”

Stung by that cruel rebuff, Margaret came to a secret resolution she did not confide even to Catherine. Six weeks after she had arrived, the good old woman from Tergou packed her bags and headed home. On little Gerard’s first birthday that soon followed, Margaret did no work at all, but put on her finest Sunday gown, tucked her boy in her arms, and went to the village church, where she prayed long and fervently for Gerard’s safe return.

That very same day and hour, half a continent away in the minster church at Basle, Father Clement celebrated a mass for Margaret’s departed soul, in the full and pious belief that she was long dead.
