You, who dwell in the luminous realm of Three Dimensions, bathed in both shadow and radiance, gifted with dual ocular organs that grant you the mastery of perspective and the delight of chromatic variety—you, who possess the miraculous capacity to *see* an angle, to behold the entire circumference of a circle without obstruction—how shall I possibly convey to your enlightened minds the labyrinthine difficulty we endure in Flatland to recognize one another's true configuration? Recall what I have previously disclosed: every entity within our plane, whether animate or inert, regardless of its intrinsic geometric complexity, presents to our limited vision the identical, deceptively uniform silhouette of a straight line. If all appear alike, how does distinction arise? The answer unfolds in three distinct layers.

First lies the sense of hearing, a faculty in our dimension vastly superior to your own. This auditory acuity allows us not merely to identify familiar voices but to stratify society itself, distinguishing—at least among the three lowest orders—the Equilateral, the Square, and the Pentagon. I need not elaborate on the Isosceles, whose chaotic sounds fall outside this rudimentary taxonomy. Yet, as we ascend the social ladder, the reliability of this auditory method fractures. Voices become homogenized, and the innate ability to discriminate pitch and timbre—a virtue cherished by the plebeians—is largely absent among the Aristocracy. Thus, whenever imposture looms, sound becomes a treacherous guide. Among the lower castes, vocal apparatuses are grotesquely exaggerated to match our hyper-developed ears; an Isosceles can effortlessly mimic the resonance of a Polygon, and with sufficient training, even simulate the harmonious hum of a Circle.

Consequently, a second, more common method prevails: **Feeling**. For our women and the lower classes—and I shall address the upper echelons anon—this tactile examination is the primary arbiter of identity, particularly between strangers or when determining class rather than individual. What constitutes a formal "introduction" in your Spaceland is, for us, the ritual of mutual sensation. In the rustic districts inhabited by our older gentry, the customary formula remains archaic and explicit: *"Permit me to ask you to feel and be felt by my friend Mr. So-and-so."* However, in the bustling towns and among the mercantile class, the phrase "be felt by" is deemed superfluous, the invitation truncated to, *"Let me ask you to feel Mr. So-and-so,"* with the tacit assumption that the sensation is reciprocal. Even more striking is the linguistic evolution among our modern, dashing youth, who recoil from unnecessary exertion and disdain the purity of speech. They abbreviate the formula further, employing "to feel" in a technical, bureaucratic sense meaning *"to recommend for the purposes of feeling and being felt."* Indeed, the slang of our polished, fast-paced high society now sanctions the barbarism: *"Mr. Smith, permit me to feel Mr. Jones."*

Do not mistake this "feeling" for the tedious, exhaustive circumnavigation you might imagine. Through rigorous schooling and lifelong conditioning, we have honed a tactile intuition that allows us to instantly differentiate the angles of an equilateral triangle, square, or pentagon. The obtuse, brainless vertex of an acute-angled Isosceles is immediately apparent to the most insensitive finger. Therefore, it is rarely necessary to trace every side; sensing a single angle suffices to classify the individual, barring membership in the higher nobility. Here, the challenge intensifies dramatically. Even a Master of Arts from our University of Wentbridge may conflate a decagon with a dodecagon, and few Doctors of Science, whether inside or outside that hallowed institution, can confidently and swiftly distinguish between a icosagon and an icositetragon.

Consider the peril inherent in this intimate exchange. Those who remember my earlier citations from the Legislative Code regarding Women will understand why introductions require such scrupulous caution. Should the angles collide with the unwary Feeler, the consequences can be catastrophic. Safety dictates that the Felt remain absolutely motionless. A sudden start, a nervous shift, or even a violent sneeze has proven fatal to incautious souls, snuffing out countless budding friendships. This danger is particularly acute among the lower classes of Triangles. Their eyes are positioned so distantly from their vertex that they are practically blind to the extremity of their own frames. Coupled with their rough, coarse constitution, they lack sensitivity to the delicate touch of the refined Polygon. It is no surprise, then, that an involuntary jerk of the head has repeatedly cost the State valuable lives.

I recall the lamentations of my esteemed grandfather—one of the most disciplined members of the unfortunate Isosceles caste, who secured four of seven votes from the Sanitary and Social Board just prior to his death to promote his elevation to the class of Equilaterals. He often shed tears in his aged eye recounting a tragedy befalling his great-great-great-grandfather, a respectable working man possessing an angle of precisely fifty-nine degrees and thirty minutes. Afflicted by rheumatism during the act of being felt by a Polygon, the ancestor suddenly started, inadvertently transfixing the nobleman through the diagonal. The resulting imprisonment and the moral shock rippling through the family lineage caused a regression of one and a half degrees in the next generation, settling the family brain at a mere fifty-eight degrees. It took five generations of arduous striving to recover the lost ground, achieving the full sixty degrees required to break the cycle of servitude and ascend to the Regulars. All this ruin stemmed from a singular accident during the mundane process of Feeling.

At this juncture, I anticipate the protest of my more educated readers: *"How can you in Flatland comprehend angles, degrees, or minutes? We *see* an angle because we observe two lines diverging; you, seeing only a single line—or a collection of collinear segments—cannot possibly discern divergence, let alone quantify it."* My response is that while we cannot *see* angles, we can *infer* them with remarkable precision. Our sense of touch, sharpened by necessity and cultivated through generations of discipline, distinguishes angular differences far more accurately than your unaided sight. Furthermore, Nature herself provides us with a divine scaffold. By immutable law, the brain of the Isosceles begins at half a degree and increments by half a degree each generation until the threshold of sixty degrees is reached, marking the transition from serfdom to freedom. Consequently, every elementary school houses an ascending alphabet of angles, ranging from thirty minutes to sixty degrees.

However, due to occasional regressions, intellectual stagnation, and the prodigious fertility of the criminal and vagrant classes, there exists a glut of individuals in the half-degree and single-degree categories, along with a surplus up to ten degrees. Devoid of civic rights and often lacking the cognitive faculties for warfare, these specimens are conscripted by the State for educational purposes. Immobilized to eliminate risk, they populate the classrooms of infant schools, serving as living protractors for the offspring of the Middle Class, imparting the very tact and intelligence these wretched creatures lack. In some jurisdictions, these specimens are fed and allowed to live for years; yet in more temperate and regulated regions, it is deemed more economically prudent to withhold sustenance, renewing the specimens monthly—the approximate lifespan of the unfed criminal class. In cheaper institutions, the longevity of the specimen is offset by the rising costs of feeding and the degradation of angular accuracy after a few weeks of constant handling. Moreover, the pricier systems offer a subtle yet significant demographic advantage: a gradual reduction in the redundant Isosceles population, a goal every statesman vigilantly pursues.

Thus, despite the reactionary sentiments of certain popularly elected School Boards favoring the "cheap system," I maintain that in this instance, expense is the truest economy. Yet, I must not allow the politics of pedagogy to obscure my central thesis. Sufficient has been stated to demonstrate that Recognition by Feeling is neither as tedious nor as indeterminate as it appears, and it remains inherently more trustworthy than Recognition by Hearing. Nevertheless, as noted, this method carries inherent dangers. For this reason, many in the Middle and Lower classes, and all without exception in the Polygonal and Circular orders, eschew it in favor of a third method, the description of which I reserve for the following section.
