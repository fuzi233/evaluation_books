It was the second-to-last day of the 1999th year of our era, and the first day of the Long Vacation. After spending the evening immersed in my favorite pastime—geometry—I retired to rest with an unsolved problem still swirling in my mind. That night, I dreamed.

Before me stretched an endless procession of tiny straight lines, which I instinctively took to be women, scattered among even smaller, gleaming points—beings that seemed like luminous dots. All moved back and forth along a single, unbroken line, each at roughly the same speed. From time to time, they emitted a chaotic chorus of chirps and tweets, but whenever they stopped, silence fell as complete as if the world had ceased to breathe.

I approached one of the largest figures—what I assumed was a woman—and spoke directly to her. But she did not answer. A second attempt brought no response. A third went unanswered too. Frustrated by what seemed to me sheer rudeness, I placed my mouth directly before hers, blocking her path, and shouted:
“Woman! What is this gathering? This strange, jumbled noise? This endless motion back and forth along a single line?”

“I am no woman,” the small line replied. “I am the Monarch of this world. And you—where do you come from, intruding into my realm of Lineland?”

Taken aback by the abruptness, I apologized for any disturbance I may have caused. I introduced myself as a stranger and humbly asked the King to describe his domain. But it proved difficult to extract meaningful information. The Monarch kept assuming that everything familiar to him must also be known to me, as if I were pretending ignorance for sport.

Yet through persistent questioning, I learned the following:

This poor, self-proclaimed ignorant king believed that the straight line he called his kingdom—and in which he lived his entire existence—was all there was: the whole of space, the totality of reality. He could neither move nor see beyond that line. To him, anything outside it did not exist—not even as emptiness, for emptiness implies space. It was pure non-being.

His subjects—the straight lines were men, the points were women—were just as confined. They could only move along the line, and their vision extended only to a single point. No one could ever see more than a dot. A man, a woman, a child—each appeared as a mere point to another’s eye. Only voice revealed sex or age.

And because each individual occupied the full width of the narrow path that made up their universe, no one could step aside to let others pass. Movement was impossible without collision. Once neighbors, always neighbors. Neighbors stayed together until death parted them—just as marriage binds us in Flatland.

Such a life, limited to sightless points and rigid motion, struck me as profoundly bleak. Yet the King seemed remarkably cheerful. I wondered how such a society could sustain affection or family bonds under such constraints. Finally, I dared ask about his household.

“My wives and children are well and happy,” he said.

I hesitated, stunned. Around him, I saw only men—no women in sight. “But Your Majesty,” I said, “how can you possibly see or reach your family when half a dozen others block your way? Can’t you see through them? Or pass them? Is proximity truly unnecessary for marriage and procreation?”

“How can you ask such a foolish question?” he snapped. “If it were as you suggest, the world would vanish in a generation. No, no—neighbourhood is irrelevant. Love does not depend on closeness. And childbirth is far too vital to rely on chance. You must know this. But since you pretend ignorance, I’ll explain as if you were a newborn in Lineland.”

He continued: “Marriages are formed through sound and hearing. Every man has two voices—one bass, one tenor—at opposite ends of his body. Two mouths, two eyes. I should mention this, though I’ve struggled to hear your tenor during our talk.”

I admitted I had only one voice and hadn’t realized he had two.

“That confirms it,” he declared. “You’re not a man. You’re a monstrous female with a bass voice and an untrained ear. But let me continue.”

“Nature decrees that every man shall marry two women,” he said.

“Why two?” I asked.

“You carry your pretense too far!” he roared. “How can harmony exist without the union of four voices? The man’s bass and tenor, the two women’s soprano and contralto—only then is balance achieved!”

“But what if a man prefers one wife—or three?” I ventured.

“It’s impossible!” he thundered. “As absurd as saying two plus one equals five, or that anyone can see a straight line. Impossible!”

I tried to interrupt, but he pressed on.

“Once a week, a natural law compels us to move rhythmically—violently—for the time it takes you to count to one hundred and one. At the fifty-first pulse, we pause mid-motion. Then each being emits their most powerful, sweetest tone. In that instant, marriages are sealed.”

“The harmony between bass and treble, tenor and contralto is so perfect,” he added, “that lovers separated by twenty thousand leagues often recognize each other’s voice at once. Love transcends distance. And in that moment, a triad of offspring—a male and two females—is born, joining the line of Lineland.”

“Always three?” I asked. “Must one wife always bear twins?”

“Bass-voiced monster!” he spat. “Yes! Otherwise, how could the balance of sexes remain intact? Would you ignore the very alphabet of nature?”

He fell silent, furious. Minutes passed before he resumed.

“Do not suppose every bachelor finds his partners on the first try. Most undergo repeated trials. Rarely does a pair instantly harmonize. Often, one voice matches, but not both. Sometimes neither does. Or the women’s voices clash slightly. But Nature provides: each weekly chorus brings the three closer. With every attempt, discord reveals itself, and slowly, imperceptibly, the imperfect adjusts their tone to match the perfect. Over many weeks, many attempts, harmony emerges.

Then comes the day—when the universal chorus sounds, and the distant trio suddenly sings in perfect unison. Before they even realize it, the wedded triad is joined in song, and Nature rejoices over a new marriage and three new lives.”
