Generations had seasoned the heavy oak beams of the house with the olfactory history of sustenance—the rich, resinous smoke of applewood, the oxidized tang of hanging pheasants, and the sweet, earthy memory of slow-braised root vegetables. It was a domestic sanctuary whose acoustic ecology was usually governed by the rhythmic, percussive chop of the kitchen knife and the gentle, harmonic simmer of the cast-iron pot. The cyclical nature of time in this space was measured by the seasons of the harvest. Yet tonight, this ancestral rhythm was abruptly ruptured.

Martin Wittenhaagen, an agrarian elder whose hands knew the intimate geography of butchery and the delicate art of curing meats, stood at the foot of the stairs. His hunting knife, forged for dressing wild boar and separating marrow-rich joints, was tucked behind his belt. His bow was drawn nearly to the head, the taut string vibrating with a hum that belonged to the wild, acoustic quiet of the forests, not this hearth. But when the men lumbered back through the door without Gerard, the old forester was struck with profound amazement. He lowered his weapon, his mouth falling open.

The men, their boots scraping discordantly against the stone floor, were equally puzzled by the lethal posture they had caught him in.

“Why, mates, was the old fellow making ready to shoot at us?” one asked, his voice a harsh, guttural intrusion into the room’s quiet resonance.

“Stuff!” said Martin, recovering his stolid composure. He reached for a grounding, utilitarian excuse, though his native, agrarian cadence felt increasingly fragile against the dominant, martial lexicon of his adversaries. “I was but trying my new string. There! I'll unstring my bow, if you think that.”

“Humph!” said Dierich suspiciously, his tone carrying the abrasive edge of unsympathetic authority. “There is something more in you than I understand: put a log on, and let us dry our hides a bit ere we go.”

A blazing fire was soon resurrected. The men gathered around the hearth, their damp wool garments and long, rain-soaked hair soon smoking. The olfactory landscape grew dense—the comforting, caramelized aroma of burning sap clashing violently with the sour, metallic stench of wet armor and unwashed men. For a brief moment, the hiss and crackle of the wood restored a semblance of acoustic harmony. 

Then, the shrieks bled down from Margaret's room.

It was a sound of sheer, untranslatable terror, an utterance completely devoid of linguistic structure yet carrying the full, devastating weight of human vulnerability. It shattered the psychological well-being of the space, a piercing auditory violence that severed the connection between the safety of the hearth and the sanctuary above.

The men started up in a sudden, chaotic clamor. One of them seized a thick tallow candle—its scent of rendered mutton fat suddenly cloying in the tense air—and ran toward the steps that led to the bedrooms. Martin rose hastily. Confused by the sudden screams and profoundly understanding the danger posed by the man's crude curiosity, the old steward tried to throw himself in the path to prevent him from ascending. 

But the power dynamics of this new, violent vocabulary overwhelmed him. Dierich threw his heavy arms around Martin from behind, bellowing over the din for the others to keep him down. The man with the candle broke clear away, his heavy footfalls thundering up the stairs. The rest fell upon Martin. 

What followed was a fierce, discordant struggle, a tragic erasure of the elder’s dignity. More than once they were all rolling over the flour-dusted floorboards, Martin caught in the center of the fray. Despite his deep, marrow-deep strength, the sheer number of the men succeeded in mastering the old Samson. They bound him hand and foot with a coarse hempen rope originally intended for Gerard, pulling the knots tight with grunts that polluted the room's soundscape. 

Martin groaned aloud, a deep, guttural sound of cultural and personal defeat. He saw that the man had made his way to Margaret's room during the chaotic struggle, and here he lay, entirely powerless on the stones where he had spent decades preparing feasts of celebration and comfort.

“Ay, grind your teeth, you old rogue,” panted Dierich, breathless from the scuffle. “You shan't use them.”

“It is my belief, mates, that our lives were scarce safe while this old fellow's bones were free,” another soldier muttered, the sharp consonants of his dialect ringing with unearned triumph.

“He makes me think this Gerard is not far off,” put in a third.

“No such luck,” replied Dierich, brushing the domestic dust from his tabard. “Hallo, mates. Jorian Ketel is a long time in that girl's bedroom. Best go and see after him, some of us.”

Their rude laughter erupted—a shallow, ugly noise that mocked the historical sanctity of the home and demonstrated the casual cruelty of those who view others not as individuals, but as mere objects of conquest. 

The laughter had barely subsided into the smoky air when hasty footsteps were heard running along the floorboards overhead, a frantic, rhythmic drumming that promised further upheaval in the saga of the house.

“Oh, here he comes, at last,” said Dierich, looking upward. “Well, Jorian, what is to do now up there?”
