"I know that Deformed; he has been a vile thief these seven years; he goes up and down like a gentleman: I remember his name." — *Much Ado About Nothing*.

Amyas slept that night a weary and troubled slumber. Beside his pillow, his mother and Frank watched, seeing clearly that his mind was consumed by restless dreams.

It was no surprise. Beyond the sheer excitement of the day, the memory of John Oxenham had seized hold of his thoughts. Throughout the evening, sitting in the bay-windowed room where he had last seen the missing adventurer, Amyas had replayed every glance and gesture of the lost man, marveling at his own obsession until sleep claimed him, only to renew the fantasy in his dreams.

In his dream, he found himself sailing endlessly westward, chasing the fading sun, pursuing a tiny vessel that bore John Oxenham's identity. A terrible urgency gripped him; if he could not catch up, disaster awaited. Yet the ship refused to move. Surrounding them were the Sargasso Sea's tangled weeds, coiling like snakes to block the bow. Still, he strained against the water, forcing his mind to imagine motion, until darkness swallowed the sky. Then the moon rose, and suddenly John Oxenham's ship drifted close. Its sails were rent and flapping; tar leaked from its seams; the wood itself seemed to rot away. What hung from the mainyard? A line of hanged men. Worse still, from the yardarm directly overhead, John Oxenham's corpse stared down with grave, luminous eyes. He beckoned, pointing desperately—not ahead, but backward along their course. When Amyas turned to look back, the snow-capped peaks of the Andes glittered in the moonlight. He realized he was in the South Seas once more, with all of America separating him from home. The corpse continued to point back, its eyes filled with agonizing longing, its lips moving as if to whisper an awful secret. Startled awake by a cry of terror, Amyas found himself in the small coved chamber of dear old Burrough, the gray autumn morning already filtering in.

Feverish and unable to rest, he rose after an hour of tossing and dressed to take a bath on his beloved pebble ridge. Passing his mother's door, he paused. The dim morning light revealed the bed, untouched since the previous night. Inside, his mother, clad in a long white nightgown, knelt at her prayer desk, deeply absorbed in devotion. Without a word, he slipped in and knelt beside her. She turned, smiling, and draped an arm around him, continuing her silent prayers. Why not? They were prayers for him, and he knew it; he prayed for her, for the lost John Oxenham, and for his vanished crew.

Finally, she rose. Standing above him, she brushed the yellow locks from his brow, gazing into his face with deep affection. Between these two souls, clear as glass, there was nothing to conceal. Each understood the other perfectly. After a long, mutual gaze, she stooped to kiss his forehead, then began to turn away when a single tear fell onto his skin. Her bare feet peeked out from beneath her dress. He bent down, kissing them repeatedly, then looked up as if seeking forgiveness.

"You have such pretty feet, mother!"

With instant feminine instinct, she hid them. Once a beauty herself, though her hair was now gray and her youth long past, she remained radiant to anyone who looked beyond surface appearances.

"Your dear father used to say so thirty years ago."

"And I say so still: you were always beautiful; you are beautiful now."

"What is that to you, silly boy? Will you play the lover with an old mother? Go take your walk, and think of younger ladies, if you can find any worthy of you."

Thus the son departed, and the mother returned to her prayers.

He walked down to the pebble ridge, where the ocean waves, over ages, had built a rampart of gray boulders—some two miles long, curved and smoothed with such cunning it seemed crafted by human hands—to protect a fertile strip of alluvial turf from the high tides of spring and autumn. Sniffing the crisp salt air like a seasoned sailor, he stripped and plunged into the breaking surf. He dove, rolled, and tossed amidst the foam with strong arms until he heard a hail from the shore. Looking up, he saw the tall figure of his cousin, Eustace, standing atop the rampart.

Amyas felt a twinge of disappointment. Love-struck as he was, he had dreamed the entire journey of Rose Salterne and had no desire for a companion to interrupt his reverie. Yet, having not seen Eustace in three years, civility dictated he scramble out and dress while his cousin paced the grass below.

Eustace Leigh was the son of a younger brother of the Leighs of Burrough, a man who had largely severed ties with his family and country by remaining a Catholic. Though born a Papist, he had converted to Protestantism under Edward VI, only to revert under Mary. However, unlike many others who switched allegiance again under Elizabeth, Eustace possessed enough integrity to stop there. Thus, he remained a Catholic, living in seclusion in a large, rambling, shadowy house known as "Chapel," perched on the Atlantic cliffs in Moorwinstow parish, near Sir Richard Grenville's estate at Stow. The penal laws rarely bothered him; they targeted only those who made conspiracy and rebellion core tenets of their faith, or those foolish enough to demand martyrdom despite the counsel of Burleigh. Furthermore, his remote location served as a perfect sanctuary. Dozens of plots could be hatched there unheard of, and Jesuits and seminary priests moved in and out unnoticed, finding a peculiar thrill in their clandestine lives—celebrating Mass in secret chambers in the roof, playing at persecution, and preaching of hiding in caves.

Once, when the zealous local parson discovered the presence of "mass priests" and complained formally to Sir Richard, invoking the Act of the Fourteenth of Elizabeth, the knight merely rebuked him soundly as a fanatical Puritan and told him to mind his own business. Temporal authority kept a firm grip on spiritual affairs in those days, and since Mr. Thomas Leigh paid his tithes faithfully, the parson subsided, content to eat Mr. Leigh's dinners and ignore the vocation of Father Francis, who sat opposite him disguised as a layman and tutor.

Yet, the burden of conscience weighed heavily on poor Mr. Thomas Leigh regarding certain lands formerly belonging to the Abbey of Hartland. He feared losing them for holding them, yet he did not fully believe he would, nor did he surrender them—a compromise typical of many "Catholic" subjects of Mary Tudor, whose consciensces demanded return to the Church but not necessarily the restitution of stolen wealth. Like many, Mr. Leigh felt the abbey rents burning in his pocket. Following the common logic that two wrongs might make a right, he compounded his error by befriending foreign priests, listening to their schemes, and sacrificing sons here and there as offerings to the altar of superstition, sending them to Douai, Rheims, or Rome to be trained in the arts of villainy.

One such unfortunate sacrifice was Eustace Leigh, whom his father sent to Rheims as payment for his soul's sins.

He had indeed become a skilled liar. Not inherently wicked, he had been selected by the harpies at home for his "peculiar vocation"—his capacity for vague, hysterical fears of the unseen (what we now call religion) combined with a latent tendency toward roguery, traits favored by superstitious minds. Now a tall, handsome man with a high forehead, a small mouth, and a dry, set expression, Eustace constantly struggled to appear free and cheerful. He forced smiles and dimples, believing that Christian love required cheerfulness. Yet his premeditated kindness looked no more inviting than premeditated malice. Had he not been truly handsome, women who praised his sweetness would have compared his "frankness" to a skeleton dancing in chains.

Returning to England a month prior, obeying a proclamation ordering parents to recall children abroad within four months, Eustace stayed briefly with his father at Chapel before attending to private matters for the virtuous society that raised him—one of which duties had brought him to Bideford the night before.

Sitting beside Amyas on the pebbles, Eustace scanned him with gentle, cornered glances, careful not to offend either his cousin or the flies on his back. Amyas turned fully, offering a hearty smile and extending a massive hand. Eustace grasped it rapturously, leading to a vigorous handshake. Amyas gripped with a round fist, vibrating slightly to convey, *"I am glad to see you,"* while Eustace pinched hard with pale, straight fingers, sawing the air violently to scream, *"Don't you see how glad I am?"* A stark contrast to their previous parting.

"Hold fast, old lad," Amyas warned, "before you break my elbow. And where do you come from?"

"From wandering the earth and walking up and down in it," Eustace replied with a smug nod of mysterious importance.

"Like the devil, eh? Well, everyone has their model. How is my uncle?"
