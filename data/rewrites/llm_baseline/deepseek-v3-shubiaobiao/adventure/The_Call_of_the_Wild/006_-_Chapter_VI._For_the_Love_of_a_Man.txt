The brutal winter had nearly claimed John Thornton—his partners had left him behind to recover while they pushed on toward Dawson to haul a raft of logs. By spring, the last of his limp faded, and he spent the long, sun-drenched days by the riverbank with Buck, the great dog slowly mending beside him.

Rest was a luxury after three thousand miles of torment. Buck’s wounds closed, his muscles thickened, and his ribs vanished beneath renewed flesh. They were all idle—Thornton, Buck, and the two other dogs, Skeet and Nig—waiting for the raft that would carry them downstream. Skeet, a gentle Irish setter, had nursed Buck as tenderly as a mother cat cleans her kittens. Nig, quieter but just as kind, was a massive black crossbreed with laughing eyes.

To Buck’s surprise, neither resented him. They shared Thornton’s generosity. As Buck regained strength, they lured him into wild games, and even Thornton would abandon dignity to join. Through laughter and roughhousing, Buck discovered something new: love. Not the dutiful bond of Judge Miller’s household, but something fierce, consuming. Thornton had saved him, yes—but more than that, he was a master unlike any other. Other men cared for their dogs out of obligation; Thornton did it as if they were his own blood.

Buck adored him. He didn’t nudge for attention like Skeet or lean heavily like Nig. Instead, he watched, studied, memorized every flicker of Thornton’s expression. He would lie for hours, gaze fixed, heart pounding, until Thornton turned—and then their eyes would meet, wordless, brimming with understanding.

Yet beneath this devotion, the wildness in Buck still stirred. He fought with the same ruthless cunning as before, dominating any challenger. Mercy was a foreign concept—kill or be killed, the law of the wild. At night, whispers from ancient ancestors coiled in his dreams, urging him toward the forest’s depths. But always, Thornton’s love pulled him back.

Others meant nothing. Travelers might praise him; Buck remained indifferent. Even Thornton’s partners, Hans and Pete, earned only tolerance. But for Thornton? There was nothing Buck wouldn’t do.

One afternoon, perched on a cliff’s edge, Thornton—reckless with affection—pointed to the abyss below. *“Jump, Buck!”* The dog lunged without hesitation, and Thornton barely caught him in time.

*“It’s uncanny,”* Pete muttered afterward.

*“No,”* Thornton said, shaken. *“It’s terrible. And magnificent.”*

Months later, in a Circle City saloon, a brute named Burton struck Thornton without warning. Buck moved like lightning, his jaws closing on the man’s throat before the crowd could react. The attack earned him a reputation—and Thornton’s life, again, when their boat capsized in violent rapids. Buck fought the current, dragging Thornton to safety despite broken ribs.

But his greatest trial came in Dawson, where a boast turned wager: could Buck pull a sled laden with a thousand pounds? The stakes were high—Thornton’s pride, their fortune. Men laughed, doubting. Yet when Thornton knelt, whispering *“As you love me,”* Buck answered with a pressure of teeth against his hand—not in warning, but promise.

Harnessed, he strained. The sled groaned, ice cracking beneath the runners. The crowd fell silent. Then, with a surge, Buck moved it—not just breaking free, but hauling it a hundred yards. Cheers erupted. Thornton, breathless, stumbled forward as Buck returned to him, panting, triumphant.

In that moment, Buck was more than a dog. He was force, loyalty, love incarnate. And though the wild still called to him, its voice grew dim beside the one thing that bound him to the world of men—John Thornton.
