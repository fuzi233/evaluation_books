Did tragedy always strike with such uncalculated ferocity? Had inconsistencies in human nature always surfaced with such impulsive violence from the dawn of time until this very hour? Yet, not a single soul present—whether haunted by the memory of Bartow’s silent pantomime or oblivious to it alike—failed to register a profound skepticism regarding the tale of Mr. Adams's demise. As Thomas Adams spoke, pouring out his version of events with a volubility that bordered on the frantic, a cold, collective disbelief settled over the room.

Young Thomas, possessing a susceptibility to emotion that was nearly acute, felt the weight of their gaze. His head bowed, the burden of their doubt pressing down upon him like a physical weight.

"I shall add nothing to and take nothing from what I have said," he muttered, his voice dogged and hollow. "Make of it what you will."

Inspector Gryce, directing the inquiry, exchanged a dubious glance with Detective Bartow as these words hung in the air. Whereupon the detective stepped forward, his tone professional yet probing.

"We are sorry you have adopted such a rigid resolution, Mr. Adams," Gryce said. "There remain many threads unspooled, matters yet to be explained. For instance, if your brother indeed slew himself in this unexpected fashion, why did you leave the house with such precipitous haste? Why did you depart without raising an alarm or even formally declaring your relationship to him?"

"You need not answer, you know," the Inspector interjected quickly, his voice cutting through the tension. "In this free and independent country, no man is compelled to incriminate himself."

A shadow, the saddest expression ever etched onto mortal lips, drifted across the prisoner's pallid face for a fleeting moment. Then, lifting his chin, Thomas replied with an air that borderd on sheer desperation.

"Incrimination is not my primary fear now. By the looks you all cast upon me, I perceive that I am already lost; I possess no evidence to substantiate my story."

This admission, which might easily have been mistaken for the lamentation of an innocent man crushed under suspicion, caused the Inspector to stare in surprise.

"You forget," the Inspector suggested gently, "that you were accompanied by your wife. She can corroborate your account, and undoubtedly, she will prove an invaluable witness in your favor."

"My wife!" Thomas repeated, the word catching in his throat as if choked by grief. "Must she be dragged into this? So sick, so weak a woman? To subject her to this would kill her, sir. She loves me—she..."

"Was she with you in Mr. Adams's study?" the Inspector pressed, undeterred. "Did she see him raise the dagger against his own breast?"

"No." With this denial, a flicker of renewed courage ignited within the young man. "She had fainted mere moments prior, while the altercation between my brother and myself reached its fever pitch. She witnessed neither the final blow nor the aftermath. Gentlemen, I might as well speak the absolute truth—I have nothing to gain by silence. She finds it just as difficult as you do to believe that Mr. Adams struck himself. I have exhausted every art to impress the reality upon her, but oh, what hope remains for a man when the wife of his bosom—an angel, too, who loves me with a devotion that transcends reason—is unable to testify? She is too conscientious, too faithful to her own convictions. If I were to force her hand, I would lose her, and she... she would perish."

Bartow attempted to interrupt, but Thomas would not be silenced.

"Spare me, sirs! Spare my wife! Write me down guilty, condemn me to any fate you please, rather than compel that fragile young creature to speak!"

The Inspector, sensing the emotional fracture tearing at the hearts of everyone present, cut short these anguished pleas. "Have you read the newspapers for the past few days?" he asked abruptly.

"Yes, yes, sir," Thomas stammered, the rhythm of his speech faltering. "How could I avoid it? Blood is blood; the man was my brother. I had left him dying—I was naturally anxious, naturally saw my own peril looming—and I read them, of course."

"Then you are aware that he was discovered with a large wooden cross pinned to his breast? A cross that formerly adorned the wall. Explain how it came to be torn down. Who placed it upon his bosom?"

"I, sir," Thomas admitted, his voice steady despite the tremor in his hands. "Though I am not Catholic, Felix was. Seeing him dying without absolution, without the sacraments, I thought of the holy symbol. I tore down the only cross I could find and placed it in his arms."

"A pious act," the Inspector noted. "Did he recognize it?"

"I cannot say. I had my fainting wife to attend to. She consumed all my thoughts."

"And you carried her out," the Inspector continued, weaving a net around the suspect, "so absorbed in caring for her that you failed to notice Mr. Adams's valet?"

"He is innocent, sir!" Thomas protested vehemently. "Whatever the public may whisper, he had absolutely nothing to do with this crime!"

"You did not observe him, I say," the Inspector countered, his voice rising, "standing in the doorway, watching you?"

At this, the Inspector's eyes narrowed. He knew that Bartow had not been standing in the open doorway, but concealed at the loophole above. Yet, the opportunity to entrap the witness was too potent to waste.

Thomas appeared trapped in the snare, or so it seemed from the beads of sweat that suddenly bloomed upon his pale forehead. Still, he struggled to maintain his precarious stance, shouting hotly:

"That man is mad! And deaf-and-dumb, as the papers report! Surely his testimony is worthless. You would not dare confront me with him?"

"We confront you with no one. We merely posed a question. You did not observe the valet, then?"

"No, sir."

"Nor do you comprehend the mystery of the colored lights?"

"No, sir."

"Nor the arrangement of the steel plate and the other contrivances with which your brother enlivened his solitude?"

"I do not follow you, sir." However, a subtle shift in his tone betrayed a growing unease.

"I see," the Inspector declared, leaning forward, "that the complexities which have disturbed us and necessitated this lengthy delay in gathering testimony were not part of the crime as you have described it. This suggests two possibilities: either you have omitted significant details, or the narrative you have constructed is fundamentally flawed. Nevertheless, there remains a specific circumstance requiring explanation—a minor detail, yet one of paramount importance. Your wife mentioned it to you immediately upon regaining consciousness. I refer to the six or more words penned by your brother immediately preceding his death. The paper containing them has been recovered, and it is evident that it was a central factor in your quarrel, evidenced by her regret that it remained behind you, and his... Do you know where we found this paper?"

The eyes Thomas raised toward this query were devoid of intelligence. The mere sight of that scrap of paper seemed to drain him instantly of every faculty he had summoned to sustain his unequal struggle. He shook his head frantically, reaching out a hand that failed to grasp the document offered by the Inspector. Then, a sound erupted from him—a loud, ragged cry.

"Enough! I cannot endure this further, supported only by a wicked lie! I killed my brother for reasons as valid as any man has ever had for taking a life. But I shall not reveal them. I would rather stand trial for murder and hang than live with this deception!"

It was a total collapse, pitiful in its stark contrast to the man's herculean physique and the fine, though contracted, lines of his features. If it were an ending, it was a tragic one. Mr. Gryce, a deep furrow having formed between his brows, slowly rose and positioned himself beside the young man, who appeared ready to succumb entirely. The Inspector, conversely, remained motionless. He had begun tapping a rhythmic code with his fingers upon the table, seemingly intent on continuing the beat when another sudden, piercing cry shattered the silence.

"What is that?" Thomas demanded, his eyes locking onto the door, his entire frame convulsing with violent shaking.

"Nothing," the Inspector began, but the heavy oak doors swung wide, and a figure emerged. A woman, pale as a ghost and radiant with a sort of holy, terrifying passion, darted from the grasp of a man seeking to restrain her. She stood before them, palpitating with a protest that momentarily defied utterance.

It was Adams's young, invalid wife, whom he had abandoned three hours prior at Belleville. Her form was so frail, her features so exquisite, that she might have passed for some unearthly visitor save for the human anguish radiating from her countenance. Soon, this anguish found vent in a touchingly desperate cry:

"What is he saying? Oh, I know perfectly well what he is saying! He claims that he killed his brother, that he held the dagger that removed a monster from the world, a wickedness known to none. But you must not heed him! Indeed, you must not heed him! He is innocent! I, his wife, have traveled twenty miles, rising from a bed of weakness and suffering, to tell you so. He—"
