I have no words to paint the full, bone-deep horror of the night that swallowed us. Mercifully, exhaustion dulled the edge a little—even men marked for death cannot fight their own bodies forever, and sleep tugged at the edges of our consciousness when we least expected it. But I never slept long.

Set aside the cold, crawling terror of the fate waiting for us—even the bravest man alive would have quailed at the thought of slow, dark starvation miles under the earth, and I never pretended to be brave. The silence itself was a torture no man could have borne for long. You may have laid awake at home, thinking the quiet of midnight oppressive, but you can never know what perfect stillness feels like: a heavy, tangible thing that presses against your eardrums, that smothers every small noise you take for granted on the surface. Up above ground there is always a faint hum—wind in grass, a distant bird, the faint thrum of living things, no matter how quiet the hour. But here there was nothing.

We were buried in the guts of a snow-capped peak. Thousands of feet over our heads, gales ripped across white drifts, but not a single breath of that wind reached us. A long stone tunnel and five feet of solid rock separated us even from the silent chamber of the dead, where Foulata lay cold and still. Not the roar of every artillery gun on earth could have cut through that stone to reach us. We were cut off from every echo of the living world—we were men already in their graves.

The irony of it gnawed at me. All around us, stacked in chests, lay enough diamonds to pay off a small nation’s entire national debt, enough to build a whole fleet of ironclad warships. We would have traded every last carat without a second thought for the faintest crack of a door, for a single chance to crawl back to the air. Soon, we knew, we would happily barter those same treasures for a crust of biltong, a sip of water. After that, we would give all of it away just for the pain to end. The wealth men spend their whole lives chasing is worth nothing at all when you are trapped in a vault, dying of thirst.

The hours dragged on, no sun, no moon, only the faint tick of my watch I could feel against my wrist.

“Good,” Sir Henry’s voice boomed out of the dark so sharp it made us all jump, cutting through the dead silence like a knife, “how many matches do you have left in that box?”

“Eight, Curtis.”

“Strike one. Let’s check the time.”

The tiny flame flared so bright after hours of total blackness it nearly blinded all three of us. It was five o’clock. Far above our heads, the dawn would be blushing pink on the snow drifts, the morning wind stirring mist in the mountain hollows, and we would never see it.

“We ought to eat something, keep our strength up,” I suggested.

“What’s the point?” Good’s voice was hoarse. “The sooner we’re done the better.”

“While there’s life, there’s hope,” Sir Henry said, steady as ever.

We forced ourselves to chew a little dry meat, sip from our dwindling water skins, and more time slipped by. Then Sir Henry suggested we make our way to the sealed stone door and yell, on the one in a million chance someone outside would hear. Good, who’d spent decades at sea and had a piercing, far-carrying yell trained to cut through storm winds, stumbled down the passage and howled. I never heard such a racket. It might as well have been a mosquito buzzing for all the good it did. No sound traveled through that five feet of rock. He stumbled back thirsty, drank half his remaining water, and we agreed to stop yelling—we couldn’t waste the moisture.

We slumped back against the chests of useless diamonds, trapped in that terrible, motionless waiting, and I broke. I rested my head on Sir Henry’s broad shoulder and burst into tears. I could hear Good sniffling on the other side, swearing hoarsely at himself for crying like a child.

Sir Henry was gentle as a nurse tending two sick babies. He forgot his own terror entirely, telling us stories of explorers who’d been buried in avalanches and trapped in cave-ins, who’d fought their way out against all odds. When that didn’t cheer us, he pointed out that death came for all men eventually, that dying of exhaustion would be fast and painless. Then, quiet and a little shy, he suggested we pray to a higher power, and I did so with more fervor than I ever had in my life. That man’s character was as solid as the mountain around us: quiet, unshakable, good.

What felt like another full day of waiting dragged by, no light, no sound. When I struck another match, it was seven o’clock. We ate the last of our rations, and that was when the thought hit me.

“Wait a minute,” I said. “How is the air in here still fresh? It’s thick, but it’s not stale. We should have suffocated hours ago.”

Good shot straight up, so fast he bashed his head on a chest. “Great heavens— I never thought of that. That sealed stone door is airtight. There has to be a draught somewhere.”

That tiny spark of hope lit a fire under all of us. We dropped to our hands and knees immediately, groping across the rock floor for the faintest stir of moving air. I ran my hand straight into something cold and soft a minute later: Foulata’s dead face. My heart lurched.

We crawled on for an hour, bashing our heads on ivory tusks and chest corners, scraped raw, until Sir Henry and I collapsed, ready to give up. But Good kept crawling, muttering that it was better than sitting still waiting to die.

“Hey, you two,” he called out a minute later, his voice tight with excitement. “Come over here.”

We scrambled to his side as fast as we could.

“Quatermain, put your hand right here where mine is. Feel that?”

“I think— I think I feel cool air coming up through the floor.”

“Stand back.” He stamped hard on the rock beneath his feet. The sound rang hollow.

My hands shook so bad I nearly dropped the last three matches we had left. The tiny flame showed we were in the far corner of the vault, a spot we’d never properly checked in our earlier frantic searches. There was a thin, hairline crack in the rock floor, and set perfectly flush with the stone, carved from the same solid rock, was a stone ring.

We were too breathless to speak. Good pulled out his pocket knife, the one with the small hook for picking stones out of horses’ hooves, and scraped around the edge of the ring, working the hook under it carefully. The stone hadn’t rusted shut in all the centuries it had lain there, as an iron ring would have. He pulled it up until it stood straight upright.

We all took turns heaving on it, straining every muscle, and the stone slab wouldn’t budge. Finally Good looped his neat black silk handkerchief— he’d never taken it off, even through all the mess— through the ring, and all three of us braced our feet. Sir Henry put his enormous, explorer’s strength behind the pull, his back muscles cracking with the effort, and Good and I hauled for all we were worth.

“Heave! It’s giving!” Sir Henry gasped. There was a long, grinding scrape of stone on stone, a rush of cold fresh air that hit us full in the faces, and all three of us went tumbling backwards as the heavy flagstone slammed down on top of our legs.

“Light a match, Quatermain,” Sir Henry panted, once we’d scrambled to our feet. “Carefully, now.”

I did. Staring up at us from the dark hole under the floor was the first step of a cut stone stair.

“Where does it go?” Good asked.

“Down, for one thing. We follow it. Trust to Providence,” Sir Henry said. “Grab the last of the biltong and the water skins, Quatermain. We might need it.”

I crawled back to our old spot by the diamond chests to grab them. For twenty four hours I’d barely thought of the gems— the very sight of them made me sick, they were the reason we were trapped here. But old habits die hard. I stuffed every pocket of my old shooting coat and my trousers full of the biggest, clearest diamonds from the nearest chest, then grabbed Foulata’s empty woven basket and filled it heaped to the brim with stones too.

“Hey, you two! You want to grab some diamonds before we go?” I called. “My pockets and the basket are full.”

“Come on, Quatermain, hang the diamonds!” Sir Henry laughed, half exasperated. “I never want to see another gem for the rest of my life.”

Good didn’t answer. I knew he was kneeling beside Foulata’s body, saying his last goodbyes to the girl who’d loved him enough to die for him. If you’d spent twenty eight hours trapped in that dark vault, starving and thirsty, you’d never waste energy hauling heavy diamonds into an unknown tunnel, either. If it wasn’t for my old explorer habit of never leaving something valuable behind if there was even a sliver of a chance I could carry it out, I would have left them too.

Sir Henry stepped onto the first stair, leading the way. We counted fifteen steps down before his feet hit flat ground. “It’s a tunnel,” he said. “Follow me.”

Good went next, I came last hefting the heavy diamond basket, and I lit the second to last match. By its faint light we could see a narrow stone gallery stretching off left and right, straight out from the stairwell. The flame burned my fingers and went out before we could see more. But we’d seen it: when the match was lit, the draught of air had blown the flame hard to the left.

“Go against the draught,” Good said. “The air’s being pulled in from outside.” We felt our way along the wall, one hand on rock, the other testing the ground in front of every step, and left that cursed treasure vault behind us forever. No living soul would likely find that vault again, but if they did, they’d see our open chests of jewels, our empty lamp, and Foulata’s bones, waiting there in the dark.

We stumbled for what felt like hours through the tunnels, one gallery branching off into the next, winding like a maze. We figured they must be the old ancient diamond mine workings the Kukuana people had never known existed, tunnelled centuries before for the ore veins. By the time we ran out of the last of our food and water, our throats as dry as lime kilns, we were certain we’d just traded one dark grave for another.

Then I heard it. A faint, faraway murmur. The others froze, and they heard it too. After all those hours of perfect dead silence, the soft sound of running water was the most beautiful thing I’d ever heard.

“Come on!” Good yelped, and we stumbled as fast as we could toward the sound. I almost dropped Foulata’s basket a dozen times, but I hung onto it anyway— might as well die rich as poor, I thought. Good was leading when we got right up to it, and he slipped. Splash. A yell echoed out of the dark.

“Good! Where are you?” we screamed, terrified.

“I’m alright!” His voice came out choked. “I caught hold of a rock. Light a match!”

I lit the very last match. Its tiny glow showed a wild, rushing underground river cutting right across the tunnel, far too wide to see the far bank, and Good clinging to a jagged rock sticking out of the current.

“Stand clear, I’m swimming for it!” he shouted. He stroked hard through the racing water, grabbed Sir Henry’s outstretched hand, and we hauled him back onto dry stone, dripping and gasping. That river ran as fast as a mill race, and there was no bottom— if he’d missed that rock, he’d have been swept away into total darkness forever.

We drank our fill of the cold, fresh river water, scrubbed the grime off our faces, then turned back from the bank to pick a new gallery. Sir Henry led us, half delirious with exhaustion, stumbling for what felt like another hour. Then he stopped so abruptly we all bumped into his back.

“Look,” he whispered. “Is my mind playing tricks on me, or is that light?”

Far ahead of us, no bigger than a cottage window pane, was a faint, silvery glimmer. No eye that hadn’t stared at total pitch black for 30 hours could have spotted it. We pushed forward, gasping, and the light got brighter. The rock walls ended, and the tunnel turned to packed earth, narrowing smaller and smaller, until it was no bigger than a fox’s den.

We crawled through it on our bellies, squeezing through the narrow dirt gap, and suddenly we were out. The blessed stars blazed right over our heads, fresh mountain air filled our lungs, and we went tumbling head over heels down a steep grassy slope, rolling through wet brush and soft loam. The diamond basket caught on a bush and stopped me. I sat up and yelled as loud as I could.

An answering shout came from down the slope, where Sir Henry had come to a stop on level ground. Good was a little further down, wedged in a forked tree root, bruised and shaken but fine. We all collapsed on the grass, and cried like children for joy. We were alive. The dawn was blushing rose pink on the snow peaks far above us, the same dawn we’d been certain we’d never see again.

When the light got bright enough to see each other, we laughed at the state of us: gaunt, hollow-eyed, caked head to toe in mud and dust, scraped and bleeding, and Good’s monocle was still stuck firm in his eye. He’d never once taken it out, not through the dark, not through the river plunge, not through rolling down that slope.

We dragged ourselves up the steep clay slope of the great pit that sat before the hidden cave mouth, hauling ourselves up by grass roots and scrub branches, until we hit the main trail on the far side from the three giant stone colossi that guarded the cave entrance. A hundred yards off, a fire burned in front of a ring of huts, with dark shapes gathered around it.

We stumbled toward them, supporting each other, stopping every few steps to catch our breath. One man looked up, saw us, and screamed, falling flat on his face in terror.

“Infadoos! Infadoos, it is us, your friends!”

The old warrior scrambled to his feet, ran to us, staring wild eyed like he was seeing ghosts. He threw himself at Sir Henry’s knees, weeping so hard he could barely speak.

“Oh my lords! My lords! You have come back from the dead! Come back from the grave!”
