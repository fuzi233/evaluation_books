Every year on the fifteenth of October, Fifth Avenue’s brownstone shutters creaked open for the first time since the summer exodus to Newport, wool carpets stiff with camphor unfurled across oak floors, and triple layers of heavy silk window curtains were hung to block the autumn drafts seeping off the Hudson. By the first of November, that annual household ritual was complete, and New York society drifted back into town to lay their visiting cards on hall tables and take stock of one another again. By the fifteenth of the month, the season was in full blast: opera posters were plastered to gas lampposts, dinner invitations stacked high on mantels, and dance dates scribbled in the leather backs of young ladies’ diaries.

And as regular as clockwork, Mrs. Archer always declared at this exact point that New York was very much changed. Perched on her high embroidered armchair as a happy non-participant in the bustle, she enlisted Mr. Sillerton Jackson and Miss Sophy Jackson to help her trace every new crack snaking across the surface of the old familiar social order, every strange, unruly weed pushing up between the neatly planted rows of “proper” social vegetables. As a boy, Newland Archer had loved waiting for this annual pronouncement, listening to her list the tiny, quiet signs of disintegration his own careless, young gaze had never noticed. To Mrs. Archer’s mind, New York never changed except for the worse, and Miss Sophy agreed with her whole heart. Mr. Sillerton Jackson, worldly old gentleman that he was, suspended his judgment and listened to their lamentations with amused impartiality – but even he never denied the city was shifting under their feet.

Two years into his marriage to May, Newland found himself forced to admit the same.

All this came up over Mrs. Archer’s Thanksgiving dinner, the table heaped with bronze chrysanthemums and slices of spiced pumpkin pie. It was her custom, on the day the whole city was officially enjoined to count its blessings, to take a mournful, un-bitter stock of the world around her, and wonder what exactly there was to be grateful for. Certainly not the state of fashionable society, which the new progressive rector of St. Matthew’s, Dr. Ashmore, had thundered against that very morning in a fiery Thanksgiving sermon pulled straight from the book of Jeremiah. He spoke constantly of the “trend” of modern high society, and Mrs. Archer found it both terrifying and thrilling to feel she was part of something so tangible and measurable, like a crack running down the wall of your own house.

“There is no doubt of it, that trend is very marked,” she said, tapping her knitting needles together.

Miss Sophy tittered that it was odd to preach of such things on Thanksgiving, and Mrs. Archer dryly replied that Dr. Ashmore simply meant they ought to give thanks for all the old, good things that were left.

Archer had always smiled at his mother’s annual vaticinations, but this year, as they ticked through the list of new oddities, he could not deny the trend was impossible to miss.

“The extravagance of the dresses!” Miss Sophy exclaimed. “Sillerton took me to the opera opening night, and Jane Merry’s gown was the only one I recognized from last year – and even that had a new front panel sewn into it. I know for a fact she bought it from Worth only two years ago, my seamstress always goes to remade her Paris gowns before she wears them, after all.”

“Ah, Jane Merry is one of us,” Mrs. Archer sighed. No one her age dreamed of wearing a new Paris gown straight off the Customs boat, the way every woman in town was doing now, instead of locking them away in camphor chests for a full year to “mellow.” Miss Sophy recalled that in her youth, wearing the absolute latest fashion was considered vulgar, and in Boston, proper ladies stored their Worth gowns for two full years before wearing them. She told the story of old Mrs. Baxter Pennilow, who ordered twelve new Paris dresses a year as a standing habit, and when she died after two years of illness, her family found forty-eight gowns still wrapped in tissue paper, unworn, that the girls wore to symphony concerts for years without ever looking out of date.

Mrs. Archer conceded Boston was more conservative, but keeping a new French gown for at least a season was a safe, sensible rule. “Beaufort started this reckless new nonsense, making Regina put every new garment on her back the second they dock,” she said, and Miss Sophy trailed off, not wanting to say in front of Janey Archer that poor Regina now risked looking just as gaudy and common as the social climbers she once outshone. Sillerton Jackson supplied the quip smoothly: “Like her rivals,” and the ladies murmured in agreement.

The tone shifted dark, fast, when Mrs. Archer brought up Beaufort’s rumored bad speculations. Everyone had heard the whispers, and no one wanted to confirm them out loud. The whole table fell into a gloomy quiet. No one had ever liked Julius Beaufort, and no one minded gossiping about his messy private affairs – but the thought that he might drag his wife’s respected Dallas family into financial dishonor was too shocking even for his enemies to enjoy. Old New York turned a blind eye to hypocrisy in private lives, but business demanded total, spotless honesty. It had been decades since a well-known local banker had disgraced himself through fraud, but no one had forgotten how his entire family had been erased from society overnight. Not all the power of the combined Dallas clan would save poor Regina if the rumors were true.

The talk drifted back to safer topics, but every new thing they named only confirmed Mrs. Archer’s sense that the trend was speeding up faster every day. “I know, Newland, that you let dear May go to Mrs. Struthers’s Sunday evenings –” she began, and May cut in brightly, laughing, “Oh, everyone goes there now! She was even invited to Granny Mingott’s last reception.”

Archer smiled to himself, thinking that this was exactly how New York did its quiet social revolutions. They conspired to ignore the change until it was well and truly settled, then pretended it had always been that way. There was always a traitor inside the citadel, handing over the keys – once everyone had tasted Mrs. Struthers’s easy hospitality, no one was going to sit at home and pretend to be scandalized that her champagne was once advertised as shoe polish.

“I know, dear, I know,” Mrs. Archer sighed. “Amusement is all anyone cares about these days, I suppose. But I have never quite forgiven your cousin Madame Olenska for being the very first person to countenance Mrs. Struthers.”

A hot, vivid blush flared across May’s face so suddenly it surprised Archer as much as every other guest at the table. “Oh, Ellen –” she murmured, in that exact same soft, disapproving, apologetic tone her parents would have used to say “Oh, the Blenkers,” that bohemian crowd of writers and artists the family refused to acknowledge. Archer stared at her, a strange cold tingle running down his spine – for a second she felt like a stranger, perfectly molded to every unspoken rule of the world around her, a part of him he had never seen before. His mother insisted that someone like Ellen, who had grown up in old European aristocratic circles, ought to be the one helping them guard their social lines, not blurring them. May’s blush did not fade, it stayed bright and unmistakeable, heavy with some secret meaning beyond simple disapproval of Ellen’s social missteps. “I do not think Ellen cares for society at all,” she said, groping for something neutral. “No one really knows what she does care for.”

Everyone in the family knew Ellen was no longer in their good graces. Even old Catherine Manson Mingott, her most loyal champion, had been unable to defend her refusal to return to her husband Count Olenski. The Mingotts would never air their disapproval in public – their sense of family solidarity was too strong. They had simply, as Mrs. Welland put it, “let poor Ellen find her own level” – and to their shock and horror, that level was down in the dim, messy world of the Blenkers, where “people who wrote” held untidy parties and no one cared a fig for old social rules. Everyone agreed it was a fatal mistake on Ellen’s part to refuse to go back to her husband; after all, a young woman’s place was under her husband’s roof, especially when she had left it under circumstances no one cared to dig too deeply into.

“Madame Olenska is a great favorite with all the gentlemen,” Miss Sophy said, sweet and sharp, dropping her little dart gently. Mrs. Archer mournfully agreed that that was exactly the danger a woman like Ellen was always exposed to, and the ladies gathered up their silk trains to drift towards the drawing room, where the soft carcel gas globes glowed above the velvet sofas, leaving Archer and Sillerton Jackson alone in the Gothic library.

Settled in front of the crackling grate, smoking a perfect cigar to make up for the mediocre dinner, Sillerton Jackson grew portentous, and loose-lipped. “If the Beaufort crash comes,” he said, “there are going to be disclosures. Ugly ones. He has not spent all his money on Regina.”

Archer dismissed it at first, saying he thought Beaufort would pull himself out of the hole, but Sillerton insisted Beaufort had gone to beg favours from the city’s most powerful men that very day – and even if they tided him over this time, poor Regina might end up stuck in some shabby European spa for bankrupts for the rest of her life.

Archer said nothing, his mind wandering. What had that blush of May’s meant, when they mentioned Ellen? Four whole months had passed since that midsummer day he had spent with Madame Olenska, and he had not seen her once. He knew she had returned to Washington, the tiny quiet house she shared with Medora Manson. He had written her once, a short note asking when they might meet again, and she had replied even more briefly: Not yet. No word had passed between them since. He had built a little secret sanctuary in his own mind, where Ellen reigned as the centre of his real life – all the books he read, all the thoughts he could not say out loud, all the feelings that nourished him, were kept there. The rest of his days, his actual life, felt thin and unreal, like he was wandering through a house that was not his, bumping into familiar furniture of tradition and prejudice like an absent-minded man who no longer recognized his own home.

Sillerton cleared his throat, dropping his next, far heavier bombshell. The family had cut Ellen’s allowance drastically the second she confirmed she would not return to Olenski. She had also forfeited the large marriage settlement that came with her wedding, money Olenski had been fully ready to sign over to her the second she came back to him. Worse, every last penny Medora Manson owned was tied up in Beaufort’s speculations. If Beaufort crashed, Ellen and Medora would be completely penniless. The only person who could give her enough money to live on was old Catherine Mingott – and everyone knew how fiercely she hated to part with her gold. The rest of the family had no reason at all to keep Ellen in New York, away from her husband.

Archer jumped to his feet, his fist slamming down hard on the edge of the walnut writing table, making the brass double inkwell rattle in its sockets. He was burning with helpless, furious rage, and he knew he was on the verge of doing something stupid – but he remembered Sillerton was his mother’s guest, and old New York never allowed hospitality to be broken by a fight. He suggested curtly that they go rejoin the ladies.

On the drive home in the dark carriage, May remained oddly, stubbornly silent, that heavy blush still clinging to her face like a threat he could not name. When they got home, she turned to walk down the hall to her bedroom instead of following him into the library, and he called her back, grumbling that the lamp was smoking. She came back, bright, pleasant, using the cool, firm tone her mother had taught her to humour fussy, difficult relatives. He stared at her young, beautiful face in the lamplight, and felt a cold little horror curl in his chest – he had decades and decades of this quiet, empty life stretching out in front of him.

“I have to go to Washington next week,” he said suddenly. “For a few days. There is a patent case coming up before the Supreme Court.” He rattled off the name of the inventor, all the tiny little plausible details, smoothly, as if he had learned the glib trick from Lawrence Lefferts himself. May nodded, listening attentively, and when he finished she said simply that the change would do him good. “And you must be sure to go and see Ellen while you are there,” she added, looking him straight in the eye with a perfectly cloudless, bright smile, as if she was reminding him not to neglect some tedious family duty.

Archer understood the message instantly, written in the silent unspoken code every well-bred New Yorker of their generation had been raised on. She knew everything. She knew the family was pressuring Ellen to go back to her husband, she knew he was the one who had encouraged her to stay, she knew he was not going to Washington for the patent case at all. She would not make a scene, would not yell, would not accuse him. She was giving him her full, explicit approval to go, and urging him to tell Ellen exactly what her choice to stay in New York, away from her husband, would cost her – the gossip, the poverty, the total social ruin. She turned the wick of the smoking lamp down, lifted off the glass globe, and blew out the flame gently. “They smell less if you blow them out,” she explained, with her bright, practical housekeeping air, pausing on the threshold to wait for his goodnight kiss.
