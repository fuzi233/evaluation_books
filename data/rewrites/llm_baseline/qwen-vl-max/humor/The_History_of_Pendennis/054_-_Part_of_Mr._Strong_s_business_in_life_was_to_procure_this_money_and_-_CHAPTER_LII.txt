CHAPTER LII. Which Had Very Nearly Been the Last of the Story—But Wasn’t, Thanks to a Few Cigars and a Good Irishman

Doctor Portman’s letter—full of moral thunder, pastoral concern, and enough religious advice to fill a small cathedral—was duly posted off to London, like a solemn missive from the heavens. The good doctor then set about calming Mrs. Pendennis, who was currently in a state of agitation that could only be described as “a woman who has just discovered her son might not be entirely beyond reproach.” He assured her (though he didn’t quite believe it himself) that the reply would be satisfactory—morally speaking, at least. And while Helen Pendennis longed to march into town and personally warn her son of his sins, the apothecary forbade it—on grounds of health, not virtue. So she remained confined to her sofa, with Laura by her side, silent as a saint, patient as a stone, and twice as noble.

Unfortunately for everyone involved, Arthur Pendennis never read this sermon of conscience until weeks later—by which time the damage had been done, the fever had begun, and the world had already decided that Pen was either a man of principle or a complete fool, depending on whether you’d seen him last night at Vauxhall or this morning in bed with a temperature.

Each day passed without word from Arthur, and each day made Helen sicker. Laura, meanwhile, bore the weight of it all with the quiet strength of someone who knows that duty is the only thing worth keeping when love turns cold. She prayed—not loudly, not dramatically—but in the stillness of her room, where God, perhaps, heard better than any human ear ever could. And though we can’t describe her virtues in detail—because such things are too sacred for public display, like a rose blooming in silence—we will say this: a good woman is the most beautiful flower in the garden of life. Let us admire her silently, as one admires moonlight on water. And let us mourn when she fades—not because she deserved it, but because the world is crueler than it should be.

Meanwhile, back in Lamb Court, Arthur Pendennis was doing his best to forget Fanny Bolton—though forgetting her was like trying to stop a river by holding your breath. He resolved not to see her again. No more Vauxhall. No more lingering glances. No more dreams of a girl who lived in a porter’s lodge and thought he was a prince. He tried distraction: work, walking, riding, smoking cigars like they were oxygen. But nothing worked. The image of Fanny—her eyes, her voice, her ridiculous little laugh—kept returning, like a ghost haunting a poorly ventilated room.

So he overdid it. He worked too hard. He walked too far. He ate too much, drank too much, smoked too much, and finally collapsed—literally—into a feverish heap on his own bed. And thus began the tragic comedy of bachelorhood in chambers.

Ah, the bachelor’s life! A condition so dire, so lonely, so utterly absurd, that it deserves its own series of satirical illustrations by Cruikshank or Leech—preferably with captions like *“The Bachelor’s Breakfast: A Study in Despair”* or *“The Shirt That Refused to Button: A Tragedy in One Act.”*

Imagine: the black kettle hissing in midsummer, the fire gone out at Christmas, the laundress long gone, leaving behind only the ghost of her presence and a pile of unpaid bills on the table. You enter, shivering, like a condemned man arriving at his execution, and must first light the fire, find the coal, clean the hearth, and then—only then—attempt to eat breakfast. All before you’ve even opened your newspaper.

Then there’s dinner. The shirt, alas, has no buttons. You try to wear it anyway, looking like a man who’s been attacked by a tailor and left for dead. Or worse: you return from a merry holiday at a country house full of pretty faces and warm embraces, only to find your portmanteau at the barber’s, your candle flickering like a dying ember, and your room filled with Christmas bills—each one a personal insult from the universe.

And if you’re ill? Oh, then it’s truly tragic. To lie in chambers, fevered and alone, with no one to bring you water, no hand to smooth your pillow, no kind voice to say, “You’ll be alright.” Instead, you serve yourself medicine by the glow of your own watch, and spend the night listening to your own thoughts—some of which may be about Fanny Bolton, others about how badly you need a servant.

This was Arthur’s fate. After a week of self-denial and excess, he fell ill. One night, he went to bed feeling unwell. The next morning, he woke up worse. His only visitors were the laundress and the Printer’s Devil from the *Pall Mall Gazette*, whom he managed to satisfy with half a column of copy—though not nearly enough to save the paper from looking like it had been written by a drunken monkey.

Mr. Finucane, bless him, came rushing up to check on the invalid. Seeing how bad Arthur looked, he took matters into his own hands. He wrote the entire political section of the Gazette—complete with passionate odes to Ireland, glowing tributes to Rafferty the painter (who, according to Finucane, was clearly being wronged by the Academy), and news that Mr. Rooney, M.P., was almost certainly going to be appointed Governor of Barataria. Shandon, the editor, reading the paper from Boulogne-sur-Mer, laughed and said to his wife, “Look here, Mary, Jack’s at it again.” And indeed, Jack was. He was also, for a few days, Arthur’s nurse.

Finucane offered to skip his weekend to stay with Arthur, but Arthur refused. “I’m fine,” he insisted. “I can manage myself.” Which was true—up to a point. He could manage himself, but only until he couldn’t.

On Friday night, after finishing the paper, Finucane told Captain Costigan about Arthur’s illness. Two days later, the Captain visited Lamb Court.

He found Mrs. Flanagan, the laundress, weeping in the sitting-room. Arthur was in a terrible state. She had taken brandy to calm herself—three times, possibly four—and was now trembling, red-eyed, and deeply ashamed. When she tried to care for Arthur, he snapped at her. “Don’t come near me!” he growled. This broke her heart. She wept harder. She reached for the bottle again. The Captain, seeing this, scolded her like a father scolding a child who’s been caught stealing cake. “You’re killing yourself!” he said. “And what good does it do anyone?”

Arthur, meanwhile, was in a fevered state, but delighted to see the Captain. He called out eagerly, “Captain! Come in! Thank you for coming!” He made the Captain sit down, though he himself was tossing about in bed like a fish on a hook. They talked about the Back Kitchen, about Vauxhall, about when they’d go again—then Arthur asked, “How is little Fanny?”

That question, simple as it was, sent a tremor through the whole story.

Fanny, poor dear, had returned home on Sunday evening, her heart broken. She had seen Arthur light his lamp—just as Bows had watched. Then Bows came back to his room, face as long as a fiddlestick. He didn’t speak. He just stared into space. That night, Fanny couldn’t sleep. She kept the book under her pillow—*Walter Lorraine*—and felt for it in the dark. Her children stirred early. She was furious with Bows. Yet, despite everything, she went to his room the next day.

“I saw Mr. Pendennis last night,” Bows said.

“Did you?” Fanny replied, glaring. “I thought you did.”

“I’ve liked you ever since we came here,” Bows continued. “You were a child when I arrived. You used to like me—until three or four days ago. Until you met this gentleman.”

“And now,” Fanny said, “you’re going to say bad things about him, aren’t you? Go ahead. It’ll make me like you better.”

“No,” Bows said. “I think he’s a good young man. Honest.”

“Honest?” Fanny shouted. “Then why won’t he come see me? Why won’t he talk to me? You know he loves me!”

“He does,” Bows said gently. “But he can’t marry you.”

Fanny’s defiance turned to despair. “Why not?” she whispered. “What have I done?”

Bows explained: Arthur was a gentleman. He had a future. He couldn’t marry a girl from a porter’s lodge. It wouldn’t be right. It wouldn’t be fair. It would ruin his prospects. And a decent girl wouldn’t want to ruin a man’s life for love.

Fanny wept. “I don’t care,” she sobbed. “I’d give up everything. I’d work for him. I’d learn. I’d become worthy. I’d die for him.”

She told her mother everything. She spoke of Pauline in *The Lady of Lyons*, of how she stayed faithful to Claude. “If everyone admires Pauline,” she said, “why can’t a gentleman love a poor girl? Not that Arthur loves me—I’m not worthy of him. Only a princess is worthy of such a man. But if I could serve him… just see him once a day… I’d be happy.”

Her head fell on her mother’s shoulder. Tears flowed. The mother cried too. “You must forget him,” she said. “If he doesn’t come, he’s a wicked man.”

“Don’t say that,” Fanny whispered. “He’s the best man alive. Bows says he’s unhappy. It wasn’t his fault we met. It wasn’t his fault I can’t see him. He says I must forget him—and I will. But I’ll never stop loving him. Never. I’ll pray for him. I’ll love him until I die. And when I die, my spirit will go to him.”

“You’ll break my heart,” the mother said. “But maybe he’ll come back. Maybe he’ll come today. If ever I saw a man in love, it’s him.”

At that moment, Mr. Bolton entered the lodge. The conversation stopped instantly. Mrs. Bolton smiled, patted her husband’s arm, and said, “Lor, Mr. B., who’d have thought you’d be away from the Club on a Saturday night?” Fanny wiped her tears, took up her knitting, and sat quietly in the corner—so still, so demure, that her father never suspected a thing.

Thus, fate played its cruel joke. Every person around Fanny—her mother, Bows, even the Captain—only deepened her passion. Pen wasn’t a villain. He was a gentleman. He was avoiding her because he loved her too much. And yet, that very act of restraint only made her love him more.

Was he wrong? Perhaps. Was he right? Probably. But in matters of the heart, right and wrong often look like twins separated at birth—one wearing a crown, the other a halo.

And so Fanny waited. She waited for a week. And on the seventh day, she heard from Captain Costigan that Arthur was ill—very ill.

That same evening, Arthur’s uncle, the excellent Major, arrived in town from Buxton. He had come to shoot partridges at Lord Steyne’s estate. He sent Morgan, his valet, to find Arthur and invite him to breakfast the next morning.

Morgan returned with a face like a storm cloud. “Mr. Arthur is very ill,” he reported. “In bed. Fever. Needs a doctor. I think it’s serious.”

The Major gasped. “Is he alone?”

“There’s someone nursing him,” Morgan said.

“Has he seen a doctor?”

“No, sir. He hasn’t.”

The Major sighed. “Well, I suppose I must go. I can’t leave Lord Steyne hanging. But I’ll send a doctor. And I’ll visit him tomorrow.”

And so the story continued—not with a bang, but with a cough, a fever, and a single, unspoken hope: that somewhere, in the midst of all this chaos, love might still find a way.
