As I wandered through the overgrown gardens of Oakhurst, the scent of damp earth and decaying leaves enveloped me like a shroud. The trees, once meticulously pruned, now stood wild and untamed, their branches tangled with vines. I couldn't help but think of the countless afternoons I'd spent beneath their shade, watching the world go by with a sense of detachment. The rhythmic creaking of the wooden gate echoed through the stillness, a morose accompaniment to my thoughts.

Mrs. Lambert's face floated before me, a map of lines etched by worry and concern. Her eyes, once bright with warmth, now seemed to hold a perpetual hint of sadness. I recalled the way she'd watch us depart, her handkerchief clutched tightly in her hand, a token of the love and longing she couldn't express. The trees, those sentinel limes, had borne witness to her quiet tears, their bark worn smooth by the touch of her fingers.

As I walked, the crunch of gravel beneath my feet served as a reminder of the passage of time. The trees, once a symbol of stability and tradition, now seemed to lean in, their leaves whispering secrets in the wind. I felt a pang of nostalgia for the carefree days when the world seemed full of possibility, and the trees were merely a backdrop for our games and laughter.

Colonel Lambert's parting words echoed in my mind: "The friend at whose house we shall sup tonight has a son, who is an old friend of our family." I remembered the way he'd smiled, his eyes twinkling with amusement, as he spoke of James Wolfe, the young lieutenant-colonel with a name that seemed to hold a hint of myth. The thought of him, of his bravery and good fortune, served only to deepen the ache within me. I felt a sense of disconnection, as if I'd left a part of myself behind, much like the trees that stood sentinel, guarding the memories of a bygone era.

The rustling of leaves seemed to grow louder, a cacophony of sound that accompanied me as I walked further into the woods.
