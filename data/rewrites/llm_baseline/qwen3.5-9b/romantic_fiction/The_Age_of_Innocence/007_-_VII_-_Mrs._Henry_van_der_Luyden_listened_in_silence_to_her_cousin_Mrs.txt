Mrs. Henry van der Luyden listened in silence to her cousin Mrs. Archer's narrative. One could easily convince oneself beforehand that Mrs. van der Luyden was naturally silent, kindly by disposition, and devoted to those she truly cared for. Yet, even knowing these facts did not shield one from the sudden chill that settled in the high-ceilinged, white-walled drawing-room of Madison Avenue. The pale brocaded armchairs stood uncovered, awaiting guests who had yet to arrive, while gauze curtains still draped the ormolu mantel ornaments and framed Gainsborough's *Lady Angelica du Lac*.

Directly opposite hung Mrs. van der Luyden's own portrait by Huntington—a masterpiece in black velvet and Venetian point, widely regarded as "as fine as a Cabanel." Painted twenty years prior, it remained a perfect likeness. In fact, the living Mrs. van der Luyden, sitting beneath it and listening to Mrs. Archer, seemed the twin sister of the fair, slightly youthful woman depicted drooping against a gilt chair before a green rep curtain. She still donned black velvet and Venetian point for society calls—or rather, since she never dined out, whenever she opened her own doors to receive others. Her fair hair, faded but untouched by gray, fell in flat, overlapping points across her forehead. The straight nose dividing her pale blue eyes was only marginally more pinched at the nostrils than in the painting. To Newland Archer, she appeared gruesomely preserved within the airless atmosphere of a perfectly irreproachable life, much like a body trapped in a glacier, retaining a rosy semblance of life-in-death for decades.

Like all his family, Archer esteemed and admired Mrs. van der Luyden. However, he found her gentle, bending sweetness less approachable than the fierce grimness of some of his mother's old aunts—spinsters who said "No" on principle before they even knew what was being asked. Mrs. van der Luyden offered neither a definitive yes nor no; instead, her demeanor inclined toward clemency until her thin lips wavered into the shadow of a smile, delivering the almost inevitable reply: *"I shall first have to talk this over with my husband."*

She and Mr. van der Luyden were so identical that Archer often wondered how, after forty years of closest conjugality, two such merged identities managed to separate themselves enough to deliberate on matters as controversial as this. Since neither had ever reached a decision without prefaceing it with this mysterious conclave, Mrs. Archer and her son, having laid out their case, waited resignedly for the familiar phrase.

Instead, Mrs. van der Luyden, who rarely surprised anyone, reached her long hand toward the bell-rope.
*"I think,"* she said, *"I should like Henry to hear what you have told me."*

A footman appeared. With grave precision, she instructed him: *"If Mr. van der Luyden has finished reading the newspaper, please ask him to be kind enough to come."* She spoke of "reading the newspaper" with the same tone a Minister's wife might use regarding a "Cabinet meeting"—not from arrogance, but because a lifetime of habit and the deferential attitudes of her circle had taught her to regard Mr. van der Luyden's slightest gesture as possessing almost sacerdotal importance. Her promptness indicated she viewed the matter as pressing as Mrs. Archer did, yet, lest she appear to have committed herself prematurely, she added with her sweetest look: *"Henry always enjoys seeing you, dear Adeline; and he will wish to congratulate Newland."*

The double doors swung solemnly open, revealing Mr. Henry van der Luyden. Tall, spare, and frock-coated, he possessed faded fair hair, a straight nose mirroring his wife's, and the same expression of frozen gentleness in eyes that were merely pale gray instead of pale blue. He greeted Mrs. Archer with cousinly affability, offered Newland low-voiced congratulations phrased identically to his wife's, and seated himself in one of the brocade armchairs with the simplicity of a reigning sovereign.

*"I had just finished reading the Times,"* he said, laying his long fingertips together. *"In town, my mornings are so occupied that I find it more convenient to read the newspapers after luncheon."*

*"Ah, there is much to be said for that plan—in fact, I believe my uncle Egmont used to say he found it less agitating not to read the morning papers until after dinner,"* Mrs. Archer responded warmly.

*"Yes: my good father abhorred hurry. But now we live in a constant rush,"* Mr. van der Luyden replied in measured tones, surveying the large, shrouded room with pleasant deliberation. To Archer, the space was a complete image of its owners.

*"But I hope you **had** finished your reading, Henry?"* his wife interjected.

*"Quite—quite,"* he reassured her.

*"Oh, it's really Newland's story,"* his mother smiled, proceeding to rehearse once more the monstrous tale of the affront inflicted upon Mrs. Lovell Mingott. *"Of course,"* she concluded, *"Augusta Welland and Mary Mingott both felt that, especially given Newland's engagement, you and Henry **ought to know**."*

*"Ah—"*, Mr. van der Luyden drew a deep breath.

A silence ensued, during which the ticking of the monumental ormolu clock on the white marble mantelpiece grew as loud as the boom of a minute-gun. Archer gazed with awe at the two slender, faded figures seated side by side in a kind of viceregal rigidity. They appeared as mouthpieces of some remote ancestral authority, compelled by fate to wield power they would far rather have spent digging invisible weeds from the perfect lawns of Skuytercliff or playing Patience in the evenings.

Mr. van der Luyden broke the silence first. *"You really think this is due to some... some intentional interference of Lawrence Lefferts's?"* he enquired, turning to Archer.

*"I'm certain of it, sir,"* Archer replied. *"Larry has been pushing it harder than usual lately—if cousin Louisa won't mind my mentioning it—he's having rather a stiff affair with the postmaster's wife in their village, or someone of that sort. And whenever poor Gertrude Lefferts begins to suspect anything, and he fears trouble, he stages a fuss of this kind to demonstrate how awfully moral he is, shouting at the top of his voice about the impertinence of inviting his wife to meet people he doesn't wish her to know. He's simply using Madame Olenska as a lightning-rod; I've seen him try the same trick many times before."*

*"The **Leffertses**!..."* gasped Mrs. van der Luyden.

*"The **Leffertses**!..."* echoed Mrs. Archer.

*"What would uncle Egmont have said of Lawrence Lefferts pronouncing judgment on anyone's social position? It shows what Society has come to."*

*"We'll hope it hasn't quite come to that,"* Mr. van der Luyden stated firmly.

*"Ah, if only you and Louisa went out more!"* sighed Mrs. Archer. Instantly, she realized her mistake. The van der Luydens were morbidly sensitive to any criticism of their secluded existence. As the arbiters of fashion and the Court of Last Appeal, they knew their status and bowed to their fate. Yet, being shy and retiring souls with no natural inclination for public life, they lived as much as possible in the sylvan solitude of Skuytercliff. When they came to town, they declined all invitations on the plea of Mrs. van der Luyden's health.

Newland Archer stepped to his mother's rescue. *"Everybody in New York knows what you and cousin Louisa represent. That is precisely why Mrs. Mingott felt she ought not to allow this slight on Countess Olenska to pass without consulting you."*

Mrs. van der Luyden glanced at her husband, who returned the glance.

*"It is the principle that I dislike,"* said Mr. van der Luyden. *"As long as a member of a well-known family is backed up by that family, it should be considered final."*

*"It seems so to me,"* his wife agreed, as if producing a brand-new thought.

*"I had no idea,"* Mr. van der Luyden continued, *"that things had come to such a pass."* He paused, looking at his wife again. *"It occurs to me, my dear, that the Countess Olenska is already a sort of relation—through Medora Manson's first husband. At any rate, she will be when Newland marries."*

He turned toward the young man. *"Have you read this morning's Times, Newland?"*

*"Why, yes, sir,"* Archer answered, who usually devoured half a dozen papers with his morning coffee.

Husband and wife locked eyes again. Their pale gazes clung together in prolonged, serious consultation, followed by a faint smile fluttering over Mrs. van der Luyden's face. She had evidently guessed and approved.
