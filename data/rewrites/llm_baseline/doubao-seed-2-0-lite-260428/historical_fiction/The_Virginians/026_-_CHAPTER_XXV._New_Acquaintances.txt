Cousin Maria made her entrance attended by a pair of grinning gardener’s boys heaping wicker baskets full of early summer blooms, intended to deck Madame de Bernstein’s drawing room in readiness for her ladyship’s evening assembly. Three footmen in livery laced thick with worsted bustled about the space, unfurling six card tables between them. A stately major-domo in black broadcloth and powdered bag wig, his ruffled cuffs edged with fine lace and bearing the proud bearing of a man who ought by rights to wear a sword at his hip, followed after them carrying armfuls of wax tapers, setting two apiece on every card table and tucking pairs into the silver sconces nailed to the wainscot. The slanting golden sun of late afternoon gilded the paneling, and spilled out over the green common beyond the windows: over weathered rocks, clumps of gnarled oak, and distant cottage roofs twinkling in the light. Groups of strollers in hoops, powder, and glinting brocade wandered across the grass, their colored silhouettes dappling the turf. On the other side of the Baroness’s windows stretched the Pantiles, where a perpetual fair hummed with chatter and clattering hooves, a band of musicians playing for the benefit of the Wells’ visitors below.

Madame de Bernstein’s chief sitting room would never have suited a recluse or a scholar, but for anyone fond of bustle, gaiety, and bright cross-light, and a full view of all the cheerful, busy life unfolding outside, no lodging in the whole of Tunbridge Wells could be more pleasant. When the lamps were lit after dark, every passerby on the pavement below knew her ladyship was at home holding a card assembly, and an introduction to the evening’s entertainment was easy enough to come by. It strikes me, thinking on it now, that the night-life of high society a hundred years ago was a comparatively dim affair. There was not one wax candle then for ten that light a modern lady’s drawing room today, to say nothing of gas and the wondrous new club illuminations we now enjoy. Horrible guttering tallow candles smoked and stank in every back passage, and the official candle-snuffer was a notorious, well-loved character in every playhouse. Look at Hogarth’s prints if you doubt it: how dark they are, how his great feasts seem begrimed with tallow smoke! In *Marriage A La Mode*, in Lord Viscount Squanderfield’s grand saloon, where the earl and his wife yawn over the remains of their party while their steward cowers in horror, there are but eight candles total: one on each card table, and half a dozen stuck in a chipped brass chandelier. Jack Briefless, inviting his friends to oysters and beer in his Pump Court chambers, would likely have twice that number. We may comfort ourselves with the thought that even Louis Quatorze in all his glory held his most lavish revels in comparative dimness, and bless Mr. Price and all other luciferous benefactors of mankind for banishing the rank, stinking “mutton tallow” of our youth.

So Maria, fairest bloom of all the flowers she carried, tucked roses and sweet-williams and honeysuckle into every vase and dragon jar, adorning the room as best her artistic taste could manage. She lingered over every arrangement, casting only sly, timid glances at her young cousin Harry, whose own deep blush would have done credit to any modest young woman. You might have thought she meant to linger behind after the others left, for a quiet few moments alone with him—until the Baroness, settled in her great armchair with her crooked tortoiseshell cane in her hand, began ordering the servants about imperiously, rating them one by one soundly: Tom for a darn in his stocking, John for slathering too much hair grease out of the tallow candle box, and so on, keeping a stern, unyielding rule over her whole household. It is worth another note on the poor footmen of a hundred years ago: two shared a bed, four a room, and that room was often a drafty cellar, where they washed in a stone trough such as you would barely find anywhere in modern London outside of Her Majesty’s Foot Guards barracks.

If Maria had hoped for a private interview, her fond heart was disappointed. “Where do you intend to dine, Harry?” Madame de Bernstein asked. “My niece Maria and I will make do with a single chicken in the little back parlor, and I think you ought to make for the best ordinary in town. There is a very good one at the White Horse, whose dinner bell will ring any minute now. You must understand, sir, you ought not to spare expense, but comport yourself like the son of Princess Pocahontas, for all the world to see. Your trunks have already been carried over to the lodgings I’ve taken for you. It’s not good for a strapping young lad to be always hanging around the aprons of two old women, is it, Maria?”

“No,” said Maria, dropping her meek eyes to the floor, while the Baroness’s own glinted in triumph. I do believe poor Andromeda had been left exposed to her cruel dragon for the better part of the past five or six days; if Perseus had cut the creature’s head clean off, it would have been no unjustifiable monstricide. But the young man drew no sword or shield, only stared absently at the footmen in their tawny-and-blue livery creaking across the room.

“And the London mercers and tailors who follow the crowds to the Wells will be happy to wait on you,” the Baroness went on. “You ought to call on them, my dear, for your suit is not quite of the latest fashion—you want a little more lace, a little finer cut—”

“I cannot go out of mourning for my brother, ma’am,” said the young man, looking down at the sober black of his coat.

“Ho, sir!” cried the lady, rustling up from her chair and heaving herself to her feet on her cane. “Wear black for your brother till you are as old as Methuselah, for all I care. I have no wish to stop you. I only want you to dress properly, do as other gentlemen do, and make a figure worthy of the Esmond name.”

“Madam,” said Mr. Warrington, drawing himself up with great dignity, “I have not done a single thing to disgrace that name, so far as I know.”

Why did the old woman stop and give a little sharp start, as if she had been struck? Let bygones be bygones. She and the boy had a dozen little fencing matches of the sort already, crossing swords and parrying thrusts as fast as they could speak, and she liked Harry none the worse for the courage to stand up to her. “Surely a little finer linen than that shirt you have on will not disgrace you, sir,” she said, with a rather forced laugh.

Harry bowed and blushed. That plain linen shirt had been one of the homely, loving gifts from his friends at Oakhurst. He felt a secret pleasure in wearing it, thinking of those good, pure, simple, kind people, and it seemed to him that as long as he wore that garment, no evil could touch him. He said he would return to his lodging at once, and make sure he came back arrayed in the very best linen his trunks held.

“Come back here directly, sir,” said Madame Bernstein, “and if our guests have not arrived, Maria and I will find you some extra ruffles to wear!” And with that, a footman showed the young fellow out, and guided him to his new apartments.

Harry found not only handsome, spacious rooms waiting for him, but a groom standing by ready to enter his service, and a second valet, should he choose to hire one to tend to Mr. Gumbo. Barely minutes after he had settled in his rooms, emissaries from the London tailor and bootmaker were knocking at his door, bearing the compliments and calling cards of Messrs. Regnier and Tull. Gumbo laid out the best pieces in his modest wardrobe, the finest linen his thrifty Virginian mother had packed for him before he set sail. Visions of his snowbound home across the Atlantic rose up before him: crackling log fires, the trim quiet ladies of Castlewood stitching by the hearth. For the first time, a small, strange thought crossed his mind: that his homely country clothes were not quite smart enough, that the home-wrought linen was not as fine as that worn by the young men of quality he saw about him now. That he should ever be ashamed of anything belonging to Castlewood! It felt absurd. The simple folk back home were only too well satisfied with every object made, said, or produced on their estate, and Madam Esmond, when she sent her son off on his travels, had thought no young nobleman in England could be better provided for. The clothes could have fitted better, to be sure, and been of a later cut—but the young man cut a very comely, handsome figure when he finished his toilet and stepped back out onto the street, Gumbo calling for a sedan chair and marching alongside it until they reached the White Horse ordinary.

He had expected to find the dashing acquaintance he had made at his aunt’s lodgings a few hours prior, who had told him the White Horse was the most modest dining spot at the Wells, and he mentioned his friend’s name to the landlord. But the host and his waiters led him into the dining room with endless smiles and bows, assuring his honour that he needed no other introduction than his own good name, helped him hang his coat and sword on a wall peg, asked him whether he would drink Burgundy, Pontac, or champagne with his dinner, and ushered him to a table.

Though the most fashionable eating house in the village, the White Horse was not crowded that day. Monsieur Barbeau, the landlord, told Harry there was a grand fete taking place at Summer Hill that had drawn away most of the town’s guests. Indeed, when Harry stepped into the room there were only four other gentlemen present. Two of them were finishing their wine after their meal, and the other two were younger men halfway through their dinner. The landlord must have whispered Harry’s name as he passed the latter’s table, for they looked over at him with obvious interest, and gave him a slight bow across the table as the smiling host bustled away to bring his dinner. Mr. Warrington returned their salute, and the two young men bade him welcome to Tunbridge, and hoped he would like the place better the more he got to know it. Then they smiled and exchanged waggish, knowing glances, the meaning of which Harry did not understand, nor why they cast the same knowing look over at the other two older guests still drinking their wine.

One of those two was dressed in a tarnished velvet coat, with a huge powdered queue tied in a bag, and voluminous, heavily embroidered ruffles at his wrists. The other was a small, beetle-browed, hook-nosed, high-shouldered man, whom his companion addressed out loud as “milord” in a very loud voice. My lord barely glanced at the new arrival as he sipped his wine, then turned back to his friend.

“And so you know the nephew of that old woman—Croesus the newly arrived heir, they say?”

“You missed your mark there, Jack!” one of the two young diners called across the room.

“Never could get the hang of the lingo,” Jack replied. The two older men had switched to speaking French.

“But assuredly, my dear lord!” said the gentleman with the long queue.

“You have shown wonderful energy, my dear Baron! He has been here but two hours. My own servants only told me of him just as I arrived for dinner.”

“I knew him long before! I met him often in London, with the Baroness and his cousin, the earl,” said the Baron.

A plate of smoking hot soup was set down before Harry just then, borne by the grinning host. “Behold, sir! Behold my own fashion of potage!” Monsieur Barbeau said, leaning down to whisper the celebrated nobleman’s name across the room into Harry’s ear. Harry thanked the man in fluent French, upon which the foreign gentleman turned around, grinned most graciously at him, and said, “Fous bossedez notre langue barfaidement, monsieur.”

Mr. Warrington had never heard French pronounced in that strange, mangled way back in Canada. He bowed politely in return.

“Tell me more of this Croesus, my good Baron,” his lordship went on, speaking rather superciliously to his companion, and taking no notice of Harry at all, which nettled the young Virginian not a little. “What can you tell me? Croesus is a youth like any other youth, tall, a little awkward, with black hair, as all men from the Indies have. They have taken lodgings for him at Mrs. Rose’s toy-shop.”

“I have lodgings there too!” Harry thought to himself. “Who on earth is this Croesus they are talking of? But this soup is excellent.”

“He travels with a huge retinue,” the Baron continued. “Four servants, two post-chaises, a pair of outriders. His chief attendant is a black man who saved his life from savage raiders in America, and who will not hear of being freed on any account. He insists on wearing mourning for his elder brother, from whom he inherits his great principality.”

“Could anything console you for the death of your own brother, Chevalier?” one of the young diners called over.

“Milor! His property could,” said the Chevalier. “Which, as you know, is no small sum.”

“Your brother lives on his great patrimony, which you tell me is immense, while you live by your own industry, my dear Chevalier.”

“Milor!” cried the man addressed as Chevalier.

“By your industry, or your wit—how much more noble! Will you be at the Baroness’s card assembly tonight? She ought to be some relation of yours, is she not, Chevalier?”

“Again I do not follow your lordship,” the other man said, sulkily. “Why, she is a woman of great wit, noble birth, who has undergone the strangest adventures, and has precious little principle (there, you happily have the advantage of her). But what do men of the world care for that? I suppose you intend to go and play cards with the young Creole, and get as much money out of him as you possibly can. By the way, Baron—what if this young Creole is a trap? What if our excellent friend the Baroness invented him back in London, and brought him down to prey on all the innocent, unsuspecting folks here at the Wells?”

“J’y ai souvent pense, milor,” said the little Baron, tapping his nose very knowingly. “That Baroness is capable of anything.”

“A Baron—A Baroness, que voulez-vous, my friend? I mean her late lamented husband. Do you know who he was?”

“Intimately. A more notorious villain never dealt a hand of cards. At Venice, at Brussels, at Spa, at Vienna—he knew the insides of every gaol in every one of those cities. I knew the man well, my lord.”

“I thought you would. I saw him at The Hague, where I first had the honour of meeting you, and a more disreputable rogue never crossed my threshold. A minister must open his doors to all sorts of people, Baron—spies, sharpers, ruffians of every description.”

“Parbleu, milor, you treat them charmingly!” his companion laughed.

“A man of my rank, the rank I held then, of course, must meet all sorts of people, including your acquaintance. What that woman ever saw fit to take the name of such a man for, I cannot imagine.”

“Apparently, it was better than her own name.”

“Effectively! Just as I have heard of my friend Paddy exchanging coats with a scarecrow. I do not know which name is more distinguished, that of the English bishop, or that of the German baron.”

“My lord!” cried the other gentleman, rising and laying his hand on the large star pinned to his coat breast. “You forget that I too am a Baron, and a Chevalier of the Holy Roman—”

“—Order of the Spur! Not in the least, my dear knight and baron! You will take no more wine? We shall meet at Madame de Bernstein’s tonight.”

The knight and baron quit the table, fumbling in his embroidered pockets as if for money to tip the waiter, who held out his great laced hat for him. He waved the menial off with a hand ringed with blazing gemstones, sticking out of a mass of fine lace ruffles, and stalked grandly out of the room. It was only after the man they had called “my lord” began to speak of the late bishop’s widow and the German baron’s wife that Harry Warrington realized he and his aunt had been the entire subject of their conversation. Before the full realization had settled in his mind, one of the speakers had quit the room, and the other turned to the table where the two young diners sat. “What a little sharper that man is! Everything I said about the Bernsteins, mutato nomine, applies fully to him. I knew the fellow to be a spy and a rogue. He has changed his religion half a dozen times, for all I know. I had him kicked out of The Hague myself when I was ambassador, and I know he was publicly caned in Vienna.”

“I wonder my Lord Chesterfield associates with such a villain!” Harry called out sharply from his table. The other two diners looked over at him, and to Harry’s surprise the nobleman he had addressed continued talking without pause. “There cannot be a more confirmed rogue than this Poellnitz. Thank Heaven, he actually left my snuff-box behind! You laugh? The man is quite capable of stealing it.” And my lord thought it was his own witty satire the two young men were laughing at.

“You are quite right, sir,” said one of the two young diners, turning to Mr. Warrington, “though, saving your presence, it is hardly your affair. My lord will play cards with anybody who will sit down to the table with him. Do not be alarmed, though—he is as deaf as a post, and did not hear a single word you said. That is exactly why he will play with anyone who puts a pack of cards before him, and that is the reason he consorts with such a rogue.”

“Faith, I know other noblemen who are not particular about their company,” said Mr. Jack.
