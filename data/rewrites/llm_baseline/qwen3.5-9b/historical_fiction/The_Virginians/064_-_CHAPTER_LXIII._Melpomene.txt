George Warrington managed his legal studies with a distinct lack of solemnity, ensuring they never impeded his comfort, his pleasures, nor his precious health. Through letters from Madam Esmond, he had been instructed that despite wearing the student's gown and dining among nameless commoners at the hall, he bore an ancient name worthy of support and could rank alongside the foremost figures of his nation. She urged him to study as a gentleman, not as a mere professional drudge. The young man obeyed this injunction with sufficient obedience; consequently, he may be described not as belonging to the rank-and-file of the law, but as a volunteer in its service, akin to certain young gentlemen recently mentioned. Although the law a century prior was far less exacting than it is today—allowing her disciples ample leisure, pleasure, punch, coffee-house visits, and holidays, whereas modern jurisprudence scarcely permits time for amusement, recreation, instruction, sleep, or dinner—it remained a jealous mistress demanding exclusive attention. As history notes, Murray might have chosen to be an Ovid adorned with bays rather than a Lord Chief Justice draped in ermine; perhaps Mr. Warrington might have ascended to a peerage and the woolsack had he studied longer, worked harder, mastered courtiership, and gained the favor of attorneys. Had he been other than he was, in short. Yet, he treated Themis with decent respect, loving letters far more than law. The black-letter of Chaucer held infinite charm for him, vastly superior to the Gothic pages of Hale and Coke.

Indeed, letters were cherished in those quaint times, and authors served as living authorities. Gentlemen appealed to Virgil or Lucan in the Courts or the House of Commons. "What does Statius say?" "What does Juvenal declare?"—let alone Cicero or Tacitus—regarding any given point? Their reign has ended, the good old Heathens; the worship of Jupiter and Juno is no more fashionable than the cultivation of pagan poetry or ethics. The age of economists and calculators has superseded them; Tooke's *Pantheon* is now deserted and ridiculed. Occasionally, perhaps, a Stanley sacrifices a kid, a Gladstone wreaths a laurel, or a Lytton burns incense in honor of the Olympians. But what concern do they hold for Lambeth, Birmingham, or the Tower Hamlets regarding ancient rites, divinities, or worship? Who exactly are the Muses, and what utility lies in all that Greek and Latin rubbish? What is Mount Helicon, and who cares? Who was Thalia, pray, and what is the length of her 'i'? Does anyone know whether Melpomene's name comprises three or four syllables? And can you tell from whose design I stole the figure of Tragedy that adorns the initial 'G' of this chapter?

It has been noted how Mr. George, in his youth and during the long leisure afforded him at home and later in his imprisonment within the French fort on the banks of the Monongahela, spent his idle hours courting Melpomene. The result of this union was a tragedy, omitted from Bell's Theatre, though I suspect it is no inferior to many pieces printed therein. Most young men pay homage to the Tragic Muse first, much as they fall in love with women significantly older than themselves. Let the candid reader admit, if he possesses a literary inclination, that his ambition was once of the highest order. However, in his riper years, he may have lowered his pretensions, deeming the translation of an ode by Horace or the versification of a song by Waller or Prior into decent Alcaics or Sapphics the apex of his capability. Yet, tragedy and epic engaged his green, unknowing youth, and no prize but the highest seemed fit for him.

Upon arriving in London, George Warrington attended theatrical performances at both houses, frequented the theatrical coffee-houses, and listened to the opinions of the critics. He might be seen at the Bedford between plays or supping at the Cecil alongside wits and actors post-performance. Here, he gradually befriended the players and those writers and poets known to the public. One might occasionally spot the tough old Macklin, the frolicsome Foote, the vivacious Hippisley, or the sprightly Mr. Garrick himself in these houses of entertainment. Our gentleman, through his wit, modesty, and the high reputation for wealth he possessed, became exceedingly popular in the coffee-house circles. Actors drank bowls of punch with him; critics dined at his expense with great affability. To maintain intimacy with an author or actor has long been a delight for many young men; to hob-nob with Bobadil or Henry the Fifth, to accept a pinch from Aristarchus's own box, to escort Juliet into her coach, or to help Monimia into her chair are privileges that would enchant most youths of a poetic bent. No wonder George Warrington loved the theatre. Furthermore, he derived satisfaction from knowing his mother only half-approved of plays and playhouses, allowing him to feast on fruit strictly forbidden at home. He hosted several elegant entertainments for the players, and it was rumored that one or two distinguished geniuses had even condescended to borrow money from him.

As he polished and embellished his masterpiece with new beauties, he naturally sought the counsel of certain friends, who offered applause and advice. Mr. Spencer, his new acquaintance from the Temple, hosted a breakfast at his chambers in Fig Tree Court, where Mr. Warrington read portions of his play. The gentlemen present declared it possessed uncommon merit. Even the learned Mr. Johnson, invited to the gathering, graciously remarked that the piece demonstrated talent. It did, to be sure, wage war against the unities; yet these had been violated by other authors, and Mr. Warrington might sacrifice them as readily as any. There was something in Mr. W.'s tragedy that reminded Mr. Johnson of both *Coriolanus* and *Othello*. "And two very good things too, sir!" the author pleaded. "Well, well, there was no doubt on that point," replied Mr. Spencer. "'Tis certain your catastrophe is terrible, just, and being in part true, is not the less awful."

The plot of Mr. Warrington's tragedy was replete with battle and murder. A favorite book of his grandfather had been the life of Old George Frundsberg of Mindelheim, a colonel of foot-soldiers in the Imperial service at the Battle of Pavia and during the wars of the Constable Bourbon. Among Frundsberg's military companions was a certain Carpzow, or Carpezan, whom our friend selected as the hero of his tragedy. The first act, as it currently stands in Sir George Warrington's manuscript, is set before a convent on the Rhine, which the Lutherans, under Carpezan, are besieging. These are depicted as a godless gang; they have pulled the beards of Roman friars and torn the veils of hundreds of religious women. A dozen tremble within the convent walls, the garrison of which has promised to surrender unless expected reinforcements arrive before midday. Meanwhile, an armistice holds, and the sentries inside watch with hungry eyes as soldiers and camp followers gamble on the grass before the gate.

Twelve o'clock strikes: *ding, ding, dong!* upon the convent bell. No succours have arrived. "Open gates, warder! Admit the famous Protestant hero, the terror of Turks on the Danube and Papists in the Lombard plains—Colonel Carpezan!" See him approach, clad in complete steel, his battle-hammer resting over his shoulder, having battered countless infidel sconces, his flags displayed, his trumpets blaring. "No rudeness, my men," commands Carpezan. "The wine is yours, and the convent larder and cellar are excellent: the church plate shall be melted. Any of the garrison wishing to take service with Gaspar Carpezan are welcome and shall receive good pay. No insult to the religious ladies! I have promised them a safe-conduct, and he who lays a finger on them shall hang! Mind that Provost Marshal!" The Provost Marshal, a huge fellow in a red doublet, nods his head.

"We shall see more of that Provost Marshal, or executioner," Mr. Spencer explains to his guests.

"A very agreeable acquaintance, I am sure—I shall be delighted to meet the gentleman again!" says Mr. Johnson, wagging his head over his tea. "This scene of mercenaries, camp followers, and their wild sports is novel and stirring, Mr. Warrington. I offer you my compliments. The Colonel has entered the convent, I believe? Now let us hear what he intends to do there."

The Abbess and one or two of her oldest ladies appear before the conqueror. Despite his status, they grant him an audience in their sacred halls. They have heard tales of his violent behavior in conventual establishments before. That hammer, which he always carries into action, has shattered many sacred images in religious houses. Pounds of convent plate are known to have been melted by this sacrilegious plunderer! No wonder the Abbess-Princess of St. Mary's, a lady of violent prejudices, free speech, and noble birth, harbors a dislike for the low-born heretic who now lords it in her convent, and tells Carpezan a bit of her mind.
