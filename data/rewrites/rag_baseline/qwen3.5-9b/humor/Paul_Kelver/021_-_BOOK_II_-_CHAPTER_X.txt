To reclaim the integrity of my soul, I ascended the ladder of discipline with a slow, unyielding steadiness, casting aside every phantom of idle dreaming to focus exclusively on the grim necessity of commerce. Within me dwelt another entity—a diminutive Paul with eyes clouded by sorrow—who constantly sought to seduce me from this arduous labor. Yet, my resolve was a fortress of iron; driven by a vehement vow never to cease until I had bought back my lost honor, and fueled by a craving that grew daily into a physical hunger—to once again feel the crushing, comforting pressure of Norah's strong white hand clasping mine—he could scarcely gain ground. One wonders what fate awaited me had he prevailed: perhaps a politician, a minor poet, or a capable editor shackled by rigid convictions, a figure of limited utility to himself and the world. Occasionally, granted a week's respite as my humor took a holiday and left me with nothing but patience to await its return, I would pen tales solely for my own amusement. These yielded no fame; indeed, triumph born of purposeful toil grows far more slowly. Had I persisted... but there were debts to settle. And by the time my obligations were cleared, a reputation would inevitably follow.

"Madness!" argued my pragmatic acquaintances. "You would be discarding a certain fortune for, at best, a dubious competence. The craft you know you possess, versus the other—it would be starting your career anew."

"You would find it nearly impossible now," explained those who spoke, I knew, with the weight of hard-won wisdom. "The world would never lend an ear. Once a humorist, forever a humorist. Just as ill-advised as a comic actor insisting upon portraying Hamlet. Even if it were the finest Hamlet ever witnessed upon the stage, the audience would only laugh—or flee in silence."

Drawn together by a mutual thirst for sympathy, "Goggles" and I would retreat to some quiet alcove within the Club, pouring our souls into one another's cups. He would lay before me, at length, his vision of Romeo—an excellent conception, I have no doubt, though I confess it failed to ignite any spark within me. Somehow, I could not conjure the image of myself as Romeo. Still, I listened with every sign of encouragement. It was the price I paid him for, in turn, allowing him to unfold to me my grandiose theories on how monumental literature, beneficial to mankind, should be imagined and constructed.

"Perhaps in a future existence," Goggles laughed one evening, rising as the clock struck seven, "I shall be a great tragedian, and you a famous poet. Meanwhile, I suppose, as your friend Brian puts it, we are both sinning our mercies. After all, to live is the most important thing in life."

I had accompanied him as far as the cloak-room, assisting him into his coat.

"Take my advice"—tapping me firmly on the chest, he fixed his peculiar, fish-like eyes upon me. Had I not known his intent to be sincere, I should have laughed; his expression was so comically earnest. "Marry some dear little woman" (he was wed himself to a placid lady of approximately twice his own weight); "one never truly understands life until the babies arrive to explain it to us."

I returned to my easy-chair before the fire. Wife, children, home! Was not that the true vocation of man—the living man, not the dreamer? I saw them surrounding me, imbuing my existence with dignity and responsibility. The fair, sweet woman, helper, comrade, comforter; the little faces molded in our image, their questioning voices offering the keys to life's riddles. All other hopes, ambitions, dreams—what were they? Mere phantoms of the morning mist, dissolving under the glare of the sun.

Hodgson visited me one evening. "I require you to write me a comic opera," he declared, holding an open letter in his hand which he was perusing. "The public appears weary of these eternal translations from the French. I desire something English, something new and original."

"The English is straightforward enough," I replied; "but I should hesitate to clamor for anything new and original if I were you."

"Why not?" he asked, lifting his gaze from the paper.

"You might secure it," I answered. "And then you would be disappointed."

He chuckled. "Well, you know what I mean—something we could label 'new and original' on the program. What do you say? It will be a significant opportunity for you, and I am willing to risk it. I am convinced you can do it. People are beginning to speak of you."

I had penned a few farces and *comediettas*, and they had met with success. Yet, the primary burden of the evening was a grave responsibility. A young man may be excused for hesitating. Such a venture can forge a man, but it can also break him. Above all other artistic forms—if I may be pardoned for invoking the sacred term in connection with such a trivial subject—a comic opera demands experience.

I laid bare my fears. I omitted to mention that in my desk rested a four-act drama throbbing with humanity, with life itself, which had been my hope—fading day by day—to seize the theatrical public by storm and establish myself as a serious dramatist.

"It is exceedingly simple," Hodgson urged. "Provide Atherton ample comic business; you ought to manage that without difficulty. Give Gleeson something pretty in waltz time, and assign Duncan a role requiring her to change her frock every quarter-hour, and the task is accomplished."

"I'll tell you what," Hodgson continued, "I will take the entire troupe down to Richmond on Sunday. We shall hire a coach and depart the theater at half-past ten. It will afford you an opportunity to observe them. You can converse with them and discern precisely what they are capable of. Atherton harbors ideas; he will elucidate them for you. Then, next week, we shall draft a contract and commence work."

It was an opportunity too splendid to relinquish, even though I knew that if successful, I would find myself pinned down more firmly than ever to my role of jester. Yet, the composition of comic opera is lucrative.

A small throng had gathered in the Strand to witness our departure.

"Nothing wrong, is there?" inquired the leading lady, her tone laced with anxiety as she alighted a quarter-hour late from her cab. "Is it not a fire, is it?"

"Merely assembled to behold you," Mr. Hodgson explained, without raising his eyes from his correspondence.

"Oh, good gracious!" cried the leading lady. "Do let us escape swiftly."

"Box seat, my dear," returned Mr. Hodgson.

Accepting the proffered assistance of myself and three other gentlemen, the leading lady mounted the ladder with charming hesitation. Some delay occurred during her dismounting due to our low comedian, who twice feigned missing his footing, sliding back down into the arms of the stolid door-keeper. The crowd, composed largely of small boys delighted by the endeavor to amuse them, erupted in laughter and applause. Encouraged by this reception, our low comedian made a third attempt upon his hands and knees, and, gaining the roof, sat down upon the tenor, who smiled somewhat mechanically.

As we passed the first dozen or so buses, our low comedian greeted them by rising to his feet and bowing profoundly, thereafter falling back upon either the tenor or myself. Except for the tenor and myself, his performance appeared thoroughly appreciated. Upon reaching Charing Cross, however, nobody seemed interested in our progress; to the relief of the tenor and myself, he settled down.

"People occasionally ask me," said the low comedian, brushing dust from his knees, "why I engage in such antics off the stage. It amuses me."

"I was traveling up to London recently from Birmingham," he continued. "At Willesden, when the ticket collector opened the door, I sprang out of the carriage and ran off down the platform. Naturally, he chased after me, shouting at everyone to stop me. I eluded them for about a minute. You cannot imagine the excitement generated. Approximately fifty people abandoned their seats to discover the commotion. I explained to them when they caught me that I had been traveling second class with a first-class ticket, which was the truth. People assume I do this to attract attention. I do it for my own pleasure."

"It must be a troublesome method of amusing oneself," I suggested.

"Exactly what my wife says," he replied; "she can never comprehend the impulse that overtakes us all, I suppose, at times, to play the fool. As a rule, when she is with me, I refrain."

"She is not present today?" I asked, glancing around.

"She suffers terribly from headaches," he answered, "she hardly ventures anywhere."

"I am sorry." My words were not spoken out of mere politeness; I genuinely felt regret.

Throughout the drive to Richmond, this irrepressible urge to entertain himself overwhelmed him repeatedly. Through Kensington, he drew considerable attention by balancing a horn upon his nose. At Kew, he halted the coach to request permission from a young ladies' boarding school to enter for sixpence. At the base of Richmond Hill, he provoked a gathering while attempting to persuade a deaf elderly gentleman in a Bath-chair to permit his attendant to race us up the hill for a shilling.
