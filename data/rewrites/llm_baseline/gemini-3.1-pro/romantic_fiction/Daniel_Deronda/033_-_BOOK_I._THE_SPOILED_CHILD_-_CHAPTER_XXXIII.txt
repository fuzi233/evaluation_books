Deronda found his most profound escapes not in the manicured, horse-drawn clatter of Rotten Row, but amidst the narrow, pungent arteries of London’s eastern quarters. Here, the mythic occurrences of an ancient diaspora seamlessly integrated into everyday domestic routines. He walked through acoustic landscapes where the harsh, mechanized screech of the industrial city gave way to the rhythmic thud of cleavers against wooden butcher blocks and the low, guttural cadence of ancestral tongues. He inhaled the olfactory tapestry of the streets: the rich, schmaltz-heavy aroma of roasting fowl, the sharp bite of pickling brine fermenting in wooden barrels, and the sweet, yeasty breath of baking challah rising from basement hearths. To Deronda, these were not mere sustenance; they were profound, edible archives of a people’s resilience, recipes passed down through generations like sacred texts. He searched for a mother named Cohen and a brother named Ezra, but he also sought the culinary and linguistic markers of Mirah’s past, terrified that the warmth of her memory might be lost to the sterile, unaccommodating reality of the modern world.

The clatter of Holborn was an acoustic assault, a barrage of industrial noise pollution that eroded the spirit. Seeking refuge, Deronda paused, only to have his attention caught by chased silver missal-clasps in a shop window. It was a pawnbroker’s—a space where sacred, domestic artifacts and heirlooms were stripped of their history and reduced to the superficial commercialization of art. *Watches and Jewelry exchanged.* An unmistakably Jewish man of about thirty appeared at the door, his voice ringing with a transactional, hollow cheer that grated against Deronda’s ears like a poorly tuned instrument. It was Ezra Cohen. Deronda winced at the discordant sound and immediately hailed a hansom cab, fleeing the likelihood that this purveyor of fenced goods could be the custodian of Mirah’s lineage.

His retreat only brought him back to the oppressive memory of his recent dining room debate with Sir Hugo. The baronet, viewing their meticulously prepared roast pheasant and complex juniper reduction as mere utilitarian fuel for his rhetoric, had spoken over the delicate clinking of silver. “Confound it, Dan! Why don’t you make an opportunity of saying these things in public?” Sir Hugo had demanded, his booming baritone an exercise in the dominant lexicon's subtle power dynamics. He wanted Deronda in Parliament, adopting the authorized vocabulary of the state. But Deronda, savoring the untranslatable, grounding bitterness of the juniper on his tongue, had refused to compromise his sensory and moral truth. “I don’t want to make a living out of borrowed opinions,” he had replied. Sir Hugo’s insistence that one must “rule men through their own ideas” and practice “a little acting” felt to Deronda like a betrayal of one's native dialect, a willing participation in the erosion of cultural truth for the sake of political expediency.

Days later, driven by an interwoven sense of past and present, Deronda returned to the Holborn streets. Before confronting the pawnshop, he lingered at a second-hand bookshop, where the literature of the ages rested on tables like forgotten, exotic spices. He found *Salomon Maimon’s Lebensgeschichte*, a textual artifact of a Polish Jew’s life. Stepping inside to pay, the acoustic ecology shifted; the shop was a sanctuary of muffled silence, insulated by walls of decaying paper. 

There, against a backdrop of shadowed shelves, sat a man with skin like an old ivory carving. He was not a traditional tradesman, but rather a solitary figure reading yesterday’s *Times*. When the man looked up, his gaze carried the mythic weight of a medieval Hebrew poet or a prophet of the Exile. His face was a map of strenuous, eager experience, steeped in the tragic untranslatability of an inner life that the modern world had ignored. 

“What is the price of this book?” Deronda asked, his voice feeling inappropriately loud in the hushed space.

The man held the book close on his lap. “There is no mark, and Mr. Ram is not in now. What are you disposed to give for it?” Then, observing Deronda with an intense, far-off gaze, his voice dropped to a deepened tone of eager inquiry, rich with the melodic remnants of a fading tongue. “You are a man of learning—you are interested in Jewish history?”

“I am certainly interested in Jewish history,” Deronda said quietly.

The strange scholar rose, his thin hand gripping Deronda’s arm. In a hoarse, excited whisper that transcended mere words, he asked, “You are perhaps of our race?”

Deronda colored deeply. The question was a delicate bridge of cultural memory, but Deronda’s truth forced him to shatter it. “No,” he said. 

With that single, dominant English syllable, the connection died. The grasp relaxed; the eagerness collapsed into uninterested melancholy, as if a possessing spirit had sunk back into the margins of history. “I believe Mr. Ram will be satisfied with half-a-crown, sir,” the man said with distant civility. Dismissed, Deronda paid and left, feeling the humiliation of a profound linguistic and spiritual failure.

He stepped back into the street and entered the jarring reality of Ezra Cohen’s shop. The acoustic environment was dominated by the clinking of plated stoppers and the glistening shopkeeper’s commercial patter. “Mother! Mother!” Ezra called out, then smiled at Deronda. “Coming, sir—coming directly.”

Deronda watched with sinking dread as a vigorous woman beyond fifty emerged from the back. Despite her garish earrings and the twilight haste of her toilette, he noted the strong, capable hands of a woman who might still knead dough for the Sabbath. She possessed an underlying, agrarian domesticity that made it impossible to completely erase the fear that she might be the mother of a refined daughter like Mirah. The uncherishing years had masked her, but the mild, guttural tone of her voice as she asked, “How can I serve you, sir?” held echoes of an older, resonant tradition.

“I should like to look at the silver clasps in the window,” Deronda said.

Ezra intervened with a booming, performative eagerness, eager to cater to his distinguished customer. “I’ll reach ’em, mother. Mother’s too proud: she wants to do everything herself. First-rate workmanship, sir—worth twice the money; only I get ’em a bargain from Cologne.” The son’s voice was an abrasive intrusion, stripping the silver of its artisanal history and replacing it with the shallow lexicon of commerce. 

Then came the call of “Addy!” A young woman in a bright blue dress, carrying a black-eyed baby, entered from the back, accompanied by a robust boy of six and a younger girl. They looked fiercely Semitic, like puppy lions showing the spots of far-off progenitors. Addy’s lively, unrefined presence deepened Deronda’s unwelcome impression of the matriarch’s enduring dignity by sheer contrast. 

The boy stomped forward into the shop. Setting himself four feet from Deronda, hands thrust into the pockets of his miniature knickerbockers, he surveyed the stranger.

“What is your name, sirrah?” Deronda asked, patting the boy’s head.

“Jacob Alexander Cohen,” he declared. His small voice was hoarse and glib, mimicking the aged commercial soul of generations past—a tragic example of a native dialect warped entirely into the language of the marketplace.

“You are not named after your father, then?”

“No, after my grandfather; he sells knives and razors and scissors—my grandfather does,” said Jacob. “He gave me this knife.” The boy drew a pocket-knife, his fingers dark, swiftly opening two blades and a cork-screw with practiced ease.

“Is not that a dangerous plaything?” Deronda asked the grandmother, disturbed by this intersection of youthful innocence and sharp utility.

“*He*’ll never hurt himself, bless you!” she said, contemplating her grandson with placid rapture.

“Have *you* got a knife?” Jacob asked, stepping closer, his hoarse voice demanding participation in his world.

“Yes. Do you want to see it?” Deronda replied, taking a small penknife from his waistcoat-pocket, drawn inexorably into the cyclical, inescapable rhythm of the family’s existence.
