CHAPTER XXV. Though Mrs. Jennings usually spent much of the year with her children and friends, she had a home of her own—a modest house near Portman Square where she lived each winter since her husband’s passing. As January drew near, she began to think of returning there, and one day, without warning, invited the elder Misses Dashwood to join her in London. Elinor, not noticing the flush of excitement on Marianne’s face or the eager light in her eyes, quickly refused on behalf of both sisters, believing it reflected their shared desire. Her reason was simple: they could not leave their mother at such a time.

Mrs. Jennings blinked in surprise and repeated her invitation at once. “Oh, Lord! Your mother can spare you perfectly well,” she insisted, her voice warm with conviction. “And I do beg you’ll come—my heart is set on it. Don’t think I’ll make any trouble. Just sending Betty by coach will do, and I’m sure I can afford that. We three could fit nicely in my chaise. Once we’re in town, if you don’t care to go wherever I do, no matter—you can always go with one of my daughters. I’m certain your mother won’t mind. In fact, she’ll be pleased—after all, I’ve managed to get my own children off my hands, so she’ll trust me to look after you. And if I don’t see at least one of you safely married before I’m done with you, it won’t be for lack of trying. I’ll speak well of you to every young man I meet.”

Sir John chimed in, half-jokingly: “I suspect Miss Marianne wouldn’t mind at all—if only her sister would agree. It’s cruel to deny her pleasure just because Miss Dashwood isn’t keen. So I say, when you’re tired of Barton, just set off for town—no need to tell Miss Dashwood a thing.”

“Nonsense!” Mrs. Jennings laughed. “I’d love Miss Marianne’s company whether Miss Dashwood comes or not. The more, the merrier, I always say. And it’ll be better for them to go together—so if they tire of me, they can talk among themselves and laugh at my odd ways behind my back. But one or both—I must have at least one. Good heavens, how can I bear being alone? I’ve always had Charlotte with me this winter. Come now, Miss Marianne—let’s shake on it. If Miss Dashwood changes her mind later, so much the better.”

Marianne turned to her with sudden warmth. “I thank you sincerely, ma’am,” she said, her voice trembling slightly. “Your kindness moves me deeply. To accept your invitation would bring me almost the greatest joy I know. But my mother—my dear, dear mother—how can I leave her? Elinor is right. If my absence would cause her even a moment’s sorrow, I would not go. Not for anything in the world.”

Mrs. Jennings assured her again that Mrs. Dashwood could manage perfectly well without them. Elinor, now seeing clearly how completely Marianne’s heart was fixed on Willoughby, made no further resistance. She simply deferred the decision to their mother, though she doubted she would find support there. She knew her mother would favor anything that brought happiness to Marianne—and especially something that promised the chance to see Willoughby again. Elinor dared not explain her own reasons for wanting to stay. It was painful enough to witness how Marianne, who had always been so sensitive and fastidious, could overlook Mrs. Jennings’ coarse manners, her loud laughter, her vulgar ways—all of which had once caused her such irritation. This blind devotion revealed just how deeply she was attached to one man, and it shook Elinor more than she cared to admit.

When Mrs. Dashwood heard of the invitation, she was delighted. She saw only the joy it would bring her daughters. “It’s exactly what I wanted,” she declared. “You’ll both benefit from it. Margaret and I will be so happy together—just books and music, quiet and peaceful. When you return, you’ll see how much Margaret has improved! I’ve even planned some small changes to your rooms—now’s the perfect time. You should go to London. Every young woman in your position should experience its society, its life. You’ll be under the care of a kind, motherly woman—I have no doubt about her goodness. And perhaps you’ll see your brother. Whatever his faults—or those of his wife—I cannot bear to have you estranged from him. He’s still Edward’s son, after all.”

Elinor hesitated. “Though you’re always so thoughtful for our happiness,” she said gently, “there is still one concern I cannot ignore.” Marianne’s face fell. “What is it?” asked Mrs. Dashwood, smiling. “Don’t tell me it’s about cost. That’s nonsense.”

“It’s not about money,” Elinor replied. “It’s that while I respect Mrs. Jennings’ intentions, I do not believe her company will add to our comfort, nor her presence give us any real standing.”

Her mother nodded thoughtfully. “True,” she said. “But you won’t be spending much time alone with her. You’ll often appear with Lady Middleton. That matters far more.”

“If Elinor fears Mrs. Jennings,” Marianne said lightly, “it need not stop me. I have no such worries. I can endure whatever discomforts come my way—easily.”

Elinor smiled faintly. She remembered how hard it had been to get Marianne to behave politely toward Mrs. Jennings before. Yet now, her sister seemed willing to overlook everything for the sake of a single hope. Still, Elinor decided: if Marianne went, she would go too. She could not leave her sister entirely to her own judgment, nor abandon Mrs. Jennings to Marianne’s unpredictable moods. Besides, Edward Ferrars, according to Lucy Steele, would not arrive in London until February. Their visit could end before he came—and that gave her some peace.

“I want you both to go,” Mrs. Dashwood insisted. “These objections are foolish. You’ll enjoy yourselves. Especially together. And Elinor—when you stop thinking so much, you might actually find pleasure in meeting your sister-in-law’s family.”

Elinor had long wished to lessen her mother’s faith in Edward’s affection for her. Now, though she knew it was hopeless, she tried once more. “I like Edward very much,” she said calmly, “and I’ll always be glad to see him. But the rest of the family—it makes no difference to me whether I ever meet them.”

Mrs. Dashwood smiled and said nothing. Marianne looked up in surprise. Elinor sighed inwardly—she might as well have kept silent.

After little more discussion, the plan was settled. The invitation was accepted. Mrs. Jennings rejoiced, promising endless kindness and attention. It wasn’t just her joy—Sir John was thrilled, for the idea of two new people in London eased his dread of loneliness. Even Lady Middleton feigned delight, though it was an effort. And the Miss Steeles—especially Lucy—were beside themselves with happiness.

Elinor submitted to the decision with less resistance than she expected. For herself, it mattered little whether she went or stayed. But seeing her mother so pleased, and Marianne radiant with anticipation—her spirits lifted, her voice bright, her whole manner transformed—she found it hard to regret the choice. Marianne’s joy was almost overwhelming, a wild ecstasy that left her breathless with impatience. Only her deep love for her mother held her back, and at parting, her grief was profound. Her mother wept nearly as much. Elinor alone seemed to understand this separation might not be forever.

They left in the first week of January. The Middletons were to follow shortly. The Miss Steeles remained at the park, waiting to depart with the rest of the family.
