Love, in its most potent manifestations, possesses the nature of an isolated fatality, rendering the mind's usual opinions and resolutions utterly irrelevant. Consider Daphnis, whose frenzy rendered Heraclitus's doctrines useless; or Tristan, whose philtre-induced passion would have shattered the sharpest logic of Duns Scotus; or Romeo, whose sudden devotion to Juliet made any philosophical objections regarding Ptolemy moot. Yet, not all love operates in such solitary isolation. This passion possesses a vast capacity to ally itself with every operation of the soul, acknowledging effects from the imagined light of unproven firmaments and calibrating its scale to the grander orbits of what has been and what shall be.

Upon his return to town, Deronda assured Sir Hugo that he had successfully implanted in Grandcourt's mind the distinct understanding that he could secure fifty thousand pounds by relinquishing a prospect that was likely distant and far from certain. Beyond this, however, Deronda possessed no further insight into Grandcourt's true disposition, save for the observation that the baronet remained inclined to maintain friendly communications.

"And what did you think of the future bride on a nearer survey?" Sir Hugo asked, his tone shifting to one of agreeable playfulness.

"I thought better of her than I did in Leubronn," Deronda replied. "Roulette was not a fitting setting for her; it brought out something of the demon. At Diplow, she seemed much more womanly and attractive—less hard and self-possessed. I perceived a quite different expression in her mouth and eyes."

"Don't flirt with her too much, Dan," Sir Hugo warned, feigning concern. "If you provoke Grandcourt into savagery when they convene at the Abbey for Christmas, it will interfere with my own affairs."

"I can remain in town, sir," Deronda offered.

"No, no. Lady Mallinger and the children simply cannot do without you at Christmas. Only, pray, avoid making mischief—unless you contrive a duel and manage to shoot Grandcourt, which might be worth a trifling amount of inconvenience."

"I don't believe you have ever witnessed me flirt," Deronda said, devoid of amusement.

"Oh, haven't I, though?" Sir Hugo retorted provocatively. "You are perpetually casting tender glances at women and engaging them in a manner that borders on the Jesuitical. You are a dangerous young fellow—a modern Lovelace who ensures that Clarissas run after you rather than you pursuing them."

Was there any point in allowing oneself to be exasperated by such a tasteless jest? Exasperation, after all, precedes reflection on utility. Few friendly remarks are more irritating than the assertion that we constantly appear to be doing precisely what we never intend to do. Sir Hugo's definition of flirting was, it was hoped, peculiar; for his own part, Deronda was certain he had never flirted. Nevertheless, he felt a relief that the baronet lacked knowledge regarding the repurchase of Gwendolen's necklace, thereby sparing him from feeding the baronet's appetite for such gallant rallying.

Resolved to exercise greater caution in the future, particularly during his upcoming visit to Mrs. Meyrick's—the first since his arrival from Leubronn—Deronda knew he would tread carefully. Mirah was undoubtedly a creature in whom it was perilously easy to display a tender interest, both in demeanor and speech.

Mrs. Meyrick had recently sent Deronda a detailed report on Mirah's well-being within her household. "We are growing fonder of her every day," she had written. "At breakfast, we all turn toward the door with expectation to see her enter; we watch her and listen to her as if she were a native from a newly discovered land. I have heard not a single word from her lips that casts the slightest doubt upon her. She is perfectly contented and overflowing with gratitude. My daughters are learning from her, and they eagerly hope to acquire her other pupils, for she is anxious not to eat the bread of idleness but to work, just as my girls do. Mab declares our life has transformed into a fairy tale, and her sole fear is that Mirah will revert to becoming a nightingale once more and fly away from us. Her voice is simply perfect—not loud and commanding, but searching and melting, akin to the contemplation of what has passed. That is precisely how old people like myself perceive a beautiful voice."

Yet, Mrs. Meyrick omitted details that would have required her to admit that Amy and Mab, who had accompanied Mirah to the synagogue, found the Jewish faith less reconcilable with their aspirations in Mirah's case than in that of Scott's Rebecca. Out of delicacy toward Mirah, whose religion was too tender a subject to be touched lightly, they maintained a silence. However, eventually, Amy, driven by her nature as a practical reformer, could not restrain a question.

"Excuse me, Mirah, but does it truly seem right to you that women should sit behind rails in a separate gallery?" Amy asked.

"Yes, I never thought of anything else," Mirah replied with mild surprise.

"And you prefer seeing the men with their hats on?" Mab ventured cautiously, proposing the smallest possible point of divergence.

"Oh, yes. I cherish what I have always seen there, because it evokes in me the same feelings—the feelings I would not exchange for anything else in the entire world."

Following this declaration, any criticism, whether of doctrine or practice, would have appeared to these generous young women as an inhospitable cruelty. Mirah's religion was woven into the very fiber of her affections and had never presented itself to her as a collection of abstract propositions.

"She says herself she is a very bad Jewess and does not fully know her people's religion," Amy remarked later, once Mirah had retired to bed. "Perhaps it will gradually melt away from her, and she will pass into Christianity like the rest of the world, provided she grows to love us very much and never finds her mother. It is so strange to belong to the Jewish religion now."

"Oh, oh, oh!" cried Mab. "I wish I were not such a hideous Christian. How can an ugly Christian, who is perpetually dropping her work, convert a beautiful Jewess who possesses not a single fault?"

"It may be wicked of me," admitted shrewd Kate, "but I cannot help wishing that her mother may not be found. There might be something unpleasant."

"I do not think so, my dear," Mrs. Meyrick reassured her. "I believe Mirah is cast in the mold of her mother. And what a joy it would be for her to have such a daughter returned! But a mother's feelings are not worth reckoning, I suppose"—she shot a mischievous glance at her own daughters—"and a dead mother is worth more than a living one?"

"Well, and so she may be, little mother," Kate conceded; "but we would rather hold you cheaper and have you alive."

It was not only the Meyricks, whose varied knowledge had been acquired through the irregular foraging typical of clever girls, but Deronda himself—who, despite his extensive masculine instruction—had been roused by this apparition of Mirah to the realization that he knew hardly anything about modern Judaism or the inner history of his own race. The Chosen People had commonly been treated as a group selected for the benefit of others; their thinking regarded as something (whatever it might be) that ought to have been entirely different. Deronda, like his neighbors, had viewed Judaism as a sort of eccentric, fossilized form that an accomplished man might dispense with studying and leave to specialists. But Mirah, with her terrified flight from one parent and her ardent yearning for the other, flashed upon him the hitherto neglected reality that Judaism was something still throbbing in human lives, still forging for them the only conceivable vesture of the world. During the idle excursion he immediately undertook with Sir Hugo, he began to seek out the exteriors of synagogues and the titles of books concerning the Jews.

This awakening of a new interest—this transition from the supposition that we hold correct opinions on subjects we are careless about, to a sudden care for them and a recognition that our previous views were merely ignorance—proved an effective remedy for ennui, a malady unfortunately incapable of being cured by a physician's prescription. Yet, Deronda carried this change with him, enduring his weeks of lounging all the better. It was on this journey that he first entered a Jewish synagogue at Frankfort, where his party rested on a Friday. While exploring the Juden-gasse, which he had observed long before, he recalled its picturesque old houses; what his eyes now chiefly dwelt upon were the human types inhabiting them. His thoughts, busily connecting them with the past phases of their race, stirred that fiber of historic sympathy which had helped determine in him certain traits worth noting for those interested in his future.

True, when a young man possesses a fine appearance, lacks eccentricities of manner, bears the education of a gentleman, and enjoys a present income, it is not customary to feel a prying curiosity about his way of thinking or his peculiar tastes. He may very well settle into life as an agreeable, clever youth without passing a special examination on such matters. Later, when he becomes somewhat slovenly and portly, his peculiarities become more distinctly discernible, and it is considered a mercy if they are not highly objectionable. But anyone wishing to comprehend the effect of subsequent events on Deronda should know a little more of what he was at twenty-five than was evident in ordinary intercourse.
