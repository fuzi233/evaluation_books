“Rise, my daughter,” said Fra Girolamo at last, his voice calm and steady. “Your servant waits not far off with the mules. It is time for me to continue my journey to Florence.”

Romola rose slowly from her knees. The silence she had kept was more than mere stillness—it had been a sacred act, a surrender of self to a new way of being. In renouncing her decision to leave her husband, she had felt her will shattered, as if every thread of personal resolve had been severed. Now, even the smallest choices seemed beyond her. She lifted the edge of her cowl, glancing toward the hill’s edge where Maso and another Dominican stood with their backs turned, ten yards away. But her eyes returned to Savonarola, lingering on him in quiet appeal. She did not speak. She waited for him to send Maso back—because to command would have felt like defiance, and she could not yet bear that.

“I will go and call them,” he said, interpreting her look. “And I wish to place you under the care of the brother who travels with me. You have chosen to submit yourself to guidance, to seek wisdom once deemed foolishness. One gateway to such wisdom is confession. You will need a confessor, my daughter—and I entrust you to Fra Salvestro, a brother of San Marco, whom I trust above all others.”

“I would rather have no guide but you, father,” Romola whispered, her voice trembling. Her brow furrowed with anxiety.

“My daughter,” he replied gently, “I do not serve as a confessor. My calling keeps me apart from such duties—those that would draw me into constant contact with the world, distracting me from my mission.”

“Then shall I never speak with you privately?” she asked, her voice rising slightly. “If I falter—if I—” She stopped herself, her breath catching. A sudden fear gripped her: what if the strength she had found in surrender began to fade without his presence?

“Should your soul need words from me,” Savonarola said softly, “you will let Fra Salvestro know. I will meet you in the sacristy or choir of San Marco. And know this—I will not cease to watch over you. I will instruct my brother well, so that he may lead you along the path of service—to the poor, the hungry, the suffering. For now, you are called not only as a woman, but as a daughter of Florence in these hard times. I wish to see you among the weaker sisters as the apple tree among the forest trees—your beauty and gifts not ends in themselves, but vessels through which the divine light shines more clearly. I must go now. I will call your servant.”

When Maso had moved ahead, Fra Salvestro stepped forward. Savonarola led Romola to him. Before, Romola had felt an instinctive resistance to accepting a stranger as her spiritual guide. Yet to oppose Savonarola’s choice now would be to reclaim independence when she had just abandoned it. Her mind was bent not toward ease, but toward sacrifice. She bowed deeply before Fra Salvestro, her gaze lowered, then raised her eyes.

But when she saw him fully, her hesitation turned to unease.

Some men inspire reverence by their very presence; others require us to bring our reverence already formed. That distinction struck Romola sharply as Savonarola disappeared from view and Fra Salvestro Maruffi took his place. There was nothing overtly unpleasing in the man’s face or manner—no trace of hypocrisy, no coarseness. Indeed, his features were finer than Savonarola’s, his frame taller. He was a well-known confessor to Florence’s elite, seasoned in spiritual direction. Yet his expression lacked depth—a wavering quality, as if his thoughts could not fix upon one great passion or conviction. It was not insincerity, but a shallowness of spirit. Such souls often strive earnestly to fulfill noble roles, reciting lofty truths with solemn faces, only to betray themselves through subtle shifts in expression—like a poet who cannot summon true verse, whose mouth betrays prose instead.

Fra Salvestro was prone to visions, perhaps born of a constitution inclined toward somnambulism. Savonarola believed these visions came from divine sources; Fra Salvestro himself had once doubted them, even rebuking Savonarola for preaching prophecy. This contrast proved again that greatness is not measured by belief in superstition, but by the fire within. Between the two, there was no question: one was a prophet, the other a well-meaning man shaped by circumstance.

The difference became clear to Romola as Fra Salvestro began speaking—offering words of encouragement, exhortation. After her initial rebellion against Savonarola had passed, she had forgotten her old dread of falling into fanaticism, of being drawn into cold, rigid piety. But now, that dread returned—not with force, but like a chill wind stirring through a clearing after sunrise. It could not undo the transformation she had undergone, but it dimmed the glow of her new resolve. Like grey clouds gathering over dawn, it made her return seem long and dull.

And perhaps no path is harder than the one we retrace after walking it with fierce determination.

As they crossed the city gates, delicate snowflakes drifted down around them. The grey sister hurried home from the Piazza di San Marco, stepping across the bridge, turning into the large door on Via de’ Bardi. Her footsteps left dark marks on the thin layer of snow, her cowl heavy and damp against her face.

She climbed to her room, shed her serge cloak, burned the letters she had written to Dino, restored her small treasures to their places, unbound her hair, and slipped into her plain black dress. Instead of embarking on a distant journey, she would sit where she always sat. Snow fell softly against the windows. She was alone.

Yet she did not feel despair. The solitude was sharp, yes—but her spirit burned with purpose. She was beginning again, guided by a new understanding. She had poured her entire will into renunciation. The empty tabernacle remained locked, and she placed Dino’s crucifix outside it, a silent witness.

No sound broke the quiet until night descended like a white ghost at the windowpane.

And yet—for Romola—this Christmas Eve of 1494 would remain the most unforgettable of her life.
