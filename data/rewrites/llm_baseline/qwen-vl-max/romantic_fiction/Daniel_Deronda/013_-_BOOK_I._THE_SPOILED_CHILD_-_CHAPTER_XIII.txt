“Philistia, be thou glad of me!”

Grandcourt had made up his mind—firmly, decisively—to marry Miss Harleth. And once that decision was fixed, he moved with a quiet precision that spoke of a man accustomed to shaping the world around him. Over the next fortnight, he ensured that their paths crossed daily, not by chance but by design. He saw her, or at least made it known that she occupied his thoughts, in ways both visible and subtle. His cousin, Mrs. Torrington, now presided over the household at Diplow, allowing Grandcourt to host a grand gathering where Gwendolen and her mother could be guests of honor. The event was carefully orchestrated: Grandcourt’s attentions were lavished upon Gwendolen—the dowerless beauty—while the heiress remained conspicuously ignored. The effect was unmistakable. The local society, from Mr. Gascoigne down to the most distant neighbor within visiting distance of Pennicote, took the matter as settled. Even the rector, Mr. Gascoigne, found himself compelled to act—he resolved to ensure the settlements would be generous enough for his niece. Indeed, the only mystery left was why Grandcourt hadn’t already proposed. The delay baffled everyone, including Grandcourt himself.

He had confided his intentions to Lush, expecting swift resolution. But time passed, and though he repeatedly told himself in the morning that today would be the day, each evening came without the words spoken. It was odd, even disconcerting. He had never allowed himself to consider that Gwendolen might refuse him. Yet something held him back—not doubt, exactly, but an invisible barrier, like a shadow cast by a fear he couldn’t name. He was certain of her acceptance, yet the certainty felt fragile. We are often paralyzed not by uncertainty, but by the horror of what might happen if we’re wrong. Like a blind worm, harmless in theory, yet terrifying in its potential bite—we avoid it, not because we think it dangerous, but because we can't bear the idea of being hurt.

He had asked permission to bring one of his finest horses for Gwendolen to ride. Mrs. Davilow would accompany them in the carriage, and they would go to Diplow for lunch, Grandcourt riding beside them. It was a perfect autumn afternoon—harvest season, golden and still, with poppies glowing along field edges, a gentle breeze stirring the wheat, and clouds casting fleeting shadows across the hills. Sheaves stood in neat rows; horses strained under heavy loads on stubbled fields. Pastures stretched wide and green, cattle resting beneath broad trees. The road wound through a countryside unchanged for generations—dairy farms nestled quietly, untouched by the rush of progress, while far off, the train’s whistle echoed like a faint reminder of modernity.

But peace did not settle easily in Mrs. Davilow’s heart. As she watched Gwendolen and Grandcourt ride ahead, then slow to a walk so they could talk, she felt the familiar ache of anxiety. This was the moment—a lover’s opportunity to speak plainly, to end all suspense. Yet no proposal came. All she could do was pray, trembling, that her daughter would choose wisely. If Rex had been the suitor, his passion might have repelled her. But Grandcourt? He was different—calm, composed, dignified. And Gwendolen had fallen silent around him, which was unusual. She rarely refrained from sharp remarks, especially about men. Now she said nothing. Mrs. Davilow dared not break the silence, afraid to disturb whatever lay beneath.

Was he the kind of man who could make her happy? That was the question. “Perhaps as happy as any other woman,” she told herself, trying to find comfort. She couldn’t imagine Gwendolen content in modest circumstances. She needed grandeur, freedom, distinction. And Grandcourt offered all that.

Grandcourt, too, felt the weight of hesitation. He longed to speak, to rid himself of this lingering uncertainty. As for whether she would accept him—well, that was a different matter. Not because he doubted her, but because he refused to allow himself to be placed in a position of vulnerability. He wouldn’t propose in a way that invited refusal. No formal letter, no dramatic declaration. He would wait until the moment was right—and when it came, it would be clear that she had already accepted, simply by accepting his attention.

Gwendolen enjoyed the ride. But her joy didn’t spill into laughter or spontaneous chatter as it had with Rex. She smiled, spoke lightly, but there was a distance in her tone, like echoes fading in a hollow valley. She was aware of something—some unseen tension in the air—but it wasn’t submission. Far from it. She meant to remain in control. She wanted everyone, including Grandcourt, to know that she would do as she pleased. If she chose him, it would be on her terms. She would not surrender her independence. She would not “do as other women do.”

Her conversation with Grandcourt was brief, measured—typical of a man who speaks little but carries weight. After entering the park, he asked, “How do you like Criterion’s paces?”

“He is delightful,” she replied. “I’d love to take a leap with him—if it wouldn’t frighten mamma. There was a wide channel we passed earlier. I’d like to gallop back and jump it.”

“Pray do. We can take it together.”

“No, thank you. Mamma is so timid—she might fall ill if she saw me.”

“I’ll go explain. Criterion would clear it perfectly.”

“No—indeed, you’re kind—but it would alarm her too much. I dare take risks when she’s not watching. I just don’t tell her.”

“We can let the carriage pass first.”

“No, no. Don’t think of it again—I only spoke out of impulse,” she said, suddenly retreating from her own suggestion.

“But Mrs. Davilow knows I’ll take care of you.”

“Yes, but she’d worry about my broken neck.”

A pause. Then Grandcourt, looking at her steadily, said, “I should like to have the right always to take care of you.”

Gwendolen didn’t meet his gaze. Her cheeks flushed, then paled. To her, it seemed like a long moment before she answered—lightly, almost flippantly—“Oh, I’m not sure I want to be taken care of. If I chose to risk breaking my neck, I’d like to be free to do it.”

She checked her horse, turned in the saddle, and glanced toward the approaching carriage. Her eyes brushed over Grandcourt, but there was no warmth, no meaning—only a cool detachment. In that instant, she realized she was risking more than her neck—she was risking the chance to stop him entirely. And she wasn’t at all sure she liked that possibility.

“Damn her,” Grandcourt thought, pulling his horse to a halt. He wasn’t given to internal monologue, but that single word carried a storm—frustration, irritation, a sense of being toyed with. Did she expect him to grovel? To declare his love in a melodramatic speech? No. That was not how power worked. He wouldn’t offer himself on a platter. He would wait. And if she rejected him, well—that would be her loss. But he was certain she had already chosen him. Her silence, her teasing, it was all coquetry. She played games, but he would not be fooled.

The carriage arrived, ending the private moment. At Diplow, Gwendolen became the center of attention—riding dress, hat off, surrounded by admiration. Without Lush present to leer, the attention felt flattering rather than oppressive. For a time, the inner conflict faded. She felt lighter, freer. But she also sensed Grandcourt’s presence—unseen, unchanging, unnerving.

After luncheon, Lady Flora Hollis suggested a tour of the grounds. As guests dispersed, Grandcourt had several opportunities to speak privately. But he didn’t. He stayed close, but his words remained polite, impersonal. He looked at her as before, and she returned his gaze—defiantly, unafraid—even if his eyes held no emotion.

Then, near the end of the tour, by a pool where Fetch, the spaniel, attempted to retrieve a water lily, Grandcourt turned and said, deliberately, “This is boring. Shall we go up there?” He gestured to a knoll covered in American shrubs, with a narrow winding path.

“Oh, certainly—since we’re exploring,” Gwendolen replied. She was both intrigued and wary.

The path was too narrow for an arm. They walked in silence. At the top, Grandcourt said, “There’s nothing here. Not worth climbing.”

Gwendolen didn’t laugh. She stood still, holding her robe like a statue, gripping her whip tightly—having grabbed it reflexively when they started.

“What sort of place do you prefer?” he asked.

“Different places have their charm. On the whole, I like open, cheerful ones. I don’t care for anything sombre.”

“Your home at Offendene is rather sombre…”

“It is.”

“You won’t stay there long, I hope.”

“Oh, yes, I think so. Mamma likes being near her sister.”

Silence.

“It’s not expected that *you* will live there forever, though Mrs. Davilow may.”

“I don’t know. Women can’t go searching for adventures—no North-West Passage, no Nile source, no tiger hunts. We’re planted where we grow, or transplanted by gardeners. We’re raised like flowers—to look pretty, to be dull, and not complain. That’s my view. Flowers get bored. Some become poisonous. What do you think?” She spoke quickly, nervously, tapping her whip against a rhododendron.

“I agree. Most things are bores,” Grandcourt said, his thoughts drifting. Then, after a pause, he added, in his usual broken drawl, “But a woman can be married.”

“Some women can.”

“You, certainly—unless you’re stubbornly cruel.”

“I’m not sure I’m not both.” She turned sharply to face him, sensing his gaze. She wondered what seeing him would do to her—more than to him.

He stood still, half a yard away. A strange stillness settled over her—like a dream. Then he said, “Are you as uncertain about yourself as you make others feel?”

“I’m quite uncertain about myself. I don’t know how uncertain others may be.”

“And you wish them to understand you don’t care?” he asked, his voice harder now.

“I didn’t say that,” she replied, hesitating, turning her eyes away—and whipping the bush again.

“You do care,” he said softly, not faster, but with a new depth.

“Ha! My whip!” she exclaimed, startled. It had slipped from her hand—natural in a moment of agitation—and snapped forward, catching in the branches of an azalea halfway down the slope.

Now she could run down, laughing, and Grandcourt had to follow. But she reached it first, retrieved the whip, and continued down, pausing only to glance back at him—her eyes bright, her cheeks flushed—as if she’d won a small victory. Mrs. Davilow noticed the glow when they rejoined the group.

“It’s all coquetting,” Grandcourt thought. “Next time I call, she’ll come.”

He believed it might happen soon—the picnic archery meeting at Cardell Chase, planned after the ball. That night, Gwendolen found herself torn between two futures, two versions of herself. One was the woman who would accept Grandcourt, the other who would resist. She didn’t know which she would become. The very idea terrified her. Her life motto—“do as I like”—seemed to fail her. She couldn’t predict what she might desire next.

And yet… the prospect of marrying Grandcourt felt increasingly tempting. The luxuries, the dignity, the power to live freely—these were real now, within reach. She could have it all. And Grandcourt? He was flawless in appearance, calm, refined. He had seen the world, traveled everywhere. That alone was appealing. He didn’t seem to enjoy much—perhaps that was a good thing. The less he desired, the more freedom she would have. She imagined managing him effortlessly.

So why did he unsettle her? Why was she quieter, more reserved, around him than with anyone else? His stillness was attractive—charming even—but also slightly numbing. He was formidable. A handsome lizard, perhaps—quiet, deliberate, unlike the darting, flashy creatures she knew. But she knew little of lizards. Maybe this one was gentle. Maybe it would make a fine pet.

She knew nothing about his past. Had he ever loved? Made love? It seemed impossible. He appeared untouched by passion. And yet, here he was, making her question her own resolve. She wished to marry him. He suited her purpose. Her intention, deep down, was to accept.

But could she?

She began to fear herself. Her defiance had gone too far. She wondered what she would do when he approached her again.

On the drive home, seated with her back to the horses as usual, she was watched closely by her mother. The changes in her expression—restless eyes, silence, absent-mindedness—were signs to Mrs. Davilow that something profound had happened.

“Something has happened, dear?” she asked gently.

Gwendolen turned, removed her gloves and hat, letting the breeze touch her skin. They were on a secluded stretch of road, shadows long, no one near. Her eyes met her mother’s, but she said nothing.

“Has Mr. Grandcourt said something? Tell me, dear,” Mrs. Davilow urged, pleading.

“What am I to tell you, mamma?” Gwendolen replied, evasive.

“I’m sure you’re agitated. You ought to confide in me. Don’t leave me in doubt.”

“Mamma, please don’t be miserable,” Gwendolen said, pettishly. “It only makes me worse. I’m in doubt myself.”

“About Mr. Grandcourt’s intentions?”

“No. Not at all.”

“Then about whether you’ll accept him?”

“Precisely.”

“Have you given him a doubtful answer?”

“I’ve given him no answer at all.”

“He’s spoken clearly enough for you to understand?”

“As far as I let him.”

“You expect him to keep pursuing you?”

No reply.

“You haven’t discouraged him?”

“I dare say not.”

“I thought you liked him,” Mrs. Davilow whispered.

“So I do, mamma, as liking goes. There’s less to dislike about him than most men. He’s quiet and *distingué*.” She spoke with a pout, then smiled mischievously. “Indeed, he has all the qualities of a tolerable husband—battlement, veranda, stable, etc.—no grins, no glass in his eye.”

“Be serious, dear. Do you mean to accept him?”

“Oh, pray, mamma, leave me to myself,” Gwendolen said, voice strained.

Mrs. Davilow said nothing more.

At home, Gwendolen announced she wouldn’t dine. She was tired and would come down later. She didn’t mind if her uncle heard—she was convinced he’d side with Grandcourt. And she wanted to accept him, if she could. At that moment, she would gladly have chained her own caprice.

Mr. Gascoigne did hear—not the exact words, but a softened version. Mrs. Davilow conveyed that Gwendolen was uncertain, but leaning toward acceptance. The rector, firm and decisive, felt duty-bound to intervene. Mrs. Davilow hesitated—perhaps it was better to say nothing. Gwendolen was sensitive, perhaps even willful. But the rector wouldn’t be swayed. Once he formed a judgment, he acted. Shadows of doubt were meaningless to him. He would step in.
