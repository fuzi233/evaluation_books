The whole pensionnat had fallen soft and still at last. Dinner plates were cleared away, the noisy hour of recess where the pupils ran screaming through the halls had faded to quiet, and dusk bled purple over the slate roofs. The dim study lamp glowed low in the refectory, gilding the edges of open textbooks, as the day pupils trooped out the heavy brass front door, its latch clanging shut behind them, the shrill evening bell falling silent at last. Madame Beck was settled safe in her private dining parlour, laughing low over coffee with her mother and a small group of friends, and that was my cue.

I slipped to the kitchen, cheeks warm with a tender secret, and begged my friend Goton for a single tallow candle, just half an hour’s light for something that mattered very much. “Mais certainement, chou-chou,” she laughed, pressing two fat bougies into my palm, “you shall have two, if you like.” Light in hand, I crept noiselessly up the stairs to the dormitory, only to sink into utter chagrin when I spotted a pupil curled up in one of the beds, indisposed. Worse still, I recognized the ruffled muslin edges of Ginevra Fanshawe’s nightcap, her messy, pretty face half-buried in the pillow. She looked fast asleep—but I knew better. A tiny twitch of her eyelid told me she was feigning rest, watching slyly for my movements, ready to burst into a flood of giddy, chattering questions the moment I sat down to read. I could not trust her, not at all, and I had waited all day for this one hour of peace to read my precious letter.

I slipped back down the stairs, prize tucked safe in my pocket, to try the classrooms next. But ill luck hounded me: it was the weekly cleaning day, and the maids were sweeping every room by candlelight. Benches were piled haphazard on top of desks, dust motes swirled thick in the dim air, and damp coffee-grounds (the Labassecour housekeepers’ trick for trapping dust) were strewn dark across the floor. There was no quiet here, no spot to hide. Baffled but unbroken, I turned away, resolved to find my solitude somewhere—anywhere.

I fetched a long-forgotten key I knew was tucked behind the shelf in the coat closet, climbed three flights of creaking stairs one after another, until I reached a narrow, dark, silent landing. I unlocked the worm-eaten wooden door, and stepped into the garret: cold, black, so quiet I could hear the wind whistle through the cracks in the slates. No one would follow me here. No one would interrupt. Not even Madame Beck. I shut the door behind me, set my candle on a dented, mouldy old chest of drawers, wrapped my thin wool shawl tight around my shoulders to fend off the ice-cold air, and drew out my letter. My hands trembled with that sweet, sharp impatience, and I broke the seal.

Would it be long? Would it be short? I brushed a stray strand of hair from my eyes, still damp from the soft southern rain that had fallen that afternoon. It was long. Would it be cool? Would it be kind? It was kind. To all the checked, disciplined, starved parts of me that had learned never to hope for much, it felt so much kinder than I had ever dared imagine. I had hoped so little, feared so much, that the full, honeyed delight of that moment was something many people go their whole lives never to taste. The poor English teacher, huddled in that frosty garret, reading by a guttering candle in the wintry draught, holding a letter that was nothing more than gentle, warm good-nature, was far happier than any queen sitting on a gilded throne in a palace.

Dr. John had written to me at length, with real pleasure, his mood soft and sunny as he dwelt on all the small, shared moments we had lately lived: the walks we had taken along the city ramparts, the silly conversations we had shared over tea, the little quiet afternoons that had felt so bright and warm. The very best part, the part that made my chest feel fit to burst with joy, was the certainty that he had not written those lines just to please me—he had written them because it had pleased him, too. That joy might never come again, I knew, but that was a worry for another day. This moment was perfect: no pain, no shadow, no lack. A passing seraph might have rested right there beside me, leaning his soft, healing wing against my throbbing heart. I forgave every sharp thing he had ever said to me, every slight, freely, for the sake of this one perfect, dear memory.

And then something moved.

There was a strange, soft sound in the vast silent garret, the quiet scrape of a stealthy foot on the bare wooden floor, gliding out of the dark recess where the pupils’ old winter cloaks hung. I turned fast. My candle had burned low, the room stretched long and shadowed, and there, plain as day in the middle of that ghostly space, stood a figure all stark black and white: narrow, straight black skirts, a head wrapped tight in a bandage, veiled all in white.

Say what you will—call me nervous, over-excited by the joy of the letter, say I dreamed—this I swear: I saw it. An image like a Nun.

I cried out, my throat tight with sickness. If the shape had stepped closer I would have fainted dead away. But it receded, and I ran for the door. I do not remember how I stumbled down all those flights of stairs. By instinct I avoided the refectory, and ran straight for Madame Beck’s private parlour, bursting right in.

“There is something in the garret!” I gasped, pale as death, cold and shaking all over. “I was up there, I saw something. All of you, come and look!”

The room was not as full as it felt to my frayed senses: only Madame Beck, her ailing mother Madame Kint, her brother Victor Kint, and one other gentleman, who had his back turned to the door, talking to the old lady. They all jumped up in alarm, surrounding me, and the sight of the men in the room gave me courage. I beckoned them after me, back up the stairs, and only then remembered my letter, left sitting on that chest of drawers next to the candle. My precious letter! I would defy any ghost or thief, flesh or spirit, to get it back. I ran up the stairs faster, hearing them tramping behind me, forced to follow.

When I wrenched open the garret door, the whole room was dark as a pit. My candle had been snuffed out. Happily Madame Beck had had the good sense to grab a parlour lamp before we left, and its soft ray cut through the thick blackness a moment later. The snuffed candle sat on the drawers, exactly where I left it—but my letter was gone.

I did not care about the nun anymore. “My letter! My letter!” I sobbed, scrambling on the floor, wringing my hands. It was cruel, cruel, to have that little bit of comfort snatched away before I had even half read it all. The others searched every corner, chattering about disturbed cloaks, a cracked pane in the skylight, but I paid them no mind, frantic for my lost letter.

“Lucy? My dear girl, what letter is this?” A soft, familiar voice spoke right by my ear. I looked up, and my breath caught. It was him. Dr. John Graham Bretton himself. He had been called to the pensionnat that very evening to check on Madame Kint when her illness flared up—he was that second gentleman I had seen in the parlour.

“It’s yours,” I wept. “The letter you wrote to me. I came up here to read it quiet, because there was nowhere else I could be alone. I saved it all day, never opened it until tonight, I only read it once. I cannot bear to lose it.”

“Hush, don’t distress yourself so,” he said, his warm hand curling around my cold icy fingers, pulling me to my feet. “Come out of this cold room. They can send for the police to search more, we don’t need to stay.” He led me down to a tiny unused parlour where a low fire still glowed in the stove, sat me down beside it, and soothed me, promising he would write me twenty letters to replace the one I lost. His kindness was so soft, so tender, that I carried the echo of it in my heart for the rest of my life, warm even in the darkest days.

Later, when I was calmer, he smiled at me, drawing the crumpled letter out of his waistcoat pocket. His quick eye had spotted it fall on the floor when I scrambled, and he had snatched it up, teasing, but he could not bear to see me grieve so long over it. “Here is your letter,” he laughed. “Why is it worth all these tears, Lucy?”

I did not tell him I prized it like the blood in my veins. I only said I had so very few letters worth caring for.

He asked me then, gentle now, no trace of teasing left, what exactly I had seen up in the garret. I told him the whole truth, about the veiled nun, the old legend of the ghost that haunted the pensionnat, the story I had told him that soft October afternoon when we rode together through the Bois l’Etang. He sat quiet, thinking, and told me it was no ghost, just my overworked, tired nerves, my mind playing tricks on me after so much long-held stress. He told me not to tell anyone else, not to fear, that if the nun ever came back he would come fight her himself. He laughed, light and warm, and bade her give him his compliments if she haunted my garret again.

When he was gone, and all the fuss had died down, Madame Beck told me there was nothing to be found up in that garret: the cloaks hung as they always did, the cracked skylight had been broken for months, no intruder could have gotten in. She made me promise not to tell any of the pupils or staff about what I had seen, and the whole matter was soon forgotten by everyone but me.

I sat long that night by my bed, holding my crumpled, precious letter close to my chest, half glowing with the warm, soft joy of the lines Dr. John had written, half shivering, wondering deep in my heart: had that nun been a ghost from beyond the grave, or had the long, lonely weight of my days made her real, all spun out of my own overtaxed mind?
