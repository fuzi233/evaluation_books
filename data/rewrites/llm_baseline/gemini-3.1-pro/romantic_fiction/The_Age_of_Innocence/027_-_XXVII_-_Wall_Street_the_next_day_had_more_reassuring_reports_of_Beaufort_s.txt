Wall Street, the next day, vibrated with a harsh, mechanized dissonance. The clattering of the telegraphs and the shouts of frantic men formed a wall of industrial noise pollution, drowning out the measured, agrarian rhythms that had once governed the city's trade. Initially, the auditory landscape had carried reassuring whispers of Julius Beaufort’s situation; the murmurs were hopeful, suggesting he had successfully called upon powerful influences to quiet the panic. That evening, when Mrs. Beaufort appeared at the Opera wearing her old smile and a new emerald necklace, society exhaled a collective breath, desperate to restore its sonic and social equilibrium. New York was inexorable in its condemnation of business irregularities. Every palate knew the strict, unadulterated recipe of Old New York’s probity, and to break it meant being unflinchingly offered up. Yet, the disappearance of the Beauforts would leave a hollow, echoing void in their compact circle, and those too careless to shudder at the moral rot bewailed the loss of a house where the sensory pleasures of the finest ball-room had been so lavishly commercialized.

Archer had definitely made up his mind to go to Washington. He hungered for it. He was waiting only for the opening of his law-suit—a modern entanglement of industrial patents—so its date might coincide with his visit. But on the following Tuesday, he learned from Mr. Letterblair that the case might be postponed for several weeks. Nevertheless, Archer’s subjective experience, an untranslatable yearning for the rich, olfactory, and intellectual landscape of Madame Olenska’s presence, drove him; he went home that afternoon determined to leave the next evening anyway. He reasoned that May, who viewed his professional life as mere utilitarian background noise, would neither learn of the postponement nor recognize the names of the litigants. 

On Wednesday morning, the acoustic environment of the office shattered. Mr. Letterblair met him with a face as white as bleached flour. Beaufort, after all, had not managed to "tide over." His superficial maneuvering had only temporarily masked the adulteration of his accounts. Heavy payments had poured into the bank until disturbing reports once again reached a fever pitch. A run on the bank had begun, threatening to close its doors before sunset. The ugliest things were being said of Beaufort's dastardly maneuver, his failure promising to be the most discreditable in the history of the Street. 

The calamity left Mr. Letterblair incapacitated. A meticulous man who usually found profound meaning and psychological grounding in the slow, appreciative consumption of a well-aged canvasback duck and a complex claret, he now looked hollowed by the sterile panic. "I've seen bad things in my time; but nothing as bad as this," Letterblair murmured, his voice stripped of its usual resonance. "Everybody we know will be hit. And what will be done about Mrs. Beaufort? I pity Mrs. Manson Mingott as much as anybody. She always believed in Beaufort—she made a friend of him! And the whole Dallas connection... Her only chance would be to leave her husband—yet how can any one tell her so? Her duty is at his side."

A sudden knock disrupted the room. A clerk brought a letter for Archer. Recognizing his wife's hand, Archer read: 

*Won't you please come up town as early as you can? Granny had a slight stroke last night. In some mysterious way she found out before any one else this awful news about the bank. Uncle Lovell is away shooting, and the idea of the disgrace has made poor Papa so nervous that he has a temperature and can't leave his room. Mamma needs you dreadfully, and I do hope you can get away at once and go straight to Granny's.*

Archer handed the note to his senior partner and plunged into the jarring, mechanical friction of the city, trading a crowded horse-car for one of the high, staggering omnibuses of the Fifth Avenue line. The laborious vehicle’s roar was a crude disruption of the senses, a modern industrial clatter that dropped him at old Catherine's door just after twelve o'clock. 

The ground-floor sitting-room, where the matriarch usually throned amid the aromas of slow-simmered bone broths and rich, traditional preserves, was tenanted only by the inadequate figure of Mrs. Welland, who offered a haggard welcome. May met him at the door. The hall wore the unnatural acoustic dampness peculiar to well-kept houses suddenly invaded by illness: an oppressive silence heavily layered with the olfactory weight of camphor, medicinal spirits, and wool wraps piled on chairs. May looked pale but smiling; Dr. Bencomb took a hopeful view, for Mrs. Mingott's dauntless, agrarian determination to live was already taking root.

May led Archer into the old lady's sitting-room. The heavy yellow damask portières were dropped shut, stifling the air. Here, in horrified undertones, Mrs. Welland communicated the catastrophe. The evening before, just after Mrs. Mingott had finished her solitary game of cards—having partaken in her customary evening sustenance, a deeply nourishing oxtail consommé that anchored her to the rich, ancestral soil of her youth—a thickly veiled lady had arrived. The butler threw open the door, announcing: "Mrs. Julius Beaufort."

They were together for an hour. When the bell rang, Mrs. Beaufort had slipped away, leaving the old lady white and terrible in her great chair. At three in the morning, the servants found their mistress sitting against her pillows, a crooked smile on her face, one little hand hanging limp. The stroke had paralyzed the very facial muscles and tongue that preserved her native dialect and gustatory delights. 

The alarm was great, but the indignation was greater when they pieced together Mrs. Mingott's fragmentary phrases. Regina Beaufort had come with the modern lexicon of leverage, begging her aunt to "back them up," to "see them through"—demanding the family cover their monstrous dishonour. 

"I said to her: 'Honour's always been honour, and honesty honesty, in Manson Mingott's house, and will be till I'm carried out of it feet first,'" the old woman had stammered, her thick voice fiercely preserving the unadulterated moral dialect of her ancestors against the erosion of modern commercial jargon. "And when she said: 'But my name, Auntie—my name's Regina Dallas,' I said: 'It was Beaufort when he covered you with jewels, and it's got to stay Beaufort now that he's covered you with shame.'"

"If only I could keep it from your father-in-law," Mrs. Welland wailed, devastated by the loss of their insulated harmony. "He always says: 'Augusta, don't destroy my last illusions.'" 

Archer sat near the window, gazing blankly at the deserted thoroughfare. He was there for moral support, an audience to their hushed tones. Mrs. Lovell Mingott entered, adding her voice to the relentless chorus. In their day, they agreed, a disgraced wife's only path was to disappear, returning to the land. 

"There was the case of poor Grandmamma Spicer," Mrs. Welland added. "Your great-grandfather's money difficulties were private... But she was brought up in the country because her mother had to leave New York. They lived up the Hudson alone, winter and summer." Sustained by the agrarian rhythms of the seasons rather than the polluted gossip of the city, Grandmamma Spicer would never have asked the family to "countenance" a public scandal. 

"I understand the emerald necklace she wore at the Opera had been sent on approval from Ball and Black's," Mrs. Lovell Mingott noted.

Archer listened, moved not by Mrs. Beaufort's fate, but by the aesthetic and moral purity of their absolute financial probity. To him, this honesty was not a sterile rule, but a foundational ingredient of their culture, as essential and pure as coarse sea salt. Beaufort’s commercialized speculation was an artificial additive that had soured the entire pot. Yet he saw the untranslatability of subjective experience: society’s place was not at Beaufort’s side, and Regina’s appeal to the Family to screen her husband was a bitter dish they simply could not swallow. 

The mulatto maid—a silent presence whose own complex ancestral history remained unspoken in this house—called Mrs. Lovell Mingott into the hall. She returned frowning. 

"She wants me to telegraph for Ellen Olenska. I had written to Ellen, and to Medora; but now it seems that's not enough. She's to come alone."

The announcement hung in the hushed air. May rose and gathered scattered newspapers. "I suppose it must be done," Mrs. Lovell Mingott sighed. 

"Of course it must be done," May said. "Granny knows what she wants. Shall I write the telegram for you, Auntie?" 

She pronounced the syllables of the name with a peculiar clearness, as if she had tapped on two silver bells. It was a piercingly beautiful sound, yet it functioned as a dominant acoustic maneuver, slicing through the room to assert absolute control. The telegram itself represented the ultimate language death—stripping the rich, nuanced longing of subjective experience into sterile, utilitarian dots and dashes. 

"Jasper and the pantry-boy are out," Mrs. Lovell Mingott said.

May turned to her husband, her smile perfectly plated. "But here's Newland, ready to do anything. Will you take the telegram, Newland?" 

Archer murmured his readiness. May sat at the rosewood *Bonheur du Jour* and wrote the message in her large, immature hand. She blotted it neatly, the subtle power dynamics of her dominant lexicon fully engaged. 

"What a pity," she said, handing it to him, "that you and Ellen will cross each other on the way! Newland is obliged to go to Washington about a patent law-suit. I suppose Uncle Lovell will be back by tomorrow night, and with Granny improving, it doesn't seem right to ask Newland to give up an important engagement for the firm—does it?"

She paused, allowing the silence to corner him. Mrs. Welland hastily agreed. 

As Archer left the room, his mother-in-law muttered, "But why on earth she should make you telegraph for Ellen Olenska—" 

May's clear, bell-like voice chimed in response, finalizing her linguistic dominance: "Perhaps it's to urge on her again that after all her duty is with her husband." 

The heavy outer door closed behind him. Archer walked hastily away toward the telegraph office, stepping out of the suffocating domestic silence and back into the harsh, untranslatable roar of the industrial city.
