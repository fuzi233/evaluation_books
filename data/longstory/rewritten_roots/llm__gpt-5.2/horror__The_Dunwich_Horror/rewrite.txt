When a traveler in north-central Massachusetts takes the wrong fork at the junction of the Aylesbury pike just beyond Dean’s Corners, he does not at first know he has made an error. The road still looks like road—dusty, wheel-rutted, bordered with the same New England stonework that hems in so many pastures. Yet the land begins to lift under him in a slow, steady swell, and the brier-tangled walls draw in as if they mean to narrow the world to a single track. Even the air changes its temper. It carries, not the clean hay-sweet of open fields, but a wet, cellarish breath—like old root vegetables forgotten in a bin, like apples gone mealy in a loft where the rafters sweat.

The forest belts come more frequently, and the trees have a wrong abundance, as though they have been fed too long on shade and sour water. Their leaves do not merely rustle; they keep up a low, continuous shiver that the ear cannot quite sort into wind or creature. Under them the weeds, brambles, and grasses thrive with a luxuriance that does not feel like health, but like unchecked fermentation—rank green life swelling where careful hands have ceased to weed and mow. Planted fields are few, and those few look tired, skimmed to bone. You see stony slopes with scrubby corn that seems to have been planted out of stubborn habit rather than hope; patches where pumpkin vines creep thinly, their blossoms small and pale, as if even the bees have learned not to linger.

Here and there a house squats back from the road—gambrel roofs sagging, clapboards gray with lichen, windows filmed as if with kitchen grease and years. One catches, in a doorway or on a slanting, rock-strewn meadow, a solitary figure—gnarled, spare, and watchful. The traveler finds himself swallowing questions he would ask anywhere else. It is not only the looks—though there is in the faces a cast of long hunger and closed-in blood—but the way they occupy the sound around them. They do not call to one another. They do not whistle, sing, or even raise their voices across a yard. They stand and listen as if the land itself is speaking, and as if any human noise might interrupt something older and more urgent.

When the road rises enough to show the mountains over the deep woods, the uneasiness does not lift with the view; it thickens. The summits are too rounded, too even, with a symmetry that feels made rather than grown. On certain clear stretches the sky silhouettes, with a strange precision, the circles of tall stone pillars crowning most of the hills—upright blocks like crude teeth set around the domes. They are not the sort of stones a farmer would drag to a wall. They stand with the air of a recipe remembered imperfectly, as if someone once knew exactly what must be built on such a height—and built it, and never explained why.

The way is cut by gorges and ravines whose depths are hard to judge, because the trees lean in and the shadows pool thickly. The wooden bridges thrown over them are crude, with planks that look too thin and rails too loose, so that every crossing brings a faint shift in the belly, like stepping on a rotted kitchen stair. And then the road dips again into marshy reaches one instinctively mistrusts—flats where the grass grows in a matted shine and black water lies close under the surface. At evening these places become not merely disliked but feared, for the whole country turns audible in a fashion that is hard to bear.

The whippoorwills begin first, a quick, rasping insistence that seems to come from everywhere at once. Then the bullfrogs take up their blunt piping from the wet, each note like a thumb pressed into a bruise. Fireflies come out in abnormal profusion, glittering over the reeds in swarms so dense they seem less like insects than like embers shaken loose from some unseen hearth. The rhythms of it all—bird and frog, chirr and pipe—have a raucous steadiness that does not soothe the mind as honest night-sounds can. It drives at the nerves, prying, as if the marsh itself is tapping on the traveler’s skull with a steady spoon.

Through such places runs the thin, shining line of the Miskatonic’s upper reaches. By day it glints prettily enough, but it winds so close to the feet of the domed hills that it cannot help suggesting a serpent coiled among them. The nearer one draws, the more one watches the wooded sides rather than the stone-crowned tops. Those sides loom up dark and steep, crowding the road like walls. One wishes they would keep their distance, but there is no other way; the hills have arranged the passage, and the traveler must go where the land allows.

Then a covered bridge appears, squatting over the stream like a closed mouth. Through its shadowed opening one sees, beyond, a small village huddled tight between the water and the vertical slope of Round Mountain. The roofs—rotting gambrels clustered in a way that belongs to an earlier century—give an impression not of quaintness but of being left behind by time, like a pantry jar pushed to the back and forgotten until it swells and clouds.

Up close it is worse. Most of the houses are deserted and falling in upon themselves. The church steeple is broken, and the building has been turned—of all things—into the single mercantile establishment of the hamlet, slovenly in its display, with a few faded goods in the window and a smell that reaches even into the street: sour molasses, dusty flour, kerosene, and the indefinable staleness of food kept too long in a damp room. One dreads the bridge’s tenebrous tunnel, for it swallows sound; hoofbeat and wheel creak go in and come back wrong, muffled, as though the planks have absorbed too many crossings and remember them. Yet there is no way to avoid it.

Once across, the village street carries a faint, malign odor that no simple description can settle—mold and decay, yes, but with an edge like something animal and long uncleaned. It is as though centuries of cellar damp, smoke-dark rafters, spoiled pickling brine, and old blood have been breathed out and trapped among the houses. Even the quiet feels compounded. In other towns, quiet is a pause between honest noises—doors, talk, the clatter of pans, a dog’s bark. Here it is an attentive hush, and into that hush slip small sounds the ear would rather not catch: a far-off creak that might be a signboard or might be something heavier shifting; a brief, thin laugh cut short; the steady tick of insects, magnified because no human life competes with it.

It is always a relief to get clear of the place, to follow the narrow road around the base of the hills and across the more open country beyond until it rejoins the Aylesbury pike. Only afterward does one sometimes learn that one has been through Dunwich.

Outsiders visit Dunwich as seldom as possible. Since a certain season of horror, all the signboards pointing toward it have been taken down, and the roads have been left to look like any other wrong turn a man might make. Judged by ordinary standards, the scenery is more than commonly beautiful—domed hills, deep woods, a river that catches light like hammered metal—yet there is no influx of artists or summer tourists. The beauty has a way of refusing to be merely looked at; it presses on the senses, and especially on the ear, until one feels watched not by eyes but by listening.

Two centuries ago, when talk of witch-blood, Satan-worship, and strange forest presences was not laughed at, people gave reasons for avoiding the locality. In our sensible age—since the Dunwich horror of 1928 was hushed up by those who had the town’s and the world’s welfare at heart—people shun it without knowing exactly why. Perhaps one reason, though it cannot apply to uninformed strangers, is that the natives are now repellently decadent, having gone far along that path of retrogression so common in many New England backwaters. They have come to form a race by themselves, with the well-defined mental and physical stigmata of degeneracy and inbreeding. Their average intelligence is woefully low, and their annals reek of overt viciousness and of half-hidden murders, incests, and deeds of almost unnamable violence and perversity—deeds spoken of not in clean, shared language but in mutters, in phrases clipped short, in looks that do not translate.

The old gentry, representing the two or three armigerous families which came from Salem in 1692, have kept somewhat above the general level of decay; though many branches are sunk into the sordid populace so deeply that only their names remain as a key to the origin they disgrace. Some of the Whateleys and Bishops still send their eldest sons to Harvard and Miskatonic, though those sons seldom return to the moldering gambrel roofs under which they and their ancestors were born. Names endure longer than dialects, and in Dunwich even names have begun to taste strange in the mouth—flattened by a local speech that outsiders imitate badly and then stop imitating, because it seems less an accent than a closed door.

No one, even those who have the facts concerning the recent horror, can say just what is the matter with Dunwich; though old legends speak of unhallowed rites and conclaves of the Indians, amidst which they called forbidden shapes of shadow out of the great rounded hills, and made wild orgiastic prayers that were answered by loud crackings and rumblings from the ground below. The hills, in those stories, are not silent stone but a kind of buried drum. They reply. They echo. They utter, from beneath their soil and roots, answers that do not belong to any human tongue.

In 1747 the Reverend Abijah Hoadley, newly come to the Congregational Church at Dunwich Village, preached a memorable sermon on the close presence of Satan and his imps, in which he said:

It must be allow’d that these Blasphemies of an infernall Train of Dæmons are Matters of too common Knowledge to be deny’d; the cursed Voices of Azazel and Buzrael, of Beelzebub and Belial, being heard from under Ground by above a Score of credible Witnesses now living. I myself did not more than a Fortnight ago catch a very plain Discourse of evill Powers in the Hill behind my House; wherein there were a Rattling and Rolling, Groaning, Screeching, and Hissing, such as no Things of this Earth cou’d raise up, and which must needs have come from those Caves that only black Magick can discover, and only the Divell unlock.

Mr. Hoadley disappeared soon after delivering this sermon, but the text, printed in Springfield, is still extant—words preserved in ink long after the voice that spoke them was swallowed. Noises in the hills continued to be reported from year to year, and still form a puzzle to geologists and physiographers. Other traditions tell of foul odors near the hill-crowning circles of stone pillars—smells like carrion sweetened by summer heat, or like fat burned wrong and long, clinging to stone the way smoke clings to an old kitchen ceiling. Some speak of rushing airy presences to be heard faintly at certain hours from stated points at the bottom of the great ravines—a moving breath without body, a swift pressure on the ear, as if something passes too near to be seen but too large to be denied.

Still others try to explain the Devil’s Hop Yard—a bleak, blasted hillside where no tree, shrub, or grass-blade will grow. Farmers who pass it do not speak of soil chemistry; they speak of a place that will not take seed, that rejects life the way a mouth rejects bitter food. And then, too, the natives are mortally afraid of the numerous whippoorwills which grow vocal on warm nights. It is vowed that the birds are psychopomps lying in wait for the souls of the dying, and that they time their eerie cries in unison with the sufferer’s struggling breath. If they can catch the fleeing soul when it leaves the body, they instantly flutter away chittering in demoniac laughter; but if they fail, they subside gradually into a disappointed silence.

These tales, of course, are obsolete and ridiculous—because they come down from very old times. Yet Dunwich is indeed ridiculously old, older by far than any of the communities within thirty miles of it. South of the village one may still spy the cellar walls and chimney of the ancient Bishop house, built before 1700; whilst the ruins of the mill at the falls, built in 1806, form the most modern piece of architecture to be seen. Industry did not flourish here, and the nineteenth-century factory movement proved short-lived; its clamor, its metal din and scheduled whistles, never quite took hold in a country that already had its own harsh music after dark.

Oldest of all are the great rings of rough-hewn stone columns on the hilltops, more generally attributed to the Indians than to the settlers. Deposits of skulls and bones, found within these circles and around the sizable table-like rock on Sentinel Hill, sustain the popular belief that such spots were once the burial-places of the Pocumtucks; even though many ethnologists, disregarding the absurd improbability of such a theory, persist in believing the remains Caucasian. Here, as in so many matters touching Dunwich, even the learned cannot agree on what the stones are saying—because the language of place is not written in any lexicon, and because what the hills remember is not easily rendered into words spoken in clean rooms, far from the whippoorwills and the damp, sweet-sour breath of the marsh.

In Dunwich township, in a great weather-warped farmhouse that clung to a hillside four miles from the village and a mile and a half from any other roof-light, Wilbur Whateley came into the world at five o’clock on a Sunday morning—the second of February, 1913. Folks remembered it not because new life had entered an old house, but because it was Candlemas, which Dunwich keeps by a name older than the meetinghouse ever heard; and because, all through the night before, the hills kept making their peculiar sounds and every dog in the countryside set up a ceaseless, furious barking—as if the ridges themselves were speaking in a tongue that made an animal’s blood bristle.

Domestic things went on in their usual poor order. The stove would have been banked with last night’s ash and half-burnt hardwood, the iron kettle sweating a thin steam that smelled of old beans and sour tin, and whatever bread there was would have been yesterday’s, hard at the heel and streaked with flour that never quite turned to dough. Yet the air of that house could not hold only homely odors. The place had long been full of a damp, cellarish taint—the rot of worm-shot beams, the rankness of mould rising from dirt floors, and a faint ammonia from the barn that the wind pushed through broken panes. Even when you thought you caught something as innocent as smoke or milk, it was cut with that other savor—like a book kept too long in a peat bog.

Wilbur’s mother was Lavinia Whateley—one of the decadent Whateleys: a white, deformed, unattractive albino woman of thirty-five with pink eyes that watered in strong light. She lived there with her father, old Whateley—aged, half-insane, and marked in the memories of Dunwich by the ugliest whispers of wizardry from his youth. Lavinia had no known husband; but in that countryside, where names and kin were weighed by gossip as much as by the Bible, she made no denial of the child. People speculated freely about the other half of the ancestry, and Lavinia did nothing to stop them. If anything, she was oddly proud of the dark, goatish-looking infant—so shocking against her own sickly pallor—and she was heard muttering prophecies over him as if he were a loaf rising in a warm corner, sure to swell beyond the pan and split the crust.

She was a lone creature who wandered in thunderstorms, climbing where the wind had the clearest voice and the trees clacked like dry bones. She had the habit of trying to read the great odorous books her father had inherited through two centuries of Whateleys—books so old their leather had turned to a greasy bitterness and their pages broke at a touch, tunnelled through by worms. Lavinia had never been to school, but she carried in her head disjointed scraps of ancient lore old Whateley had fed her, as one might feed a child scraps of salted meat and hard cheese from the table—enough to keep life in, not enough to make wholesome growth.

The farmhouse had always been feared, and not only because of old Whateley’s name. When Lavinia was twelve, Mrs. Whateley had died violently in a manner that no one ever got a plain account of, and that kind of silence is a spice sharper than pepper in a town’s talk. Standards of cleanliness had long since fallen away from the place. The floorboards were filmed; the table was sticky; crockery sat with gray rings of old porridge dried to stone; and the very air seemed to have been cooked too long, until it thickened. Lavinia did not trouble herself with household cares, and the kitchen—if it could be called that—held none of the comforting order that a decent farmwife keeps: no bright crocks, no neat strings of herbs, no jars of summer fruit shining like stained glass in winter. It was a poor larder in a poor house, as if the family fed on something other than honest food.

On the night Wilbur was born there was a screaming that rose above even the hill noises and the dog-barking—a hideous, tearing sound that did not belong to childbirth as Dunwich understood it. No doctor came; no midwife’s practiced hands were there. The nearest neighbors lay in their beds listening and trying not to listen, the way people do when they fear the sound might choose them next.

A week afterward, old Whateley drove his sleigh through the snow into Dunwich Village. At Osborn’s general store—where men lounged with their boots close to the stove and the smell of molasses, kerosene, and wet wool hung in the rafters—he discoursed incoherently. There was a change in him: more furtive, as if his clouded brain had shifted him from being merely a thing to fear into a mind that meant to do the fearing back. He was not the kind to be disturbed by common family events; yet amid his babble there was a pride like a knife-edge, and what he said about the child’s paternity stayed in the listeners’ minds for years, returning like a refrain whenever the hills made their odd sounds.

“I dun’t keer what folks think—ef Lavinny’s boy looked like his pa, he wouldn’t look like nothin’ ye expeck. Ye needn’t think the only folks is the folks hereabouts. Lavinny’s read some, an’ has seed some things the most o’ ye only tell abaout. I calc’late her man is as good a husban’ as ye kin find this side of Aylesbury; an’ ef ye knowed as much abaout the hills as I dew, ye wouldn’t ast no better church weddin’ nor her’n. Let me tell ye suthin’—some day yew folks’ll hear a child o’ Lavinny’s a-callin’ its father’s name on the top o’ Sentinel Hill!”

Dunwich speech is a kind of inheritance, thick as gravy, and that day it carried more than meaning; it carried power. The men at Osborn’s could not have said why his words made their skin itch. Perhaps it was the way the old dialect strained around a name that wasn’t made for human mouths—like trying to say foreign spices with a tongue raised on salt pork. Perhaps it was the way the store’s familiar clatter—tin scoop in the cracker barrel, stove door creaking, boots scraping the planks—seemed suddenly too small and petty against whatever the old man was dragging in with him from the hills.

For the first month of his life, only two outsiders were known to have seen Wilbur: old Zechariah Whateley—of the undecayed Whateleys—and Mamie Bishop, Earl Sawyer’s common-law wife. Mamie went from pure curiosity, the way some people can’t pass a covered dish without lifting the lid. She returned with tales that did justice to her eyes and her relish for talk. Zechariah came for business, leading away a pair of Alderney cows old Whateley had bought of his son Curtis. That transaction began a long course of cattle-buying by Wilbur’s household, a steady bringing-in of livestock that did not end until 1928, when the Dunwich horror came and went. Yet the Whateley barn—ramshackle, leaning, black with rot—never seemed crowded. The hillside pasture above it held at most ten or twelve anemic, bloodless-looking beasts, grazing as if the grass were not grass but something tasteless they chewed out of habit.

People said it must be blight or distemper—sprung from unwholesome pasturage or from the diseased fungi and rotten timbers of that filthy barn. The cattle that were seen had odd wounds or sores with something of the look of incisions, as if cut cleanly rather than torn or gored. Once or twice, in the earlier months, certain callers fancied they could make out similar marks about the throats of the gray, unshaven old man and his slatternly, crinkly-haired albino daughter. In Dunwich, a sore is never only a sore; it is evidence, and evidence has a taste—metallic, like blood on the tongue when you bite too hard.

In the spring after Wilbur’s birth, Lavinia resumed her rambles in the hills, carrying the swarthy child in her misproportioned arms. Interest in the Whateleys subsided once most of the country folk had had a look at the baby. No one cared to speak too long about what was unpleasant, and besides, there were plantings to see to, butter to churn, smokehouses to mend. Yet the uncanny threaded itself through the ordinary, as it always does in places where the land is old and the speech older. While women scalded pans and men sharpened scythes, Wilbur grew.

His growth was phenomenal. Within three months he had the size and muscular power not usually found in infants under a full year. His motions—his very sounds—had a restraint and deliberateness that made onlookers uneasy, as if he were tasting the world rather than merely crying at it. When, at seven months, he began to walk unassisted, people were not truly surprised; and within another month he had lost the faltering. It was as if time fed him too richly, over-proofing him past what nature intended, like dough left too long where it’s warm.

Somewhat after that—on Hallowe’en—a great blaze was seen at midnight on the top of Sentinel Hill, where the old table-like stone stands amid its tumulus of ancient bones. The sight set talk going from kitchen to store to barn, carried by the same mouths that traded recipes and seed corn. Silas Bishop—of the undecayed Bishops—added fuel when he said he’d seen the boy running sturdily up the hill ahead of his mother about an hour before the blaze was noticed. Silas had been rounding up a stray heifer, lantern swinging, and he nearly forgot his errand when he caught that glimpse in the underbrush. They darted almost noiselessly, a kind of motion that had no right to be so quiet in leaf-litter and twig. He thought—though afterward he could not swear it—that they were unclothed. About the boy he wasn’t sure; maybe there’d been some kind of fringed belt, and a pair of dark blue trunks or trousers.

From then on, Wilbur was never seen alive and conscious without complete, tightly buttoned attire. The slightest disarrangement, or even the threat of it, filled him with anger and alarm. That was notable, because his mother and grandfather went as they pleased in their squalor, indifferent to rips and stains; but on Wilbur, clothes were not comfort or pride—they were necessity. It made people wonder, and in later years, when they had learned what came in 1928, they would remember the boy’s buttoned collars and stiff concealments and feel sick at how plain the answer had been.

The next January, gossips took mild interest in the fact that “Lavinny’s black brat” had begun to talk, and at only eleven months. His speech startled for more than its quickness. It was different from the ordinary accents of the region, and it lacked the infant lisping that even children of three or four can’t always shed. He did not chatter. But when he spoke, there was an elusive element that Dunwich and its denizens did not possess. It wasn’t in the words themselves, nor even in the simple idioms; it was in the sound—his intonation, the shape of breath and throat behind it, as if the organs that made the voice were set differently than a human’s ought to be. In a place where people judged one another by how they said corn, how they said creek, how they said home, that difference was a wound in the ear. Their native dialect—already a dwindling thing, already mocked by outsiders and softened by printed schoolbooks—met in Wilbur a cadence that did not belong to any living community, and could not be translated into neighborly sense no matter how close you leaned.

His face, too, looked oddly mature. He shared his mother’s and grandfather’s lack of chin; yet his firm, precociously shaped nose and the expression in his large, dark, almost Latin eyes gave him an air of quasi-adulthood and an intelligence that seemed more than precocious. Still, he was exceedingly ugly. There was something goatish in the thick lips, the large-pored yellowish skin, the coarse crinkly hair, and the oddly elongated ears. People disliked him more decidedly than they disliked Lavinia and old Whateley. All conjectures about the boy were seasoned with references to the bygone magic of old Whateley—how the hills once shook when he shrieked the dreadful name of Yog-Sothoth within a circle of stones, a great book open in his arms like a cook’s ledger of impossible ingredients.

And the dogs—creatures of pure hearing and honest appetite—abhorred Wilbur. Their barking was not the ordinary complaint at a stranger’s boots. It was a sustained, desperate alarm, a refusal of the ear and nose together. Wherever the boy went, the acoustic peace of farm life—the cluck of hens, the wind in dry corn, the distant knock of an axe—was broken by that harsh canine clamor, as if the countryside itself were trying to warn its people in the only voice it had left. Wilbur, for his part, learned early to take defensive measures against their barking menace, and Dunwich learned, just as early, to listen in spite of itself whenever the dogs began.

Old Whateley kept buying cattle, yet the herd behind the sagging rails never seemed to thicken the way any honest ledger would demand. Calves came in on the road with their soft, farm-sweet breath and the warm smell of dung and clover on their hides, and then—after a night or two of uneasy lowing—some part of that presence would be gone from Dunwich, as if the hill itself had learned to eat without leaving bones.

He did not spend his strength only on stock. He cut timber, too, and mended what had long been left to weather. The Whateley house was a peaked-roofed, roomy thing whose rear lay shoved into the rocky hillside as if the land had decided, once upon a time, to keep the family half-buried. For years, three least-ruined ground rooms had been enough for the old man and his pale daughter; the rest had stood abandoned, smelling of mouse-dust and old rot. Yet there was a hard, surprising vigor in him. Though he would lapse into that wandering, cracked babble now and again—words like broken crockery spilled over the tongue—his hammering and planing came with the steadiness of someone who measured twice and cut once.

It had begun, folks recalled, right around Wilbur’s birth. One of the scattered tool-sheds had been suddenly put in order: straightened, clapboarded tight, and given a stout, fresh lock that looked too new and too sure on such an old place. That shed stayed windowless and shut as a jam jar. Later, when he turned to the upper story of the house, he worked with the same thoroughness—new boards, driven nails, a careful squaring of joints. His mania showed itself only in one particular: every window in the reclaimed section was boarded up so tight not even the lace of daylight could leak through. Some said it was craziness even to bother reclaiming those rooms. But no one could deny the craft of it; the boards lay as close as pie-crust crimped to keep steam in.

Less baffling—at least on the face of it—was the downstairs room he fitted for his grandson. Callers saw that chamber, though none were ever admitted to the upper story now sealed off. The room was lined with tall, firm shelving, built with a carpenter’s pride, and on those shelves Old Whateley began to arrange, in what looked like a deliberate order, the rotting ancient books and fragments of books that had once been heaped like kindling in corners.

When he worked at them he used the rusty kitchen stove the way other men used a hearth for supper: not for warmth, but for the sticky utility of paste simmered from flour and water, stirred till it went glossy and ropey. The smell of it—yeasty, bland, faintly sour when it scorched—mixed with the usual Dunwich kitchen odors: old grease clinging to iron, damp wood, and the bitter edge of smoke that never quite left the beams. He would flatten a torn black-letter page with his big hands and dab along the rip with the paste, pressing it down carefully as if mending an heirloom tablecloth.

“I made some use o’ ’em,” he would say, eyes narrowed at the fragile paper, “but the boy’s fitten to make better use o’ ’em. He’d orter hev ’em as well sot as he kin, for they’re goin’ to be all of his larnin’.”

It was a peculiar domestic picture to the neighbors who came by for trade or talk: the old man doing book-repair as if it were jam-making, and the child—too large, too watchful—already hovering near those shelves like a fly drawn to a cut fruit.

In September of 1914, when Wilbur was a year and seven months old, his size and his ways turned from strange to alarming. He had grown as large as a child of four and talked with a fluency that did not match any Dunwich cradle. Words came too quick and too sure; he asked questions that sounded like they had been overheard rather than learned. He ran freely over fields and rocky hills and went with his mother on all her silent wanderings, his feet steady where grown folk watched for loose stone.

At home he pored over the queer pictures and charts in his grandfather’s books. Old Whateley would sit with him through long, hushed afternoons, instructing and catechizing as if drilling a minister’s son—only the subject matter was not fit for pulpit or school. Those afternoons had their own soundtrack: the soft rasp of pages, the tick of cooling stove iron, and beyond the house the constant small noises of the countryside—wind in scrub, a distant axe, a bird’s thin call—broken now and again by a deeper, muffled thump from somewhere above, as if the boards had learned to breathe.

By the time the house’s restoration was done, watchers noticed an oddity that made their mouths go dry. One of the upper windows had been changed into a solid plank door. It was set in the rear of the east gable end, close against the hill where no one had any business needing a door at all. And up to it Old Whateley had built a cleated wooden runway from the ground—planks nailed crosswise for grip, like a ramp made for hauling barrels, or driving unwilling livestock, or for something heavier than a man to clamber by night. It was the kind of practical carpentry any farmer might contrive, yet no practical explanation settled the mind.

Around the same time, the locked, windowless tool-house—so carefully kept shut since Wilbur’s birth—was abandoned again. The door swung listlessly open, as if whatever had demanded secrecy no longer required that particular shell. Earl Sawyer, stopping by after a cattle-selling call, once stepped inside out of curiosity. He came out pale, swallowing hard. He told anyone who’d listen that the smell in there was like nothing he’d met in all his life except near the old Indian circles on the hills—a stench not merely of rot, but of something that had never belonged to sane earth. Still, the folk of Dunwich could not claim spotless noses; their sheds and houses had always held a democracy of odors—man, animal, damp, smoke, old milk turned sharp in its crock. Yet this was different: it sat on the tongue with a metallic bitterness and in the back of the throat like the memory of lightning.

The months after that were quiet in visible ways, but not in the ways that matter when you live close to the ground and keep half your mind in your ears. People began swearing to a slow, steady increase in the hill noises. At first it was only at night—a far-off murmur, like thunder rehearsing under blankets, or like a great wagon drawn across stone. Then it came in daylight too, faint under the normal farm-sounds, the cluck and creak and call; an extra layer of sound that didn’t belong.

On May Eve of 1915 there were tremors strong enough that even Aylesbury folk felt them—cups trembling in cupboards, a shiver in the floor as if some buried animal had rolled over. At Hallowe’en the earth gave an underground rumbling queerly synchronized with bursts of flame from the summit of Sentinel Hill. “Them witch Whateleys’ doin’s,” people muttered, as if naming it could pen it in. The fire on the hilltop was a hard, hungry color against the dark, and the rumble below seemed to answer it like a drum in the chest.

Wilbur kept growing in his wrong way. Entering his fourth year he looked like a boy of ten. He read avidly by himself now, head bent close to the pages as if drinking from them, but he talked much less than before. A settled taciturnity took him, and for the first time the village began speaking plainly of the evil dawning in his goatish face. He would sometimes mutter an unfamiliar jargon, words that did not sit right in the mouth of anyone who overheard them. At times he chanted in bizarre rhythms, and the cadence alone—without any listener understanding the sense—raised gooseflesh, as if the sounds were tuned to old fears older than language.

Dogs had always disliked him, but now their aversion became a matter of wide remark. The beasts would bristle and snarl from porches when he passed, hackles high, eyes rolling white, as if they smelled something under his skin that no human could. It grew so bad he had to carry a pistol to traverse the countryside in safety. When he used it, the sharp report cracked across the valleys like a bad note in a hymn, and the sudden silence afterward sat heavy. The owners of canine guardians did not love him more for it.

The few callers who still came to the Whateley place often found Lavinia alone on the ground floor, moving quietly about the dim kitchen like a woman accustomed to making herself small. Sometimes there would be odd cries and footsteps resounding in the boarded-up second story—sounds that did not match any ordinary pacing, and that set the spoons trembling in their crock. Lavinia never told what her father and the boy were doing up there. Yet once, when a jocose fish-peddler tried the locked door leading to the stairway—laughing like it was a joke to pry at a neighbor’s secrets—her face drained white with a fear that was too sharp to be acted. The peddler later told the store loungers in Dunwich Village that he thought he heard a horse stamping on the floor above.

That set the loungers thinking, slow and grim. They thought of the gable-door and the runway. They thought of cattle that arrived and did not stay. And then, in that way old towns have of threading present unease through the eye of old story, they remembered tales of Old Whateley’s youth—and of strange things called out of the earth when a bullock is sacrificed at the proper time to certain heathen gods. In Dunwich, even supper talk could turn to sacrifice, because the ground under your table had always been older than your prayers.

It had also been noticed that dogs had begun to hate and fear the whole Whateley place as violently as they hated and feared young Wilbur alone. Passing near the lane, they would whine and strain at their leashes, or flatten their ears and try to bolt, as if the very boards and stones gave off a warning no human dialect could translate.

Then the larger world intruded. In 1917 the war came, and Squire Sawyer Whateley, chairman of the local draft board, had hard work scraping together a quota of young Dunwich men fit even for a development camp. Government officers and medical experts were sent up, alarmed at such signs of regional decadence, and they conducted a survey that New England newspaper readers would later recall with that thin fascination city folk reserve for rural “degeneracy.” The investigators brought their own sounds with them—motorcars grinding up the roads, brisk voices, boots on porches—industrial noise cutting across the old acoustic order of hill and farm, as if modernity’s racket could drown out whatever Dunwich had been listening to for generations.

It was the publicity of that investigation that set reporters on the track of the Whateleys. Soon the Boston Globe and the Arkham Advertiser printed flamboyant Sunday stories about young Wilbur’s precociousness, Old Whateley’s black magic, the shelves of strange books, the sealed second story of the ancient farmhouse, the weirdness of the whole region and its hill noises. The articles put names to what had been half-whispered in kitchens and store corners, and in doing so flattened it—turned lived dread into a commodity for readers who would consume it with their breakfast.

By then Wilbur was four and a half and looked like a lad of fifteen. A coarse dark down fuzzed his lip and cheek, and his voice had begun to break with a crack that sounded wrong in such a young throat. Earl Sawyer went out to the Whateley place with both sets of reporters and cameramen and pointed out the queer stench that now seemed to trickle down from the sealed upper spaces. It was, he said, exactly like the smell he’d found in the abandoned tool-shed, and like faint odors he sometimes thought he caught near the stone circles on the mountains—an odor that clung in the nose like rancid tallow and wet earth after an unseasonable thaw, yet with an undernote that suggested neither pantry nor barnyard.

Dunwich folk read the stories when they appeared and grinned at the obvious mistakes, the city-bred mishearings of names and customs, the way reporters tried to translate local life into the dominant lexicon of “queer,” “backward,” and “superstitious.” They wondered, too, why the writers made so much of one detail: that Old Whateley always paid for his cattle in gold pieces of extremely ancient date. Around here, money was money—so long as it weighed right in the hand. Still, there was something in the old coins that did not belong with the age of banknotes and factories: a cold, worn gleam passed across the counter like a relic from a tongue no longer spoken.

The Whateleys received their visitors with ill-concealed distaste, tight-lipped and watchful. Yet they did not dare court further publicity by violence or a refusal to talk. The cameras clicked; the men asked questions; the motorcars coughed on the lane. And all the while, above the ground floor where Lavinia stood mute among stove-smells and shadow, the boarded-up rooms kept their own silence—full, sealed, and listening—while Dunwich listened back, measuring each night by what the hills chose to say.

For ten years the Whateleys’ doings sank into the thick, sour stew of Dunwich routine—one more family to be endured the way you endured muddy springs and thin harvests and the creak of wagons on rutted lanes. Folks said they were queer, yes, but Dunwich had long ago learned to chew and swallow queerness without comment, the way it chewed its coarse brown bread and salt pork. Twice a year—May Eve and All-Hallow—fires would crown Sentinel Hill, and the old mountain would answer with its sullen rumblings, each season louder, nearer, as if some great, hungry stomach under the granite were beginning to wake and complain.

And always, at all other seasons, there were the little portents at the lonely farmhouse: the smell of hot blood on windless evenings; the clank of pails and the nervous lowing of cattle driven behind the barn; the way strangers swore they heard noises from the sealed upper story even when the whole family sat downstairs. Men argued at Osborn’s about how long a cow ought to bawl if it was slaughtered clean and quick, about how long it might take if it wasn’t. Somebody once spoke up about the Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals, but the words died in the air like a match pinched out too soon—Dunwich folk did not like outsiders listening to Dunwich sounds.

Around 1923, when Wilbur was ten—ten in years, but not in voice, not in stature, not in that coarse, shadowy beard that already rasped his jaw like burnt stubble—there came a second siege of carpentry at the old house. It was all in that sealed upper part, and the scraps of warped boards and laths thrown out told their own story to anyone who could read wood the way a farmer reads weather. Wilbur and old Whateley had knocked out partitions, and even taken up the attic floor, leaving above the ground story one vast hollow, an open void running all the way to the peaked roof. They tore down the great central chimney, too, and made do with a flimsy outside tin stove-pipe stuck like a cheap straw through the wall—so that whatever happened above would have no brick throat rising through it, no warm, sooty spine to divide and steady the structure.

After that, spring brought a new sound. Cold Spring Glen began to spill whippoorwills into the night as if the ravine were a cracked jug leaking living voices. They came in a growing number, and they did not keep to the woods; they gathered close under old Whateley’s window, and their calls threaded themselves through the house like needles. Old Whateley took it as no ordinary sign. He told the loungers at Osborn’s, in the thick Dunwich dialect that clung to his words like grease to a skillet, that he reckoned his time was near.

“They whistle jest in tune with my breathin’ naow,” he said, leaning forward as if he could hear his own lungs as clearly as he could hear the birds. “An’ I guess they’re gittin’ ready to ketch my soul. They know it’s a-goin’ aout, an’ dun’t calc’late to miss it. Yew’ll know, boys, arter I’m gone, whether they git me er not. Ef they dew, they’ll keep up a-singin’ an’ laffin’ till break o’ day. Ef they dun’t, they’ll kinder quiet daown like. I expeck them an’ the souls they hunts fer hev some pretty tough tussles sometimes.”

In Dunwich a man could talk about his soul the way he talked about seed corn—something precious, something you guarded, something others might steal if you were careless—and the men at Osborn’s listened with the same grim patience they gave to talk of blight. Yet what made their skin prickle wasn’t the meaning so much as the manner: the whippoorwills had changed the whole night’s hearing, turning the dark into a cage of sound.

On Lammas Night in 1924—harvest’s turning-point, when the fields had begun to give up their green sweetness for a harder smell of ripening and dust—Dr. Houghton of Aylesbury was summoned in haste. Wilbur Whateley lashed his one remaining horse through the darkness and telephoned from Osborn’s, his voice a heavy bass that did not fit a boy’s body. The doctor came reluctantly, wheels sucking at the road, the night air thick with hay-scent and something coppery beneath it that made him think of slaughterhouses.

He found Old Whateley in a grave state: the heart fluttering and stumbling, the breathing thick and stertorous, like a bellows clogged with damp ash. By the bed stood Lavinia—shapeless, albino, her pale hair hanging like wet corn silk—and Wilbur, already too tall and too still. Overhead, in that emptied upper abyss, there was a sound that made the doctor’s mouth go dry: not footsteps, not the crack of settling timbers, but a rhythmical surging, a lapping as of waves sliding up on some flat shore and slipping back again, steady and patient. It suggested a body too large for the space it occupied, a presence that moved by mass rather than by limb.

Yet even that was not what most disturbed Dr. Houghton. The whippoorwills outside were beyond any natural counting—an endless legion, crying their endless message, the notes repeating and repeating in a timing that seemed not merely coincidental but cunning. Each chorus fell in with the dying man’s wheezing gasps, so that the night itself appeared to breathe with him. Dunwich was always uncanny, but this was too exact, too intimate—as if the whole region were an ear pressed to a keyhole.

Toward one o’clock Old Whateley gained a measure of consciousness. He broke off his wheezing long enough to choke out words to his grandson, and the doctor, with his bag unopened and his hands cold, listened in spite of himself.

“More space, Willy, more space soon. Yew grows—an’ that grows faster. It’ll be ready to sarve ye soon, boy. Open up the gates to Yog-Sothoth with the long chant that ye’ll find on page seven fifty-one of the complete edition, an’ then put a match to the prison. Fire from airth can’t burn it nohaow!”

The sentence came out like a recipe spoken by a madman—directions, measurements, page numbers—only the ingredients were names and gates and a prison that could not be burned. The doctor thought, with a helpless flicker of reason, that the old man’s mind had finally broken under fever and age.

After a pause the whippoorwills altered their cadence to fit the changed tempo of Old Whateley’s breath, and from far off the hills gave a low, uneasy noise like distant kettledrums.

“Feed it reg’lar, Willy, an’ mind the quantity; but dun’t let it grow too fast fer the place, fer ef it busts quarters or gits aout afore ye opens to Yog-Sothoth, it’s all over an’ no use. Only them from beyont kin make it multiply an’ work…. Only them, the old uns as wants to come back….”

Then speech failed. There were only gasps again, each one answered by a precise, almost mocking iteration from the birds. Lavinia began to scream—not words, not prayers, but a thin, raw sound like steam escaping a cracked pipe. The scream seemed to excite the whippoorwills, or perhaps to measure itself against them; the whole night became a terrible sort of music, a chorus and counter-chorus timed to a dying man’s lungs.

It went on for more than an hour, until at last the final throaty rattle came. Dr. Houghton drew the shrunken lids over the glazing grey eyes, and at that instant—so subtly it might have been imagination—the tumult of birds faded and fell away, dimming to silence as if a hand had closed over their throats.

Lavinia sobbed with a child’s helplessness. Wilbur did not. He gave a low chuckle, and somewhere far off Sentinel Hill answered with a faint rumble, like an oven settling as the fire takes hold.

“They didn’t git him,” Wilbur muttered in his heavy voice, listening—not to the doctor, not to his mother, but to the night’s changed soundscape, as if silence itself had confirmed a calculation.

After Old Whateley’s death, Wilbur’s life widened outward in one direction only: knowledge. He became, in his narrow and unwholesome way, a scholar of tremendous erudition. By correspondence he was quietly known to librarians far away—keepers of locked cases and forbidden volumes whose pages smelled of dust, old leather, and secrets too rancid to air. His letters went out, and with them the old-time gold that had once flowed from his grandfather’s pocket: regularly, increasingly, always for cattle-buying. The herds on the hills changed hands, and beasts were led up the Whateley road with the bewildered patience of creatures born to be eaten.

Around Dunwich he was more and more hated and dreaded, for there were youthful disappearances that people spoke of with their mouths half closed, the way you speak when you don’t want your words to travel. Suspicion lay at Wilbur’s door like a dog that wouldn’t leave, but inquiry was silenced—sometimes by fear of what might be found, sometimes by the knowledge that Dunwich did not survive by inviting scrutiny. Wilbur’s body itself seemed to defy the ordinary measures: his height reached what should have been the adult limit, yet did not stop. When, in 1925, a scholarly correspondent from Miskatonic University called upon him and departed pale and puzzled, Wilbur was fully six and three-quarters feet tall, and gave the impression of something still in the act of rising.

As the years pressed on, Wilbur treated his half-deformed albino mother with a contempt that hardened like cooled fat. He began by speaking to her as if she were a hindrance, ended by forbidding her to go with him to the hills on May Eve and Hallowmass. There are households where the table is the last place of tenderness—where even if there is little, the sharing makes kin of hunger. The Whateley house had no such warmth. Whatever meals were cooked there were never spoken of as meals—only as quantities, as feed, as things brought in and used up.

In 1926 Lavinia confessed her fear to Mamie Bishop in a voice that seemed always to be trying, and failing, to find the right words.

“They’s more abaout him as I knows than I kin tell ye, Mamie,” she said. “An’ naowadays they’s more nor what I know myself. I vaow afur Gawd, I dun’t know what he wants nor what he’s a-tryin’ to dew.”

That Hallowe’en the hill noises sounded louder than ever, and the fire on Sentinel Hill burned as usual; but Dunwich paid less heed to the flame than to the air’s uproar. Great flocks of whippoorwills—unnaturally belated, gathered where they had no business being—seemed assembled near the unlighted Whateley farmhouse. Their cries stitched the darkness with a relentless, rasping rhythm, and after midnight their shrill notes burst into a kind of cachinnation, a laughter that did not belong to any human throat. It filled the countryside, drowning the lesser sounds—wind in corn, frogs in marsh, the familiar night-speech that usually soothed a farmer’s mind by telling him the world was in its proper order.

Not until dawn did the birds quiet down. Then they vanished all at once, hurrying southward where they were fully a month overdue, leaving behind a silence that rang in the ears like the sudden stopping of machinery.

What it meant, no one could say at once. None of the countryfolk seemed to have died. But when daylight spread across the Whateley place, and days followed, and Lavinia did not show herself, the meaning began to thicken into certainty. The twisted albino was never seen again.

In the summer of 1927 Wilbur repaired two sheds in the farmyard and began moving his books and effects out to them. The activity had the ordinary look of farm improvement—boards nailed, hinges set, roofs tarred—yet Dunwich watched from a distance the way you watch smoke whose source you can’t find. Soon afterward Earl Sawyer told the loungers at Osborn’s that more carpentry was going on inside the Whateley farmhouse. Wilbur was closing all the doors and windows on the ground floor, and seemed to be taking out partitions as he and his grandfather had done upstairs four years before.

He lived in one of the sheds then, apart from the main house, and Sawyer thought he looked unusually worried and tremulous—as if he listened not for men’s footsteps but for some pressure and motion within the walls. People suspected him more than ever of knowing what had become of his mother, yet very few approached his neighborhood now. His height had increased to more than seven feet, and still it showed no sign of ceasing—as though the house, the hill, the season, and the whole uneasy chorus of Dunwich nights had not finished measuring him for whatever space would be needed next.

That winter, as the fields lay iron-hard and the Miskatonic ran with a thin skin of ice that crackled like old parchment when the wind worried it, there came an event no less strange than Wilbur Whateley’s first venture beyond the Dunwich hills. He had written—clumsy, blotched scrawls in an uncouth hand that seemed to grind against the very rules of spelling—to places whose names were as far away as legends: the Widener Library at Harvard, the Bibliothèque Nationale in Paris, the British Museum, the University of Buenos Aires, and, nearer than any of them and therefore most intolerable to be denied, the Library of Miskatonic University at Arkham. From each came the same polite refusal, the same tidy language of regulations and custody, as bloodless as boiled starch. A man who had spent his life in a valley where one barters eggs for nails and learns history by what grandmothers mutter over bread dough does not take kindly to a “policy.”

So at last he went in person.

He arrived in Arkham like some hungry winter rumor given legs—shabby, dirty, bearded, and raw with dialect; almost eight feet of dark, goatish grotesquerie, with a cheap new valise from Osborn’s general store banging at his knee. Even in a town that had known river fogs thick enough to muffle church bells, he did not belong. The street-sounds rose and fell around him with an urban clangor—iron rims on cobbles, a distant motor’s bark, the thin impatient bleat of a trolley bell—noises that do not soothe the mind the way a barn’s quiet does, with its slow chew of cattle and the hush of stored hay. Yet he paid them no heed. He had never seen a city before, but he moved through it with one purpose, as if dragged by a string tied to the locked cabinets of the college library.

At the university grounds he passed a great white-fanged watchdog that barked with unnatural fury and enmity. The chain snapped taut; the dog tugged and yelped as though it had scented something that did not belong to any ordinary stable, slaughterhouse, or street. Wilbur strode by without so much as a flinch, and the sound of that yelping pursued him like a curse into the quiet corridors where footsteps are softened by stone and habit.

He carried with him what was priceless to him—a copy of Dr. Dee’s English version bequeathed by his grandfather, precious and imperfect, damaged as though some earlier hand had worried certain pages the way rats worry a sack of grain. He had come because that book lacked what he needed. There was, he knew, a passage that would have fallen on the seven hundred and fifty-first page of his defective volume, and he could not be made to accept the notion that an absence on paper must be accepted as an absence in the world.

The dread volume he sought was kept under lock and key at Miskatonic: the hideous *Necronomicon* of the mad Arab Alhazred in Olaus Wormius’ Latin version, printed in Spain in the seventeenth century—an old thing, old as pickled fruit forgotten at the back of a cellar shelf, old as smoke ingrained into rafters. When access was granted under watchful procedure, Wilbur sat down at a table with the stance of a man at a workbench. There was no reverence in him, only appetite and method.

The librarian who received him was Henry Armitage—erudite, careful, the sort of mind that labels and preserves and believes that knowledge, like food, must be handled cleanly lest it poison. He had once called at the Whateley farm and had not forgotten the smell of that place: not manure, not sour milk, not the wholesome funk of pigs, but something else—something that clung to the throat and made breathing feel like swallowing.

Now, as he watched Wilbur open the Dee copy and lay it beside the Latin tome, Armitage plied him with questions as politeness requires, and because he feared what silence might allow. Wilbur, for all his ugliness and his odd, resonant speech that hinted at sound-making organs not arranged like other men’s, could not refrain from telling the truth of his purpose. He was hunting a formula—an incantation—containing the name *Yog-Sothoth*. He spoke of discrepancies, duplications, ambiguities; of how the wording shifted the way a recipe turns treacherous when a single herb is misnamed or a measure misread. It puzzled him, he admitted, to find the matter of determination far from easy.

He began to collate. The pages rustled—dry, crisp, like the flake of aged pepper-dried skin—and he copied with fierce concentration, as if transcribing not words but a method, a set of instructions that must be exact to the grain. In the library’s stillness the scratching of his pen sounded too loud, a tiny industrial rasp that seemed to scrape the air. Outside, a wind moved along the eaves and through bare branches with a thin, keening voice, and those two soundscapes—ink and winter—layered themselves uneasily together.

When Wilbur finally chose a formula and wrote it down, Dr. Armitage looked involuntarily over his shoulder at the open pages. The left-hand page, in Latin, bore matter that translated itself into the mind like rot revealing itself when you lift a lid.

Nor is it to be thought—ran the meaning as Armitage’s learning gave it voice—that man is either the oldest or the last of earth’s masters, or that the common bulk of life and substance walks alone. The Old Ones were, the Old Ones are, and the Old Ones shall be. Not in the spaces we know, but between them. They walk serene and primal, undimensioned and to us unseen. *Yog-Sothoth* knows the gate. *Yog-Sothoth* is the gate. *Yog-Sothoth* is the key and guardian of the gate. Past, present, future, all are one in *Yog-Sothoth*. He knows where the Old Ones broke through of old, and where They shall break through again.

The text went on, and with each line the library’s safe domestic order—the well-oiled ritual of stamped cards, shelved folios, whispered requests—seemed less solid. It spoke of earth’s fields being trod by Things unseen, and of men knowing Them not by sight but by smell. That detail struck Armitage with a physical force, for smell is an argument the body makes without asking the brain’s permission. In winter, in kitchens, smells are comfort: broth bones simmering until they give up their sweetness, onions browning in fat, yeast breathing into dough. But there are other odors—stale, animal, corrupt—whose very presence feels like a violation.

By Their smell can men sometimes know Them near, the passage implied, though of Their semblance no man can know—saving only in the features of those They have begotten on mankind. Many sorts, it said, from likeness near man’s truest image to a shape without sight or substance. It spoke of lonely places where Words had been spoken and Rites howled through at their Seasons; of wind that gibbers with Their voices and earth that mutters with Their consciousness. It spoke, too, of forest bent and city crushed by an unseen hand; of Kadath in cold waste, of sunken isles, of sealed towers garlanded with seaweed and barnacles; of Great Cthulhu as Their cousin who can spy Them only dimly; and then the obscene cry—*Iä Shub-Niggurath!*—like a name that should never have been fitted to human tongues.

And at last: As a foulness shall ye know Them. Their hand is at your throats, yet ye see Them not; and Their habitation is even one with your guarded threshold. *Yog-Sothoth* is the key to the gate, whereby the spheres meet. Man rules now where They ruled once; They shall soon rule where man rules now. After summer is winter, and after winter summer. They wait patient and potent, for here shall They reign again.

It was the seasonal phrasing—the circular inevitability of it—that made Armitage’s unease deepen into something like dread. After winter summer; after summer winter. Such words belonged to farmers and elders, to the old people who can read a year in the taste of water and the sound of wind in the pines. Here, that homely cycle was yoked to an alien patience.

Armitage associated what he read with what he had heard of Dunwich and its brooding presences, and of Wilbur Whateley’s dim hideous aura that stretched from dubious birth to a cloud of probable matricide. A wave of fright came over him as tangible as a draft of tomb-cold damp. The bent, goatish giant at the table seemed less like an unwashed rustic and more like an emissary—spawn of another planet or dimension, only partly of mankind, linked to black gulfs of entity that stretched beyond the neat compartments of force and matter, space and time.

Presently Wilbur raised his head. When he spoke, his voice carried a resonance that did not sit properly in a human chest; it seemed to thrum, as though the air itself were being used like an instrument.

“Mr. Armitage,” he said, “I calc’late I’ve got to take that book home. They’s things in it I’ve got to try under sarten conditions that I can’t git here, an’ it ’ud be a mortal sin to let a red-tape rule hold me up. Let me take it along, sir, an’ I’ll swar they wun’t nobody know the difference. I dun’t need to tell ye I’ll take good keer of it. It wa’n’t me that put this Dee copy in the shape it is….”

He stopped when he saw denial harden on the librarian’s face. In Wilbur’s features—those goatish planes and slants—something crafty tightened, the way a butcher’s hand might tighten when a bargain fails. Between Lammas and the equinox of 1928—when the first apples begin to sweeten in the bins and the last of the field corn hardens with its paper-dry sigh—the thing men later called the Dunwich horror pushed its shadow into Arkham’s ordinary hours. Dr. Henry Armitage remembered that season not by dates on a college calendar, but by the sounds and smells that stitched the days together: the morning clatter of milk bottles on steps, the dusk-crickets behind hedges, the steady whisper of pages in Widener and Orne, and, underneath it all, the creeping modern racket of motor traffic that never truly rested.

He had been expecting disturbance ever since word reached him of Wilbur Whateley’s grotesque pilgrimage to Cambridge—how the lad, all raw impatience and feverish courtesy, had begged to borrow or copy from the _Necronomicon_ at Widener. That attempt had failed. Armitage had already sent warnings, sharp as a cleaver and twice as cold, to every librarian who had charge of that book. He could picture Wilbur as described: shockingly nervous, eyes darting as if a timer were ticking somewhere only he could hear; hungry for the volume, yet almost equally frantic to be gone, as though absence from his hill-town kitchen—his own hearth, his own dark pantry—might spoil something left unattended.

Early in August, the half-foreseen result ripened.

In the small hours of the third, Armitage came awake as if jerked by a hook sunk behind the ribs. Across the street and lawn, on the college grounds, the watchdog’s voice tore the night open—no mere alarm-bark, but wild, savage, half-mad cries that rose and broke with hideously meaningful pauses. The animal’s growls had a wet, throat-deep timbre like a kettle boiling over, then stopping, then boiling again—each stop worse than the sound.

And then another scream answered it, from a throat not shaped for any human vowel. It was a sound that had no room in the world’s common lexicon—nothing Arkham’s tidy tongues were meant to name. It roused half the sleepers in town, and afterward it nested in their dreams as stubbornly as a refrain from a cheap popular song, except that no amount of daylight could shake it loose. It was the sort of cry you could not translate into any remembered pain; your mind tried, failed, and then—out of self-defense—went empty.

Armitage dragged on clothing and ran out, the night air tasting of damp stone and cut grass. Others were already ahead of him, dark figures converging, drawn by that scream the way a whole household is drawn toward the crash of a dropped dish. From the library came the thin, metallic shrill of the burglar alarm, a sound too modern and too trivial for what had birthed it. In the moonlight an upper window stood open—black, gaping, like a mouth that had been taught to bite.

Whatever had come had finished coming. The barking and that inhuman screaming, now thinning into a mixed low growling and moaning, rose unmistakably from within.

Some instinct—older than scholarship, older than all printed caution—told Armitage that what was happening was not a thing for unfortified eyes. He used his authority like a bar across a door, pushing the crowd back while he unlocked the vestibule. In the press he saw Professor Warren Rice and Dr. Francis Morgan—men who had heard fragments of his conjectures, enough to recognize dread when it stood upright. He motioned them in.

Inside, the building held the deadened hush that follows a kitchen’s last flame: the loud work has stopped, and now everything waits to cool. Only a watchful, droning whine from the dog continued—like a grinder left running, refusing to wind down. Yet before they reached the inner hall, another sound braided itself through the silence: a loud chorus of whippoorwills in the shrubbery had begun a damnably rhythmical piping, precise as a chant beaten out with spoons on a table. It was as if the birds kept time with the last breath of a dying man, marking each pull of air with an eager note.

Then the smell struck them.

It was a stench Armitage knew too well from old accounts and older, forbidden pages: not mere decay, but a thick, sick-sweet reek like spoiled fat rendered too long, mixed with something mineral and sharp, the way a rusted knife smells when it has been used on meat. It filled the corridors, clung to the back of the tongue, and seemed almost to have weight.

They hurried across the hall to the small genealogical reading-room whence the whine came. For a second no one dared touch the light. The dark felt protective; illumination felt like trespass. Then Armitage, forcing breath into lungs that did not wish to cooperate, snapped the switch.

One of them—no one ever agreed which—shrieked aloud.

Among disordered tables and overturned chairs, sprawled half-bent on its side in a fetid pool of greenish-yellow ichor and tarry stickiness, lay a thing almost nine feet tall. The dog had torn off all the clothing and some of the skin, and the ripped cloth lay about like rags after a kitchen fire. Shoe-leather fragments, bits of apparel, and near the open window an empty canvas sack—dropped, flung, forgotten—spoke of an intrusion planned and then broken mid-act.

Near the central desk a revolver had fallen. Later they would find the cartridge dented but undischarged, explaining why no shot had sounded. At the time that small human detail—metal, mechanism, intention—barely registered. The thing on the floor crowded out all other thoughts the way a rank odor crowds out delicate flavors.

It was not quite dead. It twitched silently and spasmodically, its chest heaving in monstrous unison with the whippoorwills’ mad piping outside, as though the birds were pumping it like bellows. To say no human pen could describe it would be trite and not wholly accurate; a pen could list features, but any mind trained only on the planet’s familiar shapes and the three known dimensions could not truly assemble them into a stable image.

There was, beyond doubt, something partly human: the hands were manlike, the head had a recognizably human architecture, and the goatish, chinless face bore the stamp of the Whateleys as surely as a family recipe bears the hand of a particular grandmother—an inherited twist that no outsider imitates correctly. But below that, the body was teratologically fabulous; only generous clothing could ever have allowed it to walk on earth without challenge, without immediate pursuit and erasure.

Above the waist it was semi-anthropomorphic, though the chest—where the dog’s rending paws still rested with tense, proprietary watchfulness—had a leathery, reticulated hide like crocodile or alligator, the pattern catching the light as if it had been stitched from an armored skin. The back was piebald with yellow and black, dimly suggesting the squamous covering of certain snakes. It looked less like a creature than like a catalogue of textures no cook would ever wish to touch: slick scale, tough hide, and the clotted, unpleasant glue of its own leaking fluids.

Below the waist was worse, because that was where resemblance ended and sheer fantasy began. The skin there was thickly covered with coarse black fur, and from the abdomen protruded a score of long greenish-gray tentacles with red sucking mouths, now limp and slack as ropes left soaking. Their arrangement was wrong—not random, not symmetrical in any earthly way, but following the cold balance of some cosmic geometry that made the eye feel cheated, as if the room had acquired an extra corner.

On each hip, deep set in a kind of pinkish, ciliated orbit, was what seemed to be a rudimentary eye—wet, useless-looking, and yet horribly suggestive. In lieu of a tail there depended a trunk or feeler with purple annular markings, and with many evidences of being an undeveloped mouth or throat. The limbs, except for their black fur, roughly resembled the hind legs of prehistoric earth’s giant saurians, terminating in ridgy-veined pads that were neither hooves nor claws. It was as if some butcher, drunk on blasphemy, had tried to portion life from mismatched carcasses and then forgotten the basic rules of anatomy.

When it breathed, its tail and tentacles rhythmically changed color, as if from some circulatory cause natural to the non-human side of its ancestry. In the tentacles the greenish tinge deepened with each pull of air; in the tail a yellowish cast alternated with a sickly grayish-white in the spaces between the purple rings. Of genuine blood there was none—only the fetid greenish-yellow ichor, trickling along the painted floor beyond the thickest radius of stickiness, leaving behind it a curious discoloration like a stain that no scrubbing would ever lift.

As the presence of the three men seemed to rouse it, the dying thing began to mumble without turning or lifting its head. The sounds were not English. At first the syllables defied correlation with any known speech; they were all throat and scrape and hollowed breath, like words spoken through a broken flute. Toward the last, however, there came disjointed fragments evidently taken from the _Necronomicon_—the monstrous blasphemy for which it had risked Cambridge and perished.

Armitage made no written record of the mouthings, yet he later asserted, with the grim certainty of a man who knows what he heard no matter what language tries to deny it, that they ran something like:

“N’gai, n’gha’ghaa, bugg-shoggog, y’hah; Yog-Sothoth, Yog-Sothoth….”

They trailed off into nothingness as the whippoorwills outside shrieked in rhythmical crescendoes of unholy anticipation. Then the gasping halted. The dog raised its head and loosed a long, lugubrious howl, the sort of sound that makes a town’s night feel suddenly older than its houses.

A change came over the yellow, goatish face. The great black eyes fell in appallingly, as if the sockets were losing their architecture. At the same instant the whippoorwills’ piping ceased—cut off so cleanly it felt like a hand had clapped over a mouth. Above the murmurs of the gathering crowd came a panic-struck whirring and fluttering; against the moon, vast clouds of feathery watchers rose and raced from sight, frantic at the loss of what they had sought for prey.

All at once the dog started up abruptly, gave a frightened bark, and leaped nervously out through the window by which it had entered.

A cry rose from the crowd outside. Armitage shouted, sharp and peremptory, that no one must be admitted until the police or the medical examiner came. He was thankful the windows were just too high for idle peering, and he drew the dark curtains carefully down over each one, shutting out moonlight as if it were a contaminant.

Two policemen arrived by then, their boots sounding too loud in the vestibule, their professional composure already fraying at the edges from the smell leaking through the halls. Dr. Morgan met them and—urging them for their own sakes—pressed for a delay until the examiner came and the prostrate thing could be covered. The air itself seemed to argue against haste; it pressed on the throat, and the old building’s usual paper-and-wood fragrance had been thoroughly overpowered.

Meanwhile, on the floor, frightful changes began.

It is not necessary to describe the kind and rate of shrinkage and disintegration that took place before Armitage and Rice, except to say that the reality of it made human categories—flesh, bone, corpse—feel like provincial terms. The thing diminished, slackened, flowed inward upon itself. Features that had seemed definite moments before lost their boundaries, as though the form had been held together only by intention and was now forgetting to be solid. Watching it was like watching a dish collapse when the binding has failed, except that no kitchen had ever produced such a substance, and no appetite—human or otherwise—should have been able to imagine it.

By the time the medical examiner came, there was only a sticky whitish mass on the painted boards, and even the monstrous odor had nearly vanished, as though the air itself were relieved to be let go. Aside from the external appearance of face and hands—those brief, misleading marks of kinship—it was plain that the really human elements in Wilbur Whateley must have been very small.

Apparently he had had no skull or bony skeleton—at least not in any true or stable sense.

He had taken, in the end, somewhat after his unknown father.

All that had happened before—Wilbur’s death in Arkham, the scholar’s astonishment, the frantic locking-away of books—was only a clearing of the throat before the true Dunwich horror began to speak.

The bewildered officials did what officials always do when the world turns inside out: they performed their formalities, and they kept the abnormal details from paper and pulpit as if secrecy were a lid strong enough to hold down any boiling pot. Men were sent out toward Dunwich and Aylesbury to search out property and notify any who might be heirs of Wilbur Whateley, and they came back with the same story in different voices—no voice steady. The whole upper Miskatonic valley seemed to be listening, not with ears but with floorboards and root-hairs, to the low rumblings that came and went under the domed hills like a great stomach working on an indigestible meal.

Dunwich itself was in agitation for reasons that had nothing to do with wills and courts. From the great boarded-up shell that had been Whateley’s farmhouse there rose, day by day, a stench that did not belong to any honest rot of pigpen or compost heap. It was not the sweet-sour breath of silage, nor the clean iron reek of slaughter. It was like thunder made tangible—like hot stone after lightning, mixed with something slimy and old, and a note of salt as if a sea had been poured where no sea should be. Along with it came sounds: a surging and lapping, as though some vast thing, sleepless and hungry, shifted its weight against the boards and the nailed seams. Earl Sawyer, who had tended the horses and cattle during Wilbur’s absence, took on a nervousness that sharpened with each night. The acoustic world of the farm—usually a comfort of known creaks, known lowings, the settled talk of animals—had been altered. Now every familiar sound was overlaid by another rhythm, one that did not match the timbers, the wind, or the season.

The men from Aylesbury and the county—men who would ordinarily stride through any farmhouse kitchen without asking, sniffing at pantry jars and counting chairs as though domestic life were a ledger—devised excuses not to enter that noisome boarded place. They were glad to confine their survey to the deceased’s living quarters and the newly mended sheds, and even that single visit they made as quickly as men can move when they tell themselves they are being sensible.

A ponderous report was filed at the court-house in Aylesbury, and litigations concerning heirship began their slow, dry chewing among the innumerable Whateleys—decayed and undecayed—scattered like old seeds up and down the valley. Among Wilbur’s effects they found an almost interminable manuscript in strange characters, written in a huge ledger and judged a sort of diary because of the spacing and the variations in ink and penmanship. It lay on the old bureau that had served as its owner’s desk, as if the act of writing had been as daily and domestic as peeling apples at the table. Yet the marks would not yield to any known tongue. After a week of debate it was sent to Miskatonic University with the deceased’s collection of strange books for study and possible translation; but even the best linguists soon saw it would not be unriddled with ease. The symbols seemed to mock the very idea of a shared lexicon, as if whatever Wilbur had written belonged to a language that did not wish to be carried into human mouths. No trace was found of the ancient gold with which Wilbur and old Whateley had always paid their debts—as though it, too, had gone back into whatever country minted such coin.

It was in the dark of September ninth that the horror broke loose.

That evening the hill noises were pronounced—long, deep mutterings that made the kettles faintly ring on their hooks and set the crockery to whisper against itself. Dogs barked frantically all night, not the quick yelp at fox or stray, but a frantic, worn-out chorus that rose and fell like a siren and then dropped into whines. Early risers on the tenth noticed a peculiar stench in the air, and noticed too how sound carried oddly, as though the valley had been scooped into a different shape. The distant calls of a rooster came thin and wrong, and the wind in the pines seemed to hold its breath.

About seven o’clock Luther Brown, hired boy at George Corey’s between Cold Spring Glen and the village, rushed frenziedly back from his morning trip to Ten-Acre Meadow with the cows. He stumbled into the kitchen so hard his boots tracked mud across the hearthside—mud with a strange oily sheen in it—while outside the herd pawed and lowed pitifully, pressing close to the yard as if the fence were a wall against a storm.

Mrs. Corey had been turning cornmeal into johnnycake batter, the bowl warm in her hands, the smell of sour milk and hot fat already rising from the skillet. When Luther burst in, the domestic sounds—the spoon against crock, the soft spit of lard—seemed suddenly brittle.

“Up thar in the rud beyont the glen, Mis’ Corey—they’s suthin’ ben thar!” Luther gulped, as if each word scraped his throat. “It smells like thunder, an’ all the bushes an’ little trees is pushed back from the rud like they’d a haouse ben moved along of it. An’ that ain’t the wust, nuther. They’s prints in the rud, Mis’ Corey—great raound prints as big as barrel-heads, all sunk daown deep like a elephant had ben along, only they’s a sight more nor four feet could make. I looked at one or two afore I run, an’ I see every one was covered with lines spreadin’ aout from one place, like as if big palm-leaf fans—twict or three times as big as any they is—hed of ben paounded daown into the rud. An’ the smell was awful, like what it is araound Wizard Whateley’s ol’ haouse....”

He faltered there and shivered afresh, as if the name itself carried a draught of cold. Mrs. Corey tried to coax more out of him, but the boy’s mouth was full of fear the way a pan can be too full of boiling syrup to take a spoon. So she did the only thing she could do with her hands still smelling of meal and grease: she went to the telephone and began to ring the neighbors, sending panic along the party line as easily as a recipe.

When she got Sally Sawyer—housekeeper at Seth Bishop’s, the nearest place to Whateley’s—Mrs. Corey found it her turn to listen instead of transmit. Sally’s boy Chauncey, who slept poorly, had been up on the hill toward Whateley’s and had dashed back after one look at the place and at the pasturage where Mr. Bishop’s cows had been left out all night.

“Yes, Mis’ Corey,” came Sally’s tremulous voice over the wire, thin and crackling, “Cha’ncey he just come back a-post-in’, and couldn’t haff talk fer bein’ scairt! He says Ol’ Whateley’s haouse is all blowed up, with the timbers scattered raound like they’d ben dynamite inside; only the bottom floor ain’t through, but is all covered with a kind o’ tarlike stuff that smells awful an’ drips daown offen the aidges onto the graoun’ whar the side timbers is blowed away. An’ they’s awful kinder marks in the yard, tew—great raound marks bigger raound than a hogshead, an’ all sticky with stuff like is on the blowed-up haouse. Cha’ncey he says they leads off into the medders, whar a great swath wider’n a barn is matted daown, an’ all the stun walls tumbled every which way wherever it goes.

“An’ he says, says he, Mis’ Corey, as haow he sot to look fer Seth’s caows, frighted ez he was; an’ faound ’em in the upper pasture nigh the Devil’s Hop Yard in an awful shape. Haff on ’em’s clean gone, an’ nigh haff o’ them that’s left is sucked most dry o’ blood, with sores on ’em like they’s ben on Whateley’s cattle ever senct Lavinny’s black brat was born. Seth he’s gone aout naow to look at ’em, though I’ll vaow he wun’t keer ter git very nigh Wizard Whateley’s! Cha’ncey didn’t look keerful ter see whar the big matted-daown swath led arter it leff the pasturage, but he says he thinks it p’inted towards the glen rud to the village.

“I tell ye, Mis’ Corey, they’s suthin’ abroad as hadn’t orter be abroad, an’ I fer one think that black Wilbur Whateley, as come to the bad eend he desarved, is at the bottom of the breedin’ of it. He wa’n’t all human hisself, I allus says to everybody; an’ I think he an’ Ol’ Whateley must a raised suthin’ in that there nailed-up haouse as ain’t even so human as he was. They’s allus ben unseen things araound Dunwich—livin’ things—as ain’t human an’ ain’t good fer human folks.

“The graoun’ was a’talkin’ lass night, an’ towards mornin’ Cha’ncey he heerd the whippoorwills so laoud in Col’ Spring Glen he couldn’t sleep none. Then he thought he heerd another faintlike saound over towards Wizard Whateley’s—a kinder rippin’ or tearin’ o’ wood, like some big box or crate was bein’ opened fur off. What with this an’ that, he didn’t git to sleep at all till sunup, an’ no sooner was he up this mornin’, but he’s got to go over to Whateley’s an’ see what’s the matter. He see enough, I tell ye, Mis’ Corey! This dun’t mean no good, an’ I think as all the men-folks ought to git up a party an’ do suthin’. I know suthin’ awful’s abaout, an’ feel my time is nigh, though only Gawd knows jest what it is.

“Did your Luther take accaount o’ whar them big tracks led tew? No? Wal, Mis’ Corey, ef they was on the glen rud this side o’ the glen, an’ ain’t got to your haouse yet, I calc’late they must go into the glen itself. They would do that. I allus says Col’ Spring Glen ain’t no healthy nor decent place. The whippoorwills an’ fireflies there never did act like they was creaters o’ Gawd, an’ they’s them as says ye kin hear strange things a-rushin’ an’ a-talkin’ in the air daown thar ef ye stand in the right place, atween the rock falls an’ Bear’s Den.”

By noon fully three-quarters of the men and boys of Dunwich were moving over roads and meadows between the new-made Whateley ruins and Cold Spring Glen. They went as they would go to a butchering or a barn-raising—out of habit, out of grim neighborliness—yet with faces set as if each step must be negotiated with an unseen will. They examined in horror the vast monstrous prints; the maimed Bishop cattle with their hideous dry wounds that looked less like biting than like being emptied; the strange wreck of the farmhouse with its boards flung wide; and the bruised, matted vegetation along fields and road-sides.

That tarry stickiness was everywhere near the ruin, hanging in ropes from splintered beams, glossing the earth as if someone had poured hot pitch and let it cool. It had an odor that clung to the nostrils, insinuating itself behind any other smell—behind manure, behind smoke, behind the faint sweetness of late-season apples. Even those who had come from kitchens still carrying the memory of bread crust and coffee found that this new scent chased all appetites away, leaving only a cold saliva and the sick, fainting knowledge of the body’s vulnerability.

Whatever had burst loose upon the world had assuredly gone down into the great sinister ravine. Along the banks, trees were bent and broken, and a great avenue had been gouged in the underbrush that clung to the precipice. It was as though a house, launched by an avalanche, had slid down through tangled growths on an almost vertical slope. From below came no sound—no splash, no growl, no bellow—only a distant, undefinable fetor that rose as if the glen exhaled. Men stood on the edge and argued, glad to keep their boots on firm ground rather than descend and beard the unknown Cyclopean thing in its lair. Three dogs with the party barked furiously at first, then fell cowed and reluctant, tails low, as they neared the lip; even their hearing, keen as it was, seemed to pick up something in the silence that frightened them.

Someone telephoned the news to the Aylesbury Transcript, but the editor—used to Dunwich and its tales, used to translating strange country fear into a city chuckle—made only a humorous paragraph. The Associated Press repeated the joke as if ridicule were a charm.

That night everyone went home and barricaded house and barn as stoutly as possible. No cattle were left in open pasturage. The ordinary nightly sounds of farm life—buckets set down, doors latched, the last sleepy cluck from a hen—were performed with exaggerated care, as though any stray clatter might draw attention. And in the gaps between those human-made noises, the valley seemed to listen back.

About two in the morning a frightful stench and the savage barking of dogs awakened the household at Elmer Frye’s, on the eastern edge of Cold Spring Glen. All agreed they could hear, somewhere outside, a muffled swishing or lapping sound—wet, heavy, patient—like some immense body shifting in mud, or a tide nosing at a shore that had never known tides. Mrs. Frye proposed telephoning the neighbors. Elmer was about to agree when the sound of splintering wood burst in upon their deliberations. It came, apparently, from the barn, and was followed by hideous screaming and stamping among the cattle.

The dogs did not charge. They slavered and crouched close to the feet of the family, whining, pressed to human ankles as if the old domestic hierarchy had been reversed and men were now the shelter. Frye lit a lantern through force of habit—because hands, when frightened, reach for ritual—but he knew it would be death to step into the black yard with that small, bobbing circle of light. The children and women whimpered, yet did not scream. Some buried instinct of defense—older than speech, older than prayer—told them their lives depended on silence.

At last the cattle-noise subsided to a pitiful moaning. Then came a great snapping, crashing, crackling—as if timbers were being wrenched and chewed, or as if some titanic weight shifted and the world’s joints complained. The Fryes, huddled in the sitting-room, did not dare move until the last echoes died away far down in Cold Spring Glen, swallowed by that ravine which seemed to drink sound and give back only whippoorwill cries.

When it was over, amidst the dismal moans from the stable and the demoniac piping of late whippoorwills in the glen, Selina Frye tottered to the telephone and spread what news she could of the second phase of the horror. Her words, crackling along the wire, entered other kitchens where doughs were rising and beans simmering, and turned those homely scents suddenly thin and unreal.

The next day the countryside was in panic, and cowed, uncommunicative groups came and went where the fiendish thing had occurred. Two titan swaths of destruction stretched from the glen to the Frye farmyard; monstrous prints covered bare patches of ground. One side of the old red barn had completely caved in. Of the cattle only about a quarter could be found and identified. Some were in curious fragments, as if they had been pulled apart without any care for joints or bone; and all that survived had to be shot, their familiar warmth and usefulness ended in a mercy that tasted of iron and smoke.

Earl Sawyer suggested help be asked from Aylesbury or Arkham, but others maintained it would be of no use. Old Zebulon Whateley—of a branch hovering between soundness and decadence—made darkly wild suggestions about rites that ought to be practised on hilltops. Tradition ran strong in his line, and his memories of chantings in the great stone circles were not altogether connected with Wilbur and his grandfather. He spoke in words that belonged to older mouths, and there were sounds in his speech—turned vowels and half-swallowed consonants—that felt like a dialect trying to hold together a crumbling cultural memory. Yet even his inherited talk could not quite name what had happened. The thing in the glen did not seem to fit inside any human vocabulary, however ancient.

Darkness fell on a stricken countryside too passive to organize for real defense. In a few cases closely related families banded together under one roof and watched in the gloom, muskets loaded, pitchforks handy, listening to the night’s acoustic ecology as if they could judge safety by the pattern of crickets and wind. But in general there was only barricading again, and the futile gesture of weapons meant for wolves and men.

Nothing occurred that night except hill noises; and when day came, many hoped the new horror had gone as swiftly as it had come. There were even bold souls who proposed an offensive expedition down in the glen—said it with mouths that tasted of morning coffee and bravado—but none ventured to set an example, and the majority remained reluctant.

When night came again, the barricading was repeated, though there was less huddling of families. In the morning both the Frye and Seth Bishop households reported excitement among the dogs and vague sounds and stenches from afar. Early explorers noted with horror a fresh set of monstrous tracks in the road skirting Sentinel Hill. As before, the road-sides showed bruising that suggested a bulk so stupendous it blasphemed against the scale of barns and boulders. The conformation of the tracks argued a passage in two directions, as if the moving mountain had come from Cold Spring Glen and returned to it along the same path.

At the base of the hill a thirty-foot swath of crushed shrubbery and saplings led steeply upward, and the seekers gasped when they saw that even perpendicular places did not deflect the trail. Whatever the horror was, it could scale a sheer stony cliff of almost complete verticality. By safer routes the men climbed around to the summit, and there they saw the trail ended—or rather, reversed. It was here the Whateleys used to build their hellish fires and chant their hellish rituals by the table-like stone on May Eve and Hallowmass. Now that very stone formed the center of a vast space thrashed around by the mountainous horror, and on its slightly concave surface lay a thick fetid deposit of the same tarry stickiness seen on the floor of the ruined farmhouse when the thing escaped.

Men looked at one another and muttered, then looked down the hill. Apparently the horror had descended by a route much like its ascent. To speculate was futile. Reason, logic, and normal ideas of motivation stood confounded, as though the human mind were a small kitchen knife set to carve a shape too hard and too slick to hold. Only old Zebulon—absent from the group—might have done justice to it or offered an explanation that fitted his half-remembered chants. Yet even he would have been naming shadows with the wrong names, translating the untranslatable into human syllables for comfort.

Thursday night began much like the others, but ended less happily.

The whippoorwills in the glen screamed with such unusual persistence that many could not sleep. Their calls did not sit in the night as they should; they became a continuous tearing, a drilling noise that made people press pillows to their ears and still hear it through cloth and bone. About three in the morning all the party telephones rang tremulously as if the line itself shook. Those who took down their receivers heard a fright-mad voice shriek, “Help, oh, my Gawd!…” and some thought a crashing sound followed the breaking off of the exclamation. Then there was nothing. The silence afterward was worse than the scream, because it made room for imagination and for that faint, far-off lapping.

No one dared do anything. No one knew till morning whence the call came. At daylight those who had heard it called everyone on the line and found only the Fryes did not reply.

The truth appeared an hour later, when a hastily assembled group of armed men trudged out to the Frye place at the head of the glen. It was horrible, yet hardly a surprise. There were more swaths and monstrous prints in the churned earth, but there was no longer any house. It had caved in like an egg-shell, and among the ruins nothing living or dead could be discovered—only that stench and that tarry stickiness, glistening on broken boards where a family’s table had stood, where bread had been sliced and milk poured and children fed in the old way.

The Elmer Fryes had been erased from Dunwich, as cleanly and wordlessly as if some vast, hungry thing had licked the place bare and left behind only a smell that no human language, however old, could ever rightly name.

Behind a closed door in Arkham, while the town’s ordinary sounds went on—streetcar bells thinning toward evening, a milk wagon’s rims rasping over cobbles, the quiet domestic percussion of students returning books—another kind of hunger worked in a shelf-lined room at Miskatonic University.

It began as a scholar’s commonplace task: a manuscript left behind by Wilbur Whateley, delivered with the other effects taken from his quarters. The university’s language men had worried it for days with the dogged patience of people who have spent whole lives tasting the world through alphabets. The symbols looked, at first glance, like a darkly inked Arabic out of some Mesopotamian margin—thick strokes, heavy hooks, a slant as if the hand that wrote it had been taught by lamplight and secrecy. Yet it was nothing any of them could name. Their dictionaries—Latin and Greek, Coptic and Syriac, the neat lattices of Hebrew points, the braided curves of Persian—gave no purchase. The letters sat on the page with the stubbornness of an herb whose scent you recognize but cannot place: familiar in effect, alien in source.

At last the linguists agreed on the only conclusion their pride could bear. The alphabet was artificial—made to look like something old, and used as a cipher. But when they tried the usual keys and substitutions, when they counted frequencies as if weighing out flour by feel, and when they tested every tongue Whateley might have used, the thing refused to yield. Even the other books taken from his room—grim volumes that made the younger men’s voices drop unconsciously, and that made the older men handle pages as if they were cold meat—offered no help. One heavy tome with an iron clasp bore yet another unknown alphabet, this one more like Sanskrit in its posture, as though the letters had been taught to sit cross-legged.

In the end they carried the ledger to Dr. Henry Armitage.

It was not only that Armitage had taken a peculiar interest in the whole Whateley business, or that his learning ranged from the respectable philologies to the formulas that clung like smoke to the edges of medieval thought. It was that he had the temperament to listen to what a text refused to say. He suspected the symbols might be the private hand of forbidden cults—fragments passed down from Saracenic wizardry and older, surviving like a family sourdough kept alive in a crock through migrations and wars. Yet he did not linger over the question of origin. If the alphabet were a cipher, one did not need to know where the signs were born—only what they had been trained to conceal. And he believed that for all its posturing, the diary would not have been written in some foreign speech throughout. There would be too much labor in that. A man—if Wilbur Whateley could be called that—would keep to his own language for bulk, saving other tongues for special incantations, for the phrases that must be said exactly as they had been handed down.

So Armitage began with an assumption as homely as it was daring: most of it was English.

He did not make the mistake of trying anything simple. The repeated failures of his colleagues told him the lock was deep. Through late August he fortified himself as a cook does before a long service—laying out every tool, sharpening each edge, arranging the ingredients of method. Night after night, while Arkham’s noises softened and the campus trees stirred in a wind that made a careful, leaf-by-leaf rustle, he waded through the lore of cryptography: Trithemius’ *Poligraphia*, Porta’s *De Furtivis Literarum Notis*, Vigenère’s *Traité des Chiffres*, Falconer’s *Cryptomenysis Patefacta*, Davys and Thicknesse, and more modern authorities like Blair, von Marten, and Klüber’s *Kryptographik*. The older writers, with their blunt confidence in correspondences and hidden grids, seemed closer to the texture of what lay before him.

He alternated between those books and the manuscript itself until the shape of the problem became unmistakable. It was one of the subtlest contrivances: not a single substitution, but many—tables like a multiplication chart, letters shifting their values by key-words known only to the initiated. An antique code, Armitage decided; something handed down through a long line of experimenters, each generation adding a twist, each keeper insisting the starter be fed and not allowed to die.

Several times he thought he saw daylight—certain clusters yielding, like a crust cracking to show steam—only to be thrown back by some unforeseen obstacle. Then, as September approached, the clouds began to lift. Certain signs, used in certain places, emerged with a definiteness that could not be argued away. He could taste English underneath the ink, as surely as one knows salt beneath a sauce.

On the evening of September second the last major barrier gave way. Armitage read, for the first time, a continuous passage from Wilbur Whateley’s annals.

It was indeed a diary. And its style—at once clogged with occult erudition and broken by a raw illiteracy—made Armitage’s stomach tighten the way it does when a familiar dish is seasoned with something faintly wrong, something that belongs to another kitchen entirely.

One of the first long passages he deciphered carried a date: November 26, 1916. It chilled him for reasons beyond the words, because he could not forget who had written it—a child of three and a half who looked like a boy of twelve or thirteen, a thing that had grown too fast, as if fed on improper fare.

The entry read:

Today learned the Aklo for the Sabaoth, which did not like, it being answerable from the hill and not from the air. That upstairs more ahead of me than I had thought it would be, and is not like to have much earth brain. Shot Elam Hutchins’s collie Jack when he went to bite me, and Elam says he would kill me if he dast. I guess he won’t. Grandfather kept me saying the Dho formula last night, and I think I saw the inner city at the 2 magnetic poles. I shall go to those poles when the earth is cleared off, if I can’t break through with the Dho-Hna formula when I commit it. They from the air told me at Sabbat that it will be years before I can clear off the earth, and I guess Grandfather will be dead then, so I shall have to learn all the angles of the planes and all the formulas between the Yr and the Nhhngr. They from outside will help, but they can not take body without human blood. That upstairs looks it will have the right cast. I can see it a little when I make the Yoorish sign or blow the power of Ibn Ghazi at it, and it is near like them at May Eve on the Hill. The other face may wear off some. I wonder how I shall look when the earth is cleared and there are no earth beings on it. He that came with the Aklo Sabaoth said I may be transfigured, there being much of outside to work on.

When morning came, Armitage found himself in a cold sweat—a terror that was not theatrical but physical, as if his body had understood before his mind finished translating. The electric light in his office had burned down the night into a thin, constant glare. He had not left his table. He turned page after page with hands that shook as though he had been fasting too long, as though the very act of reading were draining him.

He telephoned his wife with a voice that sounded strange even to him and told her he would not be home. When she brought him breakfast—a tray with the domestic reassurance of bread and coffee—the aroma rose warm and human, and it should have anchored him. Instead it turned his stomach. He managed only a few bites, as if any ordinary nourishment had become difficult in the face of what the diary described: blood as ingredient, bodies as vessels, the earth itself as something to be “cleared off” like scraps from a board.

All that day he read. Now and then he halted, maddened, to reapply the complex key. Lunch and dinner were brought, and again he ate almost nothing. The campus sounds shifted around him—students’ voices, the hollow slam of a door, the distant hum of machinery that even in 1928 had begun to seep into every hour, a modern drone that flattened the subtle gradations of quiet. In those lulls he heard, more clearly than he wished, how silence itself in that office seemed thinner, as if the manuscript had taught the room a new kind of listening.

Toward the middle of the next night he drowsed in his chair and woke snarled in nightmares almost as hideous as the truths he was uncovering. On the morning of September fourth Professor Rice and Dr. Morgan insisted on seeing him. He let them in for a while. When they left, they were trembling and ashen, as if they had leaned too close to a furnace.

That evening he went to bed but slept only in broken strips. Wednesday he was back at the manuscript and began taking copious notes—extracts and formulas, fragments he had already deciphered and passages he was tearing open now. In the small hours he slept a little in an easy-chair, waking before dawn to the feeling that time itself had become an enemy.

Before noon his physician, Dr. Hartwell, called and insisted he stop. Armitage refused. The refusal was not stubbornness for its own sake; it was the desperate insistence of a man who has heard a faint, wrong sound in the walls and knows that if he does not find its source, the house will not remain a house.

By twilight that day he finished the perusal. He sank back with the emptiness of someone who has swallowed something too large and cannot bring it up again. His wife came with dinner, and when her eyes wandered, innocently, toward the notes scattered on his desk, he cried out sharply—less as a rebuke than as a reflex, as though the notes were a pan left too hot for anyone else to touch.

With a weakness that frightened him, he gathered the scribbled pages, sealed them into a great envelope, and shoved it into his inside coat pocket—close to his body, where the heart’s beating might keep it from straying. He had enough strength to get home, but he looked so ill that Hartwell was summoned at once. As the doctor put him to bed, Armitage could only mutter, over and over, as if he were kneading one phrase into coherence:

“But what, in God’s name, can we do?”

He slept, but the next day he drifted in and out of delirium. He offered Hartwell no explanation. In his calmer moments he spoke only of the need for a long conference with Rice and Morgan. In his wilder wanderings he cried frantic appeals that something in a boarded-up farmhouse be destroyed; he babbled of a plan to extirpate the entire human race and all animal and vegetable life—scraping the earth clean, like a platter after a feast, only the feast was us.

He shouted that the world was in danger because Elder Things wished to strip it and drag it away from the solar system and the cosmos of matter into another plane—a phase of being from which it had once fallen, vigintillions of eons ago. At other times he called for the dreaded *Necronomicon* and Remigius’ *Dæmonolatreia*, clinging to the hope that in those worm-eaten pages there might be a counter-formula, some bitter spice capable of cutting rancidity.

“Stop them, stop them!” he would cry. “Those Whateleys meant to let them in, and the worst of all is left! Tell Rice and Morgan we must do something—it’s a blind business, but I know how to make the powder…. It hasn’t been fed since the second of August, when Wilbur came here to his death, and at that rate….”

Yet Armitage’s physique was sound despite his seventy-three years. He slept off the disorder without fever. Late Friday he woke clear-headed—sober, hollowed, and filled with a gnawing fear that had the weight of responsibility rather than the blur of illness.

Saturday afternoon he felt able to return to the library and summon Rice and Morgan. The three shut themselves in and spent the rest of that day and evening in speculation so wild and debate so desperate that it seemed, afterward, to Armitage that their voices had changed the room’s acoustics. Strange books came down in stacks from the shelves and out of secure storage; old leather breathed its dust and sourness, and the air took on that dry, papery smell like a cupboard of spices long neglected. Diagrams and formulas were copied with feverish haste.

Of skepticism there was none. All three had seen Wilbur Whateley’s body on the floor of this very building, and after that the diary could not be dismissed as mere ravings. They argued, briefly, about notifying the Massachusetts State Police, and decided against it. The things involved could not be believed by those who had not seen a sample—something that later investigations would make painfully clear.

Near midnight the conference broke up without a definite plan. Sunday Armitage spent comparing formulas and mixing chemicals obtained from the college laboratory. He moved among bottles and powders with a grim care, like someone preparing food that must be exact because it is meant not to please but to preserve—medicine, antidote, purgative. He measured, ground, combined, and watched for reactions. The more he reflected on the hellish diary, the more he doubted that any material agent could stamp out what Wilbur Whateley had left behind—the earth-threatening entity that was even then, unknown to him, near its hour.

Monday repeated Sunday. The task demanded an infinity of research and experiment. Each return to the diary brought some change of plan, some adjustment like a recipe rewritten mid-course because the ingredients are not what you thought. Even if he succeeded, uncertainty would remain; he knew that with the dread clarity of someone hearing a distant rumble in the ground and understanding that maps are only paper.

By Tuesday he had a line of action mapped out and believed he would try a trip to Dunwich within a week.

Then, on Wednesday, shock arrived in a form so trivial it was obscene.

Tucked obscurely away in a corner of the *Arkham Advertiser* was a facetious Associated Press item, written as if for laughs, telling what a record-breaking monster the bootleg whisky of Dunwich had raised up. The very phrasing—modern, commercial, smirking—made Armitage’s teeth clench. Here was the machinery of mass language flattening terror into a joke, packaging the unimaginable into a neat little paragraph for idle breakfast tables, letting the real thing slip past under the cover of a punchline.

Half stunned, he could only telephone for Rice and Morgan.

They talked far into the night, the three voices moving over the telephone wire and then later around a table like nervous knives. The next day was a whirlwind of preparation. Armitage knew he would be meddling with terrible powers—handling, as it were, the most poisonous herbs in existence with bare hands because the pantry had been left open by fools. Yet he also saw there was no other way to annul the deeper, more malign meddling others had performed before him. The household routines of Arkham went on, the city’s sounds went on, the newspaper presses went on—and somewhere beyond, in Dunwich, something that had not been fed since August second was beginning to make its own kind of music.

Friday morning, while Arkham’s chimneys were still drawing steady and the town kitchens were clinking through the plain business of breakfast, Dr. Armitage set out with Rice and Morgan by motor for Dunwich. The road carried them from familiar noises—the measured ring of a streetcar bell, a distant factory’s flat, unmusical hammering—into a country where the hills swallowed sound rather than made it. Even the tires seemed to hush themselves on the last miles, as if the earth here demanded a softer tread.

They reached the village about one in the afternoon. Sunlight lay bright on the oddly domed heights and on the deep ravines cut like old knife wounds through the green, yet the day’s fairness could not cleanse the air. Dread hung over the place the way rancid grease hangs about a neglected hearth—seen nowhere, but everywhere smelled and felt. Now and then, against the sky on some mountain top, they caught the bleak outline of a stone circle, gaunt as a broken ring of teeth.

At Osborn’s store the men’s voices had the brittle carefulness of people who had lately heard a sound they could not fit into any known measure. The talk came in tight bursts between long listens, as if everyone expected the valley itself to answer. From that hush of fear Armitage understood at once that something had happened; and soon the word took shape—Elmer Frye’s house and family wiped out as if by a hand that did not recognize a house as anything sturdier than paper.

All that afternoon the three rode and walked about Dunwich, questioning whoever would speak, and forcing themselves to look at what every native’s eyes slid away from. They saw the Frye ruins—drear, crushed, and smeared with a tarry stickiness that clung to boards and stones the way half-rendered fat clings to a pan when it is left to burn down. The yard held blasphemous tracks, too large and too deep, and there were cattle at Seth Bishop’s place wounded as if by blind, heavy flailing. In several spots the vegetation lay disturbed in enormous swaths, matted down as though some vast body had dragged itself across pasture and thicket without caring what it broke.

To Armitage the trail up and down Sentinel Hill had the weight of a catastrophe already written into the land. He stared long at the altarlike stone on the summit, a thing that looked less like a farmer’s fieldstone and more like a table set for a guest no human pantry could serve.

Toward evening they sought the State Police party said to have come from Aylesbury that morning after the first telephone reports. It should have been simple enough to find five officers and their car. Yet no sign of the men appeared anywhere. The automobile stood empty near the Frye yard, its presence as unnatural as an untended stove left cold in a house where someone had been cooking only minutes ago.

The natives, who had all spoken with the policemen earlier, stared blankly at one another. Then old Sam Hutchins went a sick gray, nudged Fred Farr, and jabbed a trembling finger toward the dank hollow close by—the deep mouth of Cold Spring Glen. His voice broke like a cracked crock.

“Gawd,” he gasped, “I telled ’em not ter go daown into the glen. I never thought nobody’d dew it with them tracks an’ that smell an’ the whippoorwills a-screechin’ daown thar in the dark o’ noonday….”

At that, a cold shudder ran through visitors and villagers alike. Every head turned—not to look, but to listen. In Dunwich even the soundscape had become testimony. The whippoorwills had been loud in the wrong hours, and there were subterranean mutterings under the hills like distant wagons on hidden roads, except there were no roads down there. The valley’s hush between those noises felt staged, like a pause before the knife falls.

Armitage trembled—not with mere fear, but with the heavy responsibility of having brought knowledge into a place where knowledge could not be tasted and shared as a meal might be, but only swallowed like a bitter medicine. Night would soon fall. It was at night, the accounts said, that the mountainous blasphemy lumbered upon its eldritch course. The phrase he had taken from older pages returned to him with a sick certainty—_Negotium perambulans in tenebris…_—and it did not translate cleanly into anything that would soothe the men around him. It sat in his mind like a word from a dead dialect: exact, pointed, and uselessly unshareable.

He rehearsed the formulæ he had memorized and gripped the paper containing the alternative ones he dared not trust to memory alone. He tested his electric flashlight, hearing in its small click a fragile human sound against the valley’s waiting silence. Rice opened a valise and brought out a metal sprayer of the kind used against insects—an ordinary tool, the sort a careful man might keep for orchards and pantry moths, now enlisted for something no orchard had ever suffered. Morgan uncased the big-game rifle he trusted in spite of Armitage’s warnings that no mere material weapon would be much help.

Armitage had read the hideous diary. He knew too well what kind of manifestation to expect, and he did not heighten the Dunwich people’s terror by naming it aloud. He hoped—foolishly, perhaps—that the thing might be conquered without forcing a wider world to mouth the monstrous syllables it implied. Some words, once spoken, begin to belong to whoever speaks them; and he would not give Dunwich any more vocabulary for its nightmare.

As shadows thickened, the villagers dispersed toward their homes to bar themselves in, though every face showed that locks and bolts felt like children’s toys now—thin, polite gestures against a force that bent trees and crushed houses when it chose. They shook their heads at the three visitors’ plan to stand guard by the Frye ruins near the glen. As they went, their farewells were the kind people give when they do not expect to meet again.

That night the hills rumbled. The whippoorwills piped with a sharpness that seemed less like birdsong than a warning whistle blown by something inhuman that knew the paths. Once in a while a wind swept up out of Cold Spring Glen and brought with it that ineffable fetor—an odor not merely foul, but wrong, like meat left in a sealed crock through an entire summer, then opened in a room where someone had once baked bread. It was the same smell all three had known once before, standing above a dying thing that had passed for fifteen and a half years as a human being.

Yet the looked-for terror did not appear. Whatever lay in the glen was biding its time. Armitage told his companions that to go down into that hollow in the dark would be suicide. They held their ground, listening to the valley’s uneasy orchestra—wind, whippoorwills, and those subterranean, unplaceable tremors—until a wan morning bled in and the night-sounds ceased with an abruptness that felt like a mouth snapping shut.

The day came gray and bleak, with drizzle now and then, and heavier clouds piling up beyond the northwestern hills as if stacked by a deliberate hand. Seeking shelter beneath one of the few Frye outbuildings still standing, the Arkham men debated: wait for clearer conditions, or go down into the glen and hunt what had no name. The rain thickened, and far away thunder spoke in slow, heavy syllables. Sheet lightning shimmered; then a forked bolt cracked down near at hand—so near it seemed to plunge into the accursed glen itself. The sky darkened to a bruised, unnatural shade, and they hoped the storm would spend itself quickly and leave clean air behind.

It was still gruesomely dark when, a little more than an hour later, a confused babel rose from the road—shouts, sobs, and the raw, animal noises of men who had seen their stout explanations fail. A frightened group of more than a dozen came running. The lead man’s words tangled in his throat until they broke free in a shape the Arkham men could understand.

“Oh, my Gawd, my Gawd!” he choked. “It’s a-goin’ agin, _an’ this time by day_! It’s aout—it’s aout an’ a-movin’ this very minute, an’ only the Lord knows when it’ll be on us all!”

He could not go on, and another man carried the message, the way in farm kitchens one voice takes up a recipe when another falters.

“Nigh on a haour ago Zeb Whateley here heerd the ’phone a-ringin’, an’ it was Mis’ Corey, George’s wife that lives daown by the junction. She says the hired boy Luther was aout drivin’ in the caows from the storm arter the big bolt, when he see all the trees a-bendin’ at the maouth o’ the glen—opposite side ter this—an’ smelt the same awful smell like he smelt when he faound the big tracks las’ Monday mornin’.

“An’ she says he says they was a swishin’, lappin’ saound, more nor what the bendin’ trees an’ bushes could make; an’ all on a suddent the trees along the rud begun ter git pushed one side, an’ they was a awful stompin’ an’ splashin’ in the mud. But mind ye, Luther he didn’t see nothin’ at all—only jest the bendin’ trees an’ underbrush.

“Then fur ahead where Bishop’s Brook goes under the rud he heerd a awful creakin’ an’ strainin’ on the bridge, an’ says he could tell the saound o’ wood a-startin’ to crack an’ split. An’ all the whiles he never see a thing, only them trees an’ bushes a-bendin’.

“An’ when the swishin’ saound got very fur off—on the rud towards Wizard Whateley’s an’ Sentinel Hill—Luther he had the guts ter step up whar he’d heerd it fust an’ look at the graound. It was all mud an’ water, an’ the sky was dark, an’ the rain was wipin’ aout all tracks abaout as fast as could be; but beginnin’ at the glen maouth, whar the trees hed moved, they was still some o’ them awful prints big as bar’ls like he seen Monday.”

Before he could draw breath, the first speaker cut in, trembling with the need to push the worst part forward—as if saying it quickly might lessen it.

“But _that_ ain’t the trouble naow—that was only the start. Zeb here was callin’ folks up an’ everybody was a-listenin’ in when a call from Seth Bishop’s cut in. His haousekeeper Sally was carryin’ on fit ter kill—she’d jest seed the trees a-bendin’ beside the rud, an’ says they was a kind o’ mushy saound, like a elephant puffin’ an’ treadin’, a-headin’ fer the haouse. Then she up an’ spoke suddent of a fearful smell, an’ says her boy Cha’ncey was a-screamin’ as haow it was jest like what he smelt up to the Whateley rewins Monday mornin’. An’ the dogs was all barkin’ an’ whinin’ awful.

“An’ then she let aout a turrible yell, an’ says the shed daown the rud hed jest caved in like the storm hed blowed it over, only the wind wa’n’t strong enough to dew that. Everybody was a-listenin’, an’ ye could hear lots o’ folks on the wire a-gaspin’.

“All to onct Sally she yelled agin, an’ says the front yard picket fence hed jest crumpled up, though they wa’n’t no sign o’ what done it. Then everybody on the line could hear Cha’ncey an’ ol’ Seth Bishop a-yellin’, tew, an’ Sally was shriekin’ aout that suthin’ heavy hed struck the haouse—not lightnin’ nor nothin’, but suthin’ heavy agin’ the front, that kep’ a-launchin’ itself agin an’ agin, though ye couldn’t see nuthin’ aout the front winders. An’ then… an’ then….”

Fright drew deeper grooves into every face. Armitage, shaken, forced himself to speak evenly.

“And then?”

The man swallowed as if the words were too thick to pass.

“An’ then Sally she yelled aout, ‘O help, the haouse is a-cavin’ in’… an’ on the wire we could hoar a turrible crashin’, an’ a hull flock o’ screamin’… jest like when Elmer Frye’s place was took, only wuss….”

He stopped. Another man said dully, as if reciting the end of a story that had already devoured its meaning:

“That’s all—not a saound nor squeak over the ’phone arter that. Jest still-like. We that heerd it got aout Fords an’ wagons an’ raounded up as many able-bodied men-folks as we could get, at Corey’s place, an’ come up here ter see what yew thought best ter dew. Not but what I think it’s the Lord’s judgment fer our iniquities, that no mortal kin ever set aside.”

In that moment Armitage understood that waiting was no longer a choice. The thing had moved in daylight; it had learned boldness. The village’s acoustic life—its calls across fields, its telephone line, its dogs, its birds—had become the only net they had to track an invisible bulk through a soaked countryside. He spoke decisively, putting as much steadiness into his voice as he could.

“We must follow it, boys. I believe there’s a chance of putting it out of business. You men know those Whateleys were wizards—well, this thing is a thing of wizardry, and must be put down by the same means. I’ve seen Wilbur Whateley’s diary and read some of the strange old books he used to read, and I think I know the right kind of a spell to recite to make the thing fade away.

“Of course, one can’t be sure, but we can always take a chance. It’s invisible—I knew it would be—but there’s a powder in this long-distance sprayer that might make it show up for a second. Later on we’ll try it. It’s a frightful thing to have alive, but it isn’t as bad as what Wilbur would have let in if he’d lived longer. You’ll never know what the world has escaped. Now we’ve only this one thing to fight, and it can’t multiply. It can, though, do a lot of harm; so we mustn’t hesitate to rid the community of it.

“We must follow it—and the way to begin is to go to the place that has just been wrecked. Let somebody lead the way—I don’t know your roads very well, but I’ve an idea there might be a shorter cut across lots. How about it?”

The men shuffled, glancing from the road to the hills, from Armitage’s paper to Morgan’s rifle and Rice’s sprayer. At last Earl Sawyer pointed with a grimy finger through the steadily lessening rain.

“I guess ye kin git to Seth Bishop’s quickest by cuttin’ acrost the lower medder here, wadin’ the brook at the low place, an’ climbin’ through Carrier’s mowin’ an’ the timber-lot beyont. That comes aout on the upper rud mighty nigh Seth’s—a leetle t’other side.”

Armitage started out at once with Rice and Morgan. Most of the natives followed, slower, as if the very act of moving made the danger more real. The sky lightened; the storm showed signs of breaking up. When Armitage took a wrong direction, Joe Osborn warned him and walked ahead to keep them true. Courage rose in small increments, like heat returning to a chilled room.

But the end of their shortcut led into the twilight of a steep, wooded hill whose old trees stood fantastic and close together. They scrambled up among roots and wet leaves as if climbing a ladder into a darker world. The place had a sound of its own: dripping, sliding mud; breath pulled hard; the occasional creak of a branch not touched by wind. More than once someone stopped, listening for that reported swishing, lapping undertone that did not belong to rain or brook.

At length they emerged onto a muddy road and saw the sun pushing through. They had come out a little beyond Seth Bishop’s place, but they did not need a guide. Bent trees and the hideously unmistakable tracks told the whole tale of passage.

Only a few minutes were spent at the bend where the Bishop buildings had stood. It was the Frye incident all over again: collapsed shells where house and barn had been, and nothing dead or living to be found. The stench lay thick; the tarry stickiness smeared boards and earth with the same obscene persistence. No one cared to linger where the air itself seemed contaminated, as if it had absorbed a foreign breath.

Instinctively they turned from the ruins to the line of horrible prints leading onward toward the wrecked Whateley farmhouse and the altar-crowned slopes of Sentinel Hill. As they passed the site of Wilbur Whateley’s abode, the men shuddered, and zeal began to tangle with hesitation. It was one thing to chase a menace. It was another to hunt something as big as a house that could not be seen, yet moved with a demon’s malevolence, announcing itself only by crushed fences, split bridges, and the wrongness of its odor.

Opposite the base of Sentinel Hill, the tracks left the road. A fresh bending and matting marked a broad swath—the monster’s route to and from the summit. Armitage produced a pocket telescope and scanned the steep green side. He handed it to Morgan, whose sight was keener.

After a moment Morgan cried out sharply and passed the glass to Earl Sawyer, pointing at a spot on the slope. Sawyer fumbled—clumsy with an instrument made for finer hands—until Armitage helped him focus. Then Sawyer’s cry came out unrestrained, a raw sound that seemed to rip the air.

“Gawd almighty—the grass an’ bushes is a-movin’! It’s a-goin’ up—slow-like—creepin’ up ter the top this minute, heaven only knows what fer!”

Panic began to spread like a taint through water. To follow a trail was one thing. To see the hillside itself betray the passage of an unseen bulk was another. Voices rose, questioning Armitage—what did he truly know, what exactly had he read, what kind of spell could stand against a weight that made the living land bow and part? No answer he could give quite satisfied, because what pressed upon them now was not merely fear but proximity—an intimate nearness to forbidden phases of nature and being, outside the sane experience of mankind, and outside the familiar language with which men speak of barns, bread, weather, and the ordinary mercies of a day.

In the end the three men from Arkham—old Dr. Armitage with his snowy beard, Professor Rice set and square as an iron kettle, and Dr. Morgan lean as a carving-knife—went up the mountain alone.

They had first schooled the men in the road, patiently and with a tired gentleness, in how to focus the telescope and keep it steady. Hands that knew reins and axe-handles took to the brass tube awkwardly, as if it were some newfangled churn that might explode. The glass was passed from palm to palm with a hush like the one that falls in a kitchen when the milk begins to rise too fast in the pot. Below, there was the ordinary domestic smell of wet earth and bruised weeds and old wool; above, the swath in the brush still trembled where something unseen had gone through it with a slow, deliberate appetite, bending saplings as if they were celery stalks snapped for soup.

It was hard going. More than once Armitage had to be helped, his boots sliding on the slick slope the way a man slips on grease beside a hog-killing shed. Yet even from the road it became plain that the pursuers were gaining; the great torn lane of flattened shrubbery drew on ahead, and the invisible maker of it repassed, high above them, with that snail-like steadiness that makes a cook’s skin crawl when some malign simmer refuses to boil over or die down.

Curtis Whateley—Curtis of the undecayed branch, who still carried the Whateley name like a jar of preserves that had not yet turned—held the telescope when the Arkham party abruptly detoured away from the swath.

“They’re headin’ for that leetle peak,” he said, voice thin and raw. “Overlooks the swath ahead—where the brush is fixin’ to bend next.”

And so it was. The three men gained the subordinate height not long after the invisible blasphemy had passed beneath it. The crowd shifted in the mud, boots sucking, breath steaming. Someone’s stomach growled and was answered by the valley’s deeper noises—the small, honest ones of frogs and distant cattle—until those, too, began to be smothered by something else.

Wesley Corey took the glass next and cried out that Armitage was adjusting the sprayer which Rice held. At that, the men in the road tightened like hands around a knife. They remembered what the Arkham men had said: the powder would give the unseen thing a moment of visibility, as flour dust in a shaft of light will show the currents in a room.

Two or three shut their eyes. Curtis snatched back the telescope as if he could not help himself and set his gaze with a man’s stubbornness that has watched ovens and fevers and floods and never yet looked away.

From their vantage above and behind the entity, Rice had a clean chance. Those without the telescope saw only an instant’s flash of gray cloud—about the size of a moderately large building—near the top of the mountain, as if a mill had belched dust all at once.

Curtis dropped the instrument with a piercing shriek, and it fell into the ankle-deep mud of the road with a soft, obscene thud. He reeled; and would have crumpled like a sack of meal if two or three others had not seized and steadied him.

All he could do was moan, half inarticulate, as though his mouth had forgotten its own dialect. “Oh, oh, great Gawd… that… that….”

Questions burst like popcorn in a pan—high, frantic, useless. Only Henry Wheeler, with the steady habits of a man who has wiped down counters after worse messes than mud, thought to rescue the fallen telescope and wipe it clean with his sleeve, scraping grit from the lens as if it were a burn at the bottom of a pot.

Curtis was past all coherence; even a single word cost him, like swallowing scalding broth.

“Bigger ’n a barn…” he got out at last, staring at nothing. “All made o’ squirmin’ ropes… hull thing sort o’ shaped like a hen’s egg bigger’n anything, with dozens o’ legs like hogsheads that haff shut up when they step… nothin’ solid abaout it—all like jelly, an’ made o’ sep’rit wrigglin’ ropes pushed clost together…”

His voice snagged, but the words kept coming, thick and sour, the way rancid butter will still melt and spread.

“Great bulgin’ eyes all over it… ten or twenty maouths or trunks a-stickin’ aout all along the sides, big as stovepipes, an’ all a-tossin’ an’ openin’ an’ shuttin’… all gray, with kinder blue or purple rings…”

He drew in breath that seemed to scrape his throat raw.

“An’ Gawd in Heaven—that haff face on top!”

Whatever that last memory was, it broke him. He collapsed before he could say more. Fred Farr and Will Hutchins carried him to the roadside and laid him on damp grass that already felt colder than it ought, the blades slick as fish-skin.

Henry Wheeler, trembling, turned the cleaned telescope back to the mountain. Through the lenses he could make out three tiny figures, their black movements sharp against the sky. They seemed to be running toward the summit as fast as the incline allowed. Nothing else—only those three human dots and the wild emptiness around them.

Then everyone noticed the noise in the deep valley behind, and in the underbrush of Sentinel Hill itself—strangely unseasonable and multiplying by the second. It was whippoorwills, unnumbered, piping as if the whole countryside had become one vast throat. The chorus had a shrillness that did not sit right with the woods’ usual night-music; inside it was a note of tense expectancy, like the thin keen you hear when a kettle is about to boil but the lid is clamped down.

Earl Sawyer took the telescope and reported the three figures now standing on the topmost ridge, virtually level with the altar-stone though still at a good distance from it. One of them, he said, kept raising its hands above its head at rhythmic intervals. As Sawyer spoke, the crowd began to hear a faint half-musical sound from the distance, as if a loud chant went with those gestures.

It should have been a spectacle to freeze a painter’s hand and set a poet jabbering—but no one had any appetite for art. There was too much in the air already, too much that wanted to be heard.

“I guess he’s sayin’ the spell,” Wheeler whispered, and snatched back the telescope.

The whippoorwills piped wildly, yet their rhythm was singularly irregular—out of step with the visible ritual, as if the birds answered to an older conductor than any man on the ridge. The very soundscape of the place seemed to be splitting: the human cadence trying to impose itself, the natural chorus bucking and breaking, the valley becoming an instrument badly tuned.

Suddenly the sunshine lessened—without any cloud crossing. It was not like ordinary dimming before rain. It was as if someone had bruised the daylight, turning its clean yellow toward something tainted. A rumbling began under the hills, mixed with a concordant rumbling that clearly came from the sky. Lightning flashed, and the crowd stared up in vain for storm-towers. From some farmhouse far away came frantic barking; the dogs sounded like they were throwing themselves at doors.

The chanting from the Arkham men became unmistakable. Through the glass Wheeler saw them all raising their arms in the rhythmic incantation, bodies jerking with the insistence of it. The quality of the light kept changing—pressing down, purplish, born of nothing more than a spectral deepening of the sky’s blue. It was like the bruised bloom on a plum, beautiful for an instant and then suddenly repellent because it promises rot.

The whippoorwills continued their irregular pulsation. Men braced themselves against something they could not name but felt in their teeth and lungs, the way you feel a coming train through the ground long before you see it.

Without warning came the deep, cracked, raucous vocal sounds that never left those who heard them.

Not from any human throat. No man can twist breath into that. It was not so much heard as suffered; part of it lived below the ear, in the chest and the bones, in places where memory keeps its oldest, untranslatable terrors. Yet it had shape enough to be half-articulate—as if words were being forced through organs never meant for speech.

It came from the peak—unmistakably from beside the altar-stone—and it was loud, louder than the rumblings and the thunder above which it echoed, though no visible being stood there. The crowd huddled closer, imagining a source in some world that does not show itself, and winced as if in expectation of a blow.

“Ygnaiih… ygnaiih… thflthkh’ngha… Yog-Sothoth…,” rang the hideous croaking out of space.

“Y’bthnk… h’ehye… n’grkdl’lh….”

The impulse faltered, as if a psychic struggle were grinding like stones in a mill. Wheeler strained his eye to the telescope; he saw only the three silhouetted men, arms working furiously, gestures strange as any forgotten kneading or threshing done in some dead tongue.

From what black wells of fear, from what extra-cosmic consciousness or long-latent heredity, were those thunder-croakings drawn? The very syllables seemed to bully the air, to make the hills vibrate in reluctant answer.

Then the voice gathered force again—coherence sharpening into frenzy.

“Eh-ya-ya-ya-yahaah… e’yaya-yayaaaa… ngh’aaaa… ngh’aaaa… h’yuh… h’yuh… HELP! HELP!… ff—ff—ff—FATHER! FATHER! YOG-SOTHOTH!”

The English words—plain, thick, indisputable—fell down the slope like heavy loaves dropped from a height, and the men in the road reeled as if struck. It was not merely the meaning; it was the violation of it, the way the familiar language—Dunwich’s rough, homely speech of chores and meals and weather—had been seized and made to carry something it was never bred for. In that instant, the power of naming became a kind of violence, and every man felt how a dominant lexicon can be a chain dragged through the mouth.

They were never to hear such syllables again.

A terrific report came—so deafening, so cataclysmic, that no hearer was ever able to say if it came from inner earth or sky. It seemed to rend the hills. A single lightning bolt shot from the purple zenith to the altar-stone.

Then a great tidal wave of viewless force and indescribable stench swept down from the height and out over all the countryside.

It hit like a wall. Not merely foul, but intimate—like something had been opened that should have stayed sealed, a vat of corruption whose sweetness had gone wrong beyond any farmyard reek. It filled throats, coated tongues, made eyes tear. Men gagged as if fed some spoiled broth they could not spit out fast enough. Trees, grass, underbrush whipped into fury; the crowd, weakened by the fetor that seemed about to asphyxiate them, were almost hurled off their feet.

Dogs howled from the distance. Green grass and foliage wilted to a sickly yellow-gray, as if scalded. Over field and forest fell the bodies of dead whippoorwills—scattered like blackened berries shaken from a bush.

The stench left quickly, as if it had never been, but the plants never came right again. Even now, anything that grows on and around that hill seems to carry a queer, unholy cast—like flour stored too long in a damp bin, never again truly sweet.

Curtis Whateley was only just regaining consciousness when the Arkham men came slowly down the mountain in sunlight once more brilliant and untainted. They were grave and quiet. Their faces held the shaken look of men who have smelled something no ordinary kitchen or barn could ever breed, and who have heard a sound that will keep returning in quiet rooms.

Questions met them in a tumble; they answered by shaking their heads, and by repeating one fact that mattered.

“The thing has gone for ever,” Armitage said. His voice was weary but firm, like a man setting a lid on a pot he dares not open again. “It has been split up into what it was originally made of, and can never exist again. It was an impossibility in a normal world. Only the least fraction was really matter in any sense we know. It was like its father—and most of it has gone back to him in some vague realm or dimension outside our material universe; some vague abyss out of which only the most accursed rites of human blasphemy could ever have called him for a moment on the hills.”

Silence followed—thick, as after a sudden blast in a sawmill when everyone counts fingers without saying so. In that pause Curtis’s scattered senses knitted enough for him to press his hands to his head with a moan. Memory picked up where it had broken, and the sight that had felled him burst back in full flavor, like bile.

“Oh, oh, my Gawd, that haff face… that haff face on top of it… that face with the red eyes an’ crinkly albino hair, an’ no chin, like the Whateleys….” His breath hitched. “It was a octopus, centipede, spider kind o’ thing, but they was a haff-shaped man’s face on top of it, an’ it looked like Wizard Whateley’s, only it was yards an’ yards acrost….”

He sagged, exhausted. The natives stared, bewilderment not yet hardened into fresh terror. Only old Zebulon Whateley—whose mind wandered among ancient scraps the way a food historian fingers brittle recipe cards written in a dying hand—spoke at last.

“Fifteen year’ gone,” he rambled, eyes unfocused as if listening to a voice behind the present noises, “I heerd Ol’ Whateley say as haow some day we’d hear a child o’ Lavinny’s a-callin’ its father’s name on the top o’ Sentinel Hill….”

But Joe Osborn cut across him, needing the practical handle of things. “What was it, anyhaow, an’ haowever did young Wizard Whateley call it aout o’ the air it come from?”

Armitage chose his words with care, as if each one were a spice that could ruin the whole dish if too heavy.

“It was—well, it was mostly a kind of force that doesn’t belong in our part of space; a kind of force that acts and grows and shapes itself by other laws than those of our sort of Nature. We have no business calling in such things from outside, and only very wicked people and very wicked cults ever try to.

“There was some of it in Wilbur Whateley himself—enough to make a devil and a precocious monster of him, and to make his passing out a pretty terrible sight. I’m going to burn his accursed diary, and if you men are wise you’ll dynamite that altar-stone up there, and pull down all the rings of standing stones on the other hills. Things like that brought down the beings those Whateleys were so fond of—the beings they were going to let in tangibly to wipe out the human race and drag the earth off to some nameless place for some nameless purpose.

“But as to this thing we’ve just sent back—the Whateleys raised it for a terrible part in the doings that were to come. It grew fast and big from the same reason that Wilbur grew fast and big—but it beat him because it had a greater share of the outsideness in it. You needn’t ask how Wilbur called it out of the air. He didn’t call it out. It was his twin brother, but it looked more like the father than he did.”
