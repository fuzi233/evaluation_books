To Romola, the events of that Carnival day offered little more than a renewed duty: the tender, exhausting task of supporting her poor cousin Brigida, who was resigning herself to the gray hairs and frailty of age. Yet, these domestic cares merely set the stage for a Lenten season defined by a feverish intensity of mental exertion. With the election of Bernardo del Nero as Gonfaloniere, the Medicean faction had secured a significant, albeit precarious, victory. This triumph, however, did not bring relief; rather, it deepened Romola's foreboding. She sensed a shadowy scheme brewing beneath the surface, one poised to mature within the next two months of her godfather's authority into either glorious success or catastrophic betrayal.

Every morning, as the pale dawn pierced her curtains, that haunting fear returned to haunt her. It accompanied her through the silent streets on her way to the early sermon in the Duomo. Yet, once inside the cathedral, amidst the throng of worshippers, the chill of dread evaporated, much as the terror of death fades in the heat of battle. Here, in the vast nave, she felt herself embroiled in a conflict of passion that transcended the walls of Florence. Savonarola was preaching—the final series of Lenten sermons permitted before the threat of exclamation became reality. He spoke with a terrifying clarity, holding up the rotting state of the Church in a mirror of unflinching truth, refusing polite euphemisms. With growing confidence, he proclaimed the advent of a great renovation, a moment when humanity would rise in revolt against corruption.

His own fate seemed to dance between two visions: sometimes he saw himself as the trumpet of a glorious revolution, shaking the very foundations of the Church until it rose anew like Lazarus; at other times, he foresaw only persecution and martyrdom, viewing this earthly life as a mere vigil preceding the true dawn. Such a stance possessed a magnetic gravity for all but the dull-minded, even for Machiavelli, who might have dismissed the Friar's character as merely ambitious. But for Romola, whose own fervor convinced her of Savonarola's genuine nobility, the crisis felt intimately personal. It blended with her daily labours as an exalting memory, demanding not just perseverance, but a new, fierce courage.

Famine still stalked Florence, and the prolonged suffering had hardened the hearts of the populace. Disease festered in the crowded streets, and the plague loomed on the horizon. Walking among the sick, the starving, and the muttering crowds, Romola found solace not merely in pity, but in the belief that she served a heroic struggle toward sublime ends. Her acts of kindness were but dew falling on weedy ground, preparing for a harvest she could not yet see.

Yet, the mighty music that swelled in the Duomo was not without dissonance. Since the early days of glowing hope—when the Friar preached peace, charity, and the coming of a French savior—conditions had shifted violently. Political intrigue refused to yield; the dream of French deliverance had collapsed; and hostility, both petty and grand, attacked the Prophet with renewed ferocity. Consequently, the spirit of contention and self-defense seeped into his sermons. Urged by popular demand, he began to rely increasingly on visions and private revelations, adopting a tone of defiant arrogance. Having once denied the necessity of miracles, he now claimed that Divine power would soon validate his prophecy through a sign.

In these rapid shifts of excited emotion, as the vision of triumph faded behind the grim reality of evil, threats of vengeance against tyrants and corrupt priests gained momentum, fueled by personal exasperation as much as righteous zeal. In the career of a great orator yielding to the moment, the hidden conflict of selfish and unselfish emotions erupts into open flame. These jarring notes disturbed Romola, though they thrilled others.

Among them was Baldassarre. Having discovered that the "wonderful Frate" was preaching again, he attended every sermon, drinking in the threats of a voice that sounded like justice incarnate. His motivation was partly admiration, but largely surveillance; he watched for the precise moment when external circumstances and his own mental state aligned to permit an interview with Romola. Twice he had caught her gaze in the Duomo—once when his dark stare locked onto hers. She wished to avoid him, yet she awaited his return like a portent, though any revelation regarding this old man now seemed secondary to her pressing anxieties.

As the stirring Lent drew to a close and April neared its end, nothing had materialized to fulfill her fears. Rumors of Piero de' Medici's machinations from Rome had circulated, but Bernardo's tenure was nearing expiration. Just as Romola tried to steel herself against these futile terrors, a messenger arrived on the twenty-seventh, bearing a summons from Camilla Rucellai, one of Florence's leading female seers. The matter was described as of the "highest moment."

Romola recoiled instinctively. She despised the shrill volubility of these illuminated women, and her aversion to Camilla was heightened by reports that the prophetess had recently announced revelations hostile to Bernardo del Nero. Initially, she intended to refuse outright, fearing that further knowledge would only weigh upon her spirit. However, her habitual discipline quickly overrode her impulse. Blaming her hesitation on cowardice and selfishness, she complied and proceeded directly to Camilla's residence.

She found the nervous, gray-haired woman in a chamber stripped bare, resembling a convent cell. When Camilla seized her thin hands and addressed her in a loud whisper, Romola physically shrank, finding it difficult to remain seated. Camilla delivered a vision: her Angel had revealed that Romola possessed dangerous secrets concerning her godfather, Bernardo del Nero. If disclosed, these secrets could save the Republic. As Camilla narrated, her voice climbed in pitch, urging Romola to obey her Angel and sever ties with the "enemy of God."

Romola's nature was massive and grounded; usually calm and self-controlled, she harbored a constitutional disgust for the shallow excitability of women like Camilla, whose faculties seemed entirely consumed by fantasy. Before the exhortation concluded, Romola sprang up, attempting to wrench her arm free. It was useless. Clutching her like a crab, Camilla only grew more frantic, launching into a cascade of corroborating visions. Christ Himself had appeared, commanding citizens to cast Bernardo del Nero from the window of the Palazzo Vecchio. Even Fra Girolamo, she claimed, knew of this divine mandate and dared not deny its authenticity.

"And since then," Camilla cried, her treble voice straining upward toward Romola's face with wild eyes, "the Blessed Infant has placed a wafer of sweetness on my tongue, confirming that I have done His will!"

"Let me go!" Romola roared, her voice deep with anger. "God grant you are mad! Else you are detestably wicked!"

The violence of her struggle finally broke Camilla's grip. The prophetess released her, and Romola fled the room, rushing out into the street without pausing until she reached the church of the Badia. She slipped behind the curtain under the ancient stone arch, entering a sanctuary shielded from the city's clamor, where every object whispered of eternal peace amidst turmoil. Sinking to her knees before Filippino Lippi's serene depiction of the Virgin visiting Saint Bernard, she hoped the inward tumult would subside.

The thought that plagued her most acutely was that Camilla might accuse Savonarola of sanctioning this wicked folly. Romola did not believe the Friar had approved casting Bernardo from the window; she was certain there was falsehood or error in that claim. While Savonarola had grown severe in his condemnation of malcontents, the principles of strict law and order were foundational to his political teaching. So why, knowing the potentially fatal impact of such visions, did he not publicly denounce them? Why did he not expose these pretenders who brought darkness instead of light?

The answer struck her with painful clarity: he was fettered internally by the realization that such revelations were inseparable from his own visionary experiences, and externally by the fear that condemning them would alienate his own followers, painting him as a suppressor of Divine inspiration—or worse, implying that his own confidant, Fra Salvestro, was merely a lesser vessel of the same power.

Kneeling with her face buried in the altar steps, Romola endured a sickening moment. The enthusiasm that had previously been her sole source of strength now seemed inextricably linked to vain dreams and willful blindness. Her mind drifted back to the sturdy worldly sense, the dignified prudence, and the untheoretic virtues of her godfather. He was being treated as a sacrificial lamb (Agag) for holding a restricted form of government and refusing to sever ties with the banished Medici family. Yet, this loyalty sparked a fresh presentiment: perhaps a plot existed to restore the Medici. Was the popular party's suspicion unjustified?

Again, she affirmed that keeping Florence's government pure was a sacred cause; the Friar was right here, and she had carried her understanding irrevocably with him. But at this moment, her intellectual assent was given unwillingly. Her heart recoiled from a righteousness allied with such narrowness, a system that judged men by superficial symbols rather than their inner humanity. Her affection and respect clung tenaciously to her godfather, linking him to the cherished memories of her father, who also opposed the crude division of mankind into sheep and goats based on political or religious labels.
