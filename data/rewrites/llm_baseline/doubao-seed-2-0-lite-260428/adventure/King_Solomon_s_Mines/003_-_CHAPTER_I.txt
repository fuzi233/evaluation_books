It’s a funny thing that at fifty-five years old, laid up here in Durban with a lion-mauled left leg throbbing so bad I can barely hobble to the porch, I find myself picking up a pen to write a history. I’ve spent every day since I was a boy too young for school tramping the old Cape Colony, trading, hunting, fighting, mining, and I only struck it rich eight months ago. Even with the pile of ivory and gold I’ve got stowed away now? I wouldn’t live through the last sixteen months of what we went through again — not even if I knew I’d walk away with every last penny of that fortune. I’m a timid man, hate unnecessary violence, and I was sick to death of adventures long before this mess started. But here I am, scribbling. Let me lay out my reasons plain, just to make sure I’m not being a fool for doing it:
First: Sir Henry Curtis and Captain John Good badgered me until I caved.
Second: That lion’s teeth left some rotten kind of poison in my leg, and every year around the anniversary of the mauling the wounds flare up again, bad enough to leave me bedridden. Sixty-six lions I’ve shot in my life, and the last one chewed my leg like a plug of tobacco. Rude, if you ask a man who likes his routine neat.
Third: My boy Harry’s over in a London hospital studying to be a doctor, and I know cutting up dead bodies gets dull after a while. This story won’t be dull. It’ll give him something way more exciting to read for a week, keep him out of mischief.
Fourth, and last: This is the strangest tale I’ve ever lived through. And for a story full of wild Africa, lost mines, and all kinds of deviltry? There’s barely a petticoat in it. Only Foulata, and the ancient witch Gagoola, who was a hundred if she was a day, more fiend than woman anyway. No silly romantic nonsense to bog the pace down.
The Boers say *sutjes, sutjes* — slowly does it — so I won’t rush. Let me start proper. Eighteen months back, I was fresh off a terrible elephant hunt beyond Bamangwato. Everything went wrong out there, topped off by a brutal bout of fever. The second I could stand I trekked down to the Diamond Fields, sold off what little ivory I had, my wagon, my oxen, paid off my native hunters, and took the post cart to the Cape. I hung around Cape Town a week, found the hotel wildly overpriced, saw the botanical gardens (they’ll probably do some good for the country) and the new Houses of Parliament (they almost certainly won’t) then booked passage back to Natal on the *Dunkeld*, which was docked waiting for the incoming *Edinburgh Castle* from England.
We transhipped the English passengers that afternoon, weighed anchor, and set sail. Two of those new faces caught my eye before we’d even cleared the docks.
The first was easily thirty years old, the broadest-chested, longest-armed man I’d ever laid eyes on. Yellow hair, thick yellow beard, sharp cut features, deep-set pale grey eyes. He looked like a living ancient Dane — the kind of wild raider I’d once seen in a old print, drinking mead out of a bull’s horn, chain shirt slung over his shoulders, battleaxe in one hand. Turns out later that hunch was right: Sir Henry Curtis, that was his name, really did come from Danish blood.
The man talking to him was short, stout, dark, and unmistakably a Royal Navy officer. I can never mistake one: the wide sea air blows all the petty bitterness out of them, leaves them tough, fair, and good company for a hunt, even if a few of them curse like sailors when the weather turns bad. His name was John Good, lieutenant promoted to honorary captain at thirty-one, then tossed out of Her Majesty’s service with nothing but half pay to live on after seventeen years of service, just when he’d learned his trade proper. He was absurdly neat, clean-shaven every minute of the day, and wore a stringless pince-nez stuck to his right eye like it grew there. I later found out he took it off only to wipe it, or tuck it in his trouser pocket at night right next to his two full sets of pearly false teeth that made me break the tenth commandment every time I saw them, since my own chompers are falling to bits.
Night fell fast after we set sail, and the weather turned vicious. A sharp cold gale blew off the coast, and a thick, damp Scotch mist drove every last passenger off the deck. The *Dunkeld* was a flat-bottomed old tub, sailing light, and she rolled so hard I swore she was going to tip clear over into the waves. I huddled near the warm engine room for shelter, watching the big roll pendulum swing back and forth across its marked scale to measure how far she tipped.
“That pendulum’s weighted wrong,” a testy voice said right at my shoulder. It was Captain Good. “If she’d rolled as far as that thing says she did, she’d never have righted herself again. Typical careless merchant skipper nonsense.”
Just then the dinner bell rang, and I was glad for it — there’s no worse bore than a Royal Navy officer ranting about merchant captains, save for a merchant captain ranting right back about Royal Navy officers. We trundled down to the saloon together, found Sir Henry already sat at the table, and I took the seat opposite them. Good and I chatted for ages about hunting, elephants in particular, until a passenger sitting near me nodded my way and spoke up: “You’re asking the right man there. Hunter Quatermain could tell you everything about elephants, if anyone alive can.”
Sir Henry, who’d been sitting quiet listening, jumped so hard he nearly knocked over his glass.
“Excuse me, sir,” he rumbled, his deep, booming voice perfect for a man with that huge chest, “but is your name Allan Quatermain?”
I said it was, and he muttered “fortunate” under his breath into his beard, like he’d just caught a stroke of luck.
When dinner broke up, he strolled over and invited me back to his cabin to smoke a pipe. It was a big sprawling space, two smaller cabins knocked into one — some big colonial swell named Sir Garnet Wolseley had ordered the partition torn out once for a trip up the coast, and no one had bothered putting it back. He sent the steward for a bottle of whisky, the three of us settled in with our pipes, and he cut straight to the point.
“Mr. Quatermain,” he said, fixing those sharp grey eyes right on mine, “around this time two years ago, you were camped outside Bamangwato, north of the Transvaal. You met a man there going by the name of Neville.”
I blinked, surprised he knew so much about my obscure little movements out in the bush. I confirmed it: Neville had outspanned his wagon right next to mine for two weeks, resting his oxen before pushing further north into the interior. I’d gotten a lawyer’s letter a few months back asking after him, and told what I knew: Neville had left Bamangwato at the start of May, with a driver, a voorlooper, and a Kafir tracker named Jim, said he was heading for Inyati, the last trading post in Matabele country. He’d sold his wagon there, I’d later seen it in the hands of a Portuguese trader who said the white man and his single native servant had struck north on foot for a shooting trip, and no one had heard a peep from them since.
A long quiet fell over the cabin. The ship rolled so hard then the mirror on the wall tilted almost right over our heads.
“Mr. Quatermain,” Sir Henry said suddenly, leaning forward across the table, “Neville is my younger brother George.”
That’s when I finally placed who he reminded me of. George Neville had been smaller, with a dark beard, but he’d had those exact same sharp grey eyes, the same cut to his jaw.
“Five years ago, our father died without making a will,” he went on, his voice tight with regret. All the family land, the whole estate, passed straight to me as the eldest son. George had no profession, no money, and we’d had a terrible, stupid fight just days before our father died. I was too proud to reach out to him, too stubborn to offer him his fair share of the fortune. He took the few hundred pounds he had in the bank, changed his name to Neville, and ran off to South Africa to make his own luck, without a single word to me.
“I’ve spent three years searching for him. Blood’s thicker than water, you see. I’d give half my fortune just to know he’s alive, and bring him home. Good here was kind enough to come with me on the search — he’s got nothing else to do, stuck on half pay after the Admiralty tossed him out. Now tell us: do you know anything more about where my brother was heading, out there past the edge of the map?”
