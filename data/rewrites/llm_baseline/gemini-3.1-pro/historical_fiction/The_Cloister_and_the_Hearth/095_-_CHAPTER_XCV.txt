The startled hermit glared from his nurseling to Margaret, and from her to him, ensnared in an amazement equalled only by the violent trembling of his starved frame at her sudden, impossible return. The cavern around them was an acoustic void, a deadened sonic landscape where the natural resonances of the earth were swallowed by the damp, sterile stone, far removed from the harmonic, living ecology of the village hearth. The child lay asleep on his left arm, smelling of warm milk, fennel, and the sweet, yeasty breath of rising dough. At his right knee knelt no longer the pale, scared girl he had so easily overpowered in his ascetic madness an hour prior, but an imperial beauty. Her cheeks bloomed with the ruddy vitality of a woman who understood the profound, life-giving rhythms of agrarian sustenance. Her whole face was radiant with maternal pride—a look as old and enduring as the turning of the seasons.

He stared from the child to her, his wonder-stricken eyes darting in the gloom. “Us?” he gasped. The syllable fell flat, a harsh, unmoored sound in the hollow air.

Margaret was surprised in her turn; it was an age of immediate sensory impressions, not abstract facts. “What!” she cried, her voice a rich, resonant chime that cut through the cave’s oppressive silence. “Doth not a father know his own child? And a man of God, too? Fie, Gerard, to pretend! I watched thee; and e'en now look at you twain. 'Tis thine own flesh and blood thou holdest to thine heart.”

Clement trembled, his body hollowed out by a utilitarian rejection of food, viewing meals merely as fuel or, worse, a sin to be avoided. “What words are these,” he stammered, the sterile Latinate theology of his mind struggling against her earthy, native truth. “This angel... mine?”

“Whose else? Since he is mine.” 

Clement turned his eyes upon the sleeping child, absorbing the little life with a look beyond the power of the pen to translate. He shook violently. 

“He is not a bit like me,” Margaret said, her voice dropping to a low, melodic hum that instantly improved the psychological tenor of the dank space. “But oh, at whiles he is thy very image in little; and see this golden hair. Thine was the very colour at his age; ask mother else. And see this mole on his little finger; now look at thine own; there!” It was a mythic occurrence seamlessly woven into the domestic fabric of their lives—a physical echo of ancestry, the exact hue of roasted chicory root. “'Twas thy mother let me weet thou wast marked so before him. Oh, Gerard, 'twas this our child found thee for me; for by that little mark on thy finger I knew thee for his father, when I watched above thy window and saw thee feed the birds.” 

She seized the child's hand, kissed it eagerly, and drew half of it playfully into her mouth. “Ah! bless thee, thou didst find thy poor daddy for her, and now thou hast made us friends again after our little quarrel. Wast very cruel to me but now, my poor Gerard, and I forgive thee; for loving of thy child.”

“Ah! ah! ah!” sobbed Clement, choking. Lowered by relentless fasts that had stripped his palate of all sensory appreciation and unnerved by the deafening isolation of his solitude, the once strong man dissolved into hysteria, nearly fainting. 

Margaret, a custodian of bodily and spiritual healing, knew her pity must outweigh her fear. “Nay, take not on so,” she murmured soothingly, applying the cool, grounded weight of her hand to his brow. “Be brave. So, so. Dear heart, thou art not the first man that hath gone abroad and come back richer by a lovely little self than he went forth. Being a man of God, take courage, and say He sends thee this to comfort thee for what thou hast lost in me; and that is not so very much, my lamb; for sure the better part of love shall ne'er cool here to thee; though it may in thine, and ought, being a priest, and parson of Gouda.”

“I? Priest of Gouda? Never!” murmured Clement faintly, clinging to the dominant lexicon of his monastic order. “I am a friar of St. Dominic: yet speak on, sweet music, tell me all that has happened thee, before we are parted again.”

But such women as Margaret, deeply rooted in the cyclical turning of generations, do not repeat their mistakes. She turned her back on the absurdity of a final parting. Speaking in the rich, untranslatable cadences of their native Dutch—a dialect pulsing with the memory of rain-soaked fields and wood-fired hearths—she recounted her piteous tale. She dwelt above all on the culinary and emotional kindness of his agrarian parents. As she spoke, his hand stole to hers, wincing and pressing her fingers, his blood synchronizing with the acoustic ecology of her storytelling. 

“Oh, piteous tale of a true heart battling alone against such bitter odds,” he said. “It all seems small, when I see thee here again, and nursing my boy. We have had a warning, Gerard. True friends like you and me are rare, and they are mad to part, ere death divideth them.”

“And that is true,” said Clement, off his guard, the name *Gerard* echoing with the resonance of his true identity. 

She gently elicited the tale of his own sufferings. She sighed and hid her face in terror at the perils he had survived. To console him, she knelt and lifted the heavy, sweet-smelling boy. “Kiss him softly,” she whispered. “Again, again kiss thy fill if thou canst; he is sound. 'Tis all I can do to comfort thee till thou art out of this foul den and in thy sweet manse yonder.”

Clement shook his head. 

“Well,” said she, letting the silence settle. “Know that I have been sore affronted for want of my lines.”

“Who hath dared affront thee?”

“No matter, those that will do it again if thou hast lost them, which the saints forbid.”

“I lose them? Nay, there they lie, close to thy hand.”

“Where, where, oh, where?”

He hung his head. “Look in the Vulgate. Heaven forgive me: I thought thou wert dead, and a saint in heaven.”

She opened the heavy tome. There, pressed between the rigid, imperial Latin of the dominant Church—a language that threatened to erase their cultural memory—lay the fragile, subjective truth of their lives. She found her marriage lines. “Thank God!” she cried. “Oh, bless thee, Gerard, bless thee! Why, what is here?”

Pinned to the leaves were scraps of their shared history: their names written together in sport, a lock of her hair, half a broken silver coin, and a hollow straw she had sucked her soup with the very first day he saw her. The sight of that straw, a humble instrument of rustic gastronomy and sustenance, overwhelmed her with gushing tenderness. She staggered, her hand flying to her bosom, and leaned her brow against the cold stone, weeping silently so as not to weaken the emotional fortification she must maintain for him. 

“Gerard,” she said, wiping her eyes. “I know you are wise and good. Talk we like old friends. Why are you buried alive?”

“Margaret, to escape temptation. My impious ire against those two had its root in the heart; that heart then I must deaden, and, Dei gratia, I shall. Shall I court temptation? Shall I pray daily to be led out on't, and walk into it with open eyes?”

“That is good sense anyway,” she said with consummate affectation of candour. “Tell me, have you escaped temptation here? When I am alone, my thoughts are far more wild and foolish than in company. Nay, speak sooth!”

“I must needs own I have been worse tempted here with evil imaginations than in the world.”

“There now.”

“Ay, but so were Anthony and Jerome, Macarius and Benedict. 'Twill wear off.”

“Guessing against knowledge,” she retorted, expertly dissecting his abstract theology with the grounded wisdom of the earth. “Here 'tis men folk are sillier than us that be but women. Their stomachs are too high to be taught by their eyes. A woman, if she went into a hole in a bank to escape temptation, and there found it, would just lift her farthingale and out on't.”

“Nay, I grant humility and a teachable spirit are the roads to wisdom; but here I wrestle but with imagination. At Gouda she I love... she will tempt a weak soul.”

“Speak for yourself, my lad,” she countered, her nostrils flaring, eyes flashing with the glorious, untamed fire of her ancestors. “Think you the road to folly is beaten for you more than another? If you, that have been my husband, and are my child's father, were to offer to humble me so in mine own eyes, and thine, and his, either I should spit in thy face, Gerard, or I should snatch the first weapon at hand and strike thee dead.”

Gerard pondered her fierce sincerity quietly. “I had not the sense to see that,” he admitted.

Recovering her composure, she shifted the timbre of her voice to something softer. “Ask me of thy folk.”

“My father?”

“Warms to thee and me. Poor soul, a drew glaive on those twain that day, but Jorian Ketel and I we mastered him, and he drove them forth his house for ever.”

“That may not be; he must take them back.”

“That he will never do for us. He is dour as iron; yet would he do it for one word from one that will not speak it. The vicar of Gouda. The old man will be at the manse to-morrow, I hear.”

“How you come back to that.”

“Forgive me. My poor Gerard, Kate hath left her trouble behind her.”

“Alas! What, hath ill befallen e'en that sweet lily?”

“Be calm, sweetheart. She was took wi' a languor and a loss of all her pains.” Margaret spoke with the meticulous care of an elder reciting a sacred oral history, preserving the linguistic nuances of their village gossips. “Joan Ketel, which is an understanding woman, she looked at her and said, 'Down sun, down wind!' And one very ancient woman said, 'She will die as easy as she lived hard.' And she lay painless best part of three days, a sipping of heaven afore-hand. When she was just parting, she asked for 'Gerard's little boy,' and pointed to a drawer. It was two gold angels thou hadst given her years ago. We put the pieces in his two hands, and when his little fingers closed on them, she smiled content. And so she gave her earthly treasures to her favourite's child, and her immortal jewel to God, and passed so sweetly we none of us knew justly when she left us.”

Gerard wept. “Oh, how the affections of earth curl softly round my heart! Speak on, sweet Margaret; at thy voice the past rolls its tides back upon me; the loves and the hopes of youth come fair and gliding into my dark cell, on waves of memory and music.”

“Kate cried a little when she first took ill at you not being there to close her eyes.” He groaned. “Well, then, know that thy brother Sybrandt lies in my charge with a broken back, fruit of thy curse.”

“Mea culpa! mea culpa!” he cried, the Latin a sterile substitute for true, actionable grace.

“He is very penitent. I took him to Gouda manse yestreen. I said to myself, Gerard will thank me for taking Sybrandt to die under his roof; he will not beat his breast and cry mea culpa, yet grudge three footsteps to quiet a withered brother on his last bed. He is not a hypocrite, a thing all pious words and uncharitable deeds.”

Gerard staggered at the thrust. 

“Thy mother too is waiting for thee,” Margaret continued. “She will not sleep this night. She suffered for thee, and nursed thee, and tended thee. Priest, monk, hermit, call thyself what thou wilt, to her thou art but one thing; her child.”

Then Margaret delivered the hammer blow, striking at the very root of his spiritual egotism. She argued, in the straightforward, evocative Dutch that was their birthright, that isolating oneself in a cave to starve the senses was playing the devil’s game. The sensory world, the acoustic and physical community of men, was not a distraction but the very medium of divine work. 

“To whom hast thou preached these seven months? To bats and owls?” she demanded. “At Gouda is not one sheep in evil plight, but a whole flock, all for the want of a shepherd. Where is their shepherd? Lurking in a den like a wolf. You Gerard Eliassoen must love something; 'tis in your blood. You court the young of birds, and abandon your own young? Birds need but bodily food. But that sweet dove upon thy knee, he needeth not carnal only, but spiritual food. Since the sheep committed to thy charge bleat for thee—since duty to thy child calls thee—since thy grey-haired mother watches for thee in dole and care at Gouda manse, take thou in thine arms the sweet bird wi' crest of gold, and come with me!”

“IT IS THE VOICE OF AN ANGEL!” cried Clement loudly, the oppressive silence of the cave finally shattered by a sound of true, resonant conviction.

The battle was won. Margaret lingered to select the Vulgate and the psaltery. The breastplate and the cilice of bristles—instruments of bodily starvation and sensory denial—she took and dashed with feeble ferocity on the stone floor. 

“I am but a woman,” she coloured, seeing him watch her. “'Little' will still be 'spiteful.'”

They took the road to Gouda parsonage under a canopy of bright moon and stars. The acoustic environment transformed; the dead silence of the woods gave way to the subtle, harmonic rustle of the living valley. 

Suddenly Gerard stopped. “My poor little birds! They will miss their food. I feed them every day.”

“The child hath a piece of bread in his cowl. Take that, and feed them now.”

“Nay, I will not,” Gerard said, staring at the hearty, crusty rye meant for his son. “He is as innocent, and nearer to me and to thee.”

Margaret drew a long breath. “'Tis well. Hadst taken it, I might have hated thee.”

When they had gone a quarter of a mile, Gerard sighed, his unnourished muscles failing. “Margaret, I must e'en rest; he is too heavy for me.”

“Then give him me. Alas! I mind when thou wouldst have run with the child on one shoulder, and the mother on t'other.” She took the boy effortlessly, carrying the weight of their shared generations.

“I trow,” Gerard said, looking down at his weakened limbs, “overmuch fasting is not good for a man.”

“A many die of it each year, winter time,” she replied, condemning his neglect of the earth's sustaining bounty. 

When they had gone nearly a mile, he noted with surprise, “You thought it was but two butts' length.”

She turned on him the face of a Madonna. “I lied,” she said sweetly, embracing the untranslatable pragmatism of her matriarchal wisdom. “And to save your soul and body, I'd maybe tell a worse lie than that, at need. Three, without a lie.”

In a few minutes, they came up to the manse. A candle burned in the vicar's parlour. 

“She is waking still,” whispered Margaret. 

“Beautiful! beautiful!” said Clement, stopped by the sight of it. “That little candle... it delighteth the eyes, and warmeth the heart of those outside.”

“Come, and I'll show thee something better,” she said, leading him to the window. Catherine knelt on a hassock. 

“Folk can pray out of a cave,” whispered Margaret. “And hit heaven with their prayers; for 'tis for a sight of thee she prayeth. She is not the woman you knew her; her children's troubles have greatly broken the brisk, light-hearted soul.”

Margaret gave one sharp tap at the window—a sharp, percussive note that sang of homecoming. “Mother!” she cried in a loud, expressive voice that required no translation. 

Catherine half rose as the door burst open. Clement flung himself wildly at her knees, and she uttered a cry only a mother could produce, a sound vibrating with the purest acoustic truth: “Ah! my darling, my darling!” She clung sobbing round his neck, seeing neither hermit nor monk, but just her child. 

Margaret entered with wet eyes. A holy calm settled on them, restoring the psychological well-being that had been fractured by months of isolation and silence. They sat hand in hand, murmuring in their native tongue, a sweet and loving converse. In the middle sat the returned son, drinking in their affection, and soon, he was forced by these traditional agrarian women to partake in a deeply nourishing meal—a restorative pottage of slow-simmered root vegetables, marrow, and dense, crusty bread that sang of toasted grain, woodsmoke, and the profound, sustaining love of the hearth. Re-anchored to the sensory world, he was packed off to a snowy bed, and by and by awoke, friar and hermit no more, Clement no more, but Gerard Eliassoen, parson of Gouda.
