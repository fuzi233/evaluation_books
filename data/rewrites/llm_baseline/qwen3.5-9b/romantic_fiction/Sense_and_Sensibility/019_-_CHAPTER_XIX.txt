Edward stayed at the cottage for a full week. Mrs. Dashwood urged him earnestly to remain longer, yet he seemed determined to depart just as his time among them reached its peak. For the final few days, though his spirits remained somewhat uneven, they had noticeably brightened. He grew increasingly fond of the house and its surroundings, sighing whenever anyone mentioned his departure. He insisted that his schedule was entirely free, even doubting where he would go once he left, yet he knew he must go. No week had ever flown by so quickly; he could scarcely believe it was truly over. He spoke often of this, along with other remarks that revealed the true shift in his feelings, contradicting his outward resolve. He found no joy at Norland, and he loathed life in town, yet somehow he was destined for one of those places. He cherished their kindness above all else, and his greatest happiness lay simply in being with them. Still, despite their wishes and his own reluctance, and without any restriction on his time, he had to leave.

Elinor attributed everything surprising about his behavior to his mother's influence. It was fortunate for her that her son's mother was so poorly understood by everyone else, serving as a convenient excuse for any strange conduct on Edward's part. Though disappointed and vexed, and occasionally displeased by his inconsistent treatment of herself, Elinor was generally inclined to view his actions with generous understanding—a leniency she had previously struggled to muster regarding Willoughby. His lack of spirit, openness, and consistency was usually blamed on his lack of independence and his awareness of Mrs. Ferrars' strict disposition and schemes. The brevity of his visit and his steadfast intention to leave stemmed from the same constrained inclination, the same unavoidable need to compromise with his mother. The age-old conflict between duty and desire, parent and child, was the root cause. She wished desperately to know when this struggle would end, when this opposition would yield, when Mrs. Ferrars would reform, and when her son would finally be free to be happy. Forced to turn from these vain hopes for comfort, she clung to her renewed faith in Edward's affection, recalling every sign of regard in his looks and words during his stay at Barton, and above all, the flattering ring he constantly wore on his finger.

"I think, Edward," Mrs. Dashwood remarked one last morning at breakfast, "that you would be a happier man if you had a profession to occupy your time and lend interest to your plans. Yes, there might be some inconvenience to your friends; you wouldn't be able to give them so much of your time. But (smiling) you would be materially benefited in at least one particular: you would know where to go when you leave us."

"I assure you," Edward replied, "that I have long considered this very point, just as you do now. It has been, and remains, and likely will always be, a heavy misfortune for me to have no necessary business to engage me, no profession to provide employment or any semblance of independence. Unfortunately, my own fastidiousness and that of my friends have rendered me what I am: an idle, helpless creature. We could never agree on a career. I always preferred the church, and still do. But that was not 'smart' enough for my family. They recommended the army. That was far too flashy for me. The law was deemed sufficiently genteel; many young men with chambers in the Temple made excellent appearances in high society and drove about town in stylish carriages. Yet I had no inclination for the law, even in this less abstract branch that my family approved. As for the navy, it certainly had fashion on its side, but I was too old when the topic was first raised to join it. Eventually, since there was no pressing necessity for me to hold any profession—I could be just as dashing and expensive without a red coat as with one—idleness was declared, overall, the most advantageous and honorable path. A young man of eighteen is rarely so eager to be busy as to resist his friends' pleas to do nothing. Thus, I was entered at Oxford and have been properly idle ever since."

"The consequence of which, I suppose, will be," Mrs. Dashwood countered, "that since leisure has not promoted your own happiness, your sons will be brought up to pursue as many careers, employments, professions, and trades as Columella's."

"They will be brought up," Edward said seriously, "to be as unlike myself as possible—in feeling, in action, in condition, in everything."

"Come, come!" Mrs. Dashwood dismissed him gently. "This is merely an overflow of your current lack of spirits, Edward. You are in a melancholy mood and imagine that anyone unlike yourself must be happy. Remember that the pain of parting from friends is felt by everyone, regardless of their education or station. Know your own happiness. You lack nothing but patience—or, if you prefer a more captivating term, call it hope. Your mother will secure you that independence you crave; it is her duty, and it will surely become her happiness to ensure your entire youth is not wasted in discontent. What cannot a few months achieve?"

"I think," Edward replied, "that I may defy many months to produce any good for me."

This despondent turn of mind, though hidden from Mrs. Dashwood, caused additional pain to everyone during the farewell, leaving an uncomfortable impression particularly on Elinor. Determined to overcome it and to avoid appearing to suffer more than the rest of her family, she did not follow the method Marianne had wisely adopted on similar occasions: seeking silence, solitude, and idleness to amplify and fix her sorrow. Their approaches differed as much as their goals, yet both served their purposes perfectly.

As soon as Edward left the house, Elinor sat at her drawing table, occupied throughout the day. She neither sought nor avoided mentioning his name, appearing almost as interested as ever in the family's general affairs. If this conduct failed to diminish her own grief, it at least prevented its unnecessary growth and spared her mother and sisters much worry. Such behavior, so diametrically opposed to Marianne's, held no merit in Marianne's eyes, just as Marianne's own had seemed flawed to Elinor. The task of self-command came easily to her; with strong affections, it was impossible, but with calm ones, it required no virtue. That her sister's affections *were* calm, Elinor dared not deny, though she blushed to admit it. Regarding the strength of her own, she offered striking proof by continuing to love and respect her sister, despite the mortifying realization of their difference.

Without isolating herself from her family or retreating into determined solitude, Elinor found that every day afforded her ample leisure to reflect on Edward and his behavior, exploring every nuance her varying moods could conjure—with tenderness, pity, approval, criticism, and doubt. There were countless moments when, due to the absence of her mother and sisters or the nature of their tasks, conversation was stifled, creating an atmosphere of enforced solitude. Her mind was inevitably free; her thoughts could not be chained elsewhere. Consequently, the past and future concerning such an engaging subject filled her presence, forcing her attention and consuming her memory, reflection, and imagination.

One morning, shortly after Edward's departure, a reverie at her drawing table was broken by the arrival of visitors. Happening to be alone, she noticed the small gate at the entrance of the green court close, catching her eye toward the window. She saw a large group approaching the door. Among them were Sir John and Lady Middleton, along with Mrs. Jennings, but there were also two strangers, a gentleman and a lady, whom she did not recognize. Sitting near the window, as soon as Sir John spotted her, he left the others to handle the knock, stepping across the grass and compelling her to open the casement to speak to him, despite the narrow gap making it nearly impossible to converse without being overheard.

"Well," he said, "we've brought you some strangers. How do you like them?"

"Hush! They'll hear you."

"Never mind if they do. It's only the Palmers. Charlotte is very pretty, I can tell you. You can see her if you look this way."

Certain she would see her within moments without granting that courtesy, Elinor politely requested to be excused.

"Where is Marianne? Has she fled because we arrived? I see her instrument is open."

"She is walking, I believe."

Mrs. Jennings then joined them, lacking the patience to wait for the door to open before sharing her tale. She shouted toward the window, "How do you do, my dear? How is Mrs. Dashwood? And where are your sisters? What! All alone! You'll be delighted with a bit of company to keep you. I've brought my other son and daughter to see you. Just think of their sudden arrival! I thought I heard a carriage last night while we were drinking tea, but it never occurred to me it could be them. I was thinking only of whether it mightn't be Colonel Brandon returning. So I said to Sir John, 'I do think I hear a carriage; perhaps it is Colonel Brandon come back again.'"

Elinor was compelled to turn away mid-story to welcome the rest of the party. Lady Middleton introduced the two strangers; Mrs. Dashwood and Margaret descended the stairs simultaneously, and they all sat staring at one another while Mrs. Jennings continued her narrative as she walked through the passage into the parlor, followed by Sir John.
