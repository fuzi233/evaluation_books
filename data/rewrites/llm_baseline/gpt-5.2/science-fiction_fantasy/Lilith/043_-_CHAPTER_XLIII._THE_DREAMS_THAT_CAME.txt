Existence came back to me as a taste comes back—first the bare fact of it on the tongue, then the memory of what it once meant. I knew myself alive, and with that knowing there lay upon me the profound, the infinite cold.

It was a blessing so intense my waking heart could never quite recover the shape of it. Warmth held no sweetness for me then. I knew I had once delighted in it—had leaned into it as into a hearth-scented room after rain—but I could not remember how. The cold had done what no counsel, no book, no brave resolution had ever done: it had quieted every care, dissolved every pain, smoothed the ragged edges of sorrow as frost smooths a churned field into one clean sheet.

And yet I must not call it mere comfort, as if it were a blanket laid upon misery. Sorrow itself was being swallowed up in the life drawing near—life that would restore every good and lovely thing a hundredfold, and not as an echo but as the thing itself, newly made and more itself than ever.

I lay at peace, full of the quietest expectation. The damp odours of the earth rose to me—loam and root and the dark sweetness of decay that is not corruption but pantry-work for spring. In that great stillness I became aware, not with sight but with some inward sense like smell turned to knowing, of the souls of primroses, daisies, and snowdrops waiting under the crusted ground. They waited as dough waits under cloth: patient, alive, already pledged to rise when the warmth comes.

How shall I tell the delight of that frozen, conscious sleep? I had no more to stand up. No more to choose and strain and carry myself. I had only to lie stretched out and still, as one laid on a table after long labour, hands at rest. How cold I was, words cannot reach it; yet colder and colder I grew—and welcomed it the more, as one welcomes the hush that follows when the last clanging machine is shut down and the field is given back its own small sounds.

By degrees I was less and less conscious of myself, and more and more conscious of bliss—unimaginable, yet felt. I had not made it. I had not prayed for it. It was mine in virtue of being; and being was mine because a Will dwelt in mine, as yeast lives hidden in the lump, making it more than flour and water.

Then the dreams began to arrive—and they came crowding, like generations entering a kitchen one after another, each with some inherited gesture, some old recipe of feeling.

I lay naked on a snowy peak. Below me white mist heaved like a billowy sea. The cold moon hung in the same air as I, and above the moon and me lay an even colder sky, in which both the moon and I seemed to dwell as small, pale embers in a vast ash.

I was Adam, waiting for God to breathe into my nostrils the breath of life.

I was not Adam, but a child in the bosom of a mother whose whiteness was radiant as new milk in a white bowl, light coming from her as warmth comes from bread when it is broken.

I was a youth on a white horse, leaping from cloud to cloud in a blue heaven, hasting calmly toward some blessed goal as if the very air were a road laid down for him.

For centuries I dreamed—or was it chiliads? or only one long night? Why ask? Time had nothing to do with me. I was in the land of thought—farther in, higher up than the seven dimensions, the ten senses. I think I was where I am—in the heart of God.

I dreamed away dim cycles in the centre of a melting glacier, the spectral moon drawing nearer and nearer. Wind and the welter of a torrent grew in my ears. I lay and heard them: wind and water and moon together sang, a peaceful waiting for a redemption drawing near. In that dream the world was made chiefly of sound—no harshness, no iron grinding, no invented clatter to sell itself as music. The wind had room to speak its own language; the water had room to answer; the moon, though it made no noise, seemed to press a rhythm upon both. Their speech was not my tongue, yet I understood it in the way one understands a mother’s humming before one knows words.

Cycles, I say—but perhaps it was the solemn, æonian march of a second, pregnant with eternity.

Then, suddenly—without once troubling the conscious bliss that held me—every wrong I had ever done, from far beyond my earthly memory down to the last, was with me.

Fully in every wrong lived the conscious I: confessing, abjuring, lamenting the dead, making atonement with each person I had injured, hurt, or offended. Every human soul to which I had caused a troubled thought was now unspeakably dear to me. I humbled myself before it, agonising to cast from between us the clinging offence—as one scrubs at a stain that has crept into the grain, until the very water runs clear again.

I wept at the feet of the mother whose commands I had slighted. With bitter shame I confessed to my father that I had told him two lies and long forgotten them. Now I had for long remembered them, and kept them like stones in my pocket, to crush at last at his feet.

I became the eager slave of all whom I had thus or anyhow wronged. Countless services I devised to render them. For this one I would build such a house as had never grown from the ground—stone laid true, beams scented of resin, hearth wide enough for a whole clan’s winter. For that one I would train such horses as had never yet been seen in any world—patient, swift, understanding, their hoofs falling with that good, quiet certainty that soothes the heart. For a third I would make such a garden as had never bloomed: haunted with still pools that held the sky like broth holds fragrance, alive with running waters whose music would cleanse the mind of every false note.

I would write songs to make their hearts swell, and tales to make them glow. I would turn the forces of the world into channels of invention to make them laugh with the joy of wonder. Love possessed me. Love was my life. Love was to me, as to Him that made me, all in all.

Suddenly I found myself in a solid blackness, upon which even that ghost of light that dwells in the caverns of the eyes could cast no fancied glimmer. Yet my heart, which feared nothing and hoped infinitely, was full of peace. I lay imagining what the light would be when it came, and what new creation it would bring with it.

Then, without conscious volition, I sat up and stared about me.

The moon was looking in at the lowest, horizontal, crypt-like windows of the death-chamber. Her long light slanted, I thought, across the fallen, still-ripening sheaves of the harvest of the great husbandman.

But no—those sheaves were gone.

Gathered in, or swept away by chaotic storm, not a sacred sheaf was there. My dead were gone. I was alone.

Desolation, dread—depths yet deeper than any I had known—opened under me. Had there never been any ripening dead? Had I but dreamed them and their loveliness? Why then these walls? Why the empty couches? No; they were all up—they were all abroad in the new eternal day and had forgotten me. They had left me behind, alone.

Tenfold more terrible was the tomb with its inhabitants away. The quiet ones had made me quiet with their presence, had pervaded my mind with their blissful peace. Now I had no friend; my lovers were far from me. For a moment I sat staring, horror-stricken. I had been alone with the moon on a mountain-top in the sky; now I was alone with her in a huge cenotaph, and even she seemed to be staring about, seeking her dead with a ghastly gaze.

I sprang to my feet and staggered from the fearful place.

The cottage was empty.

I ran out into the night. No moon was there. Even as I left the chamber, a cloudy rampart had risen and covered her. Yet a broad shimmer came from far over the heath, mingled with a ghostly murmuring music—as if the moon were raining a light that plashed as it fell, and the land caught it and answered.

I ran stumbling across the moor and found a lovely lake, margined with reeds and rushes. The moon behind the cloud was gazing upon the monsters’ den—water clear as glass and very still. But the musical murmur went on, filling the quiet air, drawing me after it as surely as the scent of baking draws one down a corridor.

I walked round the border of the mere and climbed the range of hills.

What rose to my eyes was like the return of a long-forgotten language to a people’s throat.

The whole expanse where, with hot aching feet, I had crossed and recrossed the deep-scored channels and ravines of the dry river-bed was alive with streams, with torrents, with still pools—a river deep and wide. The moon flashed on the water; the water answered the moon with flashes of its own—white splintering light breaking everywhere from its rock-encountered flow. From its bosom arose a great jubilant song, the song of new-born liberty.

I stood gazing, and my heart began to exult. My life was not all failure. I had helped to set this river free.

My dead were not lost. I had but to go after and find them. I would follow and follow until I came whither they had gone. Our meeting might be thousands of years away, but at last—at last—I should hold them. Wherefore else did the floods clap their hands?

I hurried down the hill: my pilgrimage was begun. In what direction to turn my steps I knew not, but I must go, and go, till I found my living dead.

A torrent ran swift and wide at the foot of the range. I rushed in: it laid no hold upon me. I waded through it. The next I sprang across; the third I swam; the next I waded again. Now and then I stopped to gaze on the ceaseless flash and flow, and to hearken to the multitudinous broken music. Every little fall had its own dialect; every eddy repeated a phrase differently; the whole wide speech of the river was one, and yet never the same twice. At times some incipient air seemed about to draw itself clear of the dulcet confusion, only to merge again in the consorted roar.

At moments the world of waters would invade as if to overwhelm me—not with the force of its seaward rush, nor the shouting of its liberated throng, but with the greatness of silence wandering into sound: that hush at the heart of singing, the space that makes music possible. I thought then of the dead house I had fled—its absence of any living noise more dreadful than a storm—and I understood how sound may be mercy, and how the wrong kind of sound, the forced and manufactured clang of it, can be tyranny over the spirit.

As I stood lost in delight, a hand was laid on my shoulder.

I turned and saw a man in the prime of strength, beautiful as if fresh from the heart of the glad Creator—young like Him who cannot grow old. He stood large and grand, clothed in a white robe, with the moon in his hair.

I looked: it was Adam.

“Father,” I cried, “where is she? Where are the dead? Is the great resurrection come and gone? The terror of my loneliness was upon me; I could not sleep without my dead; I ran from the desolate chamber. Whither shall I go to find them?”

“You mistake, my son,” he answered, and his voice, even in its breath, was consolation—as if one had been starving on dry crusts of fear and suddenly there came warm broth, plain, honest, strengthening. “You are still in the chamber of death, still upon your couch, asleep and dreaming, with the dead around you.”

“Alas! when I but dream, how am I to know it? The dream best dreamed is the likest to the waking truth!”

“When you are quite dead,” he said, “you will dream no false dream. The soul that is true can generate nothing that is not true; neither can the false enter it.”

“But, sir,” I faltered, “how am I to distinguish betwixt the true and the false, where both alike seem real?”

“Do you not understand?” he returned with a smile that might have slain all the sorrows of all his children. “You cannot perfectly distinguish between the true and the false while you are not yet quite dead; neither indeed will you when you are quite dead—that is, quite alive—for then the false will never present itself. At this moment, believe me, you are on your bed in the house of death.”

“I am trying hard to believe you, father. I do indeed believe you, although I can neither see nor feel the truth of what you say.”

“You are not to blame that you cannot. And because even in a dream you believe me, I will help you. Put forth your left hand open, and close it gently: it will clasp the hand of your Lona, who lies asleep where you lie dreaming you are awake.”

I put forth my hand. It closed on the hand of Lona—firm and soft and deathless. It was like taking hold of a thing one has thought lost beyond all measuring: like finding, in a world stripped bare, the one familiar tool whose weight in the palm says, You can still make, you can still mend.

“But, father,” I cried, “she is warm!”

“Your hand is as warm to hers. Cold is a thing unknown in our country. Neither she nor you are yet in the fields of home, but each to each is alive and warm and healthful.”

My heart was glad. Yet immediately a sharp-stinging doubt rose, as if some bitter herb had been bruised under the sweetness.

“Father,” I said, “forgive me—but how am I to know surely that this also is not a part of the lovely dream in which I am now walking with thyself?”

“Thou doubtest because thou lovest the truth,” he answered. “Some would willingly believe life but a phantasm, if only it might for ever afford them a world of pleasant dreams: thou art not of such. Be content for a while not to know surely. The hour will come—and that ere long—when, being true, thou shalt behold the very truth, and doubt will be for ever dead. Scarce, then, wilt thou be able to recall the features of the phantom.

“Thou wilt then know that which thou canst not now dream. Thou hast not yet looked the Truth in the face, hast as yet at best but seen him through a cloud. That which thou seest not, and never didst see save in a glass darkly—that which, indeed, never can be known save by its innate splendour shining straight into pure eyes—that thou canst not but doubt, and art blameless in doubting until thou seest it face to face, when thou wilt no longer be able to doubt it.

“But to him who has once seen even a shadow only of the truth, and, even but hoping he has seen it when it is present no longer, tries to obey it—to him the real vision, the Truth himself, will come, and depart no more, but abide with him for ever.”

“I think I see, father,” I said. “I think I understand.”

“Then remember, and recall. Trials yet await thee—heavy, of a nature thou knowest not now. Remember the things thou hast seen. Truly thou knowest not those things, but thou knowest what they have seemed, what they have meant to thee. Remember also the things thou shalt yet see. Truth is all in all; and the truth of things lies, at once hid and revealed, in their seeming.”

“How can that be, father?” I said, and raised my eyes with the question—for I had been listening with downbent head, aware of nothing but the voice of Adam.

He was gone.

In my ears there was nought but the sounding silence of the swift-flowing waters. I stretched forth my hands to find him, but no answering touch met their seeking. I was alone—alone in the land of dreams.

To myself I seemed wide awake, yet I believed I was dreaming because he had told me so. Even in a dream, however, the dreamer must do something. He cannot sit down and refuse to stir until the dream grows weary and departs. I took up my wandering and went on.

Many channels I crossed, and came to a wider space of rock. There, dreaming I was weary, I laid myself down and longed to be awake. I was about to rise and resume my journey when I discovered I lay beside a pit in the rock, whose mouth was like that of a grave—deep and dark, no bottom visible.

In the dreams of my childhood I had found that a fall invariably woke me. Therefore, when I desired to discontinue a dream, I would seek some eminence whence to cast myself down that I might wake. With one glance at the peaceful heavens and one at the rushing waters, I rolled myself over the edge of the pit.

For a moment consciousness left me.

When it returned, I stood in the garret of my own house—in the little wooden chamber of the cowl and the mirror.

Unspeakable despair invaded me with the knowledge: between me and my Lona lay an abyss impassable, a distance no chain could measure. Space and Time and Mode of Being—like walls of adamant, unscalable, impenetrable—shut me in from that gulf. True, it might yet be in my power to pass again through the door of light and journey back to the chamber of the dead; and if so, I was parted from that chamber only by a wide heath and by the pale starry night betwixt me and the sun, which alone could open for me the mirror-door, and was now far away on the other side of the world.

But an immeasurably wider gulf sank between us in this: that she was asleep and I was awake. That I was no longer worthy to share with her that sleep, and could no longer hope to awake from it with her.

For truly I was much to blame. I had fled from my dream. The dream was not of my making any more than was my life: I ought to have seen it to the end. In fleeing from it, I had left the holy sleep itself behind me.

I would go back to Adam, tell him the truth, and bow to his decree.

I crept to my chamber, threw myself on my bed, and passed a dreamless night.

I rose and listlessly sought the library. On the way I met no one. The house seemed dead. I sat down with a book to await the noontide: not a sentence would yield itself to me. Words that once had been bread turned now to sawdust in the mouth. The mutilated manuscript offered itself from the masked door; the sight of it sickened me—what to me was the princess with her devilry? Language itself seemed to have lost its savour, as if some dominant, empty lexicon had swept through and flattened every living turn of phrase, leaving only bright labels without the thing they once named.

I rose and looked out of a window.

It was a brilliant morning. With a great rush the fountain shot high and fell roaring back. The sun sat in its feathery top. Not a bird sang; not a creature was to be seen. Raven nor librarian came near me. No step creaked; no door whispered; no domestic stir—no kettle beginning its low song, no broom on boards, no human breath of habit—came up from the house below. The world was dead about me, and the loudest thing in it was the fountain’s continual fall, a kind of splendour that could not console.

I took another book, sat down again, and went on waiting.

Noon drew near.

I went up the stairs to the dumb, shadowy roof. I closed behind me the door into the wooden chamber and turned to open the door out of a dreary world. I left the chamber with a heart of stone.

Do what I might, all was fruitless. I pulled the chains; adjusted and re-adjusted the hood; arranged and re-arranged the mirrors. No result followed. I waited and waited to give the vision time. It would not come. The mirror stood blank; nothing lay in its dim old depth but the mirror opposite and my own haggard face—eyes like emptied cups, cheeks without colour, the mouth set as if it had forgotten the taste of any true thing.

I went back to the library. There the books were hateful to me—for I had once loved them. Now they seemed like preserved herbs whose scent is gone, their labels still legible, their power dead.

That night I lay awake from down-lying to uprising, and the next day renewed my endeavours with the mystic door. But all was yet in vain. How the hours went I cannot tell. No one came nigh me. Not a sound from the house below entered my ears—not even the small, necessary noises by which a place confesses it is inhabited. I did not once feel weary—only desolate, drearily desolate, as if all the world’s living speech had withdrawn and left me among mere echoes.

I passed a second sleepless night.

In the morning I went for the last time to the chamber in the roof, and for the last time sought an open door. There was none.

My heart died within me.

I had lost my Lona. Was she anywhere? Had she ever been, save in the mouldering cells of my brain?

“I must die one day,” I thought, “and then, straight from my death-bed, I will set out to find her. If she is not, I will go to the Father and say—‘Even thou canst not help me: let me cease, I pray thee!’”
