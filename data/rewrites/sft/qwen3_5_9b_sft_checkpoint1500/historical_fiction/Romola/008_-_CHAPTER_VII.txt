Bartolommeo Scala, the secretary of the Florentine Republic, resided in a stately mansion near the Porta Pinti, now known as the Casa Gherardesca. His coat of arms displayed an azure ladder crossing a golden field, topped with the motto *Gradatim*—"Step by Step"—emblazoned above the gate. This heraldic declaration served as proof to all passersby that the miller's son had climbed to his high station solely through his own exertions, a truth he proclaimed without shame. Though Scala possessed a vanity matched only by his pompous demeanor, he remained fundamentally honest; he genuinely believed in his own worth and saw no necessity for hypocrisy. By this time, he had ascended the highest rung of his ladder. Having held the secretaryship for twenty years, he had frequently delivered orations from the *ringhiera*, the balcony of the Old Palace, before visiting princes, with the Republican Lion wearing his golden crown and the crowd shouting "Viva Messer Bartolommeo!" He had served on an embassy to Rome, where he was appointed Titular Senator, Apostolic Secretary, and Knight of the Golden Spur. Eight years prior, he had become Gonfaloniere, the ultimate pinnacle of a Florentine citizen's ambitions. Meanwhile, his wealth had grown inexorable, and his gout had deepened, following the pattern common to successful mortals. The Knight of the Golden Spur was often forced to sit with his helpless, cushioned foot beneath the magnificent loggia he had constructed, overlooking the expansive gardens and lawns behind his palace.

On the day he granted an audience to Tito Melema, the sun of late May warmed the flowers and grass beyond the pleasant shade of his loggia. Scala had discarded his too-stately silk lucco, draping instead a light, loose mantle over his tunic. Seated beside him were his beautiful daughter, Alessandra, and her husband, the Greek soldier-poet Marullo. Opposite them sat two friends, neither sufficiently illustrious to dominate the conversation, thereby ensuring they became attentive listeners. Yet, despite his gout and his elevated status, Messer Bartolommeo's felicity was far from complete. It was embittered by a stack of papers lying before him, mostly comprising a correspondence between himself and the scholar Poliziano. It was a peculiar habit of the era—and one difficult to credit today—to recite personal quarrels and offer scholarly visitors the entirety of a lengthy exchange. This was not the first or second instance where Scala had sought the candid opinions of his friends regarding the balance of right and wrong in roughly thirty Latin letters exchanged between himself and Poliziano. These missives sprang from certain epigrams written in a tone of playful jest. It was the chronicle of a quintessential, albeit petty, dispute, one of interest here because it provided precisely that thorn of hatred necessary, according to Nello, to stimulate the sluggish pace of the cautious horse of Friendship.

Poliziano, having been a rejected suitor for the love and hand of Scala's daughter, kept a sharp and learned tooth ready against the prosperous and presumptuous secretary, who had refused the greatest scholar of the age in exchange for a son-in-law. Scala was a meritorious public servant and, furthermore, a fortunate man—traits naturally exasperating to an offended intellectual. Moreover, Scala suffered from an irresistible urge to authorship and was a poor writer—one of those exemplary individuals who, sitting in gout-ridden slippers, composed "poetical trifles" purely for their own amusement, with no intention of publication. Consequently, he dispatched them to friends, serving as the literary journals of the fifteenth century. Scala possessed an abundance of friends eager to praise his work: companions like Ficino and Landino, amiable browsers in the Medicean garden alongside him, who deemed his Latin prose style both elegant and masculine. Furthermore, the terrible Joseph Scaliger, who was destined later to pronounce Scala totally ignorant of Latinity, was still a century away. Yet, when does the fatal coquetry inherent in superfluous authorship ever find contentment with the ready praise of friends? That critical, haughty Poliziano—a companion far from amiable—must be made aware that the solid secretary demonstrated, in his leisure hours, a delightful fertility in verse, suggesting quite clearly what heights he might reach if he were not so burdened by worldly affairs.

An ineffable moment arrived when the man you secretly hate sends you a Latin epigram containing a false gender, hendecasyllables with a questionable elision, or perhaps a toe too many, attempting poetic figures that amount to manifest solecisms. This moment had come to Poliziano. The secretary had put forth his soft head from the official shell, and the terrible lurking crab descended upon him. Poliziano had exercised the liberty of a friend, correcting Scala's mistake in gendering the *culex* (a mosquito, ubiquitous on the banks of the Arno) as inferior or feminine, all within a polite Latin epigram. Scala retaliated with a bad joke, penned in suitable Latin verses, referencing Poliziano's unsuccessful romantic suit. Matters improved. Poliziano found the verses charming and highly facetious; the greater the pity that they were seriously incorrect. Since Scala had claimed to have written them in imitation of a Greek epigram, Poliziano, maintaining friendly terms, enclosed a Greek epigram of his own on the same intriguing insect. Presumably, this was not done to humble Scala, but to instruct him. This poem contained a lively conceit regarding Venus, Cupid, and the *culex*, a motif much enjoyed at the time, grounded partly on the zoological fact that the gnat, like Venus, was born from the waters.

In reply, Scala humbly requested to note that his verses were never intended for a scholar with such delicate olfactory senses as Poliziano, who stood nearest to the perfection of the ancients and possessed a taste so fastidious that sturgeon itself would seem insipid to him. He defended his own verses, though admittedly written hastily and uncorrected, meant merely as an agreeable distraction from the summer heat for himself and friends content with mediocrity. Unlike others who courted publicity through booksellers, Scala did not seek fame. As for the Greek epigram graciously sent him, he had barely enough Greek to comprehend its meaning, let alone taste its elegance. However, he added a postscript expressing fear that his incomparable friend's comparison of the gnat to Venus, based on their shared watery origins, was somewhat ticklish. On one hand, Venus might take offense; on the other, unless the poet alluded to the doctrine of Thales regarding cold and damp origins, it seemed doubtful to Scala that such a theory applied to a creature so fond of warmth. Perhaps a fish would be a better comparison, or, considering the power of flight, an eagle. Indeed, when darkness was considered, a bat or an owl offered a less obscure and more fitting parallel, and so forth.

Here lay a splendid opportunity for Poliziano. He was unaware, he wrote, that when he presented Scala's verses, there was any question of sturgeon, but rather of frogs and gudgeons. He made short work of Scala's defense of his Latin, mercilessly mangling him for the stupid criticisms he had ventured on the Greek epigram kindly forwarded as a model. "Wretched cavils," Poliziano exclaimed. Regarding the damp origin of the gnat, there was the authority of Virgil himself, who called it the "*alumnus*" of the waters. As for what his dear, dull friend had to say about fish, eagles, and the rest, it was "nihil ad rem"; for just because an eagle flies higher, it by no means follows that a gnat cannot fly at all, and so on. He was ashamed, yet reluctant, to dwell on such trivialities and swell a gnat into an elephant. Nevertheless, for his own part, he would only add that he possessed nothing deceitful or double, nor was he to be caught by the false blandishments of those who slandered him in his absence. He agreed rather with a Homeric sentiment on this matter, furnishing a Greek quotation to serve as powder for his bullet.

The quarrel could not conclude there. The logic deteriorated further, the secretary becoming increasingly pompously self-assertive, while the scholarly poet's temper grew more venomous. Poliziano had been generously willing to hold up a mirror, hoping that the too-inflated secretary, beholding his own likeness, might be induced to cease defending his ignorant Latin against ancient authorities whose consensus transcended centuries. Unless, indeed, he designed to sink in literature in proportion as he rose in honors, allowing men of letters to feel themselves his equals by compensation. In return, Poliziano was invited to examine Scala's writings, wherein nowhere would he find a more devout admiration of antiquity. The secretary was ashamed of the age in which he lived and blushed for it. Some wished to have their works praised and exalted to the level of divine monuments, but Scala could not oblige them. As for the honors offensive to the envious, they had been well earned; witness his entire life since arriving in Florence in penury. The elegant scholar, in reply, expressed no surprise that Scala found the Age distasteful, since he himself was so distasteful to the Age. Indeed, it was with perfect accuracy that he, the elegant scholar, had called Scala a "branny monster," formed from the off-scourings of monsters, born amidst the refuse of a mill, and eminently worthy of the long-eared office of turning paternal millstones (*in pistrinis sordibus natus et quidem pistrino dignissimus*).
