I had just completed my tenure at Oxford, stepping away from academic rigor for a brief respite before formally assuming stewardship of the estate. My lineage was a stark tapestry of solitude: my father perished in childhood, and my mother followed swiftly thereafter, leaving me with few ties to the living world save for the weight of inheritance.

My knowledge of my ancestors was fragmented, limited mostly to a singular trait: an obsessive devotion to study. This genetic imperative had taken root in me, manifesting as a voracious, albeit scattered, engagement with the physical sciences. What captivated me was not merely data, but the underlying architecture of reality itself. I was perpetually attuned to anomalies—the strange resonances between disparate scientific fields, the eerie parallels drawn between empirical facts and the ethereal dreams of metaphysics. I possessed a dangerous proclivity for elevating fleeting hypotheses into rigid theories, a mental quirk that I deemed irrelevant to my current predicament.

The manor itself, like the bloodline, bore the marks of deep antiquity. While its architectural evolution need not be catalogued here, one feature demanded attention: the library. A sanctuary of knowledge predating the printing press, it had expanded organically over centuries, shifting with the tides of intellectual fashion. To inherit such a repository was to witness the fluidity of ownership; like a panoramic film reel, the collection had drifted past countless generations, now slowly passing through my hands.

Though the house underwent frequent renovations, the library acted as an invasive species, consuming room after room until it dominated the ground floor. Its central chamber was vast, the walls lined with leather-bound volumes nearly to the ceiling. Overflowing alcoves connected via a labyrinthine network of doors, archways, staircases, and passageways, creating a disorienting maze of ink and paper.

I primarily inhabited the Great Room, spending hours amidst the dust motes dancing in shafts of light, devouring texts ranging from the ancients to the modern. History, particularly the trajectory of the human mind's quest for truth, held my fascination above all else. Figures like Ptolemy, Dante, and the two Bacons resonated with me more profoundly than Darwin or Maxwell; their works felt closer to the vanishing point where ignorance broke into darkness.

On a somber August evening, rain had battered the grounds all morning, leaving the air heavy and still. As twilight approached, the clouds fractured, allowing the setting sun to bathe the room in amber. Rising to gaze out the window, I watched the fountain column gleaming in the final rays. Turning back to resume my seat, the brilliance struck a single anomaly in the wall—a portrait nestled in a small, recessed niche rather than the family gallery. It depicted an ancestor, hanging alone in shadow.

Usually unnoticed, the direct sunlight rendered the image vivid. For the first time, the painting seemed to acknowledge my gaze. Struck by a sudden, unexplainable sensation, I spun around and scanned the far end of the room. There, against the backdrop of towering shelves, a tall figure stretched upward, fingers brushing the spines of books. Before my vision could correct itself to the dimming light, the figure vanished, leaving me convinced that my nerves had played a trick.

Yet, the memory clung to me. Minutes later, needing a specific volume, I noticed a hollow space in the row where it belonged—the exact spot where I had seen the stranger searching. Scouring the area yielded nothing. But by the following morning, the volume was there, precisely where it ought to be. I knew no resident likely to seek such obscure text.

Three days later, the uncanny returned with sharper teeth.

Near the library's perimeter stood a narrow, low door leading to a closet housing the most fragile and ancient artifacts. An eccentric ancestor had once installed shallow shelves across this door, filling them with fake spines bearing humorous or nonexistent titles—a whimsical mask for the mundane. One shelf, however, betrayed a secret: atop the row, a sliver of a book protruded diagonally, its binding of limp vellum torn open to reveal parchments within.

As I read, lifting my gaze, I saw the "fake" book vanish. A surge of irritation compelled me to ring the bell. The butler arrived, his face draining of color as I inquired about the missing volume. His pallor suggested a silence too heavy for a simple accident. Though he claimed ignorance, his evasion hinted at a deeper truth.

Later that afternoon, seeking solace in reflection, I lowered my book. Instantly, I perceived the back of a slender, elderly man clad in a dark, worn coat, slipping through the masked door into the closet. I lunged forward, bursting the door open to find the interior empty. Disquiet gnawed at me; perhaps I was succumbing to the same delusion as before.

Sitting down to read, I remained tense, glancing up repeatedly. Startled from my thoughts, I leaped to my feet and sprinted to the masked door—only to find the mutilated volume firmly wedged back in its false slot.

Bewildered, I summoned the butler again, recounting the visions. He confessed that the "Old Gentleman" was a ghost story everyone eventually forgot, dismissed by the stern head of the house, Sir Ralph, as a fabrication by the maids to flirt with staff. Yet, he admitted to a lingering fear. He recalled a village elder's tale of a librarian named Mr. Raven, a confidant to the infamous Sir Upward, who read forbidden, wicked texts. They had vanished simultaneously, leaving Sir Upward lost and Mr. Raven haunting the library at irregular intervals. Some whispered he was immortal; others, like the old woman, believed a dead man returning was more plausible than a mortal living for centuries.

"The description matches perfectly," the butler murmured, staring at the locked door. "And regarding the book... I've always assumed it was a fixture." He stepped forward, pulling gently. It did not budge.

"I hope it was merely a friendly visit," he offered, a tremor in his voice.

I assured him I welcomed the phantom's company, so long as he kept his counsel. As the butler departed to verify the book's immovability, I was left alone with the hum of the library, the scent of aging paper, and the unsettling certainty that the shadows held a tenant who refused to stay buried.

Silence settled over the house like a heavy blanket, unbroken for nearly a week. By the time the stillness finally gave way to motion, I had already spent countless hours wrestling with the manuscript fragment, trying to pry loose the invisible force that bound it. My efforts were futile; the object remained stubbornly locked, defying every attempt at release.

Yet, a different purpose had been brewing within me for some time. The atmosphere inside the study closet had grown oppressive, thick with a silence that unsettled me regarding the state of the books within. One afternoon, this vague intention hardened into a firm resolve. As I rose from my chair to begin the dusting, I noticed a disturbance near the door.

It was not the old librarian himself, though he moved with a familiarity that suggested presence. Instead, I perceived a shifting shadow, taking form as a slight, stooping figure clad in a shabby dress-coat that dragged near his heels. As he walked, the coat's tails parted slightly, revealing pale, thin legs encased in black stockings and oversized feet shod in wide, slipper-like shoes.

A sudden, magnetic pull drew me after him. Whether I was chasing a phantom or a man, certainty did not matter; the impulse to follow was absolute. He slipped past me into the hall and crossed toward the base of the grand staircase. I trailed close behind, watching him ascend to the first floor, passing the chambers where the family usually gathered. He pressed onward through a broad corridor, turning down a narrower flight of steps leading to the second floor.

Following him upward, I arrived at a landing that felt entirely alien to me. It was a realm I barely knew, a labyrinth of forgotten corners. I had never known siblings to drag me through such explorations, and having been taken away as a mere child, the house had remained a closed book to me until my return a month prior.

Beyond the landing, he led me through winding passages to the foot of a steep, wooden spiral stair. With every step I climbed, the wood groaned under my weight, yet the figure ahead made no sound. Halfway up, the vision faded. At the summit, the shadowy silhouette vanished completely, leaving me alone in a space filled with lingering glooms, yet devoid of the specific entity I had pursued.

I found myself standing in the main garret, a vast, cavernous space dominated by massive timber beams and soaring rafters. Great distances stretched out around me, punctuated only by occasional doors and long corridors where faint light filtered through cobwebbed windows and small, dusky skylights. Awe mixed with a thrill of discovery washed over me; this untamed expanse was truly mine, a territory waiting to be claimed.

In the center of this desolation stood a crude enclosure built of rough, unpainted planks. Its door stood slightly ajar. Assuming the old librarian might have hidden there, I pushed it open and stepped inside.

The interior was bathed in a peculiar, deserted light—a pallid illumination that seemed to belong to a world abandoned long ago. The air carried a disconsolate quality, as if the room mourned its own existence. Dust motes swirled lazily in the shafts of sunlight piercing the gloom, illuminating a tall, narrow mirror standing against the far wall. It was an antique piece, framed in dark ebony. Perched atop the frame sat a carved black eagle with wings spread wide, clutching a golden chain in its beak, from which dangled a heavy black sphere.

For a moment, I merely observed the surface, studying its craftsmanship. Then, abruptly, the illusion shattered. The glass did not reflect the chamber behind me, nor did it show my own face. For a fleeting instant, I felt the solid walls dissolve into mist, questioning whether I had ever truly looked *into* a mirror at all.

Instead, I saw a landscape transformed. Before me lay a rugged, heather-covered wilderness. In the middle distance, jagged hills of strange, brooding geometry rose against a sky that seemed too low. "You walked through the door," a voice replied. It was rough, oddly textured, lacking the smooth cadence of a human throat.

"If you accept yourself as 'yourself,' you acknowledge you are not someone else. But do you truly know you are yourself? Are you certain you are not your father? Or, forgive me, are you not merely your own folly? Who are you, really?"

Suddenly, the fog within me thickened. I realized I could offer him no definition. Indeed, who was I? To say "I am who" was meaningless. I understood then that I had lost my connection to identity, to the fundamental distinction of being one person rather than another. The name I bore in my former life had slipped away, and I did not wish to recall it; it held no weight here, and its origin mattered not a whit. I had nearly forgotten that names were even a custom in my past world! Silence, I decided, was my wisest strategy. Before a creature like this raven, who pierced accidents to reveal essence, what words could I possibly offer?

"Look at me," he commanded. "Describe who I am."

As he spoke, he pivoted, presenting his backside. Instantly, recognition flooded me. He was no longer a bird, but a man of medium stature, thin and stooped, draped in a long, somber black tailcoat. He turned again, and the transformation reversed, rendering him a raven once more.

"I have seen you before, sir," I murmured, feeling a mixture of shame and relief.

"My apologies," I hastened to say. "I believed you were the librarian of our household. Beyond that, I know nothing."

"Why do you apologize?"

"Because I mistook you for a raven," I explained, watching him with the same intensity as before.

In truth, nobody is entirely himself, and 'himself' is nobody. There is more to this than you see now, but not more than you require."

He began to walk away, and as he did, I caught a glimpse of the librarian again. He seemed unchanged, save for having adopted his shadow as a cloak. I watched him until he vanished into the heather, unsure if distance or magic had concealed him.

Had I died? Was I wandering the realm beyond the grave, searching for a place I could call mine? The raven had urged me to act. What action could I take? Would it somehow transform me from "nobody"?

I followed the direction the librarian-raven had taken. Soon, the trees of the woods ahead revealed themselves—tall, slender pines reaching for a sky I couldn't see. Their scent, resinous and ancient, called to me, and I hurried toward them, eager to lose myself in their gloom.

Panic seized me, cold and absolute. I fled the room. "I could not," he said, shaking his feathers. "But even if I could, what greater good would you achieve? You would not know it through *yourself* and *Itself*. Nevertheless, I saw clearly that this flower was unlike any other I had known. Therefore, I understood that I must be gazing at a shadow of a prayer contained within it. "Naturally not. Nor do I understand you; I cannot read your heart or your face. When my wife and I fail to understand our children, it is simply because there are not enough of them to comprehend. Only God understands foolishness."

"Then," I said, feeling exposed and utterly worthless, "will you kindly show me the nearest way home? I know there are many paths, for I have taken two already."

"There are indeed many ways."

"Please tell me how to recognize the nearest."

"I cannot," the raven answered. "You and I use the same words with entirely different meanings. Often, we cannot tell people what they *need* to know because they *want* to know something else, and thus misunderstand everything we say. Home is always so far away in the palm of your hand, and telling you how to reach it is futile. Yet you will get there; you *must* get there; you *have* to get there. Everyone who is not at home must go home. You thought you were at home when I found you; if that truly were your home, you could not have left. Nobody leaves home. And nobody ever exists or will exist at home without having journeyed there."

"An enigma stepping on another!" I exclaimed. "I did not come here to solve riddles."

"No; but you came, and found the riddles waiting for you! Indeed, you are yourself the only riddle. What you call riddles are truths, appearing puzzling only because you are not yet true."

"Worse and worse!" I cried.

"And you *MUST* answer these riddles!" he continued. "They will keep asking themselves until you understand yourself. The universe is a riddle trying to escape, and you are holding your door shut tight against it."

"Will you not, in pity, tell me what I must do? Where I must go?"

"How could I tell *your* duty, or the path to it?"

"If I am not to go home, at least direct me to some of my kind."

"I know of none. The beings most like you lie in that direction."

He pointed with his beak. All I could see was the setting sun, blinding and white.

"Well," I said bitterly, "I cannot help feeling ill-treated—plucked from my home, abandoned in a strange world, and denied instruction on where to go or what to do!"

"You forget," said the raven, "that when I offered you hospitality and you declined, you reached what you call 'home' safely. Now you have come of your own accord."

He turned and walked slowly away, his beak pointing downward. I stood dazed. It was true I had come of my own free will, but surely not without intent to make amends? My heart ached, hollow and empty, devoid of quest, purpose, hope, or desire. I watched him recede, wishing to follow, yet knowing it was useless.

Suddenly, he lunged at a patch of earth, driving the full weight of his body onto his bill, digging vigorously for moments. Then, flapping his wings, he threw back his head, and something shot from his bill, soaring high into the air. Just then, the sun dipped below the horizon, and the air plunged into deep dusk. Yet the object glowed with a soft, radiant light, pulsing toward me like a giant firefly, yellow and warm. It soared over my head. I turned and followed it.

Here I must interrupt my narrative to note the constant struggle to articulate what cannot be spoken with even approximate precision. The things recorded here, in their nature and the nature of the creatures involved, are so inexpressibly alien to any conceivable event in this world that I can present them only by describing, in the forms and languages of earthly life, the effects they had upon me—not the things themselves, but the feelings they awakened. Even this attempt is made with a continuous, abiding sense of failure. I find it impossible to convey more than a single phase of a multitudinously complex significance, or one concentric sphere of graduated embodiment. A single thing seems to be and mean many things simultaneously, possessing an uncertain identity at their hearts that keeps constantly altering their appearance. I am often driven to record what I know to be a clumsy, doubtful representation of the mere feeling intended, for no medium of communication in this world is fit to

As the air thickened into an ink-black void and the winter pressed down with crushing speed, the fluttering fire burned with a sudden, arresting luminescence. Halting its frantic flight, it hovered, waiting. The moment I stepped beneath its radiance, it drifted forward slowly, pausing occasionally above patches of jagged rock. Every time I lifted my gaze, it appeared to swell, until finally it cast a companion shadow beside me. Clearly a bird-butterfly, it moved with a swallow-like double beat. Its wings were vast, nearly square, flashing through every hue of the spectrum. Mesmerized by their splendor, I became so transfixed by their beauty that I tripped over a low stone and collapsed, stunned.

When consciousness returned, the creature circled overhead, bathing me in a chord of light filled with multitudes of shades, including colors I had never witnessed before. I rose and continued, but unable to tear my eyes from the glowing entity, I struck my foot against a stone. Terrified of falling again, I sat down to watch the tiny glory, and a fierce longing seized me to hold it in my hand. To my utter delight, it began to descend. First slowly, then swiftly, it dropped, growing larger as it neared. I felt as though the universe's greatest treasure were offering itself to me—I stretched out my hand and caught it. But the instant I grasped it, its light vanished; everything plunged into a darkness as deep as pitch. A dead book with spread-out boards lay cold and heavy in my grip. I tossed it upward, hearing it crash into the heather below. Burying my face in my hands, I sat in a paralysis of misery.

Yet the cold bit so fiercely that, fearing freezing, I forced myself upright. The moment my feet touched the ground, a faint sensation of light ignited within me. "Is it waking?" I cried, a surge of hope piercing through me. Alas, no! It was merely the edge of a moon peering up sharply over the flat horizon. It offered me illumination, but no direction. It refused to hover over me, to wait for my stumbling steps. All it could give me was an ignorant choice.

Her face swelled to fill the sky, and I began to discern my surroundings. Westward, not far away, a range of low hills cut across the skyline. I headed for them.

What a night I endured before reaching those slopes! The moon seemed to possess knowledge, for she gazed at me with an odd intensity. Her countenance was truly ice-cold, yet brimming with interest, or perhaps curiosity. This was not the moon I knew from Earth; her features were alien, and her light even stranger. Perhaps it emanated from an unknown sun! Every time I looked up, I found her staring at me with every ounce of her power. Initially, I was annoyed, chafed by the rudeness of a fellow creature, but soon I perceived—or imagined—a certain wondering pity in her gaze. Why was I wandering in her night? There, first, did I understand the horror of being awake in the cosmos: I WAS, and I could not stop it!

As I walked, my boots left the heather, treading a bare, spongy soil that resembled dry, powdery peat. To my dismay, it gave a sudden, jarring heave beneath me. Presently, I saw what appeared to be the ripple of an earthquake moving ahead of me, shadowy in the dim moonlight. It faded into the distance, but as I stared after it, a single wave rose and crept slowly toward me. A yard or two away, it burst, and from it, scrambling and leaping, emerged an animal resembling a tiger. Clumps of mold hung from his mouth and ears, and his eyes winked and flamed as he charged, baring white teeth in a silent snarl. I stood paralyzed, conscious of neither courage nor fear. He turned his head to the earth and plunged back into it.

"That moon is affecting my brain," I muttered, resuming my journey. "What life exists here but the phantasmic—the substance of dreams? I am indeed walking in a vain show!"

So I struggled to keep my heart above the waters of fear, unaware that the being I distrusted was actually my shield from the realities I mistook for illusions. Her light restrained the monsters; without it, I would scarcely have taken a second step on that hideous ground. "I will not be appalled by that which is merely seeming!" I told myself, yet it felt like a nightmare to walk upon a sea where such grotesque fish played below. Suddenly, a few paces from me, the head of a worm began to emerge slowly from the earth, massive as a polar bear's and bearing a striking resemblance to one, complete with a white mane atop its red neck. The wriggling draws with which its colossal length freed itself were horrifying, yet I dared not look away. As soon as its tail cleared the surface, it lay as if exhausted, thrashing weakly in a futile attempt to burrow again.

"Does it feed on the dead," I wondered, "and is it powerless to harm the living? If they smell their prey and emerge, why do they leave me unharmed?"

I now know it was the moon that paralyzed them.

Throughout the long night, as I walked, hideous creatures, none exactly alike, threatened me. In some, the beauty of color heightened the loathsomeness of their shapes; one large serpent was draped from head to distant tail with feathers of glorious, vibrant hues.

Eventually, I became so accustomed to these harmless threats that I began to entertain the road with inventions of monstrosities, never suspecting that I owed every breath of life to the staring moon. Though hers was not a primal radiance, it so hindered the evil things that I walked in safety. For light remains light, however faint the reflection. How quickly would not my feet have carried me across this restless soil if I had known that, should her lamp cease to shine on this cursed spot, I would instantly fall to the mercy of those who knew no mercy, becoming the center of a writhing heap of horrors, each individual as terrifying as it had merely seemed! A fool of ignorance, I watched the descent of the weary, solemn, anxious moon across the widening vault above me, troubled only by the dread of losing my way—where, as yet, I had no path to lose.

I drew near the hills I had chosen as my goal, and she was now close to their skyline, when the soundless wallowing ceased, revealing the burrow lying motionless and bare. Then I saw, walking slowly across the light soil, the form of a woman. A white mist floated around her, constantly shifting, dissolving, and reforming into garments as it gathered to her or blew away by a wind that trailed her steps.

She was beautiful, yet her countenance held such a mixture of pride and misery that I could hardly believe what I saw. Up and down she walked, vainly trying to seize the mist and wrap it around herself. The eyes in her beautiful face were dead, and on her left side was a dark spot against which she would now and then press her hand, as if stifling pain or sickness. Her hair hung nearly to her feet, and sometimes the wind mixed it so thoroughly with the mist that I could not distinguish one from the other; but when it fell and gathered again, it shone a pale gold in the moonlight.

Suddenly, pressing both hands to her heart, she collapsed to the ground, and the mist rose from her, melting into the air. I ran to her. But she began to writhe in such agony that I stood aghast. A moment later, her legs, tearing free from her body, sped away like serpents. From her shoulders fled her arms, also transforming into serpents in terror. Then something flew up from her like a bat, and when I looked again, she was gone. The ground rose like the sea in a storm; terror seized me. I turned toward the hills and ran.

I was already on the slope of their base when the moon sank behind one of the summits, plunging me into shadow. Behind me rose a wasting, sickening cry, like the lament of frustrated desire—it was the only sound I had heard since the fall of the dead butterfly. It shook my heart like a flag in the wind. I turned, seeing many dark objects bounding after me, and scrambled for the crest of a ridge where the moon still shone. She seemed to linger there, allowing me to see enough to defend myself. Soon I gained sight of her and climbed faster.

Crossing the shadow of a rock, I heard the creatures panting at my heels. Just as the foremost threw himself upon me with a snarl of greedy hate, we rushed directly into the moon. She flashed out an angry light, and he fell from me, reduced to a bodiless blotch. Strength surged through me, and I turned on the rest. One by one, as they darted into the light, they dropped with a howl. And I saw—or fancied I saw—a strange smile upon the round face above me.

I climbed to the top of the ridge; far away, the moon shone, sinking toward a low horizon. The air was pure and strong. I descended a little way, found it warmer, and sat down to wait for the dawn.

The moon dipped below, and the world fell dark once more.

Sleep claimed me swiftly, and when consciousness returned, the sun was already burning away the last remnants of the moonlit sky. Climbing back to the vantage point, I peered down into the hollow I had traversed hours prior. It lay empty, stripped of all trace of life. Had the vast, terrifying plain ahead swarmed with creatures of insatiable hunger?

I turned my gaze toward the horizon, scanning the landscape that stood between me and salvation. It appeared a boundless desert, broken only by a distant patch of anomalous color—a potential forest. There was no sign of habitation: no smoke, no drifting dust, no shadow cast by cultivated fields. The heavens were a piercing blue, utterly devoid of clouds or even the thinnest veil of haze.

Descending, I made for that imagined green refuge. If nothing existed on this side of it, surely life pulsed within those trees. Reaching the edge of the plain, I confirmed the forest, yet as I gazed across the distance, I saw not a single rivulet or trickle. The silence was absolute, a void where the "pleasant noise of waters" ought to have been.

It took the entire daylight to traverse the arid bed, a scarred expanse of rock—flat, channeled, humped, and pinnacled—the dried-out throat of a vanished river. The channels were scored by countless ancient runs, now cracked and empty save for patches of dry moss and lichens hardened like stone. I carried with me the haunting delusion of an aural mirage, convinced I could hear the roar of many waters, though my eyes told a brutal truth.

As the sun dipped toward the western rim, I entered the treeline. Below the canopy, sunlight filtered through pillar-like trunks, painting a world of blessed shadows. I had anticipated a pine grove, but instead I found a riot of botanical forms: familiar species twisted alongside horrors beyond imagination. I collapsed beneath the sprawling boughs of a tree resembling a blooming eucalyptus. Its flowers wore a hard, skull-like calyx, topped by a lid that seemed poised to release a frothing brain of petals. From beneath the shadow of its falchion-shaped leaves, I watched the forest deepen into abyssal layers.

Soon, however, the woods began to seal themselves off. Doors and windows of foliage slammed shut, closing aisles and glades alike. Night descended instantly, bringing a cold so sharp it felt like a physical blow. How do I convey the wild, spectral quality of this place?

The great tree I leaned against rose tall before branching, yet its limbs bowed so low they threatened to cage me in. As twilight faded, the outlines of the clumpy foliage began to shift. They did not merely suggest other shapes; they *became* them. A gentle breeze stirred, setting neighboring branches rocking. Every twig and leaf joined the sway, blurring their individual motion into a collective dance. Within the shifting greens, I saw a pack of wolves struggling savagely against a wizard's invisible leash, their tension far exceeding that of any living greyhound.

Another mass of leaves, denser and darker, conjured a stable full of horses. Their heads projected outward, caparisoned and restless. Necks bobbed up and down with impatience, disrupted by the wilder swaying of the wind. These were not ordinary beasts; several were bare skulls, one tight-skinned over bone, another missing its lower jaw, hanging low in unutterable weariness before jerking upward as if trying to bite through a phantom bit. Above them, floating on the very tip of a branch, stood the form of a woman, waving her arms in an imperious, commanding gesture. The sudden definiteness of these shapes startled me, followed quickly by a profound unease. What if they overpowered my mind with a false reality? Then the darkness swallowed the scene, the wind died, and I slept.

Darkness held when I awoke, but soon a confused, rushing noise drifted from afar, mingled with faint cries. It swelled into a tumult of gathering multitudes, filling the wood. Everywhere, the sound drew closer, centering on the spot where I lay. I froze, scarcely moving a finger or toe, terrified of revealing my presence to whatever hunted here.

Slowly, the moon crested the tree line and spilled silver into the forest. With her first gleam, the noises erupted into a deafening uproar, and dim shapes materialized around me. A furious battle raged. Wild cries, roars of rage, the shock of onset, and prolonged struggles mingled with articulate words. Curses, creeds, snarls, sneers, laughter, mockery, sacred names, and howls of hate collided in a chaotic interpenetration.

Skeletons and phantoms fought in mad confusion. Swords sliced through ghosts, causing them only to shiver; maces crashed upon bones, shattering them hideously, yet not one skeleton fell or ceased fighting so long as two joints remained connected. Bones of men and horses lay scattered and heaped, ground and crushed under the hooves of indomitable spectres. White, bone-gaunt steeds charged everywhere, ridden by misty, wind-driven battle-horses. Weapons and hoofs clashed and crushed, while skeleton jaws and phantom throats swelled the tumult with the war-cry of every opinion, bad or good, that had ever bred strife and cruelty. Holy words accompanied the most hateful blows; lie-distorted truths hurled through the wind of javelins and bones.

Moments passed, and someone would suddenly turn against his comrades, fighting more wildly than before, screaming, "THE TRUTH! THE TRUTH!" One figure I noted wheeled endlessly in a circle, striking on all sides. Occasionally, a pair would sit exhausted beside one another for a minute, then rise to renew the fierce combat. No one stooped to comfort the fallen, nor stepped aside to spare him.

The moon shone until the sun rose, and all night long I glimpsed a woman moving at her will above the strife-tormented multitude. She shifted fronts, one outstretched arm urging the slaughter, the other pressed against her side. "Ye are men: slay one another!" she shouted. I saw her dead eyes and the dark spot upon her brow, recalling the vision of the previous night. This was the Battle of the Dead.

Just before sunrise, a breeze swept through the forest, carrying a voice that cried, "Let the dead bury their dead!" Instantly, the contending thousands dropped noiselessly. When the sun finally peeked over the horizon, it revealed no bones, but here and there, a withered branch.

I rose and resumed my journey through a wood as quiet as any grown from the quiet earth. The morning wind had ceased, and the trees were silent. Not a bird sang, not a squirrel, mouse, or weasel showed itself, not a moth fluttered across my path. Yet I kept a vigilant watch over myself, daring not to let my eyes rest on any forest shape. Faint sounds of mattocks, spades, and hurtling bones echoed in my mind; any moment, I feared, my eyes might open to horrors I could not bear to witness. Prudence whispered that perhaps ten thousand phantoms waited for nothing less than my consenting fancy to manifest them.

By mid-afternoon, I emerged from the wood to find a second net of dry watercourses before me. At first, I thought I had wandered off course, reversing my direction, but I soon realized I had simply reached another branch of the same ancient river-bed. Crossing it immediately, I sank to the bottom of a wide channel just as the sun set.

Sitting down to await the moon, I grew sleepy and stretched upon the moss. The moment my head touched the ground, I heard the sounds of rushing streams—all manner of sweet, watery noises. The veiled melody of molten music lured me into a dreamless sleep. When I awoke, the sun was already high, illuminating the wrinkled country in broad strips. Shadows lay across the land like stripes on the skin of some wild beast. As the sun climbed, the shadows diminished, as if the rocks were re-absorbing the darkness that had oozed forth during the night.

For years, I had cherished my Arab mare and my books more than any living man or woman. Now, at length, my soul was athirst for human presence. I longed even for those inhabitants of this alien world whom the raven had so vaguely described as being nearest to my own kind. With a heavy yet hopeful heart, and a mind haunted by the doubt of whether I was traveling in any true direction, I trudged wearily north-west and by south.

As I moved through one of the winding channels, my gaze settled upon a patch of greenery that appeared to be merely a shrub—a likely outpost guarding an unseen army hidden behind the ridge. Kneeling, I peered closer, wary of the small fruit hanging low. Unfamiliar to me, it felt dangerous to touch. Little did I know that hundreds of eyes, burning with curiosity, were watching from the shadows of the rocks, waiting to see if I would succumb to temptation.

I pressed onward, encountering plants that grew progressively larger, eventually revealing themselves not as bushes but as stunted trees. By the time I approached the second arm of the dry riverbed, the landscape was choked with them, forcing me to leap across gaps too wide to traverse otherwise. One channel roared with a sound like a flock of birds taking flight from an ivy-choked wall, though I saw no sign of life. Further along, massive trees bearing coarse, uninviting fruits lined the edge of a vast depression—the dried bed of a former lake. To the left, a dense forest spilled down into the hollow, yet within the basin itself, vegetation was dominated almost exclusively by fruit-bearers.

Descending a gentle slope of grass mixed with moss, exhaustion claimed me, and I lay down to rest. A short distance further, a diminutive tree stood laden with roses-red apples, no larger than cherries, dangling within reach. I plucked one and took a bite. The flavor was exquisite. Just as I reached for a second, a burst of clear, musical laughter shattered the quiet, startling me with pure joy.

"He likes our apples!" the voices chimed, rising in a chorus. "He likes our apples! He's a good giant! A fine giant!"

A dissenting voice cut through the praise. "He *is* a giant!"

"But size doesn't define worth!" another retorted, wise beyond their years. "It can only lead to stupidity if you let it!"

I propped myself up on an elbow, staring. Surrounding me was a motley assembly of children, ranging from toddlers learning to walk to lads of thirteen or fourteen. A select group of three or four stood slightly apart, observing with a calmness that contrasted sharply with the frenzied excitement of the mob. Their chatter flowed in rapid, complex clusters—debating, arguing, and debating again with the spirited intellect of adults, yet tempered by an infectious merriment and courtesy.

I deduced quickly that by reaching for a second apple, they believed I had proven my affection for the first. The logic eluded me, though I marveled at the caution offered by even the youngest among them. I kept silent, unwilling to frighten them; listening yielded far more wisdom than speaking would. Almost miraculously, I understood nearly every word they spoke. It was a testament to the ancient truth: understanding is often easier than loving.

Suddenly, the crowd dispersed. A sweet-faced boy, looking both innocent and mischievous, stepped forward holding a large, green apple. The noise died instantly, replaced by a hushed, expectant silence.

"Eat, good giant," he whispered.

I sat upright, accepted the offering with a grateful smile, and prepared to bite. But the moment tooth met skin, I spat the apple out and hurled it far away.

Instantly, the cry erupted again. "He loves us! He loves us!" The children swarmed me, nearly burying me under a tide of hugs. They kissed my cheeks, grabbed my hands, and scrambled up my limbs, clinging to my head and neck. Overwhelmed by the sheer number of these adorable little spirits, I collapsed onto the earth.

"Good giant!" they trilled. "We knew you'd return! You're dear and strong!"

Their chatter resumed, a joyful cacophony rising from hundreds of tiny throats. Then, abruptly, silence returned. Those nearby retreated; those climbing me climbed down, their faces shifting from mirth to genuine concern.

"Hurry up, good giant!" a little girl urged, pointing up the slope. "Quickly! Quickly! He saw you toss his apple!"

Before her words finished, I was on my feet. Standing atop the incline was a grotesque figure, perhaps a foot taller than me, dressed in a manner that suggested a jester gone wrong. He radiated hostility, yet I sensed no immediate threat—he carried no weapon, and my captors had vanished without a trace.

He began his descent, and I followed, seeking higher ground. As he neared, he growled like a wounded beast. Once we reached a level patch, I halted and waited. Drawing near, he extended a hand. Instinctively, I reached for it, but he snatched it back, feigning a blow before offering it again. The message was clear: he demanded the apple I had discarded. I recoiled, making a grimace of distaste and shaking my head.

His response was a howl of fury. "How dare you insult my gift?" he roared, implying I questioned the quality of his tree.

"One rotten apple grows on the finest branch," I replied, hoping to de-escalate.

Whether he grasped the metaphor remained unclear. He lunged forward, and I braced myself. Yet, a second giant, identical in appearance, emerged from behind me, closing the gap. As the first attacked, I parried with a solid strike to his face, but the second landed a crushing blow to the back of my skull. Together, they overpowered me.

They dragged me into the woods above the valley, where their tribe dwelled in squalid huts constructed of fallen boughs and rough stones. Inside one, they shoved me down and beat me. A woman watched with cold indifference. I realized then that gender meant little to them; they were indistinguishable in form and function, resembling a fungoid race possessed of just enough cognition to manifest anger and greed. Their diet of tubers, bulbs, and harsh fruits repulsed me, yet they took offense at any sign of disdain. Refusing to eat, I endured cuffs and kicks.

That night, I lay paralyzed on the floor, barely moving, yet I managed a slumber that brought some refreshment. At dawn, they marched me back to the valley. Binding my feet with a long rope, they forced me to take a flat stone with a jagged edge in my left hand. When I switched it to my right, they kicked me; they placed it back in my left and signaled that I must strip the bark from every branch devoid of fruit. After another kick, they abandoned me.

I set to this miserable task, hoping that compliance might buy me solitude—to observe, to plan, to escape. Fortunately, a dwarf-tree grew mere paces away. Every minute or so, I plucked a small fruit, chewing it with relish. It sustained me, restoring strength and hope amidst the drudgery.

I had barely begun my labor when a cluster of faint, chiming voices drifted near. From the tangle of saplings that carpeted the forest floor, a swarm of diminutive figures emerged—creatures they called "The Little Ones." Within minutes, dozens surrounded me, their movements a blur of frantic energy. I signaled that the giants, my masters, were merely absent and likely returning soon, but they dismissed my warning with a chorus of laughter that sounded like jangling bells.

"They cannot see us!" one chirped. "And even if they could, they are too blind to notice our feet!"

"Do you like the rope around your ankle?" another asked, eyeing the crude restraint I wore.

"I wear it so they think I am helpless," I explained, hoping to maintain the illusion of captivity.

"Perfect strategy," the girl agreed. "Just take short steps. To them, it looks like a bond, not a shackle."

As she spoke, she danced with unbridled joy. A larger sister knelt beside me, her small hands deftly undoing the clumsy knot I had struggled with all morning. Before I could protest, I was free, seated upon the earth as they offered me clusters of bright, fragrant fruit. Then the games began. They pounced, rolled, and tumbled over me in a chaotic dance of pure delight, rendering any thought of work impossible. One set grew weary, only to be replaced immediately by another, and the cycle repeated until the sun dipped below the canopy, casting long, amber shadows.

Heavy footsteps approached. The forest floor erupted into motion as the Little Ones scattered, vanishing into the undergrowth. I scrambled to retie my ankles, feeling the weight of their sudden departure.

"We must be careful," warned the eldest girl, her expression grave. "A single trampling step from one of their clumsy feet could crush a life like ours!"

"Can they not see you at all?" I asked, confused by their invisibility to the giants.

"They might spot movement," she replied softly. "If a hundred piled on top of you, it would be terrifying. They hate all living things except themselves—and, truthfully, they aren't very alive either!"

She whistled a sharp, bird-like note. Instantly, the forest fell silent. The girl, and every trace of the crowd, dissolved into the air, leaving me alone with the looming threat of my masters.

My master arrived shortly after, dragging me back to his hut. He released my ankles, tossed me inside, re-bound my feet, kicked me, and departed. At that moment, freedom was within reach. Yet, having found companions who radiated warmth and wisdom, I hesitated. How could I abandon them? Their whimsical nature had stripped away the monotony and misery of my endless toil. I resolved to stay, promising myself I would return to visit them tomorrow. And indeed, from the first silence of nightfall until dawn, I heard their whispers drifting through the walls, knowing they watched me with tender affection. They rarely allowed me solitude.

I learned little about the giants; they were too simple to harbor malice, merely stupid enough to inflict harm without intent. Often, I avoided a blow by tripping the giant, a trick that usually ended his aggression for the duration of the day. But the Little Ones were a different story. Their actions delighted me, surprising me daily. My hatred for the idea of leaving them grew with each passing hour. They possessed a profound, quiet wisdom despite their simplicity.

I slept on the bare ground, yet most nights I awoke cradled in a nest of children. They rotated shifts, crawling into my arms, the smallest nestled closest. Unconsciously, I clutched them, unaware of the specific order until the light returned. The chill of the night meant nothing when I was surrounded by their heat. In the mornings and late evenings, they always ensured I had food.

One morning, I awoke to find the space beside me empty. Soon, however, the soft rustle of approach returned. The leader, the tallest and gravest of the group whom they revered as their mother, stepped from the woods, followed by the entire tribe moving in solemn jubilation. She carried a newborn boy in her arms—a shift from the usual female infants who had preceded him. Three older sisters acted as his nurses, sharing the burden of his care with the whole community. Among the Little Ones, toys were unknown; the older tended the younger, who tended the tiniest.

Lona, the mother-figure, gently placed the infant in my lap. The baby opened his eyes, gazed at me, and drifted back to sleep.

"He loves you already," Lona whispered, her eyes shining.

"Where did you find him?" I asked.

"In the wood, naturally," she replied, her voice trembling with excitement. "We searched all night. Sometimes they are hard to find!"

"How do you know when one has arrived?"

"I don't know," she admitted, tilting her head. "Everyone rushes to tell everyone else, but no one knows who spoke first. Sometimes I think someone speaks in dreams, and another hears it half-asleep. When a baby appears, there is no time for questions. By the time we find it, it is too late."

"Do more boys arrive?"

"The babies don't come to the wood; we go to them."

"Are there more boys or girls?" I asked.

Lona frowned, knitting her brow. "I don't know."

"You can count them, surely?"

"We never count. Counting isn't smooth. We prefer not to know."

"Where do they originate?"

"From the wood. Always. There is no other place."

She knew the end of their journey but remained silent on the beginning.

"How often do you find one?"

"A happy event consumes all our joy," she said, smiling warmly. "We forget the last time. Are you glad to have him, good giant?"

"Yes, indeed!" I answered. "How do you feed him?"

Lona returned with fresh plums. She touched one to the baby's lips. "He would open wide if he were awake." She squeezed a single drop onto the fruit's skin, then held it to the infant's mouth. Without stirring, the baby began to suckle. Lona slowly extracted the pulp until only the pit remained.

"There!" she declared triumphantly. "Imagine a world of giant apples with nothing for the babies! We'd abandon it instantly. Would we not, darling? We'd leave it to the wicked giants!"

A soft, terrifying rush approached from behind, and before I could pivot, a blur of white leaped over me. It struck me squarely on the forehead, knocking me flat against the earth.

I surged upward instantly, the taste of copper filling my mouth as blood trickled down my brow. All I caught of the aggressor was a fleeting flash of whiteness before it melted back into the mist. I lunged forward, chasing the beast, my heart hammering a frantic rhythm against my ribs. I took only a few paces before the air itself seemed to tear apart with a shriek of raw despair.

My fear spiked, but I pushed harder, knowing time was running out. Within moments, a pale, hunched shape stumbled toward me through the vapor-thick moonlight. At first, I thought it another hunter, slow and deliberate in its approach, making strange, floundering leaps that suggested immense pain. I pulled back, giving it space, and waited.

As it drew nearer, the truth became clear. It moved on three legs, dragging its left forepaw high above the ground. Its skin was a blinding white, marred by dark, oval spots, and it dragged a low, wetting sound—the splash of water falling onto thirsty grass—as it moved. Beside its raised limb, a stream of crimson flowed steadily.

"It is blood," I whispered to the darkness. "Someone else fought this thing."

Yet, a sudden, crushing wave of pity washed over me. Seeing the creature in agony, wounded and helpless, I felt as if an invisible chain bound my arm. Even if I had held an axe, I could not bring myself to raise it. With broken, hobbling strides, the beast limped away, disappearing into the mist. Its blood continued to seep back into the grass, a silent testament to violence. *If it bleeds like this,* I thought, *it will soon be harmless.*

I pressed on. My mind was still fixed on the fleeing woman, and I hoped to find her deliverer.

A short distance ahead, I saw her. She sat huddled on the grass, the child cradled safely in her lap.

"Is there anything I can do?" I called out gently.

Her reaction was instantaneous. She jolted violently, preparing to spring up. I threw myself down flat, hands spread wide.

"Do not be afraid!" I shouted, keeping my voice steady. "I was tracking the beast when you found a guardian sooner! It just passed me, its leg bleeding so badly it must be dying!"

"There is little hope for that!" she stammered, her voice trembling. "Do you not know whose beast this is?"

Suspicious thoughts swirled in my mind, but I feigned ignorance. "I know nothing of this brute," I lied smoothly. "What happened to your protector?"

"What protector?" she replied, eyes wide. "I saw no one."

"Then how did the monster suffer such a wound?"

"I smashed her foot with a stone!" she cried, the word 'stone' rolling like thunder. "I hit it as hard as I could! Didn't you hear her cry?"

"Well, you are a brave soul!" I said, offering a smile I didn't feel. "I thought you were the source of the scream!"

"It was the leopardess!" she hissed.

"I've never heard an animal make such a noise!" I exclaimed. "It sounded exactly like a woman screaming in torture!"

"My voice failed me!" she sobbed, rocking slightly. "When I saw those fangs at my baby's neck, I grabbed a rock and crushed her paw!"

"Tell me about this creature," I urged, leaning forward. "I am a stranger to these lands."

"You'll learn quickly enough if you're heading for Bulika!" she snapped. "I must never return there!"

"Yes, I am going to Bulika," I confirmed. "To see the Princess."

"Hold back!" she warned, her face pale. "You'd do well to stay away... But perhaps you are...! The Princess is a kind woman, truly!"

A rustle disturbed the air. Thick clouds had rolled over the moon, blotting out the light. I feared she was rising to flee again.

"You are safe from me," I promised, standing firm. "What vow would you have me swear?"

"I know by your tongue that you are not of Bulika," she said, studying me. "I trust you... nor am I of them. They never trust anyone. Only if I could see you clearly! But I like your voice..." She paused, her gaze dropping to the child. "There, my darling sleeps. The foul beast did not harm her. Yes, it was for her sake she attacked me! She would have torn me apart if I let her take the child away!"

"Some say the Princess keeps two white leopardesses," she continued, her voice dropping to a whisper. "I know only one—with the spots. Everyone knows HER! If the Princess hears of a baby, she dispatches the beast immediately to drink its blood. Then the infant either dies or is born a fool."

She hugged the child tighter, her knuckles white. "I tried to leave with my baby, but the Princess was away, and I thought I might wait until I was strong enough. But she must have taken the beast with her, returning home just as I fled. I heard the *sniff-snuff* of the leopardess behind me, and I ran—oh, how I ran! But my darling will not die! Look, there is no mark on her!"

"Where are you taking her?" I asked.

"To a place where no one ever speaks!"

"Why is the Princess so cruel?"

"There is an ancient prophecy!" she whispered fiercely. "That a child shall be the death of her! That is why she rejects all suitors."

"But what becomes of her kingdom if she destroys every newborn?"

"She cares not for her realm!" she spat. "She sends witches to teach the women spells that repel children, forcing them to eat horrors. Some say she conspires with the Shadows to wipe out our race entirely. Every night, we hear the questing beast, lying awake in shivers. She can sense the birth of a child miles away and waits at the door. There are words of power to drive her back, but they often fail..."

She fell silent, staring into the dark. "Here I sit, talking, and the beast may already be home, waiting for her mistress to send the other after us!"

With a sharp movement, she rose to her feet.

"I doubt she will ever return home," I murmured, rising with her. "Let me carry the baby for you!"

She offered no reply. As I reached for the child, she clutched it even tighter, refusing to yield.

"How is it possible," I wondered aloud, walking beside her, "that the beast could bleed so profusely?"

"Take my counsel and do not approach the palace," she warned, her eyes darting nervously. "Night falls, and inside those walls, the dead try to scream but cannot open their mouths!"

She bid me a curt farewell. It was plain she desired no further company. I stood still, listening as her footsteps faded into the silence of the grass, leaving me alone with the shadow of the beast and the mystery of the Princess.

The disorientation within me was absolute, a swirling void of helpless impatience that drove me forward like a leaf caught in a storm. Suddenly, the silence broke. I stepped directly into the path of a leopardess, yet not the beast itself, but the trail it left—a torrent of crimson blood pouring from its paw. The fluid rushed past my ankles with the force of a mountain stream, carrying a heavy, metallic weight. An instinct, raw and unformed, screamed at me to identify the owner of this gore, urging me to flee. Yet, I stayed close to the sound, tracing the stream upward along the bank, convinced it would lead me to Bulika.

But as I walked, reason cut through the panic. No leopardess, no elephant, no titan of the pre-human world could sustain such a deluge unless every vessel in its body were shattered and it drank endlessly from the earth itself. This could not be blood. I knelt and dipped a finger into the rush. It was cool, clear, and devoid of iron. A soft, murmuring rivulet of water spilled over the grass, unchanneled and wild. Sweet as its song, I dared not drink; I pressed on, eyes fixed on the horizon, listening for the familiar hum of the hot springs that lay beneath the surface. The mere touch of this water on my skin washed away the grime of my journey, restoring my limbs until the encroaching twilight faded the sky.

Then, against the pale, bruising aurora, I saw them: the wall-towers of a city. They stood ancient, seeming older than time itself. I squinted downward, searching for the brook. It was gone. For some time, I had noted its fading cadence, but finally, I had stopped listening. Looking back, I saw the grass where it had flowed bent in permanent curves, dotted with small, glistening pools. Beyond those bends, heading toward the city, there was no trace of it. Near where I stood, the fountain's flow must have paused abruptly.

Surrounding the fortress were gardens, teeming with vegetables I did not recognize. There were no flowers, no animals, no signs of life save for the green. These gardens hugged the massive walls, separated from them by great mounds of gravel and refuse dumped from the battlements.

I approached the nearest gate. It hung ajar, neither locked nor guarded, its hinges rusted in a state of perfect stasis. Through the gap, I peered down a long, ancient street. Utter silence reigned. Was this a graveyard of souls? I turned and fled back the way I came, trudging over dust-heaps and crossing intersecting roads, refusing to return until the city stirred.

*For what purpose am I here?* the question echoed in my mind. *What do I seek?*

*I must see her.* The woman I had breathed life into. I craved no company from her; she had awakened horrors in me, making friendship—and certainly love—an impossibility. Yet, her existence had altered me, forcing me to confront a strange, magnetic influence. I needed to understand the inscrutable nature of her being, to peer into marvels beyond imagination. It was a dangerous curiosity; a man should not court temptation for the sake of knowledge. Furthermore, I had resurrected an evil force teetering on the edge of extinction. I was responsible for whatever mischief she might unleash. I had learned she hated children; perhaps the "Little Ones" were now in peril. I had sought light regarding their fate, and I needed more. I needed to know how to shield them.

At length, a faint commotion rippled through the air. I slipped through the next gate, descending a narrow thoroughfare lined with tall, looming houses. I found a seat on the base of a pillar, topped by a grotesque, bat-like creature. Soon, citizens began to drift past. I addressed one, but he returned only a coarse glare and a harsh word before vanishing into the crowd.

Rising, I navigated street after street, the avenues slowly filling with idlers. As expected, I saw no children. Eventually, near a gateway, a cluster of young men cornered me. Their features reminded me of the bad giants I had known. They circled me, staring, then pushed and shoved, throwing debris at my clothes. I endured the assault, trying to avoid provoking further hostility. I called out to passersby who seemed kindlier, but no one stopped to hear me. My appearance marked me as an outsider; to these people, poverty was a crime, deformity a tax, and sickness a burden. Above all, Princess Bulika's laws, which enforced the subjugation of the poor to enrich the wealthy, were revered.

Desperate, I sprinted away. Only one clumsy follower, a man sitting by the gate devouring bread, pursued me. In his frantic stupidity, he hurled not a stone but a lump of bread. I snatched it, and he dared not follow to retrieve it; outside the walls, everyone was a coward. I ran a few hundred yards, collapsed onto the grass, and devoured the bread. Exhaustion claimed me, and I sank into a deep, restorative sleep under the blazing sun.

Night fell, and I awoke to the silver gaze of the moon. It felt like an old friend, having witnessed my first terrifying night in this strange realm. A cold wind drifted from the gate, carrying a stench of decay, yet it failed to chill me, for the day's warmth lingered in my bones. I crept back into the city, joining the few souls crouching in corners to escape the biting blast.

Walking slowly down a long, narrow street, I saw it: a huge, white shape bounding across the road, flashing briefly in the moonlight before disappearing. Instinct took hold. I turned into the next alley, driven to catch a glimpse again.

It was a lane so narrow it barely admitted a man, leading into a broader avenue. The moment I emerged, I spotted the creature on the opposite side, hidden in the gloom. Behind it, following with the devotion of a hound, was the shadow I had chased. Every few moments, the figure glanced back, ignoring the animal, offering it no command, driving it away not a step. Where the figure crossed a patch of moonlight, I realized with a jolt: he cast no shadow. He was not solid matter, but a flat, superficial illusion, existing in two dimensions. Yet, he was opaque; he blocked vision entirely, rendering anything behind him invisible.

In the shade, he was darker than the darkness itself. In the moonlight, he appeared as if he had drawn his shadow up around his form, leaving no trace beneath his feet. Beside him glided the leopardess, a flash of white against the gray. Her movement dragged a gliding shadow blacker than the night itself over the ground. When they passed from the shadow into the full moonlight, the Shadow deepened into absolute blackness, while the animal flared into blinding radiance.

I stood on the opposite side, my bare feet striking the flat stones. The leopardess never turned her head, never twitched an ear. The Shadow seemed to glance at me, though for a split second, I saw nothing but a sharp, vertical line where a face should have been. In that instant, the wind seized me, blowing straight through my body. I shuddered from head to toe, and my heart hammered against my ribs like a pebble rattling in a child's toy.

I veered sharply into a shadowed alley, seeking refuge within the mouth of a crumbling stone arch. As soon as I stepped inside, the moonlight spilled over the cobblestones, illuminating the silence until it shattered. A figure materialized from the gloom—a woman, pale and quivering—who froze beside me, her eyes wide with terror as she gazed back at the darkness. For a heartbeat, neither of us moved. Then, a roar tore through the night, and a massive leopard, its coat a chaotic tapestry of white fur stained with dark, bloody blotches, surged past the archway like a living storm. The woman screamed, pressing her body against mine, her breath hot and ragged. Pity, cold and sharp, flooded my chest. I wrapped an arm around her waist, pulling her close.

"If that beast comes near, I will kill it," I promised, my voice steady despite the chill. "But you must run."

"Please!" she whispered, clinging to me as if I were an anchor.

"Do you know this animal?" I asked, scanning the empty street.

"Yes," she choked out, tears streaming down her soot-streaked cheeks. "Many times. She is the Princess's pet. If you lived among us, you would know her name!"

"I am a stranger," I admitted. "Does she roam freely?"

"She is caged, her jaws bound by iron, her paws encased in gloves of crocodile leather," she explained, her voice trembling with a mixture of horror and shame. "Chained, yet she escapes often. She hunts children, drinking their blood. Oh, how fortunate there are so few mothers left in Bulika!"

Her composure collapsed, and sobs wracked her frame. "I want to go home!" she wailed. "The Princess returned only last night, and the leopardess is already loose! How am I to enter? She will be waiting right outside my door, watching for me! But I am a fool to speak to a stranger!"

"All strangers are not evil," I reassured her gently. "The beast cannot reach you until it has finished with me, and by then, you will be safely inside. At least you have a roof over your head! And what a dreadful wind blows tonight!"

"Lead me home safely, and I will offer you shelter from this storm," she pleaded, wiping her eyes. "But first, we must wait."

I questioned her about the city, about the lives of its people. She told me they spent their days nothing but digging for precious stones deep beneath their cellar floors. They possessed every luxury the world could offer, crafted in distant lands for their consumption.

"Why do they not work?" I asked.

"It is considered a disgrace," she replied softly. "To labor in Bulika is a sin."

"But how do they afford everything if they earn nothing?"

"Their ancestors hoarded wealth for them," she said, looking away with a mix of pride and guilt. "They never spent a coin. When funds ran low, they merely sold a handful of gems."

"There must be the poor," I pressed. "Some must suffer."

"I suppose so," she murmured, her voice devoid of empathy. "But we never think of them. Once someone falls below, we simply forget them. That is how we maintain our riches. We intend to remain wealthy forever."

"And when those mines dry up? When you sell every gem and have nothing left?"

"We have endless stores," she insisted, though a flicker of doubt crossed her face. "And countless more buried in the earth. That day will never come."

"What if a foreign army attacked? What if they took everything?"

"No foreigners would dare approach," she declared firmly. "They are terrified of our Princess. She alone guards us, keeping us safe, free, and affluent."

As she spoke, she frequently glanced over her shoulder, checking the shadows where the leopard might lurk. I asked why her people harbored such intense dread of outsiders.

"A stranger defiles the city," she answered solemnly.

"How?" I queried.

"Because we are older, nobler, and far superior to any other nation," she said, straightening her spine. "That is why we banish all strangers before the sun sets."

"So, how can you let me stay?" I asked.

"I shall make an exception for you," she said quickly.

"You say you expel strangers, yet you will not even allow a shelter?"

"If there were a place built specifically for the destitute, it would be torn down immediately," she replied, her tone sharpening. "Its owner would be burned. How can purity exist if we lower ourselves to accept the unworthy? Dignity is fragile, and it must be protected at all costs!"

She confessed that her Princess had ruled for millennia, wielding dominion over the air, the waters, the earth, and perhaps even the flames themselves. She could do whatever she wished, answerable to no authority save herself.

Finally, convinced that the coast was clear, we navigated the labyrinthine alleys and narrow passageways toward her residence. The streets grew quieter, devoid of life, leading us to a broader avenue flanked by towering, ancient houses. Her dwelling sat atop a steep, winding staircase. She ascended slowly, pausing occasionally to listen, while I trailed behind. Halfway up, however, fear seized her again. With a sudden, frantic motion, she sprinted the remaining steps, slamming the heavy wooden door shut just as I reached the threshold. I stood there, baffled and stranded on the narrow landing between the two opposing doors, barely enough space for a man to stretch out.

Exhausted and weary, I realized I had no choice. Scruples mattered less than survival in this nightmarish place. I abandoned my pride, stepping into the cramped space to seek the barest semblance of shelter, leaving the sanctified walls of Bulika open to the impurity of a tired traveler.

The moonlight spilled down the stairway onto the street below, where an unwholesome, inhospitable wind whipped about. Inside my sanctuary, however, the air remained still as I prepared for rest. Suddenly, my eyes snapped open. Rising above the topmost step was the head of the shining creature I had tracked with the Shadow. The instant our gazes met, she halted, then began to retreat, presenting her tail first. I lunged forward; trapped with no space to maneuver, she spun backward, head over tail, scrambled upright, and vanished down the stairs in a blur. I chased her to the bottom, scanning the empty thoroughfare, but she was gone. I returned to my hard couch.

Two evils now prowled Bulika: one spotted, one without. Though I held little value for men or women in this cursed city, the life of a child outweighed my own poverty. I resolved to guard the door until dawn.

Soon, the latch moved—a slow, deliberate drag. Looking up, I saw the door ajar. Stepping inside, I expected the woman I had aided, but instead, the muffled figure from the desert stood there. Silent, she guided me a few paces into an empty, stone-paved chamber and indicated a rug on the floor. Wrapped within it, I lay down once more. She sealed the inner door, and the sounds of the outer world faded, leaving only the pale glow of moonlight filtering through the cracks.

Sleep eluded me. Soon, a stifled moan drifted from the darkness, lingering until it fractured into the cry of a child, immediately followed by a terrifying shriek. Springing up, I darted into the passage. From a side room emerged the white leopardess, clutching a newborn babe in her jaws, carrying it with the same ferocity as her own cubs. I threw myself against her, forcing her to release the infant, which struck the stone slabs with a piteous wail.

At the noise, the muffled woman appeared. She stepped over the stricken beast and me, scooped up the crying child, and vanished. Moments later, returning alone, she disengaged me from the animal, opened the door, and gently pushed me out. Trailing at my heels was the leopardess.

"She has betrayed me!" I thought, anger burning cold. "Left me to settle accounts with this monster at her leisure! But we will have a struggle soon enough!"

I sprinted down the stairs, bracing for a spring attack, but she followed silently. At the bottom, I turned to seize her, but she leaped effortlessly over my head. When I faced her again, she crouched at my feet. I stooped, stroking her luminous white fur. She responded by licking my bare feet with a rough, dry tongue. I patted her, a surge of tenderness flooding my chest. She may be treacherous, yet if I must reject all displays of affection lest they be false, how could I ever discover the genuine love that must exist somewhere in this broken world?

She rose, standing beside me.

Suddenly, a heavy *squelch* echoed in the street center, a few yards away. Rushing over, I found a pulpy mass, barely retaining the form of a human female. Thrown from a nearby window? I scanned the surroundings. The Shadow walked along the opposite side of the road, and at his heel paced the white leopardess.

I closed the distance, praying inwardly that she was not acting freely. As I neared, she whirled, unleashing a hideous snarl that forced me back. Instantly, she resumed her position behind the Shadow. Approaching again, she lashed out, her eyes blazing like live emeralds. A third time, I tested her. She snapped, biting deep. My heart faltered, and I cried out. Her gaze shifted, speaking plainly: *"Why force me?"*

Angry with myself for wasting hours here, I decided to bypass the delay and head straight for the palace. High above the city's heart, I had seen it—fortified, a fortress rather than a residence. Yet, its defenses, like the city's, were neglected, crumbling under centuries of neglect. Great gates stood wide open across a rocky chasm, though no water filled the hollow before them. Everything was so still that silence seemed to seep into the stones, making the moonlight appear jarringly awake. To enter like a thief or shatter this oppressive quiet felt equally fatal.

Walking like an outcast dog along the walls, I found a small recess with a stone bench. Taking shelter from the wind, I lay down. Despite the chill, exhaustion claimed me, and I fell into a deep sleep.

Something leapt upon me, a rough feline tongue licking my face. "The white leopardess," I realized. "Here to drain my blood—and why not? Defense costs more than surrender!" I lay still, expecting the sting. Instead, a pleasant warmth spread through me. Stretched lengthwise against me, she pressed close, her body heat penetrating mine. Her breath, devoid of wild animosity, swathed my face in a gentle atmosphere. Conviction took root: her intent was good. Turning like a contented child, I draped an arm over her and sank into unconsciousness.

When consciousness returned, I imagined lying warm and soft in a proper bed. "Am I finally home?" I thought. The familiar scents of a garden crowded my mind. Rubbing my eyes, I looked out—but I lay on bare stone in the heart of a hateful city.

I sprang up. Had I truly shared a bed with a leopardess, or had it been a dream? The residual warmth of her body proved otherwise. Leaving the recess, I carried a hope, shapeless yet powerful. One truth was clear: I must find the princess. If I had any power over her, it stemmed from the debt she owed me for saving her life, even as it drained my vitality. This reflection hardened my resolve, giving me courage to face whatever awaited me.

Circling the fortress, I emerged once more through the open gates and traversed the deep, ravine-like moat. On the far bank lay a vast courtyard paved with stone, dotted at regular intervals with towering trees resembling poplars. Amidst them stood one tree, taller than the rest, its upper branches spreading outward to mimic the fronds of a palm. Through the massive trunks, I caught glimpses of the palace itself—a structure utterly alien to my experience, yet evoking an ancient, perhaps Eastern, lineage. It sprawled low and elongate, crowned by lofty corner towers and dominated by a colossal central dome that rose halfway to the turrets. The main entrance, a low arch suggesting a flattened ellipse, stood agape. Unchallenged, I stepped inside into a vast hall shaped like a long ellipse.

To my left, within a cage, coiled a magnificent leopardess. She wore a heavy steel collar, her muzzle bound, her paws muffled. Her coat was snow-white, marked with dark ovals, and her eyes were enormous, framed by green irises and canoe-shaped pupils. She lay perfectly still, a statue of flesh and fur, watching me with an intensity that froze the blood. Not an eyelid blinked, not a whisker twitched; her tail extended behind her, rigid as iron. Whether she breathed or lived, I could not tell.

Two narrow passages branched off this vestibule. I chose the right, expecting it to lead deeper into the labyrinth. Instead, it forked rapidly into a maze of tight, irregular corridors. At a choke point where space allowed barely two souls to pass, a page collided with me. He recoiled in terror, his eyes wide, but quickly recovered his composure, puffing out his chest with brazen impudence.

"To see the princess?" he asked, though his tone suggested disbelief. "A rare visitor indeed! I have not seen Her Highness this morning!"

Seizing him by the back of the neck, I shook him gently but firmly. "Lead me to her immediately, or I shall drag you until I find her. Let her know how her servants treat those who seek an audience."

He glanced at me, then at the empty corridor, and began to lead the way with the frantic energy of a guide lost in fog. We passed a large kitchen where servants moved listlessly, barely awake. They stopped, staring not at me, but at something trailing in my wake, their faces growing ghastly. Turning, I saw the white leopardess following us, her gaze fixed on them with a terrifying stillness. One servant, realizing the beast posed no immediate threat, slowly relaxed. I released the boy and repeated my command.

"She has not yet risen, your lordship," he whispered.

"Tell her I am waiting outside her chamber. Tell her the White Leech seeks an audience."

"The White Leech?" The boy paled. "If I bring that word, she will kill me. I dare not."

"You refuse?"

He shot a fearful glance at the animal trailing behind us and fled. The remaining servants continued to stare, paralyzed by the unseen predator. I turned to the leopardess, who had now approached me, her nose touching my heel. She was a vision of pure white warmth in the gloom. Stooping, I patted her head. She lifted her gaze, and the mere movement of her head caused the terrified servants to scatter in panic. Rising onto her hind legs, she placed her paws on my shoulders. I embraced her, feeling the rough texture of her fur beneath my hands. With a sudden prick of her ears, she broke away and vanished into the shadows.

Shortly thereafter, the boy returned. "Please follow me, my lord."

My heart hammered, bracing itself for the inevitable confrontation. He guided me through endless winding passages until we arrived before a door that opened into a room so vast and dark that the walls seemed to dissolve into nothingness. A single patch of light reflected on the floor, surrounded by absolute blackness. Looking upward, I spotted an oval aperture high in the roof, revealing joints of black marble. The floor itself was composed of tightly fitted slabs of the same obsidian stone, absorbing whatever faint light managed to reach it. The ceiling curved into the long half of an ellipsoid, with the opening positioned directly above one focus of the elliptical floor. For a fleeting second, I thought I saw reddish lines flicker along the edges, but they vanished before I could examine them.

Suddenly, a radiant form materialized in the center of the darkness, flooding the void with a blinding splendor. She wore a robe of soft white, her hair cascading down like a black cataract against the marble. Her eyes burned with a luminous blackness, her arms and feet gleaming like warm ivory. She greeted me with the innocent, radiant smile of a young girl—though in countenance and carriage, she seemed to stand precisely on the threshold of womanhood.

*Alas,* I thought, *my calculations of danger were flawed. Is this the woman I rescued—the one who struck me, scorned me, and abandoned me?*

I stood transfixed in the gloom as she gazed back, searching for me in the shadows. Then, just as quickly, she vanished. *She will not acknowledge me!* I thought, despair creeping in. But instantly, her eyes flared forth from the dark, locking straight onto mine. She had seen me. She had come to me.

"You have found me at last!" she exclaimed, placing a hand on my shoulder. "I knew you would!"

My body trembled under a storm of conflicting emotions, a sensation I lacked the power to analyze. I was drawn to her and repelled by her simultaneously; each impulse felt like truth.

"You shiver!" she observed softly. "This place is freezing for you! Come."

Silent, stunned into obedience by the sheer magnitude of her beauty and sweetness, I followed. Taking my hand, she led me to the spot of light where she had first appeared, flashing brilliantly before stepping back.

"You have grown brown since I last saw you," she noted.

"This is nearly the first roof I have known since you left me," I replied.

"And whose was the other?"

"I do not know the name of the woman."

"I would dearly love to learn it!" she said, her voice tinged with melancholy. "Hospitality is not a strong virtue among my people." She took my hand again, guiding me through the darkness past a curtain of black fabric. Beyond it lay a staircase of white stone, which she ascended, leading me to a chamber of breathtaking beauty.

"How you must miss the hot, flowing river!" she remarked. "Yet, here in the corner lies a bath, clear and free of the White Leeches! Beneath your couch rests a garment. When you descend, I shall await you in the room to your left, at the foot of the stairs."

Standing alone, I accused myself of presumption. How was I to treat this ethereal being as a monster, when she treated me with the tenderness of a sister? Where had the transformation occurred? She had once reviled me; now she embraced me. She had driven me away, yet she knew I would return, knowing I would search for her. Did she understand my lingering doubts? Could she unravel them? Even if she could, could I trust her explanation? Yet, regarding her kindness, I had earned it, and perhaps I deserved to accept it—at least until I formed a final judgment.

Could such divine beauty and such profound wickedness coexist within a single soul? If they did, *how* was it possible? Unable to answer the first question, I deferred the second.

Crystal-clear, the water in the great white bath sent a sparkling invitation from the corner where it sank into the marble floor. It seemed celestial, save for the absence of the hot stream. Two drafts in the cottage of the veiled woman, and the pools in the path of the wounded leopardess... these were the only waters I had seen since leaving home. It looked like paradise. I plunged in.

Instantly, my brain filled with a strange, delicate odor—one I did not entirely welcome. Doubt flared anew. Had she medicated the water? Enchanted it? Was she working upon me unlawfully? Furthermore, how could there be water in this palace when not a drop existed in the city? I recalled the crushed paw of the leopardess and sprang from the tub.

*What had I been bathing in?* Again, I saw the fleeing mother, heard the howl, beheld the limping beast. But what source mattered? Was it not sweet water? Was it not the secret of the pitcher-plant, stored for the weary traveler? Water came from heaven; did it matter where it gathered or where it burst forth? Still, I did not return to the bath.

I donned the robe of white wool, embroidered with intricate patterns at the neck and hem, and descended the stairs to the chamber indicated by my hostess. It was a circular room carved entirely of alabaster, devoid of windows. Light filtered in from everywhere, casting a soft, pearly shimmer rather than a sharp shine. Vague, shadowy forms drifted over the walls and the low dome, like loose rainclouds passing over a grey-blue sky.

The princess waited for me, clad in a robe woven with argent rings, discs, rectangles, and lozenges, pressed closely together to resemble silver mail. It fell unbroken from her neck, concealing her feet, yet its long, open sleeves left her arms bare. In the center of the room stood a table of

I collapsed onto the bed, the linen cool against my fevered skin, and let the narrative she had spun unravel in my mind. It was a tale of tragic irony, one where a single slip of speech—a word spoken without thought—had shattered her spell just enough to reveal the truth. The leopardess leaped into action; the princess lay broken upon the riverbank; the flowing water had washed away the enchantment. Her own account of the journey laid bare the peril facing the Little Ones, and the horrifying realization settled over me: I had inadvertently saved the life of their sole, terrifying foe.

Before this truth fully crystallized, surrender claimed me. Sleep, perhaps induced by wine that tasted less innocent than it appeared, dragged me under.

When consciousness returned, the world had shifted. Night had fallen, yet a solitary lamp hung from the ceiling, casting a soft, amber glow that transformed the chamber into a sanctuary of languor. I felt untethered from earth, drifting on a twilight ocean where existence itself was a form of pure, unburdened pleasure. There was no pain here, no gnawing ache. Or so I believed until the sensation hit me again.

A sudden, searing lance of agony tore through my heart, a mortal sting that made me gasp. And again! This time it was sharper, a brutal intensity that left me reeling. I tried to lift my hand to press against the wound, but some unseen force held it pinned to the mattress. The pain receded slightly, leaving behind a heavy, suffocating paralysis. Something vile clung to me, a hatred I could not name. I wanted to fight back, to scream, to move, but my limbs refused to obey. My will screamed in protest, futile and trapped within a body that had turned to stone. I yielded, lying passively as the darkness closed in.

Then, a gentle pressure touched my cheek, guiding my head deeper into the pillow. A weight settled heavily across my chest, stifling my breath.

My breathing slowly deepened, the oppressive weight lifting from my lungs. Gritting my teeth, I forced my eyes open.

Standing atop the bed was the princess. She gazed out into the room with the vacant, dreamlike air of a sleeper, though her expression betrayed a different state entirely. Her great eyes were startlingly clear and calm, devoid of fear. Her lips curved in a rictus of satisfied passion, smeared with a streak of crimson blood that she hastily wiped away.

Our gazes locked. In a flash, she lunged forward, striking me across the eyes with a handkerchief. It felt like a blade drawn swiftly across my vision, plunging me into blindness for a fleeting, disorienting moment.

As the darkness faded, I heard a heavy, muffled thud—the sound of a large, softly padded beast landing after a short leap. I blinked, squinting into the gloom, and saw the massive sweep of a long tail vanish through the partially ajar doorway. Instinct took over. I scrambled after it.

The creature had melted into the void. I plummeted down the stairs, crashing into the alabaster hall. Above, the moon hung high, bathing the interior in a pale, ghostly luminescence, making the space resemble the insides of a bleached lunar sphere. But the princess was nowhere to be seen. I needed her. In her presence, I might find protection; outside it, I was nothing. To her, I was merely a domesticated beast to feed upon, a living fountain quenching a demoniac thirst. She favored me only to make it easier to consume me. Even now, with my eyes open, I did not truly fear her. Yet, the moment they closed, she would return. Unable to see her, I felt her omnipresence, lurking in some secret cavern of slumber. Safety existed only when my gaze was fixed upon her.

Beyond the alabaster halls, absolute darkness swallowed the world. I crawled blindly, hands and feet feeling for purchase, until I brushed against a curtain. Pushing it aside, I entered a vast, silent assembly hall. It was a spectacle of the impossible: invisible yet somehow perceived, the walls, floor, and ceiling shrouded in an infinity of blackness darker than any moonless night. Yet, my failing eyes strained to separate figures from the mass, a chaotic interplay of individuals divided and surrounded by the void.

Vision failed me, struggling to coalesce from the fog. I pressed my eyeballs, focusing, refocusing, but the image remained a blur. Blackness bled into form, silence mixed with undefined motion, possessing the wide expanse. It was a dim, confused dance, flickered with recurrent glimpses of shapes not unfamiliar to my nightmares. Here, a woman with glorious eyes staring out from a skull; there, an armored figure mounted on a skeletal steed; elsewhere, the hideous, burrowing phantasms. I traced no order, no logic in the mingling currents and eddies. If I seemed to grasp the rhythm of a dance, it broke instantly, replaced by chaos. Shifting colors of seemingly solid forms mingled with a multitude of shadows, appearing independent of their origins, each moving with its own erratic shadow-will. I scanned the periphery, frantically searching for the princess, but amidst the wildly shifting kaleidoscope, I found no sign of her, no hint of her presence. Where was she? What horrors might she be orchestrating? No one paid me the slightest heed as I wandered hither and thither, a lost soul in a sea of shadows. Hope draining from me, I turned away, pressing my hand against the wall for guidance, creeping toward a curtained opening leading to a vestibule.

Moonlight filtered weakly through the curtains, illuminating the cage of the leopardess. Within it raged a desperate, silent struggle. Two vastly disparate forms—one human, one bestial—writhed together in a tangled confusion of limbs and bodies. It had lasted but an instant when the leopardess burst from the cage, walking calmly toward the open door. As I hurried after her, glancing back, I froze. Inside the cage sat the leopardess, motionless, crouching exactly as I had first seen her.

Above, the moon hung halfway up the sky, round and crystal clear. The bodiless shadow I had encountered the night before walked through the trees toward the gate, and trailing behind him was the leopardess, her tail swaying. I followed at a distance, moving as silently as they, neither pausing to look around. Through the open gate, we descended toward the city, lying quiet beneath the moonshine, its surface still as glass. The moon's face was utterly still, radiating an atmosphere of cold, expectant anticipation.

The Shadow moved directly to the staircase at the summit—the same stairs where I had lain unconscious the previous night. He ascended without hesitation, and the leopardess followed close behind. I quickened my pace, but moments later, a cry of sheer horror pierced the air. Then, the heavy, soft crash of something falling landed squarely between me and the stairs. At my feet lay a body, frightfully blackened and crushed, yet unmistakably the woman who had guided me home and barred the door.

Stunned, petrified, I watched as the spotted leopardess bounded down the stairs, clutching a baby in her jaws. Before I could dart forward to intercept her, a blinding flash of white erupted from behind me. Like a great bar of glowing silver, the white leopardess shot through the moonlight and seized the spotted one by the neck. The spotted leopardess dropped the child. I snatched the infant up, holding it tight, and stood frozen to witness the battle unfolding before me.

It was a visceral, primal tableau. One moment the spotted leopardess dominated, the next the white one gained the upper hand. Both were so intent on the kill that all sound was reduced to a low, guttural growl, a whimper, or a snarl of hate, punctuated by the frantic scratching of claws as they scrambled for footing on the pavement. The spotted leopardess was larger, and my heart ached for my friend, but I quickly realized that despite equal strength and agility, the white leopardess possessed superior endurance. She never relinquished her grip on the other's neck.

Finally, a howl of agony tore from the spotted leopardess's throat. It shifted rapidly through varying tones, culminating in the long, drawn crescendo of a woman's ultimate wail. The white leopardess released her jaws. The spotted one recoiled, rising onto her hind legs.

There, bathed in the silver light of the moon, stood the princess. A confused whirlwind of shadows raced over her whiteness—the leopard's spots crowding, hurrying, fleeing toward the refuge of her eyes, where they merged and vanished. The last few, outrun and belated, blended into the cloud of her streaming hair, leaving her radiant as the moon itself when a legion of tiny vapors has been hunted away by the wind from its silvery disk. Save for the trail of blood trickling down the white column of her throat from every wound inflicted by her adversary's terrible teeth.

With a fluid, unnatural grace reminiscent of Hecate, she turned, took a few steps, and suddenly fell. Instantly, she was covered anew in the swirling spots of the leopard, and she fled at a long, stretching gallop.

The white leopardess turned as well, springing toward me. She tore at my arms, pulling them asunder, caught the baby as it tumbled from my grasp, and launched herself down the street toward the gate with the child.

I turned and trailed after the spotted leopardess, glimpsing her only as she tore upward over the brow of the hill toward the palace gates. By the time I breached the entrance hall, the princess was in the process of draping the robe she had discarded moments prior across her shoulders. The blood from her wounds had stilled, congealing and darkening in the chill wind of her escape.

When she registered my presence, a sudden flare of irritation crossed her features, causing her to turn her head away. Then, forcing a brittle smile, she faced me again.

"I've had a minor mishap!" she exclaimed, her voice tight. "Upon hearing rumors that the cat-woman has returned to the city, I ventured forth to drive her off. But she brought one of those dreadful minions with her—it leaped upon me, burying its claws in my throat before I could make a move!"

She shivered violently. A pang of pity surged within me, though I knew the truth: her injuries were genuine, yet her expression betrayed the same haunted quality I had seen in the depths of the cave. Guilt gnawed at me for having allowed her to face danger alone, and I believe my countenance reflected the sorrow I felt.

"Child of folly!" she hissed, attempting another smile that didn't reach her eyes. "--Do not weep, surely? Wait here. I must step briefly into the Black Hall. Fetch me something for these scratches."

Yet I shadowed her closely. From outside her sight, I harbored a deep fear of her.

The instant she crossed the threshold, a low, persistent hum filled the air, like the whispering of countless unseen voices. Slowly, portions of the vast assembly began to shift, illuminated as if by a traveling ray of light that jumped from spot to spot. Here, groups would burst into clarity, glowing brightly for a fleeting second before dissolving back into the surrounding gloom, while elsewhere, shadows coalesced into form.

Some of the scenes revealed to me were familiar; I had witnessed them, or stood witness to them, alongside her. Now, present at every single moment, I saw her true nature unfolding. In the forest-hall, where skull-headed dancers trampled the grass, I saw her peering through the doorway! In the chaotic Evil Wood, where battle raged, I saw her urging it on! Yet physically, she remained motionless before me, her head bowed against her chest, trapped in a loop of her own making. The confused murmur swelled, a riot of shifting colors and forms, as the ray of vision drifted on. Finally, it settled upon the hollow in the heath. There, walking aimlessly, she tried desperately to wrap the ethereal vapor around herself. That was when the horror struck me: the old librarian stepped forward, regarded her silently, and then she collapsed. Her limbs deserted her, fleeing from the vessel, and her physical body simply vanished into thin air.

A wild shriek tore through the echoing chamber. With the fall of her eidolon—the phantom self—the princess herself, who had stood like a statue before me, crumpled heavily to the ground, lying still. Without hesitation, I turned and fled outward. Never again would I attempt to restore her! Trembling beside the cage, I realized with a jolt that inside the black ellipsoid, I had been inhabiting the mind of the princess—I could even see the tail of the leopardess tremble once more.

As I struggled to steady my breathing, the princess's voice drifted beside me.

"Come now," she commanded, her tone regaining some strength. "I shall tell you what I require of you."

She led me into the courtyard. I followed, my mind a hazy blank.

High overhead, the moon hung near the zenith, its pale silver radiance seeming far brighter than the golden warmth of the absent sun. She guided me through the grove to the tallest tree, the solitary sentinel in the center. It differed from the others; its branches drew inward at the apex, forming a cluster that resembled a giant fir-cone viewed from below. Standing directly beneath it, she gazed upward, muttering to herself.

"On the summit of that tree grows a tiny blossom capable of healing my wounds instantly! I could become a dove for a moment and retrieve it, but I spy a small serpent coiled in the leaves. Its bite would prove far deadlier to a bird than a tiger's would be to me!--How I loathe that cat-woman!"

She spun around to face me, her expression softening into one of her sweetest, most disarming smiles.

"Can you climb?"

The smile evaporated as quickly as it came, replaced by a mask of profound sadness and suffering. I should have stepped back and let her endure her torment, but the gesture of her hand pressing against her wounded neck pierced my heart.

I studied the tree. Running along the trunk from base to canopy were rough projections, resembling the jagged remnants of fallen palm fronds.

"I can scale that tree," I answered.

"Not with bare feet!" she countered sharply.

In my frantic pursuit of the leopardess, I had abandoned my sandals in my quarters.

"It matters not," I replied. "I have worn them off long ago!"

I cast another glance upward, my eyes tracing the rugged bole until it dissolved into a tangle of branches. Moonlight spilled like silvery foam over the bark, and a faint breeze rustled the tops with a sound like water trickling softly into a pool. I moved toward the tree, ready to ascend, but she stopped me.

"I cannot allow you to attempt this with bare skin!" she insisted. "A fall from such height would kill you!"

"So would the bite of that snake!" I retorted, admitting privately that I doubted any snake existed.

"It would not harm *you*!" she replied. "--Hold on."

She tore from her gown the two wide borders that overlapped in front. Kneeling, she pressed my left foot onto hers, then my right, binding them securely with the thick, embroidered strips of her clothing.

"You've left the ends dangling, Princess!" I observed.

"I lack anything to trim them with, but they are too short to snag," she explained calmly.

Turning to the tree, I began my climb.

In Bulika, the cold following sunset was rarely severe, particularly near the sexton's cottage. Yet as I ascended, a chilling numbness seized me. I grew colder with every rung, freezing utterly once I reached the upper branches. Shivers racked my frame, and it seemed as though I had lost both hands and feet to the frost.

Though the air was nearly still and the branches showed no sign of swaying, a terrible unsteadiness plagued me as I neared the summit. Every limb I touched or gripped seemed poised to give way beneath me. Just as my head broke free of the canopy, bathed in the stark moonlight, searching for the blossom, the world inverted. Instantly, I was drenched from head to toe. The next moment, as if thrown into a tempestuous sea, I was flailed about wildly, feeling myself sink. Tossed up and down, spun this way and that, rolling over and checking, rolling again, I sank deeper and deeper. Gasping, gurgling, choking on the void, I finally crashed onto a solid bottom.

"I told you so!" a voice rasped in my ear.

"You told me nothing!" I snapped, the chill of the bird's words settling in my bones.

"I instructed you to ignore anyone you distrusted," the raven corrected, his voice smooth and unyielding. "I told you to do *nothing*."

"You will not forget the consequences of forgetting it!" Mr. Raven replied, stepping closer. He leaned over the marble edge, stretching a hand across the gap between us. The gesture felt ancient, bridging worlds.

I took his hand. Instantly, the distance vanished, and I found myself standing dry on the grass beside him, though still dripping wet from the immersion.

"You must change your clothes at once!" he urged, his tone practical yet stern. "A soaking tells no one where you originate—though currently, such an accident is highly irregular. Here, it merely creates inconvenience."

Before I could respond, the transformation reversed. The raven stepped away, regaining his avian form with a stately stride, heading toward the house where the doors stood wide open.

"I haven't much need to change!" I called after him, laughing. My robes were already discarded, tossed aside as I climbed the trees.

"It has been a long time since I shed a feather," the raven murmured, pausing near the threshold.

Without a word, I collapsed onto a velvet couch. Mr. Raven followed, drawing a chair close to mine and sitting. "A profound inquiry!" he answered gently. "No man truly knows what anything *is*. A person can only grasp what a thing *means*. And whether they ever do depends entirely on how they choose to use it."

"I have used nothing yet!"

"Not significantly, perhaps—but you possess the awareness of that lack, and that counts for something. Most spend a lifetime believing they have accomplished everything, only to realize too late they have learned nothing and done less. Yet, you have harbored the desire to be useful."

"I didn't know the right way."

"Indeed, I have traveled far and found nothing. I have not discovered my work! I abandoned the children to learn how to serve them, and in doing so, I have only uncovered the peril they face."

"That is one of your world's most cherished deceptions! Does humanity's greatest knowledge surpass a little? Or is it therefore perilous? "Conceded. Yet, you should have served the Little Ones, not the Giants! You should have given them water; then, swiftly, they would have taught the Giants their proper place. "I will go at once!" I responded, moving toward the exit.

The moon, halfway up the heavens, gazed with a solemn trouble in her pale countenance. Rejoicing in the power of my steed and in the pride of my life, I sat like a king.

Where they carried me, I did not guess; I yielded to their pleasure, almost as happy as they. At first, chattering, laughing, and performing endless joyful tricks, the moment they sensed I was drifting off, they fell as still as judges.

She revealed that following my exit from the orchard valley, the Giants' faith in the tangible reality of their neighbors swelled, breeding a corresponding surge of hostility. Yet, reflection illuminated a path. Where birds dwelt, the Little Ones could find habitation. They possessed an eager sympathy for all forms of life, capable of learning from the wildest creatures. Why not flee the cold and the enemies into the canopy? Why not abandon the low brushwood for lofty foliage? Why not construct nests where scooping hollows served no purpose? All that birds achieved, the Little Ones could emulate, save for the art of flight.

She addressed the assembly, and they listened with rapt approval. They already scaled the trees and had long admired the avian architects. Thus reasoned the Lovers, embracing Lona's vision. Swiftly, they mastered the tree-tops, becoming as native as the birds themselves. The Giants, convinced they had driven the intruders from the land, eventually neglected their vigil, and the belief in the small neighbors faded.

"I have not," I admitted, "yet I can surely believe it."

"It is true," she affirmed. "The proof lies in the expansion of their minds. Since migrating upward, their intellects have matured significantly, mitigating the alarm the news caused."

As twilight waned and the moon shone silver above, the children descended to the valley to gather provisions for the morrow. The Giants, blind to the gray hour and loathing the moon—which they would have extinguished if they could—never ventured forth then. Through exploration, the children had befriended the forest's denizens. They recognized the strength and wisdom of some, the swiftness and manageability of others. Then, on a moonlit eve, they encountered a woman seated with an infant—the very woman I had met on my journey to Bulika. While the woman observed that the children struck carefully, avoiding harm to the babe, the Little Ones noted that, upon yielding, she hugged and kissed her child exactly as they desired. Concluding she was a Giantess of the "good" kind, they brought her fruit and showered her with childish attentions.

Perplexed regarding her destination, the woman dared not return to the City, fearing the Princess would uncover who had lame her leopardess. Supplementing Lona's account with conjecture, I recall the tradition shared by the Bulikan inhabitants: the Princess lived in terror of an infant destined to destroy her. Unaware of the horrific measures she employed to preserve her youth and beauty, they interpreted her deteriorating physical state and increasing hostility toward children as omens of impending doom. Now, the woman conceived a bold strategy. Could the prophecy lie with these very children? They were marvelously brave, whereas the Bulikans were cowards in abject fear of animals. If she could ignite their ambition to seize the City, she might slip away amidst the chaos, reach her house unrecognized, and await the outcome from hiding.

Though unfit for warfare by nature—quarrels rare, fights nonexistent, loving all life, hating both inflicting and suffering pain—they were malleable. She set the smallest to throw stones at targets. Soon, they were engrossed in the game, growing skilled.

Their first practical triumph was my rescue. Gathering fruit, they found me asleep. Returning home, they convened a council and returned the next day, accompanied by elephants and horses. They overwhelmed the few Giants guarding me, carrying me off in jubilation. Through lush, grassy plains and between verdant banks flowed the deep, wide, and silent river, brimming to the top. For the Little Ones, the glory of God was revealed in the limpid flow of the water. Instinctively, they plunged into the stream and began to swim, soon joined by the beasts following in their wake.

Where the desert had once been barren, it now rejoiced and blossomed like a rose. Vast forests had sprouted overnight, their undergrowth thick with flowering shrubs teeming with songbirds. Every thicket birthed a rivulet, and every stream poured out a liquid melody.

The spot where the buried hand had lain offered no sign of disturbance. Beyond it, and stretching endlessly further, the river continued in full volume from afar. We ascended higher, traversing grassy margins and wandering through forests of gracious trees. With every step, the grass grew sweeter, the flowers more varied and lovely, and the trees grew larger. The wind filled with messages, whispering secrets of the journey ahead.

Finally, we arrived at a forest unlike any we had seen before. Its trees were towering giants, grander and more beautiful, their trunks acting as live pillars that upheld a thick, arched roof. Between the leaves and blossoms, scarcely a sunbeam filtered through. The children scrambled up into the rafters of this aerial vault, leaping and shouting to the unseen elephants below, who replied with deep, resonant trumpets. Lona understood the conversations taking place among them, though I could only guess at their meaning clumsily. The Little Ones chased the squirrels, who frolicked playfully, drawing them on until they allowed themselves to be caught and petted. Occasionally, a bird, radiant in plumage and form, would alight upon one of them, singing a prophecy of what was to come before flying away. Not a single monkey could be seen anywhere in this realm.

Beneath our feet, the path was quiet, but overhead, a thunderous shout tore through the canopy. Above us, the Little Ones began their descent, tumbling like acorns from the highest branches. Their faces were alight with a frantic joy as they relayed the vision from the summit of a towering tree—the tallest sentinel in the grove. From there, they claimed to have gazed across the vast plain and beheld a marvel that defied nature's geometry: a solitary mountain that climbed so relentlessly it pierced the heavens, forcing the sky to curl inward like a heavy blanket, its crown shattered against the firmament.

"It might be a city," one of them whispered, breathless, "yet it bears no resemblance to Bulika."

Driven by a curiosity that burned hotter than the sun, I ascended again. What I witnessed stole the air from my lungs. It was a metropolis rising vertically into the azure void, where the boundaries between geology and architecture dissolved. Here, mountains were not stone but spires, skies were not empty space but palaces, and clouds were not vapor but the mortar binding it all. Rock and dwelling, precipice and palace, tangled in a glorious, chaotic symphony of shadows and glints, a broken mosaic of light and shadow that seemed to breathe.

I descended, and the Little Ones followed, their spirits lifting with every step. They raced ahead, leading the way with a mirth that bordered on madness, eyes fixed solely on the horizon, utterly blind to anything behind them. As we moved forward, the river transformed. It ceased to be merely water; it became something luminous, alive with a beauty I had never known in any realm. This was not a reflection of reality, but reality itself, unveiled. Nothing in this world could truly *be* like it; it existed in a state of pure being.

From the distance, the city emerged again, nestled within the blue mists. Yet, looming above it, closer to the mountain's jagged crest, stood a colossal tower—or perhaps a monolith—that seemed to pierce the veil of the clouds. Around it, the atmosphere churned. Gray, dark gray, and violet hues swirled in a confused, antinomian dance, churning up a froth of vapor that spun like whirlpools in a sea of ink. Suddenly, a blinding flash erupted. For a fleeting instant, it played about the children running before us, illuminating them with a terrifying brilliance. Then, a darkness so profound it felt like sleep swallowed the scene whole. But through that void, I heard their voices, hushed with ecstatic wonder.

"Did you see?"
"I saw."
"What did you see?"
"The beautifullest man!"
"I heard him speak!"
"I didn't. What did he say?"

Then, the voice of the smallest, most childlike among them, Luva, cut through the silence: "He said, 'Ous all mine's, 'ickle ones: come along!' "

I had seen the lightning, yet my ears remained deaf to the words. Lona, however, saw and heard as the children did. A second flash tore through the gloom, and suddenly, my eyes—though not my ears—opened wide. The great, quivering light was composed entirely of angelic visages. They flared into visibility and then vanished, leaving trails of ethereal dust.

A third flash followed, its substance shifting from the divine to the intimately human.
"I see my mother!" I cried out, the name bursting from my throat.
"I see lots o' mothers!" Luva replied, his voice trembling.

Again, the cloud flashed. This time, it revealed a menagerie of the impossible: horses and elephants, lions and dogs, oh, such beasts! And such birds! Great avians whose single wings shimmered with every hue of the sunset or rainbow, fluttering alongside tiny birds whose feathers sparkled as if encrusted with the entire treasury of earthly jewels. Silvery cranes, red flamingos, opal pigeons, peacocks resplendent in gold and emerald and sapphire, jewel-bright hummingbirds, great-winged butterflies, and lithe, voluminous creeping things—all condensed into one heavenly, instantaneous bloom.

"I see that serpents grow birds here, just as caterpillars used to grow butterflies!" Lona observed, her awe palpable.
"I saw my white pony," I murmured, tears pricking my eyes, "the one that died when I was a child. I needn't have been so sorry; I should just have waited!"

No thunder rolled, no clap echoed. Instead, a sweet rain began to fall, filling the air with a caressing coolness that washed away the dust of the journey. We breathed deeply, our steps growing stronger, anchored by the rhythm of the falling drops. Each drop flashed with the colors of the awakened gems of the earth, and a mighty rainbow arced across the sky, spanning the city like a bridge of light.

The blue clouds thickened, and the rain poured in torrents. The children exulted, running wild, a kaleidoscope of motion that made it nearly impossible to keep them in sight. Beside them, the river swept onward with a silent, radiant roll. It filled its smooth, soft, yielding channel to the brim, flowing not over rock or shingle or sand, but over a carpet of grass teeming with primroses, daisies, crocuses, narcissi, pimpernels, and anemones—a starry multitude, large and bright beneath the brilliant water. The river had gathered no turbidity from the storm, not even a tinge of yellow or brown; it glowed with a pale, berylline luminescence rising from its deep, dainty bed.

As we drew nearer to the mountain, we saw the truth of the source: the river burst forth from the mountain's very peak, rushing in full volume through the main street of the city. It cascaded down to the gate via a staircase of deep, wide steps, interwoven from porphyry and serpentine, continuing all the way to the mountain's foot. There, we found shallower steps on either bank, winding upward toward the gate and along the ascending thoroughfare. Without a single hesitation, the Little Ones sprinted straight up the stairs to the gate, which stood wide open.

On the landing outside, seated, was the portress, a woman-angel of dark visage. She leaned her shadowed brow on her idle hand, watching them approach. Before she could comprehend their sudden arrival, the children rushed upon her, burying her in caresses. In a heartbeat, they had taken heaven by surprise and were already inside, still mounting the stairs beside the descending torrent. A great angel, accompanied by a retinue of shining ones, came down to intercept and receive them, but the children, in merry evasion, darted past. Then, a group of woman-angels descended in joyful dance, and in a moment, they were fettered in heavenly arms. The radiants lifted them away, and I lost sight of them forever.

"Ah!" said the mighty angel, continuing his descent to meet us, who were now close to the gate and within earshot of his voice. "This is well! These are soldiers to take heaven itself by storm! I hear of a horde of black bats on the frontiers; these will make short work of them!"

Glancing back, seeing the horses and elephants clambering up behind us, he added, "Take those animals to the royal stables. Tend them, then turn them into the King's forest."

"Welcome home!" he said, bending low with the sweetest smile imaginable.

Instantly, he turned and led the way higher. The scales of his armor flashed like flakes of lightning, catching the light of the inner world. Thought failed me, struggling to articulate the weight of emotion I felt, thus received by the officers of heaven. All I desired, all I knew not how to ask, was clearly on its way to me.

We paused for a moment at the gate, whence issued the roaring, radiant river. I know not whence the stones that fashioned it were hewn, but among them I saw the prototypes of every gem I had ever loved on earth. They were far more beautiful than any earthly stone, for these were living stones. Within them, I perceived not merely the intent, but the Intender himself; not just the idea, but the Imbodyer present, the Operant Outsender. In this kingdom, nothing was dead; nothing was mere existence. Nothing was simply a "thing."

We went up through the city and stepped out. There was no wall on the upper side, only a massive pile of broken rocks, upsloping like the moraine of an eternal glacier. Through the openings between the boulders, the river came billowing out, free and untamed. Upon their tops, I could dimly discern what appeared to be three or four great steps of a stair, disappearing into a cloud white as snow. Above those steps, I saw—not with physical eyes, but with the mind's eye alone—a grand, ancient chair, the Throne of the Ancient of Days. Over, under, and between those steps issued the River of the Water of Life, born anew, ceaselessly, plentifully.

The great angel could guide us no farther; those remaining stones we must ascend alone.

The shadow of Lona remains unbroken, yet Mara's presence has become the anchor of my existence. She does not merely teach me; she unravels the threads of my being, weaving them into patterns I could never have conceived alone. A lingering uncertainty clings to me, however—a question that haunts the quiet spaces between thoughts: Was that final awakening truly real? Am I still trapped within the chamber of death, suspended in a slumber where I am not yet mature enough to breathe the air of life? Perhaps I never fell deeply enough, never surrendered wholly to the dark, and thus I woke prematurely, before the time was right.

If that transition was indeed a dream, it must have been a prophecy, a vision of a far superior waking state waiting just beyond the horizon. How could a lie, a false vision, possess such a profound, resonant beauty? There must be a truer love at the heart of this illusion than I have ever known. In those fleeting moments when doubt threatens to swallow me whole, I cry out to the void, "Could God Himself create such lovely things?" And the answer comes, gentle as a breeze, from Hope itself: "It emerged from your darkness, stepping into the light of your awareness."

"But what brought you there first, into that dark?" Hope presses gently.

"My mind was the vessel, the mother; the fever coursing through my veins was the father."

"Speak differently," Hope suggests, its voice warm with wisdom. "Consider your mind the violin, and that burning fever the bow drawing the strings. Yet, tell me, who crafted the instrument? Who guided the bow across the wood? Look deeper. Who placed the songbirds upon the branches of the Tree of Life, startling them into song in their proper order? Where did the fantasy spring from? What gave birth to the dance? Did you speak the word 'Let beauty be' in the silence of your unconscious soul, and instantly, beauty existed, and truth became mere semblance?"

A man dreams, and his desire shapes the dream, making him a plaything of his own imagination. But when Another grants the dream, that Other holds the power to fulfill it.

Since that revelation, I have turned away from the glass. The hand that sent me back warned me clearly: "Do not step through that door again!" I will wait, day after day, until the transformation that promises to remake me arrives.

Occasionally, glancing at my books, I feel them tremble as if caught in a sudden wind, ripples disturbing their solid form, threatening to reveal a hidden world breaking through the surface. Walking outdoors, the same sensation strikes—the heavens and the earth, the trees and the grass, seem poised to vanish, shaken by a force unseen, before settling once more into the familiar, comforting guise of reality. Sometimes, I catch whispers on the air, voices of those who love me speaking of me, but the moment I try to focus, to distinguish the words, the sound dissolves into a profound, heavy stillness. I cannot tell if these visions arise within my mind or arrive from the outside. I do not seek them; they come, and I let them pass.

There are strange, hazy memories that refuse to take shape, refusing identification, gazing out at me through the misty windows of the past even under the broad daylight of the present. Yet, strangely, I no longer dream. Perhaps, when I am most awake, I am simply dreaming a deeper, more vivid dream. But when I finally awaken into that life which, like a mother cradling her child, holds this current existence within its bosom, I will know with absolute certainty that I am awake, and doubt will find no purchase in me.

I wait. Whether asleep or awake, I wait.

As Novalis wrote, "Our life is no dream, but it should and will perhaps become one."
