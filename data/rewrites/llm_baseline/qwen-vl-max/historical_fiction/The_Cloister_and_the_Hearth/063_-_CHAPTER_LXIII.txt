A Shadow Falls on a Noble Heart

The light that had guided Gerard—his pure, unyielding love for Margaret—was extinguished. Without it, he drifted through life like a ship without a compass, adrift upon a sea of despair. He was not merely crushed by grief; he was consumed by rage at himself. All his sacrifice, his endurance, his courage, his virtue—what had they wrought? Nothing. Worse: they had cost him the one thing he held most dear. If only he had stayed home, selfish and idle, perhaps Margaret would still be alive. That thought gnawed at him day and night, a poison in his blood, fueling both his sorrow and his fury.

And so, with no anchor left to hold him, he threw himself into pleasure—not for joy, but to escape. In those times, pleasure was not mere indulgence—it was corruption. Wine flowed freely, women were sought for fleeting thrills, and gambling offered the briefest moments of oblivion. He plunged into these vices as if seeking death among enemy bullets. The money he had saved for Margaret’s future now poured into reckless excess. Soon, he became the leader of a pack of men he once despised—men who lived only for the moment, careless of consequence or conscience.

His soul grew colder as his body weakened. He resented his old landlady, who still tried to warn him with gentle words. Her concern only irritated him. Finally, he moved away, fleeing not just her advice, but the memories that haunted every corner of his former home. When he could no longer write with steady hand—when even the art that had once brought him peace failed him—he turned to painting. But even that he abandoned when his companions called. He painted no more, unless forced. Instead, he drowned himself in the filth of the world, chasing the hollow shell of forgetfulness.

I need not describe every sordid scene in which this young man now took part. But I must say plainly: he was a rake, a drunkard, a debauchee—one of the wildest and most reckless youths in Rome. To hide the sins of the good is not kindness; it is deceit. It denies hope to those who fall and wish to rise again. Gerard did not walk halfway into vice. He fell entirely. And he fell fast.

His former friends whispered about him. His old landlady told Teresa he was slipping into ruin, begging her to find where he had gone. Teresa spoke to her husband, Lodovico. “Do you remember his face?” she asked.

“Teresa,” Lodovico replied, “a man like me never forgets a face—especially a benefactor’s. But you know I rarely go out in daylight.”

She sighed. “And how long will it be so, Lodovico?”

“Until some nobleman runs me through with his sword. They won’t let a poor fellow like me take up honest work.”

Pietro Vanucci was one of those who suffered prosperity worse than poverty. After being thrown out by their landlady for staying out too late, he met Gerard in the street and greeted him warmly. Soon after, he moved into the same house. With him came Andrea—a young boy who ground his pigments, studied under him, and served as his model. Andrea was strikingly beautiful, sharp-witted, and full of spirit. Pietro claimed to cherish old memories and professed deep friendship for Gerard. But Gerard, whose heart seemed frozen, accepted the overtures with cold indifference. Their companionship was a strange alliance—two souls bound only by shared excess, yet utterly alien to each other.

Pietro was not only a libertine but half a misanthrope, openly mocking faith and morality. Together, they wandered through Rome’s pleasures, united only by their desire to escape themselves.

One day, when Gerard’s health had begun to fail and his beauty faded beneath the weight of wine and sleepless nights, Vanucci planned a grand outing. They would travel up the Tiber in a galley drawn by buffaloes—creatures Lorenzo de’ Medici had recently introduced to Florence, but still new to Rome. Vanucci insisted on this spectacle, declaring it the height of fashion. Each man was to bring a woman—handsome, or face a fine. The one bringing the most beautiful would be crowned with laurel and hailed as a public benefactor. Such was their twisted interpretation of *“Vir bonus est quis?”* —“Who is the good man?”

They gathered at the riverbank. A magnificent galley awaited, pulled by twelve massive buffaloes. The libertines and their companions arrived one by one. But Gerard was missing. They waited—first patiently, then with growing impatience. Vanucci made excuses. “He said he forgot to bring a fardingale,” he laughed. “The lad’s searching for a beauty worthy to stand beside these peerless ladies. Be patient, ladies—such a task demands time.”

At last, Gerard appeared, walking briskly with a woman at his side. From afar, one woman remarked, “She’s tall enough.” Another added, “Look at those strides!” Pietro joked, “It’s wise to hurry into good company.” But when they reached the boat, all speech died.

Gerard’s companion was breathtaking—so radiant that she dimmed the others like stars before dawn. Tall, yet graceful; straight as an arrow, yet lithe as a young panther. Her face was a flawless oval, her forehead pale, her cheeks a rich olive hue flushed with living color. Her eyes—large, dark, and framed by thick, silken lashes—seemed made to captivate hearts. Her lips were red and bold, her teeth white as ivory. The women stared in silence, their spirits crushed. The men roared with delight. One leapt onto the gunwale and knelt, shouting, “Divine! You are born of heaven!”

But the goddess, amused, kicked him gently, and another woman tugged his hair. He toppled backward, sprawling across the deck. She stepped lightly over him, ignoring him completely, and took her seat at the prow.

Vanucci sat stunned, mouth agape, like a fish gasping its last breath. The drover shouted, the ropes tightened, and the galley began to move upstream.

“What think you of our new beast, ladies?” someone asked.

“Monstrous creatures!” one woman scoffed. “Those horns make me afraid. Their nostrils are holes, not noses!”

“A gift from Florence,” another retorted. “Would you look a present cow in the nose?”

“They’re slow,” complained a lively lady. “Last time I went up the Tiber twice as fast with five mules and an ass.”

“Then we’ll fix that,” declared a gallant, jumping ashore. Drawing his sword, he charged the buffaloes despite the drivers' protests, urging them forward. They snorted, flicked their tails, but refused to quicken. The crowd laughed. Then he jabbed a massive bull, who turned instantly, impaling him with his horns and hurling him high into the air. He arced through space and landed splashing into the yellow Tiber, still clutching his sword.

The women shrieked and wrung their hands—except Gerard’s companion. She muttered something that sounded like an oath, seized the helm, and steered the boat toward him. He surfaced sputtering, grabbed the edge, and was hauled aboard dripping wet. Dazed, he looked around. “I do not understand this,” he muttered, annoyed. “One moment I am pricking African beef, the next—”

Gerard’s companion interrupted. “Disporting in your native stream.”

“Tell him nothing,” Vanucci cried. “Let him solve his own riddle.”

“Confound you all,” the man grumbled. “I might puzzle my brain till doomsday and never find it. Also—where is my sword?”

“Ask the Tiber,” she replied. “Your best course, signor, is to repeat the deed. Prick African beef until your mind sees the light. So shall you comprehend—like lawyers climbing heaven, or buffaloes ascending the Tiber.”

A knight observed that she surpassed all men in wit as she did all women in beauty. At this, Vanucci changed. He stopped staring, coiled himself like a snake, and fixed his eyes on her—twinkling, scintillating with hidden fire. But she paid him no mind. Her gaze passed over him as though he were part of the bench, not a man.

Soon, the harmony broke. Jealous of the attention Gerard’s companion drew, the other women began to whisper insults—then speak aloud. The girl, unaware of subtlety, heard and retaliated.

“You wear no natural colors,” she declared. “Look here—this is cerussa, stibium, purpurissum. I know them well—I use them every day—but not on my face, thank you.”

Vanucci nearly burst with laughter.

“And your lips are colored, your veins dyed. Your black hair—like a horse’s tail—is drugged to look like tow. Your bodies are false as your faces. Look at your padded bosoms, your wooden-heeled chopines lifting your tiny limbs. Skinny dwarfs, padded into fat giants. Ah, mesdames, it is rightly said of you: *grande—di legni; grosse—di straci; rosse—betti; bianche—di calcina.*”

The women fired back. “Vulgar toad! Why tell the men everything? Your cheeks are your own, your hair natural. But who is padded more? You look like a fire-shovel!”

“Liar,” she snapped.

“Oh, the well-educated young person! Where did you find her, Ser Gerard?”

“Hold your tongue, Marcia,” Gerard said, startled from his gloom. “Be not so insolent. The grave will close over your beauty as it has over fairer than you.”

“They started it,” Marcia protested.

“Then be the first to stop.”

“At your request, my friend.” She leaned close and whispered, “I only wanted to make you laugh. You are distraught, sad. Judge whether I care for their jokes or their admiration. Dear Signor Gerard, would I be what they think I am? You should not be so sad.”

Gerard sighed deeply, shook his head, then gently stroked her jet-black hair—like one petting a loyal dog.

Just then, a galley drifting downstream tangled briefly with their ropes. As the river curved, they shot out into the current, and the other vessel came between them and the bank. Under a canopy sat a lady of exquisite beauty—surrounded by attendants. Gerard looked up.

It was Princess Claelia.

He blushed and withdrew his hand from Marcia’s head.

Marcia gaped. “Ah, ladies—here is a rival, if you please. Those cheeks are Nature’s own—like mine.”

“Peace, child! Peace!” Gerard said sharply.

“But she didn’t hear,” Marcia insisted. “Oh, Ser Gerard, what a lovely creature!”

Two women had been watching Marcia closely. One now approached. “Signorina, do you love almonds?”

She held a lapful.

“Yes,” Marcia replied, eyeing them hungrily. “When I can get them. But yours is not the hand to give me any.”

“You are mistaken,” the woman said, tossing two handfuls into Marcia’s lap.

Marcia instinctively pressed her knees together.

“Aha!” the woman laughed. “You’re caught, my lad. It’s a man—or a boy. A woman parts her knees to catch nuts in her apron. A man closes them, fearing they’ll fall between his hose. Confess—have you ever worn a fardingale before today?”

“Give me another handful, sweetheart, and I’ll tell you.”

“There!” the woman exclaimed. “I knew he was too handsome for a woman.”

“Ser Gerard,” Marcia said calmly, cracking an almond, “they’ve found me out.”

The libertines laughed, convinced it was impossible. But Vanucci remembered a recent argument in which they had mocked Gerard and himself for claiming that men could be as beautiful as women in an artist’s eye.

“Where are you now?” he chuckled. “This is my boy Andrea. You’ve all knelt before him. Ha! ha! But oh, my little ladies—when he lectured you, flinging your stibium, cerussa, and purpurissum back in your faces—then I nearly burst. He grinds my colours. Ha! ha! he! he! ho!”

“The little impostor! Duck him!”

“What for, signors?” Andrea cried, flushing.

The women rushed to protect him. “The dear child! How sweet his ways are!”

“Oh, what eyes and teeth!”

“And eyebrows! Hair!”

“And lashes!”

“And nose!”

“The sweetest ear in the world!”

“And what health! Touch his cheek with a pin—the blood would squirt!”

“Who would be cruel?”

“He is a rosebud washed in dew.”

They lavished affection on him, revenging themselves for the men’s admiration of Marcia.

Yet one remained apart—no longer of the crowd, though still among it. The sight of Princess Claelia had reopened his wound. Less than three months ago, he had rejected her love—a love forbidden, mad, but at least noble. How far had he fallen since then? Once, he had resisted her charms, steadfast in loyalty. Now, he blushed at her presence—though such company was his daily routine. He hung over the boat in silence, lost in thought.

From that moment, a new phase of his torment began—and deepened. Some fools try to drown sorrow in drink. But the hangover comes. The pain remains. The mind grows heavier. Gerard had done the same. To endure his loss, he needed strength—his virtue, his health, his discipline, his rest, above all, his faith. Yet he cast all aside, trusting instead to the fleeting warmth of intoxication.

The fumes passed. The pain returned—deeper, darker. Now, remorse sat upon his heart like a vulture, tearing at it. His health was broken. His money gone. Innocence fled. Margaret was gone—divided from him by a chasm wider than death.

The hot storm of despair passed. The cold, creeping despair took hold. He turned from his companions, from the world. He wandered alone. Drank alone to numb his thoughts. Lied among the ruins of ancient Rome, stony-eyed and broken, worse wrecked than the temples themselves.

And finally came the end—solitude’s final temptation. He walked alone by dark rivers at night, staring into the water, his fear fading, replaced by longing. There, peace lay—perhaps annihilation. What else remained?

Such darkness has been broken by kind words, by loving voices, by a single smile from a humble friend. Perhaps Teresa, his kind-hearted landlady, would have seen through his pain and saved him. But she did not know where he was. Nor did anyone else.

Except one.

One pair of eyes watched him—every step, every turn, every silent hour. And that eye belonged to an enemy—an enemy to the death.
