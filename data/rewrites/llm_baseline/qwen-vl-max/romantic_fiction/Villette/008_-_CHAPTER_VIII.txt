Delivered into the care of the maîtresse, I was led through a long, narrow passage into a kitchen that felt both foreign and oddly clean—though it held no fireplace or oven. It wasn’t until later that I understood the great black furnace dominating one corner served as their cooking heart. A quiet sense of relief washed over me when, instead of being left alone in this strange space, I was guided further into a small inner room called a “cabinet.” A cook, dressed in a short jacket, petticoat, and wooden sabots, brought my supper: meat of an unknown kind, served in a sharp yet pleasant sauce; chopped potatoes seasoned with something mysterious—perhaps vinegar and sugar; a tartine of bread and butter; and a baked pear. Hungry and weary, I ate with gratitude.

After evening prayers, Madame Beck came to inspect me once more. She asked me to follow her upstairs. We passed through a series of tiny dormitories—once nuns’ cells, I would learn later—and then into the oratory, a low, dim room where a pale crucifix hung on the wall and two candles flickered in silence. At last, we reached a chamber where three children slept soundly in tiny beds. The air was thick with warmth from a stove, and laced with a strong, unexpected scent—like smoke mingled with spirits, unmistakably the smell of whisky.

Beside a table, where a candle burned low, sat a woman fast asleep. She wore a garish striped silk dress beneath a coarse apron, her clothes mismatched and absurd. On the table beside her lay a bottle and an empty glass. The scene was startling, yet Madame watched it all without expression—no smile, no frown, not even a hint of disapproval. She didn’t wake her. With calm authority, she pointed to a fourth bed and indicated it was mine. After extinguishing the candle and lighting a night-lamp, she slipped through a half-open door into her own spacious, well-appointed bedroom visible beyond.

That night, my prayers were all thanks. How far I had come since leaving London just two days before—without protection, without plan, only hope clinging like a thread. Yet here I was, sheltered, fed, and assigned a place under a roof that felt both alien and strangely safe.

I was a light sleeper. In the deep silence of the night, I woke suddenly. All was still—but a white figure stood in the room. Madame, in her nightdress, moved silently across the floor. She visited each of the three sleeping children, then approached me. I feigned sleep, but she studied me closely. A quiet drama unfolded. She sat on the edge of my bed for what seemed a quarter of an hour, watching my face. Then she leaned closer, lifted my cap slightly, turned back the border to reveal my hair, and examined my hand resting on the sheets.

Next, she turned to the chair at the foot of the bed, where my clothes lay folded. As she touched them, I opened my eyes cautiously—curious to see how far her scrutiny might go. And go it did. She inspected every article of clothing. I sensed her intent: to judge me by my garments—my station, means, habits. Her hands moved methodically. She turned my pocket inside out, counted the coins in my purse, opened my small notebook, read its contents aloud to herself, and pulled out a plaited lock of Miss Marchmont’s grey hair. Then, with special attention, she took the keys to my trunk, desk, and workbox, and carried them into her own room.

I rose quietly in bed and followed with my eyes. Those keys, reader, were not returned until they had left an impression in wax upon her dressing table—a record of their shape, a silent testament to her vigilance. Only then were my belongings restored, carefully folded and placed back in order.

What conclusions did she draw? Were they favorable? I could not tell. Her face remained unreadable—a mask of stone in the moonlight. But I knew, in that moment, that this was not England. This was a world governed by watchfulness, discretion, and control.

The next day revealed more about the woman who had occupied the room before me—Mrs. Sweeny. She had presented herself as an English lady of fallen fortune, claiming to be from Middlesex and speaking with a pure London accent. Madame, trusting her own methods of discovery, hired her without hesitation—just as she had hired me. Mrs. Sweeny became the nursery governess for the three children.

But soon enough, the truth emerged. She was not English at all—she was Irish. Her true station, I couldn’t say. She claimed to have raised the son and daughter of a marquis, though I suspected she’d been little more than a servant or nurse in some noble household. Her speech was thick, layered with affected Cockney mannerisms. Yet she possessed a wardrobe of suspicious grandeur—stiff silks ill-fitting her frame, lace-trimmed caps, and most notably, a real Indian shawl, a “véritable cachemire,” which Madame admired with genuine awe. That shawl, I believe, was her only claim to dignity. Without it, she would have vanished from the pensionnat in two days. With it, she ruled the household—commanding respect even from the teachers and servants, and momentarily bending Madame’s will.

But when she learned I was to take her place, her true nature erupted. She rose against me with full force. Madame observed this with perfect composure—so still, so unshaken, that I felt compelled to remain calm myself. For a brief moment, Madame stepped out of the room. Ten minutes later, a police officer stood among us. Mrs. Sweeny and her belongings were removed. Not a word was spoken in anger. Madame’s brow never twitched.

All of this—the summons, the arrest, the expulsion—was settled before breakfast. The room was cleaned, aired, scrubbed of every trace of her presence—even the lingering scent of whisky, that subtle clue to her downfall, erased completely. By the time Madame emerged from her chamber, radiant as dawn, she was already pouring her first cup of coffee with serene detachment.

By noon, I was summoned to assist Madame with her morning routine. (It seemed my role would blend governess and lady’s maid.) Until midday, she stayed in her wrapper, shawl, and soft slippers. One might wonder how an English headmistress would view such informality.

Dressing her hair proved difficult. She had abundant auburn locks, untouched by grey, despite her forty years. Seeing my confusion, she said gently, “You’ve never been a femme-de-chambre in your country?” Taking the brush from my hand, she arranged her hair herself, guiding me without harshness. In other tasks, she directed and helped with equal grace, never showing impatience. Note: This was the only time I ever dressed her. From then on, Rosine, the porter, took over.

Once attired, Madame Beck appeared shorter and fuller-figured, yet graceful in her own way—proportioned, balanced. Her complexion was fresh, rosy but not flushed; her blue eyes calm and observant. Her dark silk dress clung to her form with French precision. She looked well, though modestly so—bourgeoise, indeed, in every sense. There was a harmony in her bearing, yet her features offered contrast: her forehead high but narrow, expressing intelligence and kindness, but no breadth; her eyes watchful, serene, yet never stirred by passion. Her mouth was firm, lips thin—hard, even grim at times. She reminded me of Minos, the ancient judge of souls, clad in petticoats.

Yet in time, I saw she was more than that. Her name was Modeste Maria Beck, born Kint—though she should have been Ignacia. She was charitable, doing much good. Her rule was mild, though firm. I heard she never scolded Mrs. Sweeny, despite her drunkenness and neglect—yet dismissed her the moment it suited her. Similarly, she never openly criticized her staff—yet teachers and masters came and went with quiet regularity, replaced without explanation.

Her school was both a boarding house and a day institution. Over a hundred day pupils attended, while about twenty boarded. She managed them all—four teachers, eight masters, six servants, three children—while maintaining harmony with parents and visitors. She did it all without haste, without strain, without visible effort. She had a system, and it worked perfectly.

“Surveillance,” “espionage”—these were her guiding principles. The reader has already seen a glimpse: turning my pocket inside out, reading my private notes, testing my keys. She knew people deeply, and used them wisely. She wouldn’t hesitate to use the dirtiest tool for a dirty job—discarding it afterward like a spent shell. But when she found someone pure and reliable, she guarded them like treasure, wrapped in silk and cotton wool.

Yet beware: trust her too far, and you risked becoming her secret enemy. Interest was her compass—the driving force behind every decision. I’ve seen her feelings appealed to, and smiled at the attempt. No one ever swayed her through emotion. To try was to provoke her antipathy. It reminded her of her own emptiness. She gave charity generously—to classes, to causes—but rarely to individuals. She supported large-scale philanthropy, yet felt nothing for personal sorrow. Even Christ’s agony in Gethsemane, his death on Calvary, could not move her to a single tear.

Still, she was remarkable. Capable, wise, relentless. That school was too small a stage for her talents. She ought to have ruled a nation, led a turbulent assembly. No one could intimidate her, irritate her nerves, or outwit her. She combined the roles of prime minister and chief of police. Wise, firm, faithless; secretive, clever, impassive; vigilant, inscrutable; sharp-minded, yet emotionally dead—yet always impeccably composed. What more could one ask?

Let me clarify: I did not grasp all this in a month, nor even in half a year. At first, I saw only the outward glow of a thriving school. Here was a large house filled with healthy, lively girls—well-dressed, many beautiful—learning easily, without strain. Lessons were gentle, play allowed, exercise encouraged. Food was plentiful and good. No pallid faces, no frail bodies. Holidays were frequent. Sleep, washing, meals—all given ample time. Her methods were liberal, rational, healthful. Many an English schoolmistress might do well to emulate her—if only parents would allow it.

Behind the house lay a garden, and in summer, the girls lived outdoors among roses and fruit trees. Under a vast vine-covered arbor, Madame would sit in the afternoons, summoning classes to sew and read. Masters came and went, delivering lively lectures rather than rigid lessons. Students made notes—or didn’t—as they pleased, knowing they could copy others’ if needed. Monthly outings, Catholic holidays, and occasional country walks with *gaufres*, *vin blanc*, milk, *pain bis*, or butter rolls and coffee—life seemed pleasant. Madame appeared kind, the teachers tolerable, the girls cheerful, perhaps a bit boisterous, but full of life.

This was the enchanted view from afar. But soon, the distance would vanish. I would be pulled down from my nursery perch, forced into deeper contact with the world of the Rue Fossette.

One afternoon, I sat upstairs, teaching the children English while stitching a silk dress for Madame. She entered quietly, her brow furrowed in thought, her expression distant. She sat opposite me, silent for several minutes. Désirée, the eldest, read aloud from Mrs. Barbauld’s essays, translating as she went. Madame listened.

Then, without warning, she asked, almost accusingly: “Miss, were you a governess in England?”

“No, Madame,” I replied, smiling. “You’re mistaken.”

“Is this your first attempt at teaching—my children?”

I confirmed it was. She fell silent again, but when I reached for a pin, I caught her studying me—measuring, judging, weighing. She had already examined my things, my papers, my past. But from that day, for about two weeks, she tested me anew. She listened at the nursery door, followed me at a distance, hiding behind trees when we walked. Once satisfied, she made her move.

One morning, she approached me abruptly, pretending urgency. “I’m in a dilemma,” she said. “Mr. Wilson, the English master, hasn’t arrived. He may be ill. The class waits. Can you give a short dictation exercise? Just to keep the lesson going?”

“In classe, Madame?” I asked.

“Yes—in the second division.”

“Sixty pupils,” I said, already retreating into my usual cowardice. I knew the number. I feared the task. My instinct was to hide, to avoid responsibility. If left to myself, I might have spent twenty years teaching infants the hornbook, sewing dresses, making frocks—content in my sloth. Not because I loved it, but because it meant no heavy burden, no trial. Avoiding pain felt like peace. I lived in two worlds: one of thought, rich with imagination; one of reality, limited to bread, work, and shelter.

“Come,” she said, as I bent over a child’s pinafore. “Leave that.”

“But Fifine needs it, Madame.”

“Fifine must wait. I need you.”
