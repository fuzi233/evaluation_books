Durrance chose the moment of leaving with the same care a cook gives to lifting a soufflé from the oven—no lingering at the door, no chance for the thing to collapse. In the drawing-room at The Pool he said good-bye to Ethne with a brevity that felt almost like a restraint of the senses. Outside, the carriage waited at the steps of Guessens, luggage strapped tight upon the roof; the servant stood ready, as still as a pantryman before a formal dinner.

Ethne came with him onto the terrace. Above the flight of steps Mrs. Adair was stationed, a figure set like a decanter on a sideboard, all polish and purpose. Durrance held his hand out to her; she did not take it. Instead she turned to Ethne.

“I want to speak to Colonel Durrance before he goes.”

“Very well,” Ethne answered. She looked at Durrance then, and for a moment her voice softened as if she were pressing something warm into his palm. “Then we will say good-bye here. You will write from Wiesbaden? Soon, please!”

“The moment I arrive,” he said.

He went down the steps with Mrs. Adair, leaving Ethne on the terrace—alone where, for months, she had so often been made to stand alone in one way or another. The last scene of pretence had been played; the long season of watching and guarded speech was over. Durrance’s relief showed itself in the set of his shoulders and the briskness of his stride as he crossed the lawn. Mrs. Adair, by contrast, let the air hold her back, as though each step were through thick water.

“So you are going,” she said. “In two days’ time you will be at Wiesbaden and Ethne at Glenalla. We shall all be scattered. It will be lonely here.”

She had won what she had laboured for: distance. No longer would she be tormented by the sound of their voices coming together in a room while she stood outside, counting the pauses. And yet the victory tasted flat—like tea gone cold because it has been left too long for someone who never comes.

“The house will seem very empty after you are all gone,” she added; and she turned with him down into the garden.

“We shall come back, no doubt,” Durrance said, as if routine could be relied upon to mend what feeling had torn.

Mrs. Adair looked about her as they walked. The garden had been stripped to its bones. Flowers were gone; sunlight too seemed to have been taken away and put in storage. A blanket of cloud pressed down on the sky, and underfoot the grass had the dull, sodden hue of lettuce left too long in water. The stream ran grey between trees, and the wind sent russet and yellow leaves skittering along the lawns with a dry, whispering scrape—an anxious, incessant little sound that filled the spaces where summer’s bees had been. Even Durrance, sightless as he was, seemed to feel the season through the air’s thin edge and the way the wind worried at the hedges.

“How long shall you stay at Wiesbaden?” she asked.

“I can hardly tell. But as long as it’s advisable.”

“That tells me nothing at all,” she said quickly. “I suppose it was meant not to tell me anything.”

He did not answer. His silence had the clean finality of a lid shut down. It roused her like a sudden clang in a quiet room—one of those noises that make you aware, all at once, of how carefully you have been listening.

“You distrust me?” she demanded, and anger rode through her voice.

He answered gently, almost wearily, as if he had weighed the matter and found no relish in it. “Have I no reason to distrust you? Why did you tell me of Captain Willoughby’s coming? Why did you interfere?”

“I thought you ought to know.”

“But Ethne wished the secret kept. I am glad to know—very glad. But, after all, you told me, and you were Ethne’s friend.”

“Yours, too, I hope,” Mrs. Adair returned, and then, in a sudden rush, “How could I go on keeping silence? Don’t you understand?”

“No.”

He might have understood if he had ever spent much thought on her; but he had not, and she knew it with the same sharp certainty one knows the absence of salt in a broth. That simple word—no—was not merely refusal; it was proof that she had been speaking into a void.

“I spoke brutally, didn’t I?” she said. “I told you the truth as brutally as I could. Doesn’t that help you to understand?”

Again he said, “No,” and the quietness of it struck her like a slap. Something in her broke loose then. She seemed to hear herself from a distance, as though her own voice were an instrument playing too loud in a small room, yet she could not damp it down.

“I told you the truth brutally on purpose. I was so stung because you would not see what was so visible had you only the mind to see. I wanted to hurt you. I am a bad, bad woman, I suppose. There were you and she in the room talking together in the darkness; there was I alone upon the terrace. It was the same again to-day. You and Ethne in the room, I alone upon the terrace. I wonder whether it will always be so. But you will not say—you will not say.”

She struck her hands together, a small crack of sound in the wind, like the snap of a twig. Durrance walked on without answering. The garden path drew them toward the stile at the far end—toward the boundary where cultivated ground gave up to fields. He quickened his pace. Mrs. Adair had to hurry to keep beside him, her skirts catching the breeze with a faint, fretful rustle.

His speed was an answer, but not the one she wanted. Her words kept spilling out, heedless of prudence, as if a pot had boiled over.

“I do not think I would have minded so much,” she went on, “if Ethne had really cared for you; but she never cared more than as a friend cares—just a mere friend. And what’s friendship worth?”

“Something, surely,” Durrance said.

“It does not prevent Ethne from shrinking from her friend,” Mrs. Adair cried. “She shrinks from you. Shall I tell you why? Because you are blind. She is afraid. While I—I will tell you the truth—I am glad. When the news first came from Wadi Halfa that you were blind, I was glad; when I saw you in Hill Street, I was glad; ever since, I have been glad—quite glad. Because I saw that she shrank. From the beginning she shrank, thinking how her life would be hampered and fettered.”

Her voice sank to a whisper, but the whisper was fiercer than a shout, the way a knife is more dreadful than a club. And then she began, over and over, as though insisting could turn desire into fact:

“I am not afraid. I am not afraid. I am not afraid.”

To Durrance, who had known pain in many forms and had learned to take it without flinching, this outburst had a particular horror—because it came from the woman who called herself Ethne’s friend, and because it arrived so wholly unexpected. The wind worried at the trees; leaves rasped and tumbled; somewhere a gate creaked on its hinge. The garden’s small autumn noises seemed suddenly loud, as if the place itself were listening.

“Ethne wrote to you at Wadi Halfa out of pity,” she pressed on. “That was all. She wrote out of pity; and, having written, she was afraid of what she had done; and being afraid, she had not courage to tell you she was afraid. You would not have blamed her if she had frankly admitted it; you would have remained her friend. But she had not the courage.”

Durrance knew another explanation for Ethne’s hesitations and timidities, and he knew it was the true one. But tomorrow he would be gone from Salcombe estuary, and Ethne would be on her way toward Donegal. To argue now against these slanders felt as useless as shouting at the wind to change. Besides, the stile was close—freedom only a few steps away. He said only, quietly, “You are not just to Ethne.”

At that, something snapped back into place inside Mrs. Adair. The wildness went out of her as suddenly as a pan dies when the flame is turned down. She saw—clearly, bitterly—the futility of what she had said. True or false, it could achieve nothing. He was always with Ethne, never with her. She became conscious of her own abasement and began at once to scramble for excuses, as if she might hide the ugly taste with a little sugar.

“I am a bad woman, I suppose. But after all, I have not had the happiest of lives. Perhaps there is something to be said for me.”

It sounded feeble even to her. But they had reached the stile. Durrance stopped and turned toward her. The sternness in his face softened, the way a hard line in a voice can soften when a person hears, beneath the words, the human need. In the old days, when he could see, he had always associated Mrs. Adair with dignity and reticence. It seemed scarcely possible that the woman before him—breathing too quickly, voice unsteady—was the same.

“Will you tell me?” he asked, gently.

“I was married almost straight from school,” she began. “I was the merest girl. I knew nothing, and I was married to a man of whom I knew nothing. It was my mother’s doing, and no doubt she thought she was acting for the very best. She was securing for me a position of a kind, and comfort and release from any danger of poverty. I accepted what she said blindly, ignorantly. I could hardly have refused, indeed, for my mother was an imperious woman, and I was accustomed to obedience. I did as she told me and married dutifully the man whom she chose. The case is common enough, no doubt, but its frequency does not make it easier of endurance.”

“But Mr. Adair?” Durrance said. “After all, I knew him. He was older, no doubt, than you, but he was kind. I think, too, he cared for you.”

“Yes. He was kindness itself, and he cared for me. Both things are true. The knowledge that he did care for me was the one link, if you understand.” She paused as though listening to her own life for its rhythm, then went on. “At the beginning I was contented, I suppose. I had a house in town and another here. But it was dull,” and she stretched her arms outward, as if she could not bear the smallness of what she had been given. “Oh, how dull it was!

“Do you know the little back streets in a manufacturing town? Rows of small houses, side by side, with nothing to relieve them of their ugly regularity—each with the self-same windows, the self-same door, the self-same doorstep. Overhead a drift of smoke, and every little green thing, down to the plants in the window, dirty and black. The sort of street whence any crazy religious charlatan who can promise a little colour to their grey lives can get as many votaries as he wants. Well, when I thought over my life, one of those little streets always came into my mind.”

As she spoke, the air around them was full of its own small particulars: the steady shush of wind in bare branches, the stream’s low, tired murmur, the far-off clatter of a farm cart on a road beyond the fields. It was a country soundscape, honest and unforced—nothing like the harsh, metallic insistence of the city that drives thought into corners. Here, each noise seemed to leave room for feeling, rather than crowd it out.

“There are women—heaps of them, no doubt—to whom the management of a big house, the season in London, the ordinary round of visits, are sufficient,” she continued. “I, worse luck, was not one of them. Dull! You, with your hundred thousand things to do, cannot conceive how oppressively dull my life was. And that was not all!”

She hesitated, but she had gone too far to retreat with any dignity left intact.

“I married, as I say, knowing nothing of the important things. I believed at the first that mine was just the allotted life of all women. But I began soon to have my doubts. I got to know that there was something more to be won out of existence than mere dullness—at least, that there was something more for others, though not for me. One could not help learning that. One passed a man and a woman riding together, and one chanced to look into the woman’s face as one passed; or one saw, perhaps, the woman alone and talked with her for a little while, and from the happiness of her looks and voice one knew with absolute certainty that there was ever so much more. Only the chance of that ever so much more my mother had denied to me.”

Durrance’s face had lost its hardness entirely. Mrs. Adair was no longer violent; she was simple, speaking as one speaks when the performance of life drops away and only the plain story remains.

“And then you came,” she said. “I met you, and met you again. You went away upon your duties and you returned; and I learnt now, not that there was ever so much more, but just what that ever so much more was. But it was still, of course, denied to me. However, in spite of that I felt happier. I thought that I should be quite content to have you for a friend, to watch your progress, and to feel pride in it. But you see—Ethne came, too, and you turned to her. At once—oh, at once! If you had only been a little less quick to turn to her! In a very short while I was sad and sorry that you had ever come into my life.”

“I knew nothing of this,” Durrance said. “I never suspected. I am sorry.”

“I took care you should not suspect,” Mrs. Adair replied. “But I tried to keep you; with all my wits I tried. No match-maker in the world ever worked so hard to bring two people together as I did to bring together Ethne and Mr. Feversham—and I succeeded.”

The words struck him with the shock of a door flung open on a room you had forgotten existed. Durrance leaned back against the stile, and a laugh rose in him—not from mirth, but from the stunned recognition of how lives are seasoned by another hand. Here, then, was the origin of the whole sad business. From what small beginnings it had grown. A nudge here, an arranged meeting there—like altering a recipe by a pinch—and the result carried forward for years: disgrace and ruin, unhappiness, pretence piled upon pretence until it became habit. He thought himself backward into those days when he walked on his own path, heedless, not imagining that other people were at work around him, turning choices as easily as one turns a page.

“I succeeded,” Mrs. Adair repeated. “You told me I had succeeded one morning in the Row. How glad I was! You did not notice it, I am sure. The next moment you took all my gladness from me by telling me you were starting for the Soudan. You were away three years. They were not happy years for me. You came back. My husband was dead, but Ethne was free. Ethne refused you, but you went blind and she claimed you. You can see what ups and downs have fallen to me. But these months here have been the worst.”

“I am very sorry,” said Durrance.

It was plain to him now that there was, indeed, something to be said for her. The world had not dealt gently with Mrs. Adair; it had given her comfort without colour, position without language for what she needed, and then, at the end, had tempted her with the sound of a happiness she could not enter. He could understand her jealousy because he carried his own version of it—his own ache at being close to what he could not hold. He could not excuse what she had done, but he could be gentle with the suffering beneath it.

“I am very sorry, Mrs. Adair,” he repeated, because he had no more fitting words. Then he held out his hand.

“Good-bye,” she said, and this time her voice did not strike; it simply fell, tired.

He climbed over the stile and crossed the fields to his house. Behind him Mrs. Adair remained, standing by the boundary between garden and open land, listening to his footsteps diminish—first distinct on the nearer ground, then softened by distance, until the wind and the stream took back the air for themselves. She had shot her bolt and hit no one but herself and the man she cared for. That much she understood with painful clarity.

And she looked forward, too, seeing the future in the way one sees tomorrow’s weather in the present sky. If Durrance did not keep Ethne to her promise—did not marry her, did not go with her to her country—then he would come back to Guessens. The thought made her folly sharper. If she had kept silence, she might have had a true, constant friend near at hand, a steady presence like a hearth that warms a house through winter. It would have been something—more than something. But since she had spoken, they could never meet without embarrassment; and however carefully they might practise cordiality, there would always remain between them the memory of what she had said and what he had listened to, on the afternoon when he left for Wiesbaden.
