When Romola sank onto the lichen-crusted stone beneath the gnarled old cypress on the slopes of Bogoli, every breath of the morning wove around her a new feeling of unaccustomed freedom. She had slipped out of Florence before the dawn, wrapped in the rough wool of a pilgrim’s disguise, putting the labyrinthine streets, the weight of her dead father’s empty library, and the treachery of her husband behind her. By this hour, Tito was already miles along the road to Siena, every hoofbeat of his horse widening the gulf between them, while every step she took turned her further away from the life that had curdled into bitterness. Below the roadside, the land fell away into a green hollow, and the sombre bulk of the distant mountains hung softened in the haze, no human face, no familiar wall, no old obligation pressing in on her. For the first time in her life, she felt herself alone under the wide sky, answering to no one but her own will.

A voice spoke so close to her ear she could feel the faint stir of breath against her hair.
“You are Romola de’ Bardi, wife of Tito Melema.”

She knew that voice. It had boomed from the pulpit of the Duomo, it had vibrated through the crowd at the street sermons, it had pricked at her conscience more than once when she had thought to ignore it. She did not turn her head, did not lift her gaze from the wild thyme at her feet. Dread coiled cold in her chest even as she raged at the intrusion: some black-robed friar, daring to track her down, daring to break in on the solitude she had fought so hard to claim. She shook as if the very Destiny men painted as a crowned, distant goddess had stepped down from the clouds to grasp her with flesh and blood fingers.

“You flee Florence in disguise,” the voice went on, unyielding. “I hold a command from God to stop you. You are not permitted to run.”

Anger flared hot in her throat. Still she would not turn, would not let him see the resentment burning in her face at the way his gaze picked her apart. “What right have you to speak to me, or to bar my way?”

“The right of a messenger. You wear this pilgrim’s garb with no pilgrim’s purpose, only to hide your true name, to slip free of the lot God has laid at your feet, so you may carve out a new life ruled by no will but your own. I was shown you before you passed by this spot. My daughter, you must go back.”

Every word stoked her rebellion higher, and the very fact that she felt shaken, that some part of her already wavered, made her cling harder to her resolve. “I will not return. I acknowledge no right of priests or monks to meddle in my choices. You have no power over me.”

“I know you were raised to scorn obedience,” the voice said, soft but unshakable. “It is not this poor monk that commands you. It is the truth. You cannot outrun it. Obey, and it will lead you. Resist, and it will hang around your neck like an iron chain to drag you for all your days. You will obey. Your old servant, who was to travel with you, will return to you with the mules; my companion has gone to fetch him. You will go back to Florence.”

Romola sprang to her feet, anger blazing in her eyes, and spun to face him. She had known full well who it was all along: Fra Girolamo Savonarola. She stood nearly as tall as he, their faces almost level, the defiant words on her lips dying unspoken the moment their gazes met. There was no celestial beauty in his face, no otherworldly glow. His features were harsh, strong, refined only by years of fast and prayer and rigid discipline. But his gaze did not carry the prurient curiosity of a stranger, nor the greedy admiration Tito had once turned on her. It held a simple, fierce care for her, for her soul, that she had never encountered in any living human being. She could not find it in herself to argue he had no right to speak. She stood silent, looking at him.

“You are proud of your freedom, my daughter,” he said. “But who is the base debtor that ever thinks himself free?”

The words cut like a sharp sting, and Romola’s face blanched as if a pale flash of lightning had passed over it.
“You flee your debts. The debt of a Florentine citizen. The debt of a wife. You turn your back on the life appointed you, to choose a new one, as if any man or woman gets to pick their own duties any more than they get to pick their birthplace, or their father, or their mother. You are running from the presence of God into the wilderness.”

Her anger had melted away, leaving behind a strange, unnameable presentiment that there might be strength in submission, if this man, whom she had always half-disdained as a firebrand preacher, held some law worth following. She could not simply refuse any longer.
“My father,” she said, the title slipping unbidden from her lips, a title she had never once used for him before, “you cannot know the sorrow that drives me. No one knows but me. No one can judge for me. I have borne so much pain I cannot stay.”

“I know enough. My mind has been illuminated on your behalf. I do not seek the secrets of your marriage, that belong under the seal of confession. But I have a divine warrant to stop you that needs no such knowledge. You were warned, in my presence, before you ever wed Tito, that you might still choose to remain free. You chose the bond. To break it now in secret, in disguise, is to commit one of the greatest wrongs a woman and a citizen can do: to abandon a pledge you swore before God and all your Florentine neighbours. You would complain of the wrongs done to you, while you yourself break the most basic thread that ties man to man—faith to the word you have given? Is that the wisdom your old humanist tutors taught you, that you cannot see the bare duty of integrity where the Church would have taught you to see not just integrity, but holy purpose?”

Blood rushed to Romola’s face, and she shrank back as if she had been struck. She had never thought of her flight as the same kind of cowardly evasion Tito had always practiced, but the words left her too shaken to speak.

“You run from that pledge to leave Florence,” he went on. “Florence, the only home you have, the only people on this earth to whom you owe the debt of shared citizenship.”

“I would never have left Florence,” she said, her voice trembling, “as long as there was any hope I could serve my father there.”

“Do you own no tie but the love of a daughter for her fleshly father?” he said. “You have lived your life blind, surrounded by men who sit on their hill of old manuscripts and look down on the lives of their fellow citizens, chattering about the wisdom of dead pagans while they scorn the work God is doing in the present. They told you stories of pagan women who lived for their republic, but never taught you that you, a Florentine woman, owe your life to Florence itself. Your people groan under a yoke of tyranny, of hunger, of corruption, and you would slip away from under it instead of standing with them to lighten the load? The streets are full of starving children, of widows crying out for bread, and you say you will go away to ease your own private sorrow? The servants of God are fighting to build a city of justice and peace, and you would spread your wings like a bird and fly away, thinking none of it is any of your concern? You scorn the Church’s teachings, but you would be far better than you are now if you knelt beside the humblest illiterate weaver’s wife who prays for Florence, who feels sisterhood with every neighbour, who waits and endures because she knows the work God has planned for the city is too great for her small strength to abandon.”

“I am not running to ease and luxury!” Romola lifted her head, stung to defend herself. “I go to hardship. I expect no joy. It left my life long ago.”

“You still seek your own will, over the law you are bound to obey,” he said. “Good is not a thing you can pick and choose at will. It is a river that flows from the foot of the Invisible Throne, and it runs only along the path of obedience. Run from your duties, and you will find nothing but sorrow without purpose, bitter herbs with no bread to sustain you.”

“If you only knew what I have had to bear,” Romola whispered, clasping her hands tight until her knuckles went white, pleading up at him. “How impossible it felt to stay.”

“Draw forth the thing you carry tucked in your mantle, at the cord around your neck,” he said, pointing to her chest. “Look at it.”

Romola started, but her will was no longer her own to resist. She pulled out the small crucifix he had given her months before, the one she had carried through every dark day since.

“There, my daughter, is the image of the Supreme Offering, made out of perfect love, for the great need of suffering humanity.” He paused, and she held the crucifix trembling, thinking of all the miles she had walked since she first took it from his hands, all the grief she had endured. “Conform your life to that image. Make your own sorrow an offering. When the fire of divine charity burns in you, you will look back and see you were foolish to think your private pain mattered half so much as the pain of a hundred thousand souls around you. You held yourself proud, apart from the common folk of Florence, but you were not yet born to the true life of a child of God. If the love you bore your father is no longer enough to keep you here, you say, then you have no law, no faith? That is no life at all. That is the life of a beast robbed of its young, that wanders aimless in the dark. If you shared the faith of the believers, you would see Florence is the home of your soul as much as your birthplace, that there is work there for you that no one else can do. Who will fill your empty spot if you run away? The world is full of iniquity, full of groans, light fighting against darkness, and you say you will burst your bonds and go where no one will claim anything of you? Every bond in your life is a debt. The only right path is paying it. You may wander the whole earth, and you will never outrun that truth.”

“I thought my brother Dino was right!” Romola burst out, fighting the last of her resistance. “He broke all his family ties, he forsook his place, to follow God. Why can I not do the same?”

“That was a special, singular vocation. He could not attain the higher life if he had stayed, for it would have been stifled in him. You are no Dino. You are a wife. You would break your ties out of anger and self-will, not because God calls you to renounce them. The higher life begins the moment you set your own will aside to bow to divine law. It is the portal to wisdom, to true freedom, to blessedness. Your old pagan wisdom, the wisdom of dead men long turned to dust, has left you empty. It gave you no care for your neighbours, no stake in the work of regenerating Florence, no share in that divine love that burns away the petty pain of the self. Now your own sword of sorrow has pierced your soul, and you would run, leaving all the suffering of your city behind you? If the streets are full of wickedness, your steps should shine with purity. If there are cries of anguish, you should be there to still them. Sorrow has come to teach you a new worship. The sign of it is in your hand.”

Romola still wavered, seeing the path he laid out before her, and knowing it would be hard, hot with the pain of returning to a husband she no longer loved, to a life that had brought her nothing but grief. But his words had reached a place in her she had never known existed, a place that had already begun to wake when she fled Florence, sick at heart of selfish ease. She turned her eyes away from his face, stood silent for minutes, her hands hanging clasped before her.
“My husband,” she whispered, at last, the words dragged out of her, “my love for him is long dead.”

“Marriage is no mere fleshly bond built on fleeting passion,” Savonarola said, his voice softening. “It is a sacramental vow that only God can break. Your husband is no malefactor, is he?”

Romola started. “No. Heaven forbid. I accuse him of no crime.”

“If he were a malefactor,” he said, “your place would be in the prison cell beside him. If the cross of a difficult marriage is laid on your shoulders, you must carry it. You may say you will forsake him, but you can never cease to be a wife.”

“Make that sorrow an offering too,” he went on, his voice growing more intense, his face lit up with the inner fire that had moved all of Florence. “Lay it on the altar of the great work God is doing here, where sin and sorrow will be made to cease. The work is already beginning. The eyes of faith see it. It may be our blessedness to die for it, to die daily by crucifying our own selfish will, to lay our bodies on the altar at the last. You are a child of Florence. Fulfil the great inheritance that is yours. Live for your people. Bear the anguish. The iron is sharp, I know. It rends tender flesh. The draught is bitter on the lips. But there is rapture in that cup, a vision that will make all petty, personal sorrow seem as nothing.”

Romola felt all her doubts melt away in the glow of his fierce, unshakable faith. She felt a new strength rising in her, a purpose she had never known before.
“Father,” she said, in a low, praying cry, “I will be guided. Teach me. I will go back.”

Almost unconsciously, she sank to her knees in the grass. Savonarola stretched his hands out over her, and no words were needed. The morning stillness wrapped around them, and the road to Siena, the road to the empty, wandering freedom she had once craved, vanished from her mind.
