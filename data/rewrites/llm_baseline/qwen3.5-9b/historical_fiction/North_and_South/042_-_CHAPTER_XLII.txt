"When some beloved voice that was to you
Both sound and sweetness, faileth suddenly,
And silence, against which you dare not cry,
Aches round you like a strong disease and new,—
What hope? what help? what music will undo
That silence to your sense?"

The blow had struck with terrible force. Margaret collapsed into a state of utter prostration. It was not a breakdown marked by sobs or tears, nor did she find solace in words. She lay upon the sofa, her eyes closed, speaking only in faint whispers when addressed directly. Mr. Bell sat beside her, deeply perplexed. He dared not abandon her, yet he dared not suggest returning to Oxford immediately; her physical exhaustion was absolute, rendering any such journey impossible, regardless of the painful sights awaiting her there.

Seated by the dying fire, Mr. Bell wrestled with his dilemma. Margaret remained motionless, almost breathless, beside him. He refused to leave her even to dine with Dixon, who had prepared a meal downstairs and offered it with sobbing hospitality. A plate of food was carried up to him. Usually fastidious and discerning regarding flavor, Mr. Bell found the devilled chicken tasting now of sawdust. He minced some of the fowl for Margaret, seasoning it heavily, but when Dixon attempted to feed her according to his instructions, her languid shake of the head indicated that in her current condition, food would only choke her rather than nourish her.

Mr. Bell sighed heavily, rose from his easy chair with stiffened legs from the journey, and followed Dixon out of the room. "I cannot leave her," he muttered. "I must write to them at Oxford to ensure preparations are underway; they can proceed until my arrival. Could not Mrs. Lennox come to her? I shall write instructing her to do so. The girl needs a female friend nearby, someone to coax her into a proper release of grief."

Dixon was weeping profusely, enough for two, but after composing herself, she informed Mr. Bell that Mrs. Lennox was too close to her confinement to travel. "Very well," Mr. Bell replied. "We must summon Mrs. Shaw. Has she returned to England?"
"Yes, sir, she has; but I suspect she will not wish to leave Mrs. Lennox during such a critical time," Dixon said, disapproving of a stranger sharing her burden of caring for Margaret.
"Critical time, be—" Mr. Bell cut himself off with a cough. "She could have contentedly resided in Venice or Naples, or some of those Papal territories, during the last 'critical period' which occurred in Corfu, I believe. And what significance does that little prosperous woman's 'critical time' hold compared to this poor creature there—this helpless, homeless, friendless Margaret—lying as still on that sofa as if it were an altar-tomb, and she the marble statue upon it. Tell Mrs. Shaw she must come. Arrange a room for her by tomorrow night. I will ensure her arrival."

Consequently, Mr. Bell penned a letter. Mrs. Shaw later declared, with many tears, that it resembled one written by the General when preparing for a gout attack, promising to cherish it forever. Had he merely requested or urged her attendance, implying refusal was an option, she might have hesitated despite her genuine sympathy. It required the sharp, unceremonious command to overcome her inertia, prompting her to pack her belongings under the supervision of her maid.

Edith, dressed in cap, shawl, and tears, emerged onto the landing as Captain Lennox assisted his mother toward the carriage. "Do not forget, Mother," she cried. "Margaret must come to live with us. Sholto departs for Oxford on Wednesday; you must instruct Mr. Bell to notify him of our expected arrival. And if Sholto requires you, he can travel from Oxford to Milton. Do not forget, Mother; you are to bring Margaret back."

Edith retreated into the drawing-room, where Mr. Henry Lennox sat cutting open a new review. Without looking up, he remarked, "If you dislike Sholto's prolonged absence, Edith, I hope you will permit me to descend to Milton and render whatever assistance I can."
"Oh, thank you," Edith replied. "Old Mr. Bell will undoubtedly manage everything; additional aid may not be necessary. One rarely expects much *savoir-faire* from a resident Fellow. However, dearest, darling Margaret—it will be wonderful to have her here again. You were both great allies years ago."
"Were we?" he asked indifferently, feigning interest in the review. "Well, perhaps not—I forget. I was so consumed with thoughts of Sholto. But surely it is fortunate that if my uncle were to pass away, it should be precisely now, when we have returned home, settled in the ancestral house, and fully prepared to receive Margaret? Poor soul! What a transition awaits her from Milton! I shall procure new chintz for her bedroom, make it appear fresh and bright, and lift her spirits somewhat."

Mrs. Shaw traveled to Milton in a similar vein of benevolence, oscillating between dread of the initial reunion and fantasies of swiftly removing Margaret from "that dreadful place" to the comforting amenities of Harley Street. "Oh dear!" she murmured to her maid, gazing at the smokestacks. "Look at those chimneys! My poor sister Hale! I scarcely could have rested in Naples had I foreseen such a scene! I ought to have come and fetched her and Margaret away." Internally, she admitted she had always considered her brother-in-law somewhat weak, but never so frail as now, witnessing the exchange of the lovely Helstone home for this setting.

Margaret remained unchanged: pale, immobile, silent, and tearless. They had informed her that Aunt Shaw was arriving, yet she displayed neither surprise, joy, nor aversion. Mr. Bell, whose appetite had returned and whom Dixon diligently catered to, vainly urged her to taste sweetbreads stewed with oysters. She shook her head with the same quiet stubbornness as the previous day, forcing him to consume them himself.

However, Margaret was the first to detect the cab halting outside, bringing her aunt from the railway station. Her eyelids quivered, her lips flushed and trembled. Mr. Bell descended to greet Mrs. Shaw. Upon their ascent, Margaret stood, striving to steady her dizzy frame. Seeing her aunt, she stepped forward into open arms, finding immediate, passionate relief in the embrace, her tears flowing freely onto her aunt's shoulder.

All notions of quiet, habitual affection, of years-long tenderness, of kinship to the deceased—all those inexplicable similarities in gaze, tone, and gesture that seemed inherent to one family, reminding Margaret so vividly of her mother—conspired to melt and soften her numb heart into an overflow of warm tears.

Mr. Bell slipped out of the room and descended to the study, ordering a fire and attempting to distract himself by examining various books. Each volume evoked memories or suggestions of his departed friend. While this represented a shift from his two days of vigilantly watching Margaret, it was no diversion of thought. He welcomed hearing Mr. Thornton's voice inquire at the door. Dixon dismissed him rather cavalierly; with the arrival of Mrs. Shaw's maid, visions of former grandeur, the Beresford lineage, and the "station" (as she termed it) from which her young mistress had been cast, and to which she hoped to restore her, filled her mind. Dwelling on these thoughts with complacency while conversing with Mrs. Shaw's maid—skillfully extracting details of status and consequence connected to the Harley Street establishment for the instruction of the listening Martha—rendered Dixon somewhat haughty in her treatment of Milton's inhabitants. Thus, although she usually respected Mr. Thornton, she was as curt as she dared in informing him that none of the house's residents could be seen that evening.

It became slightly awkward when Mr. Bell opened the study door and called out, "Thornton! Is that you? Enter for a moment or two; I wish to speak with you."

Mr. Thornton entered, leaving Dixon to retreat to the kitchen, restoring her self-esteem with a grand tale of Sir John Beresford's coach-and-six during his tenure as High Sheriff. "I hardly knew what I intended to say to you anyway. It is merely dull enough to sit in a room where everything whispers of a dead friend. Yet Margaret and her aunt must have the drawing-room to themselves!"
"Has Mrs.—has her aunt arrived?" Mr. Thornton asked.
"Arrived? Yes! Maid and all. One might have expected her to come alone at such a time! Now I shall have to vacate and find my way to the Clarendon."
"You must not go to the Clarendon. We possess five or six vacant bedrooms at home."
"Well ventilated?"
"I trust my mother to ensure that."
"Then I shall merely ascend to bid that wan girl goodnight, offer my respects to her aunt, and depart with you immediately."

Mr. Bell lingered upstairs for some time. Mr. Thornton began to consider it excessive, as he was preoccupied with business and had barely spared time to visit Crampton to inquire about Miss Hale.
