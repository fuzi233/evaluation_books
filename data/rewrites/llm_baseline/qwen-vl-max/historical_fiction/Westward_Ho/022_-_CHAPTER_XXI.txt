“Follow thee? Follow thee? Who would not follow thee? Long have you led us, and trusted us fairly.”

Amyas might well have fallen to the yellow fever had it not been for one thing—his own unyielding duty. He could not afford to be sick while his men were dying. It was a truth many noble souls in the Crimea would later know: so long as the work is urgent, the body can endure; but once the task ends, the spirit may falter, and the strength collapse. And Amyas, though he felt the sickness creeping through his limbs, refused to yield.

The next morning, he summoned a council—not of war, but of survival. The crew was near mutiny, gripped by fear and fever. Amyas addressed them plainly: “What good will come of killing me—or being killed by me?” Then he descended below deck to consult.

The doctor offered only theory—humors, complexions, animal spirits. Jack Brimblecombe spoke of divine judgment. Cary smiled, but there was no joy in it—only despair masked in wit. Yeo stood stoic, quoting Scripture as if fate were already written. Drew, the master, said nothing. “My job,” he muttered, “is to sail the ship, not cure fevers.”

At that, Amyas clutched his hair—a familiar gesture when frustration seized him—and burst out: “Doctor! A fig for your humors! Can you change a man’s skin? Can an Ethiopian change his hue? Don’t shift your ignorance onto God. I ask what causes this sickness, and you don’t know. Jack, don’t preach God’s visitation—this looks more like the devil’s handiwork to me. We are doing God’s work, Sir John. He won’t hinder us. So down with the devil, I say. Cary, laughter won’t cure a Christian. Yeo, I’ll call it God’s will only when an angel tells me we’re meant to die like dogs in a ditch. And Drew—you say your business is sailing? Then sail her out of this poison-trap this very morning, if you can. But you can’t. The sickness is in the air. I felt it last night, smelled it like rotting sewage. If it weren’t in the air, why were my boatmen struck first?”

No one answered.

Then Amyas explained: “Because the mist rose only five or six feet above the river. We were in it. You were above it. And those taken aboard today? All slept on the main deck. All feared the fever. That tells me two things: stay high, and fear only God. Then we shall survive.”

“But the fog reached our round-tops at dawn,” said Cary.

“I know. But we on the half-deck were exposed less than those below. And we had better air. Besides, I suspect evening heat draws the poison out. When it cools at night, the venom fades.”

How it faded, Amyas could not explain—though he was right in his observations. No one then understood the true nature of such fevers. It took nearly two hundred years of suffering in America before settlers learned the simple rules now taught to children. But common sense was on Amyas’s side. Yeo rose and spoke:

“As I’ve said before, the Lord has sent us a young Daniel to judge. I remember the Spaniards saying these calentures lie only in low ground—never rise more than a few hundred feet above sea.”

“Then let us go up those hundred feet.”

All eyes turned to Amyas, then to each other.

“Gentlemen,” he said, “look the devil straight in the face if you want to strike him where it hurts. We cannot sail the ship as she is. We cannot return empty-handed. And we cannot stay here to die. We must leave the ship and go inland.”

“Inland?” echoed every voice except Yeo’s.

“To those hundred feet Yeo speaks of. Up into the mountains. Stockade a camp. Bring our sick and supplies.”

“And then?”

“And when we are strong again, cross the mountains and take St. Jago de Leon by surprise.”

Cary swore aloud. “Amyas! You’re a reckless man!”

“No,” said Amyas. “It’s plain prudence.”

“So it is, sir,” said old Yeo. “And I follow you.”

“And so do I,” squeaked Jack Brimblecombe.

“Then, Jack, you shall not outrun me,” said Cary. “I say yes too.”

“Mr. Drew?”

“At your service, sir, to live or die. I know nothing of stockades—but I believe Sir Francis himself would have given the same counsel, had he been in your place.”

“Then tell the men we move in an hour. Win over the Pelicans, Yeo and Drew. The rest will follow—like sheep over a hedge.”

The Pelicans and freed galley-slaves joined at once. The others resisted fiercely. Where were the hills? In the thick mangrove swamp, they could see no farther than fifty yards.

“The hills are three miles southwest,” said Amyas. “I marked every ridge as we came in.”

“You meant to take us there?”

That sparked suspicion—angry, whispered doubts. But Amyas cut through it with cold authority.

“Fools! If I hadn’t thought ahead of you, where would you be? Are you mad as well as reckless—rising against your captain because he has two strings to his bow? Go my way—or I swear I’ll blow up the ship and every soul on board. Save you the pain of rotting here slowly.”

They knew Amyas never threatened lightly—even if he didn’t truly intend it. The threat was enough. They agreed. And their spirits lifted when they saw the old Pelican’s crew working with cheerful resolve.

There’s no need to linger on the agony of the march—hours under a broiling sun, stumbling over roots, cutting through thorns, dragging the sick and provisions up steep slopes, plagued by snakes, mosquitoes, false alarms, curses, fatigue, and despair. Suffice it to say: by sunset, they reached a level plateau a thousand feet above the sea. Behind them loomed an impassable cliff—the shoulder of a great mountain. Below, steep wooded slopes formed a natural defense. With just a few trees felled, it could become impregnable.

Amyas set the sick beneath the arching roots of a colossal cottonwood tree. He made a second trip to the ship for hammocks and blankets. Yeo proved invaluable—pioneer, guide, heart. He found the narrow brook they followed upward, urged them past cliffs, promised water and shelter ahead. He encouraged them when they begged to stop, returned again and again to push them forward. Cary brought up the rear, driving stragglers with threats and song, carrying burdens, singing to keep morale high. Both men proved worthy of Amyas’s trust. That night, overwhelmed with relief, he whispered to them both:

“Cortez burned his ships when he landed. Why shouldn’t we?”

Yeo leapt upright, then sat back, whispering: “You say that, captain? It’s from above—certainly. It’s been on my mind all day.”

“There’s no hurry,” said Amyas. “We must clear her out first.” Cary sat silent, deep in thought. Amyas clearly had plans beyond words.

That night, exhaustion claimed the men. But by dawn, Amyas had them hard at work fortifying the site. Nature had already done much: cliffs guarded three sides. The mountain range behind barred any attack from the north. Trees hid them from view. Any assault would come from below—and Amyas prepared for it.

He felled small trees, laid them bough-out over the slope—forming an abatis, a barrier deadly to horse or man. Logs were sawn, laid lengthwise, braced with stakes and earth. Within hours, a sturdy stockade stood—strong against anything but cannon.

Amyas climbed into the branches of the giant ceiba tree, perched high above the world. From there, he surveyed the forest, the sea, and the future. To stay was impossible. To seek vengeance on La Guayra was out of reach. To search for Frank seemed equally hopeless. Yet—could he sacrifice eighty men for his personal quest?

He wept with rage. Then wept again, in prayer. At last, he decided. A hundred chances to one, Frank was dead. Even if alive, he was likely in the hands of the Inquisition. Who could free him? Not Amyas. Crying out, “God help him—for I cannot!” he resolved to move. But where?

Hours passed in silence, high in his leafy nest. Then he descended, calm and determined, pulling Cary and Yeo aside.

“We cannot refit the ship without falling to fever. Even if we did, we’d likely fight the Spaniards again. The Indian’s tale is true—they were warned. And Eustace was the traitor.”

“Then let us try St. Jago,” said Amyas. “Sack it. Strike La Guayra from the rear. Take a ship. Return home.”

“Nay, Will,” said Yeo. “If they strengthened La Guayra, they surely did the same at St. Jago. The town is large, new, and how can we cross these mountains without a guide?”

“Or with one?” asked Cary, gazing at the endless ridges. “Strange to see you, at least, dampening a bold plan.”

“What if I had a bolder one?” said Amyas. “Have you ever heard of the golden city of Manoa?”

Yeo laughed—a grim, joyful sound. “I have, sir. And so have the old hands from the Pelican and the Jesus of Lubec.”

“Then listen,” said Amyas, and told Cary everything he’d learned from the Spaniard. Yeo added rumors of his own. Cary sat stunned, as visions of gold and empires unfolded.

“So that’s why you wanted to burn the ship,” he said. “Well, nobody needs me at home. One less at table won’t be missed. You want to play Cortez, eh?”

“We shall never need to play Cortez—he wasn’t such a bad fellow after all. And I trust we’ll fear God enough not to imitate Pizarro.”

They said no more—but none forgot the talk.

For ten days, they lived in that mountain hideaway. Some sick men died—some from fever compounded by wounds, others perhaps from bloodletting. Most recovered, thanks to herbs Yeo administered—much to the doctor’s disgust. He insisted on bleeding, and nearly mutinied when Amyas stopped him.

Meanwhile, daily trips to the ship kept provisions flowing. Raccoons, monkeys, fruit, vegetables—especially the sweet mountain cabbage of the Areca palm and the fresh milk of the cow-tree—sustained them. They paid well for the hospitality of the land.

All day, watchers kept vigil in the ceiba tree. What a tree it was! Its roots rose twelve feet high, forming walls that housed the entire crew, arms, and stores. The trunk, forty feet in girth, soared like a lighthouse, smooth for a hundred feet, then branched into stately limbs—each a tree in itself. Its topmost twigs touched two hundred and fifty feet above the ground. Lianas hung like ropes, making ascent easy.

Once inside, one entered another world—between heaven and earth. Flowers dangled from every limb—cactuses, orchids, wild pines. One side was lush with leaves; the other, bare, bloomed with brilliant yellow flowers, buzzing with hummingbirds. Parrots peered from crevices. Lizards glimmered like gems. Finches chirped, butterflies danced, insects hummed. At dusk, tree-toads croaked till dawn.

More life swirled around that single tree than in a square mile of England.

Amyas, lounging among the branches, sometimes dreamed of staying forever—then snapped back to reality. The enemy would come soon. He must decide.

One noon, he spotted a large ship along the coast—under easy sail. He recognized it, or thought he did—the vessel they’d passed earlier.

If it was the same ship, it had slipped past them to La Guayra and now returned to search.

She moved slowly. He hoped she’d pass the river mouth—but she didn’t. She lay-to near shore. Then two boats rowed in, vanishing into the mangroves.

Sliding down a liana, Amyas reported what he’d seen. The men, tired of waiting, roared with joy and began preparing.

Four brass swivels were mounted, fixed in logs to cover the path. Musketeers and archers gathered, ready. Six marksmen volunteered for the cottonwood tree—with arquebuses. “A man might have very pretty shooting,” one joked.

Prayers followed. Then dinner. But two hours passed—no sign of the Spaniards.

Then smoke curled from the swamp. A caliver fired. Then the Spanish flag rose above the trees—horrible to see—flying from the masthead of the Rose.

They were signaling for reinforcements. A third boat pushed off into the forest.

Another hour passed. The men grew restless, shouting, pacing. Amyas warned them: “Silence! They may not find us. They may pass unseen. If they miss the track, they’ll return to their ship. Promise me—no noise until I give the word to fire.”

His orders had barely left his lips when a Spanish soldier’s helmet flashed in the path—then another, another.

“Fools!” whispered Amyas to Cary. “They’re coming up single file—running into their own death. Lie low, men!”

The path was narrow—two could hardly walk abreast. The slope was steep. The Spaniards hesitated, unsure. Then, from behind, a commanding voice urged them forward.

At last, a figure emerged—sword drawn—recognized instantly by Amyas and Cary.

“Is it he?”

“Those legs—I’d know them anywhere, even in armor.”

“It’s my turn now, Cary. Remember! Silence, men!”

The Spaniards seemed aware they were marching into danger. Don Guzman—there was no doubt—struggled to lead them.

“They’ve heard how gently we treated the Guayra squadron,” whispered Cary. “They don’t want to be martyrs like the captain of the Madre Dolorosa.”

At last, they reached within forty yards of the stockade—and paused.

Amyas leapt atop the wall, waving a white flag. His heart pounded at the sight of the hated man—but he forced the words out:

“Don Guzman, the quarrel is between you and me. Not your men and mine. I would have challenged you at La Guayra—but you were gone. I challenge you now—to single combat.”

“Lutheran dog! I have a halter for you, not a sword! As you served us at Smerwick, we shall serve you now. Pirate and ravisher—you and yours shall share Oxenham’s fate. Learn what it means to tread unbidden on the king of Spain’s lands.”

“The devil take you and your king!” shouted Amyas, laughing. “This land belongs to no one but Queen Elizabeth. In her name, I claim it—just as you claim Caracas. Fire, men! God defend the right!”

Both sides obeyed. Amyas dropped behind the stockade as a bullet whistled overhead. The Spaniards recoiled as muskets and swivels blazed—raking their line from front to rear.

Front ranks fell in heaps. The rear turned and fled—only to be cut down by bullets and arrows tumbling them down the slope.

“Out, men! Charge! See—the Don is running like the rest!”

Amyas scrambled over the abatis, leading thirty. He hoped to capture a prisoner—perhaps learn Frank’s fate.

But Don Guzman, miraculously, was only wounded. Seeing his men flee, he tried to rally them—but was swept away.

By the time the English reached the path, the Spaniards were lost in thick bushes. Amyas, fearing an ambush, withdrew reluctantly. He found fourteen Spaniards—dead. One wounded man, defiant, had fired as he lay. The English, enraged by their plight and stories of horror from freed slaves, had killed them all before Amyas could stop them.

“Are you mad?” he roared, striking one man’s sword. “Will you kill an Indian?”

And from the bushes, he dragged out a sixteen-year-old Indian boy—slightly wounded, crawling like a snake.

“The black vermin shot me. Poisoned, most likely.”

“God grant not,” said Amyas. “But an Indian is worth his weight in gold now.” He tucked the lad under his arm.

The boy, seeing escape was impossible, accepted his fate with quiet dignity. He was brought in, treated kindly—but refused food.

After questioning, they learned the reason: he believed the English would fatten him and eat him—like the Caribs. Only when they buried the Spaniard corpses did he believe they wouldn’t cook them for supper.

And so, under the great tree, they waited—watchful, weary, yet alive.
