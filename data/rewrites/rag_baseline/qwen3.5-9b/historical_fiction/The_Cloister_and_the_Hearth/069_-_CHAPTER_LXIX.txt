In the shadowed sanctuary of a Dominican convent, a solitary figure lay prostrate within a guest chamber, his spirit nearly extinguished by the violent, recurring spasms of nausea that had finally ceased, leaving him as fragile and broken as Margaret had been in the Dutch lands. Upon the hearth, a massive wood fire crackled, casting dancing shadows against the walls where the patient's garments hung suspended. Beside the bed knelt a giant of a friar, his voice resonating with a low, rhythmic cadence as he recited pious collects from his breviary. At intervals, the sufferer opened weary eyes to meet the monk's gaze, listening intently; at others, he slumped back, surrendering to a silent, agonizing moan. The monk dropped to his knees, his forehead pressed to the stone floor in fervent supplication, before rising to bid the dying man adieu. "Day breaks," the friar announced softly. "I must prepare for matins." "Good Father Jerome," the stranger rasped, "before you depart, how did I arrive here?" "By the hand of Heaven itself," the monk replied, his tone grave yet comforting. "You cast aside God's gift; He has restored it to you. Reflect upon this! You have tested the world and found only bitterness. Now test the Church! The Church is peace. *Pax vobiscum*." With that, he departed.

Gerard lay back, his mind reeling in a haze of wonder until exhaustion claimed him, dragging him into a fitful doze. When consciousness returned, a new guardian sat vigilantly beside him—a layman whose eyes darted with a small, restless energy, a stark contrast to the calm majesty of Friar Jerome. The man inquired earnestly of his condition. "Extremely weak," Gerard murmured. "Where have I encountered you before, sir?" "No worse for my gauntlet?" the other asked, his anxiety palpable. "I was prepared to strike you down, or both of us would have perished at the bottom of the Tiber." Gerard fixed him with a stare. "What? Was it you who saved me? How?" "Indeed, signor," the man explained, his voice trembling slightly. "I stood by the banks of the Tiber on some errand of my own. You approached me, begging desperately for a dagger's stroke. Yet, even as you spoke, I recognized you—the very signor who rescued my wife and child upon the sea." "It is Teresa's husband... and an assassin?!" Gerard exclaimed, horror cutting through his weakness. "At your service. Well, Ser Gerard, the next moment you hurled yourself into the Tiber, commanding me to stand aside." "I recall that clearly." "If it had been anyone else, believe me, I would have obeyed without hesitation, not moving a muscle. Men are my enemies; they might all hang on a single rope or drown in one river for me. But when you, sinking beneath the current, cried out 'Margaret!'..." "Ah!" "My heart screamed 'Teresa!' How could I return home and face her, knowing I had allowed you to die, thereby condemning her too? So I entered the water. Fortunately for us both, I swim like a duck. Seeing me approach, intent on mutual destruction, you attempted to seize me, hoping to end us together. But I circled you, and—accept my apologies—I struck the nape of your neck with my steel-gloved fist. You lost consciousness, and with great effort, fighting the strong current, I dragged your body ashore, insensible and filled with water. I carried you on my back to my own dwelling. 'Teresa will nurse him,' I thought, 'and she will be pleased with me.' Hard by this monastery, however, a holy friar, the largest I have ever beheld, intercepted us and demanded an explanation. I recounted the tale. He scrutinized you closely. 'I know this face,' he declared. 'It is Gerard, a fair youth from Holland.' 'The same,' I confirmed. Then his reverence said, 'He has friends among our brethren. Leave him with us! Charity is our office.' He further informed me that the convent possessed far greater means to tend to you than I did, which proved true enough. Thus, I negotiated permission to visit you daily, and here you are." As he spoke, the man cast a look upon Gerard that blended strange affection with profound interest. Gerard recoiled internally, feeling as though a serpent coiled within the room. Closing his eyes, he dismissed the intruder. "Ah, you wish to sleep," the man observed eagerly. "Then I withdraw." He retreated on tiptoe, promising his daily return.

Gerard lay with his eyes shut, though not in sleep, but in a state of deep, tormented contemplation. Saved from death by an assassin—was this not the finger of Heaven pointing at him? A Heaven he had once insulted, cursed, and defiantly rejected. Shivers ran through him at the memory of his blasphemies. He attempted to pray, finding his lips capable of forming the words, yet his heart refused to offer true devotion. "I am doomed eternally," he wailed, the despair echoing in his chest. "Doomed, doomed." Suddenly, the organ of the convent church erupted in rich, solemn harmony, filling the air. Rising from the stalls, the choir began a full service, their voices weaving a tapestry of sound. Among them hovered a voice that soared above the rest, towering toward the heavens—a sweet boy's voice, full, pure, and angelic. Gerard closed his eyes, letting the melody wash over him. The days of his own boyhood flooded back, unburdened by the earth's dross, free from the foul, fierce passions that had rent and corrupted his soul. Only peace, sweet and balmy peace. "Yes," he sighed, tears welling in his eyes, "the Church is peace of mind. Until I left her bosom, I knew neither sorrow nor sin." And the poor, torn, worn creature wept. Just as his grief peaked, the sweet and reverend face of one he had not expected to see again beamed upon him. It was Father Anselm. The good father had arrived at the convent only the previous night. Gerard recognized him instantly and cried out, "Oh, Father Anselm! You healed my wounded body in Juliers; now heal my hurt soul in Rome!" "Alas," Anselm replied gently, "you cannot." Sitting by the bedside, he placed a tender hand upon Gerard's head, first calming him with soothing words. Having learned the circumstances of Gerard's arrival, he then spoke to him with kindness tempered by solemnity, making him acutely aware of his crime and urging him toward repentance and gratitude for the Divine Power that had thwarted his will to damn his soul. "Come, my son," Anselm said, "first purge your bosom of its burden." "Ah, father," Gerard confessed, "in Juliers I could; then I was innocent. But now, impious monster that I am, I dare not confess to you." "Why not, my son? Do you think I have not sinned against Heaven in my time, and deeply? Oh, how deeply! Come, poor laden soul, pour forth your grief, pour forth your faults, withhold nothing! Do not lie oppressed and crushed by hidden sins." Soon, Gerard was kneeling at Father Anselm's feet, confessing every sin with sighs and groans of genuine penitence. "Your sins are great," Anselm acknowledged. "Your temptation was also great, terribly great. I must consult our good prior regarding your penance." The good Anselm kissed his brow and withdrew to confer with the superior. And lo! Gerard could pray now. He prayed with all the fervor of his heart. The phase through which this remarkable mind now passed may be summarized in a single word: Penitence. He turned with terror and revulsion from the world, begging passionately to remain within the convent. To him, the cloister was like a wounded bird returning, weary and battered, to its gentle nest. He passed his novitiate immersed in prayer, mortification, pious reading, and meditation. Meanwhile, the Princess Claelia's spy returned home to report that Gerard was certainly dead, the manner of his passing currently unknown. She seemed literally stunned. When, after a long interval, she found breath to speak, it was to lament her fate, cursed with such ready instruments of evil. "So soon," she sighed, "see how swiftly these monsters commit ill deeds. They come to us in our hot blood, first tempting us with their venal daggers, then enacting the mortal deeds we never dared conceive but for them." Within mere hours, her pity for Gerard and her hatred of his murderer rose to a fever pitch, indistinguishable from the fool's own blood heat. "Poor soul! I cannot call you back to life. But he shall never live, that traitor who slew you treacherously." She immediately deployed armed men into ambush, keeping them on guard throughout the day, ready to fall upon Lodovico in a specific antechamber the moment he came for his payment, hacking him to pieces. "Strike at his head," she commanded, "for he wears a private coat of mail; and if he escapes hence alive, your own heads shall answer for it." Thus she sat weeping over her victim, pulling the strings of machines designed to shed the blood of a second, having herself become the instrument to kill the first.
