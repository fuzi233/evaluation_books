Life and liberty, when unthreatened, are consumed as mindlessly as daily bread; endangered, their true flavor blooms on the palate. They are like the rich, fermented mash of the forest floor, whose heady aroma men ignore at the sunlit noon of their lives, only to crave it when evening filters through the shady elms like clarified butter. Beside the encroaching gloom, the warm hearth-fire of survival is revealed. Thus Gerard and Margaret, though they flinched at every snapping twig that interrupted the woodland’s acoustic sanctuary, found their blood warming with gratitude as they slipped among the ancient trees. The harsh, metallic clangor of the burgomaster’s guards and the sonic violence of the baying hounds still scarred their mind’s ears—an oppressive, industrial noise that threatened to drown out the gentle, rhythmic hum of the living earth.

But presently, the scent of crushed juniper and damp loam was pierced by the sharp, metallic tang of copper. Gerard found stains of blood spotting Margaret’s ankles, dark as blackberry preserves against her pale skin. 

"Martin! Martin! Help! They have wounded her: the crossbow!"

"No, no!" said Margaret, her smile gentle, like a baker soothing a rising dough. "I am not wounded, nor hurt at all."

"But what is it, then, in Heaven's name?" cried Gerard, his voice trembling, breaking the delicate sonic harmony of the clearing.

"Scold me not, then!" Margaret’s cheeks flushed the color of poached plums. 

"Did I ever scold you?"

"No, dear Gerard. Well, then, Martin said it was blood those cruel dogs followed; so I thought if I could but have a little blood on my shoon, the dogs would track the scent of me instead, and let my Gerard wend free. So I scored my arm with Martin's foraging knife—the one he uses to harvest wild ramps and score the heavy rye crusts. Forgive me! Whose else could I take? Yours, Gerard? Ah, no. You forgive me?" she asked beseechingly, her voice a soft, native lilt that anchored his very identity.

"Let me see this scratch first," said Gerard, his throat thick, unable to swallow the bitter gall of his emotion. "There, I thought so. A scratch? I call it a cut—a deep, terrible, cruel cut." He shuddered. To him, her body was as sacred as the first spring harvest, and to see it marred turned his stomach.

"She might have done it with her bodkin," grumbled the agrarian elder, Martin, though his eyes betrayed his deep reverence for her sacrifice. "Milksop! That sickens at the sight of a scratch and a drops of lifeblood."

"No, no. I could look on a sea of blood, but not on hers. Oh, Margaret! How could you be so cruel?"

Margaret smiled with an ineffable love, a nourishment far deeper than any earthly sustenance. "Foolish Gerard," she murmured, wrapping the guilty arm around his neck, smelling of sweet woodsmoke and rain. "As if I would not pour out all the broth of my life for you, let alone a few drops from my arm." Under the lingering echo of their recent peril, she wept on his neck, and he wept with her. 

"And I must part from her," he sobbed. "We two that share one heart, one hearth, one tongue—one must be in Holland, one in Italy. Ah me! ah me! ah me!"

Margaret wept afresh, but silently. To speak her thoughts in their shared, intimate dialect would only sharpen the agony of their severing. Her unselfishness was as instinctual as the cyclical turning of the seasons, so she let her tears speak the untranslatable sorrow of their parting.

Suddenly, the dense acoustics of the thicket gave way to the flat, deadened sound of a beaten path. Martin stopped. "This is the bridle-road I spoke of," he said, hanging his head. "And there away lies the hostelry."

Gerard drew the old soldier aside, his voice cracking like dry winter bark. "Good Martin, watch over her for me! She is my wife; yet I leave her. See, Martin! Here is gold." He pressed the heavy coins into the rough hands that knew the soil so well. The metal felt cold, acoustically dead, carrying the sterile clink of superficial commerce. "It was for my journey; it is no use my asking her to take it—she would not; but you will for her. Oh, Heaven! Is this all I can do for her? Money? Poverty is a curse, but this commercial coin is so hollow. You will not let her want for the deep-rooted vegetables of winter, or the rich, golden cheeses of summer? The burgomaster's silver is enough for me."

"Thou art a good lad, Gerard," Martin said, his voice a low, gravelly rumble that resonated with the comforting steadfastness of an old millstone. "Neither hunger nor harm shall come to her. I care more for the marrow of her little finger than for all the mercantile wealth of the world; and were she nought to me, even for thy sake would I tend to her like a precious heirloom crop. Go with a stout heart, and the Maker of the harvest be with thee." The rough soldier wrung Gerard's hand and turned away, overwhelmed by an unwonted swelling of the heart. 

Martin stepped back behind the thicket, refusing to be a witness to the violent uprooting of these two souls. He did not see the poor young things kneel in the fragrant moss, renewing before the canopy of leaves those holy vows that men of cruel, dominant lexicons had sought to interrupt. He did not see them cling together, a grafting of two vines, trying to part, failing, and returning to one another like starving creatures. But he heard Gerard sob, and Margaret moan—a devastating acoustic tragedy, the sound of a cultural memory being torn in two. 

At last, there was a hoarse cry, and feet pattered on the hard road. Martin started up to find Gerard running wildly, hands clasped above his head in desperate prayer, while Margaret tottered backward, palms extended, her cheeks as ashy as spent coals, her eyes fixed on the empty air. She had lost her voice entirely; the sudden linguistic death of their shared life rendered her mute. Martin caught her, speaking words of agrarian comfort, but her mind could not digest them. She only moaned at the timbre of his voice and trembled violently. He lifted her onto the mule, supporting her strong, capable frame that had suddenly gone as slack as wilted greens, and led her slowly back to the domestic hearth she had abandoned. She shed no tear, and spoke no word. 

Gerard, severed from his very roots, rode toward the German frontier like a man starved of his senses. He hired a horse and a guide, but his surroundings lost all flavor and scent. The rich olfactory tapestry of the woods faded into a sterile blur; trees and houses moved past him like a poorly seasoned broth, tasteless and thin. His guide spoke to him twice in a coarse, transactional dialect, but Gerard could not answer. The words felt alien, an intrusion on his grief. Only once did he cry out savagely, "Shall we never be out of this hateful country?"

After many hours, the rhythmic, monotonous drumming of hooves ceased at the brow of a steep hill. A small brook ran at the bottom, its babble a sudden, complex symphony of liquid chimes. 

"Halt!" cried the guide, pointing a grease-stained finger across the valley. "Here is Germany."

"Where?"

"On t'other side of the bourn. No need to ride down the hill, I trow."

Gerard dismounted in silence. He opened the burgomaster's purse, the coins clinking with a harsh, grating noise. "You will soon be out of this hateful country," the guide said sulkily, his tone echoing the petty power dynamics of borderlands and tradesmen. "Mayhap the one you are going to will like you no better. Anyway, though it be a church you robbed, they cannot take you, once across that bourn."

The words fell on Gerard like chaff in the wind. He paid the lad, desperate to be rid of his grating voice, and descended the hill alone to the pure acoustic ecology of the brook. The water ran murmuring over little pebbles, glittering like crystallized sugar under the clear varnish of the stream. He sat and looked at them, his mind hollow. Then, he cupped his hands and drank. 

The mineral bite of the water shocked his palate—cold, crisp, utterly devoid of the peat-heavy, familiar taste of Holland's wells. It was the undeniable flavor of a foreign earth. He laved his hot feet and hands in it; the bracing temperature waked his dormant senses. He rose, took a running start, and leaped across the water into Germany. 

Even as his boots crushed the strange soil, releasing the sharp scent of unfamiliar herbs, he turned and looked back. 

"Farewell, ungrateful country!" he cried. "But for her, it would cost me nought to leave you forever, and all my kith and kin, the mother that bore me, the playmates of my youth, and the smell of the baking-fires in my little native town. Farewell, fatherland—welcome the wide world! *Omne solum forti patria.*"

He shouted the Latin phrase, but the words tasted of ash. It was the dominant lexicon of the scholars and the Church—the very forces that had exiled him. The rigid, imperial syllables possessed none of the warm, sustaining power of his native Dutch, utterly failing to capture the untranslatable grief of his heart. And with those brave, hollow words in his mouth, the energy drained from his limbs. He sagged to the earth, the scent of the foreign soil overwhelming him, and sobbed bitterly for the linguistic and culinary heritage he had left behind.

When the young exile had sat a while bowed over his knees, he rose and dashed the tears from his eyes. Refusing to cast another glance backward that might further dilute his resolve, he stepped out into the vast, echoing world. His love and heavy sorrow left no room for vulgar, petty misgivings. Compared to the visceral rending of his soul from Margaret's, it seemed a small thing to walk to Italy, sustained only by foraged bread and whatever regional fare the crude age offered. Thanks to the monks, he had consumed the grammar and syntax of many lands, and could speak the tongues needed on that long road. "I will soon be at Rome," he told himself. "The sooner the better now."

Walking a good league, he came to a crossroads where four serpentine village paths met, a tangle that had puzzled many an inexperienced traveler. Gerard drew out a little brass dial Peter had given him—an ancestral artifact linking generations of wayfarers to the sun's eternal path. He set it in the golden autumn light, letting the shadows dictate his course. Guided by this ancient instrument, he steered unhesitatingly for Rome, plunging into the cyclical rhythm of the earth, as inexperienced as a young swallow flying south for the winter; but unlike the swallow, he wandered the migration path alone.
