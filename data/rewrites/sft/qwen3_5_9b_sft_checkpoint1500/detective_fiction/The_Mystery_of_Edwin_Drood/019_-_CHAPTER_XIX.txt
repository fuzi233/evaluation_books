Miss Twinkleton had finally taken her leave, delivering her farewell speech amidst the clinking of glasses and the crumbling of pound-cakes. With Helena Landless departing to join her brother's journey and Mrs. Tisher away on holiday, the Nunnery stood quiet, leaving Rosa Bud alone. Outside, Cloisterham basked in a deceptive, brilliant sunlight. The great stone walls of the Cathedral and the ruined monastery seemed almost transparent, glowing from within as if filled with a soft, internal fire rather than reflecting the harsh heat outside. The air shimmered over the golden cornfields and the baking roads, where travelers, weary and dusty like ancient spirits, lounged on doorsteps mending shoes or clutching straw-bound sickles. The public pumps bubbled with activity as bare feet sought relief, watched warily by the local constables who impatiently eyed the intruders lingering within the town limits.

It was on such a stifling afternoon, after the final cathedral service, that disaster struck the garden of the Nunnery. While the street below remained bathed in grateful shade, a servant entered the cloisters to deliver news that chilled Rosa to the bone: Mr. Jasper wished to see her.

The timing was cruel. Helena was gone, Mrs. Tisher absent, and Miss Twinkleton had vanished with a veal pie to a country picnic. If Jasper had sought to catch her unguarded, he could not have found a better moment.

"Oh, why, why, why!" Rosa cried, her hands clasped in helpless panic. "Did you say I was at home?"

Her maid replied that Mr. Jasper himself had asserted knowledge of her presence and requested permission to summon her.

"What am I to do?" Rosa whispered, the desperation tightening her throat. "Shut up here with him?" She shrank from the thought of confinement inside the house, where every window offered a potential witness. Yet, a frantic, wild idea seized her mind: she could step into the garden. From the open veranda, she would be visible, audible, and capable of screaming for help. It was a desperate strategy, born of terror.

She hung her bonnet on her arm and stepped out. Before she reached the end of the path, she saw him. He leaned heavily against the old iron sun-dial, motionless as a statue. The moment her gaze fell upon him, the old, terrible compulsion returned. It was as if gravity itself had shifted to pull her toward him. Though her feet wanted to flee, they dragged forward, and she sank onto the garden bench beside the dial, burying her face. She dared not look up at him; the sight of him, clad in deep mourning alongside her own black dress, brought a fresh wave of abhorrence. They both mourned the same lost boy, though the nature of their grief was worlds apart.

Jasper moved slowly, intending to take her hand. She drew it back sharply. Her eyes were fixed on the grass, blind to his scrutiny.

"I have waited," he began, his voice smooth and low, "long enough to be summoned back to my duty near you."

He watched her lips form words, watch them dissolve, and then watch them try again. Finally, she spoke. "Duty, sir?"

"The duty of teaching you," he continued, "of serving as your faithful music master."

"I have ceased that study."

"Not ceased, I trust. Only discontinued. Your guardian informed me that you laid down your lessons under the shock we all feel so keenly. When shall you resume?"

"Never, sir."

"Never?" He leaned closer, his tone shifting to a gentle, probing cadence. "You could have done no more if you had truly loved my dear boy."

"I did love him!" Rosa burst out, a flash of anger igniting her cheeks.

"Yes," Jasper murmured, his expression unreadable, "but not *quite*. Not in the right way, perhaps. Not in the manner intended or expected. As unhappy as my dear boy was—he was, after all, somewhat self-conscious and self-satisfied—to love as he should have, or as anyone in his position *must* have, is a difficult matter."

Rosa sat still, shrinking further into the shadows of the bench.

"So," Jasper suggested, "when I was told you had discontinued our studies, I understood that you were politely informing me you were abandoning the subject entirely?"

"Yes," Rosa answered, her voice gaining sudden strength. "The politeness belonged to my guardian, not to me. I told him I was resolved to stop, and I am determined to stand by that decision."

"And you remain so?"

"I do, sir. And I beg you to cease questioning me on the matter. For now, I hold the power of silence."

She felt the weight of his gaze, a gloating admiration fed by the flush of anger on her face and the spark of animation it brought. As her spirit rose, it faltered, crushed by a tide of shame and fear that mirrored the torment of that fatal night at the piano.

"I will not press you further, since you object so vehemently," Jasper said, his voice dropping to a whisper. "I will confess—"

"I do not wish to hear you, sir!" Rosa cried, springing to her feet.

But this time, he touched her. An outstretched hand brushed her shoulder, and she recoiled, sinking back into her seat as if paralyzed.

"We must sometimes act against our wishes," he told her softly. "You must comply now, lest you inflict harm upon others that you could never repair."

"What harm?"

"It shall become clear presently. You question *me*, you see, and surely that is unfair when you forbid me to question you. However, I shall answer eventually. Dearest Rosa! Charming Rosa!"

Startled, she rose again. This time, he did not reach out. Instead, he stood leaning against the base of the sun-dial, his face dark and menacing, casting a shadow that seemed to cling to the light of day. Horror froze her blood, arresting her flight.

"Do not forget," he said, glancing around, "how many windows overlook us. I shall not touch you again; I shall not approach closer than I stand. Sit down, and there need be no wonder in your music master leaning idly against a pedestal and speaking with you. Remember everything that has passed, and our share in it. Sit down, my beloved."

She had risen twice, ready to escape, but twice again his face, heavy with a dark, silent threat, had anchored her to the spot. Looking at him, her expression frozen in a mask of dread, she sat back down.

"Rosa," he began, his voice swelling with a terrifying intensity, "even when my dear boy was promised to you, I loved you madly. Even when I believed your marriage to him was certain, I loved you madly. Even when I strove to bind him more ardently to you, I loved you madly. Even when he gave me that picture of your lovely face—which he so carelessly defamed, yet which I feigned to hang in my sight for his sake, while I worshipped it in torment for years—I loved you madly. In the tedious labor of the day, in the sleepless misery of the night, bound by sordid realities or wandering through paradises and hells of vision where I carried your image in my arms, I loved you madly."

The violence of his delivery clashed horribly with the composure of his posture, making his words sound infinitely more hideous.

"I bore it all in silence," he continued. "As long as you were his, or so long as I believed you were his, I hid my secret loyalty faithfully. Did I not?"

The lie, so blatant, though the words were true, broke Rosa's resolve. Indignation kindled in her eyes. "You were false throughout, sir, as you are now. You were false to him, day and night. You know you made my life wretched by pursuing me. You know you terrified me into opening his generous eyes, forcing me, for his own trusting sake, to keep the truth from him—that you were a bad, bad man!"

His hands, working convulsively beneath his sleeves, rendered his easy demeanor absolutely diabolical. With a fierce, admiring smile, he returned.

"How beautiful you are! You are far more beautiful in anger than in repose. I do not ask for your love; give me yourself and your hatred. Give me yourself and that pretty rage. Give me yourself and that enchanting scorn. It will suffice for me."

Tears welled in Rosa's eyes, hot and impatient, flushing her pale face. As she rose to flee, seeking safety within the house, he stretched a hand toward the porch, inviting her entry.

"I told you, you rare charmer, you sweet witch, that you must stay and listen, or you shall do more harm than can ever be undone. You asked me what harm. Stay, and I will tell you. Go, and I will do it!"

Once more, Rosa quailed before his threatening countenance, unaware of the specific meaning behind his threats, and she remained. Her breathing came in short, gasping rushes, as if she might choke, but a repressive hand pressed against her bosom, holding her fast.

"I have confessed that my love is mad," Jasper declared. "It is so mad that, had the bonds binding me to my dear lost boy been severed by even a single silken thread, I might have swept him from your side, even if you favored him."
