The Thames, that singular smoky sparkle—a confusion and transparency that defies easy definition—was shifting from its usual grey murk toward a glittering brilliance as the sun climbed to its zenith over Westminster. Across the bridge, two figures moved in stark contrast to the river's fluid motion. One was towering, broad-shouldered, and sharp-edged; the other was compact, rounded, and draped in clerical grey. They cut a silhouette reminiscent of the architectural extremes of the city: the arrogant, needle-thin spire of Parliament versus the humble, humped shoulders of the Abbey.

The taller man was M. Hercule Flambeau, private detective, heading to his new headquarters in a freshly constructed skyscraper facing the Abbey. Beside him walked the Reverend J. Brown, attached to St. Francis Xavier's in Camberwell, traveling from a local deathbed to inspect his friend's new domain.

The building was a monument to American ambition, defined by its sky-scraping altitude and the oiled, mechanical precision of its elevators and telephone exchanges. Yet, it was unfinished, a skeletal frame of potential. Only three tenants had moved in; the offices directly above and below Flambeau were occupied, leaving the floors above and below those entirely vacant. But the structure's most arresting feature was not its height, but a glaring installation erected outside the office above Flambeau's. It was an enormous gilt effigy of a human eye, radiating golden beams, dominating the facade with the scale of two or three windows combined.

"What on earth is that?" Father Brown asked, halting mid-stride.

"Oh, a new religion," Flambeau laughed, dismissing the absurdity with practiced ease. "One of those modern cults that absolve your sins by claiming you never committed them. Reminds me of Christian Science. A fellow calling himself Kalon—though I suspect 'Kalon' is a pseudonym, given how common it sounds—has rented the flat directly above me. I have two female typists below, and this enthusiastic old charlatan above. He styles himself the New Priest of Apollo and worships the sun."

"Let him gaze outward," Father Brown remarked, his tone grave. "The sun was the cruelest of all deities. But what purpose serves that monstrous eye?"

"As I understand their doctrine," Flambeau explained casually, "it posits that a man can endure any trial if his mind remains perfectly steady. Their twin symbols are the sun and the open eye. Their claim is that a truly healthy individual could stare directly into the solar disk without wincing."

"If a man were truly healthy," Father Brown countered, "he would not feel compelled to stare at it."

"That is all I can offer regarding this new faith," Flambeau continued, brushing aside the theological implication. "They assert it cures all physical ailments."

"And the one spiritual malady?" Father Brown pressed, his curiosity piqued.

"And what constitutes the sole spiritual sickness?" Flambeau smiled.

"Believing oneself to be perfectly well," Father Brown replied.

Flambeau's attention drifted downward, drawn less by the flamboyant temple above and more by the quiet efficiency of the office below. He was a lucid Southerner, unable to reconcile himself with either Catholicism or atheism, yet he possessed a profound respect for humanity, particularly when it displayed competence. The two sisters running the typing agency below were exceptional examples. Both were slight and dark, yet the elder, Pauline Stacey, commanded the room. She possessed a dark, eager, aquiline profile—the kind of face one visualizes as the clean-cut edge of a blade. She seemed to cleave through life with relentless momentum. Her eyes burned with a startling intensity, the brilliant flash of polished steel rather than diamond. Her posture was rigid, her movements stiff, betraying a grace constrained by sheer willpower.

Pauline Stacey was the heiress to a crest and half a county, a woman raised amidst castles and manicured gardens before a peculiar, frigid fierceness drove her toward a harsher, more utilitarian existence. She had not squandered her fortune; such romantic abandonment was alien to her masterful pragmatism. Instead, she wielded her wealth as a tool for practical social engineering, funding a model typewriting empire and various leagues advancing women's work. Whether her younger sister, Joan, shared this slightly prosaic idealism was uncertain, though Joan followed her leader with a dog-like devotion that carried a tragic undertone, contrasting sharply with Pauline's hard, high spirits. Pauline, however, denied the existence of tragedy itself.

Her rigidity and cold impatience had fascinated Flambeau since their first meeting. On his initial arrival, he had waited in the lobby for the elevator boy to escort him, a standard protocol for strangers. Pauline, however, had refused to tolerate such bureaucratic delay. With sharp, commanding brevity, she declared her knowledge of the elevator's mechanics and her absolute independence from male assistance. Despite the short ascent to her third-floor suite, she delivered a barrage of fundamental views: she was a modern working woman who revered modern machinery. Her black eyes blazed with abstract fury against those who lamented the loss of romance to mechanization. "Everyone must be capable of managing machines," she insisted, as if the ability to operate an elevator proved a moral superiority. She seemed to resent Flambeau's gesture of holding the door open, a small affront to her self-sufficiency that left the detective smiling with mixed admiration and bewilderment.

She possessed a temper, sharp and practical, manifested in the abrupt, almost destructive gestures of her slender hands. On one occasion, Flambeau entered her office to discuss business and found her in the midst of an ethical crusade. She had just hurled a pair of spectacles belonging to her sister onto the floor and stepped heavily upon them. She railed against "sickly medical notions" and the morbid admission of weakness inherent in using optical aids. She dared her sister to introduce such artificial, unhealthy debris into their workplace again, questioning if they were expected to wear wooden legs, false hair, or glass eyes. As she spoke, her eyes sparkled with terrifying crystalline clarity.

Flambeau, bewildered by this fanaticism, eventually asked Miss Pauline, employing direct French logic, why spectacles represented a deeper morbidity than an elevator, and why science could aid in locomotion but not vision.

"That is fundamentally different," Pauline Stacey replied loftily. "Batteries, motors, and engines are markers of human force—of woman's force as well! We shall harness these great machines that devour distance and defy time. That is sublime—that is true science. But these nasty props and plasters sold by doctors? They are mere badges of cowardice. Doctors attach limbs and organs as if we were born crippled and enslaved by illness. I was free-born, Mr. Flambeau! People only believe they require these aids because they have been trained in fear rather than power and courage. Just as silly nurses teach children not to stare at the sun, convincing them they cannot bear it without blinking. Why should there be a single star I cannot behold? The sun is not my master, and I will open my eyes and stare at it whenever I choose."

"Your eyes," Flambeau bowed mockingly, "will dazzle the sun." He derived a perverse pleasure in unsettling this strange, stiff beauty, partly to throw her off balance. As he ascended to his own floor, he drew a deep breath and whistled softly to himself. "So she has fallen under the spell of that conjurer upstairs with his golden eye." Little did he know, nor care, about the specifics of Kalon's sun-gazing doctrine, but he had heard rumors of the practitioner's unique ability to withstand the solar glare.

Soon, Flambeau observed that a spiritual bond was forming between the floors above and below. Kalon was a magnificent creature, physically worthy to serve as Apollo's pontiff. Nearly as tall as Flambeau, he possessed a golden beard, strong blue eyes, and a mane of hair flung back like a lion's. He embodied the blonde beast of Nietzschean philosophy, yet this raw animal beauty was tempered and elevated by genuine intellect and spirituality. He resembled a Saxon king who happened to be a saint, a contradiction made vivid by the vulgarity of his surroundings: an office halfway up a commercial block in Victoria Street, a mundane clerk sitting between him and the corridor, a brass plaque bearing his name, and the gilded emblem of his creed hanging above the street like an oculist's advertisement. None of this could diminish the vivid oppression and inspiration emanating from his soul and body. Even in a loose linen jacket, he was a formidable figure; robed in white vestments and crowned with a golden circlet as he daily saluted the sun, he was so splendid that the laughter of passersby often died instantly on their lips. Three times daily, the new sun-worshipper stepped onto his small balcony, visible to all of Westminster, to recite a litany to his shining lord: at dawn, at dusk, and at the jarring noon.

It was during the aftermath of the noon service that Father Brown, driven by a mix of professional interest in ritual and personal fascination with tomfoolery, paused to gaze upward at the worshipper's balcony. Kalon the Prophet stood erect, clad in silver-grey garments, hands uplifted. His strangely penetrating voice echoed down the busy street, chanting the solar litany. He was midway through, his eyes fixed intently upon the blazing disc. Doubt existed as to whether he perceived anything tangible on Earth; it was certain, however, that he did not notice the stunted, round-faced priest below, who watched him with blinking, weary eyes.
