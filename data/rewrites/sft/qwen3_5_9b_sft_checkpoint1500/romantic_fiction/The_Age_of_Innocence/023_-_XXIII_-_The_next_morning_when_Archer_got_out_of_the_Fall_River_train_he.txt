The next morning, Archer stepped off the Fall River train into a Boston that was steaming in the grip of midsummer. The air near the station tasted of beer, strong coffee, and rotting fruit, while shirt-sleeved crowds shuffled past with the casual, intimate abandon of boarders heading for the bathroom. Seeking refuge, Archer hailed a cab and directed it to the Somerset Club for breakfast. Even the fashionable districts, usually polished by wealth, wore the disheveled air of untidy homes, a state to which excessive heat never reduces European cities. Caretakers in calico lounged on the doorsteps of the wealthy, and the Common sprawled like a pleasure ground abandoned after a massive picnic. If Archer had ever tried to imagine Ellen Olenska in improbable settings, he could not conjure a scene more jarring than this heat-stricken, deserted city.

He ate with a renewed appetite and method, slicing melon while scanning the morning papers, waiting for his toast and eggs. A new surge of energy possessed him since announcing to May the previous night that he had business in Boston and intended to catch the Fall River boat that evening, continuing to New York the next. It was understood he would return early in the week, yet a letter from the office—fatefully placed on a corner of the hall table—had justified his sudden shift in plans. He felt a sharp pang of shame regarding how easily he had executed this maneuver; it uncomfortably mirrored Lawrence Lefferts' masterful tricks for securing his own freedoms. However, the shame faded quickly, for he was not in an analytical mood.

After breakfast, Archer smoked a cigarette and reviewed the *Commercial Advertiser*. Two or three acquaintances entered, exchanging the customary greetings; it was, after all, the same world, despite Archer's peculiar sensation of slipping through the meshes of time and space. Glancing at his watch, he noted it was half-past nine. Rising, he moved to the writing-room, penned a brief line, and instructed a messenger to take a cab to the Parker House to await a reply. Sitting behind another newspaper, he mentally calculated the travel time to the hotel.

"Lady is out, sir," a waiter's voice suddenly cut in at his elbow. Archer stammered, "Out?" as if the word belonged to a strange tongue.

Rising immediately, he hurried into the hall. It must be a mistake; she could not possibly be out at that hour. Anger flushed his cheeks at his own stupidity—he should have sent the note the moment he arrived. Grabbing his hat and cane, he stepped onto the street. The city instantly transformed, appearing as strange, vast, and empty as if he were a traveler from distant lands. Standing on the doorstep, he hesitated before deciding to proceed to the Parker House. Perhaps the messenger had been mistaken, and she was still inside.

Crossing the Common, he spotted her on the first bench beneath a tree. A grey silk sunshade concealed her head—how could he have ever imagined her with a pink one? Approaching, he was struck by her listless posture; she sat as if possessing nothing else to do. Her profile drooped, the knot of hair fastened low beneath her dark hat visible, and a long, wrinkled glove covered the hand holding the parasol. He took a step closer, and she turned to look at him.

"Oh"—she said. For the first time, Archer noticed a startled look in her eyes, which swiftly melted into a slow smile of wonder and contentment.

"Oh," she murmured again, on a different note, as he gazed down at her. Without rising, she gestured for him to join her on the bench.

"I'm here on business—just arrived," Archer explained. Without understanding why, he suddenly began to feign astonishment at seeing her. "But what on earth are you doing in this wilderness?" He had no real idea what he was saying; he felt as though shouting across endless distances, fearing she might vanish before he could reach her.

"I? Oh, I'm here on business too," she answered, turning her head until they faced one another. Her words barely registered in his mind; he was acutely aware only of her voice, stunned by the fact that not a single echo of it remained in his memory. He had not even recalled that it was low-pitched, carrying a faint roughness on the consonants.

"You do your hair differently," he said, his heart hammering as if he'd uttered something irrevocable.

"Differently? No—it's only that I do it as best I can when I'm without Nastasia."

"Nastasia—but isn't she with you?"

"No. I'm alone. For two days it wasn't worth the trouble to bring her."

"You're alone—at the Parker House?"

She looked at him with a flash of her old malice. "Does it strike you as dangerous?"

"No—not dangerous—"

"But unconventional? I see; I suppose it is." She considered the remark for a moment. "I hadn't thought of it, because I've just done something so much more unconventional." A faint tinge of irony lingered in her eyes. "I've just refused to take back a sum of money—that belonged to me."

Archer sprang up, stepping back slightly. She had furled her parasol and sat absently tracing patterns in the gravel. Soon, he returned, standing before her.

"Some one... has come here to meet you?"

"Yes."

"With this offer?"

She nodded.

"And you refused—because of the conditions?"

"I refused," she said after a pause.

He sat down beside her again. "What were the conditions?"

"Oh, they weren't onerous: just to sit at the head of his table now and then."

Another silence fell. Archer's heart had slammed shut in that strange, inexplicable way, leaving him groping vainly for a word.

"He wants you back—at any price?"

"Well—a considerable price. At least the sum is considerable for me."

He paused, struggling to formulate the question he felt compelled to ask.

"It was to meet him here that you came?"

She stared at him, then burst into laughter. "Meet him—my husband? *Here*? At this season he's always at Cowes or Baden."

"He sent someone?"

"Yes."

"With a letter?"

She shook her head. "No, just a message. He never writes. I don't think I've received more than one letter from him." The allusion flushed her cheek, reflecting vividly in Archer's own blush.

"Why does he never write?"

"Why should he? What does one have secretaries for?"

The young man's blush deepened. She had spoken the word as if it held no more significance than any other in her vocabulary. For a fleeting moment, the question "Did he send his secretary, then?" hovered on his tongue, but the memory of Count Olensky's solitary letter to his wife silenced him. He paused again, then plunged forward.

"And the person?"

"The emissary? The emissary," Madame Olenska replied, still smiling, "might, for all I care, have left already; but he has insisted on waiting until this evening... in case... on the chance..."

"And you came out here to consider the chance?"

"I came out to get a breath of air. The hotel is too stifling. I'm taking the afternoon train back to Portsmouth."

They sat in silence, avoiding eye contact and staring straight ahead at the passersby. Finally, she turned her gaze back to his face. "You're not changed."

He felt the urge to answer, "I was, until I saw you again," but instead, he stood up abruptly and glanced around at the untidy, sweltering park.

"This is horrible. Why shouldn't we go out a little on the bay? There's a breeze, and it will be cooler. We might take the steamboat down to Point Arley." She glanced up at him hesitantly, but he pressed on. "On a Monday morning there won't be anyone on the boat. My train doesn't leave until evening; I'm going back to New York. Why shouldn't we?" He looked down at her, and suddenly broke out, "Haven't we done all we could?"

"Oh," she murmured again. She stood up, reopening her sunshade and glancing about as if consulting the scene for counsel, assuring herself of the impossibility of staying. Then her eyes returned to his face. "You mustn't say things like that to me," she said.

"I'll say anything you like; or nothing. I won't open my mouth unless you tell me to. What harm can it do to anybody? All I want is to listen to you," he stammered.

She produced a small gold watch on an enamelled chain. "Oh, don't calculate," he interrupted, "give me the day! I want to get you away from that man. At what time was he coming?"

Her color rose again. "At eleven."

"Then you must come at once."

"You needn't be afraid—if I don't come."

"Nor you either—if you do. I swear I only want to hear about you, to know what you've been doing. It's a hundred years since we've met—it may be another hundred before we meet again."
