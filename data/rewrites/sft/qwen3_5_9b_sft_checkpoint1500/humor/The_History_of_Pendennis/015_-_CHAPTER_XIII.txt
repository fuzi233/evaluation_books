While the household at Fairoaks grew restless, waiting for the Major's return, Dr. Portman and his wife paused at Helen's lodge-gate on their journey home to Clavering. With them was a brief note from Major Pendennis, penned at the precise moment when the battle with the dragon turned decisively against him. The Major explained that he intended to stay another day at Chatteris to consult with Messrs. Tatham, the lawyers, but conspicuously omitted any mention of the morning's disastrous transaction.

Young Pen, however, found no pleasure in venturing into town while his guardian was present. He dreaded the notion of his uncle spying upon him from the Dean's grass-plat while he attempted the art of courtship in Miss Costigan's drawing-room. The "man of the polished boots" would inevitably ruin the simple joy of a solitary walk. Pen's affections were a quiet, internal storm; they could manifest only through the violent ogling of eyes, remaining utterly silent and dumb in the presence of third parties. After all, the talk of lovers is often the vocabulary without the key, a lamp without the flame. To the uninitiated, it sounds merely silly. Yet, consider the old love letters one keeps hidden away. How blank they appear in retrospect! What spellbound infatuation once made that nonsense beautiful? One wonders how anyone could live happily on six absurd lines, until the inevitable reactionary period sets in, leaving one restless and miserable without a fresh supply of folly. That is why we respectfully decline to publish any of Mr. Pen's verses from this era; they are too spooney and wild for young ladies to read in cold blood. Bide your time, fair maidens; perhaps you will compose similar ravings on your own account soon. For now, let us tie up Mr. Pen's first outpourings with Miss Fotheringay's string and seal them with Captain Costigan's great silver seal.

The Major emerged from his interview with Captain Costigan in a state of concentrated fury, radiating a terror that made him difficult to approach. "The impudent bog-trotting scamp!" he muttered, thinking. "Dare to threaten me! Dare to suggest that his damned Costigans might marry with the Pendennises! Send me a challenge! If the fellow can find a gentleman to carry it, I shall not balk him.—Psha! What would the world say if I parried with a tipsy mountebank over a row with an actress in a barn?" Consequently, when Dr. Portman approached, asking anxiously about the outcome of his battle with the dragon, the Major feigned caution. He claimed the affair was ugly, disagreeable, and far from over, strictly enjoining the doctor and Mrs. Portman to say nothing about the business at Fairoaks. He contented himself with dispatching the aforementioned note and retreated to his hotel, where he vented his wrath upon Mr. Morgan, his valet, cursing loudly upstairs and downstairs.

Morgan relayed the news to his master, Mr. Foker. Having finished his breakfast around two in the afternoon, Foker, eager to learn the result of the interview between his two friends, donned his brocade dressing-gown and embroidered skull-cap. He knocked on the Major's door. Inside, the Major was engaged with papers and red tape, consulting with old Mr. Tatham, the lawyer who had managed his late brother's affairs. Seeing the modest youth clad in such splendor, Mr. Foker hesitated to intrude, but the Major, spotting him, smiled and beckoned him in. Foker removed his embroidered tarboosh, bowed gracefully, and seated himself, spreading out his crimson skirts with excessive kindness. "You seem to like my dressing-gown, sir," he remarked to Mr. Tatham. "A pretty thing, isn't it? Neat, but not in the least gaudy."

"There is that in Foker's manner," the narrative observes, "which would have put an Inquisitor into good humor, smoothing even the wrinkles under the Major's head of hair." The Major recounted his unsatisfactory interview with the "Irishman"—Captain Costigan—who had dared to call his nephew a liar and hinted at cowardice. Mr. Tatham, a Dissenter who avoided theaters, expressed shock at the tale of his nephew's loves, questioning why Mrs. Pendennis had not married a serious man to keep this unfortunate boy from perdition. Mr. Foker chipped in with his usual familiar commentary, noting he had known uncommon good people in the booths. However, Mr. Tatham remained firm regarding Costigan's character: a wine-bibber, an idler, and a notorious insolvent. Indeed, a writ had been issued against Costigan and a play-actor for a bill given to Mr. Skinner, a respectable grocer, but neither defendant was deemed "worth powder and shot."

As Tatham spoke, a third knock startled them. An athletic gentleman in a shabby braided frock entered, bearing a letter with a large blotched red seal. "I am the bearer of a mission from my friend Captain Costigan," the man began, pausing as he caught sight of Mr. Tatham's grave countenance. "Hullo, Garbetts, speak up!" cried Mr. Foker, delighted. "Why, bless my soul, it is the other party to the bill!" exclaimed Tatham. Garbetts, turning pale like Macbeth at Banquo's ghost, gasped inarticulate words and fled. The Major's gravity dissolved instantly into laughter, shared by Foker and the attorney. "I don't think there'll be any fight, Major," young Foker predicted, mimicking the tragedian. "If there is, the old gentleman may send the bailiffs to separate the men." The Major, relieved by the ludicrous resolution, admitted that Foker always arrived to put him in good spirits.

Later that afternoon, Foker visited Captain Costigan, who was absent, having been encouraged by his daughter to frequent the convivial club at the Magpie Hotel. Instead, Foker found Miss Fotheringay at home, washing tea cups while Mr. Bows sat opposite. "Just done breakfast, I see," Foker popped in, earning a sharp retort before launching into a harlequinade of spins and bows. Miss Fotheringay laughed with all her heart, slapping Foker's hand, a gesture that would have terrified Pen, who trembled at the thought of a slap.

The trio discussed the morning's events. Foker praised the Major's veracity and honor, describing him as a "tip-top swell" who would never submit to deceit, especially concerning a charming young woman. He delicately touched upon the marriage question, revealing a perhaps justified contempt for Mr. Pen's high-flown sentimentality. "He's too young for you: too green," Foker noted, predicting the match would fail because Pen was "poor as Job." Miss Costigan, though admitting the absurdity of the arrangement due to her father's stories and the age disparity, lamented that she could not simply reject two thousand a year. "It all comes of believing Papa's silly stories," she sighed, though the image of Lieutenant Sir Derby Oaks briefly crossed her mind.

Despite the odds, Miss Costigan declared the Major a proper gentleman, smelling of lavender and as neat as a pin. Mr. Foker invited them to dinner at his apartment at the George, hoping to mend fences after the morning's "shindy." Though Mr. Bows initially declined, Miss Costigan, possessing an honest appetite, accepted. Thus, at seven o'clock, the Major found himself seated with Mr. Bows, a professional fiddler, and Miss Costigan, whose father had recently threatened to blow his brains out. To complete the tableau, Foker fetched the intoxicated General from the Magpie, where he was singing a pathetic song. The General, tearful and effusive, embraced the Major as a dearest friend, calling him his salvation. The next day, the Major called on the General with great cordiality, expressing his desire to serve Miss Costigan on any occasion.

Back at Fairoaks the following day, the Major returned in silence regarding his adventures. He engaged Mr. Smirke to stop for dinner, displaying a constrained hilarity and unusual gracefulness toward his nephew. He gave Pen an emphatic "God-bless-you" before the lad retired, and later, in the breakfast room, pointed to a letter under the newspaper. "Will you come into the drawing-room?" he asked Mrs. Pendennis. "I want to speak to you."

In private, the Major revealed the truth: the affair was at an end. He had dictated a dismissal letter to Costigan, revealing Pen's poverty, which Costigan's father had misrepresented. "She withdrew at once," the Major insisted, "without any persuasion of mine. She is ten years older than you, perfectly unfit to be your wife, and knows it." When Pen, furious and wounded, demanded to speak to Miss Fotheringay himself, his mother interjected, urging him to forget her and comfort her. "Be a man," she pleaded, embracing him as the Major wisely shut the door upon them.

Having secured a complete victory, the Major retrieved Pen's letters from Chatteris, returning them to Costigan along with the promissory note that had caused so much trouble, which he promptly settled. Pen, devastated, rushed to Chatteris in vain. Every attempt to see Miss Fotheringay ended in rejection; the General cut him in the street.
