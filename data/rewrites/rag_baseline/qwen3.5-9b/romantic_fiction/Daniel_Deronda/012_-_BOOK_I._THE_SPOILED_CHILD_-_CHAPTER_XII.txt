On the second day following the archery contest, the morning light filtered through the open casements of the breakfast room, carrying with it the crisp, invigorating scent of the summer air and the distant, verdant hum of the park. Beyond the window, the landscape unfolded in soft, purplish hues, merging into a dense, bordering woodland that stood as a silent sentinel to the estate's tranquility. Inside, the atmosphere was one of deliberate stillness, preserved by the room's sober, antique elegance; it possessed a conscious, well-bred silence that stood in stark contrast to the chaotic restlessness of vulgar furnishings. Yet, beneath this veneer of decorum, the dynamic between the two gentlemen occupying the space was far from harmonious.

Mr. Henleigh Mallinger Grandcourt had arranged his chair to face the lawn, adopting a posture of calculated nonchalance: his left leg draped casually over the back of another seat, his right elbow propped upon the table, a large cigar burning steadily between his fingers. Beside him, Mr. Lush continued to eat, his movements lacking the same theatrical deliberation. Between them moved a motley pack of six dogs, creatures of considerable privilege who treated hunger as a game, selectively ignoring delicacies unless presented with the specific deference they demanded. Their attention flickered vacillatingly between the two masters, until the focus settled on Fetch, a magnificent liver-colored water spaniel. Unlike her peers, Fetch sat with unwavering constancy, her forepaws planted firmly, her expressive brown gaze locked onto Grandcourt.

In Grandcourt's lap rested a tiny Maltese terrier, adorned with a miniature silver collar and bell, a small parcel of animal warmth that he tended with a detached affection whenever his hand was free from the cigar or coffee cup. Fetch, sensing the slight, extended her large, silky paw onto his leg. For half a minute, Grandcourt regarded her with an impassive countenance before finally laying down his cigar to lift the diminutive Fluff to his chin, bestowing gentle pats while maintaining a grave scrutiny of Fetch. The spaniel, seemingly unable to endure the neglect, whimpered intermittently, attempting to suppress her visible discontent before resting her head beside the appealing paw, her expression one of pitiful supplication. A lover of dogs might have interpreted this as a plea for acknowledgment, and indeed, Grandcourt's reputation for canine adoration was built upon such interpretations. However, when Fetch's suppressed anguish erupted into a howling bark, Grandcourt reacted with swift irritation. He shoved the spaniel down without a word, deposited Fluff carelessly onto the table—where the dog's black nose momentarily dominated a salt cellar—and returned his attention to his cigar, annoyed that the beast had extinguished it.

Fetch, finding herself in the throes of a lament that proved difficult to cease, let out a second, even louder howl followed by a third. "Turn out that brute, will you?" Grandcourt commanded Lush, his voice devoid of inflection, his eyes fixed forward as if expecting the command to be read in the air alone. Lush rose immediately, lifting the heavy spaniel despite his aversion to stooping, and carried her out, disappearing for a couple of minutes before returning. He then relit his own cigar, settling into an angled position that allowed him to observe Grandcourt's profile without turning fully, and asked, "Shall you ride or drive to Quetcham today?"

"I am not going to Quetcham," Grandcourt replied.

"You did not go yesterday either."

Grandcourt smoked in silence for a moment before stating, "I suppose you sent my card and inquiries."

"I went myself at four," Lush explained calmly. "I informed them you were due shortly. Naturally, they assumed some accident had prevented you from fulfilling your intention, especially if you intend to go today."

A silence ensued, broken only by the crackle of tobacco. Finally, Grandcourt asked, "What men are invited here with their wives?"

Lush consulted a notebook. "The Captain and Mrs. Torrington arrive next week. Then there are Mr. Hollis and Lady Flora, along with the Cushats and the Gogoffs."

"A rather ragged assortment," Grandcourt observed after a pause. "Why did you invite the Gogoffs? When you draft invitations in my name, you ought to provide a list beforehand, rather than descending upon me with a giantess without my knowledge. She ruins the aesthetic of the room."

"You invited the Gogoffs yourself when you encountered them in Paris," Lush countered.

"What does my encounter with them in Paris have to do with this?" Grandcourt snapped, his tone shifting instantly from a languid drawl suggestive of ennui to a subdued, peremptory command that Lush recognized as the voice of absolute will. "I instructed you to give me a list."

Like many men of his station, Grandcourt possessed two distinct vocal registers. Until now, he had spoken with a superficial, interrupted drawl, but this shift revealed the underlying rigidity of his character. "Are there any other couples you would like to invite?"

"Yes," Lush replied, seizing the opening. "Consider inviting some respectable families with daughters. And one of your damned musicians, though certainly not a comic performer."

"I wonder if Klesmer would consent to join us once he departs Quetcham. Nothing but first-class music will suffice for Miss Arrowpoint."

Lush spoke lightly, yet his eyes narrowed with observation. "You need not trouble yourself excessively, perhaps. However, certain formalities must be observed before a man can marry a fortune."

"Very likely. But I have no intention of marrying a fortune."

"That is a pity—to squander such an opportunity and dismantle your own ambitions."

"_Your_ plans, I presume you mean," Grandcourt retorted.

"You carry debts, you know. Things may prove inconvenient eventually. The heirship is not absolutely secure."

Grandcourt offered no response. Lush pressed on. "Truly, it is a splendid opportunity. The parents demand nothing less than perfection, and the daughter's appearance and demeanor require no deductions whatsoever, as if she possessed not a single penny. She lacks conventional beauty, yet she possesses the bearing to sustain any rank. Furthermore, she is unlikely to reject the prospects you can offer."

"Perhaps not."

"The parents would permit you to do whatever you wished with them."

"But I should not wish to do anything with them."

Here, Lush paused, gathering his composure before speaking in a deep, reproachful tone. "Good heavens, Grandcourt! After your experiences, will you allow a mere whim to disrupt your comfortable settlement in life?"

"Spare your oratory. I know precisely what I intend to do."

"What?" Lush lowered his cigar, thrusting his hands into his side pockets as if bracing for an exasperating revelation, though striving to maintain his temper. "I am going to marry the other girl."

"Have you fallen in love?" The question carried a heavy sneer.

"I am going to marry her."

"You have already made her an offer, then?"

"No."

"She is a young woman with a formidable will, I suspect. She is extremely well-suited to create a scene. She knows exactly what she desires."

"She does not like you," Grandcourt stated, a ghost of a smile touching his lips.

"Quite true," Lush agreed, adding with marked derision, "Nevertheless, if you and she are devoted to one another, that shall suffice."

Grandcourt ignored this remark, sipping his coffee, rising, and strolling out onto the lawn, trailed by the entire pack of dogs. Lush watched him depart for a fleeting moment before resuming his cigar. He smoked slowly, running his fingers through his beard with an inspector's scrutiny, until he finally stroked it with the air of someone who had reached a definitive conclusion. "Check, old boy!"

Having known Grandcourt for fifteen years, Lush understood perfectly which measures were futile, though the efficacy of others remained a matter of doubt. Early in his career, Lush had held a fellowship and nearly taken holy orders for the sake of a college living, but, lacking a vocation for that path, he accepted the role of traveling companion first to a marquess, and subsequently to the young Grandcourt. Having lost his father prematurely, Grandcourt found Lush so conveniently adept that he allowed him to serve as prime minister in all his personal affairs. Fifteen years of this symbiotic relationship had rendered Grandcourt increasingly dependent on Lush's dexterity, while Lush grew equally reliant on the lazy luxury that his transactions afforded Grandcourt without demanding significant recompense.

One could not claim that this prolonged association had intensified Grandcourt's inherent lack of respect for his companion, for that disdain had been absolute from the outset. Rather, it had reinforced Grandcourt's conviction that he might strike Lush at will—though he never actually chose to kick any animal, for the act of kicking is a compromising posture, and a gentleman's dogs should be flogged by his servants. Instead, Grandcourt employed verbal barbs designed to expose himself to retaliation should his confidant possess an independent spirit. Yet, what son of a vicar, who has starved his wife and daughters of calico to fund his male offspring's education at Oxford, can retain an independent spirit when bent on dining with refined discrimination, riding superior horses, and living amidst the most luxuriant honey-blossomed clover—all without labor?
