“Nay, Richart,” Catherine said at last, “for Heaven’s sake, let not this one wretched woman turn our household into a battlefield. Has she not already stirred up enough ill will?”

“In truth, she has,” Richart replied. “Fear me not, good mother. Let her come and read the letter of the poor boy whom she has bewitched through devilish arts—and then let her go. Give me your word that you’ll show her no more than the barest courtesy, no more than is decent in our own home; and I shall say no more.” With that agreement, they waited.

She, for her part, prepared for the meeting with scarcely less hostility. When Denys brought word that they would not come to her, but expected her to come to them, her lip curled in scorn. “See how every feeling in them—however small—is greater than their love for Gerard,” she muttered. “Well, I have no such excuse. So why should I imitate the petty pride of the burgher class, the arrogance of all who are unlearned? I will go to them—for Gerard’s sake. Oh, how I loathe them!”

Thus poor, well-meaning Denys was bringing together in one house the ingredients of a storm.

Margaret dressed herself as a knight of her time might have armed himself for battle—he to parry blows, she to fend off glances: glances of contempt at her poverty, or mockery at her extravagance. Her kirtle was of English cloth, dark blue, and her farthingale and hose matched it in color but were a glossy roan, or claret shade. Not a scrap of ostentatious fur adorned her—only plain, snowy linen wristbands, and intricately plaited linen rising from the bosom of her kirtle to just below her throat, forming a square frame rather than a round collar. Her front hair still fell in two gentle waves, much like the fashion Mary Queen of Scots would later revive; but instead of a silver net—unsuitable now—her head was covered with a tiny, tight-fitting hood of dark blue cloth, edged with silver thread. Her shoes were red, yet the rich tones of her petticoat and hose prepared the eye for the contrast, highlighting her arched instep and shapely foot.

Beauty knew its purpose then as it does now.

And with all this, she kept her enemies waiting—though it was already three o’clock by the dial.

At last she set out, attended by her companion. Halfway across the courtyard, she paused and said thoughtfully, “Denys!”

“Well, she-general?”

“I must go back” (in a voice thick with emotion).

“What? Have you left something behind?”

“What?”

“My courage. Oh! oh! oh!”

“Nay, nay, be brave, she-general. I’ll be with you.”

“Aye, but will you stay close beside me when we’re there?”

Denys promised, and she resumed her walk—but cautiously.

Meanwhile, the family gathered in silence, each harboring a strange mix of emotions: mortification, curiosity, tender longing, resentment toward the woman who came to stir those feelings, yet also a secret desire to see what she looked like—what could have enchanted Gerard so utterly and caused so much turmoil.

At last, Denys returned alone and whispered, “The she-comrade is outside.”

“Bring her in,” Eli commanded. “Now, hush—all of you. No one speaks to her except me.”

They turned their eyes to the door, silent as stones.

A low murmur came from beyond—the rough voice of Denys and a soft, mellow feminine tone.

Then silence.

The door opened slowly.

There stood Margaret Brandt, dressed as described, pale but composed, lovely, her gaze fixed straight ahead.

All rose—except Kate—and remained motionless, staring.

“Be seated, mistress,” Eli said gravely, gesturing to a chair set apart for her.

She bowed her head slightly and crossed the room. As she moved, her condition was unmistakable—not only in her form, but in the quiet weariness that clung to her.

Cornelis and Sybrandt hated her for it. Richart thought it marred her beauty.

It softened the women, just a little.

She took the letter from her bosom and kissed it as if she were alone. Then she settled herself to read, with the air of one who knew she had come for this single purpose.

But as she began, she noticed they had placed her apart, like a leper. She glanced at Denys, then lowered her hand and made a swift, furtive signal for him to come near.

He obeyed instantly, stepping forward as if summoned by a command, standing guard at her shoulder like a sentinel. But his eagerness betrayed the fact that he had been ordered there—and at that, she flushed.

Then she began to read Gerard’s letter, their Gerard, to their eager ears, in a voice clear and sweet, soft yet deeply felt, as though every syllable carried the weight of her soul. It was a voice like a woman’s heart speaking directly from heaven.

“I do not doubt, my Margaret, that long before this reaches your eyes, Denys, my dearest friend, will have found you and told you of our sudden and most sorrowful parting. Therefore, I will begin at that bitter day. What happened afterward to him, poor faithful soul—I long to know, but may not. Yet I pray for him night and day, after you, dearest. There was never a truer, more loyal friend than David had in Jonathan—nor I in him. Be kind to him, for poor Gerard’s sake.”

At these words—unexpected, heartfelt—Denys leaned his head against the back of her chair and groaned aloud.

She turned quickly, found his hand, and pressed it.

So the lover and the friend held hands while the letter was read.

“I walked forward dazed, as if in a bad dream. Soon a gentleman approached on horseback, with servants following. He nearly rode over me. He pulled rein at the crest of the hill and sent his armed men back to rob me. They robbed me politely enough—taking my purse and my last copper—and rode away gaily. I wandered on, stunned, friendless, penniless.”

A collective sigh passed through the room, followed by an oath from Denys.

“Then a strange dimness came over me. I lay down to sleep in the snow. ’Twas foolish, with wolves nearby. Had I loved you as you deserve, I would have shown more strength. But oh, sweet love, the drowsiness that crept over me, numbing me, was more than nature. And so I slept. And had not God been kinder to us than I was to you or myself, I would never have awakened again—so all say. I suppose I slept an hour or two, no more. Then a hand shook me roughly. I woke to my troubles. And there stood a servant girl in holiday clothes. ‘Are you mad,’ she asked, seeming angry, ‘to sleep in the snow, beneath wolves’ noses? Are you weary of life, barely weaned?’ Then, more gently, ‘Come, now—get up like a good lad.’ So I rose. ‘Are you rich or poor?’ But I stared at her, amazed. ‘Why, it’s easy to answer,’ she said. ‘Are you rich or poor?’ Then I gave a great, loud cry. She jumped back. ‘Am I rich or poor? Had you asked me an hour ago, I would have said I am rich. But now I am poorer than any earth bears. An hour ago I was rich in a friend, rich in money, rich in hope and youth; but now the Bastard of Burgundy has taken my friend, another gentleman my purse, and I cannot go forward to Rome nor return to her I left in Holland. I am the poorest of the poor.’ ‘Alack!’ she said. ‘Yet if you’d been rich, I might have let you lie in the snow—no use to me. Then you’d soon have died as bare as you came into the world. But being poor, you are our man. Come with me.’ So I went—because she bid me, and because I cared not where I went. She took me to a fine house nearby, into a noble dining hall hung in black. A table was set with many dishes, but only one plate, one chair. ‘Fall to!’ she whispered. ‘What—alone?’ I asked. ‘Alone? And which of us, think you, would eat from the same dish with you? Are we thieves of the dead?’ Then she asked where I was born. ‘At Tergou,’ I said. She replied, ‘And when a gentleman dies in that country, do they not serve his dinner as usual until he is buried, and place a poor man at the table?’ I said no. She blushed for us. Here, they are better Christians. So I had to sit. But I had little appetite. Then this kind girl sat beside me and poured wine. I tasted it—and it cut me to the heart that Denys was not there to drink with me. He loves good wine, and good women, bad, or indifferent. The strong, rich wine curled around my sick heart. That day, for the first time, I understood why troubled men turn to drink. She made me eat from every dish. ‘It is unlucky to pass one. Nothing here is anything but her master’s daily meal.’ ‘He had a good stomach, then,’ I said. ‘Ay, lad, and a good heart. At least, so we all say now that he is dead. But while alive, I never heard a word about it.’ So I ate like a bird, nibbling at each dish. And when she saw me sigh, and nearly choke on the food, she took pity and told me to cheer up. I would sup and rest there that night. She went to the servant, and he gave me a fine bed. I told him everything and asked whether the law could recover my purse. ‘Law?’ he said. ‘There is no law for the poor in Burgundy. Why, it was the cousin of the Lady of the Manor who robbed you. He knew the wild spark. The matter must be judged before the lady—and she is young, more likely to hang you for accusing her cousin, a gentleman, a handsome man, than to make him return your goods. Inside a town, a poor man might sometimes see justice; but among the nobles—never.’ So I said, ‘I’ll remain robbed rather than seek justice and find the gallows.’ They were all kind to me the next day. The girl offered me some of her small wages to help me reach the Rhine.”

“Oh! Then he is coming home! He is coming home!” shouted Denys, breaking in.

She gently shook her head, reproving him.

“I beg your pardon, everyone,” he said stiffly.

“It was a sore temptation,” she continued, “but being a servant, my pride rebelled. ‘No, no,’ I said. She told me I was wrong. ‘It is pride misplaced. Poor folk should help one another—or who else would?’ I said if I could repay her, it would be well; but for a gift—no. I was already too indebted. Should I write a letter for her? ‘No,’ she said, ‘he is here in the house.’ Should I draw her portrait, and earn my way? ‘Can you?’ she asked. I told her I could try. Her habit would make a fine picture. So she was eager to be painted and give it to her lover. I placed her in good light, and made two sketches—one of which I send you, colored at odd moments. The other I did hastily, without care, for which may Heaven forgive me—time was short. They, poor things, knew no better, and were proud and joyful. Both kissed me after their custom—she, the servant, and he, her sweetheart—and bade me God-speed. I set off for the Rhine.”

Margaret paused and handed the colored drawing to Denys to pass around. The women examined it eagerly, drawn by the costume—different from Dutch domestic dress. Her hair was bound tightly in a linen bag, a yellow half-kerchief crossed her head from ear to ear, ending in a rectangular point down the center of her forehead, and meeting in two points over her bosom. She wore a red kirtle with long sleeves, kilted high in front, revealing a green farthingale and a large red leather purse hanging over it; red stockings, yellow leather shoes—low-quartered, square-toed, fastened with a strap buckling over the instep—a style ahead of its time, perhaps the crude ancestor of the diamond buckle.

Margaret continued:

“But oh! how I missed my Denys at every step! Often I sat down by the road and groaned. One afternoon, I stopped where two roads met, head in hand, heart heavy, thinking of you, my poor sweetheart, of my lost friend, and of the little house at Tergou—where once they all loved me, though now it has turned to hate.”

Catherine sighed. “Alas, that he should think so.”

Eli silenced her. “Hush, wife!”

“And I sighed loudly, often. And as I sighed, one came singing down the other road like a bird. ‘Ay, chirp and chirp,’ I cried bitterly. ‘You have not lost sweetheart, friend, father’s hearth, mother’s smile, or every penny in the world.’ And at last he sang so joyfully, I jumped up in anger to escape his jarring mirth. But before I fled, I looked down the path to see what could make a man so cheerful in this weary world—and lo! the singer was a humpbacked cripple, with a bloody bandage over his eye, both legs gone at the knee.”

“He! he! he! he! he!” Sybrandt cackled, laughing.

Margaret’s eyes flashed. She began to fold the letter.

“Nay, lass,” Eli said sharply, “do not heed him. Thou unmannerly cur—offer such insolence again and I’ll throw thee out.”

“Why, what is there to mock, Sybrandt?” Catherine asked more gently. “Is not our Kate afflicted? And is she not the most content of us all, singing like a thrush between her pains? But I am as bad as thou—praise God, read on, lass, and stop our chatter with something worth hearing.”

“Then I said, ‘May this be true?’ And I rebuked myself. ‘Gerard, son of Eli, dost thou well to lament thy lot, having youth and health—while here comes the wreck of nature on crutches, praising God’s goodness with a song like a thrush?’”

Catherine murmured, “There you see.”

Eli snapped, “Hush, dame, hush!”

“And whenever he saw me, he stopped singing and hobbled up, chanting, ‘Charity, for love of Heaven, sweet master, charity,’ in a whine as piteous as wind through a keyhole. ‘Alack, poor soul,’ I said, ‘charity is in my heart, but not in my purse. I am poor as thou.’ Then he disbelieved me, and to move me, rolled up his sleeve and showed a wound on his arm. ‘Poor cripple though I be,’ he said, ‘I am likely to lose this eye too—look.’ I saw and groaned for him. To excuse myself, I told him how I had been robbed of my last copper. At that, he ceased whining instantly and said, in a bold, manly voice, ‘Then I’ll take a rest. Here, youngster, pull this strap—nay, fear not!’ I pulled, and down came a sturdy pair of legs from his back; half his hump vanished, and the wound in his eye was no deeper than the bandage.”

“Oh!” exclaimed all the listeners in unison.

“Seeing my astonishment, he laughed in my face and told me I was not worth deceiving. Then he offered me his protection. ‘My face is prophetic,’ he said. ‘Of what?’ I asked. ‘Marry,’ he said, ‘that its owner will starve in this thievish land.’ Travel teaches even the young wisdom. Once I would have fled such a fraud as a pestilence; but now I listened patiently, hoping for scraps of advice. And I did well: for nature and his wandering life had filled the poor rogue with shrewdness and practical knowledge—childlike was I beside him. After examining me thoroughly, he said, ‘You did well to leave France and head for Germany. But forget Holland. Go on to Augsburg and Nuremberg—the paradise of craftsmen. Thence to Venice, if you wish. But you’ll never settle in Italy or any foreign land once you’ve tasted the great German cities. Why? Because there is but one honest country in Europe—and that is Germany. Since you are honest, and since I am a vagabond, Germany was made for us two.’ I challenged him: how could one country suit both honest men and rogues? ‘Why, thou novice,’ he said, ‘because in an honest land there are fewer rogues to bite the honest man—and more honest men for the rogue to bite.’ I was in luck, being honest, to have fallen in with a sharp, friendly rogue. ‘Be my pal,’ he said, ‘I’m going to Nuremberg—we’ll arrive with full pouches. I’ll teach you the *cul de bois*, the *cul de jatte*, how to beg, chant, patter, raise swellings, paint sores and ulcers so real they’d fool the devil.’”

I shivered and told him I’d rather die than shame myself and my family so.

“Eli,” he said, “good lad! good lad!”

“Why, what shame was there for one like me to become a beggar? Beggary was an ancient and honorable trade. What did holy monks, bishops, and kings do when they sought Heaven’s favor? Wash the feet of beggars—those favorites of the saints. ‘The saints were no fools,’ he told me. Then he held out his foot. ‘Look at that—it was washed by the greatest king alive, Louis of France, on the last Holy Thursday. And the next day, Friday, he was clapped in the stocks by the warden of a petty hamlet.’ So I said my foot should walk between such high honor and such low disgrace, on the same path of honesty, please God. Well then, since I lacked the spirit to beg, he would indulge my stubbornness. I would work under him—he the head, I the hands. And with that, he sat like a judge on a heap of dust by the roadside and questioned me strictly: ‘What can you do?’ I began to say I was strong and willing. ‘Bah!’ he said, ‘so is an ox. Say what you can do that Sir Ox cannot.’ I could write—I had won a prize for it. ‘Can you write as fast as printers?’ he jeered. ‘What else?’ I could paint. ‘That’s better.’ I nearly tore my hair to hear him say so—me, going to Rome to write. I could play the psaltery a bit. ‘That’s well. Can you tell stories?’ Ay, by the score. ‘Then,’ he said, ‘I hire you from this moment.’ ‘What for?’ I asked. ‘Nothing crooked, Sir Candour,’ he said. ‘I’ll feed you all the way and find you work—and take half your earnings, no more.’ ‘Agreed,’ I said, giving my hand. ‘Now, servant,’ he said, ‘we will dine. But you need not stand behind my chair—for two reasons: first, I have no chair; second, good fellowship pleases me more than state.’ From his wallet, he produced meat, fowl, pastry, a dozen spices wrapped in flax paper, and wine fit for a king. Never feasted I better than from this beggar’s wallet—now my master. After eating well, I was ready to go on. ‘But,’ he said, ‘servants should not press their masters too hard, especially after eating—then the body rests, and the mind turns to contemplation.’ So he lay on his back, gazing calmly at the sky, wondering if there were any beggars up there. I told him I knew of only one—Lazarus. ‘Could he do the *cul de jatte* better than I?’ he asked, looking quite jealous. I told him no—Lazarus was honest, though a beggar, fed daily on crumbs from a rich man’s table, and dogs licked his sores. ‘Servant,’ he said, ‘I see a foul fault in you—you lie without discretion. Now, the purpose of lying is to deceive. This is no better than playing with the devil’s tail. I pray Heaven you prove to paint better than you cut jokes, or I am done out of a dinner. No beggar eats crumbs—only the fat of the land. And dogs do not lick a beggar’s sores—they are made with spearwort, or ratsbane, or biting acids, which dogs—even pigs—abhor. My sores are made by my own recipe; but no dog would lick them twice. I’ve made a scurvy bargain—you’re a cheating knave, I suspect, as well as a nincompoop.’ I gave no reply to this bundle of lies, which accused heavenly truth of falsehood for not matching his tale. He rose, and we took the road. Soon we reached a place with two small inns, barely a furlong apart. ‘Halt,’ said my master. ‘Their signs are faded—so much the better. Go in, avoid the master, speak to the wife, flatter her to the heavens—except the sign—and offer to repaint it cheap.’ So I went in and told the wife I was a painter who could restore her sign cheaply. She sent me away with a rebuff. I returned to my master. He groaned. ‘You are all fingers and no tongue,’ he said. ‘I’ve made a scurvy bargain. Come and hear me patter and flatter.’ Between the inns was a high hedge. He stepped behind it a moment and returned as a respectable tradesman. We went to the other inn, and I heard him praise it so lavishly that even the wife blushed. ‘But,’ he said, ‘there is one little fault—your sign is dull and faded. Say the word, and for a silver franc, my apprentice here—the cleverest I’ve ever had—will make it shine as bright as ever.’ While she hesitated, the rogue told her he had done it for a little inn nearby, and now the inn’s face was like the starry firmament. ‘D’ye hear that, my man?’ she cried, ‘“The Three Frogs” have had their sign painted—shall “The Four Hedgehogs” be outshone?’ So I painted, and my master stood by like a lord, advising me, winking at me to ignore him, and I earned a silver franc. He took me back to ‘The Three Frogs,’ and along the way disguised me with a beard and flattered them, telling them how he had adorned ‘The Four Hedgehogs.’ Into the net jumped the three poor frogs, and I earned another silver franc. Then we went on, and he found his crutches, sent me forward, and showed his ‘cicatrices d’emprunt,’ as he called them, and all his infirmities at ‘The Four Hedgehogs,’ and got both food and money. ‘Come, share and share,’ he said. So I gave him one franc. ‘I’ve made a good bargain,’ he said. ‘You’re a master painter, but take too long.’ So I told him in honest craft, things could not be done quickly and well. ‘Then do them quickly,’ he said. And he told me my name was Bon Bec, and I might call him Cul de Jatte—because that was his trade at our first meeting. In the next town, my master, Cul de Jatte, bought me a psaltery and set himself up again by the roadside in state, like one who once judged Marsyas and Apollo, piping for vain glory. So I played a strain. ‘Indifferent well, harmonious Bon Bec,’ he said haughtily. ‘Now tune thy pipes.’ So I sang a sweet melody taught me by good monks—and singing it reminded poor Bon Bec, Gerard once, of his youth and home, bringing tears to my eyes. But looking up, my master’s face was like a child’s after a beating, or sipping the foulest medicine. ‘Zounds, stop that bellyache blether,’ he said, ‘that will never win a stiver from peasants’ purses—it’ll sour nurses’ milk and make cows jump into rivers to escape it. What, false knave, did I buy you a fine new psaltery to remind me of my end? Listen—these are the songs that gladden hearts and fill minstrels’ purses.’ And he sang a blasphemous, obscene verse so vile I stepped back, fearing lightning might ruin the new psaltery. But none came—being winter. Then I said, ‘Master, the Lord is gracious. If I held the thunder, that ribaldry would be your last, thou foul-mouthed wretch.’”
