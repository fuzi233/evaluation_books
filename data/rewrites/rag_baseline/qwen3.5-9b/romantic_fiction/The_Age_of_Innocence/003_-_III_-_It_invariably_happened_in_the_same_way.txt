The pattern held fast, immutable as the architecture of the house itself. On the night of the annual ball, Mrs. Julius Beaufort invariably appeared at the Opera, a ritual designed to signal her total emancipation from domestic drudgery and her command over a staff capable of orchestrating chaos in her absence. The Beaufort mansion stood as a monument to this separation; it possessed a ballroom, a relic predating even the Mingotts and the Chiverses, a cavern of gilt chairs stacked in corners and chandeliers swaddled in dark cloth, dormant for three hundred and sixty-four days of the year. This architectural excess compensated for the shadows of the Beaufort past. Mrs. Archer, architect of social maxims, had once declared, "We all have our pet common people," a sentiment that, however daring, found a guilty admission in many an exclusive heart. Yet the Beauforts transcended the label of "common"; whispers suggested they were worse. Mrs. Beaufort was Regina Dallas, a Southern beauty rendered penniless by circumstance, introduced to New York by her cousin Medora Manson, whose motives were always noble but whose actions perpetually disastrous. In a city where lineage granted a *droit de cité*, the marriage to Julius Beaufort seemed a forfeiture of status. Who was he? An Englishman, agreeable and handsome, yet ill-tempered and bitter, arriving with recommendations from an old banker and quickly ascending the ladder of worldly power despite dissipated habits and mysterious origins. When Medora announced the engagement, it was dismissed as another act of Medora's folly. But folly, like wisdom, bears fruit. Two years later, the miracle was undeniable: the Beauforts owned the most distinguished house in New York. How it happened remained a mystery. Indolent, passive, labeled dull by the caustic, she reigned in her brownstone palace, draped in pearls and growing younger with each season, drawing the entire world without lifting a jeweled finger. The knowing observed that Beaufort himself managed the machinery—the training of servants, the selection of flowers, the brewing of punch, the drafting of notes—while presenting himself to the world as a carefree millionaire strolling into his own drawing-room with the detached amusement of a guest. "My wife's gloxinias are a marvel," he would say, unaware that the flowers were his doing. He carried rumors of his questionable English departure with the ease of a man carrying a hat, sweeping them aside until New York itself swept them into his drawing rooms. For over twenty years, invitations to the Beauforts carried the same weight of security as visits to the Mingotts, promising hot canvas-back ducks and vintage wines rather than tepid champagne and reheated croquettes.

As usual, Mrs. Beaufort appeared in her box just before the Jewel Song. When she rose at the end of the third act, wrapping her opera cloak around her lovely shoulders to vanish, New York knew the clock had struck for the ball. Their house was a badge of pride for foreigners, especially on this night. They were pioneers: the first to roll their own red velvet carpet down the steps under their own awning, the first to allow ladies to remove their cloaks in the hall rather than shuffle to the hostess's chamber to re-curl their hair—a courtesy attributed to Beaufort's assumption that all his wife's friends possessed maids for such tasks. The house was boldly planned, featuring a grand vista of enfiladed drawing rooms—sea-green, crimson, and bouton d'or—leading to a conservatory where camellias and tree-ferns arched their costly foliage over seats of black and gold bamboo.

Newland Archer arrived late, as befits a young man of his station. He had left his coat with the silk-stockinged footmen—a vanity of Beaufort's—dawdled in the library hung with Spanish leather and furnished with Buhl and malachite, where men were chatting and donning dancing gloves, before joining the line of guests receiving on the threshold of the crimson drawing room. Archer was distinctly nervous. He had not retreated to his club after the Opera, as the young bloods usually did, but had walked up Fifth Avenue in the fine night before returning. He feared the Mingotts might be pushing too hard, perhaps acting on Granny Mingott's orders to bring the Countess Olenska to the ball. The gravity of such a mistake had been clear in the tone of the club box earlier. Though determined to "see the thing through," his chivalrous eagerness to champion his betrothed's cousin had waned since their brief exchange at the Opera.

Wandering into the bouton d'or drawing room, where Beaufort had displayed the audacity to hang "Love Victorious," the controversial nude by Bouguereau, Archer found Mrs. Welland and her daughter near the ballroom door. Beyond them, couples glided over the floor; candlelight caught revolving tulle skirts, girlish heads wreathed in modest blossoms, the dashing aigrettes of young wives, and the glitter of glazed shirt fronts. Miss Welland hovered on the threshold, holding her lilies-of-the-valley, her face pale, her eyes burning with candid excitement. A cluster of young men and girls surrounded her, exchanging handclasps and laughter, while Mrs. Welland stood slightly apart, offering a qualified approval. It was evident Miss Welland was announcing her engagement, playing the role of parental reluctance deemed suitable for the occasion.

Archer paused. It was his express wish that the announcement be made, yet he would have preferred a different stage. To proclaim it amidst the heat and noise of a crowded ballroom robbed the event of the private bloom belonging to matters nearest the heart. His joy was so deep that this blurring of the surface left its essence untouched, but he wished the surface remained pure too. He found satisfaction in realizing May Welland shared this sentiment. Her eyes fled to his beseechingly, communicating: *Remember, we are doing this because it is right.* No appeal could have found a more immediate response in Archer's breast, though he wished the necessity of their action stemmed from some ideal reason rather than the simple presence of poor Ellen Olenska.

The group made way for him with significant smiles. After accepting his share of felicitations, he drew his betrothed into the center of the ballroom floor and placed his arm about her waist. "Now we shan't have to talk," he said, smiling into her candid eyes as they drifted away on the soft waves of the Blue Danube. She offered no verbal reply. Her lips trembled into a smile, but her eyes remained distant and serious, fixed on some ineffable vision. "Dear," Archer whispered, pressing her closer. It dawned on him that the first hours of engagement, even spent in a ballroom, possessed something grave and sacramental. What a new life awaited, with this whiteness, radiance, and goodness at his side!

Once the dance concluded, the pair, becoming an affianced couple, wandered into the conservatory. Sitting behind a tall screen of tree-ferns and camellias, Newland pressed her gloved hand to his lips. "You see I did as you asked me to," she said. "Yes: I couldn't wait," he answered, smiling. After a pause, he added, "Only I wish it hadn't had to be at a ball." "Yes, I know." She met his glance with comprehension. "But after all—even here we're alone together, aren't we?" "Oh, dearest—always!" Archer cried. Evidently, she was always going to understand; she was always going to say the right thing. This discovery made the cup of his bliss overflow, and he continued gaily, "The worst of it is that I want to kiss you and I can't." Speaking, he cast a swift glance about the conservatory, assured himself of their momentary privacy, and catching her to him laid a fugitive pressure on her lips. To counteract the audacity of this proceeding, he led her to a bamboo sofa in a less secluded part of the conservatory. Sitting beside her, he broke a lily-of-the-valley from her bouquet. She sat silent, and the world lay beneath them like a sunlit valley.

"Did you tell my cousin Ellen?" she asked presently, as if speaking through a dream. He roused himself, remembering he had not. Some invincible repugnance to speak of such things to the strange foreign woman had checked the words on his lips. "No—I hadn't the chance after all," he said, fibbing hastily. "Ah." She looked disappointed but gently resolved to gain her point. "You must, then, for I didn't either; and I shouldn't like her to think—" "Of course not. But aren't you, after all, the person to do it?" She pondered this. "If I'd done it at the right time, yes: but now that there's been a delay, I think you must explain that I'd asked you to tell her at the Opera, before our speaking about it to everybody here. Otherwise she might think I had forgotten her. You see, she's one of the family, and she's been away so long that she's rather—sensitive."

Archer looked at her glowingly. "Dear and great angel! Of course I'll tell her." He glanced apprehensively toward the crowded ballroom. "But I haven't seen her yet. Has she come?"
