The morning came on with a dull, white heat, as if the night had merely cooled itself to begin again. Margaret lifted herself from sleep with a kind of gratitude that it was ended, though her limbs felt as if the hours had been spent in watching rather than resting. In the next room her mother had stirred only once; the house had gone on in that careful hush which makes every small sound—cloth laid down, a door eased to its latch—seem louder for being meant to be quiet.

A little breeze, rare in Milton’s sultry streets, crept through the half-open sash. There were no boughs at their window to show it, no leaves to clap and answer, no orchard branches to make that soft, green applause she had grown up with. Yet she knew—she could not help knowing—that somewhere beyond the brick and cinder, by a hedge or in a copse, there would be the old country music: a rustle like skirts over grass, a murmuring as of bees in clover, a light rushing and falling—sounds that had once been part of the day’s bread, baked into it like the taste of wheat. The thought of that lost sound came back with an almost bodily comfort, an echo of gladness in a heart that had been strained too hard.

She sat in her mother’s room by the shaded window, her needle moving through linen with the patient precision of habit. The work had the plain, domestic steadiness of it: draw the thread, set the seam, smooth it with the thumb; and with each small action she laid a kind of order upon herself. When her mother’s forenoon slumber should be over, she would help her to dress; after dinner she would go to see Bessy Higgins and take what she could—words, company, perhaps some little food that Bessy might fancy. She told herself, sharply, that she would banish all recollection of the Thorntons. There was no need to think of them until they stood before her in flesh and blood.

But the mind, like a kitchen fire that has been damped, smoulders on under the ash; the very effort not to feed it gives it air. From time to time the hot flush swept over her pallor, sudden as a sunbeam breaking through watered cloud and running across the sea. She bent closer to her stitching, as if fine thread could bind in unruly thought.

Dixon opened the door with a softness that was almost a rebuke to sound. She came in on tiptoe, as though the house were a sick-room from threshold to hearth.

“Mr. Thornton, Miss Margaret. He is in the drawing-room.”

Margaret’s hand stopped; the needle paused bright above the linen.

“Did he ask for me? Isn’t papa come in?”

“He asked for you, miss; and master is out.”

“Very well. I will come,” said Margaret, and made her voice quiet as she could.

Yet she lingered—strangely, involuntarily—as if her feet had forgotten their obedience. She drew one breath, then another, and with the second she went.

In the drawing-room Mr. Thornton stood by one of the windows, his back to the door, looking down into the street as though he watched some common passage of carts and men. In truth he was afraid of himself. The very stillness of the room, with its faint smell of the night’s closed air and yesterday’s extinguished fire, seemed to hold his heart-beat and throw it back at him. He could not forget the touch of her arms about his neck—the quick, impatient clasp that had been so unconsidered at the time and was now unbearable in remembrance. It had changed its nature in his mind, as wine turns in the cask; what was merely an act became a sign. Her clinging defence of him thrilled through him, melting every resolution, every proud self-command, as if his will were wax held to a flame.

He dreaded—yes, dreaded—that when she entered he should go forward without thought, arms opened in mute entreaty, asking her again to come and rest where she had rested yesterday, unheeded then, never unheeded again. His heart throbbed thick; his breath came short with the strain of what he had to say and of how it might be received. In one moment he saw her droop and flush and flutter into his arms as if to her natural home; in the next, he saw the passionate rejection that would wither up all future, and he refused to look fully at that image, as a man will not look over a cliff.

He was startled by the sense that he was no longer alone. He turned.

She had entered so gently that he had not heard her at all; the street noises—far wheels, a distant cry, the faint clang from some yard—had been more distinct to his inattentive ear than her slow movement in her soft muslin gown. She stood by the table and did not sit. Her eyelids were dropped half over her eyes; her lips were parted a little, showing the white line of her teeth. She breathed slowly and deeply; it was the only motion on her face. The fine-grained skin and oval cheek were wan to-day, the want of colour made more evident by the heavy shadow of her dark hair drawn down over her temples to conceal the mark of yesterday’s blow. Her head, for all its drooping eyes, was thrown a little back in the old proud attitude; her long arms hung motionless at her sides.

She looked like a prisoner falsely accused of a crime she loathed, too indignant to justify herself.

Mr. Thornton made two hasty steps forward, checked himself, and went instead to the door she had left open. He shut it with quiet firmness—as if sound itself must be kept from waking Mrs. Hale. Then he came back and stood opposite her, taking in, in one long moment, the impression of her beautiful presence before he dared to disturb it, perhaps to repel it, by what he had to say.

“Miss Hale,” he began, “I was very ungrateful yesterday—”

“You had nothing to be grateful for,” she answered, raising her eyes and looking full and straight at him. “You mean, I suppose, that you believe you ought to thank me for what I did.”

In spite of herself, in defiance of anger, thick blushes came over her face, burning up into her very eyes; yet her look did not fall.

“It was only a natural instinct. Any woman would have done just the same. We all feel the sanctity of our sex as a high privilege when we see danger. I ought rather”—she spoke hastily, as if to get the words out and have done—“to apologise to you, for having said thoughtless words which sent you down into the danger.”

“It was not your words; it was the truth they conveyed, pungently as it was expressed. But you shall not drive me off upon that, and so escape the expression of my deep gratitude, my—”

He checked himself as if he had been running downhill too fast. He would not speak in the rush of hot passion; he would weigh each word. He would—and his will, for an instant, was triumphant.

“I do not try to escape from anything,” she said. “I simply say that you owe me no gratitude; and I may add that any expression of it will be painful to me, because I do not feel that I deserve it. Still, if it will relieve you from even a fancied obligation, speak on.”

“I do not want to be relieved from any obligation,” he replied, goaded by her calmness. “Fancied or not fancied—I question not myself to know which—I choose to believe I owe my very life to you. Ay—smile, and think it an exaggeration if you will. I believe it, because it adds a value to that life to think—oh, Miss Hale!”

His voice sank, and with it came that tender intensity which is more commanding than loudness. She shivered before it, as if the air had changed.

“To think circumstances so wrought that whenever I exult in existence henceforward, I may say to myself, ‘All this gladness in life, all honest pride in doing my work in the world, all this keen sense of being, I owe to her!’ And it doubles the gladness, it makes the pride glow, it sharpens the sense of existence till I hardly know if it is pain or pleasure to think that I owe it to one—nay, you must, you shall hear—” He stepped forward with stern determination. “To one whom I love, as I do not believe man ever loved woman before.”

He had taken her hand; his grasp closed on it hard, as if he held not merely fingers but fate. He panted, listening for what should come.

When her answer came, his indignation flung her hand away as if it burned him. Her tone was icy—icy, though the words faltered, as if she scarcely knew where to find them.

“Your way of speaking shocks me. It is blasphemous. I cannot help it if that is my first feeling. It might not be so, I dare say, if I understood the kind of feeling you describe. I do not want to vex you; and besides, we must speak gently, for mamma is asleep; but your whole manner offends me—”

“How!” he cried. “Offends you! I am indeed most unfortunate.”

“Yes!” She drew herself up; dignity came back like a clasp fastened. “I do feel offended; and I think justly. You seem to fancy that my conduct of yesterday”—again the deep carnation rushed up, but now with eyes kindling more with indignation than with shame—“was a personal act between you and me; and that you may come and thank me for it, instead of perceiving, as a gentleman would—yes! a gentleman,” she repeated, with a keen remembrance of former talk on that very word, “that any woman, worthy of the name of woman, would come forward to shield, with her reverenced helplessness, a man in danger from the violence of numbers.”

“And the gentleman thus rescued is forbidden the relief of thanks!” he broke in, contempt sharp on his tongue. “I am a man. I claim the right of expressing my feelings.”

“And I yielded to the right,” she replied, proudly. “Simply saying that you gave me pain by insisting upon it.”

“But you seem to have imagined,” she went on, and here the long-held tears surged up in spite of struggle, choking her voice, “that I was not merely guided by womanly instinct, but—that I was prompted by some particular feeling for you—you! Why, there was not a man—not a poor desperate man in all that crowd—for whom I had not more sympathy—for whom I should not have done what little I could more heartily.”

“You may speak on, Miss Hale. I am aware of these misplaced sympathies of yours. I now believe it was only your innate sense of oppression—(yes; I, though a master, may be oppressed)—that made you act so nobly as you did. I know you despise me; allow me to say it is because you do not understand me.”

“I do not care to understand,” she said, and caught the edge of the table, for she thought him cruel—and she was weak with indignation.

“No, I see you do not. You are unfair and unjust.”

Margaret compressed her lips. She would not give him the satisfaction of an answer to such accusation. She stood very still, and in that stillness her wounded pride broke its bounds; tears fell, hot and fast, like scalding drops that one cannot help but feel.

For all his savage words, for all the bitterness he had flung out like iron filings, he could have cast himself at her feet and kissed the hem of her gown. He waited, craving her speech—any speech, even a taunt he might answer. But she was silent.

At last he took up his hat. He held it a moment, smoothing the nap with his coat-sleeve as if to occupy his hands, to keep them from betraying him.

“One word more. You look as if you thought it tainted you to be loved by me. You cannot avoid it. Nay, I, if I would, cannot cleanse you from it. But I would not if I could. I have never loved any woman before. My life has been too busy; my thoughts too much absorbed with other things. Now I love, and will love. But do not be afraid of too much expression on my part.”

“I am not afraid,” she answered, lifting herself straight up, as if to show she would not be bent by fear. “No one yet has ever dared to be impertinent to me, and no one ever shall. But, Mr. Thornton”—and here she changed her whole tone and bearing to a womanly softness, thinking of her father, of kindness received and owed—“you have been very kind to my father. Don’t let us go on making each other angry. Pray don’t!”

He took no notice. He looked down, busy with the hat as though it were of vast importance; and when she held out her hand, grave with regret, he rejected it as if he did not see. He turned abruptly and left the room.

She caught one glance of his face as he went. She thought—only for an instant, as a swallow flashes by—that she saw the gleam of washed tears in his eyes. And that sight changed her proud dislike into something different and kinder, though it was nearly as painful: self-reproach, sharp as salt on a cut, for having caused such mortification to any one.

“But how could I help it?” she asked herself, alone with the hush again, the house’s careful silence settling down as if nothing had happened. “I never liked him. I was civil; but I took no trouble to conceal my indifference. Indeed, I never thought about myself or him, so my manners must have shown the truth. All that yesterday, he might mistake. But that is his fault, not mine. I would do it again, if need were, though it does lead me into all this shame and trouble.”
