When Romola returned home with Tessa and the children, late April was drawing to a close. Yet another weight pressed upon her heart, heavier than before: the publication of Fra Girolamo's trial. More accurately, it was the release of the confessions extracted from him by the sixteen Florentine citizens commissioned to interrogate him. Issued by the Signoria, this document had ignited a storm of public suspicion and outrage so fierce that immediate orders were given to recall it. Copies, however, had slipped away; a second edition, unauthorized but eagerly circulated, found its way into the hands of the populace.

Despairing of ever seeing Fra Girolamo again, Romola read the evidence repeatedly, seeking clarity amidst the conflicting narratives swirling through the city. Among Savonarola's most devoted followers, the friar's lack of constancy under torture and his retraction of prophetic claims caused a profound consternation. Soon, this fear hardened into certainty: any words contradicting their faith were deemed fabrications inserted by Ser Ceccone, the disgraced notary who compiled the transcript.

Yet, glaring inconsistencies undermined the official record. Half the panel of sixteen examiners consisted of the prophet's fiercest enemies. Notorious Dolfo Spini sat among the newly elected "Eight," seemingly appointed solely to ensure a verdict against a man whose destruction had already been decided by the ruling faction. To many, the proceedings in the Old Palace resembled a slow, formalized murder. The Signoria aimed to strike a deal with the Pope and the Duke of Milan by eliminating a threat to both the corrupt clergy and greedy foreign powers. The Frate was doomed; the debate was merely whether Florence would surrender him alive to the Papacy or, in exchange for taxing rights on church property, execute him publicly on their own piazza. Under such circumstances, who could trust the so-called confession?

If the Frate had denied his gift, it was likely wrung from him by the agony of torture—a pain that, in his sensitive constitution, would surely induce delirium. What if the examiners claimed he suffered only three times over one day, confessing freely thereafter? Could anyone believe that? Romola knew better. He had been tormented far more intensely, proportional to the distress his words caused those who loved him.

Other friends of Savonarola, less ardent in their devotion, accepted the confession's substance despite the notary's manipulations. They argued indignantly that no crime warranted death or even severe punishment. To impartial observers, the evidence suggested the Frate died not for sin, but because he had become inconvenient to the Pope, to rapacious states eyeing Tuscany, and to ambitious citizens undermining the common good. No political crime was proven; no stain marred his private conduct. Fellow monks, including a former secretary renowned for his critical intellect, testified to his unimpeachable purity, even after the shock of his retraction. The Pope himself could charge him only with disobedience to mandates and ignoring excommunication. While difficult to justify legally, many felt a moral uprising against the Roman Court, blurring the line between the institution of the Church and its flawed members. Ordinary citizens sensed that the victory of the Frate's enemies was truly the triumph of gross license.

Shrewd Florentines like Soderini and Piero Guicciardini wore an angry smile at the government's clumsy pretense of judicial fairness for a predetermined act of policy. They noted that if the Frate had mixed deeper frauds with zeal less threatening to powerful figures, his deceit might have been hailed as essential lubricant for the state machine. Still, they admitted the Pope's actions were acts of self-defense. A rebel who sought to rally Europe to depose the Pontiff posed an existential threat. Florence had heard him declare he would not obey the devil; thus, a life-and-death struggle was inevitable. Yet, it was less certain that Florence should serve as the Pope's executioner.

Romola's mind was torn between the fervent faith wounded by betrayal and the sober judgment of worldly prudence. Neither offered satisfaction. Her long meditation over the printed document revealed painful truths gathered during her years of discipleship: Savonarola's retraction was not merely a spasmodic attempt to survive torture. Conversely, her soul desperately needed an explanation that preserved the purity of his life's mission. The bitterness of losing her trust in an embodiment of high motive had sparked a strange resilience, a faith rising from despair.

She concluded that the negative thoughts plaguing her soul could not reflect reality. The spirit that had once breathed through the Frate's words was real, not a hollow pretence. Any falsehood in him was a tragic fall, a gradual entanglement he struggled against, not a calculated scheme nurtured by success. Examining the text, she spotted sentences bearing the mark of clumsy fabrication—the emphatic, repetitive self-accusation typical of low hypocrisy. Yet, these contradictions stood in stark opposition to Savonarola's character and the overall tone of the document, suggesting the remainder reflected his actual words.

Hardly anything was dishonorable except regarding his prophetic claims. He remained consistent in stating his goals for Florence, the Church, and the world. Apart from the false claim of special inspiration used to sway minds, he admitted no unworthy means. Even here, stripped of the notary's malice, Fra Girolamo appeared as a man seeking glory, but through laboring for humanity's moral welfare, transforming beliefs into active forces in daily life.

One memorable passage, though possibly altered, resonated deeply: *"Everything that I have done... I have done with the design of being for ever famous... to win credit in Florence... to establish my position... to do great things in Italy and beyond... to rouse the princes of Christendom... to subdue the infidels. It was not much in my thoughts to get myself made a Cardinal or Pope... to be the head of that work was a greater thing than to be Pope, because a man without virtue may be Pope; but such a work as I contemplated demanded a man of excellent virtues."*

This blend of ambition and belief in supreme goodness was familiar to Romola, echoing the voice that once rang through the Duomo. Savonarola always conceived great tasks and believed himself capable of achieving them. Justice, purity, and love must triumph, ideally through his voice and work. In moments of ecstasy, doubtless, his sense of self dissolved into the Divine, fostering genuine humility. But toward his fellow-men, pre-eminence seemed a necessity. Perhaps this confession, acknowledging a conscious doubleness, simply reflected the wavering of belief common to humans facing drastic shifts—from adoration to hatred, from triumphant progress to the hooting crowds, the hard-faced judges, and the horrific torture.

As Romola imagined the anguish following the confession—the solitude of the prison, the battle between conscience and coerced words—it pressed upon her own heart, drawing slow, bitter tears. Vulgar voices in Florence glibly condemned him, unaware of the depth of sorrow he knew, reserved for souls that have loved perfection and beheld themselves fallen. She later witnessed the evidence of his mental state after laying his mouth in the dust.

Days passed until reports of new examinations ceased. Left alone in his cell, Savonarola was permitted pen and ink. He wrote, but not to defend his innocence or protest his treatment. His writings became a dialogue with divine purity, an outpouring of self-abasement, a continuous cry for inner renewal. The old, vehement assertion—"Look at my work, for it is good!"—had vanished. Now, the Voice of Sadness spoke: *"God placed thee in the midst of the people... Thou hast cured others: and thou thyself hast been still diseased. Thy heart was lifted up at the beauty of thy own deeds... thou art fallen into the depths of the sea."* Hope, too, softened its tone, offering not promises of future works, but comfort in his current penitence: *"Thou art not forsaken, else why is thy heart bowed?"*

There is no evidence that from imprisonment to his final hour, Savonarola viewed himself as a martyr. Martyrdom had been a dream of future triumph, a passion dividing his hope. Now, replaced by both, came a resignation he named with no glorifying title. *But therefore he may the more fitly be called a martyr by his fellow-men to all time.* Power rose against him not due to sin, but because of his greatness; not because he sought to deceive, but because he sought to elevate the world. Through that greatness, he endured a double agony: the reviling, the torture, the death-throes, and the crushing pain of sinking from a vision of glorious achievement into a deep shadow where he could only whisper, *"I count as nothing: darkness encompasses me: yet the light I saw was the true light."*
