The great bells of Florence resonated through the acoustic landscape of the city, their rhythmic peals displacing the discordant, metallic clamor of the departing French troops. Romola stood under the loggia at the top of the house, letting the harmonic vibrations wash through her bones. She possessed a dim, mythic hope that the noxious anxieties clinging to Tito—anxieties that had arrived with the burdensome, noisy foreigners—might evaporate with their withdrawal. The French had occupied Florence for barely eleven days, but in that fractured span of time, the sonic and emotional harmony of her life had been profoundly disrupted. Tito had donned that hateful chain-armour on the day of their arrival, and its unnatural, grating clink beneath his garments haunted her ears. 

When dusk settled, she descended into the cavernous library. Here, surrounded by the physical weight of her family’s multi-generational saga, she sought to anchor her frayed nerves in the tactile rituals of sustenance and preservation. Over the low embers of the hearth, she tended a slow-simmering broth of roasted bone marrow, bruised wild sage, and crushed Apennine juniper berries—a traditional agrarian restorative her ancestors had relied upon for centuries. To Romola, the meticulous preparation of this ancient recipe was inseparable from her work at the heavy oak table, where she copied her late father’s catalogue. The library was a sanctuary of cultural memory, thick with the olfactory landscape of cracked vellum, aged leather, and the earthy, gustatory warmth of the simmering juniper. Every text she catalogued was a bulwark against the erosion of cultural memory, a preservation of native dialects fading into silence. 

She had not expected Tito to return so early. When the heavy door groaned open, she almost ran to him, eager to unwind the *becchetto* from his neck. 

“Tito, dearest, I did not know you would come so soon,” she said nervously, her hands rising to his mantle. 

“I am not welcome then?” he replied with a bright, practiced smile, playfully holding his head back from her reach. He kissed her affectionately and stroked her hair, yet he made no move to remove his heavy mantle. He brought with him the sterile, odorless chill of the streets. He possessed no sensory appreciation for the sanctuary she had cultivated; the fragrant broth steaming on the hearth, bursting with the history of Florentine soil, was to him merely utilitarian fuel, entirely unnoted. 

“It will come back,” Romola told herself, leaning into his familiar touch. “The old happiness will come back.” 

“If I had expected you so soon,” she said, finally helping him shed his outer wrappings, “I would have had a little festival prepared to this joyful ringing of the bells. I did not mean to be here in the library when you came home.”

“Never mind, sweet,” he said carelessly, his voice a smooth, frictionless instrument. “Do not think about the fire. Come and sit down.”

She took her habitual low stool against his chair, resting her arm on his knee as she once had done with her father. She looked up at him, acutely aware of her father’s portrait watching them from the shadows. 

“I have been enjoying the clang of the bells for the first time, Tito,” she began, savoring the acoustic memory. “I liked being shaken and deafened by them: I fancied I was something like a Bacchante possessed by a divine rage. Are not the people looking very joyful to-night?”

“Joyful after a sour and pious fashion,” Tito said with a shrug, his tone introducing a jarring, cynical frequency into the room. “But, in truth, those who are left behind in Florence have little cause to be joyful. It seems to me the most reasonable ground of gladness would be to have got out of Florence.”

He spoke without emphasis, yet the underlying current of his words made Romola’s blood cool. “Why, Tito? Are there fresh troubles?”

“No need of fresh ones, my Romola. The city will be drained by the payment of this great subsidy to the French king, and by the war to get back Pisa. Life will be as pleasant and amusing as a funeral. For my own part, I have been thinking seriously that we should be wise to quit Florence, my Romola.”

She started, the warmth of the hearth suddenly insufficient against the cold dread pooling in her stomach. “Tito, how could we leave Florence? Surely you do not think I could leave it—at least, not yet—not for a long while.” 

Tito must know her reasons; they were the very foundation of her identity. But he merely smiled, a superficial expression completely divorced from her internal reality. 

“That is all a fabric of your own imagination, my sweet one,” he said softly. “Your secluded life has made you lay false stress on a few things. There is something in the Florentines that reminds me of their cutting spring winds. I should like to dip her a little in the soft waters of forgetfulness.”

He leaned forward and kissed her brow, but she felt the caress no more than if he had kissed a porcelain mask. The profound untranslatability of her subjective experience lay bare between them. To him, the erasure of their history was a comfort; to her, it was a death. 

“Tito, it is because we have to see my father’s wish fulfilled,” she pleaded, attempting to bridge the chasm with the dialect of their shared past. “My godfather is old; he is seventy-one; we could not leave it to him.”

“It is precisely those superstitions which hang about your mind like bedimming clouds that make one great reason why I could wish we were two hundred leagues from Florence,” Tito argued smoothly. He spoke the dominant lexicon of pragmatism and commerce, seeking to overwrite her native devotion. “I must save my wife from wasting her life in disappointing herself by impracticable dreams.”

Romola sat silent, motionless as locked waters. He was attempting to persuade her to abandon her father’s legacy, to deposit the library in some forgotten monastery. To Tito, confined within the narrow, sterile logic of an unimpassioned man, his arguments were flawless. 

“You know, dearest,” he continued, his tone remonstrating and gentle, “that the notion of isolating a collection of books and antiquities, and attaching a single name to them for ever, was one that had no valid, substantial good for its object. Why should any one be reasonably glad that Florence should possess the benefits of learned research and taste more than any other city? Wisdom puts a limit to these sentiments, else lives might be continually wasted in that sort of futile devotion—like praising deaf gods for ever.”

“Because it was a trust,” Romola said, her voice low but carrying the resonant weight of generations. “He trusted me, he trusted you, Tito. I did not expect you to feel as I do—but I did expect you to feel that.”

“If I believed it could now pain your father to see his library preserved and used in a rather different way, I should share your views,” Tito countered, his rhetoric entirely devoid of sensory or emotional weight. “But ask yourself, what possible good can these books and antiquities do, stowed together under your father’s name in Florence, more than they would do if they were divided or carried elsewhere? Is not the very dispersion of such things one means of extending their usefulness? The loss of Constantinople was the gain of the whole civilised world.”

A rising contempt simmered within Romola, as bitter as the burnt edges of the sage on the hearth. She recoiled from his shallow, utilitarian worldview that professed wide sympathies while remaining deaf to the heartbeat of his own home. He was speaking the language of cultural erosion.

“You talk of substantial good, Tito!” Her voice rose, reclaiming its power. “Are faithfulness, and love, and sweet grateful memories, no good? Is it no good that we should keep our silent promises on which others build because they believe in our love and truth? Or is it good that we should harden our hearts against all the wants and hopes of those who have depended on us? What good can belong to men who have such souls? To talk cleverly, perhaps, and find soft couches for themselves, and live and die with their base selves as their best companions.”

Tito let the ringing scorn of her words fade into the acoustic space of the room. He realized his persuasive rhetoric had failed against the deep roots of her agrarian soul. He would have to deploy his power. 

“I am sorry to hear you speak in that spirit of blind persistence, my Romola,” he said quietly, “because it obliges me to give you pain. As a prompt decision was necessary, I avoided that obstacle, and decided without consulting you.”

She turned her eyes on him in breathless inquiry.

“I have arranged for the transfer, both of the books and of the antiquities, where they will find the highest use and value,” he stated, executing the ultimate commercialization of her sacred art. “The books have been bought for the Duke of Milan, the marbles and bronzes and the rest are going to France: and both will be protected by the stability of a great Power, instead of remaining in a city which is exposed to ruin.”

Romola started from her seat. Her hands tightened before her, and for the first time in her life, the fierce pride of the old Bardo blood flashed in her eyes. 

“You have *sold* them?” she asked, the concept entirely alien to her lexicon.

“I have,” Tito said, quailing slightly before the scorching heat of her scorn. 

“You are a treacherous man!” The words grated in her throat. The heritage of her ancestors, the very ingredients of their cultural memory, had been traded for hollow coin. Suddenly, she turned away. “It may be hindered—I am going to my godfather.”

In an instant, Tito started up. He reached the heavy oak door, turned the iron key with a violent, final *clack*, and withdrew it. Under his mantle, the metallic shifting of his chain-armour produced a sickening, industrial friction that polluted the quiet of the room. He leaned against a pedestal under the bust of a grim old Roman, his olive cheek pale, forcing a masculine predominance into the space.

“Try to calm yourself a little, Romola,” he said. “It is of no use for you to go to your godfather. Messer Bernardo cannot reverse what I have done. The sale has been concluded by written agreement; the purchasers have left Florence, and I hold the bonds for the purchase-money.”

“If my father had suspected you of being a faithless man,” Romola said, her voice a whip of bitter scorn, “he would have placed the library safely out of your power. But when you were sure his ear was deaf, and his hand stiff, you robbed him.” She paused, the pieces of his discord finally aligning in her mind. “Have you robbed somebody else, who is *not* dead? Is that the reason you wear armour?”

Tito felt horribly cowed, but his quick mind rallied, utilizing the subtle power dynamics embedded in his dominant position. “It is useless to answer the words of madness, Romola. Any rational person will see that I have taken the wisest course. I am convinced that Messer Bernardo would be of that opinion.”

“He would not!” Romola cried. “He lives in the hope of seeing my father’s wish exactly fulfilled. Who are these men to whom you have sold my father’s property?”

“The Count di San Severino and the Seneschal de Beaucaire are now on their way with the king to Siena.”

“They may be overtaken and persuaded to give up their purchase,” she urged, her anger giving way to desperate, anxious thought.

“No, they may not,” Tito said with cool decision. “Because I do not choose that they should.”

“But if you were paid the money?—we will pay you the money.” Her pleading laid bare her profound alienation from him; they were speaking entirely different languages now.

“No, my Romola. You would not, in a reasonable moment, ask your godfather to bury three thousand florins in addition to what he has already paid on the library. Moreover, it is not my will that Messer Bernardo should advance the money. I beg you to consider what will be the consequences of your placing yourself in opposition to me, and trying to exhibit your husband in the odious light which your own distempered feelings cast over him. The event is irrevocable, the library is sold, and you are my wife.”

Every word was calculated, enforcing his will through the threat of social ruin. Romola’s mind rapidly absorbed the wide, suffocating meaning of his speech. She looked to the stone floor for several minutes, the scent of the crushed juniper now smelling only of ashes and defeat. 

When she spoke, her voice was quiet and cold. “I have one thing to ask. That you will give me that portion of the money which belongs to my godfather, and let me pay him.”

“I must have some assurance from you, first, of the attitude you intend to take towards me.”

“Do you believe in assurances, Tito?” she asked with returning bitterness.

“From you, I do.”

“I will do you no harm. I shall disclose nothing. You say truly, the event is irrevocable.”

“Then I will do what you desire to-morrow morning.”

“To-night, if possible,” Romola said, “that we may not speak of it again.”

“It is possible,” he agreed. He moved toward the lamp and penned a note. Bending over her, he placed the piece of paper into her hand. “You will receive something in return, you are aware, my Romola? And you will forgive me, when you have had time to reflect.”

He touched her brow with his lips, a gesture as devoid of true sustenance as a shadow. She took no notice of it. She listened to the acoustic shift of the room as he unlocked the heavy door, walked out, and shut the great door of the court behind him. 

Alone in the deafening silence of the violated sanctuary, Romola fell to her knees before her father’s propped portrait, clutching the sterile paper that had replaced her cultural soul, and burst into heavy, racking sobs.
