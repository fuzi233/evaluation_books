Three weeks had passed since Tito handed over his florins to Cennini, and though he now stood upon the threshold of the Via de' Bardi, he bore no trace of anxiety. His spirit, buoyant and unburdened by the weight of future secrets, flowed effortlessly with the rhythm of his surroundings. There was no shadow of duplicity upon his brow, nor any stain of undue candour; it was simply the smooth, square forehead of a young man fully formed, reflecting a gaze as clear as polished glass. As he emerged from the blazing noonday sun into the cool, narrow alleys, brushing back his brown curls beneath his black *berretta*, his expression remained one of serene neutrality. To the casual observer, he appeared merely as a cipher capable of infinite interpretation, yet his presence commanded a silent, willing tribute of admiration from the throngs of holiday-makers.

The fervor of the Feast of St. John had spilled even into the labyrinthine side streets. Where the grand procession had once choked the avenues, crowds now swirled outward, driven by a restless, vaguely animal energy prone to sacrificing the solitary individual for the amusement of the many. As Tito ventured toward the neighborhood of San Martino, he encountered a denser knot of people gathered near the inn of the *Bertucce*. Amidst a chorus of raucous laughter, a high-pitched, distressed cry pierced the air: "Loose me! Holy Virgin, help me!"

Curiosity drew Tito into the circle. At the center stood a young country girl, her white hood tangled and discarded, struggling frantically against the grip of a *cerretano*—a conjuror dressed in motley hues. The magician, a man with a face etched by upward-turning angles and a perpetual, impish grin, attempted to soothe her with jokes, while the surrounding spectators jeered, calling her a simpleton. For a fleeting moment, Tito saw only the back of her head, adorned with light-brown plaits secured by a silver pin. But as the struggle intensified, the girl's face turned toward him. It was the baby features of Tessa, her blue eyes swollen with tears, her lower lip trembling violently. Through the mist of her distress, a sudden spark of hope ignited in her gaze—the recognition of a familiar hand stretching out from the chaos.

Seizing the moment, Tito surged through the barrier of onlookers. "Loose this child!" he cried, his voice cutting through the laughter. "What right have you to hold her against her will?"

The conjuror released his grip instantly, shrinking back with a theatrical shrug and a half-apologetic bow. "I mean the *ragazza* no evil, Messere! Ask this respectable company. I was merely demonstrating a trick, aided by her kitten-like face, and promised her a handful of confetti. But surely, Messere, you possess finer sweets at hand?"

A ripple of laughter ensued, fueled by the look of profound relief in Tessa's eyes as she clung tightly to Tito's arm. To her, the mockery of the crowd sounded like the roar of wild beasts from which she had escaped; she attached no meaning to their jests. Tito, however, felt a pang of foreboding. Having rescued her, he now faced the awkward task of extricating himself from a situation where the deprived spectators might demand further entertainment.

"See, see, little one! Here is your hood," the conjuror chimed, flinging the white drapery over Tessa's head. "*Orsù*, bear me no malice; return when Messere can spare you."

"Aye, Maestro Vaiano, she'll come back presently, as the toad said to the harrow!" shouted a spectator.

Vexed by this delay, Tito propelled himself toward a side street, intending to shed his charge quickly. Yet, as they entered the alley, the crowd's attention shifted to the unusual pair, and he found himself compelled to address her gently. "Now, little one, where were you going? Did you come alone to the Festa?"

"No!" Tessa whispered, her fear resurfacing. "I lost my mother... and my father-in-law. They will be angry—he will beat me. Somebody pushed me in the crowd, and I couldn't stop. Oh, I don't know where they're gone! Please, don't leave me!" Her sobbing grew louder, the corners of her mouth drooping pitifully, her small chest heaving beneath the green serge gown.

Tito's heart softened instantly. Leaving her would have been brutality, and his nature abhorred cruelty. "My poor little Tessa, cease your weeping," he cooed, placing a hand on her apron to wipe away her tears and kissing her round cheek. "Let us see what can be done. Where is your home?"

Silence answered him, save for the diminishing sobs. "Come," he urged, "tell me where you wish to go, and I will escort you."

"I think I'll go home, if you'll take me," she murmured, her wide blue eyes fixed on him with a childlike, trusting calm. "Beyond Peretola, where the large pear-tree stands."

"And which gate, *pazzarella*?" Tito asked, feigning ignorance of the layout. "I am a stranger."

"The Por del Prato," she replied, gripping his arm with renewed vigor.

Undeterred by the complexity of the route, Tito steered them straight for Porta Rossa and onward to Ognissanti. The streets teemed with the festival's excess: frolicsome apprentices, bold-eyed women veiled in yellow, beggars, sharpers, and rough-hewn boys who delighted in mocking the solemn. Tessa's terror of being lost was not entirely misplaced in such a chaotic, morally ambiguous world.

At the Piazza d'Ognissanti, the heat of their walk demanded a respite. They sat upon a stone bench lining the ancient walls. "Holy Virgin!" Tessa breathed. "I am glad we have fled those women and boys. I was not frightened, because you protect me."

"You are safe with me, little Tessa," Tito smiled. "Do you feel secure?"

"Yes, because you are so beautiful—like those entering Paradise. They are all good."

Seeing the hollowness in her stomach, Tito noticed nearby stalls laden with fruit and confections. "Hungry?" he asked.

"Yes," she admitted. "If you will share."

Tito purchased apricots, cakes, and comfits, filling her apron. "Let us walk to the Prato," he suggested. "Perhaps then you need not fear the remainder of the journey."

"But you will have some of the treats," Tessa protested, gathering her apron like a bag.

"We shall see," Tito declared aloud, though inwardly he mused on the irony of his predicament. A peasant girl like Tessa inspired a pastoral idyl far superior to Lorenzo de' Medici's *Nencia da Barberino*, yet he lacked the leisure of a Theocritus. "Mischief is done," he sighed. "Another half-hour makes no difference."

As they strolled, Tessa revealed her domestic woes: a cross father-in-law, a mother who favored her younger sister, and a parish priest who was the only one to treat her kindly. "Nobody speaks nicely to me," she confessed, comparing her treatment to that of her "little black-faced kid." Unaware of the transformation in Tito's station, she viewed him not as a lord, but as a benevolent figure descended from Paradise, a voice of comfort in a harsh world.

They eventually reached the Prato, a vast, empty meadow where the Florentines practiced *Calcio*. Near the gates, where tents stood prepared for races, Tito paused. "Tessa, I must leave you to finish the path alone. *Addio!* Tomorrow, shall I purchase a cup of milk from you in the Mercato to ensure your safety?"

The suggestion caused her eyes to widen with sorrow, her mouth to drop. "I thought you would have some of the apricots," she complained, "and we could sit under a tree outside the gate and eat them together."

"Tessa, you little siren, you would ruin me!" Tito laughed, kissing both her cheeks. "I must return. You are in no danger. Take an apricot, *addio*!"

Before he could take two steps, he halted. Turning back, he saw her shoulders rising with a stifled sob, and the apricots rolling onto the grass. His conscience, quick to recoil from any scene of distress, compelled him to return. "Silly one," he said, retrieving the fruit. "We will sit under the tree. I cannot bear to see you weep, though I must depart."

Thus, they found refuge beneath a great plane-tree just beyond the gates. Tessa leaned against the trunk, her lap a mound of treats, while Tito stretched opposite, resting his elbows on the rough bark. Sunlight filtered through the leaves, dancing upon them like a winged spirit. Tessa's face regained its serenity, the flavors of the fruit seeming exquisite.

"What sins do you confess, Tessa?" Tito asked, gazing at her as she eyed the remaining sweets with hesitation.
