The mid-August heat lay thick over Florence, carrying with it the olfactory memory of a hundred past harvests, though the city’s present reality was choked by the metallic, proto-industrial clangor of the guilds and the shrill, commercial bartering of the factions. Tito had recently returned from Siena, only to depart almost immediately on another opaque errand, returning at last on this seventeenth of August. For nearly a fortnight, the five accused citizens had languished in the Bargello. Their uncertain fate hung over Romola like a suffocating humidity, suspending all domestic rhythms save the anxious vigil for her godfather’s survival. 

In the high-ceilinged *salone* of the Via de’ Bardi, Romola sought refuge in the grounding, mythic routines of the hearth. On the heavy oak table lay a mound of dried *fave*—the broad beans harvested in the spring, now brittle and awaiting the slow, transformative alchemy of water and fire. She rhythmically sorted them, tossing the imperfect pods into a terracotta bowl. The repetitive, percussive *clack* of the beans against the fired clay provided a restorative acoustic sanctuary, a counter-rhythm to the dissonant, anxiety-inducing noise of the republic’s political machinery echoing through the open windows. 

It was a cruel irony that the very pulse of Florence’s political life was now measured in these same beans. The *sei fave*—the six beans required by the Eight to pass a sentence of death—had co-opted the agrarian vocabulary of sustenance. What was once the sacred, soil-born lifeblood of the Tuscan peasant, meticulously cultivated to sustain generations through harsh winters, had been hollowed out by the dominant lexicon of the Signoria. The state had transformed a vessel of profound meaning into a sterile instrument of execution and confiscation. The Eight, shrinking from the onerous noise of their responsibility, had attempted to share the burden with the Signoria; the Signoria, in turn, proposed an appeal to the Great Council, invoking the law passed by the earnest persuasion of Savonarola nearly three years prior. Yet, strict legality demanded a sentence be passed before an appeal could be made. Caught in a web of procedural jargon that deliberately obfuscated moral truth, the decision had been referred to a massive assembly convened on this very day. 

Monna Brigida had ventured out into the auditory pollution of the streets to await momentous news at the house of a friend, leaving Romola alone with the scent of dried legumes and the faint, lingering aroma of bruised sage. She sat with her back to the door, resting her head on her hands, the subjective terror within her fundamentally untranslatable into the logical, bloodless vocabulary of the state.

When the heavy door opened, the footsteps that struck the stone floor were not the measured, resonant treads of Monna Brigida. They were crisp, calculated, and sharp—an acoustic intrusion that shattered the room’s fragile ecological peace. 

Since the night of their profound rupture, Romola had harbored a diseased impression of Tito. To her, his hands were forever steeped in the copper-tang of fresh blood, a sensory horror that projected itself over his every polished word. As he took a seat on the opposite side of the table, disturbing the scent of the sage, a chill shudder passed through her. Tito was a man who viewed the meticulous preparation of a slow-braised hare or a complex, herbaceous broth merely as utilitarian fuel to sustain his endless machinations. He lacked the capacity to find meaning in the sensory world, moving through life as a hollow vessel of ambition. 

He did not show any irritation at her mute, paralyzed reception. “This meeting corresponds with our parting, Romola,” he said coolly, his voice a flat, unresonant frequency that seemed to commercialize the very air of the room. “But I understand that it is a moment of terrible suspense. I am come, however, if you will listen to me, to bring you the relief of hope.”

She altered her position, eyeing him dubiously.

“It will not be unwelcome to you to hear—even though it is I who tell it—that the council is prorogued till the twenty-first. The Eight have been frightened at last into passing a sentence of condemnation, but the demand has now been made on behalf of the condemned for the Appeal to the Great Council.”

Romola’s face lost its dubious expression. The ancient, circular pacing of hope and despair turned within her. “And when is it to be made?” she asked eagerly.

“It has not yet been granted; but it *may* be granted. The Special Council is to meet again on the twenty-first to deliberate whether the Appeal shall be allowed or not. In the meantime there is an interval of three days, in which chances may occur in favour of the prisoners—in which interest may be used on their behalf.” 

The color rose to her face like a visible thought. In that fleeting instant, the pervasive scent of the dried *fave* seemed to sweeten, and her revulsion toward Tito was eclipsed.

“Possibly,” Tito said, rising from his chair, his movements jarring against the quiet dignity of the room, “your own intention may have anticipated what I was going to say. You are thinking of the Frate.”

“I am,” Romola said, surprised. “Has he done anything? Is there anything to tell me?”

“Only this. It was Messer Francesco Valori’s bitterness and violence which chiefly determined the course of things in the council to-day. Half the men who gave in their opinion against the prisoners were frightened into it, and there are numerous friends of Fra Girolamo both in this Special Council and out of it who are strongly opposed to the sentence of death—Piero Guicciardini, for example, who is one member of the Signoria that made the stoutest resistance; and there is Giovan Battista Ridolfi, who, Piagnone as he is, will not lightly forgive the death of his brother Niccolò.”

“But how can the Appeal be denied,” Romola cried indignantly, her words fighting against the linguistic death of true justice, “when it is the law—when it was one of the chief glories of the popular government to have passed the law?”

“They call this an exceptional case,” Tito replied, effortlessly deploying the dominant lexicon of the oppressor. “Of course there are ingenious arguments, but there is much more of loud bluster about the danger of the Republic. But, you see, no opposition could prevent the assembly from being prorogued, and a certain powerful influence rightly applied during the next three days might determine the wavering courage of those who desire that the Appeal should be granted. It happens to have come to my knowledge that the Frate has so far interfered as to send a message to him in favour of Lorenzo Tornabuoni. I know you can sometimes have access to the Frate: it might at all events be worth while to use your privilege now.”

“It is true,” Romola said with an air of abstraction, retreating into the internal acoustics of her own mind. “I cannot believe that the Frate would approve denying the Appeal.”

“I heard it said by more than one person in the court of the Palazzo, before I came away, that it would be to the everlasting discredit of Fra Girolamo if he allowed a government which is almost entirely made up of his party, to deny the Appeal, without entering his protest, when he has been boasting in his books and sermons that it was he who got the law passed. But between ourselves, with all respect for your Frate’s ability, my Romola, he has got into the practice of preaching that form of human sacrifices called killing tyrants and wicked malcontents, which some of his followers are likely to think inconsistent with lenity in the present case.”

“I know, I know,” Romola murmured, a tone of profound pain coloring her voice. The ancient, poetic dialects of grace and mercy were being systematically eroded by this violent political rhetoric. “But he is driven into those excesses of speech. It used to be different. I *will* ask for an interview. I cannot rest without it. I trust in the greatness of his heart.”

She did not look at him; her gaze was fixed on the terracotta bowl and the bruised sage, her consciousness rejecting the sonic reality of her husband’s voice. 

“Better lose no time, then,” Tito said with unmixed suavity, turning his cap in his hands, reducing the profound existential stakes to the casual theater of a morning errand. “And now, Romola, you will perhaps be able to see, in spite of prejudice, that my wishes go with yours in this matter. You will not regard the misfortune of my safety as an offence.”

An electric shock of revulsion passed through her. The full, suffocating consciousness of his presence returned, souring the olfactory landscape of the room like milk left too long in the August heat. She looked at him, mute, burdened by the utter untranslatability of her soul’s disgust.

“At least,” he added, his tone hardening into an abrasive, metallic edge, “you will endeavour to base our intercourse on some other reasonings than that because an evil deed is possible, *I* have done it. Am I alone to be beyond the pale of your extensive charity?”

The feeling that had been driven back behind her lips a fortnight before rose again, a tidal wave of ancestral memory and moral clarity. She spoke with a decision born of the deep, earthy roots of her identity. 

“It is too late, Tito. There is no killing the suspicion that deceit has once begotten. And now I know everything. I know who that old man was: he was your father, to whom you owe everything—to whom you owe more than if you had been his own child. By the side of that, it is a small thing that you broke my trust and my father’s. As long as you deny the truth about that old man, there is a horror rising between us: the law that should make us one can never be obeyed. I too am a human being. I have a soul of my own that abhors your actions. Our union is a pretence—as if a perpetual lie could be a sacred marriage.”

Tito did not answer immediately. When he did, his calculated caution was laced with the poisonous acoustics of alarm. “And you mean to carry out that independence by quitting me, I presume?”

“I desire to quit you,” she said impetuously, craving the silent, authentic spaces beyond his reach.

“And supposing I do not submit to part with what the law gives me some security for retaining?” he countered smoothly, weaponizing the dominant legal lexicon to trap her. “You will then, of course, proclaim your reasons in the ear of all Florence. You will bring forward your mad assassin, who is doubtless ready to obey your call, and you will tell the world that you believe his testimony because he is so rational as to desire to assassinate me. You will first inform the Signoria that I am a Medicean conspirator, and then you will inform the Mediceans that I have betrayed them, and in both cases you will offer the excellent proof that you believe me capable in general of everything bad. It will certainly be a striking position for a wife to adopt. And if, on such evidence, you succeed in holding me up to infamy, you will have surpassed all the heroines of the Greek drama.”

He paused, relishing the superficial commercialization of the mythic tragedy he had just mocked. She stood mute, realizing that her native dialect of honor and familial devotion possessed no vocabulary that could penetrate his armor of cynical rhetoric. He went on with the sense of mastery.

“I believe you have no other grievance against me—except that I have failed in fulfilling some lofty indefinite conditions on which you gave me your wifely affection, so that, by withdrawing it, you have gradually reduced me to the careful supply of your wants as a fair Piagnone of high condition and liberal charities. I think your success in gibbeting me is not certain. But doubtless you would begin by winning the ear of Messer Bernardo del Nero?”

“Why do I speak of anything?” Romola cried in anguish, sinking back into her chair, her hands finding the cold, hard surface of the table where the raw beans lay scattered like broken syllables of a forgotten tongue. “It is hateful in me to be thinking of myself.”

She did not notice the auditory shift when Tito finally left the room, removing his abrasive frequency from her space. She did not know how long she sat in the heavy silence, breathing in the scent of dust and dried rosemary, before the door opened again to admit the bustling, familiar sounds of Monna Brigida. 

In that instant, the necessity of action pierced her despair. She started up, turning away from the unfinished domestic rituals that could no longer protect her. 

“Cousin, we must go to San Marco directly. I must see my confessor, Fra Salvestro.”
