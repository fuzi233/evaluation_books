To sit in wait for a letter that carries weight upon it is to allow the mind to shift from stillness into a restless current. Margaret, having sent her earnest missive, poured out the full reservoir of her spirit and her faith—whether in her father's dying vision or merely a desperate illusion. Once the ink was dry and Luke departed, a sudden chill settled over her. She marveled at her own gullibility, and her conscience, sharp as a needle, pricked her regarding Luke. Soon enough, Catherine arrived to scold her, and Margaret paid the heavy toll of shattered illusions and lifted spirits by sinking back into a profound despondency.

In this low state, she was found by a sturdy friend she had recently acquired, Joan Ketel. Joan entered radiating a bright idea.

"Margaret, I know the remedy for thy sickness!" Joan declared. "There is a hermit at Gouda, a wondrous holy man. When he is in the right frame of mind, he can tell thee what cometh."

"Ay, I have heard tales of him," Margaret murmured, hope draining from her eyes. Yet, with some difficulty, Joan persuaded her to walk out to Gouda to seek counsel. They carried a basket with butter and eggs and made their way to the hermitage.

How had these two women become such close companions? Six weeks prior, Jorian had fallen violently ill with a bowel malady. It began with raging agony; when that subsided, leaving him frail, a terrible symptom followed. Nothing, liquid or solid, could remain in his stomach for a moment. The physician, despairing, declared, "He must die if this continues; therefore, boil thou now a chicken with a golden angel floating in the water, and let him drink that!" Alas, the gilded broth shared the fate of the humblest fare. Next, the healer soaked the thumb of Saint Sergius in beef stock, yielding the same grim result. Desperate, Joan ran weeping to Margaret to borrow linen for a shroud.

"Let me see him," Margaret implored. She entered, laid a hand upon his wrist, and checked his pulse. "Ah!" she cried. "I suspect they have not touched the root. Open the window! Thou art stifling him. Now, change all his linens."

"Alack, woman, what need?" objected the medieval wife. "Why soil more linen for a dying man?"

"Do as I bid thee," said Margaret, her voice dull with resolve.

Joan, though bewildered, obeyed. Margaret returned with her apron heavy with a flowering herb. She brewed a decoction and brought it to the bedside. Before offering it to the patient, she took a spoonful herself and smiled with feigned delight. "That is fair," Jorian whispered, making a feeble effort at humor. "Why, 'tis sweet, and now 'tis bitter."

She engaged him in conversation immediately after he drank. That bitter-sweet mixture stayed with him. Observing his reaction, she played her cards carefully: she mixed a trace of strong Schiedam in the third spoonful and a beaten yolk of egg in the seventh. With the gentle patience characteristic of her sex, she coaxed his body back from Death's clutches. Finally, Nature, patted rather than kicked, set Jorian Ketel upon his feet again. However, the healer made them both swear a solemn oath never to reveal her guilty deed to a soul, lest she be cast into prison, separated from her child.

The simple remedy that saved Jorian was known as sweet feverfew. She had gathered it from his own garden, her keen eye having spotted it growing through the window pane.

Thus, Margaret and Joan arrived at the hermit's cave and placed their offerings—a modest basket of butter and eggs—upon the small stone platform. Margaret leaned forward to the aperture designed for communication and spoke: "Holy hermit, we bring thee butter and eggs of the finest sort. And I, a poor deserted girl, a wife though not truly wed, and mother of the sweetest babe, come to pray thee tell me whether he lives or dies, and if he remains true to his vows or has faltered."

A faint, weary voice drifted from within the dark cavern: "Trouble me not with the trifles of earth. Send me a holy friar; I am dying."

"Oh, Alas!" cried Margaret, tears welling instantly. "Is it even so, poor soul? Then let us enter to aid thee."

"Saints forbid! Thy voice is that of a woman. Send me a holy friar."

They turned back as they had come. Joan, unable to contain herself, asked, "Must women then be demons of darkness, barred from approaching a dying bed?"

But Margaret was too deeply bowed by grief to answer. Joan offered rough consolation, which remained unheard until she spoke of Jorian. "And Jorian will speak out ere long; he is just on the boil. He is very grateful to thee, believe it."

"Seeing is believing," Margaret replied, her tone quiet and bitter.

"Not that he does not think thou might have saved him with something far beyond the common, yonder feverfew." Joan continued, lowering her voice. "'A man of my stature to be cured with a weed?' says he. 'If there is a sorry herb,' says he. 'Why, I was thinking of pulling up all mine,' says he. I told him remedies are naught the better for being far-fetched; thou and feverfew cured him when the grand medicines failed utterly. So I say, 'Thou mayst go down on thy four bones to feverfew.' But indeed, at bottom, he is grateful. Thou art all his thought and all his talk. But he senses Gerard's kin circling about thee, good friends, and last night—"

"Well?"

"He made me vow not to tell thee."

"Prithee, tell me."

"Well, he said: 'If I utter what little I know, it shall not bring him back, and it will set all by the ears. I wish I had more sense,' said he. 'I am sore perplexed. But least said is soonest mended.' That is his favorite phrase; he returns to it from a mile away."

Margaret shook her head slowly. "Ay, we are wading in deep waters, my poor babe and I."

It was Saturday night, and Luke was nowhere to be seen.

"Poor Luke!" Margaret whispered. "It was very kind of him to undertake such an errand."

"He is one out of a hundred," Catherine answered warmly.

"Mother, dost thou think he would be kind to little Gerard?"

"I am certain he would. So, be kinder to him when he returns! Wilt thou promise?"

"Ay."

Brother Clement, guided by the nuns, bypassed a bend in the river and strode vigorously toward a station several miles closer to the coast than where Luke lay in ambush for Gerard Eliassoen. Early the following morning, he departed and arrived in Rotterdam by noon. Making straight for the port, he intended not to keep Jerome waiting.

Upon observing several monks of his order along the quay, he approached them. Yet, Jerome was absent. He asked one if Jerome had arrived. "Surely, brother," the monk replied.

"Prithee, where is he?"

"Where? Why, there!" the monk pointed, directing Clement's gaze to a ship cutting swiftly across the water in full sail. Immediately, Clement noticed that every monk present was staring seaward.

"What? Gone without me! Oh, Jerome! Jerome!" Clement cried, his voice cracking with anguish. Several friars turned, staring at him.

"You must be Brother Clement," one said after a pause. On this, they kissed him and greeted him with warm brotherhood, handing him a letter Jerome had entrusted to them. It was a hasty scrawl. The writer conveyed a cold message: a ship bound for England was imminent, and he loathed to lose time. Though Clement might follow if he wished, Jerome urged him to stay and preach to his own countrymen. "Give glory to God, brother; thou hast a wonderful power over Dutch hearts, but thou art no match for those haughty islanders; thou art too tender."

"And know thou," the letter concluded, "that on the voyage I encountered one who asked for thee under the name thou didst bear in the flesh. Beware! Let not the world catch thee again in any silken net. Remember, Solitude, Fasting, and Prayer are the sword, spear, and shield of the soul. Farewell."

Clement was deeply shocked and mortified by this contemptuous desertion and the icy tone of the missive. He promised the benevolent monks to sleep at the convent and preach wherever the prior appointed, for Jerome had once raised him to the skies as a preacher. Yet, wounded to the quick, he withdrew abruptly, needing solitude.

He questioned himself: Was there some incurable fault in him, repulsive to so true a son of Dominic? Or was Jerome himself devoid of that Christian Love which St. Paul held above Faith itself? Shipwrecked together, saved upon the same fragment of wreckage, pupil, penitent, son in the Church—and now, for four hundred miles, a fellow traveler in Christ—to be shaken off like dirt at the first chance, with harsh, cold disdain.
