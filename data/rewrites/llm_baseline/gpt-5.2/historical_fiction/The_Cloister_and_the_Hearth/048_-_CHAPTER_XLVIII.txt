Catherine’s hands would not keep still that evening. They went from spindle to hearth and back again, as if the very tools could be coaxed to answer what no one yet could tell her: where Margaret was, and what had become of her. The house held its common sounds—the soft hiss where broth breathed at the edge of boiling, the tick of settling embers, Kate’s tread on the flags, the faint, familiar water-voice of the canal beyond the wall—yet every noise came sharp to Catherine, like a knife touched to the tongue.

Gerard would be home within a month at farthest—more like a week, she counted it, tasting the numbers as bitter as burnt crust—and how was she to meet his eyes and say she had not even kept watch upon his betrothed? Worse than her shame was the blank where knowledge ought to be. In that blank the mind breeds monsters.

“Oh, Kate,” she broke out at last, her voice thick as dough not yet kneaded smooth, “if she should have gone and made herself away!”

Kate, who had been trimming a bit of onion fine enough to vanish into the pot and sweeten it without showing, turned quick. “Mother, she would never be so wicked.”

“Ah, my lass,” Catherine said, and the words came with the rough tenderness of a hand pressing salt into meat to keep it from spoiling, “you know not what hasty fools young lasses be, that have no mothers to keep ’em straight. They will fling themselves into the water for a man that the next man they meet would ha’ cured ’em of in a week. I have known ’em jump in like brass one moment and scream for help in the next—couldn’t know their own minds, ye see, even about such a trifle as yon.”

She jerked her chin toward the window, where the canal’s running never quite ceased, a constant under-song in their lives: sometimes a lullaby, sometimes an accusation. Catherine had always loved its plain, clean music—water on stone, water under plank, the reedy gossip of rushes when wind walked through them. Tonight it sounded to her like a beckoning finger.

“And then,” she went on, “there’s times when their bodies ail like no other living creatures ever I could hear of, and that strings up their feelings so, the patience that belongs to them at other times—beyond all living souls barring an ass—seems all to jump out of ’em at one turn, and into the water they go. Therefore I say that men are monsters.”

“Mother!”

“Monsters, and no less, to go making such heaps o’ canals just to tempt the poor women in. They know we shall not cut our throats, hating the sight of blood and rating our skins a hantle higher nor our lives; and as for hanging—why, while she is a fixing of the nail and a making of the noose she has time to alter her mind. But a jump into a canal is no more than into bed; and the water, it does all the lave, will ye, nill ye.”

As she spoke, she stirred the pot, though it needed no stirring, as if the spoon could turn fear into sense. A savour rose—bones and leek, pepper bruised between fingers, a whisper of thyme—homely, honest things that belonged to living mouths. Catherine breathed it in hard, as if to prove to herself the world still held nourishment.

“Why, look at me,” she said suddenly, and there was a flash of savage humour. “The mother o’ nine—wasn’t I agog to make a hole in our canal for the nonce?”

Kate’s knife paused mid-air. “Nay, mother, I’ll never believe it of you.”

“Ye may, though.” Catherine leaned her hip to the table, and her voice changed, slipping back along the years the way a tune will carry you to a long-closed door. “’Twas in the first year of our keeping house together. Eli hadn’t found out my weak stitches then, nor I his; so we made a rent, pulling contrariwise; had a quarrel. So then I ran crying, to tell some gabbling fool like myself what I had no business to tell out o’ doors except to the saints—and there was one of our precious canals in the way. Do they take us for teal?”

At the memory, she gave a short sound, half laugh, half choke. “Oh, how tempting it did look! Says I to myself, ‘Sith he has let me go out of his door quarrelled, he shall see me drowned next, and then he will change his key. He will blubber a good one, and I shall look down from heaven’—I forgot I should be in t’other part—‘and see him take on, and oh, but that will be sweet!’ And I was all a tiptoe and going in, only just then I thought I wouldn’t.”

“Why?” Kate asked, almost despite herself, as if she were listening to a tale told by the hearth that must end in the same place each telling, yet always draws a new breath from you.

“I had got a new gown a making, for one thing, and hard upon finished.” Catherine’s mouth softened. Even in shame she remembered cloth between fingers, the good pull of thread, the patient craft that binds a life. “So I went home instead, and what was Eli’s first word? ‘Let yon flea stick i’ the wall, my lass,’ says he. ‘Not a word of all I said t’ anger thee was sooth, but this, “I love thee.”’ These were his very words; I minded ’em, being the first quarrel.”

She saw it again as clearly as if the steam before her had taken shape: her own young arms flung about his neck; her sobbing that tasted of salt and milk; the canal’s cold lure forgotten under the warmer pull of being wanted and forgiven. “So then that was better than lying in the water,” she finished, “and spoiling my wedding kirtle and my fine new shoon—old John Bush made ’em, that was uncle to him keeps the shop now. And what was my grief to hers?”

Kate’s eyes shone, but she held to her point, as a careful cook holds to a recipe and still adjusts it for the day’s flour. “Margaret loves her father too much to think of leaving him so at his age,” she said. “He is father and mother and all to her, you know.”

“Nay, Kate.” Catherine’s gaze went past her daughter, past the pot, past the neat row of bread loaves cooling on the board. “They do forget all these things in a moment o’ despair, when the very sky seems black above them. I place more faith in him that is unborn, than on him that is ripe for the grave, to keep her out o’ mischief.”

Kate’s hand drifted, unconsciously, to the bowl of flour as if to anchor herself in something that could be measured. Catherine’s voice dropped, and the kitchen’s little sounds—spoon against pot, fire settling—seemed suddenly too loud, too near, as if the house itself were listening.

“For certes,” Catherine said, “it do go sore against us to die when there’s a little innocent a pulling at our hearts to let ’un live, and feeding at our very veins.”

Kate swallowed. “Well, then, keep up a good heart, mother.” She forced steadiness into her tone, the way one pinches a flame to keep it from guttering. “Very like all these fears are overgreat.”

Then, remembering another dread that had been fermenting in the women’s talk like yeast, she added—carefully, solemnly, as if naming it might change the very substance of fate—“And at all events, mother, do not persist in naming the sex of Margaret’s infant. It is so unlucky, all the gossips tell. Dear heart, as if there were not as many girls born as boys.”

The sensible words fell into the room and were met, not with reason, but with Catherine’s instant clamour, fierce as a pan set on too hot a fire.

“Have you the cruelty to threaten me with a girl!” she cried. “I want no more girls, while I have you. What use would a lass be to me? Can I set her on my knee and see my Gerard again as I can a boy? I tell thee ’tis all settled.”

“How may that be?” Kate asked, half shocked, half amused, because her mother’s certainty had always been its own kind of weather.

“In my mind,” Catherine said, and tapped her temple with the spoon as if it were a ladle on the rim of a pot. “And if I am to be disappointed i’ the end, ’tisn’t for you to disappoint me beforehand, telling me it is not to be a child, but only a girl.”

Outside, the canal went on singing its old, untroubled song in the dark—an ordinary music that had outlived a thousand quarrels, a thousand prayers, a thousand names spoken in dialects that were already thinning from the tongues of the young. Catherine listened as if she could wrest an answer from that water-voice, but it offered only what it always had: sound without meaning, and meaning only the listener could supply.
