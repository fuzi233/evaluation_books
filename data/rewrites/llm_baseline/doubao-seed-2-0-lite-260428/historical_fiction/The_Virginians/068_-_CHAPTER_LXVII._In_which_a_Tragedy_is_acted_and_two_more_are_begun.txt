A few weeks after Harry Warrington, our Virginian, joined his new regiment, his commanding officer landed fresh from America: none other than James Wolfe, the man whose gallantry at Cape Breton had won him a promotion to lead the regiment’s newly formed second battalion. Harry went to call on his new commander with unfeigned respect and open good will, for all of England was already turning its eyes to this rising young soldier. After a string of humiliating failures by once-celebrated officers—from the 2nd Duke of Marlborough, who proved no worthy heir to his great ancestor’s fame, to Lord George Sackville, whose cowardice at Minden had thrown away a certain victory—the nation hungered for military glory, and the crown’s ministers were desperate for a general who could deliver it. Both Wolfe and General Lambert, Harry’s old mentor, had business keeping them in London, and their old easy friendship was renewed at once: no one rejoiced more than Lambert to see his young protégé Harry’s fortunes rise.
Whenever he was off duty, Harry could listen to Wolfe recounting last year’s campaigns for hours. The general spoke with a frank, generous simplicity that would later mark Nelson: he hid no part of his thoughts, owned to every feat and every misstep openly. Some of the fine powdered dandies loitering about St. James’s might sneer at his unpolished eagerness, but in our small circle of friends, we drank in every word he said. For all his military fire, there was a boyish romance to him: he loved music and poetry, and would later say on the very eve of his death that he would rather have written Gray’s *Elegy* than won a great battle. It was inevitable that George Warrington, the bookish elder Virginian brother, should grow close to a man of such literary tastes. Both were already betrothed, both eager to lay laurels at their beloveds’ feet, and no doubt their many sentimental conversations together would make for wonderful reading, could we transcribe them word for word. George wrote of the friendship years later:
> “I had the honour of seeing a great deal of the famous General Wolfe during his last stay in London. We shared an interest that never failed to hold our attention, and I could not but marvel at his simplicity, his candour, and that singular, glorious bravery that defined him. He was deep in love, and hungry for heaps of laurels to lay at his mistress’s feet. ‘If it be a sin to covet honour,’ he would cry, quoting his beloved Henry V, ‘I am the most offending soul alive.’ He got his fill of glory on that last day of his life, sure enough. He did not seem merely a resolute soldier doing his duty, but a medieval knight riding out in search of dragons. I have had the honour to know another great chief from my own Virginia, of a very different, far sterner temper, and I scarce know which I admire more: the Briton’s chivalrous fire, or our Virginian’s more than Roman constancy.”
With General Lambert’s official duties keeping him tied to London, his whole family settled contentedly in town, and George was perfectly happy in the quiet of Southampton Row, wandering the leafy green walks of Bedford Gardens with no urge to travel far. He made one short pilgrimage out to Castlewood, where he stayed a few days in the old chamber his grandfather Esmond had occupied as a boy, received politely by the members of the family in residence. But he would far rather have been back in London, by the side of the young lady whose company pleased him far more than Lord Castlewood’s circle—though the ladies of the house were all gracious, especially Lady Maria, who was utterly charmed when George and Parson Sampson read his new tragedy aloud to them. The chaplain had fallen so in love with the play that it was all down to his influence, not Samuel Johnson’s, that the work ever made it to the stage. Johnson had indeed pressed the tragedy on his friend David Garrick for Drury Lane, but Garrick had just struck a deal to stage a new play by John Home, author of the hit *Douglas*, so George’s *Carpezan* was taken over to John Rich at Covent Garden, who accepted it at once.
On opening night, George threw an elegant supper for his friends at the Bedford Head tavern just steps from the theatre, after which the whole party traipsed over to Covent Garden to claim their box. George himself stayed behind at the coffee house with a small handful of companions, where messengers ran back and forth throughout the performance to bring him updates. The great Barry played the title role of Carpezan, Shuter took the part of the aged nobleman, Reddish made a perfect Ulric, and the King of Bohemia was played by a talented young Dublin actor named Hagan, who looked and performed the part to universal acclaim. Peggy Woffington as the heroine Sybilla looked a touch too old in the first act, but her much-doubted on-stage murder in the fourth act played out to the audience’s terrified delight, and Miss Wayn sang the plaintive ballad the page sings just as Sybilla is led to execution. The whole house agreed Barry was breathtakingly terrible and tender in that final scene, and the young actor Hagan won nothing but cheers for his grace and charm. Rich had spared no expense staging the play, though some whispered he had no business wheeling out the famous pantomime elephant, borrowed from a former harlequin show, to lead the procession of Janissaries in the final act—perched on the elephant’s back in full Turkish livery was one of George’s two black footmen, Gumbo, while the other sat whooping and applauding uproariously from the footmen’s gallery at all the right moments.
Sybilla’s execution was the turning point of the play: once her head fell, George’s friends breathed easy, and one messenger after another burst into the coffee house to tell him his tragedy was a full, unqualified success. Barry stepped forward at the final curtain to announce the play would run for a second night, and revealed to roaring applause that it was the first dramatic work of a young gentleman from Virginia.
We would have given anything to sit in the Lamberts’ box that night, to watch Theo’s anxious flutters turn to bright, tearful smiles as the audience’s cheers grew louder. Harry, sat next to her, had gone deadly pale during the tense fourth act—paler, Mrs. Lambert swore, than Barry caked in stage chalk. Once the final curtain fell, he clapped so hard he could scarcely feel his hands, as if Briareus’s hundred hands were not enough to make the noise he wanted. Wolfe and General Lambert cheered themselves hoarse; Mrs. Lambert of course was sobbing through the final scene, and even Hetty, who’d teased all week that the characters got exactly what they deserved, was bouncing in her seat with glee, little Charley on leave from his boarding school and Miss Lucy brought home especially from her own school both screaming with the rest of the crowd. Lord Castlewood and Lady Maria were in a box of their own; the Earl trotted backstage to congratulate Barry and the other actors, while Parson Sampson led the cheering in the pit, having signaled to Gumbo in the gallery earlier to follow his lead and make as much noise as he did.
The celebrations did not end at the theatre. There were suppers, and more suppers, that night. Gumbo threw a feast for all his fellow footmen in fine livery who’d turned out to cheer for his master’s play; Harry hosted a raucous dinner for ten of his new brother officers at the Star and Garter in Pall Mall, who had all come up from camp expressly to back *Carpezan*. The grandest supper of all, though, was hosted by George, with the lead actors of the play, the entire Lambert party, Samuel Johnson, the painter Joshua Reynolds, Lord Castlewood and Lady Maria, and a handful of other guests. Lady Maria happened to sit right beside young Hagan, the actor who had played the King of Bohemia. George, of course, found himself sat next to Theo, and passed a very pleasant evening indeed. The mood grew more boisterous as the punch was passed around, and Lady Maria laughed gaily when her turn came to toast “The King of Hungary.” The young actor, full of fire and eloquence both on and off the stage, swore that he had “died” once already that night on the stage, and would gladly die a hundred more times on the same field; dead or alive, he vowed, he was her ladyship’s most humble servant. If only he had a real crown instead of his pasteboard stage diadem, he cried, he would lay it at her feet that very instant. Lord Castlewood and Esmond were not at all pleased by the young man’s excessive gallantry, which they rightly put down to his having taken far more than his share of the punch bowl. Theo and Hetty, new to the wild ways of theatre folk, were a little startled by his outburst, but the far more experienced Lady Maria took it all in excellent good part. When the hour grew late, the gentlemen escorted the ladies out to their waiting coaches, and many of the party drifted back to the supper room for more drink. By the end of the night, Carpezan (Barry) had to be carried home in a sedan chair, the King of Bohemia woke the next morning with a brutal headache, and the playwright himself, though he recalled making a dozen grand speeches, was utterly shocked when half a dozen of his guests showed up on his doorstep the next day for the second round of supper he’d drunkenly invited them to the night before.
George had walked Mrs. Lambert and her daughters to their coach the night before, all three ladies flurried, over-excited, and glowing with joy, and of course George was round at their Soho house the very next day, going over every detail of the play, the audience, the actors, over and over again. Mrs. Lambert, who had heard endless warnings that theatre people were dangerous company for young men, fretted a little that George would spend too much time lingering in the green room.
George smiled. “I have a perfect amulet against all green room temptations that I do not fear in the least,” he said, and looked right into Theo’s eyes, as if her gaze was all the protection he would ever need.
“Why should he be afraid, mamma?” Theo asked simply, with no thought of guile or danger in her head.
“No, my darling, I don’t think he need be afraid,” Mrs. Lambert said, kissing her forehead.
“Pray tell me, who would fall in love with that painted old creature who played the lead heroine?” Hetty tossed her head. “She must be old enough to be his mother.”
“And pray who says no woman can have a heart once she passes a certain age?” Mrs. Lambert snapped back, suddenly sharp. “I should hope your father does not think me past caring for him, miss. He does not sneer at age, whatever silly little girls just out of the schoolroom may do. You would do well to remember the fifth commandment, Hetty!”
“I did not think I was breaking it by saying an actress was old enough to be George’s mother,” Hetty protested.
“George’s own mother is exactly my age, miss! We were at school together, for heaven’s sake! I will not have our age made a mock of by our own children, and I would thank you to spare us that, if you please. Do you think I am too old to care for you, George?”
“I am only too glad that my own mother is as kind and dear as you are, Aunt Lambert,” George said warmly, and no man who has ever been newly engaged will think it strange that he was perfectly, genuinely fond of his future mother-in-law. General Lambert, for his part, joked that he was jealous: Mrs. Lambert doted on George more than any other person in the house. She fussed over him if he so much as sneezed, sent him her own special health recipes, dressed Theo in her very finest gowns to meet him, and then always found some excuse to slip out of the room and leave the two of them alone, pretending she had to tend to the younger children or give an order to the servants, lingering just outside the door for a few minutes before coming back to sit, perfectly artless, at her worktable, as if she had no idea the two young people had been blushing and stammering at each other in her absence.
At last the long-awaited letter arrived from Virginia, in Madam Esmond’s sharp, neat handwriting. George trembled and blushed before he broke the seal. It was his mother’s reply to the letter he had sent months before, telling her of Harry’s commission and his own engagement to Theo. She fully approved of Harry’s advancement, and wrote that she had no great objection to early marriages: she would take George’s glowing portrait of Theo with the appropriate allowance for lovers’ bias, and hope for his sake that the girl was all he claimed. It was a pity, she wrote, that the young lady appeared to have no fortune of her own, for though the Esmonds were wealthy in land, they had little ready cash to spare. But Heaven had blessed them with more than enough to provide for their children, and their sons’ wives would be welcomed as her own. She asked only to hear more from Mr. and Mrs. Lambert before she gave a full, formal reply, and admitted freely that she had once held higher hopes for George, who as a Warrington might have courted the greatest heiress in the kingdom—but since Heaven had seen fit for him to choose her old friend’s daughter, she would acquiesce. The letter was being brought over by a Mr. Van den Bosch, a wealthy Albany landowner who had just bought a vast estate bordering the Esmonds’ Virginian property, and was sailing to England to place his young granddaughter in a boarding school. Madam Esmond wrote that she was no mercenary woman, and did not urge her sons to pay the girl attention merely because she was one of the richest heiresses in the colonies—but if Harry could find a good, suitable companion for life in her, then Madam Esmond would have the joy of both her sons settled near her in her declining years.
The letter was not warm, and it was clear Madam Esmond was far from fully satisfied, but it held her formal consent to the engagement. George ran all the way to Soho to share the news, and the Lamberts read the joy on his face before he even opened his mouth. Mrs. Lambert took Theo’s hand and kissed her, her eyes bright, and George, pale and trembling a little, turned to General Lambert and said: “Madam Esmond has given her blessing to our attachment, sir. I can only hope you will be as kind to me as she has been.”
“God bless you, my dear boy!” the General said, laying his hand on George’s head. “I could not ask for a better son. No need for kneeling, you two—though you may thank the good Lord for giving you the best little wife in all England. You never once gave me a moment’s heartache, my dear,” he said to Theo, “and happy is the man who wins you.”
No doubt the young couple knelt before them after the custom of the day, and Mrs. Lambert kissed both of them and soaked her handkerchief through with happy tears. Hetty was not present for the soft, sentimental scene, and when she heard the news at first she tossed her head and laughed sharply, saying “Is that all? I have known the two of them were in love for months. Do you think I have no eyes to see, no ears to hear?”—but later, in private, she flung her arms around Theo’s neck and hugged her so tight it hurt, vowing no one would ever love her sister half as much as she did. She teased George mercilessly after that, but he was too happy to care, and too generous to miss that her sharp jokes were only a mask for a quiet, unspoken jealousy.
When they all re-read Madam Esmond’s letter a few days later, they noticed how vague it was: there was no promise of a marriage settlement, only a note that the young couple would be welcome to live at the Virginia estate. General Lambert shook his head over it, his face grave.
“Bah!” George laughed. “The play will earn me three hundred pounds. I can write a new tragedy every year if I put my mind to it. We can live very comfortably on that.”
“On that, and your patrimony,” the General said.
George hesitated, and then admitted the truth: what with paying off his mother’s old debts, covering Harry’s commission and his former gambling debts, and the ransom he had paid to get himself back after he was kidnapped months before, his patrimony was almost entirely spent.
The General’s face grew even graver, but when he saw Theo’s eyes fill with tears of alarm, he pulled her into his arms and swore no poverty on earth would stop his little girl from getting the happiness she wanted. George frankly admitted he had no desire at all to sail back to Virginia and live entirely dependent on his mother’s sharp, domineering temper. Why should he not stay in England, write more plays, study for the bar, and carve out a name for himself here? He set to work planning a second tragedy at once, bringing pages of his new work to Theo and Hetty to read. Hetty yawned over the verses, but Theo swore every line was even more perfect than the last.
The engagement was formally announced to the rest of the Warrington family, and George sent a ceremonious letter to his great-uncle Sir Miles Warrington. For a little while Sir Miles saw no great harm in the match, though he huffed that George, with his name and connections, could have “looked higher” than a penniless girl with no fortune of her own. The truth was, Sir Miles assumed Madam Esmond had settled a vast sum of money on her elder son, and that George was very well off indeed. When he found out the truth: that George’s patrimony was gone, that he was penniless and entirely dependent on his mother’s good will, his rage knew no bounds. He wrote off to Madam Esmond in Virginia fulminating against her two profligate, irresponsible sons, and declaring that the Lamberts were no more than scheming fortune hunters who had inveigled his foolish nephew into a ruinous match. He would barely give George half a finger to shake when his nephew called at his London house, and never once invited him in to share the famous family home-brewed small beer.
