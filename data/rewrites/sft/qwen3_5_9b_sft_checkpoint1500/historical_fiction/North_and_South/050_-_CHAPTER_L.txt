The smokestacks of Milton belched black plumes into the gray sky, joining the deafening chorus of roaring engines and clattering iron. Within the mills, wood, steam, and metal labored in a blind, endless rhythm, driven by the tireless energy of the crowds outside. Yet there were no strollers, no idlers wandering the streets for pleasure. Every face was etched with urgency or dread; the air buzzed with a fierce hunger for news. Men shoved past one another in the Mart and the Exchange, mirroring the ruthless competition that defined their lives. A heavy gloom settled over the town. Buyers were scarce, and those who appeared were viewed with suspicion by the sellers, for credit was a fragile illusion. Rumors swirled of failures sweeping through the neighboring ports, threatening to drag down even the most solid establishments. Though Milton itself had hitherto remained untouched by collapse, the shadows of massive, disastrous speculations loomed large. Whispers circulated in hushed tones: "Who has fallen?" "How does it touch me?" People clung to the names of the safe, like "Mr. Thornton," whose vast enterprises and prudent daring seemed like a shield against disaster. Yet behind closed doors, harsh truths were whispered between confidants. One man would lean in, voice lowered, to warn his neighbor: "Do not trust Thornton. He has poured every penny of his profit back into expansion. He holds no reserve. His machinery, barely two years old, cost him a fortune. If the tide turns, he will drown."

Such whispers cut deeper than they could know, for the truth was that Mr. Thornton *was* under severe pressure. It gnawed at his most vulnerable point: his pride. He had built his reputation as a self-made architect of fortune, believing that commerce offered the brave and honest a unique path to power, allowing them to command influence unseen by kings. He dreamed of a name echoing in distant lands, a legacy that would endure for generations. It had taken decades of silent toil to reach even a glimpse of this vision, only to find himself trapped in his own town, amidst the people he had once treated as strangers. The barrier between master and man had finally cracked, forged by the accidental intimacy of knowing Higgins. Until recently, Thornton had viewed his workers merely as labor units, but their recent contact had revealed a shared humanity, a "single heart" beating beneath the social divide. Now, however, the stakes had shifted. He faced the terrifying prospect of losing the trust of the very men he had begun to respect, jeopardizing delicate experimental plans that lay close to his heart. The fear was not of financial ruin alone, but of the isolation that came from failing those he cared for.

He reflected on the strike of a year prior, a wound that still bled. During those turbulent times, he had locked his capital into expensive new machinery and purchased vast quantities of cotton to fulfill contracts. The lack of skill among his imported Irish hands had damaged much of the finished product, rendering it unsellable to a firm proud of its quality. For months, this embarrassment had weighed heavily on him. Often, looking at Higgins, Thornton felt a surge of angry resentment, a desire to lash out at the source of his troubles. But he forced himself to curb this impulse. Avoidance would not suffice; he needed to prove to himself that he could master his anger. So, he allowed Higgins access, granting him audiences whenever business or schedule permitted. Slowly, the resentment faded, replaced by a bewildered admiration. How could two men, bound by the same trade, view their duties so differently? This new understanding softened the air between them, fostering a patience and charity that might prevent future clashes, or at least ensure they were borne with kindness. Furthermore, this bridge of communication revealed facts previously hidden from one side or the other, illuminating the true state of affairs.

But now, the trade had collapsed. Market values plummeted, halving the worth of Thornton's stock. Orders dried up, leaving his machinery idle and his capital frozen. Payments for completed orders were delayed, yet expenses continued to bleed away. Bills for cotton piled up, and with money scarce, he was forced to borrow at exorbitant rates, unable to liquidate any assets. Despair threatened to consume him, but he pushed forward, working day and night to anticipate disasters. At home, he remained calm and gentle with his wife, and in the mill, his brief, decisive answers carried a weight of burden that his workers sensed. They saw the strain pressing down on him, replacing former antagonism with a quiet sympathy. Higgins noted how the master sat, looking ashen and pale, like a sheet of dull wool. Higgins, moved by compassion, stayed late after hours, unknown to anyone, to finish a neglected piece of work that Thornton had ordered. The overseer, perhaps to save face, claimed credit for the job, but Higgins and a friend had done it in secret, hoping to spare their master further worry.

Later, as Higgins approached Thornton on Marlborough Street, a thought crossed his mind: *I wonder who would be more broken-hearted to see our master sitting like this?* Higgins stopped, forcing Thornton to halt his brisk walk. The merchant looked up, startled, as if woken from a trance.

"Haven't you heard anything of Miss Margaret lately?" Higgins asked.

"Miss... who?" Thornton replied, momentarily confused.

"Miss Margaret Hale—the old parson's daughter. You know who I mean?" Higgins pressed, his tone respectful despite the closeness of their proximity.

"Oh, yes!" Suddenly, the icy frost of worry vanished from Thornton's face, replaced by a warmth that lit his eyes like a summer sun breaking through winter clouds. Although his lips remained compressed, a genuine smile softened his expression. "She is my landlord now, Higgins. I hear of her through her agent occasionally. She is well, surrounded by friends. Thank you, Higgins." The "thank you" lingered, heavy with emotion, catching Higgins off guard. It was a small, golden thread woven into the dark tapestry of his fortunes. Might it lead somewhere?

"And hasn't she married?" Higgins ventured.

"Not yet," Thornton said, the cloud returning to his brow. "There is some talk of a match with a relative."

"If so, she won't be coming back to Milton, I suppose?"

"No."

"Hold on a moment, sir." Higgins stepped closer, lowering his voice. "Has the young gentleman been cleared?" He winked, adding a layer of mystery to his query.

"The young gentleman? Master Frederick?"

"Yes, her brother who was here at the lady's passing. Mary and I knew the whole story from the start, thanks to Mary working in the house. We held our tongues, but..."

"He was here? It was her brother?" Thornton confirmed, the realization settling over him.

"Sure enough. I only wished to know if they've cleared him."

"Not that I know of. I know nothing. I only hear of Miss Hale as my landlord, via her lawyer."

Thornton broke away, resuming his business, leaving Higgins baffled. Later, alone, Thornton murmured to himself, "It was her brother. I am glad. I may never see her again, but it is a relief. I knew she could not be unmaidenly, yet I yearned for certainty. Now I am content!"

This knowledge was a tiny beacon in his growing darkness. His agent had placed a fortune with an American trading house, and now that house, along with others, had collapsed like a house of cards. Engagements multiplied, debts mounted, and the interest on his locked-up capital evaporated. Night after night, he sat in his private study, buried in ledgers and papers, long after his family had retired. One morning, as dawn crept through the cracks of his shutters, he found himself exhausted, contemplating the futility of sleep. Then the door opened, and his mother stood there, dressed as she had worn the previous day. She had slept as little as he.

"Mother! Why are you not in bed?"

"Son John," she said softly, "do you think I can sleep with an easy mind while you keep awake with care? You have not told me what troubles you, but I have seen sore distress in you these many days."

"Trade is bad."

"And you dread—"

"I dread nothing," he interrupted, lifting his chin with stubborn resolve. "I know no man will suffer by me. That was my greatest fear."

"But how do you stand? Will it be a failure?" Her voice trembled with a rare fragility.

"Not a failure. I must give up business, but I will pay every man. I might redeem myself—I am sorely tempted—"

"How? Oh, John! Keep your name! Try all risks for that sake. How will you redeem it?"

"Through a speculation offered to me, fraught with danger. If it succeeds, it will lift me far above water, hiding my straits from all. But if it fails—"

"And if it fails?" She stepped forward, placing a hand on his arm, her eyes blazing with urgent light. She held her breath, waiting for the conclusion.

"Honest men are ruined by rogues," he said grimly. "As things stand, my creditors' funds are safe; every farthing is accounted for. But I do not know where my own money is. It may all be gone, leaving me penniless. Thus, it is my creditors' money I must risk."

"But if it succeeds, they need never know. Is it truly so desperate? I am sure it is not, or you would never have conceived it. If it works—"

"I would be a rich man, and my peace of conscience would vanish!"
