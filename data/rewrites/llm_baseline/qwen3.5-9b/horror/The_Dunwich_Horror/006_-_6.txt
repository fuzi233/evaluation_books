The horror of Dunwich unfolded between Lammas and the autumnal equinox of 1928, a monstrous prologue witnessed firsthand by Dr. Armitage. Even prior to this night, whispers had circulated regarding Wilbur Whateley's grotesque pilgrimage to Cambridge and his desperate, frantic attempts to secure a copy of the *Necronomicon* from the Widener Library. These endeavors had failed utterly; Armitage had issued warnings of such piercing intensity to every librarian guarding the forbidden tome that access remained impossible. Yet, Wilbur returned from Cambridge in a state of shocking nervousness. He was consumed by anxiety for the book, yet equally terrified to remain away from home, as if he sensed that prolonged absence might invite calamity.

Early in August, the anticipated catastrophe arrived. In the small hours of the third day, Dr. Armitage was jolted awake by the wild, ferocious cries of the college's savage watchdog. Deep and terrible, the snarling, half-mad growls persisted, rising in volume until they reached a deafening crescendo, punctuated by hideously significant silences. Then, a scream tore through the darkness—a sound emanating from a throat entirely alien to nature, capable of awakening the slumbering citizens of Arkham and haunting their dreams forevermore. Such a cry could originate from no creature born of this earth.

Armitage scrambled into clothes and sprinted across the street and lawn toward the college buildings. Others were already ahead of him, and the shrill wail of a burglar alarm echoed from the library. Through an open window, he saw a black, gaping void illuminated by the moonlight. The intruder had succeeded in breaching the defenses; the barking and screaming, now fading into a low, guttural growling and moaning, clearly originated from within. A primal instinct warned Armitage that what transpired inside was unfit for unfortified eyes. With authoritative force, he pushed back the gathering crowd and unlocked the vestibule door.

Among the throng, he recognized Professor Warren Rice and Dr. Francis Morgan—men to whom he had previously voiced his deepest conjectures and fears. He motioned for them to enter with him. By this time, the internal commotion had subsided, save for the watchful, droning whine of the dog. Suddenly, Armitage felt a chill as he realized that a chorus of whippoorwills in the nearby shrubbery had begun a damnably rhythmic piping, sounding as if in unison with the final breath of a dying man.

A frightful stench filled the air, one Dr. Armitage knew too well. The three men rushed across the hall to the small genealogical reading-room, the source of the low whining. For a heartbeat, no one dared touch the light switch. Finally, summoning his courage, Armitage snapped it on.

One of the three men shrieked aloud at the sight sprawled before them amidst overturned chairs and disordered tables. Professor Rice claimed he lost consciousness for an instant, though he stood firm. Lying half-bent on its side in a fetid pool of greenish-yellow ichor and tarry slime was a creature almost nine feet tall. The dog had stripped it of all clothing and much of its skin. It was not quite dead; it twitched silently and spasmodically, its chest heaving in monstrous rhythm with the expectant piping of the birds outside.

Scattered around were bits of shoe leather and fragments of apparel. Just inside the window lay an empty canvas sack, evidence of how it had been discarded. Near the central desk sat a revolver, a dented but undischarged cartridge later revealing why it had never been fired. However, the creature itself crowded out all other thoughts. To claim that no human pen could describe it would be both trite and inaccurate; rather, it could not be vividly visualized by any mind whose concepts of form and dimension are strictly bound to the common life-forms of this planet and our three known dimensions.

It was undoubtedly partly human, possessing very man-like hands and a head. Its face was goatish and chinless, bearing the distinct stamp of the Whateley family. Yet, the torso and lower body were teratological fantasies, requiring generous clothing merely to allow it to walk unnoticed on Earth. Above the waist, it was semi-anthropomorphic, though its chest, where the dog's paws still rested vigilantly, displayed the leathery, reticulated hide of a crocodile or alligator. Its back was piebald with yellow and black, faintly suggesting the scaly covering of certain snakes.

Below the waist, humanity vanished completely, replaced by sheer fantasy. Thick coarse black fur covered the skin, and from the abdomen protruded a dozen long, greenish-gray tentacles tipped with red sucking mouths, hanging limp. Their arrangement followed an odd symmetry belonging to some cosmic geometry unknown to Earth or the solar system. Set deep in pinkish, ciliated orbits on each hip were what appeared to be rudimentary eyes. Instead of a tail, a trunk-like feeler hung down, marked with purple rings and showing clear signs of being an undeveloped mouth or throat.

Its limbs, aside from the black fur, roughly resembled the hind legs of prehistoric giants, terminating in ridged, veined pads that were neither hooves nor claws. As the creature breathed, its tail and tentacles shifted color rhythmically, driven by a circulation foreign to human biology. The tentacles deepened in greenish hue, while the tail alternated between yellow and a sickly grayish-white in the spaces between its purple rings. There was no genuine blood, only the foul, greenish-yellow ichor that trickled across the painted floor, leaving a curious discoloration in its wake.

As the presence of the three men seemed to rouse the dying entity, it began to mumble without lifting its head. Dr. Armitage made no written record of its mutterings but asserted with confidence that no words of English were spoken. Initially, the syllables defied all correlation with earthly speech, but toward the end, disjointed fragments emerged, clearly drawn from the *Necronomicon*, the monstrous blasphemy for which the creature had perished. As Armitage recalled them, the phrases sounded something like: *"N'gai, n'gha'ghaa, bugg-shoggog, y'hah; Yog-Sothoth, Yog-Sothoth..."*

These utterances trailed off into nothingness as the whippoorwills shrieked in rhythmic crescendos of unholy anticipation. Then, a halt came to the gasping, and the dog raised his head in a long, lugubrious howl. A transformation swept over the yellow, goatish face of the prostrate thing, and its great black eyes fell in apparent despair. Outside, the shrilling of the birds ceased abruptly. Above the murmurs of the gathering crowd arose the sound of a panic-stricken whirring and fluttering. Against the moonlit sky, vast clouds of feathery watchers rose and raced from sight, frantic at the prey they had found.

Suddenly, the dog bolted upright, gave a frightened bark, and leaped nervously out the window by which it had entered. A cry erupted from the crowd, and Dr. Armitage shouted to those outside that no one must be admitted until the police or a medical examiner arrived. He was grateful the windows were too high to permit prying glances and carefully drew the dark curtains down over each one.

By this time, two policemen had arrived. Dr. Morgan met them in the vestibule, urgently pleading for their own safety to delay entry into the stench-filled reading-room until the examiner could arrive and cover the prostrate thing. Meanwhile, frightful changes were occurring on the floor. No detailed description is needed of the *kind* and *rate* of shrinkage and disintegration witnessed by Dr. Armitage and Professor Rice. Suffice it to say that, aside from the external features of the face and hands, the truly human elements of Wilbur Whateley must have been exceedingly small.

When the medical examiner finally arrived, there remained only a sticky, whitish mass on the painted boards, and the monstrous odor had nearly vanished. It appeared Whateley possessed no skull or bony skeleton—at least, not in any true or stable sense. He had taken somewhat after his unknown father.
