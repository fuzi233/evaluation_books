Desire trims the sails, and Circumstance brings no more than the breeze to fill them. That soft sun-dappled afternoon, Grandcourt rode his glossy black thoroughbred Yarico at a leisurely canter, his groom trailing a short distance behind on the mare Criterion, every hoofbeat eating up the lane between Diplow and Offendene, every line of his bearing radiating the quiet, unshakable assurance of a man who expects to get exactly what he wants. Upstairs in her bedroom, Gwendolen sat before the gilded mirror as her mother brushed out the full, heavy length of her pale light-brown hair, the strands glinting like spun honey in the sunlight.
“Only twist it up into a simple coil, mamma,” Gwendolen said, flicking a stray lock away from her forehead.
When the hair was arranged neat and unadorned, Mrs. Davilow’s gaze drifted to their shared reflection, and she gasped softly. The haggard, shadowed lines that had carved their way into Gwendolen’s face in the weeks since Klesmer crushed her performing dreams were gone, her eyes bright with a spark she had not seen for months. “Let me bring you your pearl drop earrings, Gwen,” she said, her voice light with unspoken hope.
Gwendolen shook her head, rising to slip off her silk dressing gown to reveal the plain, untrimmed black silk beneath. “No ornaments. Black is the only proper wear when you are going to turn a man down,” she said, flashing her old sharp, playful smile at her mother.
“Suppose he never even makes the offer at all?” Mrs. Davilow teased, a faint glint of mischief in her eyes.
“Then it will come to exactly the same thing,” Gwendolen said, tossing her head proudly. “I will have refused him before the words ever leave his mouth.”
She walked down the stairs moments later, the heavy black silk swishing around her ankles, her head held high, the spring back in her step that had vanished the day she met Mrs. Glasher at the Whispering Stones. Mrs. Davilow watched her from the landing, her chest tight with confused joy. She’s quite herself again, she thought. That can only mean she is happy he is coming. Can she really be set on refusing him?
If Gwendolen had heard that thought, she would have snapped back sharp. But the truth was, she had not known what she truly wanted for almost twenty hours, her mind flipping back and forth between opposing impulses so fast her resolve to refuse Grandcourt had turned hollow, no more alive than a “God’s will be done” murmured by someone who is leaning over a fence to watch a race, eager for a winner. She had never forgotten Mrs. Glasher’s desperate face, had never forgotten her promise not to interfere with the woman’s wishes for her boys, but over and over again she pictured the grim little cottage at Sawyer’s Lane, the sharp-eyed Mrs. Mompert drilling her to be a governess to squalling small children, her mother’s gentle, worn face lined with constant worry over their lost fortune. Everyone knows, she told herself, that society turns its cold shoulder to the illegitimate children, not the rich gentleman who sired them. What business is it of mine to throw away a whole life of ease, of being the one who calls all the shots, for the sake of a stranger’s secret? Even the old hot revulsion, the anger that Grandcourt would dare offer her a life built on top of hidden old mistakes, had dulled to a faint memory. She would not accept him, she told herself, she would not—right up until she heard the sound of his horse’s hooves crunching on the gravel drive.
Her heart lurched so hard she almost stumbled. All that steady preparation, all that resolve, melted away like snow in the sun. She had to clamp down on every trembling nerve to walk into the drawing-room slow and graceful, offer him her hand, her voice soft and low, matching his familiar lazy drawl as he said he hoped she was well. They settled into two of the wreath-painted chairs across from each other, Grandcourt leaning one arm over the back of his seat, his hat loose in his left hand, the faint, sweet scent of attar of roses drifting across the space between them. Any onlooker would have seen two people suspended in the tender hush of a new courtship, and Gwendolen could feel herself being wooed, wrapped in the quiet, complete focus of his attention that she had craved for months.
“I was gutted not to find you at Leubronn,” Grandcourt began, his drawl thick with soft, amorous languor. “The whole place was a filthy kennel without you. Don’t you agree?”
“I can’t speak for what it was without me,” Gwendolen said, a flicker of her old mischief returning as she lifted her eyes to his. “With me, I liked it well enough to stay longer if I could. But I had to come home for family troubles.”
“It was very cruel of you to run off like that,” Grandcourt said, brushing right past her mention of the troubles, as if they could not possibly matter. “You knew you were the heart and soul of every gathering there. Are you really so reckless with my feelings?”
Gwendolen blushed hot all the way down her neck, dropping her eyelids again. She could not say yes, she could not say no.
“Is there some other man?” he pressed, his voice low. “Some engagement that you owe it to me to tell me about? Is anyone standing between us?”
Her first thought was, No—but there is a woman. But the words would not come, stuck fast in her throat. She could not break her promise to Mrs. Glasher, could not bring herself to air that sordid secret out loud to Grandcourt, and suddenly the thought of him riding away for good, leaving her stuck with the governess post, the tiny cottage, no more horses, no more pretty gowns, made her chest ache with sharp, panicky dread.
“I fear you do not know what has befallen us,” she said, her voice steady with new-found dignity as she lifted her eyes to meet his. “My mother has lost all her fortune. We are leaving Offendene soon. That is why my mind has been so preoccupied.”
Grandcourt’s narrow, unreadable dark eyes held hers, and for a long, quiet moment neither of them spoke. Then he said, slow and lazy, as if it were the most trivial, obvious thing in the world, “I hope you will agree that Mrs. Davilow’s loss need never trouble either of you again. Trust me to make sure that weight never touches her. Give me the right to provide for you both.”
The words sank into her like a sip of heady, warm wine. All the scruples, all the old revulsion, all the faint fear of doing wrong faded to something distant, like a pain she had suffered months ago. She could see it all spread out before her: no more Sawyer’s Cottage, no more Mrs. Mompert looking over her shoulder, her mother happy and relaxed, herself galloping across the fields on Criterion every morning, answering to no one.
“You are very generous,” she said, her voice soft.
“You accept that future?” Grandcourt asked, no sharp eagerness in his tone, only quiet certainty. “Will you be my wife?”
Gwendolen went pale, rising abruptly to step a few feet away, her hands folded tight in front of her as she stood silent. Grandcourt stood too, his hat still loosely in his grip, that small flash of hesitation from the destitute, usually unflappable girl making his interest burn sharper than it had in years.
“Do you command me to leave?” he said.
No words could have been more perfectly chosen.
“No,” Gwendolen said. That single word was a leash, holding him fast. She could not let him go.
“You accept my devotion?” he asked, stepping a little closer, their eyes locked.
Wait as long as she might, there was no way to take that no back, no way to send him away. “Yes,” she said, as solemnly as if she were swearing an oath before a judge in a courtroom.
He accepted the word gravely, no messy, over-eager kiss, no clumsy clinging. He stepped forward and pressed a soft, respectful kiss to the back of her hand, then released it. Gwendolen thought his behavior was perfect, more charming than any suitor she had ever read of in novels.
“Will you not come meet mamma?” she said, a playful curl of her lips tugging at her mouth. “I will fetch her.”
“Let us wait a little while,” he said, standing close beside her, his left thumb and finger tucked into his waistcoat pocket as he stroked his whisker with his other hand. “I have one more thing to ask. How soon could we be married?”
“Not today,” Gwendolen said, tilting her chin saucily.
“Then tomorrow. Think on it before I come back. A fortnight, three weeks—no longer than we have to wait.”
“Ah, you think you will grow tired of my company once we are wed,” she teased, laughing bright and warm.
“You shall have whatever you like,” Grandcourt said.
“Then promise me I will never have to be around Mr. Lush,” she said, half playful, half earnest. “I do not like his company at all.”
“You shall not have to,” he said, his tone utterly indifferent. “I will get rid of him immediately. He is nothing but a poor, coarse brute who has trailed after me since I was a boy. I never could stand him.”
Gwendolen laughed, light and happy, sure that she had stumbled into the perfect life, the perfect husband. She ran up to her mother’s bedroom moments later, kissing her on both cheeks, her eyes sparkling.
“Come downstairs, mamma,” she whispered. “I am engaged to Mr. Grandcourt. You will never have to move to Sawyer’s Cottage. I will never have to be inspected by Mrs. Mompert. Everything will be exactly as I like.”
Mrs. Davilow stared, quiet, solemn surprise sweeping over her face, and Gwendolen tugged her by the hand toward the landing, giddy with the bright, unburdened relief of knowing she had escaped every grim fate the last weeks had stacked up against her.
The new chapter of her life, she thought, was already beginning.
