“In prison, sir? Good heavens, for what crime?”

“Well, she is a witness—perhaps an essential one.”

“Why, Messire Bailiff,” interjected Denys, “you don’t keep all your witnesses in chains, I trust.”

The alderman, pleased to be addressed as bailiff, grew more talkative. “In matters of bloodshed, we hold fast any testimony that might help the accused escape justice. That’s why we’re still holding the women. A man may hide his guilt for weeks, but a woman—should she do her duty to the realm on Friday, she’ll betray it by Sunday, or try. If you saw that girl now, you’d find her weeping over having sent five men to the gallows. Had they been women, we might have trusted a subpoena. They despise each other—there’s sense in that. But now I recall—there were other reasons for detaining this one. Hand me those depositions, young sir.” He put on his spectacles. “Ah! Here it is in black and white. Jean Hardy—one of the thieves—confessed that… humph? Yes, here: ‘And that the girl Manon was the decoy, and her sweetheart was Georges Vipont, one of the band; hanged last month. She has been distraught ever since, openly blaming the gang for his death, saying that had they not been cowards, he would never have been caught—and it is my belief she betrayed them out of sheer spite—’”

“His belief!” Gerard exclaimed, outraged. “What weight does the opinion of a cutthroat carry, burning with revenge against the very one who delivered him to justice? And if you go by that, what value has his word? Is a thief never a liar? Is he not always lying? And here—what motive could be stronger than revenge? Oh, sir, what madness to question a convicted felon and listen to his lies as though he were a truthful man swearing under oath, hand on the Gospel!”

“Young man,” said the alderman, “restrain your passion in the presence of authority! From your tone, I see you are a stranger. Know this: in our land, we question everyone. We are not so weak as to believe we can reach the truth by closing one ear or the other.”

“And so you would listen to Satan accusing the saints?”

“Tch-tch! The law deals only with men and women, and even they cannot utter a tale entirely false, no matter how hard they try. So we leave the barn door open to every grain. Only once inside, we winnow and sift. And who told you I accepted the thief’s story wholeheartedly like clear water? Not at all. I believed only what was confirmed by stronger evidence.”

“Stronger evidence?” Gerard asked, stunned. “Who but the thieves themselves would speak against her?”

“Herself.”

“Herself, sir? Did you question her too?”

“I tell you—we question all. Here is her deposition. Can you read? Read it yourself.”

Gerard glanced at Denys and began reading aloud:

“I am from Epinal. Two years ago, I left my home because I refused the man they chose for me. My father beat me. I fled. I went into service. I left because my mistress was jealous. She said I was impertinent. Last year, I stood in the market to be hired among other girls. The landlord of ‘The Fair Star’ took me. I stayed eleven months. A young man courted me. I loved him. I discovered travelers came and never left. I told my lover. He forbade me to speak. He threatened me. I learned he was part of a band of thieves. When travelers were to be robbed, the landlord would go out and summon the gang. Then I wept and prayed for their souls. I never told. A month ago, my lover died.

“The soldier reminded me of him. He had a beard like the one I lost. I cannot say whether I would have acted if he had not had a beard. I regret telling now.”

The paper nearly slipped from Gerard’s hands. For the first time, he truly grasped the danger Manon faced. He knew the merciless law, and the cold-hearted men who enforced it. Suddenly, he dropped to his knees before the alderman. “Oh, sir! Consider the difference between those cruel men and this poor, fragile woman! Could you bear to send her to the same fate? Could you condemn us to watch her die—when, had she not risked her life for ours, we would not now be in peril? Alas, sir! Show mercy to Denys and me, if you feel none for her, poor soul. We are honest men—you would tear our hearts out if you let that gentle girl perish. What can we do? What remains but to end our lives beneath her gallows?”

The alderman, though hardened, was human. Gerard’s fervent plea startled him, then moved him. He showed it oddly—by growing irritable. “There, rise, do,” he snapped. “I doubt anyone would speak so much for me. Hey, Daniel! Fetch the town clerk.” As the clerk entered from an adjoining room, the alderman said, “Here’s a foolish lad fretting over that girl. Can we stretch a point? Suppose we allow her to testify—question her fairly?”

The town clerk was one of those “impossible” men.

“No, sir, we cannot. She was not involved in this affair. Had she been accessory, we might offer her pardon in exchange for testimony.”

Gerard interrupted, “But she did better—she prevented the crime. She offered herself as a witness by rushing here with the news.”

“Nonsense, young man—it’s a matter of law.” The alderman and clerk debated long, the one arguing, the other denying, whether she stood legally as if she had been an accomplice in the attack on the travelers. This was fortunate for Manon. The alderman, irritated by the clerk’s constant refusals, declared, “I’ll show him I can do anything I please.” He summoned Manon, secured her with the landlord of “The White Hart” as bondsman, and received five gold pieces from Denys. After coaxing, Manon promised to attend as witness. He released her—but eased his conscience by explaining his reasoning plainly.

“The town must buy a new rope for every execution and present it to the hangman—or pay him in money. In my opinion, she’s not worth that expense. But men like her late companions—those are worth it.”

Thus, Denys and Gerard led her away. Gerard danced with joy; Denys cheered her spirits by assuring her of the demise of a troublesome figure. She wept, but silently. On the way to “The White Hart,” the public recognized her. Having heard the full story from the archers—who naturally spoke warmly in her favor—they followed, shouting encouragement. Empowered by numbers, she regained courage. The landlord, seeing she would draw customers, welcomed her kindly and gave her an upper chamber. There, alone, she wept again.

Poor little heart—like a ripple, up and down, down and up, endlessly. Bidding the landlord treat her well, to keep her as a prisoner without making her feel it, the friends stepped outside. And there—before them, coming from opposite ends of the street—were two processions.

One, loud and chaotic, filled with cries and howls—the kind that reveal, at moments, the beastly kinship hidden beneath human veneer. The other, quiet and solemn, attended only by a few nuns and friars.

The condemned, bound for public exposure in the marketplace. The bones of the victims, carried to the churchyard.

They met in the narrow street, almost at the inn door. Unable to pass, the bier carrying the relics of the dead became wedged against the cart bearing the living men who had made those bones—and would soon die for it. The mob didn’t immediately grasp the irony. Then a woman cried, “Look at your work, you dogs!” The cry spread like wildfire. A terrible roar erupted. The prisoners groaned, trying to hide their faces, but couldn’t—their hands were tied. They stood there, pale, hollow-eyed, images of despair, gazing at the bier with envy—for those whose bones they had sent ahead were already beyond suffering.

Two men stood apart, watching gravely. One, Denys, gave in to curiosity and vengeance. The other, Gerard, removed his hat and walked solemnly behind the coffin, mourning those whose fate had nearly been his own. For a time, he was the only lay mourner. But as they neared the suburbs, far from the spectacle in the market, artisans laid down tools, shopkeepers left their stalls—moved by pity, by shared humanity, perhaps inspired by Gerard—and joined the procession bareheaded. They watched the remains laid to rest in holy ground, amid the prayers of the Church.

After the rites, Gerard approached the cure respectfully and offered to purchase a mass for the souls.

Gerard, son of Catherine, always considered both sides of a coin. He tried to bargain slightly below the usual price, citing the tragic circumstances. But the good cure gently deflected his cleverness and firmly brought him back to market rate.

In conversation, they found common ground. Piety and practicality are not rare bedfellows—yet rarely seen so fully embodied as in these two men. Their meeting in the spiritual marketplace sparked mutual respect, like knights recognizing worthy foes. Moreover, the cure enjoyed gossip. Learning Gerard had fought the thieves at Domfront, he invited him into his parlour to hear the tale firsthand. His heart warmed. “God has been good to you,” he said. “I thank Him with all my soul. You are a good lad.” He added dryly, “You should have told me this in the churchyard—I might have given you the mass freely. But,” he said (the temperature suddenly dropping), “it’s ill luck to break a bargain. Still, I’ll open a bottle of my old Medoc—few guests merit that.”

He fetched the wine from his cupboard, muttering to himself, “At their old tricks again!”

“Pardon me?” said Gerard.

“I said nothing. Ah, here it is.”

“No, your reverence. You spoke—‘At their old tricks!’”

“Did I? Indeed?” He smiled. Then he uncorked the wine, poured a cup for each, and placed a log on the fire—stoves being unknown in Burgundy. “So I said ‘At their old tricks,’ did I? Now, sip this fine wine, and while it lasts, story for story—I’ll tell you a little tale.”

Gerard’s eyes lit up.

“You love stories?”

“As my life.”

“Do not raise your hopes too high. It’s a mere trifle compared to your adventures.”

Once upon a time, in France, in the duchy of Burgundy, not far from this very town where we now sip old Medoc, there lived a cure. I say he lived—but barely. The parish was small, the people greedy, and they gave him no more than he could force from them. The leaner he became, the holier their prayers would be, they thought. I know not if that was their creed, but their actions gave it color.

At last, he devised a trap.

One day, the richest farmer in the village had twins to baptize. As usual, the cure was invited to the christening feast. But before baptizing them, he demanded not just the baptismal fees—but also the burial fees.

“Saints defend us, parson!” cried the mother. “Don’t talk of burying! I’ve never seen children more likely to live.”

“Nor I,” said the cure. “Praise be to God. Yet they will die, being sons of Adam, as well as of you, dame. But when they do, it will cost them nothing—the burial fees are paid and recorded in this book.”

“For all that, it will cost them something,” quipped the miller, the biggest wag and knave in the place—so big a knave, no one knew quite how large, not even the hangman.

“Miller,” said the cure, “I say no.”

“Parson,” said the miller, “I say yes. It will cost them their lives.”

A great laugh followed. Amid the mirth, the fees were paid, and the children baptized.

But when every next child in the parish had to pay burial fees or lose their place in heaven, discontent festered quietly. One fine day, they gathered secretly, sent a churchwarden to complain to the bishop—and a thunderbolt struck the poor cure. Summoned at dinner-time to the episcopal palace, he took the parish books and went trembling.

The bishop greeted him with a frown, presented the complaint. “Monseigneur,” said the cure humbly, “does the parish accuse me of many things, or only this one?”

“In truth, only this one,” said the bishop, softening slightly.

“Then, monseigneur, I admit the fact.”

“Good. That saves time. Now, your excuse—if there is one.”

“Monseigneur, I have been cure of that parish seven years. Fifty children I have baptized. Five buried. At first, I said, ‘Heaven be praised, the air of this village is healthy.’ But checking the register, I found it had always been so. Digging deeper, I learned that most born in Domfront eventually went to Aix and were hanged. This deprived not only their cure but the entire Church of funeral dues—since ‘pendards’ are buried in air, not earth. Knowing their greed, and how they grudge the Church every sou, I set a trap to prevent them from hanging. Greed against greed—some will die in their beds rather than let the Church gain those fees for nothing.”

The bishop laughed until tears ran down. He questioned the churchwarden, who confessed too many from the parish ended at Aix. “Then,” said the bishop, “I approve the act—for myself and my successors. Let it stand until they mend their ways and die in their beds.”

Next day, the ringleaders came humbly to the cure. “Parson, you were fair to us, save for this unfortunate matter. Pray, let there be no ill will over such a trifle.”

The cure replied, “My children, I would not be worthy of your pastor if I could not forgive. Go in peace—and bring me as many children as possible, so the double fees may spare your cure from starvation.”

The bishop often told the tale, keeping the cure’s memory alive—and eventually transferred him to a decent parish, where he could offer old Medoc to those worthy of it. Their names are not legion.

A light dawned in Gerard’s eyes. His face revealed it.

“Ay!” said his host. “I am that cure. Now you understand why I said ‘At their old tricks.’ I swear they’ve tricked my successor into waiving those funeral fees. You’re lucky to have escaped that parish. And so am I.”

The cure’s niece burst in. “Uncle! The weighing—la! A stranger!” And she burst out laughing.

The cure rose instantly but kept Gerard beside him. “Wet your beard once more, and come with me.”

In the church porch, they found the sexton with enormous scales and weights of all sizes. Several humble folk stood by. Soon, a woman stepped forward with a sickly child. “Be it heavy or light,” she vowed, “in the best rye meal, whatever this child weighs, I will pay to Holy Church, if he recovers. Pray, good people, for this child and for me, his mother, come here in sorrow and care!”

The child was weighed and screamed as if the scale were the font.

“Courage, dame!” cried Gerard. “This is a good sign—there’s plenty of life to fight the illness.”

“Blessed be the tongue that says so,” replied the woman. She cradled her child close and stepped aside as another woman brought her child to be weighed.

Suddenly, a loud, commanding voice rang out: “Make way! Make way for the seigneur!”

The crowd parted like waves before a noble galley. Into the porch strode the lord of the manor—dressed in rich attire: cap adorned with a feather tipped with a topaz, fur-lined jerkin, satin doublet, red hose, skates-like shoes, diamond-hilted sword in velvet scabbard, hawk on wrist—“the lord of the manor.” He flung himself into the scales as if he ruled the zodiac as well as the land. The hawk wobbled, balanced, then blinked.

While the sexton added weights, the cure whispered to Gerard, “My lord was near death. He vowed his weight in bread and cheese to the poor, with the Church taking her tenth.”

“With your permission, my lord,” Gerard said, “if you continue pressing your staff on the other scale, you’ll disturb the balance.”

His lordship grinned and removed the staff, leaning on it instead. The cure politely objected.

“Mille diables! What am I to do with it then?” the lord protested.

“Hold it out wide, my lord, clear of both scales.”

When the lord did so—falling into his own trap—the cure whispered, “Cretensis incidit in Cretensem!” meaning, “Diamond cuts diamond.” Then, with mock deference, he added, “If your lordship begrudges Heaven full weight, you might set the hawk on your servant and save a pound.”

“Gramercy for your advice, cure!” the lord said, reproachfully. “Would I deny my poor bird the benefit of Holy Church for one measly pound? I’d sooner let the devil take me and all my house than her, any day of the year.”

“Sweet is affection,” whispered the cure.

“Between a bird and a brute,” whispered Gerard.

“Tush!” the cure said, looking terrified.

The lord’s weight was recorded. Heaven, I trust, did not weigh his gratitude in the sanctuary’s balance. My unlearned reader should not suppose the man was eccentric—nor his gratitude misplaced. Men view death and past illness through different lenses. And when bargaining, they seek the sunny side—whether with man or Heaven. In this, we are unchanged from four hundred years ago—only then, we did it more naively. In that rough age, body ruled mind, and feelings took physical form. Men repented with scourges, prayed with beads, bribed saints with candles, ate fish to sanctify the soul, endured cold water to master emotion, and thanked God for health in 1 cwt. 2 stone 7 lb. 3 oz. 1 dwt. of bread and cheese.

While I preach—though rarely and poorly—the cure has been urging the lord to enter the church and decide what shall be done with his great-great-grandfather.

“Ods bodikins! Have you dug him up?”

“No, my lord—he was never buried.”

“So the old saying was true after all?”

“So true that workers today found a skeleton standing upright in the pillar they’re repairing. I sent word to you at once—but knew you’d come.”

“It is he! It is he!” the descendant exclaimed, quickening his pace. “Let us go see the old boy. This youth is a stranger, I think.”

Gerard bowed.

“Know then,” said the lord, “that my great-great-grandfather held his head high. On his deathbed, he refused to lie beneath the aisle with his ancestors, letting common folk walk over him. So, as tradition tells, he swore his son—my great-grandfather—to bury him upright in one of the church pillars.” (They entered the porch.) “‘For,’ he said, ‘NO BASE MAN SHALL PASS OVER MY STOMACH.’ Peste!” Even as he spoke, his lordship deftly parried a skull hurled at him by a boy in the aisle—bowled by a prankster who fled screaming the moment he realized what he’d done. The lord hurled the skull back furiously. The cure gasped and reached out—too late.

The cure groaned. As if summoned by mischief, a pack of children burst from hiding and clattered out of the church like a covey rising in thick woods.

“Oh! These mischievous brats,” the cure lamented. “The workmen can’t finish their task without the church swarming with them. Pray Heaven they haven’t disturbed his late lordship—no, I remember—I hid his lordship under a workman’s jerkin, and—saints defend us! The jerkin has been moved.”

The cure’s worst fears were confirmed. The rising generation of plebeians had played havoc with the proud noble. “The little ones had jockeyed for the bones,” pocketing those suited for games popular among them.

“I’ll excommunicate them!” roared the curate.

“Never mind,” said the reckless lord, stroking his hawk. “There’s enough of him left to swear by. Put him back! Put him back!”

“Surely, my lord,” the cure said, “you wish his bones laid in holy ground, masses said for his proud soul?”

The noble stroked his hawk.

“Are you there, Master Cure?” he asked. “No—this business is too old. He’s out of purgatory by now, up or down. I won’t spend a penny on him. Every dog has his day. Adieu, Messires, adieu, ancestor.” And he sauntered off, whistling to his hawk and caressing it.

The cure watched him go, rueful.
