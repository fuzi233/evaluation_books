Good and ill will keep their own tally, as if God Himself weighed them in His hand.

The steel bow lashed across the arbalestier’s back had dragged him down; yet that same burden, perched above his shoulders, offered Gerard a purchase like a handle on a drowning man’s coffin-lid. Gerard’s fingers closed on the cold curve of it; his other hand thrust in between bow and cord—anything to keep Denys from slipping away into the Rhine’s blind green pull.

The next instant Denys, hoisted by that very crossbow, came up with a gulping jerk, half his body bobbing out of the water.

“Now, grip me not! grip me not!” Gerard cried, white with the terror of the one mistake that kills—the drowning man’s clutch.

“Pas si bête,” Denys gurgled back; and hearing sense in the wet voice, Gerard’s panic steadied into command.

“On thy back,” he ordered sharply. He seized the arbalest, struck forward, and forced Denys into the right posture. “Hand on my shoulder! Slap the water with the other. No—downward; so. Do nothing more than I bid thee.”

He got hold of Denys’s long hair—streaming like river-weed—twisted it hard, and caught the end between his side teeth. With the stout muscles of his young neck he held Denys’s head up, while his arms and legs worked in a dogged rhythm against the current.

For a heartbeat he hung between two banks, ignorant of the dreadful weight of so small a choice; then, seeing the west side a shade nearer, he made for it—turning, without knowing it, from prison as a man turns from a road that would have changed his whole tale.

The Rhine swept them slantwise. After near a hundred yards Denys muttered uneasily, “How much more of it?”

“Courage,” Gerard mumbled round the hair between his teeth. “Whatever a duck knows, a Dutchman knows; art safe as in bed.”

And then, to their astonishment, their feet scraped gravel. The water slackened; their knees emerged; they waded ashore, dripping, choking, blinking like men hauled back into the world.

On terra firma they stared at one another from head to foot, as if their eyes could eat the sight and be satisfied. Then, by one impulse, they flung an arm round each other’s neck and panted there, hearts too full for speech.

In that raw moment, with river-water running down their sleeves and the air tasting of silt and reeds, life was sweet as heaven; sweetest, perhaps, to the poor exile with love in his breast, who had dragged his friend from death. There are joys no poet can climb to: to save a human life—and that life a loved one.

When at last they could breathe like men again, they took hands and walked along the bank hand in hand, like a pair of simple sweethearts, scarcely knowing or caring whither their feet carried them.

Out on the Rhine, the boat folk were all safe on the late concave, now convex craft—Herr Turnip-face, the Inverter of Things, sitting in the midst of the muddle as if the world had only performed a trick for his diversion. The whole uproar seemed not to have discomposed his habits: he had been greeting when he shot the two into the river, and he was greeting still when they crawled out again.

“Shall we wait till they right the boat?” asked Denys.

“No,” said Gerard. “Our fare is paid; we owe them nought. Let us on—and briskly.”

Denys agreed; it was easy enough, he said, to walk all the way to Cologne on this bank.

“I fare not to Cologne,” Gerard answered, very calmly.

“Why, whither then?”

“To Burgundy.”

“To Burgundy? Ah, no! that is too good to be sooth.”

“Sooth ’tis, and sense into the bargain. What matters it to me how I go to Rome?”

“Nay, nay; you but say so to pleasure me. The change is too sudden; and think me not so ill-hearted as take you at your word. Also did I not see your eyes sparkle at the wonders of Cologne?—the churches, the images, the relics—”

“How dull art thou, Denys; that was when we were to enjoy them together. Churches! I shall see plenty, go Rome-ward how I will. The bones of saints and martyrs—alas, the world is full of them; but a friend like thee, where on earth’s face shall I find another? No, I will not turn thee farther from the road that leads to thy dear home—and her that pines for thee. Neither will I rob myself of thee by leaving thee. Since I drew thee out of Rhine I love thee better than I did. Thou art my pearl: I fished thee; and must keep thee. So gainsay me not, or thou wilt bring back my fever; but cry courage, and lead on; and hey for Burgundy!”

Denys gave a joyful caper, as if the wet on his boots were a badge and not a discomfort. “Courage! va pour la Bourgogne. Oh! soyes tranquille! cette fois il est bien décidément mort, ce coquin-là.”

So they turned their backs on the Rhine.

But as the meaning of that turn made itself plain, there was commotion among the little group that had watched their parley from the far side. The friends had not gone many steps when a voice came over the water, sharp as a thrown stone.

“HALT!”

Gerard turned. One of the four held up a badge of office and a slip of parchment. The sight struck him in the pit like hunger: he was a good citizen by breeding and habit, and the voice that now called him back towards Düsseldorf was the Law’s.

Denys did not share the scruple. He was French; and he despised every other nation’s laws, inmates, and customs together. He was a soldier; he saw the situation as he would a field: force on the far bank, a river between, the rear open—retreat made easy. The boat still drifted mid-stream, and there was no ferry nearer than Düsseldorf.

“I shall beat a quick retreat to that hill,” he said, “and then, being out of sight, quick step.”

They sauntered off, as if their feet were ruled by leisure.

“Halt! in the bailiff’s name,” cried a voice from the shore.

Denys turned and snapped his fingers at the bailiff, and went on.

“Halt! in the archbishop’s name.”

Denys snapped his fingers at his grace, and went on.

“Halt! in the emperor’s name.”

Denys snapped his fingers at his majesty, and went on.

Gerard watched this needless pantomime with regret; but when they had passed the brow of the hill and were hidden from the river, he said, “There is now but one course: we must run to Burgundy instead of walking,” and set off at such a pace that the road itself seemed to fly under him. He ran the best part of a league without stopping.

Denys, for all his hardihood, was fairly blown. “What on earth,” he panted, “has become of thy fever? I begin to miss it sadly.”

“I dropped it in Rhine, I trow,” Gerard answered.

Towards evening they came to a little village. Denys went in and came out with a loaf under his arm and a huge bottle of Rhenish wine—green glass sweating in the cool air.

“For,” said he, “we must sleep in some hole or corner. If we lie at an inn, we shall be taken in our beds.”

It was plain prudence. In that age the official net for catching law-breakers—especially plebeian ones—was drawn tight indeed. The people might care little to help the law, but innkeepers were watched and questioned, and held answerable for strangers’ comings and goings far more than for their comfort.

Both were glad when the sun went down; and delighted when, after a long trudge under the stars (the moon, if one keeps true account, not rising until about three), they found a great barn belonging to a house some distance off.

A heap of barley had been lately threshed. On one side of the threshing-floor the straw lay piled almost as high as the unthreshed corn on the other. The barn held the day’s work in its smell—broken stalk, dry chaff, the faint sweetness of grain bruised under the flail, and that clean, warm breath straw gives off when you bury your hands in it.

“Here be two royal beds,” said Denys. “Which shall we lie on—the mow, or the straw?”

“The straw for me,” said Gerard at once.

They sat on the heap and ate their brown bread, coarse but honest, with that faint sourness of rye and the grit of bran that makes a hungry mouth rejoice. Denys uncorked the Rhenish; it came out with a small sigh, and the wine ran pale into their throats—sharp as apples, smelling of river hillsides and last year’s sun. It warmed them as if the Rhine itself, so murderous a moment ago, had been pressed into kindness and bottled for their need.

When they had finished, Denys covered Gerard in straw and heaped it high above him, leaving only a breathing hole.

“Water, they say, is death to fevered men,” Denys muttered, settling the straw with the care of an old campaigner arranging a bivouac. “I’ll make warm water on’t, anyhow.”

Gerard bade him easy. “These few drops from Rhine cannot chill me. I feel heat enough in my body now to parch a kennel—or boil a cloud if I was in one.”

With that little jest his senses slid away as if the straw had been a pillow of poppy; he might truly be said to fall asleep.

Denys lay awake awhile, listening. The barn creaked softly as it cooled; somewhere a night bird measured the dark with a thin call. Then there came a sound that changed the air—horses’ hoofs ringing up from Düsseldorf, the hard drumming of pursuit. The wooden barn vibrated as they rattled past, and with them rose the howling—one of those fierce cries men used to fling into the night in the fifteenth century, understood then as plainly as a trumpet-call, but now as strange in Europe as a red Indian’s war-whoop.

Denys shook where he lay. Gerard slept like a top.

The troop swept by; the hoof-beats dwindled; the howls died into distance. Denys drew a long breath, whistled in a whisper, and at last closed his eyes and slept like a top too.

In the morning he sat up and put out his hand to wake Gerard. His palm lit on the young man’s forehead—and found it quite wet. Denys, in his quality of nurse, forbore.

“It is ill to check sleep or sweat in a sick man,” he said softly to himself. “I know that far, though I ne’er minced ape nor gallows-bird.”

He waited a good hour, gnawed by hunger. At last, in sheer self-defence, he turned and went to sleep again; poor fellow, his hard life had taught him that trick—when there is nothing to eat, let sleep do the feeding.

At high noon he was waked by Gerard moving. Gerard sat up, and the straw about him smoked faintly, as a damp dung-hill smokes when the sun strikes it—animal heat wrestling with moisture.

“Lazy loon,” Gerard called Denys.

Denys only grinned, unoffended.

They set out again; and the first thing Denys did was give Gerard his arbalest and gear, then climb a high tree by the road. From that perch he listened and watched like a man reading a landscape as if it were a map.

“Coast clear to the next village,” he said, dropping down; and on they went.

As they drew near the village Denys halted and eyed Gerard. “How feelest thou?”

“What! can you not see? I feel as if Rome was no further than yon hamlet.”

“But thy body, lad—thy skin?”

“Neither hot nor cold; and yesterday ’twas hot one while and cold another. But what I cannot get rid of is this tiresome leg.”

“Le grand malheur! Many of my comrades have found no such difficulty.”

“Ah! there it goes again—itches consumedly.”

“Unhappy youth,” said Denys, with solemn relish in the pronouncement, “the sum of thy troubles is this: thy fever is gone, and thy wound is—healing. Sith so it is,” he added indulgently, “I shall tell thee a little piece of news I had otherwise withheld.”

“What is’t?” Gerard asked, his eyes bright with sudden fear and curiosity.

“THE HUE AND CRY IS OUT AFTER US: AND ON FLEET HORSES.”

“Oh!”
