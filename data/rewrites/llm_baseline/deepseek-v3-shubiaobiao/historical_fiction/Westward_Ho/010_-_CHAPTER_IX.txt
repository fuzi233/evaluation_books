*"Take aim, you noble musketeers,
And shoot you round about;
Stand to it, valiant pikemen,
And we shall keep them out.
There's not a man of all of us
A foot will backward flee;
I'll be the foremost man in fight,
Says brave Lord Willoughby!"*

—*Elizabethan Ballad*

The blessed Christmas afternoon waned into dusk. The last hymn of even-song had faded into silence, and the good folk of Bideford, wrapped in merriment, hurried homeward—fathers with laughing children, lovers arm-in-arm—to the warmth of roaring hearths, the clatter of ale-mugs, and the age-old revels of the season.

Yet one figure moved apart from the throng: a lone woman, swathed in a black muffler, her maid trailing behind, walking swiftly toward the bridge that led to Northam town. Sir Richard Grenville and his wife, catching sight of her, paused with courteous concern.

"Come home with us, Mrs. Leigh," urged Lady Grenville. "Spend a merry Christmas night beneath our roof."

Mrs. Leigh smiled faintly, then lifted a hand westward, where the dying sun had left streaks of emerald and gold upon the horizon.

"How can I be merry," she murmured, "while that sound fills my ears?"

The others turned. Above, the sky softened into twilight, pricked by early stars. But westward, where the downs of Raleigh met the woods of Abbotsham, the air trembled with a deep, unceasing roar—not wind, nor thunder, but the relentless voice of the sea, pounding the pebble ridge with the fury of an approaching storm.

"The ridge is restless tonight," observed Sir Richard. "There has been wind somewhere."

"There is wind now," whispered Mrs. Leigh, "where my boy is. God help him."

None had noticed the sound before, drowned as it was by the town’s clamor. But now they heard it—a low, ceaseless growl, the warning of the Atlantic’s wrath. Tomorrow, those same pebbles might be hurled like shot across the marsh, driven by the hurricane’s lash.

"God keep him safe," she breathed again.

"God is as near him at sea as on land," said Sir Richard gently.

But the mother only shook her head and walked on, her heart too heavy for company. That night, she knelt in prayer, listening between her whispered pleas to the thunder of the surge—until, long before dawn, it was swallowed by the howling storm.

And where was Amyas on that Christmas afternoon?

Bareheaded in the stern of a pitching boat, spray stinging his cheeks, he laughed into the gale. "Pull, lads, and with a will! Never mind the sea—cannonballs won’t spoil for a wetting!"

His mother’s foreboding had been true. The still, heavy nights of early winter had broken; for twelve hours, the western gale had raged along the Irish coast.

Before him lay Smerwick Bay, half-shrouded in mist. Behind, the waves lashed themselves into fury, their crests torn into froth. The cliffs loomed black, veiled by sheets of salt spray. And out at sea, a sloping roof of cloud stretched inland, swallowing the Kerry mountains whole.

But Amyas cared little for the storm. His eyes were fixed on the sand-hills where Salvation Yeo’s guns barked defiance at the Spanish fort—*Fort Del Oro*, they called it, where the hated golden flag of Spain still flew. Three nights past, Amyas and Yeo had wrestled four culverins ashore from the *Admiral’s* deck, hauling them up to the battery under cover of darkness. Now, let the Dons see how Italian mercenaries fared on British soil against Devon men!

A flash, a roar—then a cheer as the Spanish flagstaff shuddered, toppled, and crashed into the surf. But before the English shouts had died, a tall officer sprang onto the parapet, lance in hand, and raised the fallen banner anew.

A dozen English bows drew at once—but Amyas halted them. "Hold! Let the man have his honor!"

Leaping onto the rampart, he doffed his hat in salute. The Spaniard, his duty done, returned the courtesy and vanished into the smoke.

Darkness fell; the firing slackened. The gunners covered their pieces and retired, leaving Amyas to keep watch. The men, gnawing stale biscuit (for rations ran low), grumbled themselves to sleep in the sand.

Pacing the battery, Amyas hummed a carol under his breath—until two figures approached, deep in debate. One wore full armor; the other, a scholar’s plain cloak. Yet their talk was not of war, but of *hexameters*.

For in that year of 1580, a battle raged in letters fiercer than any field—between the old rhyming ballads and the newfangled "reformed versifying," which sought to chain English verse to classical measures.

"Listen to our young giant," chuckled Raleigh, as Amyas’s voice rose clear above the wind:

*"As Joseph was a-walking
He heard an angel sing—
‘This night shall be the birth-night
Of Christ, our heavenly King...’"*

"*There* is poetry, Spenser! Not your prinking *‘Wote ye why his mother with a veil hath covered his face?’*"

Spenser sighed. "How he shames me! Singing his mother’s hymns before Spanish guns, while I—"

"—whine sonnets to some blue-eyed lass?" Raleigh grinned. "Come, man! Fairyland is not in Virgil’s pages—it’s here, where love and loyalty stand fast, even in a hovel. Or a sand-battery."

But Spenser’s thoughts were darker. "I waste my hours on trifles, while men like Leigh here *live* the songs I strain to write."

"Then put him in your *Faerie Queene*," scoffed Raleigh. "He’d make as fine a knight as any Guyon!"

Amyas, overhearing, laughed. "God forbid! I’d sooner face a broadside than a sonnet."

The night deepened. The rain fell in sheets. The sentinels stamped their feet; the sleeping men muttered in their dreams.

Then—a rustle in the grass. Not the wind. A sliding sound, as of stealthy feet. Amyas froze, ear pressed to the wet rampart.

Whether rabbit or Spaniard, he would soon know. Hand on his sword, he peered into the blackness—ready.
