Among Deronda's correspondence the following morning was a lengthy missive from Hans Meyrick, four quarto pages penned in the small, exquisite hand distinctive to his family.

MY DEAR DERONDA,—In exchange for your sketch of Italian movements and your broader views on worldly affairs, I report from home that the most prudent local sentiment regarding current causes is simply "time will show." As for the origins of past effects, the infamous swindling telegrams have now been linked to last year's cattle plague—a refutation of false philosophy and a justification for farmer compensation. My own theory, that a murrain will soon afflict the commercial class and reveal itself via the sudden sale of rejected paintings, has been dismissed as an unsound analogy. Yet, there are minds unwilling to deprive a neglected painter of his solace. To my sensibilities, there is profound beauty in the notion that some bad judge might bid high for my *Berenice* series, thereby punishing the men of the City for my ill-merited luck.

Meanwhile, I console myself for your absence by discovering advantages therein—shining like Hesperus when Hyperion departs. I sit with our Hebrew prophet, studying the contours of his head in the hours formerly occupied by you, earning credit as a learned young Gentile who would have been Jewish if circumstances allowed. I agree with him in the general principle that whatever is best is, by definition, Jewish. Though I lack the *forte* for severe reasoning, I see the logic: if the best is A, and B happens to be best, then B must be A. On this principle, I could have endorsed a pamphlet proving all good art is Protestant. However, our prophet is an uncommonly interesting sitter—a superior model to Rembrandt's Rabbi—and I depart from him with new discoveries. It constantly amazes me that, despite his fiery devotion to his race and traditions, he is not a rigid, spittle-spewing Jew anticipating the roasted Leviathan to dry up the Gentile tongue, nor sending up endless plates *ad libitum*. (One perceives that my studies prepare one for the Orthodox Jew.) I confess I have always treated your account of Mordecai as somewhat apologetic, a mere antediluvian point of view designed to spare the *megatherium* from injustice. But hearing him in his proper person, I find him a philosophical-allegorical-mystical believer, yet possessing a sharp dialectic point capable of silencing any argumentative rattler of peas in a bladder. This mixture may be a Jewish prerogative. Indeed, his mind seems so broad that my own correct opinions lie within it comfortably; how to align them with the vast remainder is his affair, not mine. I leave the settlement of our basis to him, for I have never encountered a basis that was not a world-supporting elephant, costly and cumbersome to maintain. My finances preclude keeping a private elephant, so I retreat into mystery—a cheap, lasting gas derived from decomposing elephants. If I favor an opinion, I treat it civilly, sparing suspicious inquiry. I harbor a genuine fondness for Mordecai's notion that a whole Christian is three-quarters a Jew, and that since Alexandria, the most comprehensive minds have been Jewish. I intend to point out to Mirah that, aside from Arabic and other incidental differences, there is little distinction between me and Maimonides.

However, I have recently observed that your shallow lover cannot help making declarations. If Mirah's ways were less distracting, and her presence less of a heaven to behold, I would long have thrown myself at her feet, begging her to tell me, with less indirectness, whether she wished me to blow my brains out. I possess a knack of hope, equivalent to an estate in reversion, provided one resists the temptation of transforming it into certainty, which may ruin everything. My Hope wanders among orchard blossoms, feels warm snow falling upon it in the sunshine, and doubts nothing. But when Certainty appears in the distance, revealed as an ugly, Janus-faced deity offering a dubious wink, I turn away swiftly. You, with your supreme reasonableness, self-nullification, and preparation for the worst, know nothing of this immortal, delicious maiden, forever courted and forever propitious, whom fools have called deceitful, as if Hope carries the cup of disappointment. It is her deadly enemy, Certainty, whom she escapes only through transformation. (Do you observe my new vein of allegory?) Seriously, however, I must allege that truth prevails, prejudice melts before it, diversity accompanied by merit becomes fascinating, and no virtuous aspiration fails—all doctrines of the schools implying that the Jewess I prefer will prefer me. Any blockhead cites generalities, but the mind-master discerns the particular cases they represent.

I am less convinced that my company compensates Mordecai for your absence, but occasionally Jacob Cohen intrudes as a substitute. It is worthwhile to catch our prophet's expression when this remarkable young Israel sits on his knee, pouring forth Semitic inspiration with a sublime look of melancholy patience and devoutness. Sometimes Jacob considers Hebrew more edifying if he plugs his ears and imitates the muffled sounds, or if he holds his fists tight. When Mordecai gently draws down the little fists, Jacob's features erupt in extraordinary activity, resembling a man walking through a menagerie, succeeding best with the owl and the peccary. You have surely seen this. He treats me with familiar ease, generally viewing me as a second-hand Christian commodity likely to depreciate, remarking on my disadvantages with a frankness implying thoughts of future purchase. It is charming, however, to see the change in him if Mirah enters. He instantly becomes a child—though his age usually resembles ancient Israelitish garments, perhaps nearing forty, he retains an air of recent production. With Mirah, he reminds me of dogs raised by women, manageable only by them. Yet, the dog is fond of Mordecai, bringing sugar-plums to share, filling his own mouth to an embarrassing extent while watching how Mordecai handles a smaller supply. Judging from this modern Jacob at six, I am astonished that his race has not purchased us all long ago, pocketing our feeble generations as stock and scrip, akin to slave property. There is one Jewess I should not mind being enslaved to. Yet I wish I did not imagine that Mirah grows a little sadder, striving to hide it. It is natural, considering she watches the slow death of this brother, whom she worships with looks of loving devoutness, leading me to wish myself in his place.

For the rest, we are slightly merrier than usual. Rex Gascoigne, whom you recall admiring in my sketches, a fellow with a fine upper lip studying law, now occupies rooms nearby, hosting a neat sister (also with a good upper lip). I have introduced them to my mother and the girls, who discovered from Miss Gascoigne that she is a cousin to your Vandyke Duchess!!! I marked the exclamation points to denote the initial shock of this information upon my feeble understanding. Upon reflection, however, I found no ground for surprise, unless I previously believed that no one could be anyone's cousin without my knowledge. This specific surprise, I conclude, depends on a lively spine coupled with a variable nullity of the brain. I recall a fellow I met in Rome, effervescent with surprise at the simplest information. Tell him you are fond of easy boots; he will invariably reply, "No! Are you?" with the same energetic wonder. He is the very fellow of whom pastoral Browne prophesied: *"A wretch so empty that if e'er there be / In nature found the least vacuity / 'Twill be in him."* I have accounted for it all—he possessed a lively spine.

Nevertheless, this cousinship with the Duchess emerged by chance one day when Mirah visited, and they discussed the Mallingers. *Apropos*; I am becoming so important that I have rival invitations. Gascoigne invites me to his father's rectory in August to view the countryside. But self-interest, well understood, directs me to Topping Abbey, for Sir Hugo has invited me, proposing—with God bless him for his rashness!—that I paint his three daughters sitting on a bank, in the Gainsborough style. He visited my studio recently, recommending I apply myself to portraiture. Of course, I know what he means. "My good fellow, your attempts at the historic and poetic are simply pitiable. Your brush is that of a successful portrait-painter—it possesses a little truth and a great facility in falsehood—your idealism will never suffice for gods and heroes, but it may fetch a high price as flattery. Fate, my friend, has made you the hind wheel—*rota posterior curras*—run behind, because you cannot help it." What effort it costs our friends to offer such candid opinions! I have even known a man summon me specifically to declare that I had irretrievably exposed my want of judgment, suggesting that if I had asked, we would have lent him our own. Such was my ingratitude and readiness at composition that, even as he spoke, I inwardly sketched a Last Judgment featuring that candid friend's physiognomy on the left. Yet, all this pales beside Sir Hugo, whose manner of implying one's gifts are not of the highest order is so exceedingly good-natured and comforting that I begin to feel it an advantage not to be among those poor fellows at the tip-top.
