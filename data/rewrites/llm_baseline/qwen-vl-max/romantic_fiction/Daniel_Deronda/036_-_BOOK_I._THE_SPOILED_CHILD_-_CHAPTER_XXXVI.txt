“Nothing weighs so heavily as a secret. Carrying it far is difficult for ladies; and I know, indeed, on this very point, many men who are women.” —LA FONTAINE.

While Deronda was being led away by Mr. Vandernoodt—eager for a brisk walk, a cigar, and a bit of idle talk—the two men stepped out into the cool evening air. There is something about being in another’s company that stirs a man to confide in someone less informed, and soon enough, Mr. Vandernoodt leaned forward with a sly glint in his eye.

“What a faded piece of lace Grandcourt is!” he declared. “Though, if he’s your friend, I’ll retract the remark.”

Deronda shook his head. “Not at all. He’s not my favorite.”

“I suspected as much,” said Vandernoodt. “Still, one wonders how such a fellow could fall so deeply again—and marry like this. Lush, his old pal, hints it was mere obstinacy. By George! That would be a very understandable kind of stubbornness. A man might decide to marry her without needing anyone to provoke him. But you must admit he’s spent a good deal of money on it, eh?”

“I know nothing of his affairs,” Deronda replied.

“What? Not even about the other place he keeps up?”

“Diplow? Of course. He took it over from Sir Hugo. But only for a year.”

“No, no—not Diplow. Gadsmere. Sir Hugo knows, I assure you.”

Deronda said nothing. He felt a flicker of curiosity, but sensed that whatever Vandernoodt had to say would come out sooner or later, whether he asked or not.

“Lush wouldn’t admit it openly, naturally. He’s Grandcourt’s go-between, you see. But I’ve heard it from the best source. The truth is, there’s another woman at Gadsmere—with four children. She’s been holding sway over him for ten years or more, and still does, so far as I can tell. Left her husband for him, traveled with him everywhere. Her husband’s dead now—I met a fellow officer who knew her before she left him. A fiery dark-eyed woman—quite beautiful in her day—he thought she’d died. People say she still has Grandcourt under her thumb. It’s amazing he didn’t marry her. There’s a fine boy, and I understand Grandcourt can do exactly as he pleases with the estate. Lush told me as much.”

“What right did he have to marry this girl?” Deronda asked, voice tight with distaste.

Vandernoodt adjusted his cigar, shrugged, and pursed his lips.

“She couldn’t possibly know,” Deronda insisted, almost too quickly. But instantly, the thought struck him: *Could she have known?*

“It’s rather a fascinating picture,” Vandernoodt mused. “Grandcourt caught between two passionate women. And believe me, this fair-haired one has fire in her. I saw it at Leubronn. It’s like Medea and Creüsa—only with a modern twist. Imagine them meeting! Grandcourt’s a new kind of Jason. I wonder what role he’ll play. At best, it’s a dog’s part. I can almost hear Ristori now, shouting, ‘Jasone! Jasone!’ These strong women always end up with a stick.”

“Grandcourt can bite,” said Deronda. “He’s no passive thing.”

“No, no—Jason, I meant. I can’t quite make sense of Grandcourt. But he’s sharp enough—well-built, too. If he inherits all this property, the estates could be divided. This girl, whose family sank into poverty, may feel lucky to have him. I don’t want to judge harshly—men get tangled in these things. But he could be more pleasant. Last night I told him a brilliant story, and he got up and walked off halfway through. I nearly kicked him. Do you think it was indifference—or arrogance?”

“A mixture,” Deronda said. “He follows the rules, but he doesn’t really listen.”

After a pause, he added, “I suspect there’s some exaggeration in what you’ve heard about this woman at Gadsmere.”

“None at all,” Vandernoodt insisted. “It’s been quiet for years. People forgot. But the nest is still there—the birds are in it. And I know Grandcourt goes there. I’ve solid proof. Still, it’s none of our business. The affair has sunk beneath the surface.”

“I’m surprised you learned so much,” Deronda remarked dryly.

“Oh, plenty knew. But stories like this are tucked away like old letters. They interest me. I like knowing how people live now—contemporary gossip, not ancient legends. Those academic types build reputations by digging up scandals about Semiramis or Nitocris, then we get a thousand poems from every amateur poet. But I don’t care about the missteps of mummies. You do, though—you’re one of the historical sort. More interested in a lady when she’s got wrinkles and bony toes showing. Does that flatter your imagination?”

“Well, if she suffered in love, at least we know she’s free of it now,” Deronda replied.

“Ah,” said Vandernoodt, “you’re thinking of Medea.”

Deronda changed the subject, pointing to a cluster of towering oaks, bare and stark against the twilight sky. He found himself drawn to the tale—yet sensed Vandernoodt had said all he intended.

Since his earliest attempts to unravel the mystery of his own origins, Deronda had never been so absorbed in piecing together another’s hidden life as he was now, trying to understand Gwendolen’s marriage. This secret relationship of Grandcourt’s—had she known about it? Was it the reason she hesitated, only to yield under pressure of need? He replayed their conversations in his mind, noticing nuances in her words—moments where she seemed to acknowledge guilt, to carry the weight of having caused harm. His own pain gave him insight into the suffering such a situation might bring upon the children and their mother. Could Mrs. Grandcourt, beneath her composed facade, be consumed by grief—by shame, disappointment, jealousy? He dwelled especially on signs of remorse, feeling inclined to forgive, to pity. He believed he had found a key to understanding her better: how crushing must it be for a young woman to bind her hopes to a man burdened by secrets? He now understood why Sir Hugo had never mentioned this affair to him. And instantly, the image of Mrs. Glasher merged with his own buried past.

Gwendolen knowing of that woman and her children, marrying Grandcourt, and appearing satisfied—such a woman would have repulsed him. But Gwendolen tasting regret, mourning the harm she may have caused—she drew close to his heart. If true, she had reached a shared depth of understanding about life’s hidden struggles—a realm most women rarely grasp with fairness or generosity. Traditionally, Gwendolen might have seen her marriage as Grandcourt’s redemption, while Mrs. Glasher represented his abandoned sin. But Deronda felt a quiet anger on behalf of those cast aside—like Hagar and Ishmael.

Undeniably, Deronda’s growing concern for Gwendolen stemmed largely from the way she treated him. And few, man or woman, would benefit from total indifference to such appeal. One sign his feelings had deepened was that he dismissed any fear she might be a coquette setting traps. He resolved to meet her honestly, to speak freely whenever the chance arose.

He slipped away from Vandernoodt and wandered into a quiet corner, lost in thought. Half an hour passed swiftly, filled with speculation about Gwendolen’s state of mind. Then he remembered: she would likely be at tea with the ladies in the drawing-room.

His guess proved correct. Gwendolen, after deciding not to descend for four hours, began to feel restless after just one. Shutting herself away meant missing everything—seeing, hearing, connecting. Her visit would last only two more days. She straightened herself, restored her composure, and descended, determined to be agreeable.

Only women were present, and Lady Pentreath entertained them with tales of Regency drawing-rooms and the fashion of 1819—the year she was presented. As Deronda entered, she paused.

“Shall I be welcome?” he asked. “Perhaps I should return and find the others. They’re probably in the billiard-room.”

“No, no,” Lady Pentreath said. “They were tired of me. Let us hear what _you_ have to say.”

“That’s rather an awkward request,” Deronda replied, pulling up a chair beside Lady Mallinger. “But perhaps I should mention our songstress—unless you’ve already spoken of her.”

“Oh, the little Jewess?” Lady Mallinger said. “No, I haven’t. Never occurred to me that anyone here needed singing lessons.”

“All ladies know someone who does,” Deronda said. “I’ve found a truly exquisite singer”—he turned to Lady Pentreath—“living with friends of mine—the mother and sisters of a Cambridge chum. She performed in Vienna, but wants to leave the stage and support herself through teaching.”

“There are plenty of such people,” the older woman remarked. “Are her lessons cheap or expensive? Those are the only two hooks I know.”

“There’s a third,” Deronda said. “Her singing is exceptional. She has such excellent training—or rather, such natural talent combined with training—that you’d swear it came entirely by instinct.”

“Why did she leave the stage?” Lady Pentreath asked. “I can’t believe first-rate talent gives up first-rate opportunities.”

“Her voice was too weak,” Deronda said. “Perfect for a room. You who endure my Schubert would be enchanted by hers,” he added, looking at Mrs. Raymond. “She could sing at private gatherings or concerts. Her voice is fully capable of that.”

“I’ll have her in my drawing-room when we go to town,” Lady Mallinger said. “You shall hear her. I haven’t yet, but I trust Daniel’s judgment. My girls will take lessons from her.”

“Is it a charitable venture?” Lady Pentreath asked. “I detest charitable music.”

Lady Mallinger, uncomfortable and bound not to reveal Mirah’s story, smiled faintly and glanced at Deronda.

“It’s charity to those who want a model of true feminine singing,” Deronda said. “Everyone with ears would benefit from refining their taste. If you heard Miss Lapidoth”—he looked at Gwendolen—“perhaps you’d reconsider giving up singing.”

“I’d likely confirm my decision,” Gwendolen replied. “I don’t feel able to enjoy my own mediocrity.”

“For my part,” Deronda said, “those who do anything well inspire me to try. Not because I think I can match them—but because they make the pursuit seem worthwhile. I can accept my own music isn unlikely to matter much, but the world would be duller if I thought music itself wasn’t worth anything. Excellence lifts life—it shows the soul’s richness.”

“But if we can’t imitate it,” Gwendolen said, irritated, “it only makes our own lives seem smaller.”

“That depends on perspective,” Deronda replied. “We’d have a poor life if we relied solely on our own performances. A little imitation of excellence is like private devotion. Most of us should practice art not to perform, but to understand and appreciate what others achieve. I think Miss Lapidoth is one of those rare ones.”

“She must be very happy,” Gwendolen said, with a hint of sarcasm, turning toward Mrs. Raymond.

“I can’t say,” Mrs. Raymond answered. “I’d need to know more.”

“It must have been painful,” Juliet Fenn murmured, “that her voice failed her.”

“I suppose she’s past her prime,” Lady Pentreath said.

“On the contrary,” Deronda said. “She hasn’t reached it. She’s barely twenty.”

“And very pretty,” Lady Mallinger interjected, eager to help. “And well-mannered. I’m sorry she’s a devout Jewess—I wouldn’t wish it for anyone else, but it doesn’t matter in singing.”

“Well, since her voice won’t let her scream much,” Lady Pentreath said, “I’ll ask Lady Clementina to put her on my nine granddaughters. I hope she convinces eight that they lack voice enough to sing anywhere but church. My belief is that many girls today need lessons not to sing.”

“I’ve had my share,” Gwendolen said, looking at Deronda. “See? Lady Pentreath is on my side.”

As she spoke, Sir Hugo entered with several gentlemen, including Grandcourt, and stood near the tea table.

“What imposition is Deronda putting on you, ladies—slipping in alone among you?”

“Wanting to pass off an obscurity as better than any celebrity,” Lady Pentreath retorted. “A Jewish singer who’s supposed to astonish these young people. You and I, who heard Catalani at her peak, aren’t easily amazed.”

Sir Hugo smiled, took a cup of tea from his wife, and said, “Well, a Liberal must believe there have been singers since Catalani.”

“Ah, you’re younger than I am. Perhaps you chased Alcharisi. But she married and left you all behind.”

“Yes, yes,” Sir Hugo said. “It’s cruel when great singers marry into silence before their voices crack. The husband becomes a public thief. Leroux once said, ‘A man might as well take down a fine peal of church bells and carry them off to the steppes.’” He set down his cup and moved away.

Deronda, who had shifted to make room, sat apart, sensing he was no longer wanted. Then he noticed Gwendolen had slipped away from Vandernoodt and stood at the piano, examining the sheet music.

Was she inviting him to join her? Perhaps she wished to make amends for her earlier resistance to his praise of Mirah—something he often noticed: her first impulse was to reject, then regret.

He approached.

“Looking for something to play or sing?”

“I’m not looking for anything,” she said softly, “but I *am* relenting.”

“May I know why?”

“I’d like to hear Miss Lapidoth—and take lessons from her, if you admire her so much. When we go to town, I mean. Lessons in rejoicing at her excellence and accepting my own shortcomings,” she added, offering him a warm, open smile.

“I’ll be glad you do,” Deronda said, returning her smile.

“Is she perfect in everything else?”

“I can’t vouch for that. I’ve seen little of her. But I’ve seen nothing I’d wish to change. She’s endured hardship since childhood, grown up amid pain. Yet I think you’ll find no advantages could have given her more grace or refinement.”

“What sort of troubles did she face?”

“I don’t know precisely. But I know she once stood on the edge of drowning herself in despair.”

“And what stopped her?” Gwendolen asked quickly, eyes fixed on him.

“A ray of light—something that made her feel she ought to live, that life was worth living,” Deronda said quietly. “She’s full of piety, and seems ready to submit to anything when it’s framed as duty.”

“Those people aren’t to be pitied,” Gwendolen said impatiently. “I have no sympathy for women who always do the right thing. I don’t believe in their great sufferings.” Her fingers fluttered over the edges of the music.

“It’s true,” Deronda said, “that knowing one has done wrong cuts deeper. We flawed creatures can never feel as much for the irreproachable as for those crushed by their own faults. It’s an ancient story—the lost sheep—but it repeats every day.”

“That’s just talk,” Gwendolen said bitterly. “It’s not real. You admire Miss Lapidoth because you think her flawless. And you’d despise a woman who’d done something you consider wrong.”

“That would depend entirely on her own view of her actions,” Deronda said.

“You’d be content if she were miserable,” she snapped.

“No,” he said gently. “Not content—full of sorrow for her. I didn’t mean to suggest the pure aren’t admirable. But those who might seem uninteresting can become worthy of compassion when they face deep remorse. Lives grow in different ways. Some never wake up unless shaken by the consequences of their choices. And when they suffer, we must care more for them than for the self-satisfied.”

Deronda forgot everything except the vision of Gwendolen’s possible torment. Compassion swelled in him, and his voice and eyes betrayed his concern.

Gwendolen had slipped onto the music stool and looked up at him, her long eyes filled with pain—like a wounded creature seeking help.

“Are you coaxing Mrs. Grandcourt to play for us, Dan?” Sir Hugo asked, stepping up and placing a hand on Deronda’s shoulder with a gentle, teasing squeeze.

“I cannot persuade myself,” Gwendolen said, rising.

Others followed Sir Hugo, ending any chance of intimacy for the evening.

But the next day was New Year’s Eve—and a grand ball was to be held in the gallery above the cloister, where numbers and movement could create pockets of privacy. As Gwendolen dressed, she longed to wear the old turquoise necklace—her only keepsake from Leubronn—but dared not offend Grandcourt by appearing shabby on a night he expected brilliance. Instead, she wound it thrice around her wrist, making it a bracelet—putting it on just before entering the ballroom.

The dance was always a breathtaking sight, preserved by family tradition as closely as possible. Red carpet covered the floor; tropical plants and evergreens formed bowers at each end and in every alcove; and generations of ancestral portraits lined the walls, silent spectators from centuries past. Neighboring gentry, high and low, were invited—making it a moment when the future master and mistress of Abbott’s and King’s Topping could glimpse their destiny: a picturesque provincial rule, embodied by prosperous tenants.

Sir Hugo hoped Grandcourt would feel flattered to be welcomed to the Abbey during this festival honoring the family estate. He also hoped his own vigor would remind his successor that the inheritance was distant—and that wisdom lay in accepting a substantial sum now, rather than waiting for a lesser title.

All present, from the humblest farmer’s daughter to the highest noble, knew they were seeing “young Grandcourt”—Sir Hugo’s nephew, presumptive heir and future baronet—now visiting with his bride after years away. Any tension between uncle and nephew had melted into cordial warmth.

The bride opening the ball with Sir Hugo became the center of attention. Less than a year ago, if a magic mirror had shown Gwendolen her fate, she would have imagined herself glowing with triumph, confident she could shape a brilliant future with her wit and spirit.

Now, she wondered why she felt so little joy in the exaltation she had dreamed of—so far from the petty, stifling world of her youth, with its endless sisters and lack of distinction. She longed to feel wildly elated, to forget everything but the moment’s glory. But her thoughts clung like persistent tormentors, refusing sleep.

Wandering through lines of admiring guests in the country dance that traditionally opened the ball, she was watched by women who envied her—this woman who had risen from nothing, without a penny, yet carried herself with astonishing grace. Even if she had been a princess, she could not have claimed the evening’s honors with greater ease.

Poor Gwendolen. Soon, it would become a skill—automatically practiced—to bear this final, great loss with perfect composure.
