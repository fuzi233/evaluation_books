Between what the world judges as meaningful, and what only seems to matter, how can any jury of ordinary people hope to see clearly? When Sigsbee Manderson — the indomitable, scheming financial titan — was gunned down by an unknown hand, the wider world lost nothing worth shedding a single tear for. All it gained was a harsh, unforgettable reminder of the vanity of the vast fortune he had spent his life hoarding: he died without one loyal friend to mourn him, without one single public act that might have left his memory even a scrap of honor. But when word of his death first spread, the men who lived and worked in the great churning vortex of global finance felt as if the earth itself had shuddered under their feet.

No figure in all the lurid, bloody history of American commerce had ever imposed itself so completely on the mind of the trading world. He had a niche all his own in the temples of high finance. Financial giants before him had grown great by directing and multiplying the power of capital, pocketing legitimate fortunes in millions for their labor. But Manderson was different: a pale, incongruous halo of piratical romance clung to him for decades, the kind of romance his countrymen loved most, even as he was universally seen as the unshakable guardian of market stability, the man who stamped out manipulated panics, the sworn enemy of the raiding chieftains who lurked at the borders of Wall Street.

His fortune had begun with his grandfather, one of those early pirate traders from the Gold Rush era, and passed down untouched through his father, who lived a quiet, long life lending money and never once margining a single stock. Manderson never knew a time in his life when he did not have huge sums of money at his disposal, and he should have fit perfectly into the newer American plutocracy, steady and grounded in generations of inherited wealth. But that was not the case. His upbringing and education had given him European ideas of how a rich man ought to live: a taste for quiet magnificence, the kind of grandeur that never shouts its own presence. But he had inherited too much of his Forty-Niner, financial buccaneer ancestor. In the first wild stretch of his career, dubbed his “early bad manners” period, he was little more than a genius gambler, his hand turned against every man on the Street: an infant prodigy who brought a sharper, more capable brain to the thrall of speculation than any opponent who dared face him. Napoleon had called war a glorious occupation on Saint Helena, and young Manderson felt exactly the same about the chaotic, dog-eat-dog brawl of the New York Stock Exchange.

Then came the shift. When his father died, and Manderson was thirty years old, he seemed to experience a sudden, blinding revelation of the power and glory of the god of finance he served. With the elastic, quick adaptability so typical of his country, he turned his back on the Street’s wild battles and settled down to steady, sober labor in his father’s old banking firm. Within a few years he controlled every part of the great institution, whose unimpeachable conservatism and financial weight stood like a solid cliff above the roiling, angry sea of the markets. All old mistrust of his wild youth faded. He was plainly a new man. No one could say for certain how the change had come about, though stories circulated of final words spoken by his father — the only man Manderson had ever respected, and perhaps loved.

He began to tower over the entire global financial system. Soon his name was spoken on every bourse in the world. To say the name Manderson was to evoke every broad, unshakable pillar of America’s vast national wealth. He built great combined capital trusts, centralized industries spanning entire continents, and financed large state and private enterprise projects with unerring judgement. More than once, when he “took hold” to smash a labor strike or federate ownership of an entire major industry, he sent ruin crashing down on thousands of small working homes. If miners, steelworkers, or cattlemen defied him and sparked unrest, he could be more ruthless and lawless than any of them. But this was all done in the name of legitimate business. Tens of thousands of poor people might curse his name, but financiers and speculators no longer feared or hated him. He stretched a hand to protect or manipulate the levers of wealth in every corner of the country. Forcible, cold, and unerring, he fed the national American hunger for bigness — and a grateful public dubbed him the Colossus.

But there was a side of Manderson in this later era that no one knew about outside a tiny circle: his secretaries, lieutenants, and a handful of old associates from his wilder, younger days. That small circle knew that Manderson, the great pillar of stable, sound business, had bouts of nostalgia for the old wild days when the entire Street shook at his name. One of them compared it to Blackbeard settling down as a respectable merchant in Bristol, living off plunder from the Main. Every so often, the pirate would glare out from behind the respectable mask, knife clamped in his teeth, sulphur matches sputtering in his hatband. During these brief lapses, he and his inner circle would draft plans for a dozen massive, tempestuous market raids in the back room of Manderson, Colefax and Company. But he never carried a single one of them out. Blackbeard would quell the mutiny of his old wild self, and go back soberly to his accounts — maybe humming a line or two of the old sea shanty *Spanish Ladies* under his breath. He allowed himself one harmless indulgence: once the window for action had passed, he would point out to some hotshot market raider that a single coup could have netted him a million dollars. “Seems to me,” he would say, almost wistfully, “the Street’s gotten a mighty dull place since I quit.”

Slowly, the financial world learned of this gentle quirk of the Colossus, and loved the story for it.

The news of his death hit the markets like a hurricane, and it came at the worst possible moment. Prices tottered and crashed like towers in an earthquake. For two full days Wall Street was a clamorous inferno of pale, desperate despair. All across the United States, wherever speculators gathered, a wave of ruin spread, and a plague of suicides followed. Even in Europe, dozens of men took their own lives, their fates pitifully tangled to the destiny of a financier most of them had never seen. In Paris, a well-known banker walked calmly out of the Bourse and dropped dead on the broad steps, a crushed phial of poison in his hand, surrounded by raving crowds. In Frankfort, a man jumped from the top of the Cathedral, leaving a dark red stain on the red stone tower where he landed. Men stabbed, shot, or strangled themselves, drank poison or breathed gas to escape, all because in a quiet corner of rural England, the life had left a single cold heart that had spent itself in service to greed.

The blow could not have fallen at a more disastrous time. Wall Street had already been holding its breath for days, just barely suppressing a coming slump. The great interests known to work for the Colossus had been fighting to contain the damage from Lucas Hahn’s sudden arrest, and the exposure of his massive embezzlement from the Hahn banks. That bombshell had dropped right when the market had been artificially inflated far beyond its real strength. In Street slang, a slump was long overdue. Corn harvest reports were poor, and two or three railway earnings statements had been far worse than investors had expected. But every time the first faint tremors of a market crash rippled through speculation, “the Manderson crowd” stepped in and propped prices up. For four feverish days, the shallow, quick-witted, greed-addicted speculators assumed the great Manderson himself was pulling all the strings from his holiday home. Newspapers all sang the same tune: Manderson was in constant hourly communication with his lieutenants in New York. One paper even published exact, round figures for the amount of money spent on transatlantic cables between New York and Manderson’s holiday home in Marlstone, England in the last 24 hours, claiming the Post Office had sent a full team of expert telegraph operators down to the tiny village to handle the flood of messages. Another revealed that Manderson had planned to abandon his holiday and sail home on the Lusitania when the Hahn crash broke, but that he had gotten the situation so well under control he’d decided to stay.

All of it was a lie, elaborated by finance editors at the prompting of Manderson’s inner circle, who knew the illusion of the great hero watching over their shoulders was the best possible tool to stop panic. The truth was, none of them had heard a single word from Manderson in days. Howard B. Jeffrey, the steel and iron tycoon, was the one running the whole rescue operation. By Saturday, Jeffrey thought his work was almost done. The market was firm, and slowly climbing again. Wall Street went to its Sunday rest, worn out but relieved, at peace.

At the start of trading on Monday, a hideous rumor spread across the sixty acres of the financial district out of nowhere, like lightning. Most suspected it had first been whispered over a telephone by a cable service employee, who passed it along right alongside a massive urgent sell order. Share prices convulsed in a single sharp spasm. In five minutes, the low murmur of the curb market in Broad Street had risen to a frantic, high-pitched din of confused terror. Inside the New York Stock Exchange itself, a droning hubbub of fear swelled, and men rushed hatless in and out of the building. Was it true? every man asked, and every trembling man answered that it was a lie, planted by some unscrupulous short seller trying to cover their own losses.

Fifteen minutes later, news arrived that share prices in London had collapsed completely right at the end of their trading day. That was all it took. New York still had four full hours of trading left. The whole strategy of propping the market up with the illusion of Manderson as their savior backfired completely, annihilating all of Jeffrey’s hard work. He stood with his ear pressed to his private telephone, jaw set, listening to the story of total disaster unfold. The new Napoleon had lost his Marengo. He watched the entire financial landscape slide and crumble into chaos before his eyes. Half an hour later, the news that Manderson’s body had been found, paired with the inevitable rumor of suicide, was rolling off the presses at a dozen newspaper offices — but before a single copy could reach Wall Street, the full tornado of panic was raging, and Jeffrey and his team were being swept away like dry leaves in the gale.

All of it had sprung from nothing. Nothing in the texture of ordinary, real life had changed. The corn still ripened in the sun. Rivers carried barges and turned the wheels of a million engines. Flocks fattened on pastures, and herds numbered in the millions grazed undisturbed. Men labored at their work all across the world, no more chafing at their lot than they had the week before. The gods of war tossed and murmured restlessly, but still slept. To all the world but a million or two half-crazed, reality-blind gamblers, Manderson’s death meant nothing at all. The work of the world went on. Weeks before he died, strong hands had already been in control of every wire in the vast network of commerce and industry he had once overseen. Before his corpse was even buried, his countrymen had made a strange discovery: the existence of the great monopoly engine known as Sigsbee Manderson had never been a requirement for national prosperity, not even of the financial kind.

The panic burned itself out in two days. The pieces were picked up, the bankrupts vanished out of public sight, and the market recovered its normal tone. Even as the brief delirium was still fading, a domestic scandal in England broke that grabbed the full attention of two continents. The next morning, the Chicago Limited passenger train wrecked, and the same day a prominent politician was shot dead in the street in New Orleans by his wife’s brother. Within a week of breaking, editors across the entire United States agreed that “the Manderson story” was cold, no longer worth front page space. The tide of American tourists pouring through Europe stopped to wander the memorials and tombs of great men who died penniless, and never once spared a thought for their most famous plutocrat. Like the poet Keats, who died young and poor in Rome a hundred years before, Manderson was buried far from his native land. But where crowds of Manderson’s countrymen flock to stand around Keats’s grave in the cemetery under Monte Testaccio, there is not one visitor, nor ever will be, to stand before the rich man’s grave beside the small country church of Marlstone.
