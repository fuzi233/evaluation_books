MR. YORKE (_continued_).

Hiram Yorke was a man of Yorkshire through and through—every inch of him. At fifty-five, he looked older, his hair a full silver white, like frost on the moors at dawn. His forehead was broad but not lofty; his face weathered yet fresh, with the sharp angles and sturdy lines that spoke of northern blood. His voice carried the rough cadence of the dales, as if shaped by wind and stone. There was nothing refined about his features—no trace of Norman grace or aristocratic elegance. To some, it might have seemed coarse; to others, unmistakably honest. But those who knew how to read faces would have seen in every crease and curve a mind of keen intelligence, stubborn independence, and a dry, unyielding wit. It was a face that refused to be led, let alone driven—a man who stood apart, not by choice, but by nature.

Tall and wiry, he moved with a quiet dignity, his posture straight and assured. There was no hint of clumsiness or awkwardness in his bearing—no suggestion of the clown. He carried himself like a man who had earned his place in the world, not inherited it.

But describing his appearance was easy compared to capturing his soul. If you expected a gentle, wise old gentleman—or even one touched by kindness—you’d be mistaken. Yes, he’d spoken with reason and feeling to Mr. Moore, but that did not mean he was always just or kind. In truth, Mr. Yorke was far more complex than appearances suggested.

He lacked the organ of veneration—a flaw that shaped his entire worldview. Without reverence, he could not bow to kings, bishops, or institutions. Thrones, parliaments, churches—all were to him little more than hollow pretensions, built on sand. He saw no purpose in them, no virtue in their traditions. He believed the world would be better off without them, their foundations razed and their occupants buried beneath the ruins. And this lack of awe robbed him of something deeper: the joy of admiration. He could not feel the electric thrill of seeing greatness, nor appreciate beauty for its own sake. He believed in God and heaven, yes—but his vision of both was stripped of wonder, of tenderness. His faith was bare bones, cold and unyielding.

Then there was his weakness in comparison—the ability to see things from another’s point of view. He could preach tolerance and fairness in general terms, yet held deep-seated prejudices against certain classes. He spoke of “parsons” and “lords” with bitter scorn, often with an insolence that went beyond criticism. He couldn’t imagine what it was like to walk in their shoes, to wrestle with their burdens. He judged them harshly, sometimes wishing for cruel measures to correct what he saw as tyranny—ironically, the same kind of tyranny he railed against. He claimed to champion equality, but at heart, he was proud. He treated his workers well—fatherly, even—but only so long as they accepted their place. If anyone dared to stand above him in rank, he resented them fiercely. Rebellion ran in his blood, passed down from father to son. He could not endure control, nor would he ever submit to it.

His lack of benevolence made him impatient with weakness. He had no mercy for fools or failures, and his sarcasm cut deep—without regret, without pause. He didn’t care how much he wounded, so long as he made his point. And when it came to imagination—he had little use for it. He dismissed poetic fancy as useless, perhaps dangerous. Imagination, he thought, was a form of madness, a distraction from real life. He admired music and art—when they produced tangible results—but the quiet poet? The dreamer? That man would have been invisible to Hiram Yorke, ignored, perhaps despised.

Yet, as many such men exist in the world, so too do poets who carry their fire within. They may seem still on the outside, but inside burns a spirit fierce and sharp. They know how to measure those who look down on them, and they understand the worth of the paths others scorn. They find joy in nature, in silence, in their own company—unbothered by the world’s indifference. And when the world calls them useless, they laugh quietly to themselves, knowing that their value lies beyond the reach of utilitarian minds.

But these are not Mr. Yorke’s thoughts. We must return to him.

I’ve told you of his faults. Now, his virtues. He was one of the most respected men in Yorkshire—even those who disliked him admitted his integrity. The poor loved him, for he was kind and generous. His workers trusted him. When he dismissed them, he tried to help them find new work or move to places where jobs might be found. And if any worker showed signs of defiance, Yorke—like many who hate being ruled—knew how to rule with firmness. He crushed rebellion before it could grow, like a weed pulled at the root. With his affairs so orderly, he felt free to criticize others, blaming their misfortunes on their own failings, siding with laborers against masters, and speaking boldly for reform.

His family was the oldest and most distinguished in the region. Though not the richest, he was one of the most influential. He’d had a good education and, in his youth, traveled across Europe before the French Revolution. He spoke French and Italian fluently, and during two years in Italy, he collected fine paintings and rare curiosities that now adorned his home. When he chose, his manners were those of a gentleman of the old school—polished, precise, dignified. His conversation, when he cared to engage, was lively and original. And though he usually spoke in the Yorkshire dialect, he did so by choice. “A Yorkshire burr,” he once said, “is as much better than a cockney’s lisp as a bull’s bellow is to a raton’s squeak.”

He knew everyone for miles around, yet few were his true friends. He preferred strong, rough characters—whether high or low. A clever cottager or a shrewd workman could hold his attention for hours. But a refined, dull gentleman or a fashionable lady would bore him. He valued originality above all, and found comfort only among those who didn’t analyze him, didn’t expect refinement, and didn’t mind his bluntness. Among such people, he was king—respected, obeyed, never questioned. They accepted him without reflection, and that ease pleased him deeply.

Still, he wasn’t entirely cold toward Mr. Moore. Several reasons drew him slightly toward the younger man. First, Moore spoke English with a foreign accent and French with perfect purity. His thin, dark face had an air of foreignness—anti-British, anti-Yorkshire. These details stirred memories in Yorke—of youthful travels, of Parisian cafés, of Italian streets. Seeing Moore reminded him of his own past, and for a moment, he felt young again.

Second, he had known Moore’s father—though their business dealings had ended in loss. Third, Robert Moore proved himself a capable businessman. Yorke sensed he’d succeed, and respected his resolve and sharp mind. And fourth, Yorke was one of the guardians of the minor whose estate included Hollow’s Mill—so Moore often consulted him on improvements.

As for the other guest, Mr. Helstone, the relationship between him and Yorke was strained—by nature and by history. The freethinker despised the disciplinarian. The lover of liberty loathed the upholder of order. And there was talk that once, they had both courted the same woman.

In his youth, Yorke had favored spirited, lively women—those with charm and wit. But then, suddenly, he fell deeply in love with Mary Cave—everything he’d once avoided. She was still, silent, serene—like a statue carved from living marble. She answered only in monosyllables, never smiled at his jokes, never returned his glances. Yet he loved her completely. For him, she was perfect—not because she matched his taste, but because she was unlike anything else. He adored her stillness, her calm, her quiet presence.

Mr. Helstone, the curate of Briarfield, also loved her—or thought he did. Many admired her beauty, but the clergyman was chosen for his position. Perhaps the office gave him a kind of dignity that others lacked. He didn’t share Yorke’s passion. He saw Mary clearly, not as a fantasy. He married her, and she accepted him without hesitation.

But nature had not designed Helstone to be a great husband—especially to a quiet wife. He assumed silence meant contentment. If she didn’t complain, she was happy. If she didn’t speak, she had no opinions. He didn:’t try to understand her, nor see her as a person. After a few years, she grew distant—quietly fading away. When she died, he mourned, but not deeply. He was not a man given to tears. His dry-eyed grief shocked the housekeeper and nurse, who had seen her illness closely. They whispered over her body, recounting stories of her suffering, of loneliness, of broken heart. They blamed Helstone for neglect, for cruelty—though none of it was true.

Rumors spread quickly. Soon, people whispered that Mary had died of a broken heart, mistreated by her husband. Yorke heard these tales—and believed some of them. Already, he resented Helstone for winning the woman he had loved. Though married now, to a woman very different from Mary, he could not forget the pain of losing her. To hear that she had been mistreated by another—by the man who had taken her from him—fueled a deep, bitter hatred. A hatred Helstone never fully understood.

He knew only that they disagreed politically and religiously. He did not know of the past, of the love, of the rumors. Had he known, he might never have entered Yorke’s house again.

* * * * *

Yorke did not continue his lecture to Moore. The conversation resumed, broader now, though still charged with tension. The unrest in the country—the recent attacks on mills—offered plenty of fuel for argument. Each man held different views.

Mr. Helstone believed the mill owners were wronged, the workers unreasonable. He condemned the growing rebellion against authority, the refusal to accept hardship as inevitable. His solution? Strong government, strict magistrates, and military force when needed.

Yorke scoffed. “Will your interference feed the hungry? Will it give work to those no one will hire?” He rejected the idea of inevitable suffering. Public patience, he said, was like a camel already carrying every ounce it could bear. Resistance was now a duty. The spirit of rebellion, he insisted, was the most hopeful sign of the age. Yes, the masters were aggrieved—but their troubles came from a “corrupt, base, and bloody” government. Pitt, Castlereagh, Perceval—madmen, demons, fools—were the true tyrants, destroying trade and national pride. Their endless war, their crushing taxes, their infamous Orders in Council—these were the chains around England’s neck.

“But what’s the use of talking?” he demanded. “What chance does reason have in a land ruled by kings, priests, and peers? Where a mad monarch sits on the throne, a debauched ruler holds power, and hereditary legislators mock common sense? Where bishops and a pampered church are revered as frauds? Where a standing army and idle clergymen live off the people’s sweat?”

Helstone rose, put on his shovel-hat, and replied firmly: “I’ve seen men like you—brave enough while strong and prosperous. But there comes a time when fear grips the heart, when the strong tremble. That moment reveals the true nature of rebellion. I’ve stood by the bedside of a dying man who once railed against God and king—now begging for mercy, finding none. I warn you, Mr. Yorke: blasphemy is deadly sin. There is judgment to come.”

Yorke nodded. “I believe in judgment. If not, how explain the success of scoundrels? Men who break hearts, abuse privilege, steal from the poor, and bow to the rich—how are they punished? But when I grow weary, I open my Bible—any page—and find a verse blazing with divine fire. I know where some folk are headed, as well as if an angel had told me.”

“Sir,” said Helstone, gathering his dignity, “the greatest wisdom is to know oneself, and the path one walks.”

“Ay, ay,” replied Yorke. “And remember—Ignorance was carried from the gates of heaven, borne through the air, and thrust into hell.”

“And I recall,” said Helstone, “that Vain-Confidence, blind to danger, fell into a pit made by the prince of the grounds to catch fools, and was shattered.”

Moore, who had watched silently, now broke in. “You’ve both blackballed each other thoroughly. You’ve shown how much you detest one another. But for me—my anger is still focused on the rogues who broke my frames. I’ve no room left for personal quarrels, let alone politics. Gentlemen, you both seem worse than I imagined. I dare not stay the night with a rebel like you, Yorke—and I scarcely dare ride home with a tyrant like Mr. Helstone.”
