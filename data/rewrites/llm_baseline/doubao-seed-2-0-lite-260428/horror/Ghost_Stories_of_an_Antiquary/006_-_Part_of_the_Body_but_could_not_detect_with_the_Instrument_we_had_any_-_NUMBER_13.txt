Viborg, tucked in the rolling green heart of Jutland, carries its old honors lightly: bishop’s seat, a gleaming new cathedral, a lily-dusted lake, storks that nest on every gable, and the nearby stone of Finderup where Marsk Stig’s party hacked fifty-six square-headed mace blows into King Erik Glipping’s skull on St. Cecilia’s Day, 1286. This is no guidebook, though: the story that follows took place not amid the well-trodden ruins, but inside the Golden Lion, one of the tiny handful of buildings that survived the 1726 fire that ate nearly every last old, carved, timber-framed structure in the town, cathedral and town hall and all.

It is a squat, imposing red brick house, its gables stepped with corbie stones, a faded biblical text carved deep above the entry lintel, its inner courtyard cobbled and lined with black half-timber that smells of centuries of beer spill and wood smoke. The first time my cousin Anderson, an English antiquary digging through suppressed late-Roman Catholic church records of Denmark, visited Viborg, he booked a stay there, and he has never dared step over its threshold since.

He’d come for the half-burned, fire-surviving papers locked in the Viborg Rigsarkiv, and told the landlord he needed a single large room that could work as both bedroom and study, no cramped cubby on the top floor. The second floor held nothing the right size, but the first floor offered a handful of options. The landlord pushed for Number 17, but Anderson turned it down at once: its windows faced nothing but the blank, mossy wall of the neighboring house, and would be pitch dark by mid-afternoon. Number 12, looking out over the cobbled main street, was perfect: long, high-ceilinged, three windows facing the road, its only notable furnishing a heavy old cast-iron stove etched with the scene of Abraham raising his knife over Isaac, the caption *1 Bog Mose, Cap. 22* stamped just above it. He settled in, washed off the travel dust, and wandered down to the dining hall a few minutes before supper was called, pausing to glance over the blackboard that listed all lodgers by room number. It was a boring list: a local lawyer, a German travelling salesman, a handful of Copenhagen bagmen. The only oddity he noted, familiar as he was with Danish hotels, was that no Number 13 appeared anywhere on the board. He laughed it off as the usual harmless folk superstition, and resolved to tease the landlord about it later that evening.

After supper he unpacked his books and papers, and realized the novel he’d been reading on the train was still tucked in the pocket of his heavy overcoat, hanging on a peg by the dining room door. He trotted down the stairs to fetch it, and turned back toward his floor, the corridor lit dim by guttering oil lamps. When he reached the door he thought was his, the handle wouldn’t turn, and he heard a quick, shuffling scurry of movement from directly inside, as if someone had been pressed to the other side, listening. He jerked back, and saw the number nailed to the wood: 13. He stepped left at once, found Number 12, and slipped inside, but long after he’d blown out his light and curled up to sleep, the thought nagged him: the blackboard had no Number 13 written on it, but the hotel very clearly had a door for it. The room even seemed to have shifted while he’d been out: the long stretch of wall by the far trestle table he’d set his portmanteau on was several feet closer now, the ceiling seeming to have stretched higher, as if the space had breathed and contracted in his absence. He told himself it was just the dim streetlamp light playing tricks, and drifted off.

The next day he spent buried in the archives, and stumbled across a cache of letters he’d never seen referenced anywhere: a furious flurry of notes between Reformation-era Protestant leaders and the last Catholic bishop of Viborg, Jörgen Friis, all arguing about a man named Nicolas Francken – a sorcerer, *Troldmand*, they called him, who practiced forbidden rites in a house the bishop owned, hidden somewhere in the town. The reformers screamed that he had sold his soul to the devil, that his magic was poisoning every household within three streets, but the bishop refused to hand him over to a secular court, demanding he be tried before the ecclesiastical tribunal instead. Anderson pored over the letters late into the afternoon, but the archivist Herr Scavenius closed the archive before he could read the final Protestant reply, and confessed as they walked back toward the town that no one had ever been able to find where Francken’s hidden house stood: the 1560 bishopric property survey that should have listed every parcel of land the church owned in Viborg had its critical town pages torn clean out, lost to history for nearly four hundred years.

Anderson wandered back to the Golden Lion after that, and remembered to pause at the stretch of corridor that should have held Number 13. The door was there, plain as day, and through the thick wood he heard slow, heavy footsteps pacing back and forth, stopping abruptly right on the other side of the panel. A thin, hissing breath pricked out through the keyhole, and he caught a whiff of sulphur and charred old parchment that made his throat tickle. He hurried to his own room, and found it had shrunk even further: the portmanteau the porter had set on the trestle by the far wall an arm’s length away from his bed was gone. He searched the wardrobe, the space under the bed, the bathroom nook, and could not find it anywhere, and told himself the silly maid must have moved it. He went to the right-hand window to get some air, and watched the street below, and after a minute spotted the shadow of the occupant of the supposed Number 13 leaning on his own windowsill, right next to Anderson’s. The shadow was tall, thin, its head wrapped in some kind of hooded drapery, and behind it a dull, flickering red light played over the wall opposite, pulsing like embers in a fire. Anderson leaned out to see more, and the shadow snapped back from the window as if it had felt his gaze, the red light snuffing out instantly. Anderson laid the end of his cigarette on the right-hand windowsill, and climbed into bed.

He woke the next morning to the maid bringing in hot water, and demanded to know where his portmanteau had been moved. The maid laughed, confused, and gestured behind him. Anderson twisted around, and there was his portmanteau, sitting exactly where the porter had set it the day before, on the trestle at the far end of the room. He stared, bewildered, and walked to the middle window to check the weather – and there, on the sill, was the stub of his cigarette. He swore he had left it on the right-hand sill. Confused, he headed down to breakfast, and spotted a pair of leather boots set outside what he thought was the Number 13 door. He turned back to glance at the number nailed to the wood, and his blood ran cold. The door was marked 14. His own room, 12, sat directly to the left of it. There was no gap, no extra door, no space at all between the two rooms that could possibly fit a thirteenth room. The stretch of wall between 12 and 14 was blank, papered over, completely solid.

That day back in the archives, Anderson found one final surviving letter from Bishop Friis, dated only two days after the Protestant leaders had threatened to storm Francken’s house and drag him out to burn him at the stake. The note said that Nicolas Francken had been “suddenly removed from among us”, and the case was therefore closed. No record existed anywhere of how he had died. No one had ever written down where his body was buried.

That evening at supper, Anderson sat next to the landlord Herr Kristensen, and asked him point blank if the Golden Lion ever had a Number 13. Kristensen laughed, and told him he’d run hotels for thirty years, and he knew perfectly well commercial travellers would refuse to sleep in a Number 13 even if you paid them. He had never numbered a room 13 at the Golden Lion, ever. There was no space for it. Room 12 sat directly next to 14, a solid brick wall between them, no extra nook, no closet, no unused chamber.

Anderson’s blood turned to ice. To calm his nerves, he invited the landlord up to his room for a cigar after ten o’clock, under the pretense of showing him photographs of old English market towns. As he waited for Kristensen, he sat down to write letters, and listened to the sounds from the other side of his far wall – the wall that the landlord had sworn was solid brick, separating him from the lawyer in Number 14. The quiet, staid lawyer who never spoke a word at breakfast was not just moving around. He was dancing. Bare feet thudded hard on the floor on the other side of the wall, a hundred rapid stamping steps, back and forth, back and forth, getting closer and closer, until Anderson could hear the faint scrape of heavy leather boots (not bare feet) scuffing on the wood right up against the panel, and the low, hissing breath he had heard through the Number 13 keyhole the night before. The wall between his room and that empty, impossible space was warm now, almost hot, as if there was a fire blazing on the other side, and he could see the dull, pulsing red glow seeping through the cracks where the wall met the floorboards. He fumbled for the door, but his own room had shrunk, the walls closing in around him, the cast iron Abraham and Isaac stove right at his elbow, and he realized with a sickening lurch that he was no longer in Number 12.

The door he’d thought was Number 12 was gone. He was inside Number 13, and it was only getting bigger.
