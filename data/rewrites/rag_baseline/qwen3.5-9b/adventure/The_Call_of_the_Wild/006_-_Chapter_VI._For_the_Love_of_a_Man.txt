The winter frost had finally thawed, leaving the Yukon River a ribbon of silver cutting through the landscape. In the previous December, when John Thornton had frozen his feet to the bone, his partners had treated him with the pragmatic efficiency of men who valued labor over sentiment, sending him to recuperate while they pushed upstream to harvest a raft of saw-logs for Dawson. By the time Buck had been pulled from the snow, Thornton was already limping, a reminder of the harsh climate, but the returning warmth of spring had done its work; the limp vanished as quickly as the ice melted.

Now, lying by the riverbank through the long, languid days of April, Buck slowly reclaimed his vitality. Three thousand miles of travel demanded a respite, and Buck succumbed to it with a profound, animalistic laziness. His wounds knit together, his muscles swelled with restored bulk, and flesh returned to cover the sharp angles of his skeleton. He was not alone in this idleness; Thornton, and the two dogs accompanying them—Skeet, a small Irish setter, and Nig, a massive black hound of mixed lineage—were all waiting for the raft that would carry them downstream.

Skeet possessed a peculiar medical instinct common to few dogs. Like a mother cat grooming her kittens, she attended to Buck's injuries with meticulous care, washing and cleansing his wounds daily until he came to anticipate her ministrations as eagerly as he sought Thornton's presence. Nig, a creature of boundless good nature with eyes that seemed perpetually amused, shared this affection though he lacked Skeet's demonstrative flair. To Buck's astonishment, these dogs harbored no jealousy. Instead, they mirrored the expansive kindness radiating from John Thornton. As Buck's strength returned, they dragged him into absurd games, forcing even the stoic Thornton to participate. Through this play, Buck transitioned from a wounded beast to a thriving companion, experiencing for the first time a love that was feverish, burning, and absolute.

This emotion was alien to his past. With the Judge Miller in the sun-drenched Santa Clara Valley, his relationship had been a transactional partnership with the boys, a patronizing guardianship with the grandsons, and a distant, stately friendship with the Judge himself. None of those bonds had ignited the fire within him. Only John Thornton could unlock such intensity. Thornton was not merely a provider; he was an ideal master who viewed his dogs not as assets managed for profit, but as children whose welfare was non-negotiable. He remembered every kind word and every cheerful greeting, treating long conversations with his dogs—"gas," as he termed it—as a source of pure delight equal to theirs.

Thornton had a unique habit of gripping Buck's head roughly between his own hands, resting his forehead against the dog's, and rocking him back and forth while hurling insults that Buck interpreted as terms of endearment. To Buck, this rough embrace and the murmur of oaths represented the pinnacle of joy, a sensation so overwhelming it felt as though his very heart would be shaken loose from his ribcage. Upon release, Buck would spring upright, his muzzle twitching, his eyes eloquent, vibrating with silent song, remaining motionless until Thornton, awestruck, would whisper, "God! you can all but speak!"

Buck's expressions of love were often paradoxical, bordering on aggression. He frequently seized Thornton's hand in his jaws, biting down with such ferocity that the flesh bore the imprint of his teeth for hours. Yet, just as Buck understood Thornton's curses as declarations of love, the man recognized this feigned violence as a caress.

Most often, however, Buck's devotion was silent and observant. Unlike Skeet, who shoved her nose under Thornton's hand seeking constant contact, or Nig, who rested his great weight on the man's knee, Buck preferred to adore from a distance. He would lie for hours at Thornton's feet, alert and eager, staring upward to study every fleeting expression, every shift in feature. Sometimes he retreated to the side or rear, watching the silhouette of the man against the light. In these moments of communion, Buck's intense gaze would pull Thornton's head around, and the two would lock eyes—a silent exchange where the man's heart shone out of his eyes just as brightly as the dog's.

In the early days of his recovery, Buck struggled with insecurity. Having lost Perrault, François, and the Scotch half-breed, he feared Thornton would eventually depart as well. Even in his dreams, this anxiety haunted him, waking him in the dead of night to creep to the tent flap and listen to the rhythmic breathing of his master. Despite this fierce attachment, the primal instincts awakened by the Northland remained potent. Faithfulness and devotion were traits born of fire and shelter, yet Buck retained his wild cunning. He could not steal from Thornton, bound by a loyalty deeper than instinct, but he showed no hesitation in pilfering from others' camps, employing a sophistication honed by years of survival.

His body bore the scars of countless battles, scored by the teeth of rival dogs. He fought with the ferocity of his ancestors, more shrewd than ever. Skeet and Nig were too gentle for conflict, and since they belonged to Thornton, they were safe. But any stranger, regardless of breed or courage, faced Buck's supremacy or met a terrifying struggle for life. Buck was merciless, having learned the brutal laws of the club and the fang. There was no middle path; one must master or be mastered. Mercy was a weakness, a misunderstanding of fear that led only to death. The ancient mandate—kill or be killed, eat or be eaten—was etched into his DNA, and he obeyed it without question.

He existed in a state of timeless duality, linking the past with the present. Sitting by Thornton's fire, a broad-chested guardian with white fangs and long fur, he was also a conduit for the spirits of all dogs and wolves that had preceded him. These shades urged him onward, tasting the meat he ate, drinking the water he drank, and sensing the wind with him. They dictated his moods, directed his actions, and filled his dreams, urging him to abandon the safety of the fire.

Deep in the forest, a call resonated, mysterious and thrumming with an imperative power. Whenever he heard it, he felt compelled to turn his back on the warmth of the hearth and plunge into the woods, driven by a force he could not comprehend or resist. Yet, whenever he reached the soft, unbroken earth of the forest floor, the magnetic pull of John Thornton drew him back to the fire.

Thornton alone held him. The rest of humanity was insignificant. Strangers might praise or pet him, but he remained cold beneath their touch, often rising to walk away from overly demonstrative men. When Thornton's partners, Hans and Pete, finally arrived on the long-awaited raft, Buck ignored them until they drew near to his master. Only then did he tolerate their presence, accepting their favors with a passive indifference that masked a subtle superiority. They were of the same sturdy stock as Thornton, living close to the earth and thinking plainly; before the raft swung into the eddy at the sawmill, they understood Buck's nature and respected his boundaries, refusing the false intimacy they offered Skeet and Nig.

With Thornton, however, Buck's bond deepened exponentially. He was capable of carrying packs during summer travel when commanded, performing feats that bordered on the supernatural. One day, as the group sat atop a precipice overlooking a three-hundred-foot drop to naked bedrock, Thornton, seized by a reckless whim, gestured for an experiment. "Jump, Buck!" he commanded, sweeping his arm over the abyss. Before anyone could react, Thornton was grappling with Buck on the razor-thin edge, while Hans and Pete scrambled to drag them back to safety.

"It's uncanny," Pete muttered later, regaining his composure.

Thornton shook his head gravely. "No, it is splendid, and it is terrible, too. Do you know, it sometimes makes me afraid."

"I'm not hankering to be the man that lays hands on you while he's around," Pete declared, nodding toward the dog.

"Py Jingo!" Hans agreed. "Not mineself either."

These apprehensions proved prescient at Circle City before the year ended. "Black" Burton, a man of malice and ill temper, had been instigating trouble at a saloon bar when Thornton stepped in to de-escalate the situation. Buck, lying quietly in a corner with his head on his paws, watched his master intently. Without warning, Burton struck out from the shoulder. Thornton spun violently, saving himself only by grabbing the bar's railing.

The onlookers heard neither bark nor yelp, but a roar that seemed to tear the air apart. Buck's body shot upward, launching himself from the floor directly onto Burton's throat. The man reacted on instinct, throwing out an arm, but was hurled backward onto the floor with Buck pinned atop him. Buck loosened his grip momentarily before driving his teeth back into the throat. This time, Burton managed to block partially, tearing his own throat open in the process. The crowd surged forward, driving the dog off, but as a surgeon tended to the bleeding man, Buck prowled up and down the room, growling furiously, repeatedly attempting to rush in until repelled by a wall of hostile clubs. A miners' meeting convened instantly, ruling that Buck had sufficient provocation, and he was discharged. Yet, his reputation was forged in blood, spreading through every camp in Alaska from that day forward.
