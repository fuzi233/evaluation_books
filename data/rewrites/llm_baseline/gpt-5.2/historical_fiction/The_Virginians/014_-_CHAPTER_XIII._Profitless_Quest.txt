With every mile Harry Warrington rode northward toward Pennsylvania, the tale of ruin swelled—like a pot left too long at the boil, each traveller lifting the lid and crying that the thing within was worse than any man had yet said. The King’s famous regiments, veterans who had marched to drums in Scotland and on the Continent, had broken in a wilderness where no parade-ground could be stamped; they had fled from an enemy half-unseen, and all their vaunted order had not saved them from a fire that came, not from a line of red coats, but from behind bark and leaf.

Men told it to Harry as they would tell a kitchen-boy how a grand feast was lost: first the confident laying-out of the work, then the sudden spoil. They spoke of the ford, the long string of troops threading through the green silence, of the first cracking shots ahead, and the cannon dragged forward as if iron might clear away invisible foes. Then—without trumpet or flag—the woods themselves seemed to spit flame. Behind every trunk, from every bush, a murderous fusillade; men dropping as if a scythe had gone through wheat. The commander—brave to the last—had his horse shot under him again and again, was wounded twice, and at last mortally, dying three days after. So ran the reports, each voice adding its own salt.

But not all of the General’s suite were dead. That rumour, at least, kept Harry’s heart in motion. An aide-de-camp—a Virginian gentleman—lay sick with fever and exhaustion at Dunbar’s camp. Which aide? Which gentleman? The very uncertainty drove Harry on as hunger drives a man toward the smell of bread.

He found a tent, and within it George Washington—stretched long, his skin hot and drawn, his eyes bright with illness. Yet sharper than fever, Mr. Washington said, was the pain of seeing Harry Warrington and having no comfort to give.

He did not tell everything—could not. His duty, for three days after the fight, had been to remain near the General. On the fatal ninth of July he had seen George Warrington ride forward with orders from the chief—and never return to the General’s side. When Braddock died, Washington, with a sick man’s stubbornness, had contrived to creep back upon the track to the field itself.

What lay there now was what battle leaves when it has been picked over. Corpses remained, stripped and horribly mutilated. One body he buried which he thought might be George’s. His illness, perhaps, had been fed by that search and the anguish of it—by crawling among the ruined, by turning faces with hands that shook, by the sickening knowledge that names and bodies, in such a place, slip from certainty.

“Ah, George! If you had loved him you would have found him dead or alive,” Harry cried; and the words came out like a knife. Nothing would satisfy him but that he too should go, and see with his own eyes.

With money he bought guides, and rode back. He forded the river at the very passage where the army had crossed, and went the length of the dreadful ground. No Indians haunted it now. The wilderness had quieted again, but it was not the quiet of peace. Birds of prey rose and settled, rose and settled; their wings made a low, steady clapping over the trees, and their cries cut through the air with a harshness that seemed to scrape the nerves. Even the insects had their terrible industry, and the wind, when it moved, moved through the leaves as through a church where some impure thing had happened.

Save for his grandfather lying very calm with a sweet smile upon his lip, Harry had never yet looked close upon the face of Death. Here death had no sweetness. The stench had its own weight, as if it pressed on the breast; it was thick, sour, and sickly, a heat-spoiled broth of blood and rot. The sight of mutilation made him turn away in shuddering loathing. What could the vacant woods tell him? What could those festering bodies under the trees give back—any syllable of his brother’s fate, any hint that could be translated into hope?

Desperation drove him toward the French fort—he would go unarmed, with a white flag. But his guides refused to advance.

“The French might maybe respect ye,” one said, “but the Injuns won’t. Keep your hair for your lady mother, my young gentleman. ’Tis enough she loses one son in this campaign.”

So Harry turned back to Dunbar’s—empty-handed, his quest profitless as a net pulled from a river after the fish have fled.

There it was his turn to be struck down. Fever took him as it takes a body already bruised by grief: quickly, heavily, with delirium close behind. He lay in a tent, on the very bed from which his friend had just risen convalescent. For days he did not know who watched him. Poor Dempster, who had tended him through more than one such malady, thought the widow must lose both her children. Yet at length the fever yielded enough for the boy to rally, to sit up, to be helped to horse.

Mr. Washington and Dempster escorted him home.

They came again to the gates of Castlewood, and though the fields stood green and the great house kept its accustomed look of plenty, the sight of it weighed like a cold stone in the heart. A servant had been sent ahead to announce them. First came Mrs. Mountain and her little daughter, with tears and embraces for Harry. She scarce gave Mr. Washington more than a nod. Then the child—little Fanny—made the young officer start and turn deadly pale by coming close with her hands behind her back and asking, with that cruel innocence children have, “Why have you not brought George back too?”

Harry did not hear; Mrs. Mountain’s sobs and caresses mercifully drowned the question before it could lodge.

Dempster was received with thanks.

“Whatever could be done, we know you would do, Mr. Dempster,” Mrs. Mountain said, giving him her hand. “Make a curtsey to Mr. Dempster, Fanny, and remember, child, to be grateful to all who have been friendly to our benefactors. Will it please you to take any refreshment before you ride, Colonel Washington?”

Mr. Washington had ridden enough, and counted on Castlewood’s shelter as naturally as on his own roof; yet he answered stiffly, as if every kindness must be measured.

“The time to feed my horse, and a glass of water for myself, and I will trouble Castlewood hospitality no further.”

“Sure, George, you have your room here, and my mother is above-stairs getting it ready!” Harry cried. “That poor horse of yours stumbled with you, and can’t go farther this evening.”

“Hush! Your mother won’t see him, child,” Mrs. Mountain whispered. “Not see George? Why, he is like a son of the house,” Harry said, bewildered.

“She had best not see him,” Mrs. Mountain answered. “I don’t meddle any more in family matters, child: but when the Colonel’s servant rode in, and said you were coming, Madam Esmond left this room—where she was sitting reading Drelincourt—and said she felt she could not see Mr. Washington. Will you go to her?”

Harry took his friend’s arm, begged the Colonel’s pardon for leaving him a little, and went up. He crossed the corridor with averted head, passing one door he could not bear to look at—the door of his brother’s room. But as he reached it, Madam Esmond came out and folded him to her heart, and led him in.

A settee stood by the bed. A book of psalms lay upon the coverlet, as if it had been left there only an hour ago. Everything in the room was as George had left it—his order, his quiet signs of habit, the very air holding something of him, like a pantry that keeps the scent of spice long after the lid is shut.

“My poor child!” she cried. “How thin thou art grown—how haggard you look! Never mind. A mother’s care will make thee well again. ’Twas nobly done to go and brave sickness and danger in search of your brother. Had others been as faithful, he might be here now. Never mind, my Harry; our hero will come back to us,—I know he is not dead. One so good, and so brave, and so gentle, and so clever as he was, I know is not lost to us altogether.”

The praise, lavish as it was, struck Harry oddly; perhaps because he remembered how, when George had been in the house, such words had not always come so readily. Yet he held his tongue, and only looked at her.

“Dry up thy tears, my dear! He will come back to us, I know he will come.”

Pressed for a reason, she said she had seen her father two nights running in a dream, and he had told her George was a prisoner among the Indians.

Grief had not prostrated her; it had kindled her into a hard brightness. Her eyes were eager, her face set, with something revengeful in its resolve. Harry, still weak, wondered at the strength—and the strange heat—of her.

Then he begged her to go down and welcome George Washington, who had brought him home. At once her excitement sharpened into pain.

She said she should shudder at touching his hand. She declared Mr. Washington had taken her son from her; she could not sleep under the same roof with him.

“He gave me his bed when I was ill, mother,” Harry pleaded; “and if our George is alive, how has George Washington a hand in his death? Ah! please God it be only as you say.”

“If your brother returns, as return he will, it will not be through Mr. Washington’s help,” Madam Esmond answered. “He neither defended George on the field, nor would he bring him out of it.”

“But he tended me most kindly,” Harry said, fighting for fairness. “He was yet ill when he gave up his bed to me, and was thinking only of his friend, when any other man would have thought only of himself.”

“A friend! A pretty friend!” she sneered. “Of all his Excellency’s aides-de-camp, my gentleman is the only one who comes back unwounded. The brave and noble fall, but he, to be sure, is unhurt. I confide my boy to him—the pride of my life—whom he will defend with his, forsooth! And he leaves my George in the forest, and brings me back himself! Oh, a pretty welcome I must give him!”

“No gentleman,” Harry cried, his weak voice gathering fire, “was ever refused shelter under my grandfather’s roof.”

“Oh no—no gentleman!” she echoed, her scorn thin and keen. “Let us go down, if you like, son, and pay our respects to this one. Will you please to give me your arm?”

She took the arm that could scarcely support itself, and walked down the broad stairs into the parlour where Colonel Washington sat. She made him a ceremonious curtsey and extended one small hand, which she allowed to rest in his for a moment—as if even that touch must be rationed.

“I wish that our meeting had been happier, Colonel Washington,” she said.

“You do not grieve more than I do that it is otherwise, madam,” he answered.

“I might have wished that the meeting had been spared,” she went on, “that I might not have kept you from friends whom you are naturally anxious to see,—that my boy’s indisposition had not detained you. Home and his good nurse Mountain, and his mother and our good Doctor Dempster, will soon restore him. ’Twas scarce necessary, Colonel, that you, who have so many affairs on your hands, military and domestic, should turn doctor too.”

“Harry was ill and weak,” the Colonel said, faltering, “and I thought it was my duty to ride by him.”

“You yourself, sir, have gone through the fatigues and dangers of the campaign in the most wonderful manner,” Madam Esmond replied, curtseying again and looking at him with those impenetrable black eyes.

“I wish to Heaven, madam, some one else had come back in my place!” he burst out.

“Nay, sir, you have ties which must render your life more than ever valuable and dear to you, and duties to which, I know, you must be anxious to betake yourself. In our present deplorable state of doubt and distress, Castlewood can be a welcome place to no stranger, much less to you, and so I know, sir, you will be for leaving us ere long. And you will pardon me if the state of my own spirits obliges me for the most part to keep my chamber. But my friends here will bear you company as long as you favour us, whilst I nurse my poor Harry upstairs. Mountain, you will have the cedar-room on the ground-floor ready for Mr. Washington, and anything in the house is at his command. Farewell, sir. Will you be pleased to present my compliments to your mother, who will be thankful to have her son safe and sound out of the war,—as also to my young friend Martha Custis, to whom and to whose children I wish every happiness. Come, my son!”

With another freezing curtsey she withdrew, her gaze fixed upon the Colonel as if she would pin blame to him by sheer force of looking. He stood dumb where she left him.

Though her belief in George’s safety sounded unshakeable, Castlewood could not help but be sad. She might forbid open mourning, but her heart wore black, whatever face she forced upon herself for the world. To look for her son was hoping against hope. No authentic account of his death had come, and no man appeared who could say he had seen George fall; yet hundreds had been struck down that day with no witnesses but the lurking enemy and comrades dying beside them.

A fortnight after the defeat—while Harry was absent on his fruitless search—George’s servant Sady reappeared at Castlewood, wounded and maimed. He could give no coherent account of the battle, only of his flight from the centre where he had been with the baggage. He had no news of his master since the morning of the action. For days Sady hid himself among the negro quarters, not daring to face Madam Esmond’s anger.

Neighbours whispered that the lady laboured under a delusion. Yet it held with such force that there were hours when Harry and the rest of the household nearly caught it, as one catches a tune that will not stop repeating. To her it seemed nothing strange that her father—out of another world—should promise her son’s life. In this world or the next, her family must be of consequence, she thought. Had not she, more than once, been visited with foreknowledge? No accident, no fever, no important illness had ever befallen her boys at a distance but she had felt it in advance. She could enumerate half a dozen instances—and her household, weary of contradiction, was obliged in part to confirm them—when she had known, by instinct, what had happened and what would follow.

No, George was not dead. George was a prisoner among the Indians. George would come back and rule over Castlewood—as surely as his Majesty would send a great force from home to wipe away the stain on British arms and drive the French out of the Americas.

As for Mr. Washington, she vowed she would never behold him of her own goodwill again. He had promised to protect George with his life. Why was her son gone and the Colonel alive? How dared he face a mother without her son? Some might plead he had to obey superior orders. Psha! A promise was a promise.

Do not tell her his coat and hat had shots through them. She had her answer ready, sharp as vinegar. Could she not go into the study and fire two shots through her paduasoy skirt—and would she be killed? She laughed at the notion; and her laugh, like the sound of a knife on bone, did not please those who heard it. Satire from a nature not given to humour is seldom sport; it lands where it means to land.

Thus, if Harry wished to meet his friend, he must do it in secret—at court-houses, taverns, places where the provincial gentry gathered; in little towns where men came for races and bargains and news. No man of spirit, she declared, could meet Mr. Washington after his base desertion. She was exceedingly excited when she heard the Colonel and her son had indeed met. What a heart must Harry have, to give his hand to one she called little better than George’s murderer!

“For shame!” she would cry, and then—without pausing—turn upon her own accusation. “How can he be George’s murderer, when I say my boy is not dead? He is not dead, because my instinct never deceived me: because, as sure as I see his picture now before me—only ’tis not near so noble or so good as he used to look—so surely two nights running did my papa appear to me in my dreams. You doubt about that, very likely? ’Tis because you never loved anybody sufficiently, my poor Harry; else you might have leave to see them in dreams.”

“I think I loved George, mother,” Harry cried once, stung. “I have often prayed that I might dream about him, and I don’t.”

“How you can talk, sir, of loving George,” she shot back, “and then—go and meet your Mr. Washington at horse-races—I can’t understand! Can you, Mountain?”

“We can’t understand many things in our neighbours’ characters,” Mrs. Mountain grumbled. “I can understand that our boy is unhappy, and that he does not get strength, and that he is doing no good here, in Castlewood, or moping at the taverns and court-houses with horse-coupers and idle company.”

And indeed the dependant spoke truth. There was grief in Castlewood, but there was disunion too, a cracking of bonds that had once held firm, like a well-made loaf that begins to stale and crumble at the slice.

“I cannot tell how it came,” Harry said later, when he told this history to his new-found English relative, Madame de Bernstein, “but since that fatal day of July, last year, and my return home, my mother never has been the same woman. She seemed to love none of us as she used. She was for ever praising George, and yet she did not seem as if she liked him much when he was with us. She hath plunged, more deeply than ever, into her books of devotion, out of which she only manages to extract grief and sadness, as I think. Such a gloom has fallen over our wretched Virginian house of Castlewood, that we all grew ill, and pale as ghosts, who inhabited it. Mountain told me, madam, that, for nights, my mother would not close her eyes. I have had her at my bedside, looking so ghastly, that I have started from my own sleep, fancying a ghost before me. By one means or other she has wrought herself into a state of excitement which if not delirium, is akin to it.

“I was again and again struck down by the fever, and all the Jesuits’ bark in America could not cure me. We have a tobacco-house and some land about the new town of Richmond, in our province, and went thither, as Williamsburg is no wholesomer than our own place; and there I mended a little, but still did not get quite well, and the physicians strongly counselled a sea-voyage. My mother, at one time, had thoughts of coming with me, but—” (and here the lad blushed and hung his head) “—we did not agree very well, though I know we loved each other very heartily, and ’twas determined that I should see the world for myself. So I took passage in our ship from the James River, and was landed at Bristol. And ’twas only on the 9th of July, this year, at sea, as had been agreed between me and Madam Esmond, that I put mourning on for my dear brother.”

So that little mistress of Virginian Castlewood—whom it is easy to respect, and not so easy to live beside—had the gift of making those around her uncomfortable; of quarrelling with the very people she loved best; of exercising jealousies and imperious humours until they were not sorry to escape. Here was money enough, friends enough, position enough, respect enough; a house stored with plenty—meat in the smokehouse, meal in the bin, tea in the caddy, the table set with silver—and yet poor Harry Warrington was glad to leave it all behind.

What good is a stalled ox for dinner every day, if content is not at table? Is it better to be loved and plagued by those you love, or to live with an easy indifference—following your fancies unmolested—and die without causing painful regret? When her boy was gone, Madam Esmond forgot the small tiffs and prickly exchanges; to hear her speak of her children, you would fancy both were perfect and had never vexed her for a moment.

With the boys removed, her sharp energies fell naturally upon Mrs. Mountain and the child, worrying and fretting them in turn. But women, perhaps, bear hard words more easily than men; forgive sooner, or dissemble anger better. Let us hope Madam Esmond’s dependants found their life tolerable; that they sometimes answered back, that if there were quarrels in the morning there were truces by night, and they could sit down again to cards and a dish of tea with something like peace.

Yet without the boys the great house of Castlewood was dreary to the widow. She left an overseer to manage the estate and paid it only occasional visits. In Richmond—growing daily into a prettier, busier little city—she enlarged and beautified her house, held company and card-assemblies, gathered preachers in plenty, and set up her small throne where the gentlefolks of the province might come and bow. Her domestic negroes, loving society as they do, were delighted to exchange Castlewood’s solitude for town life.

And there, for a time—while Harry Warrington’s story moves on across the sea—we leave the resolute little lady, with her dreams, her certainty, and the silence in her son’s room that no words, in any dialect, can truly translate.
