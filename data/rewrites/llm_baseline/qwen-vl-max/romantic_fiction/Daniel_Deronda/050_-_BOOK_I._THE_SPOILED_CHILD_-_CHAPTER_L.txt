CHAPTER L. “If some mortal, born too soon,
Were laid away in some great trance—the ages
Coming and going all the while—till dawned
His true time’s advent; and could then record
The words they spoke who kept watch by his bed,
Then I might tell more of the breath so light
Upon my eyelids, and the fingers warm
Among my hair. Youth is confused; yet never
So dull was I but, when that spirit passed,
I turned to him, scarce consciously, as turns
A water-snake when fairies cross his sleep.”
—BROWNING: _Paracelsus_.

It was a letter from an unknown hand that Sir Hugo placed into Daniel Deronda’s hands—a letter that carried no flourish of emotion, only quiet commands wrapped in silence.

My good friend and yours, Sir Hugo Mallinger, will have told you that I wish to see you. My health is frail, and I do not wish to delay what I have long withheld. Be at the _Albergo dell’ Italia_ in Genoa by the fourteenth of this month. Wait for me there. I cannot say when I shall arrive from Spezia, where I remain. It depends upon things beyond my control. Wait for me—the Princess Halm-Eberstein. Bring with you the diamond ring Sir Hugo gave you. I would like to see it again.

—Your unknown mother,

The words were plain, almost cold. They offered no clue to what lay ahead—only an invitation to meet a woman who had lived apart from him, unseen and unheard, for all his life. Yet Deronda did not hesitate. He accepted Sir Hugo’s silence as a boundary, a promise not to guess, not to shape the truth before it came. The realization that his lifelong assumptions about his origins were wrong stunned him—not with anger, but with a strange stillness. His mind raced through possibilities, but he refused to let any one take root. To fix on a hope or fear now would be to betray himself, to give weight to a dream before it became fact. He must wait. And in waiting, he must prepare—not with longing, but with courage.

He could not speak of this journey to anyone—not even to Mordecai, whose heart would beat with the same urgency, though in different measure. If he said, “I am going to learn the truth about my birth,” Mordecai’s hopes would rise like fire, dangerous and bright. So instead, Deronda said only that Sir Hugo wished him to travel, that it might be brief, and that he was uncertain how long he’d stay. He tried to sound indifferent, though his own pulse betrayed him.

“I will ask to have the child Jacob stay with me,” Mordecai said softly, as if seeking comfort in small things. “I will go round and ask Mrs. Cohen to let him come.”

“Her grandmother will deny you nothing,” Deronda replied gently. “I’m glad you were a little wrong as well as I,” he added, smiling. “You thought old Mrs. Cohen wouldn’t bear to see Mirah.”

“I undervalued her heart,” Mordecai murmured. “She can rejoice in another’s bloom, even when her own has faded.”

“Oh, they are dear good people,” Mirah said, her voice warm with affection. “I feel as if we all belong to each other.”

“What would you have felt if Ezra had been your brother?” Deronda teased, a little irritated that she welcomed someone who had caused him so much unease.

Mirah looked at him, startled, then lowered her eyes. “He is not a bad man,” she whispered. “I think he would never abandon anyone.”

And then she blushed deeply, glancing shyly at Mordecai before turning away. Her father was in her thoughts—her secret sorrow, their shared pain. “If he should come and find us!”—that thought haunted her, turning daylight into shadow, every street corner into a hiding place for ghosts.

Deronda understood. He saw the flicker of fear in her face, the echo of a past too heavy to speak. And suddenly, he recognized something in himself—the same ache, the same dread of revelation. For his mother’s letter, though it brought her closer, also pushed her farther away. The tender image he had built of her—the woman who might have suffered, who deserved love and care—crumbled under the weight of her real words. Her handwriting, her tone—cold, detached—had stripped away the fantasy. She was not the mother he had imagined. She was a stranger, veiled, enigmatic, and now, somehow, less real than the ghost he had conjured.

Yet even as he mourned that loss, he found himself thinking not of her, but of Mordecai and Mirah. Their lives, their hopes, their fragile bond—these filled his mind more than the mystery of his blood. When he set out for Genoa, it was not just to meet his mother, but to carry the weight of their dreams with him.

“God bless you, Dan,” Sir Hugo said, gripping his hand firmly. “Whatever changes, I’ll always be the oldest friend you’ve known—the one who’s loved you most. I couldn’t have loved you better if you’d been my own son—though I’d have preferred seeing you master of the Abbey, not just my fine nephew. Then you’d have taken a political path, perhaps. But such things are not ours to choose.”

It was a gentle defense, a way of softening the sharpness of unspoken truths. Sir Hugo knew, and yet he left room for silence.

When Deronda arrived at the _Albergo dell’ Italia_, the princess was not there. On the second day, a letter came—written in careful French—saying she might arrive within a week, or not for two weeks more. Circumstances made it impossible to know. She asked him to wait patiently.

And so he waited.

With nothing but time and uncertainty, Deronda turned to the city. He wandered its streets, watched the sea from a boat, studied the quay where centuries ago Spanish Jews had fled—exhausted, dying, their children at their breasts, their eyes hollow with hunger. He saw history not as distant memory, but as a living wound. And in those images, he could not help wondering—was he one of them? Was his blood part of that suffering? He wanted to shut out such thoughts, but they rose like tides. He had never admitted to himself that he *wished* Mordecai’s belief to be true—that he was descended from a line of exiled Jews, bound by faith and fate. But he could not deny that the idea stirred him. Still, wishing was weakness. It was disowning the truth before it came. He had no choice. He must accept whatever was revealed.

And yet—what if Mordecai was wrong? What if he, Daniel Deronda, was not tied to any such destiny? Then he would not say, “I wish.” But he could not help feeling where the cost lay.

Over these thoughts, another presence loomed—Gwendolen. Not with passion, not yet, but with a quiet ache that echoed deeper than desire. There was a kind of love that wasn’t possessive, not entirely romantic, yet not mere friendship either. It was the love of what might have been—“I should have loved her, if…”—the “if” carrying both lost chances and unspoken rules. In Gwendolen, he had seen beauty, strength, a soul that trembled beneath its pride. And she had spoken words that stayed with him: “You must remain near me—must not forsake me.” Those words returned to him like music, like arrows dipped in pity—“Lamenti saettaron me diversi / Che di pietà ferrati avean gli strali.”

He remembered her as she stood at the edge of her world, caught between duty and desire. He remembered the look in her eyes—fear, yearning, defiance. And now, as he waited in Genoa, her fate seemed entangled with his own. Hers was personal, immediate. His was broader—shaped by secrets, by ancestry, by a purpose he could not yet name. And yet, in his heart, he sensed that her life mattered as much as his, even if she did not understand it.

Days passed. The air hummed with news of war—Austria challenged, armies moved, the world poised for battle at Sadowa. But in Genoa, life went on. The sun grew hotter, the dust thicker, the oleanders drooped like weary travelers. Evenings came with music—mule bells, stringed instruments, laughter drifting through the lanes. The hills above, crowned with forts and gardens, seemed to wake from sleep, gazing down with full beauty as moonlight softened the stone and shadows danced across cathedral steps.

Deronda watched the days turn, as if time itself were a clock of ancient figures marching in warning. He listened for a knock that would bring the moment he had dreaded and desired. He read books, but they blurred. He walked, but the streets held no peace. Nights were spent staring into the dark sea and sky, wrestling with doubt—was his life merely a dream? Or was it possible that he could serve something greater, even if it meant sacrifice?

Sometimes, he felt despair—his future reduced to a whisper, a fleeting change in the pattern of existence. Other times, a surge of hope lifted him—he might find a love that matched his duty, a life where personal devotion and moral purpose flowed together. And sometimes, rebellion flared—why must things be this way? Why hide, why delay? Why must he suffer for truths others buried?

But at last, the moment came.

On the morning of his third week, a new knock echoed through the hall. A servant in Chasseurs livery entered, bowing slightly, and delivered a message in French:

“The Princess Halm-Eberstein has arrived. She will rest during the day. She would be grateful if Monsieur would dine early, so as to be free at seven. At that hour, she will receive him.”
