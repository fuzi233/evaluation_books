In the softer later years of the century that followed, a flood of bright young newly-ordained curates would descend like spring rain across the heather-cloaked hills of northern England, settling thick in every parish, brimming with untapped zeal to serve their flocks. But we do not turn to those dust-drenched, sun-scorched later days. We slip back instead, to the crisp, woodsmoke sharp dawn of 1811, in the West Riding of Yorkshire, where wool looms clatter from cottage to mill, and the air hums with unspoken anger, quiet longing, and the sharp, sweet thrill of change waiting just over the moor horizon.

Do not mistake this prelude, dear reader, for a gilded romance of moonlit reverie and grand, sweeping passion, not yet. The first course set before you is plain, unvarnished, as unglamorous as a Monday morning mill whistle rousing half the parish from bed: cold lentils and bitter herbs before the feasting begins. The warmth, the thrills, the soft, unexpected turns of the heart will come later—we only lay the groundwork now.

Back in 1811, that flood of curates had not yet risen. No Pastoral Aid Society stretched out purses to help worn old rectors hire bright young graduates fresh from Oxford or Cambridge. The pious, Puseyite successors to the apostles we know in later years were still swaddled in frilled net caps, being christened in washbasin water, no one guessing that the squalling babe kicking in its long linen nightgown would one day stand in a pulpit flourishing a starched surplice never before seen higher than a parish reading desk.

Yet even in that scarcity, three rare "blossoming rods of Aaron" clustered within twenty miles around the villages of Whinbury, Briarfield, and Nunnely, and we are about to meet them. Step through the gate of the neat white cottage garden on Whinbury's edge, push open the parlour door, and there they sit, halfway through dinner, guests of their fellow curate Mr. Donne, who lodges here with John Gale, a small local clothier. They are young, bursting with restless energy that their staid old vicars keep trying to funnel into school inspections and sick calls—dull, thankless work, as far as the three lads are concerned.

Instead, they chase each other in a never-ending triangle of visits all year round, blundering through snowdrifts and rain-slick mud just to share a dinner, a pot of tea, a late supper. It is not friendship, not exactly—they bicker the whole time they are together. Not piety, for they never speak of it. Not even the food, for each could get as good a roast and suet pudding in his own lodgings. Their three landladies, Mrs. Gale, Mrs. Hogg, and Mrs. Whipp, all sigh that the boys do nothing but run them ragged for no good reason.

Mrs. Gale is particularly frazzled this Thursday. The week is not half done, and on Monday Mr. Peter Malone, the fiery Irish curate of Briarfield, turned up for breakfast and stayed through dinner. On Tuesday, Malone and the shy little flutist curate of Nunnely, Mr. Sweeting, came for tea, stayed for supper, camped in the spare bed, and lingered through Wednesday's breakfast. Now they are both back again, and she knows full well they will stay the night again. The roast beef is tough, the beer flat, and the young parsons do not even bother to be polite about it, sniffing at Yorkshire ways and sneering at their landlady for working her own house without a hired servant, as her mother did before her. Mrs. Gale mutters that the old parsons are worth ten of these college lads, who have no manners at all.

"More bread!" barks Mr. Malone, his thick Irish brogue ringing through the small parlour. Tall, broad-shouldered, with a sharp, high-featured face that looks carved out of Connaught granite, he makes Mrs. Gale's blood run a little hot. She would happily have sliced him along with the loaf if she dared, so sharp is the edge to his command.

The three young men eat as if half starved: the tough roast vanishes, the flat beer disappears, the Yorkshire pudding and heaped vegetable tureens melt like leaves before locusts, the spiced dessert cake that Mrs. Gale's six-year-old son Abraham had been saving for himself vanishes so fast there is not a crumb left. The little boy weeps bitterly in the kitchen when he sees the empty platter.

Upstairs, the curates sip their cheap port wine. Mr. Malone, who has already helped himself to two glasses to his friends' one, grows boisterous and teasing, jibing Mr. Donne about his thin frame, his silly turned-up nose, his frayed chocolate overcoat he wears every time it looks like rain, and his dandy Cockney accent. Next he teases tiny Mr. Sweeting about his small stature, his habit of playing the flute and singing hymns so sweet that all the young girls in his parish dote on him, and the fact that he still writes to his mamma and sisters back home.

Sweeting laughs it off, easygoing, but soon the jabs go too far, and the other two turn on Malone, teasing him about all the village boys that yell "Irish Peter!" after him in the lane, asking if he really carries loaded pistols and a blackthorn shillelagh on his pastoral visits, mocking his thick brogue that makes "veil" sound like "vele" and "storm" sound like "storrum".

Malone explodes, yelling and thumping the mahogany table so hard the decanters jingle, roaring that the English are all snobs, declaring he will fight for his conquered Irish homeland. The other two laugh at him, the little parlour in such an uproar that the Gales in the kitchen half expect the neighbours to send for the constable. But they know the score: the curates' fights never come to anything, no matter how loud they get, and they will be thick as thieves again by breakfast tomorrow.

Just then, a sharp rap raps on the front knocker. John Gale opens the door to find Mr. Helstone, the sharp, stern old vicar of Briarfield, standing in the dark, his shovel hat pulled low over his sharp, hawk-like face.

"Who've you got up in the parlour?" he demands, his nasal voice cutting sharp through the noise. When Gale tells him the three curates are there, dining and carrying on, Mr. Helstone marches straight through the kitchen and up the stairs, throwing the parlour door open without a knock.

The three young men freeze, mid-yell, their mouths hanging open. Mr. Helstone folds his arms across his chest, surveying them like a drill sergeant catching his recruits brawling. "What's this?" he booms, his voice deep and cavernous. "Has Pentecost come back? I could hear seventeen different languages of gibberish all the way down the lane, sounded like the Tower of Babel fallen over in here. Three Babylonish masons, that's what you are, not apostles."

Mr. Donne stammers out an invitation to sit, have a glass of wine, says they were only "settling the Dissenters" together. Mr. Helstone snorts. "Sounded more like you were trying to tear each other's heads off. The Methodist revival down the lane makes less noise than the three of you do, quarrelling over half a pint of port. No wonder the Dissenters are drawing all the weaver girls to their chapels, when the three of you can't even conduct a service to more than the clerk and the organist half the time."

He turns to Malone, his gaze sharp. "I came for you. Do you have your weapons on you? The pistols I gave you, your blackthorn stick?" Malone says he does. "Good. You're coming with me out to Hollow's Mill tonight."

Malone blinks. "What's happening there?"

"Nothing yet, but it might. The mill-owner Moore is bringing two wagonloads of new labour-saving machinery in tonight from Stilbro', and the Luddite weavers are furious. He sent all his trusted workmen out to fetch the machines, and there's barely two women left in the whole mill with him. The countryside is seething with anger at these new frames that will throw half the villagers out of work. You've heard of Mike Hartley, that raving Antinomian weaver who's half drunk half the time, spouting regicide and visions? He's got it into his head that Moore, the cleverest mill-owner in Yorkshire, should be made a sacrifice, a warning to all the rest. Last Friday Mike turned up at my door at midnight, ranting about a wild vision he'd seen in Nunnely Park, of blood running down Fieldhead Lane. It would be foolhardy to leave Moore alone there tonight. You're coming with me to stand guard, just in case."

Sweeting leans forward, eyes wide, full of that childish curiosity the other two lack. "What was the vision Mike saw, sir? Tell us!"
