The morning sun cast a sharp, unyielding light over the Thornton household, illuminating the tension that had settled within the drawing room like dust motes in a shaft of light. Mr. Thornton had spent considerable effort attempting to elevate his mother's spirits to a level of mere civility. Mrs. Thornton was not a woman accustomed to casual visits; she approached social duties with the gravity of a military campaign. Her son had procured a carriage, yet she had steadfastly refused to allow him to retain the horses, insisting on hiring them only for these solemn occasions of calling. For three days, she had ridden out with borrowed steeds, systematically exhausting the patience and resources of her acquaintances, leaving them to scramble for their own transport in return. However, Crampton remained too distant for a walk, prompting Mrs. Thornton to question her son repeatedly regarding the necessity of the cab fare. She confessed a reluctance to incur such expense, fearing that establishing intimacy with the Hales might merely be the prelude to being asked to visit Fanny's dancing master's wife.

"And so I would, mother," Mr. Thornton replied, "if Mr. Mason and his wife were friendless in a strange place, like the Hales."

"Oh! you need not speak so hastily," she countered. "I am going tomorrow. I only wished you to understand the arrangement precisely."

"If you are going tomorrow, I shall order the horses."

"Nonsense, John. One would think you were made of money."

"Not quite, yet. But regarding the horses, I am determined. The last time you rode out in a cab, you returned with a headache from the jolting."

"I never complained of it, I'm sure."

"No!" Mr. Thornton said, a note of pride coloring his voice. "My mother is not given to complaints. But precisely because of that, I have all the more reason to watch over you. Now, as for Fanny, a little hardship would do her good."

"She is not made of the same stuff as you are, John," Mrs. Thornton replied softly. "She could not bear it."

Silence fell upon the room. Mrs. Thornton's final words touched upon a subject that mortified her. She harbored an unconscious contempt for weakness, and Fanny was weak in the very areas where her mother and brother possessed strength. Mrs. Thornton was not a woman inclined toward lengthy reasoning; her quick judgment and firm resolution substituted for internal debate. Instinctively, she knew that nothing could strengthen Fanny to endure hardship patiently or face difficulties bravely. Though she winced at this admission regarding her daughter, it bred only a pitying tenderness in her manner, akin to the way mothers treat their frail, sickly children. To a careless observer, Mrs. Thornton's demeanor might have suggested she loved Fanny more than John. Such an interpretation would have been profoundly mistaken. The boldness with which mother and son exchanged unpalatable truths revealed a reliance on the firm center of each other's souls. In contrast, Mrs. Thornton's uneasy tenderness toward her daughter, coupled with her shame at hiding Fanny's poverty behind the grand qualities she herself possessed and valued so highly, betrayed a lack of a secure resting place for her affection. She never addressed her son by any name other than John; terms of endearment like "love" and "dear" were reserved exclusively for Fanny. Yet, her heart gave thanks for her son day and night, and she walked proudly among women solely for his sake.

"Fanny dear!" Mrs. Thornton broke the silence. "I shall have horses attached to the carriage today to visit the Hales. Should you not go and see Nurse? It is in the same direction, and she is always so glad to see you. You could go on ahead while I am at Mrs. Hale's."

"Oh! Mamma, it is such a long way, and I am so tired."

"With what?" Mrs. Thornton asked, her brow contracting slightly.

"I don't know—the weather, I think. It is so relaxing. Couldn't you bring Nurse here, Mamma? The carriage could fetch her, and she could spend the rest of the day here, which I know she would like."

Mrs. Thornton said nothing, laying her work upon the table as if in deep thought.

"It will be a long way for her to walk back at night!" she remarked finally.

"Oh, but I will send her home in a cab. I never thought of her walking."

Just then, Mr. Thornton entered, preparing to depart for the mill.

"Mother! I need hardly say that if there is any little thing that could serve Mrs. Hale as an invalid, you will offer it, I'm sure."

"If I can find it out, I will. But I have never been ill myself, so I am not much up to invalids' fancies."

"Well! Here is Fanny then, who is seldom without an ailment. She will be able to suggest something, perhaps—won't you, Fan?"

"I have not always an ailment," Fanny said pettishly. "And I am not going with Mamma. I have a headache today, and I shan't go out."

Mr. Thornton looked annoyed. His mother's eyes were fixed on her work, where she stitched away busily.

"Fanny! I wish you to go," he said authoritatively. "It will do you good, instead of harm. You will oblige me by going, without my saying anything more about it."

He left the room abruptly. Had he stayed a moment longer, Fanny would have cried at his tone of command, even with the words "You will oblige me." As it was, she merely grumbled.

"John always speaks as if I fancied I was ill, and I am sure I never do fancy any such thing. Who are these Hales that he makes such a fuss about?"

"Fanny, don't speak so of your brother. He has good reasons of some kind or other, or he would not wish us to go. Make haste and put your things on."

However, the slight altercation between her son and her daughter did not incline Mrs. Thornton more favorably toward "these Hales." Her jealous heart repeated her daughter's question, "Who are they, that he is so anxious we should pay them all this attention?" It lingered like a burden in a song, long after Fanny had forgotten it in the pleasant excitement of admiring her new bonnet in the looking-glass.

Mrs. Thornton was shy. Only in recent years had she possessed the leisure to enter society, and she did not truly enjoy it. She found satisfaction in giving dinners and critiquing others', but making acquaintance with strangers was a wholly different matter. She felt ill at ease, appearing more stern and forbidding than usual as she entered the Hales' small drawing-room.

Margaret was busy embroidering a small piece of cambric for an article of dress for Edith's expected baby—"Flimsy, useless work," Mrs. Thornton observed to herself. She preferred Mrs. Hale's double knitting; that was sensible work of its kind. The room was filled with knick-knacks that required significant time to dust, and time was money for people of limited income.

Mrs. Thornton made these reflections as she spoke in her stately manner to Mrs. Hale, uttering the stereotyped commonplaces that most people find when their senses are blindfolded. Mrs. Hale exerted herself more in her answers, captivated by a real old lace Mrs. Thornton wore. "Lace," she later told Dixon, "of that old English point which has not been made for seventy years, and which cannot be bought. It must have been an heirloom, showing that she had ancestors." Thus, the owner of ancestral lace became worthy of more than the languid exertion required to be agreeable to a visitor, which would otherwise have been the limit of Mrs. Hale's conversational efforts. Soon, Margaret, straining her brain to converse with Fanny, heard her mother and Mrs. Thornton plunge into the interminable subject of servants.

"I suppose you are not musical," Fanny remarked, "as I see no piano."

"I am fond of hearing good music; I cannot play well myself; and Papa and Mamma don't care much about it; so we sold our old piano when we came here."

"I wonder how you can exist without one. It almost seems to me a necessary of life."

"Fifteen shillings a week, and three saved out of them!" Margaret thought to herself. "But she must have been very young. She probably has forgotten her own personal experience. But she must know of those days." Margaret's manner acquired an extra tinge of coldness when she next spoke.

"You have good concerts here, I believe."

"Oh, yes! Delicious! Too crowded, that is the worst. The directors admit so indiscriminately. But one is sure to hear the newest music there. I always have a large order to give to Johnson's the day after a concert."

"Do you like new music simply for its newness, then?"

"Oh; one knows it is the fashion in London, or else the singers would not bring it down here. You have been in London, of course."

"Yes," said Margaret, "I have lived there for several years."

"Oh! London and the Alhambra are the two places I long to see!"

"London and the Alhambra!"

"Yes! Ever since I read the Tales of the Alhambra. Don't you know them?"

"I don't think I do. But surely, it is a very easy journey to London."

"Yes; but somehow," Fanny said, lowering her voice, "Mamma has never been to London herself, and can't understand my longing. She is very proud of Milton; a dirty, smoky place as I feel it to be. I believe she admires it the more for those very qualities."

"If it has been Mrs. Thornton's home for some years, I can well understand her loving it," Margaret said in her clear, bell-like voice.

"What are you saying about me, Miss Hale? May I inquire?"

Margaret had no ready words for this question, which took her slightly by surprise, so Miss Thornton replied:

"Oh, Mamma! We are only trying to account for your being so fond of Milton."

"Thank you," said Mrs. Thornton. "I do not feel that my very natural liking for the place where I was born and brought up—and which has since been my residence for some years—requires any accounting for."

Margaret was vexed. As Fanny had put it, it seemed as if they had been impertinently discussing Mrs. Thornton's feelings; yet she also rose against that lady's manner of showing offense.

After a moment's pause, Mrs. Thornton continued:

"Do you know anything of Milton, Miss Hale? Have you seen any of our factories? Our magnificent warehouses?"

"No!" said Margaret. "I have not seen anything of that description as yet."

Then she felt that, by concealing her utter indifference to all such places, she was hardly speaking with truth; so she went on:

"I dare say, Papa would have taken me before now if I had cared. But I really do not find much pleasure in going over manufactories."

"They are very curious places," said Mrs. Hale, "but there is so much noise and dirt always. I remember once going in a lilac silk to see candles made, and my gown was utterly ruined."

"Very probably," said Mrs. Thornton in a short, displeased manner. "I merely thought that as strangers newly come to reside in a town which has risen to eminence in the country from the character and progress of its peculiar business, you might have cared to visit some of the places where it is carried on; places unique in the kingdom, I am informed. If Miss Hale changes her mind and condescends to be curious as to the manufactures of Milton, I can only say I shall be glad to procure her admission to print-works, or reed-making, or the more simple operations of spinning carried on in my son's mill. Every improvement of machinery is, I believe, to be seen there in its highest perfection."

"I am so glad you don't like mills and manufactories, and all those kind of things," Fanny whispered half-audibly as she rose to accompany her mother, who was taking leave of Mrs. Hale with rustling dignity.

"I think I should like to know all about them, if I were you," Margaret replied quietly.

"Fanny!" said her mother as they drove away, "we will be civil to these Hales: but don't form one of your hasty friendships with the daughter. She will do you no good, I see. The mother looks very ill, and seems a nice, quiet kind of person."

"I don't want to form any friendship with Miss Hale, Mamma," Fanny pouted. "I thought I was doing my duty by talking to her, and trying to amuse her."

"Well! At any rate, John must be satisfied now."
