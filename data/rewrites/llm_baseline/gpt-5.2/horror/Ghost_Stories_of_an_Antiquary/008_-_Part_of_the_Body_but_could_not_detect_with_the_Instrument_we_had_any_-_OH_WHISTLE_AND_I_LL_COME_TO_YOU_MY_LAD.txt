“Soon off, I suppose, now term’s done, Professor?” said a voice from beyond the clink of glasses and the warm, yeasty breath of the college hall.

Professor Parkins—young, correct, a man who could pare a sentence as neatly as a cook skins an apple—inclined his head. “Yes. My friends have been making me take up golf, and I mean to go to the East Coast—Burnstow—for a week or ten days. I hope to be away tomorrow.”

At his other side an antiquary (one of those men who always smell faintly of dust and calf-leather, though they have just come from dinner) leaned in. “If you’re going to Burnstow, I wish you’d look at the site of the Templars’ preceptory. Tell me if it would be any good for a dig in summer.”

Parkins’s mouth tightened—usefulness, to him, wore the gown of a professor, not the shovel of a romantic. Still, he was punctilious. “Certainly. Tell me where it lies.”

“Down close to the beach, I should think. The sea’s eaten into the land there disgracefully. About three-quarters of a mile from the Globe Inn, north end of the town. Where will you stay?”

“At the Globe,” said Parkins. “I’ve taken a room. Winter lodgings are mostly shut, so they can only give me a double-bedded room. They can’t stow the other bed anywhere. I must have space for my books and notes. I don’t quite like having an empty bed—two, rather—in what will be my study, but it’s only for a little while.”

Across the table Mr Rogers—bluff, loud, fond of making his fun with a heavy hand—threw out, “If it’s only an empty bed that troubles you, Parkins, I’ll come down and fill it. Keep you company.”

Parkins gave the smallest quiver, which with him did duty for alarm. “By all means, Rogers,” he said, courtesy first as always. “Only—you would find it dull. You don’t play golf?”

“No, thank Heaven!”

“Well, then, when I’m not writing I shall be on the links.”

“Oh, I’d find someone,” Rogers said. “Unless you don’t want me. You always say truth’s never offensive.”

It put Parkins in a corner: the truth, that sacred, sharp instrument he insisted on handing others by the blade. After a pause—just long enough for everyone to hear him being honest—he said, “If you press me: I was considering whether the room would accommodate us both comfortably. And whether you might, perhaps, be—(I should not say it if you had not asked)—a hindrance to my work.”

Rogers roared. “Well done. All right, old man. I only thought I’d keep the ghosts off.” A wink went travelling round the table like a dropped coin.

Parkins coloured, as if heat had risen straight from the wine to his cheeks. “I do not like careless talk of—of that kind,” he said, with the anxious firmness of a man policing a boundary. “A man in my position cannot appear to sanction current beliefs in such matters. Any concession—any semblance of it—is a renunciation of what I hold most sacred.”

Rogers, to do him justice, softened. “Yes, yes. We’ll have it out at Burnstow.”

That, in brief, was Parkins: neat as folded linen, brave in his own fashion, a little hen-like in his caution, and without the least appetite for a joke unless it could be footnoted.

The next day he got away, and by afternoon the Globe Inn had swallowed him into its winter quiet. The landlord made him welcome; the fire in the public room had the smell of salt-damp wood; and in his own chamber, large and draughty, the double beds stood like two pale hulks moored side by side. Parkins arranged his books and papers with a cook’s exactness laying out knives and bowls: everything squared, everything in its place, as if order itself were a charm against intrusion.

His table sat at the outer end of the room, drawn up before three windows looking seaward. The middle pane gave him the straight stare of the sea; the side windows let the coast run away north and south like a strip of grey cloth. Southward lay Burnstow with its clustered roofs; northward, nothing but beach and a low cliff. Between the Globe and the tide there were only rough grass dotted with old anchors and capstans, then a broad path, then shingle and sand. Sixty yards at most—near enough that the sea’s breath salted the glass.

The inn’s winter company was mostly golfers. The loudest among them was Colonel Wilson, an old soldier turned club secretary, with a voice that could strip bark and opinions that came out Protestant as a trumpet-call—especially after he had endured the vicar’s service, the vicar being one of those decent men who liked his ritual picturesque but kept it down out of respect for East Anglian plainness.

Parkins, who possessed pluck of the unshowy sort, spent most of the following day improving his game in company with the Colonel. Whether improvement was possible under that barrage of command and commentary is doubtful; but by late afternoon the Colonel’s face had taken on such a heated and combative colour that Parkins, catching sight of moustache and flushed cheek, decided it would be wise to let tea and tobacco cool the man before dinner forced them together again.

“I might walk back along the beach,” he thought, tightening his scarf against the northern wind. “And look for Disney’s ruins. There will be light enough.”

He found them with his foot. Coming down from the links, picking his way to the shingle, he caught it in a gorse-root and a stone and went over, hands in the sand like a man bowing to a stern altar. When he rose and dusted himself, he saw he had fallen among broken ground—little depressions and mounds, some of them no more than turf hummocks, others hard with flints set in old mortar and half-swallowed by grass. Foundations, then; the preceptory. He walked about with the slow care of someone testing pastry for doneness—tapping the place with eye and heel, measuring in his mind.

He remembered (vaguely, but enough to please his sense of pattern) that the Templars built round churches, and indeed a group of humps did seem to arrange itself into a rough circle. Parkins paced it, wrote down dimensions in his pocket-book, gratifying his friend Disney and, in secret, himself. Then he went to an oblong rise to the east which might have been a platform or altar base. At its north end the turf had been torn away by boys or some other small vandal appetite. It seemed reasonable to probe there, and he took out his knife and began scraping.

The soil gave, unexpectedly, and fell in. Beneath lay a small cavity. He struck match after match, but the wind bullied each flame into nothing. Still, by tapping the sides with his knife and feeling the surfaces, he made out the hole was artificial: rectangular, with smooth, regular walls, a little box built into masonry. Empty, of course—until, as he drew back the knife, there came a clear metallic clink that cut through the wind like a spoon struck against a cup.

He put in his hand and found, lying on the bottom, a small cylinder. He brought it out into the fading light: a metal tube, about four inches long, aged and dulled, but unmistakably made by a human hand.

Darkness pressed in too quickly for more. He examined the cavity once more, found nothing else, and pocketed his find with a pleased quickness, as if he had slipped away a rare spice. Tomorrow, he resolved, he would give a little more daylight to this.

Before turning home he looked out over the coast. Bleak and solemn: links dimming under a faint yellow strip of westering light; a squat martello tower; far lights of Aldsey; the sands like a pale ribbon, stitched at intervals with black wooden groynes; and the sea murmuring in a low, steady voice, the kind that sinks into a man’s bones. The north wind had teeth, but at least it was at his back as he clashed over shingle and gained the sand, where his steps fell quieter.

Groynes forced him every few yards to climb and drop again. It was at one such pause that he glanced behind and saw, far back along the shore, a figure—indistinct, but with the troubling air of someone hurrying. There was a look of running about the movements, and yet the distance between them did not seem to lessen.

“It is absurd to wait,” Parkins decided. He did not know the man; and on a lonely shore the wrong companion is worse than none. Still, the sea’s voice, the wind’s thin whine over the sand, the harsh thud of his shoes on timber, made him aware of how hungry the mind can become for a second human sound. He found his thoughts dragging up old tales read in childhood—meetings on waste places, things seen against a yellow sky. If he looked back and saw a black figure sharply defined, with horns and wings—should he stand, or should he run? He ran, not from any horned fancy but from the very practical nearness of dinner-time.

He dressed in haste, and at table found the Colonel restored, so far as tea and tobacco could restore any man—Peace, or something tolerably like it, holding in the military bosom. There was bridge after dinner; Parkins played well enough to keep even the Colonel from exploding; and so by midnight Parkins went upstairs feeling, with the complacency of a person who has eaten and behaved correctly, that a fortnight—even three weeks—at the Globe might be borne “especially,” he added to himself, “if I continue to improve my game.”

In the passage he met the boots, who stopped, respectful and breathless in that way of men bringing small news like a dish they fear to spill. “Beg pardon, sir, but as I was a-brushing your coat there was somethink fell out of the pocket. I put it on your chest o’ drawers, sir, in your room, sir—a piece of a pipe or somethink, sir.”

The reminder quickened Parkins’s curiosity. In his room, by candlelight, he turned the object over. Bronze, now that he could see it properly—greenish where age had bitten, smooth where hands had worn it. It was shaped exactly like a modern dog-whistle. In fact it was a whistle: no more, no less.

He put it to his lips, but it was clogged with fine caked sand or earth. Knocking did nothing; the obstruction sat as stubbornly as dried dough in a crack of a board. Tidy to the last fibre, Parkins scraped the sand out with his knife onto a piece of paper, as carefully as if he were salvaging ground spice, and carried it to the window to empty. When he opened the casement the cold salt air rushed in; the night was clear, bright with hard stars, and the sea lay black and glossy. On the shore below the inn he noticed a belated wanderer standing as if listening. People kept late hours at Burnstow, it seemed. He shut the window, mildly surprised, and returned to his find.

There were marks on it. Letters. A rub with his thumb made the cuts stand out stark in the candle’s yellow: one inscription on the front, one on the back.

FLA FUR BIS FLE

And:

QUIS EST ISTE QUI VENIT

Parkins frowned, annoyed at himself as if he had discovered a stain on a clean cuff. “I ought to make it out,” he thought. “I must be rusty at my Latin. And I don’t even know the word for whistle. The longer seems plain enough: ‘Who is this who is coming?’”

His mind, always eager to prove a point by experiment, answered itself with a little thrill. “The best way to find out is evidently to whistle for him.”

He blew tentatively.

The note that came was soft, yet it held distance inside it, as if it had travelled a long way before reaching his own ears. It was not loud; it was worse than loud. It seemed made to slip through space without spending itself, to settle into every hollow of the coast. And it did something else—something Parkins could not have explained to a colleague without feeling foolish: it painted pictures in the brain the way certain smells do when you lift a lid and the steam rises—pictures so vivid you taste them.

For a moment he saw, with an odd clearness, a wide dark expanse at night, wind moving fast, and in the midst a lonely figure. How it was occupied he could not tell—only that it was there, and that the wind knew it. The image broke when a gust hit the casement with such sudden force that he looked up, just in time to catch the white glint of a seabird’s wing beyond the dark panes.

Fascination tugged him. He raised the whistle again and blew more boldly. The note was no louder, and this time no picture came—only the wind, which had sharpened, rising so abruptly that the house seemed to take it in the chest.

“There!” Parkins exclaimed, startled into speech. “What force it can get up in a few minutes! That fastening’s no use—ah!”

The casement burst inward with a violence that made the candles gutter and die. Cold air poured into the room like water into a pan, flattening the bedclothes, worrying the papers, making the unused bed seem suddenly less like furniture and more like something waiting. Parkins lunged for the window and fought it, straining as if he were pushing back a sturdy burglar. For twenty seconds he wrestled with it, fingers numb on the latch, the wind pressing with a deliberate strength that felt—unreasonably—like intention. Then, all at once, the pressure slackened. The casement banged shut and latched itself, as if someone outside had let go.

He relit the candles. No glass was broken. Nothing looked out of place, though everything looked more aware of him. Above, the Colonel was roused; Parkins heard heavy feet and a growl, the sound of a man whose sleep has been cut across like a loaf.

The wind did not drop at once. It went on moaning past the house and rushing along the eaves, sometimes rising to a cry so thin and desolate that it made the inn’s ordinary domestic noises—the settling of boards, the distant clink of some late glass—seem insultingly small. Parkins told himself, in the dry tone he used for correcting papers, that fanciful people might be made uncomfortable by it, and that even the unimaginative might be happier without such a sound.

He lay awake. Whether it was wind or golf or the day’s small archaeology he could not say; but wakefulness took him and kept him, and with it came the usual miseries of a neat mind in a dark room: the counting of heartbeats, the suspicion of lungs and brain and liver, all those doom-thoughts that daylight always laughs at and night will not release. He found a thin comfort in imagining someone else in the inn was as restless; nearby, someone—direction uncertain in the darkness—was tossing and rustling too.

At last he shut his eyes and resolved to give sleep its chance.

Then the pictures came.

Not the harmless drifting of half-dreams, but a continuous scene, like a lantern show that refused to be dismissed. When he opened his eyes it vanished at once; when he closed them again it framed itself afresh and played through at the same pace, neither faster nor slower, as if it belonged to time outside his own body.

He saw a long stretch of shore: shingle edged by sand, crossed at short intervals by black groynes running down to the water. It might have been the very beach he had walked that afternoon—no landmark to say otherwise. The light was wrong: dim, as of late winter evening thickening into storm, with cold rain like fine grit on the face.

At first, no one. Then, far off, a black bobbing thing. It resolved itself into a man running—jumping, clambering over the groynes—every few seconds twisting back to look behind him. The nearer he came the more terrible was his fear, though the face remained indistinct. He was almost spent. Each obstacle took more from him than the last, as if the beach itself were draining him.

“Will he get over the next?” Parkins found himself thinking, absurdly compelled to take part. The next groyne was higher. The runner half climbed, half flung himself, and did get over—and collapsed in a heap on the side nearest the watcher. There he stayed, crouched under the timbers, looking up with the cramped, trembling posture of an animal cornered.

Still no sign of what pursued him.

Then, far up the shore, there appeared a flicker of something pale moving with swift, irregular motion, like a sheet caught by sudden gusts. It grew larger quickly and became a figure in fluttering draperies, ill-defined, its edges never still. It stopped, raised arms, bent down towards the sand, then ran stooping to the water’s edge and back, as if searching by scent; then it rose upright and continued forward at a speed that made the mind recoil.

The pursuer hovered from left to right only a few yards beyond the groyne where the runner hid. After two or three castings this way and that, it halted, stood upright with arms raised high, and then darted straight toward the groyne.

At that instant Parkins always broke—eyes flying open, heart hammering as if it had run the beach itself. He told himself, with the frantic rationality of the terrified, about incipient failure of sight, overworked brain, too much smoking. But the persistence of the vision—its exactness, its refusal to behave like imagination—made him at last choose a simpler defence: light.

He struck a match. The first flared and died. In the dark, something small scurried across the floor from the side of the bed—rats, or whatever makes that dry, secretive sound, the kind that seems to carry the whole building’s history of crumbs and refuse. The second match caught properly. He lit a candle, fetched a book, and sat propped against the pillows, reading with fierce attention as if words could barricade him.

At some point sleep came—wholesome, heavy—and for the first time in his orderly life he forgot to blow the candle out. When he was called at eight, there was still a feeble flicker in the socket and a sad spill of guttered grease on the little table, like cold fat hardened after careless cooking.

After breakfast he was in his room putting the final touches to his golfing clothes—fortune had again paired him with the Colonel—when one of the maids came in.
