For as long as I could remember, whispers had drifted through the Great Redoubt—tales of another refuge, a second bastion of humanity hidden somewhere in the desolate wastes of the Night Lands. Somewhere beyond the suffocating dark, beyond the crawling horrors, another stronghold endured.

I heard these stories in every city of the Pyramid, from the thousandth floor down to the deepest tiers. It was part of our training, part of our wandering. At seventeen, I began my own journey through the one thousand, three hundred and twenty cities, spending a single day in each, as was the custom. Three years and two hundred twenty-five days it took, and in that time, I met countless souls—some kind, some curious, all bound by the same unspoken duty to the Redoubt. Yet among the millions, I found no one who could answer the quiet hunger in my heart.

The archives spoke of this other refuge. Ancient records swore to its existence, though scholars of my time dismissed them as fables. But I never doubted. Not after the Master Monstruwacan himself—my mentor and friend—spoke of it with certainty.

He was the one who opened the Tower of Observation to me when I turned twenty-one. A rare privilege, for only the keenest minds served there. And I? I had the Night-Hearing. A gift, they called it, though it felt more like a curse. I could hear the whispers of the aether, the vibrations humming through the void, clearer than any instrument. And so I listened—always listened—for a voice that had haunted my dreams since childhood.

A name from another life, another age. A love lost in time, yet whispering to me still.

Then, one day, as the Sleep-Time began at the fifteenth hour, I heard it. Not in memory. Not in dream. A voice, faint but unmistakable, threading through the endless night. My pulse hammered. I sent the Master-Word into the dark, a silent pulse of recognition.

And the darkness answered.

The Master Monstruwacan entered just as the reply trembled through me. He saw my face and went still. He had not heard it himself—only the faintest echo on the instruments—but he knew.

*"Mirdath?"* I called again, shaping the name with my mind.

And then—laughter. Soft, distant, but real.

From that moment, the Tower became my sanctuary. The Monstruwacans gathered when I spoke, hungry for tales of the Days of Light, of the Sun they had never seen. I told them of green fields, of open skies, of a world alive beneath golden rays. They listened, some in awe, some in skepticism, but all spellbound.

Yet the voice in the dark was what truly held me.

Her name was not Mirdath, but Naani. A girl from that other refuge, the second Redoubt—a three-sided pyramid, smaller than ours, standing alone in the abyss. She knew my name, my old name, from some fragment of history preserved in her people’s records. A coincidence, a twist of fate, yet it bound us.

Her people were few, barely ten thousand, their Earth-Current fading. For ages, they had languished, until—recently—it stirred anew. Life quickened within them. Children were born. Hope flickered. And Naani, daughter of their Master Monstruwacan, discovered she could hear the aether as I could.

We spoke across the void. She told me of her world, of the slow withering, of the sudden resurgence. I told her of ours—the vast Pyramid, the unyielding vigilance, the ceaseless war against the Night.

And with every word, the distance between us shrank.

But the Night Lands are cruel. The silence between our messages stretched. The instruments faltered. The aether grew still.

And I knew then—I would have to go to her.

The Law forbade it. No man could venture into the Night before twenty-two. No woman, ever. The dangers were too great, the horrors too many. Yet beneath my skin, the capsule waited—a final mercy, should the dark take me.

I would go.

For her voice.

For the answer to a calling I could no longer ignore.
