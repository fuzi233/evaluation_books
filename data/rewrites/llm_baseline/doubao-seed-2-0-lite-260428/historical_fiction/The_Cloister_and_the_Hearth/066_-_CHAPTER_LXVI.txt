Two nights after those events, Pietro Vanucci and Andrea lingered at the supper table, waiting for Gerard. The trencher of warm spiced bread and roasted chestnuts had gone cold, the light red wine in their cups had turned flat, and Pietro’s patience frayed thin as the edge of an old vellum manuscript when the bell in the piazza tolled past nine o’clock. At last he snapped at Andrea to trudge up to Gerard’s chamber, desperate for the slim chance the younger man had slipped in unobserved and curled up asleep without announcing himself.

Andrea shrugged his broad shoulders, huffed under his breath, and obeyed. He returned empty of Gerard, but clutching a crumpled scrap of paper. Andrea could not read in the formal, scholar’s sense the men of their trade were trained in, but his eye was sharp as a point of a stylus: he had seen Gerard scrawl Pietro Vanucci’s name at the foot of so many small notes left on their shared workbench that he spotted the familiar looping script at once. “That’s for you, I trow,” he said, puffing out his chest proud of his clever guess.

Pietro snatched the scrap and read it out loud to Andrea, lacing every line at first with his usual dry, sardonic tone:
“‘Dear Pietro, dear Andrea, life is too great a burden.’
‘So ’tis, my lad,’” Pietro jested, “‘but that is no reason to tarry abroad past supper time. Supper is not a burden.’
‘Wear my habits!’
‘Said the poplar to the juniper bush, take my cast-offs when I’m gone,’” he chuckled, before his grin began to falter.
“‘And thou, Andrea, mine amethyst ring; and me in both your hearts a month or two.’
‘Why, Andrea?’” he said, quieting now, as a cold prickle ran down his spine.
“‘For my body, ere this ye read, it will lie in Tiber. Trouble not to look for it. ’Tis not worth the pains. Oh unhappy day that it was born oh happy night that rids me of it.
Adieu! adieu!
The broken-hearted Gerard.’”

“It’s nothing but a sorry jest from that peevish rogue,” Pietro blustered, but his cheeks were pale as unmarked parchment, and his teeth chattered so hard his words nearly rattled apart. Andrea wasted no time on pretense: he wailed so loud the whole household echoed with his cries, startling apprentice boys from their benches and maids from the kitchen fire. “O, miserable day! O, calamity of calamities! Gerard, my friend, my sweet patron! Help! help! He is killing himself! Oh, good people, help me save him!” He tore out the front door bareheaded, screaming his plea to every passerby on the cobblestone street, begging every good Christian to stop and lend a hand.

A small crowd gathered fast, but none would move. Trudge down to the Tiber after dark? No, that was no one’s business. No one could name exactly where he might have waded in. It was a fool’s errand, and worse: everyone knew the river banks after sundown were thick with ghosts of all the poor souls who had drowned themselves there, and to walk them after dark was to call ill luck down on your own head. Only a lackey who had loitered across the street for hours, unseen, stepped forward and said he would go with Andrea. That small show of courage shamed three or four of the younger idlers in the crowd to tag along.

The small band set off for the river. The lackey, who was a secret spy for Princess Claelia, plied Andrea with questions as they walked, and Andrea, sobbing so hard his words stumbled, told him of Gerard’s quiet, brooding moods of late, how he would stare out the window at the Tiber for hours on end without speaking a single word. The moment they rounded the last street corner and hit the river’s cold, sharp breeze, thick with the smell of mud and moving water, every voice went dead silent. The moon hung high and clear in a cloudless sky, silvering the ripples of the Tiber, and the crunch of their boots on the gravel bank sounded loud and ominous against the still, quiet night.

Presently they hurried around a sharp river bend and nearly ran straight into a man. He stopped, irresolute, at the sight of them. His hair was bare, dripping, glistening like strands of glass in the moonlight, and every step he took left a dark wet puddle on the stones: his clothes were soaked through to the skin. “Here he is!” one of the young men cried, who had never seen Gerard before. The stranger panicked instantly, turned on his heel, and fled as fast as his water-heavy legs could carry him.

They gave chase at full tilt, Andrea leading the pack, the princess’s lackey right at his heels. Andrea was fast gaining on the fugitive, but the man twisted sharp up a narrow, alley so small Andrea could not check his own momentum and skidded right past its mouth. By the time the rest of the party skidded to a halt behind him, the stranger had vanished into the labyrinth of twisting, unmarked lanes that crisscrossed that quarter, known only to people who lived every inch of them. It was useless to chase further.

They trundled back to the spot where they had first encountered the dripping man, to find Pietro waiting there, shaking his head. “That was not Gerard,” he told them plainly. “Wrong height, wrong face, I never laid eyes on that man in my life.”

“Are ye verily sure?” the others protested. “He was soaked through from the river. Why, in the saints’ names, did he flee at our approach?”

But the lackey cut in before they could bicker further. “See the wet prints he left all down the path,” he said. “Let us track them back to the water’s edge, to make certain Gerard is not there.” The group agreed, and followed the dark wet marks easily for fifty yards, only to find they veered sharp away from the river, straight for the heavy oak gates of a grim, shadow-shrouded monastery, its stone walls towering high overhead like a dark secret. Andrea spotted a second set of wet prints branching off the path, however, leading straight back toward the Tiber, and they followed those until they reached the very edge of the bank, where a whole bucketful of river water had evidently dripped off the fugitive’s clothes.

They found a fisherman who lived in a hut not far off, paid him liberally with copper coins, and promised him a purse full of gold if he dragged every stretch of the Tiber up and down that bank with his nets, searching for Gerard’s body. The rest of the party ran up and down the shore screaming his name, but the river flowed on calm and voiceless, holding Gerard’s fate and a thousand other strange, dark secrets tight behind its glassy surface. Suddenly Andrea gave a sharp cry of hope, and sprinted back to the house, only to return half an hour later, wringing his hands, his face streaked with tears. He had checked every tavern, every friend’s house, every shadowed nook Gerard was known to frequent, and found nothing.

“What is the hour?” the lackey asked, his voice grave.

“Four hours past midnight,” someone answered.

“My pretty lad,” the lackey said solemnly to Andrea, “go say a mass for thy friend’s soul. For he is not among living men.”

Dawn broke pink over the distant hills, and Andrea and Pietro dragged their exhausted, aching bodies back home, heartsick and hollow. The days rolled on after that, as silent and unyielding as the Tiber itself, and never once yielded a single clue as to what had become of Gerard.
