Our dear readers have already heard Sir Francis Clavering’s rather blunt assessment of the woman who had handed him both her fortune and a ticket back to England—no small feat, that. And truth be told, the Baronet wasn’t entirely off base. Lady Clavering was not, by any stretch, the most enlightened or polished of ladies. Her education? A couple of years in Europe, in a suburb of London she insisted on calling “Ackney” until her last breath. (She’d been summoned to Calcutta at fifteen, you see, and never quite shook the accent—or the geography.) On her way out east, aboard the *Ramchunder* East Indiaman under Captain Bragg—a man whose name alone could make one shudder—she met her first husband, Mr. Amory, third mate of said vessel. But before we dive into their romance, let’s pause for a moment: Captain Bragg, who was transporting Miss Snell (as she was then) to her father—also a part-owner of the *Ramchunder*—found it necessary to lock up the rebellious rascal of a mate in irons. The ship reached the Cape, where Amory was left behind like an uninvited guest at a funeral. Bragg delivered his charge safely to Calcutta after a voyage marked by storms, near-disasters, and general maritime drama.

Months later, Amory reappeared—having worked his way back from the Cape as a common sailor, married the rich attorney’s daughter against her father’s wishes, tried his hand at indigo planting (failed), became an agent (failed again), edited the *Sunderbund Pilot* (still failed), and quarreled with everyone he met—especially his father-in-law and wife. His career ended in a spectacular crash, forcing him to flee to New South Wales. Along the way, he likely crossed paths with Sir Jasper Rogers, the respected Judge of the Supreme Court of Calcutta—though how much they discussed is anyone’s guess. And here’s the kicker: Amory’s downfall came not from incompetence, but from using his father-in-law’s name—whose handwriting was perfectly legible—to forge letters. The man didn’t need a secretary; he needed a therapist. So when fortune turned her back on Amory, she did so with a flourish.

The European public, bless them, didn’t know half of this. But Mrs. Amory and her father found life in India less than cozy. So, after some thought, it was decided she should return to Europe—with her four-year-old daughter, Betsy (or Blanche, depending on who was counting), and their nurse, Mrs. Bonner, who would later become Lady Clavering’s most trusted confidante. Captain Bragg, ever the gentleman, secured them a house near his own in Pocklington Street.

It was a summer of biblical proportions—rain every day, the kind that makes you want to stay indoors and write poetry about despair. Bragg, naturally, was pompous, disagreeable, and deeply embarrassed by his Indian lodgers. Mrs. Amory, meanwhile, believed the whole of London was whispering about her husband’s ruin—surely the King and Queen were aware! She had a generous allowance from her father, no real reason to stay in England, and so, gratefully, she fled—escaping the shadow of Captain Bragg like a moth from a candle.

Abroad, she was a sensation. People welcomed her everywhere—Paris, Brussels, Milan, Naples, Rome—wherever her fancy led her. She still called Hackney “Ackney,” spoke English with a charming foreign lilt, dressed like a queen, and prepared curries and pilafs at every boarding-house she visited. Her quirks only made her more delightful. She was the most generous, cheerful, and jolly woman alive—bringing triple the champagne to picnics, handing out theater tickets like candy, paying her bills months in advance, and quietly helping poor travelers whose money hadn’t arrived yet. She lived like a whirlwind, leaving trails of kindness and laughter in her wake.

Then came news of Amory’s death—delivered to her in Rome, where Captain Clavering was staying, unable to pay his hotel bill (a shared affliction with his friend, the Chevalier Strong). The good-natured widow, seeing no great loss in her late husband (who, frankly, had been a bit of a disaster), promptly married the descendant of the ancient Clavering line. She professed no deep sorrow—only mild relief. Thus began her reign at Clavering Park, where Mr. Pinckney painted her portrait with her little boy beside her.

Her daughter, Missy, followed her mother’s travels closely and absorbed more life lessons than most girls learn in a lifetime. She had a governess, then Madame de Caramel’s prestigious pension in the Champs Elysées. When the Claverings returned to England, she came too. It wasn’t until her grandfather died and her brother Frank was born that she realized her status had changed. Miss Amory, once nobody’s daughter, now stood in the shadow of Master Francis Clavering, heir to a baronetcy and a vast estate. Had it not been for little Frank, she might have been an heiress herself. But she cared little for money—though she was romantic, dreamy, and full of poetic yearnings. Still, she couldn’t help but resent those who had altered her fate. Her stepfather was dull and weak. Her mother dropped her H’s and lacked refinement. And little Frank—spoiled, quarrelsome, and always stepping on her toes—was a constant irritation. He upset her dinner, trod on her dresses, and seemed to monopolize all affection.

None of them understood her. So her lonely heart sought elsewhere—looking for someone to love, someone to belong to. This dear girl, lacking sympathy at home, grew increasingly restless, irritating her mother and boring her stepfather so thoroughly that they were almost as eager as she was for her to find a husband. Hence Sir Francis’s wish, mentioned earlier, that Mrs. Strong die so he could marry Blanche himself. But since that was impossible, any eligible young man was welcome. And a handsome, well-educated fellow like Arthur Pendennis? He could have proposed with open arms—and Lady Clavering would have welcomed him as a son-in-law, had he shown the courage.

But Mr. Pen, alas, suffered from the peculiar affliction known as self-doubt. He was ashamed of his failures, his idle past, his poverty—which had forced his mother to pinch pennies at Fairoaks. There was vanity in his hesitation, yes, but also genuine remorse. How could he hope to win such a prize? Blanche lived in a grand park, waited on by dozens of servants, while he dined on meager meals brought in by a single maid. The obstacles seemed insurmountable—until he realized they weren’t. But instead of charging forward, he lingered in doubt, perhaps even unaware of what he truly wanted.

Many a young man fails not through lack of merit, but through a species of vanity called shyness. Pen was one of them. He wasn’t certain he loved Blanche—only that he liked her. She was charming, lively, and could charm the birds from the trees. But there were deeper reasons holding him back.

For one thing, his mother saw through her. Mrs. Pendennis sensed something amiss in the girl—light-mindedness, frivolity, a lack of reverence for family and sacred things. Laura, too, began to see flaws beneath the dazzling surface. At first, she defended her new friend fiercely. But over time, she noticed the contradictions. Blanche complained of being misunderstood, yet treated others with cruelty. She lamented her mother’s ignorance, yet showed no patience. She criticized Sir Francis for being unintellectual, yet demanded everything be done her way. She spoke tenderly of her brother, calling him “my pretty baby sweet,” promising to love him always, even if the world proved false—but then boxed his ears when he spilled water on her drawing.

Laura, with her gentle heart, couldn’t help but recall the touching verses Blanche had read from *Mes Larmes*, where she vowed eternal devotion to her brother. And now, here she was, treating him like a servant. It was a cruel lesson in hypocrisy—one Laura had seen before, though not in such a vivid form. For Pen, too, had flaws—waywardness, selfishness—but beneath them beat a kind heart. And now, watching Blanche, Laura felt the sting of disillusionment. Her admiration faded, replaced by a quiet sense of superiority. She began to speak to Blanche with calm dignity, a tone the Muse disliked intensely.

Nobody likes to be exposed. And when you’ve been worshipped, stepping down feels like falling. Blanche, sensing this shift, grew peevish, irritable, and worse. One fateful day, she was particularly awful—insolent to her mother, savage with little Frank, cruel to her attendant Pincott, whom she tormented like a spoiled child with a cat. Poor Pincott, driven from the room in tears, received a final sneer as she fled. That was the straw that broke the camel’s back.

Laura exploded. She couldn’t believe how someone so young could be so rude, so unkind. She accused Blanche of wickedness, declared her conduct sinful, and stormed out—leaving Blanche stunned and humiliated. Back at Fairoaks, she confessed to her mother: “Mamma, you were right. Blanche is selfish, cruel. She speaks of love, but has no heart. No honest girl would treat a mother so, or torment a servant. I give her up. From this day, I will have no other friend but you.”

They embraced—long, tearful, heartfelt. Mrs. Pendennis felt a secret joy. Her daughter’s rejection meant Pen would never marry Blanche. He would see her true nature soon enough. He’d be saved from a flighty creature, awakened from a dream.

But Laura didn’t tell her mother the real cause of the quarrel. Earlier that day, Blanche had been in a mischievous mood. She’d brought up Mr. Pen—how he’d once been involved with an actress, how he’d been “false.” Laura, blushing, defended him: “He was generous. She was the one who was untrue. He nearly broke his heart.” Blanche, archly, asked if he’d written her poems. Laura admitted only to reading two: *The Fisherman and the Diver*, and his Prize Poem—“pompous and prosy,” she laughed. Then Blanche, with a sly smile, whispered, “Promise not to tell anyone, and I’ll show you something.”

She fetched a mother-of-pearl desk, opened it with a silver key, and pulled out crumpled papers stained with green ink. Love poems—about Undine, about Naiads, about rivers. Laura read them, but her eyes blurred. “Have you answered them?” she asked. “No, not for worlds,” Blanche replied, slipping them back. Then she sang Rossini songs with perfect trills, while Laura sat silently, lost in thought.

After lunch, they walked arm-in-arm down the corridor. Just before entering the hall, Laura paused, looked at her friend kindly, and kissed her with sisterly warmth. Something—perhaps Frank’s manners, or mamma’s mistakes—set Blanche off again. And thus began the storm.

So ends the tale of a lady who danced through life with charm and grace, only to reveal herself as a mortal, flawed, and very human. And of a young man who hesitated, not because he was unworthy, but because he was afraid to be happy.
