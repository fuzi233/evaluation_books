Sally’s mood, as she sat in her New York apartment on the morning of her return, was like that of a swimmer who, after hours of hesitation at the edge of a freezing pool, finally dives in—shivering, yes, but also alive. She was hurting, sure, but it was a good kind of pain: the kind that comes from doing something brave instead of pretending you don’t care. For months she’d been avoiding the fight, dodging decisions like a cowardly boxer. But not anymore. She was in the middle of the ring now, gloves up, ready to take whatever came. And it hurt. She’d expected that. But this wasn’t the dull ache of regret—it was the sharp, electric sting of being *real*. She felt awake. Alive. Defiant.

She’d unpacked, cleaned, and now, there was only one thing left to do: see Ginger. The thought of him had suddenly become urgent. His quiet, dependable presence would be like a life raft in this stormy sea of emotions. She wished she’d sent him a cable—then he could’ve met her at the dock. As it was, the customs sheds had echoed with emptiness, sucking the courage right out of her. She’d stood there, suitcase in hand, feeling like a stray cat lost in a city of giants.

She checked her watch. It was still early. Perfect. She could catch Ginger at the office and drag him out for lunch. She put on her hat and stepped into the street.

The restless spirit of change had not spared Fillmore Nicholas Theatrical Enterprises Ltd. In the few months since Sally had last visited, the outer office had undergone a full-scale identity crisis. Gone was the cheerful, pimpled boy she’d once chatted with; in his place stood a new stripling—also generously dotted with zits, but otherwise completely different. This one wore a permanent scowl and radiated suspicion like a bad Wi-Fi signal. He peered at Sally as if she’d just stolen the blotting paper (which, truthfully, she hadn’t), then demanded, with icy disdain, “State your business.”

“I want Mr. Kemp,” said Sally.

The boy scratched his cheek with a ruler, his face grim as a judge who’d just ruled against a dog named Poochie. No one would’ve guessed that mere seconds before, he’d been trying to balance the ruler on his chin while juggling two paperweights. His dream? Vaudeville. One day, he’d be the star of a show where people paid money to watch him do ridiculous things with office supplies.

“What name?” he asked, cold as a January wind.

“Nicholas,” said Sally. “I’m Mr. Nicholas’ sister.”

On a previous visit, this announcement had caused chaos. Today, however, it worked like a charm. It hit him like a well-placed uppercut. He jumped, dropped the ruler, and stared at her as if she’d just declared herself queen of the universe. By the time he bent down to retrieve it, he’d already recalibrated his entire demeanor. What had started as an opportunity to vent frustration on a random visitor had turned into a golden chance to impress the boss’s sister. He straightened up with a smile so warm it could’ve melted snow in Siberia.

“Will you take a seat, lady?” he asked, even going so far as to dust off a chair with the sleeve of his coat. “Fine morning, isn’t it?”

“Thank you,” said Sally. “Could you tell Mr. Kemp I’m here?”

“Mr. Nicholas is out, miss,” said the boy, with exaggerated sorrow. “He’s back in town, but he’s gone out.”

“I don’t want Mr. Nicholas. I want Mr. Kemp.”

“Mr. Kemp?”

“Yes. Mr. Kemp.”

The boy’s face fell like a deflated balloon. “Don’t know anyone by that name around here,” he said, apologetically.

“But surely…” Sally trailed off. A cold dread crept over her. “How long have you been working here?”

“All day, ma'am,” he replied, sounding like a man who’d just witnessed a tragedy.

“I mean—how long have you been employed here?”

“Just over a month, miss.”

“So… Mr. Kemp hasn’t been here all that time?”

“Name’s new to me, lady. Does he look like anything? You know—what does he look like?”

“He has very red hair.”

“Never seen him,” said the boy flatly.

And that was that. The truth hit Sally like a punch to the gut. She blamed herself. Why had she ever left? Of course Ginger would mess up. Left to his own devices, he always did. And this time, apparently, he’d really gone and done it. Not just failed—he’d made such a hash of things that Fillmore had probably fired him. After all, how many times could you protect someone before they became a liability?

“Where is Mr. Nicholas?” she asked. “You said he was out?”

“Really out, miss,” the boy said, grinning like a man who’d just found a winning lottery ticket. “He went to White Plains in his car half an hour ago.”

“White Plains? Why?”

The boy, now fully engaged in conversation, leaned forward. Usually, he liked solitude—his juggling practice required focus—but Sally had somehow unlocked his inner garrulousness.

“I guess he’s going to check on Bugs Butler,” he said.

“Whose butler?” Sally asked, confused.

The boy smiled—a tolerant, pitying smile. Women, he knew, were often clueless about the real world. It wasn’t their fault. It was biology. Or destiny. Or something.

“Bugs Butler is training in White Plains,” he explained. “He’s a fighter.”

“Oh.” Sally blinked. “Who is Bugs Butler?”

A shadow crossed the boy’s face. This was serious business.

“Ah!” he said, losing all pretense of politeness. “Who has Bugs Butler ever licked? That’s what everyone’s asking. Nobody. Just some nobodies out west—ginks no one’s heard of, except Cyclone Mullins. And even then, it took fifteen rounds to get a decision. The boss wants to give him a shot at the champ, but I told him—K-leg Binns is the real contender. K-leg knocked out Cyclone in five. So if anyone thinks Bugs Butler can go six rounds with Lew Lucas, I’ve got two bucks in my vest pocket that says he won’t.”

Sally began to understand.

“Oh—so Mr. Butler is one of the boxers in the fight my brother is involved in?”

“That’s right. He’s fighting the lightweight champion. Lew Lucas. He’s a bird!”

“Is he?”

“Yes, sir!” the boy said, head tilted like a curious bird. “Lew Lucas is a hot sketch. Used to live on the next street to me. I’ve seen his old mother—close as I am to you. Say, I’ve seen her a hundred times. Is any stiff of a Bugs Butler going to beat a guy like that?”

“It doesn’t seem likely.”

“You spoke it!” the boy snapped, swatting at a fly that had landed on the blotting paper.

They paused. Sally stood up.

“And there’s another thing,” the boy said, reluctant to let the conversation end. “Can Bugs Butler make a hundred and thirty-five ringside without being weak?”

“It sounds hard.”

“They say he’s clever.” The boy laughed bitterly. “Well, what’s that gonna get him? The poor fish can’t punch a hole in a nut-sundae.”

“You don’t seem to like Mr. Butler.”

“Oh, I’ve nothing against him,” he said magnanimously. “I just think he’s no license to mix it with Lew Lucas.”

Sally nodded. Enough boxing talk. More important matters awaited.

“How do I find my brother when I get to White Plains?”

“Oh, anyone’ll show you the way to the camp. If you hurry, you can catch the train now.”

“Thanks.”

“You’re welcome.”

He opened the door for her with the grace of a man who’d forgotten how to be polite. Then, with the air of someone returning to work after a pleasant distraction, he picked up the paperweights and placed the ruler carefully on his chin—ready to begin again.

Fillmore sighed with relief and began to sneak toward the door. The room was massive—half barn, half gymnasium—lined with ropes, punching bags, and other equipment that looked like it had been designed by a mad scientist. In the center, a roped-off ring held a small crowd, mostly plutocrats with fifty cents to spare. In today’s world, boxing training was public entertainment. If you had the cash, you could watch. If you didn’t, you could still come—especially if you were a reporter or artist. Reporters would write glowing accounts of Bugs Butler’s “superb condition” and “lack of apprehension,” while artists would draw him in a state of semi-nudity with feet twice the size of normal.

Fillmore was relieved because Mr. Lester Burrowes—the manager of Bugs Butler—had finally wandered off to deal with some urgent matter. That meant Fillmore could escape to the hotel and eat something. He hadn’t eaten since breakfast, and his stomach was now conducting its own protest march.

So he headed for the door—only to collide with Sally.

“Hallo, Fillmore!”

She spoke softly. But the effect was like a bomb going off in a library. Fillmore leapt three inches off the ground, eyes wide, mouth agape, tongue darting out to moisten dry lips. He stared at her as if she’d just materialized from thin air.

Men in moments of weakness deserve sympathy. Fillmore had every reason to jump. He’d left Sally in England less than a week ago—at Monk’s Crofton. She’d said nothing about leaving. Yet here she was, in White Plains, New York, whispering in his ear like a ghost. No wonder he was stunned. And no wonder, as he regained composure, a chill of dread settled in.

Because Fillmore hadn’t missed the significance of that invitation to Monk’s Crofton. When a man invites a woman and her family to his country home, gathers the whole clan, and treats them like royalty—it’s not just a friendly gesture. It’s a declaration. And Fillmore had deduced, with chilling accuracy, that Bruce Carmyle was in love with Sally. He’d mentally joined their hands and blessed the union. And now—here she was, standing in a boxing gym, clearly having changed her mind.

“Sally!” he croaked. “What... what...?”

“Did I startle you? I’m sorry.”

“What are you doing here? Why aren’t you at Monk’s Crofton?”

Sally glanced at the ring.

“I decided I wanted to come back to America. Circumstances made staying unpleasant.”

“Do you mean to say…”

“Yes. Don’t let’s talk about it.”

“Do you mean,” Fillmore pressed, “that Carmyle proposed and you said no?”

Sally flushed.

“I don’t think it’s nice to discuss that sort of thing, but—yes.”

Fillmore felt the world tilt. Everything had been perfect. The match was ideal. He liked Carmyle—quiet, dignified, wealthy, everything a girl like Sally should want. And now she’d ruined it. With the kind of capriciousness only women possess, she’d thrown away a golden future.

“But why?”

“Oh, Fill!” Sally said, annoyed. “Shouldn’t the reason be obvious?”

“You don’t like him?”

“I don’t know whether I do or not. I certainly don’t like him enough to marry him.”

“He’s a darned good fellow.”

“Is he? You say so. I don’t know.”

His hunger returned with a vengeance.

“Let’s go to the hotel and talk. I’ll get you something to eat.”

“I don’t want anything to eat, thanks.”

“You don’t want anything to eat?” Fillmore said, horrified. “I’m starving.”

“Well, run along then.”

“Yes, but I want to talk…”

But he wasn’t alone in wanting to talk. At that moment, Mr. Lester Burrowes bounded up. He wore a checked tweed suit and a brown bowler hat—defying fashion and logic. He clutched Fillmore’s coat-button.

“Say, Mr. Nicholas, you ain’t going? Bugs is just getting ready to spar!”

He looked at Sally.

“My sister—Mr. Burrowes,” Fillmore said faintly. “Mr. Burrowes is Bugs Butler’s manager.”

“How do you do?” Sally said.

“Pleased to meecher,” Burrowes replied. “Say….”

“I was just going to the hotel,” Fillmore said.

Burrowes grabbed him tighter.

“Yes, but before-you-go-lemme-tell-ya-somef’n. You’ve never seen this boy of mine when he’s feeling right. Believe me, he’s there! He’s a wizard! He’s a Hindoo! Say, he’s been practising up a left shift that…”

Fillmore looked at Sally, helpless. She pitied him. Soon, she’d demand an explanation for firing Ginger. But for now, she remembered he was her brother—and he was suffering.

“He’s the cleverest lightweight,” Burrowes raved, “since Joe Gans. I’m telling you, I know!”

“Can he make a hundred and thirty-five ringside without being weak?” Sally asked.

The effect was catastrophic. Burrowes dropped Fillmore like a hot potato. His mouth hung open. It was as if a child had just asked him a question about quantum physics.

“Whazzat?” he whispered.

“It took him fifteen rounds to get a referee’s decision over Cyclone Mullins,” Sally said severely. “And K-leg Binns…”

Burrowes rallied.

“You ain’t got it right!” he protested. “The referee was dead against us. Cyclone was down once—for half a minute—and they wouldn’t count him out. Gee! You gotta kill a guy in some towns before they’ll give you a decision. Still, they couldn’t do nothing so raw as make it anything but a win for my boy, after him leading by a mile all the way. Have you ever seen Bugs, ma'am?”

Sally admitted she hadn’t.

Burrowes fumbled in his pocket and pulled out a postcard. “That’s Bugs,” he said. “Take a slant at that and tell me if he don’t look the goods.”

The photo showed a young man in minimal clothing, crouching as if in agony.

“I’ll call him over and have him sign it,” Burrowes said, before Sally could react. “Here, Bugs—wantcher.”

A young man in a bathrobe, talking to admirers near the ring, turned and sauntered over. Then saw Sally. His pace quickened.

Burrowes introduced them.

“Bugs, this is Miss Nicholas, come to see you work out. I’ve been telling her she’s going to have a treat.” To Sally: “Shake hands with Bugs Butler, ma'am—the coming lightweight champion of the world.”

Sally studied the man. The photo had flattered him. In person, he was repulsive—mean lips, cold eyes, a dangerous aura. And he smirked at her.

Still, she forced a smile.

“I hope you win, Mr. Butler.”

The smirk widened. He was convinced he’d charmed her. He always did. It was something about him. His chest swelled beneath the robe.

“You betcher,” he said.

Burrowes checked his watch.

“Time you were starting, Bugs.”

Bugs tore his gaze from Sally and glanced at the audience. Too small. Too many free riders.

“All right,” he said, bored.

But then he saw Sally again. His spirits lifted. Even a small crowd could be fun if it included bright eyes.

“I’ll go a couple of rounds with Reddy for a starter,” he said. “Seen him anywheres? He’s never around when he’s wanted.”

“I’ll fetch him,” Burrowes said. “He’s back there.”

“I’m going to show that guy up this afternoon,” Bugs said coldly. “He’s been getting too fresh.”

Burrowes hurried off. Bugs gave Sally one last smirk and slipped under the ropes.

The crowd stirred. The reporters remained impassive.

Then Burrowes reappeared, dragging a young man who was pulling a sweater over his head. The sweater slid off—revealing red hair, tousled and wild.

Sally gasped.

The red-headed man froze, staring at her, face turning crimson.

It was Mr. Burrowes who broke the silence.

“Come on, come on,” he said impatiently. “Li'l speed there, Reddy.”

Ginger Kemp startled awake, then slowly began putting on his gloves. Embarrassment etched his features. His face matched his hair.

Sally tugged at Burrowes’s sleeve. He turned irritably, then beamed when he saw her.

“Who—him?” he whispered. “He’s just one of Bugs’ sparring-partners.”

“But…”

Burrowes, now in action mode, cut her off.

“You’ll excuse me, miss, but I have to hold the watch. We mustn’t waste time.”

Sally stepped back. She felt like an intruder at a sacred ceremony. This was Man’s domain. Women stayed in the shadows. She felt tiny, yet conspicuous—like a puppy in a cathedral.

She watched Ginger, who was fiddling with his clothes in the corner, avoiding her gaze. He seemed miles away. She stared, wide-eyed. Ginger shuffled his feet, tugged at his gloves.

And the world stopped.
