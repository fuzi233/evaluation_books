Tito came back from Siena, and almost at once was gone again on some other errand, vanishing out of Florence as readily as a man steps from a bright loggia into a shadowed street. He did not return until the seventeenth of August.

Nearly a fortnight had slipped by since the arrests, and the five accused men were still behind stone and iron, still without sentence, still held in that feverish uncertainty which can gnaw a family more cruelly than a judgment. For Romola, those days had narrowed into one continual watching—like a cook who cannot leave the hearth because the pot’s fate depends on the moment the boil turns. Everything else—her household, the city’s quarrels, even her own weariness—had become a far-off murmur beside the one question: would her godfather live?

Sometimes the chances seemed to rise in the prisoners’ favor simply because time was passing. Delay meant more hands could be set to work, more doors tried, more voices softened. And delay was the easiest virtue for magistrates who feared hatred whichever way they leaned. One party cried that the Republic was in danger, that mercy would be a trumpet-call to enemies. Another knew well that to condemn five citizens of eminent name to death and confiscation would plant rancor like bitter seed in every allied house, and that those who cast the fatal votes would have to harvest that bitterness for years.

The judgment properly belonged to the Eight who ruled criminal justice, and the sentence depended on a majority of six votes—the infamous six beans, those small pale counters by which Florence measured life and death as if with pulses shaken into a bowl. But the Eight shrank, as men will shrink when they feel the weight of the axe-handle in their own palms. They sought to share responsibility with the Signoria. The Signoria, in turn, shrugged away the burden and proposed an appeal to the Great Council; for a law urged through nearly three years before—one of the proud, often-quoted ornaments of the new popular government—gave any citizen condemned by the six beans the right to appeal.

Yet even that hope became a contest. The friends of the accused resisted an appeal at this stage, chiefly because they wanted delay, and strict legality demanded that sentence come before appeal. Their resistance prevailed. A middle course was taken: the matter was referred on the seventeenth to a large mixed assembly—higher magistracies, the Senate of Eighty, and certain chosen citizens—so that judgment might be diffused like responsibility in a watered stew.

That day, with anxiety tightened by the thought that before evening her godfather’s fate might be fixed beyond recall, Romola had gained leave to see him a second time, but only under watch and with witnesses. She returned afterward to the Via de’ Bardi with her cousin Brigida, still ignorant of what the council had done. Brigida, restless and practical, went out again to wait near sources of sure news, at the house of a friend connected with the magistracies, promising to bring back authentic tidings the moment they could be had.

Left alone, Romola sank on the first seat in the bright saloon as if her body had been set down there by other hands. The room glimmered with costly order, with the polished surfaces and carved edges that declared comfort; yet her mind took no hold of any of it. She sat with her back toward the door, her head pressed into her hands. Outside, Florence spoke in its accustomed chorus—footsteps striking stone, a distant cart’s wooden complaint, the thin call of a street-seller, the fluttering hush of pigeons—and under it, like a continual undertone, the Arno’s dull breathing. Each sound came as a reminder that the city kept living, kept bargaining and gossiping and cooking its soups, while men’s lives were measured out by councils.

It seemed long since Brigida had gone. Romola expected her return. But when the door opened, the step that entered was not Brigida’s.

Since that night of parting from Tito, Romola had had no outward proof that could be held up like a written charge to warrant her belief that he had bought his safety by betrayal. On the contrary, she had heard enough to know that the Mediceans still trusted him and believed him busy with errands in Romagna, cloaking their work as government service. The obscurity surrounding the evidence had allowed everyone to suppose that Tito had slipped free of implication.

But her suspicion would not be washed away by any public opinion. The image of his conduct toward Baldassarre had stained every thought of him. It was as if she had once seen him at some dreadful work—blood on the hands—and afterward every ordinary gesture carried a remembered redness. At the sound of his step on the stone floor, a chill passed through her like a draught through an unlatched shutter. She could not turn. She could not rise. Greeting was impossible.

He did not speak at first. After an instant’s pause he took a seat on the opposite side of the table, facing her. Then she lifted her eyes.

He met her look without impatience and said coolly, as if they were speaking of business to be set in order: “This meeting corresponds with our parting, Romola. But I understand it is a moment of terrible suspense. I am come—if you will listen—to bring you the relief of hope.”

She started, shifting her posture, and looked at him with distrust that did not yet harden into refusal.

“It will not be unwelcome to you to hear,” he went on, “even though it is I who tell it, that the council is prorogued till the twenty-first. The Eight have been frightened at last into passing a sentence of condemnation; but the demand has now been made on behalf of the condemned for the appeal to the Great Council.”

The doubt fell away from her face. Her voice came quickly: “And when is it to be made?”

“It has not yet been granted; but it may be granted. The Special Council is to meet again on the twenty-first to deliberate whether the appeal shall be allowed or not. In the meantime there is an interval of three days—three days in which chances may occur in favor of the prisoners—in which interest may be used on their behalf.”

Romola sprang up. Color rose in her cheeks like a sudden flame under the skin, as if her blood had caught the word “interval” and ran with it. Her hands trembled. In that moment she forgot even Tito. Three days were like three fresh loaves set before the starving: not deliverance, but something to hold the soul from fainting.

“Possibly,” Tito said, rising also, “your own intention anticipates what I was going to say. You are thinking of the Frate.”

“I am,” Romola said, turning her startled gaze on him. “Has he done anything? Is there anything to tell me?”

“Only this. Messer Francesco Valori’s bitterness and violence chiefly determined the course of things to-day. Half the men who gave their opinion against the prisoners were frightened into it. But there are numerous friends of Fra Girolamo both in the Special Council and out of it who are strongly opposed to a sentence of death—Piero Guicciardini, for example, one member of the Signoria who resisted stoutly; and Giovan Battista Ridolfi, Piagnone as he is, will not lightly forgive the death of his brother Niccolò.”

“But how can the appeal be denied,” Romola said, indignation sharpening her voice, “when it is the law—when it was one of the chief glories of the popular government to have passed the law?”

“They call this an exceptional case. Of course there are ingenious arguments.” Tito’s tone was lightly ironic. “But there is much more loud bluster about the danger of the Republic. Still—no opposition could prevent the prorogation. And a certain powerful influence, rightly applied during these three days, might determine wavering courage in favor of granting the appeal, and might even check Valori’s headlong enmity.”

He paused, watching the effect of each phrase as a man might watch a sauce thicken, cautious not to let it curdle. “It happens to have come to my knowledge that the Frate has so far interfered as to send a message to Valori in favor of Lorenzo Tornabuoni. I know you can sometimes have access to the Frate. It might at all events be worth while to use your privilege now.”

“It is true,” Romola said, as if speaking to herself rather than to him. “I cannot believe the Frate would approve denying the appeal.”

“I heard it said by more than one person in the court of the Palazzo,” Tito continued, “that it would be to the everlasting discredit of Fra Girolamo if he allowed a government almost entirely made up of his party to deny the appeal without entering his protest—when he has been boasting in his books and sermons that it was he who got the law passed. But between ourselves”—his voice softened into a familiarity that stung—“with all respect for your Frate’s ability, my Romola, he has got into the practice of preaching that form of human sacrifice called killing tyrants and wicked malcontents, which some of his followers are likely to think inconsistent with lenity in the present case.”

“I know, I know,” Romola said, pain tightening her mouth. “But he is driven into those excesses of speech. It used to be different.” Her words came with a weary tenderness, as if she were defending some old, half-forgotten household song in a dialect few now understood. “I will ask for an interview. I cannot rest without it. I trust in the greatness of his heart.”

Her eyes were bent toward the floor, her mind already moving away from the room, away from Tito, toward San Marco and its echoing cloisters. She scarcely felt that the voice she heard belonged to her husband.

“Better lose no time, then,” Tito said, with unmixed suavity, turning his cap between his fingers as if ready to take his leave. “And now, Romola, you will perhaps be able to see, in spite of prejudice, that my wishes go with yours in this matter. You will not regard the misfortune of my safety as an offence.”

The words struck her like a sudden harsh note in an otherwise tolerable harmony—an ugly clang that made all the surrounding sounds jar. Full consciousness of him returned, not as domestic familiarity but as something intrusive.

She looked at him without speaking.

“At least,” he added, and his tone hardened slightly, “you will endeavor to base our intercourse on some other reasonings than that because an evil deed is possible, I have done it. Am I alone to be beyond the pale of your extensive charity?”

The feeling driven back from her lips a fortnight earlier surged up with gathered force, as if every day of silence had fed it. She spoke with a decision that told him she no longer weighed consequences.

“It is too late, Tito. There is no killing the suspicion that deceit has once begotten. And now I know everything. I know who that old man was: he was your father, to whom you owe everything—to whom you owe more than if you had been his own child. Beside that, it is a small thing that you broke my trust and my father’s. As long as you deny the truth about that old man, there is a horror rising between us: the law that should make us one can never be obeyed. I too am a human being. I have a soul of my own that abhors your actions. Our union is a pretence—as if a perpetual lie could be a sacred marriage.”

Tito did not answer at once. When he did, the caution in his voice was no longer lazy; it was sharpened by alarm, calculated like a merchant’s figures.

“And you mean to carry out that independence by quitting me, I presume?”

“I desire to quit you,” Romola said, impetuously.

“And supposing I do not submit to part with what the law gives me some security for retaining?” Tito’s words came more quickly now, as if he wished to leave no pause in which her resolve could find breath. “You will then, of course, proclaim your reasons in the ear of all Florence. You will bring forward your mad assassin—who is doubtless ready to obey your call—and you will tell the world that you believe his testimony because he is so rational as to desire to assassinate me.”

His voice grew colder with each sentence, like water poured over fire. “You will first inform the Signoria that I am a Medicean conspirator, and then you will inform the Mediceans that I have betrayed them; and in both cases you will offer the excellent proof that you believe me capable in general of everything bad. It will certainly be a striking position for a wife to adopt. And if, on such evidence, you succeed in holding me up to infamy, you will have surpassed all the heroines of the Greek drama.”

He paused, enjoying the weight of his own mastery. Romola stood mute.

“I believe you have no other grievance against me,” he went on, “except that I have failed in fulfilling some lofty indefinite conditions on which you gave me your wifely affection—so that by withdrawing it you have gradually reduced me to the careful supply of your wants as a fair Piagnone of high condition and liberal charities. I think your success in gibbeting me is not certain. But doubtless you would begin by winning the ear of Messer Bernardo del Nero?”

“Why do I speak of anything?” Romola cried at last, as anguish broke through like a sob that cannot be stifled. She sank back into her chair. “It is hateful in me to be thinking of myself.”

She did not notice when Tito left the room, nor how long the air held his presence after the door had closed. The sounds of the house came back—some far clink, a footfall, the faint rustle of fabric—and outside, the city’s unceasing breath, like a great creature that never sleeps.

When the door opened again, Monna Brigida entered; and in that instant Romola sprang up as if roused by a bell.

“Cousin,” she said, “we must go to San Marco directly. I must see my confessor, Fra Salvestro.”
