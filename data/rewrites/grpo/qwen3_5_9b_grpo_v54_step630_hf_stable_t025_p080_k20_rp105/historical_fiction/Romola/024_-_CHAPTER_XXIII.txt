"You are easily frightened, though," Piero remarked, his laughter cutting through the tension like a blade. "My portrait lacks the ferocity of the original. Yet, the old man truly possessed a tiger's gaze; I must return to the Duomo to study him once more."

"It is hardly pleasant to be seized by a madman, if indeed he is one," Lorenzo Tornabuoni replied, offering a polite defense for Tito's trembling. "Perhaps he is merely a ruffian. We shall discover the truth soon. However, we must determine if we possess the authority to halt this disturbance between our citizens and your countrymen," he added, turning his gaze toward the French soldier.

The group moved forward, swords drawn, a silent phalanx of quiet onlookers parting to clear their path. Tito followed, compelled by necessity to learn what others already knew of Baldassarre. The initial paralysis of terror was rapidly giving way to the frantic calculations that mortal danger forces upon the timid.

The mob of men and boys, prone more to jeering and tormenting the soldier than inflicting serious harm, parted instantly at the sight of the nobles with steel in hand. The Frenchman was interrogated. He and his companions had merely escorted their prisoners into the city to solicit ransom: two were Tuscan soldiers captured in Lunigiana, while the third, an elderly man, had accompanied a party of Genoese who had clashed with French foragers near Fivizzano. He might be mad, but he posed no threat. The soldier knew nothing further, unable to comprehend a single word the old man uttered. Tito listened, deaf to the surrounding chatter until Tornabuoni addressed him directly.

"Will you return with us, Melema? Or, since Messere is departing for Signa, would it be wise to follow the current fashion and attend the Friar? He will be like a torrent in full flood this morning. It is what we must all do to preserve our Medicean skins. I myself would go if I had the leisure."

Tito's face had regained its color, and he managed a smile of forced gaiety.

"Naturally, I am among the admirers of the inspired orator," he said smoothly. "Unfortunately, I shall be occupied with the Segretario until the procession begins."

"I shall enter the Duomo to examine that savage old man again," Piero declared.

"Then, Piero mio, have the charity to show him to one of the hospitals for travelers," Tornabuoni suggested gently. "The monks may ascertain whether he requires confinement in a cage."

The party dispersed. Tito turned toward the Palazzo Vecchio, where he intended to meet Bartolommeo Scala. The distance was short, yet for Tito, it stretched out like the agonizing minutes of a waking dream. The brief intervals of street and piazza were filled with memories, premonitions, and torturing fears that could have spanned months. He felt as though a serpent had begun to coil around his limbs.

Baldassarre alive and present in Florence was a living revenge, a coiling viper that would not rest until it had crushed its prey. It was not in that man's nature to let an injury pass unavenged. His love and hatred burned with a passionate fervor that subjugated all else, driving a man to sacrifice himself to his passion as if worshipping a deity through self-destruction. Baldassarre had loosened his grip and vanished. Tito understood this well: it meant the vengeance was being studied, refined to ensure its certainty.

If he had not spoken those decisive words—"He is a madman"—if he could have summoned the mental fortitude to admit his recognition of Baldassarre, would the risk have been less? He might have claimed he believed Baldassarre dead. The only witnesses capable of contradicting him were Fra Luca, who was deceased, and the crew of the companion galley that had delivered the news of the pirate encounter. The odds were infinite against Baldassarre having met any of that crew again. Tito thought with bitter resentment that a timely, well-crafted falsehood might have spared him from fatal consequences. Yet, uttering such a lie would have demanded perfect self-command during a moment of convulsive shock. He seemed to have spoken without preconception; the words had leaped forth like a sudden birth, nurtured in the darkness.

Tito was subject to that inexorable law of human souls: we prepare ourselves for sudden deeds through the reiterated choice of good or evil, which gradually hardens character.

Now, there was but one chance: Baldassarre's failure to find his revenge. And then, a thought more actively cruel than any he had previously entertained struck him: might there be some truth in his own unpremeditated words? Enough truth, at least, to support his denial should Baldassarre attempt to identify him? The old man appeared strange and wild; with his eager heart and fevered brain, suffering had likely driven him to madness. If so, the vengeance seeking to inflict disgrace might be baffled.

Yet, there existed another form of vengeance that no ingenious lying could parry. Baldassarre belonged to a race for whom the thrust of the dagger feels as natural an impulse as the tiger's leap. Tito shrank with shuddering dread from disgrace, but he also harbored a physical terror inseparable from a soft, pleasure-loving nature—a fear that prevents a man from meeting wounds and death as a welcome relief from shame. His thoughts immediately sought some hidden defensive armor to protect him from a vengeance no subtlety could deflect.

He marveled at the power of the passionate fear that possessed him. It was as if he had been struck by a blighting disease, suddenly transforming the joyous sense of youth into pain.

One resource remained. He could have turned back, sought Baldassarre again, confessed everything—to Romola, to the world. But he never considered this. True repentance, which severs all moorings to evil, demands more than selfish fear. He possessed no sense that strength and safety lay in truth; the only strength he trusted was his ingenuity and his dissimulation. Now that the initial shock, which had betrayed the treacherous signs of fear, had passed, he hoped to be prepared for all emergencies through cool deceit and defensive armor.

It was a defining characteristic of Tito's experience at this crisis that no direct measure for removing Baldassarre ever occurred to him. Every other possibility swept through his mind, even flight from Florence itself, yet he never conceived a scheme to eliminate his enemy. His dread generated no active malignity; he would still have preferred not to cause pain to any mortal. He had simply chosen to make life easy for himself—to carry his human lot in a way that pinched him nowhere. That choice had, at various times, landed him in unexpected positions. The question now was not whether he should share the common pressure of destiny with his suffering fellow-men, but whether all the resources of lying would suffice to save him from being crushed by the consequences of that habitual choice.
