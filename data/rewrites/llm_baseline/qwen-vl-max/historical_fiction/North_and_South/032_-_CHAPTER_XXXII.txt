“What! remain to be denounced—dragged, perhaps, in chains.”

The next day passed slowly, the three of them seated together in silence, as if time itself had paused in mourning. Mr. Hale spoke rarely, only when his children pressed him with questions that pulled him reluctantly into the present. Frederick’s grief, once raw and overwhelming, had settled into quiet sorrow; the first storm had passed, and now he was ashamed of having been so undone by it. Though his love for his mother remained deep and enduring, he would never speak of it again. Margaret, less outwardly emotional at first, found her pain growing heavier with each passing hour. She wept often, and even in ordinary conversation, her voice carried a softness edged with sorrow—especially when her eyes met Frederick’s, knowing how soon he must leave.

She was glad he was going—glad for her father’s sake, though her own heart ached at the thought. The fear that consumed Mr. Hale—the constant dread that his son might be recognized and seized—outweighed any comfort he took in Frederick’s presence. Since Mrs. Hale’s death, his anxiety had worsened, sharpened by isolation and the weight of silence. He jumped at every sudden noise, and could not relax unless Frederick sat out of sight from anyone entering the room.

As evening approached, he turned to Margaret. “You’ll go with Frederick to the station, won’t you? I need to know he’s safely off. Will you bring me word that he’s clear of Milton—at least?”

“Of course,” she said. “I’d like to go, if you won’t be lonely without me, papa.”

“No, no!” he insisted. “I’d never rest easy unless I knew you’d seen him depart. Go to Outwood station. It’s just as near, and fewer people about. Take a cab. Less chance of being seen. What time is your train, Fred?”

“Ten minutes past six—nearly dark,” Frederick replied. “So what will you do, Margaret?”

“Oh, I can manage,” she answered, forcing a smile. “I’m getting braver, harder. The road home is well lit, even if it’s dark. And I was out much later last week.”

She was relieved when the moment came to part—not just from the dead, but from the living, too. She hurried Frederick into the cab, eager to shorten a scene that tore at her father’s heart. He would walk with his son to see him one last time at his mother’s side.

They reached Outwood earlier than expected, thanks in part to a common error in the Railway Guide, which listed the arrival time incorrectly. They had nearly twenty minutes to spare. The booking office was closed, so they couldn’t purchase tickets. Instead, they descended the steps leading down to the level ground beneath the tracks. A wide cinder path cut diagonally across a field beside the carriage road, and there they walked back and forth, waiting.

Margaret’s hand rested in Frederick’s arm. He gently squeezed it.

“Margaret,” he said softly, “I’ve decided to consult Mr. Lennox about clearing my name. Not for myself alone—but for you. I can’t bear the thought of you left alone if anything should happen to Father. He looks so worn—so broken. I wish you’d consider the Cadiz plan. If he were taken away, what would you do? You have no close friend nearby. We’re oddly isolated.”

Margaret fought back tears at the depth of his concern, for she knew the same fate was possible, given how frail her father had become. But she steadied herself. “These past two years have brought such strange changes to my life that I’ve learned not to dwell too closely on what might come. I try to live only in the present.”

She paused. They stood still, near the stile leading into the road. The sun was setting, casting golden light across their faces. Frederick held her hand tightly, reading more sorrow in her eyes than she would ever admit aloud.

“We’ll write often,” she continued. “And I promise—I know it will ease your mind—to tell you everything that troubles me. Papa is…” She faltered slightly, a barely perceptible tremor in her hand.

Frederick felt it. He turned sharply toward the road. A horseman was approaching slowly, passing right by the stile where they stood.

Margaret bowed stiffly. The rider returned the gesture with equal formality.

“Who is that?” Frederick asked, almost before the man was out of earshot.

“That’s Mr. Thornton,” she said quietly. “You saw him before.”

“Only his back,” Frederick replied. “He’s not an appealing man. Look at that scowl.”

“Something has upset him,” she said gently. “You wouldn’t think him unkind if you’d seen him with mamma.”

“I suppose it’s time to get our ticket,” Frederick said, glancing at the sky. “If I’d known it would be this dark, I wouldn’t have sent the cab back.”

“Oh, don’t worry,” she said, brushing aside his concern. “I can catch a cab here, or return by rail. From Milton station, there are shops, people, lamps—all the way. Don’t think of me. Just take care of yourself. I’m terrified Leonards might be on the same train. Check the carriage carefully before you board.”

They returned to the station. Margaret insisted on stepping inside, under the bright gaslight, to buy the ticket. A few idle young men loitered near the station master. One caught her eye—she thought she recognized him—and returned his bold stare with a look of quiet dignity. She hurried to Frederick, who waited outside.

“Have you got your bag?” she asked, gripping his arm. “Let’s walk around the platform.”

She was flustered, suddenly afraid of being left alone. Her courage, so strong just moments ago, began to slip.

A step followed them along the platform flags. It stopped when they did. They stood in silence, watching the distant line, listening to the whistle of the approaching train. Their hearts pounded. One moment more, and the train would arrive. Another second, and he would be gone.

Margaret regretted urging him to leave by train. It seemed reckless—more chances of discovery. If he had sailed from Liverpool to Spain, he might have been out of reach in hours.

Frederick turned, facing the lamp, where the gas flared brightly in anticipation of the train. Suddenly, a man dressed as a railway porter lunged forward—coarse, brutish, clearly drunk, yet fully aware. He shoved Margaret violently aside and grabbed Frederick by the collar.

“By your leave, miss!” he snarled. “Your name’s Hale, I believe?”

In a flash—how, Margaret didn’t know—Frederick twisted, tripped the man, and sent him crashing down from the elevated platform onto the soft earth beside the tracks. He lay motionless.

“Run, run!” Margaret gasped. “The train’s here! Was it Leonards? Oh, run! I’ll carry your bag!”

She seized his arm, pushing him forward with all her strength. A carriage door opened—he leapt in. As he leaned out, he called, “God bless you, Margaret!”

Then the train roared past, leaving her standing alone.

She was sick, faint, trembling. She stumbled into the ladies’ waiting-room and sank into a seat, gasping for breath. It had been so fast—so terrifying—so close. Had the train not arrived when it did, the man would have risen and shouted for help.

She wondered if he had moved. Had he been hurt? She forced herself to go back to the platform. It was lit, but empty. She walked to the edge and looked down—no one was there. She was grateful she had checked. Otherwise, nightmares would haunt her.

Still, she shivered. The road home seemed long and dark from the station’s glare. She could not walk it alone. She would wait for the down train. But what if Leonards recognized her as Frederick’s companion?

She hesitated before stepping into the booking office, counting her change with shaking fingers, her back to the men standing near the counter.

“So Leonards has been drinking again,” one said, sounding exasperated. “He’ll need all his influence to keep his job this time.”

“Where is he?” another asked.

“I don’t know. He came in five minutes ago, babbling about a fall, swearing like mad. Wanted to borrow money to catch the next up-train to London. Made all sorts of promises—but I told him to mind his own business. He left through the front door.”

“He’s probably at the nearest vaults,” the first man muttered. “Your money would’ve gone there too, if you’d been foolish enough to lend it.”

“Catch me!” the second laughed. “I knew better what his London meant. Why, he hasn’t paid me that five shillings yet.”

They went on, unaware of the woman listening behind them.

Now, Margaret’s only hope was for the train to arrive quickly. She retreated again to the waiting-room, every sound making her jump—every loud voice echoing like Leonards’. But no one came near her until the train pulled in.

A porter helped her aboard. She avoided his face until the train was moving. Then she glanced up—and breathed a silent sigh of relief. It wasn’t Leonards.
