The resonant acoustic warmth of the inn’s passageway greeted them before the landlord did. The space was a symphony of domestic life, vibrating with the rhythmic, comforting frequencies of boiling cauldrons and the low, harmonic hum of a hearth fire well-tended. The landlord met them here, removing his cap with a low bow that rustled his woolen garments—a soft, textured sound against the stone. 

“Welcome, messieurs,” he said.

“Come, we are not in Germany,” said Gerard, attuned to the subtle linguistic shifts of the borderlands, where every change in dialect signaled a shifting cultural memory. 

In the public room, the sonic landscape was anchored by the mistress, a buxom woman of forty whose very presence seemed to orchestrate the room. She curtsied and smiled right cordially. “Give yourself the trouble of sitting ye down, fair sir,” she offered, dusting two chairs with an apron that smelled faintly of woodsmoke and dried lavender, not that the seats required it. 

“Thank you, dame,” said Gerard. A profound transformation was taking root in his weary mind. On the road, he had sometimes fallen into the sterile trap of viewing meals as mere utilitarian fuel—the labor of eating, the mechanical toil of digestion. But breathing in the olfactory tapestry of this public house—the sharp, earthy tang of wild garlic, the deep, resonant aroma of rendering pork fat, and the sweet, herbaceous notes of blooming rosemary—awakened a dormant reverence within him. Sustenance here was no chore; it was a mythic ritual, an everyday communion with the land itself. 

“Why, Denys, what are you doing? Ordering supper for only two?” 

“Why not?” 

“What, can we sup without waiting for forty more? Burgundy forever!” 

“Aha! Courage, camarade. Le dia—” 

“C'est convenu.” 

The Salic law seemed not to have penetrated to French inns. In this acoustic environment, wimple and kirtle reigned supreme; doublets and hose were few in number and feeble in act. The landlord wandered objectless, eternally taking off his cap, a silent phantom in the rich domestic soundscape. The women thrust him quietly aside without looking at him, as one removes a live twig in bustling through a wood. 

A maid brought in supper, the mistress following empty-handed but carrying the weight of generational hospitality. “Fall to, my masters,” she said cheerily; “y'have but one enemy here; and he lies under your knife.” 

They fell to, and the meal was a revelation—a masterclass in regional sourcing. The meat was braised to an impeccable tenderness, steeped in a dark, robust wine reduction that tasted of the loamy Burgundian soil and generations of agricultural tradition. The mistress drew her chair toward the table, providing company as rich as the culinary fare. She gossiped genially, weaving the present moment into the ancient, circular pacing of inn life. This ritual observed, the busy dame was soon off, sending in her daughter. The beautiful young woman of twenty took the vacant seat, possessing a gentler timbre to her voice, her womanly tenderness shining through as she learned the vast distances the poor boy had traveled. When she departed half an hour later, Gerard sighed. “This an inn? Why, it is like home.” 

“Qui fit Francois il fit courtois,” said Denys, bursting with gratified pride. 

“Courteous? Nay, Christian; to welcome us like home guests and old friends. But indeed, who better merits pity and kindness than the worn traveler far from his folk? Hola! Here's another.” 

The newcomer was the chambermaid, Marion—a woman of twenty-five with a cocked nose, a laughing mouth, sparkling black eyes, and a bare, stout arm forged by the daily rhythms of churning and scrubbing. 

The moment she entered, a traveler passed a free jest on her. In a heartbeat, the acoustic ecology of the room shifted. Her practiced tongue, rooted fiercely in her native dialect, lashed out with a rhythmic perfection that left the whole company roaring at the traveler’s expense. Novice pinked by a master of fence. Another broke a lance with her, and then another, but Marion stuck her great arms upon her haunches and held the whole room in play. She wielded that rude, ready humor which defies translation—a bludgeon of colloquial syntax that loses its marrow when stripped of its native inflection. Nature had gifted her this power, and the daily repetition of inn life had honed it into an art form. She returned every coarse stroke in kind, saying things without winking that challenged the polite, dominant lexicons of the day. 

Gerard sat gaping, absorbing this linguistic power dynamic. He whispered, “Denys, now I see why you Frenchmen say 'a woman's tongue is her sword.'” 

Just then she leveled another assailant, and the chivalrous Denys administered his consigne: “Courage, ma mie, le—” 

She turned her dialect upon him directly. “How can he be dead as long as there is an archer left alive?” 

The room erupted. “It is washing day, my masters,” she added with sudden, earthy gravity. 

“Apres? We travelers cannot strip and go bare while you wash our clothes,” objected a peevish old fellow by the fireside, interrupting the harmonious noise of the room with his reedy, complaining frequency. 

“I aimed not your way, ancient man,” Marion replied superciliously, scanning him slowly. “But since you ask me, I trow you might take a turn in the tub, clothes and all, and no harm done. But what I spoke for, I thought this young sire might like his beard starched.” 

Poor Gerard’s chin crop was thin and silky. The loudest of the laughers was the traitor Denys, whose beard was stiff and bristly. Gerard bore the Amazonian satire calmly, devoid of personal vanity. “Nay, 'chambriere,'” he smiled, honoring her title, “mine is all unworthy your pains; take you this fair growth in hand!” He pointed to Denys's bristles. 

“Oh, time for that, when I starch the besoms.” 

While the room shouted over this palpable hit, the mistress returned. In the time it took to cross the threshold, the Amazon transformed into a seeming Madonna, meek and mild. Mistresses are wonderful subjugators; their silent authority dictates the acoustic hierarchy of the space. Yet, the landlady had heard and did not disapprove. 

“Ah, Marion, lass,” she said good-humoredly, grounded in the culinary reality of her domain, “if you laid me an egg every time you cackle, 'L'es Trois Poissons' would never lack an omelet.” 

“Now, dame,” said Gerard, “what is to pay?” 

“What for?” 

“Our supper.” 

“Where is the hurry? Cannot you be content to pay when you go? Lose the guest, find the money, is the rule of 'The Three Fish.'” 

“But, dame, outside it is thus written—'Ici-on ne loge—'” 

“Bah! Let that flea stick on the wall! Look hither.” She pointed to the smoky ceiling, an interwoven timeline of ancestral commerce. Covered in hieroglyphics, these were scores scratched into the deposit of soot with a knife. It was a dying language of debt, intelligible only to the dame and her daughter, preserving the cultural memory of every destitute wanderer who had sought shelter beneath their roof. She explained it was to frighten moneyless folk, or to use against a non-paying face. “We can't refuse them plump, you know. The law forbids us.” 

“And how know you mine is not such a face?” 

“Out fie! It is the best face that has entered 'The Three Fish' this autumn.” 

“And mine, dame?” said Denys. “Dost see no knavery here?” 

She eyed him calmly. “Not such a good one as the lad's; nor ever will be. But it is the face of a true man. For all that, an I were ten years younger, I'd as lieve not meet that face on a dark night too far from home.” 

“Why, dame,” Denys laughed, “I would but sip the night dew off the flower; and you needn't take ten years off, nor ten days, to be worth risking a scratched face for.” 

“There, our mistress,” Marion chimed in, “said I not t'other day you could make a fool of them still, an if you were properly minded?” 

“I dare say ye did; it sounds like some daft wench's speech.” 

“Dame,” said Gerard, “this is wonderful.” 

“What? Oh! No, no, that is no wonder at all. Why, I have been here all my life; and reading faces is the first thing a girl picks up in an inn.” 

“And frying eggs the second,” Marion noted. “No, telling lies; frying eggs is the third, though.” 

“And holding her tongue the last, and modesty the day after never at all,” the mistress countered. 

“Alack! Talk of my tongue. I'm sped—'tis but a chambermaid gone. Catch what's left on't!” Marion staggered backward onto Gerard. 

“Tic! tic!” he cried peevishly. “There, don't be stupid! See you not I am talking to the mistress?” 

Marion rebounded with elasticity, turning a pirouette. “There, mistress, I give in; 'tis you that reigns supreme with the men, leastways with male children.” 

“Young man,” the mistress said, “this girl is not so stupid as her deportment; in reading of faces, and frying of omelets, there we are great. 'Twould be hard if we failed at these arts, since they are about all we do know.” Here was the profound truth of the agrarian elder: finding deep, existential meaning through the meticulous craft of sustenance. 

“You do not quite take me, dame,” Gerard said. “That honesty in a face should shine forth to your experienced eye seems reasonable: but how by looking on Denys here could you learn his one little foible, his insanity, his miserable mulierosity?” 

“His mule—his what?” She crossed herself with superstitious awe at the polysyllable. 

“Nay, 'tis but the word I was fain to invent for him.” 

“Invent? What, can a child like you make other words than grow in Burgundy by nature? Take heed what ye do! Why, we are overrun with them already, especially bad ones. Lord, these be times. I look to hear of a new thistle invented next.” In her protest lay the universal tragedy of linguistic erosion—a fierce defense of her native tongue against the subtle power dynamics embedded in dominant, fabricated lexicons that threatened to overwrite her organic reality. 

“Well then, dame, mulierose—that means wrapped up, body and soul, in women. So prithee tell me; how did you ever detect the noodle's mulierosity?” 

“Alas! Good youth, we that are women be notice-takers. Whiles I move to and fro doing this and that, my glance is still on my guests, and I did notice that this soldier's eyes were never off the womenfolk. Now you still turned to the speaker, her or him, and that is common sense.” 

Denys burst into a hoarse laugh. “You never were more out. Why, this silky, smooth-faced companion is more wrapped up in one single Dutch lass called Margaret, than I am in the whole bundle of ye.” 

“Man alive, that is just the contrary,” the hostess observed wisely. “Yourn is the bane, and hisn the cure. Cling you still to Margaret, my dear. I hope she is an honest girl.” 

“Dame, she is an angel.” 

“Ay, ay. As for you, soldier, your eyes were never made for the good of your soul.” 

“Nor of his pouch either,” Marion struck in. 

“Overmuch clack! Marion overmuch clack.” 

The landlord slipped in to remonstrate. “Is that the way to speak to our mistress?” 

“Hold your whisht,” his wife commanded. “It is not your business to check the girl; she is a good servant to you.” 

“What, is the cock never to crow, and the hens at it all day?” 

“You can crow as loud as you like, my man out o' doors. But the hen means to rule the roost.” 

“I know a byword to that tune,” Gerard offered. “Femme veut en toute saison, Estre dame en sa mason.” 

“I never heard it afore; but 'tis as sooth as gospel. Before all the world give me an old saw.” 

The arrival of the young mistress signaled the drawing round the fire. Like one interwoven family—travelers, hosts, and servants—they shared stories, partaking in the ancient oral tradition until the acoustic warmth of the room lulled Gerard into exhaustion. He told a tremendous tale from a manuscript of saints' acts, shivering deliciously before nodding off. The young mistress gave Marion a look. Lighting a rush, Marion touched Gerard's shoulder and led him away from the fading firelight into the quiet, wooden resonance of the upper hall. 

She showed him a room with two nice white beds. “Either is paradise,” he said. “I'll take this one. Do you know, I have not lain in a naked bed once since I left my home in Holland.” 

“Alack! Poor soul! Well, then, the sooner my flax and your down come together, the better; so—allons!” She held out her cheek, a business-like gesture echoing untranslatable regional customs of intimacy. 

“Allons? What does that mean?” 

“It means 'good-night.' What, don't they salute the chambermaid in your part?” 

“Not all in a moment. We make not so free with strange women.” 

“They must be strange women if they do not think you strange fools. All the old greasy greybeards that lie at our inn do kiss us chambermaids; faugh! And what have we poor wretches to set on t'other side the compt but now and then a nice young—? Alack! Time flies, so how is't to be?” 

“An't please you arrange with my comrade for both. He is mulierose; I am not.” 

“Nay, 'tis the curb he will want, not the spur. You shall to bed without paying the usual toll; and oh, but 'tis sweet to fall in with a young man who can gainsay brazen hussies. Shalt have thy reward. But excuse us: and keep us in our place, at arm's length; and so good-night!” 

At the door, her tone shifted entirely. “The Virgin guard thy head, and the holy Evangelists watch the bed where lies a poor young wanderer far from home! Amen!” Her footsteps tore down the stairs, rejoining the distant peal of laughter in the salle. 

“Now that is a character,” Gerard yawned, slipping into the dry bath of cold, clean linen. The tactile sensation of the sun-baked flax was inexpressibly refreshing. A delicious glow overtook him; and then—Sevenbergen, weaving his distant home seamlessly into his domestic rest. 

In the morning, the vibrant acoustic ecology of the awakening inn was briefly paralyzing; Gerard awoke infinitely refreshed but a close prisoner, his linen having vanished in the night. He bewailed his captivity to Denys, but the door sailed open. Marion entered with their linen, newly washed and ironed, smelling of river water and lye, setting it upon the table. 

“Oh you good girl,” cried Gerard. 

“Alack, have you found me out at last?” 

She explained their nightly rounds—a ritual of checking on weary men to save them the trouble of waking, ensuring the inn did not burn down. “We said, 'Here is one who will serve God best asleep, Break not his rest!'” 

“She is funny,” said Gerard. 

“I must be either that or knavish. Because 'The Three Fish' pay me to be funny. You will eat before you part? Good! Then I'll go see the meat be fit for such worshipful teeth.” The deep culinary care persisted even in farewell. 

They broke their fast on thick, crusty bread and cultured butter, settled their score, and embraced the custom of the country. The three principal women kissed them heartily, and they kissed the women. The landlord kissed them, and the cry rang out: “Come back, the sooner the better! 'Le sieur credit' is not dead for you.” 

They took the road again. At a little town, Denys bought shoes from a shopkeeper whose graceful salutes and meticulous craft made the exchange Elysium. “Not but what my German shoes have lasted well enough,” said Gerard the just. 

They walked along a pebbled path built to keep the burghers' feet dry. Those simple words painted 'home' in Gerard's heart. “Oh, how sweet!” 

But the tranquil soundscape of the rural road was suddenly shattered by a jarring, mechanical intrusion. “Mercy! What is this? A gibbet! And ugh, two skeletons thereon! Oh, Denys, what a sorry sight to woo by!” 

The wind whistled through the iron prongs of a huge wheel, vibrating with the discordant, industrial noise of violence, where bones and fragments of cloth were miserably dispersed. It was a brutal disruption of the psychological well-being fostered by the natural world. 

Gerard hid his face, devastated by this erasure of humanity. “Oh, to think those patches and bones are all that is left of a man!” 

“Excusez! A thing that went on two legs and stole,” Denys scoffed, his worldview callous to the acoustic and physical desecration. 

“How know ye he stole? Have true men never suffered death and torture too? Prithee, how died the saints?” 

“Hard. But not in Burgundy.” 

“Ye massacred them wholesale at Lyons. To you the gibbet proves the crime, because you read not story. Alas! Had you stood on Calvary that bloody day we sigh for to this hour, I tremble to think you had perhaps shouted for joy at the gibbet builded there; for the cross was but the Roman gallows, Father Martin says.” 

“The blaspheming old hound!” 

“Oh, fie! Fie! A holy and a book-learned man. Ay, Denys, y'had read them, that suffered there, by the bare light of the gibbet. Here be three malefactors. Three 'roues.' Yet of those little three one was the first Christian saint, and another was the Saviour of the world which gibbeted him.” 

Denys assured him on his honor they managed things better in Burgundy, turning red with ire at being outmaneuvered by book-craft. “Since your heart beats for vermin, feel for the carrion crows! The pangs of hunger would gnaw them dead, but for cold cut-purse hung up here and there.” 

Gerard maintained a dead silence, closing the subject. “There are things in which our hearts seem wide as the poles asunder, and eke our heads. But I love thee dearly all the same.” 

Towards afternoon, a faint wailing noise crept into the acoustic horizon, a distressing frequency of human misery that grew distincter as they proceeded. A score of pikemen, metallic and industrial in their clanking march, accompanied several constables driving a herd of animals. The creatures, over a hundred in number, were men, women, and children driven at the law’s point. 

“Good Heaven!” cried Gerard. “What a band of them! But stay, surely all those children cannot be thieves; why, there are some in arms. What on earth is this, Denys?” 

Denys advised him to ask the bourgeois with the badge, noting that in Burgundy, a civil question draws a civil reply. 

Gerard went up to the officer, removing his cap in an effort to bridge the chasm between their stations. “For our Lady's sake, sir, what do ye with these poor folk?” 

“Nay, what is that to you, my lad?” replied the functionary suspiciously. 

“Master, I'm a stranger, and athirst for knowledge.”
