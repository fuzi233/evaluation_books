How strange it seems, reflecting on our earliest days at home—when Harry and I were so notoriously undutiful to our tutor—that Mr. Esmond Warrington of Virginia should eventually rise to become "Bearleader" himself. My mother, when we reunited, could never bring herself to speak directly of this turbulent period of my life. Instead, she would allude vaguely to "that terrible time, my love, which I can't bear to think of," or "those dreadful years when there was difference between us." Even though my former pupil, a worthy and grateful man, shipped several barrels of the very liquor that built his great fortune to Jamestown for me, Madam Esmond referred to him only as "your friend in England" or "your wealthy Lambeth friend," never using his actual name. Furthermore, she never tasted a drop of his beer. We brewed our own at Warrington Manor, yet our excellent Mr. Foker faithfully shipped two butts of his finest ale to Ipswich annually. His son is a fashionable young sprig who married an earl's daughter, while the father remains a very worthy and kind gentleman. It was sheer luck in making his acquaintance that secured for me some of the most welcome guineas I ever possessed in my life.

Yet, it was not merely the sum of money that mattered to me, but the dignity and hope attached to the office of Governor—a post I assumed voluntarily. Mr. Foker's brewery, located near Pedlar's Acre in Lambeth (though the site has since changed), employed a surgeon who attended my wife during her confinement; this physician was a Bavarian originally named Voelker. I presume Mr. Lance, the surgeon, introduced the brewer to my name and history. The worthy doctor would smoke many a pipe of Virginia tobacco in my garden, developing a deep affection for my family. He brought his patron to my house. When Mr. Foker discovered I had a smattering of his language and could sing *"Prinz Eugen the noble Ritter"*—a song my grandfather brought home from the Marlborough wars—the German conceived a profound friendship for me. His lady placed her carriage and chair at Mrs. Warrington's disposal; his little daughter developed a prodigious fondness for our baby (and to do him justice, the Captain, who appears as ugly now as ever he wore a queue, was as beautiful as an infant): indeed, he was the very image of the Squire at thirty, according to everyone. Moreover, his son and heir, Master Foker, having been severely maltreated at Westminster School due to his father's trade as a brewer, begged if I would take charge of him. The parents agreed, paying me a generous sum for supervising his education.

Mr. Foker was a shrewd businessman, and since he and his family genuinely cared for me and mine, I laid all my financial affairs before him without reservation. Upon hearing my statement, he expressed delight, saying it augmented the respect and regard he already held for me. He laughed at our tales of aid from noble relatives—my aunt's coverlet, Lady Castlewood's mouldy jelly, Lady Warrington's contemptuous treatment—but he wept openly over the story of little Miles's moidore. Regarding Sampson and Hagan, he declared, "I vow, dey shall have so much beer als ever dey can drink." He sent his wife to call upon Lady Maria, treating her with the utmost respect and obsequiousness whenever she visited. It was with Mr. Foker that Lady Maria stayed when Hagan traveled to Dublin to complete his college terms; the good brewer's purse also supplied our friend's needs and outfitted him.

Once Mr. Foker fully understood my situation, he spoke of me with enthusiastic terms, as if my conduct displayed extraordinary virtue. I have mentioned how my mother saved money for Harry, leaving both of us in debt. Yet, when Harry spent money, he did so believing it to be his own. Madam Esmond could never comprehend that she was dealing harshly with me; the money was paid and gone, and that was the end of it. By the end of 1662, Harry sent over a considerable remittance for his promotion, reminding me that he was in my debt and urging me to draw on his agents if needed. He did not realize the magnitude of my need, nor how my small capital had been consumed.

To accept my brother's money would have delayed his promotion, so I naturally refrained from drawing on him, though I admit I was tempted. Similarly, knowing General Lambert's limited means, I hesitated to impoverish him by requesting supplies. These simple acts of forbearance, my worthy brewer considered, were instances of exalted virtue. Consequently, he wrote privately to my brother in America, lauding me and my wife as the most admirable humans, and called upon Madame de Bernstein—who never informed me of his visit, though I noticed she subsequently treated us with singular respect and gentleness, surprising me in someone I viewed as selfish and worldly. Later, I asked him how he gained access to the Baroness. He laughed: "De Baroness!" he said. "I knew de Baron when he was a waiter at Munich, and I was a brewer's apprentice." Perhaps our family had best avoid curiosity regarding our uncle the Baron.

Thus, the portion of my life that ought to have been most melancholy was rendered pleasant by numerous friends, happy circumstances, and strokes of fortunate chance. The bear I led was a docile little cub, dancing readily to my piping. It was far better to lead him than to loiter outside booksellers' shops or wait upon the whims of managers. During our exile, my wife and I spent many delightful evenings with these kind friends and benefactors. We were not devoid of intellectual enjoyment either; Mrs. Foker and Mrs. Warrington sang beautifully together, and sometimes, when in the mood, I read my own play *Pocahontas* to this friendly audience, in a manner Mr. Foker delighted to acknowledge was superior to Hagan's recitation.

Following the minor escapade of Miles Warrington, junior, I saw little of him and heard rarely of my paternal relatives. Sir Miles was assiduous at court (as I believe he would have been at Nero's), and I laughed one day when Mr. Foker told me he had heard on the Exchange that they intended to make my uncle a "Beer." "A Beer?" I asked in wonder. "Can't you understand the word when I say it?" replied the testy old gentleman. "Well, well, a Lord!" Sir Miles indeed was the obedient, humble servant of the Minister, whoever he might be. I was surprised he did not speak English with a Scottish accent during the First Duke's brief reign. I once saw him and his wife returning from court when Mrs. Claypool was presented to Her Majesty upon her marriage. I carried my little boy on my shoulder. My uncle and aunt stared resolutely at me from their gilded coach window; the footmen looked blank over their nosegays. Had I worn the Fairy's cap and been invisible, my father's brother could not have passed me with less notice.

We did not avail ourselves often of Lady Castlewood's peculiar invitation to drink tea and supper with her when no other company was present. Old Van den Bosch, however shrewd his intellect and great his skill in wealth-making, was not amusing in conversation, except to his daughter, who discussed household and city matters, bullion and bearer bonds, raising and selling livestock, with equal keenness and acumen. Neither was my Lord Castlewood frequently at home, nor greatly missed by his wife when absent, nor particularly at ease in the old father's company. The Countess confided all this to my wife in her simple manner. "Guess," she said, "my lord and father don't pull well together. Guess my lord is always wanting money, and father keeps the key of the box, which is quite right. If he could have his fingers on all our money, my lord would soon squander it, and then what becomes of our noble family? We pay everything, my dear (except gambling debts, which we won't tolerate). We pay cooks, horse dealers, wine merchants, tailors, and everyone—and lucky for them too—I reckon my lord wouldn't pay them! We always ensure he has a guinea in his pocket so he goes out like a real nobleman. What that man owes us, what he did before we arrived—gracious goodness only knows! Me and father do our best to make him respectable, but it's no easy job. Law! he'd melt the plate, only father keeps the key of the strong-room. And when we go to Castlewood, my father travels with me, armed, as do the others."

"Gracious heavens!" cried my wife, "does your ladyship mean to say you suspect your own husband of a desire to..."

"To what? Oh, no, nothing, of course! And I would trust our brother Will with untold money, wouldn't I? As much as I'd trust a cat with a cream-pot! Tell you, my dear, it's not all pleasure being a woman of rank and fashion. If I have bought a countess's coronet, I have paid a heavy price for it—that I have!"

Indeed, my Lord Castlewood paid a large price to free his estate from encumbrances, furnish his houses and stables, and discharge his debts. He became the slave of the little wife and her father.
