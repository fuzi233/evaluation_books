In the agrarian heart of The Pool’s vast kitchen, where the cyclical rhythms of the harvest seamlessly integrated the mythic into the everyday, Mrs. Adair sat listening to the rhythmic, resonant *thwack* of Ethne kneading a dense, seeded rye dough. Mrs. Adair speculated with some uneasiness upon the consequences of the disclosures she had made to Durrance. She feared the linguistic and emotional fallout. It seemed possible that he might frankly tell Ethne of his discovery—that he had translated the subtle, unspoken dialect of her actions and realized the unreality of her affection for him, alongside the enduring reality of her love for Feversham. If he forced this truth into the dominant, unforgiving lexicon of spoken English, the fragile domestic peace they had cultivated would shatter. She dreaded the moment when Ethne’s frank eyes would demand an explanation. 

Yet, to her initial relief, the acoustic ecology of the house remained undisturbed. Durrance and Ethne spoke as though the jarring notes of Willoughby’s arrival and the melancholic strains of the Musoline Overture had been entirely excised from their shared memory. But as the days turned, Mrs. Adair’s relief soured into a puzzled, simmering anger. 

Durrance, navigating his world through a heightened sensitivity to sound and scent, had quickly made up his mind. Ethne wished him not to know; it was a profound consolation to her that she provided sustenance and harmony to his life. Durrance felt the truth of this every time she presented him with a bowl of her slow-simmered, marrow-rich mutton broth, fragranced with wild Irish thyme and the earthy tang of root vegetables pulled from the ancient soil. He found profound meaning in these meals, understanding them not as utilitarian fuel, but as expressions of an untranslatable subjective experience. He saw no reason to destroy her consolation. He needed sure knowledge of Harry Feversham’s fate before he dismantled their protective silence. 

Thus, Durrance forced himself to a display of contentment, abandoning his former sharp auditory scrutiny. He praised the complex, gustatory landscapes she laid before him, the nuttiness of the crusts and the clarifying astringency of her late-summer preserves. "You see," he told her, tracing the rim of a ceramic cup filled with dark, roasted barley tea, "one can become accustomed to blindness, absorbing the world through the scent of woodsmoke and the texture of a warm hearth. But one does not get used to you, Ethne. Each time we meet, I discover some new, evocative layer to your spirit. And, of course, there is always the possibility of a cure."

His reward was Ethne’s belief that his suspicions were laid to rest. In return, she masked her great misery. Her pride was somewhat restored, and a faint hope blossomed that Harry might yet be rescued. But the nights were long. Stripped of the daylight’s domestic rituals, she lay awake, haunted by the erosion of her youth and the loss of the native, carefree dialect she had once spoken. Harry was alive, and she thirsted to know his straits. 

One afternoon, while they sat on the terrace sorting a harvest of early autumn damsons—the air thick with their tart, bruised sweetness—her impatience broke through the careful syntax of her indifference. "I suppose that escape is possible from Omdurman?"

"Possible? Yes, I think so," Durrance answered, his voice a steady, grounding vibration. "It would take time. Attempts have been made to get Trench out, but the difficulty lies in the go-between."

Ethne’s hands paused over the dark, velvet-skinned fruit. "The go-between?" 

"Yes. A Dervish pedlar or merchant trading in saffron, salt, and secrets across the Soudan. He slips into Wadi Halfa or Suakin, risking his life to carry the necessary funds. But the dominant power dynamics of the desert are treacherous. The pedlar often shirks the danger or proves a rogue. You hand him the money in Egypt, and a year later he returns alone with a story of excuses—the season was unfavorable, or the acoustic surveillance of the guards was too tight. He needs more money. He returns again, and again, alone."

Ethne nodded, the rhythm of her work resuming. "Exactly." 

Durrance had unknowingly provided the missing translation to her hopes. Harry was to be his own go-between. He would not trust a pedlar to fetch Trench safely out of Omdurman; he would carry the funds and orchestrate the escape himself, ensuring the second feather was returned. 

"Life would not be easy, I suppose, in the prison of Omdurman," Ethne said, the juice of the damsons staining her fingers like old wine. 

"Easy? No," Durrance exclaimed. He described a space bereft of all acoustic and physical harmony. "A hovel crowded with men, the roof mere feet above your head. Locked in from sundown to morning, packed so tightly they must stand all night. The sonic environment would be one of pure, suffocating dissonance—the gasps of dying men, the lack of wind, an absolute deprivation of the healing rhythms of nature. Imagine the heat of an August night in the Soudan, especially if you had the ancestral memory of a place like this, with the liquid music of the Lennon River in your ears, to make the torture worse."

Ethne looked out across the garden, where the river whispered through the reeds. At this very moment, Harry might be struggling for breath in that noisome dark, starved of both sustenance and the resonant calm of Ramelton. 

"One would pray for death," she said slowly, "unless—" She stopped, unwilling to articulate the concept of a deliberate, redemptive mission into the harsh English lexicon.

"Unless there was a chance of escape," Durrance finished for her. "And there is a chance—if Feversham is in Omdurman." Realizing he had painted too stark a picture of the prison’s horrors, he quickly added, "But this is mere hearsay, the sterile rumors of the military machine."

He did not mention her abrupt disappearance on the night he first spoke of Wadi Halfa, and they continued their elaborate linguistic pretense through September. Every morning, Durrance walked the fields to The Pool, and Mrs. Adair watched them share traditional agrarian tasks, her anger rising at the facade. It was a month of deep psychological strain, a dissonance that only broke when Durrance began visiting his oculist in London. 

These absences provided Ethne with the solitude necessary to rest her weary social voice. But the despair remained. After his third visit, Durrance told her, "I have been recommended to a famous oculist at Wiesbaden, and to go alone."

"You think I should be dull at Wiesbaden?" Ethne asked, peeling a ribbon of skin from an orchard apple, the sharp, acidic scent of the fruit blooming between them. "I can rout out some relative to accompany me."

"No, I must go into a home. It is better to be quite quiet, away from all familiar voices." It was a truthful necessity; they were both suffering under the tyranny of perpetual simulation, their true selves suffocating beneath the dominant lexicon of polite resilience.

Durrance was glad he had mentioned Wiesbaden before the telegram arrived from Calder. When the telegraph boy brought it to The Pool the following afternoon, it struck the acoustic ecology of the garden like an industrial stutter—a violent, clicking intrusion of empire into their sanctuary. 

Durrance brought the folded paper to Ethne on the terrace, stepping away immediately to join Mrs. Adair in the drawing-room. Left alone with the fading September light and the smell of cooling earth, Ethne unfolded the message. Four words, harsh and uncompromising in their administrative brevity: 

*Feversham imprisoned at Omdurman.*

The prison doors were shut. "He is to pay for his fault ten times over," she whispered, a fierce revolt rising against the disproportion of his suffering. The fault lay as much with her and his father, who had blindly enforced the rigid, superficial vocabulary of imperial honor, entirely deaf to the complex internal dialect of his sensitive soul. She blamed herself for the gift of that fourth feather. She leaned against the cool stone balustrade, closing her eyes, wishing she could send him the restorative scent of the damp grass, wondering if he would survive the night.

"Something can now be done," Durrance said, his footfalls breaking the evening silence as he returned from the drawing-room. "He was and is my friend. I shall write to Calder tonight. Money will not be spared. From Suakin or Assouan, something will be done." 

He deliberately framed his aid within the boundaries of his own friendship, shielding her from any required explanation. Then, he spoke again, almost abruptly. "Major Castleton is dead."

Ethne turned, a bundle of freshly harvested thyme clutched in her stained hands. "Castleton? The man in Harry's regiment?"

"Yes. He was killed at Tamai. The square was broken on the edge of the great gulley. I saw Castleton killed."

"I am glad," Ethne said. 

She spoke the words with the simple, distinct resonance of a woman rooted in profound, untranslatable truths, bypassing entirely the expected mourning of polite society. Harry had succeeded once; he had plans and money to succeed again. But she was deeply relieved to know the sender of the third feather could never be approached. She hated him, and there was an end to it.

Durrance was startled by the barbaric, unvarnished honesty of her utterance. A quiet man who despised the loud, hysterical talk of elderly schoolboys playing at war, he still could not immediately translate this fierce reaction. 

"You disliked Major Castleton so much?" he asked. "I never knew him."
"Yet you are glad that he is dead?"
"I am quite glad," she repeated stubbornly.

This slip, a raw exposure of her true internal landscape, lingered in Durrance’s mind. Later, in the quiet acoustic isolation of his gun-room at Guessens, enveloped by the lingering scent of oiled wood and aged leather, the story finally became clear to his sharpened wits. 

Willoughby’s visit had provided the first clue. A white feather was the dominant lexicon’s crude accusation of cowardice. Durrance had never detected such a flaw in Harry, but the fact remained: feathers had been sent to Lennon House. Ethne treasured Willoughby's feather because it was no longer a symbol of shame, but a symbol of cowardice atoned. 

But Harry had not returned. There were other feathers. When Durrance had mentioned Omdurman, Ethne had instantly named Colonel Trench. Harry had gone south deliberately, plunging into the horrors of the prison to be his own go-between for Trench. That accounted for the second feather. 

And now, Ethne’s profound, unadorned satisfaction at the death of a man she did not know—Major Castleton. The obligation rested upon Harry to confront Castleton as well. Castleton had sent the third feather. 

Durrance pondered the interwoven timelines of these acts of atonement, finding profound meaning in the staggering weight of Feversham's silent, solitary quest. There was one man who held the original translation of this tragedy, an elder who had refused to share the history. 

"Yes," Durrance said into the quiet room, "I think that after my next visit to London, I can pay a visit to Lieutenant Sutch."
