“Room to let, suitable for a single gentleman, no weird hidden surprises (mostly).” Sometimes when I’m feeling a little daft, I’ll loiter outside that same scuffed old terraced lodging house I knew as a lad and knock on the door, just for the bit. It takes forever to creak open, and there she is: the legendary urchin “slavey,” unchanged by the decades, not a day older, not an inch taller. Morning slavey has her scrawny red, dish-scored arms tucked up tight under a stained apron, afternoon slavey has a lopsided frilly cap perched on her touzled hair, round bewildered eyes like she just got caught swiping a jam tart off the counter. It takes every ounce of my willpower not to push past her, yell “Sorry, forgot my key!” and bound up the stairs two at a time — carpeted only up to the first landing, bare scuffed boards the rest of the way, worn thin by a hundred skittish lodgers. Instead, I feign politeness and say, “Oh, I say, do you have rooms to let?” and off she scuttles up the kitchen stairs, yelling over the banisters like she’s announcing a visiting monarch: “GENT TO SEE THE ROOMS!”

Up huffs the landlady, harassed, stout, decked out in stiff, genteel black, shoving past the tiny slavey like she’s a stray cat in the hallway, to size me up like I might turn out to be a bloke who eats kippers in bed and stains the curtains.
“I’ve got a very nice spot on the first floor,” she informs me, “and a perfectly respectable little room right at the back on the third.”

I agree to see both, explaining I’m hunting for a place for a young mate of mine. We have to squeeze past the hat-and-umbrella stand so tight we’re plastered to the wallpaper, like we’re sneaking past a sleeping giant. The first-floor room is fancy by lodging-house standards: there’s a marble-topped sideboard a whopping three feet by two, and the only way the doors stay shut is if you jam a crumpled bus ticket between them. The green tablecloth matches the green curtains, laid over the rickety loo table — the lamp is 100% safe, so long as you never, ever move it an inch from the exact dead centre, no exceptions. A crinkly paper fire ornament propped on the mantel gives the whole room an air of weird, unearned chastity, like no one ever had a bad thought within four walls of it. Above that, a fly-blown mirror, with enough gap between the peeling gilded frame and the glass to stuff party invites in — there’s still a couple stuck there, proof that even bed-sit dwellers get to have fun sometimes, if they’re very lucky. The wall opposite has one of those oleographs the Cheap Jacks auction off on Saturday nights down Pimlico Road, guaranteed “hand-made, mate, swear on me nan’s grave” — it’s always a Swiss landscape, got more oomph than any boring old English field scene, right? You get a busted mill, foaming torrent, craggy mountain, a buxom maiden and a cow, 100% guaranteed, no swapsies.

Then there’s the “easy chair” — and let’s be clear, I disavow all responsibility for that adjective. It’s stuffed full of coiled steel springs, every single one of them with a sharp business end just waiting to stab you in the thigh, covered in horsehair so shiny it might as well be polished leather. There is exactly one way to sit in it: perch on the absolute tippy edge, feet planted flat on the floor like you’re bracing for a hurricane. If you so much as lean back an inch, you slide right out onto the carpet, like the chair is politely but firmly saying “Oh, do pardon me, you’re far too heavy for this, you’d be way more comfortable on the floor, thanks so much.” The bed’s tucked behind the door, the washstand tucked behind the bed, so if you sit facing the window you can pretend neither of them exist. The second three mates turn up for tea? Suddenly everyone dives straight for that bed, no one will touch the easy chair at gunpoint. Makes total sense.

“And this one rents for?”
“Eight shillings a week, sir, attendance included, of course.”
“Any extras I ought to know about?”
“Lamp’s eighteenpence a week, sir. Kitchen fire, if the gent wants to dine at home, two shillings.”
“And coals?”
“Sixpence a scuttle, sir.”
“Bit of a tiny scuttle, that one, isn’t it?”
The landlady bridles like I just insulted her firstborn. “That’s the standard size, sir.” Turns out there’s a special, secret breed of coal scuttle manufactured exclusively for lodging-house keepers, no regular housewife has ever even seen one, let alone bought it.

I say fine, I may as well see the third-floor back too. The landlady flings the door open but stays planted firm on the landing, too big a lady to risk stepping inside and dwarfing the tiny apartment entirely. That room does not let you pretend the bed doesn’t exist: it’s the main character, the star of the show, it will not be ignored. Its only rival is the straw-coloured washstand, glowering defiantly from the corner like “Yeah I’m tiny, but I’m here, and you can’t pretend I’m not.” The rest of the furniture is a pair of chairs that don’t even pretend to be easy, they’re just hard as nails, a tiny chest of light-painted drawers with white china handles, topped with a dinky little looking glass, and a wobbly home-made four-legged table that takes up all remaining floor space except for the three square feet reserved for the poor sod who’s going to live there. The only decoration is a framed funeral card above the mantel, mounted in a border of beer corks — exactly like the ancient Egyptians shoving a mummy into their banquet hall to remind everyone that death is coming for you, so cheer up, you could be dead right now and you wouldn’t even have this room.

This one’s three and six a week, attendance included, lamp still eighteenpence, kitchen fire only a shilling.
“Hang on, why’s the first floor kitchen fire twice as expensive as this one?”
“Well, the gents on the first floor generally do more cooking, sir.”
Fair play, madam, I was forgetting. The third-floor back gent barely touches the stove anyway. His breakfast is plain, his lunch he eats wandering the quiet square, sneaking bites out of a paper bag when he thinks no one’s looking. Saveloys for twopence, meat pies for twopence, fruit pies for a penny, cold penny slices of roly-poly, pease pudding for cold days. For tea he’ll fry two eggs or a fourpenny haddock, or even a chop now and then — everything goes in that same single frying pan, all the flavours bleed into each other, so he can close his eyes, sniff, and pretend he’s working his way through a seven-course feast from herring to toasted cheese, no monotony at all. For dinner he’ll wander out to the chippy, where three halfpence gets you a fried plaice, a penny gets you a handful of chips, fourpence gets you a bowl of stewed eels with all the vinegar you can pour on, and sevenpence — total red letter night — gets you half a sheep’s head, a supper fit for any king who happens to be skint and ravenous.

I tell her I’ll run the offer past my young friend when he turns up, slip a whole shilling into the startled, lukewarm palm of the little slavey, and wander back out into the long dreary street, where some bloke is yelling “MUFFINS!” at the top of his lungs like he’s announcing the end of the world.

Some evenings I’ll drift back in memory to visit all those old lodger mates, no need to knock or climb the stairs. I can see you all clear as day through the thin walls, fashions a little changed but otherwise exactly the same. We wore bell-bottomed trousers at eight-and-six to measure, seven-and-six off the rack, fastened our neckties with dashing horseshoe pins, and our waistcoats were way gayer and bolder than anything you lot wear now. Our paper cuffs and collars were sixpence ha’penny a dozen, three ha’pence a pair: bright white and glistening on Sunday, a bit splotchy on Monday, absolutely dappled by Tuesday morning. But come Tuesday evening, out strolling down Oxford Street at the fashionable nine to ten p.m. promenade hour, we’d swing our canes and hold our chins up with the best of them, even if half our collar had peeled off. Your fancy washable rubber linen’s no doubt more practical, but our paper collars were dazzling for those first few hours, no contest.

For the rest of it, you lads haven’t changed a bit. I wave to you from the misty street. God rest you, gallant lonely boys, making the best of joyless days, scraping by genteel on twelve, fifteen, even eighteen shillings a week (absolute plutocrats, the lot of you), tucking a bit of that away to send home to your old mum in the country, who brags to all the neighbours about her “gentleman” son who’s got book learning and works “in the City.” May nothing you dismay. Bullied and badgered all day at the office, come evening you’re rorty young dogs: you brush the half-guinea top hat that “the Prince of Wales wears” (and that cost you three meals worth of cash) until it shines, yank your second best pair of trousers out from under the bed, and with a little gaslight trickery that crisp crease looks brand new. Hope your cheap necktie impresses everyone you pass! Hope your penny cigar gets mistaken for a Havana! Hope the barmaid teases you and calls you “Baby!” Hope the pretty shop-girl smiles at you and asks you to walk her home! Hope your terrible jokes make her snort-laugh, and all the other blokes glare at you jealous, and no harm comes of it!

You dreamers, you lot, who stay up half the night poring over Self-Help and *Psalm of Life* before tackling *French Without a Master* or Bookkeeping in Six Lessons, while all your other mates are out getting drunk or playing darts. You turn away from the bright streets and the pretty girls to trudge to the stuffy Birkbeck classes, the Polytechnic schools. Hope you get that partner’s office chair you’ve been daydreaming about! Hope you end up in a twelve-room house in Clapham one day!

And we had our moments, didn’t we? The Saturday night trip to the theatre, hanging outside the doors with all the other drama nerds chatting about Florence and Kate, our favourite actresses whose photos are taped to our mantelpieces, turning our dinky little room into a shrine. We’ve almost finished that poem we’re going to send her, we’ll mail it tomorrow, she’s bound to write back, we’ll kiss the envelope her hand touched. Then the great theatre doors groan open, and it’s every man for himself: charge, wriggle, squirm, get pushed around till your collar’s half off, you’re panting, and you’ve fought your way to the very front row of the pit. Would you trade that feeling for the rich bloke in the stalls who saunters in ten minutes late like he owns the place? Never. The once-a-week fancy French dinner in Soho, one-and-six all in including wine — no fancy Tortoni restaurant ever served anyone a meal that tasted half that good.

My very first lodging was an attic on the other side of Blackfriars Bridge, three shillings a week if you wanted cooking privileges, half a crown if you didn’t. I bought a methylated spirit stove with a kettle and a frying pan, and opted for the no-cooking deal, saved sixpence a week.

Old Hasluck would’ve slipped me cash in a heartbeat, and there were other folks I could’ve called on, but stupid boy’s pride wouldn’t let me. I was going to make it entirely on my own. I only told Hal, who I knew would understand exactly.
“If your mum was still here,” he said, “I’d have argued you out of this. But as it is? Don’t worry, I’m not gonna offer you handouts, and I’d turn you down flat if you begged. Stick your copy of Carlyle in your pocket. He’s not all wisdom, but he’s the best man-maker I know. The whole big lesson life’s got to teach you is not to be scared of it. Pop round and see me now and then, we’ll chat about stars, the future of Socialism, the Woman Question — anything at all, except your daft little twopenny-halfpenny work troubles.”
From anyone else that’d sound brutal, but I got it. We shook hands and parted, and neither of us guessed we wouldn’t see each other for years, not long after the Franco-German War broke out, and Hal, who was always mad for adventure, volunteered to go over, and off he went.

Back then, there was a tiny brass plate nailed to a house in Farringdon Street near the Circus, announcing the Ludgate News Rooms on the third and fourth floors, admission one penny. Every morning the place was packed with a seedy bunch of us: clerks, shopmen, skilled artisans, travellers, warehousemen, all of us out of work. Most were young, but there were a few older blokes mixed in, the quietest, saddest ones in that whole whispering army of down-at-heel blokes. We split into two groups: the newbies, cheery and cocky, who flitted from newspaper to newspaper picking job ads like they were choosing treats off a fancy menu, then wrote replies like they were doing the employer a massive favour. “Dear Sir, in reply to your ad in today’s Standard, I shall be pleased to accept the vacant post. I am of good address, speak fluent French, know German enough for commerce, bookkeeping and arithmetic are child’s play to me, I have a passion for hard work, and my moral character would do a Free Kirk elder proud. Salary required: two guineas a week.” You’d never guess any of us were skint.

The old stagers didn’t buzz around. They sat there hour after hour writing steadily, methodically, every day a little less hopeful. “Sir, your advertisement in today’s Daily Telegraph — I am X years old, my qualifications are modest, I will work as hard as I can to satisfy you, I won’t ask a high starting salary, you can contact me any time that’s convenient.”

I wrote dozens of the cocky new kid letters, hundreds of the humble old stager ones, taking painstaking care to pick out my nicest pen, mark a perfect one-inch margin down the left side of every sheet. Out of all of them, I got three or four curt replies telling me to turn up for an interview. But the crippling shyness that’d dogged me as a kid was back, worse than ever. Shy little kids people can sympathise with, but a shy young bloke? Everyone just assumes you’re a daft idiot. I came off awkward, stupid, sullen. The harder I tried to act normal, the ruder and more out of place I seemed. Every interview ended the exact same way: “Thanks, we’ve got a couple more blokes to see, we’ll write to you,” and I never heard back.

My few pounds of savings dwindled no matter how hard I guarded them. Looking back now, it’s easy to laugh at those struggles, because I know how it all turned out, happy ending and all. But back then? I had no clue if my life was going to be a comedy or a tragedy. Some days I was dead sure it was going to end badly. I felt tiny, helpless, like the whole huge world was going to trample right over me, never even noticing I was there.

One morning at the news rooms, there was a letter waiting for me. “Will Mr P. Kelver call at the above address tomorrow morning between 10:30 and 11.” It was headed Lott & Co., Indian Commission Agents, Aldersgate Street. I didn’t have much hope, but I trundled back to my lodgings, changed into my only good suit, polished my silk hat, pulled on my one pair of gloves, tucked my holey umbrella into its silk case, and trundled off to fight fate. I turned up too early, so I paced the pavement for fifteen minutes staring up at their second-floor windows, trying and failing to remember what their ad had said, or what I’d written back. The place looked run down, not like a thriving firm. For half a second I daydreamed about a kindly Cheeryble brother offering me thirty shillings a week to start, then snapped back to reality wondering if it was even worth climbing the stairs just to go through the whole humiliating interview dance again.

A fine drizzle started falling. I didn’t want to get my hat wet, but I didn’t want to open my umbrella either. It was five minutes to 10:30, so I trundled across the road and climbed the bare stairs to the second floor. Two doors faced me, one marked Private. I tapped lightly at the other, no answer, tapped louder, no answer, banged a third time. A shrill plaintive voice called back:
“Oh, do come in, for heaven’s sake.”

I turned the handle and stepped inside. It was a tiny, dim little room, window so dirty the bottom half was covered in pasted tissue paper to make it opaque. It didn’t look like an office at all, it looked like the stockroom of a random village general store. Pots of jam, jars of pickles, bottles of wine, biscuit tins, rolls of drapery, boxes of candles, bars of soap, pairs of boots, packets of stationery, cigar boxes, tinned food, guns, cartridges — every single available surface was littered with supplies enough to stock a desert island. At a tiny desk under the window sat a youth with a ridiculously tiny body and a ridiculously massive head, so disproportionate I half expected him to reach up and pop his whole head off and set it on the desk for a rest.
