The coroner, acutely aware that for this singular day he held the attention of the nation, carried himself with the gravity of a man presiding over a momentous affair. He was a broad-chested man with a florid countenance, his natural joviality tempered by the solemnity of the occasion—though the sensational nature of Manderson’s death had undeniably invigorated him. His talent for arranging facts with rhetorical flourish was well-known, and juries often found themselves swayed as much by his eloquence as by the evidence itself.

The inquest was held in a makeshift courtroom—a long, unfurnished hall annexed to the hotel, its bare walls echoing with murmurs from the assembled crowd. Reporters crowded the front rows, pencils poised, while witnesses sat in stiff-backed chairs to one side. The jury, a collection of solemn-faced men, fidgeted under the weight of their duty. The public filled the remaining space, their hushed anticipation thickening the air.

The first witness called was Mrs. Manderson.

She approached the stand with measured composure, lifting her veil only when she began to speak. Her pallor was striking, her beauty undimmed by grief, yet it was the quiet strength in her bearing that commanded attention. There was no melodrama in her demeanor—only the unmistakable effort of a woman holding herself together by sheer will.

The coroner guided her gently through her testimony. She recounted the last night of her husband’s life—how he had returned to his bedroom late, how she had drowsily exchanged a few words with him before drifting back to sleep. She had not heard him leave, nor had she suspected anything amiss until the terrible news came the following morning.

“Did he say why he had returned?” the coroner pressed.

“Yes,” she replied, her voice steady. “He mentioned sending Mr. Marlowe to Southampton on business. He said he had changed his mind about driving himself.”

There was a pause. The coroner’s next question carried a heavier weight.

“Mrs. Manderson,” he began carefully, “I must ask—though I do so with regret—whether your relations with your late husband had been… harmonious in recent months.”

A ripple passed through the courtroom. Her chin lifted slightly, defiance flickering beneath her sorrow.

“If you insist upon an answer,” she said, her tone cool and precise, “then I will give one that leaves no room for misinterpretation. In the last months of his life, my husband became distant. Withdrawn. He spoke little to me of his affairs, and I did not press him. I tried, in my own way, to bridge whatever had come between us—but I never learned the cause.”

Her composure wavered for the briefest moment before she regained control. The coroner, satisfied, dismissed her.

As she stepped down, the room seemed to exhale. The next witnesses—servants, policemen—offered little new insight. Then came Mr. Bunner, Manderson’s American secretary, whose testimony sent a jolt through the proceedings.

He spoke of Manderson’s growing paranoia, of whispered threats from unknown enemies, of the shadow that had seemed to dog his employer’s final days. The reporters scribbled furiously. Here, at last, was the spark of drama they had been waiting for.

The coroner, in his closing remarks, was careful not to lean too heavily on any single theory. The absence of a weapon, the nature of the wound, the peculiar circumstances—all pointed to a puzzle yet unsolved. But he allowed that the possibility of foul play could not be dismissed.

The jury retired to deliberate. Outside, the world waited.

And in the shadows, unseen by most, Trent watched it all—his thoughts a storm of suspicion, his heart an unwilling traitor to his reason.
