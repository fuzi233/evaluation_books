Since that night in Grosvenor Place—the night which had been fatal to Mr. Harry Foker’s peace and delicious to his vanity—his heart behaved with a most indecorous want of philosophy. You would have sworn, to look at him formerly, that he was one of those young gentlemen who have swallowed the world whole at nineteen, and digest it afterwards with a perpetual yawn: a person who had counselled Pendennis on love as he might have counselled him on boots; who, being blessed with money and leisure, ought by every rule of the fashionable sciences to have become daily more cynical, more satisfied with himself, and more indifferent to all created beings except Harry Foker.

And yet—this is the shameful part—he fell into the common accident, and grew uneasy about a woman; like Achilles in a helmet, Ajax in a sulk, Lord Nelson in a uniform, or Adam in a fig-leaf. Love, that ancient conqueror who has beaten better men with worse weapons, had made a prisoner of him; and the prisoner did not even clank his chains with spirit.

When he returned that night to the Back Kitchen after parting with Arthur Pendennis at the staircase-door in Lamb Court, the gin-twist was no longer a cordial but a reproach; the devilled turkey, once a thing to be toasted and championed, smelt suddenly like yesterday’s bravado. The room was in its usual uproar—pipes ticking, glasses clicking, wit sparking like poor gas in a draughty lantern—yet all the noises of it seemed muffled to him, as if somebody had put cotton in his ears and Blanche Amory in his head. Mr. Hodgen, that amiable minstrel of horrors, sang a new chant—more dreadful, more funny, more fit to make respectable hair sit upright than even “The Body Snatcher.” Harry, being a chief of the place, did his duty, smiled the proper smile, and said “Bravo, Hodgen” with the benevolence of a man awarding a prize at a cattle-show; but he never distinctly heard a syllable of “The Cat in the Cupboard,” which under that title has since gone prowling through London, alarming timid people in drawing-rooms.

Late, and worn as if he had been up roasting himself instead of turkey, he slipped to his rooms, sought his downy pillow, and found that down, which had hitherto been a faithful friend, turned traitor. The fever in him would not let him sleep; and the instant he woke—and he woke a dozen times—Miss Amory’s face sat upright at the foot of his bed like a bright ghost, saying, plainly as if she had spoken through the curtain rings, Here I am. I am your princess. You have discovered me. You shall care for nothing else.

At breakfast-time—if a gentleman may call it breakfast who eats because it is ten o’clock, and not because he is hungry—his former delights looked as stale as cold cutlets. Up to this point he had not been much accustomed to the female society of his own rank. When he spoke of those ladies he called them “modest women,” as if modesty were a kind of starch by which they were stiffened. No doubt the virtue existed; but it had not, in his opinion, made up for a want of other seasoning—pepper, lemon, a dash of daring—that he found more liberally among Mesdemoiselles of the theatre. His mother, Lady Agnes, was tender enough to baste a turkey with her own tears for him, but she did not amuse him. His cousins, the daughters of his maternal uncle, the respectable Earl of Rosherville, wearied him beyond measure. One was blue, and would talk of strata while other people were trying to swallow soup; one rode like a dragoon and smoked cigars as if the world were a barrack; one was Low Church and had views so heterodox that her own sisters shuddered; while the Highest Church sister made a little chapel of her cupboard, and fasted on Fridays with a firmness that would have made a butcher weep. Their paternal house of Drummington Harry could scarcely be got to visit. He swore he would rather go upon the treadmill than stay there; and, in truth, the inhabitants were little fonder of him than he was of them.

Lord Erith, the heir, found Harry deplorably vulgar. Harry found Erith a philanthropic nightcap; the dreariest spouter that ever tried to warm the House of Commons with cold water. Nor could the old earl, George Robert, ever forget a certain evening when he condescended to play billiards with his nephew, and the nephew—upon witnessing a stroke which did not please him—poked his lordship in the side with his cue and remarked, with friendly candour, “Well, old cock, I’ve seen many a bad stroke in my life, but I never saw such a bad one as that there.” His lordship played the game out with angelic sweetness, because Harry was a guest and also a connection; but he was nearly having a fit in the night, and kept to his own rooms afterwards until the young barbarian departed for Oxbridge. The blow was so severe that the incident was never mentioned in family conversation; the earl shunned Harry in London and in the country; and could scarcely be brought to gasp out “How d’ye do?” when compelled to meet him. But he would not break his sister Agnes’s heart by banishing her darling; nor, indeed, could he afford to quarrel with Mr. Foker, senior, between whom and his lordship there had been private transactions—cheques travelling one way, autographs travelling the other—with the letters I O U written, as it were, across the dignity of the signature.

Besides those four daughters with their various peculiarities, his lordship had a fifth girl, Lady Ann Milton; and Lady Ann, from pinafore days, had been destined like a joint in a larder—labelled, reserved, and never to be touched by any other hand. It was ordained between her parents and her aunt that when Mr. Harry Foker came to a proper age, Lady Ann should become his wife. The idea had been put into her mind as naturally as the names of months and the use of a thimble. Harry, the dirtiest of little boys, had come back with black eyes from school to Drummington or Logwood, where Ann lived much with her aunt; and the two young creatures accepted the arrangement with the placidity of children accepting porridge.

It no more entered Lady Ann’s mind to question her father’s order than it would have entered Esther’s to dispute Ahasuerus. The heir-apparent of the house of Foker was equally obedient; for when his father said, in that curt brewer’s fashion which sounds like a mallet on a barrel, “Harry, your uncle and I have agreed that when you’re of a proper age, you’ll marry Lady Ann. She won’t have any money, but she’s good blood, and a good one to look at, and I shall make you comfortable. If you refuse, you’ll have your mother’s jointure, and two hundred a year during my life”—Harry, who knew his sire was a man of few words and strict performance, acquiesced at once, and said, “Well, sir, if Ann’s agreeable, I say ditto. She’s not a bad-looking girl.”

“And she has the best blood in England, sir. Your mother’s blood, your own blood, sir,” said the Brewer with fervour, as though blood were an ale of which the family had the patent. “There’s nothing like it, sir.”

“Well, sir, as you like it,” Harry replied. “When you want me, please ring the bell. Only there’s no hurry, and I hope you’ll give us a long day. I should like to have my fling out before I marry.”

“Fling away, Harry,” answered the benevolent father. “Nobody prevents you, do they?” And so very little more was said. Harry went on with the pursuits that suited him; and in his sitting-room he hung a little picture of his cousin—a simple round face with ringlets—among the French prints, the favourite actresses and dancers, the racing and coaching pieces, the whole gallery of his heart. The little portrait made but a poor show beside Mademoiselle Petitot dancing over a rainbow, or Mademoiselle Redowa grinning in red boots and a lancer’s cap; yet it was there, like a plain biscuit on a plate of sugared confectionery, because the elders had ordered it.

Being engaged and disposed of, Lady Ann Milton did not go so much into the world as her sisters. Often she stayed at home in Gaunt Square when mamma and the other ladies went abroad. They danced with one man after another; the men came and went; the stories about them were various, like dishes at a ball-supper, tasted and discussed and forgotten. But there was only one story about Ann: she was engaged to Harry Foker; she never was to think about anybody else. It was not an amusing story; it had no savour; it was bread-and-water betrothed to champagne.

Now consider the change. The morning after Lady Clavering’s dinner, Harry woke with Blanche’s image looking at him with those clear grey eyes and that smile that seemed to taste of something bright—like a sip of cold wine when you have been drinking hot punch. Her tune, too, rang in his ears: “Yet round about the spot, ofttimes I hover, ofttimes I hover,” and poor Foker began piteously to hum it as he sat up in bed under his crimson silk coverlet.

The room was decorated, as a young gentleman’s room should be if he wishes to prove himself a connoisseur of the lighter arts. Opposite the bed a French print exhibited a Turkish lady and her Greek lover surprised by a venerable Ottoman husband—who looked as if he had been interrupted in his coffee. On the other wall a gentleman and lady rode at full gallop and kissed each other with the reckless gallantry of people who do not care what becomes of the horse. All around were more French prints—gauzy nymphs of the Opera, illustrations of novels, and here and there an English chef-d’oeuvre in which Miss Calverley of T. R. E. O. appeared in tight pantaloons as a page, or Miss Rougemont as Venus, the value of each masterpiece enhanced by a signature in exquisite facsimile: Maria Calverley, Frederica Rougemont, and so forth. Honest Harry delighted in these treasures. He was no worse than many of his neighbours: an idle, jovial, kindly fast man about town; and if his walls were rather too knowing, and his air rather too fragrant with Latakia, why, he was richer than most young men and could afford to gratify his taste. Simple Lady Agnes, when she entered his apartments, was often bewildered by the novelties—like a countrywoman in a spice-shop, sneezing and admiring and not quite understanding.

On the bed-table lay a letter from Miss Calverley, written in a very dégagé style both of spelling and handwriting, scrawling freely over filigree paper, beginning by calling him her dear Hokey-pokey-fokey; and reminding him of the dinner at the Elephant and Castle at Richmond which he had promised to stand. There was a card for a private box at Miss Rougemont’s approaching benefit. There was a bundle of tickets for “Ben Budgeon’s Night, the North Lancashire Pippin, at Martin Faunce’s, the Three-cornered Hat, in St. Martin’s Lane,” where Conkey Sam, Dick the Nailor, and Deadman (the Worcestershire Nobber) were to put on the gloves, and the lovers of the good old British sport were invited to attend. There were keys, sovereigns, cigar-cases—and a little sprig of verbena which Miss Amory had given him, dried already into a kind of aromatic accusation.

Yesterday, all these things had promised pleasure as plainly as a cook-book. This morning they were insipid. What cared he for Conkey Sam or the Worcestershire Nobber? The French prints ogled him in vain. Calverley’s bad spelling and her “Hokey-fokey”—confound her impudence! The notion of dining at Richmond with that old woman (for she was seven-and-thirty if she was a day) filled him with such disgust as a man might feel who has ordered a delicate supper and finds, at the last moment, he must dine upon cold mutton and civility.

When his fond mother saw him, she remarked on the pallor of his cheek and the general gloom of his aspect. “Why do you go on playing billiards at that wicked Spratt’s?” Lady Agnes asked. “My dearest child, those billiards will kill you, I’m sure they will.”

“It isn’t the billiards,” Harry said, gloomily.

“Then it’s the dreadful Back Kitchen,” said Lady Agnes. “I’ve often thought, d’you know, Harry, of writing to the landlady, and begging that she would have the kindness to put only very little wine in the negus which you take, and see that you have your shawl on before you get into your brougham.”

“Do, ma’am. Mrs. Cutts is a most kind motley woman,” Harry said. “But it isn’t the Back Kitchen, neither,” he added, with a sigh as deep as a soup-tureen.

Lady Agnes never denied her son anything, and fell into all his ways with the fondest acquiescence. In return she received his entire confidence. He never thought it necessary to disguise from her his haunts; on the contrary, he brought her home choice anecdotes from clubs and billiard-rooms, which the simple lady relished even if she did not understand. “My son goes to Spratt’s,” she would say to her confidential friends with maternal pride. “All the young men go to Spratt’s after their balls. It is de rigueur, my dear; and they play billiards as they used to play macao and hazard in Mr. Fox’s time. My dear father often told me they sate up always till nine o’clock next morning with Mr. Fox at Brookes’s—whom I remember at Drummington, when I was a little girl, in a buff waistcoat and black satin small-clothes. My brother Erith never played as a young man nor sate up late—he had no health for it; but my boy must do as everybody does, you know. And then he often goes to a place called the Back Kitchen, frequented by all the wits and authors whom one does not see in society, but whom it is a great privilege and pleasure for Harry to meet; and there he hears the questions of the day discussed; and my dear father always said it was our duty to encourage literature—and he had hoped to see the late Dr. Johnson at Drummington, only Dr. Johnson died. Yes, and Mr. Sheridan came over, and drank a great deal of wine—everybody drank a great deal of wine in those days—and papa’s wine-merchant’s bill was ten times as much as Erith’s is, who gets it as he wants it from Fortnum and Mason’s and doesn’t keep any stock at all.”

Harry, who wished this morning to manoeuvre his mother’s good-nature as cleverly as a cook skims a sauce to make it look clear, began artfully on yesterday’s dinner. “That was an uncommon good dinner we had yesterday, ma’am. Their clear soup’s better than ours. Moufflet will put too much tarragon into everything. The suprême de volaille was very good—uncommon—and the sweets were better than Moufflet’s sweets. Did you taste the plombière, ma’am, and the maraschino jelly? Stunningly good that maraschino jelly!”

Lady Agnes expressed agreement, as she did with most sentiments of her son. He went on. “Very handsome house, that of the Claverings. Furniture got up regardless of expense. Magnificent display of plate, ma’am.” She assented again.

“Very nice people the Claverings.”

“H’m!” said Lady Agnes, whose “h’m” could cover a multitude of moral reservations.

“I know what you mean. Lady C. ain’t distangy exactly, but she is very good-natured.”

“Oh, very,” mamma said—she being herself one of the best-natured women alive.

“And Sir Francis—he don’t talk much before ladies; but after dinner he comes out uncommon strong, ma’am—a highly agreeable, well-informed man. When will you ask them to dinner? Look out for an early day, ma’am.” And, peeping into Lady Agnes’s pocket-book as if it were a pantry list, he chose a day only a fortnight hence—an age that fortnight seemed to him—when the Claverings were to be invited to Grosvenor Street.

The obedient Lady Agnes wrote the required invitation without consulting her husband, who had his own society and habits, and left his wife to see her own friends alone. Harry looked at the card when it was done—and found fault with it in a way that would have done credit to a man detecting the absence of a favourite spice.

“You have not asked Miss—whatdyecallem—Miss Emery—Lady Clavering’s daughter.”

“Oh, that little creature!” Lady Agnes cried. “No! I think not, Harry.”

“We must ask Miss Amory,” Foker said, with an air of desperate reasonableness. “I—I want to ask Pendennis; and—and he’s very sweet upon her. Don’t you think she sings very well, ma’am?”

“I thought her rather forward, and didn’t listen to her singing. She only sang at you and Mr. Pendennis, it seemed to me. But I will ask her if you wish, Harry.” And so Miss Amory’s name was written on the card with her mother’s.

This diplomacy achieved, Harry embraced his fond parent with the utmost affection—as if she had just saved him from starvation—and retired to his own apartments, where he flung himself on his ottoman and lay brooding, sighing for the day that would bring Miss Amory under his paternal roof, and devising a hundred wild schemes for meeting her which would, doubtless, have been admirable had he not been Harry Foker.

After his grand tour, Mr. Foker, junior, had imported a polyglot valet, who took the place of Stoopid and condescended to wait at dinner attired in worked muslin shirt-fronts, gold studs, and chains enough to moor a boat. This man—of no particular country, speaking all languages indifferently ill—made himself useful in a variety of ways: read his master’s correspondence, knew his haunts and the addresses of his acquaintance, and officiated at the private dinners the young gentleman gave. As Harry lay on his sofa, robed in a wonderful dressing-gown and puffing in gloomy silence, Anatole must have perceived that something was wrong; but he did not commit the vulgarity of sympathy.

When Harry began to dress for going out, he was intolerable. Pantaloons of many different stripes, checks, and colours were tried and cursed; all the boots were villainously varnished; the shirts too “loud.” He scented his linen and person with peculiar richness this day—as if he meant to enter a room by smell before he entered it by sight; and then, after some blushing and hesitation, he said, “I say, Anatole—when I engaged you—didn’t you—hem—didn’t you say you could—hem—dress hair?”

The valet replied that yes, he could.

“Cherchy alors une paire de tongs,—et—curly moi un peu,” Mr. Foker said with an air of elegant indifference; and Anatole, wondering whether his master was in love or going masquerading, went in search of the tongs—first from the old butler who waited on Mr. Foker senior, on whose bald pate no tongs could have found enough hairs to seize; and finally from the lady who had charge of Lady Agnes’s meek auburn fronts. The tongs being procured, Monsieur Anatole twisted his young master’s locks until Harry’s head was as curly as a negro’s; after which the youth dressed himself with the utmost care and splendour and prepared to sally out.

“At what dime sall I order de drag, sir, to be to Miss Calverley’s door, sir?” the attendant whispered as his master was going forth.

“Confound her!—Put the dinner off—I can’t go!” cried Foker. Then, remembering that Poyntz and Rougemont and ever so many more were coming, and that a gentleman may be a victim without being a defaulter, he added, “No—hang it—I must go. The drag at Pelham Corner at six o’clock, Anatole.”

The drag was not one of Mr. Foker’s own equipages, but hired for festive purposes; yet he put his own carriage into requisition that morning—and for what? To drive down to Lamb Court, Temple, taking Grosvenor Place by the way, which lies in the exact direction of the Temple from Grosvenor Street, as everybody knows; where he had the pleasure of peeping up at Miss Amory’s pink window-curtains; after which feat—having looked at a set of curtains as solemnly as if they were an altar-cloth—he drove off to Pendennis’s chambers.

Why did he need to see Pen so urgently? They had parted in perfect health the night before. Pen had lived two years in London, and Foker had not paid half a dozen visits to his rooms. What sent him now in such haste?

If any young ladies read this page, they may accept the explanation without blushing: when this mishap befalls you—which now, for more than twelve hours, had befallen Harry Foker—people grow interesting to you for whom you did not care sixpence the day before; and persons of whom you fancied yourself fond become suddenly insipid and disagreeable. Eliza or Maria, to whom you wrote letters and sent locks of hair yards long, becomes as indifferent as your stupidest relation; whilst, about his relations, you begin to feel an astonishing tenderness. You will long to ingratiate yourself with his mamma; you will discover a liking for that dear kind old man his father; if he visits any house, what advances you will make to visit there too. If he has a married sister you will spend long mornings with her. You will fatigue your servant by sending notes twice or thrice a day for the most pressing occasions imaginable. You will cry if your mamma objects to your going too often to see his family. The only one of them you will dislike is perhaps his younger brother, who will persist in staying in the room when you come to see the dear new-found friend—his darling second sister.

Something like this happens; let us hope it may. You must go through the hot fits and cold fits of the pretty fever. Your mothers—if they would confess it—passed through it before you were born, your dear papa being the object of the passion, of course; who could it be but he? And as you suffer it, so will your brothers, after their kind—more selfish than you, more headstrong. They rush at their destiny when the doomed charmer appears. Or if they do not, heaven help them; for to love and win is the best thing, and to love and lose is the next best. You do not die of the complaint: very few do. The generous wounded heart suffers and survives it; and he is not a man, nor she a woman, who is not conquered by it—or who does not conquer it—in good time.

So if you ask why Henry Foker, Esquire, was in such a hurry to see Arthur Pendennis, the difficulty is none. Pen had become valuable in his eyes—not because Pen was the rose, but because he had been near the fragrant flower. Did not Pendennis go to her house in London? Did he not live near her in the country? Did he not know all about the enchantress—her very voice, the way her song hung in the air like perfume, and then was gone? What, one wonders, would Lady Ann Milton—Mr. Foker’s cousin and prétendue—have said if her ladyship had known what was going on in the bosom of that funny little gentleman?
