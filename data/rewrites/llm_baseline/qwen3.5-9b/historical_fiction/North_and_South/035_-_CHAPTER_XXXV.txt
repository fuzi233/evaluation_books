"There's nought so finely spun But it cometh to the sun."

Mr. Thornton sat on and on. He sensed that his presence brought a quiet comfort to Mr. Hale, and he was moved by the silent, earnest plea within his friend's gaze that he stay a little longer—the soft, lingering "Don't go yet" that escaped his weary lips from time to time. He wondered where Margaret had gone, yet he lingered not with the hope of seeing her. In the hour of profound sorrow, and in the company of a soul so acutely aware of earthly transience, Mr. Thornton found himself unusually reasonable and self-possessed. He listened with deep attention to everything his friend said.

"Of death, and of the heavy lull, And of the brain that has grown dull."

It was remarkable how Mr. Thornton's mere presence unlocked secrets in Mr. Hale that remained tightly guarded even from Margaret. Perhaps Mr. Hale feared that Margaret's sympathy, so keen and vividly expressed, would overwhelm him; perhaps, to his speculative mind, doubts arose at such a critical juncture, clamoring to be resolved into certainty, and knowing she would recoil from hearing such uncertainties—or from believing him capable of conceiving them. Whatever the reason, he could unload his frozen thoughts, fears, and fancies onto Mr. Thornton more easily than onto anyone else. Mr. Thornton spoke little, yet every sentence he offered strengthened Mr. Hale's trust and affection. Did Mr. Hale pause while expressing a remembered agony? Mr. Thornton's two or three words would complete the thought, revealing how deeply he understood its weight. Was it a doubt, a fear, a wandering uncertainty seeking rest but finding none, its eyes blinded by tears? Instead of shock, Mr. Thornton seemed to have traversed that very mental landscape himself, able to point toward the precise ray of light that would illuminate the darkest corners. Though a man of action, busy in the world's great battles, there existed within him a deeper, quieter religion binding him to God, persisting through his wilfulness and mistakes, a depth Mr. Hale had never imagined. They never discussed such matters again, but this single conversation transformed them into unique companions, knitting them together in a bond that casual religious discourse could never forge. When all doors are open, where remains the Holy of Holies?

All the while, Margaret lay as still and pale as death upon the study floor. She had succumbed to her burden. It had been heavy in weight and long carried; she had been meek and patient until, suddenly, her faith faltered, and she groped in vain for aid. A pitiful contraction of suffering marred her beautiful brow, though no other sign of consciousness remained. The mouth, which had recently projected defiantly, was now relaxed and livid.

"E par che de la sua labbia si mova Uno spirto soave e pien d'amore, Chi va dicendo a l'anima: sospira!"

The first sign of returning life was a tremor around the lips—a mute, soundless attempt at speech—but her eyes remained closed, and the trembling sank back into stillness. Then, leaning feebly on her arms to steady herself, Margaret gathered her strength and rose. Her comb had fallen from her hair. With an instinctive desire to erase the marks of weakness and restore order, she searched for it, occasionally sitting down to regain her strength during the quest. Her head drooped forward; her hands lay meekly one upon the other. She tried to summon the memory of the temptation that had thrown her into such terror, recalling the specific details, but she could not. Only two facts remained clear: Frederick had been in danger of pursuit and detection in London, accused not merely of manslaughter but of leading a mutiny; and she had lied to save him. Yet there was solace: her lie had bought him time. If the inspector returned tomorrow, after she received the letter she longed for confirming her brother's safety, she would endure the shame, standing before a crowded courtroom as "a dog" who had done this thing. But if he returned sooner—as he had half-threatened, within hours—why! She would tell the lie again. Though she could not foresee how those words would emerge after such terrible introspection without betraying her falsehood, she knew repeating it would buy time for Frederick.

Her reverie broke with Dixon's entry into the room; she had just dismissed Mr. Thornton.

Hardly ten steps into the street had Mr. Thornton taken when an omnibus halted beside him. A man alighted and approached, tipping his hat. It was the police inspector.

Mr. Thornton had secured the inspector's first position in the force and had heard updates on his protégé's career, yet they rarely met. Initially, Mr. Thornton did not recognize him.

"My name is Watson—George Watson, sir, the one you employed—"

"Ah, yes! I've heard you're doing wonderfully."

"Yes, sir. I owe you thanks. But I ventured to speak to you regarding a small matter of business. I believe you were the magistrate who recorded the deposition of the poor man who died in the Infirmary last night."

"Yes," replied Mr. Thornton. "I heard a rambling account the clerk deemed useless. I'm afraid he was drunk, though undoubtedly he died violently. One of my mother's servants was betrothed to him, I believe, and she is distraught today. What of him?"

"Why, sir, his death is strangely connected with someone in the house I saw you leaving moments ago. I believe it was Mr. Hales's residence."

"Yes!" Mr. Thornton turned sharply, fixing the inspector with sudden interest. "What connection?"

"Well, sir, it appears I possess a fairly distinct chain of evidence implicating a gentleman who was walking with Miss Hale that night at Outwood Station as the man who struck or pushed Leonard off the platform, causing his death. However, the young lady denies being there at that time."

"Miss Hale denies she was there!" Mr. Thornton repeated, his voice altered. "Tell me, what evening was it? What time?"

"About six o'clock, Thursday the twenty-sixth."

They walked in silence for a minute or two. The inspector broke the quiet first.

"You see, sir, a coroner's inquest is likely. I have a young man who was quite positive—at least, initially. Since learning of the young lady's denial, he hesitates to testify, yet he remains convinced he saw Miss Hale at the station, walking with a gentleman, just minutes before a porter witnessed a scuffle. The porter attributed it to Leonard's impudence, but it resulted in the fall that killed him. Seeing you exit the very house, sir, I thought I might ask if... well, it is awkward dealing with disputed identities, and one does not doubt a respectable young woman's word without strong proof."

"And she denied being at the station that evening!" Mr. Thornton repeated in a low, brooding tone.

"Yes, sir, twice over, distinctly. I told her I would return, but since I saw you just as I left the young man who claimed it was her, I sought your advice, both as the magistrate who saw Leonard on his deathbed and as the gentleman who hired me."

"You were quite right," said Mr. Thornton. "Take no steps until you see me again."

"The young lady expects me to call, based on what I said."

"I only wish to delay you an hour. It is now three. Come to my warehouse at four."

"Very well, sir!"

They parted ways. Mr. Thornton hurried to his warehouse, sternly ordering his clerks to leave him undisturbed. He retreated to his private room and locked the door. Then he subjected himself to the torture of revisiting every detail. How could he have allowed himself the unsuspicious calm in which her tearful image had reflected only two hours prior, pitying her and forgetting the savage, distrustful jealousy that the sight of her—an unknown figure at such an hour, in such a place—had ignited? How could one so pure stoop from her dignified bearing? Yet, was it decorous? Was it? He hated himself for the fleeting thought, yet it thrilled him with its old power of attraction. And then this falsehood—how dreadful must the fear of shame be to drive truthful Margaret to lie! He could almost pity her. What would be the outcome? She could not have grasped the full extent of her peril; if an inquest occurred and the young man testified. Suddenly, he sprang up. There must be no inquest. He would save Margaret. He would assume the responsibility of preventing it. The medical testimony was vague; he had heard the attending surgeon mention an advanced internal disease destined to kill, noting that death might have been hastened by the fall or subsequent exposure. If he had known how Margaret would be ensnared, how he would stain her purity with a falsehood, he could have saved her with a single word. The decision regarding the inquest had hung in the balance the night before. Miss Hale might love another, be indifferent to him, but he would perform faithful acts of service she would never know. He might despise her, but the woman he once loved must be shielded from shame. To pledge oneself to a lie in a public court, or to admit preferring darkness to light, would be shameful.

Mr. Thornton appeared gray and stern as he emerged from his office, his clerks watching in wonder. He was absent for half an hour, and no less stern when he returned, despite the success of his mission.
