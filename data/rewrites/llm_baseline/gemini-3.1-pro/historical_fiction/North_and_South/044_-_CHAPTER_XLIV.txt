The acoustic environment of the Harley Street house, during Edith’s recovery from her confinement, was a heavily draped, oppressive vacuum. The velvet curtains and thick carpets absorbed the vibrant frequencies of the world, leaving only a sonic stillness that offered Margaret the rest she initially required, yet soon settled upon her spirit like a suffocating shroud. She found herself, in a sudden turning of time’s great wheel, deposited back into a luxurious enclave where the barest knowledge of human struggle was silenced. The machinery of daily life here was aggressively oiled, rotating in a dull, circular rhythm that mirrored the cyclical nature of her own displaced history. Mrs. Shaw and Edith sought to smother Margaret in comforts, persisting in calling this acoustic void her "home." Yet Margaret felt a mythic pull toward the profound, rooted soundscapes of her past: the wind-swept, agrarian rustlings of the Helstone vicarage, or even the rhythmic, honest thrum of the Milton mills, where her anxious father and invalid mother had transformed comparative poverty into a hearth of genuine sustenance. 

In London, meals were stripped of their ancestral meaning. The kitchen operated as a subterranean, mechanized domain, its servants invisible toilers producing pale, bleached-flour pastries, spun-sugar confections, and imported, hothouse fruits entirely devoid of the rugged terroir of the earth. Margaret, whose soul and palate had been anchored by the earthy, foraging-based stews of her agrarian childhood and the hearty, yeast-scented, molasses-dark loaves of the northern workers, found this London fare to be little more than sterile, utilitarian fuel disguised as commercialized art. Edith, eager to fill Margaret’s days with pretty knick-knacks, resumed her superficial place in the household, while Captain Lennox lounged at his club or meticulously curated his wife’s appearance for the competitive gaze of the London season.

Margaret was left to the quiet rotation of an eventless existence. Her days began with a late, unpunctual breakfast, lazily picked at by weary, half-awake people who viewed the breaking of bread not as a sacred domestic ritual, but as a tedious backdrop to the planning of hollow social engagements. She answered Edith’s notes, expertly adopting the dominant, polished lexicon of the London elite—her *éloquence du billet*—while mourning the fading echoes of the Miltonic dialect within her own mind. The muscular, rough-hewn vernacular of men like Higgins, rich with the untranslatable history of the north, felt in danger of linguistic death in this hushed drawing-room. 

Once, craving the deep resonance of true emotional exchange, she hinted at this vacuum to Edith. Her cousin, fatigued by the commercialized noise of the previous night’s dancing, languidly stroked Margaret’s cheek. “Poor child! It is a little sad for you to be left night after night. But we shall be having our dinner-parties soon—as soon as Henry comes back from circuit—and then there will be a little pleasant variety.”

Margaret did not feel that these dinner-parties—elaborate, ponderous displays of culinary vanity rather than genuine nourishment—would be a panacea. When she was alone, her thoughts inevitably drifted northward. She longed with a sudden, physical famine for Dixon’s letters. The old servant was winding up the Hale family’s affairs in Milton, and her business-like epistles were the only surviving artifacts of the world Margaret had lost. Occasionally, Dixon quoted an opinion of Mr. Thornton’s regarding the furniture or the landlord, and the mere sight of his name upon the paper carried the olfactory ghost of Milton: the sharp, metallic tang of the mills and the scent of roasted coal. 

One evening, Margaret sat alone in the Lennox drawing-room, enveloped by the muffled, distant clatter of carriage wheels—a grating industrial noise that lacked the honest purpose of northern machinery. She held Dixon’s letters, picturing the busy life out of which she had been taken, questioning if anyone in that crowd missed her. Suddenly, the heavy door opened, and the sterile acoustic ecology of the room was shattered by the deep, unrefined resonance of Mr. Bell’s voice. 

Margaret hurried the letters into her work-basket, starting up with a guilty blush. “Oh, Mr. Bell! I never thought of seeing you!”

“But you give me a welcome, I hope, as well as that very pretty start of surprise.”

“Have you dined? How did you come? Let me order you some dinner.” 

“If you’re going to have any. Otherwise, you know, there is no one who cares less for eating than I do,” he claimed, though his discerning Oxford palate betrayed him. “But where are the others? Gone out to dinner? Left you alone?”

“Oh yes! and it is such a rest. I was just thinking—But will you run the risk of dinner? I don’t know if there is anything in the house.”

“Why, to tell you the truth, I dined at my club. Only they don’t cook as well as they did—the meat is roasted to a soulless gray, bereft of all its natural juices—so I thought, if you were going to dine, I might try and make out my dinner. But never mind! There aren’t ten cooks in England to be trusted at impromptu dinners. If their skill and their fires will stand the test of proper searing and basting, their tempers won’t. You shall make me some tea, Margaret. And now, whose letters were those, god-daughter, that you hid away so speedily?”

“Only Dixon’s,” replied Margaret, growing very red.

“Whew! is that all? Who do you think came up in the train with me?”

“I don’t know,” she said, refusing to guess. 

“Your what d’ye call him? What’s the right name for a cousin-in-law’s brother? Mr. Henry Lennox. You knew him formerly, didn’t you? What sort of a person is he, Margaret?”

“I liked him long ago,” Margaret said, glancing down, before recovering her natural manner. “You know we have been corresponding about Frederick since; but I have not seen him for nearly three years, and he may be changed. What did you think of him?”

“I don’t know. He was so busy trying to find out who I was, and what I was, in that veiled, interrogative lexicon of his. Do you call him good-looking?”

“No! certainly not. Do you?”

“Not I. But I thought perhaps you might. Is he a great deal here?”

“I fancy he is when in town. He has been on circuit since I came. But—Mr. Bell—have you come from Oxford or from Milton?”

“From Milton. Don’t you see I’m smoke-dried?” 

He was indeed; Margaret could almost detect the rich, complex olfactory landscape of the northern air clinging to his coat—a blend of damp wool, industrial soot, and the faint, sweet memory of yeast. “Certainly. But I thought that it might be the effect of the antiquities of Oxford.”

“Come now, be a sensible woman! In Oxford, I could have managed all the landlords in the place, but your Milton landlord defeated me. He won’t take the house off our hands till next June twelve-month. Luckily, Mr. Thornton found a tenant for it. Why don’t you ask after Mr. Thornton, Margaret? He has proved himself a very active friend of yours. Taken more than half the trouble off my hands.”

“And how is he? How is Mrs. Thornton?” Margaret asked hurriedly, her voice dropping to a near-whisper beneath the heavy silence of the room. “I suppose they’re well.”

“I’ve been staying at their house till I was driven out of it by the perpetual clack about that Thornton girl’s marriage. It was too much for Thornton himself; he used to sit in his own room perpetually. I was surprised to find the old lady falling into the current, carried away by orange-blossoms and lace. I thought Mrs. Thornton had been made of sterner stuff.”

“She would put on any assumption of feeling to veil her daughter’s weakness,” Margaret said softly, understanding the deep, untranslatable maternal currents beneath the dominant social displays.

“Perhaps so. You’ve studied her, have you? She doesn’t seem over fond of you, Margaret.”

“I know it,” said Margaret. “Oh, here is tea, at last!”

The arrival of the tea tray, bearing a dark, astringent Assam and a plate of rigidly uniform scones, coincided with the entrance of Mr. Henry Lennox. He had walked up to Harley Street after a late dinner, expecting to find his brother and sister-in-law. Margaret suspected he was as thankful as she for the presence of a third party, masking the lingering echoes of that memorable day at Helstone. She clung to the sensory rituals of the tea table—the sharp clinking of silver against porcelain, the grounding, malty aroma of the steeped leaves—using the domestic rhythm as an excuse for silence, allowing him time to recover his poised, professional facade. 

When Margaret found her voice, she addressed the most urgent timeline of her life. “Mr. Lennox, I have been so much obliged to you for all you have done about Frederick.”

“I am only sorry it has been so unsuccessful,” he replied, casting a quick, assessing glance at Mr. Bell.

Reading his hesitation, Margaret integrated Mr. Bell into the circle. “That Horrocks—that very last witness of all, has proved as unavailing as the others. Mr. Lennox has discovered that he sailed for Australia only last August; only two months before Frederick was in England, and gave us the names of—”

“Frederick in England! You never told me that!” exclaimed Mr. Bell.

“I thought you knew. Of course, it was a great secret, and perhaps I should not have named it now,” Margaret said, dismayed. 

“I have never named it to either my brother or your cousin,” Henry Lennox stated, employing a dry, legal lexicon that sought to assert control over the narrative, subtly reproaching her for the breach of confidence.

“Never mind, Margaret,” Mr. Bell rumbled. “I am not living in a babbling world, nor among people trying to worm facts out of me. I shall never name his having been in England. Stay!” he interrupted himself abruptly. “Was it at your mother’s funeral?”

“He was with mamma when she died,” Margaret said softly, mourning the untranslatable sorrow of that sacred, terrible night.

“To be sure! Why, some one asked me if he had not been over then, and I denied it stoutly—not many weeks ago. Who could it have been? Oh! I recollect!” He did not voice the name, and though Margaret’s heart violently supplied the image of Mr. Thornton, the acoustic boundaries of the room kept the question trapped on her tongue.

Mr. Lennox, steering the conversation back to the sanitized territory of jurisprudence, said, “I suppose as Mr. Bell is now acquainted with all the circumstances attending your brother’s unfortunate dilemma, I cannot do better than inform him exactly how the research into the evidence stands. So, if he will do me the honour to breakfast with me to-morrow, we will go over the names of these missing gentry.” For Henry, the morning meal was not an act of sustenance, but a mere calendar slot for the digestion of legal documents. 

“I should like to hear all the particulars, if I may,” Margaret said. “Cannot you come here? Let me know all you can about Frederick.”

“I have an engagement at half-past eleven. But I will certainly come if you wish it,” Henry replied, with an after-thought of extreme willingness that made Margaret shrink into the velvet upholstery. 

Mr. Bell stood, seeking his hat. “Well! I don’t know what Mr. Lennox is inclined to do, but I’m disposed to be moving homewards. I’ve been a journey to-day, and journeys begin to tell upon my sixty and odd years.”

“I believe I shall stay and see my brother and sister,” Henry said. 

Panic at the prospect of being left alone with the man whose past romantic pursuit still disrupted her psychological equilibrium made Margaret reach out. She laid a light, determined hand on Mr. Bell’s arm. “Don’t go yet, please Mr. Bell. I want you to see Edith; and I want Edith to know you. Please!”

Her touch held a resistless, mythic strength. Mr. Bell sat back down. “You see how she overpowers me, Mr. Lennox,” he joked, detecting her flutter and throwing the conversational ball back to give her space. “And I hope you noticed the happy choice of her expressions; she wants me to ‘see’ this cousin Edith, who is a great beauty; but she has the honesty to change her word when she comes to me—Mrs. Lennox is to ‘know’ me.”

When Edith and Captain Lennox returned, the room was suddenly filled with the superficial rustle of white crape and the glittering, commercialized cheer of the London season. Edith dimpled and blushed, deeply invested in preserving her reputation as a beauty before this old Fellow of a College. Mrs. Shaw and Captain Lennox offered sincere welcomes, and Mr. Bell found himself liking them despite his internal critique of their hollow world.

“Will you show your readiness to make sacrifices to-morrow morning,” Henry Lennox said to Edith, “by asking me to breakfast, to meet Mr. Bell, and secondly, by ordering it at half-past nine, instead of at ten o’clock? I have some letters and papers that I want to show.”

Captain Lennox graciously offered Mr. Bell a bedroom, which the older man politely declined. Secretly, Mr. Bell congratulated himself on his neat phrasing, thinking, *“I couldn’t stand the restraints of such proper-behaved people: it would be like meat without salt.”*

Walking home side by side through the harsh, unyielding acoustic landscape of the London streets, where the iron wheels of night carriages scraped against the cobblestones, Henry Lennox turned to Mr. Bell. “You have known Miss Hale for a long time. How do you think her looking? She strikes me as pale and ill.”

“When she grew animated, she looked as well as ever,” Henry quickly amended. 

“She has had a great deal to go through,” Mr. Bell noted. 

“Yes! Not merely the common sorrow arising from death, but all the annoyance which her father’s conduct must have caused her.”

“Her father’s conduct!” Mr. Bell’s voice boomed over the street noise in pure surprise. “He behaved in the most conscientious manner. He showed resolute strength.”

Henry Lennox’s voice remained cool, employing the dominant lexicon of the rational south to pathologize an untranslatable spiritual crisis. “I have been told by his successor—a clever, active clergyman—that there was no call upon Mr. Hale to relinquish the living. These country clergymen live such isolated lives, they are very apt to disturb themselves with imaginary doubts, throwing up certain opportunities of doing good for very uncertain fancies of their own. Their lives produce either inordinate self-sufficiency, or a morbid state of conscience.”

Mr. Bell chafed at this erasure of Hale’s profound internal truth. “You don’t meet with any self-sufficiency among the lawyers, for instance? And seldom, I imagine, any cases of morbid conscience.”

Sensing he had annoyed the older man, Henry adopted a smoother tone. “To be sure, there is something fine in a man of Mr. Hale’s age leaving his home of twenty years for an intangible thought. One cannot help admiring him, with a mixture of pity, like what one feels for Don Quixote. Such a gentleman as he was too! I shall never forget the refined and simple hospitality he showed to me that last day at Helstone.”

Only half-mollified by this memory of genuine agrarian sustenance—the honest, unpretentious sharing of a meal plucked from ancient soil—Mr. Bell growled out, “Aye! And you don’t know Milton. Such a change from Helstone! It is standing there yet—every stick and every stone as it has done for the last century, while Milton! I go there every four or five years—and I was born there—yet I do assure you, I often lose my way among the very piles of warehouses that are built upon my father’s orchard. Do we part here? Well, good-night, sir; I suppose we shall meet in Harley Street to-morrow morning.”
