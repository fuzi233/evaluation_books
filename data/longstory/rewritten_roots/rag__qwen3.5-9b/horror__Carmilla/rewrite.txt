Upon a sheet of aged parchment affixed to the narrative that follows, Doctor Hesselius has penned a note of considerable elaboration, accompanied by a referral to his treatise on the singular phenomenon illuminated by this manuscript. In that scholarly inquiry, he addresses the matter with his customary erudition and piercing insight, stripping away ambiguity with remarkable directness and compression. Were I to present the full scope of his findings, it would constitute but a single volume within the vast series of that extraordinary man's collected works. Yet, since my sole intent in publishing this case is to engage the layperson, I shall defer to the intelligent lady who recounts it, allowing her voice to stand unobstructed. After due deliberation, I have resolved to withhold any précis of the Doctor's intricate reasoning or extracts from his assertions regarding a subject he deems to involve, not improbably, some of the most profound arcana of our dual existence and its elusive intermediates. My initial impulse upon uncovering this paper was to reignite the correspondence commenced by Doctor Hesselius so many years prior with an informant of such formidable intellect and meticulous care. Regrettably, however, I discovered that she had passed away during the intervening decades. Had she lived, she might have contributed little to the narrative she now communicates in the following pages, rendered with a conscientious particularity that, to the best of my judgment, remains undimmed by time.

In the high, mist-shrouded valleys of Styria, we dwell within a fortress of stone and shadow, a *schloss* that commands the silence of the forest. Here, wealth is measured not in gold but in acres of solitude; a mere eight or nine hundred florins annually stretches far beyond its value in the bustling capitals of Europe, affording us a life of peculiar, rustic plenty. My father, an Englishman by birth and name though never having set foot upon his ancestral soil, acquired this feudal stronghold and its surrounding lands as a retirement purchase, a bargain sealed with the quiet satisfaction of a man escaping the clamor of the world. The estate is a masterpiece of isolation, perched upon a slight eminence where the ancient road, worn smooth by centuries of carriage wheels, passes beneath an unraised drawbridge. Below lies a moat, a mirror of stillness populated by drifting swans and fleets of white water-lilies, encircling towers and a Gothic chapel that pierce the gloom. To the right, a steep bridge spans a stream winding through deep shade, while to the left, the forest unfolds in a vast, irregular glade. From the hall door, the woods stretch fifteen miles east and twelve west; the nearest living village lies seven miles to the left, a distant speck in the green expanse. Even closer, three miles westward toward the domain of General Spielsdorf, stands a ruined hamlet, its roofless church housing the moldering tombs of the Karnsteins, an extinct noble family whose desolate chateau looms over the silent ruins of their former town. Legends whisper of the curse that drove them from this melancholy spot, tales I reserve for another time.

My household was a circle of four souls, excluding the silent attendants who drifted through the wings of the castle. There was my father, aging but kind, and I, at the time of this chronicle, nineteen years of age. Eight years have passed since those early days. My mother, a Styrian lady, departed in my infancy, leaving behind only a vague memory of warmth. Her absence was filled by Madame Perrodon, a Swiss governess from Bern whose fat, benignant face became the anchor of my childhood. She formed a trinity with my father and myself at our small dinner table. Completing the quartet was Mademoiselle De Lafontaine, a finishing governess fluent in French and German, while Madame Perrodon spoke French and halting English. We, in turn, maintained our English tongue daily, a deliberate act of patriotism and preservation, creating a linguistic babel that amused passing strangers but bound us tightly together. Occasionally, young ladies of my own age visited for varying durations, returning the favor in kind, serving as the primary texture of my social life alongside the sporadic calls of neighbors from leagues away. Yet, despite these interactions, my existence remained profoundly solitary. My governesses held sway over me with the cautious authority reserved for a favored child, whose indulgent parent granted carte blanche in all matters.

The first fracture in my peace, an incident so trivial it seems scarcely worthy of record, nonetheless carved a scar upon my mind that has never healed. I recall being no older than six when the nursery—a spacious chamber in the upper story crowned by a steep oak ceiling—became the theater of my terror. One night, awakening in the dark, I found the room empty. The nursery maid was gone, the nurse absent, and I was utterly alone. Unburdened by the superstitious fears that plague most children, I felt not dread but indignation at this perceived neglect. Whimpering grew to a roar of protest, ready to erupt into full-blown screaming, when a figure emerged from the shadows beside my bed.

It was a young lady, kneeling with her hands tucked beneath the coverlet, her face solemn yet undeniably beautiful. I stared at her with a mixture of confusion and delight, my anger dissolving instantly. She reached out, caressing me gently, and lay down beside me, drawing me close with a smile that radiated pure comfort. In moments, I was soothed, lulled into a slumber that felt like safety itself.

But sleep betrayed me. Suddenly, a sensation akin to two hot needles piercing my breast simultaneously woke me with a gasp. I screamed, a sound of pure panic. The lady recoiled, her eyes wide and fixed upon me, before slipping silently to the floor and vanishing beneath the bedframe. Fear, cold and absolute, seized my throat. I shrieked until my lungs burned.

Chaos erupted as the nurse, the maid, and the housekeeper burst into the room. Hearing my tale, they soothed me with practiced voices, yet their faces betrayed a pallor of genuine alarm. They frantically searched the room, lifting the bedclothes, peeking under tables, and prying open cupboards. The housekeeper leaned close to the nurse, her voice hushed with grave certainty: "Place your hand along that hollow in the mattress. Someone *did* lie there, surely as you breathe; the fabric is still warm."

They examined my chest, searching for the phantom punctures I claimed to feel, declaring with scientific detachment that no injury existed. Yet, the horror lingered. All three servants remained awake throughout the night, their vigil marking the end of my innocence. From that evening forward, a sentinel was stationed in the nursery until I reached the age of fourteen.

For months thereafter, I lived in a state of heightened nervousness. A physician was summoned, a pallid, elderly man with a long, saturnine face marred by the scars of smallpox and topped with a stiff chestnut wig. Every other day, he administered bitter medicines I detested, his presence casting a gray shadow over my recovery. The morning following the vision, daylight brought no relief; I could not endure being left alone for a single moment.

My father ascended the stairs, standing by my bedside with cheerful composure. He asked the nurse questions, laughed heartily at her responses, patted my shoulder, and kissed my forehead, insisting that the encounter was merely a dream, harmless and fleeting. His reassurances rang hollow. I knew, with a terrifying clarity, that the stranger's visit was no fabrication of the sleeping mind. I was awestruck by the magnitude of my fright.

A shred of consolation arrived from the nursery maid, who insisted it was she who had knelt beside me, that I must have been half-asleep to fail to recognize her features. Though the nurse corroborated this, doubt gnawed at me. Later that day, a venerable figure entered the room: an old man in a black cassock, accompanied by the nurse and housekeeper. His face was sweet and gentle, and he took my hands, joining them in prayer. "Lord, hear all good prayers for us, for Jesus' sake," he intoned softly. These were the exact words I would repeat for years, taught by my nurse until they became the rhythm of my soul. I recalled the old man's thoughtful expression, the stark contrast of his white hair against the black cloth, standing amidst the crude, towering furniture of a style three centuries old. Through the small lattice window, scanty light filtered into the shadowy room, illuminating the three women kneeling beside him. He prayed aloud, his voice earnest and quavering, for what seemed an interminable duration.

I possess no memory of the years preceding this event, nor of the time immediately following; those periods dissolve into obscurity. Only the scenes I have described remain vivid, etched onto my consciousness like isolated images in a phantasmagoria, surrounded by an eternal, consuming darkness.

The narrative demands a suspension of disbelief, a leap of faith required to accept the strangeness of what follows, yet I assure you, the foundation of this tale rests not on fiction, but on the hard, unyielding bedrock of eyewitness testimony. It was a summer evening of such crystalline sweetness that the air itself seemed to hum with a latent vitality. My father, as he occasionally indulged his solitary wanderlust, invited me to accompany him along the forest vista that framed our estate, a verdant corridor leading directly from the Schloss.

"We are delayed," my father observed, his stride matching the rhythm of the crunching gravel beneath our boots. "General Spielsdorf cannot arrive as swiftly as I had hoped." His disappointment was palpable, a shadow cast over the golden light filtering through the canopy. We had anticipated his presence within the week, a visit intended to last several weeks, bringing with him a young lady, his niece and ward, Mademoiselle Rheinfeldt. Though I had never beheld her face, I had consumed descriptions of her charm in endless anticipation, constructing elaborate daydreams of shared laughter and intellectual camaraderie. The prospect of this new acquaintance had fueled my imagination for weeks, making the sudden deferral of her arrival a blow harder than any young woman, confined to the stifling walls of a town or a bustling district, could truly comprehend.

"And when does he come?" I pressed, the disappointment tightening my chest.

"Not until autumn," my father replied, his tone gentle but final. "Perhaps two months hence."

A silence fell between us, heavier than the encroaching twilight. Then, with a mixture of mortification and piercing curiosity, I asked, "And why the delay?"

"Because the poor young lady is dead," he answered, the words striking me with the force of a physical blow.

"I... I didn't know," I stammered, feeling the heat rise in my cheeks. "But wasn't she ill? The General mentioned in his first letter, weeks ago, that she was not well..."

"There was no suggestion of danger," my father corrected softly. "I simply forgot to inform you until this evening, when I received his correspondence. You were not in the room when it arrived."

He handed me the letter, and I scanned the page, my heart sinking. The General's handwriting was frantic, jagged lines of ink that seemed to writhe with distress. "He is in great affliction," my father murmured. "Read it, and you will understand the chaos consuming him."

We found a seat on a rough-hewn bench beneath a grove of majestic lime trees, their branches interlacing overhead. The sun was dipping below the sylvan horizon, painting the sky in hues of melancholic crimson that reflected in the winding stream flowing past our home. The water, passing under the steep old bridge, mirrored the fading light, turning the current into a ribbon of fire.

I read the letter twice, the first time silently, the second aloud to my father, struggling to reconcile the coherent tragedy with the disjointed, almost hysterical syntax of the writer. Grief, I concluded, had clearly unsettled his mind.

*"I have lost my darling daughter, for as such I loved her,"* the letter began, the words tearing at my soul even though I had never seen Bertha Rheinfeldt. *"During the last days of dear Bertha's illness I was not able to write to you. Before then I had no idea of her danger. I have lost her, and now learn all, too late. She died in the peace of innocence, and in the glorious hope of a blessed futurity. The fiend who betrayed our infatuated hospitality has done it all. I thought I was receiving into my house innocence, gaiety, a charming companion for my lost Bertha. Heavens! what a fool have I been! I thank God my child died without a suspicion of the cause of her sufferings. She is gone without so much as conjecturing the nature of her illness, and the accursed passion of the agent of all this misery. I devote my remaining days to tracking and extinguishing a monster. I am told I may hope to accomplish my righteous and merciful purpose. At present there is scarcely a gleam of light to guide me. I curse my conceited incredulity, my despicable affectation of superiority, my blindness, my obstinacy—all—too late. I cannot write or talk collectedly now. I am distracted. So soon as I shall have a little recovered, I mean to devote myself for a time to enquiry, which may possibly lead me as far as Vienna. Some time in the autumn, two months hence, or earlier if I live, I will see you—that is, if you permit me; I will then tell you all that I scarce dare put upon paper now. Farewell. Pray for me, dear friend."*

Tears pricked my eyes, hot and sudden, despite the absence of a personal connection to the deceased. I was startled, profoundly shaken, and utterly disappointed. The sun had fully surrendered to the night, casting the world into a soft, clear twilight. We continued our walk, the distance to the main road stretching out before us, nearly a mile of path that led us toward the road passing the front of the Schloss. As we walked, the moon ascended, shedding a brilliant, silver radiance over the landscape.

At the drawbridge, we encountered Madame Perrodon and Mademoiselle De Lafontaine. They had discarded their bonnets, stepping out to bask in the exquisite luminescence of the full moon. Their voices bubbled forth in an animated dialect as we approached, drawing us into their circle. We paused at the bridge, turning to admire the tableau before us. The glade we had traversed lay spread out like a green carpet beneath our feet. To our left, the narrow road vanished under clusters of lordly trees, swallowed by the thickening forest; to our right, the road crossed the steep, picturesque bridge, flanked by a ruined tower that once guarded the pass. Beyond the bridge, an abrupt eminence rose, cloaked in trees and dotted with grey, ivy-choked rocks. A thin film of mist drifted over the sward and low grounds, moving like smoke to mark the distances with a transparent veil, while the river flashed faintly in the moonlight.

No scene could be conceived softer or sweeter. Yet, the news I had just received draped a layer of melancholy over the perfection, transforming the enchantment into a profound, haunting serenity. My father, who possessed a keen eye for the picturesque, and I, stood in silent contemplation of the expanse. Behind us, the two governesses discoursed passionately on the scenery, their voices rising in praise of the lunar sphere.

Madame Perrodon, a rotund, middle-aged woman of romantic disposition, sighed poetically, her words drifting on the cool air. Mademoiselle De Lafontaine, claiming descent from a German father and adopting an air of psychological and metaphysical profundity, took a more analytical turn.

"When the moon shines with such intensity," she declared, gesturing to the heavens, "it indicates a special spiritual activity. The effect of the full moon in such brilliance is manifold. It acts upon dreams, upon lunacy, upon nervous constitutions. It possesses marvelous physical influences connected to life itself."

She recounted a chilling anecdote regarding her cousin, a mate of a merchant vessel. *"On such a night, he took a nap on deck, lying on his back with his face full in the light of the moon. He awakened, after dreaming of an old woman clawing him by the cheek, with his features horribly drawn to one side. His countenance has never quite recovered its equilibrium since."*

"The moon, this night," she continued, her eyes wide with fervor, "is replete with idyllic and magnetic influence. And observe, when you look behind you at the front of the Schloss, how all its windows flash and twinkle with that silvery splendor, as if unseen hands had lit the rooms to receive fairy guests."

There exists an indolent style of spirit in which, disinclined to speak oneself, the chatter of others becomes a pleasant sedative for the listless. I gazed on, amused by the tinkling of their conversation, momentarily forgetting the weight of the letter.

"My dear father," I interrupted gently, breaking the spell. "I have fallen into one of my moping moods tonight."

My father chuckled, quoting Shakespeare to maintain our linguistic decorum. "'In truth I know not why I am so sad. It wearies me: you say it wearies you; But how I got it—came by it.' I forget the rest. But I feel as if some great misfortune were hanging over us. I suppose the poor General's afflicted letter has had something to do with it."

Our reverie was shattered by the unwonted, thunderous sound of carriage wheels and the clatter of many hooves upon the road. The noise seemed to emanate from the high ground overlooking the bridge. Soon, the equipage emerged from the darkness. Two horsemen crossed the bridge first, followed by a grand carriage drawn by four horses, with two men riding alongside. It was clearly the traveling carriage of someone of high rank, and we were instantly captivated by the unusual spectacle.

The tension mounted as the carriage crested the summit of the steep bridge. Suddenly, one of the lead horses, spooked by something unseen, reared and plunged. Madame Perrodon responded with tenderness, weaving a tapestry of comforting assurances. Then came the next query, fragile and disoriented: "Where am I? What is this place?" Finally, panic sharpened her tone: "I don't see the carriage; and Matska, where is she?" Madame Perrodon answered every syllable within her grasp. Gradually, the fog of the accident lifted; the girl recalled the chaos, and relief washed over her to learn that neither the carriage nor its attendants bore a scratch. Yet, the revelation that her mother had abandoned her here, to return only in three months' time, broke her composure, and she wept.

I stood ready to offer my own consolations, joining Madame Perrodon's, when Mademoiselle De Lafontaine gently interposed, placing a restraining hand upon my arm. "Do not approach," she advised softly. "One presence is all she can bear at present; a spark of further excitement might shatter her now."

As soon as she is settled in bed, I resolved, I shall ascend to her chamber. Meanwhile, my father had dispatched a servant on horseback to fetch the physician, who dwelt two leagues distant, while a bedroom was hurriedly prepared for our guest. The stranger rose, leaning heavily on Madame's arm, and traversed the drawbridge with slow, deliberate steps into the castle gate. Servants awaited in the hall to receive her, conducting her immediately to her quarters.

This room, which we typically utilized as our drawing room, was long and imposing, boasting four windows that overlooked the moat, the drawbridge, and the forest scene I had just witnessed. Furnished in antique carved oak, it featured massive cabinets and chairs upholstered in crimson Utrecht velvet. The walls were draped in tapestries framed by towering gold borders, depicting figures of immense scale in ancient, curious costumes engaged in hunting, hawking, and festive revelry. It possessed a stately grandeur that never compromised comfort. Here, we gathered that night, candles flickering against the gloom, to dissect the evening's adventure. Both Madame Perrodon and Mademoiselle De Lafontaine joined us. The young stranger had barely succumbed to a deep slumber when the ladies departed her side, entrusting her to a servant.

"How do you like our guest?" I asked the moment Madame re-entered. "Tell me everything about her."

"I like her exceedingly," Madame replied, her voice warm with admiration. "She is, I almost believe, the prettiest creature I have ever encountered; of your age, so gentle and refined."

"She is absolutely beautiful," Mademoiselle interjected, having peeked briefly into the stranger's chamber.

"And such a sweet voice!" Madame Perrodon added.

"Did you notice a woman in the carriage, once it was set upright again, who failed to alight?" Mademoiselle inquired sharply. "She merely gazed from the window?"

"No, we saw none," Madame answered.

Then she recounted the vision: a hideous black woman, crowned with a sort of colored turban, staring eternally from the carriage window. This figure nodded and grinned derisively at the ladies, her eyes gleaming with madness, revealing large white sclerae and teeth bared in fury.

"Did you observe what an ill-favored pack of men the servants were?" Madame asked.

"Yes," my father replied, having just entered the circle. "Ugly, hang-dog creatures as I have rarely beheld in my life. I pray they do not rob the poor lady in the forest. They are cunning rogues, however; they restored order in a trice."

"I suppose they are exhausted from such prolonged travel," Madame suggested. "Moreover, their faces were strangely lean, dark, and sullen. I am very curious, I admit; but surely the young lady will recount the tale tomorrow, provided she has recovered sufficiently."

"I doubt she will," my father said, a mysterious smile playing on his lips accompanied by a slight, knowing nod, as if he harbored secrets he preferred to keep buried. This cryptic gesture only intensified our curiosity regarding the brief, earnest exchange that had preceded her departure.

Scarcely were we alone when I implored him to reveal the truth. He required little persuasion. "There is no particular reason why I should withhold it. She expressed a reluctance to burden us with the care of her daughter, citing delicate health and a nervous constitution. However, she volunteered explicitly that she was not subject to seizures—or to any illusion; being, in fact, perfectly sane."

"How remarkably odd to assert such a thing!" I interjected. "So unnecessary."

"At all events, it *was* said," he laughed lightly. "Since you wish to know the entirety of our interaction—which was, indeed, minimal—I shall tell you. She stated, 'I am undertaking a long journey of *vital* importance—she emphasized the word—rapid and secret; I shall return for my child in three months; in the interim, she will remain silent concerning who we are, whence we come, and whither we travel.' That was the extent of her discourse. She spoke flawless French. When she uttered the word 'secret,' she paused for several seconds, fixing her gaze upon me with stern intensity. I suspect she places great weight on that vow. You observed how swiftly she departed. I hope I have not committed a folly by assuming guardianship of the young lady."

For my part, I was delighted. I yearned to see and speak with her, awaiting only the physician's blessing. Those who dwell in cities cannot comprehend the magnitude of introducing a new friend into such profound solitude. The doctor did not arrive until nearly one o'clock, yet I could no more retreat to my bed and sleep than I could have overtaken, on foot, the carriage in which the princess in black velvet had driven away.

When the physician descended to the drawing room, his report was overwhelmingly favorable. His patient was now sitting upright, her pulse regular, appearing perfectly well. She had sustained no physical injury, and the initial shock to her nerves had dissipated harmlessly. With this clearance, I immediately sent inquiries as to whether she would permit a brief visit to her room. The servant returned promptly to confirm she desired nothing more. Naturally, I did not delay in availing myself of this permission.

Our visitor lay in one of the most magnificent chambers in the *schloss*. It was perhaps slightly too stately, dominated by a somber tapestry opposite the foot of the bed depicting Cleopatra with the asp coils at her bosom, alongside other faded classical scenes. Yet, the gold carvings and rich, varied colors of the other decorations more than redeemed the gloom of the ancient hangings. Candles burned brightly at the bedside. She sat propped up, her slender, pretty figure enveloped in a soft silk dressing gown embroidered with flowers and lined with thick quilted silk, which her mother had draped over her legs as she lay upon the ground.

What struck me dumb in an instant, causing me to recoil a step or two, was the sight of her face. It was the very visage that had haunted my childhood nights, fixed in my memory and ruminated upon with horror for so many years, when no one suspected the depths of my dread. It was pretty, even beautiful, wearing initially the same melancholy expression. But this mood instantly transformed into a strange, fixed smile of recognition. A silence of a full minute hung between us before she finally spoke; I remained unable to utter a sound.

"How wonderful!" she exclaimed. "Twelve years ago, I saw your face in a dream, and it has haunted me ever since."

"Wonderful indeed!" I repeated, struggling to overcome the horror that had momentarily paralyzed my speech. "Twelve years ago, in vision or reality, I certainly saw you. I could not forget your face. It has remained before my eyes ever since."

Her smile softened. Whatever strangeness I had perceived vanished, replaced by a delightfully pretty and intelligent countenance, her cheeks dimpling warmly. Reassured, I resumed the tone dictated by hospitality, welcoming her and expressing how much joy her accidental arrival had brought us all, particularly the happiness it represented for me. Taking her hand as I spoke, I felt a mixture of shyness typical of the lonely, yet the situation rendered me eloquent and bold. She pressed my hand, laying hers atop it, her eyes glowing as she looked hastily into mine, smiling again and blushing. She accepted my welcome with charming grace.

I sat beside her, still bewildered, as she began her confession. The rain lashed against the leaded glass of the library windows, a rhythmic drumming that felt, to Lady Violet Lebas, like the frantic, indifferent heartbeat of London itself. She sat at her mahogany desk, the heavy scent of beeswax and damp wool filling the room, her gaze fixed upon the thick sheaf of letters that had arrived by the morning post. They were from Mr. Andrew Lang, his sprawling, confident script detailing his latest thoughts on literature.

Violet sighed, tracing the edge of the parchment with a silver letter-opener. Lang was a man of undeniable erudition, yet his letters were suffocated by the very patriarchal complacency she so deeply despised. He wrote to her of Virgil, of modern English poetry, of Fielding and Rochefoucauld, positioning himself as the benevolent instructor to her "undefeated resolution to admire only the right things."

"The right things," she murmured aloud, the words tasting like ash. What he meant, of course, was the established canon—the texts elevated by centuries of wealthy, privileged men who saw their own reflections in the exploits of conquerors and the musings of comfortable philosophers.

She turned to his pages on Fielding. Lang excused the novelist's coarse morality, arguing that men must be pardoned the "vices of young blood" so long as they were brave and generous. He painted a nostalgic picture of a "lusty old militant world," entirely blind to the fact that this merry England was built upon the bruised backs of women and the indentured silence of the poor. He dismissed the systemic inequalities that left women like Sophia Western with no recourse but to forgive the betrayals of their husbands.

"It is a curious thing," Violet continued, her voice sharpening with a feminist rage that cut through the gloom of the study, "how easily a man can claim to understand the 'human condition' while ignoring the specific, crushing conditions of half the species. He speaks of honor and duty as if they are universal constants, failing to see that for the woman trapped within the domestic sphere, these virtues are often the very chains that bind her to servitude. He writes of 'charms' and 'amulets' against the vampire, unaware that the true monster is the social order that allows such horrors to fester in the shadows of polite society."

She stood abruptly, pacing the length of the room, her silhouette framed by the storm-lit window. The narrative she was constructing in her mind was not merely a retelling of a Gothic tale; it was an excavation of the psyche, a confrontation with the absurdity of existence where the boundaries between sanity and madness, victim and predator, were terrifyingly porous.

"There is a coldness, it seems to me, beyond her years, in her smiling melancholy persistent refusal to afford me the least ray of light," Violet recited, her tone shifting from critique to a haunting reenactment of the original text, her eyes drifting closed as she inhabited the memory of Carmilla. "I dare say I was unreasonable... curiosity is a restless and unscrupulous passion, and no one girl can endure, with patience, that hers should be baffled by another."

She paused, letting the weight of the sentence settle. "Had she no trust in my good sense or honor? Why would she not believe me when I assured her, so solemnly, that I would not divulge one syllable of what she told me to any mortal breathing?"

The irony was palpable. Here was a woman, Carmilla, whose very existence defied the rigid categorizations of Lang's world, operating in a realm of fluid identity and suppressed instinct, while Lang himself remained trapped in the static, male-dominated discourse of the dead authors he claimed to address.

"What she did tell me amounted, in my unconscionable estimation—to nothing," Violet whispered, a bitter smile touching her lips. "It was all summed up in three very vague disclosures: First—Her name was Carmilla. Second—Her family was very ancient and noble. Third—Her home lay in the direction of the west."

She walked to the bookshelf, pulling down a volume of old stories, flipping through the brittle pages. "She would not tell me the name of their family, nor their armorial bearings, nor the name of their estate, nor even that of the country they lived in. You are not to suppose that I worried her incessantly on these subjects. I watched opportunity, and rather insinuated than urged my inquiries."

Violet ran a finger along the spine of the book, imagining the scene. "Once or twice, indeed, I did attack her more directly. But no matter what my tactics, utter failure was invariably the result. Reproaches and caresses were all lost upon her. But I must add this, that her evasion was conducted with so pretty a melancholy and deprecation, with so many, and even passionate declarations of her liking for me, and trust in my honor, and with so many promises that I should at last know all, that I could not find it in my heart long to be offended with her."

"And when she had spoken such a rhapsody," Violet continued, her voice dropping to a conspiratorial whisper, mimicking the seductive cadence of the original narrator, "she would press me more closely in her trembling embrace, and her lips in soft kisses gently glow upon my cheek. Her agitations and her language were unintelligible to me. From these foolish embraces, which were not of very frequent occurrence, I used to wish to extricate myself; but my energies seemed to fail me. Her murmured words sounded like a lullaby in my ear, and soothed my resistance into a trance, from which I only seemed to recover myself when she withdrew her arms."

She stopped, shaking her head slightly. "In these mysterious moods I did not like her. I experienced a strange tumultuous excitement that was pleasurable, ever and anon, mingled with a vague sense of fear and disgust. I had no distinct thoughts about her while such scenes lasted, but I was conscious of a love growing into adoration, and also of abhorrence. This I know is paradox, but I can make no other attempt to explain the feeling."

Violet returned to her desk, picking up her pen, the nib scratching sharply against the paper. "I now write, after an interval of more than ten years, with a trembling hand, with a confused and horrible recollection of certain occurrences and situations, in the ordeal through which I was unconsciously passing; though with a vivid and very sharp remembrance of the main current of my story. But, I suspect, in all lives there are certain emotional scenes, those in which our passions have been most wildly and terribly roused, that are of all others the most vaguely and dimly remembered."

She dipped the pen deeper into the inkwell, the liquid swirling like blood. "Sometimes after an hour of apathy, my strange and beautiful companion would take my hand and hold it with a fond pressure, renewed again and again; blushing softly, gazing in my face with languid and burning eyes, and breathing so fast that her dress rose and fell with the tumultuous respiration. It was like the ardor of a lover; it embarrassed me; it was hateful and yet over-powering; and with gloating eyes she drew me to her, and her hot lips traveled along my cheek in kisses; and she would whisper, almost in sobs, 'You are mine, you shall be mine, you and I are one for ever.' My father chuckled, dismissing the intensity of the moment. I often pondered whether our lovely guest ever uttered a prayer. I had never witnessed her upon her knees. In the mornings, she descended only after our family devotions had concluded; at night, she remained sequestered in the drawing room, absent from our brief evening prayers in the hall. Had it not been for the casual revelation during a momentary lapse in conversation that she had indeed been baptized, I would have questioned her faith entirely. Religion was a topic she never broached. Had I possessed deeper worldly wisdom, this specific neglect—or perhaps active antipathy—might not have so startled me.

The precautions of the neurotic are contagious. Persons of similar temperament inevitably mimic them after a time. I had adopted Carmilla's habit of locking my bedroom door, succumbing to her whimsical alarms regarding midnight intruders and prowling assassins. I had also embraced her ritual of conducting a brief, frantic search of my quarters to ensure no lurking thief or murderer was "ensconced" within the shadows. Having fortified myself with these wise measures, I retired to my bed and surrendered to sleep.

A solitary candle burned in my room, a relic of an ancient habit dating back to my earliest days, a vigilance nothing could tempt me to abandon. Thus fortified, I believed I could rest in peace. Yet, dreams traverse stone walls; they illuminate dark chambers or shroud bright ones, and their inhabitants enter and exit at will, mocking the efforts of locksmiths.

That night, I experienced a dream that marked the genesis of a very strange agony. I cannot classify it as a nightmare, for I remained acutely conscious of my state of sleep. Simultaneously, I was hyper-aware of my location: in my room, lying in bed, precisely as I physically was. I perceived, or perhaps imagined perceiving, the room and its furnishings exactly as I had seen them moments prior, save for the encroaching darkness. At the foot of the bed, something moved, indistinct at first. Soon, clarity returned: it was a soot-black creature resembling a monstrous cat. It appeared to span four or five feet in length, measuring fully the expanse of the hearthrug as it traversed it. I could not cry out, though terror seized my throat. Its pace accelerated, and the room plunged into a darkness so absolute that I could discern nothing but its eyes. Suddenly, I felt it spring lightly onto the bed. Two broad eyes approached my face, and instantaneously, a stinging pain pierced my chest, as if two large needles had darted, an inch or two apart, deep into my heart.

I woke with a scream.

The room was illuminated by the candle that had burned throughout the night. Standing at the foot of the bed, slightly to the right, stood a female figure. She wore a dark, loose gown, her hair cascading down to cover her shoulders. She was as motionless as a block of stone. There was not the slightest stir of respiration. As I stared, transfixed, the figure seemed to shift position, moving closer to the door. Then, the door creaked open, and she stepped out into the corridor.

Relief flooded my lungs, restoring my ability to breathe and move. My immediate thought was that Carmilla had played a prank upon me, forgetting to secure the latch. I rushed to the door, only to find it locked securely from the inside as usual. Fear, cold and paralyzing, gripped me. I was horrified. I threw myself back into the bed, pulling the covers over my head, lying there more dead than alive until the dawn broke.

It would be futile for me to attempt to convey the sheer magnitude of the horror that clings to my memory of that night, even now. It was not a fleeting dread, the kind a nightmare leaves behind to fade with the dawn; rather, it was a terror that deepened with the passage of time, seeping into the very walls and saturating the furniture that had witnessed the apparition. I could not bear to remain alone the following day for a single moment. I considered confessing to my father, yet two opposing fears held me back. First, I dreaded that he would dismiss my tale as a childish jest, unable to endure the trivialization of my experience; second, I feared he might believe I had fallen victim to the mysterious affliction plaguing our neighborhood. I harbored no such suspicions myself, but given his recent frailty as an invalid, I dared not risk alarming him further.

Fortunately, I found comfort in the company of my good-natured companions, Madame Perrodon and the vivacious Mademoiselle Lafontaine. Both sensed my disturbed spirit and nervous agitation. Eventually, burdened by the weight of my secret, I confided in them. Mademoiselle laughed, a brittle sound, while Madame Perrodon wore an expression of genuine anxiety.

"By the way," Mademoiselle remarked, her laughter failing to mask the tension in her voice, "the long lime-tree walk behind Carmilla's bedroom window is haunted!"

"Nonsense!" Madame Perrodon exclaimed, likely deeming the subject inappropriate for polite conversation. "Who tells such stories, my dear?"

"Martin claims he ascended twice, while the old yard gate was being repaired before sunrise, and each time he beheld the same female figure traversing the lime-tree avenue."

"So he well might, provided there are still cows to milk in the river fields," Madame retorted pragmatically. "I dare say; but Martin chooses to be frightened. Never did I witness a fool more easily spooked."

"You must not mention this to Carmilla," I interjected urgently, "because she can see down that walk directly from her window. And she is, if anything, a greater coward than I."

Carmilla descended later than usual that day. As soon as we were reunited, she confessed her own terrors. "I was so frightened last night," she whispered, her voice trembling. "Well, listen to me," I began, recounting my own ordeal. Her reaction was immediate and visceral; she appeared genuinely horrified. "Did you keep the charm near you?" she asked, her eyes wide with earnest concern.

"No," I admitted, "I had dropped it into a china vase in the drawing room. However, I shall certainly carry it with me tonight, given your immense faith in it."

At this remove in time, I cannot fully articulate—or perhaps comprehend—how I managed to overcome my paralyzing horror sufficiently to lie alone in my room that night. I distinctly remember pinning the charm to my pillow. Sleep claimed me almost instantly, deeper and more sound than I had known in years. The following night passed equally well. My slumber was delightfully profound and utterly dreamless.

I pinned the charm to the breast of my nightdress; it was positioned too far away the previous evening. I am convinced it was all fancy, save for the dreams. I used to believe evil spirits generated nightmares, but our physician assured me otherwise. "And what do you suppose the charm actually is?" I queried.

"It has been fumigated or submerged in some potent drug; it serves as an antidote against malaria," she explained confidently.

"Certainly. Do you imagine evil spirits are cowed by mere ribbons or the perfumes of a druggist's shop? Had I been able to align completely with Carmilla's rationalizations, I might have found peace. Instead, I did my utmost to convince myself, and the initial impression of supernatural dread began to lose its potency. For several nights, I slept deeply. Yet, every morning brought the same crushing lethargy, a languor that weighed heavily upon me throughout the day. I felt transformed into a stranger. A strange melancholy began to steal over me, a sorrow I would not have interrupted. Vague thoughts of death started to unfurl, and the notion that I was slowly sinking took a gentle, strangely welcoming hold on my consciousness. If the mood was sad, the tone of mind it induced was sweet. Whatever the nature of this state, my soul acquiesced to it. I refused to admit I was ill, unwilling to consent to informing my father or summoning a doctor.

Carmilla, conversely, became more devoted than ever, her strange paroxysms of languid adoration growing increasingly frequent. She would gloat over me with mounting ardor as my strength and spirits waned. This behavior always shocked me, akin to witnessing a momentary flare of insanity. Unknowingly, I had entered an advanced stage of the most bizarre illness a mortal could suffer. There was an inexplicable fascination in its early symptoms that more than compensated for the incapacitating effects of the disease. This allure intensified for a time until it reached a tipping point, after which a sense of the horrific began to mingle with it, deepening until it discolored and perverted the entirety of my existence.

The first alteration I experienced was, paradoxically, agreeable. It marked the turning point from which the descent of Avernus commenced. Certain vague, strange sensations visited me during sleep. The predominant feeling was that pleasant, peculiar cold thrill we experience when bathing against the current of a river. Soon, this was accompanied by dreams that seemed interminable, so vague that I could never recall their scenery, inhabitants, or any coherent sequence of action. Yet, they left an awful impression, a sense of exhaustion as if I had endured a prolonged period of immense mental exertion and peril.

Following these dreams, upon waking, I retained a memory of being in a place nearly devoid of light, speaking to figures I could not see. Most distinctively, I remembered a clear, female voice—very deep—that spoke as if from a great distance, slowly, evoking a sensation of indescribable solemnity and fear. My heart would race, my breathing rising and falling rapidly and fully drawn. A sobbing ensued, escalating into a sense of strangulation, culminating in a dreadful convulsion wherein my senses fled, leaving me unconscious.

It had now been three weeks since the onset of this inexplicable state. My sufferings had taken their toll on my appearance during the final week. I had grown pale, my eyes dilated and shadowed beneath, while the languor I had long felt began to manifest visibly on my countenance. My father questioned me repeatedly regarding my health. With an obstinacy that now seems incomprehensible, I persisted in assuring him I was perfectly well. In a sense, this was true. I felt no pain and could identify no physical derangement. My complaint appeared to reside within the imagination or the nerves. Though my sufferings were horrific, I kept them, with a morbid reserve, almost entirely to myself.

It could not be the terrible ailment the peasants termed *oupir*, for I had been suffering for three weeks, whereas they rarely endured the misery beyond three days before death ended their torment. Carmilla complained of dreams and feverish sensations, but by no means of such alarming intensity as mine. I assert that my condition was extremely terrifying. Had I been capable of comprehending my state, I would have invoked aid and counsel on my knees. A narcotic influence, unsuspected by all, acted upon me, numbing my perceptions.

One night, instead of the voice I was accustomed to hearing in the darkness, I heard one sweet, tender, and simultaneously terrible, declaring, "Your mother warns you to beware of the assassin." The stream was prepared to be dragged; my father stood on the brink of collapse, terrified of the nightmare he would have to recount to the grieving mother upon her return. Then, the clock struck one. Still, no news. In a surge of instinct, I rushed to Carmilla's room. She stood before her dressing table, bathed in the pale morning light. By then, Madame, Mademoiselle, my father, and a throng of servants filled the room. I caught Carmilla's eye following him, tracking his movements with a sly, dark glint that unsettled me.

When the servants were finally dismissed, and Mademoiselle had sought a vial of valerian and sal volatile, leaving only my father, Madame, and myself with Carmilla, my father approached her gently. He took her hand, guided her to the sofa, and sat beside her.

"Will you forgive me, my dear, if I risk a conjecture and pose a question?" he asked softly.

"Who possesses a better right?" she replied immediately. "Ask whatever you wish, and I shall tell you everything. Yet my story is one of sheer bewilderment and darkness. I know absolutely nothing. Question me freely, but remember the strict limitations Mother has imposed regarding certain topics."

"Quite so, my dear child. I need not tread upon ground where silence is demanded. The marvel of last night lies in your removal from your bed and room without awakening, occurring while the windows were secured and the interior doors fastened. Let me offer my theory and ask a single question."

Carmilla leaned on her hand, dejected; Madame and I listened, breath held tight.

"Now, my question: Have you ever been suspected of somnambulism?"

"Never, since I was very young," she answered.

"But you *did* walk in your sleep when you were young?"

"Yes; I know I did. My old nurse told me so countless times."

My father smiled, nodding knowingly. "Very well. Here is what transpired. You arose in your sleep, unlocking the door—not leaving the key in the lock as custom dictates, but removing it and securing it from the outside. You took the key with you, carrying it to one of the twenty-five rooms on this floor, or perhaps ascending or descending stairs. This old house is a labyrinth of chambers, closets, and accumulated lumber; it would take a week to search it thoroughly. Do you see now?"

"I understand partially, but not fully," she admitted. "And how do you account for my discovery on the sofa in the dressing room, which we had already searched so meticulously?"

"She came there after you had finished searching, still asleep. Eventually, she awoke spontaneously, just as surprised to find herself there as any of us." My father laughed, a sound of pure relief. "Let us congratulate ourselves. The most natural explanation requires no drugging, no tampering with locks, no burglars, poisoners, or witches—nothing to alarm Carmilla or anyone else concerning our safety."

Carmilla looked radiant, her beauty seemingly enhanced by a graceful languor unique to her. I sensed my father silently comparing her features to my own, for he sighed, saying, "I wish my poor Laura looked more like herself."

Thus, our alarms were happily quelled, and Carmilla was restored to her circle of friends, the mystery dissolved by the simplest of explanations.

The atmosphere within the castle shifted palpably once the decision was made to deny Carmilla the solitude of her chamber. My father, driven by a paternal instinct that bordered on paranoia, arranged for a servant to sleep directly outside her door, creating a sentinel barrier designed to intercept any further nocturnal excursions before they could culminate in catastrophe. That night passed in a silence so profound it felt heavy, pressing against the walls. By dawn, before the light had fully claimed the room, the doctor arrived—a summons issued by my father without so much as a whisper to me.

Madame escorted me to the library, where the physician awaited. He was a figure of stark gravity, his white hair stark against the dim light, spectacles perched firmly on his nose. I recounted the terrors of the previous nights, and as my voice faltered, his expression hardened into a mask of somber intensity. We stood in the deep recess of a window, framed by stone and shadow, facing one another. When my confession ended, he leaned back, his shoulders resting against the cold wall, his eyes locking onto mine with a gaze that burned with a mixture of clinical interest and primal horror. After a moment of silent contemplation, he signaled for my father.

When my father entered, his customary smile was already in place, a defensive shield against whatever dread lay ahead. "I dare say, doctor," he began, his tone light despite the gravity of the summons, "you are going to tell me that I am an old fool for having brought you here; I hope I am." But the smile died instantly, swallowed by the shadow of the doctor's stern countenance as he gestured imperiously. For some time, the two men engaged in a hushed, fervent debate within that same window niche. From afar, where Madame and I stood burning with anxious curiosity, we could see nothing but the faint outline of my father's arm and shoulder, obscured by the thick masonry. Their voices were reduced to indistinguishable whispers, muffled by the acoustic prison of the alcove.

Finally, my father's face emerged into the main room, transformed. It was pale, etched with thought, and undeniably agitated. "Laura, dear, come here for a moment," he commanded softly. "Madame, we shan't trouble you; the doctor requires privacy." Trembling with a weakness that defied reason—I felt drained, yet strangely lucid—I approached. My father extended his hand, but his gaze remained fixed on the physician. "It certainly is very odd," he admitted, his voice tight. "I don't understand it quite. Laura, listen to Doctor Spielsberg, and compose yourself."

"You mentioned a sensation," the doctor intoned, his voice devoid of warmth, "like two needles piercing the skin, somewhere about your neck, on the night of your first nightmare. Is there still any soreness?"

"None at all," I replied, trying to steady my breath.

"Can you indicate with your finger the precise point?"

"Very little below my throat—here," I answered, lifting my sleeve. I wore a morning dress that covered the area, but the fabric parted easily.

"Now you can satisfy yourself," the doctor said, his tone dropping to a conspiratorial urgency. "You won't mind your papa lowering your dress a very little. It is necessary to detect the symptom."

My father hesitated, then complied. The fabric fell just an inch or two below the collarbone. A collective gasp seemed to fill the room. "God bless me!" my father exclaimed, his face draining of color. "So it is."

"You see it now with your own eyes," the doctor declared, a grim triumph coloring his features.

"What is it?" I cried, panic rising in my chest.

"Nothing, my dear young lady," he lied smoothly, "but a small blue spot, about the size of the tip of your little finger." He turned sharply to my father. "The question is, what is best to be done? Is there any danger?"

"I urge you," I pleaded, my voice trembling, "to tell me if there is danger."

"I trust not, my dear," the doctor answered, his eyes darting between us. "I don't see why you should not recover. In fact, I see every reason for you to begin improving immediately. That is the exact point where the sense of strangulation begins, isn't it?"

"Yes," I whispered.

"And recall," he pressed, "that same point was the center of that thrill you described earlier, like the current of a cold stream running against you?"

"It may have been; I think it was."

"Ay, you see?" he muttered, addressing my father. "Shall I speak to Madame?"

"Certainly," my father said, his composure cracking. He summoned Madame forward. "I find my young friend here far from well. It won't be of great consequence, I hope, but immediate steps are required. For the present, Madame, you will ensure Miss Laura is not left alone for even a single moment. That is the only instruction I need give, and it is indispensable."

"We may rely upon your kindness, Madame, I know," my father added, his voice strained.

Madame nodded eagerly, her relief evident. "And you, dear Laura, I know you will obey the doctor's directions."

"I shall have to ask your opinion upon another patient," the doctor interjected, shifting tactics. "A young lady, our guest, whose symptoms slightly resemble those of my daughter—much milder in degree, but undoubtedly of the same sort. Since you are scheduled to pass this way again this evening, you cannot do better than take your supper here. You will see her then. She does not descend until the afternoon."

"I thank you," the doctor said curtly. "I shall be with you at about seven this evening."

With these final instructions delivered to both of us, my father departed. I watched him walk out with the doctor, their strides synchronized as they paced up and down the grassy platform between the road and the moat, completely absorbed in their urgent discourse. The doctor eventually mounted his horse, took his leave, and rode eastward through the forest, disappearing into the trees.

Almost simultaneously, a rider arrived from Dranfield bearing a bag of letters for my father. While the household buzzed with speculation regarding the doctor's ominous decree—that we feared a sudden seizure that could cost me my life—or perhaps, as Madame later suggested, a precautionary measure to curb youthful indiscretions like overexertion or unripe fruit, my father returned. He carried a letter in his hand.

"This letter had been delayed," he announced, his demeanor sour. "It is from General Spielsdorf. He might have been here yesterday, or tomorrow, or perhaps today." He thrust the open letter into my hands. Yet, there was no joy in his delivery. If anything, he looked as though he wished the General were lost at sea. Something weighed heavily on his mind, a secret he refused to divulge.

"Papa, darling, will you tell me this?" I implored, seizing his arm and looking up with wide, pleading eyes.

"Perhaps," he answered, smoothing my hair over my forehead with a gesture that felt increasingly mechanical. "Does the doctor think me very ill?"

"No, dear," he replied, his voice dry and brittle. "He thinks, if the right steps are taken, you will be quite well again, at least on the high road to a complete recovery, in a day or two."

"I wish our good friend, the General, had chosen any other time," he continued, avoiding my gaze. "That is, I wish you had been perfectly well to receive him."

"But do tell me, papa," I insisted, my frustration mounting. "What does he think is the matter with me?"

"Nothing," he snapped, his irritation flaring brighter than I had ever witnessed. "You must not plague me with questions." Seeing my wounded expression, he softened slightly, kissing my brow. "You shall know all about it in a day or two; that is, all that I know. In the meantime, you are not to trouble your head about it."

He turned to leave but returned moments later, his resolve hardening. "I am going to Karnstein," he stated abruptly. "I have ordered the carriage to be ready at twelve. You and Madame will accompany me. I am going to see the priest who lives near those picturesque grounds upon business. Since Carmilla has never seen them, she may follow when she comes down, accompanied by Mademoiselle, who will bring materials for what you call a picnic, which we shall lay out in the ruined castle."

At twelve o'clock, prepared and anxious, we set out. We crossed the drawbridge, turned right, and followed the road over the steep Gothic bridge, heading westward toward the deserted village and the looming silhouette of the ruined castle of Karnstein. No sylvan drive could be imagined more picturesque. The ground broke into gentle hills and hollows, draped in wild, untamed woods that lacked the stiff formality of cultivated gardens. The terrain forced the road to wind unpredictably, snaking around broken hollows and climbing the steep slopes of the hills, a path of natural irregularity.

As we rounded a bend, we suddenly encountered our old friend, the General, riding toward us, attended by a mounted servant. His portmanteaus trailed behind in a hired wagon. The General dismounted as we pulled up. After exchanging the usual greetings, he was easily persuaded to abandon his horse and accept the vacant seat in the carriage, sending his servant and the horses onward to the *schloss*.

It had been roughly ten months since we last encountered him, yet that span of time had wrought an alteration equivalent to years upon his visage. He had wasted away; the cordial serenity that once defined his features had been supplanted by a heavy cloak of gloom and anxiety. His dark blue eyes, invariably piercing, now burned with a sterner, harder light beneath his shaggy grey brows. This was not the hollowing effect of ordinary grief alone; darker, angrier passions seemed to have carved their share of the damage.

We had not driven far when the General began to speak, employing his customary soldierly directness to address the bereavement he had endured following the death of his beloved niece and ward. Suddenly, he erupted in a tone of intense bitterness and fury, railing against the "hellish arts" to which she had succumbed. With more exasperation than piety, he voiced his astonishment that Heaven would tolerate such a monstrous indulgence of the lusts and malignity of hell.

My father, perceiving immediately that something extraordinary had befallen the man, asked him, if the recounting was not too painful, to detail the circumstances that warranted such vehement language.

"I should tell you all with pleasure," the General replied, "but you would not believe me."

"Why should I not?" my father pressed.

"Because," he answered testily, "you believe in nothing save what accords with your own prejudices and illusions. I remember when I was like you, but I have learned better."

"Try me," my father countered. "I am not such a dogmatist as you suppose. Furthermore, I know full well that you generally demand proof for your convictions; thus, I am strongly predisposed to respect your conclusions."

"You are correct in assuming I have not been led lightly into a belief in the marvelous—for what I have experienced *is* marvelous—and I have been compelled by extraordinary evidence to credit that which runs diametrically opposed to all my theories. I have been made the dupe of a preternatural conspiracy."

Notwithstanding his professions of confidence in the General's own insight, I observed my father cast a glance at him that, in my estimation, bore a marked suspicion of his sanity. Fortunately, the General failed to notice it. Instead, he stared gloomily and curiously into the glades and vistas of the woods unfolding before us.

"You are heading to the Ruins of Karnstein?" he asked.

"Yes, it is a fortunate coincidence; indeed, I was intending to request that you escort me there to inspect them. I possess a specific objective in this exploration. There is a ruined chapel, is there not, containing numerous tombs of that extinct family?"

"So there are—highly interesting," my father confirmed. "Do you intend to claim the title and estates?"

My father spoke this gaily, yet the General appeared oblivious to the laugh or the smile that courtesy demands of a friend's jest. On the contrary, he looked grave, even fierce, ruminating on a matter that stirred both his anger and his horror.

"Something very different," he growled. "I mean to unearth some of those fine people. I hope, by God's blessing, to accomplish a pious sacrilege here, thereby relieving our earth of certain monsters and enabling honest folk to sleep in their beds unassailed by murderers. I have strange tales to impart to you, my dear friend, stories that I myself would have scoffed at as incredible mere months ago."

My father regarded him again, but this time not with suspicion, but with an eye of keen intelligence and alarm.

"The house of Karnstein," he stated, "has been extinct for generations—at least a century. My dear wife was maternally descended from the Karnsteins. Yet the name and title have long ceased to exist. The castle stands in ruins; the very village is deserted. It has been fifty years since the smoke of a chimney was seen there; not a single roof remains standing."

"Quite true. I have heard much regarding that since our last meeting; much that will astonish you. However, I deem it best to relate everything in the chronological order of its occurrence," the General said.

"You saw my dear ward—my child, I may call her. No creature could have been more beautiful, and only three months prior, none more blooming."

"Yes, poor thing! When I saw her last, she was certainly quite lovely," my father agreed. "I was grieved and shocked beyond measure, my dear friend; I knew precisely how devastating a blow this was to you." He took the General's hand, and they exchanged a kind, firm pressure. Tears gathered in the old soldier's eyes, and he made no attempt to conceal them.

"We have been very old friends," he continued, his voice thick. "I knew you would feel for me, childless as I am. She had become an object of very near interest to me, and she repaid my care with an affection that cheered my home and made my life happy. All of that is gone. The years remaining to me on this earth may not be many; but by God's mercy, I hope to accomplish a service to mankind before I die, and to subserve the vengeance of Heaven upon the fiends who have murdered my poor child in the spring of her hopes and beauty!"

"You stated earlier that you intended to relate everything as it occurred," my father reminded him gently. "Please do; I assure you that it is not mere curiosity that prompts me."

By this juncture, we had reached the divergence where the Drunstall road, by which the General had arrived, split from the path we were traversing toward Karnstein.

"How far is it to the ruins?" the General inquired, peering anxiously ahead.

"Approximately half a league," my father answered.

"Then pray, let us hear the story you were so gracious as to promise."

"With all my heart," the General rasped, the words tearing from his throat with visible effort. After a brief, agonizing pause to rearrange the fractured pieces of his narrative, he launched into one of the strangest tales I have ever heard. "My dearest child was looking forward with immense anticipation to the visit you had so graciously arranged for her to meet your charming daughter." He offered me a bow that was simultaneously gallant and steeped in a profound melancholy. "Meanwhile, we received an invitation from my old friend, Count Carlsfeld, whose castle lies roughly six leagues beyond Karnstein. We were summoned to witness the series of festivities he hosted in honor of his illustrious guest, the Grand Duke Charles."

"Yes; and I believe they were truly splendid," my father interjected. "Princely! Yet his hospitality is quite regal. He possesses something akin to Aladdin's lamp."

"The night from which my sorrow originated was consumed by a magnificent masquerade," the General continued, his voice dropping to a reverent whisper. "The grounds were cast open, the trees draped in cascades of colored lanterns. There was a display of fireworks that Paris itself had never witnessed. And the music... music, you understand, is my greatest weakness. Such ravishing melodies! The finest instrumental ensemble the world could possibly muster, alongside the most exquisite singers gathered from the great opera houses of Europe. As you wandered through these fantastically illuminated grounds, the moonlight filtering through the chateau's long rows of windows casting a rosy glow, you would suddenly hear those ravishing voices stealing from the silence of hidden groves or rising from the water upon the lake. In that moment, looking and listening, I felt myself transported back into the romance and poetry of my earliest youth."

"When the fireworks faded and the ball began, we retreated to the noble suites of rooms thrown open to the dancers. A masked ball is a beautiful sight in itself, but I had never before beheld such a brilliant spectacle. It was an assembly of pure aristocracy; I was nearly the only 'nobody' present. My dear child looked radiant. She wore no mask. Her excitement and delight added an indescribable charm to her features, which were already lovely. I noticed a young lady dressed magnificently, yet concealed behind a mask, who appeared to be observing my ward with extraordinary intensity. I had seen her earlier in the great hall, and again, for a few moments, walking near us beneath the castle windows, similarly engrossed. Accompanying her was another lady, also masked, richly and gravely attired, possessing a stately air reminiscent of high rank, serving as her chaperone. Had the young lady not worn her veil, I could certainly have been far more certain regarding whether she was truly watching my poor darling. Now, I am fully assured that she was."

"We had moved into one of the salons. My poor child, having danced extensively, rested briefly in a chair near the entrance; I stood nearby. The two ladies I had described approached, and the younger one took the seat directly beside my ward, while her companion remained standing beside me, addressing her charge in a hushed tone. Availing herself of the anonymity granted by her mask, she turned to me, adopting the tone of an old friend and addressing me by name. This opened a conversation that piqued my curiosity immensely. She referenced numerous scenes where we had crossed paths—at Court, at distinguished households. She alluded to trivial incidents I had long ceased to recall, yet which, I discovered, had merely lain dormant in my memory, springing instantly to life at her touch.

"I grew increasingly desperate to ascertain her identity, every passing moment. She deflected my attempts to uncover her secret with remarkable adroitness and pleasantness. The knowledge she displayed regarding various passages of my life seemed almost inexplicable, and she appeared to derive a natural, almost malicious pleasure in frustrating my curiosity, watching me flounder from one conjecture to another in my eager perplexity. Meanwhile, the young lady, whom her mother occasionally addressed by the peculiar name of Millarca, engaged my ward with the same ease and grace. She introduced herself by claiming her mother was a very old acquaintance of mine. She spoke of the agreeable audacity that masks render possible; she conversed like a confidante, admiring her dress and insinuating, with charming subtlety, her admiration of her beauty. She amused her with witty critiques of the crowd flooding the ballroom and laughed at my poor child's jests. When she chose, she was incredibly witty and lively. Eventually, they became fast friends, and the young stranger lowered her mask, revealing a face of remarkable loveliness. Neither I nor my dear child had ever seen it before. Though unfamiliar to us, the features possessed such an engaging radiance that it was impossible not to feel the attraction powerfully. My poor girl fell for her instantly. I have rarely witnessed anyone so captivated at first sight, unless perhaps it was the stranger herself, who seemed to have completely lost her heart to her."

"In the interim, availing myself of the liberties of a masquerade, I directed several questions to the elder lady. 'You have utterly puzzled me,' I said, laughing. 'Is that not enough? Will you not now consent to stand on equal footing and do me the kindness of removing your mask?'

'Can any request be more unreasonable?' she replied coolly. 'To ask a lady to yield an advantage! Besides, how do you know you would recognize me? Years bring change.'

'As you see,' I answered with a bow and a rather melancholy little laugh.

'As philosophers tell us,' she countered. 'And how do you know that a glimpse of my face would assist you?'

'I should take my chances for that,' I responded. 'It is futile to try to pass yourself off as an old woman; your figure betrays you.'

'Nevertheless, years have passed since I last saw you, and rather since you saw me, for that is what concerns me. Millarca there is my daughter; I cannot therefore be considered young, even in the eyes of those whom time has taught to be indulgent. Moreover, I may not relish being compared to what you remember me to be. You possess no mask to remove. You can offer me nothing in exchange.'

'My petition appeals to your pity; remove it.'

'And mine appeals to yours; let it remain where it is,' she retorted.

'Well, then, at least you will tell me if you are French or German; you command both languages with perfection.'

'I do not think I shall disclose that, General. You intend a surprise and are meditating a specific point of attack.'

'At all events, you will not deny this,' I pressed, 'that being honored by your permission to converse, I ought to know how to address you. Shall I say Madame la Comtesse?'

She laughed, and undoubtedly would have met me with another evasion—if, indeed, I can regard any occurrence in an interview, every circumstance of which I now believe was prearranged with the profoundest cunning, as liable to modification by mere accident.

'As to that,' she began, but was cut off almost immediately by a gentleman dressed in black. He looked particularly elegant and distinguished, save for one drawback: his face was the most deadly pale I had ever witnessed, save perhaps in death. He wore no costume, only the plain evening attire of a gentleman. Without a smile, yet with a courtly and unusually low bow, he declared: 'Will Madame la Comtesse permit me to say a very few words which may interest her?'

The lady turned swiftly to him, touching her lip in a token of silence. Then she said to me, 'Keep my place for me, General; I shall return when I have spoken a few words.'

With this playful injunction, she stepped aside with the gentleman in black, talking for several minutes with apparent earnestness. They then walked slowly away together into the throng, and I lost sight of them for a few moments.

I spent the interval beating my brains against the wall of mystery concerning the identity of the lady who remembered me so kindly. I contemplated turning to join the conversation between my pretty ward and the Countess's daughter, attempting to gather her name, title, chateau, and estates by the time she returned, thereby securing a surprise for her. But just then, she reappeared, accompanied by the pale man in black, who stated: 'I shall return and inform Madame la Comtesse when her carriage arrives at the door.' He withdrew with a bow."

"Then we are to lose Madame la Comtesse, but I hope only for a few hours," I said, bowing low.

"It may be that only, or it may be a few weeks," she replied, her voice carrying a sudden, electric tension. "It was very unlucky his speaking to me just now as he did. Do you now know me?"

"I assured her I did not."

"You shall know me," she said, "but not at present. We are older and better friends than, perhaps, you suspect. I cannot yet declare myself. In three weeks I shall pass your beautiful *schloss*, about which I have been making enquiries. Then I shall look in upon you for an hour or two, and renew a friendship which I never think of without a thousand pleasant recollections. This moment, however, a piece of news has reached me like a thunderbolt. I must set out now, and travel by a devious route, nearly a hundred miles, with all the dispatch I can possibly make. My perplexities multiply. I am only deterred by the compulsory reserve I practice as to my name from making a very singular request of you. My poor child has not quite recovered her strength. Her horse fell with her, at a hunt which she had ridden out to witness; her nerves have not yet recovered the shock, and our physician says that she must on no account exert herself for some time to come. We came here, in consequence, by very easy stages—hardly six leagues a day. I must now travel day and night, on a mission of life and death—a mission the critical and momentous nature of which I shall be able to explain to you when we meet, as I hope we shall, in a few weeks, without the necessity of any concealment."

She went on to make her petition, and it was in the tone of a person from whom such a request amounted to conferring a favor, rather than seeking one. This was only in manner, and, as it seemed, quite unconsciously. In the terms in which it was expressed, nothing could be more deprecatory. It was simply that I would consent to take charge of her daughter during her absence.

This was, all things considered, a strange, not to say, an audacious request. She in some sort disarmed me, by stating and admitting everything that could be urged against it, and throwing herself entirely upon my chivalry. At the same moment, by a fatality that seems to have predetermined all that happened, my poor child came to my side, and, in an undertone, besought me to invite her new friend, Millarca, to pay us a visit. She had just been sounding her, and thought, if her mamma would allow her, she would like it extremely.

At another time I should have told her to wait a little, until, at least, we knew who they were. But I had not a moment to think in. The two ladies assailed me together, and I must confess the refined and beautiful face of the young lady, about which there was something extremely engaging, as well as the elegance and fire of high birth, determined me; and, quite overpowered, I submitted, and undertook, too easily, the care of the young lady, whom her mother called Millarca.

The Countess beckoned to her daughter, who listened with grave attention while she told her, in general terms, how suddenly and peremptorily she had been summoned, and also of the arrangement she had made for her under my care, adding that I was one of her earliest and most valued friends.

I made, of course, such speeches as the case seemed to call for, and found myself, on reflection, in a position which I did not half like.

The gentleman in black returned, and very ceremoniously conducted the lady from the room. The demeanor of this gentleman was such as to impress me with the conviction that the Countess was a lady of very much more importance than her modest title alone might have led me to assume.

Her last charge to me was that no attempt was to be made to learn more about her than I might have already guessed, until her return. Our distinguished host, whose guest she was, knew her reasons.

"But here," she said, "neither I nor my daughter could safely remain for more than a day. I removed my mask imprudently for a moment, about an hour ago, and, too late, I fancied you saw me. So I resolved to seek an opportunity of talking a little to you. Had I found that you had seen me, I would have thrown myself on your high sense of honor to keep my secret some weeks. As it is, I am satisfied that you did not see me; but if you now suspect, or, on reflection, should suspect, who I am, I commit myself, in like manner, entirely to your honor. My daughter will observe the same secrecy, and I well know that you will, from time to time, remind her, lest she should thoughtlessly disclose it."

She whispered a few words to her daughter, kissed her hurriedly twice, and went away, accompanied by the pale gentleman in black, and disappeared in the crowd.

"In the next room," said Millarca, "there is a window that looks upon the hall door. I should like to see the last of mamma, and to kiss my hand to her."

We assented, of course, and accompanied her to the window. We looked out, and saw a handsome old-fashioned carriage, with a troop of couriers and footmen. We saw the slim figure of the pale gentleman in black, as he held a thick velvet cloak, and placed it about her shoulders and threw the hood over her head. She nodded to him, and just touched his hand with hers. He bowed low repeatedly as the door closed, and the carriage began to move.

"She is gone," said Millarca, with a sigh.

"She is gone," I repeated to myself, for the first time—in the hurried moments that had elapsed since my consent—reflecting upon the folly of my act.

"She did not look up," said the young lady, plaintively.

"The Countess had taken off her mask, perhaps, and did not care to show her face," I said; "and she could not know that you were in the window."

She sighed, and looked in my face. She was so beautiful that I relented. I was sorry I had for a moment repented of my hospitality, and I determined to make her amends for the unavowed churlishness of my reception.

The young lady, replacing her mask, joined my ward in persuading me to return to the grounds, where the concert was soon to be renewed. We did so, and walked up and down the terrace that lies under the castle windows. Millarca became very intimate with us, and amused us with lively descriptions and stories of most of the great people whom we saw upon the terrace. I liked her more and more every minute. Her gossip, without being ill-natured, was extremely diverting to me, who had been so long out of the great world. I thought what life she would give to our sometimes lonely evenings at home.

This ball was not over until the morning sun had almost reached the horizon. It pleased the Grand Duke to dance till then, so loyal people could not go away, or think of bed.

We had just got through a crowded saloon, when my ward asked me what had become of Millarca. I thought she had been by her side, and she fancied she was by mine. The fact was, we had lost her.

All my efforts to find her were vain. I feared that she had mistaken, in the confusion of a momentary separation from us, other people for her new friends, and had, possibly, pursued and lost them in the extensive grounds which were thrown open to us. Now, in its full force, I recognized a new folly in my having undertaken the charge of a young lady without so much as knowing her name; and fettered as I was by promises, of the reasons for imposing which I knew nothing, I could not even point my inquiries by saying that the missing young lady was the daughter of the Countess who had taken her departure a few hours before.

Morning broke. It was clear daylight before I gave up my search. It was not till near two o'clock next day that we heard anything of my missing charge.

At about that time a servant knocked at my niece's door, to say that he had been earnestly requested by a young lady, who appeared to be in great distress, to make out where she could find the General Baron Spielsdorf and the young lady his daughter, in whose charge she had been left by her mother.

There could be no doubt, notwithstanding the slight inaccuracy, that our young friend had turned up; and so she had. Would to heaven we had lost her!

She told my poor child a story to account for her having failed to recover us for so long. Very late, she said, she had got to the housekeeper's bedroom in despair of finding us, and had then fallen into a deep sleep which, long as it was, had hardly sufficed to recruit her strength after the fatigues of the ball.

That day Millarca came home with us. I was only too happy, after all, to have secured so charming a companion for my dear girl.

"There soon, however, appeared some drawbacks. In the first place, Millarca complained of extreme languor—the weakness that remained after her late illness—and she never emerged from her room till the afternoon was pretty far advanced. In the next place, it was accidentally discovered, although she always locked her door on the inside, and never disturbed the key from its place till she admitted the maid to assist at her toilet, that she was undoubtedly sometimes absent from her room in the very early morning, and at various times later in the day, before she wished it to be understood that she was stirring. She was repeatedly seen from the windows of the schloss, in the first faint grey of the morning, walking through the trees, in an easterly direction, and looking like a person in a trance. This convinced me that she walked in her sleep. But this hypothesis did not solve the puzzle. How did she pass out from her room, leaving the door locked on the inside? How did she escape from the house without unbarring door or window?"

"In the midst of my perplexities, an anxiety of a far more urgent kind presented itself. 'My dear child began to lose her looks and health, and that in a manner so mysterious, and even horrible, that I became thoroughly frightened.' 'She was at first visited by appalling dreams; then, as she fancied, by a specter, sometimes resembling Millarca, sometimes in the shape of a beast, indistinctly seen, walking round the foot of her bed, from side to side. Lastly came sensations. One, not unpleasant, but very peculiar, she said, resembled the flow of an icy stream against her breast. At a later time, she felt something like a pair of large needles pierce her, a little below the throat, with a very sharp pain. A few nights after, followed a gradual and convulsive sense of strangulation; then came unconsciousness.'"

I could hear distinctly every word the kind old General was saying, because by this time we were driving upon the short grass that spreads on either side of the road as you approach the roofless village which had not shown the smoke of a chimney for more than half a century. You may guess how strangely I felt as I heard my own symptoms so exactly described in those which had been experienced by the poor girl who, but for the catastrophe which followed, would have been at that moment a visitor at my father's chateau. You may suppose, also, how I felt as I heard him detail habits and mysterious peculiarities which were, in fact, those of our beautiful guest, Carmilla!

A vista opened in the forest; we were on a sudden under the chimneys and gables of the ruined village, and the towers and battlements of the dismantled castle, round which gigantic trees are grouped, overhung us from a slight eminence. "And this was once the palatial residence of the Karnsteins!" said the old General at length, as from a great window he looked out across the village, and saw the wide, undulating expanse of forest. The squared block of wood, which lay on the grass-grown pavement of the chapel, formed a bench on which I was very glad to seat myself, and in the meantime the General called to the woodman, who had been removing some boughs which leaned upon the old walls; and, axe in hand, the hardy old fellow stood before us. He could not tell us anything of these monuments; but there was an old man, he said, a ranger of this forest, at present sojourning in the house of the priest, about two miles away, who could point out every monument of the old Karnstein family; and, for a trifle, he undertook to bring him back with him, if we would lend him one of our horses, in little more than half an hour.

"Have you been long employed about this forest?" asked my father of the old man.

"I have been a woodman here," he answered in his patois, "under the forester, all my days; so has my father before me, and so on, as many generations as I can count up. I could show you the very house in the village here, in which my ancestors lived."

"How came the village to be deserted?" asked the General.

"It was troubled by revenants, sir; several were tracked to their graves, there detected by the usual tests, and extinguished in the usual way, by decapitation, by the stake, and by burning; but not until many of the villagers were killed."

"But after all these proceedings according to law," he continued—"so many graves opened, and so many vampires deprived of their horrible animation—the village was not relieved. He did so thus: There being a bright moon that night, he ascended, shortly after sunset, the towers of the chapel here, from whence he could distinctly see the churchyard beneath him; you can see it from that window. From this point he watched until he saw the vampire come out of his grave, and place near it the linen clothes in which he had been folded, and then glide away towards the village to plague its inhabitants."

"My beloved child," he resumed, his voice trembling with a mixture of grief and exhaustion, "was now growing rapidly worse. This consultation, then, left me precisely where I was. I walked out into the grounds, all but distracted. The doctor from Gratz, in ten or fifteen minutes, overtook me. He apologized for having followed me, but said that he could not conscientiously take his leave without a few words more. He told me that he could not be mistaken; no natural disease exhibited the same symptoms; and that death was already very near. There remained, however, a day, or possibly two, of life. If the fatal seizure were at once arrested, with great care and skill her strength might possibly return. But all hung now upon the confines of the irrevocable. One more assault might extinguish the last spark of vitality which is, every moment, ready to die. 'And what is the nature of the seizure you speak of?' I entreated. 'I have stated all fully in this note, which I place in your hands upon the distinct condition that you send for the nearest clergyman, and open my letter in his presence, and on no account read it till he is with you; you would despise it else, and it is a matter of life and death. Should the priest fail you, then, indeed, you may read it.' The ecclesiastic was absent, and I read the letter by myself. At another time, or in another case, it might have excited my ridicule. But into what quackeries will not people rush for a last chance, where all accustomed means have failed, and the life of a beloved object is at stake? Nothing, you will say, could be more absurd than the learned man's letter. It was monstrous enough to have consigned him to a madhouse. He said that the patient was suffering from the visits of a vampire! The punctures which she described as having occurred near the throat, were, he insisted, the insertion of those two long, thin, and sharp teeth which, it is well known, are peculiar to vampires; and there could be no doubt, he added, as to the well-defined presence of the small livid mark which all concurred in describing as that induced by the demon's lips, and every symptom described by the sufferer was in exact conformity with those recorded in every case of a similar visitation. Being myself wholly skeptical as to the existence of any such portent as the vampire, the supernatural theory of the good doctor furnished, in my opinion, but another instance of learning and intelligence oddly associated with some one hallucination. I was so miserable, however, that, rather than try nothing, I acted upon the instructions of the letter. I concealed myself in the dark dressing room, that opened upon the poor patient's room, in which a candle was burning, and watched there till she was fast asleep. I stood at the door, peeping through the small crevice, my sword laid on the table beside me, as my directions prescribed, until, a little after one, I saw a large black object, very ill-defined, crawl, as it seemed to me, over the foot of the bed, and swiftly spread itself up to the poor girl's throat, where it swelled, in a moment, into a great, palpitating mass. For a few moments I had stood petrified. I now sprang forward, with my sword in my hand. But her victim was sinking fast, and before the morning dawned, she died." The old General was agitated. We did not speak to him. The General leaned against the wall, dried his eyes, and sighed heavily. I was relieved on hearing the voices of Carmilla and Madame, who were at that moment approaching. The voices died away. In this solitude, having just listened to so strange a story, connected, as it was, with the great and titled dead, whose monuments were moldering among the dust and ivy round us, and every incident of which bore so awfully upon my own mysterious case—in this haunted spot, darkened by the towering foliage that rose on every side, dense and high above its noiseless walls—a horror began to steal over me, and my heart sank as I thought that my friends were, after all, not about to enter and disturb this triste and ominous scene. The old General's eyes were fixed on the ground, as he leaned with his hand upon the basement of a shattered monument. Under a narrow, arched doorway, surmounted by one of those demoniacal grotesques in which the cynical and ghastly fancy of old Gothic carving delights, I saw very gladly the beautiful face and figure of Carmilla enter the shadowy chapel. I was just about to rise and speak, and nodded smiling, in answer to her peculiarly engaging smile; when with a cry, the old man by my side caught up the woodman's hatchet, and started forward. On seeing him a brutalized change came over her features. It was an instantaneous and horrible transformation, as she made a crouching step backwards. Before I could utter a scream, he struck at her with all his force, but she dived under his blow, and unscathed, caught him in her tiny grasp by the wrist. He struggled for a moment to release his arm, but his hand opened, the axe fell to the ground, and the girl was gone. He staggered against the wall. His grey hair stood upon his head, and a moisture shone over his face, as if he were at the point of death. The frightful scene had passed in a moment. The first thing I recollect after, is Madame standing before me, and impatiently repeating again and again, the question, "Where is Mademoiselle Carmilla?" I answered at length, "I don't know—I can't tell—she went there," and I pointed to the door through which Madame had just entered; "only a minute or two since." "But I have been standing there, in the passage, ever since Mademoiselle Carmilla entered; and she did not return." She then began to call "Carmilla," through every door and passage and from the windows, but no answer came. "She called herself Carmilla?" asked the General, still agitated. "Carmilla, yes," I answered. "Aye," he said; "that is Millarca. That is the same person who long ago was called Mircalla, Countess Karnstein. Depart from this accursed ground, my poor child, as quickly as you can. Drive to the clergyman's house, and stay there till we come. Begone! May you never behold Carmilla more; you will not find her here."

As the words fell from his lips, the chapel doors swung open, admitting a figure so strikingly anomalous that my breath caught in my throat. He was a man of towering height yet unnervingly narrow-chested, his posture permanently hunched beneath shoulders that seemed to bear the weight of centuries. Clad in somber black, he wore a hat of peculiar shape, crowned with a broad, unnatural leaf. His skin was the color of aged earth, etched with deep, dry furrows, while long, grizzled hair cascaded over his shoulders like a tangle of winter grass. Gold-rimmed spectacles perched upon his nose, magnifying eyes that darted between the vaulted ceiling and the dusty floor. He moved with a slow, shuffling gait, his long, thin arms swinging wildly in empty space; his hands, encased in oversized black gloves, gestured with a frantic, abstract energy. A perpetual, rictus-like smile twisted his features.

"The very man!" the General cried, stepping forward with a radiance that banished the gloom of the crypt. "My dear Baron, what joy to behold you! I dared not hope to find you so swiftly." He signaled to my father, who had just re-entered the nave, and ushered the eccentric elder—the Baron—as if he were royalty. Formal introductions were exchanged, and immediately, the three of them descended into a fervent discourse. The stranger withdrew a rolled document from his coat and unspooled it across the weathered stone of a nearby tomb. From a leather case, he extracted a pencil, sketching imaginary lines across the parchment that connected various points within the chapel's architecture. I deduced, watching his erratic glances back and forth between the map and the walls, that he was drafting a tactical plan of the sanctuary. Occasionally, he consulted a small, filthy tome with yellowed, ink-stained pages, reading passages aloud to punctuate his lecture.

Together, they sauntered down the side aisle, directly opposite my vantage point, their voices weaving a tapestry of conspiracy. They began pacing out distances, counting steps with military precision, until they halted before a section of the sidewall. Finally, they uncovered a broad marble slab, its surface bearing letters carved in high relief. Though the General was not typically a man of prayer, he raised his hands and eyes to the heavens in silent, tearless gratitude.

"Tomorrow," I heard him declare, his voice thick with emotion, "the commissioner shall arrive, and the Inquisition will proceed according to the strictures of the law." Turning to the Baron, whose gold spectacles reflected the flickering candlelight, the General seized his hands in a warm, brotherly grip. "Baron, how can I repay you? How can we all express our debt? You have liberated this region from a plague that has ravaged its inhabitants for over a century. Thank God, the hideous enemy is finally cornered."

My father guided the stranger aside, and the General followed, their voices dropping to urgent whispers. I knew they had withdrawn beyond my earshot to discuss my specific case, for I watched them cast furtive, rapid glances in my direction as their conversation intensified. When my father returned, he embraced me repeatedly, kissing my forehead with a tenderness that bordered on desperation. Leading me away from the chapel, he whispered, "It is time to depart, but before we reach home, we must summon the good priest, who resides but a short distance hence. We must persuade him to join our expedition to the castle." Our mission succeeded; the priest agreed, and I was immensely relieved to leave the sacred grounds, utterly exhausted. Yet, my relief curdled instantly into dread upon realizing that there was no word regarding Carmilla. No explanation was offered for the chaotic scene in the ruined chapel; it was evident that my father intended to withhold this truth from me for the present. The sinister absence of Carmilla rendered the memory of the discovery far more horrific than if she had been found.

The preparations for the coming night were singular and severe. Two servants and Madame were to remain awake in my chamber, while the clergyman and my father maintained vigil in the adjacent dressing room. The priest had performed certain solemn rites that evening, the meaning of which remained as obscure to me as the rationale behind these extraordinary precautions designed to ensure my safety during sleep. Only a few days later did the full significance of these actions become clear to me. The disappearance of Carmilla coincided with the abrupt cessation of my nightly torments.

You are undoubtedly familiar with the appalling superstitions that permeate Upper and Lower Styria, Moravia, Silesia, Turkish Serbia, Poland, and even Russia—the pervasive belief in the Vampire. If human testimony, gathered with meticulous care and solemnity before countless judicial commissions composed of men renowned for their integrity and intellect, constitutes valid evidence, then it becomes difficult to deny, let alone doubt, the existence of such a phenomenon. For my part, having witnessed and experienced exactly what I describe, I possess no alternative theory to explain my ordeal save the ancient, well-attested beliefs of my homeland.

The following day, the formal proceedings commenced within the Chapel of Karnstein. The grave of the Countess Mircalla was breached. Both the General and my father stared in stunned recognition at the face now exposed to the air, identifying their treacherous and beautiful guest. Although a century and a half had elapsed since her interment, her features retained the deceptive warmth of life. Her eyes were wide open; no putrid odor emanated from the coffin. Two physicians—one official, the other representing the prosecutor—attested to the miraculous nature of the event: a faint yet perceptible respiration, coupled with a corresponding beat of the heart. Her limbs were supple, her flesh elastic, and the leaden coffin floated in a pool of blood, in which her body lay submerged to a depth of seven inches. Thus, all the canonical signs and proofs of vampirism were confirmed.

In accordance with ancient custom, the body was raised, and a sharpened stake was driven forcefully through the vampire's heart. At that precise moment, she emitted a piercing shriek, indistinguishable from the final agony of any living soul. Immediately thereafter, her head was severed, unleashing a torrent of blood that soaked the stones below. The corpse and the detached head were subsequently placed upon a pyre of wood, consumed by flames until nothing remained but ash. These remnants were cast into the river, carried away by the current, and the land has never since suffered the visitation of a vampire. My father possesses a copy of the Imperial Commission's report, complete with the signatures of every witness present, serving as irrefutable verification of this account. It is from this official document that I have distilled the narrative of this final, harrowing scene.

My dear friend, you assume I recount these final events with a steady, composed hand. Such is not the case. The mere act of committing them to paper agitates me anew, unraveling nerves that have already frayed over months of torment. Only your persistent, heartfelt pleas compelled me to undertake this task, forcing me to revisit the unspeakable horrors that, even years after my physical deliverance, continue to cast a shadow over my days and nights, rendering solitude a terrifying abyss.

Before I conclude, I must address the enigmatic Baron Vordenburg, whose obscure scholarship illuminated the path to the Countess Mircalla's resting place. Having retreated to Graz, he lived on a meager stipend—the dregs of his once-princely inheritance in Upper Styria—devoting himself to the meticulous excavation of vampiric legend. His knowledge was encyclopedic; he held at his fingertips every treatise imaginable: *Magia Posthuma*, *Phlegon de Mirabilibus*, *Augustinus de cura pro Mortuis*, and John Christopher Herenberg's *Philosophicae et Christianae Cogitationes de Vampiris*, alongside countless others. From a vast compilation of judicial records, he distilled a coherent system governing the creature's condition, noting that while some laws apply universally, others operate intermittently.

Let me correct a common misconception: the deadly pallor attributed to these revenants is theatrical fiction. In the grave, and when manifesting within human society, they possess the vibrant complexion of health. Yet, when exposed to light within their coffins, they display every symptom that confirmed the undead state of the late Countess Karnstein. Their ability to traverse the earth and return to their tombs daily, without disturbing the clay or leaving a trace upon the shroud, remains an inexplicable mystery. This amphibious existence relies on nightly slumber within the grave, fueled by a horrific hunger for living blood that sustains their waking vitality. Furthermore, they become obsessively fascinated by specific individuals, a passion akin to love. To reach their chosen victims, they employ infinite patience and cunning, navigating a labyrinth of obstacles until they can drain the very essence of their prey. In such cases, they savor their cruelty with the refined patience of an epicure, prolonging the murder through the subtle arts of courtship, seemingly yearning for a semblance of consent. Conversely, in ordinary instances, they strike directly, overpowering their targets with brute force, strangling and exhausting them in a single, brutal feast.

In the unique instance of our tragedy, Mircalla appeared bound by a peculiar constraint: she demanded a name that, if not her true one, must anagrammatically replicate hers without adding or omitting a single letter. Thus, she was known as Carmilla, just as her predecessor was Millarca.

After the expulsion of Carmilla, my father invited the Baron to remain for several weeks. One evening, he recounted the tale of the Moravian nobleman and the vampire haunting the Karnstein churchyard, then asked how the Baron had located the concealed tomb. The Baron's gaunt features twisted into a cryptic smile; he glanced down at his worn spectacles, fumbling with the case before lifting his eyes. "I possess numerous journals and papers penned by that extraordinary man," he began. "The most fascinating concerns the visit you describe. Tradition, of course, distorts the narrative slightly. While he might be termed a Moravian nobleman due to his residence, he was indeed a native of Upper Styria. In his youth, he was a devoted lover of the beautiful Countess Mircalla. Her premature death plunged him into inconsolable sorrow. Vampires multiply according to a ghastly law: a suicide, under certain conditions, transforms into a vampire; a demon haunts the sleeping, causing them to die and subsequently rise as vampires themselves. This befell Mircalla. Her ancestor, Vordenburg—whose title I now hold—soon discerned the truth. He feared that suspicion would inevitably fall upon the dead Countess, whom he had idolized in life, leading to the profanation of her remains by a posthumous execution. Terrified that her expulsion from her amphibious state would project her into a far more horrific existence, he devised a plan to save her.

"He feigned a journey to remove her remains, intending instead to obliterate her monument forever. As age crept upon him, reflecting on his actions from the vantage point of declining years, a fresh horror seized him. He traced the deception, creating notes that would guide future seekers to the precise location, and drafted a confession of his ruse. Had he intended further intervention, death intervened; thus, it fell to a distant descendant to direct the pursuit to the beast's lair, too late for many."

We conversed further, and the Baron revealed another sign: the power of the hand. "Mircalla's slender grip constricted the General's wrist like a vice of steel when he raised the hatchet. Yet, this power extends beyond the grasp; it induces a lingering numbness in the seized limb, a paralysis rarely, if ever, fully recovered."

That spring, my father took me on a tour of Italy, an absence lasting over a year. Long before the terror of those recent events faded, the image of Carmilla persists in my memory with ambiguous shifts—sometimes the playful, languid beauty; sometimes the writhing fiend I witnessed in the ruined church; and often, startling me from reverie, the phantom sound of her light footsteps pausing at the drawing-room door.
