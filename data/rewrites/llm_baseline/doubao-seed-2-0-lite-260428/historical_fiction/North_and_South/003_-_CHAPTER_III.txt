*"Learn to win a lady’s faith
Nobly, as the thing is high;
Bravely, as for life and death—
With a loyal gravity.
Lead her from the festive boards,
Point her to the starry skies,
Guard her, by your truthful words,
Pure from courtship’s flatteries."

“Mr. Henry Lennox.”

Margaret Hale had been turning him over in her thoughts only a minute prior, remembering his question before he’d left London for her cousin Edith’s wedding, about how she would fill her days once they moved to the quiet Hampshire parsonage of Helstone. It was that old French proverb come true: parler du soleil et l’on en voit les rayons — speak of the sun, and you see its light. A bright flush spread over Margaret’s face as she set down her embroidery board and hurried forward to shake his hand. “Go tell Mamma he’s here, Sarah,” she called over her shoulder to the maid. “Mamma and I have a hundred questions to ask you about Edith. I can’t thank you enough for making the journey out to see us.”

“Did I not promise I would?” he replied, his tone softer than his usual crisp barrister’s register. “I’d heard you were settled so deep in the New Forest I never dreamed Hampshire would be so easy a trip from the Highlands.”

“Oh!” he went on, lighter now, “that foolish young couple you’re related to were up to every reckless prank imaginable — scaling unmarked mountains, sailing rickety little dinghies across lochs that should have swallowed them whole. I soon realised they needed a Mentor at their heels to keep them alive, and my uncle was far too frazzled chasing after them to manage it. By the time I saw how little sense the two of them had between them, I knew it was my duty to see them safely embarked on their ship for the Continent from Plymouth.”

“Plymouth?” Margaret’s eyes went wide. “Edith never wrote a word of that. She’s been dashing off her letters in such a hurry lately, half the details slip right past. Did they really sail on Tuesday, like she wrote?”

“Bright and early Tuesday morning, and off my hands at last,” he laughed, pulling a crumpled, tiny folded note from his waistcoat pocket. “Edith stuffed this in my coat before I left, full of scribbled messages just for you.”

Margaret thanked him breathlessly, half desperate to read the note in private, and made the excuse that Sarah must have forgotten to pass the word to her mother, so she would go fetch her herself. The moment she slipped out of the room, Lennox glanced slowly around the little drawing room. The morning sun streamed full tilt through the open bow window, where climbing roses and scarlet honeysuckle curved around the glass to peep inside. The small lawn outside blazed with verbenas and crimson geraniums, their brightness making the room’s furnishings look faded by contrast: the carpet was worn thin at the edges, the chintz curtains had been washed so many times their pattern had softened to a blur, the whole space far smaller and shabbier than he had expected for a girl as regal, as poised as Margaret Hale. He picked up a book from the side table: Dante’s Paradise, bound in fine old white vellum and gold, a dog-eared Italian dictionary tucked next to it, Margaret’s neat slanted handwriting filling a scrap of paper with lists of translated vocabulary. He sighed as he set it down. The living here was every bit as small as she’d described it, he thought, and it felt strange to see the daughter of a Beresford, from such a good old family, making her home in this quiet, modest parsonage.

Meanwhile Margaret had found her mother in the pantry, fretting through one of her bad days where every small task felt like an impossible burden. She was secretly flattered Henry Lennox had made the effort to call, but all she could see was the inconvenience of it. “This is such bad luck,” she moaned. “We’re dining early today, and all we have is cold roast beef — the servants are behind on their ironing, and I didn’t plan for guests. Of course we have to invite him to stay, though, he’s practically family to Edith. And your father has been so low-spirited all morning, I found him in his study just now with his face buried in his hands. I told him Helstone air was far too damp and relaxing to agree with any of us, and he snapped his head up and begged me never to say a word against the place again — he says it’s the only spot on earth he loves.”

Margaret felt a thin cold shadow fall over the bright mood she’d had since Lennox arrived. She let her mother ramble on for a little while, hoping it would ease her distress, before gently steering her back to the drawing room. “Papa liked Mr. Lennox enormously at the wedding breakfast,” she said. “His coming will cheer him up, I promise you. And cold beef will do perfectly for a two o’clock dinner — he’s a London barrister, he’ll think of it more as a late luncheon anyway. We’ll go out sketching for a few hours, he and I, and we’ll be out of your hair while you finish getting the house in order.”

Mrs. Hale smoothed the creases out of her black silk apron and adjusted her cap, and emerged a perfectly gracious, gentlewomanly hostess to greet Lennox with all the warmth due to a near relation. He accepted the invitation to stay the day with such obvious gladness that Mrs. Hale immediately regretted not having a fancier spread to offer him. He was delighted by Margaret’s suggestion that they go out sketching, said he wouldn’t dream of disturbing Mr. Hale before dinner, and the two of them set off in the highest of spirits with their tins of watercolour paints and sketchbooks slung over their arms.

“Stop here a moment,” Margaret said, pulling him to a halt before a cluster of tumbledown thatched cottages half hidden in the oak wood. “I spent all that rainy fortnight after we moved here staring at these, and feeling guilty I’d never stopped to draw them.”

“Better draw them now than wait for them to tumble down entirely, that’s for certain,” Lennox grinned. “They’re far too picturesque to waste. Where can we sit?”

“Right there!” She pointed to a broad, gnarled tree stump the woodcutters had left perfectly positioned to catch the golden morning light. “I’ll spread my plaid over it, and it’s a proper forest throne.”

“With your feet in that puddle for a footstool? No, come stand this way, I’ll shift a few stones for you. Who lives in these cottages?”

“Squatters built them fifty or sixty years ago. One’s empty, the foresters are going to tear it down as soon as the last old man in the other one passes on. Look — there he is! Old Isaac. I’ll go speak to him, he’s so deaf he won’t hear a word of any secrets we might whisper.”

The old man stood bareheaded in the sun, leaning on his walking stick at his cottage gate, his stiff rheumatic face softening into a slow smile when Margaret approached. Lennox worked quickly, sketching the two of them into the background of his landscape before she noticed, and Margaret laughed and blushed bright pink when they packed up their supplies later and compared drawings. “That’s treacherous!” she protested. “I had no idea you were making the two of us the subject, when you told me to go ask him the history of the cottages.”

“I couldn’t resist,” he said, his voice softer than she’d ever heard it. “You’ve no idea how strong the temptation was. I hardly dare tell you how much I’m going to love keeping this sketch.” She wandered off to the nearby brook to wash the paint out of her palette, flushing hot, and Lennox was glad she didn’t seem to have caught the full weight of what he’d said — it was rare for a man as deliberate, as planned in all his actions, to let such an unguarded line slip out.

When they got back to the parsonage, all the earlier shadows had cleared: a neighbour had dropped off a brace of fresh-caught carp, and Mrs. Hale’s good mood was fully restored. Mr. Hale was waiting for them by the wicket gate leading into the garden, looking every inch the gentle scholar even in his threadbare old coat and well-worn hat. Margaret was always tenderly proud of how favorably her father impressed strangers, but her sharp eye caught the faint traces of some deep unspoken disturbance on his face, pushed aside but not erased.

They all went inside to dine, the conversation flowing easily as they traded stories of Edith’s adventures in Italy, of General Shaw’s widow Mrs. Shaw’s plans to travel further south, and Lennox soon forgot the small flicker of disappointment he’d felt when he realised how modest the Hales’ home was — in Margaret’s company, the plain, unpretending warmth of the parsonage felt like far more luxury than any drawing room in Harley Street.

Halfway through the meal, Mr. Hale set down his wine glass and said, “Margaret, my dear child, run out to the south wall and pick us those few brown beurré pears I’ve been tending to, the ones the sun’s been ripening. They taste better than any foreign preserve or fruit you could buy in a London shop.”

Lennox jumped to his feet before Margaret could move. “Let us all go out to the garden and eat them there,” he said. “There’s nothing more delicious than biting into a pear warm from the sun, crisp and juicy, with the scent of the leaves still on it.” Mrs. Hale would have preferred to finish dinner properly, to bring out the finger glasses she’d stored in the pantry for guests, but when both Mr. Hale and their guest moved toward the door, she had no choice but to follow.

Margaret laid the picked pears out on a large fresh beetroot leaf that made their golden brown skins glow, and she and Lennox strolled slowly along the terrace walk under the south wall, where bees hummed around the hives tucked against the hedge. “What a perfect, serene life you lead here,” Lennox said, pausing to look up at the deep unbroken blue of the sky. “I used to mock the poets who longed for a cottage in the country, thought they were all being ridiculous — I see now I was nothing but a London cockney, blind to all this beauty. I feel like twenty years of slaving away at law in cramped chambers would be well rewarded for just one year of a life as peaceful as this.”

“Our skies aren’t always this bright, you know,” Margaret laughed. “We have weeks of rain where the leaves turn sodden and the fog hangs so thick you can’t see the wood ten paces away. Though I do think Helstone is the loveliest place on earth. Don’t you remember the night in Harley Street you laughed when I called it a ‘village out of a fairy tale’?”

“Scorned it?” he said, warm and earnest. “I’ll never speak of it like that again.” They rounded the far end of the terrace walk, and he hesitated, stopping short. Margaret looked up at him, suddenly, unaccountably uneasy — she could see he was about to say something she had no answer for. Her pride kicked in a moment later, steadying her: she was a grown woman, of course she could face whatever he had to say with dignity.

“Margaret,” he said, and caught her hand before she could step away, holding it firm in his. “I’ve waited three months to tell you this. I wish you didn’t love Helstone quite so much, didn’t seem so perfectly happy here, that you missed London a little — missed your old friends — enough to listen more kindly to what I have to say. I have very little to offer you, nothing but prospects for the future, but I love you, Margaret, almost in spite of myself.” He saw her lips tremble, and let go of her hand for half a second. “Have I startled you too much? Say something.”

She mastered her voice with an effort, her gaze steady and open, full of quiet regret. “You did startle me. I’ve only ever thought of you as a friend, nothing more. I don’t want you to be upset, but I can’t answer you the way you want. I can’t think of you as a lover.”

Lennox stared at her, struck dumb for a moment. He had expected hesitation, not a flat, gentle, firm rejection. “There’s no rush,” he said, at last. “Will you not even give me hope, that one day you might change your mind?”

Margaret was quiet for a long moment, searching her own heart, before she shook her head. “I never will. Please, let us both forget this conversation ever happened.”

He laughed, a sharp, bitter little laugh that fell back on his usual worldly barrister’s tone. “Forget it? That’s all very well in theory, but I daresay it will be hard for me, at least. A struggling barrister, with barely enough money to keep chambers in the Temple, thinking he could marry — what a fool I’ve been.”

Margaret felt a flicker of disdain stir in her chest, tangled up with her sorrow at hurting him. That sharp, cynical edge was exactly the part of him that had sometimes made her pull back even back in London. The two of them rounded the hedge and stumbled straight into Mr. Hale, still sitting on the garden bench, peeling his pear in one long unbroken silver strip of rind. They made forced small talk for the next fifteen minutes, Margaret grave and quiet, Lennox forcing himself to chatter lightly about London society, full of quiet sarcasm that made Mr. Hale frown in puzzlement, wondering what had come over the pleasant young man from the wedding breakfast.

Lennox was as desperate to leave as Margaret was to be alone with her thoughts. He said he must go at once if he meant to catch the five o’clock train back to London, and they all filed back into the house to say goodbye to Mrs. Hale. At the very last moment, as he picked up his hat from the hall table, he leaned in close to Margaret, his voice dropping so no one else could hear.

“Don’t despise me, Margaret. Underneath all this sharp, worldly talk, I have a heart. I think I love you more now than I did before, even for the flash of that quiet disdain I saw in your face a few minutes ago. Goodbye. Margaret.”

He was gone before she could think of a single word to say in reply.
