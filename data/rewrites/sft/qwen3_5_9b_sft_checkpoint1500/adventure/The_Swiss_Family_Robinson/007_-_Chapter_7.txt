The following day, our first priority was to retrieve the sledge laden with the crockery, bowls, and baskets we had crafted from the Calabash Wood. Only Fritz and I ventured forth; I instructed the younger boys to remain with their mother, deeming a large party unsuitable for the expedition ahead.

As we traversed the grove of evergreen oaks, we spotted our sow feasting on acorns. She seemed unfazed by yesterday's scare, indeed appearing more affectionate, perhaps viewing us as deliverers from the savage dogs that had threatened her. The forest teemed with life; birds sang undisturbed until Fritz, eager for sport, fired his rifle. Down fell a magnificent blue jay and a pair of parakeets—one scarlet, the other green and gold. Just as Fritz prepared to reload, a strange, dull thumping echoed through the woods. It sounded like a muffled drum, instantly alerting us to the possibility of savages nearby.

We approached cautiously, hiding within the thick undergrowth until we reached an open clearing. There, perched atop a fallen log, stood a striking bird the size of a rooster, rich chestnut-brown and mottled with dark grey. Tufts of velvety black feathers, glossed with green, adorned his shoulders. He strutted and wheeled with stately grace, performing a complex dance that captivated a circle of lesser birds gathered below. After maneuvering for some time, he fanned his tail, stiffened his wings, and began beating them in rapid, rhythmic strokes. The sound grew into a rolling rumble, like distant thunder, as his wings whirred around him like a storm cloud.

It was this drumming that had first alarmed us, amplified by the strikes landing on the decaying stump beneath him. I watched with intense fascination until a shot rang out from behind me. The playful scene ended abruptly; my overzealous son had transformed a delightful comedy into a tragic necessity. The "drummer" fell dead, and his admirers scattered in panic. I was deeply annoyed by this cruelty, blaming Fritz for firing without permission, though I admitted the bird was a magnificent specimen, likely a ruffed grouse.

We loaded the carcass onto the patient ass and pressed on. The sledge remained secure where we had left it. Early in the day, I resolved to fulfill my original intent: exploring the line of cliffs and rocky hills stretching along the coast. I sought an opening to penetrate the interior or confirm if we were truly walled in and isolated.

Leaving the Calabash Wood, we marched over terrain lush with manioc, potatoes, and exotic flora. Pleasant streams watered the fertile soil, offering views of open beauty in every direction. Bushes heavy with small, wax-like white berries caught my eye. Recognizing *Myrica cerifera*, I explained to Fritz that melting and straining these berries could yield candles—a boon for his mother, who disliked having to rest the moment the sun dipped below the horizon. Though the resulting wax would be brittle, it burned well and smelled fragrant. With the ass assisting us, we quickly gathered enough berries to fill a large canvas bag.

Soon, another natural marvel presented itself: the communal dwelling of a bird colony. Under a single roof constructed of grass and straw fibers, hundreds of birds—small, brown, and reminiscent of yellowhammers—lived together. Their nests formed a mass around the trunk and branches of a solitary tree, creating a structure resembling a sponge with countless openings serving as individual apartments. Twigs protruded from the walls, acting as perches for the young. The community bustled with activity, and among them, several beautiful little parrots contested ownership of the nests with the rightful residents.

Fritz, an adept climber, ascended the tree to inspect the nests, hoping to seize a young bird. He reached deep into several holes, finding them empty, until finally, his hand encountered a fierce grip. A powerful beak bit down sharply, forcing him to cry out and withdraw his hand in pain. Recovering, he lunged again, capturing the bird despite its shrieks and cramming it into his pocket. However, his triumph was short-lived; a swarm of relatives darted from the other holes, screeching and pecking at him in a frenzy.

Fritz's prize was not a resident of the communal nests—which belonged to the sociable grosbeaks—but a charming, small green parrot. Delighted, he determined to tame it, keeping it imprisoned in his pocket for the moment.

The colony's cheerful, cooperative habits sparked a lively discussion as we continued our journey. I compared them to other social creatures, mentioning the industriousness of bees, wasps, ants, and the coral polyps building reefs beneath the waves. Fritz recalled observing ants, noting how they tended to their larvae, which he correctly identified as the chrysalis stage, not actual eggs. I expanded on this, describing the massive anthills of South America, towering structures built with incredible strength and internal complexity. I detailed the destructive nature of these ants and their formidable enemy, the anteater (*tamanoir*), a creature with a long, toothless snout and a sticky, worm-like tongue designed to scoop up the terrified millions. I also described the smaller anteater, a sleek arboreal hunter with silky fur. These tales fascinated Fritz, occupying us for a significant portion of our walk.

Eventually, we reached a grove of tall trees with broad, thick leaves bearing round, fig-like fruits of a sour, harsh taste. Fritz noticed resin oozing from cracks in the bark, reminding him of cherry-tree gum from home. He scraped some into his hand, attempting to soften it, only to realize it possessed elasticity, springing back when stretched.

"Oh Father!" he exclaimed. "Look! This gum is elastic! Could it be India-rubber?"

"Yes!" I cried, examining it. "A valuable discovery indeed! You are absolutely right!"

"Why so valuable, Father?" Fritz asked, noting he had only seen it used to erase pencil marks.

"Ireland-rubber," I corrected gently, explaining it was a milky resinous juice tapped from specific trees in Brazil, Guiana, and Cayenne. I recounted how the native inhabitants originally used it to coat earthen flasks, breaking the molds to reveal flexible bottles, and how they decorated them with crude lines. "Caoutchouc has limitless uses," I told him. "I am thrilled to find it here. First and foremost, I intend to manufacture boots and shoes."

Shortly after, we entered the coconut wood and beheld the bay, capped by the promontory we named Cape Disappointment, which had long marked the limit of our explorations. Passing through the palms, I noted a smaller variety I had missed before. One had been snapped by the wind, revealing a pith with a peculiar mealy texture. I was convinced it was the sago-palm. Inside the pith, I found fat worms, recalling rumors that they were eaten as a delicacy in the West Indies. Curiosity overcoming hesitation, I kindled a fire and roasted a handful of them on a wooden spit. Rich fat dripped from them, and their savory aroma banished any repugnance. I ate one boldly, likening it to a pat of butter on a potato, and found it delicious enough to encourage several more bites. Fritz, summoning courage, also partook, adding a novel flavor to our dinner of baked potatoes.

Returning to the sledge in Calabash Wood, we loaded the ass with the wax berries and the bundle of sugar-canes we had cut from the brake near Cape Disappointment. Once the ass was unloaded, he readily pulled the burden. That evening, we returned to Falconhurst to a warm welcome. Our stories were met with keen interest, and the green parakeet enchanted the boys. After a hearty supper, we climbed to our tree-castle, exhausted but content.

The idea of candle-making seized the boys' imaginations. By the next morning, the word "candle" was on everyone's lips. Breakfast was consumed in discussion of the project, and afterward, they demanded to begin work immediately.

"So be it," I declared, adopting the persona of a chandler. "Let us become candle-makers."

Though I harbored doubts—I lacked animal fat for proper burning and feared forgetting the intricate manufacturing process—I hid these reservations. Under my direction, the boys worked diligently. They stripped the berries and poured them into a large iron vessel over the fire. The green, sweet-smelling wax melted, rising to the surface. We skimmed it repeatedly, collecting enough liquid to proceed. Taking the wicks my wife had prepared, I dipped them one by one into the hot wax, handing them to Fritz, who hung them on a bush to dry. The initial coating was thin, but after multiple dips, they became sturdy, substantial candles. Once the wax supply ran out, we hung the candles in a cool, shaded spot to harden.

That same night, three hours after sunset, Falconhurst was brilliantly illuminated for the first time. We sat up like civilized beings, delighted with our success. My wife praised my ingenuity, wishing I would apply it to making butter, lamenting the spoilage of her daily cream supply.

"I believe I can help," I replied. "Jack, bring me one of those gourd bottles I prepared."

Taking the bottle, which I had hollowed and corked, I filled it partially with cream. "Boys," I commanded, "continue the operation while I become carpenter and construct a cart to replace our sledge."
