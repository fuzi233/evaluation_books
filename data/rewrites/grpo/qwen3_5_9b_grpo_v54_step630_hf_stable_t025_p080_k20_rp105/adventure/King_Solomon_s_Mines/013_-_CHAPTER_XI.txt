For two long hours, the silence within the hut was heavy, broken only by the ragged breathing of men too shaken by the horrors of the night to speak. As dawn approached, the rhythmic tread of boots echoed against the dirt floor. A sentry's challenge rang out, answered not in voice but in movement, and Infadoos entered, flanked by six towering chiefs.

"My lords," Infadoos declared, gesturing to the imposing figures behind him, "I have kept my word. These are the commanders of the Kukuanas, each leading three thousand souls loyal to the rightful king. They have heard my tale and witnessed the sacred snake coiled upon Ignosi's skin. Let them now judge whether they will stand with him against Twala."

Ignosi stripped his girdle to reveal the tattooed serpent once more. Under the dim lamp-light, the chiefs inspected the mark in solemn silence before moving on. Ignosi then recounted the morning's history, detailing the massacre and the tyranny of the current ruler.

"Chiefs," Infadoos pressed, "what is your verdict? Will you aid this man in reclaiming his father's throne, or will you remain silent while the land bleeds? Two other leaders I sought out have already fallen to the hyenas. Soon you will join them unless you act. Choose!"

The eldest chief, a thick-set warrior with white hair, stepped forward. "Your words are true, Infadoos, but this is a perilous path. How can we trust a thief and a liar? Many still worship the sun that shines for Twala, not the one that has not yet risen. Furthermore, these white men possess great magic; Ignosi hides under their wing. If he is truly the king, let him give us a sign—a sign the people can see. Only then will we follow."

"The snake is the sign," I offered.

"It is insufficient," the elder countered. "A tattoo can be placed in childhood. We require a miracle. We will not move without it."

The others agreed unanimously. Turning to Sir Henry and Good, I explained the impasse.

"I think I have it," Good exclaimed, retrieving a notebook from his medical kit. "Ask them to wait."

He consulted the almanack tucked into the fly-leaf. "Tomorrow is the fourth of June. Here it is: 'Total eclipse of the moon commences at 8:15 Greenwich time, visible in South Africa.' We will darken the moon for them."

Sir Henry expressed concern regarding the accuracy of the prediction, fearing that a failed prophecy would destroy our credibility and Ignosi's claim. Good, however, was confident in his calculations, estimating the eclipse would begin around ten o'clock local time and last for ninety minutes.

"We must risk it," Sir Henry conceded.

Summoning the chiefs back, I addressed them with gravity. "We do not show our powers lightly, for it disturbs the natural order. Yet, angered by the slaughter and the cruelty of the *Isanusi* Gagool, we have decided to break our rule. Tomorrow night, two hours before midnight, we will cause the moon to vanish for an hour and a half. This darkness shall be the sign that Ignosi is king. If we succeed, will you be satisfied?"

"Yes," the old chief smiled, "if you do this, we will be satisfied."

Infadoos then outlined the plan: two hours after sunset, Twala would summon the chiefs to witness the "Dance of Girls." One hour into the dance, the fairest maiden would be sacrificed to the Silent Ones atop the distant peaks. If we could darken the moon during this ritual, saving the girl, the people would believe.

The plan was set. That evening, as we prepared for the dance, Ignosi asked if we truly could perform such a wonder. "If we survive," he promised, "you shall be repaid." Sir Henry added a condition: if Ignosi became king, he must abolish the witch-finders and end executions without trial. Ignosi agreed, acknowledging the difference between black and white ways but pledging to change his laws if given the chance.

Worn out, we slept until Ignosi roused us at eleven. After a hearty breakfast, we walked the kraal, observing the women and the unique architecture of the huts. Sir Henry voiced his hope that the eclipse would occur; I replied mournfully that failure meant certain death, as the chiefs would likely report our deception to Twala, inviting a far worse fate.

As twilight fell, a messenger arrived from Twala, inviting us to the annual dance. We donned the chain shirts the king had provided, loaded our rifles, and marched toward the great open space before the kraal. The scene had transformed; instead of grim warriors, rows of Kukuana girls danced, crowned with flowers and holding white arum lilies.

Twala sat centrally, flanked by Gagool, Infadoos, and his son, Scragga. He greeted us with feigned cordiality, his single eye fixing viciously on Umbopa. He offered us wives from his people, a prospect Good wisely declined, citing the inevitable troubles of foreign entanglements. I politely refused on behalf of all, stating we wed only our own kind.

Twala laughed, citing proverbs about women's eyes and love, before turning his attention to the dance. The girls moved in swirling patterns, mimicking warfare and grace. Finally, a beautiful young woman stepped forward, pirouetting with such vigor that she outshone the rest. When the dance concluded, Twala asked which girl was fairest.

Unthinkingly, I answered, "The first."

Regret struck me instantly. Infadoos had warned us: the fairest girl must die.

"Ay, must die!" Gagool shrieked, her eyes darting toward the terrified girl, Foulata, who stood trembling before the crowd.

"Why?" I demanded, fighting back indignation. "She has pleased us!"

"It is our custom," Twala sneered, pointing to the silent peaks. "To fail in this sacrifice brings ruin upon my house. My predecessor refused, and his lineage ended. I reign today because I obeyed. She must die!"

Guards seized Foulata as she screamed and tried to flee. Gagool mocked her, asking for her name. "Foulata of the house of Suko," the girl sobbed. "Why must I die? I have done no wrong!"

Gagool twisted her words, claiming death was better than toil, promising the girl would die by the hand of the king's son. Foulata wrung her hands in agony, lamenting the loss of her father, her mother, her future children, and the simple joys of gathering flowers. Her despair was so profound it seemed to touch the very heavens, yet it moved neither Gagool nor Twala.

Seeing Good's hand creep to his revolver, Foulata suddenly threw herself at his legs, clasping them in a desperate plea for protection. "Oh, white father from the Stars! Save me from these cruel men!"

"All right, my hearty," Good stammered in nervous Saxon, catching her hand.

"Now's your time," Sir Henry whispered urgently. "What are you waiting for?"

"I am waiting for the eclipse," I replied, watching the moon. "It looks healthy."

"Risk it now, or the girl dies," Sir Henry urged.

Twala, losing patience, signaled Scragga to advance with his spear. I stepped between the girl and the weapon, raising my voice with authority. "King! It shall not be! We will not endure this!"

Twala rose in wrath. "Back, white dog! Or die by the girl's hand!" Guards surged forward, but Sir Henry, Good, and Umbopa raised their rifles.

"Stop!" I shouted. "We, the white men from the Stars, say it shall not be. Come one pace nearer, and we will put out the moon like a wind-blown lamp, plunging this land into darkness. Dare to disobey, and taste our magic!"

The threat held. The men halted. Gagool jeered at the lie, demanding the proof or death.

I looked up at the moon, my heart pounding. To my relief, a faint rim of shadow was creeping across the orb. "Grow dark, O Moon!" I cried, lifting my hand solemnly. Sir Henry and Good joined me, reciting verses from legends, scripture, and Latin poetry. Good unleashed a torrent of classical curses in various tongues.

Slowly, the penumbra spread. Gasps of terror rose from the crowd. Some knelt in prayer; others froze in petrified dread. Twala sat pale, while Gagool insisted the shadow would pass.

"Wait," I urged, hopping with excitement. "Keep it up, Good!"

For ten minutes, Good swore in a dozen languages, while the dark ring consumed the moon. The air grew thick with crimson gloom. The great pale orb turned coppery, then ashen, its mountains glowing luridly through the encroaching shadow.

"The moon is dying—the white wizards have killed the moon!" Scragga yelled, driven mad by fear. Enraged, he lunged at Sir Henry's breast. He forgot the mail shirt beneath his tunic; the steel rebounded harmlessly. Before Scragga could strike again, Curtis snatched the spear and drove it through the prince's chest.

Scragga dropped dead.

Panic erupted. The girls scattered in wild confusion, screaming for the gates. Twala, Gagool, and his guards fled into the huts. Left alone on the scene were the would-be victim Foulata, Infadoos, the chiefs, and the dead body of Scragga.

"Chiefs," I called out, "we have given you the sign. The charm cannot be stopped; it will last an hour and a half. Let us cover ourselves in the darkness."

Infadoos nodded, turning to lead the way. As we stumbled through the gate, the moon vanished utterly, and the stars burst forth into the inky sky. Holding hands, we plunged into the profound darkness, leaving the kraal behind.
