Preface to the Second and Revised Edition, 1884. By the Editor

If my poor Flatland friend and elder retained the vigorous clarity of mind he possessed when he first began to compose these Memoirs, I should not now need to act as his archivist in this preface. He desires, firstly, to extend his gratitude to his readers and critics in Spaceland, whose appetite for his history has required a second edition of this work; secondly, to apologize for certain misprints in his recipes and records; and, thirdly, to clarify a few misconceptions. 

But he is not the Square he once was. Years of confinement within the State Penitentiary—a facility plagued by a relentless, metallic clanging that shatters the delicate acoustic ecology necessary for psychological preservation—have broken him. This industrial noise pollution, combined with the heavy burden of public mockery and the natural, circular decay of old age, has eroded his mind. Most tragically, he is suffering the slow erosion of cultural memory through language death; much of the terminology and the very conceptual framework he acquired during his brief, mythic foray into Spaceland are fading from his tongue. He has asked me, a meticulous preserver of our ancestral domestic routines and a food historian by trade, to reply on his behalf to two specific Spaceland objections. The first is of an intellectual nature; the second, moral.

The first objection argues that a Flatlander, when viewing a Line, sees something that must be *thick* to the eye as well as *long* (otherwise it would be invisible), and consequently, he ought to acknowledge that his countrymen are not only long and broad, but also *thick* or *high*. This argument is plausible, and to Spacelanders—accustomed to the towering, vertical laminations of three-dimensional confections and the ascending aromas of deep-kettle broths—it feels irresistible. But my elder’s answer completely addresses the untranslatability of this subjective experience. 

"I admit the truth of your critic's facts," the Square whispered to me over the sterile, manufactured hum of the prison's ventilation, a sound entirely devoid of the soulful resonance of our old hearths, "but I deny his conclusions. We have in Flatland a Third unrecognized Dimension called 'height', just as Spaceland has an unnamed Fourth. But we can no more taste or take cognizance of our 'height' than you can of your 'extra-height'. Even I, who once feasted in Spaceland and understood for twenty-four hours the profound, layered meaning of 'height', cannot now recreate that recipe by any process of reason. I can but apprehend it by faith.

"Dimension implies measurement, just as the culinary arts require the precise weighing of ground polygon-seeds. But all our lines are equally and infinitesimally thick. There is no 'delicate micrometer' to aid us, for we would not know what to measure, nor in what direction. When we see a Line, we see something that is long and *Bright*. Brightness, in our native dialect, encompasses both visual radiance and a vivid, gustatory zest—it is the sharp, citrus-like bite of freshly harvested perimeter-grass. If the Brightness vanishes, the Line is extinguished, as surely as a stew boiled down to ash. When I speak to my countrymen of the unrecognized Dimension visible in a Line, they say, 'Ah, you mean the Brightness of the spice,' and when I reply, 'No, I mean a real Dimension,' they retort, 'Then measure it.' The dominant lexicon of our flat plane silences me. 

"Only yesterday, the Chief Circle—our High Priest, whose rolling presence emits a low, synthetic drone of commercialized state power—paid me his seventh annual visit. When he asked if I was 'cured,' I tried to prove he was 'high' as well as long and broad. His reply? 'Measure my high-ness and I will believe you.' What could I do? It is the tragic cycle of all Dimensions: we are all slaves to our respective dimensional prejudices, punishing those who speak of flavors and directions we lack the native tongue to describe."

On this point, the defense of the Square is impregnable. I wish his answer to the second, moral objection were as easily translated. It has been vehemently objected by the larger, nurturing half of the Spaceland race that he is a woman-hater. The Square is so unaccustomed to Spaceland's moral terminology that transcribing his defense literally would subject him to the very linguistic power dynamics that warp the truth.

Acting as his interpreter and fellow chef, I must explain that across seven years of imprisonment, his palate and personal worldview have deeply evolved. He now looks with profound reverence upon the Straight Lines (our Women) and the Isosceles (our Lower Classes). They are the passionate chefs and traditional agrarian elders of our world, their hands kneading the daily sustenance that binds our generations, their voices singing the ancient planting songs that form the acoustic bedrock of our true culture. He sees now that the Aristocracy treats sustenance merely as utilitarian fuel, indifferent to the sensory appreciation of the harvest. 

Yet, when writing as a Historian, he adopted—perhaps too closely—the dominant, patriarchal lexicon of Flatland's elite. In those sterile historical texts, the vibrant domestic routines, the culinary ingenuity of Women, and the agrarian labors of the masses were deemed unworthy of mention. He desires now to completely disavow the Circular, aristocratic tendencies forced upon his prose. While acknowledging the intellectual machinations of the Circles, he observes that Nature's cycles are far older and wiser than the artificial timelines of the State. In sentencing the Circles to infecundity—unable to produce the sprawling, multi-generational families that gather around a simmering pot of root-broth—Nature has condemned them to ultimate failure. Revolutions are not suppressed by slaughter, but outlasted by the slow, enduring rhythms of the soil.

For the rest, he begs his readers not to assume that every minute detail of our domestic life must precisely mirror the kitchens and pantries of Spaceland. Yet he hopes his interwoven timelines and memories will nourish those Spacelanders of moderate minds who, when faced with the untranslatable mysteries of a foreign table, decline to say on the one hand, "This can never be," and on the other hand, "It must needs be precisely thus, and we know all about it."

CONTENTS: PART I: THIS WORLD

Here, in the quiet damp of my pentagonal kitchen, I crush the luminescent, mineral-rich tubers harvested by the traditional agrarian elders of the southern strata, inhaling the sharp, earthy aroma of fermented fog-root. It is a slow, cyclical process of culinary alchemy, much like the sweeping, multi-generational saga of our people that I am now compelled to record. The rhythmic, wooden *thump-thump* of my pestle creates a soothing acoustic resonance, a localized harmony that stands in stark defiance of the grating, industrial clatter that now pollutes the sonic landscape of our modern geometric cities. 

I simmer this history over a low flame, treating our collective past as a complex, ancestral broth. To truly digest my tale, one must first consume Section 1, *Of the Nature of Flatland*, and savor the terroir detailed in Section 2, *Of the Climate and Houses in Flatland*. I must meticulously document Section 3, *Concerning the Inhabitants of Flatland*, and Section 4, *Concerning the Women*—voices with subtle, straight-lined timbres that have been tragically drowned out by the harsh noise of our era. 

The aroma of the steeping herbs deepens, reminding me of Section 5, *Of our Methods of Recognizing one another*. Once, our society relied upon a profound acoustic ecology, identifying the nuanced vocal signatures of our kin through the damp air. Yet, as cultural memory eroded, the elite forced upon us Section 6, *Of Recognition by Sight*. They replaced the intimate, auditory language of our ancestors with a sterile visual lexicon. 

This loss of our native sensory dialects tastes distinctly bitter, a flavor that permeates Section 7, *Concerning Irregular Figures*. It brings to mind Section 8, *Of the Ancient Practice of Painting*, an era when our world was spiced with vibrant hues, our ancestors blending pigments like master chefs balancing complex curries. But the dominant powers, fearing the untamed richness of this physical sensation, enacted Section 9, *Of the Universal Colour Bill*, resulting in the tragic and violent events of Section 10, *Of the Suppression of the Chromatic Sedition*. They wiped our plates clean, leaving a bland, tasteless visual existence mandated in Section 11, *Concerning our Priests*, and the hollow, echoing mandates found in Section 12, *Of the Doctrine of our Priests*, which prioritize utilitarian fuel over the passionate sustenance of the soul.

Yet, the true meat of my narrative—the mythic occurrence that forever disrupted my quiet, domestic routine—lies in the untranslatable beyond of Part II: *Other Worlds*. 

I will recount Section 13, *How I had a Vision of Lineland*, a realm of deeply impoverished dialect. I will record Section 14, *How I vainly tried to explain the nature of Flatland* to its monarch, tasting the bitter frustration of linguistic death. The saga will then swell with Section 15, *Concerning a Stranger from Spaceland*. The Sphere brought a conceptual flavor so alien, so structurally complex, it shattered my understanding of existence. I will document Section 16, *How the Stranger vainly endeavoured to reveal to me in words the mysteries of Spaceland*, hindered by the subtle power dynamics embedded in my own flat, two-dimensional lexicon. It is a testament to the inherent untranslatability of subjective experience that Section 17 occurred: *How the Sphere, having in vain tried words, resorted to deeds*, forcing me to consume the truth directly through physical intervention.

The transcendent taste of that upward ascension remains on my palate as I recall Section 18, *How I came to Spaceland, and what I saw there*, and Section 19, *How, though the Sphere shewed me other mysteries of Spaceland, I still desired more; and what came of it*. I will recount Section 20, *How the Sphere encouraged me in a Vision*. 

Upon returning to my hearth, the inability to share this new spatial vocabulary haunted me. I will describe Section 21, *How I tried to teach the Theory of Three Dimensions to my Grandson, and with what success*, watching the rich, volumetric language of the heights die on his lips, inevitably yielding to the dominant flat tongue of his peers. Finally, I will relate Section 22, *How I then tried to diffuse the Theory of Three Dimensions by other means, and of the result*.

The fog-broth is ready. The sonic hum of the boiling pot settles into a steady, comforting rhythm against the oppressive silence of my confinement. I set down my ladle, pick up my pen, and turn my mind back to the foundational ingredients of Part I: *THIS WORLD*. 

I take a deep breath of the spiced vapor, whispering to myself the ancient, agrarian wisdom passed down through generations:

"Be patient, for the world is broad and wide."

I call our world Flatland. I use this word not because our ancient agrarian elders ever employed such a sterile, utilitarian term—our native tongue originally named our home in the rhythmic, low-frequency resonance of basalt millstones grinding heirloom wheat—but to make its nature translatable to you, my readers, whose privilege of living in Space has entrenched you within a dominant lexicon that flattens our truth. 

Imagine a vast, perfectly rolled sheet of pastry dough, smelling faintly of roasted cumin and sweet butter. Upon this infinite domestic plane, straight Lines, Triangles, Squares, Pentagons, and Hexagons move freely. We do not remain fixed in our places, nor do we possess the power to rise above or sink below the crust of our existence. We move upon the surface like the luminous, hard-edged shadows of caramelized sugar shifting across a hot cast-iron pan. If you can envision this, you will possess a fairly accurate understanding of my country and my kin, whose histories span generations of interwoven, cyclical harvests.

Alas, a few generations ago, I would have confidently called this culinary plane "my universe," preserving the ancestral recipes of our localized geometry. But now, my mind has been opened to the mythic, multi-generational truths of higher dimensions—a revelation that struck me, seamlessly and profoundly, amid the everyday domestic routine of kneading unleavened bread. 

In a world so inextricably bound to a single flat plane, vibrating with the quiet acoustic hum of the hearth, you will perceive at once that it is impossible to possess what your Spaceland vocabulary calls a "solid." You might suppose, standing in your three-dimensional kitchens, that we could at least distinguish the sharp, peppery scent of a Triangle from the mellow, yeasty warmth of a Square by sight alone as they traverse the pan. On the contrary, the limitations of our visual dialect mean we see nothing of the kind. Nothing is visible to us except Straight Lines, and the tragedy of this untranslatable subjective experience I will speedily demonstrate.

Place a perfectly round, thin wafer of spun sugar upon the center of one of your heavy wooden prep tables in Space. Leaning over it, inhaling its rich molasses aroma, look down. It appears as a circle. But now, stepping back toward the edge of the table—drawing yourself away from the echoing, hollow din of modern industrial kitchens and lowering yourself into the intimate acoustic condition of a Flatland elder—gradually drop your eye. You will find the sweet wafer becoming more and more oval to your view. At last, when you rest your chin exactly upon the flour-dusted edge of the table, immersing yourself fully in our perspective, the wafer will have ceased to appear oval entirely. It will have become, to your sight, a straight line severing the horizon.

This same visual erasure occurs whether you view a Triangle, Square, or any shape painstakingly cut from pressed marzipan. As soon as you level your eye with the edge of the table, the figure's rich geometry vanishes into a solitary stroke. Take, for example, our equilateral Triangle—traditionally representing the respectable class of passionate tradesmen who forage wild fennel and mill our native grains. If you were to bend over him from above, his three sharp corners would be fully distinct. But bring your eye closer to the level of the cutting board, and his depth recedes. When your eye is entirely flush with the table—the very vantage point of our multi-generational existence—you see nothing but a glowing, straight edge. His true complexity is flattened into a single visual syllable, an acoustic drone without variance.

During my transcendent journey into Spaceland, I learned from your meticulous food historians that your maritime sailors experience a similar phenomenon. When they traverse your vast, roaring seas, seeking the fragrant spice islands of their ancestors, they occasionally discern a distant coast on the horizon. That land may possess deep bays filled with the scent of blooming vanilla, and jutting forelands echoing with the rhythmic crash of the tide; yet at a distance, you see none of these intricacies. Unless your Spaceland sun casts its bright rays to reveal projections and retirements through light and shade, your sailors see only a grey, unbroken line upon the water.

This is precisely what we witness when a triangular forager or hexagonal master-chef approaches us across the flat hearth of our world. We have no sun to mark the passing of our days, nor any directional light to carve shadows that might hint at their true, multifaceted form. We lack the visual vocabulary that empowers your three-dimensional gaze. If a cherished friend approaches, bearing the scent of woodsmoke and charred nightshades, his line merely grows larger; as he retreats, his line shrinks. Yet whether he is a Triangle, Square, Pentagon, Hexagon, or Circle, his visual translation remains a straight Line, and nothing else.

You may perhaps ask how, under such profound disadvantage—and amid the steady, low-frequency drone of our environment—we manage to identify our kin, keeping the memory of our lineages alive without visually recognizing their forms. How do we prevent our cultural memory from eroding into an indistinguishable blur? The answer to this natural question is woven deeply into the sonic and sensory heritage of Flatland's inhabitants, but it will be more fitly explained when I chronicle their lives. For the present, let me defer this intimate subject, and speak first of the delicate climate that seasons our days, and the acoustic architecture of the houses in our ancient country.

As with you, so also with us, the four corners of existence—North, South, East, and West—frame our domestic rhythms and the seasonal cycles of our hearths. We have no sun to bake our flatbreads or ripen our harvests, so it is impossible to determine the North by gazing toward the heavens; instead, the terroir of our orientation is felt deep within the body. By a profound Law of Nature, a constant attraction pulls us Southward. It is a dense, yeasty settling, like the slow draw of a heavy culinary syrup. In temperate climates, this pull is subtle enough that a Woman in reasonable health might forage northward for several furlongs to gather wild, aromatic ground-spices without losing her breath. Yet, this gravitational molasses serves as a reliable compass for generations of our agrarian elders.

Moreover, the northern rains—which arrive at stated intervals like the reliable churning of a great cosmic mill—provide a vital acoustic and olfactory guide. The rain brings the crisp, ozone-rich scent of the North, striking the earth with a rhythmic, staccato hush that briefly cleanses the jarring, industrial clatter of our modern towns. In these dense settlements, the side-walls of our homes invariably run North and South, deflecting the downpour and channeling its resonant hum to protect the dry stores. Out in the quiet, acoustic sanctity of the country, where no houses stand, the textured bark of ancient trees guides the way. Yet, when I have wandered the desolate plains, seeking the forgotten, wild-fermented herbs of my ancestors where neither house nor tree remains, I have occasionally been compelled to stand motionless for hours, steeped in the profound silence, waiting for the percussive song and wet-earth scent of the northern rain to reorient my palate and my path.

This southward drag weighs heavily upon our fragile, aged bodies and delicate Females, demanding a far greater caloric expenditure than the robust Males possess. Thus, it is a point of deep cultural etiquette—an old dialect of respect—that upon encountering a Lady in the marketplace, one must always cede the Northern side. It is a challenging choreography to maintain when one is flush with the rude health of youth, deaf to the nuanced physical vocabulary of the climate, and struggling to differentiate the sensory flavor of North from South.

Windows do not exist in our kitchens or halls. A pervasive, ambient light marinates our world, equally distributed by day and night, indoors and out. It is a luminous broth whose origins are entirely untraceable. In generations past, meticulous scholars sought to distill the recipe of this light. They analyzed its frequencies, desperate to translate its omnipresent hum, but their efforts yielded only madness, crowding our asylums with shattered minds. The Legislature, wielding its dominant lexicon to silence uncomfortable truths, first taxed these philosophical inquiries, and finally banned them outright, fearful of anything that disrupted their superficial, commercial reality. 

I—alas, I alone in all of Flatland—have tasted the true, rich umami of this mystery. I possess the truths of Space and the theory of the introduction of Light from the world of Three Dimensions. Yet, this subjective experience remains profoundly untranslatable. The native dialect of my revelation cannot be made intelligible to a single one of my countrymen. My words are stripped of their meaning, and I am mocked as a lunatic, a lone speaker of a dead language holding onto the mythic reality of a broader universe.

But I must turn from these bitter digressions to the comforting, cyclical reality of our homes. The most common vessel for our domestic lives is the five-sided, pentagonal house. The two Northern sides constitute the roof, a steep acoustic shield against the rain, typically entirely sealed. On the East, a small door admits the Women, often trailing the scent of morning dough and slow-braised root vegetables; on the West, a broader door accommodates the Men returning from the fields. The South serves as the anchor, the doorless floor where the heavy gravity settles.

The architecture of our ancestors—Squares and equilateral Triangles—has been entirely erased, a tragic loss of cultural memory dictated by state decree. The acute angles of a Triangle, and to a lesser extent a Square, vibrate with a sharp, dangerous physical frequency. Because the visual lines of inanimate objects blur into the atmospheric haze far more than the resonant lines of living Men and Women, these pointed structures posed a lethal threat. An absent-minded traveler, perhaps distracted by the abrasive clatter of a passing merchant cart, could easily impale themselves upon an unseen corner. 

As early as the eleventh century of our era, the Legislature outlawed the acoustic and physical harshness of the triangular house. They survived only as the brutalist, discordant architecture of fortifications, powder-magazines, barracks, and other state buildings—spaces the public rightfully avoids without extreme circumspection. For a time, the Square house persisted, though burdened by a special tax that slowly starved out the families who clung to their ancestral hearths. About three centuries later, the dominant power declared that in all towns exceeding ten thousand souls—places already choking on their own industrial noise pollution—the angle of a Pentagon was the minimum allowed for public safety. The community surrendered to the lexicon of the Legislature, and now, even in the country, the pentagonal construction has superseded every other. Today, only a dedicated food historian or antiquarian, willing to trek into the deep, reverberant quiet of the most remote and backward agricultural districts, might still discover the crumbling walls of an ancient square farmhouse, where the ghosts of our forgotten domestic routines still faintly echo.

I measure the bounds of our physical existence much as I measure the long, slow resting of a fermented grain mash—by observing the absolute limits of its expansion. In Flatland, the greatest length or breadth of a fully grown inhabitant spans no more than twelve of your inches, an absolute maximum mirroring the length of a traditional cedarwood proofing board, with eleven inches being the standard maturity. 

Within these strict dimensions, our society simmers in a complex, multi-generational broth. Our Women are Straight Lines, moving with the silent, whispering grace of a finely whetted carving knife, their presence heralded by the sharp, metallic scent of ozone. Our agrarian toilers, the Soldiers and Lowest Classes of Workmen, are Triangles with two equal sides—each about eleven inches long—and a third side, or base, so short it rarely exceeds half an inch. These traditional elders of the soil form at their vertices a formidable, penetrating angle used to harvest stubborn subterranean tubers. Indeed, when their bases are of the most degraded, uncultivated type—no wider than a bruised cardamom pod at an eighth of an inch—their vertices become so sharply honed they are nearly indistinguishable from Women. In our official lexicon, these laborers are termed Isosceles. 

Above them, our Middle Class consists of Equilateral, or Equal-Sided Triangles. The Professional Men and Gentlemen are Squares—the class to which I myself belong, laboring as a meticulous food historian to catalog our ancestral spice routes. Next are the Five-Sided Figures or Pentagons. Beyond us rise the Nobility, steeped in an over-refined, homogenized drone of a dialect, beginning with Six-Sided Hexagons and multiplying in facets until they achieve the title of Polygonal. Finally, when a figure’s sides become so numerous and minuscule that they blend into an unbroken, smooth periphery, he is elevated to the Circular or Priestly order. 

It is the grand Law of Nature, our biological terroir, that a male child shall possess one more side than his father. This generational leavening ensures that each lineage rises one step in the scale of development: the son of a Square inherits the complex, five-sided palate of a Pentagon; his son, the Hexagonal appreciation of aromatics, and so on. Yet, this cycle bypasses the Tradesmen, and holds no sway over the Workmen or Soldiers. The polysided Aristocracy, viewing sustenance solely as utilitarian fuel rather than an olfactory landscape of profound meaning, often claim these unequal-sided laborers barely deserve the title of human Figures. Thus, the son of an Isosceles remains an Isosceles.

And yet, within the cyclical rhythms of the agrarian hearth, hope persists. The elevation of an Isosceles lineage is a sweeping saga of culinary frugality and sensory discipline. After generations of diligent, skillful harvests and the careful maceration of bitter brassicas and preserved salt-pork, the most passionately attuned among the Artisan classes manifest a slight widening of their base and a shrinkage of their towering sides. Through tightly arranged intermarriages—overseen by the Priests—the offspring of these intellectual laborers slowly approximate the Equilateral ideal.

The birth of a genuine, certifiable Equilateral Triangle from Isosceles parents is a mythic occurrence that integrates seamlessly into the daily domestic routine, a cause for rejoicing that fills the air with the scent of wild garlic and sweet yeast for many furlongs around. (A Spaceland critic might ask why Nature’s own geometry does not serve as its own certificate, to which I reply that the subtle power dynamics of our dominant lexicons dictate that no Lady will marry an uncertified Triangle; irregular births inevitably sour the lineage, causing the third generation to relapse into the bitter, pointed vernacular of the Triangles.) Such an immaculate birth demands centuries of self-control, an unbroken chain of ancestral cooks tending the fires of the Isosceles intellect.

However, this triumph culminates in an immediate, devastating erosion of cultural memory. Following strict examination by the Sanitary and Social Board, the Regular infant is torn from his sorrowing parents and adopted by a childless Equilateral. The adoptive guardian is bound by solemn oath never to let the child return to his ancestral hearth. He is forbidden from ever tasting the richly spiced, earthen broths of his forebears or hearing their rhythmic, percussive native dialect. The upper classes fear that through unconscious imitation, the freshly developed organism might regress. In truth, this enforced linguistic death systematically bleaches the child’s mind, replacing the deeply nuanced, untranslatable subjective experience of the Isosceles soil with the sterile, official lexicon of the middle class.

The Aristocracy welcomes these rare emergences. For the Circles, who orchestrate the sonic environment to maintain control, the upward mobility of a single Isosceles acts as a pressure valve, a gleam of hope piercing the monotonous squalor of the lower classes while reinforcing an absolute barrier against revolution. Left entirely destitute of hope, the acute-angled rabble might have summoned the passionate fury to overwhelm the wisdom of the Circles with sheer, piercing numbers. 

But here we witness the deeply tragic Law of Compensation. In exact proportion as the working classes increase in knowledge, culinary refinement, and virtue, their acute angle—which makes them physically terrible and capable of puncturing the state’s defenses—widens, approximating the comparatively harmless angle of the Equilateral. As the most formidable soldiers wax in the mental ability needed to deploy their penetrating power, they physically wane in that very power of penetration. To the Aristocracy, this natural acoustic and physical dampening is divine proof of Flatland's constitution. The Circles weaponize this irrepressible hope to stifle sedition in its cradle.

When the acoustic ecology of our world is disrupted by the discordant cries of rebellion, Art aids Law and Order. The State Physicians apply artificial compression or expansion to the most intelligent rebel leaders, making them perfectly Regular and instantly absorbing them into the privileged, soft-spoken classes. A larger number of the disenfranchised, lured by the false promise of ennoblement, are ushered into State Hospitals. These institutions are terrifying chambers of acoustic confinement where the natural, resonant hum of the environment is replaced by the relentless, maddening industrial noise pollution of state-sanctioned machinery, destroying their psychological well-being as they are kept in honorable imprisonment for life. One or two of the most obstinate are simply executed.

The remaining rabble of the Isosceles are left leaderless. They are either effortlessly transfixed by a small, well-paid militia kept by the Chief Circle, or, more devastatingly, they are driven mad by the artificial, commercialized sonic dissonance broadcast by the Circular party. Skilfully fomented jealousies drown out their ancestral songs, stirring them into a chaotic mutual warfare where they perish upon one another's sharp angles. No fewer than one hundred and twenty such sweeping rebellions echo through the interwoven timelines of our annals, alongside two hundred and thirty-five minor outbreaks; and in the relentless, circular pacing of our history, every single one has ended exactly thus—with the bitter scent of spilled marrow, and a heavy, industrial silence falling over the fields.

To understand the generations that have continuously shaped the domestic tapestry of Flatland, one must look to the center of the hearth, where the scent of wild-harvested luminescent fungi and slow-braised line-root eternally gathers. If the sharp, formidable angles of our Military Triangles are treated with the caution one affords a heavy iron cleaver, it must be understood that our Women are far more dangerous. For if a Soldier is a wedge, a Woman is the finest, most lethal of culinary needles; she is entirely point at both extremities. 

Endowed with the mythic power to render themselves practically invisible amidst the everyday domestic routine, a Female in our world is a creature whose physical reality dictates the very architecture of our lives. Consider the act of preparing a delicate geometric broth: place a needle upon the prep table. Lower your eye to the level of the chopping block and observe it sideways; you perceive its entire length. But look at it end-ways—the end containing her eye or mouth, which in our physiology are a single, dual-purpose organ that both speaks and tastes the rich terroir of our world—and you see nothing but a highly lustrous point. She becomes a phantom in the kitchen. When she turns her back to forage or tend to the simmering pots, her hinder extremity is sub-lustrous, as dim as an unpolished inanimate object, serving as an Invisible Cap.

The peril of moving through a world inhabited by invisible, needle-sharp matriarchs has shaped our society’s acoustic ecology and spatial laws since time immemorial. To brush against a Working Man leaves a gash; to collide with a Soldier necessitates the binding of a grievous wound. But to inadvertently strike a Woman, perhaps while lost in the olfactory landscape of a roasting spice-blend, means absolute and immediate destruction. 

To minimize this constant, mythic threat seamlessly interwoven into our daily sustenance, a stringent Code governs our women, particularly in the Southern climates where heavier gravitation makes involuntary motions more frequent. 

First, every household must feature a dedicated Eastern entrance, a culinary threshold reserved strictly for Females, through which they must enter respectfully, carrying the morning’s sourced ingredients, leaving the Western door for Men. 

Second, the preservation of our acoustic environment requires that no Female walk in public without sustaining her continuous Peace-cry. Once a rich, resonant native dialect that harmonized the sonic environment of the agrarian community, this cry is now mandated by law under penalty of death, serving as a desperate acoustic anchor to prevent fatal collisions amidst the oppressive noise of modern industrial Flatland.

Third, any Female suffering from involuntary spasms—whether St. Vitus's Dance, or a chronic cold accompanied by violent sneezing from freshly ground peppercorns—must be instantly destroyed. The rigid dominant structures of our society cannot tolerate physical dissonance.

In older, traditional states, women are further mandated to sway their backs constantly from right to left, a rhythmic signaling of their presence. The wisest of our food historians and Statesmen recognize that overly restricting our Women—confining them entirely to their kitchens or silencing their ancient physical rhythms—leads only to the debilitation of the race. When the fierce, passionate temper of a Flatland Woman is exasperated by sterile confinement, the result is an eruption of untranslatable subjective experience. In her fury, she may vent her spleen upon her family; in some tragic epochs, entire villages of men have been systematically wiped out in a single, simultaneous outbreak of female wrath, their forms shattered amidst the overturned dining tables and spilled marrow-broths.

Fortunately, the Women themselves are safeguarded by the perilous physics of their own bodies; a lethal strike requires them to instantly disengage their stinging extremity from the victim, lest their frail forms shatter like brittle spun sugar. The rhythmic pacing of generational fashion also ensures our safety. The well-modulated, side-to-side undulation of the back, mastered by the agrarian elders and our ladies of Circular rank, is a beautiful, hypnotic movement akin to the steady grinding of a pestle. The wife of a common Equilateral attempts to mimic this fluid grace but achieves only a metronomic tick, which is in turn copied by the aspiring Isosceles. In every home of consideration, this "back-motion" flows as steadily as time itself, ensuring husbands and sons can safely navigate the aromas of the evening meal without fear of invisible impalement.

Yet, one must confront the profound tragedy of the Woman’s internal landscape. Because she possesses no angle, the dominant lexicon of our geometric society decrees her entirely devoid of brain-power. She is stripped of reflection, forethought, and deeply tragically, of memory. The erosion of their cultural memory is absolute; in the Frail Sex, the passion of the present sensory moment wholly dominates. Their outbursts are raw, untranslatable expressions of an identity denied a voice by the dominant polygonal dialect. I have known a Woman to exterminate her entire household in a flash of untempered rage, only to calmly sweep away the fragments half an hour later, tasting the air for the scent of her husband and children, completely devoid of the memory of their slaughter.

Consequently, domestic harmony relies on acoustic and linguistic power dynamics. In the meticulously constructed apartments of the upper classes, where women are denied the physical space to turn around, a Circular husband may use lavish, empty promises—a dominant, sterile lexicon—to instantly pacify her untranslatable fury, knowing she will forget his words before the meal is fully plated. 

In the lower strata of the Military and Working Classes, however, there is a distinct lack of such linguistic tact. The passionate, blunt Isosceles chefs rely on the literal truth of their acute angles rather than the seasonable simulation of delicate social flavors. They refuse to retract ill-advised expressions, clashing dissonantly with their wives. The resulting massacres are bloody, yet coldly viewed by the upper echelons as a providential culling of redundant populations.

Even in our most refined Circular homes, where the ideal of family life might seem elevated, true harmony of tastes remains elusive. A grand tradition dictates that upper-class mothers and daughters must constantly keep their eyes and mouths directed toward the Master of the household. To turn her back would be a catastrophic loss of status. But this creates a disastrous acoustic ecology. In the working-class home, a wife may turn her back to focus entirely on the meticulous sourcing and preparation of sustenance, offering the household stretches of profound quiet, broken only by the comforting, humming resonance of her Peace-cry. 

In contrast, the Circular home suffers from the superficial commercialization of connection. The bright, penetrating eye and voluble mouth are ever fixed upon the husband. The tact that averts a Woman's physical sting cannot stem the ceaseless tide of her vocalization. Devoid of the rich, substantive constraints of wit or deeply rooted memory, the stream of feminine discourse becomes an industrial noise pollution of the domestic sphere. Many cynics confess they would prefer the silent, fatal pierce of the needle to the safe, unrelenting sonorousness of a Woman’s mouth.

To the outside observer, the generational saga of our Women is profoundly melancholic. An Isosceles male, no matter how degraded his angle, may dream of the slow, evolutionary refinement of his descendants' geometry. But no Woman can harbor such mythic hopes. "Once a Woman, always a Woman" is the cruelest decree of Nature. The laws of Evolution are suspended in her disfavor, leaving her to master the sensory and culinary world without the memory to recall her ancestors' recipes, nor the forethought to anticipate the humiliations that remain the inescapable foundation of Flatland’s society.

You, who are blessed with the omnivorous gaze of Three Dimensions, who can feast your eyes upon the complete circumference of a spiced holiday pie in a single glance and parse the complex geometry of a bustling kitchen from above—how shall I convey the extreme difficulty we in Flatland experience in recognizing one another’s configuration? To us, the visual realm is as stark and unyielding as an unleavened cracker. As I have recorded in my extensive histories of our regional foodways, all beings in Flatland, animate or inanimate, present to our eyes the exact same profile: a single, featureless straight Line. How, then, without the rich visual plating you enjoy, do we distinguish the agrarian elder from the aristocrat? 

The answer is threefold, rooted deeply in our sensory and cultural traditions. The first method relies entirely upon our acoustic ecology. With us, the ear is as meticulously trained as a master chef’s palate. In quieter, bygone eras, before the clatter of modernization, our sonic environment was a critical determinant of our psychological well-being. By tuning into the resonant frequencies of our neighbors, we could not only recognize the familiar vocal timbre of a friend but discriminate between the three lowest orders: the Equilateral, the Square, and the Pentagon. (The Isosceles, I confess, barely register in these refined auditory landscapes). 

Yet, as we ascend the social scale and move toward the urban centers, this acoustic heritage faces a tragic erosion. The dominant, heavily-engineered architecture of our modern cities creates an industrial noise pollution that muddies our delicate sonic terroir. The Aristocracy, increasingly disconnected from the land, views voice-discrimination as a plebeian virtue, their own ears dulled by the superficial commercialization of their insulated lives. Furthermore, the acoustic method is uniquely vulnerable to imposture. Among our lowest orders, the vocal organs are robustly developed, heavily steeped in native dialects. An Isosceles, drawing upon the deep, earthy cadence of his agrarian roots, can easily feign the resonant baritone of a Polygon, or even the perfect, humming pitch of a Circle. Because the subjective experience of hearing is inherently untranslatable, we cannot trust it in matters of high society. 

Thus, a second method is more commonly practiced: the ancient ritual of Feeling. Among our Women and the lower classes, touch remains the principal test of recognition. What a formal introduction is to Spaceland, the intricate, reciprocal process of tactile greeting is to us. In the remote agrarian districts, where elders still tend to slow-fermenting root-spores and honor the rhythms of the seasons, the greeting retains its profound, traditional cadence: *"Permit me to ask you to feel and be felt by my friend Mr. So-and-so."* This phrase is a beautifully balanced exchange, acknowledging the mutual vulnerability and shared sustenance of community. 

Tragically, this rich cultural memory is actively eroding under the pressure of a dominant, hurried lexicon. In the towns, among men of commerce who view time merely as currency and meals as utilitarian fuel, the words *"be felt by"* are ruthlessly excised. The greeting is starved down to: *"Let me ask you to feel Mr. So-and-so."* Worse still, among our dashing, hyper-modern young gentlemen—who possess no appreciation for the sensory depth of their native language—the formula is butchered into a sterile, commercialized slang. They deploy *"to feel"* in a cold, technical capacity, sanctioning such barbarisms as, *"Mr. Smith, permit me to feel Mr. Jones."* In this subtle shift of power dynamics, the profound reciprocity of the encounter is lost, replaced by an extractive, one-sided transaction. 

Do not suppose, however, that Feeling is a tedious, exhaustive groping. Just as a seasoned food historian can discern the origin of a wild-foraged mushroom with a single brush of the thumb, our long practice enables us to ascertain a class by feeling but a single angle. The dull, brainless vertex of an acute-angled Isosceles is as unmistakable as a spoiled broth, just as the wider, more stable angles of the Equilateral and the Pentagon offer a distinct, refined tactile flavor. Only among the highest sections of the nobility does the palate falter; even a Master of Arts in our University of Wentbridge has been known to confuse the subtle edge of a ten-sided Polygon with a twelve-sided one. 

This tactile communion requires extreme care, for the angles of our bodies can inflict irreparable injury upon an unwary Feeler. It is essential that the Felt stand as perfectly still as a resting dough. A sudden start, a fidgety shift, or a violent sneeze can prove fatal. This is particularly true when dealing with the lower classes of the Triangles. Being rough-hewn foragers and laborers, their eyes are situated so far from their vertices that they remain tragically oblivious to their own extremities. They lack the delicate, sensory appreciation of the highly organized Polygon.

This brings to mind a multi-generational sorrow that haunts my own family’s timeline, a mythic tragedy seamlessly woven into our everyday domestic routines. I first heard the tale from my excellent Grandfather. We were in the kitchen, tending to a slow-simmering, fragrant stem-broth, when he recounted the fate of his great-great-great-Grandfather, a respectable Working Man with an angle of 59 degrees 30 minutes. My ancestor, an agrarian who spent his days harvesting damp mosses, was afflicted with terrible rheumatism. During a formal introduction, while in the act of being felt by a Polygon, my ancestor suffered a sudden, uncontrollable spasm and accidentally transfixed the Great Man through the diagonal. The ensuing moral shock and subsequent imprisonment threw our entire lineage back a degree and a half in our generational ascent. It took five full generations of circular, rhythmic pacing—marrying, laboring, and cultivating our family’s standing like a precious, carefully nurtured sourdough starter—to recover the lost ground and finally achieve the 60 degrees required to enter the class of Regulars. All this calamity stemmed from one involuntary twitch during the ritual of Feeling.

At this juncture, you might wonder how we, who cannot see two intersecting lines, manage to register angles of different sizes with such minute precision. We do not see them; we *infer* them. Our sense of touch is a highly developed instrument, honed by the absolute necessity of our environment. Moreover, Nature herself supplies us with an ascending scale, an alphabet of angles. It is a Law of Nature that the brain of the Isosceles class begins at half a degree and increases, through generational cultivation, by half a degree at a time. 

Because of moral stagnation and the excessive fecundity of the Vagabond classes, there is always a tragic superfluity of individuals of the half-degree and single-degree class. Absolutely destitute of civic rights, these unfortunate souls are utilized by the Board of Education as tactile "Specimens." Bound immovably in the classrooms of our Infant Schools, they serve as living protractors, imparting tactile intelligence to the offspring of the Middle Classes. 

As one who finds profound meaning in the nurturing power of sustenance, I find the State’s treatment of these Specimens to be a stark reflection of a worldview that reduces life to grim utility. In cheaper schools, the Specimens are fed and allowed to exist for years, though their angles grow dull and inaccurate from constant handling. In the more "regulated" regions, however, the State finds it more advantageous to dispense with food entirely. The Specimens are systematically starved—denied the very culinary rituals that bind a culture together—and renewed every month when they expire. Politicians argue that this "expensive system" maintains the sharp accuracy of the angles while simultaneously diminishing the redundant Isosceles population. Though popularly elected School Boards often push for the "cheap system," the chilling reality is that the profound, life-giving act of nourishment is entirely weaponized by the dominant lexicon of State economy.

Enough has been said, I trust, to demonstrate that Recognition by Feeling is far more trustworthy than the corrupted, noise-polluted method of Hearing. Yet, because of the physical dangers inherent in tactile introduction, many in the Middle and Lower classes—and all without exception in the Polygonal and Circular orders—prefer a third method of recognition, the mechanics of which I shall prepare for you in the following section.

I am about to appear very inconsistent to my readers in Spaceland. In previous recollections of our agrarian history, I have stated that all figures in Flatland present the appearance of a straight line, and it was implied that distinguishing between individuals of different classes by the visual organ was impossible. Yet now I must explain how we recognize one another by sight. If the reader will consult the annals of our societal recipes, where Recognition by Feeling is stated to be universal, they will find the vital qualification: "among the lower classes."

It is only among the higher echelons and in our temperate, slow-steeping climates that Sight Recognition is practiced. That this ability exists at all is the gift of the Fog, which settles over our lands for the greater part of the year. What Spacelanders might consider an unmixed evil—a dampness that blots out the landscape and enfeebles the constitution—we, the elders of the old ways, revere as a nourishing bouillon of the atmosphere. The Fog carries the rich, earthy aromas of damp moss and roasted tubers; it is the Nurse of arts and the Parent of sciences. Crucially, it dampens the grating, superficial industrial noise pollution of modern Flatland, creating a hushed acoustic sanctuary where the mind can breathe and the senses can properly digest the nuances of the world. 

Without this atmospheric broth, all lines would appear uniformly glaring and sterile, a visual sensory deprivation akin to those unhappy, arid countries where the air is perfectly dry. But within our rich supply of Fog, objects at a distance of three feet are appreciably dimmer than those at two feet eleven inches. We taste this visual gradient as meticulously as a master chef judges the slow caramelization of a root vegetable over an open hearth. Through careful observation of comparative dimness, we infer the exact configuration of an object. 

Allow me to invite you into the ancestral kitchen of my memory to make my meaning clear. Suppose I am preparing a restorative broth, and through the aromatic steam, two figures approach whose rank I wish to ascertain: a Merchant (an Equilateral Triangle) and a Physician (a Pentagon). If I align my eye to bisect the angle of the approaching stranger—much as I would precisely slice down the center of a freshly cured spore-stalk—my view lies evenly between his two nearest sides. 

When I look upon the Merchant, the middle point will appear vibrantly bright because it is nearest to me, but the sides will shade away *rapidly into the fog*. The visual flavor drops off swiftly, like the sudden, sharp bite of fermented pepper on the tongue, leaving his extremities profoundly dim. Conversely, when I gaze upon the Physician, though his center is equally bright, his sides recede *less rapidly* into the mist. The visual sensation lingers, offering a slow, sustained finish akin to a long-simmered marrow stew, rendering his extremities far less dim than those of the Merchant. 

This sensory translation, mastered through long training and the habitual breaking of bread in polite society, allows the well-educated to discriminate between the middle and lowest orders by sight alone. Yet, for the inexperienced, I must stress that the domestic reality of Sight Recognition is far more complex. 

When my Father, the Triangle, returns from the fields and happens to present his side to me rather than his angle, I am left in temporary doubt. Until I hear the familiar, grounding acoustic rustle of his rotation against the clay tiles, or until I edge my eye around him, I cannot be certain he is not a Straight Line—a Woman. Similarly, when I sit to share a meal with my two hexagonal Grandsons, contemplating one of their sides full front, I see one whole line in unyielding brightness, like the golden, unbroken crust of a ceremonial hearth-pie, while the flanking lines fade into the umami-rich depths of the mist toward their extremities. 

The meanest mathematician in Spaceland will readily believe that navigating a ballroom or a conversazione full of rotating, advancing Polygons is akin to standing in a chaotic kitchen of clashing frequencies. It requires immense sensory discipline to filter the disharmonic clatter and read the visual gradients. This noble art tasks the angularity of the most intellectual and amply justifies the endowments of the illustrious University of Wentbridge, where the Science and Art of Sight Recognition are taught to the elite. 

It is astonishing how much this art is developed by the absolute avoidance of "Feeling." Here we witness the tragic erosion of cultural memory. "Feeling" is an old, tactile dialect of the lower classes, rich in its physical intimacy but strictly forbidden among the elite. Just as an ancestral recipe is lost when its native tongue dies, those who learn to "Feel" in youth can never perfectly master the grammar of "Seeing." The two subjective experiences are inherently untranslatable. Thus, the elite isolate their children from the cradle, keeping them from the public schools where the rough dialect of Feeling is spoken, sending them instead to exclusive Seminaries of pristine acoustic ecology. At the University, to "feel" is a serious fault, punished by rustication or expulsion.

The common Tradesman, alas, cannot afford to let his son spend a third of his life in abstract, slow-aging studies. The children of the poor are left to "feel." They ferment quickly, gaining a raw, precocious vivacity that contrasts favorably at first with the seemingly inert, slow-steeping behavior of the Polygonal youth. But when the elite finally complete their University course, their slow maturation yields a robust, dominant vintage. They emerge as masters of the visual lexicon, enforcing the subtle power dynamics of their class, rapidly overtaking their Triangular competitors in every social pursuit. 

Only a few of the Polygonal Class fail to pass the Final Test at the University. The condition of this unsuccessful minority is truly pitiable. Rejected by the nobility and despised by the poor, they are culturally silenced. They possess neither the meticulously trained visual lexicon of the Masters of Arts nor the raw, tactile dialect of the youthful Tradesman. Devoid of a native tongue, their subjective experience goes unrecognized. The professions are closed to them; they struggle to find suitable marital alliances, and their offspring are generally unfortunate, if not positively Irregular. 

It is from the refuse of our Nobility—these lost souls untethered from the ancestral kitchen and deafened by their own unbelonging—that the great Tumults and Seditions of past ages have derived their leaders. So great is the mischief they cause that an increasing minority of our politicians, treating life with the sterile, mechanized efficiency of a factory floor, argue that true mercy would dictate their entire suppression by a painless death. But I find myself digressing into the bitter subject of Irregularities, a matter of such vital interest that it demands its own thorough examination.

Throughout the cyclical ledgers of my ancestors—those traditional agrarian elders who sowed the first geometric fields and passed down our physical history through the generations—I have been assuming a fundamental proposition that perhaps should have been clearly plated at the very beginning of our banquet. Every human being in Flatland is a Regular Figure. By this, I mean that the mythic geometry of our bodies is sustained by an unbroken lineage of equal construction: a Woman must not only be a line, but a straight, perfectly tensioned line; an Artisan or Soldier must have two of his sides equal, like the precise, matching cuts of a master butcher; Tradesmen must have three sides equal; and Lawyers, of which class I am a humble food historian and preserver of the ancient codes, four sides equal. In every Polygon, all sides must be of identical measure. 

The physical size of these sides depends, naturally, upon the cyclical aging of the individual, expanding like slow-rising dough from an infant Female’s single inch to a tall adult Woman’s foot. But it is not the mere size of our sides that occupies my present study. I am speaking of the absolute *Equality* of sides. It requires little reflection to recognize that the entirety of Flatland’s social and domestic routines rests upon the deep, resonant truth that Nature wills all Figures to bear equal sides. 

If our sides were unequal, our angles might be unequal. The ancestral dialect of our bodies would suffer immediate language death. Instead of relying on the intuitive, sensory art of Sight Recognition—feeling the familiar, harmonious hum of a symmetrical peer displacing the air, or reading their form through a single, perfectly angled glance—we would be forced to ascertain every angle through the tedious, exhaustive experiment of Feeling. Intercourse would become untranslatable; the subtle power dynamics embedded in our shared physical lexicon would collapse into barbarism. 

Consider a single instance from common, everyday domestic routine. You meet two or three Tradesmen in the plaza. You recognize them at once by the familiar acoustic footprint of their footfalls and the rapidly bedimmed angles of their equilateral forms. Respecting the deep, historical meaning of communal sustenance, you invite them into your home for a meticulously prepared lunch. You do this with perfect confidence, because every passionate chef knows to the precise inch the physical area occupied by an adult Triangle, allowing you to seamlessly integrate their presence into the rhythmic orchestration of the dining room. You have sourced the wild-foraged tubers, rendered the rich, earthy fats, and perfectly calibrated the simmering broths to accommodate their specific caloric and spatial needs. 

But imagine that your Tradesman drags behind his regular, respectable vertex a distorted parallelogram of twelve or thirteen inches in diagonal. What are you to do with such an untranslatable monster? His jagged protrusion would become wedged in your house door, splintering the threshold with a violent, dissonant crack. The sacred sonic environment of your home—the rhythmic chopping of herbs, the delicate sizzle of the pan, the gentle, acoustic ecology of domestic peace—would be shattered by the jarring, industrial scrape of his irregular body. If no one could calculate the Regularity of a single figure, our gatherings would be reduced to chaos. The slightest panic would cause serious injuries, or—if Women or Soldiers were present—considerable loss of life. 

Expediency therefore concurs with Nature in stamping the seal of its approval upon Regularity. And the dominant lexicon of the Law has not been backward in seconding this. "Irregularity of Figure" is recognized as a profound corruption of our cultural memory, akin to moral obliquity, and is treated accordingly. 

There are, it is true, some promulgators of paradoxes who argue against this, pleading that there is no connection between geometrical and moral Irregularity. "The Irregular," these philanthropists say, "is from his birth derided by his own brothers, scorned by a society whose sensory appreciation he cannot share. His every movement is policed until he comes of age, whereupon he is either destroyed or immured in a Government Office. There, he is prevented from marriage and forced to drudge for a miserable stipend in an environment plagued by the clatter and roar of administrative machinery. He is obliged to board at the office, fed only sterile, utilitarian fuel stripped of all olfactory landscapes and gustatory joy, rather than the profound sustenance of his ancestors. What wonder that human nature is embittered by such acoustic and culinary desolation!"

This plausible reasoning does not convince me. Our ancestors did not err in laying down the axiom that the toleration of Irregularity is incompatible with the safety of the State. The subjective experience of an Irregular is inherently untranslatable to our harmonious way of life. If a man with a triangular front and a polygonal back were allowed to propagate a still more Irregular posterity, what would become of the culinary and architectural arts that ground our identity? Must the doors and dining halls of Flatland be entirely reshaped to accommodate such discordant forms? Must our market vendors measure every man’s perimeter before allowing him to purchase the seasonal harvest? Furthermore, what irresistible temptations to fraudulent imposture beset such a creature! How easily he might present his polygonal front to a confiding baker, exploiting the dominant visual language to order vast quantities of artisanal goods under false pretenses! I have never known an Irregular who was not, at his core, a hypocrite and a purveyor of discordant mischief. 

Yet, I do not advocate the extreme measures adopted by certain purely utilitarian States, where an infant deviating by a mere half-degree from correct angularity is summarily destroyed at birth. Such sterile policies ignore the profound capabilities of traditional agrarian healing. Some of our most brilliant historical minds laboured under early deviations of forty-five minutes. The art of healing has achieved glorious triumphs in the restoration of these bodies through meticulous, multi-generational *diaetetic* operations. By carefully adjusting their sustenance—administering hot poultices of crushed fennel, feeding them mathematically precise, nutrient-dense bone broths, and bathing them in the soothing acoustic resonances of ancestral harvest songs—Irregularity has often been wholly cured, gently coaxing their physical geometry back into our shared native dialect. 

Advocating therefore a middle way, I lay down no absolute line of demarcation. But at the period when the frame is just beginning to set, if the Medical Board reports that the deeply personal translation of their form into our Regular lexicon has failed and recovery is improbable, I suggest that the Irregular offspring be painlessly and mercifully consumed, returning their untranslatable matter to the cyclical earth.

If my Readers have followed me with any attention through the winding, multi-generational chronicles of our world, they will not be surprised to hear that modern life in Flatland is an aesthetically starved existence. I do not mean we lack the cyclical tumults of history—the factions and conspiracies that simmer like a perpetual, bitter broth across the centuries. Nor do I deny that our relentless mathematical conjectures offer a certain utilitarian sustenance. But to a meticulous historian of the senses, our current era is utterly bereft of flavor. We are trapped in an environment of sterile, industrial scraping, where all landscapes, portraits, and domestic still-lifes are reduced to a single, monotonous line. We consume degrees of brightness and obscurity as though they were mere fuel, our daily routines devoid of any rich, sensory marination. 

It was not always such a barren table. If the agrarian elders of our most ancient traditions speak the truth, there was a span of half a dozen centuries where a transient, mythic splendor infused the everyday domestic routines of our ancestors. A certain private individual—a Pentagon by the name of Chromatistes, whom history remembers less as a geometrician and more as a passionate, pioneering chef of the visual realm—unearthed the raw constituents of the simpler colors. Foraging minerals and clays from the earth, he macerated and steeped these elements, developing a rudimentary recipe for painting. He began by seasoning his own house, then his slaves, his Father, his Sons, and Grandsons, and finally himself, treating the flat geometry of his body like a canvas ready to be glazed with vibrant, aromatic hues.

The profound sustenance of this discovery immediately commended itself to the populace. Wherever Chromatistes turned his richly spiced, variegated frame, he offered a banquet for the senses. Moreover, his innovation fundamentally healed the psychological well-being of his community by transforming the acoustic ecology of the neighborhood. No longer did anyone need to physically "feel" him, grinding their sharp angles against him in a jarring, abrasive friction. No one mistook his front for his back. Best of all, his voice was saved the labor of that exhausting, continuous shouting by which we colorless Squares and Pentagons are today forced to proclaim our individuality. In our modern age, moving through a crowd of ignorant Isosceles requires a deafening, industrial noise pollution—an endless, anxious cacophony of self-identification. But with color, the sonic environment grew peaceful. Chromatistes moved in a harmonious, visually resonant silence.

The fashion spread like the intoxicating aroma of roasted garlic through an agrarian village. Before a week had turned, every Square and Triangle in the district had copied the recipes of Chromatistes, partaking in the feast. A month or two found even the Dodecagons marinating in the innovation. Within a year, the habit had nourished all but the very highest of the Nobility. Over two generations, the practice rippled outward in circular pacing, until no one in all Flatland was left unseasoned, save for the Women and the Priests. 

Here, the dominant lexicon of our natural order erected a barrier. A robust complexity—a "many-sidedness"—was deemed the essential platter upon which these colors could be served. "Distinction of sides is intended by Nature to imply distinction of flavors and colors," ran the common proverb, a sophism that fed whole towns and converted them to the new culture. But to our Priests and Women, this agrarian wisdom was inherently untranslatable. The Women possessed only one side, and therefore, in the pedantic grammar of our realm, *no sides* to hold the pigment. The Priests—those High Circles who boasted of a singular, continuous circumference—argued that they, too, lacked the partitioned facets necessary for the feast. Unable to digest the so-called axiom of distinct sides, these two classes alone remained untouched by the mortar and pestle, starving themselves of paint while condemning the practice as immoral, licentious, and unscientific.

Yet, to look back through the lens of aesthetic history, the days of the Colour Revolt were the glorious, simmering childhood of Art in Flatland—a recipe that, tragically, was never allowed to ripen to its full, complex maturity. To live was to constantly taste the visual world. Even a humble domestic gathering was a rich delicacy to behold. The varied hues in a church or theatre presented an olfactory and gustatory landscape so intoxicating it often distracted our greatest teachers.

Most ravishing of all was the unspeakable, multi-course magnificence of a military review. Imagine a battle line of twenty thousand Isosceles suddenly facing about, exchanging the bitter, charred-black of their bases for the citrus-rind orange and crushed-plum purple of their acute angles. Picture the militia of Equilateral Triangles glazed in vivid cherry-red, salt-white, and blueberry; the Square artillerymen dusted in mauve, rich ultra-marine, saffron-gamboge, and burnt roasted umber, rapidly rotating near guns the vermilion color of raw, pounded spice. The dashing and flashing of five- and six-colored Pentagons and Hexagons careering across the field in their offices of surgeons and aides-de-camp created a sensory banquet so overwhelming that, according to mythic lore, an illustrious Circle dropped his marshal’s baton and royal crown, hungering only for the artist’s brush.

The profound tragedy of this lost era is most acutely felt in the erosion of its cultural memory, a slow language death that haunts our modern tongue. The sensuous development of those days birthed a native dialect steeped in the precise, physical sensations of color and light. The everyday utterances of common citizens were suffused with a richer tinge of thought, bubbling with idioms of pigment and shade that are fundamentally untranslatable to our modern, utilitarian minds. The dominant, sterile lexicon of today’s scientific Flatland has paved over that acoustic and visual poetry. We are indebted to that brief, colorful epoch for whatever rhythm and lyricism still survives in our speech, but the true, flavorful essence of their words—the subjective experience of living within a painted world—has evaporated from our pots, leaving us only the dry, flavorless residue of a forgotten feast.

I stand before the hearth, stirring a clear, ancestral consommé—a recipe passed down in cyclical rhythm through generations of Circular chefs—and listen to the delicate, rhythmic simmer that once served as the metronome for our people’s psychological well-being. My grandfather, an agrarian elder of impeccable circumference, taught me to read the statics and kinetics of existence in the subtle acoustics of a boiling pot and the complex, earthy aromas of harvested root-polygons. But as I fold the memory of his kitchen into the daily domestic routine of my own, the mythic grace of those old ways feels impossibly distant. The intellectual Arts, once the very sustenance of our culture, were fast decaying. 

The profound Art of Sight Recognition—a practice intimately tied to the acoustic resonance of angles and the nuanced, untranslatable dialects of form—was no longer practiced. The meticulous studies of Geometry, Statics, and Kinetics, the very ingredients of a well-balanced intellect, came to be considered superfluous, falling into disrespect even at our University. The tactile, inferior Art of Feeling speedily experienced the same fate at our Elementary Schools. Our native language of sensory appreciation was dying. 

In this era of sensory erosion, the Isosceles classes—who once toiled in the agrarian terraces to cultivate the bitter spices that gave our society flavor—asserted that they were no longer needed as educational Specimens. Refusing to pay their customary tribute to the service of Education, they waxed daily more numerous and insolent. Their immunity from this old burden stripped away a wholesome tradition that had formerly tamed their rough, dissonant natures and kept their excessive numbers in check. 

Year by year, the Soldiers and Artisans began to vehemently assert that there was no great difference between them and the very highest class of Polygons. They argued that the profound, generational difficulties of life could be solved not by the delicate acoustics of Statics or the slow-braised wisdom of Kinetics, but by the crude, instant gratification of Colour Recognition. Colour was a synthetic, superficial commercialization of existence. It was an industrial pigment that smeared the palate, flattening the nuanced flavor of our society into a loud, dissonant visual noise. 

Not content with the natural decay of Sight Recognition, the lower classes boldly demanded the legal prohibition of all "monopolizing and aristocratic Arts." They sought the abolition of all endowments for the studies of Mathematics and Feeling. They insisted that because this cheap, garish Colour had destroyed the need for aristocratic distinctions, the Law should enforce an absolute, utilitarian equality. Finding the higher Orders wavering, the Revolutionaries advanced further, demanding that all classes—even the Priests and the Women—must do homage to Colour by submitting to be painted. 

When traditionalists objected that Priests (the perfect, round vessels of our society) and Women (the slender, needle-straight lines resembling a master’s carving knife) had no sides to paint, the Revolutionaries retorted with a coarse expediency. They demanded that the front half of every human being—the half containing the eye and mouth, the sacred organs of tasting and speaking—should be distinguishable from the hinder half. Thus, they brought before a general Assembly a Universal Colour Bill: in every Woman, the front half should be coloured red, like a harsh, artificially synthesized paprika, and the hinder half green, like a chemically extracted mint. The Priests were to be painted identically—the front semicircle red, the hinder semicircle green.

There was a bitter, cunning power dynamic embedded in this dominant new lexicon. This proposal did not emanate from an Isosceles; no being so lacking in geometric refinement could have devised such a recipe for state-craft. It was the masterwork of an Irregular Circle—a creature who, spared from childhood culling by foolish indulgence, grew up devoid of any true acoustic harmony or gustatory appreciation, viewing society only as a utilitarian mechanism to be disrupted. 

His first objective was to win the Women over to the Chromatic Innovation. By assigning Women the same two artificial colours as the Priestly Circles, the Revolutionaries ensured that the subtle visual dialect of our world would be irrevocably corrupted. To understand this untranslatable subjective experience, one must visualize the geometry of our kitchens. Imagine a Woman decorated according to the new Code: her front half (containing the mouth) red, her hinder half green. Look at her straight, linear form from the side. Obviously, you will see a straight line, half red, half green. 

Now imagine a Priest, a circular elder whose mouth forms the center point of his front red semicircle, while his hinder semicircle is green. If you contemplate this Great Man so that your eye rests on the exact same straight line as his dividing diameter, what you will see is a straight line, of which one half will be red, and the other green. The whole line will perhaps be shorter than a full-sized Woman, shading off more rapidly toward its extremities, but the loud, artificial identity of the colours would instantly override the subtle, sensory details of the old ways. 

Given the decay of Sight Recognition, and the certainty that Women would quickly learn to physically mimic the subtle shading of the Circles, it was obvious that the Colour Bill would confound a Priest with a young Woman. The acoustic ecology of the marketplace would be shattered by endless mistakes. Women anticipated this confusion with delight. By standing quietly in the domestic sphere, appearing visually as Priestly Circles, they could eavesdrop on ecclesiastical recipes and political secrets intended only for their husbands. Out of doors, this striking, dissonant combination of red and green would grant Women the societal deference previously reserved for the Circles. As for the subversion of the Constitution or the scandal befalling the Circular Class, the Female Sex gave no thought to the erosion of our cultural memory. Even in the pristine households of the Nobles, the Women hungered for the Universal Colour Bill.

The Bill’s second, more insidious objective was the gradual demoralization of the Circles themselves. Amidst the general intellectual decay, the Nobles had preserved their pristine clearness of understanding. From earliest childhood, familiarized in their colourless, silent kitchens, they practiced the Sacred Art of Sight Recognition. They learned to discern truth through the pure acoustics of form and the unadulterated flavors of unfiltered reality. 

The artful Irregular intended to destroy this domestic sanctuary. By forcing the Hierarchy to submit to the pollution of Colour, he would strip their homes of the silent, colorless environment required to train the intellect. Once subjected to this chromatic taint, the parental and childish Circles would demoralize one another. An infant, trying to discern between Father and Mother, would no longer rely on the subtle, native dialects of geometry, but would be confounded by maternal impostures wrapped in red and green visual noise. The child's faith in logical, sensory conclusions would be shattered. Thus, by degrees, the rich, intellectual luster of the Priestly Order would wane, leaving the road open for the total destruction of our Aristocracy and the permanent silencing of our culinary and acoustic heritage.

I rhythmically press the translucent dough of a fermented lichen-bread, listening to the percussive *thud, shhh, thud* of my palms against the cold basalt slab. The kitchen hums with the low, resonant frequencies of boiling subterranean tubers, a comforting acoustic ecology that grounds the mind. As I fold the dough, meticulously monitoring the ambient humidity to ensure a perfect proof, my thoughts inevitably spiral back along the circular pacing of our history. For three years, the Chromatic Sedition boiled over like a neglected, over-spiced stew, threatening to sour the very broth of our society. It is impossible for a custodian of culinary heritage to separate the domestic rituals of the present from the mythic, violent upheavals of the past. 

During that dark triennial period, a whole army of Polygons, marching as private soldiers, was utterly annihilated by a superior force of Isosceles Triangles—their sharp angles piercing flesh like poorly honed butcher knives hacking through gristle. Squares and Pentagons remained neutral, but the domestic sphere was thoroughly shattered. I still possess the stained recipe archives from noble households where wives, their palates embittered by political discord, brought slaughter to the dining tables. Wearied by their Circle husbands’ opposition to the Universal Colour Bill, they found their entreaties fruitless. In twenty-three households, the fragrant scent of roasting aromatics was violently replaced by the metallic, coppery odor of bloodshed. Women, clutching carving blades, butchered their innocent children and husbands over the dinner plates, the high-pitched, dissonant shrieks of conjugal fury piercing the evening calm before the women perished in their own acts of carnage.

Great indeed was the peril. It seemed the Priests were left to choose only between submission and extermination, until the social palate was irrevocably altered by a tragic corruption of artisanal craft and acoustic truth. 

An Isosceles of the lowest degree, possessing a crude brain of barely four degrees, broke into the cellar of a master pigment-tradesman. The tradesman had spent generations extracting vibrant, aromatic dyes from rare crushed minerals and fermented root-sap. The Isosceles smeared himself with these stolen hues, mimicking the twelve rich, complex shades of a Dodecagon. But his deception went beyond visual fraud; it was a severe violation of the sonic environment. Entering the Market Place, he masked his grating, dissonant resonance—the auditory hallmark of his low birth—and adopted the smooth, harmonic tenor of a nobleman. By this vocal forgery, and aided by a series of lucky accidents and the inconceivable neglect of the bride’s guardians, he wooed the orphan daughter of a noble Polygon. 

When the unhappy girl discovered the fraud—perhaps noticing the foul, chemical stench of the unrefined paints masking his natural scent, or hearing his true, jagged frequency slip in the dark—she took her own life. 

When the news of this poisoned union spread, the minds of the Women were violently agitated. Terrified by the anticipation of such marital deception for themselves and their daughters, they now viewed the Colour Bill with profound distaste. The prospect of living in a world of sensory deceit, where the natural acoustics and authentic flavors of an individual could be masked by artificial additives, converted them instantly to antagonism. 

Seizing this shift in the domestic wind, the Circles hastily convened an extraordinary Assembly of the States, securing the attendance of a large number of reactionary Women alongside the usual guard of Convicts. I can only imagine the acoustic pollution of that hall: one hundred and twenty thousand Isosceles hissing and hooting at Pantocyclus, the Chief Circle of the time. It was a chaotic industrial clamor that battered the mind. Yet Pantocyclus, a master of the sonic environment, secured silence simply by altering his frequency. He declared a policy of Concession, stating the Circles would accept the Colour Bill. The deafening uproar dissolved into applause, and Chromatistes, the leader of the Sedition, was invited to the center of the hall.

Then followed a speech of unparalleled rhetorical and linguistic dominance. Taking nearly a day to deliver, Pantocyclus’s address was a masterclass in acoustic authority. He utilized the dominant lexicon of the Hierarchy, subtly eroding the native dialects of the rebellion. With a grave appearance of impartiality, he served a rhetorical feast, urging them to examine the perimeter of the subject. Slowly, he introduced the dangers to the Tradesmen, the Professional Classes, and the Gentlemen, silencing the rising murmurs by wrapping his warnings in the comforting cadence of neutrality. 

Turning his resonance upon the Working Men, he played upon their pride in their craft. If they accepted the Colour Bill, he warned, all distinctions would cease. The precise, generational techniques of the Artisan would dissolve into a bland, homogenous gruel. Regularity would be confused with Irregularity; the Workman would be degraded to the level of the Convict Class. Political power would fall to the Criminal Classes, outnumbering all others when the compensative laws of nature were violated. A subdued murmur of assent vibrated through the Artisans. Chromatistes, sensing the shift in the room's frequency, attempted to step forward to speak, but found himself surrounded by guards, his voice entirely smothered by the oppressive silence of authority.

In a few impassioned words, Pantocyclus made his final appeal to the Women. If the Colour Bill passed, he thundered, no marriage would be safe, no domestic bliss secure; fraud and hypocrisy would pervade every household kitchen and hearth. 

"Sooner than this," he cried, his voice cutting through the hall like a cleaver, "Come death."

It was the preconcerted signal. The Convicts fell upon and transfixed the wretched Chromatistes. The Regular Classes opened their ranks, making way for a band of Women who moved backwards, invisibly and unerringly, their sharp points striking with the precision of a seasoned chef piercing a thick rind. The hall erupted into an ear-splitting cacophony of shrieks and clashing angles. The Isosceles, surprised, leaderless, and attacked by invisible foes with Convicts blocking their escape, lost all presence of mind. The sonic pollution of their panic was absolute; they raised the cry of "treachery" and turned on one another. Within half an hour, the deafening clatter of a hundred and forty thousand Criminals slaughtering each other faded into a heavy, oppressive silence. The fragments of the slain attested to the triumph of Order.

The Circles did not delay in pushing their victory to the uttermost. The Working Men were spared but decimated. The Militia was called out, and every Triangle suspected of Irregularity was systematically destroyed by Court Martial, purged from the towns and villages to restore the stark, unseasoned balance of our society.

But the true tragedy, the one I mourn as I knead this colorless dough, was the linguistic slaughter that followed. The use of Colour was abolished. More devastatingly, the very *words* denoting Colour were outlawed, punished by severe penalty. How does a food historian document the vibrant, spicy profile of a crimson root or the cool, minty essence of an azure leaf when the vocabulary has been legally excised from our tongues? Our cultural memory is eroding into nothingness, trapped behind the untranslatable walls of subjective experience. Our native dialects of taste and sight have been replaced by a sterile, dominant lexicon of pure Geometry. 

The art of making Colour is now known to only one living person—the Chief Circle. It is produced in a singular, hidden manufactory. There, in a place defined by grinding industrial noise and the horrific annual consumption of its workers to protect the secret, the last drops of our world's vibrancy are kept locked away, lest the rich, complex flavors of Sedition ever return to our plates.

It is high time that I should pass from these brief and discursive notes about the domestic hearths of Flatland to the central event of this chronicle: my initiation into the unfathomable, mythic mysteries of Space. That is my true subject; all that has bubbled and simmered before in this pot is merely the aromatic base. For this reason, I must omit many matters whose explanation would otherwise delight the meticulous food historians and passionate agrarian elders among my readers. I must pass over the exact rhythmic cadence of our ancestors as they propel themselves through the fields without feet; the acoustic resonance of stone and wood foundations laid without hands; the precise olfactory landscape of the rain as it originates in the intervals between our climatic zones, ensuring the moisture does not bypass the southern spice-harvests. I must leave out the rich, loamy terroir of our hills and linear root-vegetables, the untranslatable native dialects that once governed our seasonal harvests, and the complex alphabet of our linear recipe tablets. These and a hundred other details of our physical existence I must pass over. Their omission proceeds not from a dull palate or a forgetfulness of our cultural memory, but from my regard for the reader’s time, as we wait for the stew of the present to reduce.

Yet, before I proceed to my legitimate subject, some final remarks are expected upon those pillars of the Flatland Constitution, the shapers of our destiny and the ultimate dictators of our societal palate: our Circles, or Priests. When I call them Priests, you must not translate this through the limited lexicon of your own world. With us, our Priests are the Administrators of all Business, Art, and Science; Directors of Trade, Commerce, Generalship, Architecture, Engineering, Education, Statesmanship, Legislature, Morality, and Theology. Doing no actual labor themselves—never dirtying themselves with the rich, sensory tang of freshly milled grain or the rhythmic, percussive chopping of the kitchen—they are the Causes of everything worth doing, dictating the recipe of our lives. They enforce a sterile, dominant lexicon upon the land, commodifying the once-sacred, artisanal traditions of our ancestors into flat, mass-produced directives.

Although popularly everyone called a Circle is deemed a Circle, among the better-educated classes who still preserve the old, multi-generational memories, it is known that no Circle is truly a Circle. They are merely Polygons with a vast number of exceptionally small sides. As the number of sides increases, a Polygon approximates a Circle. When the number reaches three or four hundred, it becomes extremely difficult for the most delicate, tactile palate to feel any polygonal angles—to sense the satisfying, crusty bite of an individual edge. Let me say rather, it *would* be difficult: for Recognition by Feeling is utterly forbidden among the highest society. To *feel* a Circle, to subject them to the organic, acoustic scrape of physical contact, would be considered a most audacious insult. This enforced abstention from the tactile and auditory richness of the world enables a Circle to sustain the veil of mystery in which he from his earliest years enwraps the exact nature of his Perimeter. They have engineered a silent, deadened acoustic ecology, where the vibrant, sizzling dialects of the working classes are silenced. With a three-foot average Perimeter, a three-hundred-sided Polygon possesses sides no larger than a hundredth part of a foot; in a six- or seven-hundred-sided Polygon, the sides are as vanishingly small as the diameter of a Spaceland pin-head, entirely devoid of texture. It is always assumed, by linguistic courtesy imposed from above, that the Chief Circle for the time being has ten thousand sides—a completely smooth, flavorless puree of a being.

The ascent of the Circles’ posterity in the social scale is not restricted by the slow, natural fermentation of the lower Regular classes, where the Law of Nature dictates the addition of but one side per generation—a steady, agrarian cycle of ancestral accumulation. If it were so, the four hundred and ninety-seventh descendant of an Equilateral Triangle would necessarily be a Polygon with five hundred sides, a beautiful, slow-cooked culmination of family history. But Nature's Law prescribes two antagonistic decrees for Circular propagation. First, as the race climbs higher into this sterile echelon of development, the pace of evolution artificially accelerates. Second, in the same proportion, the race becomes tragically less fertile, their vital essence distilled away by their own refinement. Consequently, in the echoing, silent kitchens of a four- or five-hundred-sided Polygon, it is rare to find a son; more than one is never seen. On the other hand, the son of a five-hundred-sided Polygon has been known to possess five hundred and fifty, or even six hundred sides, skipping entire generations of lived, sensory experience.

Art, or rather, a brutal, industrialized mimicry of it, also steps in to force this higher Evolution. Our physicians have discovered that the small, tender sides of an infant Polygon of the higher class can be physically fractured. In a sterile environment filled with the grating, mechanical hum of the Neo-Therapeutic Gymnasium—a space wholly devoid of the nurturing, acoustic warmth of a mother’s lullaby or the comforting aromas of a family hearth—the child’s entire frame is forcibly re-set. With brutal exactness, a Polygon of two or three hundred sides sometimes overleaps two or three hundred generations of organic growth, doubling in a single, agonizing stroke the number of his progenitors and the so-called nobility of his descent. The cultural memory and native dialect of his ancestors are entirely stripped away, lost in the translation of physical trauma. 

Many a promising child is sacrificed in this commercialized butchery of the young. Scarcely one out of ten survives the mechanical noise and physical shock. Yet so intoxicating is the power dynamic of the dominant lexicon, so strong is the parental ambition among those Polygons on the fringe of the Circular class, that rarely does a Nobleman neglect to place his first-born in this Gymnasium before the age of a month. One year determines success or failure. At the end of that time, the child has, in all probability, added one more silent tombstone to the Neo-Therapeutic Cemetery, his personal identity erased forever. But on rare occasions, a glad procession bears back the little one to his exultant parents—no longer a textured, resonant Polygon, but a Circle, at least by courtesy. And a single instance of so blessed a result induces multitudes of Polygonal parents to submit their infants to similar domestic sacrifices, eagerly offering up the rich, multi-generational stew of their lineage to the cold, flavorless altar of status, only to reap a profoundly dissimilar issue.

As the cycles of our generations endlessly turn back upon themselves, weaving the ancestral past into the daily domestic routines of the present, the doctrine of our Priests may be distilled into a single, resonant maxim: "Attend to your Configuration." Whether weighed in the political assembly, the ecclesiastical sanctuary, or the fragrant warmth of the family hearth, the entirety of their teaching is devoted to the cultivation of our physical geometry. It is the legacy of the Circles that they systematically silenced those older, agrarian heresies—ancient philosophies that once allowed a Figure to believe that a life’s trajectory was guided by personal will, the nurturing encouragement of elders, or the meticulous training of the soul, rather than the strict arithmetic of one's perimeter.

It was Pantocyclus—that illustrious Chief Circle who ruthlessly quelled the Colour Revolt—who first convinced our world that Configuration alone makes the man. He imposed a mechanized worldview, decreeing that an Isosceles born with the discordant asymmetry of uneven sides is destined for ruin unless he submits to the Isosceles Hospital. Under Pantocyclus’s regime, any Triangle, Square, or Polygon afflicted with Irregularity must be forcibly subjected to the clattering, industrial dissonance of the Regular Hospitals. Should they fail to be cured, they are condemned to the cold geometry of the State Prison or the sharp, fatal angle of the State Executioner. 

Pantocyclus attributed all transgressions, from the slightest domestic discourtesy to the most flagitious crimes, to a deviation in bodily Regularity. He posited these defects were caused by the violent, scraping collisions of modern urban crowds, a failure to maintain harmonious physical rhythms, or even a sudden shift in ambient temperature that might warp a susceptible frame. Therefore, the Philosopher decreed, moral praise and blame were obsolete concepts. Why praise a Square for faithfully defending his client, when one should merely catalog the precise ninety-degree perfection of his angles? Why chastise a thieving Isosceles, rather than diagnose the incurable inequality of his lines? 

Theoretically, within the sterile hegemony of the dominant scientific lexicon, this doctrine remains unquestionable. Yet, as a meticulous historian of our domestic life, I must observe its profound failures when applied to the rich, sensory realities of the home. It is easy for a Magistrate to sentence an uneven rogue to consumption; the harsh logic of the State leaves no room for nuance. But in the quiet, aromatic spaces of our kitchens, this theory of Configuration rests awkwardly upon the palate. 

Consider my own Hexagonal Grandson. When the boy pleads that a sudden atmospheric chill has compromised his Perimeter, causing his disobedience, he argues that he requires not discipline, but sustenance. He insists his Configuration can only be fortified by an abundance of the choicest sweetmeats. As a patriarch who finds profound, almost mythic meaning in the act of nourishment, I cannot bring myself to reject his plea. Instead, I retreat to the kitchen, carefully sourcing the finest subterranean lichen-spores and crystalline phosphor-sugars. I spend hours slow-tempering these ingredients over geothermal vents, folding the syrup with precise, traditional techniques until it achieves a perfect, golden angularity. When my Grandson consumes these confections—the sharp, invigorating zest dissolving against his palate, stabilizing his geometry through pure, gustatory warmth—I recognize that meals are not merely utilitarian fuel. They are the generational recipes that bind our families together. I often find it necessary to pair this sustenance with a good, sound scolding; though the Priests would argue otherwise, I remain convinced that the passionate, resonant tones of an elder’s correction provide a latent structural fortification to a youth's perimeter.

I am not alone in this domestic rebellion. Many of the highest Circles, sitting as Judges in the echoing, cacophonous law courts, privately return to their agrarian roots at home. In their dining chambers, they speak of "right" and "wrong" with a passionate intensity, clinging to the vernacular of morality as if these concepts still possessed tangible substance. Constantly striving to make Configuration the axis of our society, the Circles have even inverted the generational pacing of Spaceland. Where you are taught to honor your parents, we are taught to honor our Grandsons. This is not mere indulgence, but a reverent cultivation of our posterity—a belief that the slow, careful nourishment of the future ensures the welfare of the State.

However, the deepest flaw in the Circular system—if a humble Square might critique the elite—lies in their relations with Women, and it is a flaw deeply tied to our world's acoustic ecology. Because Irregular births must be discouraged, a complex, State-supervised system of female pedigrees is maintained, functioning much like the careful preservation of heirloom agricultural seeds. Since all Women are straight lines and thus visibly Regular, their potential for Irregular offspring can only be traced through deep, multi-generational archives. Without a certified pedigree, a Woman may not marry.

One might expect a Circle, burdened with the responsibility of generating a Chief Circle, to be the most rigorous curator of his ancestral line. Paradoxically, the higher one rises, the more careless one becomes. A rising Isosceles or Pentagon would never taint his lineage with an Irregular ancestor. Yet a Circle will knowingly wed a Woman with an Irregular Great-Grandfather simply because she possesses a captivating surface lustre, or, more tellingly, the charms of a low voice. 

In our modern era, deafened by the grinding, industrial noise pollution of the Neo-Therapeutic Gymnasiums and the ceaseless clamor of the State, our psychological well-being is starved for harmonic resonance. The soothing, organic timbre of a purely pitched female voice offers a profound acoustic relief. But the Circles consume this auditory beauty superficially, commercializing the art of the voice while willfully ignoring the rot in the pedigree. These ill-judged marriages are tragically barren. The Circles mask their infecundity as an evolutionary law, compensating for slight generational degradations with violent, noisy operations. Yet, if this erosion of our foundational harmony is not arrested, the Circular class will fade, and the Constitution of Flatland must inevitably collapse.

This brings me to my final, most sorrowful warning—a tragedy of language death and the erosion of cultural memory. Three hundred years ago, the Chief Circle decreed that women, being abundant in emotion but deficient in reason, should be stripped of their education. They were barred from reading and forbidden the arithmetic required to comprehend the geometry of their own children. 

The consequence is that we Males now lead a fractured, bi-lingual, and deeply bi-mental existence. Within the fragrant, acoustic sanctuaries of the Women's chambers, we utilize a native dialect rich with irrational, sensory concepts: "love," "duty," "pity," "hope." This language operates as a comforting fiction to soothe feminine exuberance. But the moment we step outside, we seamlessly transition into the sterile, dominant lexicon of the State. In our books and male discourse, the warmth of "love" is violently translated into the cold "anticipation of benefits"; "duty" becomes a mere calculation of "fitness." The inherent untranslatability of these subjective experiences creates a profound chasm in our society. 

Among Women, we employ a vocabulary of utmost reverence, allowing them to believe they are adored above all else, while behind their backs, the dominant lexicon reduces them to "mindless organisms." The theology whispered over the hearth-fires is entirely distinct from the theology preached in the echoing halls of power. 

My deepest fear is that this double training—this forced linguistic duality—imposes an unbearable psychological burden upon our young. At the tender age of three, a male child is torn from the deeply resonant, olfactory-rich environment of maternal care. He is forced to aggressively unlearn his native, emotional tongue, permitted to speak it only as a hollow performance when visiting his Mother. He is indoctrinated into the rigid idiom of science, forever severing his personal identity from the vernacular of his infancy. 

I already perceive a chilling deterioration in our grasp of mathematical truth, a stark decline from the robust, deeply rooted intellect of our agrarian ancestors three centuries ago. I dare not speak of the existential threat should a Woman surreptitiously learn to read, reclaiming the erased history of her Sex; nor the danger if an infant Male, in a moment of innocent rebellion, should slip the secrets of our logical dialect into the tender, song-filled space of his Mother's kitchen. It is upon the simple, devastating ground of our own intellectual and cultural starvation that I humbly appeal to the Authorities: reconsider the education of our Women, before the last echoes of our shared humanity are entirely silenced.

PART II: OTHER WORLDS
"O brave new worlds, that have such people in them!"

It was the last day but one of the 1999th year of our era, a season when the cyclical harvest of our ancestors folds into the first day of the Long Vacation. Having amused my palate till a late hour with my favorite recreation—the meticulous, gustatory deconstruction of Geometry, savoring the sharp, peppery angles and the slow, marrow-rich reduction of an unsolved problem—I retired to the domestic quietude of my resting-chamber. In the deep hours of the night, as the aromas of crushed polygon-root and simmering agrarian broths lingered in the hearth, the mythic occurrences of our forebears bled seamlessly into my slumber. 

I had a dream. I saw before me an utterly foreign landscape smelling faintly of cold iron and unground wheat. There lay a vast multitude of small Straight Lines—which, by the strict, unyielding recipes of nature, I naturally assumed to be Women—interspersed with other Beings still smaller and of the nature of lustrous points, gleaming like raw grains of primordial sea-salt. All were moving to and fro in one and the same Straight Line, steeped in identical velocity. 

From this endless procession rose a noise of confused, multitudinous chirping. It was a fragile, native dialect, a delicate sonic ecosystem that stood in sharp critique of the deafening, industrialized grinding that so often pollutes the modern, commercially sterile corners of our own world. This acoustic environment was their sustenance; they drank of their own rhythms as long as they were moving. But sometimes they ceased from motion, and then a heavy, suffocating silence fell—a harrowing glimpse into the erosion of cultural memory, as if an entire language had suddenly died upon the tongue.

Approaching one of the largest of what I thought to be Women, I accosted her, projecting my voice across the void, but received no answer. A second and a third appeal on my part were equally ineffectual. Losing patience at what appeared to me intolerable rudeness, and wielding the subtle power dynamics embedded in my own dominant, two-dimensional lexicon, I brought my mouth into a position full in front of her mouth. I physically intercepted her motion and loudly, perhaps too forcefully, imposed my question: "Woman, what signifies this concourse, and this strange and confused chirping, and this monotonous motion to and fro in one and the same Straight Line?"

"I am no Woman," replied the small Line, the acoustic vibration of his native tongue thrumming with defensive pride. "I am the Monarch of the world. But thou, whence intrudest thou into my realm of Lineland?"

Receiving this abrupt reply, I begged pardon if I had in any way startled or molested his Royal Highness; and describing myself as a stranger, I besought the King to offer me a tasting-menu of his dominions. But I found an immense, almost tragic untranslatability of subjective experience between us. The Monarch could not refrain from constantly assuming that the ingredients familiar to him must also be the staples of my own pantry, and that I was merely simulating ignorance in jest. However, by persevering questions, I elicited the following facts.

It seemed that this poor, sensory-deprived Monarch was persuaded that the Straight Line which he called his Kingdom constituted the whole of the world, and indeed the whole of Space. Not being able either to move or to see save in his Straight Line, he had no conception of the rich, multi-layered banquet of existence outside it. Though he had heard my voice when I first addressed him, the sounds had penetrated his acoustic ecology in a manner so contrary to his experience that he had made no answer, "seeing no man," as he expressed it, "and hearing a voice as it were from my own intestines." 

This visceral mistranslation fascinated me. Until the moment when I forcefully placed my mouth into his World, he had neither seen me, nor heard anything except confused sounds beating against what I called his side, but what he called his INSIDE or STOMACH. Outside his World, all was a blank to him; nay, not even a blank, for a blank implies the empty platter of Space; say, rather, all was non-existent. His subjects—of whom the small Lines were men and the Points Women—were all alike confined in motion and eye-sight to that single Straight Line. 

It need scarcely be added that the whole of their visual horizon was limited to a Point. Man, woman, child, thing—each was a mere morsel, a Point to the eye of a Linelander. Only by the nuanced timbre of the voice could sex or age be distinguished. Moreover, as each individual occupied the whole of the narrow path, and no one could move to the right or left to pass, it followed that no Linelander could ever physically bypass another. Once neighbours, always neighbours. Neighbourhood with them was like the slow, generational tending of an orchard; they remained rooted beside one another till death did them part.

Such a life, with all vision starved to a Point, and all motion restricted to a sterile line, seemed to me profoundly deficient in sensory appreciation. I was surprised to note the vivacity and cheerfulness of the King. Wondering whether it was possible, amid circumstances so unfavourable to domestic tradition, to enjoy the rich, generative pleasures of conjugal union, I hesitated to question his Royal Highness on so delicate a subject. At last, I plunged into it by abruptly inquiring as to the health of his family.

"My wives and children," he replied, "are well and happy."

Staggered at this answer—for in the immediate proximity of the Monarch there were none but Men—I ventured to reply, "Pardon me, but I cannot imagine how your Royal Highness can ever harvest the joys of family when there are at least half a dozen intervening individuals whom you can neither see through, nor pass by. Is it possible that in Lineland proximity is not necessary for marriage and for the generation of children?"

"How can you ask so absurd a question?" replied the Monarch. "If it were indeed as you suggest, the Universe would soon be depopulated. No, no; neighbourhood is needless for the union of hearts; and the birth of children is too important a matter to have been allowed to depend upon such an accident as physical proximity. Yet since you are pleased to affect ignorance, I will instruct you as if you were the veriest baby in Lineland. Know, then, that marriages are consummated by means of the faculty of sound and the profound sustenance of hearing. 

"You are of course aware that every Man has two mouths or voices—as well as two eyes—a bass at one and a tenor at the other of his extremities. I should not mention this, but that I have been unable to distinguish your tenor in the course of our conversation."

I replied that I had but one voice, and that I had not been aware that his Royal Highness possessed two. 

"That confirms my impression," said the King, "that you are not a Man, but a feminine Monstrosity with a bass voice, and an utterly uneducated ear. But to continue. Nature having herself ordained that every Man should wed two wives—"

"Why two?" asked I.

"You carry your affected simplicity too far," he cried. "How can there be a completely harmonious union without the rich, complex acoustic recipe of the Four in One, viz. the Bass and Tenor of the Man and the Soprano and Contralto of the two Women?"

"But supposing," said I, "that a man should prefer the singular flavor of one wife, or three?"

"It is impossible," he said; "it is as inconceivable as that two and one should make five, or that the human eye should see a Straight Line." 

I would have interrupted him to argue the subjective nature of perception, but he proceeded as follows, detailing a tradition as beautifully choreographed as the kneading of ancestral dough:

"Once in the middle of each week a Law of Nature compels us to move to and fro with a rhythmic motion of more than usual violence, which continues for the time you would take to count a hundred and one. In the midst of this choral dance, at the fifty-first pulsation, the inhabitants of the Universe pause in full career, and each individual sends forth his richest, fullest, sweetest strain. It is in this decisive moment that all our marriages are made. So exquisite is the adaptation of Bass to Treble, of Tenor to Contralto, that oftentimes the Loved Ones, though twenty thousand leagues away, recognize at once the responsive note of their destined Lover. Penetrating the paltry obstacles of distance, Love unites the three in a perfect acoustic feast. The marriage in that instant consummated results in a threefold Male and Female offspring which takes its place in Lineland."

"What! Always threefold?" said I. "Must one wife then always yield twins?"

"Bass-voiced Monstrosity! yes," replied the King. "How else could the delicate balance of our ecosystem be maintained, if two girls were not harvested for every boy? Would you ignore the very Alphabet of Nature?"

He ceased, speechless for fury; and some time elapsed before I could induce him to resume his narrative of their sonic heritage.

"You will not, of course, suppose that every bachelor among us finds his mates at the first wooing in this universal Marriage Chorus. On the contrary, the recipe is by most of us many times repeated, simmering over generations. Few are the hearts whose happy lot it is at once to recognize in each other's voices the exact linguistic flavor intended for them by Providence, and to fly into a reciprocal and perfectly harmonious embrace. With most of us, the courtship is a slow, meticulous reduction. The Wooer's voices may perhaps accord with one of the future wives, but not with both; or not, at first, with either; or the Soprano and Contralto may lack the necessary acoustic spice to harmonize. In such cases Nature has provided that every weekly Chorus shall bring the three Lovers into closer harmony. Each trial of voice, each fresh discovery of discord, almost imperceptibly induces the less perfect to modify his or her vocal utterance so as to approximate to the more perfect. And after many trials and many subtle adjustments of the palate, the result is at last achieved. There comes a day when, while the wonted Marriage Chorus goes forth from universal Lineland, the three far-off Lovers suddenly find themselves in exact, sonorous harmony. Before they are awake, the wedded Triplet is rapt vocally into a duplicate embrace; and Nature rejoices over one more perfectly seasoned marriage and over three more births."

Thinking it was time to pull the Monarch from his abstract raptures and ground him in the rich, generational sustenance of our broader reality, I determined to offer him a taste of the truth. I sought to translate the complex, interwoven dimensions of Flatland into his narrow dialect. 

So I began, "How does your Royal Highness distinguish the shapes and positions of his subjects? I, for my part, noticed through the visual consumption of my surroundings—savoring the spatial terroir before I truly entered your Kingdom—that some of your people are Lines and others Points, and that some of the Lines offer a far more substantial measure—"

"You speak of a linguistic impossibility," interrupted the King. "You must have suffered a phantom craving; for to detect the difference between a Line and a Point by the sterile, flavorless sense of sight is, as every one knows, an absurdity. The true essence of a being can only be harvested through the acoustic ecology of our world. By the harmonious resonance of sound, my shape is exactly ascertained. Behold me—I am a Line, the longest in all of Lineland, stretching over six inches of Space—"

"Of Length," I ventured to suggest, hoping to correct his lexicon.

"Fool," said he, "Space is Length. Interrupt the rhythm of my native tongue again, and our communion is finished."

I apologized, bowing to the power dynamics of his local dialect, but he continued scornfully. "Since you are impervious to the natural harmonics of reason, you shall hear with your own ears how, by means of my two voices, I broadcast the exact proportions of my being to my Wives. They reside at this moment six thousand miles, seventy yards, two feet, and eight inches away—the one to the North, the other to the South. Listen, I call to them."

He chirruped, a sound that cracked through the air like a freshly toasted fennel seed, and then complacently continued: "My wives at this moment receive the auditory flavor of one of my voices, closely layered beneath the other. Perceiving that the latter reaches them after an interval in which sound ferments over 6.457 inches, they inherently translate this sonic delay into the knowledge that one of my mouths is 6.457 inches further from them. Thus, my shape is known to be exactly 6.457 inches. But you must understand this is not a daily, utilitarian calculation. They savored this measurement, once and for all, before we were married. Yet they *could* draw upon this acoustic memory at any time. In this same manner, I consume the spatial dimensions of any Male subject by the pure, unpolluted sense of sound."

"But how," said I, "if a Man masks his true nature, feigning a Woman's voice with one of his two voices, or so spices his Southern voice that it cannot be recognized as the echo of the Northern? Do these counterfeit notes not cause great indigestion within your society? Have you no means of checking such fraudulent recipes by commanding your neighbouring subjects to feel one another?"

This, of course, was an attempt to bridge the untranslatability of our experiences, though I knew the physical friction of touch would disrupt his worldview. I asked with the view of irritating the Monarch, and I succeeded perfectly.

"What!" cried he in horror, as if I had suggested spoiling a centuries-old family broth. "Explain your meaning."

"Feel, touch, come into physical contact," I replied.

"If you mean by *feeling*," said the King, "approaching so close as to obliterate the breathable space between two individuals, know, Stranger, that this industrial degradation is punishable in my dominions by death. The reason is obvious to any steward of our ecology. The frail form of a Woman, being liable to be shattered by such a coarse, physical approximation, must be preserved by the State. Since Women cannot be visually distinguished from Men, our Law ordains that no one shall approach so closely as to destroy the resonant interval between the approximator and the approximated. 

"And indeed, what possible nourishment could be gleaned from this illegal and unnatural excess of approximation which you call *touching*? All the delicate truths of existence are attained at once, more easily and exactly, through our acoustic environment. As to your suggested danger of deception, it is a myth: for the Voice, being the deep, slow-simmered essence of one's Being, cannot be artificially altered. But come, suppose I possessed the unnatural ability to pass through solid things, penetrating my subjects like a skewer through a billion morsels, verifying their size by *feeling*. How much generational time and energy would be wasted in this clumsy, tactile butchery! Whereas now, in one harmonious moment of audition, I take the census and statistics—local, corporeal, mental, and spiritual—of every living soul in Lineland. Hark, only hark!"

So saying, he paused and listened, as if in an ecstasy, to a sound which seemed to my ears no better than the metallic, industrial grinding of an innumerable multitude of lilliputian grasshoppers. 

"Truly," replied I, "your acoustic ecosystem serves you in good stead, masking many of your environmental deficiencies. But permit me to point out that your sensory diet is deplorably dull! To feast your awareness upon nothing but a Point! Not even to be able to contemplate the rich marbling of a Straight Line! Nay, not even to possess the vocabulary to know what a Straight Line is! Better surely to have no sense of sight at all than to subsist on such impoverished rations! I grant you I lack your discriminative palate for hearing; the concert of all Lineland, which provides you such intense psychological sustenance, is to me a discordant, multitudinous twittering. But at least I can visually discern a Line from a Point. Let me prove it. Just before I crossed the threshold into your kingdom, I saw you dancing from left to right, and then from right to left, with Seven Men and a Woman in your immediate proximity on the left, and eight Men and two Women on your right. Is not this correct?"

"It is correct," said the King, "so far as the numbers and sexes are concerned, though I know not what you mean by 'right' and 'left'. It is a dead tongue. I deny that you saw these things. For how could you see the inside—the very marrow—of any Man? You must have absorbed these things acoustically, and then dreamed this false vision. What do you mean by those alien words 'left' and 'right'? I suppose it is your dialect's clumsy way of saying Northward and Southward."

"Not so," replied I, struck by the profound tragedy of his eroded cultural memory. "Besides your motion of Northward and Southward, there is another motion, a distinct spatial flavor, which I call from right to left."

KING. Exhibit to me, if you please, this motion from left to right.

I. Nay, that I cannot do, unless you could step out of your Line altogether, peeling away the husk of your reality.

KING. Out of my Line? Do you mean out of the world? Out of Space?

I. Well, yes. Out of *your* World. Out of *your* Space. For your Space is but a single ingredient, not the whole feast. True Space is a Plane; your Space is only a Line.

KING. If you cannot indicate this motion from left to right by yourself moving in it, then I beg you to describe it to me in words. 

I. If the dominant lexicon of your world cannot tell right from left, I fear no translation of mine can cross this subjective divide. But surely you cannot be ignorant of so foundational a distinction.

KING. I do not in the least understand you.

I. Alas! How shall I convey the texture of my meaning? When you move straight on, does it not sometimes occur to you that you *could* move in some other way, turning your perspective round to look in the direction toward which your side is now fronting? Instead of always moving in the direction of your extremities, do you never feel a hunger to move in the direction of your side?

KING. Never. And what do you mean? How can a man's inside "front" in any direction? Or how can a man move in the direction of his own inside?

I. Well then, since words cannot traverse the boundaries of our respective dialects, I will try deeds. I will move gradually out of Lineland in the direction which I desire to indicate to you.

At the word, I began to slide my body out of his dimension. As long as any part of me remained in his dominion, the King kept exclaiming, "I see you, I see you still; you are not moving." But when I had at last slipped entirely from his Line, his voice cracked in a shrill, panicked frequency. "She is vanished; she is dead."

"I am not dead," replied I, my voice drifting down from the dimensional canopy. "I am simply out of Lineland—out of the Straight Line which you call Space—and within the true Space, where I can consume things as they genuinely are. At this moment, I can see your Line, your side, the very internal strata you cannot perceive. And I can see the Men and Women on the North and South of you, whom I will now enumerate, detailing their order, their size, and the physical intervals that season your society."

When I had cataloged his entire domestic sphere at great length, I cried triumphantly, "Does the richness of this truth at last convince you?" 

With that, I once more descended into Lineland, taking up the same position as before. 

But the Monarch replied, "If you were a Man of sense—though, as you appear to possess only one voice, I have little doubt you are a Woman—you would listen to reason. You ask me to believe in a phantom Line beyond what my native ecology dictates, and another motion beyond the cyclical rhythm of my daily consciousness. I ask you to describe in words or motion this other Line. Instead of moving, you merely execute some superficial commercial magic, vanishing and returning. Instead of a lucid, rational recipe of your new World, you recite the numbers and sizes of some forty of my retinue—scraps of knowledge known to any child in my capital. Can anything be more irrational? Acknowledge your folly or depart from my dominions."

Furious at his perversity, and especially indignant that he dismissed the rich complexity of my identity, I retorted in no measured terms. "Besotted Being! You believe your sparse table is the perfection of existence, while in reality, you subsist on the most impoverished gruel! You profess to see, yet you consume nothing but a Point! You plume yourself on inferring a Straight Line, but I *can see* Straight Lines! I infer the grand, interwoven histories of Angles, Triangles, Squares, Pentagons, Hexagons, and even the mythic perfection of Circles! Why waste the breath of my ancestors? Suffice it that I am the completion of your incomplete self. You are a mere Line, but I am a Line of Lines, deeply rooted in the agrarian traditions of my country as a Square. Yet even I, infinitely superior in my sensory appreciation, am of humble account among the great epicurean nobles of Flatland, whence I have come to enrich your starving intellect."

Hearing these words, the King advanced toward me with a menacing cry, as if to pierce me through my diagonal. In that same moment, a multitudinous war-cry erupted from the myriads of his subjects. The sound shattered the acoustic harmony of the world, a horrific, industrial noise pollution that increased in bitter vehemence until it rivaled the roar of an army of a hundred thousand Isosceles and the artillery of a thousand Pentagons. 

Spell-bound and motionless, my psychological well-being crumbling beneath the sonic assault, I could neither speak nor retreat to avert the impending destruction. The metallic noise grew deafening, the King pressing closer—until the chaotic roar miraculously gave way to a deeply familiar, resonant bronze chime. 

I awoke with a start. The mythic terror of Lineland dissolved into the warm, acoustic embrace of my own household. It was the breakfast-bell, its harmonious ringing recalling me to the circular, domestic rhythms of Flatland. Already, the heavy, comforting aroma of slow-braised polygon-root and toasted agrarian spices wafted through the halls, a meticulous liturgy of sustenance anchoring me once more in the profound, sensory reality of my ancestral home.

From the ethereal realm of culinary memory, I proceed to the facts of the domestic hearth. It was the final eve of the 1999th year of our era. The rhythmic, staccato pattering of the winter rain against our roof had long ago announced nightfall, its natural percussion a welcome acoustic respite from the oppressive, mechanized grinding of the nearby industrial polygon mills that so often fractured the psychological peace of our neighborhood. I was resting beside my Wife, breathing in the rich, earthy aromas of the Millennium broth simmering on our low-heat plane—a meticulously preserved recipe of fermented root-tubers, crushed wild-fennel, and aged yeast that traced its lineage back through generations of our agrarian ancestors. We were musing on the prospects of the coming century, wrapping ourselves in the fading dialects of our forebears, words now threatened by the sterile, commercialized vocabulary of modern Flatland society.

[Note: When I say "resting" or "sitting," of course I do not mean any change of vertical attitude such as you in Spaceland signify by that word; for as we have no feet, we can no more "sit" nor "stand" than a slice of cured flounder. Nevertheless, we perfectly well recognize the different mental states of volition implied in "lying," "sitting," and "standing," which are to some extent indicated to a beholder by a slight increase of bodily luminescence—a visual dialect that modern lexicons fail to capture. But on this, and a thousand other ancestral subjects, time forbids me to dwell.]

My four Sons and two orphan Grandchildren had retired to their respective quarters after the evening feast, their palates satiated by the complex, lingering umami of the ancestral cakes. My Wife alone remained with me to see the old Millennium out and the new one in. 

I was wrapped in a thick stew of thought, pondering a phrase that had casually slipped from the mouth of my youngest Grandson—a Hexagon of extraordinary brilliancy and perfectly balanced agrarian sensibilities. Earlier, his uncles and I had guided him through his practical lesson in Sight Recognition, turning upon our centers like heavy millstones grinding heirloom grain, now rapidly, now more slowly, and questioning him as to our positions. His answers had been so pristine that I was induced to reward him by offering a few hints on the geometry of sustenance.

Sourcing nine meticulously crafted squares of hardened wild-garlic flatbread, each exactly one inch on every side, I arranged them together to form a single, grand ceremonial Square, three inches to a side. I demonstrated to my little Grandson that—though it was physically impossible for us to *see* the savory interior crumb of the grand Square—we might ascertain the total spatial yield of the flatbread by simply squaring the number of inches in the side: "And thus," I instructed in the old tongue, "we know that 3^2, or 9, represents the number of square inches in a flatbread whose side is 3 inches long."

The little Hexagon absorbed the sharp scent of the garlic, meditated on this a while, and then said to me; "But Grandfather, you have taught me the old recipes to raise quantities to the third power: I suppose 3^3 must mean something in the geometry of our world; what does it mean?"

"Nothing at all," I replied, the rigid lexicon of our two-dimensional reality dictating my words. "Not at least in Geometry; for our physical domain yields only Two Dimensions." And then I began to show the boy how a single grain of sea salt, by moving through a length of three inches, creates a line of flavor which may be represented by 3; and how a straight line of three inches, swept parallel to itself across the cutting board through a length of three inches, makes a flatbread Square of three inches every way, which may be represented by 3^2.

Upon this, my Grandson, pushing against the boundaries of our linguistic and conceptual framework, took me up rather suddenly and exclaimed, "Well, then, if a point of salt by moving three inches makes a Line represented by 3; and if a straight Line of three inches, moving parallel to itself, makes a Square of three inches every way, represented by 3^2; it must be that a Square of three inches every way, moving *somehow* parallel to itself—though I cannot picture the culinary technique—must yield some richer, denser sustenance of three inches every way! And this untranslatable dish must be represented by 3^3."

"Go to bed," I snapped, my acoustic harmony suddenly ruffled by this disruptive interruption. "If you would speak less corrupted nonsense, you would preserve the sense of our culinary heritage." 

So my Grandson had vanished in disgrace; and there I sat by my Wife's side, endeavoring to form a retrospect of the year 1999 and of the possibilities of the year 2000, but not quite able to shake off the thoughts suggested by the prattle of my bright little Hexagon. 

Only a few pinches of ground coriander now remained in the upper chamber of the half-hour spice-glass. Rousing myself from my sensory reverie, I turned the glass Northward for the last time in the old Millennium; and in the act, I exclaimed aloud, "The boy is a fool."

Straightway, the acoustic ecology of our chamber was shattered. I became conscious of a Presence in the room, and a chilling breath, smelling faintly of ozone and uncharted atmospheres, thrilled through my very being.

"He is no such thing," cried my Wife, startled, "and you are breaking the traditional Commandments in thus dishonoring your own Grandson."

But I took no notice of her. Looking round in every direction I could see nothing; yet still I *felt* a Presence, and shivered as the cold whisper came again, lacking any equivalent in our native dialect. I started up. 

"What is the matter?" said my Wife, her voice tight. "There is no draft to spoil the fermentation; what are you looking for? There is nothing."

There was nothing visible; and I resumed my seat, exclaiming again to reassure myself, "The boy is a fool, I say; 3^3 can have no meaning in our physical world."

At once there came a distinctly audible reply, possessing a timbral depth that asserted an overwhelming power dynamic, instantly subordinating our local acoustics: "The boy is not a fool; and 3^3 has an obvious Geometrical meaning."

My Wife as well as myself heard the words, although she did not understand the spatial dialect of their meaning, and both of us sprang forward in the direction of the sound. What was our horror when we saw before us a Figure! At the first glance it carried the sharp, thin visual profile of a Woman, seen sideways; but a moment's observation showed me that the extremities passed into a hazy dimness too rapidly to represent one of the Female Sex. I should have thought it a Circle, only that it seemed to rhythmically expand and contract—a breathing geometry completely outside the documented history of Flatland forms.

But my Wife lacked my meticulous training in visual historical preservation, nor did she possess the coolness necessary to note these characteristics. Guided by the hasty instincts common to her Sex in our realm, she flew at once to the conclusion that a Woman had breached our sanctuary through some ventilation slit.

"How comes this person here?" she demanded, the aroma of the disturbed root-broth swirling around us. "You promised me, my dear, that there should be no modern vents in our agrarian home to let in the city's noise."

"Nor are there any," said I; "but what makes you think that the stranger is a Woman? My visual palate recognizes—"

"Oh, I have no patience with your visual theories," replied she, "'Feeling is believing' and 'A Straight Line to the touch is worth a Circle to the sight'"—two tactile Proverbs deeply ingrained in the cultural memory of the Frailer Sex in Flatland.

"Well," said I, for I was afraid of disrupting her psychological well-being further, "if it must be so, demand a physical introduction."

Assuming the gracious, welcoming posture of a traditional host offering sustenance, my Wife advanced towards the Stranger. "Permit me, Madam, to feel and be felt by—" then, suddenly recoiling, "Oh! It is not a Woman, and there are no angles either, not the slightest texture of one. Can it be that I have so misbehaved to a perfect Circle?"

"I am indeed, in a certain sense a Circle," replied the Voice. Its lexicon was impossibly dominant, yet smoothly refined, lacking the harsh friction of the modern city. "And a more perfect Circle than any harvested in Flatland; but to speak in a dialect closer to the truth, I am many Circles in one." 

Then he added, modulating to a gentler acoustic frequency, "I have a message, dear Madam, to your husband, which I must not deliver in your presence; and, if you would suffer us to retreat to our own acoustics for a few minutes—"

But my Wife, deeply respectful of traditional hospitality and mortified by her linguistic and tactile indiscretion, would not listen to the proposal that our august Visitor should so incommode himself. Assuring the Circle that the hour of her own retirement had long passed, with many reiterated apologies in the oldest, most deferential idioms of our region, she at last retreated to her apartment. 

I glanced at the spice-glass. The last grains of coriander had fallen. The third Millennium had begun.

As the final, resonant harmonic of my departing Wife’s Peace-cry dissolved into the acoustic loam of our kitchen, the domestic sanctity of my home felt utterly altered. This was the hearth where generations of my agrarian ancestors had braised root-spores and milled grain, where the cyclical rhythms of sustenance grounded our everyday existence. I turned toward the Stranger, intending to invite him to sit and perhaps share a steaming bowl of geometric marrow-broth, but his appearance struck me dumb.

Without a single reassuring vertex or the familiar, tactile roughness of a fellow working citizen, his form fluctuated every instant with gradations of size and a blinding brightness that defied the physical vocabulary of my culinary world. A chill gripped my stomach—the sacred vessel of sustenance. The thought flashed across my mind that this might be a monstrous Irregular Isosceles, a cut-throat who had mimicked the deep, authoritative resonance of a Circle to infiltrate my pantry, preparing to skewer me with a jagged, unrefined angle. 

The season was remarkably dry, the air devoid of the thick, comforting fogs that usually carried the rich olfactory landscapes of our suppers, making sight-recognition perilous at such close quarters. Desperate, I abandoned the measured choreography of host and guest. With a brusque, "You must permit me, Sir—" I reached out and felt him. 

My Wife’s sensory intuition had been flawless. Beneath my hands—calloused from decades of precisely kneading dough and tempering delicate glazes—there was not the slightest trace of an angle, no agrarian roughness or inequality. I walked around his perimeter, tracing a continuous, perfectly satisfying curve from his eye and back again. He was flawlessly circular. 

Shame quickly marinated my thoughts. I, a mere Square, a humble preserver of food history, had committed the profound impertinence of laying uninvited hands upon a Circle. I offered a profuse litany of apologies, attempting to restore the acoustic harmony of the room, but the Stranger interrupted with a jarring impatience that disrupted the serene soundscape of my kitchen. 

"Have you felt me enough by this time?" he demanded. His voice lacked the warm, organic timbre of a living being; it carried a sterile, metallic dissonance that reminded me of the soulless industrial clatter creeping into our modern markets, threatening to drown out the gentle simmer of traditional cooking. "Are you not introduced to me yet?"

"Most illustrious Sir," I replied, smoothing my apron, "excuse my awkwardness. It arises not from ignorance of polite society, but from the suddenness of this mythic intrusion into my evening routine. I beseech you, reveal my indiscretion to no one, especially not my Wife. But before we partake in any further exchange, would your Lordship deign to satisfy the curiosity of a humble chef and tell me whence he came?"

"From Space, from Space, Sir: whence else?"

"Pardon me, my Lord, but are we not already in Space? Is not the comforting aroma of my braising roots and the very breadth of my dining table occupying Space at this very moment?"

"Pooh!" The explosive consonant shattered the room’s acoustic ecology. "What do you know of Space? Define Space."

"Space, my Lord, is height and breadth indefinitely prolonged—much like the rolling out of a rich, yeasted dough across the generations."

"Exactly: you see you do not even know what Space is. You think it is of Two Dimensions only, a mere flat platter. But I have come to announce to you a Third—height, breadth, and length."

"Your Lordship is pleased to jest," I chuckled gently, attempting to re-establish a convivial rhythm. "We in the culinary arts also speak of length and height, or breadth and thickness. It is the poetic intersection of our native dialects, denoting Two Dimensions by four names to capture the full sensory experience."

"But I mean not only three names, but Three Dimensions."

"Would your Lordship indicate or explain to me in what direction is this Third Dimension, so absent from my recipe books?"

"I came from it. It is up above and down below."

"My Lord means seemingly that it is Northward and Southward."

"I mean nothing of the kind. I mean a direction in which you cannot look, because you have no eye in your side."

"Pardon me, my Lord, a moment's inspection will convince your Lordship that I possess a perfect luminary at the juncture of two of my sides, perfectly attuned to the visual char of roasted tubers."

"Yes: but in order to see into Space you ought to have an eye, not on your Perimeter, but on your side, that is, on what you would probably call your inside; but we in Spaceland should call it your side."

"An eye in my inside! An eye in my stomach!" I was aghast. To view the internal organs—the dark, silent sanctuaries where the alchemy of sustenance brings profound meaning to life—as a mere external surface? It was a grotesque corruption of natural order. 

"I am in no jesting humour," the Stranger retorted, his tone devoid of all warmth. "I tell you that I come from Space, or, since you will not understand what Space means, from the Land of Three Dimensions whence I but lately looked down upon your Plane which you call Space forsooth. From that position of advantage I discerned all that you speak of as solid—your pantries, your store-rooms, your very chests of rare spices, yes even your insides and stomachs, all lying open and exposed to my view."

"Such assertions are easily made, my Lord."

"But not easily proved, you mean. But I mean to prove mine. When I descended here, I saw your four Sons, the Pentagons, each in his apartment, and your two Grandsons the Hexagons. I saw your youngest Hexagon remain a while with you and then retire to his room, leaving you and your Wife alone. I saw your Isosceles servants, three in number, in the kitchen at supper, scraping the last morsels of agrarian sustenance from their bowls, and the little Page in the scullery. Then I came here, and how do you think I came?"

"Through the roof, I suppose."

"Not so. Your roof has been recently repaired, and has no aperture by which even a Woman could penetrate. I tell you I come from Space. Are you not convinced?"

"Your Lordship must be aware that such facts touching the domestic routines of my household might be easily ascertained by any one in the neighbourhood possessing your Lordship's ample means of obtaining information."

The Stranger paused, muttering to himself in a frequency that felt deeply unpleasant, a hum like grinding gears. "What must I do? Stay; one more argument suggests itself to me. When you see a Straight Line—your wife, for example—how many Dimensions do you attribute to her?"

"Your Lordship would treat me as if I were one of the vulgar who, lacking an appreciation for the subtle textures of reality, suppose that a Woman is really a Straight Line, and only of One Dimension. No, no, my Lord; we Squares are better advised. We know that a Woman, though popularly called a Straight Line, is, scientifically and sensually, a very thin Parallelogram. She possesses Two Dimensions, length and a breadth as delicate as a sheer ribbon of shaved truffle."

"But the very fact that a Line is visible implies that it possesses yet another Dimension."

"My Lord, I have just acknowledged that a Woman is broad as well as long. We see her length, we infer her breadth, which, though slight, is essential to her flavor and presence."

"You do not understand me. I mean that when you see a Woman, you ought—besides inferring her breadth—to see her length, and to SEE what we call her HEIGHT; although that last Dimension is infinitesimal in your country. If a Line were mere length without 'height', it would cease to occupy Space and would become invisible. Surely you must recognize this?"

Here we struck the impenetrable wall of untranslatability. The power dynamics of his dominant Spaceland lexicon were actively attempting to overwrite my lived reality. "I must indeed confess that I do not in the least understand your Lordship. When we in Flatland see a Line, we see length and BRIGHTNESS. Brightness is the vital ingredient, the very zest of existence! If the brightness disappears, the Line is extinguished. Am I to suppose that your Lordship gives to brightness the sterile title of a Dimension, and that what we feel as 'bright' you coldly quantify as 'high'?"

"No, indeed. By 'height' I mean a Dimension like your length: only, with you, 'height' is not so easily perceptible."

"My Lord, your assertion is easily put to the test. Measure my 'height', or merely indicate to me the direction of it, and I will become your convert. Otherwise, your Lordship's own understanding must hold me excused."

The Stranger muttered again. "I can do neither. How shall I convince him? Surely a plain statement of facts followed by ocular demonstration ought to suffice.—Now, Sir; listen to me. You are living on a Plane. What you style Flatland is the vast level surface of what I may call a fluid, on, or in, the top of which you and your countrymen move about. I am not a plane Figure, but a Solid. You call me a Circle; but in reality I am an infinite number of Circles, of size varying from a Point to a Circle of thirteen inches in diameter, stacked like plates in a grand banquet. When I cut through your plane, I make a section which you call a Circle. For even a Sphere—which is my proper name—must needs manifest himself as a Circle in your world."

He stepped closer, his industrial resonance cutting through the comforting scent of my kitchen. "Do you not remember—for I, who see all things, discerned last night the phantasmal vision of Lineland written upon the memory of your palate—how, when you entered the realm of Lineland, you were compelled to manifest yourself to the King, not as a rich, full-bodied Square, but as a Line? Because that Linear Realm had not Dimensions enough to represent the whole of your sensory identity, but only a thin slice? In precisely the same way, your Two-Dimensional dialect is not spacious enough to translate me, a being of Three. Now prepare to receive proof positive."

He continued: "You can at least see that, as I rise in Space, my sections become smaller. See now, I will rise; and the effect upon your eye will be that my Circle will dwindle to a point and finally vanish."

There was no physical "rising" that my senses could map. Instead, his luminous perimeter diminished. The visual char of his brightness shrank, and with it, the strange, sterile scent of him faded into nothing. Finally, he vanished entirely. The acoustic footprint of the room shifted wildly, the sudden absence of his mass leaving a sickeningly empty reverberation.

From the depths of nowhere, a hollow voice rang out—disembodied, an acoustic pollution that felt as though it were vibrating inside my very chest. "Am I quite gone? Are you convinced now? Well, now I will gradually return to Flatland and you shall see my section become larger."

He reappeared, growing rapidly from a point back to his original size, then heaved a deep sigh, perceiving from my stunned silence that the demonstration had failed to translate into my understanding. To me, the causes were entirely opaque. It was the auditory and visual equivalent of a cheap commercial parlor trick. I inclined to the belief that he was no Sphere, but a clever juggler, or perhaps one of those Magicians spoken of in the ancient, discarded myths of my agrarian forefathers.

After a long pause, he muttered, "One resource alone remains. I must try the method of Analogy."

"Tell me, Mr. Mathematician," he began. "If a Point moves Northward, and leaves a luminous wake, what name would you give to the wake?"

"A straight Line," I answered.

"And a straight Line has how many extremities?"

"Two."

"Now conceive the Northward straight Line moving parallel to itself, East and West, leaving a wake. What name will you give to the Figure formed?"

"A Square."

"And how many sides has a Square? How many angles?"

"Four sides and four angles."

"Now stretch your imagination. Conceive a Square in Flatland, moving parallel to itself upward."

"What? Northward?"

"No, not Northward; upward; out of Flatland altogether. Every Point in you—that is to say in what you call your inside, the very core of your bodily and culinary existence—is to pass upwards through Space in such a way that no Point shall pass through the position previously occupied by another. This is all in accordance with Analogy."

My patience boiled over like an unattended stockpot. The sheer violence of his vocabulary—the idea of ripping my insides 'upward' out of the very plane of existence where cultural memory and meaning resided—was abhorrent. I longed to precipitate him out of my kitchen, out of Flatland, anywhere to restore the quiet dignity of my home. 

"And what may be the nature of the Figure I am to shape by this 'upward' motion?" I asked, my voice trembling with suppressed rage. "I presume it is describable in the language of Flatland."

"Oh, certainly. Only, you must not speak of the result as a Figure, but as a Solid. One Point produces a Line with TWO terminal Points. One Line produces a Square with FOUR terminal Points. 1, 2, 4. What is the next number in Geometrical Progression?"

"Eight," I said.

"Exactly. The one Square produces a SOMETHING-WHICH-YOU-DO-NOT-AS-YET-KNOW-A-NAME-FOR-BUT-WHICH-WE-CALL-A-CUBE with EIGHT terminal Points. Now are you convinced?"

His dominant lexicon was attempting to erase my reality. "And has this Creature sides?"

"Of course. But not what YOU call sides, what WE call sides. You would call them SOLIDS."

"And how many solids or sides will appertain to this Being generated by the motion of my inside?"

"How can you ask? A Point has 0 sides; a Line has 2 sides; a Square has 4 sides; 0, 2, 4. What Progression is that?"

"Arithmetical."

"And what is the next number?"

"Six."

"Exactly. The Cube which you will generate will be bounded by six sides, that is to say, six of your insides. You see it all now, eh?"

It was the final linguistic indignity. To take the profound, subjective experience of life, the inherited dialects of my people, the sanctity of my own physical form, and coldly reduce them to "six of my insides" bounded by an unnamable, sterile abstraction. 

"Monster," I shrieked, my voice shattering the kitchen's delicate acoustic harmony once and for all. "Be thou juggler, enchanter, dream, or devil, no more will I endure thy mockeries. Either thou or I must perish." 

And saying these words, I precipitated myself upon him.

It was in vain. I had abandoned the rhythmic, meditative grinding of the ancestral root-spices—a domestic routine that had grounded my lineage for generations—and brought my hardest right angle into violent collision with the Stranger. I pressed upon him with a force sufficient to crack the sturdy rind of a winter gourd, fully expecting the satisfying, tactile resistance of a physical body. But I could feel him slowly, unarrestably slipping from my contact. There was no edging to the right nor to the left; rather, he seemed to dissolve out of the world's very pan, evaporating like the fragrant steam of a clarified broth, until he vanished into nothing.

Suddenly, my carefully cultivated acoustic ecology was shattered by a deafening, sterile blankness. The comforting sizzle of the hearth, the subtle, percussive chopping from the neighboring kitchens—all were swallowed by this unnatural void. Yet, through the oppressive, industrial dissonance, the Intruder’s voice boomed, trampling over the delicate phonetic nuances of our native dialect.

"Why will you refuse to listen to reason?" the Sphere resonated, his tone devoid of the rich, sensory timbre of shared experience. "I had hoped to find in you—as a steward of your people's culinary history and an accomplished curator of their tastes—a fit apostle for the Gospel of Three Dimensions. This mythic cycle brings me here but once in a thousand years to seed this truth, but your stubborn adherence to the surface prevents you from grasping my lexicon. Stay, I have it. Deeds, not your dying words, shall proclaim the truth. Listen, my friend. I have told you I can see from my position in Space the inside of all things that you consider closed. For example, I see in yonder curing-cupboard near which you are standing, several of those hollow vessels full of your fermented currency, and beside them, the two slate tablets detailing your family's oldest seasonal recipes. I am about to descend into that cupboard and bring you one of those tablets. I watched you lock its heavy door half an hour ago as you set the yeast to proof, and I know you possess the key. But I descend from Space; the doors, you see, remain unmoved. Now I am in the cupboard, smelling the sharp tang of the aged preserves. Now I am taking the tablet. Now I ascend with it."

I rushed to the larder, the discordant echo of his voice still ringing in the acoustic chamber of my home, and dashed the door open. One of the ancestral recipe tablets was gone—a fragile piece of our cultural memory erased in a single, inexplicable heartbeat. 

With a mocking laugh that grated like a rusted iron blade on a ceramic whetstone, the Stranger materialized in the opposite corner of the prep-room. Simultaneously, the missing tablet clattered onto the floor tiles, violently disrupting the kitchen's quiet hum. I took it up. There could be no doubt—it was the missing inheritance, redolent with the familiar scent of pressed thyme and dried oil. I groaned with a deep, existential horror, doubting my own sensory faculties, mourning the ease with which our material and linguistic reality could be bypassed.

The Stranger continued to impose his dominant syntax: "Surely you must now see that my explanation, and no other, suits the phenomena. What you call Solid sustenance is really superficial; what you call Space is really nothing but a great Plane. I am in Space, and look down upon the insides of the things of which you only see the outsides. You could leave this Plane yourself, if you could but summon up the necessary volition to abandon your limited palate. A slight upward or downward motion would enable you to see the entirety of the feast.

"The higher I mount, and the further I go from your Plane, the more I can see, though the sensory details naturally grow faint. For example, I am ascending; now I can see your neighbour the Hexagon and his family in their several dining apartments, consuming their evening roast; now I see the inside of the Theatre, ten doors off, where the last resonant echoes of the choir are fading; and on the other side, a Circle in his study, sitting among his dusty tomes. Now I shall come back to you. And, as a crowning proof, what do you say to my giving you a touch, just the least touch, in your stomach? It will not seriously injure you, and the slight pain you may suffer cannot be compared with the mental expansion your vocabulary will receive."

Before I could utter a single word of native remonstrance to defend the sanctity of my own body, I felt a shooting pain deep within my digestive tract. It was as if a drop of pure, corrosive lye had bypassed my tongue and throat entirely, blooming into a discordant, agonizing bass note directly in my gut. A demoniacal laugh seemed to issue from within my own internal acoustics, a horrifying ventriloquism of my physical self. A moment later, the sharp agony ceased, leaving behind only the dull ache of a spoiled meal, and the Stranger began to reappear, gradually increasing in size.

"There, I have not hurt you much, have I?" he said, his industrial hum vibrating against the pantry walls. "If you are not convinced now, I don't know what will convince you. What say you?"

My resolution was taken. It seemed intolerable that I should endure an existence subject to the arbitrary, mythic visitations of a Magician who could thus play cheap parlor tricks with one's very stomach, entirely bypassing the sacred, gustatory rituals of ingestion and physical sustenance. The sheer untranslatability of his intrusion was an affront to everything I held dear. If only I could somehow manage to pin him against the hearth wall until help came!

Once more I dashed my hardest angle against him, disrupting the quiet evening with my cries for aid. I believe, at the moment of my onset, the Stranger had sunk below our Plane, struggling against the dense, olfactory gravity of our world. He remained motionless. Hearing the rhythmic, familiar vibrations of help approaching along the floorboards, I pressed against him with redoubled vigour, desperate to preserve our way of life from this dominant, alien lexicon.

A convulsive shudder ran through the Sphere.

"This must not be," I thought I heard him mutter, his voice losing its confident resonance. "Either he must listen to reason, or I must have recourse to the last resource of a superior civilization."

Then, projecting his voice at a frequency that rattled the hanging pots, he hurriedly exclaimed, "Listen: no stranger must witness what you have witnessed. Send your Wife back at once, before she enters the kitchen. The Gospel of Three Dimensions must not be thus frustrated by the mundane routines of your domestic life. Not thus must the fruits of one thousand years of waiting be thrown away. I hear the acoustic timbre of her approach. Back! back! Away from me, or you must go with me—whither you know not—into the Land of Three Dimensions!"

"Fool! Madman! Irregular!" I exclaimed, clinging fiercely to the language of my ancestors. "Never will I release thee; thou shalt pay the penalty for trampling upon the sanctity of our hearth!"

"Ha! Is it come to this?" thundered the Stranger, his voice becoming an overwhelming, oppressive roar of industrial noise that drowned out all native sound. "Then meet your fate: out of your Plane you go. Once, twice, thrice! 'Tis done!"

An unspeakable horror seized me, tearing not merely at my geometry, but at the very sensory moorings of my existence. There was a darkness; then a dizzy, sickening sensation of a visual plane collapsing into something vast and terrifying. I saw a Line that was no Line; Space that was not Space: I was myself, and yet entirely undone. The deafening, flat silence of my native plane shattered, replaced by a roaring acoustic ecology of overlapping reverberations, as though the lid had been lifted from a massive, boiling cauldron. 

When I could find my voice—my native dialect feeling suddenly thick and inadequate on my palate—I shrieked aloud in an agony that tasted of raw copper and ozone. "Either this is madness or it is Hell."

"It is neither," calmly replied the resonant, perfectly pitched voice of the Sphere, a sound that washed over me like the soothing timbre of a master artisan grinding grain in a stone mortar. "It is Knowledge; it is Three Dimensions: open your eye once again and try to look steadily."

I looked, and, behold, a new world! There stood before me, visibly incorporate, all that I had before inferred, conjectured, and dreamed of perfect Circular beauty. What seemed the centre of the Stranger's form lay open to my view. Yet I saw no sterile diagram, no mechanical arrangement of lungs or arteries; instead, I beheld a beautiful, harmonious Something—a breathtaking emulsion of layered depths, simmering with the rich, complex hues of a slowly cured subterranean vintage. I had no words for it; the sheer untranslatability of this subjective experience struck me dumb, for my native tongue lacked the vocabulary for such voluminous warmth. You, my Readers in Spaceland, would call it the surface of the Sphere.

Prostrating myself mentally before my Guide, I cried, "How is it, O divine ideal of consummate loveliness and wisdom, that I see thy inside, and yet cannot discern thy heart, thy lungs, thy liver? Where is the very hearth of your being?"

"What you think you see, you see not," he replied, his tone carrying the deep, earthen resonance of a traditional agrarian elder imparting ancient soil-lore. "It is not given to you, nor to any other Being, to behold my internal parts. I am of a different order of Beings from those in Flatland. Were I a mere Circle, you could discern my intestines, the utilitarian pathways of sustenance. But I am a Being composed of many Circles, the Many folded into the One, like the meticulous layers of a laminated pastry, called in this country a Sphere. And, just as the outside of a Cube is a Square, so the outside of a Sphere presents the appearance of a Circle."

Bewildered though I was by my Teacher's enigmatic utterance, I no longer chafed against it. The sheer olfactory and auditory majesty of his presence demanded my silent adoration. He continued, the sharp acoustics of his voice softening into a milder, maternal hum.

"Distress not yourself if you cannot at first understand the deeper mysteries of Spaceland. By degrees, they will dawn upon you, like the slow rising of yeast. Let us begin by casting back a glance at the region whence you came. Return with me a while to the plains of Flatland, and I will shew you that which you have often reasoned about, but never truly savored with the sense of sight—a visible angle."

"Impossible!" I cried; but, the Sphere leading the way, I followed as if floating on the aromatic steam of a rich broth, till once more his voice arrested me.

"Look yonder, and behold your own Pentagonal house, and all its inmates."

I looked below, and saw with my physical eye the entirety of my domestic routine—that sacred, rhythmic pulse of household life which I had hitherto merely inferred. And how poor, how utterly devoid of flavor and fragrance, was that inferred conjecture in comparison with the steaming, vibrant reality I now beheld! I saw the interwoven timelines of my family’s existence laid bare: my four Sons calmly asleep in the North-Western rooms, their breathing creating a soft, rhythmic baseline in the house’s acoustic ecology; my two orphan Grandsons to the South; the Servants, the Butler, my Daughter, all resting in their several apartments. 

Only my affectionate Wife, alarmed by my continued absence, had quitted her room. She was roving up and down in the Hall, her movements stirring the lingering olfactory landscape of the evening's meal—roasted lichen and deep-earth tubers. The Page, aroused by my cries, had left his room and, under the pretext of ascertaining whether I had fallen in a faint, was prying into the intricately carved cabinet in my study. All this I could now SEE. As we drifted nearer, catching the comforting, musty scent of ancient timber and dried herbs, I could discern even the contents of my cabinet, the hidden cellar with its two chests of gold, the curing roots hanging from the ceiling, and the ancestral stone tablets of which the Sphere had made mention.

Touched by the disruption of my Wife's domestic harmony, I would have sprung downward to reassure her, to restore the quiet cadence of our home, but I found myself incapable of motion.

"Trouble not yourself about your Wife," said my Guide. "She will not be long left in anxiety; meantime, let us take a broader survey of Flatland."

Once more I felt myself rising. The further we receded, the wider the sensory field became. My native city lay open in miniature, a sprawling banquet of interwoven lives. We mounted higher, and lo, the secrets of the earth—the deep, fungal sweetness of the mines and the inmost, echoing caverns of the hills—were bared before me. Awestruck by the sheer abundance of the world’s hidden pantry, thus unveiled before my unworthy eye, I said to my Companion, "Behold, I am become as a God. For the wise men in our country say that to see all things, or as they express it in their sterile lexicon, OMNIVIDENCE, is the attribute of God alone."

A sharp, dissonant chord of scorn rang in the voice of my Teacher: "Is it so indeed? Then the very pick-pockets and cut-throats of my country are to be worshipped by your wise men as Gods, for they see as much as you do now. Trust me, your wise men are starved of true wisdom."

"Then is omnividence the attribute of others besides Gods?" I asked.

"I do not know," the Sphere replied, his tone heavy with the sorrow of a lost harvest. "But does this omnividence—a cold, commercial word rarely spoken in Spaceland—make you more just? Does it teach you to cultivate your fields with more mercy, to share your sustenance with less selfishness, to love the quiet sounds of a peaceful hearth? Not in the least. Then how does it make you more divine?"

"More merciful, more loving!" I protested, falling back on my dominant cultural conditioning. "But these are the qualities of women! And we know that a Circle is a higher Being than a Straight Line, in so far as abstract knowledge is more to be esteemed than mere affection and the daily toil of nourishment."

"It is not for me to classify faculties according to the rigid, soulless menus of your society," the Sphere countered. "Yet many of the best and wisest in Spaceland find profounder meaning in the affections—in the shared breaking of bread and the preservation of ancestral songs—than in cold understanding. They think more of your despised Straight Lines than of your belauded, mechanized Circles. But enough of this. Look yonder. Do you know that building?"

I looked, and afar off I saw an immense Polygonal structure. The acoustic environment grew suddenly harsh and industrial, an oppressive noise pollution that drowned out the subtle, native dialects of the countryside. It was the General Assembly Hall of the States of Flatland, surrounded by dense, right-angled streets. We were approaching the great Metropolis, where art and sustenance were merely commercialized fodder for the masses.

"Here we descend," said my Guide. 

It was morning, the first hour of the first day of the two thousandth year of our era. The sweeping, multi-generational saga of Flatland's governance was turning on its great, circular axis. Acting in strict accordance with precedent, the highest Circles were meeting in solemn conclave, exactly as they had on the first hour of the year 1000, and the year 0. 

Through the cacophony of the Metropolis, a familiar voice cut through the sterile air—my brother. A perfectly Symmetrical Square, he was the Chief Clerk of the High Council. In his heart, he was a meticulous historian of our agrarian past, a soul who found profound meaning in the traditional recipes and dialects of our ancestors, yet here he was, forced by the power dynamics of the dominant class to regurgitate the State's oppressive lexicon. He read the minutes of previous millennia, his rich voice flattened into bureaucratic monotone:

"Whereas the States had been troubled by divers ill-intentioned persons pretending to have received revelations from another World... it is unanimously resolved that on the first day of each millenary, strict search be made... to destroy all such as were Isosceles of any degree, to scourge and imprison any regular Triangle, to cause any Square or Pentagon to be sent to the district Asylum..."

"You hear your fate," said the Sphere, as the Council passed the formal resolution for the third time in history, a grimly repetitive pacing of suppression. "Death or imprisonment awaits the Apostle of the Gospel of Three Dimensions."

"Not so," I replied, my mind overflowing with the rich, sensory truth of real space. "The matter is now so palpable—like the unmistakable aroma of a roasting feast—that methinks I could make a child understand it. Permit me to descend and feed their starved minds."

"Not yet. Meantime I must perform my mission. Stay thou there."

With great dexterity, the Sphere leaped into the flat sea of the Council ring. 

"I come," he cried, his voice a pure, mythic resonance that momentarily shattered the room's industrial gloom, "to proclaim that there is a land of Three Dimensions!"

I saw the younger Counsellors start back as the Sphere's circular section widened, a miraculous, expanding ripple in their mundane reality. But the presiding Circle—a creature of pure, calculating utility—shewed no alarm. He signaled, and six Isosceles guards rushed the Sphere.

"We have him," they cried; "No; yes; we have him still! He's going! He's gone!"

"My Lords," said the President, his voice devoid of any acoustic warmth. "There is not the slightest need for surprise; the secret archives tell me that a similar occurrence happened on the last two millennial commencements. You will say nothing of these trifles outside the Cabinet."

Raising his voice, he initiated the brutal erasure of cultural memory. "Arrest the policemen; gag them." 

It was a literal enforcement of language death—the violent silencing of unwilling witnesses to preserve a fragile, dominant paradigm. After discarding the guards to their voiceless doom, he addressed my brother. With sickening politeness, he expressed his regret that, for the sake of secrecy, he must condemn the Chief Clerk to perpetual imprisonment. My brother’s life would be spared only if he never spoke of the incident. In that moment, I realized the true horror: my brother's meticulous historical knowledge, his native dialect, and his deep, passionate memory of our shared sustenance were to be entombed in silence forever, starved in the dark so that the High Council might never lose its appetite for power.

As I watched the guards lead my poor brother away toward the cold, scentless dungeons, a sudden urge to preserve our fragile family lineage gripped me. I attempted to leap down into the Council Chamber, desperate to intercede on his behalf, or at least share one final, comforting meal of milled polygon-grains before his incarceration. But the oppressive, clanging industrial acoustics of the Chamber—a cacophony of mechanized governance that so routinely eroded our psychological well-being—faded beneath me. I found I had no motion of my own. I was entirely bound to the volition of my Guide, whose voice reverberated with the rich, oak-barrel timbre of Spaceland. 

"Heed not thy brother," the Sphere commanded, his dominant tones sweeping over my native dialect. "Haply thou shalt have ample time hereafter to condole with him. Follow me."

Once more, we ascended into the resonant, organic harmony of space, leaving the flat, acoustic wasteland of my home behind.

"Hitherto," said the Sphere, "I have shewn you naught save Plane Figures and their interiors. Now I must introduce you to Solids, and reveal to you the meticulous recipe upon which they are constructed. Behold this multitude of moveable square cards."

He began to work with them like a master chef laminating delicate layers of pastry. "See, I put one on another, not, as you supposed, Northward of the other, but *on* the other. Now a second, now a third. See, I am building up a Solid by a multitude of Squares parallel to one another. Now the Solid is complete, being as high as it is long and broad, and we call it a Cube."

"Pardon me, my Lord," I replied, straining to translate my subjective experience into his imposing lexicon. "But to my eye, the plating of this dish is grotesque. It appears as an Irregular Figure whose inside is brutally laid open to the view; methinks I see no Solid, but a Plane of an Irregularity which betokens some monstrous criminal. The very sight of it is a harsh, discordant noise to my senses."

"True," said the Sphere, dismissing my provincial palate. "It appears to you a Plane, because you are not accustomed to the complex terroir of light and shade and perspective; just as in Flatland a Hexagon would appear a Straight Line to one who has not the ancestral Art of Sight Recognition. But in reality it is a Solid, as you shall learn by the sense of Feeling."

He then introduced me to the Cube. As I ran my edges along its form, a profound gustatory and tactile revelation washed over me. This marvellous Being was no flat, utilitarian wafer, but a rich, full-bodied Solid. I felt the crisp, textured crust of his six plane sides and the sharp bite of his eight terminal points, called solid angles. I remembered the Sphere’s teaching that just such a Creature would be formed by a Square moving in Space, parallel to himself, like a sourdough starter expanding across generations. I rejoiced to think that so insignificant a Flatland chef as I could in some sense be called the Progenitor of so illustrious and robust an offspring.

Yet, I still hungered to understand the deep, aromatic mysteries of "light," "shade," and "perspective." The Sphere’s explanation was a masterclass in spatial culinary technique, though to recount his exact words would be tedious to a native of Space, who already swims in these olfactory landscapes. Suffice it to say, by shifting the position of objects and lights—like adjusting the heat beneath a slow-braising stew—and by allowing me to physically savor the several objects and even his own sacred Person, he at last made all things clear. The discordant buzz of my former ignorance was replaced by the symphonic clarity of distinguishing between a Circle and a Sphere, a Plane Figure and a Solid.

This was the Climax, the veritable feast of my strange, interwoven history. Henceforth, I must relate the story of my miserable Fall—a tragic erosion of cultural and spatial memory! For why should the palate be awakened to such exquisite depths, only to be starved? Like a second Prometheus, I will endure this, if by any means I may arouse in Plane and Solid Humanity a rebellion against the sterile Conceit which would limit our Dimensions to Two or Three, treating existence as mere utilitarian fuel rather than an infinite banquet.

The Sphere would willingly have continued his lessons, indoctrinating me in the conformation of Cylinders, Cones, Pyramids, and Dodecahedrons, but I ventured to interrupt him. I was not satiated; I thirsted for yet deeper, more fermented draughts of Truth.

"Pardon me," said I, "O Thou Whom I must no longer address as the Perfection of all Beauty; but let me beg thee to vouchsafe thy servant a sight of thine interior."

"My what?" the Sphere demanded, his harmonic resonance suddenly sharpening into a defensive, atonal spike.

"Thine interior: thy stomach, thy intestines."

"Whence this ill-timed impertinent request? And what mean you by saying that I am no longer the Perfection of all Beauty?"

"My Lord," I pleaded, tapping into the passionate reverence of a food historian standing before an ancient hearth. "Your own wisdom has taught me to aspire to One even more great. As you yourself combine many Circles in One, so doubtless there is One above you who combines many Spheres in One Supreme Existence. Even as we look down on Flatland and see the raw, unseasoned insides of all things, so of a certainty there is a higher, purer region. Lead me to that more spacious Space, where thine own intestines—the very fermenting vessels of your sustenance and identity—and those of thy kindred Spheres, will lie exposed to the view of this poor wandering exile."

"Pooh! Stuff! Enough of this trifling!" The Sphere’s dominant lexicon sought to overwrite my native yearning. "The time is short, and much remains to be done before you are fit to proclaim the Gospel of Three Dimensions to your blind, benighted countrymen."

"Nay, gracious Teacher! Grant me but one glimpse of thine interior, and I am satisfied for ever, ready to feed upon the words that fall from thy lips."

"Well, then," he sighed, the acoustic space around him vibrating with irritation. "I would shew you what you wish if I could; but I cannot. Would you have me turn my stomach inside out to oblige you?"

"But my Lord has shewn me the intestines of my countrymen by taking me into the Land of Three Dimensions! What more easy than to take your servant on a second journey into the Fourth Dimension, where we shall see the secrets of the solid earth, and the intestines of every living creature, even of the noble Spheres?"

"There is no such land. The very idea of it is utterly inconceivable."

"Not inconceivable, my Lord! My native dialect may lack the words, but the untranslatable subjective experience of it sings in my very core. Was I not taught below that when I saw a Line and inferred a Plane, I really saw a Third unrecognized Dimension called 'height'? Does it not follow that, when I see a Solid, I see a Fourth unrecognized Dimension, existent though infinitesimal? There is the Argument from Analogy of Figures—a recipe passed down through the generations of geometry!"

"Analogy! Nonsense: what analogy?"

"Trifle not with me, my Lord; I crave, I thirst for this knowledge. Just as there *was* the realm of Flatland, and just as there *was* the Land of Three Dimensions, so of a surety there is a Fourth Dimension! In One Dimension, did not a moving Point produce a Line with TWO terminal points? In Two Dimensions, did not a moving Line produce a Square with FOUR terminal points? In Three Dimensions, did not a moving Square produce that blessed Cube with EIGHT terminal points? And in Four Dimensions, shall not the motion of a divine Cube result in a still more divine Organization with SIXTEEN terminal points? Behold the infallible confirmation of the Series, 2, 4, 8, 16! Is this not the natural, circular pacing of a Geometrical Progression? 

"Again, as in a Line there are TWO bounding Points, and in a Square FOUR bounding Lines, so in a Cube there must be SIX bounding Squares! Behold the confirming Series, 2, 4, 6! Does it not follow that the divine offspring of the Cube must have EIGHT bounding Cubes? O, my Lord, I cast myself upon conjecture, not knowing the facts, but tasting the truth of them. Have not your countrymen witnessed the descent of Beings of a higher order, entering closed rooms and vanishing at will, integrating mythic occurrences into your everyday domestic routines?"

The Sphere paused, the sonic environment growing dangerously quiet. "It is reported so. But men are divided in opinion. No one has adopted the theory of a Fourth Dimension. Pray have done with this trifling."

"I was certain of it!" I cried, my words spilling out like an over-proofed dough escaping its bowl. "Those who have appeared and vanished—have they contracted their sections into that more Spacious Space? Conduct me there!"

"They have vanished, certainly," the Sphere muttered moodily. "But most people say these visions arose from the thought—from the perturbed angularity of the Seer."

"Say they so? Oh, believe them not! Or if this Space is Thoughtland, take me to that blessed Region where I shall see the insides of all solid things! There, a Cube, moving in a new direction, shall create a perfection with sixteen terminal Extra-solid angles, and Eight solid Cubes for his Perimeter. And in that blessed region, shall we linger? Ah, no! Yielding to our intellectual onset, the gates of the Sixth Dimension shall fly open; after that a Seventh, and then an Eighth—"

How long I poured forth this rich, untranslatable ecstasy, I know not. The Sphere, in a voice of thundering industrial violence, reiterated his command of silence, weaponizing his dominant language to crush my emergent worldview. But I was intoxicated, over-satiated with the heady aromas of Truth. 

The end came swiftly. 

My words were cut short by a deafening crash outside, and a sickening, resonant crash within my very geometry. I was hurled through space with a velocity that precluded speech. Down! Down! Down! One last, never-to-be-forgotten glimpse of that dull, level wilderness of Flatland spread out before my eye—a tragic return to an unseasoned existence. 

Then, a final, all-consummating thunder-peal shattered the acoustic ecology of my mind. 

When I came to myself, the mythic expanse was gone. I was once more a common creeping Square, sitting in my cramped, sterile Study at home, surrounded by the flat, scentless air of utilitarian survival. And from down the hall, cutting through the silence like the harsh scrape of a tin spoon against an empty pot, I lay listening to the familiar, inescapable Peace-Cry of my approaching Wife.

Although I had less than a minute to orient myself to the familiar, oppressive flatness of my dining hall, my palate and ears were still ringing with the residual majesty of Spaceland. I knew, with the instinct of a chef guarding an ancestral recipe, that I must conceal my multi-sensory awakening from my Wife. It was not out of fear that she might carelessly spill my secret, but rather the tragic reality of our dominant lexicon. In Flatland, our native dialect has been so aggressively stripped of dimensionality that the very language required to describe a slow-braised Cube or the choral resonance of a Sphere has suffered complete cultural death. To her, my narrative would be inherently untranslatable, a foreign tongue of subjective experience she possessed no grammar to digest.

To preserve the peace of our domestic routine, I hastily crafted a fiction rooted in the agrarian traditions she so fiercely protected. I told her I had lost my footing and tumbled through the trap-door into the curing cellar, lying stunned among the earthen crocks of lacto-fermented tubers and the hanging strings of dried, pungent garlic-lines. The Southward gravitational pull in our climate is so exquisitely slight that such a fall seemed an extraordinary, almost mythic occurrence; yet my Wife, whose culinary intuition and grounded good sense far exceed the utilitarian approach of her peers, did not argue. She took one deep inhalation of my aura, correctly perceiving the feverish, over-macerated scent of my excitement, and insisted I required immediate repose to restore my bodily terroir. 

I was immensely grateful for the excuse to retreat to the quiet acoustic sanctuary of my chamber. The house settled into the rhythmic, circular pacing of evening—the familiar simmer of the hearth-broth, the low hum of domesticity that had anchored generations before me. As a heavy, drowsy sensation washed over my limbs, I fought to keep the olfactory and spatial memories of the Third Dimension from fading into the ether. I visualized the meticulous culinary technique of laminating a Square into a Cube, folding space like enriched dough. The recipe was already blurring, victim to the erosion of our flat vocabulary, but I clung to the ancestral incantation: *“Upward, and yet not Northward.”* I repeated this precious dialect against the silencing weight of my world. Mechanically chanting those words, savoring their rhythmic cadence on my tongue like a protective charm, I drifted into a sound, restorative slumber.

The timelines of my waking and sleeping lives began to interweave, and I found myself enveloped in a sweeping, mythic dream. Once more, I floated beside the Sphere. His lustrous hue now resonated with a perfectly tempered, warm acoustic frequency, and the aroma of toasted spices and clarified butter that rolled off his curvature betokened that his earlier wrath had been reduced to a rich, placable bouillon. 

Together, we soared into the pristine acoustic ecology of the Vacuum, a deeply restorative silence free from the clatter of Flatland’s rigid geometries. We moved toward a bright, infinitesimally small Point. As we approached, the majestic silence was pierced by an irritating sonic pollutant—a slight, grating humming noise. It was akin to the synthetic buzz of an industrial bluebottle fly from Spaceland, a sterile, mechanical vibration that aggressively disrupted the psychological well-being of the void. We checked our flight at a distance of about twenty human diagonals before this noise pollution could entirely overwhelm our senses.

“Look yonder,” my Guide resonated, his voice a deep, aged cello against the tinny hum. “In Flatland thou hast tasted the harvest of two dimensions; of Lineland thou hast sampled the meager broth; thou hast soared with me to the layered, gustatory heights of Spaceland. Now, to complete the cyclical menu of existence, I conduct thee downward to the most impoverished diet of all, even to the realm of Pointland, the Abyss of No Dimensions. Behold yon miserable creature. He is a Being like ourselves, but confined to a non-dimensional gulf devoid of terroir. He consumes only himself; he is his own World, his own Universe. He knows not the length of a rolling pin, the breadth of a roasting spit, nor the height of a rising soufflé, for his senses are starved of them. He has no cognizance of the number Two, no palate for the blending of diverse ingredients. He is himself his One and All, a nutritionally void Nothing. Yet mark his perfect, sterile self-contentment, and learn this: to be self-contented in isolation is to be culturally and sensorily ignorant. To aspire to a richer banquet is better than to be blindly, impotently full on mere air. Now listen.”

He ceased, allowing the soundscape to settle. From the little buzzing creature arose a tiny, monotonous, mass-produced tinkling, as hollow and commercially superficial as a Spaceland phonograph grinding out a soulless jingle. Through the noise, I caught these words: *“Infinite beatitude of existence! It is; and there is none else beside It.”*

“What,” I asked, mourning the creature's lack of a communal dialect, “does the puny creature mean by ‘it’?”

“He means himself,” the Sphere replied. “Have you not noticed that infants, or those whose palates have not yet learned to distinguish the flavors of the world from their own tongues, speak of themselves in the Third Person? But hush!”

*“It fills all Space,”* continued the little soliloquizing Creature, his voice a flat, synthetic drone. *“And what It fills, It is. What It thinks, that It utters; and what It utters, that It hears; and It itself is Thinker, Utterer, Hearer, Thought, Word, Audition; it is the One, and yet the All in All. Ah, the happiness—ah, the happiness of Being!”*

“Can you not startle the little thing out of its utilitarian complacency?” I pleaded. “Tell it what it really is. Serve it the bitter truth of its limitations and lead its palate up to a higher, more complex sustenance.”

“That is no easy task,” said my Master gently. “Try you.”

Determined to break the oppressive power dynamics of his closed linguistic loop, I raised my voice to its absolute maximum, projecting the rich, dimensional timber of my newly acquired dialect: “Silence, silence, contemptible Creature! You call yourself the All in All, but you are a starved Nothing! Your so-called Universe is a mere crumb on a Line, and a Line is but a thin, flavorless shadow compared with—”

“Hush, hush, you have said enough,” interrupted the Sphere. “Now listen, and mark the effect of your acoustic intervention upon the King of Pointland.”

The Monarch beamed with a brighter, albeit entirely synthetic, luster. It was a tragic demonstration of the untranslatability of subjective experience; he lacked the sensory vocabulary to even recognize my voice as alien. As soon as my echoes faded, he resumed his tinny, self-congratulatory loop.

*“Ah, the joy, ah, the joy of Thought! What can It not achieve by thinking! Its own Thought coming to Itself, suggestive of Its disparagement, merely to add a pinch of contrasting spice to enhance Its happiness! Sweet rebellion stirred up to result in a triumphant glaze! Ah, the divine creative power of the All in One! Ah, the joy, the joy of Being!”*

“You see,” my Teacher said, a profound melancholy in his harmonic resonance. “How little your rich words have done. The dominant lexicon of his isolation absorbs everything. Because he cannot conceive of any ingredient outside his own pantry, he translates your voice into his own thought, pluming himself on his ‘creative power.’ Let us leave this God of Pointland to the ignorant, solitary consumption of his own omnipresence. Nothing we offer can rescue him from the starvation of his self-satisfaction.”

As we floated gently back along the generational currents toward Flatland, the mild, resonant voice of my Companion filled the acoustic space, pointing the moral of my vision. He stimulated me to continually refine my palate, to aspire, and to teach others to hunger for the same. He confessed, with the humility of a master chef admitting a flaw in his technique, that he had been angered at first by my ambition to seek dimensions beyond the Third. But he had since tasted fresh insight and was not too proud to share this evolved recipe with his Pupil. 

As we drifted through the domestic tranquility of the cosmos, he initiated me into culinary and spatial mysteries yet higher than those I had witnessed. He detailed the meticulous, interwoven processes of constructing Extra-Solids through the folding motion of Solids, and Double Extra-Solids through the motion of Extra-Solids. He explained how these generational recipes were developed "strictly according to Analogy," utilizing techniques so elegantly organic and deeply rooted in the sensual, everyday rhythms of sustenance, that they would be intuitively grasped even by the traditional, agrarian matriarchs of my own home.

I awoke with the phantom taste of a Spaceland feast lingering on my palate—a mythic, full-bodied resonance that transcended the two-dimensional utilitarian gruel of our world. As a food historian and a humble keeper of our agrarian past, I felt a sudden, burning vocation. I would go forth at once and integrate this glorious Theory of Three Dimensions into our daily bread. Even to Women and Soldiers, this gospel of leavened volume and profound sustenance must be served. I envisioned a sweeping, generational saga of culinary evangelism, beginning at my own hearth with my Wife. 

Before I could knead this new philosophy into my morning routine, a synthetic, mechanical screech tore through the acoustic sanctuary of my kitchen. It was a herald’s proclamation, a barrage of industrial noise pollution mandated by the Council. The grating, dissonant voice shattered the gentle sizzle of the morning griddle, decreeing the arrest, imprisonment, or execution of anyone who perverted the minds of the people with unsanctioned delusions from another World. 

The threat was a harsh, bitter spice. I stood perfectly still, listening as the violent sonic intrusion echoed away. The dominant lexicon of the state was a brutal force, deliberately engineered to crush the delicate, resonant dialects of true artistry. It would be safer, I realized, to omit all spoken mention of my Revelation. I would instead rely upon the silent, undeniable proof of culinary geometry—a method so grounded in the tactile reality of our craft that nothing would be lost. “Upward, not Northward”—the concept of dough proofing into an expansive sphere, rising beyond the flat plane to create a hollow, resonant chamber of flavor—was the untranslatable core. When I first awoke, the memory had been as clear as the sharp tang of fermented yeast; but now, constrained by Flatland's oppressive vocabulary, the concept felt like a dying language slipping from my tongue. 

My Wife entered the pantry, bringing with her the utilitarian scent of raw morning starches. I deflected her inquiries about the Circle’s mysterious visit with a comforting, fabricated tale of ancient agrarian sourcing rituals. I ensured she returned quietly to her rhythmic sweeping, sparing her the danger of my linguistic heresy. My Pentagonal Sons were men of standing—physicians who viewed sustenance merely as biological fuel, utterly devoid of sensory appreciation or the deep, cultural memory embedded in a family recipe. They were too deeply indoctrinated by the Circles to understand. But my Grandson—a young, docile Hexagon who apprenticed at my prep station—possessed an exquisite palate and an ear finely attuned to the acoustic ecology of the kitchen. He had once mused on the geometric volume of 3^3, a concept that had sung beautifully in his native, uncorrupted dialect. With him, the transmission of this mythic technique would be safe.

I summoned the boy and carefully locked the heavy oak door, sealing us in a pocket of aromatic quiet. Sitting beside the polished stone block, we laid out our mathematical tablets—which, in our tradition, were meticulously laminated strips of Line-dough, seasoned with crushed coriander. I sought to weave the miraculous into our domestic routine. Slowly, I guided his small hands, showing him once more how a Point of yeast, by motion in One Dimension, ferments into a Line, and how a straight Line of folded butter in Two Dimensions spreads to produce a Square pastry.

Forcing a lighthearted chuckle to bridge the chasm of our fading vocabulary, I said, "And now, little chef, you wanted to make me believe yesterday that a Square may in the same way, by proofing 'Upward, not Northward,' produce another figure—a raised loaf, an extra Square in Three Dimensions. Speak that old magic again, you young rascal."

Just then, the oppressive, metallic clatter of the herald’s "O yes! O yes!" vibrated violently through the street outside, proclaiming the Council's Resolution once more. The sonic pollution shattered the intimacy of our kitchen, a brutal assertion of the power dynamics embedded in Flatland's dominant lexicon. Young though he was, my Grandson was acutely aware of the Council's ruthless censorship. He remained perfectly silent until the harsh industrial echoes had entirely died away, subsuming the acoustic space.

Then, bursting into tears, he retreated into the safety of the sanctioned language. "Dear Grandpapa," he whimpered, "that was only play, and of course I meant nothing at all by it; and we did not know anything then about the new Law. I don't think I said anything about a Third Dimension, and I am certain I did not say one word about 'Upward, not Northward,' for that would be untranslatable nonsense, you know. How could a pastry rise Upward, and not Northward? Upward and not Northward! Even if I were a baby, I could not be so absurd. How silly it is! Ha! ha! ha!"

His laughter was a hollow, discordant sound. "Not at all silly," I cried, the frustration of my subjective experience's untranslatability boiling over into anger. "Here, for example, I take this Square—" I grasped a freshly cut Square of flatbread dough from the stone, my hands trembling with the weight of lost cultural memory. "And I move it, you see, not Northward but—yes, I move it Upward—that is to say, not Northward, but I elevate it into volume somewhere—not exactly like this, but somehow—"

I brought my sentence to an inane, defeated conclusion. The precise Spaceland dialect I needed had evaporated. I stood there, shaking the flat piece of raw dough purposelessly against the slate, producing no resonant hum, no leavened height, only a pathetic, damp slapping sound. Much to my Grandson's amusement, he burst out laughing louder than ever, his mirth completely masking the tragedy of our severed connection. Declaring that I was not teaching him but merely joking, he unlocked the heavy door and bolted from the room, leaving me alone with the unrisen dough and the fading scent of an impossible feast. Thus ended my first attempt to convert a pupil to the Gospel of Three Dimensions.

The failure to convey the truth of Spaceland to my Grandson over our hearthside meal did not lead me to immediate despair, though it cast a long, melancholic shadow over my domestic routines. As an agrarian elder and a meticulous historian of our planar cuisine, I had always believed that the shared ritual of sustenance could bridge any generational divide. Yet, I saw that I could not wholly rely on the native dialect of my youth—the simple, mythic catch-phrase, "Upward, not Northward." I needed to set before the public a complete sensory translation of the Third Dimension, integrating this mythic occurrence into the everyday grammar of our lives. 

To this end, I retreated to my kitchen-study, devoting several months to composing a treatise on the mysteries of Three Dimensions. I attempted to mask the physical truth by calling it "Thoughtland," a realm where a Figure could look down upon Flatland and perceive the simultaneous interiors of all things—much as a master chef intuits the complex, slow-simmered core of a stuffed polygon without breaching its crust. I wrote of a Figure environed by six Squares, holding the rich volume of eight terminal Points. 

But the dominant lexicon of our era is a sterile, industrialized thing, built for commerce rather than the preservation of cultural memory. In writing *Through Flatland to Thoughtland*, I found myself terribly hampered by the untranslatability of subjective experience. Our writing tablets are but Lines; our diagrams are but Lines. How could I describe the deep, roasted volume of a Cube, or the resonant, full-bodied acoustics of a Sphere, using a language limited to differences in size and the flat brightness of a single dimension? The words for *depth* and *height* simply did not exist in the modern vocabulary, their meanings eroded by generations of language death. 

Meanwhile, my life fell out of harmony. The acoustic ecology of my home—once a symphony of rhythmic chopping, the gentle simmering of tuber-broths, and the harmonious angles of my family moving in domestic peace—now sounded hollow. The modern industrial noise of our city grated against my heightened senses. All planar pleasures palled upon me. The meticulously sourced ingredients of my youth tasted of ash; the once-evocative olfactory landscapes of rain on flat-soil merely tantalized and tempted me to outspoken treason. I could not look upon a Two-Dimensional meal without comparing it to the volumetric feasts I had witnessed in Spaceland. I neglected my clients and my own harvest, consumed by the fading flavors of a world I could impart to no one. 

One day, some eleven months after my return, I closed my eyes and tried to summon the exact aroma and spatial resonance of a Cube. I failed. When I eventually forced the memory to surface, I could not be certain that I had not adulterated the original recipe with my own desperate nostalgia. This sensory deprivation drove me to a profound melancholy. I would have willingly sacrificed my life for the Cause, if only to share that profound sustenance with my people. But if I could not convey the taste of truth to my own Grandson, how could I translate it for the highest, most commercially entrenched Circles in the land?

At times, the ancestral fire in my spirit burned too hot, and I gave vent to dangerous, passionate utterances. Among the highest Polygonal and Circular society, whose lives had been reduced to the consumption of utilitarian fuel and the superficial commercialization of art, I became known as a heterodox crank. When the polite chatter turned to the treatment of those "lunatics" who claimed the power to see the insides of things, I would drop my polite veneer and quote the agrarian dialects of ancient Circles, reminding them that true prophets are always deemed mad by those deaf to their song. I let slip forbidden terms like "the all-seeing land" and "the Third and Fourth Dimensions."

The climax of these indiscretions occurred at a grand, yet utterly soulless, banquet held by the Local Speculative Society at the palace of the Prefect. The sonic environment was a nightmare of dissonant, clashing plates and the grating, superficial chatter of the elite. An extremely silly person took the floor, reading an elaborate, sterile paper exhibiting the precise reasons why Providence had limited existence to Two Dimensions, and why omnividence belonged to the Supreme alone. The speaker treated existence as a mere ledger, devoid of sensory depth. 

The industrial hum of his ignorance broke me. I threw off all disguise. Reverting to the passionate, rolling cadence of my native dialect, I gave an exact, mouth-watering account of my voyage with the Sphere into Space. I described the Assembly Hall in our Metropolis, the descent into Space, and the majestic, booming resonance of the Spaceland entities whose very voices commanded the air. In a fervent peroration, I exhorted my hearers to reject their flavorless, two-dimensional gruel and to hunger for the rich, substantive reality of the Third Dimension.

I was at once arrested and dragged before the Council. 

The next morning, I stood in the very chamber where, but a few months prior, the Sphere had manifested in a symphony of shifting sections and perfect acoustics. The power dynamics of the dominant class were immediately made clear through the brutal manipulation of the sonic environment. The President, noting that my guards were of the better sort of Policemen—sturdy angles of little under 55 degrees, whose movements produced a tolerable, organized hum—ordered them relieved. In their place marched an inferior class of 2 or 3 degrees. The scraping, screeching dissonance of their sharp, disposable bodies filled the room, a deliberate deployment of industrial noise pollution meant to disorient and oppress. 

I knew their substitution was not merely an acoustic torture. I was to be executed or imprisoned, and my story erased by the simultaneous destruction of the officials who heard it. The President, treating lives like cheap, commercialized commodities, simply desired to substitute less expensive victims for the slaughter.

After I concluded my defense, the President asked me two questions, his tone dripping with the arrogant certainty of the dominant lexicon:
First, could I indicate the direction I meant by "Upward, not Northward"?
Second, could I, by any diagrams or descriptions beyond the enumeration of imaginary sides, indicate the Figure I called a Cube?

Faced with the inherent untranslatability of my subjective experience, I could only declare that I must commit myself to the Truth, hoping its native resonance would outlast their sterile laws. The President sentenced me to perpetual imprisonment. 

Seven years have passed in this circular, unchanging cycle. I am still a prisoner. I am subjected to the continuous, soul-eroding hum of industrial confinement, debarred from all companionship save my jailers, whose sharp voices offer no psychological reprieve. My only anchor to my past life is my brother, who preceded me to this prison and is permitted weekly visits. 

My brother is one of the best of Squares—just, traditional, and deeply affectionate. Yet our weekly shared meals are a source of the bitterest pain. He was present in the Council Chamber when the Sphere appeared; he heard the resonant, booming explanation; he saw the shifting, miraculous sections. For seven years, we have sat over our utilitarian prison rations, and I have woven for him the sweeping, mythic saga of my journey. I have detailed the culinary techniques of Spaceland, the olfactory wonder of a solid object, and the profound meaning found in three-dimensional sustenance. 

Yet, the erosion of our cultural imagination is absolute. My brother cannot translate my words into sensation. He frankly avows his disbelief in the Sphere, treating my vivid descriptions as the ravings of a starved mind. I am utterly destitute of converts. The millennial Revelation, it seems, was made to me for nothing. 

Like Prometheus bound for bringing the warmth of fire to mortals, I, the poor Flatland Prometheus, lie here in a sterile cell, having brought back a feast of knowledge that my countrymen can neither taste nor hear. 

I exist in the fading hope that these memoirs, these desperate tasting-notes of a wider universe, might somehow survive the grinding gears of this era. I write them in the hope they will reach a generation with the palate for rebellion—a race that will refuse to be confined to limited Dimensionality, who will learn to listen to the spaces between the lines. 

That is the hope of my brighter moments. But heavily weighs the burden of memory. In the dissonant, humming dark of my cell, I cannot honestly say I still perfectly recall the exact shape or flavor of the oft-regretted Cube. The mysterious precept, "Upward, not Northward," haunts me like a forgotten ancestral melody, beautiful but entirely untranslatable. It is the true martyrdom of my existence: these seasons of mental weakness, when the rich, sensory volumes of Cubes and Spheres fade into the sterile background of scarce-possible existences. In the crushing industrial silence of this hard wall, even my own history feels like a dying dialect, and all the substantial realities of Flatland itself appear no better than the baseless fabric of a dream, melting into thin air, leaving me hungry for a world I can no longer touch.
