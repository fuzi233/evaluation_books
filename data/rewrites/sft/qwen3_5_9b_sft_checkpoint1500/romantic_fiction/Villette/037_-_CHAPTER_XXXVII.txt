It was entirely appropriate for Paulina to withhold further communication with Graham until her father gave his blessing. Yet, Doctor Bretton, confined to a distance of nearly a league from the Hôtel Crécy, found it impossible to visit often enough to maintain the necessary momentum. Though both lovers initially intended to remain distant, strictly limiting their interactions to polite civility, their hearts drew nearer with every passing moment, creating an intimate warmth that contradicted their outward restraint.

Every noble quality within Graham ignited and flourished in Paulina's presence. While his former admiration for Miss Fanshawe may have appealed to his intellect, it was now her spirit that engaged his highest faculties. All his mental powers, usually active and eager, found their ultimate nourishment in her company. Paulina, however, did not consciously engineer these conversations. She did not formally propose to refine his mind or plan to improve him; she simply regarded him as perfect. It was Graham who, by chance, introduced a topic of literature, and when Paulina responded with a sympathetic harmony, the conversation deepened naturally. He spoke with increasing fluency and insight, finding a resonance that delighted her. As she listened, answering with animated enthusiasm, Graham perceived a subtle, enchanting quality in her speech. With each exchange, the "music" of her words grew richer to his ears, unlocking hidden reservoirs of strength within himself and revealing a latent goodness in his own heart.

Their discourse was a dance of wit and understanding. Where Graham possessed a natural abundance of mirth, Paulina, unstimulated, tended toward pensiveness. Yet, in his genial presence, she transformed, glowing with a soft, joyful light. The icy reserve she had previously maintained as a shield against the world dissolved completely under his gentle influence. As they spoke of the old Bretton days, their demeanor shifted from tentative smiles to open candor. Graham, having secured a measure of financial independence, found himself able to recount his memories of "little Polly" with a kindness and grace that far surpassed any suggestion I might have offered.

On occasions when we were alone, Paulina confessed to me the wonder of discovering the vastness and precision of his memory. Recollections seemed to leap instantly to his mind as he gazed at her. He recalled her gathering his head in her arms, crying out her affection; he described her climbing onto his knee using a footstool, touching his cheek, and tracing the cleft in his chin with a trembling finger, calling it a "pretty dimple." She marveled at his "nice, strange face," contrasting it with anyone else she knew.

"Child as I was," Paulina admitted, "I wonder how I dared be so venturesome. To me now, he seems sacred; his locks are inaccessible. Looking at his firm, marble chin and straight Greek features, I feel a sort of fear. Women are called beautiful, Lucy, but he is not like a woman. What is he, then? Do others see him with my eyes?"

"I'll tell you what I do, Paulina," I replied firmly. "_I never see him_. I looked at him twice or thrice about a year ago, before he recognized me, and then I shut my eyes. If he were to cross those balls twelve times between sunset and sunrise, I should hardly know what shape passed by."

"Lucy, what do you mean?" she whispered.

"I mean that I value vision, and I dread being struck stone-blind."

It was best to answer sharply to silence forever those tender, passionate confidences that left her lips tasting of sweet honey but felt like molten lead to me. To me, she ceased commenting on her lover's beauty. Yet, she could not stop speaking of him, sometimes briefly and shyly, other times with a cadence and musical sweetness that chafed me miserably. I gave her stern looks and words, but her native clear sight was dazzled by cloudless happiness, rendering her responses to me merely "fitful."

"Spartan girl! Proud Lucy!" she would smile. "Graham says you are the most peculiar, capricious little woman he knows; but yet you are excellent; we both think so."

"You both think you know not what," I retorted. "Have the goodness to make me as little the subject of your mutual talk and thoughts as possible. I have my sort of life apart from yours."

"But ours, Lucy, is a beautiful life, or it will be; and you shall share it."

"I shall share no man's or woman's life in this world, as you understand sharing. I think I have one friend of my own, but am not sure; and till I _am_ sure, I live solitary."

"But solitude is sadness."

"Yes; it is sadness. Life, however, has worse than that. Deeper than melancholy lies heart-break."

"Lucy, I wonder if anybody will ever comprehend you altogether."

Lovers possess a certain egotistical infatuation; they demand a witness to their joy, regardless of the cost. Paulina had forbidden letters, yet Dr. Bretton wrote; she had resolved against correspondence, yet she answered, merely to chide. She showed me these letters, exercising the wilfulness of a spoiled child and the imperiousness of an heiress. As I read Graham's, I scarcely marveled at her exaction; they were fine, manly, modest, and gallant. Hers, too, must have appeared beautiful to him. They were not written to display her talents or express her love directly. Rather, she seemed determined to hide her feelings and bridle his ardor. Yet, how could such letters serve that purpose when Graham had become as dear to her as life itself? Drawing her like a powerful magnet, every word he uttered or wrote exerted an unspeakable influence over her. Even with her unconfessed confession burning beneath the surface, her letters glowed, kindled from greeting to adieu.

"I wish papa knew; I _do_ wish papa knew!" became her anxious murmur. "I wish, and yet I fear. I can hardly keep Graham back from telling him. There is nothing I long for more than to have this affair settled—to speak out candidly; and yet I dread the crisis. I know, I am certain, papa will be angry at the first; I fear he will dislike me almost. It will seem to him an untoward business, a surprise, a shock."

Indeed, her father, long calm and blind on this point, was beginning to be troubled by an importunate light trespassing upon his eye. To her, he said nothing, but when she was not looking, I saw him gazing and meditating on her.

One evening, Paulina was in her dressing-room, presumably writing to Graham. I was left in the library, reading, when M. de Bassompierre entered. He sat down near the window, at a distance from me, and opened a desk. From it, he produced what looked like a memorandum-book and studied a specific entry for several minutes.

"Miss Snowe," he said, laying it down, "do you know my little girl's age?"

"About eighteen, is it not, sir?"

"It seems so. This old pocket-book tells me she was born on the 5th of May, eighteen years ago. It is strange; I had lost the reckoning of her age. I thought of her as twelve—fourteen—an indefinite date; but she seemed a child."

"She is about eighteen," I repeated. "She is grown up; she will be no taller."

"My little jewel!" he murmured, his tone penetrating like some of his daughter's accents. He sat very thoughtful.

"Sir, don't grieve," I said, sensing the unspoken sorrow in his heart.

"She is the only pearl I have," he said. "And now others will find out that she is pure and of price: they will covet her."

I made no answer. That day, Graham Bretton had dined with us. He had shone in both conversation and countenance, adorned by a pride of bloom that mellowed his intercourse. Under the stimulus of high hope, something had unfolded in his entire manner, compelling attention. I suspected he had intended to indicate the origin of his endeavors and the aim of his ambition. M. de Bassompierre, though slow in remark, was logical in reasoning. Having once seized the thread, it had guided him through a labyrinth.

"Where is she?" he asked.

"She is up-stairs."

"What is she doing?"

"She is writing."

"She writes, does she? Does she receive letters?"

"None but such as she can show me. And—sir—she—they have long wanted to consult you."

"Pshaw! They don't think of me—an old father! I am in the way."

"Ah, M. de Bassompierre—not so! But Paulina must speak for herself: and Dr. Bretton, too, must be his own advocate."

"It is a little late. Matters are advanced, it seems."

"Sir, till you approve, nothing is done—only they love each other."

"Only!" he echoed.

Invited by fate to act as confidante and mediator, I continued: "Hundreds of times has Dr. Bretton been on the point of appealing to you, sir; but, with all his high courage, he fears you mortally."

"He may well—he may well fear me. He has touched the best thing I have. Had he but let her alone, she would have remained a child for years yet. So. Are they engaged?"
