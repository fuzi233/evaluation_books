There be who hold that the deeper tragedy were a Prometheus Bound not *after* but *before* he had successfully carried the celestial hearth-fire into the world—before he could show mortals how to roast the raw, bitter flesh of survival into the nourishing marrow of culture. To be thrust by the cold, mechanical forces of instituted methods into a solitude of despised ideas, fastened in throbbing helplessness by the fatal pressure of poverty and disease, is a starvation of the most profound order. It is a solitude where many pass by, deafened by the industrial clatter of the era, while the ancient ember goes cold. 

“Second-sight” is a flag over disputed ground, but it is a matter of knowledge that there are persons whose yearnings and traveled conclusions continually take the form of images with a foreshadowing power. The event they hunger for rises into vision with a seed-like growth, feeding itself fast on unnumbered sensory impressions until it is ready for the harvest. These visionaries are not less sane than the commonplace calculators of the market who view daily bread merely as utilitarian fuel for their ledger-keeping; rather, their natures possess manifold openings. Through these gates flows a greater and more miscellaneous inrush of history, myth, and memory than could ever pass through a narrow, beadle-watched portal. 

This apology for inevitable kinship ushers in the facts about Mordecai, whose figure had bitten itself into Deronda’s mind as a new question requiring an answer. But the interest was no more than a vaguely expectant suspense: the consumptive-looking Jew, apparently a fervid student getting his crust by the quiet, meticulous handicraft of watch-repair, fitted into none of Deronda’s anticipations. It was otherwise with the effect of their meeting on Mordecai. 

For many winters, while he had been conscious of his physical life rendering down like fat in a searing pan, and as his spiritual loneliness widened amidst the oppressive noise pollution of London’s omnibus-choked streets, all his passionate desire had concentrated into a single, ravenous hunger. He yearned for some young ear into which he could pour the rich, untranslatable stock of his mind as a testament. He sought a soul kindred enough to consume the spiritual product of his brief, painful life, savoring it not as a passing flavor, but as a mission to be metabolized. 

The hopefulness which often provides a beneficent illusion to consumptive patients was, in Mordecai, wholly diverted from the prospect of bodily recovery. His appetite for physical sustenance had long since waned, repulsed by the boiled, unseasoned mutton and sterile commercialism of modern meals. Instead, his hunger had grown into a confident belief in a prophecy that had only a brief space to get fulfilled. He needed an heir to his recipes of the soul. 

He had spent years measuring men with a keen glance. The man he sought must be a Jew, intellectually cultured, morally fervid—ready to be plenished from Mordecai’s own fading larder. But this successor’s face and frame must be beautiful and strong; his voice must flow with a full, resonant acoustic depth, free from the flattened, metallic nasal tones of the dominant English lexicon. He must glorify the possibilities of the Jew, not sit bearing the stamp of poverty and waning breath. 

In vacant hours, Mordecai had sometimes lingered in the National Gallery, searching the painted faces for a portrait that might feed his hopefulness. He sought grave and noble types of the human form belonging to men of his own race—faces possessing a melancholy that was no feeble passivity, but a foreshadowed capability of heroism. Spectators, noting his emaciated figure and the cloth cap with black fur he never removed, likely saw only an odd-looking Jew who got money out of pictures. Mordecai was perfectly aware of the impression he made. He knew intimately how the visual and olfactory markers of poverty cheapened a man’s ideas in the marketplace, masking the rich, complex bouquet of his intellect behind the scent of damp wool and street dust. Because his own incapacities had sentenced him to exclusion, his imagination had constructed another man: a blooming human life ready to incorporate all that was worthiest in an existence whose visible, palpable part was burning itself away to ash. 

His inward need for this expanded self reflected an outward necessity. The thoughts of his heart were too closely interwoven with the ancient, generational growth of his people to be allowed to rot in the grave. And so, the long-contemplated figure took shape in his mind, often distantly approaching or turning his back toward him, darkly painted against a golden sky. 

The reason for this golden sky lay in one of Mordecai’s habits. Keenly alive to the sonic and visual poetry that occasionally pierced the city’s industrial gloom, his favorite resort was the parapet of Blackfriar’s Bridge at sunrise or sunset. Here, the acoustic ecology shifted. The abrasive shriek of factory whistles and the grinding of carriage wheels were softened by the immense, rhythmic breathing of the river. The grand dim masses of world-commerce, the oncoming barges emerging from hazy distance into sound and color, blent into his mood like a fine, slow-simmering broth. The low, resonant hum of the water provided a medium that bore up his spiritual wings. In this auditory sanctuary, the visionary form of his successor advanced: youth, beauty, refinement, Jewish birth, and noble gravity, gathered from his memory of faces among the Jews of Holland and Bohemia. 

Of late, the gradual choking of his breath had turned Mordecai’s trust into an agitated watch. The deliverer’s footstep must be near—the one to rescue Mordecai’s spiritual travail from oblivion and preserve the cultural memory of his people before the language of their ancestors died entirely on his lips. 

Yet Mordecai’s confidence in the friend to come did not make him passive. For two years, he had lived under Ezra Cohen’s roof, regarded as a compound of workman, dominie, vessel of charity, and inspired idiot. Ezra Cohen was a man to whom a meal was merely utilitarian fuel for the next transaction, his shop a cacophony of pawned goods and haggling that grated against Mordecai’s sensitive ears. During this time, little Jacob Cohen had advanced into knickerbockers and into a quickness of apprehension for hardware and exchange. Jacob had also formed an attachment to Mordecai, treating the wasting scholar’s helpful cleverness as he might the services of an enslaved Djinn. 

Though Mordecai knew the spiritual distance between the parents and himself, the boy moved him. He saw in Jacob the possibilities of a long, interwoven future. This feeling drew him to pour into the boy’s ear what would have seemed wild nonsense to any excellent man of business. 

In the quiet hours when the pawnshop’s clatter subsided, Jacob would stand at his teacher’s knees in the dusty upper room, lured by the mending of a mechanical toy. With the boy thus tethered, Mordecai would begin to recite a Hebrew poem of his own making, a rich concoction of blended past and future. He instructed Jacob to repeat the syllables. 

*The boy will get them engraved within him,* thought Mordecai. *It is a way of printing.* More than that, it was a way of seasoning the child’s palate with the native dialect, preserving a sonic heritage from the erosion of language death. Jacob, ever ready for the game of imitating unintelligible sounds, would mouth the deep, guttural fricatives and rolling consonants. Mordecai threw into each repetition the fervor of a master chef passing down a sacred, time-intensive recipe. Often, Jacob would survey his pockets or distort his cheeks, but the fervid reciter would not pause, satisfied if the young organs of speech submitted to the ancient, untranslatable acoustics. 

He waited with the patience of a prophet, saying inwardly, *My words may rule him some day. Their flavor may suddenly bloom on his tongue. It is so with a nation—after many days.* 

But the superficial commercialization of the streets frequently poisoned this domestic sanctuary. One week, Jacob had seen a street mountebank, and his muscular imitativeness diverged sadly from New Hebrew poetry. Mordecai had arrived at a fresh passage in his poem. His consumptive voice, a strong high baritone mingled with a hoarse, incipient gasp—like the scent of roasting spices cut with woodsmoke—gave forth verses with a meaning akin to this: 

*Away from me the garment of forgetfulness,*
*Withering the heart;*
*The oil and wine from presses of the Goyim,*
*Poisoned with scorn.*
*Solitude is on the sides of Mount Nebo,*
*In its heart a tomb:*
*There the buried ark and golden cherubim*
*Make hidden light:*
*There the solemn gaze unchanged,*
*The wings are spread unbroken:*
*Shut beneath in silent awful speech*
*The Law lies graven.*
*Solitude and darkness are my covering,*
*And my heart a tomb;*
*Smite and shatter it, O Gabriel!*
*Shatter it as the clay of the founder*
*Around the golden image.* 

In his absorbing enthusiasm, intoning the sacred syllables that carried the very terroir of their ancestors, Mordecai was unconscious that Jacob had slipped away. Pausing, he saw by a sudden flash that the lad had thrown himself onto his hands, feet in the air, mountebank fashion, picking up a bright copper farthing with his lips. 

The metallic *clink* of the coin against the boy’s teeth jarred horribly through the room’s acoustic space. It was the harsh, piercing noise of industrial greed shattering the delicate, centuries-old vessel of their native tongue—a Satanic grin upon his prayer. 

“Child! child!” Mordecai cried out with a strange, piercing agony that startled Jacob to his feet, before sinking backward with a shudder. 

“What?” Jacob asked quickly. He pressed Mordecai’s knees, shaking him. 

Mordecai opened his eyes with a fierce expression, grasped the little shoulders, and hissed in a quick, hoarse whisper: “A curse is on your generation, child. They will open the mountain and drag forth the golden wings and coin them into money, and the solemn faces they will break up into earrings for wanton women! And they shall get themselves a new name, but the angel of ignominy shall know them, and their heart shall be the tomb of dead desires that turn their life to rottenness.” 

The terrible, obscure threat in those hoarse accents, the thin grappling fingers, and the sudden disruption of their domestic routine shook Jacob’s little frame into awe. As Mordecai spoke, the boy stood trembling, struck by a profound, primal terror that the house was tumbling in, the hearth fires were extinguished, and they were never going to have dinner again. 

When the pinch relaxed, Jacob lifted his small patriarchal countenance and wept aloud, mourning the sudden starvation of his comfort. The sign of childish grief recalled Mordecai to his gentle self. With a maternal action, he drew the curly head to his breast, holding him until Jacob, feeling the danger passed, merely howled at ease to improve upon his performance. 

But Mordecai suffered a lasting check from this futile eagerness. He felt discredited, the untranslatability of his subjective experience weighing heavier than ever. All the more fiercely, his mind strained toward the discernment of that friend to come, with whom he would share a calm certainty of fellowship, a mutual tongue, and a shared feast of understanding. 

It was just then, during his midday guardianship of the old book-shop, that he was struck by the appearance of Deronda. Mordecai’s glance took on a sudden eager interest: here was a face and frame that realized the long-conceived type. Deronda’s voice carried the acoustic richness he had hungered for. But the young man’s disclaimer of Jewish birth was a backward thrust of double severity. 

Nevertheless, when he later found Deronda seated at the Cohens’ table—amidst the smell of boiled domesticity and the clatter of the family meal—the disclaimer was momentarily nullified. The mythic had stepped into the everyday routine. In asking Deronda if he knew Hebrew, Mordecai was so possessed by the belief of finding his successor that he forgot all counter-evidence. He offered the ancient language like a rare, fermented wine poured for a returning king. 

But the answering “No” struck them all down again. The frustration was more painful than before. The subtle power dynamics of the dominant lexicon had claimed this beautiful vessel; the native dialect was foreign to him. 

After turning his back on the visitor that Sabbath evening, Mordecai went through days of deep discouragement, like starving men on a doomed ship who behold a provisioning sail that never advances. Yet the long-contemplated figure had been wrought from the rich, complex imagery of his most passionate life. It inevitably reappeared. Deronda’s face met Mordecai’s inward gaze, raying out the influence of breathing flesh. Discouragement turned into a new obstinacy of resistance. 

It was Deronda now who was seen in the painful night-watches, painted on that golden sky that smelled of distant rain and river-silt—the doubly blessed symbol of advancing day. In the January weeks, Mordecai felt the increasing agitation of an anticipated change. He could no longer go on with his attempt to print the Hebrew tongue onto little Jacob’s mind; the ingredients were wrong, the soil too shallow. Something else was coming. The one thing he longed for was to get as far as the river, to escape the crushing noise of the streets, and to stand beneath the wide sky where the fluctuating lights on the water seemed to breathe with a life that could shiver, mourn, and ultimately, be comforted.
