Now that the question of bread had been answered—answered not with idle wishing, but with hands blackened by work and a hearth that finally smelled like a true home—my mind kept circling back to the wreck, as a tongue circles a sore tooth. There were stores still sealed inside her ribs: flour that would spare us more cassava-grinding, rice that would soften in a pot instead of fighting the teeth, casks that might hold salt or sugar. And above all, the pinnace: that neat, beautiful little vessel like a promise folded up and stowed away.

To win her, I needed all the strength we could muster. So I pressed my wife—gently at first, then with the stubbornness of a man who hears the sea calling through every quiet hour—to let me go to the wreck with all the boys save Franz.

She resisted as any sensible woman would; there are bargains you strike with necessity and then regret the moment you speak them aloud. At last she consented, but only after I pledged—reluctantly, because I knew how much time the work would swallow—that I would never pass a night aboard the wreck. We separated with the uneasy tenderness of those who agree, yet do not feel entirely satisfied.

The boys, however, were light-hearted as sparrows. They slung provision-bags over their shoulders—cassava-bread wrapped carefully against damp, potatoes tucked in like round stones of comfort—and set out as if the walk itself were a holiday.

At Safety Bay we first visited our marsh tenants. The geese and ducks greeted us with their low, gossiping gabble, the soft percussion of bills in water and the wing-rustle that made the reed-beds sound alive. We fed them and saw they prospered. It pleased me—this small husbandry in a wild place—to hear their contented murmur, a domestic music that answered the harsh, indifferent thunder of the surf beyond.

Then each buckled on his cork-belt. We stepped into our ridiculous tub-boat, towed the raft behind, and pushed out toward the wreck, the water slapping in hollow notes against the staves of our craft.

Once aboard, I set the boys to gathering whatever came first to hand and loading it on the raft so our return would not be empty. Meanwhile I went straight to the pinnace and began a careful inspection.

What I found sobered my triumph. The little vessel lay at the far end of the hold, packed into a cramped, narrow compartment like a delicate tool wedged in stone. There was no room to assemble her there. Yet the parts were so heavy that hauling them out piece by piece into a wider space seemed equally hopeless. The wreck had swallowed her and intended to keep her.

I sent the boys off to rummage—partly to keep their spirits from souring, partly because I needed silence—and sat down amid the dim, dusty air to think.

As my eyes adjusted to the gloom, I noticed something that made my heart lift: every part of the pinnace had been carefully arranged and numbered. Whoever had stowed her here had expected her to be fitted together again. Time, space, and patience—those were what she demanded, like any good bread that refuses to be hurried.

“Room,” I called when my sons came back, bright-eyed and trusting as if the world never withheld anything from determined hands. “Room to work in, boys! That’s what we must win first.”

I bade them fetch axes. We attacked the wooden partition, chopping and hewing to clear space around the imprisoned craft. The sound of our blows reverberated strangely through the ship’s belly—a dull, suffocating thud that made the wreck seem to groan. But evening fell before we had made much impression. The timberwork had been built to endure storms, not boys with axes.

We had to admit the truth: an immense labour and steady perseverance would be required before we could claim the pinnace. She lay in that vast hulk like a fossil shell embedded in rock—beautiful, entire, and stubbornly out of reach.

We returned to shore with tired limbs and little relish for the long walk back to Falconhurst—only to find, to our astonishment and joy, my wife and little Franz waiting for us at Tentholm. She had come with quiet resolution and that practical courage which so often outlasts our noisier kind.

“In this way,” she said, smiling as if it were the simplest thing in the world to uproot herself for our sake, “you will live nearer your work, and I shall not quite lose sight of you.”

I could have embraced her on the spot. “You are a good, sensible, kind wife,” I cried, delighted by her plan. “And we shall work the more diligently, that you may return the sooner to your dear Falconhurst.”

“Come and see what we have brought you, mother!” Fritz exclaimed. He and his brothers rolled forward two small casks of butter and three of flour, with corn, rice, and other treasure besides—things that spoke to my wife more eloquently than any boast. Butter meant pastry, frying, richness; flour meant real loaves, not merely survival-cakes; rice meant bowls of steam and softness on days when teeth were weary of root and fibre.

Her eyes shone, not with greed, but with that deep satisfaction known only to those who keep a household alive: the food-historian’s pleasure in stores, the farmer’s gratitude in provisions, the cook’s visions kindled by ingredients.

So began a stretch of days that welded toil to hope. We rose early, took the tub-boat and raft to the wreck, and worked until dusk. First we cut away and cleared an open space around the pinnace; then we began assembling her, matching numbered pieces like a vast, patient puzzle. Every evening we brought back a freight: tools, cloth, casks, anything of use. Tentholm became a place of industry and supper-smells—cassava and potatoes at first, then richer meals as flour and butter transformed our table. Even in our exile, the ritual of eating together gathered the day into meaning.

At last, after labour that felt beyond belief, the pinnace stood complete—ready to be launched—and yet still imprisoned. Massive wooden walls hemmed her in, mocking our strength.

She looked awake, that graceful little vessel, as though she had dreamt for years in darkness and now longed to leap into the free blue sea and spread her wings to the wind. To have come so far only to fail would have been a cruelty.

I turned the problem over and over, as one turns over a stubborn dough, seeking the moment it yields. Despair began to sour my thoughts—until an idea came like a sudden change of wind.

I said nothing to the boys. Instead I fetched a large cast-iron mortar, filled it with gunpowder, secured a block of oak atop it, and pierced a hole for the match. I placed this great petard so that its force would blow out the side of the wreck next to the pinnace. Then I chained it fast so the recoil would do no harm.

When all was ready, I told the boys I meant to go ashore earlier than usual and calmly ordered them into the boat. My heart hammered as if it wished to betray me. I lit the prepared match—long-burning, slow as a fuse in a nightmare—and hurried after them.

We brought the raft close to shore and began unloading, talking as if nothing were unusual. I left the other boat afloat and ready, and while my family worked, I listened with every nerve. The sea’s ordinary sounds—lap, hiss, gull-cry—seemed sharpened, waiting.

Then it came.

A flash. A mighty roar. Smoke burst up like a storm-cloud torn from the ocean.

My wife and children froze, terror-stricken, eyes flying seaward. The blast rolled along the cliffs and returned in echoes, the landscape suddenly full of thunder. In that moment I could not help thinking how sound itself rules the heart: how a single violent noise can turn peace into panic, and how much worse it must be in lands where such noise is constant—where machines and clamor grind the ear until fear becomes the everyday air.

My wife looked to me, searching my face for explanation. “Perhaps,” she said, because I held my tongue, “perhaps you have left a light burning near some of the gunpowder, and an explosion has taken place.”

“Not at all unlikely,” I replied, forcing calm into my voice. “We had a fire below when we were caulking the seams of the pinnace. I shall go off at once and see what has happened. Will anyone come?”

The boys needed no second invitation; they sprang into the boat. I lingered only long enough to whisper a few words to my wife—enough to steady her—and then joined them. We pulled for the wreck faster than ever before, oars biting water with sharp, urgent splashes.

On the side where we usually boarded, nothing looked changed. We rounded to the other side—and there a marvellous, ruinous sight awaited us. A huge rent gaped in the ship. Decks and bulwarks were torn open. The sea was strewn with floating fragments. The wreck looked as though it had taken one last, fatal breath.

And the compartment where the pinnace had slept lay open to the sky.

There sat our little beauty, to all appearance uninjured.

The boys stared at the scene of destruction, too overwhelmed to speak. But I—who saw through the smoke to the one thing that mattered—shouted in a joy that sprang up like a hymn: “Hurrah! She is ours! The lovely pinnace is won! We shall be able to launch her easily after all. Come, boys, let us see if she has suffered from the explosion which has set her free.”

They looked at me, then comprehension dawned. “You planned it!” they cried. “You clever, cunning father! That machine we helped to make—it was to blow it up!”

They followed me into the shattered opening. I examined her carefully, hands running over planks and seams, listening to the quiet creak of wood that had been woken and not broken. To my intense satisfaction, the pinnace had suffered nothing.

The boys, fascinated, pestered me for an explanation. I told them the principle and management of a petard, warning them that such power is a servant that must be handled with fear and knowledge, never with vanity.

Now the launch itself was plain. I had already placed rollers beneath her keel. With levers and pulleys and the united strength of all hands, we worked her forward toward the water. A rope was fixed to regulate her descent. Shoulders braced, breath held, we set her in motion. She slid—slowly at first, then more willingly—until at last she slipped gently into the sea.

She floated as if she had always belonged there, as if she recognized the taste and cradle of her native element. We cheered until our throats burned.

We secured our prize at the most sheltered point and returned to Tentholm. There we spoke of the explosion in general terms, saying only that one side of the ship had been blown away and that we should now be able to reach her remaining contents with far fewer days of labour.

Those days we spent in completing the rigging, mounting the two little brass guns, and making all necessary arrangements. It was astonishing how quickly the possession of two real guns awakened martial ardour. The boys talked incessantly of savages, fleets of canoes, attacks and defenses, and final annihilations—turning danger into a kind of play-song.

I reminded them that, brilliant as their imagined victories might be, we should have cause to thank God if their fighting powers were never put to the proof.

At last the pinnace was fully equipped and ready. Yet my wife still suspected nothing of the surprise. The boys, who had kept the secret with admirable discipline, begged leave to fire a salute as we entered the bay. I consented, though I feared the harsh crack would assault her ears and heart.

We cast off from the wreck, spread the sail, and began our voyage. The pinnace glided swiftly, neat and obedient. I stood at the helm. Ernest and Jack took the guns. Fritz, puffed up with command, gave the word: “Fire!”

Bang! Bang!

The reports rang out, sharp and thrilling, and the cliffs flung them back in echo after echo until the whole bay seemed full of cannon. The boys shouted and hurrahed, drunk on their own noise.

On shore my wife and little Franz rushed forward from near the tent. Even at a distance I could see alarm in their posture: the sudden violent sound had turned the day strange. But when she recognized our voices, her fear melted into relief, and she waved and hurried down to the landing-place.

With careful handling we brought the pinnace close to a projection of the bank. Fritz helped his mother aboard. Breathless with haste and excitement, she burst out, “You dear, horrid, wonderful people—shall I scold you or praise you? You have frightened me out of my wits! To see a beautiful little ship come sailing in was startling enough, for I could not conceive who might be on board; but the report of your guns made me tremble with fear—and had I not recognized your voices directly after, I should have run away with Franz, Heaven knows where!”

Then, as her eyes took in the craft more calmly, wonder overcame reproach. “Have you really done all this work yourselves? What a charming little yacht! I should not be afraid to sail in this myself.”

We basked a little in her admiration—because praise is a kind of nourishment too, especially after long labour—then she said, “Now you must come with me and see how little Franz and I have improved our time every day of your absence.”

We landed and followed her up the river toward the cascade. There, to our astonishment, she had laid out a garden: beds and walks neatly planned, the soil turned and tamed. The place smelled of fresh earth and crushed green, the scent that rises when ground is opened to seed. Even the sound was different there: water murmuring over stones, leaves whispering above, a gentle acoustic shelter from the sea’s harsher voice.

“We don’t frighten people by firing salutes in honour of our performances,” she said, giving us a look that mixed humor with reproof, “though by and by I too shall want fire—in a peaceable form. Look at my beds of lettuce and cabbages, my rows of beans and peas! Think what delicious dinners I shall be able to cook for you, and give me credit for my diligence.”

My mind leapt at the images: lettuce crisp with cool river-water, cabbages sweetened by sun, peas that would pop green and tender, beans that would simmer with butter. In that moment the island felt less like exile and more like a new chapter of older human stories—seed and stew, work and worship.

“My dear wife,” I exclaimed, “this is beautiful! You have done wonders! Did you not find the work too hard?”

“The ground is light and easy to dig hereabouts,” she replied. “I have planted potatoes, and cassava-roots. There is space for sugar-canes and the young fruit trees. And I shall want you to contrive to irrigate them by leading water from the cascades in hollow bamboos. Up by the sheltering rocks I mean to have pineapples and melons; they will look splendid when they spread there. To shelter the beds of European vegetables from the heat of the sun, I have planted seeds of maize round them. The shadow of the tall plants will afford protection from the burning rays. Do you think that is a good plan?”

“It is capital,” I told her—because it was. She spoke like an elder of an old agrarian line, shaping the land with knowledge and patience, making a future out of soil and water. “Now, as sunset approaches, we must return to the tent for supper and rest, for both of which we are all quite ready.”

That evening passed in happy talk. Everyone had that deep, clean satisfaction which attends successful labour, and the added joy of giving unexpected pleasure to others. I pointed out to my sons how real happiness is found there—in the shared bread, the shared work, the shared surprise—rather than in mere self-gratification.

Next morning my wife said, “If you can exist on shore long enough to visit Falconhurst, dear husband, I should like you to attend to the little fruit trees. I fear they have been too much neglected. I have watered them occasionally and spread earth over the roots as they lay, but I could not manage to plant them.”

“You have done far more than I could have expected,” I replied, “and provided you do not ask me to give up the sea altogether, I agree most willingly. I will go to Falconhurst as soon as the raft is unloaded and everything safely arranged here.”

Life on shore became a pleasant change. The boys worked with energy; the stores were quickly carried up to the tent, piled in order, and covered with sailcloth, pegged down all round. The pinnace, newly anchored, lay properly moored. Her elegant shape transformed our harbour, which until then had been graced only by our grotesque tub-boat and flat, uninteresting raft.

We took an ample supply for Falconhurst and soon were reestablished in that charming home. Its shade felt cooler than ever after the heat and hard work at Tentholm; even the quiet there seemed richer, like a familiar dialect heard again after days of harsher tongues.

Several Sundays had passed at Tentholm, and now the Day of Rest returned. We observed it with heartfelt devotion and grateful praise. I did not attempt long sermons, knowing well the limits of young attention, but we read from the Bible, drew simple instruction, and sang favourite hymns my wife knew from memory—her voice carrying the tunes as if she were preserving them in a place where no church bell would ever ring. In such songs there is a kind of cultural memory: without them, the language of home thins and fades, and a family becomes merely bodies surviving together.

In the evening I had the boys show their skill at running, leaping, wrestling, and climbing, telling them they must keep their bodies strong and active—powerful to repel danger, agile enough to escape it.

“No man can be truly courageous and self-reliant,” I said, “without an inward consciousness of physical power and capability. I want to see my sons strong, both morally and physically. That means, little Franz”—for his great blue eyes were fixed on me—“brave to do what is good and right, and strong to work, hunt, and provide for themselves and others, and to fight if necessary.”

The following day, seeing them eager for exercise, I encouraged them with the promise of a curious new weapon—provided they did not neglect archery, for their guns I knew they would never forget.

I took a long cord and fastened a leaden bullet to each end. Instantly came the storm of questions.

“This is a miniature lasso,” I explained. “Mexicans, Patagonians, and various tribes of South America use such a weapon in hunting with marvellous dexterity. They have ropes far longer than this and stones instead of bullets. One end is swung round and round the hunter’s head and then cast with skill toward the animal. Drawing it back, he can strike again, killing or wounding his prey.

“Sometimes, however, they wish to take the animal alive—wild horse, buffalo, whatever it may be. Then the lasso is thrown in such a way that it twists many times round the neck, body, or legs of the fugitive, arresting him even at full speed.”

“Oh, father!” cried the boys. “Try it now! There is the donkey! Catch the donkey!”

Not trusting my own skill upon a living creature, I refused and chose a stump at no great distance. My success surprised even myself: the cord whipped out and wound round the stump so neatly that there could be no doubt of the weapon’s use. At once the boys clamoured for lassos of their own. Because the making was simple, I granted their petitions, and lasso-practice became the order of the day. Fritz, being strongest and most adroit, soon excelled.

That night the weather changed. Early next morning I saw a gale rising; from the height of our trees the sea looked violently agitated. I thought with satisfaction of our hard-won pinnace lying safe at anchor, and of the fact that nothing called us to the wreck for some days.

My attention was not held only by the boys’ amusements. My wife had much to show me, asking approval here, advice there, help where it was needed. She had snared a good supply of wild pigeons and ortolans and had partly cooked and preserved them in lard. She showed me the small cask well filled—meat protected against spoilage, ready to be reheated and served when labour left no time for hunting. She showed me too the nests of our tame pigeons, but her chief anxiety remained the fruit trees, dry and withered from neglect.

We set about planting them at once, proposing afterward an excursion to the Calabash Wood to make a great supply of vessels and utensils. Everyone was eager for the expedition. So the orchard planting was carried on with surprising vigour, though it was not finished until toward evening. Then we prepared for an early start next day. My wife and Franz would come too; their equipment took time, for we meant to make a grand family excursion—complete with our domestic pets and servants.

By sunrise we were all astir. The sledge, loaded with ammunition and baskets of provisions and drawn by the donkey, would carry home our gourd manufactures and any other prizes we might find.

Turk headed the procession, as usual, clanking in his coat of mail. Then came the boys with guns and game-bags. My wife and I followed. Behind trotted Juno, out of sorts, because Master Knips refused to be left behind and insisted on riding her back like a little tyrant.

I carried two guns: one loaded with shot for game, one with ball for defence against beasts.

We crossed Flamingo Marsh and came into the magnificent country beyond, spread out in beauty and fertility. It was new to my wife and to two of the boys. Their delight was plain; they breathed in the scents of crushed grass and warm resin, and even the air seemed to ring with a different chorus—birds calling, insects humming, wind moving through leaves in a sound like distant rain.

Here Fritz and Jack darted into the bush. Loud barking followed, then the quick report of a gun. A large bird sprang from the thicket and fell heavily to the ground before us.

But it was not resigned to death. Unable to fly, it leapt up and rushed away with extraordinary speed. Turk barked furiously and gave chase; Fritz ran after it, panting. Juno, eager, sprang aside so suddenly that poor Knips was flung into the sand as she darted to intercept the bird’s retreat. She succeeded in cutting it off, but the bird defended itself fiercely, striking with powerful legs and sharp claws so that neither Fritz nor the dogs could master it.

I hurried to assist and found Juno holding bravely to a wing while the bird—a magnificent bustard—struggled and fought. Watching my moment, I threw a large handkerchief over its head and, with difficulty, bound its legs and wings. We carried it back in triumph to the others, who had been resting on the sand.

“What have you got?” they cried, jumping up.

“A bustard!” Jack shouted in delight. “Splendid! It is the very one we missed that day, don’t you remember, mother? Ah-ha! Old fellow, you are done for this time!”

Ernest examined it gravely. “I think this is a hen bustard—the mother bird.”

At that my wife’s face softened with concern. “Ah, poor thing. It is most likely the same, and I know she had a brood of young birds. Now they will be left unprotected and miserable. Had we not better let her go?”

“My dear, kind-hearted wife,” I said, “that was weeks ago. Those young birds will be strong by now, and I daresay Mrs. Bustard has forgotten the nursery cares. Besides, she is badly wounded; we must try to cure her. If we succeed, she will be a valuable addition to our poultry-yard. If not—then you shall roast her for dinner.”

The words “roast her” drew a different light into the boys’ faces, for in their minds already she was browning over a fire, her fat hissing, the smell filling our tent. Even in adventure, the thought of supper is never far; and perhaps it should not be, for food is the oldest story we tell ourselves in any land.

We marched on and soon reached the Monkey Grove—the very place of Fritz’s tragicomic adventure that had made him guardian to the orphan ape. He entertained us with a lively account, acting out the scene so well that even my wife laughed in spite of herself.

Ernest, however, stood apart beneath a splendid cocoanut palm, gazing upward with fixed admiration at the immense stem and the graceful crown of leaves. The cluster of nuts below the fronds held his attention like a pantry shelf high out of reach. As I drew near I heard him sigh and murmur, half to himself, “It’s awfully high! I wish one would fall down!”

No sooner had he spoken than, as if the island understood his words and answered them in its own language, a huge nut plumped down at his feet.

He sprang aside, startled, looking up with timid awe. Then another nut dropped.

“Why, this is just like the fairy tale of the wishing-cap!” Ernest cried. “My wish is granted as soon as formed!”

“I suspect the fairy,” I said, “is less inclined to give gifts than to pelt us and drive us away. There may be a cross-grained old ape up there among those leaves.”

We examined the fallen nuts. They were not old; they were not even quite ripe. That made the matter stranger. We all surrounded the tree, craning our necks and staring into the shadowy crown.

“Hollo! I see him!” Fritz shouted suddenly. “Oh, a hideous creature! What can it be? Flat, round, as big as a plate—and with a pair of horrid claws! Here he comes! He is going to creep down the tree!”
