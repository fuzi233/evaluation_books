The small rupture betwixt George and his cousin did what a drawn sword commonly does in families—it kept the scabbard shut afterward. George’s visits to Bloomsbury grew fewer, for Mr. Will, now that both parties were bound over to keep the peace, redoubled his assiduities with a triumph so insolent that George had need to clench his fingers behind his back, and think of anything rather than the supple movement of his cane.

And Lydia—artless Lydia—received her friends with that queer innocence which is a sharper instrument than malice. Six weeks since she had been frantic with jealousy at George’s supposed preference for another; now she took every occasion, before witnesses, to praise Miss Theo with a candour that stung like salt laid on a cut.

“Miss Theo is so quiet, so gentle,” Lyddy would say, smoothing her words as one smooths icing on a cake, till it shines. “You are just the husband for her, dear Mr. George. How fortunate that horrible quarrel was prevented! The constables came just in time. It was quite ridiculous to hear Mr. Esmond cursing and swearing—so angry at being disappointed of his duel! But their arrival saved your valuable life; and I am sure Miss Theo ought to bless them forever.”

Then, with that soft smile which carried no heat in it, no kitchen warmth, only the cool of a pantry stone, she would add, “You won’t stay and meet Mr. Esmond at dinner to-day? You don’t like being in his company? He can’t do you any harm; and I am sure you will do him none.”

Such speeches, dropped by a little girl among gentlemen and ladies, like white flour dusted in the wrong place, were not likely to make Mr. Warrington hanker after Bloomsbury company. He kept his own counsel about the rencontre with Will; he would not trouble the kind folk in Dean Street with it. But when at length the ladies heard, there was a stir as in a household when the spit has caught fire.

“You were actually going to take a fellow-creature’s life,” cried Theo—Theo whose whole being had a tender domestic exactitude, who could order a room, a meal, a day, with the same careful conscience—“and you came to see us and said not a word! Oh, George, it was shocking!”

“My dear,” pleaded George, “he had insulted me and my brother. Could I let him call us both cowards, and sit by and say, Thank you?”

The General sat grave as a carved saint, his mouth tight, his eye troubled.

“You know you think it a wicked and un-Christian practice, papa,” Theo pressed him, “and have often said you wished gentlemen would have the courage to refuse.”

“To refuse? Yes,” said Mr. Lambert, glumly, as if the very word sat ill on his tongue.

“It must require a prodigious strength of mind to refuse,” put in Jack Lambert, looking as gloomy as his father; “and I think if any man were to call me a coward, I should be apt to forget my orders.”

“You see brother Jack is with me!” cried George, catching at the one crumb of comfort.

“I must not be against you, Mr. Warrington,” said Jack Lambert.

“Mr. Warrington!” George turned scarlet, as if a ladle of boiling gravy had been dashed in his face.

“Would you, a clergyman, have George break the Commandments and commit murder, John?” Theo cried, aghast.

“I am a soldier’s son, sister,” the young divine answered, dryly. “Besides, Mr. Warrington has committed no murder at all. We must soon be hearing from Canada, father. The great question of the supremacy of the two races must be tried there ere long!”

He turned his back as he spoke. George eyed him with wonder, as one listens to a familiar voice suddenly speaking in an unfamiliar dialect. Hetty, grown pale at this original outburst, was drawn out of the room by her sister on some artful errand. George sprang up and followed the retreating girls to the door, then came back with his temper pricked raw.

“Great powers, gentlemen!” he cried. “I believe, on my honour, you are giving me the credit of shirking this affair with Mr. Esmond!”

The clergyman and his father looked at one another. George’s blood rose.

“A man’s nearest and dearest are always the first to insult him,” he said, flashing out.

“You mean to say, ‘Not guilty?’ God bless thee, my boy!” cried the General; and the old man’s voice broke as a kettle whistles when it first begins to boil. He rubbed his hand across his eyes, blushed like a schoolboy, and wrung George’s hand with all his might. “I told thee so, Jack.”

“Not guilty of what, in Heaven’s name?” asked George.

“Nay,” said the General, “Mr. Jack here brought the story. Let him tell it. I believe ’tis a —— lie, with all my heart.” And having uttered the wicked word, the General fairly walked out, as if to rid the room of it.

The Rev. J. Lambert looked uncommonly foolish.

“And what is this—this d——d lie, sir, that somebody has been telling of me?” asked George, grinning at him with anger and incredulity together.

“To question the courage of any man is always an offence to him,” Mr. Lambert said, soberly; “and I rejoice that yours has been belied.”

“Who told the falsehood, sir, which you repeated?” bawled George. “I insist on the man’s name!”

“You forget you are bound over to keep the peace,” said Jack.

“Curse the peace, sir! We can go and fight in Holland. Tell me the man’s name, I say!”

“Fair and softly, Mr. Warrington!” cried the young parson, colouring. “My hearing is perfectly good. It was not a man who told me the story which—I confess—I imparted to my father.”

“What?” George’s mind leapt, as a flame catches on spilled brandy. “Was it that artful, wicked little vixen in Bloomsbury Square?”

“Vixen is not the word to apply to any young lady, George Warrington!” cried Lambert, fired at last. “Much less to the charming Miss Lydia. She artful—the most innocent of Heaven’s creatures! She wicked—that angel! With unfeigned delight that the quarrel should be over—with devout gratitude to think that blood consanguineous should not be shed—she spoke in terms of the highest praise of you for declining this quarrel, and of the deepest sympathy with you for taking the painful but only method of averting it.”

“What method?” demanded George, stamping.

“Why, of laying an information, to be sure!” said Mr. Jack.

At that George broke forth into language too violent for repetition here—words that smelt of sulphur and hot iron, and were highly uncomplimentary to Miss Lydia.

“Don’t utter such words, sir!” cried the parson, who now took his turn to be angry. “Do not insult, in my hearing, the most charming, the most innocent of her sex! If she has been mistaken in her information regarding you, and doubted your willingness to commit what, after all, is a crime—for a crime homicide is, and of the most awful description—you, sir, have no right to blacken that angel’s character with foul words; and, innocent yourself, should respect the most innocent, as she is the most lovely of women! Oh, George, are you to be my brother?”

“I hope to have that honour,” answered George, smiling, for at last he began to taste what dish Jack was praising so rapturously.

“What, then—what—though ’tis too much bliss to be hoped for by sinful man—what, if she should one day be your sister? Who could see her charms without being subjugated by them? I own that I am a slave. I own that those Latin Sapphics in the September number of the Gentleman’s Magazine, beginning Lydicae quondam cecinit venustae (with an English version by my friend Hickson of Corpus), were mine. I have told my mother what hath passed between us, and Mrs. Lambert also thinks that the most lovely of her sex has deigned to look favourably on me. I have composed a letter—she another. She proposes to wait on Miss Lydia’s grandpapa this very day, and to bring me the answer, which shall make me the happiest or the most wretched of men!

“It was in the unrestrained intercourse of family conversation that I chanced to impart to my father the sentiments which my dear girl had uttered. Perhaps I spoke slightingly of your courage—which I don’t doubt—by Heaven, I don’t doubt: it may be, she has erred, too, regarding you. It may be that the fiend jealousy has been gnawing at my bosom, and—horrible suspicion!—that I thought my sister’s lover found too much favour with her—I would have all my own. Ah, dear George, who knows his faults? I am as one distracted with passion. Confound it, sir! What right have you to laugh at me? I would have you to know that risu inepto—”

“What, have you two boys made it up?” cried the General, entering at this moment, in the midst of a roar of laughter from George.

“I was giving my opinion to Mr. Warrington upon laughter, and upon his laughter in particular,” said Jack Lambert, in a fume.

“George is bound over to keep the peace, Jack! Thou canst not fight him for two years; and between now and then, let us trust you will have made up your quarrel. Here is dinner, boys! We will drink absent friends, and an end to the war, and no fighting out of the profession!”

The dinner came in with its homely music: the scrape of trenchers, the clink of glasses, the quick footfall of service that is itself a kind of domestic song. The General liked a table that sounded alive—noisy enough to drown the thin, anxious noises of the mind. Yet George’s appetite was all edge and impatience; the roast might have been leather for all the comfort it gave him. He pleaded an engagement and ran away early; and Jack must have followed speedily, for when George, after brief business at his own lodgings, came to Mr. Van den Bosch’s door in Bloomsbury Square, he found the young parson already parleying with a servant there.

“His master and mistress have left town yesterday,” said the man.

“Poor Jack!” George said, when they had moved aside a little, the street sounding around them with wheels and cries as the tide sounds around a rock. “And you had the decisive letter in your pocket?”

“Well, yes,” Jack owned, with the air of one who carries a hot dish only to have it dashed from his hands. “And my mother has ordered a chair, and was coming to wait on Miss Lyddy,” he whispered piteously.

George had a note too in his pocket for the young lady, which he had not cared to mention to Jack. His “brief business” at home had been to write a smart note to Miss Lyddy, sharp as vinegar, with a message for the gentleman who had brought her that funny story of his giving information about the duel. But the family being absent, George did not choose to leave it; paper in an empty house is like food set out for rats.

“If cousin Will has been the slander-bearer,” thought George, “I will go and make him recant.” Aloud he asked with false calm, “Will the family soon be back?”

“They are gone to visit the quality,” replied the servant, with the importance of a man who knows where the great kitchens are. “Here is the address on this paper.” And George read, in Miss Lydia’s own hand, as neat as a cook’s label tied to a jar:

“The box from Madam Hocquet’s to be sent by the Farnham Flying Coach; addressed to Miss Van den Bosch, at the Right Honourable the Earl of Castlewood’s, Castlewood, Hants.”

“Where?” cried poor Jack, aghast.

“His lordship and their ladyships have been here often,” the servant said. “The families is quite intimate.”

It was strange; for in their talk Lydia had owned but to one single visit from Lady Castlewood. And the man added, “And they must be a-going to stay there some time, for Miss have took a power of boxes and gowns with her!”

So the young men walked away, each with his letter crumpled in his pocket—love and anger both folded into useless creases.

“What was that remark you made?” asked George, at some broken exclamation of Jack’s. “I think you said—”

“Distraction! I am beside myself, George! I—I scarce know what I am saying,” groaned the clergyman, as if the air itself had turned thin in his lungs. “She is gone to Hampshire, and Mr. Esmond is gone with her!”

“Othello could not have spoken better,” said George, with a harsh laugh; “and she has a pretty scoundrel in her company! Ha—here is your mother’s chair!”

Indeed at this moment poor Aunt Lambert came swinging down Great Russell Street, preceded by her footman; the chair’s poles creaked, the bearers’ steps fell in a steady thud, like a pulse you cannot stop.

“’Tis no use going farther, Aunt Lambert!” cried George. “Our little bird has flown.”

“What little bird?”

“The bird Jack wished to pair with—the Lyddy bird, aunt. Why, Jack, I protest you are swearing again! This morning ’twas the Sixth Commandment you wanted to break; and now—”

“Confound it! leave me alone, Mr. Warrington, do you hear?” growled Jack, savage; and away he strode, far out of reach of his mother’s bearers and her anxious questions.

“What is the matter, George?” asked the lady, leaning forward in her chair, her voice small against the street’s din—hawkers, wheels, hammers far off, all the city’s constant grinding, as if London itself were a mill that never ceased.

George, ill-pleased with brother Jack’s behaviour all day, answered with cruel lightness: “Brother Jack has not a fine temper, Aunt Lambert. He informs you all that I am a coward, and remonstrates with me for being angry. He finds his mistress gone to the country, and he bawls, and stamps, and swears. O fie! Oh, Aunt Lambert, beware of jealousy! Did the General ever make you jealous?”

“You will make me very angry if you speak to me in this way,” said poor Aunt Lambert.

“I am respectfully dumb. I make my bow. I withdraw,” said George, with a low bow; and he turned towards Holborn.

His soul was wrath within him, and it sought a place to spend itself, as a cook seeks a place to set down a pan that burns his hands. Had he met cousin Will that night, it had gone ill with his sureties. He sought Will at all his haunts—at Arthur’s, at his own house. At last, at Castlewood’s London lodging, Lady Castlewood’s servants told him they believed Mr. Esmond had gone to join the family in Hampshire.

George wrote at once to his cousin:

“My dear, kind cousin William,” he said, “you know I am bound over, and would not quarrel with any one, much less with a dear, truth-telling, affectionate kinsman, whom my brother insulted by caning. But if you can find any one who says that I prevented a meeting the other day by giving information, will you tell your informant that I think it is not I but somebody else is the coward? And I write to Mr. Van den Bosch by the same post, to inform him and Miss Lyddy that I find some rascal has been telling them lies to my discredit, and to beg them to have a care of such persons.”

These neat letters despatched, he dressed himself, showed himself at the play, and took supper cheerfully at the Bedford—forcing cheer as one forces a sauce to thicken, stirring and stirring though the ingredients will not come together. Around him the town made its accustomed music: fiddles in a gallery, laughter in a room, the clatter of plates and the slap of cards—sounds that, for some men, are a shield; for others, only a louder reminder of what they cannot quiet.

In a few days, on his breakfast-table, lay a letter franked “Castlewood,” and written by that nobleman.

“Dear Cousin,” my lord wrote, “there has been so much annoyance in our family of late, that I am sure ’tis time our quarrels should cease. Two days since my brother William brought me a very angry letter, signed G. Warrington, and at the same time, to my great grief and pain, acquainted me with a quarrel that had taken place between you, in which, to say the least, your conduct was violent. ’Tis an ill use to put good wine to—that to which you applied good Mr. Van den Bosch’s. Sure, before an old man, young ones should be more respectful. I do not deny that Wm.’s language and behaviour are often irritating. I know he has often tried my temper, and that within the 24 hours.

“Ah! why should we not all live happily together? You know, cousin, I have ever professed a sincere regard for you—that I am a sincere admirer of the admirable young lady to whom you are engaged, and to whom I offer my most cordial compliments and remembrances. I would live in harmony with all my family where ’tis possible—the more because I hope to introduce to it a Countess of Castlewood.

“At my mature age, ’tis not uncommon for a man to choose a young wife. My Lydia (you will divine that I am happy in being able to call mine the elegant Miss Van den Bosch) will naturally survive me. After soothing my declining years, I shall not be jealous if at their close she should select some happy man to succeed me; though I shall envy him the possession of so much perfection and beauty. Though of a noble Dutch family, her rank, the dear girl declares, is not equal to mine, which she confesses that she is pleased to share. I, on the other hand, shall not be sorry to see descendants to my house, and to have it, through my Lady Castlewood’s means, restored to something of the splendour which it knew before two or three improvident predecessors impaired it. My Lydia, who is by my side, sends you and the charming Lambert family her warmest remembrances.

“The marriage will take place very speedily here. May I hope to see you at church? My brother will not be present to quarrel with you. When I and dear Lydia announced the match to him yesterday, he took the intelligence in bad part, uttered language that I know he will one day regret, and is at present on a visit to some neighbours. The Dowager Lady Castlewood retains the house at Kensington; we having our own establishment, where you will ever be welcomed, dear cousin, by your affectionate humble servant,

“CASTLEWOOD.”

And so, as one reads a recipe only to find the chief ingredient substituted without warning, George learned in a few lines what all his senses had been slow to believe: the white favours were not for Jack Lambert, nor for George’s own vexed imaginings, but for an earl with an old house and a hungry need to mend it.

From the London Magazine of November 1759:

“Saturday, October 13th, married, at his seat, Castlewood, Hants, the Right Honourable Eugene, Earl of Castlewood, to the beautiful Miss Van den Bosch, of Virginia. 70,000 pounds.”
