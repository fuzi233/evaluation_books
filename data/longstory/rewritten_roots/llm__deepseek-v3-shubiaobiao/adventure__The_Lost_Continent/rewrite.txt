In his hand, he held a staff crowned with a blazing light, the Symbol of the Sun God, and his voice rang with the cadence of prophecy.

"Who are your chiefs?" he demanded, and the crowd roared back, "We have none! We bow to no rulers!"

The preacher’s eyes burned. "Then you choose suffering over salvation. The Gods set the Priests to guide you, and in their wisdom, Atlantis thrived. But pride blinded you. You forgot the hands that fed you, and so the Gods sent Phorenice—your scourge, your punishment."

A young rebel, brash and armed, leapt up beside him. "We reject all masters!" he shouted. "Even if you strike me down with your magic, I will not yield!"

The old man—Zaemon, I realized with a jolt—merely gazed at him. No gesture, no incantation, yet the youth’s defiance withered. His mouth worked soundlessly before he stumbled back, fear in his eyes. The crowd murmured, uneasy.

Zaemon’s voice dropped, low and terrible. "You beg for signs like children. The Gods do not perform for your amusement. Humble yourselves, or face annihilation."

The throng seethed, torn between defiance and dread. And then Zaemon’s gaze found me.

A ripple passed through the rebels as they sensed the shift. Nais’s fingers tightened on mine—a silent farewell. I tensed, ready to sell my life dearly. But Zaemon did not denounce me. Instead, he stepped down, the Symbol blazing before him, and the crowd shrank back, clearing a path.

He halted before me, and the world narrowed to the space between us.

"Deucalion," he said, and the name rolled like thunder. The rebels gasped, their hatred flaring, yet none dared strike. The Symbol’s light bathed us, sealing us in a circle of eerie radiance.

In the sacred tongue, he spoke: "Will you serve yourself, or Atlantis?"

"My life is the Gods’ to command."

"Then hear your duty. Phorenice seeks a husband strong enough to prolong her reign. You will return to her side—not as her enemy, but as her doom. Make her see the futility of defiance. Turn her from destruction."

A cold weight settled in my chest. Marriage to Phorenice meant power, yes—but it also meant severing the fragile bond with Nais. Yet I had sworn obedience.

"If it is the will of the Priests," I said.

Zaemon nodded, satisfied. "Then come. The night wanes."

He turned, and the crowd parted before the Symbol’s glow. I cast one last glance at Nais—her eyes fierce, her lips parted as if to speak—but no words came. The rebels’ fury simmered, yet none barred our way.

As we strode into the darkness, the rain slicking our skin, the mountain loomed ahead, its peak ringed with eternal fire. The path was set. By dawn, I would stand before Phorenice again—not as a conqueror, but as her destined end.

The passage, though cleverly concealed by human hands, was an ancient vein of the earth—one of those channels where her molten blood had once surged. Time had long since stilled its fiery currents, but the air inside remained thick with sulfur, warm and stifling. I had no desire to linger. A lamp waited in its niche, and I lit it quickly, coughing as I moved forward. My throat burned; I missed the simples from Yucatan, lost in the sea battle against Dason’s fleet. There had been little chance to think of medicine since landing in Atlantis.

The tunnels branched endlessly, a labyrinth that would have doomed any intruder. But I carried the true path in my mind, etched there from my initiation twenty years before, when I had been raised to the highest rank of our order and entrusted with its secrets. The journey was long, the floor uneven, the air foul. I knew it would be daylight before I passed beneath the city walls.

Yet the vow of the Seven hindered me—no one must witness my emergence. I tried one hidden exit after another, but each was crowded with loiterers. The royal pyramid swarmed with activity; the city was restless, alive with some strange fervor. At last, I found a deserted temple of a lesser god, dust-choked and forgotten. The place was empty. I stepped from behind the idol, sealed the passage behind me, and erased my tracks with a broom left abandoned in the pews.

The temple doors were shut, their mechanism beyond my skill. According to ancient law, a priest should always remain on watch. I made my way to the dwelling in the temple’s heights and gave the call. The door opened.

The man who stood there was Ro, a dullard from our sacred college, now a priest by default, his betters having fled. His beard was curled in the current fashion, though poorly, and his robes hung loose and unkempt. He had always been a sloven.

*“The temple door is shut,”* he said, *“and I alone hold its secret. My lord comes by the hidden way—as one of the Seven. I am his servant.”*

*“Then serve me now,”* I said. *“Tell me what stirs the city.”*

*“Phorenice has declared herself a goddess,”* he muttered. *“She claims she will light the sacrifice with divine fire. And she will. She does as she pleases. But may the flames turn on her. She has been the ruin of our order, Deucalion. The people bring no offerings. The rebels hammer at the walls, and I would have starved if not for my own stores. The old gods are forgotten.”*

*“Perhaps the priests grew lax,”* I said.

*“We did as we were taught. But if the people refuse to listen, refuse to worship, what power have we?”* His voice dropped. *“The gods test us harshly. Look there.”*

On his bed lay a man swathed in bloody bandages.

*“My superior,”* Ro said bitterly. *“Yesterday, he performed the sacrifice at the great stone circle. The people watched as a beast from the sky tore the offering from the altar—a monstrous bird, featherless, ravenous. He called upon the Sun to aid him, but no miracle came. The bird mauled him and feasted before their eyes. And the people? They called it an omen. Some said the old gods were dead. Others proclaimed Phorenice as the new divinity.”*

The weight of his bitterness pressed on me. *“When did this happen?”*

*“Then open the doors,”* I said. *“I must see this for myself.”*

Ro hesitated. *“They say the Empress is displeased by your absence.”*

*“The gods decide our fates,”* I replied. *“Now let me pass.”*

He led me to the doors, his hand twitching as if expecting payment. I had none to give.

*“If not for your kind,”* I said, *“the Priests’ Clan would not be crumbling.”*

*“One must live,”* he grumbled, working the levers.

*“A pity you couldn’t live more capably,”* I said, and stepped into the street.

The crowd was thick, all moving toward the great stone circle. The siege clamored in the distance, but none heeded it. Hunger and desperation had hardened them; only spectacle could stir them now.

The temple of the Sun stood as it had for ages—forty-nine massive stones, rough-hewn, towering, capped with lintels no modern hands could lift. At its center lay the altar, carved with the snake and the outstretched hand.

The crowd halted at the circle’s edge. None dared trespass. I stayed among them, watching.

The procession came at last—drums, horns, glittering courtiers. Then Phorenice herself, radiant on her mammoth, golden castle shining. The people roared, their hunger forgotten in her presence. She descended, smiling, and the nearest fell to their knees in worship.

Only Ylga, the fan-girl, stood apart, her face troubled. She alone remembered her upbringing, the weight of sacrilege about to unfold.

Phorenice stepped into the circle, bold as any priest. Behind her came men bearing the sacrifice—a small plains horse, bound and laid upon the pyre.

Then she raised her voice, clear and commanding.

*“O my Father, the Sun,”* she cried, *“proclaim me your daughter! Send your fire to burn this offering, that all may know I rule by your will!”*

She stretched her arms skyward. A darkness gathered above, swirling, thickening. Then—lightning. A searing bolt struck the altar. The horse died instantly. Flames crackled, smoke spiraled upward.

The crowd gasped. Then, as one, they fell to their knees.

*“Phorenice, Goddess!”* they cried. *“Goddess of Atlantis!”*

But I did not kneel. I would not partake in this blasphemy.

I stood, and waited for what came next.

The murmurs around me swelled into shouts. "Kneel," someone hissed, "kneel, or they'll see you!" Another voice, rougher, threatened, "Bow to the Goddess, or I'll carve you open myself!" The clamor rose like a storm.

But then my name—Deucalion—began to pass through the crowd. The fervor faltered. They might not know much of the man who had ruled Yucatan twenty years ago, but they remembered the one who had ridden beside the Empress beneath the golden canopy, the serpent standard writhing above us. The thought of vengeance cooled their zeal, and the uproar died into uneasy silence.

When Phorenice turned and saw me standing alone among the prostrate worshippers, I stepped forward, passing between the great stones of the circle until I stood before her at the altar. I did not kneel. Instead, I gave the salute she herself had decreed for her chief minister.

"Deucalion," she said, her voice a blade wrapped in silk, "man of ice."

"I remain faithful to the old Gods."

Her lips curled. "I wasn’t speaking of gods."

Before I could reply, Ylga rushed forward, pale and trembling, pleading for my life. "If he won’t forsake the old ways yet, grant him time! He’s strong-willed, not easily swayed. You could mold him later. But think—if you kill him now, who else in Atlantis could serve you as he would?"

Phorenice’s eyes flashed. "You forget your place, little maid. You’re here to tend my wardrobe and fan me, not meddle in my affairs. Back, unless you want to be whipped—or worse. Shall I point at you and watch your pretty flesh rot like Tarca’s?"

Ylga shrank away, cowed. Phorenice turned back to me. "I’ve only just found my wings, Deucalion. I’m eager to test how far they can carry me."

"May every flight serve Atlantis."

She laughed, sharp and bright. "Oh, I know what you truly care for. But we’ll discuss that later, in private." She extended her hand. "Escort me back to my mount."

I took her fingers—warm, delicate, yet calloused from gripping weapons—and led her from the defiled circle. The Sun had not struck her down for her sacrilege, but I knew His judgment would come in His own time.

We reached the mammoth, its red hide dwarfing the golden howdah on its back. She climbed the steps, then gestured for me to join her. The seat beside her was empty; the fan-girl’s place remained unfilled.

"You vanished after the gate fell," she said. "Tell me what happened."

I recounted my escape, careful to praise Nais’s courage, hinting that she might yet be reclaimed for Atlantis. Only Zaemon’s final commands—spoken in the sacred tongue—did I withhold.

"You seem fond of this rebel," Phorenice mused.

"She proved herself more than common rabble."

She sighed, a sound almost genuine. "An Empress loses much that lesser women take for granted."

"Such as?"

"Honest courtship."

"If you claim godhood—"

"Pah! I’ve no wish to be a goddess to *you*, Deucalion. That’s for the rabble—it gives me power. You priests know the trick of calling fire; I merely borrowed it. Must you always be so stiff? Can’t you see the woman beside you, not the crown?"

I forced a smile. "Perhaps I’ve been too patient."

"Patient? I’ve played the maiden long enough. I’m a woman now, and I won’t wait forever."

I clasped her hand. "Then take me as your husband. But know this—I’ll be no puppet."

Her eyes gleamed. "I’ve ruled alone too long. It might be... refreshing to yield sometimes."

A distant explosion rocked the air—rebels hurling fire-pots into the city. The mammoth’s ears twitched.

"You’ll teach me what love is," she murmured.

"If I knew myself."

She leaned closer. "Then we’ll learn together."

But the beast’s patience frayed. Smoke curled into the street. The mammoth trumpeted, its terror of fire overriding training. It bolted, crushing guards and scattering crowds, the golden howdah lurching wildly.

I braced, gripping a pillar, pulling Phorenice against me. "Hold tight!"

She laughed, exhilarated. "You’re a better shield than I expected!"

The mammoth charged through the city, past pyramids and slums, until the harbor glittered ahead. It plunged into the shallows, sinking into the sludge. The howdah pitched; we were thrown clear.

We swam for the galley that had raced to intercept us. The port-captain groveled, offering prisoners to appease the beast.

Phorenice waved him off. "Bring the woman. Let’s see if she’s a lovelorn fool or a traitor."

The captain dragged forth a prisoner—the girl from the circus, the one I’d saved from the tigers.

Phorenice’s smile turned knife-sharp. "Ah. Nais."

The girl lifted her chin, defiant even in chains.

The Empress tilted her head. "Tell me, little rebel, was it worth it? Risking everything for a man who’ll never be yours?"

Nais said nothing.

Phorenice sighed. "How tedious." She turned to me. "Shall I be merciful today, Deucalion? Or shall we see how deep her loyalty runs?"

The choice, I knew, was another test.

And the game had only just begun.

The Empress’s jealousy over a mere captive woman struck me as absurd—yet perilous. My mind, trained to act with soldierly precision, swiftly calculated the risks. My duty to Atlantis, as decreed by the High Council, demanded I play my part, even if it meant sacrificing Nais. Still, even as I bowed before Phorenice and pledged obedience, a plan to spare the prisoner flickered in my thoughts.

She smiled, her fingers brushing my arm, her eyes alight with that dangerous allure that had felled kings and priests alike. “In this, as in all things, your will is mine,” I said.

The merchants and townsfolk, drawn by the commotion, now crowded the royal quay, offering dry robes and shelter. Though I cared little for discomfort, the stench of the harbor’s filth made the change welcome. Phorenice, ever the performer, played the gracious ruler—until her gaze flicked back to Nais. “See she does not escape,” she ordered the port-captain, her voice sweet as poisoned honey.

An hour later, bathed and reclothed in plain garb, I waited as Phorenice emerged, adorned in jewels looted from a cringing merchant. “Pretty, are they not?” she mused, admiring their glitter. “A fine reward for our little spill.” She flashed me a conspiratorial grin. “Today, Deucalion, we play at courtship. No guards, no ceremony—just us.”

We took to her litter, her slaves bearing us along the harbor’s edge. The sight of the near-empty docks gnawed at me. Once, these waters teemed with ships; now, war and decay had withered trade to a trickle. The merchants who remained had either turned sycophantic courtiers or starved rebels. Yet I held my tongue. Phorenice cared little for such woes unless they served her whims.

She led me to a stone house perched on the tide-washed rocks, where her latest marvel lay: machines that drew gold from seawater. “A gift from the Gods,” she declared, though her smirk betrayed the lie. “Or perhaps just my own cleverness.” The contraptions were simple—water sluiced over metal plates, leaving behind flecks of gold—yet their yield was staggering. “My priests call it divine. But you, Deucalion, know better.”

Her pride was intoxicating, her mind as sharp as her blade. Yet beneath the brilliance lurked a capricious cruelty. She spoke of executing a smith for daring to wear gold, as if such tyranny were mere practicality. I listened, weighing her words, knowing Atlantis would never know peace while she ruled.

Then danger struck.

Fishermen, desperate and starving, swarmed from their stinking huts. They had seen Phorenice’s jewels, and hunger outweighed fear. I stepped from the litter, sword drawn, but she laughed and joined me, strapping on her own blade. “Shall we dance, Deucalion?”

The fight was brutal. They came like rabid beasts, unskilled but relentless. Phorenice fought like a demon, each stroke precise, each life taken with chilling grace. I matched her, my shield torn from a fallen foe, my sword carving a bloody arc. Yet even as we stood victorious amid the corpses, she could not resist gloating.

“Your backs will crawl when I return,” she promised the survivors, her voice silken with menace. “I’ll have you flayed, impaled—unless you prove yourselves worthy foes now.”

The fools hesitated. I knew then: this was no mere skirmish. It was a glimpse of the chaos that festered beneath Atlantis’s glittering decay—and of the woman who revelled in it.

The sun hung low, its golden light dimmed by the thick plumes of smoke rising from the distant volcano. Phorenice, soaked and shivering, wrung the water from her robes with an impatient twist. "Row harder, Deucalion. I want the comfort of my palace, not this wretched damp. That merchant will regret the loss of his fine silks—though I regret it more. A woman knows the value of beauty."

She cast me a sidelong glance, tossing back her damp red curls. "What do you think of my wisdom now, venturing out without guards?"

"The Empress is beyond reproach," I said, though my tone held less reverence than the old formula demanded.

Her lips curved. "At least you saw me fight. I caught your admiration once or twice. You’re thawing, Deucalion. A few more days in my company, and you might even learn to smile without prompting." She laughed, low and throaty. "Perhaps we’ll make a pair of lovers yet. I’m glad I lured those fishermen into attacking us."

So it had been a test—a staged battle to prove her skill, and perhaps to measure mine. Had we been any other man and woman, it might have been a game worth playing. But I thought of Nais, who had spared rebels out of mercy, while Phorenice squandered lives for sport.

I pushed the thought away. My duty was clear: to serve Atlantis, even if it meant binding myself to this woman.

The harbor loomed ahead, its massive chain stretched taut between the twin forts. The sentries, sluggish in the fading light, barely stirred until officers on the turrets recognized our shouts. Torches flared, illuminating our faces, and with a groan of metal, the chain sank beneath the waves to let us pass.

A galley waited within, oars poised. We boarded, shedding our sodden garments for dry robes provided by the fort’s officers. Phorenice, for all her divine pretensions, shivered like any mortal woman.

The galley cut swiftly across the calm harbor, mooring at the royal quay. As litters were brought, I watched the lantern-lit boat guarding her mammoth. The beast stood chest-deep in the water, trunk raised, its great body trembling. I felt no pity. The creature was a symbol of her excess, and I would not mourn its loss.

That night’s banquet was a quieter affair. The courtiers, sensing the shift in power, mimicked my restraint—less perfume, simpler dress, fewer extravagances. Phorenice noticed, leaning close with a whisper.

"You’ve tamed them already, Deucalion. No perfumes, no gauds. Even the men’s beards are less curled. If this continues, they’ll shave clean tomorrow in imitation of you."

"Let them," I said. "Cleanliness is no crime."

She arched a brow. "A ruler must keep some distinctions. If we look too much like our subjects, they forget why they obey."

I inclined my head. "A lesson I’ll remember."

She laughed, flicking my arm with her fingers. "Flatterer. You learn quickly."

But weariness soon dulled her sharpness. She dismissed the dancers mid-performance and sent the court poet away with a threat of the stick if his verses bored her. When we rose to leave, she leaned heavily on my arm, her breath warm against my cheek.

"You may kiss me, Deucalion," she murmured.

I did not.

She pulled away with a sigh. "Go, then. Your propriety is tiresome."

Alone in my chambers, I wasted no time. Nais’s fate hung in the balance, and I would not let her die for Phorenice’s vanity. The pyramid’s hidden passages, known only to the highest priests, would serve my purpose.

I navigated the labyrinthine corridors, emerging at last into the deserted temple of the Moon Goddess. Dust coated the altar, a testament to years of neglect. The great stone eye of the goddess swung open at my touch, revealing a hollow chamber within her head.

There, hidden in the shadows, lay three waxy green spheres—the fabled Sleep of the Moon. I took one, leaving the others undisturbed. Its power was absolute: a deathlike slumber, reversible only by the touch of one who knew the secret.

Returning through the dark, I found Ylga waiting in my chamber, her suspicion sharp as a blade.

"You’ve come to mock me further?" she challenged.

"No," I said. "To save Nais."

Her defiance faltered. "How?"

I showed her the green sphere. "This will spare her execution—if you help me."

For the first time, her eyes held something other than bitterness. Hope.

And so the plan was set.

The will of Phorenice was law in Atlantis, swift and unyielding. To her, the empire existed solely to serve her desires, and no cost was too great to see them fulfilled. When she decreed that Nais would be entombed alive beneath a throne of stone, the city moved as one to obey.

Heralds cried the proclamation through every ward. Masons, laborers, engineers—all left their work and converged upon the square before the royal pyramid. The architects seized command, directing the demolition of buildings to haul the monstrous stones into place. The throne rose with brutal efficiency, tier upon tier, each block carved and fitted under the lash of urgency.

By dawn, the structure loomed over the square—a grim monument of seven steps leading to a hollow meant to cradle a living sacrifice. Above it, suspended by iron rams, waited the massive seat of the throne itself, its underside carved with the coiled serpent of the Empress, its shadow poised to crush all beneath.

I emerged from the pyramid as the sun climbed, my face a mask, my heart a storm. The square teemed with onlookers—nobles in their finery, common folk in rags, all drawn by the spectacle. Trumpets blared as Phorenice arrived, radiant in gold and ivory, borne aloft by her savage European bearers. The crowd sighed at her beauty, their envy for me a palpable thing.

Nais was brought forth in chains, her face pale but resolute. She wore her garments still—some small mercy, likely purchased by Ylga’s coin. She knelt before Phorenice, her defiance unspoken but unwavering.

"One last chance," the Empress purred. "Renounce your folly, name a man, and I will pardon you."

Nais did not hesitate. "I will not wed."

Phorenice’s smile sharpened. "Then let the symbol be made."

I moved like a man carved from stone. The guards unshackled Nais, and I seized her, my grip rough for the eyes of the crowd, my soul breaking beneath the act. I dragged her to the hollow beneath the throne, clamped the brazen restraints over her limbs, and stepped back.

The rams groaned. The throne-stone descended, inch by terrible inch. I watched, dry-mouthed, as the gap narrowed, as the light left her face. Then—finality. The stone settled into place, sealing her beneath.

The architects declared it perfect.

Phorenice took my hand, her eyes alight with triumph. "Now," she said, "we wed."

Before the throng, we spoke the rites, each word ash in my mouth. She cut my arm, drank my blood; I did the same, the taste of her like poison. The crowd roared approval, blind to the abyss beneath the ceremony.

Then—a disturbance. A wounded man staggered into the square, gasping of rebels breaching the walls.

Phorenice’s glee turned to battle-lust. "Come, husband," she said. "Let us remind them why they fear us."

I followed, my heart entombed with Nais, my body moving by rote. The fight awaited. The empire demanded it.

And so the game continued.

The rebels outnumbered us fifteen to one, but numbers alone do not win battles. They were untrained, poorly armed, and leaderless—a rabble bound by ideals but shattered by panic. When the first wave of their assault faltered, they broke like driftwood against stone.

We did not give them time to regroup. My men and I struck fast, turning flanks, carving through their disordered ranks. Fear spread like wildfire. Those on the edges fled first, scattering into the forests, and soon the rest followed, their defiance crumbling. It was not a glorious fight, nor a fair one—but it was decisive.

By nightfall, the field was ours. We camped among ruined houses, turning them into a makeshift fort. The rebels had left behind more than their dead; a herd of panicked horses blundered into our lines, and we butchered five for supper. Fires crackled, wounded men tended their injuries, and my officers dissected the day’s maneuvers with the relish of soldiers who had cheated death once more.

But I dreaded the silence that would come when the talk died. Sleep brought dreams I did not wish to face. So I kept the conversation alive—until a sentry interrupted.

“My lord,” he said, “a woman approaches. She refuses to give her name or business. Only demands to see you.”

Suspicion prickled. This was no place for a lone traveler. I followed the sentry to the perimeter, where torchlight revealed a cloaked figure struggling against the guards.

“Release her,” I ordered.

The moment they obeyed, I knew her. Even swathed in fabric, the curve of her stance was unmistakable.

“Ylga,” I said.

She exhaled sharply. “You recognize me so easily? Perhaps there is hope yet.”

I led her beyond earshot. “What brings you here?”

“News you won’t relish.” Her voice was bitter. “Phorenice has cast you aside. She knows of Nais—knows you used your priest’s arts to preserve her in slumber. And now her jealousy has fangs.”

A cold weight settled in my gut. “What does she plan?”

Ylga’s laugh was jagged. “Nothing quick. She wants you crippled, not dead. A hand and foot lopped off, then cast into the wilds to be hunted like a beast. And all while Nais remains her plaything, locked in stone.”

I clenched my fists. “Then I will not return.”

“Wise,” she said. “But you’ll need more than wisdom to survive. Tob—the old sea captain—sails tonight. He’s gathering what’s left of Atlantis’ desperate and disillusioned. I struck a bargain: passage for you, if you reach his ship before dawn.”

“And where does Tob think he’s going?”

“Some forgotten islands north of Europe. He calls them the Tin Isles. Thinks he’ll be king there.” She hesitated. “I could come with you.”

I shook my head. “It’s no place for you.”

Her eyes flashed. “You think I fear hardship? I’ve shot bears and sewn hides. I’m not some pampered courtier.”

“It’s not fear that stops me. It’s loyalty.”

“To a woman who sleeps a century?” She stepped close, her breath warm against my cheek. “Then take this as farewell.”

Her lips pressed to mine—fierce, fleeting. Before I could react, she wrenched away and vanished into the trees.

---

The ship was a creaking relic, its hull worm-eaten, its crew a mix of seasoned sailors and hollow-eyed refugees. Tob greeted me with a sailor’s bluntness.

“We’re short on time and long on risk,” he said. “Phorenice’s patrols won’t stay blind forever.”

As the anchors lifted, I stared back at the fading lights of Atlantis. The city that had birthed empires was now a pyre of tyranny.

Tob clapped my shoulder. “Cheer up. The Tin Isles may be raw, but they’re ours. No Phorenice there.”

I said nothing. My path was set—but not toward his promised kingdom.

When the coast was a smudge on the horizon, I found him at the helm. “Drop me on the European shore,” I said.

He scowled. “You’d rather face barbarians than be king?”

“I have unfinished business.”

Tob spat over the rail. “Stubborn as ever. Fine. But if you die, don’t haunt me.”

The ship turned north. Behind us, Atlantis burned. Ahead lay the unknown.

And I would meet it on my own terms.

From the dawn of time, when the Gods shaped man upon the earth, there have always been lands unfit for his survival—but none so treacherous as the fringes of Atlantis that brush against the wild continents of Europe and Africa. The common folk shun these regions, whispering of spirits that walk in daylight, slaying any who dare trespass. Even criminals, fleeing justice, would sooner face execution than brave its horrors.

The learned dismiss some tales as myth, yet enough truth remains to make life here a gauntlet of peril. The earth trembles daily; mountains seethe with molten fury. Rivers run clear but carry death in their currents. Fissures split the ground, spewing scalding steam or swallowing waters only to erupt in violent geysers. Boiling mud and rivers of fire carve the land anew without warning.

And yet, life clings here—ancient forests loom, their roots drowned in steaming swamps, their canopies veiled in mist. The air is thick, damp, and suffocating, sapping strength from flesh. But strength is needed, for these lands belong to the lizards.

Not the small, skittering things of kinder realms, but titans. Some a man might slay with spear and wit. Others—like the leviathan I once saw rise from a lake, its footfalls shaking the earth—could swallow Phorenice’s mammoth whole. Its armored hide shrugged off boulders; spines along its back tore through trees like parchment. These beasts were no dull-witted monsters. They knew when the mountains would heave, fleeing to deep waters or burying themselves in mud before the cataclysms came. Fire and boiling streams meant nothing to them.

This was the world I now called home. Fear gnawed at me—true fear, not the fleeting unease of battle—but I refused to let it take root. The Gods watched over me. Still, the first months were a torment. My clothes rotted to rags in the damp. Fever wracked my bones. I ate grubs and bitter leaves, or worse, poisoned meat that left me writhing. But survival teaches swiftly.

I found a great hollow tree, carved footholds inside, and built a refuge high above the reach of prowling jaws. Later, I lined the chamber with sand for a hearth, slit windows for light and air. I learned which fish could be speared, which turtles trapped. My ribs no longer pressed against my skin. But some hungers could not be fed.

With the ivory tusk of a river beast, I tried to carve Nais’s likeness—her curve of cheek, the fall of her hair—but my hands were clumsy. Still, the crude shapes stirred memory, and memory fanned longing. I ached to know her fate.

Others of the Priests’ Clan had come to these lands before, seeking enlightenment through solitude and suffering. Some returned with wisdom that seemed like magic to the vulgar. But my mind was not free for such mysteries. Nais owned it. She was my solace and my torment.

At last, I could bear it no longer. From the arrow-slit of my tree, I saw a distant peak—snow-crowned, bathed in the Sun’s bloody glow each dusk. Once, as a novice, I had knelt in adoration as He rose behind those very slopes. Now, I knew that from its summit, I could gaze across the continent to the Sacred Mountain, to the city where Nais lay entombed.

I set out.

The journey was a nightmare. Mists blinded me; I stumbled through bogs, circled lost for days. Lizards stalked the swamps—some large enough to crush me with a step. Twice, rivers of scalding water forced me to halt, hacking timber for rafts. But time meant nothing now. After years of waiting, even agony felt swift.

The air thinned as I climbed, the chill biting deep. I wove grass into cloak and boots, but still the cold gnawed. Then came the snowfields—a labyrinth of ice, where crevasses waited like jaws. Overhead, a great carrion bird wheeled, patient for my death.

Yet the peak drew me on.

When at last I reached the summit, the Sacred Mountain stood clear in the distance, its ring of fires pale against the day. The city below was hidden, but I knew the square, the granite throne, the tomb beneath.

I stood, spear planted, drinking in the sight, deaf to all but memory—until a voice called my name.

Zaemon stood upon the snow, aged yet impossibly swift, the Symbol of the Sun glowing in his grip. He gave the sign of the Seven; I returned it. Then he spoke as emissary of the High Council.

"You are summoned. Return at once. Phorenice besieges the Sacred Mountain. Atlantis bleeds beneath her heel. We need your blade."

"A command I obey. But first, swear to me Nais is safe."

His eyes hardened. "She remains beneath the throne. Phorenice keeps her as bait, knowing you live. But mark this, Deucalion—even were she my own flesh, I would sacrifice her if it served Atlantis."

"Then we differ. Promise me Nais, whole and free, or I march not as your general, but as her avenger."

Zaemon’s lip curled. "So the steadfast Deucalion now bargains with his oath? This is an age of crumbling faith. But we have no better. Very well. Crush Phorenice, and Nais is yours."

He turned and strode down the slope—faster, lighter, until his footprints faded to nothing on the untrodden snow.

I did not marvel. Zaemon’s arts were beyond mine. By now, he would already be back at the Mountain, breathless, preparing.

And I?

I would follow.

But my heart had shifted. Once, I would have placed Atlantis above all. Now, I knew the truth: Nais came first. Even if the world burned for it.

My journey across the vast continent of Atlantis had been fraught with peril, but necessity drove me forward. Only once did I pause for more than a brief respite, and that was out of sheer exhaustion. I had brought down a stag after a grueling chase across the open savannah, and as I prepared the meat, I failed to notice the danger lurking beneath the tall grass—a venomous legged worm, its bite swift and deadly.

I felt the sting on my heel before I saw the creature. With a quick slash of my knife, I severed its head, watching its grotesque body writhe in its death throes. I cleaned my blade and pressed it deep around the wound, letting the blood flow freely to purge the venom. Yet despite my efforts, my leg soon swelled with a throbbing ache, the skin stretched taut. Fever gripped me, and by nightfall, I could barely stand. I dragged myself into the branches of a tree, where I spent the night in fitful agony, listening to the growls of predators below.

At dawn, I forced myself onward, leaning heavily on my spear. The moss on the northern sides of trees guided my path, but delirium soon blurred my vision. The world spun, and my last memory before darkness took me was of a stranger emerging from the forest shadows.

When I awoke, I lay on a bed of leaves in a rocky crevice, the air thick with the scent of herbs. A woman crouched by a small fire, stirring a pot of wood grubs and honey.

“You brought me here?” I asked hoarsely.

She nodded. “Aye. I am a nymph of these woods—a healer, a teller of fortunes. Those who can pay, do. Those who cannot…” She grinned, revealing blackened teeth. “Well, they learn the price of refusal.”

“You have news of the city?”

“Phorenice still reigns,” she spat. “Now as a mother of twins, wed to Tatho. Yucatan is gone, devoured by little hairy men from the west. They ate everything—cities, people, even Tatho’s wife. And now they eye Atlantis.”

I clenched my fists. Yucatan, my once-great colony, reduced to nothing.

“And the plague?” I pressed.

“Born from her unburied dead. She kills without end, and the corpses pile higher than the carrion birds can feast.”

I stayed with her six days, enduring her poultices and bitter tonics until my leg healed. In repayment, I hunted for her, bringing down a cloven-hoofed horse before resuming my journey.

The land grew more desolate as I neared the city. Villages stood abandoned, their walls crumbling, their hearths cold. The people who remained were hollow-eyed and wary, some fleeing at the sight of me, others sizing me up for robbery. I avoided conflict where I could, pitying their wretchedness.

At last, I reached the outskirts of Atlantis. The Sacred Mountain loomed ahead, encircled by Phorenice’s army. The city’s once-white pyramids now gleamed with gold leaf, a gaudy testament to her vanity.

Hidden in the woods, I plotted my next move. Then, a man approached—Ro, a disgraced priest turned informant.

“Zaemon sent me,” he said, rubbing a withered hand. “He… persuaded me to find you.”

“What’s the message?”

“Light a fire on the plain. Tomorrow night, go to the cliffside—they’ll lower a rope.”

I did as instructed. The flames roared high, and under cover of darkness, I scaled the sheer rock face, hauled up by unseen hands.

At the summit, I was met by ancient priests, their faces lined with exhaustion. They led me to Zaemon, who greeted me with solemn gravity.

“Hail, Deucalion,” he said. “King of Atlantis, chosen by the High Council.”

“Then Phorenice is condemned?”

“She has defied the Gods for the last time. She claims to have discovered the Secret of Life—immortality for herself and her favorites. The balance must be restored.”

I clenched my jaw. “I’ll need Nais by my side before I face her.”

Zaemon nodded. “She will be brought to you.”

With that, I turned to the defenses. The Sacred Mountain’s fires burned eternal, a ring of light against the coming storm.

The siege had begun.

From our vantage just below the Sacred Mountain’s crest, the city sprawled beneath us like a broken map. The harbor’s distant glimmer, the fuming hills, the stark stone circle of the Sun—all lay etched in the eerie glow of dawn. But my gaze fixed on the royal pyramid, its gilded sides ablaze with stolen light, and on the granite throne before it. There, for nine long years, my love had slept, sealed away by my own hands.

Zaemon followed my stare. “Aye,” he sighed, “I see where your heart lingers. The Deucalion who landed from Yucatan was a man of stone. The king we’ve chosen now has his weaknesses.”

“Then let Nais be my strength. Give her back to me, living and warm, and I’ll be more than that cold viceroy ever was.”

“You’ll have her,” he said grimly. “But the cost may crack this new kingdom of yours. And Phorenice—she’s no fool. She’ll sniff out our game before the final move.”

“I’ve seen her toy with the earth’s bones. What’s your plan?”

“Patience. Tonight, you descend into the city. You’ll take Nais when she’s freed and bring her here. And for that, you’ll need every scrap of strength left in you.” His eyes raked over me. “No one will recognize the polished Deucalion in this wild-haired savage. Just pray no rich fool mistakes you for a slave.”

I bared my teeth. “He’ll regret it.”

Zaemon’s rare laugh was short-lived. “Get your rest. I must prepare in the Ark of Mysteries. The powers we wield tonight aren’t gentle. If we don’t meet again—” He clasped my arm. “Farewell, brother.”

***

I slept like a soldier—deep and dreamless—and woke to darkness thick enough to drown in. The moon had fled; the air reeked of impending doom. The rope lowered me into the abyss, and I landed among tumbled rocks, the cliff a shadow at my back. Phorenice’s sentries huddled by their fires, restless as hunted beasts. I slipped past them, a shadow among shadows, until the tunnel mouth swallowed me whole.

The passage stank of rot and ancient secrets. The air itself seemed to writhe, thick with the promise of upheaval. Zaemon had already begun his work. But the city had changed—new masonry choked old exits, forcing me to emerge near the harbor walls, far from my goal. The ground shuddered underfoot like a living thing.

I ran.

Through alleys where panicked figures stumbled, through streets that groaned and spat dust, I ran. The earth bucked beneath me, and buildings groaned like dying giants. The royal pyramid loomed ahead, its stones grinding like teeth. And before it, the granite throne—its capstone shifting, sliding, inch by torturous inch.

Nais.

The thought of her lying there, exposed to the rabble’s groping hands, near drove me mad. I clawed my way through the crowd, a snarling specter, driving them back with fists and fury. They shrieked, calling me devil, demon—but I cared nothing. The throne’s stone grated, tilted—

Then fell.

The impact shook the square. Dust billowed, choking, blinding. Lightning split the sky, and in its glare I saw Phorenice herself, standing calm amid the chaos, red hair dusted but unshaken. Her fan-girl, Ylga, retched at her feet.

I scrambled onto the throne’s wreckage, fingers scrabbling at the clamps. The metal groaned, gave way—and there she was.

Nais.

Pale as moonlit marble, her hair a dark river, her body light as a husk. I lifted her, cradled her, kissed her cold lips—but she did not stir.

The city still heaved. Time was short.

I slung her over my shoulder and fled, through ruined gates, over broken walls, past the wretches who wailed that Atlantis was accursed. None stopped me. The rope dangled from the cliff like a lifeline.

Up we went.

In the dim light of the mountain’s refuge, I saw her clearly at last. Her beauty was pared to bone, her nails twisted like talons, her skin translucent as parchment. But she was mine.

I bathed her in tinctured warmth, the steam curling like a prayer.

And waited.

For life. For warmth.

For Nais.

The clamor of battle reached us even in the quiet of Nais’ sickroom. Her eyes fluttered open, weak but alert. “What is that sound?” she murmured.

I knelt beside her, smoothing the damp hair from her brow. “Atlantis makes war upon itself,” I said. “Phorenice’s army storms the Sacred Mountain.”

A faint smile touched her lips. “And you—you are King now?”

“By the will of the Priests, yes. Though the crown feels lighter than the duty.”

She reached for my hand, her fingers cool and fragile. “You should not have risked so much for me.”

“Atlantis will endure,” I said. “But you, Nais—you are irreplaceable.”

A blush warmed her pallor. “And you, my lord, have grown handsomer without the beard.”

I laughed, but the thunder of the assault outside pressed urgency upon me. “Rest now. I must see to our defenses.”

Her grip tightened briefly before she let me go. “Come back to me.”

***

The mountain’s defenses were crumbling. Phorenice’s forces, driven by her fury, had breached gate after gate, their bodies piling high in the narrow pass. Our Priests hurled boulders down, crushing whole companies, but for every wave of attackers slain, another surged forward.

I found Tatho leading them, his face grim beneath his helmet.

“Deucalion!” he called up, mocking. “A ghost from the past.”

“A ghost who still breathes,” I shot back. “Call off this madness, Tatho. The Mountain’s secrets are not meant for her hands.”

He shook his head. “You know I serve Phorenice above all. Even old friendship won’t stay me.”

“Then you march these men to their deaths for nothing.”

“They die for her glory,” he said, and raised his sword.

The rocks we loosed crushed another charge, but the cost was brutal. Our own numbers dwindled with every arrow or fire-tube dart that found its mark. The defense was unsustainable.

A desperate plan took shape. If the pass itself could be destroyed…

I ordered the retreat, pulling our surviving Priests back to the highest gate. Behind us, we shattered the ancient pathway—balustrades, statues, the very stones of the road—sending it all crashing into the abyss. The last slender ledge trembled beneath my feet as I crossed, then collapsed into the void.

The way to the Mountain was severed.

For now.

***

Phorenice, ever ingenious, was not so easily thwarted. By dusk, her engineers swarmed below, assembling some monstrous device. Timber and metal gleamed in the fading light as they pieced together a bridge or a siege tower—something to span the chasm we had carved.

Zaemon remained locked within the Ark, communing with powers beyond my grasp. I could only watch and wait, the weight of kingship heavy upon me.

And through it all, one thought anchored me: Nais, waiting in the quiet of her chamber, her trust like a beacon in the storm.

The battle was far from over. But neither was hope.

The old priest approached, his robes whispering against the stone. His voice was thin but urgent. "The Three have sent word, my King. They know of what comes."

I tightened my grip on my sword. "Then they know I stand here to meet it. When the fight is done, I’ll greet the Gods beyond the stars—and welcome them when they follow."

He shook his head. "They forbid it. You are not to die here. They have one last use for you."

I turned, fury rising. "I take no such order. If I am king, if I am a priest, if I am a soldier of Atlantis, then I hold this gate until my last breath. Go back and tell them that."

A faint smile touched his lips—a soldier’s envy. "I said you would refuse. So they gave me this." He held out a jewel, its facets catching the dim light.

My breath caught. "Nais wears that."

"Then you understand. She waits inside the Ark of Mysteries. The Three command you to join her."

For a heartbeat, the battle-cry of the coming storm sang in my blood. But Nais’s face rose before me, and I bowed my head. "I obey."

To the priests who remained, I gave my farewell. "Fight well, brothers. I envy you the glory. But the Three speak with the voice of the Gods, and I am bound to it."

They saluted, blades flashing, and I left them to their final stand.

---

The mountain shuddered beneath my feet as I climbed. The gates below would not hold much longer. The sea roared, devouring the land, swallowing the city whole. The skies burned.

Zaemon met me at the sacred boundary, his face carved from stone. "You see it," he said, gesturing to the drowning world below.

"I see."

"The Gods’ patience is spent. Atlantis falls. Only the Ark endures."

"Then let me join Nais and be done with it."

"There is a rite," he said, and though the ground trembled and the enemy howled at our heels, the old priest would not be rushed. The words of the highest initiation rolled from his tongue, binding me to the mysteries, to the last duty of the priesthood.

When it was done, he gripped my arm. "Go now. The Ark waits. You and Nais alone are chosen to carry what remains of us beyond this ruin."

---

I climbed into the Ark’s belly, sealing the door behind me. Nais stood in the dimness, her face pale but steady.

"They would not come?" she whispered.

"No."

She swallowed, then lifted her chin. "Then we will remember for them."

Outside, the world tore itself apart. The mountain groaned. The seas rose like ravenous beasts. Through the Ark’s thick hull, we heard the screams of the drowning, the final cries of a dying empire.

Then the waves took us.

The Ark lurched, spun, and tore free of the earth. We were weightless, adrift in the blackness, the last of Atlantis.

Nais pressed close. I held her as the ocean roared its victory.

And then—silence.

Only the dark. Only the two of us.

And the future.

The Ark was adrift, a helpless husk on the mercy of the sea—no rudder to steer her, no oars to guide her, no engine to propel her. She moved only as the gods willed. Inside, the air was thick with the scent of age, the darkness broken only by the groans of timbers and the restless shifting of unseen things. The fallen lamp lay useless, its flame extinguished, leaving us to endure the blackness and the chaos in blind silence.

The waves toyed with us, hurling the Ark like a plaything, and I clung to Nais, bracing her against the heaving floor. Words were scarce between us, but in that shared silence, in the grip of loss and survival, we found a bond deeper than speech.

When Atlantis fell, she left the sea a tempest of her dying—whirlpools churned where cities had been, plumes of steam hissed from drowned fires, and then, as if the gods demanded one final spectacle, the winds came screaming. For seven days and nights, the storm raged, a fury so relentless I wondered if it would ever end. But at last, the winds stilled, and the ocean settled into a slow, rolling swell, thick and heavy as oil.

Only then did I dare climb the stair, push open the roof-hatch, and let the clean air rush in. The stench of confinement lifted, replaced by salt and sunlight. I brought Nais up, and together we stood beneath the open sky, offering silent thanks to the Sun God for His mercy. The light was a balm, the warmth a promise that we still lived, though all else had perished.

Exhaustion weighed on me, but sleep came reluctantly. My eyes traced the horizon—no land, no break in the endless blue. Only wreckage bobbed alongside us: splintered trees, the carcass of a hide boat, bloated bodies of beasts and men. The remnants of a drowned world.

When sleep finally took me, it was with Nais’s hand in mine, and I woke to find her watching me, her gaze soft with something like peace. Rested, we were readier to accept our fate—to trust that the gods had spared us for purpose, not cruelty.

Below deck, the Ark revealed its secrets. A marvel of ancient craft, it showed no seam or leak, its timbers fused by forgotten arts into something harder than iron. Within its belly lay stores enough for a voyage without end: grain in vast tanks, sweet water in others, vials of medicines, tools, and weapons.

And then, the writings.

In a great chest at the Ark’s heart lay the accumulated knowledge of Atlantis—secrets of power, of life and death, of forces that had shattered empires. I read some, and fear coiled in me. These were the same mysteries Phorenice had sought, the same that had doomed our homeland. The weight of guarding them was too great.

So I took fire to the wax tablets, melting the words away. If the gods willed these secrets to endure, they would find new scribes. I would not be their keeper.

And when at last we found land, I wrote anew—not of the old world’s forbidden arts, but of its fall, and of the woman beside me, who made survival more than mere endurance.
