It is a universal law that all things are vanity, and everyone is vain. Women are notoriously vain; men are equally so, perhaps even more so if such a comparison is possible. Children, too, are deeply vain—especially children. Right now, one of them is pounding on my legs, demanding my verdict on her brand-new shoes. To be perfectly candid, I hold them in low regard. They lack symmetry, curve, and grace, possessing an indefinable lumpy quality (though I suspect they may have been put on the wrong feet entirely). However, honesty is not what she seeks; she demands flattery. Consequently, I pour over her footwear with a level of effusive praise that feels, in my own conscience, utterly degrading. Nothing less would satisfy this self-important cherub.

I once attempted the "conscientious friend" approach with her, but it failed miserably. She asked for my judgment on her overall conduct, specifically phrasing it as, "What do you think of me? Are you pleased with me?" Seizing the moment for some moral instruction, I replied bluntly, "No, I am not pleased with you." I reminded her of that very morning's events: waking the entire house at five A.M., knocking over a water jug and tumbling down the stairs at seven, attempting to bathe the cat at eight, and sitting on her father's hat at nine-thirty-five. My question was simple: How could a wise and good uncle remain satisfied with such behavior? Would she be grateful for my candor? Would she reflect on my words and resolve to lead a better life? Not a bit of it. Instead, she erupted into a howl of rage, declared herself "naughty" and labeled me a "bad man," and promptly ran to tell her mother.

Since that incident, whenever my opinion is solicited, I keep my true thoughts hidden, opting instead to express boundless admiration for her actions regardless of their merit. She nods approvingly and scampers off to broadcast my praise to the household. She treats my approval as a currency for mercenary gain; shortly after, I hear distant shouts of, "Uncle says I'm a good girl—I get two biscuits!" Off she goes, staring rapturously at her toes and muttering "pretty," a towering monument of two-foot-ten inches of pure conceit.

They are all alike. I recall sitting in a suburban London garden on a sunny afternoon when a shrill treble voice suddenly rang out from a top-floor window, addressing someone invisible in a neighboring yard: "Grandma, my good boy, my very good boy, Grandma! I got on Bob's knickerbockers!" Indeed, even animals are vain. Recently, I spotted a massive Newfoundland dog sitting before a shop mirror in Regent's Circus, scrutinizing himself with a smug satisfaction rarely seen outside a vestry meeting.

Years ago, during a festive holiday at a farmhouse, they placed a flower garland around the head of a cow. That absurd quadruped spent the entire day walking as perkily as a schoolgirl in a new frock. When they removed the wreath, she became instantly sulky and refused to stand still until it was replaced. This is not a tall tale; it is plain, sober truth. Cats, meanwhile, rival humans in their vanity. I have known a cat to storm out of a room if a visitor uttered a derogatory comment about felines, yet sit and purr for hours if offered a neat compliment.

I adore cats precisely because they are so unconsciously amusing. There is a comic dignity to them, a distinct "How dare you!" or "Get away, don't touch me" aura. Dogs, conversely, are the epitome of "Hail, fellow, well met." When I encounter a familiar dog, I slap his head, hurl opprobrious epithets, and roll him onto his back. He lies there gaping, completely unfazed. Try doing that to a cat! She would never speak to you again. To win a cat's favor, one must proceed with extreme caution. Start with "Poor kitty," followed by "Did I hurt you?" spoken in a tone of soothing sympathy. You likely mean neither, but the sentiment implies proper concern, often touching the cat's feelings so deeply that, if you are decently dressed and mannered, she will arch her back and rub her nose against you. Once this stage is reached, you may gently tickle her chin and the side of her head. The intelligent creature will then dig her claws into your leg, and suddenly, friendship reigns supreme, beautifully summarized by the nursery rhyme: *"I love little pussy, her coat is so warm... And pussy will love me because I am good."* The final lines reveal the cat's narrow definition of human goodness: it consists merely of stroking, patting, and feeding.

Fear not, however, that this limited view of virtue is exclusive to cats. We all tend to adopt a similar standard when judging others. A good man is one who is good *to us*; a bad man is one who fails to do what we want. Deep down, we harbor the conviction that the entire universe exists as a necessary appendage to ourselves. Our fellow creatures were created to admire us and serve our needs. In our own minds, we are each the center of the cosmos. You, dear reader, were brought into existence by a considerate Providence solely to read and pay for my writings; while I, in your estimation, am merely an instrument sent forth to provide entertainment for you. The stars exist to make the night sky interesting for our viewing, and the moon, with its mysterious face, is arranged specifically for us to flirt beneath.

We are much like Mrs. Poyser's bantam cock, who fancied the sun rose every morning just to hear him crow. "It is vanity that makes the world go round." I doubt any man has ever existed without it; if he did, he would be an incredibly uncomfortable companion. Such a person would undoubtedly be a good man—one worthy of being placed under glass, mounted on a pedestal, and studied like a school exercise. We would reverence him, but we would not love him. He would not be a human brother whose hand we wished to grip. Even angels, while excellent in their own right, might prove tedious company for us poor mortals. It is in our faults and failings, not our virtues, that we connect and find sympathy. We differ greatly in our noble qualities, but we unite in our follies. Whether pious, generous, honest, or truthful, these traits vary wildly among us. Yet, in vanity and kindred weaknesses, we can all join hands. Vanity is one of those natural touches that make the whole world kin.

From the Indian hunter boasting of his belt of scalps to the European general swollen with pride beneath rows of stars and medals; from the Chinese rejoicing in the length of his queue to the "professional beauty" enduring torturous diets to achieve a waistline resembling a peg-top; from the ragged little Polly Stiggins strutting through Seven Dials with a tattered parasol to the princess sweeping a drawing-room with a four-yard train; from 'Arry, winning laughter through vulgar chaff, to the statesman delighted by the cheers greeting his lofty speeches; from the African trader exchanging rare oils for glass beads to the Christian maiden selling her body for jewels and titles—all march, fight, bleed, and die beneath the banner of vanity. Yes, vanity is truly the motive power of humanity, and flattery is the grease that keeps the wheels turning.

If you wish to win affection and respect in this world, you must flatter. Flatter the high and the low, the rich and the poor, the silly and the wise. You will succeed brilliantly. Praise a man's virtues and another's vices. Compliment everyone on everything, especially on what they lack. Admire handsome men for their looks, fools for their wit, and boors for their breeding. Your own discernment and intelligence will soon be praised to the heavens. Everyone can be swayed by flattery. The "belted earl"—whatever that peculiar title entails—is easily overcome by it, just as every other human being is, from a duchess to a cat-food salesman, from a plow boy to a poet. Interestingly, poets are far easier to flatter than plow boys, for butter sinks better into wheaten bread than into oatcakes.

As for love, flattery is its very lifeblood. As a certain witty Frenchman once noted—a name I cannot currently recall, which is typical of me when I need facts—"Fill a person with love for themselves, and what runs over will be your share." Tell a girl she is an angel, only more angelic; a goddess, only more graceful and heavenly; fairer than Titania, more beautiful than Venus, more enchanting than Parthenope. More adorable, lovely, and radiant than any woman who ever lived, does live, or could live, and you will make a profound impression on her trusting heart. Sweet innocent! She will believe every word. It is so easy to deceive a woman this way. Though they claim to hate flattery, they secretly crave it. When you assure her, "Darling, it isn't flattery; it is plain, sober truth. You are, without exaggeration, the most beautiful, good, charming, divine, and perfect human creature that ever walked this earth," she will offer a quiet, approving smile, lean against your shoulder, and admit you are a dear good fellow after all.
