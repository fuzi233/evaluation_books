The first sound of the morning was a sharp rap at the door. Romola woke to find the room bathed in the stark, cold light of dawn. Maso stood there, having come for the traveling wallet. When she opened the door, the old man started visibly; the graceful silhouette he had grown accustomed to—the woman crowned with the brightness of her hair—was replaced by the heavy folds of a grey mantle and a pale face obscured by a dark cowl.

"It is well, Maso," Romola said, striving for a voice of absolute calm to soothe his alarm. "The wallet is ready. Go on quietly; I shall not be far behind. Once you pass the gates, you may walk slower, for I hope to catch up with you before you reach Trespiano."

She closed the door behind him, leaving the silence of the house intact. Then, her hand went to the key resting in the casket beside her bed. It was the original key to the little painted tabernacle. Tito had failed to drown it in the Arno; instead, it had ended up tucked into the corner of the embroidered *scarsella* worn beneath his purple tunic. Years later, Romola had discovered it there, setting it aside unused yet finding a quiet satisfaction in knowing it remained within reach.

The cabinet holding the tabernacle had been shifted near a window, where the pale morning light illuminated the painted figures just enough for Romola to recognize them. There was the triumphant Bacchus, laden with grapes and clutching a vine-covered spear, embracing the crowned Ariadne. Nearby, the Loves scattered roses around a wreathed vessel and cunning-eyed dolphins swimming in rippled seas, all framed by a floral border resembling a paradise garden.

On this chilling morning, however, the familiar images struck her with renewed bitterness and revulsion. They appeared as a more pathetic mockery than ever, a cruel irony to wake into such profound loneliness. They had served not as a sanctuary for sorrow, but as a deceptive screen. *Poor Ariadne!* she thought, her gaze lingering on the painting's expression of love. As if that radiant face, with its hyacinthine curls twisting like tendrils among the vines, truly held the deep secret of her life!

"Ariadne is wonderfully transformed," Romola murmured to herself. "She would look strangely out of place among these vines and roses now."

She picked up the mirror and gazed at her reflection once more. The sight was so jarring in the harsh daylight that she set it down again, feeling a recoil nearly as intense as when she had turned away from the joyful painting. Seeing her own face, veiled by the cowl, revived a primal dread: the fear that she might eventually be swept into the fellowship of some wretched superstition, joining the ranks of the howling fanatics and weeping nuns whom she had despised since childhood.

With hurried movements, she inserted the key into the tabernacle, unlocked it, and withdrew the crucifix without pausing to examine it. With trembling fingers, she threaded a cord through its small ring, hung it around her neck, and concealed it deep within the bosom of her mantle.

"For Dino's sake," she whispered to herself.

Yet, the task was not complete. There were still the letters for Maso to carry back to Bologna. They were brief. The first was addressed to Tito:

*"Tito, my love for you is dead; and therefore, so far as I was yours, I too am dead. Do not attempt to enforce any laws to fetch me back; it would bring you no happiness. The Romola you married can never return. I need say nothing further after the words I spoke to you during our last long conversation. If you believed them merely words of transient anger, you will now understand they marked an irreversible change.*

*I believe you will fulfill my wish regarding my bridal chest, sending it to my godfather, who gifted it to me. It holds my wedding clothes and the portraits and other relics of my parents."*

She folded the ring inside this letter and wrote Tito's name on the exterior. The second letter was for Bernardo del Nero:

*"Dearest Godfather,—Had I been able to serve your life by remaining, I would not have fled to such a distance. But now I am gone. Do not ask the reason; and if you love my father, strive to prevent anyone from seeking me. I could not endure life in Florence. I cannot bear to explain why. Help to cover my fate in silence. I have requested that my bridal chest be sent to you; when you open it, you will understand the cause. Please entrust all items belonging to my mother to my cousin Brigida, and ask her to forgive me for failing to bid her farewell.*

Placing the letters alongside the crucifix in the depths of her mantle, Romola felt a finality settle over her. Everything was done. She was prepared to depart.

No one stirred in the house. Moving almost as silently as a grey phantom, she descended the stairs and stepped into the silent street. Her heart hammered violently, yet she derived a strange pleasure from the sensation of her firm tread upon the broad flagstones—the swift movement felt like a chained resolution finally released. The urgent drive to execute her deed and the terror of encountering obstacles pushed away all sorrow. Reaching the Ponte Rubaconte, she sensed less the proximity of Santa Croce and more the widening yellow streak of morning light cutting through the grey. Unless she quickened her pace, she feared meeting faces.

Her direct route led straight to the Borgo Pinti, then along the walls toward the Porta San Gallo, passing through the Piazza di Santa Croce. Yet, she walked with steady, rapid strides through the square, refusing to look toward the church. The thought that curious or recognizing eyes might fall upon her, prompting indifferent minds to speculate on her private griefs, caused her to shrink physically, as though recoiling from imagined torture. She felt degraded even by the act of her husband, an act from which she suffered helplessly.

However, no signs indicated that eyes watched from the windows. This tall grey sister, with her firm step and the proud, hidden posture of her cowl, passed unnoticed. Her path avoided the bustle of early traffic, and by the time she reached the Porta San Gallo, she slipped through easily amidst a dispute over tolls for panniers of eggs and market goods entering the gate.

Out! Beyond the houses of the Borgo, she was past the last fringe of Florence. Above her, the sky stretched wide and boundless. She had entered a new existence—one of loneliness and endurance, yes, but also of freedom. She possessed the strength to sever the bonds she had once accepted in blind faith. Whatever happened next, she would no longer feel the warmth of soft, hated lips on her cheek, nor the suffocating breath of an odious mind stifling her spirit.

The bare winter morning and the chill air were welcome in their severity. The leafless trees and sombre hills were no longer haunted by the gods of beauty and joy, whose worship she had forever renounced.

Suddenly, the light burst forth with sudden intensity, casting long shadows across the road. It seemed the sun intended to chase away the greyness. Perhaps light is never felt more profoundly as a divine presence stirring the inarticulate sensibilities of our deepest lives than in these moments when it instantly awakens the shadows. A certain awe, inevitable in such a momentous act, became a conscious element in Romola's feelings as she found herself in the sudden presence of that impalpable golden glory and the long shadow of herself—a shadow that could not be escaped.

Hitherto, she had encountered only occasional countrymen with mules, and the winding nature of the road had kept her from noticing that Maso was not far ahead. But as she passed Pietra and climbed onto rising ground, she lifted the hanging roof of her cowl to look eagerly forward. Immediately, she lowered it again.

She had not seen Maso, but two monks approaching within a few yards. The edge of her cowl acted as a penthouse over her brow, blocking anything above eye level; for the last few moments, she had stared only at the brightness of the path and her own shadow, tall and shrouded like a dread spectre. Now, wishing she had not looked up, she realized her disguise made encounters with monks particularly unpleasant. They might expect pious passwords unknown to her. Walking with a careful appearance of unconsciousness until the black skirts of their mantles had passed her, she felt her heart beat uncomfortably. There was an unease in wearing this religious disguise, a shame at this studied concealment, heightened by the effort to appear unaware under actual scrutiny.

But the black skirts would soon disappear as they went downhill. Spotting a large flat stone against a cypress tree rising from a projecting green bank, she yielded to the impulse born of the slight shock she had received. She sat down to rest. Turning her back on Florence, she did not intend to look at the city until the monks were entirely out of sight. Raising the edge of her cowl once more, she discerned Maso and the mules at a distance where overtaking them was still possible, assuming the old man would linger in expectation. For now, she might simply pause. She was free and alone.
