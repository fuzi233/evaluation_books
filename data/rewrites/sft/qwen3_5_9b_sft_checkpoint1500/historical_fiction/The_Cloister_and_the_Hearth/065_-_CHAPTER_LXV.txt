Two days after the turmoil that had shaken his soul, Gerard wandered lost through the labyrinthine alleys of Rome, seeking refuge from a sudden, violent tempest. The heavens opened in a fury, drenching the wretched street where he stumbled, until he sought shelter within a low, smoky tavern. There, he demanded wine, and as the downpour continued unabated outside, the liquor turned his thoughts to a sluggish haze. Soon, his limbs grew heavy, and he slumped over a rickety table, resting his weary chin in his palms, drifting toward a stupor.

Time passed, and the tavern filled with a rough, boisterous crowd whose clamor eventually jolted him back to awareness. Yet, amidst the din, his ears caught the hushed murmur of two figures standing nearby, engaged in a clandestine negotiation. One was a pardoner, a man of loose morals and brazen speech. Beside him stood another, dressed in clothes that were neat enough to pass for a respectable tradesman, though the manner in which he dragged his wide-brimmed hat low over his brow betrayed a different nature. This gesture served to conceal every feature save a scruffy beard, marking him as a man who hid his face from the gaze of honest folk and the reach of the law. Together, they were trading souls in a marketplace of sin, sealing a celestial contract before the crime itself was committed.

The man with the slouched hat grumbled about the soaring costs of indulgences. "If prices climb any further," he lamented, "we poor wretches will be locked out of heaven entirely."

The pardoner scoffed at the notion. "Indulgences have never been so affordable for the prudent householder."

"And who, pray, purchased them?" the buyer asked.

"Why, those who sin openly, behaving like reasonable creatures," the pardoner replied with a shrug. "But if you are so stubborn as to seek absolution for a specific offense the Pope has explicitly condemned, then you must blame your own conscience, not my ledger!"

To demonstrate that even grave transgressions fell within the reach of all but the destitute, the pardoner unrolled a parchment and recited the rates. It was a chilling catalogue, far too dark for print, yet its contents were well known to the archives of the church. What struck Gerard most was the disparity in value: the price for the life of a common layman was laughably cheap compared to the sum required to wipe away the sins of a clergyman. With a few trivial concessions, the deal was struck. Gold changed hands, and the aspiring pilgrim was absolved in advance for the murder of a layman. The word *assassination* was never spoken, yet the transaction hung heavy in the air.

Gerard heard every syllable, though he did not lift his head from the table nor stir from his drunken stupor. He merely listened, a hollow vessel for the horror unfolding around him. Only when the conspirators rose and parted ways did he force his eyes open. As the figure of the purchaser walked away, Gerard let out a guttural growl, his vision narrowing onto the retreating back.

"If Margaret were still alive," he whispered, the words thick with venom, "I would seize you by the throat and crush you, you cowardly murderer! But she is dead... utterly dead. Let the whole world perish; it matters nothing to me now. So, may I join her first in the dust."

Later, when Gerard returned to his lodging, he found a man wearing a slouched hat pacing restlessly on the sidewalk across the narrow street. "There is that cur again," Gerard thought, his heart beating faster. Yet, in the shadowy aftermath of his despair and the feverish delirium of the storm, the sight of the stranger failed to register as anything more than a fleeting, meaningless detail in the chaos of his broken mind.
