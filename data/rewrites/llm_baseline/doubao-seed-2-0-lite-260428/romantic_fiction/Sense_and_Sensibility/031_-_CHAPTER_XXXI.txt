Marianne woke the next morning after a night of far more rest than she’d expected, the sharp, heavy weight of her heartbreak settling over her the second her eyes fluttered open, no softer for the few hours of unconsciousness that had blotted it out. Elinor sat with her before breakfast, coaxing her gently to speak every painful thought out loud, and by the time the maid called them to the table, they had circled the same raw, ugly truth again and again: Elinor steady, reasonable, laced with quiet affection, Marianne volatile, swinging wildly between wild hope and black despair. One minute she was certain Willoughby must be as innocent and wronged as she was, caught in some terrible trap no one had yet uncovered; the next, she could find no excuse cruel enough to exonerate him. At times she swore she did not care who stared at her out in public, at others she swore she would lock herself away forever from prying eyes, and on rare bursts of energy, she thought she could face the world’s pity and whispers head on.
Only one feeling never shifted: she would not, if she could help it, be in Mrs. Jennings’ company, and if she was forced to, she would not speak a single word of her grief to the older woman. She was fully convinced Mrs. Jennings felt no real compassion for her. “No, no, it cannot be,” she cried. “She cannot feel. Her kindness is not sympathy, her good nature is not tenderness. All she wants is gossip, and she only likes me now because I give her something to talk about.”
Elinor did not need this outburst to know how easily Marianne’s over-sensitive, exacting mind led her to unfair judgments of anyone whose manners did not match her strict standard of soft, delicate grace. Half the world might be clever and good, and Marianne had brilliant gifts and a warm, generous heart, but she was neither reasonable nor candid: she expected everyone else to hold the exact same feelings as her own, and judged every action by how it made her feel in the moment.
An incident not long after breakfast only deepened her low opinion of Mrs. Jennings, through no fault of the older woman’s own. She burst into their room grinning, holding a letter outstretched, certain she was bringing the girls exactly what they needed to cheer them. “Now, my dears, I’ve got something I know will do you both a world of good.”
Marianne heard enough. In a single, bright, feverish moment, her imagination conjured the rest: it was a letter from Willoughby, scrawled in his bold familiar handwriting, full of tenderness, contrition, a perfect, satisfying explanation for every cruel thing that had passed, and he would rush in right behind it, fall to his knees at her feet, and prove every word with the love shining in his eyes. The illusion shattered the very next second. The handwriting on the envelope was her mother’s, a hand she had never once thought unwelcome before. The agony of that brutal crash from wild, giddy hope to total disappointment was sharper than any pain she had felt since Willoughby’s rejection. She could not even find words to reproach Mrs. Jennings for the cruel trick of that false hope, only dissolved into wild, heaving sobs. Mrs. Jennings, bewildered, murmured several soft expressions of pity before she left, still urging them to read the “comforting” letter she had brought.
But the letter gave little comfort. Willoughby’s name filled every page. Their mother, still fully confident in their secret engagement, and as warm in her faith in his constancy as she had always been, had written only after Elinor’s own warning note to beg Marianne to be open with them both about what was going on. Every line was soft with love for Marianne, affection for Willoughby, and unshakable conviction that the two of them would be happy together in the end, and Marianne sobbed through every word, the desperate urge to rush home to Barton overtaking her. Her mother felt dearer than ever, dearest for that very misplaced faith in the man who had hurt her, and she begged Elinor to let them leave at once. Elinor had no certain idea if staying in London or returning to Barton would be better for Marianne, so she only persuaded her to wait a little longer, until they had their mother’s clear instructions to guide them.
Mrs. Jennings left the house earlier than usual that day, unable to rest until she had shared the sad news with the Middletons and Palmers, and refusing Elinor’s offer to accompany her, she headed out for the rest of the morning alone. Elinor sat down at the writing desk with a heavy heart, knowing the full unvarnished news she was about to send their mother would be a brutal shock, and Marianne hovered beside her, fixed to the table, watching her pen move, grieving for the terrible burden Elinor had to carry, and grieving even more for the pain that their mother would feel when she read those lines.
A quarter of an hour later, a sharp rap at the door made Marianne’s frayed nerves jolt so hard she nearly knocked over the inkwell.
“Who can that be?” Elinor said. “It’s far too early for callers. I thought we would be safe for a little longer.”
Marianne darted to the window, and her face twisted with annoyance. “It’s Colonel Brandon. We are never safe from him.”
“He won’t come in, not while Mrs. Jennings is out,” Elinor said.
“I won’t count on that,” Marianne said, already retreating fast to her own bedroom. “A man with nothing useful to do with his own time will never hesitate to intrude on other people’s.”
Marianne’s unfair, hasty guess was proved right: the servant showed Colonel Brandon in. Elinor could see immediately he had come out of nothing but deep concern for Marianne, his face pale, his eyes heavy with melancholy, his brief, anxious question after her showing how much the grief of the last days had weighed on him. She could not forgive her sister for dismissing him so lightly.
“I met Mrs. Jennings on Bond Street,” he said after their first polite greeting. “She encouraged me to stop by, and I was glad of the excuse, because I hoped I might find you alone, which is what I very much wanted. My purpose—my only wish in coming here—is to give your sister comfort, or not immediate comfort, but lasting conviction of the truth. My regard for her, for you, for your mother… will you allow me to prove it by telling you some circumstances that I would never share if I did not believe it would help you? I have spent hours convincing myself this is the right thing to do, but I still fear I may be making a mistake.”
“I understand you,” Elinor said softly, leaning forward. “You have more to tell me of Mr. Willoughby, that will lay his true character bare. It will be the greatest act of friendship anyone could show Marianne. My gratitude is yours already, and hers will come in time. Please, tell me everything.”
“I will, though I make a very awkward narrator, Miss Dashwood. I hardly know where to begin. I must go back far earlier than last October, when I left Barton.” He paused, heaved a heavy, sorrowful sigh. “You probably do not even remember a conversation we had one evening at Barton Park, after the dance, when I spoke of a young woman I had once known, who bore a striking resemblance to your sister Marianne.”
“Indeed, I have not forgotten it,” Elinor said.
He looked relieved, and went on. “If my tender memory does not deceive me, they are alike not just in face, but in every part of their nature. The same warm, impulsive heart, the same vivid, eager imagination and bright, unquenchable spirit. That woman was my orphaned cousin, raised as my father’s ward, and we grew up side by side, playmates first, then lovers. I cannot remember a time when I did not love Eliza. By the time we were seventeen, we had planned to elope together for Scotland, but our secret was betrayed by the treachery of her maid. My father banished me to a relative’s home miles away, and locked Eliza up until he had forced her to marry my older brother, against every wish she held. Our family estate was deeply in debt, and her fortune was the only thing that could save it. My brother did not love her, he did not even treat her with basic kindness. I thought her love for me would carry her through any hardship, but it was not enough. By the time I got the news that she had divorced him, two years later, after I’d been sent off to my regiment in the East Indies, her fate was already sealed. She had been left with no friend to turn to, no one to guide her, and she fell into ruin. That news is what laid this permanent gloom over me, even to this day.”
He could not speak for a moment more, rising to pace the length of the small drawing room to regain his composure. Elinor could not speak either, her heart aching for the pain he had carried for decades. He came back to her a minute later, took her hand, pressed it gently with grateful respect, and went on.
“It was three years before I came back to England, and my first act was to search for her. I tracked her for months, until I found her in a debtor’s prison, wasted, thin, fading fast from consumption, nothing left of the bright, blooming girl I had once loved. The only comfort I had was that her life was almost over, and I could see her settled in quiet, comfortable lodgings, visit her every day until she drew her last breath. Before she died, she left me her three-year-old daughter, her child from the relationship that had led to her ruin. That little girl was my sacred trust. I raised her as my distant ward, and three years ago, when she turned fourteen, I sent her to live with a respectable woman in Dorsetshire who ran a small school for girls. Everything went well for two years, until last February, when she disappeared. She had begged me to let her travel to Bath with the family of one of her school friends, and I agreed, foolish as it turned out. I did not see her for eight whole months. I searched everywhere, and no one would give me a single clue where she had gone. Until October, the very morning we were all supposed to go to Whitwell for that picnic, I got her letter. That is why I left Barton so suddenly, no explanation, which I know confused and offended all of you. She told me the name of the man who had seduced her, who left her pregnant, penniless, no address, promising he would come back for her, and never returned. That man was Willoughby.”
“Good heavens!” Elinor gasped.
“His character is laid bare now for you to see,” Colonel Brandon said gravely. “Extravagant, dissipated, far worse than any of us guessed. For weeks, I watched Marianne love him, confident he would marry her, and I did not know what to do. I hoped that her goodness might reform him, but after he discarded her for Miss Grey’s ten thousand pounds, there is no telling what cruel designs he may have had on her, if he thought he could get away with it. When Marianne compares her own grief, which comes from no fault of her own, no shame, no ruin, to the fate of my poor Eliza, young, abandoned, her life broken before it had barely begun, she will see her own suffering is lighter than she imagines. That is the only thing that might ease her pain. I would never have told you all this, burdened you with my family’s long unhappiness, if I did not truly believe it will help her heal.”
Elinor thanked him with tears in her eyes, agreeing that the truth of Willoughby’s actions would ease Marianne’s desperate, fruitless efforts to find innocence in him, even more than the knowledge of his rejection had. When she asked if he had seen Willoughby since the Whitwell incident, he nodded, gravely. “We met by appointment. Eliza had named him, and I would not let him go unpunished for what he did. We dueled. Neither of us was hurt, and no one ever found out. I moved the younger Eliza out to a quiet cottage in the country, as soon as she recovered from giving birth to her child. She is safe now.”
He said his goodbyes not long after that, not wanting to intrude further on Marianne, leaving Elinor sitting alone, her chest full of deep compassion for all his long hidden grief, and a brand new, profound respect for the quiet, honorable man who had carried so much sorrow, and still acted only out of kindness for the people he loved.
