“Margaret,” her father said the next day, drawing the words out as if he feared to give them shape, “we must return Mrs. Thornton’s call. Your mother is not very well, and thinks she cannot walk so far; but you and I will go this afternoon.”

They set out while the day still held a thin, pale light, and the town’s breath met them at the first turning—coal-smoke and damp stone, with the faint sourness that always seemed to cling to Milton, as if the very air had been boiled too long in iron pots. Mr. Hale, who had walked as one might in a country lane, began now to speak with a guarded earnestness, as though the clatter of the streets had driven his thoughts inward.

“Did you consult the doctor, Margaret? Did you send for him?”

“No, papa. You spoke of his coming to see me, and I was well. But if I only knew of some good doctor, I would go this afternoon and ask him to come—for I am sure mamma is seriously indisposed.”

She said it with the plainness she had used once before and had been met with a gentle rebuke for “fancies.” She could not bear that softness now; her mother’s very voice seemed to have thinned of late, like broth that has been watered and warmed again and again until it holds no strength.

He answered, and the note in his tone was new to her—despondent, as if he had been holding a door shut against fear and at last felt the latch give.

“Do you think she has any hidden complaint? Do you think she is really very ill? Has Dixon said anything? Oh, Margaret! I am haunted by the fear that our coming to Milton has killed her. My poor Maria!”

“Oh, papa! don’t imagine such things,” Margaret cried, shocked into tenderness. “She is not well, that is all. Many a one is not well for a time; and with good advice gets better and stronger than ever.”

“But has Dixon said anything about her?”

“No. You know Dixon enjoys making a mystery out of trifles; and she has been a little mysterious about mamma’s health, which has alarmed me rather, that is all. Without any reason, I dare say. You know, papa, you said the other day I was getting fanciful.”

“I hope and trust you are. But don’t think of what I said then. I like you to be fanciful about your mother’s health! Don’t be afraid of telling me your fancies.” His voice shook into a quicker warmth. “We will ask Mrs. Thornton if she can tell us of a good doctor. We won’t throw away our money on any but some one first-rate. Stay—we turn up this street.”

The street they turned into seemed too mean to contain the commanding figure Margaret had pictured. Marlborough Street ran in long ranks of small houses and blank walls, the bricks darkened as if rubbed daily with soot. No porch announced importance; no trees softened the line. Only the town’s constant sound went on—wheels scouring the stones, a far-off hammering, and underneath it that ceaseless industrial note, like a giant breathing through metal.

“He told me he lived in Marlborough Street, I’m sure,” Mr. Hale said, perplexed.

“Perhaps it is one of the economies he still practises,” Margaret answered. “But here are people about; let me ask.”

She questioned a passer-by and was told, not of a house-front, but of gates: Mr. Thornton lived close to the mill. They were directed to a lodge-door at the end of the long dead wall.

It was no grand entrance—only a door like one to a garden, with great closed gates beside it for wagons and lorries. The lodge-keeper admitted them, and they passed into a large oblong yard. On one side, offices sat primly for the transaction of business; opposite rose the mill itself, immense, many-windowed, and alive with sound. The continual clank of machinery struck the ear like blows. The steam-engine’s groan rolled out and back again, so that the air seemed thick with it; it was a noise that had weight, that pressed on the skull. Margaret found herself listening as one listens to a storm—not for beauty, but for endurance—and she thought how a place’s sounds might wear a person down more surely than hunger.

On one of the narrow sides of the yard stood the house, handsome in its proportions, stone-coped and older in fashion—built, perhaps, fifty or sixty years ago—blackened by smoke, yet with paint and windows and steps kept scrupulously clean. Flights of steps rose to the front door from either side, guarded by railings that spoke of an age that still liked symmetry. Margaret wondered, with a sudden pang, why those who could afford such order did not choose a smaller dwelling somewhere with hedgerows and quiet; why anyone would choose to sleep and eat and speak under the perpetual mill-roar.

Her ears, unused to such a din, could scarcely catch her father’s voice as they stood on the steps. The yard’s dead wall made the view from the windows a prison-yard prospect; and when they entered and were shown upstairs into the drawing-room, the place struck her as a sort of sealed chamber set down in the midst of labour—kept as if to deny that labour.

There was no one there. It seemed as though no one had lived in it since the furniture had been bagged up with care enough for a house to be buried by lava and unearthed centuries later. Pink and gold walls gleamed. A carpet patterned with flower-bunches lay hidden beneath a linen drugget, glazed and colourless from long protection. Lace curtains, netted veils over each chair and sofa, alabaster groups under glass shades—everything guarded from dust as if dust were a moral failing. In the centre, beneath the bagged chandelier, a circular table shone with smartly-bound books arranged at set intervals like coloured spokes. Everything reflected light; nothing absorbed it. The room had an icy purity that made Margaret’s skin draw tight. It was clean in the way a pantry may be clean that never sees bread kneaded on its boards, never feels the warm steam of broth, never hears the domestic stir that belongs to nourishment and rest. There was evidence of labour everywhere, but of labour that served ornament alone—made and preserved against the town’s soot—rather than labour that made a home easeful.

They spoke in low voices while they waited, as if the unused echoes might be startled awake. Then Mrs. Thornton came in, rustling in her usual handsome black silk. Her muslins and laces rivalled the room’s severe whiteness without ever softening it.

Margaret explained why her mother had not come; but in her anxiety not to revive her father’s fears, she spoke awkwardly, and left Mrs. Thornton with the impression of a temporary, fine-ladyish indisposition—something that might have been put aside for a stronger motive, or, if it were real, might have warranted deferring the call. Mrs. Thornton, remembering her own carriage and horses hired for her visit to the Hales, and how Fanny had been ordered by Mr. Thornton to go pay them every respect, drew up slightly offended and gave Margaret no sympathy—hardly any credit.

“How is Mr. Thornton?” Mr. Hale asked. “I was afraid he was not well, from his hurried note yesterday.”

“My son is rarely ill; and when he is, he never speaks about it, or makes it an excuse for not doing anything,” she replied. “He told me he could not get leisure to read with you last night, sir. He regretted it, I am sure; he values the hours spent with you.”

“I am sure they are equally agreeable to me,” Mr. Hale said. “It makes me feel young again to see his enjoyment and appreciation of all that is fine in classical literature.”

“I have no doubt classics are very desirable for people who have leisure.” Mrs. Thornton’s voice took on that tone which made opinion sound like law. “But I confess, it was against my judgment that my son renewed his study of them. The time and place in which he lives require all his energy and attention. Classics may do very well for men who loiter away their lives in the country or in colleges; but Milton men ought to have their thoughts and powers absorbed in the work of to-day. At least, that is my opinion.”

“But surely,” Margaret said, “if the mind is too long directed to one object only, it will get stiff and rigid, and unable to take in many interests.”

“I do not quite understand what you mean by a mind getting stiff and rigid. Nor do I admire those whirligig characters that are full of this thing to-day, to be utterly forgetful of it in their new interest to-morrow. Having many interests does not suit the life of a Milton manufacturer. It is—or ought to be—enough for him to have one great desire, and to bring all the purposes of his life to bear on the fulfilment of that.”

“And that is—?” Mr. Hale asked.

Mrs. Thornton’s sallow cheek flushed; her eye lightened, as if she were putting her hand on a cherished loaf and feeling its warmth through the cloth.

“To hold and maintain a high, honourable place among the merchants of his country—the men of his town. Such a place my son has earned for himself. Go where you will—I don’t say in England only, but in Europe—the name of John Thornton of Milton is known and respected amongst all men of business. Of course, it is unknown in the fashionable circles,” she added scornfully. “Idle gentlemen and ladies are not likely to know much of a Milton manufacturer, unless he gets into parliament, or marries a lord’s daughter.”

Mr. Hale and Margaret were both conscious—half uneasy, half absurdly amused—that they had never heard this great name until Mr. Bell had written to them that Mr. Thornton would be a good friend to have in Milton. Mrs. Thornton’s world of ledgers and markets was not the world of Harley Street or of country parsonages. Margaret tried to keep her face neutral, but the proud mother caught the shade of feeling.

“You think you never heard of this wonderful son of mine, Miss Hale. You think I’m an old woman whose ideas are bounded by Milton, and whose own crow is the whitest ever seen.”

“No,” Margaret answered, with spirit. “It may be true that I was thinking I had hardly heard Mr. Thornton’s name before I came to Milton. But since I have come here, I have heard enough to make me respect and admire him, and to feel how much justice and truth there is in what you have said of him.”

“Who spoke to you of him?” Mrs. Thornton asked—mollified a little, yet jealous, as if praise were a spice she wished to measure herself.

Margaret hesitated; she disliked this authoritative questioning. Mr. Hale, thinking to save her, said—

“It was what Mr. Thornton said himself that made us know the kind of man he was. Was it not, Margaret?”

Mrs. Thornton drew herself up.

“My son is not the one to tell of his own doings. May I again ask you, Miss Hale, from whose account you formed your favourable opinion of him? A mother is curious and greedy of commendation of her children, you know.”

“It was as much from what Mr. Thornton withheld,” Margaret replied, “of that which we had been told of his previous life by Mr. Bell—it was more that, than what he said, that made us all feel what reason you have to be proud of him.”

“Mr. Bell! What can he know of John? He, living a lazy life in a drowsy college. But I’m obliged to you, Miss Hale. Many a missy young lady would have shrunk from giving an old woman the pleasure of hearing that her son was well spoken of.”

“Why?” Margaret asked, looking at her in bewilderment.

“Why! because I suppose they might have consciences that told them how surely they were making the old mother into an advocate for them, in case they had any plans on the son’s heart.”

She smiled grimly. Margaret, startled by the picture, laughed outright—so frankly that it grated upon Mrs. Thornton’s ear, as if the notion must be entirely ridiculous.

Margaret stopped as soon as she saw the offended look.

“I beg your pardon, madam. But I really am very much obliged to you for exonerating me from making any plans on Mr. Thornton’s heart.”

“Young ladies have, before now,” Mrs. Thornton returned stiffly.

“I hope Miss Thornton is well,” Mr. Hale put in, eager to change the stream.

“She is as well as she ever is. She is not strong,” replied Mrs. Thornton shortly.

“And Mr. Thornton? I suppose I may hope to see him on Thursday?”

“I cannot answer for my son’s engagements. There is some uncomfortable work going on in the town; a threatening of a strike. If so, his experience and judgment will make him much consulted by his friends. But I should think he could come on Thursday. At any rate, I am sure he will let you know if he cannot.”

“A strike!” Margaret said. “What for? What are they going to strike for?”

“For the mastership and ownership of other people’s property,” Mrs. Thornton answered, with a fierce snort. “That is what they always strike for. If my son’s work-people strike, I will only say they are a pack of ungrateful hounds. But I have no doubt they will.”

“They are wanting higher wages, I suppose?” Mr. Hale asked.

“That is the face of the thing. But the truth is, they want to be masters, and make the masters into slaves on their own ground. They are always trying at it; they always have it in their minds; and every five or six years, there comes a struggle between masters and men. They’ll find themselves mistaken this time, I fancy—a little out of their reckoning. If they turn out, they mayn’t find it so easy to go in again. I believe the masters have a thing or two in their heads which will teach the men not to strike again in a hurry, if they try it this time.”

“Does it make the town very rough?” Margaret asked.

“Of course it does. But surely you are not a coward, are you? Milton is not the place for cowards. I have known the time when I have had to thread my way through a crowd of white, angry men, all swearing they would have Makinson’s blood as soon as he ventured to show his nose out of his factory; and he, knowing nothing of it, some one had to go and tell him, or he was a dead man; and it needed to be a woman—so I went. And when I got in, I could not get out. It was as much as my life was worth. So I went up to the roof, where there were stones piled ready to drop on the heads of the crowd, if they tried to force the factory doors. And I would have lifted those heavy stones, and dropped them with as good an aim as the best man there, but that I fainted with the heat I had gone through. If you live in Milton, you must learn to have a brave heart, Miss Hale.”

“I would do my best,” Margaret said, rather pale. “I do not know whether I am brave or not till I am tried; but I am afraid I should be a coward.”

“South country people are often frightened by what our Darkshire men and women only call living and struggling. But when you’ve been ten years among a people who are always owing their betters a grudge, and only waiting for an opportunity to pay it off, you’ll know whether you are a coward or not, take my word for it.”

The visit ended under that harsh prophecy. As Margaret and her father went back through the yard, the mill’s voice followed them like a held note. It was not merely loud; it was unceasing. In the pauses between one clang and the next, she could hear—strangely—her own heart, as if her body were trying to make a softer music for itself.

That evening Mr. Thornton came to Mr. Hale’s. He was shown into the drawing-room, where Mr. Hale was reading aloud to his wife and daughter; the lamplight made a small, warm circle, and the quiet cadence of Mr. Hale’s voice was like water poured steadily into a cup—so different from the mill’s roar that Margaret felt, for an instant, as if this domestic sound were itself a kind of sustenance.

“I am come partly to bring you a note from my mother,” Mr. Thornton said, “and partly to apologise for not keeping to my time yesterday. The note contains the address you asked for—Dr. Donaldson.”

“Thank you,” Margaret said hastily, holding out her hand for the note; she did not wish her mother to hear that they had been inquiring about a doctor. Mr. Thornton seemed at once to understand the wish. He put the paper into her hand without explanation, and the small discretion touched her more than many loud sympathies could have done.

Mr. Hale soon began to speak of the strike. At once Mr. Thornton’s face took on that hard expression Margaret had noticed in his mother—the look of one who stands, not in debate, but in defence.

“Yes,” he said. “The fools will have a strike. Let them. It suits us well enough. But we gave them a chance. They think trade is flourishing as it was last year. We see the storm on the horizon and draw in our sails. But because we don’t explain our reasons, they won’t believe we’re acting reasonably. We must give them line and letter for the way we choose to spend or save our money. Henderson tried a dodge with his men out at Ashley, and failed. He rather wanted a strike; it would have suited his book well enough. So, when the men came to ask for the five per cent. they are claiming, he told ’em he’d think about it, and give them his answer on the pay day; knowing all the while what his answer would be, of course, but thinking he’d strengthen their conceit of their own way. However, they were too deep for him, and heard something about the bad prospects of trade. So in they came on the Friday and drew back their claim, and now he’s obliged to go on working. But we Milton masters have to-day sent in our decision. We won’t advance a penny. We tell them we may have to lower wages; but can’t afford to raise. So here we stand, waiting for their next attack.”

“And what will that be?” Mr. Hale asked.

“I conjecture a simultaneous strike. You will see Milton without smoke in a few days, I imagine, Miss Hale.”

“But why,” Margaret said, “could you not explain what good reason you had for expecting a bad trade? I don’t know whether I use the right words, but you will understand what I mean.”

“Do you give your servants reasons for your expenditure, or your economy in the use of your own money? We, the owners of capital, have a right to choose what we will do with it.”

“A human right,” Margaret murmured, very low—so low it seemed meant to be swallowed by the room.

“I beg your pardon, I did not hear what you said.”

“I would rather not repeat it,” she replied; “it related to a feeling which I do not think you would share.”

“Won’t you try me?” he pleaded, and his eagerness was so sudden it altered the air between them, as a hand might change a conversation’s temperature by moving nearer to a fire.

She was displeased by his pertinacity, yet would not make her words into a greater matter than they were.

“I said you had a human right. I meant that there seemed no reason but religious ones why you should not do what you like with your own.”

“I know we differ in our religious opinions,” he said, lowering his voice as if speaking to her alone, “but don’t you give me credit for having some, though not the same as yours?”

Margaret did not wish to be singled out thus. She answered in her usual tone, letting it be heard as part of the room’s general talk.

“I do not think that I have any occasion to consider your special religious opinions in the affair. All I meant to say is, that there is no human law to prevent employers from utterly wasting or throwing away all their money, if they choose; but that there are passages in the Bible which would rather imply—to me at least—that they neglected their duties as stewards if they did so. However, I know so little about strikes and rate of wages, and capital, and labour, that I had better not talk to a political economist like you.”

“Nay, the more reason,” he said eagerly. “I shall only be too glad to explain to you all that may seem anomalous or mysterious to a stranger; especially at a time like this, when our doings are sure to be canvassed by every scribbler who can hold a pen.”

“Thank you,” she answered coldly. “Of course I shall apply to my father in the first instance for any information he can give me, if I get puzzled with living here amongst this strange society.”

“You think it strange. Why?”

“I don’t know—because, on the very face of it, I see two classes dependent on each other in every possible way, yet each evidently regarding the interests of the other as opposed to their own. I never lived in a place before where there were two sets of people always running each other down.”

“Who have you heard running the masters down?” he asked quickly. “I don’t ask who you have heard abusing the men; for I see you persist in misunderstanding what I said the other day. But who have you heard abusing the masters?”

Margaret reddened, then smiled—more out of refusal than mirth.

“I am not fond of being catechised. I refuse to answer your question. Besides, it has nothing to do with the fact. You must take my word for it, that I have heard some people—or it may be only some one of the workpeople—speak as though it were the interest of the employers to keep them from acquiring money—that it would make them too independent if they had a sum in the savings’ bank.”

“I dare say it was that man Higgins who told you all this,” Mrs. Hale said from her chair. Mr. Thornton kept his face as if he had not heard; but Margaret could tell he had caught the name and stored it, like a ledger-entry made without looking down.

“I heard, moreover,” Margaret continued, “that it was considered to the advantage of the masters to have ignorant workmen—not hedge-lawyers, as Captain Lennox used to call those men in his company who questioned and would know the reason for every order.”

She had meant the last words rather for her father; but she saw Mr. Thornton’s expression alter at the name, as if it had struck him like an unwelcome sound—a note from some other world that did not belong in his.

Who is Captain Lennox? he asked himself, with a sharp displeasure that held him silent for a moment.

Mr. Hale took up the thread. “You never were fond of schools, Margaret, or you would have seen and known before this how much is being done for education in Milton.”

“No,” she said, suddenly meek. “I know I do not care enough about schools. But the knowledge and the ignorance I was speaking of did not relate to reading and writing—the teaching or information one can give to a child. I am sure what was meant was ignorance of the wisdom that shall guide men and women. I hardly know what that is. But he—that is, my informant—spoke as if the masters would like their people to be merely tall, large children—living in the present moment—with a blind unreasoning kind of obedience.”

“In short, Miss Hale,” Mr. Thornton said, and the offended edge returned to his voice, “it is very evident that your informant found a pretty ready listener to all the slander he chose to utter against the masters.”

Margaret did not reply. The personal thrust stung her; she felt as if he had taken her very words and stamped them with a meaning she had not intended. A silence fell that made the room’s gentler sounds—Mrs. Hale’s faint breathing, the small settling of the fire—more distinct, as if quiet itself were trying to patch what harshness had torn.

Mr. Hale spoke next.

“I must confess that, although I have not become so intimately acquainted with any workmen as Margaret has, I am very much struck by the antagonism between the employer and the employed, on the very surface of things. I even gather this impression from what you yourself have from time to time said.”

Mr. Thornton paused before answering. Margaret had left the room, and he was vexed—vexed at the misunderstanding and at the sense that he had lost ground with her. Yet the annoyance cooled him into thoughtfulness, and when he spoke again his manner had more dignity than heat.

“My theory is that my interests are identical with those of my workpeople, and vice-versa. Miss Hale, I know, does not like to hear men called ‘hands,’ so I won’t use that word—though it comes most readily to my lips as the technical term, whose origin, whatever it was, dates before my time. On some future day—in some millennium—in Utopia, this unity may be brought into practice—just as I can fancy a republic the most perfect form of government.”

“We will read Plato’s Republic as soon as we have finished Homer,” Mr. Hale said, and his voice, falling again into its accustomed scholar’s cadence, tried to bring the room back under the older, quieter dominion of books—those preserved words that outlast storms, and yet cannot always translate what a living voice means when it speaks in anger or in fear.
