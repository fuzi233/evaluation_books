"It fell about the Martinmas, When nights were long and dark, That wife's two sons came home again, And their hats were of the birch.

"It did not grow by bush or braye, Nor yet in any thorn; But by the gates of Paradise That birch grew fair and born."

It was the evening of February 15th, 1587. At Burrough, Mrs. Leigh paced slowly up and down the terrace walk, her gaze fixed upon the winding river, the hazy sandhills, and the vast, western sea. She had performed this ritual every evening, in fair weather and foul, for three weary years. More than three years had passed, yet there remained no tidings of Frank or Amyas, nor of the gallant ship and the brave souls within her. The loving eyes of Bideford, Appledore, Clovelly, and Ilfracombe had grown hollow from watching and weeping for those who had sailed westward, vanishing like a dream into the infinite unknown—just as John Oxenham had done before them.

Three years of silence. Once, a flush of hope had ignited when Sir Richard, unbeknownst to Mrs. Leigh, dispatched a horseman to Plymouth upon hearing that Drake, Frobisher, and Carlisle had returned from the Spanish Main. Alas, the messenger brought glorious news of the sackings of Cartagena, San Domingo, and Saint Augustine; of the relief of Raleigh's Virginian Colony. Yet, amidst the triumph, there was no word of the *Rose*, nor of those who had sailed in her.

Mrs. Leigh bowed her head in worship, murmuring, "The Lord gave, and the Lord hath taken away; blessed be the name of the Lord!" Her hair had turned gray; her cheeks were wan; her step was feeble. She rarely ventured from home except to church or the neighboring cottages. She never uttered her sons' names, allowing not a single word to escape her lips that might betray thoughts of them. However, whenever the tide swelled high and the red flag atop the sandhills signaled water over the bar, she would pace the terrace, devouring the horizon with greedy eyes in search of a sail that never appeared.

Stately ships entered and exited the channel as of old; white sails hung off the bar for hours, days, months, and years. Yet, an inner instinct assured her that none were the vessel she sought. She knew that ship, every line of her hull, the cut of every canvas; she could have identified her from miles away amidst a whole fleet, but she never came. Mrs. Leigh bowed her head, worshipped, and moved among the poor, who regarded her as an awe-inspiring figure, one whom God had drawn near to Himself within that mysterious heaven of sorrow known so well to them. Lone women and bedridden men gazed into her steadfast eyes, finding strength there, for they understood—even though she never spoke of her own grief—that she had descended into the fiercest depths of the fiery furnace and walked there unharmed beside One whose form was akin to the Son of God. All the while, she blamed herself for her "earthly" longings, confessing nightly to Heaven the weakness she could not shake, which drew her feet to the terrace beneath the wind-clipped trees at every high tide.

That evening, however, Northam was in a ferment. The pebble ridge thundered far below, as it had years prior, but Northam was noisy enough without the rolling surge. The tower rocked with the pealing bells; the people filled the streets, shouting and singing around bonfires. They burned the Pope in effigy, toasted the Queen's health, and cried, "So perish all her enemies!" Hillsides glowed red with bonfires in every village. Far away, the bells of Bideford answered those of Northam, echoing their song from seven years ago when Amyas returned from circumnavigating the globe. Today, news had arrived that Mary, Queen of Scots, had been beheaded at Fotheringay. All England, like a dreamer shaking off a hideous nightmare, erupted in one tremendous shout of jubilation, lifting the terror and danger of seventeen anxious years forever.

"She is gone," the people whispered, "to answer at a higher tribunal than that of the Estates of England, for all the noble English blood spilled for her, and all the noble hearts she tempted into treachery, rebellion, and murder." Elizabeth's own prophecy had finally been fulfilled after years of long-suffering:

*"The daughter of debate, That discord aye doth sow, Hath reaped no gain where former rule Hath taught still peace to grow."*

Now, she could do no more evil. Murder, adultery, the unforgiving heart, and the tongue incapable of speaking truth even for its own sake had passed; perhaps they were atoned for. Even those who remembered that either she or England must perish recalled her fair face as a pitiful dream.

"Nothing is left of her now, but pure womanliness."

Even Mrs. Leigh, a staunch Protestant, breathed a prayer for mercy upon that soul, describing it as "clear as diamond, and as hard," as she had once described herself. She pondered the final scene before the fatal block—it could not have been mere acting. Mrs. Leigh had learned many priceless lessons in the last seven years; might not Mary Stuart have learned something in seventeen? Having been a courtier, she knew, as far as a chaste Englishwoman could know, of the godless profligacy of the French court in which poor Mary had been trained among the Medicis, the Guises, and Cardinal Lorraine. She shuddered and sighed, thinking, "To whom little is given, of them shall little be required!" Yet the bells continued to peal, refusing to cease.

What answered them from the deepening twilight? A flash, followed by the thunder of a gun at sea.

Mrs. Leigh halted. The flash occurred right outside the bar. It could not be a ship in distress; the wind was light and westerly, and it was a high spring tide. What could it be? Another flash, another gun. The noisy throng of Northam fell silent instantly, rushing into the churchyard overlooking the broad flats and the river.

There was a gallant ship outside the bar, running in with all sails set. She was a large vessel, nearly a thousand tons, but not of English rig. What was the meaning of it? A Spanish cruiser seeking reprisals for Drake's raid along the Cadiz shore? Unlikely. The Don favored scientific warfare with admirals, rear-admirals, vice-admirals, transports, and avisos, adhering strictly to approved methods.

Easily, on the flowing tide and fair western wind, she had slipped up the channel between the twin lines of sandhills. She was almost off Appledore now. She was no enemy; if she were a foreigner, she was a daring one, for she had never veiled her topsails—a requirement for any foreign ship within sight of an English port, lest it risk war. This was proven by the Spanish admiral who, many years prior, sailed into Plymouth Sound in peace to fetch Anne of Austria, Philip II's last wife. In his pride, he failed to veil his topsails or lower the Spanish flag. Upon this, John Hawkins, the Port Admiral, rushed forth in his famous *Jesus of Lübeck* and fired a shot between the admiral's masts. When this failed to produce the desired effect, bold Captain John ran alongside and, as his son later testified, "lacked the admiral through and through," causing the offending flag to drop. Apologies were made, though not immediately accepted by the stout guardian of her Majesty's honor. If John Hawkins acted thus against a Spanish fleet in peacetime, there were many old sea-dogs in Appledore ready to act similarly against a single ship in wartime, if they could find even an iron pot to burn powder with.

The strange sail disappeared behind the hill of Appledore, and then a cheer rose into the quiet evening air from a hundred throats. Mrs. Leigh stood still, listening. Another gun thundered among the hills, followed by another cheer.

Perhaps twenty minutes passed before the vessel hove in sight again, rounding the dark rocks of the Hubbastone as she turned up the Bideford River. During this entire time, Mrs. Leigh had stood perfectly motionless, a pale, scarcely breathing statue, her eyes fixed upon the Viking's Rock.

Finally, she rounded the stone. Music blared from the deck—drums, fifes, shawms, and trumpets, waking ringing echoes from every knoll of wood and slab of slate. As she opened fully onto Burrough House, another cheer burst from her crew, rolling up to the hills from the silver waters a mile below.

Mrs. Leigh walked swiftly toward the house and summoned her maid.

"Grace, bring me my hood. Master Amyas is come home!"

"No, surely? Oh, joyful sound! Praise and bless the Lord, then; praise and bless the Lord! But, madam, how did you know?"

"I heard his voice on the river; but I did not hear Mr. Frank's with him, Grace!"

"Oh, be sure, madam, where the one is, the other is. They would never part company. Both come home or neither, I warrant. Here is your hood, madam."

With Grace trailing behind, Mrs. Leigh started with rapid steps toward Bideford.
