When the wandering Trojan hero Aeneas spun his tales of shipwreck and peril to Queen Dido of Carthage, the old fable tells us the queen hung on every word: so enchanted by his eloquent recounting of misadventure that the ensuing romance left her far more heartbroken than any of the hero’s many escapes had ever done. She rued the day she ever let that smooth-spoken, dangerous refugee cross her threshold, after all.

Harry Warrington had none of pious Aeneas’s silver tongue, and his elderly great-aunt, Madam Bernstein, was hardly the soft, sentimental Carthaginian princess. Even so, the young Virginian’s unvarnished, artless tales of his life across the Atlantic, told in his warm, earnest voice, worked their own quiet magic. More than once as he spoke, Madam Bernstein felt her old, hard heart soften in a way it had not allowed itself to for decades. Hers was a life long desert of loneliness, you see—there had been so few oases, so few cool, sweet resting places in all those years, until this bright, friendly lad’s voice filled her rooms, and woke a strange, tender ache of affection and sympathy she had forgotten she was capable of feeling.

She doted on him, clung to this new, unaccustomed sense of care for another human being. Save for a faint, brief flicker of soft feeling in her very earliest girlhood, she had never loved anyone in all her long life. A woman of her sharp, observant nature watched every flutter of her own pulse, of course, and would smile to herself after he left, when she marked how her normally steady heartbeat had quickened just at his nearness. She found herself blushing with foolish, unlooked-for pleasure when he stepped into the room. Her eyes lit up to greet him, and followed his every movement with quiet fondness. Ah, if only she had had a son just like him! How she would have cherished him!

“Wait,” a dark, mocking voice whispered inside her, “wait, Beatrix Esmond! You know you will tire of this fancy, just as you have tired of every other. When this passing infatuation fades, the boy could waste away and die, and you would not shed a tear for him. He could prattle on about his plantations and his hounds, and you would soon grow weary of his stories. Your fate is to be lonely—lonely forever.” Well? Even if life is a desert, there are shade trees by the trail, and springs of cool water to drink. Why not rest here, and enjoy this day? We all know we will have to strike camp at dawn tomorrow, and trudge onward along the path destiny has laid for us.

She smiled to herself as he talked, recognizing little familiar traits in his accounts of his mother that bore the stamp of old Esmond family blood. Madam Esmond was fiercely jealous? Harry admitted as much, with a sheepish grin. She was very fond of Colonel Washington? Harry insisted she only thought of him as a dear friend, no more—he had heard his mother swear that a hundred times over. He even blushed to own that he, fool that he had been, had once been absurdly jealous of the Colonel himself.

“Well, mark my words, my dear,” Madam Beatrix laughed, “my half-sister will never forgive that man for living. And do not look so surprised, sir, that women take a fancy to younger men—after all, do I not dote on you? You can be sure all the rest of the Castlewood household is positively bursting with jealousy over you.”

For all their hidden resentment of Madam de Bernstein’s new favourite, the rest of the Castlewood family never let a hint of ill will show in their words or behaviour to their young kinsman. After just two days staying in the old ancestral stone house, Harry Warrington was “Cousin Harry” to everyone, from the smallest child to the grey-haired elders. The Countess of Castlewood was especially gracious to him whenever Madam Bernstein was in the room, and seized every private opportunity to gush to the Baroness about what a charming young “Huron” they had as a guest, marvelling aloud at the elegance of his manners, and asking how on earth a boy raised in a far-off American wilderness could ever have learned to be so perfectly polite.

The Baroness took all these seemingly admiring comments in perfect good humour, and here the chronicler must pause a moment to note with great admiration the incomparable skill ladies display with one another: the things they say, the things they carefully do not say, the things they whisper behind each other’s backs. How they smile and curtsey while stabbing one another! How they heap compliments on each other while they seethe with hatred! How they bear every petty slight with endless, unflappable long-suffering, never once giving the satisfaction of being openly offended! With what deft, innocent ease they can slip a single drop of poison into a conversation, then hand the brimming goblet around to the rest of the family with a smile, leaving the entire domestic circle miserable without anyone ever being able to point a finger at who did it.

The two great ladies smiled at each other across their drawing room a hundred years ago now, it is easy to picture, pressing cold cheeks together and calling each other “my dear”, “my dearest creature”, “my dear Countess”, “my dear Baroness”, even “my dear sister” while both were fully ready to tear each other’s throats out the second the other turned their back.

“You wonder that the boy is so well-bred?” Madam de Bernstein replied, cool as a cucumber. “His mother was raised by two of the most perfect gentlefolk the Esmond line ever produced. Colonel Esmond carried a grave, stately courtesy and a grandeur of bearing you will not find among the gentlemen of the present day, I assure you.”

“Ah, my dear, we all praise the days we grew up in!” the Countess trilled back. “My own grandmother used to swear there was no court to compare with Whitehall under King Charles the Second.”

“My mother was only at King James the Second’s court for a little while,” the Baroness said, “and you know she was not raised a court lady at all—her father was a simple country parson. But she was still exquisitely well-bred. Her second husband, Colonel Esmond, was a man who had travelled all over Europe, was learned to boot, and moved in the very finest society the continent had to offer. They could not very well leave all their good manners behind when they retreated to their country home in Virginia, now could they? Our Harry inherited those graces naturally.”

“Nay, my dear, you are far too partial to your half-sister,” Lady Castlewood simpered. “She could never have been that perfect, not when she turned cold on her own daughter, as you yourself have admitted. No woman with a truly good heart could ever be that unkind. Manners are all very well, of course, and for a parson’s daughter, your mamma was very decent. I know plenty of clergymen who are perfectly pleasant—our chaplain Mr Sampson, Dr Young, Mr Dodd, all very nice fellows, but none of them have that true air of high birth, do they? For a complete woman, you see, you must have what I have, thank heaven: a warm, feeling heart. Without affection, all the world is nothing but vanity. I protest I do not live, eat, drink or sleep for anything but my own sweet children! My wicked, wild Willy, my wilful little Fanny, my naughty darlings!” She pressed two miniature bracelets to her lips, each holding a tiny portrait of the two children, then called to the two shivering Italian greyhounds that were napping on the rug, who bounded up into her lap and lapped at her painted hands, careful not to smudge the thick layer of rouge on her cheeks. “Nothing do I thank heaven for more than my great sensibility and tender heart, even though it gives me agonies of distress on a daily basis! I would die if any harm came to my dears, or to you, my dear Baroness!”

“You are famous far and wide for your tender heart, Anna,” Madam Bernstein said dryly. “Do share a little of that great sensibility with our young Virginian cousin, won’t you?”

“Nay, I am only a guest here at Castlewood,” the Countess laughed back. “This house belongs to my lord, not me, and I cannot do more for the boy than we already have. He is perfectly delightful, of course! We are not jealous of him, are we, even if it is so obvious how wildly fond the Baroness has grown of him? My own children could almost be mortified, the way he has won everyone’s favour in a handful of days when it took them years to feel half as welcome, but are they angry? Not at all! Willy has already taken him out hunting and riding every morning. This very morning, I passed the music room and saw Fanny teaching him to dance the minuet, while Maria played for them on the harpsichord. It was such a pretty, tender little scene, it brought tears to my eyes!”

But Madam Bernstein had heard the real story of that scene, from her lady’s maid who had been lingering by the door. Here is what actually happened: Lady Castlewood had walked into the music room with her son Willy, who was still caked in mud from the kennels, and clapped her hands loudly, gushing “Bravi! How perfectly charming!” while she beamed her very best smile on Harry, and shot a sharp, significant look at her daughter Fanny that made the girl freeze. Fanny knew exactly what that look meant, and Maria, who was pounding away at the harpsichord, caught it too, and started playing twice as fast, her curls flying as she nodded over the keys.

“Damned little Choctaw!” Willy had grumbled, glowering at Harry. “Is he teaching Fanny a war dance? Is she going to make a fool of herself over him now?” That was exactly what his mother’s look had said, too.

Fanny dropped Harry’s hand mid-step and curtsied so low she almost fell over, while Harry blushed so bright red he could have put the Countess’s finest carmine cosmetics to shame. Maria rose from the harpsichord and made to leave the room.

“Nay, do not stop on my account!” the Countess trilled, seating herself at the instrument. “Let me play for you, go right on dancing!”

“I don’t know how to dance, ma’am,” Harry mumbled, hanging his head.

“Fanny was teaching you? Go right on, dearest Fanny, finish your lesson!”

“Go on,” Willy growled from the corner, half sneering.

“I—I would rather not make a fool of myself in company,” Harry recovered himself, grinning. “When I can dance a minuet properly, I promise you I will ask cousin Fanny to walk one with me.”

“That will be very soon, I am sure, dear Cousin Warrington,” the Countess said, all honeyed sweetness. “What scheme is she hatching now?” Willy thought to himself, for he never could quite figure out what his mother was up to. She called Fanny over to her side, and they left the room together.

No sooner were they out in the tapestry-hung corridor than the Countess’s soft, smiling mask dropped entirely away. “You little fool!” she hissed, pinching Fanny’s arm hard enough to leave black and blue bruises. “You absolute idiot! What do you think you are doing with that wild Huron? You do not want to marry a creature like that, and go be a squaw living in a log cabin in the middle of a swamp!”

“Mamma, stop!” Fanny whimpered, trying to pull her arm away. “You yourself said he was very nice!”

“Nice, yes! Heir to a few thousand acres of swamp, a handful of negro slaves, a rickety log cabin, and a barn full of tobacco leaves! My Lady Frances Esmond, have you forgotten your rank, your name, who your mother is, that three days after meeting this boy from Virginia you are out there dancing cozily with him like you are a tavern girl?”

“But he is our cousin!” Fanny protested.

“Nobody knows where he came from! How can we even be sure he is related to us? He could be a valet who stole his master’s luggage and ran off in his post-chaise for all we know.”

“But Madame de Bernstein says he is our cousin, and he looks exactly like the old portraits of the Esmond family!”

“Madame de Bernstein picks up new favourites one day and throws them away the next! She is besotted with this boy now, but what makes you think she will still care about him a month from now? In public, and when your aunt the Baroness is around, you will be as polite to him as you have to be. But in private? I forbid you to seek him out or encourage him in any way.”

“I don’t care if you forbid me or not!” Fanny cried, stamping her foot, all her patience gone. “Very well then,” her mother snapped back. “I will speak to your father, and we will go straight back to our house in Kensington. If I cannot make you see reason, your brother will.” The rest of their conversation was lost to Madam Bernstein’s eavesdropper.

Madame de Bernstein never told Harry any part of that scene right away. All the young Virginian saw was that his family received him kindly: Lady Castlewood was perfectly gracious, the two young ladies were pleasant, Lord Castlewood, who was cold and haughty to everyone, was no more distant to Harry than he was to his own children, and Willy was happy to ride with him, drink with him, go to the races and play cards with him. When Harry mentioned he might soon take his leave, every single member of the household pressed him to stay longer. He never guessed that the warm welcome he received was part of a dozen hidden schemes, that he had already stepped into the middle of feuds he knew nothing about, or that some of the very people who pressed his hand at breakfast and shared a glass of wine with him at dinner were secretly his enemies. He thought he was loved simply for being their kinsman, fresh from the old Virginia home they had all heard so much about. It never once crossed his mind that he was walking through a den of plotting vipers, all smiling to his face while they calculated how best to use him for their own ends.
