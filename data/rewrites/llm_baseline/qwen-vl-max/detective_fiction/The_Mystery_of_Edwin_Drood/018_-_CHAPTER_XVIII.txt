Around this time, a stranger arrived in Cloisterham—a man with a head of thick, white hair and dark, striking eyebrows. Dressed in a tight blue surtout, a buff waistcoat, and gray trousers, he carried himself with an air that suggested military discipline, though he declared at the Crozier Hotel (the town’s most respectable inn) that he was nothing more than a retired gentleman living off his savings. He announced further that he intended to rent lodgings in the old city for a few months, perhaps longer—possibly settling there permanently.

He made these remarks in the hotel’s coffee room, standing before the empty fireplace, waiting for his fried sole, veal cutlet, and pint of sherry. The waiter—whose duties were sparse enough to allow him full attention—was the only audience.

The newcomer had an unusually large head and a shock of white hair so abundant it seemed to defy gravity. As he shook it like a Newfoundland dog preparing for dinner, he turned to the waiter.

“I suppose,” he said, “a decent lodging for a single man might be found hereabouts?”

The waiter assured him it could.

“Something old,” the man added. “Take my hat down from that peg, will you? No, don’t bring it—just look inside.”

The waiter read the name stitched on the lining: *Datchery*.

“Now you know my name,” said the man. “Dick Datchery. Hang it up again.”

He continued: “I’d prefer something aged, odd, out of the way—something venerable, architecturally interesting, and inconvenient.”

“We’ve a fair number of inconvenient lodgings,” replied the waiter, confident in the town’s offerings. “But architectural… well, that’s harder to find.”

“Anything Cathedral-like?” Mr. Datchery suggested.

The waiter brightened. “Mr. Tope,” he said, rubbing his chin, “would be the best person to ask about such things.”

“Who is Mr. Tope?” asked Datchery.

The waiter explained that he was the Verger—the keeper of the Cathedral’s gatehouse—and that Mrs. Tope had once offered rooms for rent. But no one ever took them, and her sign, long a landmark in Cloisterham, had vanished—likely fallen and never replaced.

“I’ll call on Mrs. Tope after dinner,” said Datchery.

And so, after finishing his meal, he set off with directions from the waiter. But the Crozier was a quiet, unassuming place, and the instructions were too precise to be helpful. Soon, Datchery found himself wandering near the Cathedral Tower, circling it aimlessly, convinced Mrs. Tope’s house was nearby—warm when he saw the tower, cold when he didn’t.

Eventually, he stumbled upon a patch of burial ground where a sheep grazed unhappily. A small boy, armed with stones, was pelting it through the railings. The animal had already been injured in one leg, and the boy was thrilled by the idea of crippling the rest.

“’It ’im agin!” he shouted as the sheep jumped. “Made a dint in his wool!”

“Leave it alone,” said Datchery. “Don’t you see you’ve hurt it?”

“Yer lie,” the boy retorted. “’E went and lamed isself. I seen ’im do it. Gave ’im a shy as a warning not to bruise the master’s mutton no more.”

“Come here,” Datchery ordered.

“I won’t. I’ll come when yer can ketch me.”

“Stay where you are,” said Datchery, “and point out Mr. Tope’s house.”

“How can I stay and show you where Topeseses is, when Topeseses is t’other side the Kinfreederal, over the crossings, and round ever so many comers? Stoo-pid! Ya-a-ah!”

“Show me where it is,” said Datchery, “and I’ll give you something.”

“Come on, then.”

With that, the boy led him. After some walking, they stopped near an arched passage. The boy pointed.

“Lookie yonder. That winder and door?”

“That’s Tope’s?”

“Yer lie—it ain’t. That’s Jarsper’s.”

“Indeed?” Datchery said, intrigued.

“Yes, and I ain’t a-goin’ no nearer ’IM. Not if I knows it. Wait till I fling a jolly good flint at the back o’ ’is jolly old ’ed!”

He gestured to the other side of the arch. “There’s a low door, down two steps. That’s Topeseses—with ’is name on a hoval plate.”

“Good,” said Datchery, pulling out a shilling. “You owe me half of this.”

“Yer lie! I don’t owe yer nothing. Never seen yer.”

“I tell you you do,” said Datchery. “Because I have no sixpence. So next time we meet, you’ll do something else for me—to settle the debt.”

“All right, give us ’old.”

“What’s your name?”

“Deputy. Travellers’ Twopenny, ’cross the green.”

The boy darted off with the coin, just in case Datchery changed his mind. He paused at a safe distance, dancing wildly to mock the idea of returning it.

Datchery removed his hat, shook his white hair once more, and walked on—calm, resigned, purposeful.

Mr. Tope’s dwelling, connected to Mr. Jasper’s gatehouse by an upper stair, was modest and dim—more dungeon than home. Thick ancient walls, groined ceilings, narrow windows set deep into stone. Two chambers opened into each other, both dark and cramped. These were the rooms Mrs. Tope had offered for years—unwanted, until now.

Datchery, however, liked them. With the main door open, he could watch passersby. If Mr. and Mrs. Tope used their private side stair leading directly into the Precincts, he would have privacy. The rent was reasonable. Everything was quaintly inconvenient—exactly what he wanted.

He agreed to take the lodgings immediately, paying in advance. Possession would begin the following evening, on condition he could reference Mr. Jasper, who lived across the gateway.

Mrs. Tope mentioned that Mr. Jasper was very solitary, very sad—but she believed he would vouch for her.

“Have you heard anything about what happened here last winter?” she asked.

Datchery admitted his memory was fuzzy—so much death and disappearance in the world that a man like himself, merely passing through life on his own means, could scarcely keep track.

“I beg your pardon,” he said, “if I get the details wrong.”

“It’s all right,” Mrs. Tope said kindly. “Just a little confused.”

When Mr. Jasper confirmed he would speak for her, Datchery sent up his card and was invited upstairs.

The Mayor was present, Mrs. Tope noted—but not as company. He and Mr. Jasper were close friends.

“I beg your pardon,” said Datchery, bowing with his hat under his arm, “but as a man trying to live quietly on his savings, I must ask: Are the Tope family reputable?”

Mr. Jasper answered without hesitation.

“That is sufficient,” said Datchery.

“The Worshipful the Mayor,” added Jasper, gesturing toward Sapsea, “will also confirm their character.”

“The Worshipful the Mayor,” said Datchery, bowing deeply, “places me under great obligation.”

“Very good people,” said Mr. Sapsea condescendingly. “Well-behaved. Respectful. Approved by the Dean and Chapter.”

“The Worshipful the Mayor gives them a character,” said Datchery, “they may well be proud of.”

He then asked whether the city held many points of interest.

“We are an ancient, ecclesiastical city,” said Sapsea. “Constitutional, as befits such a place. We uphold our privileges.”

“This,” said Datchery, bowing again, “inspires me to learn more—and confirms my wish to end my days here.”

“Retired from the Army?” Sapsea suggested.

“His Honour does me too much credit,” Datchery replied.

“Navy?”

“Again, His Honour overestimates.”

“Diplomacy is a noble profession,” Sapsea remarked.

“There,” said Datchery, smiling, “His Honour has me beaten. Even a diplomatic bird must fall to such a gun.”

Sapsea was pleased. Here was a man who spoke with grace and deference—yet without fawning. The third-person style, the formal address—it flattered the Mayor’s sense of dignity.

“But I must beg pardon,” said Datchery. “I’ve taken up His Honour’s time. My hotel, the Crozier, claims my attention.”

“Oh, not at all,” said Sapsea. “I’m going home. If you’d like to see the Cathedral, I’ll show it to you.”

“His Honour is more than kind,” said Datchery.

As they descended, Datchery followed with his hat under his arm, his white hair fluttering in the evening breeze.

“Might I ask,” he said, “whether the gentleman we’ve just left is the one rumored to be grieving deeply over the loss of a nephew—and seeking vengeance?”

“That is he,” said Sapsea. “John Jasper.”

“And are there strong suspicions?”

“More than suspicions,” replied the Mayor. “All but certainties.”

“Only think!” said Datchery.

“But proof,” said Sapsea, “must be built stone by stone. Justice must be not only morally certain—but legally so.”

“His Honour reminds me,” said Datchery, “of the nature of law. Immoral. How true!”

“The arm of the law,” continued Sapsea, “is strong and long. That’s how I put it.”

“How forcible—and yet, how true!” murmured Datchery.

“Without betraying the secrets of the prison-house,” said Sapsea, “I predict—knowing the iron will of that man—that the long arm will reach, and the strong arm will strike.”

They reached the Cathedral. Sapsea pointed out every feature, as if he had designed it himself. A few flaws he dismissed lightly—workmen’s mistakes, made in his absence.

Then they walked through the churchyard. Sapsea paused to admire the evening light—near Mrs. Sapsea’s epitaph.

“And by the by,” he said, as if remembering suddenly, “that is one of our small lions. Our people favor it. Strangers sometimes copy it. It’s a little work of mine. Troublesome to turn, sir—I may say, difficult to turn with elegance.”

Datchery praised it so warmly that he nearly copied it on the spot—though he resisted, knowing he might return later.

At that moment, Durdles, the local stonemason, approached. Sapsea hailed him proudly.

“Ah, Durdles! This is the mason, sir—one of our worthies. Mr. Datchery, this is Durdles, a gentleman who’s going to settle here.”

“I wouldn’t do it if I was him,” growled Durdles. “We’re a heavy lot.”

“You surely don’t speak for yourself, Mr. Durdles,” said Datchery, “any more than for His Honour.”

“Who’s His Honour?” demanded Durdles.

“His Honour the Mayor.”

“I never stood before him,” said Durdles, looking less than loyal. “And it’ll be time enough to honour him when I am. Until then—‘Mister Sapsea is his name, England is his nation, Cloisterham’s his dwelling-place, Aukshneer’s his occupation.’”

Then Deputy appeared—preceded by a flying oyster shell—and demanded three pence “chucked” to him from Durdles as overdue wages.

While Durdles slowly counted out the coins, Sapsea told Datchery about the mason’s habits, reputation, and home.

“Suppose a curious visitor wished to see your works?” asked Datchery.

“Any gentleman welcome any evening,” said Durdles, a penny between his teeth, “if he brings liquor for two. Or twice two—he’ll be doubly welcome.”

“I shall come,” said Datchery. “Master Deputy, what do you owe me?”

“A job.”

“Mind you pay me honestly—with a job showing me Mr. Durdles’s house when I want to go there.”

Deputy whistled sharply through his gap-toothed mouth—receipt complete—and vanished.

The Mayor and Datchery parted with formal courtesies at the Mayor’s door. Even then, Datchery kept his hat under his arm, letting his white hair stream in the wind.

That night, at the Crozier, Datchery stood before the gas-lit mirror above the coffee-room mantel, shaking his hair.

“For a single buffer,” he said to himself, “living idly on his means, I’ve had a rather busy afternoon.”
