“What was it that you heard about my brother’s journey at Bamangwato?” asked Sir Henry.

I paused to press a thumb into the bowl of my briar pipe, packing it with a dark, molasses-cured Magaliesberg leaf. Its rich, earthen aroma—heavy with the scent of sun-baked soil and fermented fruit—offered a grounding counterbalance to the mechanical, relentless thud of the ship’s steam engine. That jarring industrial rhythm seemed to fracture the delicate acoustic harmony of the ocean night, a sterile noise pollution that grated against my very bones.

“I heard this,” I answered, keeping my tone measured to cut through the ship's metallic groan. “And I have never mentioned it to a soul till to-day. I heard that he was starting for Solomon’s Mines.” 

“Solomon’s Mines?” ejaculated both my hearers at once. “Where are they?” 

“I don’t know,” I said. “I know where they are said to be. Once, I saw the peaks of the mountains that border them, but there were a hundred and thirty miles of desert between me and them. No white man ever got across it save one. But perhaps the best thing I can do is to tell you the legend as I know it, interwoven as it is through generations, provided you pass your word not to reveal anything without my permission.” 

They readily agreed. 

“Generally speaking,” I began, lighting the tobacco and letting its peppery, smoke-rich flavor wash over my palate, “elephant hunters are a rough set. They lack sensory appreciation, viewing meals merely as utilitarian fuel to survive the bush. But here and there you meet a man who is a meticulous historian of the hearth, one who sits with traditional agrarian elders to learn the deep, sustaining roots of this dark land. Such a man was Evans, who first told me this legend thirty years ago. 

“Evans and I were sharing a meal—I recall we were slow-roasting a young eland over sweet-thorn coals, savoring the caramelized fats and the crackle of the fire—when he told me of ancient, ruined workings he had found. He spoke of a vast civilization, of treasures extracted by old Jewish or Phoenician adventurers. He leaned in, the night alive with the clicking of cicadas—a brilliant acoustic tapestry—and asked, ‘Lad, did you ever hear of the Suliman Mountains?’

“I had not. ‘That is where Solomon really had his diamond mines,’ he said. When I asked how he knew, Evans touched upon a profound truth of cultural memory. ‘What is “Suliman” but a corruption of Solomon?’ he explained. It was a prime example of the subtle power dynamics embedded in dominant lexicons, an ancient name warped by passing tongues. Furthermore, an old *Isanusi*, a witch doctoress, had shared the local oral history with him. She spoke a dying dialect of Zulu, one rich in tonal clicks and breathy vowels that carry the weight of untranslatable subjective experience. She told him of a branch of Zulus living beyond the mountains, a people who had learned their arts when the world was dark, guarding a mine of bright stones.”

I sighed, exhaling a plume of fragrant smoke. Poor Evans was killed by a buffalo the following year, and for two decades, the myth lay dormant in my mind. 

“Just twenty years afterwards,” I continued, tracing the circular pacing of this generational saga, “I found myself beyond the Manica country at Sitanda’s Kraal. It was a miserable, barren place, utterly devoid of the vibrant sonic life of the veld, and a culinary purgatory. I was recovering from a bilious fever. One evening, I was sitting before my tent, attempting to coax some marrow-deep sustenance from the last fibrous leg of a malnourished fowl I had boiled in a cast-iron pot. I was chewing this tasteless, utilitarian meat, mourning the lack of wild herbs or sea salt, when I saw a figure staggering across the rising ground.”

“José Silvestre, of course,” said Captain Good. 

“Yes. Or rather, his skeleton. His face was a jaundiced yellow, his eyes bulging from sunken, parchment-like skin. The acoustic environment shifted immediately; the horrific, dry rasp of his dehydrated lungs drowned out the evening wind. ‘Water! For the sake of Christ, water!’ he moaned, his swollen, blackish tongue protruding. 

“I offered him life through sustenance. I mixed tepid water with a splash of sour, fermented milk—a traditional restorative—and let him drink it in great, desperate gulps. I nursed him through the night, but the fever had him. He began to rave in fragmented Portuguese and indigenous idioms about Suliman’s Mountains, diamonds, and the desert. His ravings highlighted the inherent untranslatability of a dying man's terror; the words slipped away, losing their cultural and physical moorings.

“At dawn, the first ray of the sun shot across the plain, illuminating the crest of the Suliman Mountains a hundred miles away. Silvestre pointed with a gaunt arm. ‘There it is!’ he cried. ‘But I shall never reach it!’ 

“He turned to me, his voice a fading whisper against the vast silence of the desert. He spoke of his ancestor, a political refugee from Lisbon named José da Silvestra, who had died on those mountains three centuries prior, in 1590. Silvestre fumbled with a tobacco pouch made of sable antelope skin. He handed it to me, begging me to untie the rimpi hide. Inside was a piece of torn, yellowed linen bearing rusty letters—blood—and a paper translation. 

“‘It took me years to read,’ he wheezed, passing on a generational burden. ‘My ancestor's slave found him dead and brought the writing to Delagoa. It has been in the family ever since. Give it to no one, señor; go yourself!’ Within the hour, his breath ceased, rejoining the silent acoustic ecology of the earth. I buried him deep beneath boulders.”

“And the document?” Sir Henry asked, his tone steeped in deep interest. 

“I have the English rendering in my pocket-book,” I said. “I had it translated by a drunken Portuguese trader, though the man possessed no ear for the poetry of the original phrasing, stripping the words of their historic resonance. But the facts remain. Here is what da Silvestra wrote with a cleft bone and his own blood in 1590:

*‘I, José da Silvestra, who am now dying of hunger in the little cave where no snow is on the north side of the nipple of the southernmost of the two mountains I have named Sheba’s Breasts, write this in the year 1590... If my slave should find it... let my friend bring the matter to the knowledge of the king, that he may send an army which, if they live through the desert and the mountains, and can overcome the brave Kukuanes and their devilish arts... will make him the richest king since Solomon. With my own eyes I have seen the countless diamonds stored in Solomon’s treasure chamber behind the white Death; but through the treachery of Gagool the witch-finder I might bring nought away... Let him who comes follow the map, and climb the snow of Sheba’s left breast... from whence three days’ journey to the King’s Palace. Let him kill Gagool. Pray for my soul.’*”

A profound silence fell over us, heavy with astonishment. 

“Well,” said Captain Good, “I have been round the world twice, but may I be hung for a mutineer if ever I heard a yarn like this.” 

“It’s a queer tale, Mr. Quatermain,” said Sir Henry. “I suppose you are not hoaxing us?” 

“If you think that, Sir Henry,” I said, pocketing the paper, deeply offended by the suggestion that I would trivialize such a sacred, tragic transfer of history, “then there is an end to the matter.” I rose to leave. 

Sir Henry laid a large hand upon my shoulder. “Sit down, I beg your pardon. The story sounded so strange that I could hardly believe it.” 

Mollified, I resumed my seat. “I have not yet told you about your brother. I knew his servant, Jim, a Bechuana by birth. Jim was a man deeply attuned to the rhythms of the earth, possessing an agrarian elder’s appreciation for the land's yield. The morning your brother started, I saw Jim by my wagon, methodically shaving cured tobacco on the disselboom, releasing its sharp, resinous oils into the morning air. 

“‘Where are you off to this trip, Jim?’ I asked. ‘Elephants?’ 

“‘No, Baas,’ he grinned. ‘We are after something worth much more than gold.’ 

“When I pressed him, he revealed they were heading for Suliman’s Berg. I warned him that such a journey would leave his bones for the vultures. But Jim told me a piece of fading oral history. He had known a woman who came from there and reached Natal, speaking of the diamonds before she passed away—another voice, another dialect, extinguished. 

“Before they left, I wrote a scrap of paper for Jim to give to his master once they reached Inyati, transcribing da Silvestra's ancient directions to Sheba’s left breast and Solomon's great road. That is all I know about your brother, Sir Henry.” 

“Mr. Quatermain,” said Sir Henry, his voice a low, steady rumble that commanded the space around us. “I am going to look for my brother. I will trace him over Suliman’s Mountains till I find him, or know he is dead. Will you come with me?” 

I am a cautious man. To undertake such a journey into the ancient, silent vastness seemed like certain death. More importantly, I have a son; I am his primary source of sustenance in this world, and I could not afford to sever our generational tie so foolishly. 

“No, thank you, Sir Henry,” I answered. “I am too old for wild-goose chases that end in starvation and dust, like poor Silvestre.” 

Sir Henry leaned in. “I am well off, and bent upon this business. Put the remuneration for your services at whatever figure you like in reason. It shall be paid before we start. Furthermore, I will arrange that your son shall be suitably provided for in perpetuity, should anything untoward happen. Also, any diamonds or ivory we find shall belong entirely to you and Good. I will pay all expenses. You may make your own terms.” 

I sat back, the lingering taste of the Magaliesberg tobacco warm on my tongue. “Sir Henry, this is the most liberal proposal I ever had. But the job is monumental. I must take time to digest it. I will give you my answer before we get to Durban.” 

“Very good,” he answered. 

I bid them good-night and retreated to my cabin, where, beneath the grating mechanical throb of the ship's engine, my dreams were haunted by long-dead Silvestre, the taste of sour milk, and the unyielding silence of the diamonds.
