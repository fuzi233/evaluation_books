Once the theatrical performances drew to a close, our companions departed in a carriage for Mr. Warrington's lodgings, where the Virginians had arranged an elegant supper. Mr. Warrington, eager to treat them with the utmost distinction, extended an invitation that the General and his wife gladly accepted. They were pleased to know they could afford their young friends such pleasure. The gathering comprised the General and Mrs. Lambert, their son recently returned from college, their two radiant daughters, and Mr. Spencer of the Temple—a new acquaintance George had met at the coffee-house. All partook cheerfully of the landlady's fare.

While I could not precisely determine the seating arrangement, it seems Miss Theo found herself beside the chickens, while Mr. George Warrington sat opposite her. Miss Hetty and a substantial ham shared the attention of Mr. Harry. Mrs. Lambert likely occupied George's right hand, leaving the positions of the General, his son, and the Templar to be deduced. Mr. Spencer had attended the same theater earlier, having even introduced George to the greenroom on a prior occasion. Conversation naturally reverted to the play, with several members of the party insisting they had been thoroughly delighted.

"As for what our gentlemen say, sir," cried Mrs. Lambert to Mr. Spencer, "do not believe a word of it. 'Tis a delightful piece, yet my husband and Mr. George behaved as poorly as possible."

"We laughed in the wrong places and wept when we should have remained dry-eyed," admitted the General. "That is the truth."

"You caused everyone in the boxes around us to glance about and shout 'Hush!' You made the pit cry, 'Silence in the boxes, yonder!' Such conduct I have never witnessed, and I quite blushed for you, Mr. Lambert!"

"Mamma considered it a tragedy, whereas we viewed it merely as amusement," the General explained. "George and I behaved perfectly, did we not, Theo?"

"Not when I happened to look your way, Papa!" Theo replied.

At this, the General exclaimed, "Was there ever such a saucy baggage seen?"

"You know, sir, I did not speak until commanded," Theo continued, adopting a modest tone. "I confess I was deeply moved by the play, particularly the beauty and acting of Mrs. Woffington. I was sorry that the poor mother should find her child and then lose him. I am sorry, too, Papa, if I ought not to have been sorry!" she added, offering a gentle smile.

"Women are not so clever as men, you know, Theo," Hetty interjected from across the table, casting a sly glance at Harry. "Next time we attend the theater, pray, Brother Jack, pinch us when we ought to cry, or give us a nudge when laughter is appropriate."

"I wish we had witnessed the fight," said General Lambert. "The clash between little Norval and the gigantic Norwegian—that would have been rare sport. You should write, Jack, and suggest it to Mr. Rich, the manager."

"I have not seen that particular scene," Harry stated gravely, wondering if his comment had been witty as the entire company laughed. "However, I saw Slack and Broughton at Marybone Gardens!" he added. "It would require no giant to knock over that little fellow in the red boots. For my part, I could throw him over my shoulder."

"Mr. Garrick is indeed a small man," noted Mr. Spencer. "Yet there are moments when he appears a giant. How magnificent he was in *Macbeth*, Mr. Warrington! That dagger scene was truly awful! You should have seen our host, ladies! I presented Mr. Warrington in the greenroom to Mr. Garrick and Mrs. Pritchard, and Lady Macbeth graciously took a pinch from his snuffbox."

"Did the wife of the Thane of Cawdor sneeze?" the General asked in a dramatic whisper.

"She thanked Mr. Warrington in tones so hollow and tragic that he recoiled, nearly upsetting his rappee, for Macbeth subsequently sneezed thrice," Mr. Spencer recounted.

"Macbeth, Macbeth, Macbeth!" cried the General. "And the great philosopher standing by Mr. Johnson declared, 'You must mind, Davy, lest thy sneeze awaken Duncan!' who, by the way, was conversing with the three witches as they sat against the wall."

"What! Have you been behind the scenes at the play? Oh, I would give worlds to go behind the scenes!" Theo exclaimed.

"And to see the ropes pulled, smell the tallow candles, examine the pasteboard gold, the tinsel jewels, and the painted old women, Theo?" George asked skeptically, demurely draining a glass of hock. "Do not look too closely."

"You were angry with your father and me," Theo protested.

"Nay, George!" the girl cried.

"Nay? I say, yes! You were angry with us because we laughed when you desired to cry." Turning to the General, George bowed. "If I may speak for you, sir, as well as myself, I believe we were not inclined to weep like the ladies because we stood behind the author's scenes, as it were. Up close to the young hero, we saw how much of him was mere rant and tinsel; and regarding the pale, tragical mother, her pallor was but white chalk, and her grief merely a pocket-handkerchief. Admit now, Theo, that you thought me very unfeeling?"

"If you discover it without my confession, what is the point of admitting it?" Theo retorted.

"Suppose I were to die?" George pressed. "And you saw Harry in grief? You would witness genuine affliction, a true tragedy, and you would grieve accordingly. But you would not be affected if you saw the undertaker in weeds and a black cloak!"

"Indeed, but I should, sir!" Mrs. Lambert assured him. "And so, I promise, would any daughter of mine."

"Perhaps we might find weeds of our own, Mr. Warrington," Theo suggested.

"Would you?" George cried, his cheeks flushing alongside Theo's; presumably, both noticed Hetty's bright, watchful eyes.

"The elder writers understood little of the pathetic," Mr. Spencer, the wit of the Temple, remarked.

"What do you think of Sophocles and *Antigone*?" called out Mr. John Lambert.

"Faith, our wits trouble themselves little about him unless an Oxford gentleman reminds us," someone replied. "I did not intend to go further back than Shakespeare, who, as you will agree, does not comprehend the elegant and pathetic as well as the moderns. Has he ever approached Belvidera, or Monimia, or Jane Shore? Can you find in his comic female characters the elegance of Congreve?" As the Templar distributed snuff, the conversation shifted.

"I think Mr. Spencer himself must have tried his hand?" someone asked.

"Many gentlemen of leisure have done so. Mr. Garrick, I admit, returned a piece of mine."

"And I confess I possess four acts of a play in one of my boxes," George announced.

"I'll bet it's as good as any of theirs," Harry whispered to his neighbor.

"Is it a tragedy or a comedy?" Mrs. Lambert inquired.

"Oh, a tragedy, containing two or three dreadful murders at least!" George replied.

"Let us perform it, and let the audience beware of their eyes! Yet my chief humor lies with a tyrant," the General declared.

"The tragedy! The tragedy! Fetch it instantly, Gumbo!" Mrs. Lambert shouted to the black servant.

Gumbo bowed low. "Tragedy? Yes, madam."

"In the great cowskin trunk, Gumbo," George said solemnly.

"Yes, sir," Gumbo responded with equal gravity.

"But my tragedy is buried beneath layers of linen, packages, books, and boots, Hetty," George noted.

"Never mind; let us retrieve it and fling the linen out the window!" Miss Hetty cried.

"And the great cowskin trunk resides with our agent in Bristol; thus, Gumbo must secure post-horses, and we shall wait until he returns the day after tomorrow," George explained.

The ladies emitted a comical "Oh!" While the father, perhaps more seriously, proposed, "Let us be grateful for this escape. Let us consider returning home. Our young gentlemen have treated us nobly. Let us all raise a parting bumper to Madam Esmond Warrington of Castlewood, in Virginia. Suppose, boys, you encounter a tall, handsome stepfather upon your return? Ladies as old as she have married before now."

"To Madam Esmond Warrington, my old schoolfellow!" Mrs. Lambert cheered. "I shall write to inform her how prettily her sons entertained us; and, Mr. George, I shall refrain from mentioning how poorly you behaved at the play!" With this final toast, the company dispersed. The General's coach and servant, accompanied by a flambeau, awaited to escort his family home.

After such an entertaining evening hosted by Mr. Warrington, was it not natural and proper for him to visit his guests later, ensuring they had arrived safely and rested well? One might imagine their coach taking the open country behind Montague House toward Oxford Road, only to be ambushed by footpads in the fields. Perhaps the ladies had caught cold or suffered restless sleep
