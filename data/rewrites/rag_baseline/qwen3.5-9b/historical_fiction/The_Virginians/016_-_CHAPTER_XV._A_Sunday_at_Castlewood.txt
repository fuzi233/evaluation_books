The second day after Harry's arrival at Castlewood was a Sunday. The chapel belonging to the castle served as the village church, a single edifice where the house opened directly onto a great state pew reserved for the family. After the appointed hour, they filed into their assigned seats in strict order, while a considerable congregation from the village filled the pews below. Ancient, dusty banners hung from the rafters; Harry found pleasure in imagining them carried by retainers during the Commonwealth wars, conflicts in which his ancestors had fought with loyalty and distinction. Behind the altar rails knelt the effigy of the Esmond of King James the First, the common progenitor of the assembly.

Madame de Bernstein, in her capacity as Bishop's widow, attended without fail. Her devotion possessed a gravity nearly equal to that of the stone ancestor, who knelt perpetually on his marble hassock before his great desk and book, beneath his emblazoned shield. The clergyman, a tall, high-complexioned young man, read the service in a lively, agreeable voice, imparting a theatrical emphasis to the scriptural chapters. The music was competent, provided by one of the young ladies at the organ, though it lacked polish due to an interruption—a burst of laughter emanating from the servants' pew. This was caused by Mr. Warrington's lacquey, Gumbo. Knowing the air intended for the psalm, Gumbo launched into it with a voice so excessively loud and sweet that the entire congregation turned toward the African warbler. The parson covered his mouth with a handkerchief, and the liveried gentlemen from London were thrown into disarray. Perhaps pleased with the sensation he generated, Gumbo continued his performance until it resembled a solo, silencing the clerk entirely. Though Gumbo held the book alongside Pretty Molly, the porter's daughter who had welcomed the strangers to Castlewood, he sang and recited by ear, unable to read a single syllable of the verses before him.

Once this choral disruption concluded, a brief sermon followed, which Harry deemed far too short. In a lively, familiar, and striking discourse, the clergyman recounted a scene witnessed the previous week: the execution of a horse-thief following the Assizes. He described the man's former good character, his family, and the agony of separation. He depicted the execution with startling, terrible, and picturesque vividness, omitting the scriptural phrasing Harry was accustomed to from the somewhat Calvinistic preachers his mother favored. Instead, he spoke as one man of the world addressing other sinners, offering advice that might prove profitable. The unfortunate man had once been a farmer of promise; he had succumbed to drinking, card-playing, horse-racing, and cock-fighting—the vices of the age—which the young clergyman condemned with generous indignation. Then came poaching and horse-stealing, for which he suffered. The divine rapidly sketched striking, fearful images of these rustic crimes. He startled his hearers by revealing that the Eye of the Law watched the poacher at midnight, set traps to ensnare the criminal, galloped the stolen horse over highways and commons from county to county, yet showed Retribution ever pursuing, seizing the malefactor at a country fair, dragging him before the justice, and refusing to unlock his manacles until they dropped at the foot of the gallows. Heaven be pitiful to the sinner! The clergyman enacted the scene. He whispered in the criminal's ear at the cart; he dropped his handkerchief onto the clerk's head. Harry recoiled as the cloth fell. The clergyman had spoken for over twenty minutes. Harry could have listened for an hour more and would not have realized he had been in the pulpit for five. The gentlefolk in the great pew were thoroughly enlivened. Occasionally, Harry, observing the pew where the household servants sat, noted their intense attention, particularly Gumbo, his own man, seated in an attitude of profound consternation. Yet the smock frocks seemed unconcerned, exiting the church with total indifference. Gaffer Brown and Gammer Jones accepted the matter as it unfolded, while the rosy-cheeked, red-cloaked village lasses sat under their broad hats entirely unmoved.

"My lord," the clergyman observed from his pew, nodding slightly as the divine's head and wig surged up from the cushion, "Sampson has been strong today. He has assaulted the Philistines in great force."

"Beautiful, beautiful!" exclaimed Harry.

"Bet five to four it was his Assize sermon. He has been over to Winton to preach and to see those dogs," cried William.

The organist led the small congregation out into the sunshine. Only Sir Francis Esmond, temp. Jac. I., remained kneeling on his marble hassock before his prayer-book of stone. Mr. Sampson emerged from his vestry in his cassock and nodded to the gentlemen still lingering in the great pew.

"Come up, and tell us about those dogs," said Mr. William, and the divine nodded with a laughing assent.

The gentlemen passed from the church into the gallery connecting it to the house. Mr. Sampson traversed the court and soon joined them. Introduced by my lord to the Virginian cousin of the family, Mr. Warrington, the chaplain bowed profoundly, hoping Mr. Warrington would benefit from the virtuous example of his European kinsmen. Was he related to Sir Miles Warrington of Norfolk? Sir Miles was Mr. Warrington's father's elder brother. What a pity he had a son! 'Twas a pretty estate, and Mr. Warrington looked as if he would secure a baronetcy and a fine holding in Norfolk.

"Tell me about my uncle," cried Virginian Harry.

"Tell us about those dogs!" said English Will, breathlessly.

"Two more jolly dogs, two more drunken dogs, saving your presence, Mr. Warrington, than Sir Miles and his son, I never saw. Sir Miles was a staunch friend and neighbor of Sir Robert's. He can drink down any man in the county, except his son and a few others. As for the other dogs regarding which Mr. William is anxious—for Heaven hath made him a prey to dogs and all kinds of birds, like the Greeks in the Iliad—"

"I know that line in the Iliad," said Harry, blushing. "I only know five more, but I know that one." His head fell. He was thinking, "Ah, my dear brother George knew all the Iliad and all the Odyssey, and almost every book that was ever written besides!"

"What on earth" (mentioning only a place under the earth) "are you talking about now?" asked Will of his reverence.

The chaplain reverted to the dogs and their performance. He believed Mr. William's dogs were superior matches. From dogs, the conversation drifted to horses. Mr. William was eager regarding the Six Year Old Plate at Huntingdon. "Have you brought any news of it, Parson?"

"The odds are five to four on Brilliant against the field," said the parson, gravely, "but mind you, Jason is a good horse."

"Whose horse?" asked my lord.

"Duke of Ancaster's. By Cartouche out of Miss Langley," said the divine. "Have you horse-races in Virginia, Mr. Warrington?"

"Haven't we!" cried Harry; "but oh! I long to see a good English race!"

"Do you—do you—bet a little?" continued his reverence.

"I have done such a thing," replied Harry with a smile.

"I'll take Brilliant even against the field, for ponies with you, cousin!" shouted Mr. William.

"I'll give or take three to one against Jason!" said the clergyman.

"I don't bet on horses I don't know," said Harry, wondering to hear the chaplain now, remembering his sermon half an hour prior.

"Wouldn't you better write home and ask your mother?" said Mr. William, with a sneer.

"Will, Will!" called out my lord, "our cousin Warrington is free to bet, or not, as he likes. Have a care how you venture on either of them, Harry Warrington. Will is an old file, in spite of his smooth face, and as for Parson Sampson, I defy our ghostly enemy to get the better of him."

"Him and all his works, my lord!" said Mr. Sampson, bowing.

Harry was highly indignant at this allusion to his mother. "I'll tell you what, cousin Will," he said, "I am in the habit of managing my own affairs in my own way, without asking any lady to arrange them for me. And I'm used to make my own bets upon my own judgment, and don't need any relations to select them for me, thank you. But as I am your guest, and, no doubt, you want to show me hospitality, I'll take your bet—there. And so Done and Done."

"Done," said Will, looking askance.

"Of course it is the regular odds that's in the paper which you give me, cousin?"

"Well, no, it isn't," growled Will. "The odds are five to four, that's the fact, and you may have 'em, if you like."

"Nay, cousin, a bet is a bet; and I take you, too, Mr. Sampson."

"Three to one against Jason. I lay it. Very good," said Mr. Sampson.
