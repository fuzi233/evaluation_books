“Forget the professors,” Madame Beck had said. A wise woman, no doubt—but this was a misstep. That night, she should have left me calm—unstirred, untouched by emotion, unbound to anyone or anything. Instead, she planted in my mind the image of a man I was meant to forget.

Forget him? Ah! They thought they were clever, these wise ones. They showed me how noble he was—how deeply, how purely he loved. They painted him as a hero, pure and steadfast. But what did I know before that day? Only that he could be jealous, quick to suspicion; that his tenderness came like a fleeting warmth, his mercy like morning dew that vanished under the sun’s heat. That was all I had seen—fragments, shadows. And then Père Silas and Modeste Maria Beck—working together, I felt certain—opened the hidden chamber of his heart. They revealed a love so strong, born of youth and southern fire, that it had defied death itself. It had scorned the grave, held fast to the soul, and watched beside a tomb for twenty years.

This was not idle sentiment. He had proven his devotion not with words, but with deeds—by giving up vengeance, taking up sacrifice, devoting his life to those once dear to another woman. His loyalty was written in silence and service.

As for Justine Marie—I knew her well enough without seeing her. She was the kind of girl who lived in Madame Beck’s school: quiet, pale, slow-moving, harmless—neither remarkable nor wicked. If she wore wings, I knew whose imagination had given them to her. If light shone on her brow, I knew whose eyes had conjured that halo.

So why should I fear her? Why let the ghost of a dead nun stand between us? And what of his charities, draining his wealth? What of his vow of celibacy?

Madame Beck, Père Silas—you should not have raised these questions. They were the deepest mystery, the greatest obstacle, and yet the most powerful spark I had ever known. For days, nights, hours—I dreamed of them, woke to them, lived with them. There was no answer in the world—except where one small, dark man stood, sat, walked, lectured, wrapped in a threadbare paletôt, ink-stained and worn, beneath a bandit’s cap.

After my visit to the Rue des Mages, I _wanted_ to see him again. Now that I knew, I felt as if his face might reveal something new—something deeper than before. I longed to read in his expression the mark of that ancient devotion, that half-knightly, half-saintly courage the priest had spoken of. He had become my Christian hero—and I wanted to see him as such.

And fate did not delay. The next day, my new thoughts were tested. I was granted an audience with my “hero”—an audience neither grand nor poetic, but sharp and vivid in its own way.

At three o’clock, peace reigned in the first classe. Madame Beck herself presided, calm and orderly, teaching with clarity and grace. I sat quietly at my desk, drawing—a careful copy of a detailed engraving. I took pride in such work, in making the lines match perfectly, though it was little more than needlework in ink. Yet I found joy in it, and could produce delicate imitations of steel engravings—useless, perhaps, but beautiful to me.

Then—chaos.

A wild burst of motion shattered the stillness. My drawing, pencils, paper—all crumpled into a heap, swept from sight. I seemed flung from my chair like a dried nutmeg shaken from a spice box. The chair and desk, seized by a rushing figure in a paletôt, were carried far across the room. In seconds, I was standing in the center of the grand salle—a vast space rarely used, reserved for dancing and singing.

I regained my wits slowly. Before me stood two men—gentlemen, I supposed—one dark, one fair, both with thick mustaches, whiskers, and imperial beards. One wore a braided surtout, stiff and military; the other looked more like a student or artist, careless and aloof. M. Emanuel stood apart, his face dark with anger, hand outstretched like a speaker addressing a crowd.

“Mademoiselle,” he said, “your duty is to prove to these gentlemen that I am not a liar. Answer their questions as best you can. Write on whatever theme they choose. To them, I am a fraud—an impostor who writes essays and signs them with my pupils’ names. You will disprove this.”

Heaven above! This was the trial I’d feared, now crashing down upon me like thunder. These two—Boissec and Rochemorte—were professors from the college, dandies and pedants, cynics and mockers. It seemed M. Paul had shown them something I’d written—something he’d never praised, never mentioned. An essay, unremarkable in truth, only notable compared to the usual work of foreign girls. In England, it would have passed unnoticed. But these men doubted its origin. Now, I was to defend it, endure their scrutiny.

The scene that followed was unforgettable.

They began with classics. I was silent. Then French history. Mérovée? Pharamond? I barely knew the names. They moved through various subjects—philosophy, science, literature. Each time, only a shake of my head: “Je n’en sais rien.”

After a pause heavy with mockery, they turned to general knowledge—topics I knew well, ones I’d pondered often. M. Emanuel, watching silently, stirred slightly. Perhaps, he thought, I would show some wit now.

He was wrong.

Answers surged in my mind, clear and fast—but words failed me. I couldn’t speak. Or perhaps I wouldn’t. My nerves were frayed, my spirit crossed. I heard one of them whisper to the other: “Est-elle donc idiote?”

“Yes,” I thought, “an idiot—forever one, for men like you.”

But I suffered. I saw the sweat gather on M. Paul’s brow, his eyes speaking sorrow and frustration. He refused to believe I lacked intelligence—he thought I *could* respond, if only I *would*.

At last, to spare him, I stammered:

“Gentlemen, please let me go. You’ll gain nothing from me. As you say—I am an idiot.”

I wished I could have spoken calmly, or kept silent. But my tongue betrayed me. Seeing the judges turn to M. Emanuel with triumphant looks, hearing my voice tremble, I broke into choking sobs. It was not grief—it was rage. Had I been a man, I would have challenged them there. But it *was* emotion, and I would rather have been beaten than let it show.

Fools! Could they not see the clumsy hand of a novice in that essay they called a forgery? The subject was classical. I’d never heard it before. I had no foundation. But I read, researched, built a skeleton from dry facts, clothed it with language, tried to give it breath. It was hard, anxious work—until I was satisfied with the structure. I cared deeply about truth. I avoided error, not because I knew much, but because I refused to lie. Knowledge wasn’t stored in my head—it had to be gathered, fresh each time, like herbs from the wild. Messieurs Boissec and Rochemorte missed this. They mistook my effort for scholarship.

Still, they would not let me leave. I must write before them.

With trembling hand, I dipped my pen. My eyes blurred, tears threatening. One of them began to apologize—softly, with false delicacy.

“We act for truth’s sake. We do not wish to wound you.”

Scorn gave me strength. I replied simply:

“Dictate, Monsieur.”

Rochemorte chose the theme: “Human Justice.”

Human Justice? A cold abstraction, offering no inspiration. And there stood M. Emanuel—sad as Saul, stern as Joab—while his accusers smiled in triumph.

I looked at them. I was gathering courage to tell them I would write nothing more—that their theme insulted me, their presence mocked me—that to question his honor was to attack truth itself. I meant to say it all—when suddenly, a memory struck.

Those faces—buried in hair, moustache, beard—were the same ones I’d seen, lit by gaslight behind a portico, on the night I arrived in Villette. The very men who had chased me, breathless, through the city streets, driven me beyond endurance.

“Pious mentors!” I thought. “Pure guides for youth! If ‘Human Justice’ were truly just, you two would not hold your posts—or enjoy your reputations.”

An idea seized me. I began to write.

Human Justice appeared before me—not as a noble ideal, but as a red-faced, rude hag, arms akimbo. I saw her in her house—a den of disorder. Servants called for orders she ignored. Beggars waited outside, starving. Children crawled around her feet, screaming for help. She cared for none. She sat warm by the fire, smoked her pipe, sipped her syrup, and enjoyed her solitude. When a cry pierced her ears too sharply—she grabbed the poker or hearth-brush. If the sufferer was weak, she silenced him. If strong, she threatened, then reached into her pouch and tossed sugar-plums.

That was my sketch—hurried, biting, placed before them. M. Emanuel read over my shoulder. Without a word, I curtsied and left.

Later that day, M. Paul and I met again. At first, the encounter was tense. The examination had left a sting. A short, sharp exchange ended with me called “a little mocker, heartless,” and with him walking away.

But I did not want him gone entirely. Only to feel that such passion could not be indulged without consequence. Soon after, I saw him gardening in the berceau. He approached the glass door. I drew near. We spoke of flowers.

By degrees, he laid down his spade. Conversation resumed, grew easier. Then he touched on a painful point.

Half-apologetic, half-regretful, he admitted the excesses of that day—the volatility of his moods. “Yet,” he said, “some allowance must be made. But I cannot expect it from you, Miss Lucy. You know nothing of me—my past, my position, my sacrifices, my sorrows.”

His history. I seized the word.

“No, Monsieur,” I replied. “You are right. I know nothing of your history, your duties, your trials, your affections. Nothing. You are a stranger to me.”

“Hein?” he murmured, raising his brows.

“You see me only in class—strict, demanding, impatient. In town, you are active, bold, quick to lead, slow to persuade. A man without ties, without family—no attachments, no duties. To us, you are a machine operator, moving us here and there without regard for our feelings. You seek recreation in public, under chandeliers. This school, that college—your workshops, where you manufacture pupils. I don’t even know where you live. It’s natural to assume you have no home.”

“I am judged,” he said. “Your opinion is exactly what I expected. To you, I am neither man nor Christian. You see me void of affection, faith, principle. Well, Mademoiselle—such is our reward in this life.”

“You are a philosopher, Monsieur—a cynical one,” I said, glancing at his paletôt, which he brushed with a sudden movement. “Contemptuous of human weakness, above comfort, independent of pleasure.”

“And you, Mademoiselle?” he countered. “You are neat, cozy, and terribly insensitive.”

“But, Monsieur,” I pressed, “you must live somewhere. Tell me—where? And what servants do you keep?”

With a fierce thrust of his lower lip, he snapped:

“Je vis dans un trou! I live in a hole—a cave you wouldn’t dare enter. Once, I spoke of my ‘study’ in the college—know now that this ‘study’ is my entire home. My bedroom, my sitting room. As for servants…” (mimicking my tone) “…they number ten.”

He spread his fingers before my eyes.

“I polish my boots,” he continued, “brush my paletôt.”

“No, Monsieur,” I interrupted. “It’s obvious—you never do.”

“Je fais mon lit et mon ménage. I eat at a restaurant. My supper takes care of itself. I pass days laborious and loveless, nights long and lonely. I am fierce, bearded, monkish. Nothing living loves me—except a few old hearts worn like mine, and some poor souls, broken in body and spirit, whom this world rejects, but who inherit the kingdom of heaven.”

“Ah, Monsieur—but I know!”

“What do you know? Much, I suspect. Yet not me, Lucy.”

“I know you have a pleasant old house in the Basse-Ville—why don’t you go live there?”

“Hein?” he muttered.

“I liked it,” I said. “The steps leading to the door, the grey stones, the trees behind—real trees, tall and ancient. The boudoir-oratoire—make that your study. It’s quiet, solemn.”

He studied me closely. A faint smile touched his lips.

“Where did you learn this? Who told you?”

“Nobody. Did I dream it, Monsieur?”

“Can I enter your dreams? Can I guess a woman’s thoughts—awake or asleep?”

“If I dreamed it, I saw people too—a priest, old and bent; a servant, old and picturesque; and a lady—splendid, strange. Her head barely reached my elbow. Her gown was bright as lapis-lazuli, her shawl worth a thousand francs. She wore jewels so brilliant I’ve never seen anything sparkle so. But her body seemed broken, bent double. She looked older than human years—worn by sorrow. Morose, almost cruel. Yet someone cared for her—someone forgave her sins, hoping to be forgiven. Three old souls—mistress, chaplain, servant—all sheltered under one roof.”

He covered his face with his hand, but his mouth twitched with something like amusement.

“You’ve entered my secrets,” he said. “How?”

So I told him—the storm, the commission, the waiting, Père Silas’s story.

“As I waited,” I said, “he told me a tale.”

“A story? Père Silas is no novelist.”

“Shall I tell you?”

“Yes. Begin at the beginning. Let me hear Miss Lucy’s French—her best or worst. Give me a good handful of errors and a dose of English accent.”

“It’s not a long tale,” I said. “‘The Priest’s Pupil.’”

“Bah!” he said, his cheek flushing. “The old father chose the worst subject—his weakest point. But what of the pupil?”

“Oh, many things.”

“What things? Be specific.”

“The pupil’s youth, his manhood—his greed, ingratitude, implacability, fickleness. Such a bad pupil—thankless, cold, unchivalrous, unforgiving.”

“Et puis?” he asked, lighting a cigar.

“Et puis—he suffered calamities we didn’t pity, bore them with a spirit we didn’t admire, endured wrongs without sympathy. Finally, he took the unchristian revenge of piling coals of fire on his enemy’s head.”

“You haven’t told me all.”

“Nearly. I’ve given you the chapters.”

“You forgot the part about his lack of affection—his cold, monkish heart.”

“Yes. Père Silas said his vocation was nearly that of a priest—his life considered consecrated.”

“By what bonds?”

“By the ties of the past and the charities of the present.”

“So you know the whole story?”

“I’ve told you everything I heard.”

Silence fell. Then he said, “Now, Mademoiselle Lucy, look at me. With the truth you always keep, answer me one question. Raise your eyes. Look into mine. No hesitation. Trust me—I am a man worthy of trust.”

I raised my eyes.

“Now that you know me—my past, my burdens—knowing my faults—can we still be friends?”

“If Monsieur wants a friend in me, I shall be glad to have one in him.”

“But a close friend—intimate, real—kindred in all but blood. Will you be the sister of a poor, burdened, imprisoned man?”

I could not speak. But I think I answered. He took my hand. And in that touch, I found comfort—solid, sure, like rock.

“When I speak of friendship,” he repeated, “I mean true friendship.” I could scarcely believe such earnest words had reached my ear. The look in his eyes—kind, anxious—felt real. If he truly desired my trust, and truly offered his—then life offered nothing greater. In that moment, I was rich, strong, happy. To confirm it, I asked:

“Are you serious? Do you really need me? Can you care for me as a sister?”

“Surely, surely,” he said. “A lonely man like me, without a sister, must be glad to find one in a woman’s heart.”

“And may I rely on your regard? May I speak to you when I wish?”

“My little sister must test me,” he said. “I make no promises. She must tease and try until she shapes her brother to her will. After all, he is not unyielding in the right hands.”

As he spoke, his voice softened, his eyes warmed. A joy I had never known filled me. I envied no lover, no bride, no wife. I had this—this voluntary, self-offered friend. If he proved true—and he looked true—what more could I desire? But if it all faded, as once before had done—?

Still, for now, I believed.
