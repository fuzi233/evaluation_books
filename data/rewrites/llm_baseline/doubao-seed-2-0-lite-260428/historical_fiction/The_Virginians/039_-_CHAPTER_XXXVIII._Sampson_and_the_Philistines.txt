In my youth, a happy chance made me close acquaintance with a most garrulous gentleman hailing from that fabled “first gem of the ocean” island all impartial judges of maritime trinkets praise so highly. The stories he told of his kinsfolk there made my young blood curdle: every crime you can name, every line of the Ten Commandments shattered to splinters, rogueries no penny dreadful scribbler could dream up, murders and highway robberies even the notorious Thurtell or Turpin never dared pull off. He recounted every last one of them with perfect, unflinching accuracy, to any soul who would listen. It was a miracle the whole brood hadn’t been clapped in the prison hulks long before.

“Me brother Tim brought his fawther’s grey hairs to the grave,” he would say. “Me brother Mick robbed the parish church three times over. Me sister Annmarouia jilted the Captain, ran off with the Ensign, forged her grandmother’s will, and stole the family silver spoons—for which poor Larry the knife-boy got hanged in her place.” The cursed house of Atreus was nothing beside his clan, of course; not for all the world would I name the isle that spawned them.

What astonished my naïve young self most was that these parricides, forgers, and cutthroats wrote to one another as “my dearest brother” and “my dearest sister,” and lived for months at a stretch in perfect harmony. Tim, his hands still reeking of his father’s blood, would mix a spiced rum punch and pass his sister a glass without a second thought. Mick, his lips still black with the perjuries he’d sworn over the stolen will, would kiss his sister Julia’s cheek and weep with her over tales of their childhood “dear old castle,” and their sainted mother who had cheated them all out of the family estate. They embraced so warmly at meetings and partings you could forget all those little confessions that, if they told half the truth, every last one of them could hang the rest.

What finer gift than forgiveness? Is there anything more rational than screaming every insult under the sun at a man, then apologizing for your hasty words, pulling the decanter you hurled at his head out of the way, and making up? Some souls carry this angelic gift of forgiveness in their bones. The two ladies of Tunbridge Wells we followed last chapter were possessed of it in spades. Only yesterday they had screamed and snapped at each other over cards; today they laughed, teased, and played side by side, each taking a wing of the roast chicken, pulling the wishbone together, and tearing every other resident of the resort to shreds in conversation, while never so much as hinting at their own recent quarrel. They were like the Peninsular War soldiers who fraternized across truce lines, trading tobacco and wine, ready to grab their muskets and blow each other’s heads off the second the bugle blew to resume fighting.

The Baroness Bernstein’s threatened gout? All the twinges had vanished entirely, she said, and her niece Lady Maria was overjoyed to hear it. Maria’s frequent fainting fits? The Baroness clucked that they were quite gone, save for a tiny little recurrence the night before, and insisted Maria take iron tonics and fortify her constitution long after they left Tunbridge. She even offered to ship cases of the spa’s medicinal waters to Maria’s London lodgings. What kindness! Though we have no way of knowing what the Baroness whispered to her lady’s maid later that evening: “Fainting fits? Pah. Inherited epilepsy from that horrible scrofulous German mother of hers.” Nor can we be sure Maria did not order her own maid Betty to taste every glass of the Baroness’s offered spa water first, declaring her aunt was perfectly capable of slipping poison into it. These are merely precautions—those loaded muskets the truce-time soldiers keep cocked beside them, lying quiet in the grass for the moment.

With Harry Warrington’s signed bond tucked safe in her pocket, the wily Lady Maria had no intention of pressing for immediate payment. She knew the world far too well. She would give her young captive plenty of day-rule, plenty of leave to wander and amuse himself in London, no need to chafe or anger her pretty young mouse when the cat could wait purring by the wall, watching with sharp green eyes. Both ladies knew exactly what diversions a gentleman of Harry’s age sought in the capital, and spoke of them with the frank ease common to that unbuttoned era. Maria consoled herself readily enough during his absence, taking her amusement where she found it: she played cards for hours with the agreeable Mr. Jack Morris, who counted Lord March and Harry himself among his close friends, and who counted it almost a pleasure to lose small sums to an earl’s daughter. She wangled an invitation for him to the Countess of Yarmouth’s assembly, and everyone went home perfectly pleased.

The first two days after Harry’s departure passed very cheerfully indeed, until that Friday arrived—the day the Reverend Mr. Sampson had spent so long preparing his famous sermon for. The whole of Tunbridge Wells was ready to turn out. Sampson had a widespread reputation as a wildly entertaining, eloquent preacher, and when there were no breakfast parties, conjurors, or dancing bears performing, the spa’s visitors were happy enough to sit through a good sermon. Sampson knew full well the Countess of Yarmouth was the King’s dearest favourite, that she alone held the power to hand out fat church livings and even bishoprics, and that the King trusted her judgement on all spiritual matters far more than any bishop’s. So he spared no expense for the occasion: he bought a brand new flowered periwig on credit, and a crisp, rustling new cassock from a local mercer who was foolish enough to trust a popular chaplain’s promise to pay. He marched proudly from the Baroness’s pre-sermon breakfast to the church, his two patronesses walking at his side, their footmen trailing behind carrying their giant gilt-edged prayer books.

The Baroness looked radiant that day, laughing, greeting every acquaintance with a stately smile and a tap of her tortoiseshell cane. At the church door, all of Tunbridge’s fashion had assembled: the Countess of Yarmouth conspicuous in a flame-coloured taffeta gown and bright vermilion cheeks, a whole throng of lords and ladies chattering as they filed in. But there were odd visitors among the crowd too. Two shabby men in ragged coats slipped in just as the organ stopped, one with bright carroty hair sticking out under his ill-fitting scratch wig. He crossed himself unconsciously as he stepped over the threshold, and muttered to his friend: “Bedad, Tim, I forgot this was a Protestant church.” You could smell whisky rolling off them from ten feet away. Then two more men, sallow, hook-nosed, and very greasy, tried to push past the beadle. “We’re here to convert, governor,” one whispered in his ear, slipping a guinea into his palm, and the beadle stepped aside to let the two Jewish moneylenders enter.

The organ blared. The doors clanged shut. Sampson walked up the aisle ahead of the gold-laced beadle, rosy and jolly as any parson alive—until he lifted himself up over the pulpit cushion, glanced over the congregation, and turned as pale as death. There on either side of the west door stood the two Hebrew strangers. Slipping into a bench by the vestry door were the two whisky-scented Irishmen, their eyes meekly cast down to the floor, blocking every exit.

No one in the congregation noticed his terror. The sermon was hailed as the greatest any of them had ever heard. The Countess of Yarmouth declared it was “bewdiful, and made me tremble all over.” The Baroness called it excellent. Lady Maria waved her handkerchief up at the pulpit to congratulate their family chaplain on triumph. But Sampson barely saw her. His eyes were fixed on the four blocking his escape, long after the last “amen” was spoken.

The crowd streamed out, two by two, then one by one, until the church was almost empty. The Baroness and her niece lingered in the rector’s pew, chatting with the parson, until Maria stepped forward to make their exit. The two Irishmen stepped up to her at once. One, Mr. Costigan, pulled a long warrant from his pocket. “At the suit of Mrs. Pincott, mercer of Kensington, madam, I have the honour of arresting your ladyship,” he said, bowing very politely. “My name is Costigan, a poor gentleman from Oireland, forced by circumstances to follow this disagreeable profession. Will you walk, or shall my man fetch a sedan chair?”

Lady Maria shrieked three times, and fainted dead away on the stone floor. The Baroness’s eyes twinkled sharply as she ordered her maid to unlace her stays. As soon as the maid’s hands were busy, she snipped the thin hair chain around Maria’s neck that held a tiny sealed bag, stuffed it into her own pocket, and called for someone to dash cold water on her face. The debt, Costigan said, was one hundred and thirty-two pounds.

Meanwhile, Sampson had surrendered meekly to his own pursuer, the bailiff Simons. “At whose suit?” he sighed. “Buckleby the cordwainer,” Simons said. “Forty-eight pounds and charges, I know,” Sampson said. He had no money to pay. The two prisoners were carried off in waiting sedan chairs to the bailiff’s comfortable “spunging house” lodging, the two Philistines having captured their Sampson and his great patroness both.

That evening, the Baroness sent her servant Case with a gushing note to her niece, full of professions of ardent affection, but explaining that her own heavy losses at cards meant she could not possibly cover the debt. She said she had written post-haste to Mrs. Pincott to beg for more time. Maria and her maid could scrape together only eighteen shillings between them, so they decided Betty should pawn Maria’s gold chain to ride post haste to London, and find Harry Warrington, the champion they were certain would rush to pay their debts and rescue them. But when Betty brought the chain to the very toy shop where Harry had originally bought it as a gift for Maria, the shopkeeper thought she was a thief, and called for a constable. Betty had to confess the whole story: Lady Maria was arrested, locked up in a bailiff’s house. By dusk, every last soul in Tunbridge Wells knew the news.

But polite society pretended nothing had happened. That night at Lady Trumpington’s card assembly, the Baroness arrived looking perfectly cheerful. No one said a word about the arrest while she was in earshot. The Countess of Yarmouth asked after Maria, and the Baroness said she had fainted in church that morning and was keeping her room. Cards were dealt, fiddles played, the footmen waited for supper, the chairmen drank and swore outside, and the world went on spinning exactly as it would if no parson and no earl’s daughter were locked up in a spunging house. The Countess whispered to her servant Fuchs to find out exactly how much Maria’s debt was, she said—she might pay it herself, just to vex that cold old Baroness everyone knew was behind the whole arrest.

The Baroness went home at midnight, slept like a log, and did not wake until late the next morning, when her maid brought her tea laced with a little cordial, as was the custom for fine ladies of that era. She roused, and asked for the latest news.
“Someone’s here to give it you,” her maid said sullenly.
Before she could ask who, the bed curtains were drawn back.
It was Lady Maria, rosy cheeked, perfectly composed, dropping her aunt a low mocking curtsy. Harry Warrington had ridden into Tunbridge at midnight, paid all her debts and Sampson’s too, and sprung them both out of custody before the sun rose. With that little surprise, we may close our chapter.
