My method of commencing this narrative has thus far yielded two unsatisfactory results. First, I attempted to rely solely on my own recollection, a process that involved considerable scratching of the head and ultimately produced nothing but confusion. Second, I consulted my daughter, Penelope, whose suggestion sparked a completely novel approach. Her proposal was that I record events chronologically, day by day, starting specifically from the moment we received word that Mr. Franklin Blake was expected to visit. As anyone who has ever tried to anchor memories to specific dates knows, such a rigid framework forces the mind to retrieve details with remarkable precision. The initial hurdle, however, was locating the actual dates. To assist me, Penelope offered to consult her personal diary, a habit instilled during her schooling which she has maintained without interruption.

When I suggested an alternative—that perhaps she should narrate the tale directly from her journal's entries—Penelope reacted with visible fury. Her face flushed crimson, and she declared with a fierce intensity that her journal was intended strictly for her private eye, and that no living soul should ever possess knowledge of its contents save herself. Upon my gentle inquiry as to the meaning of such secrecy, she simply retorted, "Fiddlesticks!"

Accepting her original plan, I shall therefore begin by stating that on a Wednesday morning, the twenty-fourth of May, 1848, I was summoned by my mistress into her private sitting room.

"Gabriel," she said, "there is news that will astonish you. Franklin Blake has returned from abroad. He has been residing with his father in London and is arriving tomorrow to stay until next month, coinciding with Rachel's birthday."

If I had possessed a hat in my hand at that precise moment, nothing short of profound reverence would have kept me from hurling it against the ceiling. I had not seen Mr. Franklin since his boyhood, when he lived within these very walls. In my memory, he was, without exaggeration, the finest boy ever known for spinning tops or breaking windows. However, Miss Rachel, who was present, offered a starkly contrasting recollection. She described him as "the most atrocious tyrant that ever tortured a doll," and the hardest taskmaster imaginable for an exhausted child forced to pull a string harness.

"I burn with indignation, and I ache with fatigue," Miss Rachel summarized, "whenever I reflect upon Franklin Blake."

Given this introduction, you may naturally wonder how Mr. Franklin managed to disappear from our lives for so many years, growing from a boy into a man while absent from his homeland. The explanation lies in the unfortunate circumstances of his father. My mistress's eldest sister married the renowned Mr. Blake, a man equally infamous for his immense wealth and his protracted legal battle. For years, Mr. Blake waged war against the nation's tribunals, seeking to displace the rightful Duke and claim the title for himself. He drained countless lawyers' pockets and tore apart the reputations of numerous innocent citizens in his quest to determine if he was right or wrong.

Tragically, both his wife and two of his three children died before the courts finally decided to dismiss his claims. Once the legal dust settled and the Duke retained his inheritance, Mr. Blake concluded that the only way to balance the scales with his country was to deny it the honor of raising his son.

"How can I trust my native institutions?" he famously argued, "after the manner in which they have treated *me*?"

Compounding this sentiment was Mr. Blake's inherent dislike of all boys, including his own. Consequently, Master Franklin was removed from England and dispatched to educational establishments in Germany, a nation his father deemed trustworthy. Meanwhile, Mr. Blake remained comfortably ensconced in England, continuing his parliamentary career and publishing an unfinished treatise regarding the Duke's succession—a document that remains incomplete to this day.

Thus ends the saga of Mr. Blake, the senior. You and I need concern ourselves no further with the Dukedom; let us focus instead on the Diamond, which serves as the true catalyst for Mr. Franklin's involvement in our household.

Our pleasant youth did not forget us during his exile. He corresponded periodically with my mistress, Miss Rachel, and myself. Prior to his departure, we had a minor transaction wherein he borrowed a ball of string, a four-bladed knife, and seven shillings and sixpence. Regrettably, I have never seen the color of that coinage, nor do I anticipate seeing it again. His letters primarily concerned requests for further loans. Through my mistress, I learned of his progress abroad. Having mastered the German system, he subsequently toured France and Italy, emerging as something of a universal genius—or so I understood. He wrote, painted, sang, played instruments, and composed music, though I suspected he borrowed inspiration in all these endeavors just as he had borrowed money from me.

Upon reaching maturity, his mother's fortune of seven hundred pounds a year devolved upon him, yet it vanished from him as quickly as water passes through a sieve. The more wealth he acquired, the greater his desire became; there existed a gaping hole in Mr. Franklin's pocket that no amount of sewing could repair. Despite this financial instability, his lively and affable nature made him universally welcomed wherever he traveled. He lived a peripatetic existence, his self-proclaimed address being "Post Office, Europe—to be left till called for."

He resolved twice to return to England, but on both occasions, some unnamed woman intervened to prevent his journey. His third attempt, however, proved successful, as my mistress has already recounted.

On Thursday, the twenty-fifth of May, we were to witness the transformation of our beloved boy into a man. By our calculation, he was twenty-five years of age. He was of noble birth, possessing high courage, and arrived under skies of perfect summer clarity. My mistress and Miss Rachel, anticipating his arrival only at dinner, departed for lunch with acquaintances in the neighborhood.

With them gone, I inspected the prepared guest chamber to ensure everything was in order. Subsequently, acting in my dual capacity as butler and cellar steward—a role I assumed at my own insistence due to my resentment at others holding the keys to Sir John's vintage cellars—I retrieved some of our esteemed Latour claret. I placed the bottles in the warm summer air to remove the chill prior to dinner.

Deciding that what benefits old wine also benefits old age, I retreated to my beehive chair in the back courtyard to enjoy the sunshine. My repose was interrupted by a sound resembling the soft beating of a drum emanating from the terrace above. Circling around, I discovered three individuals of mahogany complexion dressed in white linen frocks and trousers, gazing upward at the house. Each carried a small hand-drum slung across their chest. Trailing behind them was a slender, fair-haired English boy bearing a satchel.

Judging them to be itinerant conjurers with the boy serving as their apprentice, I approached the leader. He spoke fluent English and displayed manners of such elegance that I felt compelled to acknowledge his refinement. He politely requested permission to demonstrate his arts for the benefit of the lady of the house.

Though I am not a grumpy elder and generally favor amusement, I am quick to distrust strangers who appear slightly darker than myself, especially when I am aware that a valuable plate-basket is currently exposed on the pantry table. My caution, however, was driven less by prejudice and more by the fear of theft. I informed the Indian gentleman that the lady was away and warned him and his party to depart. He responded with a graceful bow, and they promptly withdrew.

Returning to my chair, I allowed myself to drift toward sleep, lulled by the warmth of the sun. I was abruptly awakened by my daughter, Penelope, rushing out as if the building were ablaze.

"What is the matter?" I demanded.

She insisted immediately that the three Indian jugglers be apprehended. Their motive, she claimed, was malicious intent toward Mr. Franklin Blake. She explained that she had recently spoken with the lodge-keeper's daughter, and the two girls had witnessed the Indians leaving after I had dismissed them. Believing the boy was being abused by the foreigners—though I could discern no logical reason for such abuse given his delicate appearance—they had secretly slipped along the inner side of the hedge to spy on the proceedings from the outside.

Their actions were indeed extraordinary. After ensuring the coast was clear by scanning the road in both directions, the trio turned to stare fixedly at our house. They began speaking rapidly in their own tongue, exchanging glances of uncertainty, before turning their attention to the English boy, seemingly expecting him to aid them.

Then, the chief Indian, who spoke English, addressed the boy: "Hold out your hand."

At these ominous words, Penelope confessed that her heart nearly leaped from her body. Privately, I suspected it might have been her corsets, but aloud I merely remarked, "You make my flesh creep."

The boy recoiled, shaking his head and refusing. The Indian, however, asked kindly if he preferred to be returned to London, abandoned in an empty market basket, hungry, ragged, and forsaken. This ultimatum apparently resolved the conflict. Reluctantly, the boy extended his hand.

The Indian then produced a bottle from his tunic and poured a dark substance, resembling ink, into the boy's palm. Touching the boy's head and making gestures in the air, he commanded, "Look."

The boy froze, standing rigid like a statue, staring intently into the ink pooled in his hand. While I initially dismissed this as mere juggling accompanied by a wasteful display of ink, Penelope's subsequent words snapped me back to alertness.
