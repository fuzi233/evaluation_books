The fire crackled, a low, rhythmic percussion against the silence of the room. I watched the flames climb, casting shadows that danced with a logic of their own, detached from the rigid order of the outside world. Across the hearth, seated in the chair that had held my wife for half a century, she observed the same spectacle. We are middle-aged now, a stage where repetition becomes a form of prayer; we repeat our jokes, our inquiries, our stories until the edges blur.

"Do we recall," I asked, the words hanging in the warm air, "how foolishly we wed?"

Miles, my son, paused in his contemplation of the gaps and rivers he had recently traversed with such perilous ease. He spoke of the terror that followed his triumphs, the astonishment at his own audacity. Yet, he remains the most eager sportsman in the two counties, his love for the hunt consuming everything else. He has broken his collarbone and suffered a hundred falls, to his mother's horror. And yet, so did I. I recall falling from my mare, Sultana, slipping on a stone while we dreamed along a park road at four miles per hour. For Miles, reckless sport is the delight of life; for me, marriage was the blessing. I never think of it without thanking Heaven. Do not mistake my devotion for an example. I do not advise young couples to marry on twopence a year; the vestry meetings would turn black against me. But my wife is a desperate matchmaker. When Hodge and Susan appear in my justice room discussing allowances, we urge them to spend their half-crown at home, add a contribution of our own, and summon the vicar.

Now, when I pose a question to my dear oracle, I know the answer before I speak. This certainty is why I consult her so often. I wear a particular expression, and my Diana reflects it instantly. If I say, "My dear, do you not think the moon was made of cream cheese tonight?" she replies, "Well, Papa, it did look very like cream cheese, indeed—there's nobody like you for droll similes." Or if I suggest, "My love, Mr. Pitt's speech was fine, but I doubt he equals his father," she answers, "Nobody was equal to my Lord Chatham." Then one of the girls cries out, "Why, I've often heard our Papa say Lord Chatham was a charlatan!" And Mama retorts, "How like she is to her Aunt Hetty!"

As for Miles, *Tros Tyriusve* means nothing to him. He reads only the sporting announcements in the Norwich paper. As long as the scent is good, the state of the country is irrelevant. I suspect the rascal has never read my poems, let alone my tragedies. I mentioned *Pocahontas* to him recently, and the dunce believed she was a river in Virginia. Regarding my Latin verses, how can he comprehend them when he cannot construe Corderius? This notebook lies openly on the little table at my corner of the fireside. Any passerby could lift my spectacles from their cover and read it. Yet Miles never has.

Inside the loose pages lie caricatures of Miles: jokes against him. He never knows, nor does he heed them. Only once, replacing a neat drawing of myself in China ink—a portrait of Miles asleep after dinner, a work my friend Bunbury would not disown—I found a crude sketch of myself going over my mare Sultana's head, titled "The Squire on Horseback, or Fish out of Water." The fellow roared with laughter, and all the girls tittered when I discovered the page. My wife confessed she had never been in greater fright than when I approached the book. I can bear a joke against myself; I have heard many, though strangely, for one who lived among the chief wits of the age, I never heard a truly good one in my life.

Never mind, Miles. Though thou art not a wit, I love thee none the worse. There has never been any love lost between two wits in a family. Thou hast no great beauty, yet thy mother thinks thee as handsome as Apollo, or his Royal Highness the Prince of Wales, born in the very same year. Indeed, she believes Coates's picture of the Prince resembles her eldest boy, keeping the print in her dressing-room to this day.

In that same year, with vastly different prospects, my Lord Esmond, son of Lord Castlewood, appeared to adorn the world. My Lord C. and his humble servant had already fallen into a coolness at that time. Heaven knows, my honest Miles's godmother brought no gold pap-boats to his christening! Matters have mended since, *laus Deo*—indeed, *laus Deo*, for I suspect neither Miles nor his father would ever have achieved much by their own wits.

Castlewood House wears a quite different face now from the venerable visage it bore in my youth. Back then, it was covered with the wrinkles of time, the scars of old wars, the cracks and blemishes marked by years on its hoary features. I love best to remember it in its old shape, as I saw it when young Mr. George Warrington descended at the owner's invitation to attend his lordship's marriage with Miss Lydia Van den Bosch—"an American lady of noble family of Holland," as the county paper announced. Then the towers stood as Warrington's grandfather, the Colonel—the Marquis, as Madam Esmond would like to call her father—had seen them. The woods, thinned considerably, still stood; nay, some of the very same rooks may have cawed over them which the Colonel had witnessed sixty years prior. His picture hung in the hall, a frame he might have owned had he not preferred love and gratitude to wealth and worldly honor.

Mr. George Esmond Warrington—that is, *Egomet Ipse* writing this page—as he paced the long corridors, the smooth dew-spangled terraces, and the cool, darkling avenues, felt momentarily as if he were one of Mr. Walpole's cavaliers, complete with ruff, rapier, buff-coat, and gorget. He imagined an Old Pretender or a Jesuit emissary emerging from behind any tall tree-trunk or antique carved cupboard. I harbored strange, sad, pleasant, old-world fancies walking the place: imagining tragedies, intrigues, serenades, escaladoes, Oliver Cromwell's Roundheads battering the towers, or bluff Hal's Beefeaters pricking over the plain before the castle.

I was then courting a certain young lady. Your ladyship's eyes needed no spectacles then, and on the brow above them there was never a wrinkle or a silver hair. I remember writing a ream of romantic description, under my Lord Castlewood's franks, to the lady who never tired of reading my letters. She says I now send her only three lines when I am away in London or elsewhere. 'Tis that I may not fatigue your old eyes, my dear!

Mr. Warrington thought himself authorized to order a genteel new suit of clothes for my lord's marriage. With Mons. Gumbo in attendance, he made his appearance at Castlewood a few days before the ceremony. I may mention it had become expedient to send my faithful Sady home aboard a Virginia ship. A great inflammation attacking the throat and lungs, fatal in many cases that year of Wolfe's expedition, had seized and nearly killed my poor lad, for whom his native air was pronounced the best cure. We parted with abundant tears, and Gumbo shed as many when his master went to Quebec. But he possessed attractions in this country and none for the military life, so he remained attached to my service.

We found Castlewood House full of friends, relations, and visitors. Lady Fanny was there upon compulsion, a sulky bridesmaid. Some virgins of the neighborhood also attended the young Countess. A bishop's widow herself, the Baroness Beatrix, brought a holy brother-in-law of the bench from London to tie the holy knot of matrimony between Eugene Earl of Castlewood and Lydia Van den Bosch, spinster. For some time before and after the nuptials, the old house in Hampshire wore an appearance of gaiety to which it had long been unaccustomed. Country families came gladly to pay their compliments to the newly married couple. The lady's wealth was the subject of everyone's talk, and no doubt did not decrease in the telling. Those naughty stories rife in town, spread by her disappointed suitors, took some time to travel into Hampshire. When they reached the country, they found it disposed to treat Lord Castlewood's wife with civility, not inclined to be too curious about her behavior in town. Suppose she had jilted this man and laughed at the other? It was her money they were anxious about, and she was no more mercenary than they. The Hampshire folks were determined that it was a great benefit to the country to have Castlewood House once more open, with beer in the cellars, horses in the stables, and spits turning before the kitchen fires.
