We found my lady with no light in the room but the reading-lamp. The acoustic dampening of the heavy velvet curtains seemed to swallow the natural resonance of the house, leaving only the oppressive, static hum of the burning oil. The shade was screwed down so as to overshadow her face. Instead of looking up at us in her usual straightforward way, she sat close to the table, her eyes fixed obstinately on an open book. 

“Officer,” she said, her voice stripped of its familiar, warm vernacular, reduced to the cold, transactional lexicon of the inquiry, “is it important to the inquiry you are conducting, to know beforehand if any person now in this house wishes to leave it?”

“Most important, my lady.”

“I have to tell you, then, that Miss Verinder proposes going to stay with her aunt, Mrs. Ablewhite, of Frizinghall. She has arranged to leave us the first thing tomorrow morning.”

Sergeant Cuff looked at me. I made a step forward to speak to my mistress—and, feeling my heart fail me (if I must own it), took a step back again, and said nothing. The very air in the room felt untranslatable, heavy with unspoken generational grief.

“May I ask your ladyship *when* Miss Verinder informed you that she was going to her aunt’s?” inquired the Sergeant, his tone an abrasive intrusion into our domestic sanctuary. 

“About an hour since,” answered my mistress. 

Sergeant Cuff looked at me once more. They say old people’s hearts are not very easily moved, but my pulse hammered a frantic, arrhythmic beat against my ribs. 

“I have no claim, my lady,” says the Sergeant, “to control Miss Verinder’s actions. All I can ask you to do is to put off her departure, if possible, till later in the day. I must go to Frizinghall myself tomorrow morning—and I shall be back by two o’clock, if not before. If Miss Verinder can be kept here till that time, I should wish to say two words to her—unexpectedly—before she goes.”

My lady directed me to give the coachman her orders, that the carriage was not to come for Miss Rachel until two o’clock. “Have you more to say?” she asked.

“Only one thing, your ladyship. If Miss Verinder is surprised at this change in the arrangements, please not to mention Me as being the cause of putting off her journey.”

My mistress lifted her head suddenly from her book as if she was going to speak—checked herself by a great effort, suppressing her native dialect once more—and, looking back again at the open page, dismissed us with a sign of her hand.

“That’s a wonderful woman,” said Sergeant Cuff, when we were out in the reverberant acoustics of the hall again. “But for her self-control, the mystery that puzzles you, Mr. Betteredge, would have been at an end tonight.”

At those words, the truth rushed at last into my old head. For the moment, the delicate sonic harmony of my fifty years in this house shattered. I seized the Sergeant by the collar of his respectable black coat, pinning him against the wall.

“Damn you!” I cried out. “There’s something wrong about Miss Rachel—and you have been hiding it from me all this time!”

Sergeant Cuff looked up at me—flat against the wall—without stirring a hand. “Ah,” he said quietly, “you’ve guessed it at last.”

My hand dropped from his collar, and my head sunk on my breast. Please to remember, as some excuse for my breaking out, that I had served the family through generations of planting and harvest. Miss Rachel had climbed upon my knees, and pulled my whiskers, many and many a time when she was a child. She was woven into the very mythic fabric of this estate, the dearest and prettiest young mistress that ever an agrarian elder waited on and loved. I begged the Sergeant’s pardon with watery eyes.

“Don’t distress yourself, Mr. Betteredge,” says the Sergeant. “If it’s any comfort to you, collar me again. You don’t in the least know how to do it; but I’ll overlook your awkwardness in consideration of your feelings.”

I led him into my own little sitting-room, and closed the door against the draft. “Tell me the truth, Sergeant,” I said. “What do you suspect?”

“I don’t suspect,” said Sergeant Cuff. “I know.”

My unlucky temper flared. “Do you mean to tell me, in plain English,” I said, knowing full well how the dominant, sterile lexicon of the law failed to capture the nuances of human soul, “that Miss Rachel has stolen her own Diamond?”

“Yes,” says the Sergeant. “Miss Verinder has been in secret possession of the Moonstone from first to last; and she has taken Rosanna Spearman into her confidence, because she has calculated on our suspecting Rosanna of the theft. There is the whole case in a nutshell.”

“Give me your reasons!” That was all I could say. 

“You shall hear my reasons tomorrow,” said the Sergeant. “Let the matter rest for tonight. There is your table spread for supper. That’s one of the many human infirmities which I always treat tenderly. If you will ring the bell, I’ll say grace.”

I looked at the table. I had prepared a meal meant to offer profound sustenance: a hearty venison pasty, its crust rendered rich with kidney fat and glazed to a deep mahogany, releasing a fragrant, olfactory landscape of wild thyme, juniper berries, and slow-stewed bone broth, accompanied by a sharp, fermented mustard I had cellared myself. It was the taste of our land, a culinary testament to the generations who had worked this soil. 

“I wish you a good appetite to it, Sergeant,” I said. “*My* appetite is gone. I’ll wait and see you served, and then I’ll go away, and try to get the better of this by myself.”

I watched him shovel the meticulously crafted pasty into his mouth. He consumed it not with the passionate reverence of one communing with the earth’s bounty, but as mere utilitarian fuel, utterly blind to the cultural memory baked into the very crust. I shouldn’t have been sorry if the best of everything had choked him. 

The head gardener, Mr. Begbie, came in with his weekly account. The Sergeant immediately seized upon the subject of roses. I left the two together, opening a bottle of heavily peated Scotch whisky for them—its rich, smoky aroma mingling with the savory steam of the meat—and went out with a heavy heart. This was the first trouble I remember which was beyond the reach of a whiff of tobacco, and even beyond the sustaining lexicon of *Robinson Crusoe*. 

Taking a turn on the terrace, my troubled mind was drawn back to the physical world by a shift in the acoustic ecology of the night. Samuel, bringing a written message from my mistress, remarked on a change in the weather. Now my attention was roused, I heard the uneasy whimpering of the hounds in the kennels, and the wind moaning low—a mournful, barometric frequency that vibrated deep in my bones. Looking up, I saw the rack of clouds hurrying faster and faster over a watery moon. The circular rhythm of the seasons was turning violent; wild weather was coming. 

The message informed me that the magistrate at Frizinghall intended to release the three Indians early in the coming week. If we had questions, there was no time to lose. I returned to my room, where the air was now thick with the scent of hot grog and peat smoke. Sergeant Cuff and Begbie were head over ears in an argument on whether the white moss rose required to be budded on the dog-rose to make it grow well. 

“Gentlemen,” I remarked, stepping into the temporary lull of their agricultural debate, “there is much to be said on both sides.” 

I laid my lady’s message under Cuff’s eyes. In half a minute, his mind had processed the text, translating our local crisis into his bureaucratic filing system. He recalled a certain traveler from Superintendent Seegrave’s report who understood the Indians and their language, and asked for the gentleman's address to seek him out in Frizinghall tomorrow. He intended to see if Seegrave was as wrong about the Indians as he was about everything else. With that, Cuff returned precisely to his debate on soils and seasons with the gardener.

Out in the passage, I found Penelope hanging about. Further inquiry revealed that Miss Rachel, enraged by the delay of her departure, had declared the house unendurable. She could no longer bear the odious presence of a policeman under the same roof. My girl was in wretchedly low spirits. “I feel as if some dreadful misfortune was hanging over us all,” she whispered, her voice a fragile thread in the gloom.

Just as I approached the swing-door leading into the hall, it was violently opened. Rosanna Spearman ran by me. Her face was a portrait of miserable pain, one hand pressed hard over her heart. 

“What’s the matter, my girl?” I asked. “Are you ill?”

“For God’s sake, don’t speak to me,” she answered, twisting out of my hands. It was a tragic refusal of language—a stark acknowledgment of the inherent untranslatability of her subjective agony. She fled toward the servants’ staircase.

Sergeant Cuff darted softly out of my room, asking what the matter was. I answered, “Nothing.” On the other side of the hall, Mr. Franklin pulled open the door to the billiard-room and beckoned me in. He inquired if I had seen Rosanna. 

“I am afraid I am innocently the cause of that disturbance,” Mr. Franklin said. “If the girl *is* concerned in the loss of the Diamond, I do really believe she was on the point of confessing everything—to me, of all people—not two minutes since.”

Through the open door, I caught a glimpse of Sergeant Cuff’s black coat disappearing around the corner. Knowing the Sergeant was employing his underground ways, I closed the door and asked Mr. Franklin what had happened. 

Mr. Franklin picked up his cue. The sharp, dissonant *clack-clack* of the ivory billiard balls echoed against the wood-paneled walls—a harsh, commercial noise that violently disrupted the organic acoustic harmony of the house. 

“I was knocking the balls about,” he said, the percussive sound punctuating his words, “and there stood Rosanna Spearman. I asked her if she wished to speak to me. She answered, ‘Yes, if I dare.’ I didn’t wish to invite her confidence. I said, ‘I don’t quite understand you.’ The poor girl can’t help being ugly. I kept knocking the balls about to take off the awkwardness. She suddenly turned away. ‘He looks at the billiard balls,’ I heard her say. ‘Anything rather than look at *me!*’” 

The acoustic violence of his indifference had drowned out her fragile attempt at connection. Mr. Franklin asked me to tell her he meant no unkindness, admitting he had almost hoped the loss of the Diamond might be traced to her. It was a cold, utilitarian logic. I knew what he left unspoken: only tracing the gem to our second housemaid could save Miss Rachel from the infamous suspicion now resting upon her in the Sergeant’s mind. 

I checked the servants’ offices; Rosanna had retired to her room and declined all sustenance and assistance, seeking only silence. 

I reported this to Mr. Franklin, who went up to bed. As I was making the windows fast against the rising gale, Samuel informed me the gardener had gone home and the Sergeant was nowhere to be found downstairs. My sitting-room held only the ghosts of the evening: two empty tumblers and the lingering, heavy olfactory ghost of hot lemon, sugar, and whiskey.

I went upstairs. Reaching the second landing, my ears, attuned to the micro-frequencies of our household, caught a sound on my left—a sound of quiet, regular breathing. 

There, coiled up on three chairs placed right across the corridor leading to Miss Rachel’s room, lay Sergeant Cuff. He had tied a red handkerchief around his grizzled head and rolled his coat for a pillow. He woke instantly and quietly, like a dog.

“What are you doing here?” I asked.

“I am not in my proper bed,” he answered in a low murmur, “because there was a coincidence this evening between Rosanna Spearman’s return and Miss Verinder’s resolution to leave. Whatever Rosanna has hidden, your young lady couldn’t go away until she knew it *was* hidden. The two must have communicated their private dialect once already tonight. If they try to communicate again, I want to be in the way. Don’t blame me, Mr. Betteredge—blame the Diamond.”

“I wish to God the Diamond had never found its way into this house!” I broke out, mourning the erosion of our peace, the severing of our shared history by this cursed stone.

Sergeant Cuff looked with a rueful face at the three chairs on which he had condemned himself to pass the night. “So do I,” he said, gravely.
