Returning from the hospital, an hour later than usual, Romola chose a path that circled widely toward the river, reaching the banks far from the Ponte Vecchio. Her intention was to hurry to San Stefano and seek Baldassarre. Though she dreaded hearing more of his condition, she felt a strange reluctance to leave him behind, as if doing so meant abandoning a vital claim upon her soul.

As she neared the junction where the Por' Santa Maria curved to her right and the Ponte Vecchio lay to her left, she found herself engulfed in a sudden throng of kneeling figures. Without hesitation, she joined them. The Great Cross of the Duomo was passing, leading a solemn procession. Being late, she waited until the massive timber had cleared before rising. The transition from kneeling to standing, with nothing to occupy her mind but the scene before her, made her acutely aware of her exhaustion.

A shopkeeper beside her spoke kindly, "Madonna Romola, you must be weary. Gian Fantoni would be delighted to offer you a seat in his house; his door is right there. Let me open it for you. He loves God and our Brother as we do. His home is yours."

Romola was used to such fraternal greetings from citizens whose faces she knew well from the crowds filling the cathedral. To her, the concept of "home" had shifted away from the lonely solitude of the Via de' Bardi. Instead, it now encompassed the entire towered circuit of Florence, a city where every street turn seemed to greet her with looks of appeal or friendship. Gladly accepting the invitation, she stepped through the open door, guided by the hose-seller to an upstairs window. There, a sturdy woman, dressed plainly among her three children in the garb of the Piagnoni, received her with deep reverence, seating her above bright hanging draperies.

From this vantage point, Romola watched the procession pour slowly between the lines of houses on the Ponte Vecchio, alongside the view of the river and the Lung' Arno stretching toward the bridge of Santa Trinita. Silence and sorrow characterized the march. No wailing chants broke the air; there was only the rhythmic tramp of footsteps and the soft sweep of woolen robes.

First came a long train of Florentine youth, their faces fresh and beardless, bearing high in the center the white image of the youthful Jesus, crowned with golden glory, standing beside the tall cross where thorns and nails awaited. Following them were the mysterious Companies of Discipline, bound by secret vows of self-chastisement and devout praise. Dressed in garments that covered their heads and faces entirely, leaving only their eyes exposed, they had shed their individual identities as sons, husbands, or fathers. They walked merely as symbols of a shared vow, each company distinguished by color and badge, yet united in a shrouded uniformity that expressed only fellowship.

In contrast, the multitude of monks appeared as distinct individuals despite their common tonsures and frocks. First flowed a white stream of reformed Benedictines, followed by the much longer column of the Frati Minori (Franciscans), clad in grey with knotted cords around their waists and some wearing wooden *zoccoli* sandals beneath bare feet. Perhaps the most numerous order in Florence, they were known for their zeal, their love of mankind, and their animosity toward the Dominicans. After the grey came the black of the Augustinians of San Spirito, men of cultivated countenances who had inherited Boccaccio's library and formed Florence's most learned circle during an age when knowledge was rare. Next appeared the white-over-dark habit of the Carmelites, followed by the unmixed black of the Servites, a famous Florentine order founded by seven merchants who renounced their wealth to adore the Divine Mother.

The hearts of the onlookers quickened, beating with a mixture of hatred and love, as a stream of black mantles over white scapularies crossed the bridge—the Dominicans. Those from Fiesole led the way. One black mantle parted by another, one tonsured head following another, suspense hung heavy in the air. Their mantles were coarse, many threadbare or ragged, for the Prior of San Marco had reduced the fraternities under his rule to the strictest poverty and discipline. Yet, amidst the worn line, one mantle stood out slightly less tattered, topped by a tonsured head that might seem unremarkable to a stranger unless they recognized it from bronze medals depicting the sword of God, or remembered it surrounded by an armed guard en route to the Duomo, or transformed by the inner fire of an orator addressing a rapt multitude.

As Savonarola's approach became clear, no one dared break the silence with a sound louder than the solemn tramp of feet. Nevertheless, his ear, like others, caught a mingled hiss that longed to become curses, interwoven with murmurs longing to be blessings. Perhaps it was the dominance of the hissing that caused two or three of his disciples in the foreground to fall to their knees as if something divine were passing. This movement of silent homage spread like a subtle shock along the street sides, leaving some unmoved while compelling others to bend their knees and lower their heads. Yet, hatred too gathered intensity; as Savonarola ascended the Por' Santa Maria, Romola saw someone at an upper window spit upon him.

Monks returned—the Frati Umiliati, or Humbled Brethren, from Ognissanti, heirs to a glorious tradition as early workers in the wool trade. Then came more monks, Vallombrosan and various Benedictines, whose careful forms and colors reminded the observant eye that reformers had long since arisen to mark a spiritual change through altered habits. Finally, the shaven crowns ended, giving way to the train of untonsured secular priests.

Following them marched the twenty-one incorporated Arts of Florence in long array, their banners floating proudly above them, declaring their distinct functions from bakers to judges and notaries. Then came the secondary officers of state, ascending from lesser to greater rank, until the line of seculars was broken by the Canons of the Duomo. They carried a sacred relic: the very head of San Zenobio, the immortal bishop of Florence, enclosed in silver, whose virtues were believed to have saved the city a millennium prior. This was the nucleus of the procession.

Behind the relic walked the Archbishop in a gorgeous cope, with a canopy held high above him. Following him came the mysterious, hidden Image. First concealed by rich brocade curtains enclosing an outer painted tabernacle, and within that, by an ancient inner shrine never opened in living memory—or perhaps in the memory of the fathers themselves. Inside rested the image of the Pitying Mother, discovered ages ago in the soil of L'Impruneta, crying out as the spade struck it.

Hitherto, this unseen Image had rarely entered the Duomo without rich gifts borne before it. Lists of precious offerings from emulous men and communities—veils, curtains, mantles—were usually recited. It was said the richest gift came from a poor abbess and her nuns, who, lacking funds, wove a mantle of gold brocade with their prayers, embroidered it with devotion, and saw their work presented to the Blessed Virgin in the great Piazza by two beautiful youths who spread white wings and vanished into the blue sky.

But today, no gifts preceded the tabernacle. No donations were offered except to the poor. This was the counsel of Fra Girolamo, whose preaching never urged gifts to invisible powers but focused solely on aid for visible needs. Altars had been raised at various church entrances where oblations for the destitute were placed. Not even a torch was carried. Surely the hidden Mother cared less for torches and brocade than for the wails of the hungry. Florence was in extremity; she had done her utmost and could only wait for a divine intervention beyond her own power.

The friar in the torn mantle had promised help would surely come, and many of the faint-hearted clung to his word rather than the virtues of the unseen Image. Conversely, not a few fierce-hearted souls rejoiced secretly, hoping the friar's promise might prove false.

Slowly the tabernacle moved forward, and knees bent once more. Profound stillness reigned, for the train of priests and chaplains from L'Impruneta stirred no passion in the crowd. The procession was nearing its close with the Priors and the Gonfaloniere. The long train of symbolic companies, possessing a silent music that stirred the mind like a chorus, was fading from sight. Now, a faint yearning hope struggled against the accustomed despondency.

Romola, whose heart had swelled with half-fear and half-the enthusiastic fellowship born of the last two years, let out a deep sigh as if releasing a long-held mental tension. She remained on her knees, overcome by languor. Suddenly, something brightly colored flashed between the houses onto the distant bridge.

In an instant, Romola sprang up, stretching her arms and leaning from the window. Black drapery fell from her head, and the golden gleam of her hair and the flush on her cheeks seemed illuminated by a single burst of light. A shout erupted simultaneously. The last troops of the procession paused, all faces turning toward the distant bridge. But the bridge was already passed. A horseman pressed forward at full gallop along the Arno; the flanks of his bay horse, streaked with foam, appeared white from the speed of his passage. His cap flew loose from his red *becchetto*, and he waved an olive branch in his hand.
