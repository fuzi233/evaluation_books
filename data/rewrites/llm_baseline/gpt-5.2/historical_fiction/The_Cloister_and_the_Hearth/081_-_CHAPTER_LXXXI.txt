“Eli,” said Catherine, laying down her distaff as if it were a ladle and she were about to season a pot, “answer me one question like a man, and I’ll ask no more to-day. What is wormwood?”

Eli, caught as a cook is caught when asked the name of a spice he has only ever tasted in another man’s broth, stared a moment, his honest face blank with the effort. Then he rallied.

“It is summat as tasteth main bitter.”

“That is a fair answer, my man,” said Catherine, “but not the one I look for.”

“Then answer it thyself,” said Eli, a little nettled.

“And shall,” replied she; and her voice, for all its homeliness, had the sharp clean ring of a knife on a board. “Wormwood is—to have two in the house a-doing nought, but waiting for thy shoes and mine.”

Eli groaned, as if she had thrust the bitter herb beneath his tongue.

The hearth snapped softly; the kettle sang in its thin, patient way; outside, the town had its own tide of sound—wooden soles on stone, a far cry from the quay, the clink of harness. Yet within, the quiet had grown heavy, a silence that was not peace but stoppage, like dough left too long without kneading. Catherine had heard it for days: the idle cough, the restless shifting, the long stretches where no work’s rhythm saved a man from his own thoughts.

“Methinks,” she went on, not cruelly, but with that practical pity that turns to action, “waiting for their best friend’s coffin, that and nothing to do, are enow to make them worse than Nature meant. Why not set them up somewhere, to give ’em a chance?”

Eli was willing—he always was, when Catherine’s mind was made up—but fear clung to him.

“They’ll drink and gamble their very shelves away,” said he, imagining barrels tapped and coins rattled away, the shop stripped bare as a larder after a feast-day.

“Nay,” said Catherine, “dost take me for a simpleton? Of course I mean to watch them at starting, and drive them wi’ a loose rein, as the saying is.”

“Where didst think of?” asked Eli. “Not here; to divide our own custom.”

“Not likely.” Catherine’s eyes brightened, quick as flame takes a new brand. “I say Rotterdam against the world. Then I could start them.”

She spoke it as if the word itself—Rotterdam—had a taste: salt air, tar, fish-skins, rye-bread, foreign tongues in the market, and money changing hands with a sound like dry beans poured from a sack. She called it prudence; and perhaps it was. Yet somewhere beneath, like a bay-leaf hidden in stew, lay the truer savor of her resolve: to be nearer little Gerard.

So there were many talks, and many promises. Cornelis and Sybrandt—men with hands made for work and mouths too easily tempted—vowed amendment upon these terms, swearing it with more heat than steadiness. Catherine listened as she might listen to a cook promising cleanliness: approving, but already planning how often she must look in on him, how sharply she must smell his pans.

At last she went to Rotterdam shop-hunting, and took Kate with her for a change. The journey had its own music: wheels grumbling over ruts, the whisper of skirts, the occasional bell, and then, nearer the city, the thicker chorus—water lapping against timber, rope squealing through pulleys, gulls crying, and every voice wearing its own dialect like a cap. To Catherine, tongues were not mere words; they were a people’s memory in the mouth. In Rotterdam, the speech ran faster, clipped and traded like herring; at times Kate had to lean close, catching meaning by tone when the vowels would not sit still. There are things one cannot translate—grief, longing, the exact comfort of one’s mother-sounds—and Kate, weary and pale, felt that keenly.

They soon found a shop in a good street; but it was sadly out of order. The place smelt of old dust and damp wood, as if it had once held spices and now held only their ghosts. Shelves sagged; corners were grey with neglect; the back room had the sour breath of a house where fires have gone out too often.

However, they got it cheaper for that, and Catherine, who could not abide a disorderly kitchen nor a disorderly shop, set to work at once. Brush in hand, she raised little storms of dust; she sent for boards and had proper shelves fitted, stout and square, to bear honest wares; she aired the dwelling-house until the stale air fled and the rooms began to smell again like sun on wood. Kate, though frail, sat where she could, sewing, sorting, making small things tidy—those quiet labors that are half housekeeping, half prayer.

Meanwhile Luke Peterson was always asking Margaret what he could do for her. He had the look of a man who would gladly fetch water till his back broke, if only it might be for the one face he watched.

The answer used to come, ever in the same sad tone, as if nothing in the world were worth the asking: “Nothing, Luke, nothing.”

“What,” said he, amazed and wounded by emptiness, “you that are so clever, can you think of nothing for me to do for you?”

“Nothing, Luke, nothing.”

But at last—whether the sight of Kate’s weakness pricked her, or whether Margaret’s own heart, tired of being entreated, sought any outlet—she changed her reply.

“If you could make something to help my sweet sister Kate about.”

The slave of love consented as joyfully as if she had bid him build a chapel. He went to it with hands and mind both eager. He chose wood that would not splinter, planed it smooth, fitted the joints close, and made Kate a little cart; then he cushioned it so that her bones should not ache the more for going abroad. And when it was done, he yoked himself into it as a horse is yoked, and at eventide drew her out of the town and along the pleasant boulevard, with Margaret and Catherine walking beside.

Evening had its own solace: wind moving through leaves like a soft broom, distant church-bells rounding the air, and the fading clatter of trade as stalls were shut and pots began to simmer in their homes. It looked a happier party than it was, because grief can walk out in fresh air and still keep step with you.

Kate, for one, enjoyed it keenly; for little Gerard was put in her lap, and she doted on him. His breath had the warm sweet-milk smell of infants; his fingers grasped at her sleeve; his small noises were like birds trying their first notes. In that cart, he seemed a cherub carried by a little angel, or a rosebud lying in the cup of a lily.

So the vulgar jeered; for there are always men who must spoil music with a coarse shout. They called after Luke, asking him how a thistle tasted, and whether his mistress could not afford one with four legs, and other such sour wit. Their laughter rang harsh, a cheap noise that scraped the ear and left a grime behind, like smoke from bad fuel.

Luke did not mind these jeers; they struck his back and slid off, for his mind was fixed on Margaret’s smallest wish as on a star. But Kate minded them for him; she heard every barbed syllable, and each one hurt her as if it had been flung at the child in her arms.

“Thou hast made the cart for me, good Luke,” said she gently, “’twas much. I did ill to let thee draw me too; we can afford to pay some poor soul for that. I love my rides, and to carry little Gerard; but I’d liever ride no more than thou be mocked for’t.”

“Much I care for their tongues,” said Luke, his jaw set. “If I did care I’d knock their heads together. I shall draw you till my mistress says give over.”

“Luke,” said Margaret, and there was quiet authority in her voice, “if you obey Kate, you will oblige me.”

“Then I will obey Kate,” said he at once; for her word was law to him, and Kate’s mercy became his obedience.

An honorable exception to the street’s cruelty was Jorian Ketel’s wife, Joan. She spoke not loudly, but with that steadiness that can change the whole sound of a moment. Watching Luke’s straining shoulders and Kate’s white face, she said, “That is strength well laid out, to draw the weak. And her prayers will be your guerdon; she is not long for this world; she smileth in pain.”

Luke, simple-hearted as bread, answered bluntly that he did not want the poor lass’s prayers; he did it to please his mistress, Margaret. In his mouth the word mistress carried no gaudy savor of power, but something quieter and more binding: the one voice he lived to hear approve him.

After that Luke often pressed Margaret to give him something to do—without success. Day by day he asked; day by day she fenced him off with “Nothing,” as if work were scarce as saffron and she dared not waste a thread of it.

But one day, as if tired of his importuning, she turned on him; and in her face there was a look, and in her voice an accent, that no plain telling can fully carry—like the difference between reading a recipe and tasting the dish.

“Find me,” said she, each word falling as if it had been weighed like spice, “my boy’s father.”
