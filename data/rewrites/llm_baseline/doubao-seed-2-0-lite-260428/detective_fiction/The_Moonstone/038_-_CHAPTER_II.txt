“Betteredge!” I said, nodding straight at the tattered, page-curled copy of *Robinson Crusoe* resting on his knee, the same well-worn volume he’d carried through half his life, “did Crusoe himself warn you this evening to expect a visit from Franklin Blake?”

“By the Lord Harry, Mr. Franklin!” the old man cried, scrambling to his feet with my help, “that’s exactly what he did!” He swayed for a moment, glancing back and forth between his beloved book and my face, clearly unable to decide which of the two had surprised him more. The verdict fell in the book’s favour: he held it open in both hands, staring at the pages with a look of unutterable, giddy anticipation, as if he half expected Robinson Crusoe himself to step out of the print and shake our hands.

“Here’s the exact line, sir!” he blurted once he’d recovered his wits. “As I live by bread, it was right under my nose the second you knocked! Page one hundred and fifty-six, word for word: ‘I stood like one Thunderstruck, or as if I had seen an Apparition.’ If that isn’t a prophecy of your sudden turning up, there’s no sense left in the English language!” He slammed the covers shut hard enough to puff a cloud of old dust off the top edge, freed one hand at last to clasp mine warmly.

Naturally, I expected him to flood me with questions at once. But no—hospitality won out first, as it always did for the old servant the moment any member of the Verinder household turned up at the door, no matter the circumstances.

“Walk in, Mr. Franklin,” he said, stepping back with that quaint, old-fashioned bow of his. “I’ll ask what brings you later—first we’ll get you comfortable. There’s been sad changes since you left, you know. The manor’s shut up, all the other servants are gone. Never you mind that! I’ll cook your dinner myself, the gardener’s wife will make up a bed for you, and if there’s a bottle of that famous aged Latour claret left in the cellar, it’s going straight down your throat tonight. You’re heartily welcome, sir—welcome home!” The old fellow fought valiantly against the hush and gloom of the empty, shuttered house, greeting me with all the warm, easy courtesy he’d extended to me as a boy.

It grieved me to disappoint him. But this was Rachel’s house now. After the bitter, unexplainable quarrel that had driven us apart in London, no amount of affection for Betteredge could make me cross that threshold. My self-respect forbade it, plain and simple. I took him by the arm and led him out into the overgrown garden, where I had no choice but to lay the full truth out before him. Torn between his loyalty to Rachel and his fondness for me, he fidgeted and frowned for a long minute, before delivering his verdict in his usual unvarnished style, steeped in that no-nonsense “Betteredge school” of positive philosophy he was so famous for.

“Miss Rachel has her faults, I’ll never deny that,” he began. “Riding the high horse every now and then is one of ’em. She’s been trying to ride roughshod over you, and you’ve put up with it. Lord, Mr. Franklin, you ought to know women better than that by now! You’ve heard me talk of my late wife, haven’t you?”

I’d heard him invoke the late Mrs. Betteredge dozens of times, holding her up as living proof of the inherent perversity of the female sex, and he called on her example now.

“Very well, then. Every woman’s got her own way of climbing on that high horse. My missus would mount hers every time I turned down something she’d set her heart on. Sure as I came home from work, she’d holler down the kitchen stairs that my brutal treatment had left her too broken to cook my dinner. I put up with it for a while, just like you’re putting up with Miss Rachel now. At last my patience ran out. I marched upstairs, picked her up gentle, carried her holus-bolus straight into the best parlour where she received her visitors, and said ‘That’s the proper place for you, my dear.’ Then I went back down, locked myself in the kitchen, took off my coat, rolled up my sleeves, and cooked my own dinner. Ate every bite of it with a fine appetite, had my pipe and my glass of grog afterwards, scrubbed every pot and pan, polished the knives, swept the hearth until the whole kitchen was brighter and cleaner than she’d ever made it. Then I unlocked the door and let her in. ‘I’ve had my dinner, my dear,’ I said, ‘and I hope you find I’ve left the kitchen exactly how you like it.’ For the rest of her life, Mr. Franklin, I never had to cook a single meal again! Moral of the story: you put up with Miss Rachel’s nonsense in London, don’t put up with it here in Yorkshire. Come back to the house with me!”

It was quite unanswerable. I could only tell my dear old friend that even his formidable powers of persuasion could not move me that day.

“It’s a lovely evening,” I said. “I’ll walk to Frizinghall and stay at the inn there, and you must come and breakfast with me tomorrow morning. I’ve something very important to tell you.”

Betteredge shook his head gravely. “I’m right sorry to hear that. I’d hoped you’d come back to find things smooth and pleasant between you and Miss Rachel again. But if you’re set on your own way, sir, there’s no need to trudge all the way to Frizinghall for a bed. You can get lodgings far closer. Hotherstone’s Farm is barely two miles off, down the valley. You can’t object to staying there on Miss Rachel’s account, now can you?” he added with a little sly grin. “Hotherstone lives on land he owns free and clear, no ties to the manor at all.”

I remembered the place the second he named it: a cosy farmhouse tucked beside the prettiest trout stream in that part of Yorkshire, with spare rooms the farmer and his wife let out to artists, anglers, and passing tourists. It was far more pleasant a spot to stay for the coming weeks than any Frizinghall inn.

“Are the rooms free?” I asked.

“Mrs. Hotherstone herself begged me just yesterday to recommend her lodgings to any visitors passing this way.”

“Then I’ll take them, Betteredge, gladly.”

We walked back to the yard where I’d left my travelling bag. Betteredge slipped a stick through its handle, heaved it over his shoulder, and fell back into the bewildered daze my arrival had stirred in him earlier. He stared disbelievingly at the shuttered manor house, then swivelled around to stare at me even more disbelievingly.

“I’ve lived a long while in this world,” the dearest of all old servants grumbled, “but I never thought I’d live to see this sight. There stands the Verinder house, here stands Mr. Franklin Blake—and, damme if the pair of them aren’t turning their backs on each other, and you’re going to sleep in a farmer’s lodgings!” He led the way out of the yard, shaking his head and growling playfully. “There’s only one miracle left that can top this. Next thing I know, Mr. Franklin, you’ll be paying me back that seven-and-sixpence you borrowed from me when you were a boy to buy a fireworks show.”

That little jab put him in far better humour. We passed through the lodge gates, and the moment we were clear of the manor grounds, Betteredge’s duties of hospitality fell away, and his long-buried curiosity burst to the surface. He slowed his step to walk level with mine.

“Fine evening for a stroll, Mr. Franklin,” he said, as if we’d just accidentally bumped into each other on the lane. “If you’d gone to that Frizinghall inn, I’d have had the honour of breakfasting with you tomorrow, wouldn’t I?”

“Of course you would. You can still come and breakfast with me at Hotherstone’s Farm instead.”

“Much obliged, sir. But it wasn’t exactly breakfast I was after. You said you had something important to say to me? If it’s no secret,” he said, dropping all pretense at once, “I’m burning to know what’s brought you rushing down here all of a sudden like this.”

“What brought me here before, years back?” I asked.

“The Moonstone, Mr. Franklin. But what brings you now?”

“The Moonstone again, Betteredge.”

The old man stopped dead in his tracks, staring at me through the gathering grey twilight as if he could not trust his own ears. “If that’s a joke, sir, I’m getting too dull in my old age to see the funny side of it.”

“It’s no joke,” I said. “I’ve come back to pick up the investigation that was dropped when I left England. I’m here to do what no one has done yet—find out who stole the Diamond.”

“Leave that cursed jewel be, Mr. Franklin! Take my advice, and let it rest. That accursed Indian stone has led every soul who got near it astray. Don’t waste your youth, your money, and your temper meddling with the Moonstone. How do you expect to succeed, saving your presence, when Sergeant Cuff himself made a total mess of the case? Sergeant Cuff!” he repeated, shaking his forefinger at me sternly. “The greatest police detective in all of England!”

“My mind is made up. Even Sergeant Cuff’s reputation won’t daunt me. By the by, do you know where he is these days? I may need to speak to him before long.”

“The Sergeant can’t help you, Mr. Franklin.”

“Why not?”

“Something happened in police circles right after you left England. The great Cuff retired. He’s got a tiny cottage down at Dorking now, and he’s up to his eyebrows growing roses. I had a letter in his own hand last month saying he’d managed to breed a white moss rose without budding it on dog rose stock first. Old Mr. Begbie, the head gardener up at the manor, is going to travel down there any day now and admit Cuff finally beat him at his own game.”

“It doesn’t matter much,” I said. “I’ll do without Sergeant Cuff. I’m relying on you to help me get started.”

I must have spoken a little carelessly, because Betteredge looked distinctly piqued. “You could do a lot worse than rely on me, sir, I can tell you that.” The sharpness in his tone, and the faint, unmistakeable restlessness that followed it, told me he was holding back some secret he was hesitant to share.

“I expect you to help me sift through all the fragments of evidence Sergeant Cuff left behind,” I said. “I know you can do that. Can’t you do more than that?”

“What more could you possibly ask of me, sir?” he asked, feigning perfect humility.

“I expect more than that, from the way you just spoke.”

“Mere boasting, Mr. Franklin,” the old man insisted stubbornly. “Some folk are born boasters, and they never grow out of it till their dying day. I’m one of them.”

There was only one way to move him: I appealed to his loyalty to Rachel, and to his long fondness for me.

“Betteredge, would you be glad to hear that Rachel and I were friends again, no hard feelings between us?”

“I’ve served this family my whole life, sir, if you need to ask that you’ve got no sense at all!”

“You remember how Rachel treated me just before I left England? No one, not even her own mother, could work out why she was so mortally angry with me for trying to get her diamond back.”

“As well as if it was yesterday!”

“Exactly. And when I came back from travelling just last week, I found she was still that angry. She won’t let me see her. She won’t answer any of my letters. The only possible way I can clear this whole mess up, the only chance Rachel has left me to make things right, is to solve the mystery of the Moonstone once and for all.”

The words shook him. He asked a question that proved his last defenses were crumbling. “You’re not doing this out of spite, are you, Mr. Franklin?”

“I was angry when I left London, yes,” I said. “But that’s all gone now. All I want is for Rachel to talk to me, to clear up whatever secret she’s been hiding.”

“And you’re not afraid, sir—if you dig up the truth, that you’ll find out something about Miss Rachel that will change how you see her?”

I understood that fierce, protective loyalty to his young mistress that made him ask that. “I trust her as completely as you do. Whatever her secret is, it can never make me think less of her.”

All of Betteredge’s remaining scruples vanished. “If helping you is wrong, Mr. Franklin,” he exclaimed, “I’m as innocent of seeing that as an unborn babe! I can put you right on the path to solving this, if you’ll go the rest of the way yourself. You remember that poor girl we had working at the manor, Rosanna Spearman?”

“Of course I do.”

“You always thought she had some kind of confession about the Moonstone that she was dying to tell you, didn’t you?”

“I could never explain her strange behaviour any other way.”

“You can set that doubt to rest right now, Mr. Franklin.”

I stopped dead in my tracks, squinting to make out his face in the dark. “What do you mean?”

“Steady, sir! Rosanna Spearman left a sealed letter behind her, addressed straight to you, and no one else.”

“Where is it?”

“With that friend of hers who lives down at Cobb’s Hole. You must have heard of Limping Lucy, the fisherman’s daughter who walks with a crutch?”

“Aye, the one with the bad leg.”

“That’s her. She refused to hand that letter over to anyone but you. You’d already left England before I could even write to tell you about it.”

“Come on then, Betteredge—let’s go get it right now!”

“Too late tonight, sir. All the folk down on the coast hoard their candles, and they all turn in at sundown. You’ll knock on Lucy’s door and no one will answer.”

“Nonsense! We can be there in half an hour.”

“You might, sir. And you’d still find the door bolted fast.” He pointed to a warm, glimmering light glowing below us in the valley, and at that exact moment I heard the soft bubbling of the trout stream through the still evening air. “There’s Hotherstone’s Farm, right there. Make yourself comfortable tonight, come and fetch me tomorrow morning, and we’ll head down to Cobb’s Hole together—first thing, whenever you like.”

We headed down the winding path towards the light, the murmur of the stream growing louder at our feet.
