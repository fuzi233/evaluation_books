A Catherine in a strange house is both blessing and tempest. Her presence brightens the hearth, sets the windows gleaming, and breathes life into every corner—yet she is a force unstoppable, a whirlwind of order that spares neither chair nor soul.

When Margaret’s time drew near, Catherine marshaled her forces with quiet cunning. She turned her gaze upon Denys and Martin, those good-natured giants, and with a smile as sweet as it was treacherous, she suggested—nay, insisted—that a lengthy stroll might serve them well. "A very lengthy stroll," she added, eyes sharp as needles. "Perhaps into next week."

The men, slow to take a hint but quick to flee one, soon learned the art of vanishing at dawn and creeping home at dusk, tiptoeing like thieves beneath a tyrant’s roof. Even then, their mere presence seemed to grate upon Catherine’s nerves. Was it some primal instinct that bristled at the sight of warriors in a house of cradles? Whatever the cause, her tongue grew sharper than any sword, and before long, the kitchen stood emptied of its heroes.

Martin, too stiff for flight, had retreated only as far as the street, where he perched on a stool like a displaced monarch, sunning his grievances. Margaret, descending at last, found no Denys beside him.

"Gone?" she echoed, her violet eyes wide with hurt. "Without a word?"

Catherine scrubbed a bowl with sudden vigor. "He left while you slept. No sense in waking you."

A tear trembled on Margaret’s lashes. "Poor Denys."

Catherine’s conscience pricked her then. Without another word, she marched outside, softening her voice to honey. "Come in, Martin. The sun’s too harsh for your old bones."

But Martin, stubborn as oak, shook his head. "I’ve grown used to the air. No meddling out here."

Catherine’s sweetness curdled. "Oh, you cross old wretch!" she shrieked, flouncing back inside, where she soothed her ruffled spirit with the sovereign remedy of all maternal grievances—she washed the baby.

Margaret hovered, restless, dipping a tentative finger into the water—as if testing fate itself. Then her gaze caught on the child’s tiny hand. "Look!" she breathed. "His finger—just like *his*."

Catherine peered. A mole, no bigger than a freckle, marked the infant’s skin. Her breath caught. "Nature’s a meddler," she muttered. "Strong as sin and twice as sly. You never noticed it on *him*?"

Margaret shook her head, eyes shining. "But now I’ll kiss it every morn and night, till the one who bears its twin comes home to us." And she swept the babe into her arms, smothering him in kisses—a wolf devouring her lamb in love.

Catherine watched, her stern face softening. She had seen such fierce tenderness before.

So passed Margaret’s days—a quiet rhythm of motherhood, each moment stitched with joy. While far away, in the icy silence of a cloister, a man who had once been her Gerard moved like a ghost among the stones, hands folded, eyes downcast.

Two lives, two worlds: on one side, the towering Alps—harsh, sublime, piercing heaven. On the other, the gentle slopes of home, green and warm and humming with life. And between them? Only a page. Only the width of a single breath.
