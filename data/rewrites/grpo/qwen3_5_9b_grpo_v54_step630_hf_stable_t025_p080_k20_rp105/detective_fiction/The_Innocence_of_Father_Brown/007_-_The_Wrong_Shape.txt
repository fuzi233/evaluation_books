The roads stretching north from London often lose their urban rhythm, becoming attenuated spectres of streets—long lines punctuated by gaps of fields, public houses, and market gardens. Along one such thoroughfare stands a house that seems to beckon the passerby with a silent, inexplicable allure. It is a long, low structure painted in white and pale green, featuring a veranda, sun-blinds, and porches topped with quaint, umbrella-like cupolas. It feels distinctly English, yet strangely suited to a tropical climate, evoking images of puggarees and palm trees, as if designed by an Anglo-Indian architect.

On the Thursday before Whit-Sunday, at half-past four, this house became the stage for a tragedy. Father Brown emerged from the front door, smoking a large pipe, accompanied by his tall French friend, Flambeau, who clutched a tiny cigarette. They were not the only figures present, however. The house itself was peculiar, shaped like a T with a long cross-piece facing the street and a short tail extending backward. The front housed the main rooms, while the rear tail contained the study of Leonard Quinton, a poet obsessed with wild Oriental imagery, and a glass conservatory bursting with monstrous, vibrant blooms.

Quinton, a man who drank and bathed in color, had arranged his home to reflect his chaotic genius. He wrote of burning gold skies and heroes riding purple elephants, a morbid talent that mirrored his own frail health and addiction to opium. His wife, a hardworking woman, tolerated his eccentricities, including the presence of a live Indian hermit in white robes who served as a spiritual guide.

Father Brown and Flambeau stepped onto the porch with visible relief, escaping the stifling atmosphere inside. Before they could settle, a disheveled young man named Atkinson tumbled up the steps, breathless and eager to see Quinton. He was denied entry by Dr. Harris, the family physician, who claimed Quinton was under a sleeping draught. In truth, Harris was merely locking Atkinson out to prevent him from borrowing money he would never repay.

The group moved around the curved end of the conservatory, where the glass offered a clear view into the interior. Inside, Quinton lay on an ottoman amidst blood-red poinsettias, appearing half-asleep. Standing motionless outside the glass was a tall man draped in faultless white, his bronze skin gleaming in the setting sun. He was the Indian hermit. When Father Brown approached, the man bowed slowly, his yellow eyelids sealed as if in deep sleep. He spoke three times in perfect English, "I want nothing," before vanishing into the twilight.

Later, Mrs. Quinton appeared, escorting the group back toward the house. As they neared the front door, the white-robed figure appeared a third time, seemingly materializing from the locked study. Harris, dismissing the impossibility, locked the door again to keep Atkinson out. Moments later, Atkinson managed to slip inside, only to be met by Quinton's voice calling from the study. A half-sovereign coin flew through the crack, settling in Atkinson's hand, confirming the poet was alive.

Harris led the group back to the garden, leaving Atkinson to jingle his coin and the Indian to sit rigidly on a grassy bank. As the storm began to gather, Father Brown noted the Indian's rhythmic swaying, a hypnotic motion that unsettled him. Suddenly, Harris rushed forward, accusing Atkinson of foul play. They entered the study to find Quinton dead, his head hanging over the ottoman, the strange Oriental dagger driven into his left side.

Scattered on the table was a single sheet of paper bearing the words: "I die by my own hand; yet I die murdered!" Father Brown examined the note closely. It was written in Quinton's distinctive, difficult-to-read hand, but the paper itself was irregular—a square with one corner snipped off. Harris dismissed this as a quirk of Quinton's, noting that he often cut his paper this way. However, Father Brown counted the remnants: twenty-three sheets had been cut, but only twenty-two corners remained. One piece was missing.

Realizing the significance, Father Brown deduced that the missing corner likely contained a crucial detail, perhaps quotation marks or a denial, that would have exonerated Quinton. The shape of the paper was the "wrong shape," a deliberate alteration to frame the death as suicide.

As the police arrived, Father Brown confronted Harris with a letter the doctor had secretly written to him. In it, Harris confessed to the entire crime. He had been infatuated with Quinton's wife and believed he was acting for her happiness. He had staged the suicide by forging the note, removing the incriminating quotation marks, and snipping the paper to match Quinton's habits. He had waited for Father Brown to examine the false confession before striking the fatal blow. Overcome by a sudden, unnatural guilt, Harris fled the scene, leaving Father Brown to face the consequences of a murder disguised as a tragedy.
