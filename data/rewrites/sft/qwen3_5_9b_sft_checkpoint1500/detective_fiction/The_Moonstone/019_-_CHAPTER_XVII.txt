Not a single event marred the night, and to my relief, neither Miss Rachel nor Rosanna managed to slip past Sergeant Cuff's watchful eye. I had anticipated that the Sergeant would depart for Frizinghall immediately upon waking, yet he lingered, seemingly occupied with some unseen business. Leaving him to his devices, I ventured into the grounds shortly thereafter and encountered Mr. Franklin pacing along his favorite path beside the shrubberies.

Our exchange had scarcely begun when the Sergeant appeared unexpectedly. He approached Mr. Franklin with a forced cordiality, though the gentleman received him with a distinct air of hauteur. "Have you anything to say to me?" Mr. Franklin snapped, offering no warmth to the Sergeant's greeting.

"I have something to say to you, sir," the Sergeant replied, "concerning the inquiry I am conducting here. You discerned the true direction of this investigation yesterday. It is natural, in your position, that you should feel shocked and distressed. Equally natural is your tendency to project your anger regarding your family's scandal onto me."

"What do you want?" Mr. Franklin interjected sharply.

"I wish to remind you, sir, that I have not yet been *proven* wrong. Keeping that in mind, please recall simultaneously that I am an officer of the law, acting under the sanction of the mistress of the house. Under these circumstances, is it, or is it not, your duty as a good citizen to assist me with any special information you may possess?"

"I possess no special information," Mr. Franklin stated flatly.

Sergeant Cuff dismissed this response as if it had never been uttered. "You may save my time from being wasted on inquiries at a distance," the Sergeant continued, "if you choose to understand me and speak out."

"I don't understand you," Mr. Franklin answered, "and I have nothing to say."

"One of the female servants—I won't mention names—spoke to you privately last night, sir."

Again, Mr. Franklin cut him short. Again, he declared, "I have nothing to say."

Watching silently, I recalled the movement in the swing-door from the previous evening and the coat-tails disappearing down the passage. The Sergeant, undoubtedly, had gathered sufficient evidence to suspect that Rosanna had relieved her conscience by confiding in Mr. Franklin Blake. This thought had just formed in my mind when Rosanna Spearman emerged at the end of the shrubbery walk in her own person, closely followed by Penelope, who was clearly attempting to guide her back inside. Observing that Mr. Franklin was not alone, Rosanna halted, her countenance filled with perplexity as to how to proceed. Penelope waited patiently behind her. Both Mr. Franklin and I spotted them instantly, but the Sergeant, employing his devilish cunning, feigned total ignorance of their presence. Within seconds, everything unfolded. Before either Mr. Franklin or I could utter a word, the Sergeant intervened smoothly, maintaining the appearance of continuing our prior conversation.

"You need not fear harming the girl, sir," he addressed Mr. Franklin, speaking in a loud tone intended for Rosanna's ears. "On the contrary, I recommend that you honor me with your confidence if you harbor any genuine interest in Rosanna Spearman."

Instantly, Mr. Franklin adopted the guise of having overlooked the girls entirely. Speaking loudly, he countered: "I take no interest whatever in Rosanna Spearman."

I cast a glance toward the distant end of the walk. All I perceived was that Rosanna suddenly turned around the moment Mr. Franklin spoke. Instead of resisting Penelope as she had moments before, she now allowed my daughter to take her arm and lead her back toward the house.

As the two girls vanished, the breakfast bell rang. Even the astute Sergeant Cuff was compelled to abandon the chase! Addressing me quietly, he remarked, "I shall go to Frizinghall, Mr. Betteredge, and I shall return before two." With that, he departed, leaving us free from his scrutiny for several hours.

" You must make it right with Rosanna," Mr. Franklin said to me once we were alone. "I seem fated to say or do something awkward whenever that unfortunate girl is near. As you observed, Sergeant Cuff laid a trap for both of us. Had he confused me or irritated her into an outburst, either she or I might have revealed something serving his purposes. On the spur of the moment, I saw no better escape than the path I chose. It prevented the girl from speaking and demonstrated to the Sergeant that I saw through him. He was evidently listening, Betteredge, when I was speaking to you last night."

"He had done worse than listen," I thought privately. He had remembered my confession to him that the girl loved Mr. Franklin, and he had calculated precisely on that fact when appealing to Mr. Franklin's supposed interest in Rosanna, within her earshot.

"As to listening, sir," I remarked, withholding the other point, "we shall all be rowing in the same boat if this continues. Prying, peeping, and listening are the natural occupations of those in our situation. Within another day or two, Mr. Franklin, we shall all be rendered speechless—for we shall all be listening to surprise each other's secrets, and everyone will know it." I excused my abruptness. "The horrid mystery hanging over this house gets into my head like liquor, making me wild. I will not forget what you have told me. I shall take the first opportunity to make it right with Rosanna Spearman."

"Haven't you said anything to her yet about last night?" Mr. Franklin asked.

"No, sir."

"Then say nothing now. I had better not invite the girl's confidence with the Sergeant on the lookout to spring a trap upon us. My conduct is not very consistent, Betteredge—is it? I see no way out of this business, which is dreadful to contemplate, unless the Diamond is traced to Rosanna. Yet I cannot, and will not, assist Sergeant Cuff in finding her out."

Unreasonable, no doubt. But it mirrored my own state of mind. I thoroughly understood him. For once, if you will remember that you are mortal, you may understand him as well.

While the Sergeant was en route to Frizinghall, the domestic scene unfolded thus: Miss Rachel waited for the carriage destined for her aunt, remaining obstinately confined to her chamber. My lady and Mr. Franklin breakfasted together. Afterward, Mr. Franklin took one of his impulsive resolutions and stepped out hastily, seeking to quiet his mind with a long walk. I was the sole witness to his departure; he assured me he would return before the Sergeant's arrival. The weather, foreshadowed the previous night, had deteriorated. Heavy rain followed swiftly by a high wind. Though the air blew fresh as the day progressed, clouds threatened intermittently, yet the rain held off. It was a fair day for a walk, provided one possessed youth and strength to brave the gale-swept gusts rolling in from the sea.

Attending to my mistress after breakfast, I assisted her in settling our household accounts. She alluded to the matter of the Moonstone only once, doing so by forbidding any immediate discussion of it between us. "Wait until that man returns," she said, referring to the Sergeant. "We *must* speak of it then; we are not obliged to speak of it now."

Departing from my mistress, I found Penelope awaiting me in my room.

"I wish, Father, you would come and speak to Rosanna," she pleaded. "I am exceedingly uneasy about her."

I divined the nature of her concern readily. However, a maxim of mine dictates that men, being superior creatures, are bound to improve women if they can. Whenever a woman requests action—from my daughter or anyone else—I invariably insist on knowing the why. The more frequently you compel them to rummage their own minds for a reason, the more manageable you will find them in all relations of life. It is not their fault, poor wretches, that they act first and think afterwards; it is the fault of the fools who indulge them.

Penelope's reason, on this occasion, expressed in her own words, was as follows: "I am afraid, Father, that Mr. Franklin has hurt Rosanna cruelly, without intending it."

"What brought Rosanna to the shrubbery walk?" I inquired.

"Her own madness," Penelope replied. "I can call it nothing else. She was bent on speaking to Mr. Franklin this morning, regardless of consequences. I did my utmost to stop her; you saw that. If I could only have got her away before she heard those dreadful words—"

"There! There!" I soothed her. "Do not lose your head. I cannot recollect anything happening to alarm Rosanna."

"Nothing to alarm her, Father. But Mr. Franklin said he took no interest whatever in her—and, oh, he said it in such a cruel voice!"

"He said it to stop the Sergeant's tongue," I answered.

"I told her that," Penelope admitted. "But you see, Father (though Mr. Franklin isn't to blame), he has been mortifying and disappointing her for weeks and weeks past; and now this comes on top of it all! She has no right, of course, to expect him to take any interest in her. It is quite monstrous that she should forget herself and her station in that manner. But she seems to have lost pride, and proper feeling, and everything. She frightened me, Father, when Mr. Franklin said those words. They seemed to turn her into stone.
