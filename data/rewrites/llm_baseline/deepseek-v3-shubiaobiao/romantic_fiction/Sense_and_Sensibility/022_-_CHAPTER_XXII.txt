Marianne, whose patience was easily exhausted by anything she deemed vulgar or beneath her tastes, found the Miss Steeles particularly grating to her sensibilities. Her current melancholy only sharpened her disdain, and her coldness toward them was so unwavering that any attempt at familiarity was swiftly rebuffed. It was no surprise, then, that the sisters—Lucy in particular—turned their attentions to Elinor, whose quiet courtesy offered a far more welcoming reception.

Lucy, though lacking refinement, possessed a quick wit that made her tolerable company in small doses. Yet her ignorance was impossible to overlook, and Elinor, though pitying the waste of such natural cleverness, could not ignore the calculating nature beneath Lucy’s flattery. There was an artfulness in her manner, a deliberate charm wielded like a blade, and Elinor found little comfort in the company of one so adept at feigned sincerity.

One afternoon, as they walked from the park toward the cottage, Lucy ventured a question with affected nonchalance.

“You will think my inquiry odd, I’m sure,” she began, “but tell me—are you acquainted with Mrs. Ferrars, your sister-in-law’s mother?”

Elinor did indeed find the question peculiar. “I have never met the lady,” she replied, cautious.

Lucy sighed. “I only wondered what she was like. You see, I have reason to be curious.”

Elinor’s brows lifted slightly. “Reason?”

Lucy hesitated, then lowered her voice conspiratorially. “Mrs. Ferrars is nothing to me now, but in time… well, we may be very closely connected indeed.”

Elinor’s pulse quickened, though her expression remained composed. “You cannot mean you are acquainted with Mr. Robert Ferrars?”

“Oh no,” Lucy said, her gaze fixed on Elinor with unsettling intensity. “His elder brother.”

For a moment, the world seemed to tilt. Elinor forced herself to breathe, to think. It was impossible. A mistake. A lie.

“You are surprised,” Lucy continued, feigning bashfulness. “And no wonder—he would never have spoken of me. It has been a secret these four years.”

*Four years.* The words struck like a blow. Elinor’s mind raced, grasping for reason. “You expect me to believe,” she said carefully, “that you have been engaged to Mr. Edward Ferrars all this time?”

“Indeed!” Lucy’s smile was triumphant. She drew a miniature from her pocket. “See for yourself.”

Elinor’s fingers trembled as she took it. The face gazing back was undeniably Edward’s. Her stomach twisted, but she handed it back with forced calm.

“I have longed to give him my portrait in return,” Lucy sighed. “But we have had to be so very discreet. His mother would never approve—I’ve no fortune, you see.”

Elinor fought to keep her voice steady. “Why confide in me?”

“Because I trust you,” Lucy said sweetly. “And after my questions about Mrs. Ferrars, I owed you some explanation.”

Elinor said nothing. The pieces fell into place with cruel clarity—Edward’s melancholy, his evasiveness. Had it all been guilt?

Lucy prattled on, lamenting the trials of their secret love, but Elinor scarcely heard. When they reached the cottage at last, she bid Lucy a stiff farewell and retreated to the sanctuary of solitude.

Alone, the weight of it crashed upon her. The letters, the ring—proofs too damning to dismiss. Edward, whom she had believed so honorable, bound to another.

She pressed a hand to her mouth, stifling the cry that threatened to escape. Then, with a slow, steadying breath, she straightened.

There would be no tears. Not yet.

For now, she would endure.
