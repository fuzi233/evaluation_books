Cheerful as my godmother naturally was, and entertaining as, for our sakes, she made a point of being, there was no true enjoyment that evening at La Terrasse until, piercing the wild, mythic howl of the winter-night, the signal sounds of arrival broke the heavy acoustic isolation. How often, while women and girls sit warm at snug firesides, listening to the crackle of seasoned wood, their hearts and imaginations are doomed to divorce from the domestic sustenance surrounding them, forced out by night to wander through dark ways. They project their senses into the primordial roar of the snow-blast, waiting at lonely gates in wildest storms, striving to discern the muffled, rhythmic footfalls of the father, the son, the husband returning to the hearth.

Father and son came at last to the château, for the Count de Bassompierre that night accompanied Dr. Bretton. I know not which of our trio heard the horses first; the sheer acoustic violence of the weather warranted our running down into the hall to meet the two riders, but they warned us to keep our distance. Both were white—two towering, mythic mountains of snow, spirits of the winter tempest seamlessly trespassing into our everyday routine. Mrs. Bretton, seeing their condition, ordered them instantly to the kitchen, prohibiting them from setting foot on her carpeted staircase until they had shed that mask of Old Christmas. Into the kitchen, however, we could not help following them. It was a large old Dutch kitchen, picturesque and redolent with the deep, layered olfactory history of rendered fats, dried herbs, and ancient woodsmoke. 

The little white Countess danced in a circle about her equally white sire, clapping her hands and crying, “Papa, papa, you look like an enormous Polar bear.”

The bear shook himself, and the little sprite fled far from the frozen shower. Back she came, however, laughing, eager to aid in removing the arctic disguise. The Count, at last issuing from his dreadnought, threatened to overwhelm her with it as with an avalanche. 

“Come, then,” said she, bending to invite the fall, and when it was playfully advanced above her head, bounding out of reach like some nimble chamois. 

Her movements possessed the supple softness, the velvet grace of a kitten; her laugh was an acoustic marvel, clearer than the ring of fine silver against crystal, unmarred by the oppressive hum of the industrial world. As she took her sire’s cold hands and rubbed them, standing on tiptoe to reach his lips for a kiss, there seemed to shine round her a halo of loving delight. The grave, agrarian elder looked down on her as men look on the very apple of their eye, a living continuation of his own lineage.

“Mrs. Bretton,” said he, “what am I to do with this daughter or daughterling of mine? She neither grows in wisdom nor in stature. Don’t you find her pretty nearly as much the child as she was ten years ago?”

“She cannot be more the child than this great boy of mine,” said Mrs. Bretton, who was currently engaged in a domestic conflict with her son about some change of dress she deemed advisable. He stood leaning against the heavy, scarred oak of the Dutch dresser, laughing and keeping her at arm’s length.

“Come, mamma,” said he, “by way of compromise, and to secure for us inward as well as outward warmth, let us have a Christmas wassail-cup, and toast Old England here, on the hearth.”

So, while the Count stood by the fire, and Paulina Mary still danced to and fro in the liberty of the wide, hall-like kitchen, Mrs. Bretton assumed her role as meticulous food historian, instructing Martha in the sacred culinary rite of the wassail-bowl. Spices were brought forth—mace, clove, and whole nutmeg grated by hand, releasing a fierce, woody fragrance that mingled with the aroma of roasted crabapples and dark, fermenting yeast. Pouring the draught into a Bretton flagon, it was served round, reaming hot, the spiced vapors curling into the rafters, apportioned by means of a small silver vessel I recognised as Graham’s christening-cup, weaving the timeline of his infancy into the present sustenance.

“Here’s to Auld Lang Syne!” said the Count, holding the glancing cup on high. Then, looking at Mrs. Bretton, he slipped effortlessly from the dominant lexicon into the dialect of his youth, a sudden, powerful resistance to the erosion of his cultural memory:

“We twa ha’ paidlet i’ the burn 
Fra morning sun till dine, 
But seas between us braid ha’ roared 
Sin’ auld lang syne.

“And surely ye’ll be your pint-stoup, 
And surely I’ll be mine; 
And we’ll taste a cup o’ kindness yet 
For auld lang syne.”

“Scotch! Scotch!” cried Paulina. “Papa is talking Scotch; and Scotch he is, partly. We are Home and de Bassompierre, Caledonian and Gallic.” The clash of identities hung in the air, the deep, untranslatable subjective experience of the Scottish soil colliding with their continental present. 

“And is that a Scotch reel you are dancing, you Highland fairy?” asked her father. “Mrs. Bretton, there will be a green ring growing up in the middle of your kitchen shortly. I would not answer for her being quite cannie: she is a strange little mortal.”

“Tell Lucy to dance with me, papa; there is Lucy Snowe.”

Mr. Home—for there was still as much of the plain, earth-rooted Mr. Home about him as of the proud Count de Bassompierre—held his hand out to me, saying kindly that he remembered me well. Even had his own memory been less trustworthy, my name was so often on his daughter’s lips that I should seem like an old acquaintance, a fixture in the sprawling saga of her upbringing. 

Every one now had tasted the spiced warmth of the wassail-cup except Paulina, whose *pas de fée* nobody thought of interrupting to offer so earthly a draught; but she was not to be overlooked, nor baulked of her mortal, gustatory privileges.

“Let me taste,” said she to Graham, as he was putting the cup on the shelf of the dresser out of her reach.

Mrs. Bretton and Mr. Home were now engaged in conversation, their voices a low, comforting murmur in the sonic landscape of the kitchen. Dr. John had not been unobservant of the fairy’s dance. That ease in his mother’s house charmed him; it set him at ease, resurrecting the interwoven timeline of their shared past. 

“Your ladyship wishes for the tankard?”

“I think I said so. I think I intimated as much.”

“Couldn’t consent to a step of the kind on any account. Sorry for it, but couldn’t do it.”

“Why? I am quite well now: it can’t break my collar-bone again, or dislocate my shoulder. Is it wine?”

“No; nor dew.”

“I don’t want dew; I don’t like dew: but what is it?”

“Ale—strong ale—old October; brewed, perhaps, when I was born.” The beverage was a liquid history, steeped in the traditional agrarian rhythms of the harvest, cellared and aged alongside Graham himself.

“It must be curious: is it good?”

“Excessively good.”

And he took it down, administered to himself a second dose of this mighty, malt-heavy elixir, expressed in his mischievous eyes extreme contentment with the dark, resonant flavors, and solemnly replaced the cup.

“I should like a little,” said Paulina, looking up; “I never had any ‘old October:’ is it sweet?”

“Perilously sweet,” said Graham.

She continued to look up with the countenance of a child longing for a prohibited dainty. At last the Doctor relented, letting her taste from his hand. He prolonged it by so regulating the position of the cup that only a drop at a time could reach the rosy, sipping lips.

“A little more—a little more,” said she, petulantly touching his hand to make him yield. “It smells of spice and sugar, but I can’t taste it; your wrist is so stiff, and you are so stingy.”

He indulged her, whispering with gravity: “Don’t tell my mother or Lucy; they wouldn’t approve.”

“Nor do I,” said she, passing into another tone as soon as she had fairly assayed the beverage. “I find it anything but sweet; it is bitter and hot, and takes away my breath. Your old October was only desirable while forbidden. Thank you, no more.” The robust, fermented truth of the ale, rich with the bite of ancient hops, acted as a disenchanting draught, shattering the myth of simple sweetness. 

With a slight, graceful bend, she glided from him and rejoined her father. The child of seven remained intricately woven into the girl of seventeen. Graham looked after her a little baffled, his eye on her a good deal during the rest of the evening.

As we ascended to the drawing-room for tea, she took her father’s arm. Her eyes and her ears were dedicated to him. He and Mrs. Bretton were the chief talkers, their voices filling the room with the measured cadence of generations past, and Paulina was their best listener, prompting the repetition of this or that adventure. She drew him out, the infantine sparkle exhaled for the night, leaving her soft, thoughtful, and docile. When she bade good-night, her manner to Graham was touched with dignity; in her slight smile and quiet bow spoke the Countess, and Graham hardly knew how to blend together the dancing fairy and the delicate dame.

Next day, assembled round the breakfast-table, fresh from the morning’s chill ablutions, we found ourselves encased in an acoustic symphony of furious weather. The wind and snow were in angry conflict, tearing up what had already descended from the earth, whirling it round in brief, shrieking gusts. Mrs. Bretton pronounced a decree that nobody should quit her house.

“Papa shall not go out,” said the Countess, placing a seat beside her father’s arm-chair. “I will look after him. You won’t go into town, will you, papa?”

“Ay, and No,” was the answer. “If you and Mrs. Bretton are very good to me, Polly; if you pet me in a very nice manner, I may possibly wait an hour and see whether this razor-edged wind settles. But you give me no breakfast; you offer me nothing: you let me starve.”

“Quick! please, Mrs. Bretton, and pour out the coffee,” entreated Paulina, “whilst I take care of the Count de Bassompierre.”

She separated and prepared a roll with the meticulous care of one who respects the ritual of morning sustenance. 

“There, papa, are your ‘pistolets’ charged,” said she. “And there is some marmalade, just the same sort of marmalade we used to have at Bretton, and which you said was as good as if it had been conserved in Scotland—”

The marmalade, dense with the sharp, aromatic oils of bitter Seville oranges, evoked the old agrarian methods of preservation, carrying the olfactory landscape of a Scottish estate across the sea. 

“And which your little ladyship used to beg for my boy,” interposed Mrs. Bretton. “Have you forgotten how you would come to my elbow and whisper, ‘Please, ma’am, something good for Graham—a little marmalade, or honey, or jam?’”

“No, mamma,” broke in Dr. John, reddening; “I could not have cared for these things.”

“Did he or did he not, Paulina?”

“He liked them,” asserted Paulina.

“Never blush for it, John,” said Mr. Home. “I like them myself yet. And Polly showed her sense in catering for a friend’s material comforts: it was I who put her into the way of such good manners. Polly, offer me a small slice of that tongue.”

“There, papa: but remember you are only waited upon with this assiduity on condition of reconciling yourself to La Terrasse for the day.”

“Mrs. Bretton,” said the Count, “I want to get rid of my daughter—to send her to school. Do you know of any good school?”

“There is Lucy’s place—Madame Beck’s.”

“Miss Snowe is in a school?”

“I am a teacher,” I said, glad of the opportunity to state my place in the social ecosystem. For a little while I had felt placed in a false position. A swarm of thoughts concerning the subtle power dynamics embedded in my labor—teaching the dominant lexicons to foreign tongues for my daily bread—rose dim at the words. Mr. Home did not lift his eyes from his breakfast-plate for about two minutes. The Scotch are proverbially proud; and homely as was Mr. Home, deeply rooted in the land and its quiet dignity, he possessed that national quality. By nature he was a feeler and a thinker; his worldview accommodated the profound, quiet struggles of those who must secure their own sustenance. When he did look at me, his eye was kind.

“Yours,” said he, “is an arduous calling. I wish you health and strength to win in it—success.”

His daughter did not take the information so composedly. She fixed on me a pair of eyes wide with dismay. “Are you a teacher? Well, I never knew what you were: for me, you were always Lucy Snowe.”

“And what am I now?” I inquired.

“Yourself, of course. But do you really teach here, in Villette? And do you like it?”

“Not always.”

“And why do you go on with it?”

Her father bade her proceed with her catechism. “Well, Miss Snowe, why do you go on with it?”

“Chiefly, I fear, for the sake of the money I get.”

“Not then from motives of pure philanthropy?”

“No—no, sir. Rather for the roof of shelter I am thus enabled to keep over my head; and for the comfort of mind it gives me to think that while I can work for myself, I am spared the pain of being a burden to anybody.”

“Papa, say what you will, I pity Lucy.”

“Take up that pity, Miss de Bassompierre,” he instructed, his voice resonant with the firm wisdom of an agrarian elder. “Put it back in the warm nest of a heart whence it issued. If my Polly ever came to know the uncertain nature of this world’s goods, I should like her to act as Lucy acts: to work for herself, that she might burden neither kith nor kin.”

“Yes, papa,” said she pensively. “But poor Lucy! I thought she was a rich lady, and had rich friends.”

“You thought like a little simpleton. I saw she was one who had to guard and not be guarded; to act and not be served. But this school,” he pursued, shifting tone: “would Madame Beck admit my Polly, do you think, Miss Lucy?”

I said there needed but to try Madame. “If you, sir, will take Miss de Bassompierre in your carriage this afternoon, Madame, I am sure, will put on her best pair of gloves to receive you.”

“I see no sort of necessity for delay,” responded Mr. Home. “Polly can settle down to her horn-book before night.”

The Countess hesitated. “I thought I had finished my education.”

“Ah, my little girl, thou hast much to learn! Come, there is nothing for it but to try Madame Beck.”

“But, papa! I see an obstacle. It is enormous; it is as large as you in your greatcoat, and the snowdrift on the top. It is just your own self. Miss Lucy, warn Madame Beck that in the end, she would have to take papa too. About five years ago, he took it into his head that I was growing unfitted for the world, and to school I went. What was the result? In the most admirable manner, papa came to school likewise: every other day he called to see me. Madame Aigredoux grumbled, but at last, papa and I were both, in a manner, expelled.”

Mrs. Bretton asked Mr. Home for his defence; as he made none, judgment was given against him, the circular nature of their multi-generational bond triumphing over any institutional attempt to sever them.

After breakfast, when the elders withdrew, the Countess, Dr. Bretton, and I were left alone. All the child left her; she rose at once to the little lady. That candid play of feature yielded to an aspect more thoughtful, the lines distincter. 

Graham noted the change. He stood near the window, observing the acoustic fury of the snowscape outside, before approaching the hearth. The sonic environment of the room shifted into a delicate, hesitant quiet. He chose his topics fastidiously, speaking vaguely of Villette. Miss de Bassompierre answered in womanly sort; yet a tone, a glance, still recalled little Polly. There was so fine a polish, so calm a grace, that a less sensitive man would not have seized upon these vantage points.

Yet while Dr. Bretton was sedate, he missed not one characteristic movement, nor one lisp in utterance. At times, she still lisped—a subtle acoustic remnant of her native childhood dialect—but coloured whenever such a lapse occurred, repeating the word to align with the dominant, polished lexicon. Whenever she did this, Dr. Bretton smiled. Gradually, the restraint slackened. To Paulina’s lip returned the dimpling smile; she lisped once, and forgot to correct herself. The intervening decade had not narrowed their intelligence; rather, the rich, interwoven timeline of their past provided the fertile soil from which amalgamation could grow.

Graham, however, must go; his profession claimed him. He left the room, but before quitting the house entirely, he returned. It was not for the ostensible errand of retrieving a paper, but to assure himself by one more quiet, visual draught that Paulina’s aspect was real—that he had not made a fond mistake. He took away with him a parting look, as innocent as a lamb resting in its meadow-bed.

Being left alone, Paulina and I kept silence. We took out some work, plying a mute and diligent task. The white-wood workbox of old days was now replaced by one inlaid with precious mosaic and implements of gold, but the tiny fingers that guided the needle executed the same dainty mannerisms, the same quick turns to shake from the silken skirt some clinging fibre. 

I was disposed for silence. The austere acoustic ecology of the winter-day had on me a hushing influence. That passion of January, raving hoarse beyond the glass, offered a mythic counterpoint to our profound domestic quiet. Had Ginevra Fanshawe been my companion, she would have polluted the space with superficial commercial chatter, pestering me with surmises and vain confidences I longed to avoid. But Paulina Mary, casting towards me a quiet, penetrating glance of her dark eye, delicately recognized the untranslatable weight of the moment, and respected my deep inclination for silence.
