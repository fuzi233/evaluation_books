Margaret tore a great gobbet from the venison—still steaming where the knife had opened it, the browned edge sweating salt and rosemary—and ran straight to the casement. She flung it outward as one tosses a crust to a barn-cat; only these were no barn-cats.

Two green points, like coals blown alive in a dark hearth, slid forward from the hedge. The beast sprang, and the night answered with a savage, tearing snarl; bone grated, sinew cracked; there came that sickening, busy crunching a kitchen hears when a cleaver finds cartilage—only this cleaver had fangs.

At that same instant a hound gave tongue so near and so huge that it seemed the very boards of the house bayed with it. The sound rolled through the rooms, ricocheting from rafter to rafter; and all three at the window shrank together, as folk do when thunder breaks too close for comfort. Gerard felt Margaret’s shoulder against him, and even Martin, who would face a fair fight, drew back at the violence of the noise: there is a kind of sound that makes the heart believe in claws.

The leopard, fearing for her supper, gathered the meat and glided away—not a rush, but a smooth, stealthy withdrawal, as if the darkness itself had learned to walk. The crunching receded toward the woods.

Then, almost before the last snarl died, the chase burst past the window—horses, men, and dogs in a mad, clattering ribbon. Hooves struck flint from the hard road, harness jingled, men shouted, and the pack’s full cry poured after the vanished quarry like wind through winter branches. The house stood still again, but the night kept the echo; even after the riders had gone, the air seemed to throb where their noise had been.

Martin and his companions breathed at last. The leopard was swift, and the pack would not have her within a league of their hearth. Hands met—rough palm to rough palm—and for a moment the very act of gripping one another was like setting a potlid hard upon boiling fear.

Margaret seized her respite to cry, as women do when the danger has passed and the body remembers it has a throat. Gerard kissed the tears away, tasting salt, and, with it, a tenderness sharper than any spice.

They went back to table. The venison, though diminished, still sent up its rich, forest smell—blood tamed by fire, fat rendered to sweetness. Gerard lifted his cup and drank to woman’s wit.

“’Tis stronger than man’s force,” said he.

“Ay,” said Margaret, wiping her cheek and forcing a steadier breath, “when those she loves are in danger; not else.”

That night he stayed later than his custom. When at last he went out, the air smelt of damp earth and the cold green of leaf-shadow. Behind him, through the shutters, he could still hear domestic sounds—the settle creaking, a ladle rinsed, a foot on stone—small, homely noises that made the late uproar seem a nightmare a man wakes from into the comfort of his own bed.

He had not gone far when two figures rose out of the black under some trees and almost barred his way. The starlight caught their faces: his father and his mother.

So late. So set. Something in their stillness turned the night chill.

He stopped. They stood grim and silent, like folk at a graveside who have resolved their grief into judgment.

He stammered some question.

“Why ask?” said his father. “You know why we are here.”

“Oh, Gerard!” said his mother; and in that one sound reproach and love lay twisted together like two strands in a cord.

Gerard’s heart quaked. He could not find speech that did not sound guilty, nor silence that did not sound defiant, and so he stood dumb.

His father, seeing his confusion, softened only enough to sting.

“Nay, you need not hang your head. You are not the first young fool that has been caught by a red cheek and a pair of blue eyes.”

“Nay, nay!” put in Catherine. “It was witchcraft; Peter the Magician is well known for that.”

“Come, Sir Priest,” resumed his father, “you know you must not meddle with women folk. But give us your promise to go no more to Sevenbergen, and here all ends: we won’t be hard on you for one fault.”

“I cannot promise that, father.”

“Not promise it, you young hypocrite!”

“Nay, father, miscall me not.” Gerard’s voice shook, but not with fear alone; something in him was weary of swallowing words. “I lacked courage to tell you what I knew would vex you; and right grateful am I to that good friend, whoever he be, that has let you wot. ’Tis a load off my mind. Yes, father, I love Margaret; and call me not a priest, for a priest I will never be. I will die sooner.”

“That we shall see, young man. Come, gainsay me no more; you will learn what ’tis to disrespect a father.”

Gerard held his peace. The three walked home under the trees, their footsteps muffled by last year’s leaves, silence thickening between them. Only now and then Catherine’s deep sigh broke it, like a kettle letting out its grief.

From that hour the little house at Tergou was no longer the abode of peace.

Next day they took Gerard to task before the whole family, as if he had spilled sour ale into the bread-dough and must answer for the waste. Every voice was loud against him, except little Kate’s—and the dwarf’s, who took his cue from her as naturally as a dog looks to a child that feeds it. Cornelis and Sybrandt were bitterer than their father; there is a spite in brothers that takes the form of righteousness.

Gerard, who had been yesterday the universal pet, stood dismayed in the storm of tongues. His eyes went wistfully to his little sister: her lashes were wet, and the corners of her mouth trembled at the harsh words flung upon him. Yet she gave him no comfort. She turned her head away as if looking at him made the pain sharper, and said, piously and cruelly at once, as youth can:

“Dear, dear Gerard, pray to Heaven to cure you of this folly!”

“What, are you against me too?” said Gerard, softly; and something seemed to give way in him, not with a crack, but with a slow, aching tear.

He rose with a deep sigh and left the house, and went to Sevenbergen.

At the beginning of such quarrels—where affection binds those whose interests and convictions clash—the wrong is not always plain. Both can be honest at the outset, and then a calm friend who sees two sides is a gift rarer than fine pepper in a poor pantry. But let dissension simmer; let it be stirred each day by temper and pride, and it thickens. Human nature, like a pot neglected, burns at the bottom for want of patience, and at last both parties are in the wrong.

The combatants were unequally matched. Elias was angry; Cornelis and Sybrandt spiteful; but Gerard, having a larger and more cultivated mind, saw both sides where they saw but one. His trouble did not sharpen into wrath; it sank into unhappiness. He was lonely in it too. He could open his heart to no one.

Margaret Brandt was high-spirited. He dared not tell her all he endured at home; she was capable—so clean was her pride—of siding with his people by resigning him, though it would cut her own heart to do it. As for Margaret Van Eyck, she had once been a great comfort to him; but now he shrank from making her his confidant. Her story was common talk: in early life she had many offers of marriage and refused them, choosing art—an art that a wife’s and mother’s duties so often smother. Thus she remained single and painted with her brothers. How could he tell her that he meant to decline the benefice she had won him, and for the sake of the very thing she had once sacrificed with so steady a hand?

Gerard, at this time, bade fair to succumb.

But his adversaries had a strange ally—Catherine senior.

Good-hearted, uneducated, she could not, like her daughter, act quietly and firmly; still less could she act upon a plan. At times she irritated Gerard and so—oddly—helped him; for anger, like strong broth, sustains when appetite fails. At other times she whirled round and fell upon her own side like a hen pecking her own chicks.

Once, when they were all together, Cornelis said with a sneer, “Our Gerard wed Margaret Brandt? Why, it is hunger marrying thirst.”

“And what will it be when you marry?” cried Catherine in a flash. “Gerard can paint, Gerard can write, but what can you do to keep a woman, ye lazy loon? Nought but wait for your father’s shoon. Oh, we can see why you and Sybrandt would not have the poor boy to marry. You are afraid he will come to us for a share of our substance. And say that he does, and say that we give it him, it isn’t yourn we part from, and mayhap never will be.”

On such days Gerard smiled to himself, sly and grateful, and took heart. Temporary confusion fell on Catherine’s allies, as when a cook’s sharp answer silences grumblers round the board.

But after more than six months of fretting came the climax. Elias told his son, before the whole household, that he had ordered the burgomaster to imprison him in the Stadthouse rather than let him marry Margaret.

Gerard turned pale; anger drained the colour from him as if he had been bled. Yet by a great effort he held his peace.

His father went on, relentless. “And a priest you shall be before the year is out, nilly-willy.”

“Is it so?” cried Gerard; and now the restraint broke like a cord. “Then hear me, all. By God and St. Bavon I swear I will never be a priest while Margaret lives. Since force is to decide it, and not love and duty, try force, father; but force shall not serve you, for the day I see the burgomaster come for me, I leave Tergou for ever, and Holland too, and my father’s house, where it seems I have been valued all these years, not for myself, but for what is to be got out of me.”

He flung out of the room, white with anger and desperation.

“There!” cried Catherine. “That comes of driving young folks too hard. But men are crueller than tigers, even to their own flesh and blood. Now, Heaven forbid he should ever leave us, married or single.”

Outside, the air hit Gerard like cold water on a fevered brow. His heart panted; his cheeks were bloodless. At the threshold he met Reicht Heynes, who had been watching for him with that look servants get when they bring urgent news and hope it will not be blamed on them.

She had a message: Mistress Margaret Van Eyck desired to see him.

He went, and found the old lady seated grim as a judge, as if her very chair were a tribunal. She wasted no time in soft words.

She inquired coldly why he had not visited her of late. Before he could answer, she said, with a dry sting, “I thought we had been friends, young sir.”

Gerard stood the picture of doubt and consternation, like a boy caught with honey on his fingers.

“It is because you never told her you were in love,” said Reicht, pitying his distress.

“Silence, wench!” cried Margaret Van Eyck. “Why should he tell us his affairs? We are not his friends: we have not deserved his confidence.”

“Alas! my second mother,” said Gerard, “I did not dare to tell you my folly.”

“What folly? Is it folly to love?”

“I am told so every day of my life.”

“You need not have been afraid to tell my mistress; she is always kind to true lovers.”

“Madam—Reicht—I was afraid because I was told…”

“Well, you were told—?”

“That in your youth you scorned love, preferring art.”

“I did, boy; and what is the end of it?” The old woman’s voice changed; it took on a rough sincerity that rasped like a knife on a whetstone. “Behold me here a barren stock, while the women of my youth have a troop of children at their side, and grandchildren at their knee. I gave up the sweet joys of wifehood and motherhood for what? For my dear brothers. They have gone and left me long ago. For my art. It has all but left me too. I have the knowledge still, but what avails that when the hand trembles?

“No, Gerard; I look on you as my son. You are good, you are handsome, you are a painter—though not like some I have known. I will not let you throw your youth away as I did mine: you shall marry this Margaret. I have inquired, and she is a good daughter. Reicht here is a gossip. She has told me all about it. But that need not hinder you to tell me.”

Joy went through him like warm wine. To be permitted—almost commanded—to speak Margaret’s name aloud with praise, and to one who could comprehend what he loved in her, was like being allowed to open a shutter in a close room.

Soon two pairs of eyes were wet over his story; and when he saw that, his own blurred too. Reicht sniffed and wiped her nose with her apron, as if tears were only another kitchen inconvenience.

Women, when they love, have a courage that is their own kind: not the brute, solitary daring that struts and strikes, but a vicarious hardihood that sends others forth and will not let them falter. Under the quiet of that room Gerard felt it press upon him—steady as kneading-hands working stubborn dough into obedience.

Mistress Van Eyck and Reicht agreed: a man should take the bull by the horns. Gerard’s course was to marry Margaret Brandt off-hand; the old folk would come to after a while, once the deed was beyond recall. Whereas, the longer things stood as they were, the more each day would season bitterness into habit.

“See how pale and thin they have made him amongst them,” said Mistress Van Eyck.

“Indeed you are, Master Gerard,” said Reicht. “It makes a body sad to see a young man so wasted and worn. Mistress, when I met him in the street to-day, I had liked to have burst out crying: he was so changed.”

“And I’ll be bound the others keep their colour; ah, Reicht? such as it is.”

“Oh, I see no odds in them.”

“Of course not. We painters are no match for boors. We are glass; they are stone. We can’t stand the worry, worry, worry of little minds; and it is not for the good of mankind we should be exposed to it. It is hard enough, Heaven knows, to design and paint a masterpiece, without having gnats and flies stinging us to death into the bargain.”

Exasperated as Gerard was by his father’s threat, he listened to these friendly voices urging rebellion. Yet his heart resisted.

“I do not fear my father’s violence,” he said, “but I do fear his anger. When it came to the point he would not imprison me. I would marry Margaret to-morrow if that was my only fear. No; he would disown me. I should take Margaret from her father and give her a poor husband, who would never thrive, weighed down by his parent’s curse.

“Madam! I sometimes think if I could marry her secretly, and then take her away to some country where my craft is better paid than in this; and after a year or two, when the storm had blown over, you know, could come back with money in my purse, and say, ‘My dear parents, we do not seek your substance, we but ask you to love us once more as you used, and as we have never ceased to love you’—but, alas! I shall be told these are the dreams of an inexperienced young man.”

The old lady’s eyes sparkled, and not with mockery.

“It is no dream,” said she, “but a piece of wonderful common-sense in a boy; it remains to be seen whether you have spirit to carry out your own thought. There is a country, Gerard, where certain fortune awaits you at this moment. Here the arts freeze, but there they flourish, as they never yet flourished in any age or land.”

“It is Italy!” cried Gerard, as if a bell had been struck inside him. “It is Italy!”

“Ay, Italy! where painters are honoured like princes, and scribes are paid three hundred crowns for copying a single manuscript. Know you not that his Holiness the Pope has written to every land for skilful scribes to copy the hundreds of precious manuscripts that are pouring into that favoured land from Constantinople, whence learning and learned men are driven by the barbarian Turks?”

“Nay, I know not that,” said Gerard; “but it has been the dream and hope of my life to visit Italy, the queen of all the arts; oh, madam! But the journey—and we are all so poor.”

“Find you the heart to go, I’ll find the means. I know where to lay my hand on ten golden angels: they will take you to Rome—and the girl with you, if she loves you as she ought.”

They sat till midnight over this, as if planning a household’s provisions for a long winter: what could be carried, what must be bought on the road, what dangers must be accounted for. Outside, the world had its own acoustics—far dogs, a late cart, the wind in bare branches—and the quiet between those sounds seemed full of possibility, as if silence itself were a map with roads drawn in it.

After that day Gerard’s spirits lifted, and he moved through the gibes and harsh words at home as if he wore a secret charm under his shirt. And Mistress Van Eyck gave him not only money, but money’s worth.

“I will tell you secrets,” said she, leaning toward him with eyes that lit as a fire lights when fat drips upon it, “secrets I learned from masters that are gone from me and have left no fellow behind. Even the Italians know them not; and what I tell you now in Tergou you shall sell there in Florence.

“Note my brother Jan’s pictures: time, which fades all other paintings, leaves his colours bright as the day they left the easel. The reason is, he did nothing blindly, in a hurry. He trusted to no hireling to grind his colours; he did it himself, or saw it done. His panel was prepared and prepared again—I will show you how—a year before he laid his colour on. Most men are content to have their work sucked up and lost, sooner than not be in a hurry. Bad painters are always in a hurry.

“Above all, Gerard, I warn you: use but little oil, and never boil it. Boiling melts that vegetable dross into its heart which it is our business to clear away; for impure oil is death to colour. No; take your oil and pour it into a bottle with water. In a day or two the water will turn muddy: that is muck from the oil. Pour the dirty water carefully away and add fresh. When that is poured away, you will fancy the oil is clear. You’re mistaken.”

“Reicht,” she called, without turning her head, “fetch me that!”

Reicht brought a glass trough with a glass lid that fitted tight.

“When your oil has been washed in bottle, put it into this trough with water, and put the trough in the sun all day. You will soon see the water turbid again. But mark: you must not carry this game too far, or the sun will turn your oil to varnish. When it is as clear as crystal, not too luscious, drain carefully, and cork it up tight.

“Grind your own prime colours, and lay them on with this oil, and they shall live. Hubert would put sand or salt in the water to clear the oil quicker. But Jan used to say, ‘Water will do it best; give water time.’ Jan Van Eyck was never in a hurry, and that is why the world will not forget him in a hurry.”

To Gerard these “receipts” were not dry counsel but a kind of inheritance. He took them as a cook takes a mother’s knowledge—how much salt to pinch, when to lift the lid, what heat will coax and what heat will ruin. There are arts that enter through the senses first, and only afterward through the mind.

Thus furnished with means and craft, Gerard decided to marry and fly with his wife to Italy.

Now nothing remained but to tell Margaret Brandt his resolution, and to publish the banns as quietly as might be. He went to Sevenbergen earlier than his wont, his heart beating like a pestle in a mortar.

He began with Margaret—told her of Mistress Van Eyck’s goodness, of Italy, of the plan made with such grave hope. He asked her to stand with him in it.

She refused him flat.

“No, Gerard. You and I have never spoken of your family; but when you come to marriage—” She stopped, as if the word itself had pricked her pride. Then she began again, more steadily. “I do think your father has no ill-will to me more than to another. He told Peter Buyskens as much, and Peter told me. But so long as he is bent on your being a priest—you ought have told me this instead of I you—I could not marry you, Gerard, dearly as I love you.”

He strove to shake her. He argued; he pleaded; he promised. He could make her cry easily—her tears came as readily as clear whey from pressed curds—but he could not make her yield.

Then impatience and hurt made him unjust.

“Very well!” he cried. “Then you are on their side, and you will drive me to be a priest, for this must end one way or another. My parents hate me in earnest, but my lover only loves me in jest.”

And with that wild bitter speech he flung away home again, leaving Margaret weeping.

Yet when a man misbehaves, the effect on a girl who truly loves is strange: it makes her pity him. Not because she finds the fault sweet, but because she hears the misery behind it. “How unhappy, how vexed, how poor he must be to misbehave,” her heart reasons. “Poor thing.”

Margaret was full of that womanly pity when—scarce an hour and a half after he left—Gerard came running back again, panting with anger and grief. In his hands he carried the torn fragments of a picture.

“There, Margaret! see! see! the wretches! Look at their spite! They have cut your portrait to pieces.”

She looked. Some malicious hand had indeed sliced the painted panel into five parts, as one quarters a loaf with rage rather than hunger. A cut went straight through the very face—the face he had laboured over as carefully as a baker scores a crust for beauty and for rise. Six months’ patient work—layer upon layer, colour coaxed and dried, glazes laid like thin honey—now hacked and ruined.

Margaret was good, but she was not ice. Blood rushed to her forehead; shame and anger and a fierce tenderness all at once.

“Who did it?”

“Nay, I know not,” he cried. “I dared not ask; for I should hate the hand that did it, ay, till my dying day. My poor Margaret! The butchers, the ruffians! Six months’ work cut out of my life, and nothing to show for it now. See, they have hacked through your very face—the sweet face that every one loves who knows it. Oh, heartless, merciless vipers!”

“Never mind, Gerard,” said Margaret, breathing fast, her hands clenched as if she could knead the world into fairness. “Since this is how they treat you for my sake—Ye rob him of my portrait, do ye? Well, then, he shall have the face itself, such as it is.”

“Oh, Margaret!”

“Yes, Gerard. Since they are so cruel, I will be the kinder. Forgive me for refusing you. I will be your wife—to-morrow, if it is your pleasure.”

He kissed her hands with rapture—kissed them as if they were the very means of his salvation—and then her lips. In a tumult of joy he ran for Peter and Martin.

They came and witnessed the betrothal; and in those days a betrothal was no light, whispered thing, but a solemn act—bread and salt to it in spirit, as binding as any oath, and for more than a century later so held, though now the world has grown clever at unbinding what once held fast.
