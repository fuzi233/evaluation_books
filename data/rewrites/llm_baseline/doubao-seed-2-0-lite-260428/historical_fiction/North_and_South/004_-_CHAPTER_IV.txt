I shall no gentler calm implore.”

He was gone. The vicarage doors were latched for the evening, the soft glow of the southern sunset fading fast from Helstone’s hedgerows. No more of the deep, bright blue skies Margaret loved to paint, no more streaks of crimson and amber spilling over the ancient beech woods. She climbed the stairs to change for early tea, to find Dixon in a sharp ill humour, the visitor’s unexpected call having thrown off her schedule of chores for the day. The maid made her irritation plain by brushing Margaret’s hair far harder than necessary, all under the pretense of rushing to attend to Mrs. Hale.

Even so, Margaret was left waiting alone in the drawing room for long stretches after she finished dressing. She sat by the crackling hearth, the candles on the table behind her left unlit, turning the events of the day over in her mind: the happy walk through the woods that morning, the quiet pleasure of sketching the gnarled old oak at the edge of the common, the warm, easy dinner with their guest, then the sharp, miserable tension of their talk in the garden after.

How very differently men and women were made, she thought. Here she was unmoored, wretched, because every instinct of hers had made anything but a refusal impossible, and her heart ached at the thought of what she had turned down. Meanwhile, the man who had just had the most sacred, life-altering proposal of his life rejected could speak moments later of court briefs, professional success, fine houses and clever society, as if those superficial prizes were the whole of his desire. Oh, how she could have loved him, if only he had been different—if that light, careless exterior had not run so deep. Then she softened, wondering if his levity was only a mask, to hide the sharp, bitter disappointment that would have settled like a stone in her own chest had she been the one to love and be turned away.

Her mother swept into the room before her tangled thoughts could settle to any order, and Margaret had to shake off every last trace of that garden conversation to listen sympathetically to Mrs. Hale’s stories of Dixon burning the ironing blanket for the third time that month, and of Susan Lightfoot being spotted wearing artificial flowers in her bonnet, proof positive that the girl was vain and giddy beyond redemption. Mr. Hale sipped his tea in distant silence, leaving Margaret to do all the conversing. She wondered how both her parents could be so forgetful, so wrapped up in their own small concerns, that they had not even mentioned their visitor’s name. It did not occur to her that neither of them knew he had made her an offer at all.

When the tea things were cleared, Mr. Hale rose to his feet, leaned his elbow on the mantelpiece, and rested his head on his hand, sighing deeply every few moments as he turned over some heavy thought. Mrs. Hale left the parlour to consult Dixon over winter clothing for the parish poor, and Margaret set to sorting out the worsted her mother used for knitting, shrinking from the thought of the long empty evening ahead, wishing bedtime would come fast so she could re-trace every moment of the day in quiet.

“Margaret!” Mr. Hale said at last, in a sudden, desperate tone that made her start. “Is that tapestry mending you hold urgent right now? Can you set it aside and come to my study? I need to speak to you about something that is very serious for all of us.”

For one wild moment Margaret thought the news concerned Mr. Lennox. She felt guilty and hot-cheeked, abruptly aware that she was old enough now for people to think of her as a woman to be married, and feared her father might be angry she had turned down a perfectly good suitor on her own authority. But it was soon clear the matter had nothing to do with a recent proposal. He made her sit in a chair close beside his, stirred the fire to a bright blaze, snuffed the candles, and sighed two or three times before he forced the words out, sharp and sudden:
“Margaret! I am leaving Helstone.”

“Leaving Helstone, papa? Why ever would we leave here?”

Mr. Hale did not answer for a minute or two. He fumbled nervously with the loose papers scattered across his desk, opening his mouth to speak half a dozen times only to close it again, too cowardly to say what was on his mind. Margaret could not bear the suspense, which she saw was distressing him far more than it distressed her. “Tell me, dear papa! Please tell me what it is.”

He looked up at her all at once, and spoke with a slow, forced calm. “Because I can no longer serve as a minister in the Church of England.”

Margaret had been daydreaming that her mother’s long-held wish for a grander church preferment had finally come true, that they would move to one of those stately, quiet cathedral closes she had visited once or twice—places noble and imposing, yes, but leaving beloved Helstone forever would have been a long, lingering pain. This new shock was far worse. She went cold all over. Could it have something to do with Frederick? Her brother was an outlaw, run off to serve in the Spanish navy after a mutiny aboard his ship. Could her father have hidden him away, or aided him in some way?
“Oh—what is it? Speak, papa, tell me everything! Surely if the bishop knew all the unfair, unjust things that happened to Frederick—”

“It has nothing at all to do with Frederick. The bishop has no business with that matter. This is all my own doing, Margaret. I will answer every question you have this once, but after tonight, we will never speak of it again. I can bear the consequences of my painful, long-held doubts, but to talk of the years of suffering they have caused me is a burden I cannot carry twice.”

“Doubts, papa? Doubts about religion?” Margaret asked, more horrified than she had ever been in her life.

“Not doubts about religion—never that. Nothing to shake my faith in God, only my faith that I can keep my vows to the church honestly.” He paused, and Margaret sighed, feeling as if she stood on the edge of some new, unnameable horror. He spoke fast now, as if rushing through a painful task he had put off for months. “You could not possibly understand all of it—the years I have spent fretting over whether I had any right to hold this living, the ways I tried to smother my smouldering doubts by leaning on the authority of the church. Oh, Margaret, how I love the holy Church I am soon to be shut out of!”

He could not speak for a moment or two, and Margaret did not know what to say. The news felt as impossibly, terrifyingly alien as if he had told her he intended to convert to Islam.
“I was reading today of the two thousand clergymen who were ejected from their parishes in 1662,” he went on, with a faint, wobbly smile, “trying to steal some of their bravery for myself. But it is no use. I still feel every sharp stab of this grief.”

“But papa—have you truly thought this all the way through? It feels so terrible, so shocking,” Margaret burst out, tears spilling over. The steady foundation of her home, the image of her gentle, beloved father she had carried her whole life, was reeling and rocking right under her.

Her distress steeled Mr. Hale, and he swallowed down the dry, choking sobs that had been rising in his throat. He crossed to his bookshelf and pulled down a well-worn volume he had been re-reading for months, the book he said had given him the courage to take this step. “Listen, dear Margaret,” he said, slipping an arm around her waist. She gripped his hand tight in hers, but could not lift her tear-stained face, nor could she properly attend to the words he read, for the chaos of her own thoughts. “This is the soliloquy of a country parish clergyman just like me, written by Mr. Oldfield, who was minister of Carsington in Derbyshire more than a hundred and sixty years ago. His trials are long over. He fought the good fight.” He murmured those last two sentences to himself, then read aloud:
“When thou canst no longer continue in thy work without dishonour to God, discredit to religion, foregoing thy integrity, wounding conscience, spoiling thy peace, and hazarding the loss of thy salvation; in a word, when the conditions upon which thou must continue (if thou wilt continue) in thy employments are sinful, and unwarranted by the word of God, thou mayest, yea, thou must believe that God will turn thy very silence, suspension, deprivation, and laying aside, to His glory, and the advancement of the Gospel’s interest. When God will not use thee in one kind, yet He will in another. A soul that desires to serve and honour Him shall never want opportunity to do it; nor must thou so limit the Holy One of Israel, as to think He hath but one way in which He can glorify Himself by thee. He can do it by thy silence as well as by thy preaching; the laying aside as well as thy continuance in thy work. It is not pretence of doing God the greatest service, or performing the weightiest duty, that will excuse the least sin, though that sin capacitated or gave us the opportunity for doing that duty. Thou wilt have little thanks, O my soul! if, when thou art charged with corrupting God’s worship, falsifying thy vows, thou pretendest a necessity for it in order to a continuance in the ministry.”

As he read, and glanced over the pages he did not speak aloud, his own resolve strengthened, and he thought he could be brave and firm in doing what his conscience bid him. But when he finished, he heard Margaret’s low, convulsive sobs, and his courage dissolved in the sharp, keen sense of her suffering.
“Margaret, dear,” he said, pulling her closer, “think of the early martyrs. Think of the thousands who have suffered far worse for their conscience.”

“But father,” she said, lifting her flushed, wet face at last, “the martyrs suffered for the truth they believed. You—oh, dearest papa!”
“I suffer for conscience’ sake, my child,” he said, with a quiet dignity that only shook from the sharp, tender sensitivity of his character. “I must do what my heart tells me is right. I have borne this burden of self-reproach for years, long enough to have roused any mind less torpid and cowardly than mine.” He shook his head sadly. “Your poor mother’s fondest wish, which came true in the mocking, bitter way such over-great wishes often do—Sodom’s apples, crumbling to ash in your mouth—brought this crisis to a head not a month back. The bishop offered me another, better living then. If I had accepted it, I would have had to make a fresh declaration of conformity to the Liturgy at my institution. Margaret, I tried to do it. I tried to be content with turning down the preferment quietly, staying on here, strangling my conscience the same way I had strained and twisted it for years before. God forgive me for that.”

He rose and paced the room, murmuring low words of self-reproach Margaret was grateful not to overhear most of. At last he stopped. “Margaret, I come back to that old, sad truth. We must leave Helstone.”

“Yes. I see that. But when?”
“I already wrote to the bishop,” he said, sinking back into his usual depressed, distant tone now that he was speaking of practical arrangements. “I told him I intended to resign this vicarage. He has been endlessly kind, argued with me, tried to persuade me out of it, but nothing he said could shift me. It is all the same arguments I have already run through my own head, to no avail. I will take my deed of resignation to him myself, to bid him farewell. That will be a hard trial. But far worse, far far worse, will be parting from my people here. A curate named Mr. Brown has already been appointed to read prayers. He will come stay with us tomorrow. Next Sunday I will preach my farewell sermon.”

Margaret thought it would be far better to leave all at once, no lingering to drag out every last sting of pain, no chance to grow attached to the familiar walls and lanes all over again. She drew a deep, heavy breath. “What does mamma say?”

To her surprise, her father started pacing the room again, before he stopped to answer, looking wrung out. “Margaret, I am a poor coward after all. I cannot bear to give pain. I know your mother’s married life has never been half all she hoped for, all she had a right to expect. This will be such a terrible blow to her that I never had the heart, the strength, to tell her. But someone must tell her now,” he said, looking at his daughter with a wistful, helpless gaze.

Margaret was almost overpowered by the realization that her mother had no clue at all, even though every arrangement was nearly finalised. “Yes, of course she must be told. She will be shocked, that is certain.” A new thought struck her. “Where are we going to live?”

“Milton-Northern,” he answered, dull and indifferent, for he had seen that for all Margaret’s love was trying to soothe him, her own grief was still raw.

“Milton-Northern? That big smoky manufacturing town up in Darkshire?”
“Yes.”
“Why ever there, papa?”
“Because there I can earn bread for my family. Because no one there knows Helstone, no one here knows Milton. No one will ever remind me of the life I have lost.”

“Bread for our family? I thought you and mamma had independent means apart from the living,” Margaret stopped herself, seeing the dark, heavy gloom settle over his brow. But he read her concern in her face like a mirror, and forced himself to soften. “I will tell you everything, Margaret. Only help me break this to your mother. I will do anything but that part. The thought of her distress turns me sick with dread. I am going out all day tomorrow to say goodbye to Farmer Dobson and all the poor families on Bracy Common. Could you break the news to her tomorrow? Would you mind it very much?”

Margaret did mind it. She shrank from the task more than anything she had ever been asked to do in her life. She could not speak at first.
“You hate the thought, don’t you, Margaret?” her father said.

She conquered herself in that moment, and looked up at him with a bright, steady, brave face. “It is a painful thing, but it has to be done. I will do it as well as I can. You have so many far more painful things to get through yourself.”

Mr. Hale shook his head despondently, and squeezed her hand in gratitude. Margaret nearly burst into tears all over again, so she turned to practical plans to distract herself. “Tell me, what are our finances? You and mamma have about a hundred and twenty pounds a year of your own, don’t you, apart from the living? Aunt Shaw gave that to you, I know.”

“Yes. Seventy of that has always gone to Frederick, ever since he was exiled abroad. I do not know if he needs all of it,” he added, hesitating. “He must get some pay serving in the Spanish army.”

“Frederick will not go short,” Margaret said firmly. “He is in a foreign country, treated so unjustly by his own. That leaves a hundred a year. Could the three of us not live on that in some quiet, cheap little village in the south? I am sure we could manage.”

“No,” Mr. Hale said. “That would never work. I need something to do, to keep busy, so I do not sink into morbid, brooding thoughts. Besides, if I stayed in the south, every lane, every church I passed would remind me of Helstone. I could not bear it. And a hundred a year would not stretch far enough to give your mother all the small comforts she has been used to, and deserves. We have to go to Milton. It is settled. I can make far better decisions on my own, without the people I love arguing with me. I cannot stand objections. They make me too undecided.”

Margaret held her tongue. Compared to the terrible, life-altering change they were facing, it hardly mattered where they went.
“A few months ago, when my doubts grew too heavy to bear alone, I wrote to Mr. Bell,” he went on. “You remember Mr. Bell, Margaret? Frederick’s godfather, my old tutor at Oxford?”

“I never met him, but I know who he is. He is a Fellow at Plymouth College, right?”
“Yes. He was born in Milton-Northern, I believe, and he owns property there that has doubled and tripled in value now the town has grown into such a huge manufacturing hub. I knew he would sympathise, even if he has lived a soft, easy life at college and never had to face such a trial himself. It is all thanks to him we are going to Milton.”

“How so?”
“He has mills, houses, tenants there. Even though he hates the bustling, noisy place, he has to keep an eye on his investments. He tells me there is a very good opening for a private tutor there. Many of the mill owners are self-made men, painfully aware of their own gaps in education. Some are determined to learn classics and literature even as grown men, others want their children to get the education they never had. He has recommended me to a Mr. Thornton, one of his tenants, a very intelligent man, from what I have seen of his letters. In Milton, Margaret, we will find people and scenes so different from Helstone, we will never be reminded of what we left behind.”

Margaret recognised that secret motive all too well, from her own aching heart. The north was a foreign, bleak, discordant place, all she had ever heard of its smoky mills and rough population filled her with dread—but it was not Helstone. That was its only, and its greatest, recommendation.
“When do we leave?” she asked, after a short, heavy silence.

“I do not know exactly. I wanted to talk it over with you first. Your mother still knows nothing, but I think in a fortnight. Once my resignation is formally accepted, I will have no right to stay on in the vicarage.”
