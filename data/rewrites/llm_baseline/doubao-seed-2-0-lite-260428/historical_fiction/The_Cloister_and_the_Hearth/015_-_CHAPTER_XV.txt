Show me the woman who cannot play a part when the man she loves hangs in the balance. Show me the one who will not throw every scrap of her native wit to the task, and play it better than any court actor, to shield the heart that is dearer to her than her own life. On these desperate, fateful occasions, even the simplest, most unassuming girl finds herself gifted with a cleverness that outwits the wisest of men—Solomon himself could be led astray by the softest of the sex, when love gives her the lead.

No sooner had the last of the search party’s footsteps vanished over the threshold than Margaret slipped off the long oak chest where she had been lying, fully clad in her skirt, petticoat, and woolen stockings, with her linen nightgown pulled on over all. She heaved the heavy lid, bedclothes and all, propped it flush against the wall, then glided barefoot to the door, her breath catching in her throat, to listen. The tramp of heavy boots echoed slow past her father’s bedchamber, then faded step by step down the creaking wooden stairs to the ground floor.

That old chest held a secret no stranger on earth could ever spot. A section of the room’s floorboards had rotted through some months prior, and when Gerard had been called in to patch the damage, short of coin for new lumber, he had sawed a narrow slot in the chest’s base just wide enough to fit snugly over the gap, then used the discarded rotted boarding to mend the rest of the floor. The chest now rested fully on the rafters a full foot below the visible floorboards, making its true hidden depth a full two feet—though to any casual eye, it looked no deeper than a common clothes chest, barely a foot high, far too shallow to ever hide a man.

All was still in the quiet house. Margaret sank to her knees on the cold planks, breathed a quick, tearful prayer of thanks to the Virgin and all the saints, then leaned over the chest’s dark opening, whispering soft as a lullaby: “Gerard.”
No answer came.
She whispered a little louder, her voice thrumming with hard-won relief: “Gerard, all danger is past, thank the good Lord. You may rise now—only do be careful, make no noise.”
Still no reply.

A prickle of icy dread ran down her spine. She laid her hand on what she thought was his shoulder, and found it stone cold and utterly still. “Gerard!”
No movement.
“Oh, what is this?” she cried, and her hands flew wild over his face, his chest, his neck. She seized him by the shoulders, shook him hard, hauled him up half out of the chest—only for his heavy, limp form to slip right out of her trembling grasp and fall back into the dark wood, not like a living man, but like a dead weight.

A paralyzing horror settled over her. The lid had been shut tight the whole time. She had lain her full weight upon it for the long minutes the men searched the room. With a strength born of frenzy that no one would have thought the slight, quiet girl possessed, she hauled his full limp body out of the chest, dragged him across the floor to the casement window, and flung it wide open. The cool, sweet night air flooded in, silver moonlight spilling over their faces. She laid him there, face upturned to the wind and the moon.

His skin was the pale, ashen grey of ash left on a hearth after the last embers have died. His limbs were loose, utterly motionless. She pressed her fingers to the pulse in his neck, then to his heart. Horror of horrors. It was still. She had smothered him, trapped in that narrow dark space, under the weight of her own body on the closed lid.

The human mind reels from a calamity so sudden, so monstrous, it cannot grasp it at first. Gerard, who had climbed alive and laughing into that chest not five minutes before—how could he be dead? She called him every endearing name her heart had ever dared to whisper to itself, names she had never breathed aloud to any living soul. She kissed his cold cheeks, stroked his hair, pleaded, coaxed, begged him to open his eyes, to speak one word to her. No answer. Not a flicker of movement.

Then the poor girl, shaking so hard her teeth chattered, began to babble soft, foolish, broken little phrases over his ashy face, half pitying, half distraught. “Oh Gerard, I am so sorry you are dead. I am so sorry I killed you. Forgive me for not letting those wicked men take you. That would have been a thousand times better than this. Oh Gerard, I am so, so sorry for what I have done.”

Then her grief snapped, and she raved, half mad with the injustice of it. “No! No! This cannot be. If this is true, there is no God in heaven. It cannot be. My Gerard cannot be dead. I, Margaret, who love him more than my own life—how could I kill him? You see all this, O Lord. You know how I love him. He never even knew. I never told him how deep my love runs. If he did, he would speak to me now. He would not be so cruel, so deaf to his own Margaret. This is just a trick, is it not? A game to make me cry out and give you away to the men downstairs? But I will not. I love you too much to betray you. I will choke on my own screams before I make a sound that gives you away.” She seized her own throat hard, nails digging into her flesh, to choke back the wild, ragged scream clawing its way up her throat.

“Only speak one word, Gerard. Do not die without a word. Have mercy on me. Scold me. Curse me if you are angry. I deserve it. I am the idiot, the fool who killed the man she loved better than her own soul. I am a murderess. The worst wretch in all the world. Help! Help! I have murdered him!”

The shrieks tore out of her, wild, piercing, unearthly, ringing through the silent house like the tolling of a funeral bell. Downstairs, Dierich Brower and his men, just rising to take their leave, heard the sound. Every man froze, and stared at one another, wide-eyed.
