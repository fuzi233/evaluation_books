The rain lashed against the leaded glass of the millmaster's dining room, a rhythmic drumming that felt less like weather and more like the frantic, indifferent heartbeat of the industrial age. Inside, the air was thick with the scent of beeswax, damp wool, and the lingering, metallic tang of violence. Margaret Hale had not been gone five minutes when Mr. Thornton burst through the doorway, his face flushed with a heat that seemed to radiate from within, defying the chill of the storm.

"I could not come sooner," he panted, his voice tight with adrenaline, "the superintendent would—Where is she?"

His gaze swept the room, landing with almost fierce intensity on his mother, who was quietly rearranging the displaced chairs, her movements efficient and unflappable. She did not answer immediately.

"Where is Miss Hale?" he demanded again.

"Gone home," Mrs. Thornton replied, her tone clipped, cutting through the tension like a shears blade.

"Gone home!" The exclamation hung in the air, charged with disbelief.

"Yes. She was a great deal better. Indeed, I don't believe it was so very much of a hurt; only some people faint at the least thing."

"I am sorry she has gone home," Thornton muttered, pacing the rug with restless energy. "She could not have been fit for it."

"She said she was; and Mr. Lowe said she was. I went for him myself."

"Thank you, mother." He paused, extending a hand in a gesture of gratitude. She ignored it, her focus returning to the furniture.

"What have you done with your Irish people?" she asked, her voice devoid of warmth.

"Sent to the Dragon, for a good meal for them, poor wretches. And then, luckily, I caught Father Grady, and I've asked him in to speak to them, and dissuade them from going off in a body. How did Miss Hale go home? I'm sure she could not walk."

"She had a cab. Everything was done properly, even to the paying. Let us talk of something else. She has caused disturbance enough."

"I don't know where I should have been but for her."

"Are you become so helpless as to have to be defended by a girl?" Mrs. Thornton asked, her scorn dripping like acid.

Thornton flinched, the color rising higher in his cheeks. "Not many girls would have taken the blows on herself which were meant for me;—meant with right down goodwill, too."

"A girl in love will do a good deal," she replied, her voice hardening.

"Mother!"

He stepped forward, then halted, his chest heaving with the effort to suppress a surge of raw emotion. The atmosphere in the room shifted, becoming electric with an unspoken conflict. She was unsettled by the sheer force of his restraint, unsure of the tempest he was trying to dam. Was it anger? Joy? Pride? A panting mixture of desperate hope and terrifying doubt? She could not decipher the code written in his glowing eyes, yet the mere presence of such volatile feeling made her skin crawl.

She moved to the sideboard, opening a drawer to retrieve a duster, ostensibly to wipe a spot of eau de Cologne from the sofa arm. But she lingered with her back turned to her son far longer than necessity required. When she finally spoke, her voice sounded strained, artificial.

"You have taken some steps about the rioters, I suppose? You don't apprehend anymore violence, do you? Where were the police? Never at hand when they're wanted!"

"On the contrary," Thornton countered, his composure snapping back into place. "I saw three or four of them, when the gates gave way, struggling and beating about in fine fashion; and more came running up just when the yard was clearing. I might have given some of the fellows in charge then, if I had had my wits about me. But there will be no difficulty, plenty of people can identify them."

"But won't they come back to-night?"

"I'm going to see about a sufficient guard for the premises. I have appointed to meet Captain Hanbury in half an hour at the station."

"You must have some tea first."

"Tea! Yes, I suppose I must. It's half-past six, and I may be out for some time. Don't sit up for me, mother."

"You expect me to go to bed before I have seen you safe, do you?"

"Well, perhaps not." He hesitated. "But if I've time, I shall go round by Crampton, after I've arranged with the police and seen Hamper and Clarkson."

Their eyes locked. For a minute, the room seemed to hold its breath under the weight of their mutual scrutiny. Then she broke the silence.

"Why are you going round by Crampton?"

"To ask after Miss Hale."

"I will send. Williams must take the water-bed she came to ask for. He shall inquire how she is."

"I must go myself."

"Not merely to ask how Miss Hale is?"

"No, not merely for that. I want to thank her for the way in which she stood between me and the mob."

"What made you go down at all? It was putting your head into the lion's mouth!"

He glanced sharply at her, sensing the barrier she erected between them, knowing she knew nothing of the private exchange that had occurred in the drawing room. He deflected with another question.

"Shall you be afraid to be left without me, until I can get some of the police; or had we better send Williams for them now, and they could be here by the time we have done tea? There's no time to be lost. I must be off in a quarter of an hour."

Mrs. Thornton left the room. Her servants watched her departure with confusion; the woman whose commands were usually sharp as flint now moved with a strange uncertainty. Thornton remained in the dining room, ostensibly planning his route to the police office, but his mind was already drifting, tethered to the memory of Margaret. The future seemed dim and vague, overshadowed only by the phantom sensation of her arms around his neck, the soft clinging that had sent a flush racing across his cheeks.

The tea service was stiflingly silent, broken only by Fanny's incessant narration of her own terror—how she had been alarmed, then relieved, then sick with trembling dread.

"There, that's enough," Thornton said abruptly, rising from the table. "The reality was enough for me."

He reached for the door, but his mother's hand fell upon his arm, halting him.

"You will come back here before you go to the Hales'," she whispered, her voice low and fraught with anxiety.

Fanny, listening from the corner, pressed her lips together. *Why? Will it be too late to disturb them?*

"John, come back to me for this one evening. It will be late for Mrs. Hale. But that is not it. To-morrow, you will—— Come back to-night, John!"

It was rare for Mrs. Thornton to plead with her son; her pride was a fortress she rarely breached. Yet, she had never pleaded in vain.

"I will return straight here after I have done my business. You will be sure to enquire after them?—after her?"

Mrs. Thornton was not a talkative companion, nor a patient listener while her son was away. But upon his return, her senses sharpened, eager to dissect every detail of his actions, to verify the steps he had taken to fortify his position and protect those he deemed worthy. He saw his objective clearly now: punishment and suffering were the natural consequences for the rioters. Property must be protected, and the will of the proprietor enforced with the clean, sharp precision of a sword.

"Mother! You know what I have got to say to Miss Hale, to-morrow?"

The question struck her unexpectedly, breaking through her reverie. She looked up at him.

"Yes! I do. You can hardly do otherwise."

"Do otherwise! I don't understand you."

"I mean that, after allowing her feelings so to overcome her, I consider you bound in honour—"

"Bound in honour," he scoffed. "I'm afraid honour has nothing to do with it. 'Her feelings overcome her!' What feelings do you mean?"

"Nay, John, there is no need to be angry. Did she not rush down and cling to you to save you from danger?"

"She did!" he admitted.

"But, mother," he continued, stopping dead in his tracks directly before her, "I dare not hope. I never was faint-hearted before; but I cannot believe such a creature cares for me."

"Don't be foolish, John. Such a creature! Why, she might be a duke's daughter, to hear you speak. And what proof more would you have, I wonder, of her caring for you? I can believe she has had a struggle with her aristocratic way of viewing things; but I like her the better for seeing clearly at last. It is a good deal for me to say," Mrs. Thornton smiled slowly, tears welling in her eyes, "for after to-night, I stand second. It was to have you to myself, all to myself, a few hours longer, that I begged you not to go till to-morrow."

"Dearest mother!"

In that instant, love proved its inherent selfishness. He retreated into his own hopes and fears, casting a cold, creeping shadow over his mother's heart.
