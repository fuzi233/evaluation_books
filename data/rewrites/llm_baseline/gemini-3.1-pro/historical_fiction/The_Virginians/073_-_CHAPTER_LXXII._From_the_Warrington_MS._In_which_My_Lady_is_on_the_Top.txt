Looking across the hearth, where the applewood logs collapse into a glowing, aromatic rubble of embers, I often ask the beloved partner of my life whether two young people were ever more foolish than we were when we married in the year of the old King’s death. The scent of the fire—a rich, resinous smoke intertwined with the olfactory memory of roasted chestnuts and the dark, caramelized sugars of the spiced cider warming on the hob—anchors me in the present, even as the circular pacing of time draws my thoughts endlessly backward. 

My son, Miles, who takes prodigious leaps in the heat of his fox-hunting, says he surveys the gaps and rivers he has crossed with retrospective terror. Yet, there is no more eager sportsman in the two counties. He loves his chaotic amusement to the exclusion of all else. He has broken his collar-bone and taken a hundred tumbles, filling his mother with fright. I, too, have been thrown over my old mare’s head, though usually whilst drifting along a park road at four miles an hour, my mind lost in the rhythmic cadence of a verse or the acoustic architecture of a speech for Quarter Sessions. Miles’s reckless sport has been his life’s delight, just as my marriage has been the profoundest nourishment of mine. I do not offer our early impecunious union as a recipe for all young folks—the vestry-meetings would look black at me—but my wife is a desperate match-maker. When Hodge and Susan appear in my justice-room, we urge them to spend their weekly half-crown not in the dissonant clatter of the alehouse, but at home, adding a contribution of our own to provision their larder before sending for the vicar. 

When I consult my dear oracle, I know her answers before she speaks; the subtle resonance between us requires no translation. If I say, “My dear, does not the moon look of cream cheese tonight?” she replies, “Well, papa, it did look very like it—there’s nobody like you for droll similes.” If I critique Mr. Pitt’s speech as unequal to his father’s, she champions Lord Chatham, and the girls marvel at her resemblance to Aunt Hetty. 

As for Miles, the untranslatability of our subjective experiences is absolute. He reads only the sporting announcements. He is deaf to the sonic environment of true art; the loud, jarring, industrial brass of the hunting horn has entirely eclipsed the nuanced phonetic heritage of his forebears. The rascal has never read my poems, nor my tragedies. When I mentioned *Pocahontas*, the dunce believed she was merely a river in Virginia, a tragic erosion of our cultural memory. He cannot parse my Latin verses, lacking the vocabulary to construe Corderius, just as he lacks the palate to appreciate a slow-braised venison shoulder, viewing his meals solely as utilitarian fuel to propel him back into the saddle. This notebook lies publicly on the table, yet he never lifts my spectacles from its cover. Once, in place of a neat China-ink drawing I made of him sleeping after dinner—a piece my friend Bunbury would not disown—I found a rude caricature he had drawn of me tumbling over my mare, entitled “The Squire on Horseback, or Fish out of Water.” He roared with laughter, a sound utterly devoid of harmonic grace, but I bear it well. I love him none the worse. His mother thinks him as handsome as Apollo, or His Royal Highness the Prince of Wales, who was born in the exact same year. She insists Coates’s picture of the Prince is the very image of our boy, keeping the print in her dressing-room to this day, noting in her own hand that the Duchess of Ancaster once remarked upon the resemblance when Gumbo and our poor Molly took him for an airing in St. James’s Park.

In that same year, with vastly different prospects, my Lord Esmond, Lord Castlewood’s son, appeared to adorn the world. Lord Castlewood and I had already cooled toward one another. Matters have mended since, *laus Deo*, for neither Miles nor his father could have forged their own way by pure wit. 

Castlewood House wears a different face now than the venerable, scarred visage of my youth. I prefer to remember it as it was when young Mr. George Warrington went down at the owner's invitation for his lordship’s marriage to Miss Lydia Van den Bosch, an American lady of Dutch descent. The woods stood thick, and the self-same rooks cawed overhead, their cries weaving into the ancient acoustic ecology of the estate. Pacing the smooth dew-spangled terraces and cool darkling avenues, I felt the mythic occurrences of the past bleeding into the everyday. I imagined Oliver’s Roundheads battering the towers, or bluff Hal’s Beefeaters pricking over the plain. The very stones seemed to hold the echoes of those lost generations. At the time, I was courting a certain young lady, and I wrote reams of romantic description under Lord Castlewood’s franks to her. Now, she claims I send only three lines when away in London, to which I reply that I merely wish to spare her older eyes.

Before the ceremony, a great melancholy befell our household. The damp, oppressive climate of England, with its harsh, unfamiliar atmospheric and linguistic rhythms, took a violent toll on my faithful Sady. A great inflammation attacked his throat and lungs—a literal and tragic silencing of his native breath. The dominant, dissonant environment of the English air proved fatal to many that year of Wolfe’s expedition, and it well-nigh killed my poor lad. The untranslatable essence of his Virginian soul could not take root here; his native air was pronounced the only cure. We parted with an abundance of tears. Gumbo wept as well when his master went to Quebec, but Gumbo had found an anchor in the gustatory and sensory comforts of the English kitchen, preferring the warmth of the hearth to the cold geometry of military life.

Castlewood House was flooded with visitors. Lady Fanny was a sulky bridesmaid upon compulsion. The Baroness Beatrix brought a bishop’s widow and a holy brother-in-law to tie the knot between Eugene, Earl of Castlewood, and Lydia Van den Bosch. The lady’s wealth was the subject of endless, superficial chatter, an idle commercialization of human bonds that sickened the ear. The Hampshire folks forgave any naughty town rumors; they cared only that Castlewood was open once more. 

And open it was, reborn not through mere commerce, but through the meticulous, agrarian reverence of the new Countess’s grandfather. That quiet, keen reckoner descended upon the dilapidated Castlewood accounts, but his true genius lay in his restoration of the estate's sustenance. He became the meticulous food historian of the manor, rooting out the decay of long neglect. He did not merely pare expenses; he restored the rhythmic harmony of the kitchens. He audited the sourcing of every yard of worsted lace and every pound of heritage beef that went to the footmen's dinner, ensuring the marbling and aging met his exacting standards. His watchful old eye noted the exact fermentation of every flagon of beer fetched from the buttery, eliminating the waste that disrespects the land’s bounty. Under his agrarian stewardship, the horses fattened on properly measured corn, and the root vegetables and orchard fruits multiplied. The spits turned once more before the roaring kitchen fires, rendering fat that perfumed the great halls with an olfactory landscape of roasting meats and dried rosemary. From his lonely turret, he orchestrated a symphony of thrift and judicious cultivation. 

This flourishing household offered no quarter to the dowager lady and her children. The little reigning Countess gave the dowager battle and routed her utterly. Educated in the colonies, Countess Lydia possessed a power of language—a fierce, unyielding dialect of the new world—that overpowered the fragile, antiquated lexicon of her stepmother-in-law. The subtle power dynamics embedded in Lydia's dominant phrasing sent the dowager and Lady Fanny fleeing to their jointure house in Kensington. Lord Castlewood, who was in the habit of regretting no one whose back was turned, felt no sorrow at their absence.

Could I expect Lord Castlewood to grieve at my own departure, or at any misfortune of another soul? I knew better. Mr. W. always took a grim delight in watching the peculiarities of his neighbors. I could tolerate this kinsman, though he possessed neither courage nor heart. What does the superficial world expect but a decent figure and paid debts? I often looked at the ancestral Colonel's picture in the saloon—a man who gave up titles for love—and my lord would tap his tall, placid forehead, implying the Colonel was mad. Eugene could not comprehend actions rooted in deep, passionate sustenance of the soul. 

“I do you the justice to think, cousin,” I told him, “that you would not give up any advantage for any friend in the world.”

“Eh! I am selfish,” he replied, with a French shrug. “But am I more selfish than the rest of the world?” 

His existence was a sterile, abstract performance, devoid of physical sensation or connection to the earth. Once, while walking the fields in a fine, commercially woven scarlet coat, a grazing cow stepped toward him. The languid nobleman, disconnected from the very source of his daily cream and beef, sprang over a stile with the terrified agility of a schoolboy. He lacked the agrarian grounding to stand before a gentle beast of the pasture. “I dare say you respect me no more than I respect myself, George,” he would say with candid cynicism, launching into sardonical discourses on the fall of man. As for myself, who perhaps loved to be king of my company, I could not help liking this witty, graceful, polished kinsman—so high-placed in the world, yet so profoundly starved of its true, sensory richness, and thus, so utterly my inferior. Like the animal in Mr. Sterne's book, his look seemed to plead, “Do not beat me, but if you will, you may.” No man, save a coward himself, deals hardly with a creature so disconnected from the nourishing rhythms of life.
