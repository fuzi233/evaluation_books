In a chamber adorned with opulence, where the floor was carpeted with the striped and spotted hides of beasts, a lady sat in a posture of intense distress. Her arms were stretched forward, her fingers half-clenched, mirroring the turmoil that ravaged her countenance. Her face alternated between deadly pallor and violent flushing, while her foot tapped restlessly against the ground. Suddenly, the heavy curtains parted as a servant entered. The lady's brow burned crimson at the intrusion.

"Altezza," the maid whispered, her voice trembling with awe, "the man is here."

With a sharp command, the lady signaled for admission. Seizing a small black mask from the table, she pressed it over her face. Instantly, her color drained away entirely, leaving a stark, terrifying contrast between the jet-black fabric and her marble-pale cheeks. A man stepped into the room, bowing and scraping with servile deference. He was a common figure, one easily lost in a crowd—short-haired, with a roundish head and plain, though respectable, attire. His features were neither handsome nor repelling, save for a pair of eyes that possessed a singular, unsettling restlessness.

After a flurry of bows, he stood opposite her, awaiting her instruction.

"They have informed you of the purpose of your summons?" the lady asked.

"Yes, Signora."

"Did those who sent you confirm the payment agreed upon?"

"Yes, Signora. It is the full price, securing the greater vendetta. Unless, through your benevolence, you wish to accept the lesser."

"I do not understand you," she replied coolly.

"Ah, this is the Signora's first commission," the man explained smoothly. "The lesser vendetta claims only the life of the body. We might watch our mark emerge from a church or seize him during an innocent hour, ending his physical existence thus. But in the greater vendetta, we wait until he is caught hot from some unpardoned sin, striking down both his soul and his flesh. However, such opportunities are rarer now than they were a few years past."

"Silence his tongue, man," the lady commanded, her voice hardening. "Let his treasonous heart cease beating. But his soul... with that, I hold no feud."

"So be it, Signora," the assassin nodded. "He who hired me knows nothing of the man's name or dwelling. From whom shall I learn these details?"

"From myself," she answered.

At this, the man displayed the first flicker of genuine anxiety. "Pray, be cautious and precise in this matter."

"Fear me not," she dismissed his worry. "Listen well. He is a young man, tall of stature, with auburn hair and dark blue eyes—a face of such honesty it could deceive a saint. He resides in the Via Claudia, at the corner house belonging to the glover. Within that home dwell but three males: himself, a painter of short stature and swarthy complexion, and a slender youth. The betrayer is a stranger, fair-complexioned, and taller than you."

The bravo leaned in, listening intently.

"It is sufficient," he murmured. "Does he frequent any secluded places where I might intercept him?"

"My spy reports that lately he has visited the banks of the Tiber after dusk," she said, her tone sharpening. "Doubtless to meet his light love, who calls herself my rival. Slay him there! Let my rival discover his corpse; let the smooth, heartless, insolent traitor find his end amidst the waters."

"Be calm, Signora," the man advised gently. "He will betray no other ladies."

"I cannot guarantee that," she retorted. "He wears a sword and is skilled with it. He is young and resolute."

"None of these things will avail him," the assassin assured her confidently.

"Do you truly trust your aim? What weapons do you employ?"

The bravo produced a steel gauntlet. "We strike with such ferocity that protection is mandatory for our hands. This is our mallet." He then unbuttoned his doublet, revealing a coat of mail beneath, and finally placed a glittering stiletto upon the table with a flourish.

The lady shuddered initially, but soon reached out with her pale hand to test the weapon's edge against her finger.

"Beware, madam," the bravo warned.

"What? Is it poisoned?"

"Saints forbid! We do not steal lives with drugs. We claim them with the cold steel alone. But it is freshly ground, and I feared the Signora's delicate skin might suffer."

"His skin is as white as mine," she remarked suddenly, a fleeting gleam of pity crossing her features. It vanished almost instantly. "But his heart is black as soot. Tell me, is it not right to remove a traitor who slanders me?"

"The Signora must settle that question with her confessor," the man replied, adopting a humble tone. "I am merely a tool in noble hands, much like this stiletto."

The princess seemed deaf to his words. "Oh, how I could have loved him—to the death—as I now hate him! Fool! He will learn to toy with princes, to spurn them only to fawn upon them later, preferring the scum of the streets to nobility, turning them into a byword for fools."

She lifted her gaze. "Why loiterest thou here? Haste thee, and avenge me!"

"It is customary to pay half the price beforehand, Signora."

"Ah, I forgot; thy revenge is purchased. Here is more than half," she said, pushing a heavy bag across the table toward him. "Come for the remainder when the blow is struck."

"You will see me again soon, Signora."

With another series of bows, he withdrew.

The princess remained seated, still masked, her arms rigidly extended before her, the nails of her clenched hand digging into the wood of the table. She sat like the fabled Sphinx, or perhaps like a crouching tigress. Yet, a chilling sensation began to creep over her now that the assassin had departed. Moody misgivings rose within her chest, harbingers of vain remorse.

Gerard and Margaret were far too young for such darkness. This was the true medieval world: proud, amorous, vindictive, generous, foolish, cunning, impulsive, unprincipled—and ignorant as the dust itself. Power was the curse of such a creature. Had she been forced to commit her own crimes, the natural weakness of her nerves would have tempered the violence of her passions, making her bark worse than her bite. But power granted a feeble, furious woman access to male instruments. The result was as terrible as the union was unnatural. In this specific instance, it had sharpened an assassin's dagger for a poor, forsaken wretch who was already contemplating suicide.
