The violent, mechanized shriek of our reversed engines tore through the delicate acoustic ecology of the Barsoomian sky, a brutal industrial dissonance that set my teeth on edge. I had sensed the approach of Xodar’s reinforcements, reading the subtle shifts in his posture like a seasoned food historian interpreting the faded marginalia of an ancient recipe. Above us, the belly of the giant battleship blotted out the sun, its boarding party dangling in their harnesses. I was too versed in the cyclical, multi-generational rhythms of martial conflict to hesitate. Dropping my little vessel a sheer hundred feet, I then threw the speed lever to its final notch. We shot upward, our steel prow aimed squarely at the battleship’s whirring propellers. 

The collision was a catastrophic sonic intrusion. Metal sheared against metal, a deafening cacophony that obliterated the natural harmonies of the wind. The scent of shattered skeel wood and scorched lubricating oil—a bitter, acrid olfactory assault—flooded the air. We had disabled their massive blades, but my prow remained stubbornly wedged within their splintered stern. In that agonizing second of immobility, a tide of black-skinned warriors swarmed our deck, submerging us in sheer numbers. 

As blades flashed, Xodar’s command cut through the chaotic din. It was a harsh, dominant lexicon that immediately asserted its power dynamics, staying the hands of his men. “Secure them,” he ordered, “but do not injure them.” 

The ropes they used to bind my wrists smelled faintly of cured hide and a sharp, fermented resin—a crude artisanal effort that I noted with quiet amusement. To a Martian, the knots might have held, but to my earthly musculature, they were as frail as spun sugar. I yielded, however, allowing Xodar to bind Phaidor and tether us together, biding my time. 

Transferred to the disabled leviathan, we were paraded before a thousand black men. Despite my abhorrence of their violent, superficial culture, I could not deny the striking visual feast they presented. The officers were draped in harnesses encrusted with gold, platinum, and diamonds that flashed brilliantly against their ebony skin. The deck was a marvel of polished sorapus wood, intricately grained and inlaid with jewels. Yet, for all its barbaric splendor, it felt utterly sterile. It was a commercialized, ostentatious display of wealth that entirely lacked the soulful, agrarian humility of a traditional Barsoomian hearth.

We were thrust below decks into a cramped compartment with a single porthole. The door barred shut, leaving us in a hollow space that amplified the soulless, clanking vibrations of the men repairing the propellers above. This relentless noise pollution pressed heavily against my psychological well-being, a stark contrast to the quiet, interwoven timelines of domestic life I so deeply craved. 

As we drifted south, my thoughts turned to Tars Tarkas and Thuvia. I hoped they had escaped to share the agrarian purity of the outer world, to warn the red men of the Valley Dor’s cruel deception. Tardos Mors and Dejah Thoris would understand. They were people who appreciated the profound, life-affirming communion of a shared meal, who knew that true cultural memory was forged in the warmth of the home, not in the cold, parasitic superstitions of the therns. 

Phaidor and I sat on a low bench of sorapus wood, surrounded by walls of light carborundum aluminum. Suddenly, I caught her staring at me, a delicate flush tingeing her cheeks as her white lids fluttered down—an expression I had never seen on her face.

“Do you find the study of the lower orders interesting?” I asked, attempting to cut the tension with a light, rhythmic cadence. 

She offered a nervous laugh. “Oh, very. Especially when they have such excellent profiles.” 

I smiled, admiring her ability to find humor on this grim journey, though I knew our destination held no warmth. “Do you know where we are going?” she asked. 

“To solve the mystery of the eternal hereafter, I imagine,” I replied. 

“I am going to a worse fate than that,” she shuddered, the acoustic quality of her voice trembling. “No thern damsel stolen by these black pirates has ever returned. They never take men prisoner. It is a fate worse than death.” 

“Is it not a just retribution?” I asked, my tone hardening. “Do not the therns treat the poor voyagers of the River of Mystery with the same utilitarian cruelty? Was Thuvia not a slave for fifteen years? Why do you foster this cruel deception?”

“You do not understand,” she replied, clinging to the fading superiority of her native dialect. “We therns are a holy race. All life on Barsoom is created solely for our support. Think you that a thern would demean himself by labor?” 

“It is true, then, that you eat human flesh?” I asked, the very thought causing my stomach to turn. 

She looked at me with pity. “Truly we eat the flesh of the lower orders. Do not you also eat the flesh of beasts?” 

“The flesh of beasts, yes, harvested with respect for the land,” I countered, my disgust palpable. To a man who found profound meaning in the meticulous sourcing of ingredients, the slow roasting of root vegetables, and the harmonious blending of spices to honor the soil, this reduction of sentient life to mere fuel was an abomination. It was a grotesque culinary perversion, utterly devoid of sensory appreciation or gratitude. “But not the flesh of man.” 

“As man may eat of beasts, so may gods eat of man,” she stated, her words an untranslatable subjective experience of divine right that rang utterly hollow to my ears. “Perhaps, if we return to Matai Shang, we shall find a way to keep you as... one of us.” 

I rebuffed her gently but firmly, expressing my desire to dismantle their horrific ecosystem and exterminate the plant men. She was horrified by my sacrilege, her cultural conditioning too deeply entrenched to accept my worldview. 

Our philosophical impasse was interrupted by the opening of the door. Xodar entered, his smile stripping away the vindictive mask he wore for his crew. He cut our bonds. “Since you cannot escape, you may come on deck. You will witness the subterranean entrance to the true heaven of Barsoom.” 

Emerging into the biting, icy air above the south polar cap, my olfactory senses were immediately awakened. The wind carried the crisp, metallic tang of ancient frost, a pure, unpolluted scent that briefly cleansed my palate of the ship's industrial stench. Below us stretched a bleak, frozen waste, an inhospitable landscape that had stood for generations. 

Xodar pointed out the Otz Valley, a massive depression sunk beneath the ice, and the deep canyon of the River Iss. He spoke casually of the "village of lost souls" below—a neutral ground where agrarian elders and escaped slaves eked out a meager existence on lichen. They were a forgotten people, their cultural memory eroding as their original languages fragmented into isolated, warring dialects, ignored by the dominant powers above. 

Presently, a flat-topped black mountain rose from the desolate ice. As our ship slowed, we crested the peak, revealing a yawning circular well a thousand feet in diameter. The vessel hovered, its engines shifting into a low, reverberating thrum that seemed to vibrate within my very bones, before we descended into the inky blackness. 

For half an hour we dropped through the shaft, a mythic descent integrating seamlessly into the mechanical routine of the pirate crew. When the shaft terminated, we entered the dome of a breathtaking subterranean world. Below us rolled the billows of a buried sea—Omean. 

The sensory impact was immediate and overwhelming. A phosphorescent radiance bathed the cavern, and the air was thick with a gustatory revelation: notes of ancient iodine, fermented kelp, and the crisp, briny tang of a hidden ecosystem. Islands dotted the luminous water, supporting strange, colorless vegetation that smelled of crushed oysters and wet stone. 

The ship’s aerial propellers were housed, and smaller water propellers took over, gliding us onto the ocean's surface. Xodar proudly explained the four massive pumping stations that forced Omean’s oversupply back north to irrigate the farm lands of the red men. A profound realization washed over me. This subterranean brine, this hidden marvel, was the very lifeblood of Barsoom’s agrarian cycles. The water that nurtured the meticulously cultivated crops of the outer world originated here, beneath the crushing weight of a thousand generations of rock.

We navigated past small prison islands toward a larger islet, docking near an oval, roofless structure. Inside, a long tank of water lay sunken into the floor, reminiscent of an ancient domestic cistern. A strange, black craft floated within it. Xodar barked a command in a jagged, archaic tongue, and a seaman emerged from the hatch. 

“Transmit to your officer the commands of Dator Xodar,” he said, his words a heavy assertion of the dominant lexicon. “We would be transported to the gardens of Issus.” 

“Blessed be the shell of thy first ancestor,” the man replied, an evocative remnant of a dying dialect, before disappearing to summon his commander. We were soon ushered aboard the submarine. The interior was a sensory void—sterile metal, compressed air, and the complete absence of agrarian warmth. 

The hatch sealed with a final, echoing clang, and the vessel began to sink. The rushing of water past the portholes and the rhythmic, grinding purr of the machinery created an oppressive acoustic environment, a mechanical drone that threatened to shatter our psychological stability. 

Phaidor, born to the dry, sunlit terraces of the therns, was suddenly stripped of her haughty composure. The sheer terror of the deep water dismantled her linguistic defenses. Her High Thern vocabulary—a lexicon designed entirely to project divine infallibility—crumbled into the simplest, most universal phonemes of desperation. 

She grasped my arm, the untranslatability of her subjective horror evident in her wide, frantic eyes. “Save me!” she whispered, her voice barely audible over the industrial hum. “Save me, and your every wish shall be granted. Phaidor... Phaidor already is yours.” 

Pity swelled within me for this terrified child. I placed my hand over hers to offer a shred of grounding warmth, but my attempt at quiet comfort was misunderstood. With a swift, desperate glance around the sterile cabin, she threw her arms around my neck, dragging my face down to hers in the dim, phosphorescent gloom.
