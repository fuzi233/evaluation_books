Yes, friend, we must finally tally the accounts. Having briefly recounted the fates of those with whom we have walked, it is time for us to shake hands and part.

First, let us turn to the clergy—the modest, long-neglected servants of the parish. Come forward, Merit! Malone answers the call instantly; he recognizes his own portrait. Yet, Peter Augustus, we must pass you by. It simply cannot be done. The public, with its capricious tastes, refuses the unvarnished truth. A raw, honest tale of your deeds would send them into hysterics, screaming for sal-volatile and burnt feathers. They would dismiss reality as "untrue" and "inartistic," casting it aside like refuse, while adoring your own fabrications—the spurious little wretch you created, petted as sweet and natural, whereas the honest, lawful child you actually were receives only cuffs. So, you must step down, Peter; the world demands the fiction, not the fact.

Make way for Mr. Sweeting.

Here he arrives, arm-in-arm with the weightiest and most splendid woman in Yorkshire, Mrs. Sweeting, formerly Miss Dora Sykes. United under the happiest auspices, following Sweeting's induction to a comfortable living and Sykes's generous provision, they lived long and happily. Beloved by their flock and a circle of friends, they remained together until the end. The varnish on their story was applied with exquisite care.

Advance, Mr. Donne.

This gentleman turned out remarkably well—far surpassing expectations. He married a sensible, quiet, lady-like woman who became the making of him. An exemplary domestic character and an active parish priest, he burned the outside of his cup and platter with polishing powder, tending the altar furniture with the zeal of an upholsterer. His school, church, and parsonage were models erected solely by his unassisted efforts. While he lacked consistency in faith, mastering the art of begging, he was useful in his day. By his unique grasp of plan, he secured funds from everyone—from the shoeless cottage brats to crowned dukes—even extracting five pounds from the close-fisted Queen Charlotte and two guineas from her profligate eldest son. Armed in brazen mail, he possessed the audacity to demand twice what he had received yesterday. People gave to escape his presence, yet he did some good.

As for Malone, his premature disappearance from Briarfield remains a mystery, a secret stolen from curious eyes to preserve the elegance of the narrative. In his stead came Mr. Macarthey, an Irish curate who brought credit to his country where Malone had brought disgrace. Decent, decorous, and conscientious, he contrasted sharply with Peter Augustus's boisterous nature. His schools flourished like green bay trees. However, humanity showed in his faults: a tea invitation from a Dissenter unhinged him for a week, and the sight of a Quaker wearing a hat in church caused strange havoc in his economy. Yet, sane, rational, and charitable, he served faithfully.

I must confess a criminal remissness regarding the would-be assassin of Mr. Robert Moore. A dance of law and gospel, dungeon and dock, awaited us, but I found facts exonerating me entirely. The murderer, Michael Hartley—a half-crazed weaver, a frantic Antinomian and mad leveller—was never caught, nor pursued. The magistrates, seeing Moore himself lying still on his couch, laughing in his sleeve with a sneering foreign face, prudently resolved to let the matter drop. Moore knew his killer, and Briarfield knew it too. Hartley died of delirium tremens a year later, and Robert gave his wretched widow a guinea to bury him.

Winter has fled; spring has arrived with beamy and shadowy flights, culminating in mid-June, 1812. The air burns with deep azure and red gold, fitting the age. Nineteenth-century adolescence wantsons in giants; the Titan boy uproots mountains. In Russia, Bonaparte rides with six hundred thousand souls, marching on Moscow, trusting in the snow-clouds and barbarian stoicism of the Cossacks. These elements—air, fire, water—are the terrible archangels of Jehovah, pouring out vials of wrath. The earth is scorched, the sea turns to blood, and mountains vanish.

Across the Channel, Lord Wellington assumes the reins in Spain. He takes Badajos, fights Vittoria, captures Pampeluna, storms San Sebastian, and wins Salamanca. Yet to you, men of Manchester, Lord Wellington is merely a decayed old gentleman, a dotard mocked for his age. You scoff at his loss of vigor, unaware that your scorn can never break his grand old heart.

But let us hold a peace congress and vent our venom quietly. On June 18, 1812, the Orders in Council were repealed, and blockaded ports opened. Yorkshire and Lancashire shook with shouts; ringers cracked bells in Briarfield that remain dissonant to this day. Merchants and manufacturers dined at Stilbro' in a state unfit for wives to behold. Liverpool snorted like a river-horse; American merchants threatened with apoplexy bled themselves, eager to dive into speculation. Stocks soared, warehouses emptied, wages rose. The good times seemed truly come, and many solid fortunes were realized in that single month.

When a province rejoices, the humblest inhabitant tastes festal feeling. Caroline Helstone, dressing in her neatest muslin, traveled to Fieldhead to supervise millinery preparations for a great event, leaving the final choices to her unimpeachable taste. She selected robes and veils for the altar, largely ignoring the bride's impracticable mood.

Louis had foreseen difficulties and found them. His mistress, exquisitely provoking, had delayed their union day by day, week by week, until a tempest-shock brought her to a fixed point. Conquered by love and bound by a vow, she pined like a denizen of deserts. In his absence, she sat alone, speaking little, eating less. She abdicated all authority without a struggle; "Go to Mr. Moore, ask Mr. Moore," was her answer when pressed for orders. Thus vanquished, she developed a dependency akin to a premier whose sovereign's incapacity had forced her to assume power.

Fortune had destined Caroline another part than bridesmaid. Returning home to water her plants, specifically a rose-tree in a quiet green nook, she climbed a fragment of sculptured stone to survey the view. Beyond the wall lay lonely fields, dusk trees, and a solitary lane. Over the moors, bonfires kindled, their blue smoke soft, flames bright. Above them twinkled a silver point—the star of love.

Caroline was not unhappy, yet as she gazed, she sighed. A hand circled her waist. "I am looking at Venus, mamma," she whispered, expecting Mrs. Pryor. Instead, she encountered a dark, manly visage. Dropping her watering-pot, she stepped down.

"I have been sitting with 'mamma' an hour," said the intruder. "Where have you been?"

"To Fieldhead. Shirley is as naughty as ever, Robert. She sits alone, neither saying yes nor no. I cannot tell if she is melancholy or nonchalant. What Louis will make of her, I cannot tell. For my part, if I were a gentleman, I would not dare undertake her."

Robert dismissed these fears. "Never mind them. They were cut out for each other. Louis will manage her if anyone can." He had sought her out to ask about the ringing bells.

"For the repeal of your terrible law," Caroline replied. "Are you pleased?"

"Yesterday evening I was packing books for a sea-voyage to Canada. I was going to leave you."

"To leave *me*?" Her little fingers fastened on his arm, affrighted. "Not now... Examine my face. Is the despair of parting legible there?"

She looked into an illuminated countenance, beaming despite the dusk. "Will the repeal do you good? Much good, immediate good?"

"It saves me. I shall not turn bankrupt, give up business, or leave England. I shall not be poor. I can pay my debts. My warehouses will clear, commissions will follow. This day lays a broad, firm foundation for my fortunes."

Caroline devoured his words, holding his hand. "You are saved? Your heavy difficulties are lifted?"

"They are lifted. I breathe. I can act."

"At last! Oh, Providence is kind!" She looked up devoutly. "And I also, for your sake!"

"Now I can take more workmen, give better wages... Now I can have a house—a home which I can truly call mine—and now..." He paused, then resumed. "Now I can think of marriage, now I can seek a wife."

This was no moment for speech. "Will Caroline, who meekly hopes to be forgiven as she forgives, pardon all I have made her suffer? All that long pain I wickedly caused? Will she forget what she knows of my poor ambition, my sordid schemes? Will she let me expiate these things? Prove that, as I once deserted cruelly, I can now love faithfully?"

His hand remained in hers, receiving a gentle pressure. "Is Caroline mine?"

"Caroline is yours."
