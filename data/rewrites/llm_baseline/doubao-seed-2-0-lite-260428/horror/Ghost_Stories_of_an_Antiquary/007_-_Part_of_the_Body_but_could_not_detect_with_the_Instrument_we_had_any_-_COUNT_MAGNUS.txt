I shall not tell you by what strange winding of chance the papers that make up this account came into my possession until the very last page of this telling. But first, I must explain exactly what form these documents take.

They are half the raw draft of the kind of garrulous, detail-heavy travel book that flooded print in the 1840s and 50s—of which Horace Marryat’s *Journal of a Residence in Jutland and the Danish Isles* is a perfect example. These volumes chased little-known corners of the European continent, filled with woodcuts and steel engravings, tips for inns and stagecoaches, pages and pages of transcribed chats with sharp-tongued innkeepers and garrulous peasants. They were cozy, unassuming, the sort of book you could flip through on a rainy evening with no sense you were about to stumble on something that would curdle the blood. But as the writing progressed, the travel notes fell away entirely, leaving nothing but a tight, breathless record of a single terrible personal experience, written almost up to the very eve of the events that ended it.

Its author was a man named Wraxall. All I know of him comes from these pages. He was past middle age, of independent means, entirely alone in the world, no permanent home in England, drifting from hotel to boarding house to hotel. He had published one prior book, a memoir of a holiday in Brittany, that I have never been able to trace—no bibliography lists it, so it must have come out anonymously or under a pseudonym. I judge he missed being elected a Fellow of Brasenose College, Oxford, by a hair. His one fatal flaw, the one for which he paid far too high a price, was unbridled, insatiable curiosity.

In the summer of 1863, he set out to write his new book on Scandinavia, a place almost entirely unknown to ordinary English tourists of the time. He chased down old Swedish histories and family memoirs, secured letters of introduction to minor nobility, and sailed for Stockholm. After a few weeks there, a local antiquary passed him a tip: a hoard of untouched family papers was stored in the herrgård, or manor house, of Råbäck, tucked deep in the forests of Vestergothland, and the owners had granted him full permission to study them.

Råbäck stands almost exactly as it does in Dahlenberg’s 1694 *Suecia antiqua et moderna* engraving: a red brick manor with stone trim, built soon after 1600 by a scion of the great De la Gardie family, ringed by ancient gnarled timber that blocks the wind and dims the summer sun. Past its walled kitchen garden, a dark close wood crowds the shore of one of the hundreds of small, still kettle lakes that pit the region, and beyond the park wall a steep knoll of bare rock rises, its thin soil dotted with firs. At the top of that knoll sits the parish church, hemmed in by tall dark spruce trees that swallow light even at midday. Its low aisles are crammed with painted wooden pews and galleries, the western gallery holds a gaily painted old organ with silver pipes, and the flat ceiling is covered by a 17th century Last Judgement so lurid it makes the skin crawl: leaping flames, falling cities, burning ships, screaming souls, and broad grinning brown demons reaching for them. Brass coronae hang from the rafters, the pulpit is carved to look like a dollhouse crammed with tiny painted cherubs and saints, and a stand holding three hourglasses is hinged to the preacher’s desk. No English church ever smelled like that one: of cold stone, old candle wax, and something sharp, like rot.

The strangest addition juts off the north aisle: an eight-sided mausoleum built for the De la Gardie family, lit by oval windows, topped by a black copper domed roof and the lumpy pumpkin-shaped spire that 17th century Swedish architects were so fond of. There is no door connecting it to the church. It has its own set of stone steps and a heavy oak portal on the north side, half-hidden by overgrown ivy. The path down from the churchyard to the village inn takes barely four minutes.

On his first day at Råbäck, Wraxall found the church door propped open, and scribbled down all those notes. He could not get into the mausoleum, though—he could only peer through the keyhole to make out marble effigies, engraved copper sarcophagi, and a riot of armorial carvings that made him desperate to get inside. The De la Gardie family received him with great kindness, but he chose to stay at the village inn rather than the manor, to avoid straining his faltering Swedish and give himself leave to wander unobserved at all hours.

The family papers he had come to study were perfect for his book: stacks of personal letters, diaries, neatly kept 17th century account books full of vivid, anecdotal detail about the district. The founding owner of the house, Magnus De la Gardie, loomed over all of them: a brutal, iron-willed man who had crushed a local peasant uprising with unsparing force, executing ringleaders and meting out punishments so harsh the stories of them had lingered for two hundred and fifty years. His portrait hung in the manor hall: Wraxall wrote that it was not a face of beauty or kindness, but of terrible, almost preternatural power—Count Magnus was, by all accounts, one of the ugliest men who ever walked the earth. That night, he walked back to the inn at dusk, the northern summer light still pale and golden, and jotted in his notebook: I must ask the sexton to let me into that mausoleum. I saw him tonight, standing on the north steps, fumbling with the lock.

The next morning, he cornered the innkeeper to dig for local traditions about the old count. The man would not meet his eye. He spoke of the wooden horse in the manor yard, where tenants who were late to work on the count’s demesne days were tied and flogged and branded. He spoke of the families who lived on land the count wanted, whose farmhouses burned to the ground on the coldest midwinter nights, with every soul inside, no trace of an arsonist ever found. But what he kept circling back to, the thing that made his voice drop to a hoarse whisper, was that Count Magnus had gone on the Black Pilgrimage, and brought something back with him.

The moment Wraxall asked what the Black Pilgrimage was, the innkeeper panicked. He mumbled that he had been called away to Skara, ducked out the door, and did not come back until long after dark.

Wraxall turned back to the manor papers, and soon forgot the exchange poring over 1705-1710 letters between Sophia Albertina in Stockholm and her cousin at Råbäck. When he finished that stack, he reached for the nearest unopened volume on the shelf, and found it was not an account book, but a 16th century manuscript full of alchemical treatises: The Book of the Phoenix, The Book of the Thirty Words, The Book of the Toad, the *Turba Philosophorum*. Tucked into its middle pages, on a blank leaf, was a few lines scrawled in Count Magnus’s own hand, titled *Liber nigræ peregrinationis*—the Book of the Black Pilgrimage. The text read: If any man desires to obtain a long life, if he would obtain a faithful messenger and see the blood of his enemies, it is necessary that he should first go into the city of Chorazin, and there salute the prince… One word had been scrubbed out, not well enough for Wraxall to miss it: *aëris*, of the air. Below it, a single line in Latin: *Quære reliqua hujus materiei inter secretiora*. The rest of this matter is hidden among the more private things.

Wraxall stared at the portrait of Magnus in the hall for a long time that evening before heading home, his head full of the grim old noble, half-mystic half-monster, who had dabbled in magic long buried. He walked the path from the manor in a daze, not noticing the scent of pine, not seeing the pale light glint off the lake, until he almost tripped over a weathered gravestone and realized he was already at the churchyard gate. He found himself muttering out loud, half to himself: Count Magnus, there you are. I should dearly like to see you. A clatter from inside the church—some woman cleaning dropped a metal candlestick on the stone floor—snapped him back to his senses. He laughed at himself for talking to a tomb, and walked down to the inn for supper.

That night, the innkeeper brought the parish deacon to meet him, and they arranged a tour of the mausoleum for the next day. Wraxall asked the deacon if he knew anything of the village of Chorazin. The deacon went very still, then said sharply that it was the cursed town from the gospels, where old tales said the Antichrist would one day be born. When Wraxall pressed him for more, he said he had forgotten all the old stories, and made his excuses to leave.

Left alone with the innkeeper, Wraxall badgered him until the man caved. He stood so close his breath fanned Wraxall’s face, and told the story his grandfather had witnessed, ninety two years prior:

Two local poachers laughed at the warnings, and said they would go hunt in the count’s forbidden wood after dark. The rest of the village begged them not to go, said they would meet folk walking who should not be walking, who should be six feet under the ground. The two men laughed them off. It was a light midsummer night, no foresters dared patrol that wood.

My grandfather sat right here in this inn parlour, the window open, the innkeeper said. He sat with three other men, and they listened. First there was nothing. Then they heard a scream, far off, as if a man’s very soul was being twisted right out of his body. All four of them clutched at each other, and sat shivering for three quarters of an hour. Then they heard someone laugh, barely three hundred ells away. It was not a man’s laugh. No man on God’s green earth ever laughed like that. Then they heard a great heavy door slam shut.

At dawn, they all went up to the priest, and told him to put on his robes and come bury Anders Bjornsen and Hans Thorbjorn. They were sure the two men were dead. The priest said he had heard the scream and the laugh too, from his own cottage, and he did not think he would ever sleep again. They went into the wood, and found the two men at the edge of the treeline. Hans Thorbjorn was alive, his back pressed to an oak tree, his hands pushing wildly at empty air, over and over, as if he was fending something no one else could see. They took him to the asylum at Nykjoping, and he died before the winter, still pushing. Anders Bjornsen was dead. His face was gone. All the flesh was sucked clean off the bones. There was nothing to hold his eyes shut. They laid him on a bier, covered his head with a cloth, and walked back to the village, singing the psalm for the dead. Halfway through the first verse, the man carrying the head of the bier stumbled. The cloth fell off. They all saw his eyes, staring up at them, no lids, no skin. They could not bear it. So the priest laid the cloth back over him, and they fetched a spade, and buried Anders Bjornsen right there in the wood. No one has ever dug him up.

The next morning, the deacon called for Wraxall after breakfast, and took him into the church, then out to the mausoleum. Wraxall noticed the iron key to the tomb was hanging on a nail right behind the pulpit, and the church door was never locked. A private return visit, to copy down all the epitaphs he did not have time for that morning, would be trivially easy. The mausoleum inside was imposing: carved marble monuments to generations of De la Gardies, and three large copper sarcophagi in the center of the domed room. Two of them had large crucifixes nailed to their lids. The third, Count Magnus’s, had no cross. It was engraved with a full-length portrait of the count, and bands of carvings around the sides: one showed a battle, cannons smoking, pikemen marching, one showed a public execution. The third carving showed a man running for his life, hair flying, hands outstretched, pursued by a short, hooded figure swathed in a cloak that swept the stone floor. The only part of the creature that stuck out from the hooded fabric was no hand, no arm: a writhing, suckered tentacle, like that of a devil fish. At the far edge of the panel, a cloaked man leaning on a stick stood watching the chase, his posture calm, intent. Three heavy steel padlocks were fixed to the lid of Magnus’s sarcophagus. One of them was unfastened, lying on the pavement.

Wraxall hurried back to the manor to finish his work, but that evening he drifted into a daze again, walking the path from the house without noticing where he was, until he snapped out of it to find himself at the churchyard gate, chanting out loud under his breath: Are you awake, Count Magnus? Are you asleep, Count Magnus? He could not remember how long he had been walking like that.

He fetched the mausoleum key from behind the pulpit, and went inside to copy the epitaphs. The light was failing fast, and he leaned close to Magnus’s sarcophagus, and realized two of the three padlocks were now lying loose on the stone floor, not one. He picked them both up, tried and failed to snap them shut, and left them on the oval window ledge. Only one padlock remained fastened. He wrote later, that the urge to pry that last lock open, to lift the lid and look inside at what lay there, was so strong his hands were shaking. He almost did it. Some strange luck held him back, for that one night.

The next morning, he received a batch of urgent letters from England about his failed investments, requiring his immediate return. His work at Råbäck was nearly done, travel across the North Sea was slow, and he packed his bags that afternoon, bade his hosts farewell, and set off for the coast. He never went back to Råbäck. He never locked the mausoleum door behind him.

He did not know, then, that the last remaining padlock on Count Magnus’s sarcophagus was a spring lock, that would pop open of its own accord the moment the last living person who had stood in that tomb and looked upon the carvings on the casket had crossed the churchyard threshold. He did not know that two of the things Count Magnus had brought home from the Black Pilgrimage were still waiting, free to follow him over the sea, all the way back to his hotel in London.
