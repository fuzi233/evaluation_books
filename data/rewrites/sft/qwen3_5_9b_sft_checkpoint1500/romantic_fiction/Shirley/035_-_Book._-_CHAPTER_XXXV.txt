Martin Moore's plans were intricate, woven with the skill of a master architect, designed solely for his private diversion. Yet, history teaches us that even the most dexterous schemes often crumble before the sweeping broom of Fate—a domestic figure whose red arm, wielded with irresistible force, knows no master. In this particular instance, however, the broom was forged from the unyielding fibers of Moore's own obstinate will, bound tightly by his determination. As his strength began to return, he launched a series of surprising counterattacks against Mrs. Horsfall. First, he dismissed her from her duties as his dresser, insisting on dressing himself. Then, he rejected the coffee she brought, preferring to breakfast with the family. Finally, he barred her from entering his chamber. On the same day, amid the scandalized shrieks of the household's womenfolk, he thrust his head out into the corridor. The following morning, he accompanied Mr. Yorke to his counting-house, requesting a carriage from the Red House Inn, declaring his resolve to return to the Hollow that very afternoon. Instead of obstructing him, Mr. Yorke actively facilitated the departure, despite Mrs. Yorke's fears that the journey would prove fatal. The carriage arrived, and Moore, reticent in speech, compensated the servants and Mrs. Horsfall generously with coins from his purse. This display of gratitude silenced all prior grievances; the matron and her former charge parted as devoted friends.

Having secured peace in the kitchen, Moore ventured to the parlour to appease Mrs. Yorke, a task far more difficult than silencing the maids. There she sat, enshrouded in a gloomy resentment, lost in somber reflections on human ingratitude. He approached and bowed low; she was compelled to look up, merely to bid him depart. Yet, there remained a lingering beauty in his pale, wasted features, and an earnest, sweet quality in his hollow eyes, softened by a faint smile.

"Good-bye!" he said, and as the words left his lips, the smile shimmered and faded. His emotional control had slipped; a fragile tear threatened to surface in his weakened state.

"And whither are you fleeing?" she asked, her voice thick with concern. "We will keep you, do anything in the world for you, if you will only stay until you are stronger."

"Good-bye!" he replied again, adding, "You have been a mother to me; grant your wilful son one embrace."

Like a stranger offering gifts, he presented one cheek, then the other. She kissed them.

"What a trouble, what a burden I have been to you!" he murmured.

"You are the greatest trouble now, a headstrong youth!" she answered sharply. "I wonder who will nurse you at Hollow's Cottage? Your sister Hortense knows as little about such matters as a babe."

"Praise God!" he exclaimed. "I have had enough nursing to last a lifetime."

Just then, the little girls appeared—Jessie, weeping, and Rose, solemn and quiet. Moore led them into the hall to soothe and kiss them, knowing intimately that it was not in his mother's nature to tolerate anyone caressing a living creature save herself; she would have been annoyed by the fondling of a kitten in her presence.

Outside, the boys stood around the carriage as Moore stepped inside. For them, he offered no farewell. To Mr. Yorke, he simply said, "You have a good riddance of me. That was an unlucky shot for you, Yorke; it turned Briarfields into an hospital. Come and visit me at the cottage soon." He raised the glass, and the carriage rolled away. Within half an hour, he alighted at the wicket of his own garden. After paying the driver and dismissing the vehicle, he leaned against the gate, resting and reflecting.

"Six months ago I passed out at this gate," he mused aloud, "a proud, angry, disappointed man. I return sadder, yet wiser; physically weak, but spirit-wise unburdened. A cold, gray, yet tranquil world surrounds me—a realm where, if I hope little, I fear nothing. All slavish terrors of embarrassment have vanished. Let the worst come, I can work, as Joe Scott does, for an honorable living; in such a fate, I see hardship but no degradation. Once, financial ruin equated to personal dishonor in my eyes. Not so now; I comprehend the distinction. Ruin *is* an evil, but one for which I am prepared; the day of its arrival is fixed, for I have calculated. I can postpone it six months—not an hour longer. If circumstances shift by then, which seems improbable, and fetters, currently deemed indissoluble, loosen from our trade (an impossibility, surely), perhaps I could conquer this long struggle... I might—good God, what might I not do? But this thought is a fleeting madness. Let me view reality with sane eyes. Ruin will come, raise her axe to the roots of my fortune, and hew them down. I shall snatch a sapling, cross the sea, and plant it in American woods. Louis will accompany me. Will anyone else follow? I cannot tell—I have no right to ask."

Entering the house, he found it twilight without, starless and moonless, masked by heavy, frozen clouds. Though the air bit with a dry, black frost, the mill-dam was ice-bound, and the Hollow lay in profound silence. Indoors, however, it was already dark. Sarah had kindled a generous fire in the parlour and was preparing tea in the kitchen.

"Hortense," Moore said as his sister hurried forward to remove his cloak, "I am pleased to come home."

Hortense did not register the peculiar novelty of this sentiment from her brother, who had never before considered the cottage his home, finding its narrow limits restrictive rather than protective. Nevertheless, whatever contributed to his happiness pleased her, and she voiced her approval.

He sat down briefly, then rose again, moving to the window before returning to the fire.

"Hortense!"

"Mon frère?"

"This little parlour appears very clean and pleasant—unusually bright, somehow."

"It is true, brother; I have had the entire house scrubbed and dusted with scrupulous care during your absence."

"Sister, I think that on this first day of your return, you ought to have a friend or two for tea, merely to show how fresh and spruce you have made this little place."

"True, brother. If it were not late, I might send for Miss Mann."

"So you might; but it is far too late to disturb that good lady, and the evening is excessively cold for her to venture out."

"How thoughtful in you, dear Gérard! We must postpone it to another day."

"I desire someone today, dear sister—some quiet guest who would tire neither of us."

"Miss Ainley?"

"An excellent person, they say; but she lives too far away. Send word to Harry Scott to ride to the rectory with a request from you that Caroline Helstone should come to spend the evening."

"Would it not be better tomorrow, dear brother?"

"I wish her to see the place as it stands just now; its brilliant cleanliness and perfect neatness reflect so strongly upon you."

"It might serve as an example to her."

"It might and must; she ought to come."

Moving to the kitchen, Moore instructed Sarah to delay tea by half an hour. He then tasked her with sending Harry Scott to the rectory, handing her a hastily scrawled note in pencil, addressed "To Miss Helstone."

Before Sarah could express impatience over the potential damage to the toast already prepared, the messenger returned, bringing the invited guest.

She entered through the kitchen, quietly removing her bonnet and furs in Sarah's presence, and descended as silently, her beautiful curls neatly smoothed, her graceful merino dress and delicate collar immaculate, holding a small work-bag. She lingered to exchange kind words with Sarah, admiring the new tortoise-shell kitten basking on the hearth, and cooing to the canary bird, which had been startled by a sudden flare from the fire. Then she ascended to the parlour.

The gentle salutations and friendly welcomes exchanged between cousins were marked by a tranquility fitting their relationship. A subtle, quiet pleasure, akin to a faint perfume, diffused through the room. The newly-lit lamp burned brightly, and the tray and singing urn were brought forward.

"I am pleased to come home," Mr. Moore repeated.

They gathered around the table. Hortense spoke primarily, congratulating Caroline on the evident improvement in her health. Her color had returned, and her cheeks were plumper. Indeed, an obvious transformation had occurred in Miss Helstone. Everything about her seemed elastic; depression, fear, and forlornness had retreated. No longer crushed, saddened, slow, or drooping, she appeared as one who had tasted the cordial of heart's ease and been lifted on the wings of hope.

After tea, Hortense retired upstairs. Having not opened her drawers for a month, an irresistible impulse drove her to do so. During her absence, the conversation passed into Caroline's hands. She assumed it with ease, falling into her best conversational tone.
