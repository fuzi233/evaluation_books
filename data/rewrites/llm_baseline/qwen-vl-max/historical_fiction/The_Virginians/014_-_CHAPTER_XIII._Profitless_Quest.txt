With every mile Harry Warrington traveled toward Pennsylvania, the tales of the British disaster grew darker and more certain. The two famed regiments—veterans of battles in Scotland and across Europe—had fled before an enemy they scarcely saw. Their vaunted discipline and courage had failed them against a scattered force of French soldiers and Native warriors hidden among the trees. The commander of the expedition, though grievously wounded four times and shot from his horse three times, had fought with unyielding bravery. He died three days after the battle, his final wound proving mortal.

Harry heard these stories from various travelers, each adding grim detail to the tale. They spoke of the river crossing, the long march through dense wilderness, the sudden gunfire ahead, the futile attempts to advance, and the artillery’s struggle to clear a path. Then came the ambush—the deadly shots from behind every tree and bush, the relentless fusillade that felled at least half the army. But not all were lost. One of the General’s aides-de-camp, a Virginian gentleman, was said to be ill at Dunbar’s camp. A survivor—though which one?—was all Harry could think.

He rode on without delay, reaching the encampment at last. There, beneath a canvas tent, he found George Washington, pale and weakened by fever. It was not his brother.

A deeper sorrow than illness seized Harry when he saw the officer. “Ah, George!” he cried, voice breaking. “If you truly loved him, you would have brought him back—alive or dead!”

But Washington could not speak of it fully. For three days after the battle, he had been at the General’s side, tending to him as he lay dying. On July 9th, he had seen young George Warrington ride forward with orders from Braddock—orders that sent him into the thick of the fight. He never returned.

After Braddock’s death, Washington had made his way back to the battlefield. The bodies left behind were stripped, mutilated, and ravaged by wild beasts. Among them, he buried one he believed to be George Warrington’s. The grief of that search had worsened his own illness.

“Why did you not bring him back?” Harry demanded again, refusing to accept the silence.

Nothing would satisfy him but to go himself. With money and resolve, he hired guides and crossed the river where the army had passed. He walked the entire length of the battlefield—once a forest now haunted only by carrion birds and decay. The corpses, still under the trees, were festering, torn apart by time and scavengers. Harry had never seen death so brutally revealed. Only once before—when he had seen his grandfather pass peacefully, a soft smile upon his lips—had he witnessed such stillness. Now, this horror turned his stomach and chilled his blood.

What could the empty woods or the rotting remains tell him?

He resolved to go to the French fort, where the victors had retreated. Perhaps they held news. But his guides refused. “The French might spare us,” one warned, “but the Indians will not. Keep your hair for your mother, young sir. She’s already lost one son. Don’t let her lose another.”

So Harry returned to Dunbar’s camp, only to fall ill himself. Fever seized him, followed by delirium. He lay in the same bed where Washington had recovered, watched over by Dempster, who had nursed him through many sicknesses. For days, Harry knew not who stood beside him. Dempster feared the widow would lose both sons. Yet, slowly, the fever broke. Harry regained strength enough to mount his horse.

Mr. Washington and Dempster escorted him home.

It was with heavy hearts that they approached Castlewood. A servant had gone ahead to warn of their arrival. First to meet them was Mrs. Mountain and her daughter, Fanny. She wept and embraced Harry, but barely acknowledged Washington. Then little Fanny stepped forward, hands behind her back, and asked, “Why haven’t you brought George back too?”

Harry did not hear. The sobs and embraces of his nurse drowned out the child’s words.

Dempster was welcomed warmly. “We know you would do anything for us, Mr. Dempster,” Mrs. Mountain said, offering her hand. “Make a curtsey to Mr. Dempster, Fanny. And remember to be grateful to those who helped our family.”

Then she turned to Washington. “Will you take refreshment before you ride, Colonel?”

He declined. “Only water for myself, and a moment to rest my horse. I’ll trouble Castlewood no further.”

“You have your room here, George,” Harry insisted. “Mother is getting it ready!”

“Hush,” Mrs. Mountain whispered. “She won’t see him.”

“Not see George? But he’s like a son of the house!”

“She won’t see him,” the woman repeated firmly. “When the Colonel’s man arrived, Madam Esmond left this room—where she’d been reading Drelincourt—and said she could not face him. Will you go to her?”

Harry took Washington’s arm and excused himself. “I’ll return in a moment.” He hurried up the stairs, avoiding the door to his brother’s room—until Madam Esmond emerged from it, arms around him, leading him inside.

The room was untouched. A settee sat near the bed. A book of psalms lay open on the coverlet. Everything was exactly as George had left it.

“My poor child,” she said, eyes glistening. “How thin you’ve grown! How haggard you look. Never mind. A mother’s care will restore you. You went bravely to find your brother. If others had shown such loyalty, he might still be here. But don’t despair. Our hero will return. I know he is not dead.”

(Perhaps Harry thought to himself that his mother had never spoken of George so tenderly before.)

“Dry your tears, my dear. He will come back. I know it.”

When Harry pressed for proof, she said, “Two nights running, I’ve seen my father in dreams. He told me George is alive—held captive by the Indians.”

Her grief had not broken her; it had hardened her. Her eyes burned with anger, her spirit stirred. Harry was startled by her intensity.

When he begged her to greet Washington, she recoiled. “I cannot touch his hand,” she declared. “He took my son from me. I cannot sleep under the same roof with him.”

“But he gave me his bed when I was sick,” Harry protested. “He was ill himself, thinking only of his friend. Would any other man have done the same?”

“A friend? A fine friend!” she sneered. “Of all the General’s aides, he alone returns unharmed. The brave fall, but he lives. I entrusted my son—my pride—to him, and he leaves him in the woods while he comes home safe! What welcome should I give such a man?”

“No gentleman,” Harry said fiercely, “has ever been refused shelter under my grandfather’s roof.”

“Oh, no—no gentleman!” she retorted. “Come, son. Let us pay our respects to this one.”

And so, with trembling arms, she descended the stairs, entered the parlor, and offered Washington a formal curtsey. She extended her hand—only briefly—then withdrew it.

“I wish our meeting had been happier, Colonel,” she said coldly.

“You grieve no more than I do, madam,” he replied quietly.

“I might have wished this meeting spared,” she continued. “That my boy’s illness hadn’t delayed you. Home, good nursing, and your mother and Doctor Dempster will heal him soon.”

“It was my duty to ride with him,” Washington murmured.

“You’ve endured the campaign with remarkable endurance,” she said, bowing again. “I wish someone else had returned in your place.”

“Your son’s safety is more important than mine,” he said gently.

“And yet,” she insisted, “you must leave soon. My spirits are not fit for company. But your friends may stay. I will nurse Harry upstairs. Mountain, prepare the cedar room for Mr. Washington. Anything in the house is his.”

She turned sharply. “Farewell, sir. Please send my compliments to your mother and to young Martha Custis. Come, my son.”

And with another icy bow, she left, her eyes fixed on Washington as if daring him to speak.

Though Madam Esmond clung to belief, Castlewood remained shrouded in sorrow. She forbade mourning, but her heart wore black. To seek her son was to hope against hope. No confirmed report of his death had come. No one had seen him fall. Yet hundreds had perished unseen, their last moments known only to enemies and dying comrades.

Twelve days after the defeat, Sady, George’s servant, limped back to Castlewood—wounded, disfigured. He could not recount the battle clearly, only that he had fled from the baggage train. He had no news of his master since the morning of the fight. For days, he hid in the slave quarters, afraid to face Madam Esmond’s wrath.

Neighbors whispered she was deluded. Yet her conviction was so strong that even Harry sometimes began to believe. It seemed natural to her that her father, now in the next world, should appear to her in dreams and promise her son’s survival. She believed her instincts had never failed her. When her boys fell ill or faced danger, she always sensed it—before word arrived. She listed half a dozen such instances, all confirmed by her household.

George was not dead. He was a prisoner. He would return. As surely as King George would send reinforcements to reclaim British honor, so would her son return to rule Castlewood.

As for Washington—he would never enter her presence willingly. He had sworn to protect George with his life. Why was her son gone, and he alive? How dared he stand before her?

She claimed she bore no ill will—but as an Esmond, she demanded honor. Washington had forfeited hers. Orders, some argued, bound him. “Psha!” she scoffed. “A promise is a promise. He swore to guard George’s life. Where is my boy? And why is he whole, while George lies lost?”

She mocked the idea that bullets had pierced his coat. “Can’t I fire two shots through this skirt right now?” she laughed. “Would I die? Ha! Death is nothing to me.”

Her laughter was sharp, cruel—a weapon born of grief and pride.

So Harry met Washington in secret—taverns, court-houses, provincial gatherings. No man of honor, she vowed, could meet him after his betrayal.

She raged when she learned they had met. “What heart must you have, Harry, to shake hands with the man I consider George’s murderer?”

“For shame!” she cried. “To forget your noble brother for that tall, awkward colonel with his foul oaths!”

“How can he be George’s murderer?” Harry asked. “You say George is alive.”

“Because my instinct never fails,” she replied. “Because I see him now—though not as noble as he once was. Because my father appeared to me twice in dreams.”

“You doubt that?” she challenged. “It’s because you’ve never loved anyone deeply enough. Only those who love truly can see their loved ones in dreams.”

“I think I loved George,” Harry said softly. “I’ve prayed for dreams of him. But I’ve seen none.”

“How can you talk of loving him, and then meet Washington at horse races?” she snapped. “Can you, Mountain?”

“We understand that our boy is unhappy,” Mrs. Mountain grumbled. “He grows weaker, spends his days at taverns with idle men. This place is not healing him.”

She was right. Grief had turned to division.

“Since that day in July,” Harry confessed later to Madame de Bernstein, “my mother has not been herself. She praised George constantly, yet never seemed to enjoy his presence. She buried herself in books of devotion—books that only deepen sorrow. A darkness fell over Castlewood. We all grew ill, pale as ghosts. Mountain told me she stayed awake for nights. I’ve seen her at my bedside, so gaunt I thought I saw a specter.”

She had driven herself into a state bordering on madness. Harry fell ill again and again. No medicine could cure him. They moved to Richmond, where they hoped for better air. He improved slightly, but not enough. Doctors advised a sea voyage.

At first, she considered joining him. But—

(Here the lad blushed and lowered his head.)

“They did not agree,” he said. “Though we loved each other dearly, we parted ways. So I sailed from the James River, landed at Bristol. And it was only on July 9th this year, at sea, as we had agreed, that I put on mourning for my brother.”

Thus, the mistress of Castlewood, though respected and wealthy, had the gift of making those around her miserable. She quarreled with those she loved most, ruled with jealous pride, until they were glad to leave.

Money, status, a grand house—yet Harry Warrington was glad to escape it all.

Who is truly happy? What good is a feast of roasted ox if one finds no joy in it? Is it better to be loved and tormented, or to live in quiet indifference? To follow one’s desires, undisturbed, and die without sorrow?

When her sons were gone, Madam Esmond forgot their faults. Speaking of them, she painted them as perfect saints—never flawed, never troublesome.

Without them, she turned to Mrs. Mountain and little Fanny, tormenting them in turn. But women endure more easily than men. They forgive—or pretend to. Let us hope the servants found peace, that they returned kindness for cruelty, that quarrels ended with tea and cards.

But without the boys, Castlewood was a hollow shell. She left an overseer to manage the land, visiting only occasionally. In Richmond, she built a new home, expanded it, filled it with guests and preachers, and set herself upon a throne of social influence. The slaves, fond of society, rejoiced in the change.

And so, as Harry Warrington sails onward to Europe, we leave Madam Esmond in her bright city, ruling her little kingdom, surrounded by the comfort she could not find in the solitude of her great house.
