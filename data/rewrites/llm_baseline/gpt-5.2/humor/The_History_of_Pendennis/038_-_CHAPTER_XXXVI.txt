Success, like a soufflé in a draughty kitchen, rose for the Pall Mall Gazette with a confidence that made onlookers swear there must be some noble cook at the range and a whole party of grave gentlemen passing the salt in secret.

There were rumours—always the first cheap spice in London—that the journal had an influential political patronage simmering behind it; that great names were tied to the ladle and stirring the pot. Whether those names belonged there, we are not at liberty to state. But we may let fall, as one drops a clove into broth, that a weighty article on foreign policy, generally credited to a noble Lord with a very knowing acquaintance in the Foreign Office, was in truth whisked up by Captain Shandon in the parlour of the Bear and Staff public-house near Whitehall Stairs—where the air smelt of wet greatcoats, pipe-smoke, and yesterday’s porter, and where the printer’s boy, tracking him like a truffle-hound, at last found him. Mr. Bludyer, a literary ally with a temporary lodging there, sat within reach—one of those men who can live for a week on sarcasm and neat spirits, and consider both nourishing.

A whole series on finance, which the town insisted must be the work of a great Statesman of the House of Commons, was actually composed by Mr. George Warrington in the Upper Temple—among old books that rustled like dry corn in a loft, and with the steady scratch of a pen keeping time against the distant clatter of London, that endless mill-wheel of sound. If the Pall Mall Gazette had dealings with an influential party, that too was quite possible. Percy Popjoy, son of Lord Falconet and as fond of climbing stairs as of being seen doing it, was often observed mounting to Warrington’s chambers; and certain items of information appeared in the paper which had the smell of private pantries—facts that do not come from the common market-stall, but from back doors, side passages, and confidential servants.

There were poems, too—thin in thought but loud in voice—signed “P. P.”; and it must be confessed that the same initials performed a species of trumpet-service over Mr. Popjoy’s novel, praising it with a gusto so outrageous that you would have supposed the reviewers had been paid in truffles. In the political department Mr. Pen took no share; but for the literary columns he was as busy as a young cook at his first great dinner, flour on his cuffs, pride in his eye, and an appetite for applause sharper than mustard.

The Gazette’s offices were in Catherine Street, Strand. Thither Pen often hurried with manuscripts in his pocket, with a bustle and pleasure which are very like hunger—an eager, half-ashamed craving to see one’s own name served up hot in print, and to fancy, for a delicious moment, that the world is pausing to taste it. The place itself had its own soundscape: the slap of copy on desks, the snip of scissors, the soft wicked rasp of paste-brush on paper, the murmur of men manufacturing society out of scraps, and outside the street’s ceaseless grinding—omnibuses, hawkers, wheels on stone—modern London chewing and chewing.

Here Mr. Jack Finucane, sub-editor, presided with paste and scissors as if they were knife and cleaver. He watched the newspapers with the hungry vigilance of a man who has dined more on other people’s dinners than his own. With an eagle eye he scanned every paragraph that touched the world of fashion. Not a death among the aristocracy, not a dinner-party with plate enough to catch the candles, not a christening where a duke had sneezed at the font, but Jack would have it recorded. From remote provincial prints, and from Scotch and Irish papers that arrived like damp parcels of peat, he fished out astonishing intelligence regarding the upper classes—extracting them as a cook draws marrow from a bone.

It was a grand—nay, a touching—sight for a philosopher, to see Jack Finucane, in tattered trousers and dingy shirt-sleeves, dining off a plate of meat from the cookshop and washing it down with porter from the public-house, while he described the feasts of the great as if he had sat at the carving beside the host. There he was, chewing his own coarse ration, and, between bites, arranging the most brilliant fêtes in the world of fashion with the brisk neatness of a maître d’hôtel. The incongruity of the man—his poverty and his pompous paragraphs—amused Pen mightily. Since leaving his native village, where his rank had probably not been very lofty, Jack had seen little society except the tavern parlours he frequented; and yet from his writing one would have believed he lounged daily in the bow-window of White’s and took his soup among ambassadors.

Occasional errors of description did slip from his pen—like a cook calling parsley “tarragon” in the hearing of a gardener. But it was the Ballinafad Sentinel, for which he was his own correspondent, that suffered from these lapses, not the Pall Mall Gazette. In London Jack’s chiefs thought his scissors and paste were safer tools than his pen; and so he clipped other people’s grandeur into their columns with the skill of a man making a pie out of leftovers.

Pen, on his side, took pains with his reviews. He had a fair share of desultory reading, acquired early—books tasted here and there like bites taken from many loaves. He had, too, a keen sense of fun, and that quick relish which is half palate and half temper. His articles pleased his chief and the public, and he was proud to think he earned his money honestly—an honest wage being, to a young man, as sweet as warm bread when one is not yet sick of it.

We may be sure the Pall Mall Gazette was taken in regularly at Fairoaks, and read with delight by the two ladies there—each number arriving like a basket from town, full of strange herbs and sharp flavours. It was received at Clavering Park too, where there was a young lady of great literary tastes; and old Doctor Portman himself—after the widow had got her son’s articles by heart and forwarded the paper along like a family relic—signified his approval, saying the lad had spirit, taste, and fancy, and wrote, if not like a scholar, at any rate like a gentleman.

But the best dish of praise was served at the Major’s own table of vanity.

Major Pendennis, walking into one of his clubs—the Regent—found Wenham, Lord Falconet, and other gentlemen of good reputation and fashion gathered round a number of the Pall Mall Gazette, talking it over as men talk over a sauce whose piquancy pleases them. An article had appeared making bitter fun of a book recently published by the wife of a celebrated member of the opposition party: a Book of Travels in Spain and Italy, by the Countess of Muffborough. It was hard to decide which was more astonishing, the French or the English, in which her ladyship wrote indifferently—each language served half-cooked, with lumps of blunder floating to the top like suet.

The critic pounced with delightful mischief. He jumped and danced round his subject with jocularity and high spirits—showing up the Countess’s faults with mock gravity and an air of decorum so perfectly polite that it felt like being stabbed with a silver fork. Not a word was ungentlemanlike; and yet the unfortunate book was scarified and laughed at through the whole operation, as neatly as a fish may be filleted while it still seems intact.

Wenham’s bilious countenance puckered with malignant pleasure as he read. Lady Muffborough had not asked him to her parties during the last year—an omission he remembered as a man remembers a missed inheritance. Lord Falconet giggled outright; Lord Muffborough and he had been rivals ever since they began life; and these gentlemen complimented Major Pendennis. Until now the Major had scarcely noticed certain hints from Fairoaks about “dear Arthur’s constant and severe literary occupations, which I fear may undermine the poor boy’s health.” Newspaper connexions had seemed, to him, below the dignity of a Major and a gentleman—ink being, in his opinion, a substance best kept on other people’s fingers.

But now—when the oracular Wenham praised the boy’s production; when Lord Falconet, tipped off by Percy Popjoy, approved young Pen’s genius; when the great Lord Steyne himself, to whom the Major referred the article, laughed and sniggered over it, swore it was capital, and that the Muffborough would writhe under it like a whale under a harpoon—the Major’s family pride began to cook rapidly.

“By gad,” he said, “the young rascal has some stuff in him—he’ll do something. I always said he’d do something.”

And with a hand tremulous with pleasure, the old gentleman sat down to write to the widow at Fairoaks all that the great folks had said; and to the young rascal too, asking when he would come and eat a chop with his old uncle. He added that he was commissioned to take him to dinner at Gaunt House; for Lord Steyne liked anybody who could entertain him—whether by folly, wit, dulness, oddity, affectation, good spirits, or any other seasoning that made the dish go down.

Pen flung the letter across the table to Warrington. Perhaps he expected Warrington to taste the praise, smack his lips, and declare it excellent. Warrington did nothing of the kind.

Young critics have courage enough to season an elephant. They clamber up to the judgment-seat and, with scarce a hesitation, pronounce upon works intricate as a stuffed capon or profound as a cellar. Had Macaulay’s History or Herschel’s Astronomy been put before Pen at that period, he would have looked through the volumes, meditated his opinion over a cigar, and then delivered his august approval as if he were their born superior—an indulgent master and patron granting permission for genius to exist.

By the help of the Biographie Universelle or the British Museum, he could take a rapid résumé of a period, and allude to names, dates, and facts with a masterly ease that astonished his mamma at home (who wondered where her boy had got such a prodigious store of reading), and astonished Pen himself when he read his own articles two or three months later and had forgotten both subject and sources, as a man forgets yesterday’s dinner until he finds the bill in his pocket.

At that time Pen would not have hesitated, at twenty-four hours’ notice, to pass opinion upon the greatest scholars or give judgment on the Encyclopaedia. Luckily Warrington laughed at him and kept down his impertinence by a constant and wholesome ridicule—as a sensible elder might slap the hand of a child reaching for a pan too hot. For Shandon liked the dash and flippancy of his young aide-de-camp, and was better pleased with Pen’s light brilliant flashes than with the heavier metal Warrington brought. Still, though Pen might be blamed for prematurity of judgment, he was a perfectly honest critic—too candid for Mr. Bungay’s purposes, indeed, who grumbled sadly at his impartiality.

Pen and Captain Shandon disputed upon this one day.

“In the name of common-sense, Mr. Pendennis,” Shandon asked, “what have you been doing—praising one of Mr. Bacon’s books? Bungay has been with me in a fury this morning at seeing a laudatory article upon one of the works of the odious firm over the way.”

Pen’s eyes opened wide, as if someone had proposed putting soot into the soup. “Do you mean to say,” he asked, “that we are to praise no books that Bacon publishes? Or that, if the books are good, we are to say they are bad?”

“My good young friend,” said Shandon, “for what do you suppose a benevolent publisher undertakes a critical journal—to benefit his rival?”

“To benefit himself certainly,” Pen answered, “but to tell the truth too—ruat coelum—to tell the truth.”

“And my prospectus?” Shandon said, with a laugh and a sneer. “Do you consider that was a work of mathematical accuracy of statement?”

“Pardon me—that is not the question,” Pen replied; “and I don’t think you very much care to argue it. I had qualms of conscience about that prospectus, and debated the matter with my friend Warrington. We agreed, however,” Pen said, laughing, “that because the prospectus was rather declamatory and poetical, and the giant painted upon the show-board rather larger than the original who was inside the caravan, we need not be too scrupulous about this trifling inaccuracy, but might do our part of the show without loss of character or remorse. We are the fiddlers, and play our tunes only; you are the showman.”

“And leader of the van,” Shandon added.

“Well,” said Pen, with a fine sense of the dignity of his position, “we are all party men in England, and I will stick to my party like a Briton. I will be as good-natured as you like to our own side—he is a fool who quarrels with his own nest—and I will hit the enemy as hard as you like; but with fair play, Captain, if you please. One can’t tell all the truth, I suppose; but one can tell nothing but the truth. And I would rather starve, by Jove, and never earn another penny by my pen”—this redoubted instrument had now been in use for some six weeks, and he spoke of it with vast enthusiasm and respect—“than strike an opponent an unfair blow, or, if called upon to place him, rank him below his honest desert.”

“Well, Mr. Pendennis,” Shandon said with fatal good-nature, “when we want Bacon smashed, we must get some other hammer to do it.” And very likely he thought, without speaking it: “A few years hence perhaps the young gentleman won’t be so squeamish.”

The veteran condottiere himself was no longer scrupulous. He had fought and killed on so many sides for so many years that remorse had long since fled his camp. “Gad,” he said, “you’ve a tender conscience, Mr. Pendennis. It’s the luxury of novices. I may have had one once myself; but that bloom wears off with the rubbing of the world, and I’m not going to the trouble of putting on an artificial complexion like our pious friend Wenham, or our model of virtue, Wagg.”

“I don’t know,” Pen answered, “whether some people’s hypocrisy is not better than others’ cynicism.”

“It’s more profitable, at any rate,” said the Captain, biting his nails. “That Wenham is as dull a quack as ever quacked: and you saw the carriage in which he drove to dinner. Faith, it’ll be a long time before Mrs. Shandon takes a drive in her own chariot. God help her, poor thing!”

Pen went away from his chief after their dispute, drawing his own moral as a careful cook salts his own stew: “Behold this man,” he thought, “stored with genius, wit, learning, and a hundred good natural gifts—see how he has wrecked them by paltering with his honesty and forgetting to respect himself. Wilt thou remember thyself, O Pen? thou art conceited enough. Wilt thou sell thy honour for a bottle? No—by heaven’s grace—we will be honest, whatever befalls, and our mouths shall only speak the truth when they open.”

A trial was waiting for him—served up promptly, as London serves lessons, without so much as a napkin.

In the very next number of the Pall Mall Gazette, Warrington read out—between bursts of laughter—an article which did not amuse Arthur Pendennis at all. Pen himself was at work on a criticism for the coming week, when this new piece appeared, in which the Spring Annual was ferociously maltreated by some unknown writer. The poor spring flowers were pounded into a paste.

And among those most cruelly mauled was Pen himself.

His verses had not been printed in the Annual under his own name, but under an assumed signature. He had refused to review the book; and Shandon, with the easy conscience of a man who stirs all sauces with the same spoon, handed it to Mr. Bludyer, instructing him to “dispose of it.”

Bludyer disposed of it as effectually as a butcher disposes of a lamb. He was a man of considerable talent, and of a race—now, perhaps, extinct—who treated literature as something to be broken open with a mallet. He possessed a savage humour and a professional notoriety for using it. He smashed and trampled down the Spring Annual with no more mercy than a bull would show a parterre; and when he had cut up the volume to his heart’s content, he went and sold it at a bookstall, and purchased a pint of brandy with the proceeds—turning printed flowers into spirits with the brisk practicality of the age.
