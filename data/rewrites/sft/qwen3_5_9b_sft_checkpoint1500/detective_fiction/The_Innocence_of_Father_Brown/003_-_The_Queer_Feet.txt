"If you encounter a member of that exclusive circle, 'The Twelve True Fishermen,' descending the stairs of the Vernon Hotel for their annual banquet, you will notice that his evening coat is green, not black. Should you dare to inquire why, he will likely shrug and claim it prevents him from being mistaken for a waiter. You will then depart, humbled. Yet, you will leave behind an unsolved enigma and a narrative worthy of telling.

If, following this same thread of improbable speculation, you were to meet a gentle, industrious little priest named Father Brown and ask him what the greatest stroke of luck in his life had been, he would probably reply that his finest achievement occurred at the Vernon Hotel. There, by merely listening to a few footsteps in a hallway, he averted a crime and perhaps saved a soul. He might be a trifle proud of this wild and wonderful deduction, referring to it often. But since it is virtually impossible for you to ascend high enough in society to join "The Twelve True Fishermen" or descend low enough into the criminal underworld to find Father Brown, I fear you will never hear the story unless I recount it to you.

It was a topsy-turvy creation—an "exclusive" commercial enterprise. Unlike normal businesses that attract patrons, it thrived by repelling them. In the heart of a plutocracy, traders became so fastidious they created obstacles for their wealthy, weary clients to overcome with money and diplomacy. Imagine a fashionable London hotel admitting no one shorter than six feet, forcing society to assemble parties of tall men solely to dine there. Picture an expensive restaurant open only on Thursday afternoons by the proprietor's whim, becoming packed exclusively on those days. The Vernon Hotel stood incongruously in a corner of Belgravia. It was small, inconvenient, yet these flaws served as protective walls around a specific class. Above all, the vital constraint was capacity: practically only twenty-four people could dine simultaneously. The sole large table was the famous terrace table, exposed to the air on a veranda overlooking one of London's most exquisite ancient gardens. Consequently, even the twenty-four seats at this table were accessible only in warm weather, making the experience a difficult-to-obtain luxury.

The current owner, a Jew named Lever, amassed nearly a million by making entry difficult. He paired this exclusivity with meticulous polish. The wines and cuisine rivaled the best in Europe, and the attendants' demeanor perfectly mirrored the fixed, detached mood of the English upper class. Lever knew his fifteen waiters intimately; it was harder to become a Member of Parliament than a waiter there. Each server was trained in terrible silence and smoothness, functioning as a gentleman's personal servant. Indeed, there was usually at least one waiter for every diner.

The Twelve True Fishermen would dine nowhere else, demanding luxurious privacy and recoiling at the thought of any other club sharing the building. On their annual dinner, they displayed all their treasures, treating the establishment like a private home. Central to their regalia was a celebrated set of fish knives and forks, exquisitely crafted in silver in the shape of fish, each hilt encrusted with a single large pearl. Laid out for the fish course—the meal's climax—these utensils signified the society's vast array of ceremonies and observances, devoid of history or objective purpose, rendering them purely aristocratic. One need not *be* anything to belong, unless one was already the requisite sort of person, in which case one never even heard of them. The club had existed for twelve years, presided over by Mr. Audley, with the Duke of Chester as vice-president.

If my description conveys the suffocating atmosphere of this appalling hotel, the reader may naturally wonder how I, an ordinary observer, learned so much about it, or how Father Brown found himself there. My story is simple, perhaps even vulgar. There exists an aged rioter and demagogue who breaks into refined retreats with the dreadful proclamation that all men are brothers; wherever this leveller goes on his pale horse, Father Brown follows. An Italian waiter had suffered a paralytic stroke that afternoon. Lever, marveling mildly at such superstitions, agreed to summon the nearest Catholic priest. What the waiter confessed to Father Brown remains unknown, for the cleric kept it to himself; however, it apparently involved drafting a note to convey a message or rectify a wrong. Father Brown, displaying a meek impudence he would show equally in Buckingham Palace, requested a room and writing materials. Mr. Lever was torn. Kind-hearted yet loathing difficulty or scenes, he hesitated. To the Vernon Hotel, a new stranger was akin to a speck of dirt on freshly polished silver. There were no borders, no waiting halls, no chance visitors. Fifteen waiters served twelve guests. Finding a new guest would be as startling as finding a new brother at breakfast. Moreover, the priest's appearance was second-rate, his clothes muddy; a glimpse of him might precipitate a crisis. Finally, Lever devised a plan to conceal, if not obliterate, the disgrace. Upon entering the hotel, one passes a short, dingy passage adorned with important pictures, arriving at the main vestibule and lounge. To the left lay a glass office abutting the lounge—a "house within a house"—and further along, on the way to the servants' quarters, was the gentlemen's cloak room, the final boundary of the gentlemen's domain. Between the office and the cloak room lay a small private room, used by the proprietor for delicate matters like lending a duke a thousand pounds or refusing sixpence. It was a testament to Lever's magnificent tolerance that he allowed this holy space to be profaned by a scribbling priest for about half an hour. The story Father Brown was writing was likely far superior to mine, though it will never be known. I can only state it was nearly as long, and the concluding paragraphs were the least absorbing.

By the time Father Brown reached these final lines, his thoughts began to wander, his keen animal senses awakening. Darkness and dinner drew near. His forgotten room was unlit, and perhaps the gathering gloom sharpened his hearing. As he penned the least essential part of his document, he found himself writing to the rhythm of a recurrent noise outside, much like composing to a train's tune. Becoming conscious of it, he realized it was merely the ordinary patter of feet passing the door, unlikely in a hotel but not unheard of. Still, he stared at the darkened ceiling, listening. After a few seconds of dreamy attention, he rose, listening intently with his head cocked, then sat again, burying his brow in his hands, listening and thinking.

The footsteps outside, individually unremarkable, formed a collective strangeness. There were no other footsteps. The house was silent; guests retired promptly, and waiters were instructed to be invisible. Irregularity was inconceivable. Yet these footsteps were odd, defying classification as regular or irregular. Father Brown traced them with his finger along the table's edge, as if learning a melody on a piano.

First, a long rush of rapid little steps, like a light man winning a race. At a certain point, they halted and shifted to a slow, swinging stamp, occupying roughly the same duration but using a quarter of the steps. Just as the echo faded, the ripple of light feet returned, followed again by the thud of heavier walking. It was certainly the same pair of boots, evidenced by their small, unmistakable creak. Father Brown possessed a head that could not resist questions, and this trivial query nearly split it. He had seen men run to jump, run to slide. But why run to walk? Or walk to run? No description fit the antics of these invisible legs. The man walked rapidly down one half of the corridor to walk slowly down the other; or walked slowly at one end to enjoy the rapture of speed elsewhere. Neither suggestion made sense. His brain grew dark, mirroring his room.

Yet, as he thought steadily, the blackness of his cell made his visions vivid. He saw the fantastic feet capering in unnatural, symbolic attitudes. Was it a heathen religious dance? A new scientific exercise? Focusing on the slow step first: it was not the proprietor's. Such men waddle rapidly or sit still. It sounded neither like a servant nor a messenger awaiting orders. While poorer orders might lurch when drunk, in such glorious settings, they stood or sat in constrained attitudes. No, that heavy yet springy step, with a careless emphasis, not specially noisy yet indifferent to noise, belonged to only one creature on earth. It was a gentleman of Western Europe, likely one who had never worked for a living.

As this certainty solidified, the step changed to the quicker one, running past the door feverishly like a rat. The listener noted that though swift, it was remarkably noiseless, almost as if performed on tiptoe. Yet it was not associated with secrecy, but with something else he could not recall. Maddened by half-memories that make one feel half-witted, he suddenly sprang to his feet with a new idea. He walked to the door. His room had no direct outlet onto the passage but led via a glass office and the cloak room beyond. He tried the office door, which was locked. He looked at the window, a square pane filled with purple clouds cleft by a livid sunset, and for an instant, he smelled evil, as a dog smells rats.
