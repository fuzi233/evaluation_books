Three days later, the moon, just cresting the rooftops of the Piazza della Signora before midnight, cast no familiar pattern of broad light and deep shadow upon the cobblestones. There was not a single square foot of pavement visible; the ground was entirely swallowed by the heads of a frantic, surging multitude. Gone was the hushed backdrop of the city's nocturnal wanderers—their soft footsteps, murmured conversations, the strumming of lutes, and the quick scurry of cats—against which such sounds usually stood out in sharp relief. In its place rose a deafening roar of mixed shouts, curses, trampling feet, pushing bodies, and the occasional clash of steel. Through this cacophony, nothing could be distinguished save a sudden, piercing shriek or the heavy, rhythmic tolling of a bell.

Almost everyone in Florence who considered themselves part of the public was awake at this hour, either trapped within the walls of the Piazza or fighting desperately to get inside. Inside the Old Palace, the Council Chamber remained filled with the city's highest magistrates, the eighty senators, and the select citizens who had debated furiously through long days and nights by torchlight. They argued whether the appeal for clemency should be granted or if the sentence of death should be carried out immediately to prevent the dangers inherent in any delay. Their debate had become so fierce, so much like a violent quarrel, that the noise leaked out to the crowd below.

Only in the last hour had the decision been reached. The Signoria had remained deeply divided; four members stood immovably for the appeal, despite the terrifying warning that if they refused to yield, their homes would be sacked. Finally, Francesco Valori, in a brief and furious speech, clarified his party's resolve ominously: if the Signoria would not defend the liberties of the Florentine people by executing those five "perfidious" citizens, there would be others willing to seize that cause, risking the lives of all who opposed them. The Florentine Cato had triumphed.

When the votes were recounted, the four stubborn "white beans" vanished; the entire panel voted "black"—nine to zero—deciding the immediate death of the five prisoners. This verdict also decided, though tacitly and with greater delay, the death of Francesco Valori himself. As the Judicial Eight departed for the Bargello to prepare the execution site, the five condemned men were led barefoot and shackled through the center of the Council Chamber. It was a deliberate act by their friends: they hoped that seeing such cruel ignominy visited upon two venerable elders, Bernardo del Nero and Niccolò Ridolfi—who had supported the new order before the Medici influence waned—and two brilliant young leaders, Tornabuoni and Pucci, would move the populace. Yet, it was futile. Any pity stirred now was of a hopeless kind, leading not to rescue, but to the slower, darker fuel of revenge.

While this scene unfolded above, Romola stood below, leaning against a massive pillar in the palace courtyard. She waited for the moment her godfather would emerge on his way to execution. Through powerful intervention, she had secured permission to see him that evening and stay until the Council's fate was sealed. Now, she waited alongside his confessor, ready to follow the guard leading him to the Bargello. Her heart was set on holding onto the presence of this childless old man until the very last second, just as her father would have wished. She had silenced all objections.

Giovan Battista Ridolfi, a devoted follower of Savonarola who went forward in bitter sorrow to witness the death of his elder brother Niccolò, had promised to guard her. He stood beside her now. Tito was also in the palace, though Romola had not seen him. Since the evening of the seventeenth, they had avoided one another. Tito knew only by inference from the friar's neutral reports that her pleas had failed. Surrounded by officials and dignitaries, both Florentine and foreign, who awaited the Council's conclusion, Tito maintained a subdued gravity, caught in a painful conflict between public duty and private feeling. When anyone mentioned his wife in relation to these events, he claimed that her violent mental agitation meant that merely holding office under a government condemning her godfather aroused a "diseased hostility" in her toward him. Thus, for her sake, he deemed it best not to approach her.

"The old Bardi blood!" scoffed Cennini, shaking his head. "I shall not be surprised if this affair shakes *her* loose from the Friar, as surely as it will loosen several others I could name."

"It is excusable in a woman," remarked a young French envoy, smiling and bowing to Tito, "who is doubtless beautiful, since she is the wife of Messer Tito. To believe that her affections must override the good of the State, and that no one should be beheaded unless they are someone's cousin... but such a view cannot be encouraged in the male population. It seems to me your Florentine polity is greatly weakened by it."

"That is true," replied Niccolò Machiavelli. "But where personal ties are strong, the hostilities they generate must be accounted for. Many of these half-measures are merely hot-headed blunders. The only safe blows to strike against men and factions are those too heavy to be avenged."

"Niccolò," said Cennini, "there is a clever wickedness in your talk sometimes that makes me distrust your pleasant young face as if it were a mask of Satan."

"Not at all, my good Domenico," Machiavelli smiled, placing a hand on the elder's shoulder. "Satan was a blunderer, an introducer of novelty who met with a stupendous failure. Had he succeeded, we would all be worshipping him, and his portrait would be far more flattering."

"Well, well," Cennini conceded. "I do not say your doctrine is not too clever for Satan; I only say it is wicked enough for him."

"I tell you," Machiavelli continued, "my doctrine is the philosophy of all men who seek an end slightly farther away than their own noses. Ask our Friar, our prophet, how his universal renewal is to be achieved. He will tell you: first, establish a free and pure government. Since this cannot be done by making all Florentines love one another, it must be accomplished by cutting off every head that stubbornly stands in the way. Only if a man incurs hatred by sanctioning a severity that is not thorough enough to be final does he commit a blunder. And I suspect the Friar has committed exactly that blunder. On this occasion, he might have gained lustre by exerting himself to maintain the appeal. Instead, he has lost lustre and gained no strength."

Before anyone else could speak, the announcement came that the prisoners were leaving the Council Chamber. Most present hurried toward the door, eager to secure the clearest path to the Bargello behind the prisoner's guard, for the sight of the execution drew both the deeply passionate and the coldly curious. Tito, however, remained behind. He possessed a natural repugnance to scenes of death and pain. Five days prior, whenever he had imagined this execution as a possibility, he had hoped it would never happen, believing the maximum sentence would be exile—a outcome sufficient for his own safety. But now, he felt that confirming Bernardo del Nero's death would serve as a welcome guarantee of his security.

The new knowledge and attitude revealed to him by Romola on the day of his return had instilled in him a fresh dread of the power she held to render his position precarious. If any act of hers succeeded in making him an object of suspicion and loathing, he foresaw not only frustration but frustration under unpleasant circumstances. Her belief in Baldassarre had clearly hardened her wavering feelings against further submission; if her godfather lived, she would easily win him to share her beliefs. Romola seemed increasingly an unmanageable fact in his destiny. But if Bernardo del Nero were dead, the difficulties that would beset her in opposing her husband would likely be insurmountable to her shrinking pride. Therefore, Tito felt relieved knowing the Eight had gone to the Bargello to order the immediate erection of the scaffold. Four other men—his intimates and confederates—were to die alongside Bernardo del Nero. Yet, a man's own safety is a god that sometimes makes very grim demands. Tito felt them to be grim; even in pursuing what was agreeable, this paradoxical life forced upon him the desire for what was disagreeable. Having experienced this before, as he heard through the open doorway the shuffle of many feet and the clanking of metal on the stairs, he answered the young French envoy's questions without betraying any emotion other than a sad resignation to the necessities of the State.

Those sounds struck Romola as if her power of hearing had been heightened along with every other sense. She needed no arm to support her; she shed no tears. She felt an intensity of life that transcended both grief and joy—an awareness that the mind becomes akin to ancient forces that shaped existence before the birth of pleasure and pain. Since her godfather's fate was decided, the previous turmoil within her had subsided into an identification of self with him during these supreme moments. She was inwardly asserting for him that, if he suffered the punishment of treason, he did not deserve the name of traitor; he was the victim of a collision between two forms of faithfulness.
