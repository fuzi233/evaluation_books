There are those who say the truer, deeper tragedy of Prometheus is not the pain of being chained after he has stolen celestial fire for mankind—but the agony of being thrust into a desolation of ignored, scoffed-at ideas by the unyielding force of unthinking custom, long before he ever manages to tuck that first spark into his fennel stalk to carry to the mortal world. Helpless, throbbing, trapped between the slow drag of poverty and the creep of fatal disease, he finds himself passed by on every side by people who never once bother to glance his way.

People call this kind of unaccountable, foreshadowing intuition “second sight,” and mock the ground it stands on. But anyone who has felt their own deepest yearnings take shape as vivid, living visions knows the truth of it: the deed you are meant to do rises before you whole and complete, a quiet, unshakable blueprint; the future you hunger for (or dread) grows like a seed fed by every small, half-unnoticed impression you absorb through the days. These are not flighty, addled fools, these people—they are no less logical, no less grounded than the sharpest, most practical traders haggling in the market squares. Often their natures are stitched through with a hundred openings, like the hundred-gated Thebes, where far more life and light can pour in than ever could through one narrow, doorman-guarded portal. Of course there are small, pathetic specimens of the visionary, the same way there are pygmy mammals tiny enough to fit inside the finger of a glove. That tiny distant cousin to the elephant does no harm to anyone, but what great, noble kind of person is entirely free of small, ugly, useless specimens that drag the whole genus down? It is a terrifying thing to think of all the different kinds of people the label “patriot” has been applied to; or the crush of elbowing crowds we will find on judgment day when every man who ever called himself an author shows up hauling armfuls of books, or rolling stacks of them on carts.

This long, gentle preamble is only here to lead us to Mordecai. That gaunt, quiet Jewish workman had settled somewhere soft and persistent in Daniel Deronda’s mind after their first meeting, a new, unanswered question he found himself turning over in quiet moments, curious to find out more. Still, that interest was no more than vague, gentle suspense: the consumptive, fervent young scholar who eked out a living mending trinkets at his workbench much the same way Spinoza polished lenses, fit none of the paths Deronda had expected his life to follow.

But that meeting had struck Mordecai like a lightning bolt. For years now, he had felt his physical life ebbing away, even as the vast, quiet loneliness of his spiritual world kept stretching wider. Every last thread of his passionate desire had wound itself tight into one single, all-consuming yearning: to find a young, willing ear he could pour his whole mind into, as a final, living testament. To find a soul kindred enough to take every precious, hard-won truth his short, painful life had birthed, and carry it forward as a sacred mission he was meant to fulfill. Remarkably, that bright, hopeful delusion that so many consumptive patients carry, the fantasy of one day waking well again, had never settled on the thought of his own body healing. All that hopeful energy had been diverted full force into this single, burning hunger for transmission. That yearning, which had first panted up from the dark under the weight of a hundred overwhelming discouragements, had grown into a hope—and that hope into a unshakable, devout belief. Far from being doused by the clear, sharp knowledge that his own body was wasting away fast, that belief only burned brighter, the kind of fierce, expectant faith a person has in a prophecy that only has a handful of days left to come true.

Years back, he had begun to look at every man he passed with a sharp, searching glance, hunting for a possibility that grew clearer and more defined with every month. At first he shaped that ideal man by contrast: he wanted someone who was nothing like him. Tracing all the ways his own weaknesses and disadvantages had brought him rebuffs and barriers, he dreamed of a man who held every trait he needed to feel seen and understood, but wrapped in a life nothing like his own. This man must be Jewish, intellectually sharp, morally fervid—every part of him ready to be filled with all the sacred truths Mordecai carried. But his face must be beautiful, his frame strong, he must have known all the soft refinements of a gentle life, his speech flowing easy and full, his hands free from the sordid grind of scraping for bread. He must be the kind of man who could make the world see how bright and noble the possibilities of his people were, not a wasted, breathless figure like Mordecai, stamped all over with the marks of poverty and fading life.

Mordecai was sensitive to every line of a face, every line of a body, and abroad, then in England, he stared at paintings as often as he stared at strangers, lingering for hours in the National Gallery when he had the time, hunting for grave, noble faces that might feed his hope, faces that could easily belong to a man of his own people. But he always left disappointed. There are barely a handful of paintings across all the galleries of Europe where even the greatest masters managed to capture a face young, grand, and beautiful, where the quiet melancholy there is no weak, passive sorrow, but a foreshadowing of great, brave heroism yet to come. Any visitor to the gallery might remember his gaunt, hollow-cheeked figure, dark eyes sunk deep in his sockets, standing motionless in front of a single canvas, lost in meditation. He always wore a cloth cap edged with black fur, and no painter would ever have asked him to take it off. The other visitors would glance at him and dismiss him as a odd-looking Jewish man who probably made his money somehow scamming tourists out of their cash near the artworks, and Mordecai always knew exactly what they thought of him.

Experience had taught him, painfully, how much a man’s poverty and physical frailty cheapen every idea he speaks out loud—unless he is a Peter the Hermit, thundering from a pulpit that whips the rabble into a frenzy. But he was too sane, too generous, to blame all his spiritual exile on other people’s ignorant prejudices. He knew his own limitations had earned him that sentence of exclusion. And that was why his imagination had built that other man: not just a “second soul” the Kabbalah speaks of, sent to help carry the weight of an insufficient first life, but a blooming, full, vigorous human existence, ready to weave all the most precious parts of Mordecai’s life that was burning away fast into its own, so they might live on together. His deep, inner need for this expanded, prolonged version of himself felt to him like an outward necessity. The thoughts of his heart (that old soft phrase is the only one that fits the truth) felt too precious, too tightly woven into the growing good of the world, to vanish into oblivion. And as that stronger, brighter, more capable version of his ideal companion took shape in his mind, he loved him in advance, with an affection half merged into his own identity, half soft, reverent, and grateful, like the love a man has for his long-awaited beloved.

Mordecai’s mind worked constantly in vivid images, so his trains of thought felt like the meaningful, sacred dreams that waking people in their most creative moments imagine for sleepers—even, sometimes, like real dreams, the kind that blur the line between the world you know and the world you are still reaching for. For years, he pictured the Being he ached for as a figure standing far off, facing away from him, dark and clear against a blazing golden sky. That golden sky was tied to one of Mordecai’s favorite small habits. He saw the quiet poetry hidden in the chaos of London, and whenever his strength and free time allowed, he would walk out to the bridges over the Thames, most often at sunrise or sunset. Even when he was hunched over tiny watch wheels and trinkets at his workbench, or sitting in his tiny garret room staring out at grimy bricks and cracked window panes, his imagination would carry him off to a place with a wide, unbroken view, and his thoughts would unfurl in wide, open spaces. As often as he could, he sought out the real thing, the wide sky, the open air. Leaning on the parapet of Blackfriars Bridge, he would meditate on the wide, calm stretch of the river, its long vista half hazy, half glowing, the grand, dim shapes of the tall warehouses that marked the endless hum of world trade, the barges drifting toward him out of the distant haze, slowly coming into sound and color. All of that blended seamlessly into his thoughts, the way a fine symphony you barely are even aware of listening to holds you up, its music carrying your spiritual wings. That was why the figure of his longing always stood dark against that flood of golden sunset light.

Slowly, as his imagination filled in more and more detail, the figure stopped facing away from him. He began to walk forward, and a face grew clear: the words youth, beauty, refinement, Jewish birth, noble gravity, all resolved into a form that was not just one specific man, but the perfect type, drawn from his memories of the faces of Jews he had seen in Holland and Bohemia, and the old paintings that brought those memories back to life. It is no exaggeration to say that this mature, deep spiritual hunger was akin to the soft daydreams of a young boy or girl picturing the lover they have not yet met—but the faint stirrings of that young desire are nothing next to the fierce, rushing current of ideal life straining to find flesh and blood, made sharper by the constant, imminent threat of dissolution. That visionary form became his constant companion and audience, not just in his waking imagination, but in those soft, light half-slumbers where the truest saying is “I sleep, but my heart waketh”—where the trivial small events of the day before are suddenly charged with all the impassioned purpose of years.

Lately, the pressure of time running out, marked by the way his breath grew shorter and his chest tighter every week, had turned his quiet trust into a restless, watchful waiting for the fulfillment that could not be far off. Was the bell about to toll? Was the sentence he had waited for about to be carried out? The deliverer’s footsteps must be near—this deliverer who would rescue all of Mordecai’s spiritual labor from oblivion, and give it a permanent home in the most precious heritage of his people. Many would have called this yearning an insane, overinflated belief in his own worth, even if his ideas were as true and precious as Columbus’s or Newton’s. They would say it is nobler to shrug and say “If not I, then another” and care nothing for the meaning of your own individual life. But the fullest, most alive natures crave to be the one who acts, the one who creates, not just the one who watches from the sidelines. Strong love hungers to give blessing, not just to see it given to someone else. And as long as there is warmth enough in the sun to feed an energetic, living heart, there will be men who feel, “I am the master of this moment’s turn, and I will fill it with my whole soul.”

Yet for all that certainty in the friend who was coming, Mordecai could not make himself wait passively. He tried a hundred small, pathetically humble little tricks, whatever was within his limited reach, to plant small pieces of himself in the world. It had been two years now since he moved into the Cohen household, where everyone treated him with great good will, a strange mix of skilled workman, tutor, recipient of their charity, pious man, inspired fool, and (if anyone ever dug too deep) dangerous heretic. In that time, little Jacob had grown old enough for knickerbockers, and had developed that sharp, quick eye for trades and bargains that everyone who knew the family had already seen. He had also grown very fond of Mordecai, though he considered him a slightly inferior person, a sentiment that only made him fonder. He took Mordecai’s clever help with his toys and lessons the same way he would take the services of an enslaved, all-powerful genie. Mordecai had given Jacob his first lessons, and his natural soft tenderness had easily turned into the gentle, fatherly affection of a teacher. He was fully aware of the vast spiritual gulf between him and the Cohens, and he would never have dreamed of trying to share his private, sacred world with them—but little Jacob moved him, that soft idealizing love that sees past any small flaws in a child to the bright glow of childhood itself, the endless potential of a long, unwritten future. That feeling had led him, first without planning, then with quiet purpose, to pour bits of his own poems and ideas into the boy’s ear, rambling recitations that would have sounded wildly absurd to any practical businessman who happened to overhear.

No one ever did overhear, though. Jacob would climb up to Mordecai’s garret on a slow workday, or after all the trinkets for the day were mended, and after a quick lesson in English reading or numbers, he would hang around leaning against his tutor’s knees, or clamber astride them, no matter how the wasted, thin limbs ached under his weight. The lure was usually Mordecai’s knack for fixing broken toys, or some tiny clever mechanical trick his years of working with tiny parts had perfected. With the boy thus tethered, Mordecai would begin to repeat a Hebrew poem he had written years before, where he had poured all his youthful first ardor for his sacred, beloved vision of his people’s past and future, telling Jacob to repeat the words after him. “The boy will have these words engraved deep inside him,” Mordecai thought. “It is a kind of printing, a way of planting seeds.” Jacob was happy to play the game, parroting the strange, unintelligible sounds with no complaints, and if no other distraction popped up he would keep at it as long as Mordecai’s breath held out. Mordecai poured all the fervor of a sacred ritual into every repetition. Jacob barely fidgeted, only occasionally pausing to rummage through the treasures in his pockets, or pull at his cheeks to make his eyes look big and scary, or reach out to test the size of his own nose next to Mordecai’s, curious as a small monkey. Mordecai never paused, happy as long as the boy’s young vocal cords kept repeating the words. But more often than not, some sudden impulse would send Jacob leaping away to chase some wild, energetic game, and he would only repeat the last few words he had heard back to himself in a silly singsong rhythm, bouncing around the room, mangling the verse Mordecai had poured so much of his limited strength into writing. But Mordecai waited with the patience of a prophet, undiscouraged, and tried his strange little printing game again the next day, telling himself, “One day these words will rule him. Their meaning will flash bright in his mind. That is how it works for a whole nation, after many, many days.”

Meanwhile, Jacob had a whole new store of magical, unintelligible sounds he used to make his baby sister giggle, chase the big house cat into a dark corner, or threaten to terrify any Christian boy his own age he met on the street. One week he saw a street mountebank performing tricks, and all his new muscle memory went completely off track, away from the beautiful new Hebrew poetry he was supposed to be learning, modeled after the great Yehuda Halevi. Mordecai had just gotten to a new, impassioned section of his poem, the consumptive man’s strong high baritone made hoarse by a haze of cough, his breath catching every few moments, rising almost into a chant as he recited verses that meant:
“Away from me the garment of forgetfulness,
That withers the heart.
The oil and wine from the presses of strangers,
Poisoned through and through with scorn.
Solitude hangs over the slopes of Mount Nebo,
A tomb is hidden in its heart.
There lie the buried ark and golden cherubim,
Shining with hidden light.
There the solemn, unchanging gaze waits,
Their wings spread unbroken.
Shut beneath that silent, awful speech,
The Law lies carved in stone.
Solitude and darkness wrap me around,
My own heart is a tomb.
Strike and shatter it, O Gabriel!
Shatter it like the soft clay of the caster,
Wrapped around the golden image.”

Lost in the wild enthusiasm of that final invocation, Mordecai did not notice Jacob had climbed off his knees and run off. He paused, and suddenly saw the boy was on his hands, feet in the air, doing a perfect mountebank handstand, trying to pick up a shiny farthing he kept among his favorite pocket treasures with his lips. It was a silly trick, one Mordecai was used to—but at that exact moment, it felt like a horrifying, mocking grin from Satan himself, right at the prayer he had just poured his whole soul into. “Child! Child!” he cried out, in a sharp, strange voice that made Jacob jump to his feet in terror. Then he sank backward, shuddering, and closed his eyes. “What?” Jacob yelped. When he got no answer, he shook Mordecai’s knees roughly to rouse him. Mordecai opened his eyes, a fierce light burning in their dark sunken depths, leaned forward, and grasped the little boy’s shoulders, whispering hoarse and fast, “A curse is on your generation, child. They will break open the mountain, drag the golden cherubim wings out, and melt them down to make coins, and they will break those solemn, holy faces into tiny trinkets, earrings for vain, wanton women! They will take new, empty names for themselves, but the angel of shame, with his fiery brand, will know every last one of them. Their hearts will become tombs of dead, hollow desires that rot their lives away from the inside out.”

Mordecai’s face, his voice, his thin fingers digging gently into Jacob’s shoulders were so new, so mysterious, so full of vague, overwhelming threat, it felt like the soft, kind tutor he had always known had turned into some terrible, unknown monster. The little boy shook all over with awe, certain the whole house was going to tumble down around them, that they would never get to eat dinner ever again. But when the terrifying speech ended, and the fingers on his shoulders loosened their grip, the shock dissolved into tears. Jacob lifted his round, little face and wailed at the top of his lungs.
