London arrived in June, and the heat of the city seemed to sear the last remnants of Egypt onto Durrance's skin. By afternoon, seeking relief from the urban clamor, he wandered into Hyde Park. Beneath the canopy of trees, he sat alone, observing the elegance of the local women and the subtle shifts in fashion, his face bearing the indelible stamp of long absences—the gaze fixed, the posture still, marking him as a man carved apart by distant corners. Yet, amidst the strolling crowds, one pair of eyes lingered. As Durrance rose, a woman approached. It was Mrs. Adair. Her scrutiny was swift, almost furtive, scanning him from head to toe as if measuring the distance between the man standing there and the ghost of the boy she knew three years prior. She searched for the scars of time, the erosion of youth, but her attention caught on a single detail: she was dressed in black.

Without waiting for inquiry, she spoke softly. "My husband died eighteen months ago," she said. "He was thrown from his horse during a hunt with the Pytchleys. He died instantly."

"I had not heard," Durrance replied, his words clumsy with awkwardness. "I am sorry."

Mrs. Adair sat beside him, offering no further comment. She was a woman of enigmatic silences; her pale, placid countenance offered no window into the turmoil within. She remained motionless, a statue of grief, leaving Durrance uncomfortable. His memories of Mr. Adair were of a cheerful man, devoted above all to his wife, yet he had never truly considered how his wife had viewed him. To Durrance, Mr. Adair had often been a shadowy figure, and drawing upon his recollection for genuine sorrow proved difficult. Abandoning the attempt, he changed the subject.

"Are Harry Feversham and his wife in town?"

Mrs. Adair paused before answering. "Not yet," she said. Then, quickly correcting herself, she added, "I mean... the marriage never took place."

Durrance, though easily startled, did not show it in his tone. "I don't quite follow. Why did it fail?"

Mrs. Adair regarded him sharply, as if testing the depth of his curiosity. "I don't know why," she said. "Ethne can keep a secret if she chooses." She nodded slightly, acknowledging the unspoken truth. "The engagement was broken off the night of a dance at Lennon House."

Durrance turned fully to her. "Just before I left England three years ago?"

"Yes. So, you knew?"

"No. Until now, you've told me something about the night I departed Dover. What has happened to Harry?"

Mrs. Adair shrugged. "I know nothing. I haven't met anyone who knows. I doubt I've met anyone who has seen him since then. He must have left England."

Durrance reflected on this sudden vanishing act. The man with the haunted, despairing face he had seen on the pier as the steamer pulled away—this was his friend, Harry.

"And Miss Eustace?" he asked, his voice tinged with a strange timidity. "Has she remarried?"

Mrs. Adair took her time. "No," she said finally.

"Then she remains at Ramelton?"

Mrs. Adair shook her head. "A fire consumed Lennon House a year ago. Have you ever heard of a constable named Bastable?"

"I recall him. He introduced me to Miss Eustace and her father. I was traveling from Londonderry to Letterkenny and received a letter from Mr. Eustace, a stranger to me, inviting me to stay. I naturally refused, and Bastable arrested me on a magistrate's warrant the moment I stepped off the ferry."

"That is the man," Mrs. Adair confirmed. She recounted the tragedy of the fire. It seemed Bastable's bond with Dermod Eustace rested on a specific recipe for toddy, requiring a single oyster simmered in the pot to achieve perfection. Around two o'clock on a June morning, the spirit lamp tipped, spilling flame. Both men, intoxicated, were unaware until it was too late. Before the fire could be extinguished, water hoses had saturated the building, ruining the interior.

"There were worse consequences than the destruction itself," she continued, her voice flat, devoid of emotion. "The fire served as a beacon for Dermod's creditors. Already burdened by debt, Dermod collapsed into total ruin. Furthermore, he was soaked by the hoses and contracted a severe chill that nearly killed him, from which he has never fully recovered. You will find him a broken man. The estates have been leased, and Ethne now lives with her father in a small mountain village in Donegal."

Throughout her account, Mrs. Adair did not look at Durrance. Her gaze remained fixed forward, speaking not from feeling, but from a rigid necessity to utter the truth. When she finished, she did not turn to meet his eyes.

"So she has lost everything?" Durrance asked.

"She still has a home in Donegal," Mrs. Adair replied.

"And that means a great deal to her," Durrance mused slowly. "Yes, I suppose you are right."

"It means," Mrs. Adair observed, "that despite her ill fortune, Ethne is envied by many other women."

Durrance did not answer directly. Instead, he watched the carriages roll past, listening to the laughter and chatter of the park-goers, refreshed by the light-colored dresses of the ladies. Yet, beneath the surface, his mind worked slowly toward a grim conclusion. Finally, Mrs. Adair grew impatient.

"What are you thinking?" she asked.

"That women suffer far more than men when the world turns against them," he answered, his statement sounding more like a question. "I know little, of course. I can only guess. But I believe women absorb their sorrows, making them a permanent part of themselves, akin to a missing limb. For men, the past is merely external—a rung on a ladder, or perhaps a weight on the heel. Don't you agree? My phrasing is poor, but consider it this way: women look backward; we look ahead. Therefore, misfortune strikes them harder, doesn't it?"

Mrs. Adair responded in her usual restrained manner. She did not explicitly agree, but a note of humble resignation colored her voice. "The mountain village where Ethne resides is called Glenalla. A path leads up to it from the road halfway between Rathmullen and Ramelton." She rose, extending her hand. "Shall I see you?"

"You are still in Hill Street?" Durrance asked. "I shall be staying in London for some time."

Mrs. Adair raised her eyebrows. By nature, she sought intricate, concealed motives, often baffled by actions driven by simple reasons. Now, she was puzzled by Durrance's decision to remain in the capital. If his thoughts traveled to Donegal, surely his feet should follow. Hearing of his frequent visits to his Service Club, she could not comprehend his hesitation. Even when he later revealed that he had traveled to Surrey to spend a day with General Feversham, she failed to suspect his true intent.

That visit with the General had been futile. The old man kept him firmly bound to the history of the recent campaign, refusing to acknowledge anything beyond the battlefield. Only once did Durrance broach the subject of Harry's disappearance. At the mere mention of his son's name, the General's face hardened into a mask of blank, unresponsive stone. "We will discuss something else, if you please," the General declared. Consequently, Durrance returned to London not an inch closer to Donegal.

For days thereafter, Durrance sat beneath a large tree in the inner courtyard of his club. He engaged in conversation with every passerby, but the thread always led back to Harry Feversham. Every question he posed yielded only a shrug and a dismissive, "Hanged if I know!" Harry's absence had become a blank space in the social fabric, ignored by friends and acquaintances alike.

Then, toward the end of June, an elderly retired naval officer limped into the courtyard. Upon seeing Durrance, the old man hesitated, then moved away with surprising alacrity.

Durrance leaped from his seat. "Mr. Sutch! You've forgotten me?"

"Lieutenant Sutch, sir!" the embarrassed man stammered. "Colonel Durrance, certainly! It has been some time, but I remember you perfectly now. Let me see... where did we meet? An old man's memory is like a leaking ship; it arrives at harbor with its cargo of recollections swamped."

Durrance noted the lieutenant's hesitation and subsequent shame. "We met at Broad Place," he said. "I require news of my friend, Feversham. Why was his engagement to Miss Eustace broken? Where is he now?"

Sutch's eyes gleamed briefly with satisfaction. He had always suspected Durrance was ignorant of Harry's disgrace. Plainly, the colonel did not know.

"There is only one person in the world, I believe," Sutch said, "who can answer both your questions."

Durrance was undeterred. "Yes. I have been waiting here a month for you."
