“And therewith he blent, and cried ha! As though he had been stricken to the harte.” — *Palamon and Arcite*

So it came to pass with Chaucer’s knight in prison; so it came to pass with Don Guzman de Soto. And this is how it happened.

After his release on parole, Don Guzman settled quietly into life at Bideford, lodged in quarters far better than any he’d known in years. He bore himself like a true soldier of fortune—accepting what came without complaint, content to wait for opportunity to present itself. He had been under Sir Richard Grenville’s roof scarcely a month when old Salterne, the Mayor of Bideford, arrived for supper.

At first, Don Guzman found it strange—indeed, almost absurd—that Englishmen should invite merchants and tradesmen to dine at the same table as noblemen, especially above the salt. But he was no fool. He knew that Richard Grenville, though a man of humble origin by Spanish standards, was a gentleman of principle and honor. If he chose to sit beside a ledger-keeper, it was because such customs were right and proper in England. So, though inwardly raising an eyebrow, Don Guzman accepted the arrangement. He ate and drank with dignity, even as the Mayor sat beside him, penning accounts and managing apprentices, speaking plainly but wisely with Grenville.

Before the evening ended, the Spaniard found himself exchanging words with Salterne—not out of obligation, but because the man proved courteous, prudent, and keenly aware of the nobleman’s rank. Yet he did not bow to it. Instead, he spoke with the quiet authority of a seasoned merchant prince, much like those of Genoa or Venice whom Don Guzman had once met in the courts of Europe.

After supper, Salterne asked Grenville to return the next night, and then turned to the Spaniard, saying frankly: “I know well how great a condescension it would be for a nobleman of Spain to grace my humble table. But if you would do me the honor, I promise you the fare shall be worthy of any rank, however low the company may seem.”

Don Guzman, finding little else to occupy his time, graciously accepted. The supper was excellent, and the wine—though he barely touched it—was generous.

Now, Mr. Salterne was not only a merchant, but a man of vision. His mind was ever drawn toward distant lands and ventures beyond the sea—a trait that would later mark him as one of the chief architects of Virginia’s settlement. And so, when a guest who might know something of foreign shores sat before him, he saw an opportunity. First, he offered strong sack in hopes of loosening tongues; second, he began asking shrewd, well-placed questions about the Spanish Main, Peru, the Moluccas, China, and the Indies.

The first plan failed. Don Guzman drank little more than water—so abstemious was he, he might have been mistaken for a monk. The second fared better, yet still fell short. For the Spaniard was cunning as a fox and answered with careful vagueness, spinning tales that pleased without revealing truth.

Then, just as the conversation grew thin, in stepped Rose Salterne—the Rose of Torridge—her beauty as radiant as ever. Hearing her father’s inquiries, she added her own questions, artless and bright. And now, unable to refuse her, Don Guzman found himself speaking freely—without betraying secrets, but with enough charm and detail to make the evening memorable.

And so, the seeds of desire took root.

For though some may call Eros the son of Jove and Venus, others—like Burton in his *Anatomy of Melancholy*—declare him born of idleness and fullness of bread. And indeed, Don Guzman, idle in captivity and well-fed at Sir Richard’s table, had begun to seek amusement where he could find it. Lady Grenville, being nearest, was the first to cross his mind. But he was a man of honor—and Sir Richard was his host. So he cast aside the thought, proud of his self-restraint. Such a chase, though thrilling to both pride and passion, was too dangerous, too unseemly.

As for sin? In that age, such matters were less discussed than lived. Southern Europeans cared little for moral censure, and the English, later under the Stuarts, would prove no different. Still, Don Guzman kept his distance from Lady Grenville. And thus, space opened for another—Rose Salterne.

Not from malice, nor with intent to wound, but simply for amusement—and perhaps because he could not help it—he found himself drawn to her. There was a freshness about her, a natural charm, that stirred him. She loved admiration, yes—but it was innocent, unpretentious. To him, she seemed like “the train of the peacock,” dazzling in appearance, yet possessing “the heart of the dove”—a rare and captivating blend.

Had she consented to love only him, he might have become foolish enough to love her alone. But at that thought, a sudden panic seized him—prudence overcame passion. He resolved to keep away.

Yet days dragged slowly. Lady Grenville, when home, spoke only of her husband. When she went to Stow, leaving the Don alone in the great house, what could he do but wander down to the butt-gardens? There, he showed off his fine black cloak and feathered hat, watched the shooting, played rackets with the young men, bowls with the elders—and inevitably found himself invited back to supper by Mr. Salterne.

And there, he ruled the evening. Not merely by rank, but by wit and experience. For these were the dawn days of English commerce. Every merchant in Bideford, nay, all across England, burned with dreams of discovery, trade routes, patents, and colonies. They spoke of Sir Edward Osborne’s Turkish Company, the Sultan Murad’s new privileges to English traders, Roger Bodenham’s voyages in the *Aucher*, John Fox, Lawrence Aldersey, John Rule—all sailing the Levant. The fall of Famagusta and Elizabeth’s alliance with the Grand Turk had opened the Mediterranean to English ships.

And so, every word Don Guzman spoke of the East or West Indies found fertile ground. Master Edmund Hogan had returned from Morocco. John Hawkins and George Fenner had sailed to Guinea—Walter Wren, a native of Bideford, among them—and traded for musk, civet, gold, and grain. African news was nearly as valuable as news from the Americas.

Just two months prior, Captain Hare had set sail from London in the *Minion*, bound for Brazil, carrying Sheffield hardware, Devonshire kersies, hollands, and Manchester cottons. A man named John Whithall, who had married a Spanish heiress and owned an ingenio in Santos, had opened the door for English goods.

And Drake—Drake had proven not only that the American coasts could be plundered, but that an East Indian trade was possible. Frobisher and Davis had begun probing the Arctic ice, seeking a northern passage to China. Anthony Jenkinson, traveler and agent for the Muscovite Company, had journeyed through Russia, Persia, Armenia, Tartary, Siberia—even the Arctic shores where Sir Hugh Willoughby had perished thirty years before.

Under Elizabeth’s wise rule, English commerce flourished. And as Don Guzman listened, he realized—shrewdly—that these islanders were not mere traders. They were a people determined to defy Spain’s dominion. They hated war, yes—but when provoked, they fought with grim resolve. Their merchant ships sailed armed to the teeth, manned by men trained since childhood to fight, ordered to defend their profits with steel.

One evening, after civilly warning that Englishmen who trespassed might be sent back, Don Guzman heard a volley of defiance:

“We’ll see that, sir.”
“Depends on who says ‘No right.’”
“You found might right when you claimed the Indian seas; we may find right might when we try them.”

He rose, furious. “You are insolent burghers!”

But old Salterne stepped forward. “Sir, we are plain men, and perhaps we’ve spoken rashly. We beg your pardon. It was the strength of my wine. We meant no offense to a noble gentleman and foreigner.”

Don Guzman refused to be appeased. He stormed out, calling himself a fool, a blinkard—how could he have lowered himself to such company? Yet he forgot that he had brought it upon himself.

Next day, Salterne—driven by the great devil Mammon—came again, begging forgiveness. He promised that none who had offended would be invited again, if only the Don would return.

And so, the Spaniard returned—sneering at himself the whole way. “Fool that I am! That girl has bewitched me. Go I must, and eat my share of dirt, for her sake.”

And once inside, he cleverly hinted to Salterne that he had grown so fond of his hospitality that he might reveal things he wouldn’t tell others. “The Spaniards,” he said, “do not fear single traders. But they dread any general effort to strip us of our wealth.” Then he added, with a smile, “Yet there are still many chances for one man here and there to grow rich.”

Salterne, sharp as he was, had a weakness—and the Spaniard struck it. He delighted in learning the secrets of Spain’s monopoly. Often, he urged Rose to question Don Guzman, blind to the danger—believing that no daughter of a merchant would marry a Catholic Spaniard, and no nobleman would marry a burgher’s daughter. He trusted religion and pride to keep them apart.

Love without marriage? Impossible. Rose’s virtue was beyond question—her father had staked his fortune on it. And the Don? Well, he valued his life—though Salterne gave him credit for that, even though Don Guzman cared little for his own neck.

So it came to pass that for weeks and months, the merchant’s house became the Don’s favorite haunt. He saw Rose daily. She heard him speak of distant lands—of vast caverns, deserts, cannibals, men whose heads grew beneath their shoulders. She listened, entranced.

And he, in turn, seasoned his tales with scraps of trade secrets—just enough to keep Salterne eager for more.

His stories were worth hearing. He seemed to have seen everything: born in Peru, sent to Spain at ten; raised in Italy; a soldier in the Levant; an adventurer to the East Indies; back to America, to Mexico, to Spain, Rome, Ireland. Shipwrecked. Captive among savages. Standing at the edge of volcanic craters. Present at courts across Europe. Fighting Turks, Indians, lions, elephants, alligators—every beast and enemy imaginable. By thirty-five, he had lived three lifetimes.

He had endured the siege of Famagusta, escaped the Turks, narrowly avoiding death by flaying or impalement. He had fought at the Battle of the Three Kings, seen Stukely fall beneath a hundred lances, held the dying head of the King of Portugal in his hands.

And now, he told Rose one evening, what did he have left? A heart trampled harder than stone. No one to love. No one who loved him. Only fame remained—and even that was denied him, a prisoner in a foreign land.

“Have you no kin?” asked Rose, pitying.

“My two sisters are in a convent—they had neither money nor beauty. Dead to me. My brother is a Jesuit. Also dead to me. My father died by Indian hands in Mexico. My mother, a penniless widow, serves as companion to some princess in Seville—no better blood than herself. And I—devil! I’ve lost even my sword. Thus ends the house of De Soto.”

He meant to be pitied—and he was.

Then he shifted tone, telling Italian tales—romantic ones when Rose was near, bawdy ones when she was gone. She wept over Juliet and Desdemona long before they graced English stages. The men laughed at jests that would echo through centuries—if only Cinthio and Bandello could rise from their graves to claim them.

And so, time passed.

Before a year had passed, Rose Salterne was deeply in love with Don Guzman—more than he with her. Both sensed the truth, yet neither confessed it. She feared; he, pride.

For he soon realized: to marry her would mean dishonor to his blood. To compromise his lineage for a heretic’s daughter? All his efforts would be wasted.

He had come to admire the English custom—saluting ladies on the cheek at meeting. It was graceful, though it scandalized Romish visitors. He had seen Bideford burghers, reeking of onions, profane that gesture with Rose.

So one day, he offered it—to her alone. Not before her father.

But she refused. Quietly, firmly.

“No, sir. You should know my cheek is not for you.”

“Why?” he asked, anger rising. “It seems free enough to every counter-jumper in town.”

She replied, apologetically: “True, Don Guzman. But they are my equals.”

“And I?”

“You are a nobleman. You should remember that.”

“Ah,” he sneered, “so you prefer the shopkeeper?”

“Prefer?” she laughed. “It is only form. They mean nothing to me.”

“And I—less than nothing?”

She flushed. But answered with courage: “And why should you be anything to me? You have already condescended too much—giving us pleasant evenings. Do not condescend further. You wrong yourself—and me. No, sir. Not a step nearer. I will not! A salute between equals means nothing. But between you and me—it would mean everything. I vow, if you do not leave me now, I will complain to my father.”

“Do so, madam,” he said coldly. “I care as little for his anger as you for my misery.”

“Cruel!” she cried, trembling.

“I love you, madam!” he cried, throwing himself at her feet. “I adore you! Never mention rank again. I have forgotten it. Forgotten all but you! My light, my lodestar, my goddess! I plead as a suppliant, a beggar—though one who may one day be king! A prince now, a Lucifer of pride to all but you. To you, a wretch who grovels at your feet, crying, ‘Have mercy on me, on my loneliness, my homelessness!’ Ah, Rose—forgive the madness of my passion. You do not know the heart you break. Cold Northerners, you dream not how a Spaniard can love. Love? Worship, rather. I bless the captivity that brought me to you, the ruin that made me rich. Is it possible, saints and Virgin? Are there tears in your eyes too?”

“Go, sir!” she cried, regaining composure. “And let me never see you again.”

And with that, she fled.

“The slave obeys,” said Don Guzman, bowing low. “I kiss your hands and feet forever.” Then, straightening, he walked calmly from the house.

Upstairs, Rose peered from the window, watching him go—his jaunty stride, his careless air. Her heart sank.

How much of that speech was true? How much rehearsed? She began to think it all a script—until she saw he had truly kept his word. He never returned.

And so, she reproached herself. If he died, she would be his murderess. She watched for him at the window, hoping he would look up—then hid in terror when he appeared around the corner.

Such is love. One tale much like another. Since Adam and Eve, it has been so—ready-made, not made.

But while Rose mourned, greater destinies loomed. Elizabeth’s marriage to Anjou hung in balance. Sir Humphrey Gilbert stirred heaven and earth, striving to fulfill his patent—bringing England Newfoundland, Nova Scotia, Canada, the Northern States. And for Gilbert himself—something greater than a world: another world, and a crown that never fades.
