Within the quiet seclusion of a Dominican convent, a solitary stranger lay in a guest chamber, his strength nearly spent by waves of violent nausea that had finally receded. His condition mirrored the fragility of Margaret in Holland, leaving him as weak as dust. A roaring wood fire crackled on the hearth, casting warm light upon the patient's hanging garments, while a towering friar knelt nearby, his voice resonating softly as he read sacred collects from his breviary.

At intervals, the dying man opened weary eyes to gaze upon the monk, listening intently, only to close them again in a moan of pain. The friar prostrated himself, pressing his face to the earth in fervent prayer, before rising to bid farewell. "Day breaks," the monk announced gently. "I must prepare for Matins."

"Good Father Jerome," the stranger gasped, his voice thin. "Before you depart, tell me—how did I arrive here?"

"By the hand of Heaven," the friar replied, his tone grave yet kind. "You cast aside God's gift, and He has returned it to you. Reflect on this! You sought the bitterness of the world and found only gall. Now turn to the Church! Here lies peace. *Pax vobiscum*."

With those words, the friar departed. Gerard lay back, his mind reeling in meditation, until exhaustion dragged him into a heavy slumber.

When consciousness returned, a new figure sat by his side—a layman whose eyes were small, darting, and far less tranquil than the majestic stillness of Friar Jerome.

"How do you feel?" the man inquired with genuine concern.

"Extremely weak," Gerard murmured. "Messer, where have I encountered you before?"

"None the worse for my gauntlet?" the man asked, his anxiety evident. "I intended to strike you down, or both of us might well have perished at the bottom of the Tiber."

Gerard stared, bewildered. "Did you save me? How?"

"At your service, signor," the man explained. "I was by the banks of the Tiber on some errand of my own. You approached me, pleading desperately for a dagger's stroke. Yet, even as you spoke, I recognized you—the very gentleman who rescued my wife and child upon the sea."

"It is Teresa's husband!" Gerard exclaimed, horror dawning. "And an assassin?!"

"At your disposal," the man continued calmly. "Next, you threw yourself into the river and commanded me to stand aside. If anyone else had done so, I would have obeyed instantly, neither stirring a finger. My enemies are many enough to warrant such caution. But when you sank in the Tiber and cried out 'Margaret!'"

"Ah!" Gerard interrupted.

"My heart screamed 'Teresa!'" the man confessed, his voice trembling slightly. "How could I return home and face her, knowing I had allowed you to perish—the same man who had saved her life? Thus, I entered the water. Fortune favored us both, for I swim with the ease of a duck. Seeing me approach, intent on destruction, you grasped at me, seeking to drag us both under. But I swam around you—and forgive me, Sir Gerard—and struck the back of your neck with my steel-gloved fist. You lost consciousness, and with great effort, battling the strong current, I drew your body to shore. You were insensible, drowned. I carried you on my back to my home. I reasoned, 'Teresa will nurse him, and she will approve of my deed.'"

"But a holy friar, the largest I have ever beheld, intercepted us just outside the monastery," the man recounted. "He questioned me. I told him everything. He gazed at you sharply. 'I know this face,' he declared. 'It is Gerard, the fair youth from Holland.' 'The same,' I confirmed. Then his Reverence spoke, 'He possesses friends within our order. Leave him with us! This is charity, our divine office.'"

"He further advised me that the convent possessed superior means to tend to you than I did," the man concluded. "His counsel proved sound. Consequently, I negotiated permission to visit you daily, and behold—you are here."

As he spoke, the man cast a peculiar glance upon Gerard, blending affection with intense interest.

Gerard offered no response. A cold chill ran through him, as though a serpent coiled within the room. He shut his eyes tight.

"Ay, you wish to sleep," the visitor pressed eagerly. "I withdraw." He retreated silently on tiptoe, promising his daily return.

Gerard remained motionless, his eyes closed, yet his mind raced with profound turmoil. Saved from death by a murderer? Was this not the work of Heaven itself? Of that Heaven he had once insulted, cursed, and defied?

Shuddering at the weight of his blasphemies, Gerard attempted to pray. Words formed on his lips, yet they failed to carry the spirit of devotion.

"I am damned eternally!" he cried out, his voice filled with dread. "Doomed, doomed!"

Suddenly, the organ of the convent church erupted in a rich, solemn harmony, filling the air. Above the swelling chorus of the choir, a single voice soared—a sweet, boyish tenor, pure and angelic, seeming to hover toward the heavens.

Gerard closed his eyes and listened. In those ethereal harmonies, the days of his childhood flooded back, untainted by earthly corruption, devoid of foul and raging passions that had rent his soul.

"Peace," he whispered, tears streaming down his cheeks. "Sweet, balmy peace. Until I abandoned her bosom, I knew no sorrow, no sin."

And the broken, weary creature wept bitterly.

Just as his grief overwhelmed him, the soft, reverend visage of one he believed lost forever appeared before him. It was Father Anselm. The good father had arrived at the convent only the previous night. Gerard recognized him instantly and called out, "Oh, Father Anselm! You healed my wounded body in Juliers; now heal my shattered soul in Rome! Alas, you cannot."

Anselm sat beside the bed, placing a gentle hand upon Gerard's head. First, he soothed the agitated youth with kind words, then, having learned the truth of Gerard's arrival, he spoke to him with solemn gravity. He laid bare the magnitude of Gerard's crime, urging him toward repentance and gratitude for the Divine Power that had frustrated his will to damn his soul.

"Come, my son," Anselm said warmly. "First, cleanse your breast of its burden."

"Oh, Father," Gerard sobbed. "In Juliers, I could do so; then I was innocent. But now, an impious monster that I am, I dare not confess to you."

"Why not, my son?" Anselm pressed gently. "Do you think I have not sinned against Heaven in my time? Oh, how deeply! Come, poor, laden soul, pour forth your grief, unburden yourself of your faults, hide nothing! Do not lie crushed beneath the weight of secret sins."

Moved by the father's compassion, Gerard eventually fell to his knees, confessing every sin with deep sighs and groans of penitence.

"Your sins are grievous," Anselm acknowledged. "Yet your temptation was equally terrible. I must consult our Prior regarding your penance."

Kissing Gerard's brow, the good father departed to confer with the superior. And lo! Gerard could now pray. He prayed with all the fervor of his heart.

The extraordinary journey of this mind may be summarized in a single word: Penitence. With terror and revulsion, Gerard turned away from the world, begging passionately to remain within the convent walls. To him, the monastery was like a wounded bird returning to its gentle nest. There, he spent his novitiate in prayer, self-mortification, pious reading, and deep meditation.

Meanwhile, far away, Claelia's spy hastened home, reporting that Gerard was certainly dead, the circumstances of his passing still unknown. She stood utterly stunned. When she finally found breath to speak, her words were laced with despair. "So swiftly," she sighed, "these monsters commit evil. They arrive in our hour of passion, first tempting us with venal daggers, then enacting the deadly deeds we never imagined without their instigation."

Hours passed, and her pity for Gerard mingled with a burning hatred for his killer, rising to a fever pitch, fueled by the fool's bloodlust. "Poor soul!" she lamented. "I cannot call thee back to life. But he who treacherously slew thee shall never live."

She immediately dispatched armed men into ambush, keeping them in vigil throughout the day. Her plan was set: when Lodovico arrived to collect his payment, they would spring from concealment in a specific antechamber and hack him to pieces.

"Strive for his head!" she commanded fiercely. "For he wears a private coat of mail. Should he escape alive, your own heads shall answer for it."

Thus, she sat weeping over her fallen victim, pulling the strings of machinery designed to spill the blood of a second life, having used it merely as a tool to take the first.
