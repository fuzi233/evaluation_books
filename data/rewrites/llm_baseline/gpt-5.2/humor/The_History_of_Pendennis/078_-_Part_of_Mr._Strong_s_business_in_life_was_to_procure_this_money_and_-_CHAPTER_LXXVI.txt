Our little world had turned another page of the calendar, and—like a stew that tastes the better for being left overnight—everybody’s affairs had had time to simmer, separate, and come back together again at the same small country town where we first saw them. Clavering, in short, was once more full of people who had business with one another, and full of those louder, newer sounds—hammers, wagons, engineers’ whistles—that a railway drags after it like tin kettles tied to a bride’s chaise.

The Clavering Arms, that old inn whose very beams seemed seasoned with generations of port and parish gossip, had been taken by Frederic Lightfoot—formerly Sir Francis Clavering’s man of ceremonies and steward of sauces—who now begged leave to inform the nobility and gentry of the county, by means of a printed flourish, that his house had been repaired, decorated, and made “comfortable,” with stabling for hunters, a billiard-room for the idlers, and cellars selected “without regard to expense.” When a man tells you that last sentence, you may generally assume he means, “without regard to my own sobriety.”

Still, if comfort can be measured by warmth, smell, and the happy racket of knives and plates, the old place had never been so alive. Fresh paint shone over the gateway; Christmas holly in the windows threw its sharp green scent into the coffee-room; and even the town’s justice—those solemn men who love a bench nearly as much as they love a dinner—held their petty sessions in the card-room as if the law itself required a sideboard.

Most important of all, the farmers’ ordinary had returned in force, drawn less by politics than by Mrs. Lightfoot’s cooking. She was a woman who could make domestic routine feel like a small myth: a pot on the hob, a ladle, and suddenly you were travelling. Her curries came out with that yellow confidence which announces turmeric before you’ve even lifted the spoon; her mulligatawny arrived steaming like a sermon, with a breath of pepper and sour apple that made the nose sit up, attentive and cheerful. Major Stokes of Fairoaks Cottage, Captain Glanders, H.P., and the rest of the local worthies had pronounced in favour of these dishes in the weighty manner of men who believe taste is a kind of public duty. They had pronounced in favour more than once, too—both in private and at the dinner of the Clavering Institute, when the reading-room was incorporated and the town congratulated itself on learning to read as if it were discovering fire.

At that dinner Sir Francis Clavering sat in the chair, supported by Dr. Portman the rector; and Barker, Esq., the enterprising ribbon-maker and chief director of the Clavering and Chatteris Branch of the Great Western Railway, occupied the vice-chair with the air of a man who hears the future clanking toward him on iron rails. Outside, indeed, the works were already busy: engineers shouting, picks biting, carts creaking, and that harsh new grammar of industry beginning to over-talk the older language of wind in hedgerows and rooks in elms. A village has its own acoustic memory, and you could hear it being overwritten.

Meanwhile the county paper—the Chatteris Champion, Clavering Agriculturist, and Baymouth Fisherman, which loved the British oak with a loyalty as profitable as it was loud—had been whispering, printing, and practically singing through its columns about an “interesting event” likely to occur in the life of Arthur Pendennis, Esq. It understood (it always understands) that Mr. Pendennis had relinquished notions of offering himself for the borough because of a matrimonial alliance with an accomplished and lovely lady connected by nearest ties with Clavering Park. Lady Clavering and Miss Amory, it said, had arrived for Christmas; aristocracy would gather; festivities would commence with the new year; and if Sir Francis’s health failed, a young gentleman of colossal fortune and the highest relations would take the seat. The paper’s sentences strutted about like footmen in borrowed livery.

From all which an ingenious reader might deduce what had happened in the month’s pause of our story.

Lady Rockminster, who had constituted herself guardian to Laura Bell, grumbled at first—as old ladies do, from principle—at Laura’s preference for Pendennis over the more showy Bluebeard who had once seemed to loom in the distance. But even Lady Rockminster could not ask a girl to marry a secret; and those who knew the secret knew Laura had, in fact, no choice at all. Besides, the old Countess—who took a stern pleasure in doing what she pleased—was not ill satisfied that her young charge was about to accomplish the great purpose assigned by society to young ladies: namely, to marry.

She announced the news that very night to her maid, Mrs. Beck; and of course Mrs. Beck, who was perfectly aware of every circumstance, and who kept Martha of Fairoaks perfectly aware in return, performed the proper ceremonial astonishment as neatly as she would have folded a shawl.

“Mr. Pendennis’s income is so much; the railroad will give him so much more, he says; Miss Bell has so much, and may have a little more one day,” Lady Rockminster summed up, as if she were tasting and measuring ingredients. “For persons in their degree, they will manage. And I shall speak to my nephew Pynsent—who I suspect was once rather attached to her, but of course that was out of the question.”

“Oh! of course, my lady; I should think so indeed!” cried Mrs. Beck, in the tone of a woman who could deny the sun at noon if it pleased her employer.

“I shall speak to George Pynsent,” continued her Ladyship, “who is now chief secretary of the Tape and Sealing Wax Office, and have Mr. Pendennis made something. And, Beck, in the morning you will carry down my compliments to Major Pendennis and say I shall pay him a visit at one o’clock.”

Then, when the maid had gone, the Countess muttered to herself—more honestly than she had spoken aloud—“Yes. The Major must be reconciled. And he must leave his fortune to Laura’s children.”

Accordingly, at one o’clock Lady Rockminster swept into Major Pendennis’s lodgings, and the Major—who could no more resist nobility than a moth can resist a candle—was delighted to receive so great a visitor. He had been prepared, if not for the actual announcement, at least for the intelligence that Pen’s projected marriage with Miss Amory was broken off; for Arthur had bethought himself of his uncle that day (for the first time, it must be owned, with a nephew’s usual punctuality), and had sent a note from the hotel coffee-room after meeting his new servant in the hall.

“Dear uncle,” Pen wrote, “if there has been any question between us, it is over now. I went to Tunbridge Wells yesterday and found somebody else had carried off the prize about which we were hesitating. Miss A., without any compunction for me, has bestowed herself upon Harry Foker, with his fifteen thousand a year. I came in suddenly upon their loves and found and left him in possession.

“And you’ll be glad to hear Tatham writes me that he has sold three of my fields at Fairoaks to the Railroad Company at a great figure. I will tell you this and more when we meet; and am always your affectionate,—A. P.”

So, when Lady Rockminster sat down with her great air of doing everybody a favour by existing, the Major met her with a bow as courtly as if he had been carved by a Frenchman.

“I think I am aware of what you were about to tell me,” he said, smiling and fawning with perfect elegance. “It was a very great kindness of your Ladyship to bring me the news. How well you look! How very good you are! How very kind you have always been to that young man!”

“It was for the sake of his uncle,” Lady Rockminster returned, with that neat cruelty of politeness which makes a compliment feel like a pinch.

“He has written me a nice note—yes, a nice note,” the Major continued, purring. “And I find he has had an increase to his fortune—yes. All things considered, I don’t much regret that this affair with Miss Amory is manquée, though I wished for it once. In fact, all things considered, I am very glad of it.”

“We must console him, Major Pendennis,” said the Countess. “We must get him a wife.”

Then the truth—like an oyster sliding off its shell—came across the Major’s mind, and he saw for what purpose her Ladyship had honoured him with her ambassage.

It is not necessary to report their entire negotiation. There was no reason why Arthur should not marry according to his own and his mother’s wish; and Lady Rockminster supported the marriage with certain intimations which weighed wonderfully with the Major—intimations we shall not repeat, since her Ladyship is still alive and families are sensitive. The old gentleman was overcome by her determined graciousness and her fondness for Laura. Nothing could be more bland than her demeanour—except for one moment, when the Major spoke of his boy “throwing himself away,” at which her Ladyship burst into a little speech that made him understand (as Pen and his friends very humbly acknowledge) that Laura was a thousand times too good for him. Laura was fit to be a queen; Laura was a paragon of virtue; Laura was excellence served up plain and hot, without any need of sauce.

And it must be confessed that when Major Pendennis found a Countess seriously admired Miss Bell, he instantly began to admire her too—because certain people can only taste virtue when it is approved by the aristocratic palate.

So, when Herr Frosch was requested to go upstairs and inform Miss Bell and Mr. Arthur Pendennis that the Major would receive them, and Laura came in, blushing and happy, hanging on Pen’s arm, the Major gave a shaky hand to each with unaffected emotion. Then he went through another salutation to Laura—quite a separate bow, as if she were newly ennobled in his eyes—which made her blush still more. Happy blushes; bright eyes; the hush of a room when love is present, as if even the furniture were listening.

While Pen thus retreated into the safe harbour of being engaged to Laura, Blanche Amory—having bestowed her affections upon a blushing bridegroom with fifteen thousand a year—set off an outbreak of happiness at Clavering Park such as the good Begum, Lady Clavering, had not known for many years. Blanche and her mother were now on the most delightful terms of cordiality. The ardent Foker pressed the happy day forward with the zeal of a man who has at last got a dish promised and cannot bear the cook should keep him waiting. Milliners and jewellers were at work; the paraphernalia of Hymen glittered; Lady Clavering came up from Tunbridge in excellent humour; and Sir Francis, benefiting by sunshine that had nothing to do with his own deserts, found himself so reconciled to his lady that he came to London, sat at the head of his own table once more, and appeared tolerably flush of money again at billiard-rooms and gambling-houses.

One day, when Major Pendennis and Arthur went to dine in Grosvenor Place, they found an old acquaintance established as major-domo. The gentleman in black, who offered them with perfect gravity their choice of sweet or dry champagne, was no other than Mr. James Morgan. The Chevalier Strong was also present—ruddy, high-spirited, well-fed with foreign adventure—and entertained the party with accounts of his amusements abroad.

“It was my Lady who invited me,” Strong said to Arthur under his voice. “That fellow Morgan looked as black as thunder when I came in. He is about no good here. I will go away first and wait for you and Major Pendennis at Hyde Park Gate.”

Mr. Morgan helped the Major into his great-coat with an air of humility that did not fit his eyes, and muttered something about having accepted only a temporary engagement with the Claverings.

“I have got a paper of yours, Mr. Morgan,” said the Major.

“Which you can show, if you please, to Sir Francis, sir, and perfectly welcome,” Morgan replied, eyes downcast. “I’m very much obliged to you, Major Pendennis; and if I can pay you for all your kindness I will.”

Arthur overheard, and saw the look of hatred that came with the words, like a knife slid quietly back into a sleeve. He cried suddenly that he had forgotten his handkerchief and ran upstairs again. In the drawing-room Foker was still lingering, as men linger near sirens. Pen found the handkerchief easily enough—truth is obliging when you hunt for it in the right place—and gave Blanche a look full of meaning. We may suppose she understood meaning looks; for when he was going out again, she called after him with a laughing voice, “Oh, Arthur—Mr. Pendennis—I want you to tell dear Laura something!” and came to the door.

“What is it?” she asked, shutting it quickly behind her.

“Have you told Harry? Do you know that villain Morgan knows all?”

“I know it,” she said.

“Have you told Harry?”

“No, no,” she whispered. “You won’t betray me?”

“Morgan will,” said Pen.

“No, he won’t,” Blanche answered, clinging to the foolish hope of a frightened woman bargaining with a wolf. “I have promised him—n’importe. Wait until after our marriage—oh, until after our marriage—oh, how wretched I am.” And the girl who had been all smiles and brilliant playing a moment before suddenly looked like a child choking over a too-sweet mouthful.

Pen begged her—implored her—to tell Foker that very night. “It is no fault of yours. He will pardon you anything. Tell him now.”

But she had already put her bright mask on again, and in the next breath she was fluttering back into the room with a commission fit for a milliner: “And give her this—Il est là—with my love, please; and I beg your pardon for calling you back; and if she will be at Madame Crinoline’s at half-past three, and if Lady Rockminster can spare her, I should so like to drive with her in the park.”

Then she went in, singing, and kissing her hand as Morgan the velvet-footed came noiselessly up the carpeted stair. Blanche’s piano broke out into brilliant music again—a shower of quick, bright notes that tried to drown thoughts as a waterfall drowns voices. But music cannot always cleanse a room; sometimes it only makes the silence sharper when it stops.

Arthur joined his uncle, and they walked away together through London’s winter air. Pen told him briefly what had occurred.

“What was to be done?” Arthur asked.

“What is to be done, begad?” cried the Major. “What is to be done but to leave it alone? Begad, let us be thankful”—and he shuddered—“that we are out of the business, and leave it to those it concerns.”

“I hope to Heaven she’ll tell him,” said Pen.

“Begad, she’ll take her own course,” the Major replied. “Miss Amory is a dev’lish wide-awake girl, sir, and must play her own cards; and I’m doosid glad you are out of it—doosid glad, begad. Who’s this smoking? Oh, it’s Mr. Strong again. He wants to put in his oar, I suppose. I tell you, don’t meddle in the business, Arthur.”

Strong began once or twice as if he meant to speak of it, but the Major would not hear a word. He remarked on the moonlight on Apsley House, on the weather, on cabstands—anything rather than that subject; and bowed stiffly to Strong, clinging to his nephew’s arm as they turned down St. James’s Street, cautioning Pen again to let the affair alone. “It had like to have cost you so much, sir, that you may take my advice,” he said.

Later, when Arthur came out of the hotel, Strong’s cloak and cigar were visible a few doors off. The Chevalier met him with a jolly laugh.

“I’m an old soldier too,” he said. “I wanted to talk to you, Pendennis. I have heard of all that has happened, and all the chops and changes during my absence. I congratulate you on your marriage—and I congratulate you on your escape too; you understand me. It was not my business to speak, but I know this: a certain party is as arrant a little—well—well, never mind. You acted like a man and a trump, and are well out of it.”

“I have no reason to complain,” Pen said. “I went back to beg and entreat poor Blanche to tell Foker all. I hope, for her sake, she will; but I fear not. There is but one policy, Strong—there is but one.”

“And lucky he that can stick to it,” said Strong. “That rascal Morgan means mischief. He has been lurking about our chambers for the last two months. He has found out poor mad devil Amory’s secret. He has been trying to discover where he was—pumping Bolton, making old Costigan drunk several times. He bribed the inn-porter to tell him when we came back, and he has got into Clavering’s service on the strength of his information. He will get very good pay for it, mark my words—the villain.”

“Where is Amory?” asked Pen.

“At Boulogne, I believe. I left him there, and warned him not to come back. I have broken with him after a desperate quarrel, such as you might expect with such a madman. And I’m glad to think he is in my debt now—and that I have kept him out of more harms than one.”

“He has lost all his winnings, I suppose,” said Pen.

“No. He is rather better than when he went away—or was a fortnight ago. He had extraordinary luck at Baden: broke the bank several nights, and was the fable of the place. He allied himself there with a fellow named Bloundell, who gathered about him a society of sharpers of all sorts, male and female—Russians, Germans, French, English. Amory got so insolent that I was obliged to thrash him one day within an inch of his life. I couldn’t help myself; the fellow has pluck, and I had nothing for it but to hit out.”

“And did he call you out?” Pen asked.

“You think if I had shot him I should have done nobody any harm? No, sir. I waited for his challenge; it never came. Next time I met him he begged my pardon and said, ‘Strong, I beg your pardon; you whopped me and you served me right.’ I shook hands—but I couldn’t live with him after that. I paid him what I owed him the night before,” Strong added, blushing. “I pawned everything to pay him. Then, with my last ten florins, I had a shy at roulette. If I had lost, I should have let him shoot me in the morning. I was weary of my life. By Jove, sir, isn’t it a shame that a man like me—who may have had a few bills out, but never deserted a friend, never did an unfair action—shouldn’t be able to turn his hand to anything to get bread? I made a good night at roulette, and I’ve done with that. I’m going into the wine business. My wife’s relations live at Cadiz. I intend to bring over Spanish wine and hams; there’s a fortune to be made by it—here’s my card.”

And the Chevalier produced a handsome card declaring that Strong and Company, Shepherd’s Inn, were sole agents of the celebrated Diamond Manzanilla of the Duke of Garbanzos, Grandee of Spain of the First Class, and of the famous Toboso hams, fed on acorns only in the country of Don Quixote.

“Come and taste ’em,” he said, delighted at his own prospectus. “Come and try ’em at my chambers. You see I’ve an eye to business, and by Jove this time I’ll succeed.”

Pen laughed as he took the card. “I don’t know whether I shall be allowed to go to bachelors’ parties,” he said. “You know I’m going to—”

“But you must have sherry, sir. You must have sherry.”

“I will have it from you,” Pen said. “And I think you are very well out of your other partnership. That worthy Altamont and his daughter correspond, I hear.”

“Yes. She wrote him the longest rigmarole letters, that I used to read—the sly little devil; and he answered under cover to Mrs. Bonner. He was for carrying her off the first day or two, and nothing would content him but having back his child. But she didn’t want to come, as you may fancy; and he was not very eager about it.” Strong burst out laughing. “Why, sir, do you know what was the cause of our quarrel and boxing match? There was a certain widow at Baden—a Madame la Baronne de la Cruche-cassée—not much better than himself; and the scoundrel wanted to marry her, and would, but that I told her he was married already. I don’t think she was much better than he was. I saw her on the pier at Boulogne the day I came to England.”

And thus our narrative returned to the point to which the county paper’s announcement had already marched so proudly.

Only a few days remained before that blissful one when Foker should call Blanche his own. The Clavering folks had pressed in crowds to see the most splendid new carriage in the whole world—at least in their whole world—standing in the coach-house at the Clavering Arms, and shown, in grateful return for drink, by Mr. Foker’s head-coachman. Madame Fribsby was busy with gowns for the tenants’ daughters who were to figure as a sort of bridesmaids’ chorus; silks rustled, pins pricked, and the shop’s small language of hems and fastenings went on chattering its ancient dialect against the new iron noises outside.

Immense festivities were to take place at the Park upon this occasion.

“Yes, Mr. Huxter, yes; a happy tenantry, its country’s pride, will assemble in the baronial hall, where the beards will wag all,” cried Foker, flourishing his good humour in the bar of the Clavering Arms. “The ox shall be slain, and the cup they’ll drain; and the bells shall peal quite genteel; and my father-in-law, with the tear of sensibility bedewing his eye, shall bless us at his baronial porch. That shall be the order of proceedings, I think, Mr. Huxter. And I hope we shall see you and your lovely bride by her husband’s side; and what will you please to drink, sir? Mrs. Lightfoot, madam, you will give to my excellent friend and body-surgeon, Mr. Huxter—Mr. Samuel Huxter, M.R.C.S.—every refreshment that your hostel affords, and place the festive amount to my account. And Mr. Lightfoot, sir, what will you take? though you’ve had enough already, I think—yes, ha!”

He had apartments at the hotel and gathered a circle round him there, treating all to drink who came, hail-fellow with every man. He was so happy he seemed to carry a small band of music in his own chest: laughter, the clink of glasses, the thump of friendly hands on tables—sounds that, in another mood, might have been vulgar, but in his became almost wholesome. He danced round Madame Fribsby, Mrs. Lightfoot’s ally, as she sat pensive; he consoled Mrs. Lightfoot, who had already begun to discover that matrimony can sour like milk if left too long in the wrong cupboard.

For the truth must be told: young Lightfoot, now having full command of the cellar, had none over his own unbridled desires. The wines he had selected with such care now selected him in return. He was tippling and tipsy from morning till night; and it was a piteous sight for his fond wife to see the big youth reeling about the yard and coffee-room, or drinking with farmers and tradesmen those very neat wines and carefully chosen spirits that were meant to prove his respectability. The rich smell of cork and spirit, which should have promised comfort, began to promise only trouble.

When he could find time, Mr. Morgan the butler came from the Park and took a glass at the landlord’s expense. He watched Lightfoot’s drunken vagaries with savage sneers, as if each stagger were a line of a book he enjoyed reading. Mrs. Lightfoot felt doubly uncomfortable whenever her unhappy spouse was under his comrade’s eye. Married only a few months—and to be brought to this! Madame Fribsby could feel for her; Madame Fribsby could tell her stories of men every bit as bad, in that tone of experienced sympathy which is half consolation, half prophecy. So it is that nobody seems happy altogether; there are bitters, as Mr. Foker observed, in the cup of every man’s life.

And yet there did not seem to be any in his. His cup brimmed with good humour and expectation.

Mr. Morgan, for his part, was constant in his attentions to Foker—too constant, perhaps, like a cat that will not leave a pantry door.

“And yet I don’t like him somehow,” said the candid young man to Mrs. Lightfoot. “He always seems as if he was measuring me for my coffin somehow. Pa-in-law’s afraid of him—pa-in-law’s— ahem! never mind. But ma-in-law’s a trump, Mrs. Lightfoot.”

“Indeed my Lady was,” Mrs. Lightfoot admitted with a sigh, thinking perhaps that service under a mistress, however proud, had been quieter than partnership with a husband and a cellar.

“No, I do not like thee, Dr. Fell; the reason why I cannot tell,” Foker continued. “And he wants to be taken as my head man. Blanche wants me to take him. Why does Miss Amory like him so?”
