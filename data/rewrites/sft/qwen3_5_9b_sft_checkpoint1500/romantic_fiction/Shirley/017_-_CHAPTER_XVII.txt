It was not a gathering born of war, nor a quest for enemies, yet the band led by the priest and managed by the women played marches that stirred a martial longing in the hearts of the listeners. Miss Keeldar, for one, found these sounds awakening a spirit akin to battle, though not quite identical. Old Helstone chanced to look into her face and laughed, and she returned the laugh.

"There is no battle in prospect," Helstone remarked gently. "Our country does not ask us to fight for it. No foe threatens our liberty. We are merely taking a walk. Keep your hand on the reins, Captain, and dampen the fire of that spirit. It is not needed, and the pity of it is real."

"Take your own advice, Doctor," Shirley replied. To Caroline, she murmured, "I shall borrow from imagination what reality refuses to grant. We are not soldiers—bloodshed is not my desire—or if we must be, we are soldiers of the Cross. Time seems to have rolled back centuries, binding us on a pilgrimage to Palestine. But no, that is too visionary. I require a sterner dream. Imagine ourselves as Lowlanders of Scotland, following a Covenanting captain up into the hills to hold a meeting beyond the reach of persecuting troopers. We know that battle may follow prayer; and believing that even in the worst outcome heaven must be our reward, we are ready to redden the peat-moss with our blood. That music stirs my soul; it awakens all my life, making my heart beat not with its usual temperate pulse, but with a new, thrilling vigour. I almost long for danger—for a faith, a land, or at least a lover to defend."

"Look, Shirley!" Caroline interrupted, pointing upward. "What is that red speck above Stilbro' Brow? You have keener sight than I. Just turn your eagle eye to it."

Miss Keeldar squinted. "I see," she said, then quickly added, "there is a line of red. They are soldiers—cavalry soldiers. They ride fast. There are six of them. They will pass us. No; they have turned off to the right. They saw our procession and are avoiding it by making a circuit. Where are they going?"

"Perhaps they are only exercising their horses."

"Perhaps so. We see them no more now."

Mr. Helstone spoke then. "We shall pass through Royd Lane to reach Nunnely Common by a short cut," he said.

Into the straits of Royd Lane they filed. It was so narrow that only two could walk abreast without falling into the ditch that ran along each side. They had gained the middle of the lane when excitement became palpable among the clerical commanders. Boultby's spectacles and Helstone's glasses trembled; the curates nudged one another nervously; Mr. Hall turned to the ladies and smiled.

"What is the matter?" someone demanded.

He pointed his staff toward the end of the lane before them. Behold! Another procession was entering—an opposing party, headed by men in black and followed, as the sound now revealed, by music.

"Is it our double?" Shirley asked, her voice tight. "Our manifold wraith? Here is a card turned up."

"If you wanted a battle, you are likely to get one—at least of looks," Caroline whispered, laughing.

"They shall not pass us!" cried the curates unanimously. "We will not give way!"

"Give way!" Helstone retorted sternly, turning around. "Who talks of giving way? You, boys, mind what you are about. The ladies, I know, will be firm. I can trust them. There is not a churchwoman here who will not stand her ground against these folks, for the honour of the Establishment.—What does Miss Keeldar say?"

"She asks what it is."

"The Dissenting and Methodist schools, the Baptists, Independents, and Wesleyans, joined in an unholy alliance and deliberately turned into this lane with the intention of obstructing our march and driving us back."

"Bad manners!" Shirley exclaimed. "And I hate bad manners. Of course, they must have a lesson."

"A lesson in politeness," suggested Mr. Hall, who was ever inclined toward peace. "Not an example of rudeness."

Old Helstone moved on. Quickening his step, he marched some yards in advance of his company. He had nearly reached the other sable leaders when the man acting as their commander-in-chief—a large, greasy figure with black hair combed flat on his forehead—called a halt. The procession paused. He drew forth a hymn-book, gave out a verse, set a tune, and they all struck up the most dolorous of canticles.

Helstone signaled to his bands. They clashed out with all the power of brass. He commanded them to play "Rule, Britannia!" and ordered the children to join in vocally, which they did with enthusiastic spirit. The enemy was sung and stormed down; his psalm was quelled. As far as noise went, he was conquered.

"Now, follow me!" Helstone exclaimed. "Not at a run, but at a firm, smart pace. Be steady, every child and woman of you. Keep together. Hold on by each other's skirts, if necessary."

He strode on with such a determined and deliberate gait, seconded perfectly by his scholars and teachers who did exactly as he told them—neither running nor faltering, but marching with cool, solid impetus—that the curates, trapped between Helstone and Miss Keeldar, were compelled to do the same. Both watched any deviation with lynx-eyed vigilance, ready to rebuke the slightest breach of orders, the least independent or irregular demonstration, with his cane and her parasol respectively. Thus, the body of Dissenters was first amazed, then alarmed, then borne down and pressed back, until finally forced to turn tail and leave the outlet from Royd Lane free. Boultby suffered in the onslaught, but Helstone and Malone, working in tandem, held him up and brought him through the business whole in limb, though sorely tried in wind.

The fat Dissenter who had given out the hymn was left sitting in the ditch. He was a spirit merchant by trade, a leader of the Nonconformists, and, it was said, drank more water in that one afternoon than he had swallowed for a twelvemonth prior. Mr. Hall had taken care of Caroline, and Caroline of him. He and Miss Ainley exchanged quiet comments on the incident afterwards. Miss Keeldar and Mr. Helstone shook hands heartily when they had fairly got the whole party through the lane. The curates began to exult, but Mr. Helstone presently put the curb on their innocent spirits. He remarked that they never had sense to know what to say and would do better to hold their tongues, reminding them that the business was none of their managing.

About half-past three the procession turned back, and at four they regained the starting-place. Long lines of benches were arranged in the close-shorn fields round the school. There the children were seated, and huge baskets, covered with white cloths, and great smoking tin vessels were brought out. Ere the distribution of good things commenced, a brief grace was pronounced by Mr. Hall and sung by the children. Their young voices sounded melodious, even touching, in the open air. Large currant buns and hot, well-sweetened tea were then administered in the proper spirit of liberality. No stinting was permitted on this day; the rule for each child's allowance was that it should have about twice as much as it could possibly eat, thus leaving a reserve to be carried home for those prevented from coming to the feast by age, sickness, or other impediments. Buns and beer circulated amongst the musicians and church-singers; afterwards the benches were removed, and they were left to unbend their spirits in licensed play.

A bell summoned the teachers, patrons, and patronesses to the schoolroom. Miss Keeldar, Miss Helstone, and many other ladies were already there, glancing over the arrangement of their separate trays and tables. Most of the female servants of the neighbourhood, together with the clerks', singers', and musicians' wives, had been pressed into the service of the day as waiters. Each vied with the other in smartness and daintiness of dress, and many handsome forms were seen amongst the younger ones. About half a score were cutting bread and butter, another half-score supplying hot water brought from the coppers of the rector's kitchen. The profusion of flowers and evergreens decorating the white walls, the show of silver teapots and bright porcelain on the tables, the active figures, blithe faces, and gay dresses flitting about everywhere formed altogether a refreshing and lively spectacle. Everybody talked, not very loudly, but merrily, and the canary birds sang shrilly in their high-hung cages.

Caroline, as the rector's niece, took her place at one of the three first tables; Mrs. Boultby and Margaret Hall officiated at the others. At these tables the *élite* of the company were to be entertained, strict rules of equality not being more in fashion at Briarfield than elsewhere. Miss Helstone removed her bonnet and scarf that she might be less oppressed with the heat.
