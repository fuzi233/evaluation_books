Bartolommeo Scala, the esteemed secretary of the Florentine Republic, resided in a grand palazzo near the Porta Pinti—a home befitting his hard-won station. His coat of arms, an azure ladder upon a golden field, crowned with the motto *Gradatim*, declared his rise from humble origins to prominence, a journey he wore without shame. A man of vanity, perhaps, but not of false modesty; Scala believed in his own merit and saw no reason to pretend otherwise.

By now, he had climbed to the highest rung of his ambition. Twenty years as secretary, embassies to Rome, the title of Senator, Knight of the Golden Spur—even the Gonfaloniere’s office had been his. Yet success had brought its burdens: gout plagued him, forcing him to sit cushioned and immobile beneath the shaded loggia of his palace, overlooking the lush gardens where the May sun warmed the grass and flowers.

On this particular afternoon, he was not alone. His daughter Alessandra and her husband, the Greek poet Marullo, sat beside him, along with a pair of agreeable listeners—men not so illustrious as to overshadow their host. Yet despite the pleasant company, Scala’s mood was soured by the papers spread before him: a bitter exchange of letters between himself and the scholar Politian.

It was a quarrel born of petty insults disguised as scholarly debate. Years ago, Politian had sought Alessandra’s hand and been refused. Since then, his pen had been sharpened against Scala. The secretary, though a capable statesman, fancied himself a writer—a dangerous indulgence for a man of his pride. He penned Latin verses in leisure, sending them to friends as if they were gifts rather than indulgences.

Then came the fatal misstep. In a playful epigram, Scala had made the *culex*—the gnat—feminine. Politian, ever the meticulous scholar, pounced. His correction, wrapped in mock courtesy, was a barbed Greek epigram of his own, invoking Venus and Cupid, a fashionable conceit of the time.

Scala, stung, retaliated with a jest at Politian’s failed courtship. The scholar’s reply was swift and scathing. What began as a light exchange devolved into venomous pedantry. Politian mocked Scala’s Latin; Scala dismissed Politian’s Greek as overwrought. The secretary accused the scholar of arrogance; the scholar derided the secretary as a pompous dilettante.

By the time Tito Melema arrived, the quarrel had reached its peak. Scala, eager for validation, produced the letters, seeking judgment from his new guest. Tito, ever the diplomat, obliged—flattering Scala’s wounded pride while deftly avoiding outright insult to Politian.

The gems Tito carried—an agate with a striking likeness of Cupid astride a lion, a "Jew’s stone" with a serpent design—provided further diversion. Scala, ever the collector, purchased both, one for admiration, the other as a talisman against his gout.

By the end of the visit, Tito had secured an open invitation to return. He left the palazzo in high spirits, amused by the absurdity of the *culex* debate. Fortune, it seemed, was smiling on him at last.
