I have no mind to set down, step by step, every weary mile that carried us from Durban to Sitanda’s Kraal near where the Lukanga and the Kalukwe make acquaintance; a thousand miles is a long dish to serve whole, and most of its ingredients—heat, thirst, sore feet, the same horizon tasted day after day—are those that every man who has travelled and hunted in Africa already knows by smell alone.

We left Durban at the end of January; it was in the second week of May when we camped at last within reach of Sitanda’s people. The last three hundred miles we made on foot, for the curse of the tsetse lay over the country like an invisible net: it kills every beast that pulls a wagon save donkeys, and every beast that walks upright save man. At Inyati, that outlying trading-station in the Matabele land—where Lobengula, a great and cruel scoundrel, sits king—we parted, with honest regret, from the wagon that had been our little moving homestead.

Only twelve oxen were left to us out of the fine span of twenty I had bought at Durban. One had died by cobra bite; three by sheer poverty and want of water; one had strayed; three had been poisoned by the herb the Boers call “tulip.” Five more took that sickness too, but we saved them with an infusion made by boiling down the tulip leaves—bitter as burnt tea and rank as old cabbage-water, but if given in time it drags life back into an ox like a hand hauling a bucket from a well.

We left wagon and oxen to Goza and Tom—good boys both, driver and leader—and asked a worthy Scotch missionary there to keep an eye upon our property. Then Sir Henry Curtis and Captain John Good and I set off on foot upon our wild quest, Umbopa with us, and Ventvögel the Hottentot, and Khiva, and half a dozen bearers hired on the spot.

I remember the first hours after we turned our backs on the wagon: we tramped in a kind of hush, as though the road itself had swallowed our voices. The bush had its own talk—the tick of beetles in dead leaves, a far wood-dove calling as if it remembered some lost word, the steady pad-pad of many feet—but no man seemed eager to add his speech to it. I think each of us, in his own fashion, was asking whether we should ever see our wagon again. For my part I did not expect it.

Then Umbopa, who marched ahead with his head high and his shoulders loose and easy, broke out into a Zulu chant. It was one of those songs that travel from mouth to mouth without ever being written down, a whole history carried in rhythm and breath. It told how brave men, sick of tameness and of living only to eat and sleep, went into a vast wilderness to find new things or to die; and how, when they had gone far enough to lose the old tracks, the wilderness turned, like a pot with its lid lifted, and showed itself not barren at all but full of young wives and fat cattle, of game to hunt and enemies to kill.

The words were not mine; I could never put the exact colour of his sounds into English, for the sense was more than meaning—it was the weight of the vowels, the snap of the consonants, the way his voice rose and fell like wind in long grass. Yet even without perfect understanding, the song worked upon us. We laughed, and the laugh tasted good after silence; we took it for an omen. Umbopa was a cheerful savage in a dignified way, when he was not overtaken by those brooding fits that made him sit apart and look as though he listened to drums none of us could hear. He had a gift for keeping men’s spirits from drying up. We grew very fond of him.

Now, of all the incidents of that march, there is one I shall not deny myself; for I dearly love a hunting yarn, and this one, too, had a beginning that smelled of fruit and ended with the hot iron tang of blood.

About a fortnight from Inyati we came into a bit of woodland country so beautiful that it seemed almost indecent that it should be empty of houses and gardens. There was water everywhere: in the kloofs of the hills it ran under shadow, and the bush there was thick—idoro bush the natives call it—and in places the wacht-een-beche, the “wait-a-little thorn,” that lays hold of your clothes with a spiteful patience. There were many machabell trees, glossy-leaved and laden with yellow fruit, each fruit with a stone in it like a little round hammer. The fruit was refreshing—sharp-sweet, with a scent like crushed peel—and it is the elephant’s favourite food. The country told its own story in broken branches and uprooted trunks. An elephant feeds as a fire feeds: not neatly, but with appetite and destruction.

One evening, after a long day, we came to a place lovelier than the rest. At the foot of a bush-clad hill lay a dry river-bed, but in the sand were pools of water as clear as glass, rimmed all round with the stamped signatures of game—hoofprints laid over hoofprints in a kind of crowded writing. Facing the hill was a plain like a park, dotted with flat-topped mimosa and here and there the dark polish of machabell leaves. Beyond it all stretched the sea of bush, pathless and, for the moment, so still that you could hear the creak of leather and the little clicks as rifles knocked against belt-buckles.

As we stepped down into the river-bed we started a troop of giraffes. They went off in that strange sailing gallop of theirs, tails cocked, long legs moving with a clumsy grace, their hoofs rattling like castanets on hard ground. They were a good three hundred yards away—out of shot in any reasonable sense—but Good, who walked ahead with his express loaded with solid ball, could not bear to let so much meat and marrow float away untested.

He lifted the gun and fired at the last—a young cow. By some extraordinary chance, the ball struck full on the back of the neck and shattered the spine. That giraffe went over and over as neatly as a rabbit shot in a cabbage patch.

“Curse it!” cried Good—he had a habit of strong language when excitement warmed him, a habit bred, no doubt, on a man-of-war. “Curse it! I’ve killed him.”

“Ou, Bougwan,” said the Kafirs—“ou! ou!” They called him Bougwan, Glass Eye, because of his eye-glass.

“Oh, Bougwan!” Sir Henry and I echoed, and from that hour Good’s reputation as a marvellous shot stood firm among the boys. In truth he was not; but whenever he missed afterwards, we forgave him for the sake of that giraffe.

We set some of the bearers to cut off the best of the meat, and then we built a scherm near one of the pools, about a hundred yards to its right. A scherm is made by cutting thorn bushes and piling them in a circle like a hedge. Inside you smooth the ground and, if you can get it, spread dry tambouki grass for bedding. Then you light your fires. There is a domestic comfort in such work even in wild places: hands busy, knives scraping, grass shaken out, the first smoke rising and carrying away the day’s sweat smell. The bush, too, seems to draw closer, as though curious.

By the time the thorn circle was finished, the moon had peeped up, and our dinner was ready—giraffe steaks and roasted marrow-bones. To roast a marrow-bone properly you set it near enough to the coals that the fat within warms and loosens, but not so close that it scorches and turns bitter; then you crack it—no delicate business—and scoop out the marrow while it is still steaming and sweet, with that rich, clean taste that seems to carry sunlight and grass inside it. We ate by moonlight, and more than once stopped to praise Bougwan’s wonderful shot. I know no greater luxury than giraffe marrow—unless it be elephant’s heart, which we tasted on the morrow.

After supper we smoked and yarned. It was a curious picture: three white men squatting round firelight in the wilderness, while a few yards off the Kafirs sucked their intoxicating daccha from a pipe whose mouthpiece was made of eland horn. The night had its own orchestra—the low crackle of thorn, a night-jar’s call, the faint rustle of something small hunting among dead leaves. Such sounds soothe a man’s mind; they are honest, not the hammering, screaming clatter of civilised places that so often passes for life and leaves the head ringing with emptiness.

As for our looks: I, with my short grizzled hair sticking up, am thin and short and dark—nine stone and a half; Sir Henry is tall and broad and fair with yellow hair growing long—fifteen stone. But the oddest-looking, all things considered, was Captain John Good, R.N. He sat on a leather bag as though he had just stepped out from a comfortable day’s shooting at home—clean as a new plate, tidy as a ship’s cabin, dressed as if a tailor waited in the bush. He wore a brown tweed shooting suit with a hat to match and neat gaiters. He was beautifully shaved. His eye-glass and his false teeth appeared in perfect order. He even wore a collar—white gutta-percha, and he had a supply.

“They weigh so little,” he said to me innocently when I stared. “And I always like to turn out like a gentleman.”

Ah—if he could have foreseen the raiment the future had in store for him.

One by one the boys rolled themselves in blankets and slept by the fire, all except Umbopa, who sat a little apart, chin on hand, thinking as though his thoughts had a language of their own and preferred not to be translated. He never mixed much with the others.

Presently, from the bush behind us, came a loud “woof, woof!”

“That’s a lion,” I said, and we all started up to listen.

Hardly had we done so when, from the pool a hundred yards away, came the strident trumpeting of an elephant.

“Unkungunklovo! Indlovu!” whispered the Kafirs—“Elephant! Elephant!” A few minutes later we saw huge shadowy forms moving slowly from the water toward the bush, one after another, like black ships gliding out of moonlight.

Good was on his feet at once, burning for slaughter, perhaps thinking elephant were as easy as that giraffe. I caught his arm and pulled him down.

“It’s no good,” I whispered. “Let them go.”

“It seems we are in a paradise of game,” Sir Henry said presently. “I vote we stop here a day or two and have a go at them.”

I was surprised, for hitherto Sir Henry had pressed forward hard, especially since we learned at Inyati that an Englishman named Neville had sold his wagon there and gone on up-country two years before. But hunter blood will speak, and so will temptation. Good jumped at it; and to tell the truth, so did I. It goes against a man’s conscience to let such a herd pass without at least trying his luck.

“All right, my hearties,” said I. “We want a little recreation. Turn in now. We must be off by dawn, and perhaps we’ll catch them feeding before they move.”

Good stripped, shook out his clothes as though dust were a moral failing, put his eye-glass and false teeth into his trousers pocket, folded each article neatly, and laid them under a corner of his mackintosh to keep off the dew. Sir Henry and I made rougher arrangements. Soon we were rolled in blankets, and the bush’s steady breathing pulled us into that dreamless sleep that is the traveller’s true luxury.

Going, going, go—

What was that?

From the direction of the water came violent scuffling. Next instant the air split with a succession of roars so dreadful that the skin on a man’s neck tightens of itself. Only a lion can make such a noise, as though the world were being torn.

We jumped up and looked. Toward the pool we saw a confused mass, yellow and black, staggering and struggling our way. We seized rifles, slipped on veldtschoons—shoes of untanned hide—and ran out of the scherm. Before we reached it the mass fell and rolled over and over; then it lay still.

On the grass lay a sable antelope bull—the most beautiful of African antelopes—dead. Transfixed upon its great curved horns was a magnificent black-maned lion, also dead. The story read plain enough: the sable had come down to drink where the lion lay in wait. The lion sprang and was received upon those hooked spears, driven clean through. Unable to free himself, he had torn and bitten the bull’s back and neck; and the bull, maddened with pain, had rushed on till both dropped dead together. I had seen the like once before, and even then it seemed a savage sort of justice.

We called the Kafirs, and between us dragged both carcases up to the scherm. The smell of fresh blood followed us—hot, metallic, and heavy in the nostrils. Then we lay down again and slept till dawn.

With first light we were up and making ready for the fray. We took the three eight-bore rifles, plenty of ammunition, and our large water-bottles filled with weak cold tea, which I have always found the best drink to shoot on—cool, faintly bitter, steadying the stomach when heat would otherwise turn it. After swallowing a little breakfast—what there was—we started, Umbopa, Khiva, and Ventvögel with us. The other Kafirs we left to skin lion and sable and cut up the latter.

We found the elephant spoor easily enough: the broad trail ran through bush like a road, and Ventvögel, after peering and sniffing and setting his hand to droppings, pronounced it the work of twenty to thirty elephants, most of them full-grown bulls. They had moved on in the night, and it was nine o’clock—already fiercely hot—before we came upon the signs that told us we were close: broken trees, bruised leaves and bark, and droppings still smoking faintly as though the earth itself cooked in this sun.

Presently we saw them: the herd standing in a hollow, their morning meal finished, flapping their great ears. There were between twenty and thirty, as Ventvögel said. It was a splendid sight—vast bodies, wrinkled and grey, moving with a slow majesty, and now and again the pale curve of a tusk catching light.

I took a handful of dry grass and tossed it up to read the wind. If they winded us they would be off before a man could blink. The grass drifted from them toward us, or near enough. We crept forward, using what cover we could, until we were within forty yards.

Just in front, broadside on, stood three splendid bulls, one carrying enormous tusks. I whispered our plan: I would take the middle bull; Sir Henry the left; Good the big-tusked bull.

“Now,” I whispered.

Boom! boom! boom! The three heavy rifles spoke like cannon.

Sir Henry’s elephant dropped dead as a hammer—shot through the heart. Mine fell to its knees; for an instant I thought it done, but it heaved itself up and came tearing straight past me, so close I could smell the warm, dusty hide and the rank breath of crushed leaves. As it went I gave it the second barrel in the ribs. That brought it down in earnest. I jammed in two fresh cartridges, ran up close, and a ball through the brain ended its struggling.

Then I turned to see how Good had fared. I had heard the big bull scream with rage and pain.

I found the captain in a great state of excitement. His bull, hit, had turned and come straight for him; Good had only just leapt clear, and the beast went charging on blindly in the direction of our camp. The herd meanwhile crashed off in wild alarm the other way, smashing bush as though it were tambouki grass.

We debated whether to follow the wounded patriarch with the great tusks or to go after the herd. In the end we chose the herd and left the wounded bull, thinking we had seen the last of those tusks. I have often wished since that we had.

It was easy enough to follow the herd—their flight left a track like a carriage road. But to come up with them under that broiling sun was another matter. We struggled on for over two hours, sweat running into eyes, tongues thick in our mouths, the bush’s dry smell baking into us. At last we found them again.

All but one bull stood bunched together. Their uneasiness showed in their constant shifting and in the way they lifted trunks to test the air. Fifty yards in front of them stood a solitary bull, keeping sentry; he was about sixty yards from us. The ground here was more open; if we tried to creep closer he would surely see or scent us and start the herd.

We all aimed at the sentry, and at my whispered word we fired. The three shots took effect and he went down dead. Again the herd started, and their luck failed them: a hundred yards ahead lay a nullah—a dried-out water track—with steep banks, very like the place where the Prince Imperial was killed in Zululand. Into it the elephants plunged. When we reached the edge we saw them struggling in wild confusion to climb the far bank, filling the air with screams and trumpeting, pushing and shouldering one another aside in selfish panic—just like so many human beings.

Now was our chance. We fired as fast as we could load. Five elephants fell. And no doubt we should have killed the whole herd, had they not suddenly given up trying the bank and rushed headlong down the nullah away from us.

We were too tired to follow, and perhaps a little sick of slaughter. Eight elephants is a heavy bag for one day, and the smell of blood and burst stomach-sourness hangs round a man’s head until he begins to feel it in his dreams.

After we rested a while, the boys cut out the hearts of two of the dead elephants for supper. Elephant heart, when fresh, is like nothing else—dark, dense meat that must be cut with respect, sliced across the grain, and set over coals not too fierce. If you scorch it, it turns tough and bitter; if you let it cook slowly, the fat sweats out and the centre stays tender, with a taste at once iron-rich and sweet, as though the animal’s strength has become edible. We started homeward very well pleased with our day, and meant to send bearers next morning to chop away the tusks.

Shortly after we passed the spot where Good had wounded the great bull, we came across a herd of eland. We did not shoot—there was meat enough already, and our mouths were still full of elephant-heart richness and woodsmoke. The eland trotted past, then stopped behind a small patch of bush about a hundred yards away and wheeled to stare at us, mild-eyed and curious.

Good, never having seen an eland close, wanted a nearer view. He handed his rifle to Umbopa and, with Khiva following, strolled toward the patch of bush. Sir Henry and I sat down, glad of the excuse to rest, letting the evening cool gather round us. The sun was sinking in its reddest glory, and the whole world smelled of dust settling and crushed herb, and the bush’s sounds shifted to their dusk register—small birds calling, insects beginning their steady, living hiss.

Then, suddenly, we heard an elephant scream.

We looked up and saw its huge form rushing with uplifted trunk and tail, black against the great fiery globe of the sun. Next instant we saw Good and Khiva tearing back toward us with the wounded bull—yes, the patriarch with the great tusks—charging after them.

For a moment we did not dare fire; at that distance it would likely have been useless, and we feared to hit one of them. In the next breath a dreadful thing happened—Good fell, and it was his passion for civilised dress that tripped him. Had he hunted in a flannel shirt and veldtschoons like the rest of us, he might have kept his feet. But his trousers and gaiters hampered him. When he was about sixty yards from us, his polished boot—slick with dry grass—slipped, and he went down full on his face right in the elephant’s path.

We gasped, knowing what should follow, and ran toward him as hard as men can run with horror in their bellies.

In three seconds it was ended—but not as we thought.

Khiva saw his master fall. Brave lad, he turned and flung his assegai straight into the elephant’s face. It stuck in the trunk. With a scream of pain the brute seized the boy, hurled him down, set one vast foot upon his body, and with its trunk twined round him and tore him in two.

We rushed up mad with horror and fired again and again. At last the elephant fell, collapsing upon the fragments of the Zulu.

Good rose, wrung his hands, and stood over the man who had given his life to save him. Though I am an old hand, a lump grew in my throat, for there are deaths that do not grow easier with years, and the bush itself seemed to hold its breath, as if the evening’s quiet sounds had been cut away like language lost from a people.

Umbopa stood looking on at the huge dead elephant and at what was left of poor Khiva. His face was set, and when he spoke his words came slowly, in the plain, weighty manner of a man pronouncing a judgment that needs no ornament.

“Ah, well,” he said at last, “he is dead, but he died like a man!”
