To truly comprehend the architecture of the human heart, one must first look into the deepest currents of the Rhine or the thickest waters of the Thames, painting the silt beneath rather than skimming the surface. Lower a bucket into the well of self-deception, and what rises must be the immortal truth, must it not? Yet, no son of Adam ever reads his own heart directly; such insight is merely a reflection, a sagacity borrowed from years of perusing the lives of others. Even armed with this reflected light, we can only decipher our own souls, never read them fluently. Thus, halfway to Sevenbergen, Gerard paused to interrogate his own spirit regarding his journey. His heart replied without hesitation: *"We go out of curiosity—to know why she spurned us, to prove our hearts remain unbroken, and to demonstrate our contentment with our honors and future benefice, needing neither her nor any ally of her fickle sex."*

Soon, Gerard located Peter Brandt's cottage. In the doorway sat a girl stitching with a needle, while a stalwart figure leaned upon a longbow, conversing with her. A sharp, unaccountable pang pierced Gerard at the sight of the man. He was past fifty, an old soldier whose reputation for shooting at the butts with admirable force and skill lingered in Gerard's memory. Within a heartbeat, the youth stood before them. Margaret looked up, dropping her work with a faint cry, her complexion shifting rapidly between pale and flushed. Yet, these fleeting signs of emotion were swiftly suppressed; she turned far colder and more indifferent than she would have if she had not already betrayed such agitation.

"What! Is it you, Master Gerard?" she asked, her tone laced with feigned surprise. "What on earth brings you here, I wonder?"

"I was passing by and saw you," Gerard replied, masking his turmoil. "So I thought I would give you good day and inquire after your father."

"My father is well. He will be here anon."

"Then I may as well stay till he comes."

"As you will. Good Martin, step into the village and tell my father here is a friend of his."

"And not of yours?"

"My father's friends are mine."

"That is doubtful," Margaret retorted, her eyes flashing. "It was not like a friend to promise to wait for me, and then make off the moment my back was turned. Cruel Margaret—you little know how I searched the town for you; how, for want of you, nothing was pleasant to me."

"These are idle words," she countered sharply. "If you had truly desired my father's company, or mine, you would have returned. There, I had a bed laid for you at my cousin's, and he would have made much of you. Who knows, I might have made much of you too. I was in the humour that day. You will not catch me in the same mind again, neither you nor any young man, I warrant me."

"Margaret, I came back the moment the Countess let me go; but you were not there."

"Nay, you did not," she insisted, "or you would have seen Hans Cloterman at our table; we left him specifically to bring you on."

"I saw no one there, only a drunken man who had just tumbled down."

"At our table? How was he clad?"

"Nay, I took little heed: in sad-coloured garb."

At this, Margaret's face gradually warmed, but she quickly assumed an air of incredulous severity, peppering Gerard with shrewd questions. He answered them all most loyally. Finally, the clouds parted, and they deduced the source of the misunderstanding. Then came a revulsion of tenderness, all the more potent because they had wronged one another; and following that, more dangerous still, came mutual confessions. Neither had been happy since; neither ever would have been happy but for this fortunate meeting.

Gerard spotted a manuscript of the Vulgate lying open on the table and pounced upon it like a hawk. Manuscripts were his delight, but before he could reach it, two white hands slammed flat upon the page, covering it, while a red face pressed over them.

"Nay, take away your hands, Margaret, that I may see where you are reading," Gerard pleaded softly. "I will read there too at home; so shall my soul meet yours in the sacred page. You will not? Nay, then I must kiss them away."

He kissed them so often that, overcome by sheer shame, they were forced to withdraw. Lo! the sacred book lay open to, *"An apple of gold in a setting of silver."*

"There, now," she exclaimed, "I had been hunting for it ever so long, and found it but even now—and to be caught!" With a touch of inconsistency, she pointed it out to Gerard with her white finger.

"Ay," he said, "but today it is all hidden in that great cap."

"It is a comely cap, I'm told by some."

"Maybe; but what it hides is beautiful."

"It is not: it is hideous."

"Well, it was beautiful at Rotterdam."

"Ay, everything was beautiful that day," she murmured, letting out a little sigh.

Now Peter entered, welcoming Gerard cordially and inviting him to stay for supper. Margaret disappeared, only to reappear moments later with her hair arranged in her silver net. She shot a glance that was half-arch, half-coy, gliding about them as she spread the supper, beaming with gaiety and happiness. In the cool evening, Gerard coaxed her outside; she objected, yet came. He urged her further along the road to Tergou; she declined, yet came. They strolled up and down, hand in hand. When he eventually had to depart, they pledged each other never to quarrel or misunderstand one another again, sealing the promise with a long, loving kiss. Gerard went home on wings.

From that day forward, Gerard spent most of his evenings with Margaret. Their attachment deepened on both sides until the hours they shared were the hours they truly lived; the rest of life they merely endured. At the outset of this profound bond, all went smoothly. Obstacles existed, but they seemed distant and trivial to the eyes of hope, youth, and love. The feelings and passions of so many others that this attachment threatened to thwart gave no warning smoke to reveal their volcanic nature and power. The course of true love ran smoothly, placidly, until it had drawn these two young hearts into its current forever. And then...
