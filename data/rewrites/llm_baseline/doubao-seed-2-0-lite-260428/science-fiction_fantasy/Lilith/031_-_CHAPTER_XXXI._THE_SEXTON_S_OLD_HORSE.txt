I stood rooted, watching the last pale glimmer of the white leopardess’s pelt melt into the haze of the far heath, then turned to follow my guide—my steps heavy with sharp reluctance. What place had sleep for me now? Surely reason wove its same unbroken thread through every world that ever was, and what logic could there be in curling up to slumber beside the dead, when the hour was calling every living soul to rise? Worse, no one would wake me. How could I be sure I would rouse early? That I would rouse at all? The sleepers in that strange house let dawn bleed into noon, noon bleed to dusk, never once stirring in their shadowed beds. I muttered half-strangled protests under my breath, but followed all the same, for I knew no other path to take.

The librarian walked on in utter silence, and I matched his quiet stride. Time and unmarked space slipped past us uncounted. The sun sank below the ragged line of the horizon, dusk seeped over the moor, and I tasted on the rising wind the spreading, bone-deep cold of the death-chamber. My heart sank lower with every step. Soon I lost sight of his lean, long-coated frame, and at last could no longer hear the soft swish of his passage through the heather stems. But in their place came the slow, heavy flap of raven wings, and at intervals, a firefly, or a glowing ghost-butterfly, would rise and drift through the rayless dark. By and by the moon lifted, slow and heavy, crawling across the far rim of the sky.

“You are tired, are you not, Mr. Vane?” the raven said, alighting on a lichen-crusted gravestone. “You must make the acquaintance of the horse that will carry you where you need to go at dawn.” He let out a strange, piercing whistle through his long black beak. A dark, moving spot bloomed on the face of the half-risen moon. The drumming of swift, soft hoofbeats rolled toward me, and in the space of a few breaths, the terrible steed thundered straight out of the moon’s very disc. His mane streamed behind him like the crest of a wave fighting a gale, torn loose in hoary sprays of frosty white, and the flick of his tail kept blinding the moon’s silver eye every time it lashed out. He stood near nineteen hands, huge of bone, skin stretched taut as parchment over hard, corded muscle—a steed even holy Death himself might choose to mount when he rode abroad to reap. The moon seemed to gaze at him in awe; in her eerie, wavering light he looked a living skeleton, its great joints loosely strung together. For all his terrific size, he moved with the weightless lightness of a winged moth. As he drew near, his speed slackened, mane and tail drifting softly around his frame as they settled.

I had always loved horses, loved every one I ever laid eyes on. I had never spent a penny save on them, never sold a horse that had once been mine. The sight of this mighty, terrible beast woke in me a sharp, hungry longing to possess him: pure greed, rank covetousness, an evil thing no matter what world you walked. I do not mean I would have stolen him, but that, blind to whatever purpose he was made for, I would have paid any price to call him mine. I laid my hands on him, stroking the prominent bones that humped under a hide so thin, smooth, and shiny as satin—so shiny the whole round shape of the moon was reflected clear on its surface. I fondled his sharp, pointed ears, whispered soft words into them, and breathed the warm breath of a living man straight into his flaring red nostrils. He breathed back into mine the hot, wild breath of a horse’s life, and in that instant, we loved one another. What eyes he had! Filmy pale blue like the eyes of the newly dead, with a smoldering coal of fire glowing deep behind each pupil. The raven, wings half unfurled, looked on, pleased at the way I fussed over his magnificent mount.

“That is well, be friends with him,” he croaked. “He will carry you all the swifter tomorrow! Now we must hurry home.”

My longing to ride him swelled to a fever pitch. “May I not mount him at once, Mr. Raven?” I cried.

“By all means!” he answered. “Mount, and ride him home.”

The horse bent his great head over my shoulder, nuzzling me with soft affection. I twisted my hands in his streaming mane and scrambled up onto his back, hauling myself up with the aid of the jutting bones of his shoulders. “He could outrun any leopard that ever walked the earth!” I exclaimed.

“Not at night, not by this road,” the raven corrected me. “The path is full of pitfalls. But come: what looks like loss now will be gain later. To wait is harder than to run, and its reward is far fuller. Go on, my son, straight to the cottage. I shall be there as soon as you. It will make my wife’s heart glad to see her son riding that horse.”

I sat silent, the horse stock still under me like a block of polished marble. “Why do you linger?” the raven asked.

“I long so much to ride after the leopardess,” I answered, “that I can barely hold myself back.”

“You have given your word.”

“My debt to the Little Ones, I confess, feels far heavier than my bond to you.”

“Yield to that temptation, and you will bring ruin down on them—and on yourself as well.”

“What do I care for my own fate? I love them, and love does no harm. I am going.”

The truth was, I had forgotten the children entirely, infatuated as I was with the strength of the great beast beneath me. Eyes glowed through the dark, and I knew Adam stood beside me in his true, human shape. I could hear the suppressed indignation in his voice, barely held back from bursting out. “Mr. Vane,” he said, “do you never wonder why you have not yet done a single thing worth doing?”

“Because I have been a fool,” I answered.

“Foolish in what way?”

“Everything.”

“Which of your acts do you count the most unwise?”

“Bringing the princess back to life. I should have left her to her just fate.”

“Nay, now you speak foolishly. You could not have done otherwise, not knowing she was evil! But you never brought anyone back to life. How could you, when you are dead yourself?”

“Dead?” I cried, stunned.

“Yes,” he said. “And you will remain dead, so long as you refuse to die.”

“More of your old riddles,” I scoffed.

“Be persuaded, come home with me,” he went on, his tone softening. “The most—nearly the only foolish thing you ever did, was to run from our dead.”

I pressed my heels to the horse’s lean ribs, and he shot forward like a sudden gust of wind. I patted his neck, and he wheeled sharp around, low to the ground like a cat chasing a scurrying mouse, his mane sweeping the tops of the heather as he leaned into the turn. I heard the raven’s wings beat behind me, five quick flaps, and he perched right on the horse’s forehead. The great beast checked instantly, ploughing up clods of turf with his hooves.

“Mr. Vane,” the raven croaked, “think what you are doing! Twice before misfortune has befallen you—once from fear, once from heedlessness. To break your word is far worse: it is a sin.”

“The Little Ones are in terrible danger, and I am the one who brought that danger down on them!” I cried. “But I swear I will not break my word to you. I will come back, and spend as many nights, days, years in your house as you wish.”

“I tell you once more, you will do them far more harm than good if you ride out tonight,” he insisted. But that false, rootless sense of power, the strength I had siphoned off from the great horse under me, had turned me stupid, deaf to all warning.

“Would you steal my last chance to make amends?” I shouted. “This time I will not shirk my duty! I am going, if I perish for it!”

“Go then, foolish boy!” he spat, anger sharp in his croak. “Take the horse, ride to failure. May it at last teach you humility!” He spread his wings and vanished into the dark.

Again I pressed my heels to the horse’s ribs. “After the spotted leopardess,” I breathed in his ear. He turned his great head back and forth, snuffling the air, then started off at a slow, uncertain walk. All at once he quickened his pace, broke into a trot, then a gallop, and in moments his speed was shattering. He seemed to see perfectly in the dark; he never stumbled, never faltered, never hesitated. I sat on his back as if perched on the ridge of a racing wave. I could feel every individual muscle shift and flex under me, his joints so elastic every movement melted seamless into the next, not a single jolt to unseat me. His speed grew until he flew rather than ran, the wind tearing past us like a tornado. We shot across the evil hollow like a bolt fired from a crossbow. No monster lifted its neck from the mud below; every creature there knew the thunder of those hooves passing overhead. We rushed up steep hills, shot down their far slopes, never swerving even when we charged across the jagged chasms of the riverbed, holding that same terrible, fierce gallop. The moon, halfway up the sky, gazed down with solemn, troubled face. Drunk on the power of my steed and the rush of my own life, I sat like a crowned king as I rode.

We were nearly at the center of the river’s tangled many channels, the horse clearing one, sometimes two gaps with every stride, gathering himself now for a great bounding leap, when the moon reached the highest point of her arch. Then came a wonder, and a terror: she began to descend, rolling like the nave of Fortune’s wheel bowled by the hands of the gods, faster and faster. Just like the moon I had known in my old world, this one bore a human face, and now the broad forehead, now the sharp chin turned to the sky as she rolled over and over. I stared aghast. The howling of wolves rose from the ravines far below. An ugly, cold fear began to creep into the hollows of my heart, my confidence draining away. The horse held his breakneck speed, ears pricked forward, flaring nostrils drinking deep of the wind his own passage had stirred up. But the moon jolted down the slope of the sky like an old, cracked chariot wheel, heavy with awful omen. At last she rolled over the edge of the horizon and vanished, taking every scrap of her light with her.

The mighty steed was in the middle of clearing a wide, shallow river channel when we were caught in the total net of that unbroken darkness. His head dropped. The momentum of his leap carried his bulk across to the far bank, but he crumpled into a heap the second he landed, and lay still where he fell. I clambered off, knelt beside him, ran my hands over every inch of his great frame. Not a single bone was broken—but he was a horse no more. I sat down on his motionless body, buried my face in my hands, and the unforgiving dark closed all around me.
