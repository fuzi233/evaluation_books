The cry rose high, the horses neighed, and the beacons flared against the night sky, a desperate signal to save the treasure they had so narrowly secured. Yet, Amyas Preston, having "fleshed his dogs" in the recent skirmish, refused to linger on the islands of Margarita and Cubagua. With the alarm raised, he steered his ship southwest, cutting across the great bay that stretched from the Peninsula of Paria to Cape Codera. To their right lay the jagged silhouette of Tortuga; to their left, the Piritoos islands rose like twin green ridges barely kissing the tideless sea.

Yeo and Drew, veterans of this coast who had once traded with Hawkins, navigated the waters with practiced ease. Before them, the majestic range of the Caracas mountains pierced the heavens, towering so high that the hills familiar to most of the crew seemed mere mounds in comparison. Even Frank, who knew the Alps, and Amyas, who had traversed the Andes, found themselves dwarfed by this colossal barrier. Cary, whose horizon was limited to the rugged peaks of M'Gillicuddy's Reeks, spent a day gripping the rigging, his chin raised unnaturally high, staring upward until his neck stiffened, all to the amusement of the others.

As dawn approached, the sea grew choppy despite a gentle breeze. The massive wall of Cape Codera loomed a thousand feet to their left, beyond which lay endless banks of mountains bathed in the pale yellow moonlight. By morning, a large ship appeared on the horizon, having passed them during the night, now ten miles to the eastward. Yeo immediately proposed turning back to intercept her, arguing that it was the Lord's work to be thorough. But Amyas and Frank hesitated.

"Why, Yeo," Frank asked, "you said one more day would bring us to La Guayra."

"All the more reason," Yeo replied solemnly, "to do the Lord's work thoroughly when He has brought us safely this far."

"She can pass well enough, and no loss," Amyas countered.

"Ah, sirs," Yeo insisted, "she is delivered into your hands, and you must give an account of her."

"My good Yeo," Frank interjected, "I trust we shall give good account of many a tall Spaniard before we return. But you know La Guayra, and the salvation of the lady we seek, was our first object."

Yeo shook his head sadly. "A lady brought Captain Oxenham to ruin."

"You dare not compare her with this one?" Frank and Cary exclaimed in unison.

"God forbid," Yeo said. "But no adventure prospers without a single eye to the Lord's work: to cripple the Spaniard and exalt Her Majesty. I believed that was dearer to Captain Leigh's heart than anything."

Amyas stood torn. His duty to the Queen demanded he pursue the Spanish vessel; his vow to the lady demanded he press on to La Guayra. It was a practical dilemma, not merely theoretical. Ultimately, Frank's counsel prevailed, and they sailed eastward. Had they not, the story would have ended differently.

Around midday, a canoe staggered toward them under a triangular sail, carrying two Indians.

"Metal floats in these seas," Cary remarked. "A fresh marvel for you, Frank."

"Expound," Frank said, ready to swallow any wonder.

"How else would those bronze statues dare to sea in such a cockleshell?" Cary asked. "Have I given you the *dor* now, master courtier?"

"I am past *dors*, Will. But what noble creatures! How fearlessly they approach! Can they know we are English, the avengers of their kind?"

"They likely take us for Spaniards wishing to buy coconuts. See, the canoe is laden with vegetables."

"Hail them, Yeo!" Amyas commanded. "You speak the best Spanish; I need to speak with one of them."

Yeo hailed them. The canoe pulled alongside, lowering its sail as a splendid Indian scrambled aboard with the agility of a cat. He stood six feet tall, bold and graceful, mirroring the bearing of Frank or Amyas himself. He smiled broadly, revealing white teeth, but his expression shifted instantly. Springing to the side, he shouted to his companion in Spanish, "Treachery! No Spaniard!" and attempted to leap overboard. A dozen strong men caught him before he could escape.

It took effort to subdue him; his naked limbs were slippery and his strength considerable. Amyas alternately pleaded with the men not to harm the Indian and urged the stranger to be quiet, promising no injury. After five minutes of confusion, the Indian surrendered sulkily.

"Don't bind him," Amyas ordered. "Let him loose, form a ring around him. Now, my man, here is a dollar."

The Indian's eyes gleamed as he took the coin.

"All I ask," Amyas said, "is to tell me what ships are in La Guayra, and then to guide me to the governor's house and the customs house."

The Indian placed the coin on the deck, crossed himself, and stared Amyas in the face.

"No, senor! I am a freeman, a cavalier, a Christian Guayqueria. My forefathers swore fealty to the King of Spain, and he calls them his most loyal subjects to this day. God forbid I should aid his enemies, who are my enemies too."

A growl rose from the men who understood him, with several hinting that a cord around the head or a match between the fingers would yield the truth quickly.

"God forbid!" Amyas declared. "He is a brave and loyal man, and as such I will treat him. Tell me, my brave fellow, how do you know we are His Catholic Majesty's enemies?"

With a shrewd smile, the Indian pointed to various objects on the ship, declaring each "Not Spanish."

"Well, and what of that?" Amyas asked.

"None but Spaniards and free Guayquerias have the right to sail these seas."

Amyas laughed. "Thou art a valiant piece of copper. Pick up thy dollar and go in peace. Make room for him, men. We can learn what we want without his help."

The Indian paused, astonished. "Overboard with you!" Amyas commanded. "Don't you know when you are well off?"

"Most illustrious senor," the Indian began in a drawling, sententious tone, "I have been deceived. I heard you heretics roasted and ate all true Catholics, and that your priests had tails."

"Plague on you, sirrah!" squeaked Jack Brimblecombe. "Have I a tail? Look here!"

"Quien sabe? Who knows?" the Indian retorted through his nose.

"How do you know we are heretics?" Amyas asked.

"Humph! But in repayment for your kindness, I warn you, illustrious senor, not to go to La Guayra. Ships of war await you there. Moreover, Governor Don Guzman sailed eastward yesterday to hunt for you. I wonder you did not meet him."

"To hunt for us! On the watch for us!" Cary cried. "Impossible; lies! Amyas, this is some trick to frighten us away."

"Don Guzman came out but yesterday?" Amyas pressed. "Are you sure?"

"As I live, senor, he and another ship, for which I took yours."

Amyas stamped on the deck. That was the ship they had passed!

"Fool that I was to stay close to my enemy and let my opportunity slip!" he lamented. "If I had done my duty, all would have gone right!"

But it was too late to regret. The Indian's story was likely false.

"Off with you!" Amyas commanded. The Indian bounded over the side into his canoe, leaving the crew marveling at the stateliness of this bold sea-cavalier.

Thus, Westward-ho they ran beneath the mighty northern wall, the highest cliff on earth—seven thousand feet of rock separated from the sea by a narrow strip of bright green lowland. Patches of sugar-cane and coconut trees near the water reminded them of the tropics, while above, the landscape was savage, rough, and bare as an Alpine precipice. Deep clefts allowed the southern sun to blaze down upon the sea, revealing strange, stately trees lining the glens and a veil of perpetual mist shrouding the inner summits. White, fleecy clouds hung motionless in the burning air, enhancing the sense of vastness and solemn rest.

"Within those mountains, three thousand feet above our heads," said Drew, the master, "lies Saint Yago de Leon, the great city founded by the Spaniards fifteen years ago."

"Is it a rich place?" Cary asked.

"Very, they say."

"Is it a strong place?" Amyas asked.

"No forts at all, they say. The Spaniards boast that Heaven has made such good walls that man need make none."

"I don't know," Amyas said. "Lads, could you climb those hills?"

"Higher than Harty Point, sir: but it depends on what lies behind them."

Now the last point was rounded, and La Guayra lay in full view, the object of their four-thousand-mile voyage. A low black cliff crowned by a wall, batteries at either end. Within, narrow streets of white houses ran parallel to the sea along a strip of flat land no wider than two hundred yards, backed by the mountain wall in deepest shade. How one ascended that wall remained a puzzle, but Drew pointed out a narrow path winding upward through a glen, seemingly sheer. That was the road to the capital, if any dared try it. Despite the mountain's shadow, the place wore a dusty, glaring look. The air from the land was stifling; La Guayra, heated by the vast fire-brick of rock, was one of the hottest spots on earth.

Where was the harbor? There was none, only an open roadstead where five vessels tossed at anchor. The two outer ones were small merchant caravels. Behind them lay two long, low, ugly-looking craft, at the sight of which Yeo let out a long whistle.

"Galleys, as I'm a sinful saint!" Yeo exclaimed. "And what's that big one inside? She has more than hawseholes in her idolatrous black sides."

"We shall open her astern of the galleys," Amyas said. "Look out, Cary, your eyes are better than mine."

"Six round portholes on the main deck," Will noted.

"And I see the brass *patararoes* glittering on her poop," Amyas added. "Will, we're in for it."

"In for it we are, captain," Yeo replied.

"Farewell, farewell, my parents dear. I never shall see you more, I fear."

"Let's go in, nevertheless, and pound the Don's ribs, my old lad of Smerwick. Eh? Three to one is fair odds."

"Not underneath those fort guns, I beg leave to say," Yeo warned. "If the Philistines come out, we will make them like unto Zeba and Zalmunna."

"Quite true," said Amyas. "Game cocks are game cocks, but reason is reason."

"If the Philistines do not come out, they will send a messenger," Cary predicted. "Look out, all thin skulls!"

As he spoke, a puff of white smoke rolled from the eastern fort, and a heavy ball plunged into the water between the ship and the shore.

"I don't altogether like this," Amyas muttered. "What do they mean by firing without warning? And what are these ships of war doing here? Drew, you told me the armadas never lay here."

"No more, I believe, they do, sir, because the anchorage is so bad," Drew replied. "I'm mortal afeared that rascal's story was true, and the Dons have got wind of our coming."

"Run up a white flag, at all events. If they expect us, they must have known some time since."

"True, sir. They must have come from Santa Marta or Cartagena. That would take a month."

Amyas suddenly recalled Eustace's threat at the wayside inn. Could he have betrayed their purpose? Impossible!

"Let us hold a council of war, Frank."

Frank was, however, absorbed in a different matter. Half a mile eastward, two hundred feet up the steep mountain side, stood a large, low white house embosomed in trees and gardens. No other house of similar size existed nearby. Was not that the royal flag of Spain flaunting before it? That must be the governor's house; that must be the abode of the Rose of Torridge! Frank stood devouring it with wild eyes, until he convinced himself he saw a woman's figure walking on the terrace, none other than the one he sought. Amyas could hardly tear him away to the council of war, a sad and peevish affair.

The three adventurers, with Brimblecombe, Yeo, and Drew, retired to the poop. Each looked at the others. What was to be done? Months of plans and hopes were brought to naught in an hour.

"It is impossible," Amyas said finally, "to surprise the town by land while these ships are here; for if we land our men, we leave our ship undefended."

"As impossible as to challenge Don Guzman while he is not here," Cary added.

"I wonder why the ships have not opened fire already," Drew observed.

"Perhaps they respect our flag of truce," Cary suggested. "Why not send a boat to treat?"

"For her?" Frank interrupted. "If we show we know of her existence, her name is blasted in the eyes of those jealous Spaniards."
