The little band was no army, no troop of men sworn to strike, and yet their instruments persisted in rousing tunes as if the very hedgerows were a battlement and every green lane a parade-ground. The brass flung out its bright defiance; the drum beat with a hard, heart-steadying certainty; and even the children’s thin piping, caught up on the air, seemed to borrow courage from the rhythm.

Shirley Keeldar walked as if she heard more than music in it. It touched something old and impatient in her—some stored-up appetite for hazard, as if peace were a diet too pale to nourish her. Dr. Helstone, turning by chance, met the look in her face, and laughed outright; she answered him with a laugh as keen.

“There is no battle toward,” he said. “No tyrant at our throat, no invader on our shore. Nothing to do—only a walk. Keep your hand steady on the reins, captain, and spare your fire; it is not wanted.”

“Take your own counsel, doctor,” Shirley returned. Then, lowering her voice for Caroline alone, she added with that sudden, half-sweet intensity she could slip into as into a softer garment: “If the world will not give me an occasion, I must borrow one. We are not soldiers—no, I do not thirst for blood—but we might be soldiers of the Cross: time rolled back, we pilgrims with staves, bound for Palestine. No—too airy. I want something rougher. Let us be Scots Lowlanders, stealing up the moors behind a Covenanting captain to pray beyond the reach of troopers. Prayer, and then battle—heaven sure even if the peat is reddened. That music—do you feel it? It shakes one awake. It makes my heart beat not as it does on common days, but as if it had been starved and suddenly fed. I almost long for danger—for a faith, a land, or—at least—a lover to defend.”

Caroline, to break the spell she feared and yet half envied, pointed quickly. “Look, Shirley! What is that red speck above Stilbro’ Brow? Your eyes are better than mine.”

Shirley narrowed her gaze. “I see it. A line of red… soldiers—cavalry.” Her voice sharpened. “Six. They ride fast. They will pass us—no, they turn off. They saw our procession and go round. Where are they bound?”

“Exercising their horses, perhaps.”

“Perhaps.” Shirley watched until the red had melted into distance. “They are gone.”

Mr. Helstone, with the brisk authority that made children step in and curates fall silent, announced their short cut. “We shall go through Royd Lane, and reach Nunnely Common by it.”

Into Royd Lane they streamed—narrow, dug in between ditches, hedges leaning close so that two abreast was almost a presumption. The soundscape changed at once: the open field’s wide breathing was traded for cramped echoes. Every footfall and whispered remark struck back from thorn and bank; the brass grew harsher, the drum more emphatic, as if the lane itself forced aggression out of any tune.

They had gained the middle when the leaders began to twitch with a kind of anticipatory excitement. Boultby’s spectacles trembled; Helstone’s old, leonine brows lifted; curates nudged and craned. Mr. Hall, gentlest of commanders, turned with a smile that tried to be light.

“What is it?” the ladies demanded.

He pointed. At the far end of the lane, another procession entered—men in black at its head, music behind, and on the air a different sort of sound: not brass and drum, but the drawn-out, plaintive rise and fall of a hymn.

Shirley’s eyes flashed. “Our double? Our wraith turned up in broad day.”

“If you wanted a battle,” Caroline whispered, laughing, “you will have one—of looks, at least.”

“They shall not pass us!” cried the curates together, as if the narrow lane were a doctrine to be defended. “We’ll not give way!”

“Give way?” Mr. Helstone’s rebuke cracked like a cane. He wheeled on them. “Who talks of giving way? Mind yourselves. The ladies, I can trust; there is no churchwoman here who will be shoved aside by these folk, not when the honour of the Establishment is concerned. What says Miss Keeldar?”

Shirley lifted her chin. “I ask only—what are they?”

“The Dissenting schools,” said Helstone, grimly pleased to name an enemy—“Methodists and the rest, joined in an unholy alliance. They have turned into this lane on purpose—to stop us, to drive us back.”

“Bad manners,” said Shirley with sudden coldness. “And I detest bad manners. They must be taught.”

“A lesson in politeness,” urged Mr. Hall, always reaching for a gentler utensil than a weapon. “Not an example of rudeness.”

But Helstone was already striding forward, his step measured and inexorable, as if the packed earth under his shoes were a pulpit and he meant to preach with his body. He neared the opposing leaders. Their chief—large, oily-faced, black hair plastered flat—called a halt. The Dissenter procession stopped as one creature stops. Out came the hymn-book; out came a verse, and then a tune, and the lane filled with the most dolorous chant, heavy as wet wool.

Helstone turned his head and signed. The brass clashed, bright and brazen; the drums answered; and on his order the band struck up “Rule, Britannia!” The children, coached into boldness by the sheer noise of it, sang with a spirit that would have startled them in their own kitchens. The two sound-worlds collided—psalm against patriotic roar—till the hymn was battered down, broken, swallowed. If victory could be measured in decibels, the Church had conquered.

“Now, follow me!” Helstone called. “Not at a run. At a firm, smart pace. Be steady—every child and woman. Keep together. Hold one another’s skirts if you must.”

And forward they went, not with the foolish heat of a charge, but with a cool, compact thrust—solid as a cartwheel. The curates, wedged between Mr. Helstone in front and Miss Keeldar behind, dared no flourish; Helstone’s cane and Shirley’s parasol were ready law. The Dissenters, astonished at first, then alarmed, gave ground. The lane’s tight walls threw every sound back on itself—panting, brass, protest, the scrape of shoes—and then, at last, the opposing procession broke, turning aside and yielding the outlet.

Boultby suffered most; for a moment his breath failed him like an ill-stoked fire, and he would have gone down. But Helstone and Malone held him up—one on each side—forcing him through whole in limb, though the lane had wrung him like a soaked cloth.

The greasy Dissenter who had started the hymn ended sitting in the ditch, winded and humbled. He was a spirit-merchant by trade, a leader among the Nonconformists, and it was said afterwards—half in malice, half in wonder—that he drank more water that afternoon than he had swallowed in twelve months past. Mr. Hall, gentle even in triumph, had kept Caroline safe; and Caroline, with her quiet courage, had watched him too, steady as a hand on a cup that threatens to spill.

When they were clear of the lane, Helstone and Shirley shook hands with real relish—warriors after a skirmish, agreeing wordlessly that the manoeuvre had been neat. The curates began to crow; Helstone shut the stable door on their exuberance at once, telling them they had never sense to know what to say, and had best hold their tongues; it had not been their business to manage, and should not become their business to boast.

By half-past three the procession turned back; by four they were again at the school. The close-shorn fields had been set as if for a rustic rite: long benches in lines; huge baskets swaddled in white cloths; great tin vessels breathing steam, their mouths lifting the scent of strong tea into the air. Before any hand reached for food, Mr. Hall pronounced a brief grace, and the children sang it—young voices untrained and yet strangely moving in the open, as if the sky itself were a chapel roof.

Then came the feast: currant buns—big, dark-studded, their sweetness warm and sticky; tea poured hot and well-sugared, not miserly but generous, a brew that carried comfort on its tannined breath. There was a rule—unspoken like the best domestic laws—that no child should be stinted. Each was given twice what small stomach and small patience could consume, so there would be a reserve to carry home: a bun wrapped like treasure for an old grandmother; a crust and a pocket of sweetness for a sick brother. Meanwhile buns and beer went round among musicians and singers; and afterwards, when benches were removed, the players were turned loose into laughter with official permission.

A bell called the teachers, patrons, and patronesses into the schoolroom. It had been dressed for the occasion in a homely splendour: white walls brightened with evergreens and flowers; silver teapots catching the light; porcelain gleaming; trays laid out; and everywhere movement—women pressed into service as waiters, neighbours’ servants and clerks’ wives, eager to look their smartest. Their dresses rustled; their shoes clicked; their voices ran in a cheerful current, not loud, but lively. Above it all the canary birds trilled in their high cages—sharp, insistent music, like little coins dropped on metal, a reminder that even gaiety has its shrillness when shut indoors.

Caroline, as the rector’s niece, took her place at one of the first tables. Mrs. Boultby and Margaret Hall presided at the others. Equality was not served here with the tea: the élite had their benches, and the rest their own places, as in most parishes and most hearts. Miss Helstone removed bonnet and scarf, oppressed by the heat. Her curls fell on her neck like a veil; her muslin dress was modest as a novice’s, needing no shawl to complete its chastity.

The room filled. Mr. Hall took his station beside Caroline. As she set cups straight and counted spoons with quick fingers, she murmured to him about Royd Lane—trying to make light of it, because she saw the gravity in his face. Shirley sat near—oddly still, not laughing, not scattering sparks, but watching the door with a vigilance that felt like hunger disguised. More than once she spread her satin skirt over too much bench, or laid her gloves and embroidered handkerchief upon the space beside her, as if claiming it by occupation.

Caroline noticed at last. “Whom do you expect, Shirley?”

Shirley leaned so close her whisper was warm at Caroline’s ear, and softened by some secret tenderness. “Mr. Moore. I saw him last night. I made him promise to come with his sister—and to sit at our table. He will not fail me; but he will come too late and be separated. Here—people are arriving; every place will be taken. It is provoking.”

Indeed, the magistrate, Mr. Wynne, entered with his family in full ceremony—wife, son, and two daughters, as if they were an institution rather than persons. Their place was, of course, at the first table; and they were led there, filling what remained.

To Shirley’s discomfort, Sam Wynne—her particular aversion, made worse because he had the dull presumption to aim at her hand—planted himself precisely in the seat she had reserved, settling heavily on her gown, her gloves, her handkerchief, and all her patience. Rumour had even carried to her ears the old gentleman’s public jest that Fieldhead and the De Walden estate were delightfully contagious—his own mistaken word repeating itself through the neighbourhood like a bad tune no one can stop humming.

Caroline’s ears still rang with that whisper—“I expect Mr. Moore”—when the organ pealed over the hum. Dr. Boultby, Mr. Helstone, and Mr. Hall rose; all rose; grace was sung, music lifting and holding the room for a minute as if it could cleanse the air of its heat and small rivalries. Then tea began in earnest.

Caroline was kept busy, pouring and serving, until the last cup was filled. Only then did she glance about with restless eagerness. Some ladies and gentlemen still stood, unseated. She recognized, among them, Miss Mann—her solitary spinster friend—drawn from her drearness by weather or persuasion. Miss Mann looked worn with standing; a lady in a yellow bonnet brought her a chair.

Caroline knew that satin-yellow bonnet, knew the black hair and the kind face that could yet look opinionated and froward; knew the black silk gown and the gray linen shawl. Hortense Moore. The recognition struck her like a sudden sweetness: she felt an impulse to spring up, run to her, kiss her—once for Hortense herself, twice for her brother’s sake.

She half rose with a smothered exclamation. But a hand, light and decisive, pressed her back into her seat; and a voice behind her whispered, “Wait till after tea, Lina, and then I’ll bring her to you.”

When she looked up—when she dared—Robert Moore stood close behind, smiling at her eagerness. He looked better than she had ever seen him: his face clearer, his air steadier. To her partial eyes he was so handsome it hurt, as if the brightness of him were too keen for the day’s ordinary light, and branded itself on her memory as if lightning had done the engraving.

He moved on to speak to Shirley. Shirley, annoyed by Sam Wynne’s attentions, by his weight on her belongings, and by Moore’s late arrival, was in no mood to be gracious. She shrugged and spoke bitterly of his “insupportable tardiness.” Moore neither excused himself nor snapped back; he stood quietly, waiting, as if giving her time to come back to herself. In a few minutes she did. She offered him her hand. He took it with a smile that corrected without scolding, and thanked without servility; the faint shake of his head said one thing, and the gentlest pressure of his fingers another.

“You may sit where you can now, Mr. Moore,” Shirley said, smiling as if nothing sharp had passed. “There is not an inch of room for you here. I see plenty at Mrs. Boultby’s table—between Miss Armitage and Miss Birtwhistle. Go. John Sykes shall be your vis-à-vis, and you will sit with your back to us.”

He did not go. He lingered near, taking a turn down the long room, exchanging greetings with other men condemned to stand; and each time he returned to Shirley as if drawn by some magnet, bringing observations he chose to deliver in a low voice at her ear.

Sam Wynne, meanwhile, looked increasingly wretched. Shirley could not keep still: she fanned herself; complained of heat, lack of air, lack of space; declared that when people had finished tea they ought to leave the tables; announced, with perfect distinctness, that she expected to faint if matters continued. Sam offered to take her into the open air; she told him he would give her her death of cold. In short, having swallowed his tea, he judged retreat wise and evacuated the coveted seat.

Moore ought to have taken it at once. He was at the far end of the room, in talk with Christopher Sykes. Timothy Ramsden—the great corn-factor, stout and puffy, a man as large in property as in person—advanced instead, weary of standing, and minded to sit.

Shirley’s ingenuity answered like a match struck in a dark pantry. With a sweep of her scarf she upset her teacup. Tea spilled over bench and satin, steaming and staining. A waiter must be called; napkins fluttered; apologies were made; there was a little domestic tempest. Mr. Ramsden held aloof from the commotion with an air that implied he did not sit where women made accidents.

Shirley, usually indifferent to such trifles, now acted the delicate, distressed creature to perfection—declaring herself on the verge of swooning, fussing over the damage with a tremor no one had ever seen in her over any real hurt. Ramsden opened his mouth, reconsidered, withdrew, and beat a heavy retreat.

Moore returned at last. He surveyed the bustle calmly, scanned Shirley’s face with a faintly quizzical look, and remarked that indeed this end of the room was the hottest; that the climate would agree with none but cool temperaments like his. Then, pushing waiters and napkins and all the turmoil aside with quiet decisiveness, he installed himself in the vacated place as if destiny had reserved it.

Shirley subsided at once. Her brow unknit; the restless angles of her movements softened; the mischief and wilfulness that had tormented Sam Wynne fell away as if a spell had been lifted. Yet she did not reward Moore with a gracious glance. Instead she accused him of giving her “a world of trouble,” and charged him roundly with being the cause of her losing the esteem of Mr. Ramsden and the invaluable friendship of Mr. Samuel Wynne.

“I would not have offended either gentleman for the world,” she insisted. “I have always treated both with the most respectful consideration—and see, through you, how they have been used! I shall not be happy till I make it up. To-morrow I must make a pilgrimage to Royd corn-mill, soothe the miller, and praise the grain; and next day I must call at De Walden—where I hate to go—and carry in my reticule half an oatcake for Mr. Sam’s favourite pointers.”

“You know the surest path to the heart of each swain, I doubt not,” Moore replied, perfectly composed.

His composure suited him. It made him look handsomer, not by any softness, but by a steadiness that implied equality even where inequality stared from every circumstance. He sat beside a rich woman without looking poor; his calm denied the whole vulgar arithmetic of wealth. Sometimes, when he looked down at Shirley as he spoke, there was an odd effect—as if his station, by some inward right, stood above hers. A stern light would cross his brow and glint in his eyes; and though their talk remained low, it grew animated. Shirley pressed him with questions; he refused her the full food of answers, giving her instead careful morsels. She sought his eye with a softness that asked plainly for more; he smiled pleasantly and kept his lips sealed. She turned away, piqued; within two minutes he drew her back again, offering promises—soothing, substituting—until she accepted them as a child accepts sweetmeats when denied the feast.

The heat did not suit Caroline. She grew paler as the tea-making dragged. The moment thanks were returned, she slipped from the table and hurried after Hortense, who, with Miss Mann, had already sought the open air.

Robert Moore rose when Caroline did—perhaps meaning to speak; but Shirley detained him with a parting word, and in that brief interval Caroline vanished.

Outside, Hortense received her former pupil with more dignity than warmth. She had been seriously offended by Mr. Helstone’s proceedings, and held that Caroline was to blame for obeying her uncle too literally.

“You are a very great stranger,” she said austerely, though Caroline pressed her hand with affection.

Caroline knew Hortense too well to argue. She let the punctilious chill pass, sure the deeper kindness underneath—her bonté, neither mere goodness nor mere good-nature, but something living between—would rise.

It did. Hortense examined her face, saw the change in the thinner features, and her severity melted. She kissed Caroline on both cheeks and questioned her anxiously about her health. Caroline answered gaily; yet she would have been subjected to a long, thorough cross-examination and a lecture besides, had not Miss Mann called Hortense away by asking to be conducted home. The poor invalid was already fatigued; fatigue made her sharp. Caroline’s white dress and brighter look displeased her; she preferred her young friend in brown stuff or gray gingham, with the familiar air of melancholy. She scarcely seemed to recognize her tonight, and took leave with a cool nod. Hortense promised to accompany her, and they departed together.

Caroline, left, looked round for Shirley. She saw the rainbow scarf and purple dress in the midst of a throng of ladies—women Caroline knew well, and just as systematically avoided when she could. Shy by nature, and shyer in this moment, she had no courage to press into them. Yet she could not stand solitary where others paired and clustered.

She approached instead a group of her own scholars—older girls, nearly young women—standing to watch the younger children at blind-man’s buff. Caroline knew they liked her; nonetheless she felt awkward with them out of school, as if her authority belonged to the classroom air and would not travel into the open. They, by instinct and good feeling, respected her timidity. They did not tease or take advantage; they stood round her with civil friendliness, taking her slight smiles and hurried attempts at talk as kindly as if she had given them eloquence. Their good breeding sprang, as it so often does, from good heart.

Mr. Sam Wynne came up in haste, insisting that the elder girls should join the game as well as the younger; off they went, and Caroline was again alone. She was meditating a quiet retreat to the house when Shirley, seeing from afar her isolation, hastened to her side.

“Let us go to the top of the fields,” Shirley said. “I know you do not like crowds, Caroline.”

“But it will deprive you of pleasure,” Caroline answered, looking past Shirley to the group that courted her. “All those fine people seek you so assiduously; you can be pleasant to them without effort. Why should I steal you away?”
