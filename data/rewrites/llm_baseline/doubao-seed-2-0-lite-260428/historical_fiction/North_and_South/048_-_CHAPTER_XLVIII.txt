“My own, my father’s friend! I cannot part with thee!
I ne’er have shown, thou ne’er hast known,
How dear thou art to me.”

The famous dinner parties Mrs. Lennox hosted ran on a gentle, well-blended chemistry: her wide circle of connections supplied all the soft, bright charm of their beauty, Captain Lennox contributed easy, unforced knowledge of every topic of the day from parliamentary debates to the new opera, while Mr. Henry Lennox and his coterie of sharp, rising young professional men brought the wit, the clever insight, the broad and precise learning they wielded so deftly no one ever thought to call them pedantic, no one ever felt the flow of conversation drag under the weight of a lecture. Those dinners were universally counted delightful, but even in that warm, glittering candlelight, Margaret’s quiet discontent found a home. Every talent, every tender feeling, every hard-earned accomplishment—even every flicker of impulse towards goodness—was treated as nothing more than fuel for fireworks. The hidden, sacred fire of real conviction burned out fast in a shower of sparks and empty crackle. They discussed art only in the shallowest, most sensuous terms, lingering over brushstrokes and composition as surface effects, never pausing to let the work teach them anything deeper. They whipped themselves up into gushing, performative enthusiasm about grand, noble causes in company, then never spared those subjects a single thought when they were home alone. They squandered every capacity for genuine appreciation into nothing but a stream of perfectly turned, appropriate words.

One evening, after the gentlemen had finished their port and joined the ladies in the drawing room, Henry Lennox made his way straight to Margaret, speaking to her almost of his own accord for the first time since she had come back to live in Harley Street.
“You did not look at all pleased at what Shirley was saying at dinner.”
“Did I not? My face must be far too easy to read,” Margaret replied.
“It always was. It never lost that trick of being eloquent, even when you tried to hold your tongue.”
“I did not like,” Margaret said hurriedly, “his way of arguing in favour of something he knew full well to be wrong—glaringly wrong—even when it was all just a jest.”
“But it was wonderfully clever. Every single word hit its mark. Did you take note of all those perfect, sharp little epithets he used?”
“Yes.”
“And you despise them, you want to add. Pray do not hold back on my account, even if he is my friend.”
“There!” Margaret began, then cut herself short, “that is exactly the tone in you that—” She stopped, flushing bright, and turned away. He waited a moment for her to finish her thought, but before she could move out of earshot, he spoke in a low, clear voice only she could hear.
“If my tones, or my habits of thought, are what you dislike, will you do me the justice to tell me so? Give me the chance to learn to please you.”

Weeks drifted by with no word that Mr. Bell had made the journey to Milton he had spoken of at Helstone, a trip he had said he would make almost immediately after they left. Margaret soon assumed he had settled all his business there entirely by post. She knew well he hated Milton, a smoky, loud manufacturing town entirely alien to his quiet Oxford life, and she guessed he did not fully grasp the quiet, urgent weight Margaret had placed on that in-person explanation between her and Mr. Thornton that could never be sorted out through letters. He knew it needed to be done, eventually, but to him it hardly mattered if it was done in summer, autumn, or the dead of winter.

It was now August, and there had been no mention of the Spanish trip he had once teased Edith about. Margaret had slowly, reluctantly begun to reconcile herself to that bright little illusion fading to nothing. But one morning a letter arrived: he would be coming up to London the following week, he wrote, wanted to speak to her about a plan he had been turning over in his head, and had finally agreed to “indulge in a little doctoring” now that he had come round to her way of thinking, that it was far pleasanter to blame his constant irritable, cross moods on poor health than on his own ill temper. There was a faint, forced cheer running through every line of the letter Margaret would notice later, but at the time her attention was completely taken up by Edith’s exclamations.
“Coming up to town! Oh dear, I am so worn through by this sweltering August heat I do not believe I have a single scrap of strength left for another dinner party. Besides, every single person of our acquaintance has left London for the country—we are the only silly souls left stuck here, still arguing over where we ought to go for our holiday. There will be no one at all to meet him.”
“I am certain he would far rather dine with just our small family than with the most entertaining strangers you could hunt up. And besides, if he is unwell, he will not want a crowd of invitations anyway. I am glad he has finally admitted it. I knew he was ill from the tone of all his recent letters, but he would never answer my questions when I wrote to ask after him, and I had no other person I could turn to for news of him.”
“Oh, he cannot be very ill, or he would never be thinking of Spain.”
“He has not mentioned Spain at all.”
“No, but this new plan he says he has is obviously tied to that. But would you really be willing to travel all the way there in this dreadful heat?”
“Oh, the weather will cool day by day. Yes! Think of it. I am only afraid I have wanted this far too fiercely, in that greedy, all-consuming wilful way that always ends in disappointment—or else the wish is granted exactly to the letter, but the spirit of it gives no joy at all.”
“That is just superstition, Margaret, I am sure of it.”
“No, I do not think it is. It is only a warning to me, to stop giving way to these passionate, all-or-nothing wishes. It is the same cry as the old biblical one: ‘Give me children, or else I die.’ I am afraid my own cry is, ‘Let me go to Cadiz, or else I die.’”
“My dear Margaret! You will be so charmed by the place you will want to stay there forever, and what on earth will I do then? Oh, I wish I could find you someone to marry here in London, someone I know and trust, so I could be sure of keeping you near.”
“I shall never marry.”
“Nonsense, and double nonsense! Why, as Sholto says, you are such an attraction to this house that he knows dozens of men who are already making plans to come visit us next year, just for the chance to meet you.”
Margaret drew herself up haughtily. “Do you know, Edith, I sometimes think all that time you spent living in Corfu has taught you—”
“Well?”
“Just a shade or two of coarseness.”

Edith immediately burst into bitter, heaving sobs, wailing vehemently that Margaret no longer loved her, no longer considered her a friend, until Margaret decided she had spoken far too harshly, out of nothing more than her own wounded pride. She ended up waiting on Edith hand and foot for the rest of the day, while the little lady, overcome by her hurt feelings, lay prostrate on the sofa like a wronged martyr, occasionally letting out a deep, mournful sigh, until at last she cried herself to sleep.

Mr. Bell never appeared even on the second date he had deferred his visit to. The next morning, a letter arrived from his servant Wallis, saying his master had been quietly unwell for weeks, that had been the real reason he kept putting off his journey to London. That very morning he had been due to leave, Wallis wrote, when he had been struck down by an apoplectic fit. The doctors all agreed he would never survive the night, and it was more than likely that by the time Miss Hale received this letter, her poor old master would already be gone.

Margaret turned deathly pale as she read the note at the breakfast table. Silently she handed it to Edith, then slipped out of the room. Edith was terribly shocked as she read it, and broke down into childish, frightened, gasping sobs, much to her husband’s distress. Mrs. Shaw was breakfasting in her own room, so it fell to Captain Lennox to calm his wife, to help her wrap her head around the fact that the man who was supposed to sit at their dinner table that very night was either dead or dying—her very first close brush with death in her entire sheltered life. It was some time before Edith even thought of Margaret, then she jumped to her feet and ran upstairs after her, to find Dixon packing a small case of toiletries, while Margaret hastily tied the ribbons of her bonnet, tears streaming down her face so fast her hands shook too badly to tie the strings properly.
“Oh, dear Margaret! How dreadful! What are you doing? Are you going out? Sholto will send a telegram, he will do anything you ask him.”
“I am going to Oxford. There is a train leaving in half an hour. Dixon offered to come with me, but I would go even if I had no one. I must see him one more time. Besides, he might still rally, and need someone to care for him. He has been like a father to me. Do not stop me, Edith.”
“But I must. Mamma will never approve of this. Come and ask her permission first, Margaret. You do not know what you are doing, traveling alone. I would not mind if he had his own private house, but he lives in a Fellow’s rooms in college! Come to Mamma, it will only take a minute.”

Margaret yielded to her pleading, and missed her train. In the sudden chaos of the news, Mrs. Shaw grew flustered and hysterical, and all their precious time slipped away. But there was another train due in a couple of hours, and after endless debates over what was proper and what was improper, they could not shake Margaret’s resolve. She was going, she told them, alone if she had to, no matter what anyone said, no matter how they argued over decorum. The man who had been her father’s oldest, dearest, most loyal friend was lying at the point of death, and the thought of it burned so bright and sharp in her chest that she surprised even herself with the firmness of her demand for the right to act on her own wishes. Five minutes before the train pulled out of the station, she found herself settled in a first-class carriage, sitting opposite Captain Lennox.

Margaret would always be glad she had made that trip, even though all she learned when she arrived was that he had died peacefully in the night, before Wallis had even posted the letter. She wandered slowly through his quiet, book-lined Fellow’s rooms, and forever after those rooms were tangled up in her most tender memories, tied to the thought of her father, and the one friend he had cherished so deeply. They had promised Edith before they left that if all had ended as they feared, they would return for dinner, so Margaret had to tear herself away from the little mementos of her father Mr. Bell had kept for decades, press a soft kiss to his still, kind face, and bid her quiet final farewell.

On the journey home, Captain Lennox fell asleep, and Margaret could cry freely at her leisure, thinking of this terrible, fatal year, and all the woe it had heaped upon her. This new loss did not erase the grief of the ones that had come before, but it tore open wounds that had barely started to scab over. But when she stepped through the door of the warm, lamplit Harley Street drawing room, she heard her aunt’s soft, tender voice, little Sholto trotted over to carefully carry Aunt Margaret’s cup of tea to her without spilling a drop, and there was Edith, pale and pretty, brimming with tender sympathy for her grief. Margaret roused herself out of that heavy, hopeless, superstitious fog of despair. She sank into Edith’s usual spot on the sofa, and by the time she went upstairs to dress, she could thank God that her dear old friend had been spared a long, lingering, painful illness.
