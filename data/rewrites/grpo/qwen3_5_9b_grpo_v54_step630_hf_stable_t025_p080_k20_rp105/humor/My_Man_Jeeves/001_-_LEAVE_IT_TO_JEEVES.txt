Jeeves—my man, you know—is truly a marvel. So capable. Honestly, I'm lost without him. On a grand scale, he resembles those solemn figures perched on the marble railings at Pennsylvania Station, stationed in the "Inquiries" booth. You know the types. You approach one and ask, "When's the next train to Melonsquashville, Tennessee?" and they reply, without a flicker of hesitation, "Two-forty-three, track ten, change at San Francisco." And they're invariably correct. Jeeves projects exactly that same aura of omniscience.

Take, for instance, my encounter with Monty Byng in Bond Street. He was sporting the ultimate grey check suit, and I felt a desperate void in my wardrobe until I could replicate it. I extracted the tailor's address, and within an hour, the garment was being stitched.

"Jeeves," I announced that evening. "I'm commissioning a check suit identical to Mr. Byng's."

"Injudicious, sir," he replied, his tone leaving no room for argument. "It will not become you."

"What absolute rot!" I protested. "It's the finest thing I've laid eyes on in years."

"Unsuitable for you, sir."

Well, the verdict was inevitable. The suit arrived, I donned it, and upon catching my reflection, I nearly fainted. Jeeves was spot-on. I resembled a cross between a music-hall clown and a disreputable bookmaker. Yet, Monty had looked dashing in the exact same fabric. These are the mysteries of Life, and that is the sum total of it.

But Jeeves's sartorial infallibility is merely the tip of the iceberg. The man knows everything. Recall the tip regarding the horse "Wonderchild" on the "Lincolnshire." I'd acquired it from a reliable source; it smelled of hot, spicy victory.

"Jeeves," I said, feeling charitable toward the man, "if you seek a quick profit, back Wonderchild for the 'Lincolnshire.'"

He shook his head.

"I'd prefer not to, sir."

"But it's the straight goods. I intend to ride him to the wire."

"I do not recommend it, sir. The animal is not destined to win. Second place is the stable's objective."

Pure nonsense, I thought. How could Jeeves possibly know? Yet, as events unfolded, Wonderchild led until he was practically breathing down the post, only for Banana Fritter to surge forward and nose him out. I returned home immediately and rang the bell.

"After this," I declared, "no further steps without your counsel. From now on, consider yourself the brain of the establishment."

"Very good, sir. I shall endeavor to give satisfaction."

And he has, by Jove! I am somewhat deficient in cerebral capacity; the old bean seems constructed more for decoration than utility. However, after five minutes of consultation with Jeeves, I am ready to advise anyone on anything. Thus, when Bruce Corcoran arrived with his troubles, my first instinct was to ring the bell and entrust the matter to the lad with the bulging forehead.

"Leave it to Jeeves," I said.

I first met Corky upon arriving in New York. He was a friend of my cousin Gussie, who frequented the Washington Square district. I must confess, my departure from England was orchestrated by Aunt Agatha to prevent Gussie from marrying a vaudeville performer. The situation became so entangled that I decided exile in America was preferable to returning home to endure Aunt Agatha's lectures. I dispatched Jeeves to secure a suitable apartment, and thus began my period of banishment. I must admit, New York is a splendid place for exile. Everyone was exceedingly kind, life was bustling, and being wealthy ensured everything ran smoothly. I was introduced to various circles, from the dollar-rolling residents near the Park to the struggling artists and writers around Washington Square.

Corky was one of these artists. He claimed to be a portrait painter, though he had yet to complete a single portrait. He sat on the sidelines, wrapped in a blanket, awaiting his break. The paradox of portrait painting is that one cannot begin until clients request it, yet clients do not request until one has a portfolio. Corky managed by sketching occasional cartoons for comic papers and designing furniture for advertisements. However, his primary income stemmed from biting the ear of a rich uncle—Alexander Worple, a magnate in the jute industry. While I remain vague on the specifics of jute, it appears to be a lucrative venture, for Mr. Worple had amassed a considerable fortune.

Many assume having a wealthy uncle is a breeze, but Corky disagreed. His uncle was a robust, seemingly immortal figure, fifty-one years old and looking decades younger. Corky's distress wasn't his uncle's longevity, but the relentless pressure to abandon art for the jute business. Jute had become an obsession for Mr. Worple, a spiritual imperative. Corky, convinced of his artistic destiny, relied on the utmost tact to extract a grudging quarterly allowance. This was possible only because Mr. Worple possessed a peculiar hobby: ornithology. He had authored *American Birds* and was penning *More American Birds*, intending to continue until avian species were exhausted. Corky would visit every three months, listening to his uncle's endless lectures on birds, which secured his allowance. It was a tedious existence, filled with suspense and boredom, for Corky found birds unappealing unless broiled or paired with a cold bottle.

Mr. Worple was also prone to erratic tempers, viewing Corky's artistic pursuits as proof of innate idiocy. I suspect Jeeves feels similarly about me.

One afternoon, Corky entered my apartment, ushering a girl ahead of him. "Bertie, I want you to meet my fiancée, Miss Singer," he announced. My immediate concern was not the romance, but the uncle.

"Corky, how about your uncle?" I asked.

The poor chap emitted a mirthless laugh, looking anxious, as if he had committed a crime but lacked a plan for the aftermath.

"We are terrified, Mr. Wooster," the girl interjected. "We hoped you might suggest a way to break it to him."

Muriel Singer was a quiet, appealing young woman with large, innocent eyes that made one feel protective, yet simultaneously daring. She evoked a desire to shield her, yet also stirred a knight-errant impulse to reform the world. I felt entirely aligned with her cause.

"I don't see why your uncle shouldn't adore Miss Singer," I assured Corky. "He'll view her as the ideal wife."

Corky remained despondent. "You don't know him. Even if he liked Muriel, he wouldn't admit it. He's pig-headed. It would be a matter of principle for him to object. He'd raise Cain automatically."

I strained my brain for a solution. "Perhaps arrange a meeting where he encounters Miss Singer without realizing you know her?"

"But how?"

"There's only one thing to do," I said.

"What's that?"

"Leave it to Jeeves."

I rang the bell.

"Sir?" Jeeves appeared, manifesting from thin air as only he can. He is like a phantom who dissolves and reassembles where needed. The moment he registered my presence, a weight lifted from my shoulders.

"Jeeves, we require your advice."

"Very good, sir."

I summarized Corky's dilemma. "We need a method for Mr. Worple to meet Miss Singer without knowing Mr. Corcoran already knows her."

"Perfectly, sir."

"I have a solution, sir," Jeeves stated. "It cannot fail, but it requires financial outlay."

"He means," I translated, "he has a brilliant idea, but it costs money."

Corky's face fell, but Muriel's gaze encouraged me to step up. "You can count on me for expenses, Corky."

"I suggest Mr. Corcoran leverage Mr. Worple's passion for ornithology," Jeeves proposed. "Given the construction of New York apartments, I have overheard Mr. Corcoran's enthusiasm for the subject. Why not have Miss Singer publish a small volume titled *The Children's Book of American Birds*, dedicated to Mr. Worple? A limited edition, funded by you, praising his work. A presentation copy sent with a letter requesting an introduction would achieve the desired result."

"That's absolutely ripping!" I exclaimed.

"But I can't write a book," Muriel objected. "I can't even write good letters."

"Muriel's talents lie in drama," Corky clarified. "She is in the chorus of *Choose Your Exit* at the Manhattan. This might alarm Mr. Worple, given his disdain for the stage."

Jeeves offered a remedy. "We can engage an impecunious author to compose the volume for a fee, with Miss Singer's name on the title page."

"Sam Patterson would do it for a hundred dollars," Corky agreed. "I'll contact him."

Days later, the book arrived. It featured a red cover with a bird illustration. Inside, the text praised Mr. Worple's expertise on the purple finch linnet and the yellow-billed cuckoo. It was a masterpiece of flattery.

"It's a cert!" I declared.

"An absolute cinch!" added Corky.

Soon, Corky reported success. Mr. Worple had written a letter dripping with affection, inviting Muriel to call.

Months passed. I returned to New York and found Muriel alone at a restaurant. She denied waiting for Corky, claiming she had left the stage. Suddenly, it dawned on me: she was married.

Her husband, Alexander Worple, stood nearby—a formidable figure with grey hair and a healthy complexion. He warmly greeted me, mentioning his nephew's recent transformation into a "serious" painter. He invited me to dinner.

Upon returning to my apartment, I summoned Jeeves. "Jeeves, you remember Mr. Corcoran and the bird book scheme?"

"Yes, sir."

"Well, she's slid. She's married the uncle."

Jeeves took it in stride. "That was a development to be feared, sir."

"You expected it?"

"It crossed my mind."

I felt guilty for avoiding Corky, fearing his reaction to the marriage. But eventually, pity overtook caution. I visited his studio, only to find him painting a portrait of a severe-looking woman holding a baby—the very child who had displaced him from his inheritance.

"It's my uncle's idea," Corky explained. "The portrait is a surprise for Muriel. The nurse takes the baby out, and they sneak in. If you want irony, meet this. Here's my first commission, and the sitter is the human poached egg who stole my future."

He lamented the irony of painting the child who ruined his life, describing the baby's vacant gaze as revolting.

Later, Corky called. "I've finished the portrait. But it doesn't look right. My uncle arrives in half an hour. I need moral support."

"I'll come," I promised. "But only if I bring Jeeves."

"Why Jeeves? He suggested the scheme that led to this!"

"If you think I'll face that uncle without Jeeves, you're mistaken. I'd sooner fight a lion."

We arrived at the studio. Corky stood defensively before the painting. "How does it strike you?"

I examined it. "Of course, I only saw the kid briefly, but... wasn't he an ugly sort of kid?"

"As ugly as that?"

"Honesty compels me to say... I don't see how it could have been."

Corky groaned. "I've painted the soul of the sitter. The child's soul."

"But could a child have such a soul?"

"I doubt it," Jeeves interjected.

"It leers at you, doesn't it?"

"You've noticed that?"

"I couldn't help it."

"All I tried to do was give him a cheerful expression, but he looks dissipated."

"He has a decidedly inebriated air, sir," Jeeves noted.

Just then, the door opened. Mr. Worple entered, jovial and shaking hands. "Well, Bruce, the portrait is finished? Bring it out. This will be a wonderful surprise for your aunt."

He approached the canvas, expecting praise. Instead, he recoiled. "Oosh!" Silence filled the room. "Is this a practical joke?"

"You want to stand farther away," I advised.

"You're right! I want to stand so far away I need a telescope!" He turned on Corky like a tiger. "This is what you've wasted my money on! A painter! I wouldn't let you paint my house! This is an extract from a comic supplement!"

He stormed out, declaring that unless Corky reported to his office on Monday to abandon art and start at the bottom of the jute business, there would be no more allowance.

"Corky, old top!" I whispered.

Corky stared at the painting, defeated. "Well, that finishes it!"

"What will you do?"

"Do? I can't stay if he cuts off supplies. I'll have to go to the office."

The silence was heavy, akin to visiting a friend sentenced to prison. Then, a soothing voice broke through.

"If I might make a suggestion, sir!"

It was Jeeves. He had emerged from the shadows, gazing gravely at the painting.

"I recall a Mr. Digby Thistleton, now Lord Bridgnorth. After a patent depilatory failed, he marketed it again as 'Hair-o,' guaranteeing hair growth. It became a huge success, earning him a peerage. There is always a way, sir. Mr. Worple compared the portrait to a comic supplement. Editors would gladly consider it as a foundation for humorous drawings. Mr. Corcoran's talent lies in humor. This picture is bold and vigorous. It would be highly popular."
