When the last chord of service had been coaxed into silence beneath the old square tower, the Cathedral began to empty with the orderly unhurriedness of creatures long trained to routine. The choir boys scuffled away, their soles rasping the stone like brushes on a drum-skin; the canons and dignitaries drifted apart with the same measured decorum as rooks making for their roost. And as with those clerical birds, two figures detached themselves from the flock and doubled back, as if some private current of meaning ran counter to the general flight.

The Close received them with its own voice: the damp flags held shallow pools that trembled when the wind passed; the great elms shed leaves in sighing showers, their wet boughs ticking softly as beads. Not only the day waned, but the year. A low sun, fierce in colour yet cold to the skin, burned behind the broken monastery wall; half the Virginia creeper’s deep red had let go and lay like spilt spice on the paving-stones. The rain of the afternoon still hung in the air, giving the stone that particular smell—old lime and moss and soot—that rises when water wakes the pores of ancient masonry.

Some timid leaves, hustled by a gust, tried to slip inside the low arched door of the Cathedral, seeking warmth like small creatures. Two men came out and, without thinking, brushed them back with their feet, casting them to the wet again. One of the two turned a broad key with a competent clink; the other vanished with a folio music-book hugged to his breast.

“Mr. Jasper was that, Tope?” asked the Dean.

“Yes, Mr. Dean.”

“He has stayed late.”

“Yes, Mr. Dean. I have stayed for him, your Reverence. He has been took a little poorly.”

“Say ‘taken,’ Tope—to the Dean,” murmured the younger clergyman at the Dean’s elbow; and he said it with the neatness of a man who hears language as other men hear a sour note.

Mr. Tope—Chief Verger and showman, accustomed to be lofty with excursion parties—refused with silence to notice that any correction had been offered.

“And when and how has Mr. Jasper been taken—taken—” repeated the Dean, tasting the corrected word as if it were a better seasoning; “when and how has Mr. Jasper been Taken—”

“Taken, sir,” Tope murmured, deferential.

“—poorly, Tope?”

“Why, sir, Mr. Jasper was that breathed—”

“I wouldn’t say ‘that breathed,’ Tope,” interposed Mr. Crisparkle, with the same gentle touch of trimness. “Not English—to the Dean.”

“Breathed to that extent,” the Dean suggested, not sorry to be thus served as standard.

“Mr. Jasper’s breathing was so remarkably short,” Tope said, working round the obstacle as a practised guide will lead you past the worn corner of a tomb, “when he came in, that it distressed him mightily to get his notes out: which was perhaps the cause of his having a kind of fit on him after a little. His memory grew DAZED.”

He launched DAZED with a pointed look at Mr. Crisparkle, as if daring him to better it.

“And a dimness and giddiness crept over him as strange as ever I saw: though he didn’t seem to mind it particularly, himself. However, a little time and a little water brought him out of his DAZE.”

He repeated it—DAZE—with the satisfied air of a man who has managed a word as he might manage a reluctant visitor through a narrow doorway.

“And Mr. Jasper has gone home quite himself, has he?” asked the Dean.

“Your Reverence, he has gone home quite himself. And I’m glad to see he’s having his fire kindled up, for it’s chilly after the wet, and the Cathedral had both a damp feel and a damp touch this afternoon, and he was very shivery.”

All three looked towards the old stone gatehouse that crossed the Close like a relic of fortification turned domestic. Through its latticed window a fire shone out on the fast-darkening scene, throwing ivy and creeper into heavy shadow. The great Cathedral bell struck the hour; and at the sound a ripple of wind went through those hanging leaves as if the air itself were a skin, stirred by the solemn vibration. The Close heard it and held it; the stone made an echo of it; and the sound seemed to go on humming through tomb and tower, broken niche and defaced statue, like a remembered tune no one could quite translate into words.

“Is Mr. Jasper’s nephew with him?” asked the Dean.

“No, sir,” replied the Verger. “But expected. There’s his own solitary shadow betwixt his two windows—the one looking this way, and the one looking down into the High Street—drawing his own curtains now.”

“Well, well,” said the Dean, briskly breaking up the little conference as his dinner-bell called him by stronger claims than any bell in the tower. “I hope Mr. Jasper’s heart may not be too much set upon his nephew. Our affections, however laudable, in this transitory world, should never master us; we should guide them, guide them. I find I am not disagreeably reminded of my dinner, by hearing my dinner-bell. Perhaps, Mr. Crisparkle, you will, before going home, look in on Jasper?”

“Certainly, Mr. Dean. And tell him that you had the kindness to desire to know how he was?”

“Ay; do so, do so. Certainly. Wished to know how he was. By all means. Wished to know how he was.”

With a pleasant air of patronage, the Dean—his quaint hat as nearly cocked as a Dean in good spirits may—walked off towards the ruddy dining-room of the snug red-brick house where he was “in residence” with Mrs. Dean and Miss Dean.

Mr. Septimus Crisparkle, Minor Canon—fair, rosy, early-rising, musical and classical; cheerful, kind, good-natured, boy-like; one who flung himself head-foremost into every deep running water in the surrounding country as gladly as if it were a duty—went his own way towards the gatehouse, thinking of his early tea, and carrying in his ears the last clean cadence of the service like a well-baked crust that still crackles when you break it.

Jasper’s door admitted him to a room that seemed always half in eclipse. The air was warmed, yes, by the fire that had just been coaxed to brightness; yet the corners held their shadow as if they preferred it. Even in full sun, the light seldom touched the grand piano in its recess, or the folio music-books on the stand, or the shelves of books; it seldom reached the unfinished portrait over the chimneypiece: a blooming schoolgirl with flowing brown hair tied with a blue riband, her beauty remarkable for an almost babyish saucy discontent, comically conscious of itself. The picture had no artistic merit—mere daub—but it had been made humorously, almost revengefully, like the original.

Jasper, dark, thick-haired, well-arranged in whisker and manner, stood by the fire as if he were listening to it. He was six-and-twenty, yet looked older, as dark men often do. His voice, when he spoke, was deep and good; his manner, though courteous, carried a habitual shade, like a room built to keep the sun out.

“Sorry to hear from Tope that you have not been well, Jasper,” said Crisparkle.

“O, it was nothing, nothing!”

“You look a little worn.”

“Do I? O, I don’t think so. What is better, I don’t feel so. Tope has made too much of it, I suspect. It’s his trade to make the most of everything appertaining to the Cathedral, you know.”

“I may tell the Dean—I call expressly from the Dean—that you are all right again?”

“Certainly; with my respects and thanks to the Dean,” Jasper replied, with a slight smile that sat on his face as carefully as a note held in tune.

“I’m glad to hear that you expect young Drood.”

“I expect the dear fellow every moment.”

“Ah! He will do you more good than a doctor, Jasper.”

“More good than a dozen doctors. For I love him dearly, and I don’t love doctors, or doctors’ stuff.”

Crisparkle, whose geniality often overflowed into song as naturally as a kettle sings when it comes to the boil, took his leave. “We shall miss you, Jasper, at the ‘Alternate Musical Wednesdays’ to-night; but no doubt you are best at home. Good-night. God bless you! ‘Tell me, shep-herds, te-e-ell me; tell me-e-e, have you seen (have you seen, have you seen, have you seen) my-y-y Flo-o-ora-a pass this way!’”

With that melodious delivery—half music, half affectionate nonsense—he withdrew his amiable face from the doorway and went down-stairs. There came sounds of recognition at the stair-foot, the brief quick exchange of greeting that makes a house, for an instant, ring like a bowl struck with a spoon.

Jasper listened. Something tightened in him—an ear pricked, a nerve touched. He started from his chair, and in the next moment had a young fellow in his arms.

“My dear Edwin!”

“My dear Jack! So glad to see you!”

“Get off your greatcoat, bright boy, and sit down here in your own corner. Your feet are not wet? Pull your boots off. Do pull your boots off.”

“My dear Jack, I am as dry as a bone. Don’t moddley-coddley, there’s a good fellow. I like anything better than being moddley-coddleyed.”

Checked, Jasper stood still and watched him strip away coat, hat, gloves with brisk youthfulness, as if shedding weather were no more than tossing aside a napkin. And once for all there was on Jasper’s face, whenever it turned this way, a look not merely fond but hungry: exacting, watchful, devoted, concentrated—never divided.

“Now I am right, and now I’ll take my corner, Jack. Any dinner, Jack?”

Jasper opened a door at the upper end of the room and disclosed a small inner chamber pleasantly lighted and ready, where a comely dame was setting dishes on table with that efficient clatter which is the domestic counterpoint to cathedral music. The room breathed of hot gravy and browned edges, of pepper and honest fat, of plate warmed by steam. Outside, the wind worried the creeper; inside, the hearth and kitchen did their older work of making a shelter in sound as well as warmth—the soft chink of spoon against dish, the sizzle and sigh when something is set down, the comfortable mutter of a household that has fed men before and means to feed them again.

“What a jolly old Jack it is!” cried Edwin, clapping his hands. “Look here, Jack; tell me; whose birthday is it?”

“Not yours, I know,” Jasper answered, pausing to consider.

“Not mine, you know? No; not mine, I know! Pussy’s!”

As Edwin spoke the name, Jasper’s fixed look, still fixed on the boy, had in it a strange power of suddenly taking in the sketch over the chimneypiece too, as if the name had rung a bell that made the painted face answer.

“Pussy’s, Jack! We must drink Many happy returns to her. Come, uncle; take your dutiful and sharp-set nephew in to dinner.”

As the boy—little more than a boy—laid a hand on Jasper’s shoulder, Jasper gaily laid a hand on his; and so, arm to shoulder, they went in with that comradely step which is half march, half dance.

“And, Lord! here’s Mrs. Tope!” cried Edwin. “Lovelier than ever!”

“Never you mind me, Master Edwin,” retorted the Verger’s wife; “I can take care of myself.”

“You can’t. You’re much too handsome. Give me a kiss because it’s Pussy’s birthday.”

“I’d Pussy you, young man, if I was Pussy, as you call her,” Mrs. Tope retorted, blushing after being saluted. “Your uncle’s too much wrapt up in you, that’s where it is. He makes so much of you, that it’s my opinion you think you’ve only to call your Pussys by the dozen, to make ’em come.”

“You forget, Mrs. Tope,” Jasper interposed, taking his place with a genial smile that did not reach the depth of his gaze, “and so do you, Ned, that Uncle and Nephew are words prohibited here by common consent and express agreement. For what we are going to receive His holy name be praised!”

“Done like the Dean! Witness, Edwin Drood! Please to carve, Jack, for I can’t.”

The dinner went forward with the practical solemnity of appetite. The knives made their small bright music; the plates warmed the palms; the scents rose—roast and onion, pepper and browned crust—till even the damp of the afternoon seemed driven from the bones. Little was said while mouths were busy, as if speech would only spoil the honest work; and the house, content in its routine, made those low domestic sounds that mend the mind: a chair leg shifted, a dish nudged, the fire settling, the kettle’s faint hiss far away.

At length the cloth was drawn, and walnuts set down, and a decanter of rich-coloured sherry that held the light like dark amber.

“I say! Tell me, Jack,” Edwin began, the talk now flowing as naturally as wine. “Do you really and truly feel as if the mention of our relationship divided us at all? I don’t.”

“Uncles as a rule, Ned, are so much older than their nephews,” Jasper returned, “that I have that feeling instinctively.”

“As a rule! Ah, may-be! But what is a difference in age of half-a-dozen years or so? And some uncles, in large families, are even younger than their nephews. By George, I wish it was the case with us!”

“Why?”

“Because if it was, I’d take the lead with you, Jack, and be as wise as Begone, dull Care! that turned a young man gray, and Begone, dull Care! that turned an old man to clay.—Halloa, Jack! Don’t drink.”

“Why not?”

“Asks why not, on Pussy’s birthday, and no Happy returns proposed! Pussy, Jack, and many of ’em! Happy returns, I mean.”

Jasper took the toast in silence, laying an affectionate, laughing touch on Edwin’s extended hand as if steadying not merely fingers but giddy spirits too.

“Hip, hip, hip, and nine times nine, and one to finish with, and all that, understood. Hooray, hooray, hooray!—And now, Jack, let’s have a little talk about Pussy. Two pairs of nut-crackers? Pass me one, and take the other.”

The first crack rang out, sharp as a snapped twig.

“How’s Pussy getting on, Jack?”

“With her music? Fairly.”

“What a dreadfully conscientious fellow you are, Jack! But I know, Lord bless you! Inattentive, isn’t she?”

“She can learn anything, if she will.”

“If she will! Egad, that’s it. But if she won’t?”

Crack—slow, on Jasper’s part.

“How’s she looking, Jack?”

Jasper’s concentrated face again took in the portrait as he answered, “Very like your sketch indeed.”

“I am a little proud of it,” Edwin said, glancing up with complacency, then shutting one eye and sighting it over the nut-crackers held level like a surveying instrument. “Not badly hit off from memory. But I ought to have caught that expression pretty well, for I have seen it often enough.”

Crack—on Edwin’s part. Crack—on Jasper’s.

“In point of fact,” Edwin went on after dipping among walnut fragments with a piqued air, “I see it whenever I go to see Pussy. If I don’t find it on her face, I leave it there.—You know I do, Miss Scornful Pert. Booh!”

He twirled the nut-crackers at the painted girl as if teasing her into life.

Crack, crack, crack—fast now on Edwin’s side. Slowly, one crack on Jasper’s. Silence followed, and in it the fire made a low inward roar like an animal sleeping.

“Have you lost your tongue, Jack?”

“Have you found yours, Ned?”

“No, but really;—isn’t it, you know, after all—”

Jasper lifted his dark eyebrows.

“Isn’t it unsatisfactory to be cut off from choice in such a matter? There, Jack! I tell you! If I could choose, I would choose Pussy from all the pretty girls in the world.”

“But you have not got to choose.”

“That’s what I complain of. My dead and gone father and Pussy’s dead and gone father must needs marry us together by anticipation. Why the—Devil, I was going to say, if it had been respectful to their memory—couldn’t they leave us alone?”

“Tet, tet, dear boy,” Jasper remonstrated gently.

“Tet, tet? Yes, Jack, it’s all very well for you. Your life is not laid down to scale, and lined and dotted out for you, like a surveyor’s plan. You have no uncomfortable suspicion that you are forced upon anybody, nor has anybody an uncomfortable suspicion that she is forced upon you, or that you are forced upon her. You can choose for yourself. Life, for you, is a plum with the natural bloom on; it hasn’t been over-carefully wiped off for you—”

“Don’t stop, dear fellow. Go on.”

“Can I anyhow have hurt your feelings, Jack?”

“How can you have hurt my feelings?”

“Good Heaven, Jack, you look frightfully ill! There’s a strange film come over your eyes.”

Jasper, with a forced smile, put out his right hand as if to quiet Edwin and gain time at once. For a little while he seemed to fight for breath, and the sound in the room changed: the fire’s murmur grew loud; the wind outside rose and fell along the gatehouse ivy like a distant surf; even the Cathedral bell’s last vibration seemed to have left an ache in the air.

At length Jasper said faintly, “I have been taking opium for a pain—an agony—that sometimes overcomes me. The effects of the medicine steal over me like a blight or a cloud, and pass. You see them in the act of passing; they will be gone directly. Look away from me. They will go all the sooner.”

Edwin, scared, obeyed and stared down at the ashes on the hearth, as if staring could keep the moment from becoming a memory. Jasper did not look away; he stared into the fire with a fierce steadiness, gripping the elbow-chair until his hand seemed welded to it. Thick drops stood on his forehead. His breath caught sharply—once, twice—then he subsided, and in an instant was as he had been, as if the cloud had blown through and left no mark but damp.

Edwin rose and tended him with a careful, almost fussy devotion—bringing water, adjusting, waiting for colour and ease to return.

When Jasper was fully restored, he laid a tender hand upon Edwin’s shoulder, and, strangely, his tone carried a faint raillery, as if to make light of what had shown too much.

“There is said to be a hidden skeleton in every house; but you thought there was none in mine, dear Ned.”

“Upon my life, Jack, I did think so. However, when I come to consider that even in Pussy’s house—if she had one—and in mine—if I had one—”

“You were going to say,” Jasper put in, “(but that I interrupted you in spite of myself) what a quiet life mine is. No whirl and uproar around me, no distracting commerce or calculation, no risk, no change of place; myself devoted to the art I pursue, my business my pleasure.”

“I really was going to say something of the kind, Jack; but you see, you, speaking of yourself, almost necessarily leave out much that I should have put in. For instance: I should have put in the foreground your being so much respected as Lay Precentor, or Lay Clerk, or whatever you call it, of this Cathedral; your enjoying the reputation of having done such wonders with the choir; your choosing your society, and holding such an independent position in this queer old place; your gift of teaching (why, even Pussy, who don’t like being taught, says there never was such a Master as you are!), and your connexion.”

“Yes; I saw what you were tending to. I hate it.”

“Hate it, Jack?” Edwin asked, bewildered.

“I hate it. The cramped monotony of my existence grinds me away by the grain. How does our service sound to you?”

“Beautiful! Quite celestial!”

“It often sounds to me quite devilish. I am so weary of it. The echoes of my own voice among the arches seem to mock me with my daily drudging round. No wretched monk who droned his life away in that gloomy place, before me, can have been more tired of it than I am. He could take for relief (and did take) to carving demons out of the stalls and seats and desks. What shall I do? Must I take to carving them out of my heart?”

Edwin leaned forward and laid a sympathetic hand on Jasper’s knee, searching his face. “I thought you had so exactly found your niche in life, Jack.”

“I know you thought so. They all think so.”

“Well, I suppose they do,” Edwin said slowly. “Pussy thinks so.”

“When did she tell you that?”

“The last time I was here. You remember when. Three months ago.”

“How did she phrase it?”

“O, she only said that she had become your pupil, and that you were made for your vocation.”

Edwin glanced at the portrait. Jasper saw the glance as surely as if it had been a spoken word.

“Anyhow, my dear Ned,” Jasper resumed, shaking his head with grave cheerfulness as if putting a lid on something that wanted to boil over, “I must subdue myself to my vocation: which is much the same thing outwardly. It’s too late to find another now. This is a confidence between us.”

“It shall be sacredly preserved, Jack.”

“I have reposed it in you, because—”

“I feel it, I assure you. Because we are fast friends, and because you love and trust me, as I love and trust you. Both hands, Jack.”

They stood, hand in hand, looking into one another’s eyes. Jasper held Edwin’s hands as if he would keep him steady not merely on his feet but in his future.

“You know now, don’t you,” Jasper said, “that even a poor monotonous chorister and grinder of music—in his niche—may be troubled with some stray sort of ambition, aspiration, restlessness, dissatisfaction, what shall we call it?”

“Yes, dear Jack.”

“And you will remember?”

“My dear Jack, I only ask you, am I likely to forget what you have said with so much feeling?”

“Take it as a warning, then.”

Edwin paused with his hands just released, considering what application a warning could have to him. Then he spoke, touched in earnest.

“I am afraid I am but a shallow, surface kind of fellow, Jack, and that my headpiece is none of the best. But I needn’t say I am young; and perhaps I shall not grow worse as I grow older. At all events, I hope I have something impressible within me, which feels—deeply feels—the disinterestedness of your painfully laying your inner self bare, as a warning to me.”

Jasper became so still that he seemed for a moment not to breathe.

“I couldn’t fail to notice, Jack,” Edwin went on, “that it cost you a great effort, and that you were very much moved, and very unlike your usual self. Of course I knew that you were extremely fond of me, but I really was not prepared for your, as I may say, sacrificing yourself to me in that way.”

Then Jasper, without any visible transition, breathed again, lifted his shoulders, laughed, and waved his right arm as if brushing away sentiment like smoke.

“No; don’t put the sentiment away, Jack; please don’t; for I am very much in earnest. I have no doubt that that unhealthy state of mind which you have so powerfully described is attended with some real suffering, and is hard to bear. But let me reassure you, Jack, as to the chances of its overcoming me. I don’t think I am in the way of it. In some few months less than another year, you know, I shall carry Pussy off from school as Mrs. Edwin Drood. I shall then go engineering into the East, and Pussy with me. And although we have our little tiffs now, arising out of a certain unavoidable flatness that attends our love-making, owing to its end being all settled beforehand, still I have no doubt of our getting on capitally then, when it’s done and can’t be helped. In short, Jack—who knows old songs better than you?—my wife shall dance, and I will sing, so merrily pass the day. Of Pussy’s being beautiful there cannot be a doubt;—and when you are good besides, Little Miss Impudence,” he added, apostrophising the portrait again, “I’ll burn your comic likeness, and paint your music-master another.”

Jasper, with his hand to his chin and an expression of musing benevolence, watched every animated look and gesture—attentively, steadily—as if the boy’s very breath and phrasing were a music he meant to remember note for note. He stayed so, held by a fascination of interest, and then said quietly, smiling:

“You won’t be warned, then?”

“No, Jack.”

“You can’t be warned, then?”

“No, Jack; not by you. Besides that I don’t really consider myself in danger, I don’t like your putting yourself in that position.”

“Shall we go and walk in the churchyard?”

“By all means. You won’t mind my slipping out of it for half a moment to the Nuns’ House, and leaving a parcel there? Only gloves for Pussy; as many pairs of gloves as she is years old to-day. Rather poetical, Jack?”

Still with his chin on his hand, Jasper murmured, “‘Nothing half so sweet in life,’ Ned!”

“Here’s the parcel in my greatcoat-pocket. They must be presented to-night, or the poetry is gone. It’s against regulations for me to call at night, but not to leave a packet. I am ready, Jack!”

Jasper let his attitude dissolve as if it had been a posture of listening. Together they went out into the Close again, where the wet stone held the day’s last cold, the elms whispered, and the Cathedral, vast and breathing in its own echoes, kept its ancient soundscape around them like a language that outlives its speakers.
