A stranger came into Cloisterham as quietly as flour sifts through a sieve, and yet he was the sort of stranger whose very stillness rearranged a room.

He was white-haired—abundantly, astonishingly so—yet with eyebrows black as char. Buttoned close in a tightish blue surtout above a buff waistcoat and grey trousers, he wore a faint military set about the shoulders, as if he had once stood to attention for the mere pleasure of the posture. But he announced himself at the Crozier—Cloisterham’s orthodox inn, and as drowsy an establishment as if it had been laid in a pantry and forgotten—as an idle dog living upon his means; and he added, to the waiter and the empty fire-grate and whatever ghosts of travellers might be listening, that he had a mind to take a lodging in the old city for a month or two, and perhaps to settle there altogether.

He made these declarations with his back to the cold hearth, waiting for dinner: fried sole, veal cutlet, and a pint of sherry. The Crozier’s business being chronically slack, the waiter served as audience, recorder, and committee of inquiry. The sole arrived with that faint, sea-wet perfume that clings to a fish even after the pan has kissed it; the cutlet hissed when the knife found its browned edge; the sherry lifted a warm, nutty note into the stale air of the room and made it briefly more hospitable.

“I suppose, waiter,” said the gentleman, shaking out his great shock of hair as a Newfoundland might shake off river-water before applying itself to its supper, “that a fair lodging for a single buffer might be found in these parts, eh?”

The waiter had no doubt of it.

“Something old,” pursued the gentleman. “Take my hat down for a moment from that peg, will you? No, I don’t want it; look into it. What do you see written there?”

The waiter peered into the crown and read, as if it were a label on a jar: “Datchery.”

“Now you know my name,” said the stranger. “Dick Datchery. Hang it up again. I was saying, something old is what I should prefer—something odd and out of the way; something venerable, architectural, and inconvenient.”

“We have a good choice of inconvenient lodgings in the town, sir,” returned the waiter, with modest confidence in Cloisterham’s ability to discomfort. “Indeed, I’ve no doubt we could suit you that far, however particular you might be. But a architectural lodging!” He shook his head, as if architecture were an ingredient his kitchen had never stocked.

“Anything Cathedraly, now,” suggested Mr. Datchery.

“Mr. Tope,” said the waiter, brightening, rubbing his chin as a man rubs at a memory like a stubborn stain, “would be the likeliest party to inform in that line.”

“Who is Mr. Tope?” asked Mr. Datchery.

The waiter explained: Mr. Tope was the Verger; Mrs. Tope had once let lodgings, or at least had offered them—so long, indeed, that the bill in her window had become a local landmark, until one day it had likely tumbled down and, being nobody’s profit to replace, had never returned.

“I’ll call on Mrs. Tope,” said Mr. Datchery, “after dinner.”

So when he had finished—when the last of the sole’s crisped edge had yielded, and the cutlet’s pale centre had confessed its hot juices, and the sherry had left a faint sweetness on his tongue—he was directed to the spot and sallied out.

But the Crozier was an inn of the most retiring disposition, set in the town like an old pot set back on the shelf; and the waiter’s directions, being fatally precise in the way that only a man unused to being asked can be precise, soon bewildered him. He went boggling about and about the Cathedral Tower whenever he could catch sight of it, with a child’s-game logic in his mind: warm when the Tower was in view, cold when it vanished behind houses and angles. The sound of Cloisterham went with him: the disciplined hush of old stone, broken by a cart’s iron tyre grating too sharply, by a distant hammer striking a note too modern against the ancient air, by the little scuffle and squeal of boys—always too loud in places that have learnt to be quiet.

He was getting very cold indeed when he came upon a fragment of burial-ground in which an unhappy sheep was grazing.

Unhappy—because a hideous small boy was stoning it through the railings. One leg of the creature was already lamed; it shifted its weight in little panicked jolts, making no proper music of its hooves at all, only a dull scrape. The boy was much excited by the benevolent sportsmanlike purpose of breaking its other three legs and bringing it down.

“’It ’im agin!” cried the boy, as the poor creature leaped. “And made a dint in his wool.”

“Let him be!” said Mr. Datchery. “Don’t you see you have lamed him?”

“Yer lie,” returned the sportsman. “’E went and lamed isself. I see ’im do it, and I giv’ ’im a shy as a Widdy-warning to ’im not to go a-bruisin’ ’is master’s mutton any more.”

The phrase sat in the air like bad smoke: “Widdy-warning.” A child’s corruption of words, perhaps; yet Mr. Datchery lingered a moment on it, as one might on a mispronounced herb-name that threatens to take the place of the true one in a kitchen’s speech.

“Come here,” said Mr. Datchery.

“I won’t; I’ll come when yer can ketch me.”

“Stay there then, and show me which is Mr. Tope’s.”

“Ow can I stay here and show you which is Topeseses, when Topeseses is t’other side the Kinfreederal, and over the crossings, and round ever so many comers? Stoo-pid! Ya-a-ah!”

“Show me where it is, and I’ll give you something.”

“Come on, then.”

Thus concluded, the boy led the way at a trot, and the stranger followed at a measured pace, listening as much as looking—listening to how the stone corridors of the Close swallowed footsteps, how certain corners magnified a laugh, how the Cathedral’s presence seemed to lower the voice of the air itself, while the town’s small cruelties still managed to sound bright and sharp.

By-and-by, the boy stopped at some distance from an arched passage and pointed.

“Lookie yonder. You see that there winder and door?”

“That’s Tope’s?”

“Yer lie; it ain’t. That’s Jarsper’s.”

“Indeed?” said Mr. Datchery, looking again, with a second glance that had more of a reckoning in it.

“Yes, and I ain’t a-goin’ no nearer ’IM, I tell yer.”

“Why not?”

“’Cos I ain’t a-goin’ to be lifted off my legs and ’ave my braces bust and be choked; not if I knows it, and not by ’Im. Wait till I set a jolly good flint a-flyin’ at the back o’ ’is jolly old ’ed some day! Now look t’other side the harch; not the side where Jarsper’s door is; t’other side.”

“I see.”

“A little way in, o’ that side, there’s a low door, down two steps. That’s Topeseses with ’is name on a hoval plate.”

“Good. See here,” said Mr. Datchery, producing a shilling. “You owe me half of this.”

“Yer lie! I don’t owe yer nothing; I never seen yer.”

“I tell you you owe me half of this, because I have no sixpence in my pocket. So the next time you meet me you shall do something else for me, to pay me.”

“All right, give us ’old.”

“What is your name, and where do you live?”

“Deputy. Travellers’ Twopenny, ’cross the green.”

The boy darted off with the shilling at once, and then, at a safe distance, began a demon dance as if to nail down the fact of his possession. Mr. Datchery took off his hat and gave his white hair another shake—so that it rose and fell like a soft tide—and seemed resigned, as if he had paid for information the way one pays for pepper: too much, always, but necessary if you want the dish to speak.

He went where he had been directed.

Mr. Tope’s official dwelling, communicating by an upper stair with Mr. Jasper’s gatehouse—hence Mrs. Tope’s attendance on that gentleman—was of very modest proportions, and had the character of a cool dungeon. Its ancient walls were so massive that the rooms seemed not built so much as excavated from the thickness. The main door opened at once on a chamber of no describable shape, with a groined roof; that opened on another chamber equally without description, equally groined. Their windows were small and set deep, like eyes in a heavy brow. The air was close; the light was swarthy; the place held a persistent smell of cold stone and old damp, as if the Cathedral kept its own cellar here.

These were the apartments Mrs. Tope had long offered to an unappreciative city.

Mr. Datchery proved more appreciative. He found that if he sat with the main door open, he would enjoy the passing society of all comers to and fro by the gateway and have light enough to read by. He found that if Mr. and Mrs. Tope, living overhead, used for themselves a little side stair that came plump into the Precincts by a door opening outward—surprising and inconveniencing pedestrians in the narrow way—he would be alone, as if in a separate residence. He found the rent moderate, and everything as quaintly inconvenient as he could desire.

He agreed, therefore, to take the lodging then and there, money down, possession to be had next evening, on condition that reference was permitted him to Mr. Jasper as occupying the gatehouse of which, on the other side of the gateway, the Verger’s hole-in-the-wall was an appanage.

“The poor dear gentleman is very solitary and very sad,” said Mrs. Tope, “but I have no doubt he will speak for me.”

Perhaps, she added, Mr. Datchery had heard something of what had occurred there last winter?

Mr. Datchery had as confused a knowledge of the event, upon trying to recall it, as he could well manage. He begged Mrs. Tope’s pardon when she found it incumbent upon her to correct him in every detail of his muddled summary, but pleaded that he was merely a single buffer getting through life upon his means as idly as he could, and that so many people were so constantly making away with so many other people, as to render it difficult for a buffer of an easy temper to keep the circumstances of the several cases unmixed.

Mr. Jasper proving willing to speak for Mrs. Tope, Mr. Datchery, who sent up his card, was invited to ascend the postern staircase. Mr. Tope mentioned, as they went, that the Mayor was there; but he was not to be regarded in the light of company, as he and Mr. Jasper were great friends.

“I beg pardon,” said Mr. Datchery—hat under his arm, making a leg as he addressed himself equally to both gentlemen when admitted—“a selfish precaution on my part, and not personally interesting to anybody but myself. But as a buffer living on his means, and having an idea of doing it in this lovely place in peace and quiet, for remaining span of life, I beg to ask if the Tope family are quite respectable?”

Mr. Jasper answered for that without the slightest hesitation.

“That is enough, sir,” said Mr. Datchery.

“My friend the Mayor,” added Mr. Jasper, with a courtly motion of his hand towards that potentate, “whose recommendation is actually much more important to a stranger than that of an obscure person like myself, will testify in their behalf, I am sure.”

“The Worshipful the Mayor,” said Mr. Datchery, with a low bow, “places me under an infinite obligation.”

“Very good people, sir, Mr. and Mrs. Tope,” said Mr. Sapsea, with condescension measured out like spice from a tin. “Very good opinions. Very well behaved. Very respectful. Much approved by the Dean and Chapter.”

“The Worshipful the Mayor gives them a character,” said Mr. Datchery, “of which they may indeed be proud. I would ask His Honour—if I might be permitted—whether there are not many objects of great interest in the city which is under his beneficent sway?”

“We are, sir,” returned Mr. Sapsea, “an ancient city, and an ecclesiastical city. We are a constitutional city, as it becomes such a city to be, and we uphold and maintain our glorious privileges.”

“His Honour,” said Mr. Datchery, bowing, “inspires me with a desire to know more of the city, and confirms me in my inclination to end my days in the city.”

“Retired from the Army, sir?” suggested Mr. Sapsea, taking the stranger’s air for an advertisement.

“His Honour the Mayor does me too much credit,” returned Mr. Datchery.

“Navy, sir?” suggested Mr. Sapsea.

“Again,” repeated Mr. Datchery, “His Honour the Mayor does me too much credit.”

“Diplomacy is a fine profession,” said Mr. Sapsea, as one lays down a maxim.

“There, I confess, His Honour the Mayor is too many for me,” said Mr. Datchery, with an ingenious smile and bow; “even a diplomatic bird must fall to such a gun.”

This was very soothing to Mr. Sapsea. There was something in being addressed through the third person—His Honour, the Worshipful—that tasted to him like being served first at table. He warmed to it.

“But I crave pardon,” said Mr. Datchery. “His Honour the Mayor will bear with me, if for a moment I have been deluded into occupying his time, and have forgotten the humble claims upon my own, of my hotel, the Crozier.”

“Not at all, sir,” said Mr. Sapsea. “I am returning home, and if you would like to take the exterior of our Cathedral in your way, I shall be glad to point it out.”

“His Honour the Mayor is more than kind and gracious.”

As Mr. Datchery could not be induced to go out of the room before the Worshipful, the Worshipful led the way down-stairs; Mr. Datchery following, hat under his arm, his white hair streaming in the evening breeze like a banner that had forgotten its pole.

“Might I ask His Honour,” said Mr. Datchery as they went, “whether that gentleman we have just left is the gentleman of whom I have heard in the neighbourhood as being much afflicted by the loss of a nephew, and concentrating his life on avenging the loss?”

“That is the gentleman. John Jasper, sir.”

“Would His Honour allow me to inquire whether there are strong suspicions of any one?”

“More than suspicions, sir,” returned Mr. Sapsea; “all but certainties.”

“Only think now!” cried Mr. Datchery, as if the words were a seasoning he had not expected to find in so mild a broth.

“But proof, sir, proof must be built up stone by stone,” said the Mayor.

“As I say, the end crowns the work. It is not enough that justice should be morally certain; she must be immorally certain—legally, that is.”

“His Honour,” said Mr. Datchery, “reminds me of the nature of the law. Immoral. How true!”

“As I say, sir,” pompously went on Mr. Sapsea, “the arm of the law is a strong arm, and a long arm. That is the way I put it. A strong arm and a long arm.”

“How forcible!—And yet, again, how true!” murmured Mr. Datchery.

“And without betraying what I call the secrets of the prison-house,” pursued Mr. Sapsea—“the secrets of the prison-house is the term I used on the bench—without, I say, betraying them, I predict to you, knowing the iron will of the gentleman we have just left (I take the bold step of calling it iron, on account of its strength), that in this case the long arm will reach, and the strong arm will strike.—This is our Cathedral, sir. The best judges are pleased to admire it, and the best among our townsmen own to being a little vain of it.”

All this time Mr. Datchery walked with his hat under his arm and his hair to the breeze. When Mr. Sapsea touched that hat now, he had for an odd instant the look of having forgotten it altogether; he clapped his hand up to his head as if expecting to find another hat there, as one might reach for a missing word in a deadened dialect and find only the habit of speech.

“Pray be covered, sir,” entreated Mr. Sapsea, magnificently. “I shall not mind it, I assure you.”

“His Honour is very good, but I do it for coolness,” said Mr. Datchery.

Then he admired the Cathedral, and Mr. Sapsea pointed it out as if he himself had invented and built it—pausing where the evening made the stone look like baked bread, crisped at the edges by centuries; glossing over details he did not approve, as if the workmen had made mistakes in his absence. The place had its own hush, a kind of regulated quiet that seemed to ask men to keep their inner noise to themselves; but beyond it, in the town, there was always the other sound—the clatter, the call, the small shrill triumph of boys—pressing in.

The Cathedral disposed of, Mr. Sapsea led the way by the churchyard and stopped—by chance—in the immediate vicinity of Mrs. Sapsea’s epitaph, as if the path of every evening must at last circle back to a monument one has composed oneself.

“And by the by,” said Mr. Sapsea, appearing to descend from an elevation to remember it suddenly, like Apollo shooting down from Olympus to pick up a forgotten lyre, “that is one of our small lions. The partiality of our people has made it so, and strangers have been seen taking a copy of it now and then. I am not a judge of it myself, for it is a little work of my own. But it was troublesome to turn, sir; I may say, difficult to turn with elegance.”

Mr. Datchery became so ecstatic over the composition that—though intending to end his days in Cloisterham and therefore to have many opportunities—he would have transcribed it into his pocket-book on the spot, but for the slouching approach of its material producer and perpetuator, Durdles, whom Mr. Sapsea hailed; not sorry to show him a bright example of how to behave to superiors.

“Ah, Durdles! This is the mason, sir; one of our Cloisterham worthies; everybody here knows Durdles. Mr. Datchery—Durdles; a gentleman who is going to settle here.”

“I wouldn’t do it if I was him,” growled Durdles. “We’re a heavy lot.”

“You surely don’t speak for yourself, Mr. Durdles,” returned Mr. Datchery, “any more than for His Honour.”

“Who’s His Honour?” demanded Durdles.

“His Honour the Mayor.”

“I never was brought afore him,” said Durdles, with anything but loyalty in his look, “and it’ll be time enough for me to Honour him when I am. Until which, and when, and where, ‘Mister Sapsea is his name, England is his nation, Cloisterham’s his dwelling-place, Aukshneer’s his occupation.’”

At this moment Deputy—preceded by a flying oyster-shell—appeared and requested that Mr. Durdles would instantly “chuck” him threepence, being lawful wages overdue. While Durdles, with his bundle under his arm, slowly found and counted out the money—penny between his teeth, halfpence in his hands—Mr. Sapsea informed the new settler of Durdles’s habits, pursuits, abode, and reputation, as if giving the inventory of a local curiosity.

“I suppose a curious stranger might come to see you, and your works, Mr. Durdles, at any odd time?” said Mr. Datchery.

“Any gentleman is welcome to come and see me any evening if he brings liquor for two with him,” returned Durdles; “or if he likes to make it twice two, he’ll be doubly welcome.”

“I shall come. Master Deputy, what do you owe me?”

“A job.”

“Mind you pay me honestly with the job of showing me Mr. Durdles’s house when I want to go there.”

Deputy, with a piercing broadside of whistle through the whole gap in his mouth—as if the town’s small wild noise wanted to answer the Cathedral’s measured hush—vanished.

The Worshipful and the Worshipper passed on together until they parted with many ceremonies at the Worshipful’s door. Even then, Mr. Datchery carried his hat under his arm and gave his streaming hair to the breeze, as if he meant to let the city’s air—and its talk, its mispronunciations, its lingering old phrases and its new harsh ones—settle into it.

Said Mr. Datchery to himself that night, looking at his white hair in the gas-lighted looking-glass over the Crozier coffee-room chimneypiece, and shaking it out again:

“For a single buffer, of an easy temper, living idly on his means, I have had a rather busy afternoon!”
