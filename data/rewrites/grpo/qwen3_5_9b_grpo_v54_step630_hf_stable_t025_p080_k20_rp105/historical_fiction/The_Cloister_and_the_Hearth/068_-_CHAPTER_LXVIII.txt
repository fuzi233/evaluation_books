In the silent corridors of history, a woman bears her own burdens, often as heavy as any man's, yet the chronicler of the male experience rarely dares to map the labyrinth of female sorrow. We sketch the outline; it is the intuition of the reader, particularly the woman, that must breathe life into these cold lines, filling the void where description fails. Thus, I shall merely signal the path Margaret Brandt trod until that fateful day when she crossed the threshold of Eli's home as an adversary, discovered her beloved's letter, and emerged transformed into a friend.

Now, the trial most profound for a woman approached, while Gerard remained distant, lost to the winds of fate.

Margaret made scant use of Eli's sudden kindness. For this reserve, she possessed a plausible excuse at all times, though the truth lay deeper: within that house dwelt two men whose very presence triggered an instinctive shudder of dread in her soul. In their eyes, she had read wickedness and hatred; in their gestures, secret pacts of intelligence. She preferred the sanctuary of her own hearth. Catherine, her constant companion, visited daily, her heart swelling with anticipation for the coming birth.

Catherine belonged to that order of women whose sole purpose seems to be multiplication and the rearing of the multiplied. Once she ceased pelting every room with infants, she hovered over the remaining "fair scourges" of her brood, clucking with a fervor that suggested she multiplied by proxy. It was in this spirit she beseeched Eli to remain in Rotterdam while she returned to Tergou.

"The poor lass hath no soul about her who understands aught of anything," Catherine argued, her voice thick with maternal concern. "What good are a pair of soldiers? Such cattle ought to be cast out at such a time."

Needless to say, this was a great comfort to Margaret.

Poor soul, she was consumed by anxiety as the hour neared. She feared death; she feared Gerard's absence. Yet, the scales of fate balanced themselves. The poverty that had once nearly crushed her, and her father's helpless state, which had cost her such a desperate struggle, now stood as her shield. Adversity's iron hand had forged her against the lethargy that often overpowers the rich of her sex, compelling her to stand forever upon her feet, working. She maintained this vigilance until the end, guided by Catherine's counsel.

So it came to pass that on a fine evening, just as the sun dipped below the horizon, she lay weak as water, yet safe. A small face rested beside her, and the vast, open sky of maternity stretched before her.

"Why dost weep, sweetheart? All of a sudden?"

"He is not here to witness it."

"Ah, well, lass. He shall be here ere he is weaned. Meanwhile, God has been as good to thee as to any woman born. Consider, it might have been a girl; did not my very own Kate threaten me with one? And here we have secured the bonniest boy in Holland, a rare heavy one. The saints be praised for it."

"Ay, mother, I am but a sorry, ungrateful wretch to weep. If only Gerard were here to behold it. It is strange; I bore him well enough to be away from me in my sorrow, but oh, it seems so hard that he should not share my joy. Prithee, prithee, come to me, Gerard! Dear, dear Gerard!" She stretched out her feeble arms toward the empty air.

Catherine bustled about, deliberately avoiding Margaret's gaze, for she could not restrain her own tears at hearing her own absent child thus earnestly invoked.

Soon, turning around, she found Margaret regarding her with a singular, piercing expression. "Did you hear nothing?"

"No, my lamb. What?"

"I cried out for Gerard, but now..."

"Ay, ay, surely I heard that."

"Well, he answered me."

"Tush, girl: do not say such things."

"Mother, as certain as I lie here with his boy by my side, his voice returned to me. 'Margaret!' So it was. Yet, I thought it was not his happy voice. Perhaps that was the distance. All voices grow sad and faint when carried afar. Why art thou not happy, sweetheart? And I, so happy this night? Mother, I seem never to have felt a pain or known a care." Her sweet eyes turned, glowing with a strange intensity, fixed upon the little face in silence.

That very night, Gerard threw himself into the Tiber. And in that very hour, she heard him speak her name; he cried aloud in the jaws of death and the grip of despair.

"Margaret!"

Let those who can account for it explain. I cannot.
