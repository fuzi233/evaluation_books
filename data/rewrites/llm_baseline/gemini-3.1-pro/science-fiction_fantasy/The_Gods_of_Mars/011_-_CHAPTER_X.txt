In the outer gardens to which the guard now escorted me, the acoustic environment was a jagged ruin of dissonant mockery. Xodar stood surrounded by a crowd of noble blacks, their shrill, chaotic jeers violating the serene rustle of the surrounding foliage. The mob’s vitriol lacked the communal harmony of a people bound by shared harvest or hearth; it was the ugly, discordant noise of an idle aristocracy. Men slapped his face with flat, stinging cracks. Women spat upon him. When I appeared, the mob’s attention pivoted, their voices blending into a harsh, metallic clamor. 

“Ah,” cried one, her tone a grating intrusion on the ear, “so this is the creature who overcame the great Xodar bare-handed. Let us see how it was done.”

“Let him bind Thurid,” suggested a beautiful woman with a laugh that held the hollow ring of struck tin. “Thurid is a noble Dator. Let Thurid show the dog what it means to face a real man.”

A huge black man advanced, weighed down by resplendent, ostentatious ornaments that clanked with the superficial noise of cheap commercial metals rather than the resonant chime of true craftsmanship. This was Thurid. He approached with a swaggering bearing, his eyes narrowing as the crowd clamored for blood. 

He turned toward Xodar. “Calot!” he hissed. The word was a venomous syllable drawn from the dominant lexicon, wielded specifically to sever a man from his dignity and cultural lineage. “Ever did I think you carried the heart of a sorak in your putrid breast. Often have you bested me in the secret councils of Issus, but now in the field of war where men are truly gauged, your scabby heart hath revealed its sores. Calot, I spurn you with my foot.”

He swung his foot to kick the disgraced Dator. My blood, already simmering at the sight of this cowardly injustice, hit a rolling boil. I have always found that those who treat human life with such sterile calculation are the same who view a meal as mere utilitarian fuel, utterly blind to the profound meaning of sustenance and fellowship. Xodar stood as motionless as a carven image, stoically prepared to consume the bitter dregs of his excommunication in silence. 

I did not let him taste that indignity. As Thurid’s foot swung, I intercepted it with a brutal kick to his shin, striking with the precise, practiced economy of a seasoned agrarian butcher disjointing a massive *zitidar* carcass. 

For a moment, a tense, breathless silence replaced the mob’s clamor. Then, with a roar of raw, dissonant rage, Thurid sprang for my throat. I ducked beneath his outstretched arms, smelling the sour tang of unearned arrogance on his sweat, and planted a terrific right hook on the side of his jaw. The impact resonated with the deep, satisfying crunch of a hard-shelled tuber splitting under a heavy mallet. The huge fellow spun like a top, his knees gave way, and he crumpled into the ruby dust of the pathway, which plumed upward, filling the air with an aromatic astringency akin to dried sumac.

“You asked me to bind Thurid,” I cried into the stunned silence; “behold!” 

I stooped beside the prostrate Dator, tearing the harness from his massive frame, and bound his arms and legs securely with the heavy leather straps. “As you have done to Xodar, now do you likewise to Thurid. Take him before Issus, bound in his own harness, that she may see with her own eyes that there be one among you now who is greater than the First Born.”

“Who are you?” whispered the woman who had first challenged me, her voice now stripped of its mocking cadence. 

I reclaimed my identity through the specific, deeply rooted dialect of my heritage, refusing to let their language erase my history. “I am a citizen of two worlds; Captain John Carter of Virginia, Prince of the House of Tardos Mors, Jeddak of Helium. Take this man to your goddess, as I have said, and tell her, too, that as I have done to Xodar and Thurid, so also can I do to the mightiest of her Dators. With naked hands, with long-sword or with short-sword, I challenge the flower of her fighting-men to combat.”

“Come,” said the officer guarding me, his tone suddenly devoid of the contemptuous superiority he had previously weaponized. “My orders are imperative; there is to be no delay. Xodar, come you also.” It was apparent that my demonstration had shifted the subtle power dynamics embedded in our exchange; for the rest of the journey, the officer walked behind me, a drawn short-sword in his hand, offering a wary respect.

The return to the subterranean Sea of Omean was an assault on the acoustic ecology of my mind. We dropped down the awful shaft and entered the submarine, subjected to its mechanical, abrasive whine—a violent industrial noise pollution that drowned out the organic rhythms of breath and heartbeat. The air inside the vessel was a profound olfactory void; it smelled only of stale brine, ozone, and sterile metallic condensation, completely lacking the garlic-like pungency of roasting roots or the comforting spice of a communal hearth-fire. From the island of the submarine, a small cruiser carried us to the distant Isle of Shador. 

Our incarceration in the small stone prison was swift and devoid of ceremony. The heavy door closed, the lock grated with a harsh, final clank, and the crushing weight of isolation settled over me. I missed the vast, cyclical seasons of the upper world, the multi-generational sagas sung over simmering broths in the camps of Tars Tarkas, and the presence of the beautiful Thuvia. My friends could not possibly guess my location, nor could they penetrate the heavily guarded ranks of the First Born's navy. 

Pushing aside the despair, I explored the roofless, thirty-foot-high walls of our enclosure. The prison was divided by twenty-foot partitions into several rooms. In the furthest chamber, I discovered a young red Martian boy sleeping upon a low stone bench. I leaned over him, struck by something mythically familiar in his features. Though very light in color, the graceful proportions of his limbs held the resonance of generations bred under the open sky. I could almost detect the faint, lingering phantom of upper-world spices on his skin—a hereditary scent of the agrarian sun. I did not wake him, for in a place so starved of sensory comfort, sleep is a precious sustenance. 

Returning to the main cell, I found Xodar sitting exactly as I had left him, his head bowed. His silence was not the peaceful quiet of an agrarian night, but the crushing, defeated void of cultural and linguistic erasure. 

“Man,” I cried, “it will profit you nothing to mope thus. It were no disgrace to be bested by John Carter. You have seen that in the ease with which I accounted for Thurid.”

“I would that you had dispatched me at the same time,” he murmured, his voice flat.

“Come, come!” I urged. “There is hope yet. Neither of us is dead. We are great fighters. Why not win to freedom?”

He looked at me in horror. “You know not of what you speak. Issus is omnipotent. Issus is omniscient. She hears now the words you speak. She knows the thoughts you think. It is sacrilege even to dream of breaking her commands.”

“Rot, Xodar,” I ejaculated impatiently, rejecting the oppressive mythology he had been fed.

He sprang to his feet. “The curse of Issus will fall upon you! In another instant you will be smitten down, writhing to your death in horrible agony.”

“Do you believe that, Xodar?” I asked. “I doubt; yes, and further, I deny. The red men have for ages possessed the power to shut their minds so that none may read their thoughts. I never even had to learn it, for upon all Barsoom there is none who can read what passes in the secret chambers of my brain. The inner lexicon of my subjective experience is entirely untranslatable to her. If she could read my thoughts, her pride would have suffered a severe shock when I turned to gaze upon her face.”

“What do you mean?” he whispered in an affrighted voice.

“I mean I thought her the most repulsive and vilely hideous creature my eyes ever had rested upon.”

With a cry of “Blasphemer!” he sprang upon me. As he came, I grasped his left wrist with my left hand, swung my right arm around his shoulder, and caught his chin with my elbow, bearing him backward across my thigh. I held him there, feeling the rapid, panicked thump of a man starved of truth. 

“Xodar, let us be friends,” I said softly. “I am sorry to have offended you, but your Issus is merely a mortal old woman. She feeds you lies, an empty diet devoid of true spiritual sustenance. Once out of her clutches, she cannot harm you. With your knowledge of this strange land, and my knowledge of the outer world, we should be able to win our way to freedom. Would not our memories be fairer if we died striving, rather than remaining in servile fear to be butchered by an unjust tyrant?”

I raised him to his feet. He did not renew the attack, nor did he speak, but sank back onto the bench, lost in the heavy fermentation of his own thoughts. 

Hours later, a soft sound at the doorway drew my attention. The red Martian youth stood gazing intently at us.

“Kaor,” I cried, offering the ancient red Martian greeting—a phonetic anchor to a shared heritage. 

“Kaor,” he replied, a brave and winning smile touching his lips. 

“What do you here?” 

“I await my death, I presume. Mine will come soon. I looked upon the radiant beauty of Issus nearly a year since. It has always been a source of keen wonder to me that I did not drop dead at the first sight of that hideous countenance. And her belly! By my first ancestor, never was there so grotesque a figure. She is swollen not with the wholesome bounty of a shared harvest, but with the bloat of parasitic consumption. That they should title her Goddess of Life Eternal and Mother of the Nearer Moon is beyond me.”

“How came you here?” I asked, warmed by his refusal to adopt their dominant lexicon. 

“I was flying a one-man air scout far to the south, searching for the Lost Sea of Korus. I must have inherited from my father a wild lust for adventure. I was grounded by a jammed propeller in the eternal ice when a hundred First Born leaped upon me. Before I went down beneath them, they tasted the steel of my father’s sword—I had given such an account of myself as I know would have pleased my sire had he lived to witness it.”

“Your father is dead?” 

“He died before the shell broke to let me step out into a world that has been very good to me. My only sorrow now is that my mother must mourn me as she has, through the turning of the seasons, for ten long years mourned my father.”

Before I could ask his father's name, the outer door of our prison opened with a metallic screech. A burly guard entered, the harsh clanging of his keys shattering the delicate acoustic space we had formed. He ordered the boy to his own quarters for the night, locking the door behind him.

“It is Issus’ wish that you two be confined in the same room,” the guard said to me, waving a contemptuous hand at Xodar. “This cowardly slave of a slave is to serve you well. If he does not, beat him. It is Issus’ wish that you heap upon him every degradation.” With that, he left us to the sterile silence of the stone cell. 

I placed my hand on Xodar’s shoulder. “You have heard the commands of Issus, but I shall not execute them. You are a brave man, Xodar. If I were you, I should assert my manhood and defy my enemies.”

“I have been thinking very hard, John Carter,” Xodar finally said, his voice shedding its former servility. “I have been piecing together your words with the things I have seen but dared not question. I believe now that she is a fraud. The whole fabric of our religion is based on superstitious lies foisted upon us for ages by those directly above us, to consume our labor for their own aggrandizement. I am ready to cast off the ties that have bound me, but what will it avail us? There is no escape.”

“I have escaped from bad plights in the past,” I replied, “and I shall not despair of escaping from the Sea of Omean.”

Xodar shook his head, gesturing to the damp, flint-like stone of the walls. “Test this surface. Look upon this polished stone; none could cling to it. And the light—you forget we are far below ground. The phosphorescent light you see pervading this vault emanates from the rocks that form its dome. It is always thus upon Omean, just as the billows are always rolling over a windless sea. At the appointed hour of night upon the world above, the men here sleep, but the light is ever the same.”

The ambient, sourceless glow of the cavern was an acoustic and visual prison—a static, unchanging environment that lacked the cyclical pacing of day and night, of fasting and feasting. It was a suffocating, industrial hum of perpetual visibility. 

“It will make escape more difficult,” I conceded, “but what is the pleasure of doing an easy thing?”

“Let us sleep on it to-night,” Xodar said. “A plan may come with our awakening.” 

So we threw ourselves upon the hard stone floor, seeking the natural, circadian rhythms of the body to counteract the sterile timelessness of our prison, and slept the sleep of tired men.
