On the fourteenth of April, Romola stepped once more beneath the weathered stone gates of Florence.

She had been unable to rest even at Pistoja, where contradictory, wild rumors of the disastrous Trial by Fire at last pulled her further on to Prato. Every hour that passed, she felt herself being drawn inevitably toward the city she had fled, even as dread coiled tight in her chest—until she chanced upon the Augustinian monk of San Spirito who had once served as her godfather’s confessor. From him, she learned the full, terrible truth: Savonarola had been arrested, and her husband Tito was dead. The friar had been caught up in the jeering, surging crowd that followed the cart bearing Savonarola through the piazza, and repeated the tale that ran through every Florentine street: Tito had fled an angry mob by leaping straight into the Arno, only to be dragged to the bank and murdered there by an old man who had nursed a bitter grudge against him for years. But Romola understood the full weight of the catastrophe in a way no other Florentine could. Of Savonarola, the Black Brother spoke with the sneering, dismissive prejudice his order held for the preacher who wore white beneath his black habit, claiming the friar had confessed under torture that he had lied to and deceived the people.

Romola did not linger a moment longer. That same evening, she was home in Florence, sitting in shaken, heavy silence as Monna Brigida poured out a gush of joy, wailing lamentation, and breathless gossip. Brigida had slipped back into wearing a bright new false hairpiece during Romola’s absence, but she tore it off the second her cousin walked through the door, declaring she did not care if her hair turned fully grey, as long as her dear child stayed by her side.

Romola was far too stunned by the two shattering truths she had learned before returning to the city to care for the trivial, gossiping details Brigida rambled out. The tragedy of Tito’s violent death, and the news that Fra Girolamo had broken and confessed to fraud under torture, left her almost no capacity to process smaller, lighter news. All the strength of will she could summon beneath that crushing weight of awe-stricken grief was bent on two goals that outweighed all others: to find a way to see Savonarola in his prison cell, and to learn what had become of Tessa and her two small children.

“Cousin,” she said abruptly, when Brigida’s chatter had drifted far from trouble into endless plans for the two of them living together, “has anyone seen or spoken of a young woman with two little children, in the days since Tito died?”

Brigida started, her eyes rounding, and lifted both hands to the air. “Cristo! No. What! Was he so wicked as that, my poor dear? Ah, that’s why you ran off all those weeks ago, leaving me only a note saying you’d gone of your own free will! I said to myself, never telling a soul, ‘What was she running off from her husband for, just because they cut poor Bernardo’s head off? She’s got her father Bardo’s stubborn temper, that’s what it is.’ But don’t scold me, child—you can’t deny Bardo was fierce. If you’d only told me there was a young girl and children, I’d have understood it all. Seen her? No, and the less said the better. People speak enough ill of Tito without dragging that poor girl into it.”

“No, dearest cousin,” Romola interrupted her gently, earnestly. “Do not speak of her that way. More than anything, I want to find that young woman and her children, and care for them. They are completely helpless. Do not argue with it—it is the first thing I will do.”

“Well,” Brigida huffed, shrugging her shoulders and lowering her voice, bewildered, “if this is what being a Piagnone is about, I’ve been mistaking peas for paternosters! Fra Girolamo said widows ought not remarry, right? So if a girl slips in at the door it’s a sin and a scandal, but if she comes down the chimney she’s welcome? Two whole children—Santiddio!”

“The poor thing never meant any harm,” Romola told her. “She knows nothing of the world. I will explain it all to you, but not now.”

Early the next morning, Romola set out for the little cottage past San Ambrogio where she had once found Tessa. But it was exactly as she feared: Tessa was gone. Romola guessed Tito had sent her away beforehand to some safe spot, planning to join her later—she could not bring herself to believe he would abandon his two children of his own will. The neighbors had no news to give her, save that old deaf Lisa had left a week prior with all their belongings, and no one knew where they had gone. For days, Romola wandered Florence in a haze of anxious waiting. Every time she caught sight of a pair of small brown bare legs, or a short woman dressed in simple contadina linen, her heart would leap into her throat. She never once thought of her mission as great heroism or lofty charity: she only needed a soft, specific, precious thing to care for, after all the grief she had borne. She yearned to clasp the two little children in her arms, to make them love her. It was the one sweet, worthy thing that could come out of all her past pain.

She knew Tito had amassed a great deal of property that legally belonged to her, but she mistrusted the tainted, dishonest origins of most of that fortune, and decided she would sign nearly all of it over to the state—keeping only enough to buy back her late father’s beloved library, and set aside what was needed to support Tessa and the children in quiet comfort. Brigida fussed and fumed that Romola ought to move in with her for good, and that if Romola planned to raise other people’s children, she would help raise them too—only, they had to find them first.

At last, a new path for her search opened. She learned Tito had stashed horses and mules waiting for him at the San Gallo gate, meaning he had planned to flee Florence that way. She wrapped herself in her long widow’s veil and walked along the Via San Gallo, scanning every face, when she spotted the peddler Bratti haggling with a customer over his wares. He was a roving man, who saw far more of the countryside than most Florentines—he might know something. As she lifted her veil to cross the street toward him, her eyes landed on something hanging from the edge of his wicker basket that made her heart soar.

“Bratti, my friend,” she said, breathless, “where did you get that little shell necklace?”

“Your servant, madonna,” Bratti said, turning to her slow, never one to be surprised by anything. “It’s worth good silver, but I won’t make much profit off it. My heart’s too soft for a proper trader. I’m holding it in pledge for a poor soul.”

“Tell me where you got it—from a small young woman named Tessa, isn’t it?” Romola pressed.

“Ah! If you know her,” Bratti said, “and would redeem it from me for a small profit, and give it back to her, you’d be doing a great charity. She cried so hard when she had to hand it over you’d have thought she was about to throw herself in a brook. I’ll let you have it for one florin.”

Romola pressed the coin into his palm and took the necklace, her hands shaking with joy. “Where is she?”

“Outside that gate there, at the far end of the Borgo, at old Sibilla Manetti’s cottage. Anyone will show you the way.”

Romola’s feet carried her there as if they had wings, her mind going back to the Carnival feast, when she had memorized every little detail of that necklace around Tessa’s neck. She found the cottage in minutes. The landlady told her Tessa and the children had been waiting more than two weeks to be fetched, penniless, with nothing but their own clothes to pay the widow for their bread and shelter. Romola did not wait to hear more. She pushed open the door to the inner room.

Tessa was sitting on the low straw bed, her tears dried to silent, hollow sobs, staring blankly at the two little children playing in the far corner: Lillo had pulled his tunic over his own head, roaring as loud as he could to scare little Ninna, before peeking out again to see if she was crying. Tessa had not turned at the sound of the door, expecting it was only the old landlady—hope had long since burned out of her. Romola set down her veil, held up the little necklace so Tessa could see it, and spoke in that soft, clear voice that had once cheered her father through his long, blind days:

“Tessa.”

Tessa jumped to her feet, spinning around. Romola stepped forward, and clasped the familiar necklace back around her neck. “God has sent me to find you again.”

The poor girl screamed and sobbed, clinging tight to Romola’s arms, too overwhelmed to speak. Lillo and Ninna crept out of their corner, clutching at their mother’s skirts, staring up at Romola with wide, unblinking eyes.

That very day, all four of them walked home to Monna Brigida’s house in the Borgo degli Albizzi. Romola broke the news to Tessa gently, bit by bit, that Naldo would never come back to her—he had not run away to be cruel, he was dead. “But do not grieve alone, my Tessa,” she told her. “I am here to take care of you, always. And we have Lillo and Ninna with us.”

Monna Brigida’s mouth twitched, fighting between her awe of Romola and her urge to speak her unruly mind. She resolved that someday soon, she would tell this little contadina girl exactly who Romola was, so she remembered her place in the house—though by supper, she was sneaking the children handfuls of honeyed figs and almond sweets, and by the time she went to bed, she confessed to Romola that it would be a sin not to dote on such perfect little cherubs.

“But you have to give me a little say over what they eat, and all those little things,” she grumbled, half laughing. “You’ve never raised a baby. I had twins once, you know. They died soon after they were born, but I know exactly how to care for little ones.”
