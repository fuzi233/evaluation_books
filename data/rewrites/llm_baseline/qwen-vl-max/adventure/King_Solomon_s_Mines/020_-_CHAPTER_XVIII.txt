I cannot begin to describe the horror of the night that followed. Mercifully, sleep offered some relief—though even in our dire straits, exhausted nature still found a way to claim its due. Still, I could not rest much. Beyond the crushing dread of what lay ahead—any man, no matter how brave, might crumble under such a fate—I was haunted by something far more oppressive: silence.

You may have lain awake at night and felt the quiet weigh on your mind, but you’ve never known true silence. On Earth, there is always a whisper—a breeze, a distant bird, the hum of life. It may be faint, but it dulls the edge of emptiness. Here, nothing. We were buried deep within a snow-capped mountain, miles beneath the sky where wind roared over ice and snow. Not a sound reached us. The tunnel sealed off the chamber of the dead, and the dead make no noise. And we knew this well—lying beside poor Foulata’s cold form.

Even if every cannon on Earth had fired at once, we would not have heard it. We were cut off from the world, as though already in the grave.

Then came the irony: around us lay riches beyond imagination—enough to pay a nation’s debt, enough to build an entire fleet—and yet, we would have traded it all for a single breath of fresh air, a sip of water, or even just a swift end to our suffering. Wealth, which men spend their lives chasing, meant nothing now. It was ash in our mouths.

The hours crawled by. Then, finally, Sir Henry’s voice broke the stillness—deep, hoarse, and terrifying in the quiet.

“Good,” he said, “how many matches are left?”

“Eight,” Curtis replied.

“Strike one. Let’s see the time.”

He did. The flame burst into life, blinding in the blackness. My watch read five o’clock. Outside, dawn was staining the snow above with rose and gold. The night mists must have been lifting in the valleys.

“We should eat something,” I suggested. “Keep our strength.”

“What’s the point?” Good answered. “The sooner we die, the better.”

“While there’s life, there’s hope,” Sir Henry said firmly.

We ate a little, drank sparingly. Then, after a while, Sir Henry proposed we try shouting—just in case someone outside might hear us.

Good, whose voice carried far from years at sea, crept down the passage and began to yell. He made a hellish racket—like a beast in torment. But it might as well have been a mosquito buzzing. Nothing stirred. After a few minutes, he gave up, parched and drained. We stopped yelling—we couldn’t afford to waste water.

We sat again, back against the chests of diamonds, useless and heavy as stone. That dreadful inaction was perhaps the worst part. I broke down. I leaned my head on Sir Henry’s shoulder and wept. I think I heard Good choking back sobs on the other side, cursing himself for weakness.

But oh, what a man he was! If we had been children lost in the dark, and he our guardian, he could not have cared for us more tenderly. He forgot his own pain, soothing our nerves with stories of men who had survived impossible odds. When those failed, he reminded us that death was inevitable, that this was merely hastening it—and that dying slowly was merciful (which it isn’t). Then, quietly, as he had done before, he urged us to trust in something greater than ourselves. I did so, wholeheartedly.

His character—quiet, unassuming, yet strong as steel—was a light in that darkness.

And so the day passed, like the night before, though the word “day” seemed meaningless when all was pitch black. I lit another match—only three remained—and saw it was seven o’clock. We ate again, and then something struck me.

“How,” I asked, “is the air here still fresh? It’s thick, yes—but clean.”

“Great heavens!” Good jumped up. “I never thought of that. It can’t come through the door—it’s sealed tight. There must be another source. If there were no airflow, we’d have suffocated long ago. Let’s look.”

That tiny spark of hope changed everything. In seconds, we were crawling on hands and knees, feeling every inch of the floor, searching for a draft.

Then I touched something cold—Foulata’s face. I recoiled. For hours we searched, banging heads on tusks and chests, until Sir Henry and I gave up. But Good kept going, muttering that it was better than doing nothing.

“Come here,” he called suddenly, his voice strained.

We scrambled to him.

“Quatermain, put your hand where mine is. Feel anything?”

“I think… I feel air.”

He stood and stamped. A hollow thud echoed through the chamber.

Hope flared.

With trembling hands, I lit a match. Only three left. We were in the far corner—explaining why we hadn’t noticed the sound before. As the flame flickered, we saw it: a seam in the rock floor. And set level with it—stone ring.

We didn’t speak. Our hearts pounded too hard.

Good pulled out a knife with a hoof-pick at the back. He scratched around the ring. Then, gently, he levered it upward. Stone, unlike iron, hadn’t rusted. Slowly, it lifted.

He tried to pull it open. Nothing moved.

“Let me,” I said, impatient. The angle made it impossible for two to pull at once.

I tugged. No good.

Sir Henry tried. Failed.

Good scraped the crack again. Then:

“Curtis,” he said, “you’re strongest. Tackle it. Put your back into it.”

He took off his silk handkerchief and threaded it through the ring.

“Quatermain, grab Curtis’s waist. Pull when I say—_now._”

Sir Henry braced himself. Good and I pulled with every ounce of strength.

“Heave! Heave! It’s moving!”

I heard the crack of muscles. Then—grinding stone, rushing air—and we were thrown backward, crushed beneath a fallen flagstone.

Sir Henry’s strength had done it.

“Light a match,” he gasped, once we were up.

I did.

And there—beyond the stone—was the first step of a stair.

“Now what?” Good asked.

“Down the stairs,” Sir Henry said. “Trust in Providence.”

“Wait,” he added. “Quatermain, get the last biltong and water. We may need them.”

I went back to the chests. As I turned, a thought occurred: the diamonds. We hadn’t thought of them in days—their very idea sickened us. But what if we escaped? Might as well carry some.

So I plunged my fist into the nearest chest, filling every pocket of my coat and trousers. Then, with a grin, I scooped handfuls of large stones from a third chest. And stuffed Foulata’s basket full.

“Hey!” I shouted. “Want some diamonds? I’ve got pockets full and the basket packed.”

“Come on, Quatermain!” Sir Henry snapped. “Forget the diamonds!”

“I hope I never see another,” he muttered.

Good said nothing. I think he was saying goodbye to her.

And yet, reader, though you sit safe and warm, thinking of the wealth we left behind—vast, immeasurable—I assure you: if you’d spent twenty-eight hours in that tomb with nothing to eat or drink, you wouldn’t care about diamonds either. You’d rather die light.

Still, habit made me carry them. It wasn’t greed—it was instinct.

“Come on,” Sir Henry repeated, already descending.

“Steady. I’ll go first.”

“Mind your feet,” I warned. “There might be a drop.”

“More likely another room,” he said, counting steps.

At fifteen, he stopped.

“Bottom,” he said. “Looks like a passage. Follow me.”

Good went next. I brought up the rear, carrying the basket. At the bottom, I lit the last match.

By its weak glow, we saw a narrow tunnel branching right and left. The match burned my fingers and died.

Now came the question: which way?

We had no clue. One path might lead to salvation, the other to doom.

Then Good remembered: when I lit the match, the flame bent left. Air flows inward, not outward.

“Let’s go against the draught,” he said.

We followed the wall, feeling each step, testing the ground. We left the cursed treasure chamber behind—our only trace: open chests, empty lamp, and Foulata’s bones.

For nearly an hour, we crawled through the tunnels. They twisted, branched, looped. We were lost in a labyrinth—likely ancient mine shafts, following veins of ore. That was the only explanation.

Eventually, worn out, we stopped. Our biltong was gone. Our water—just a final sip. Throats like furnaces.

It seemed we’d escaped death in the chamber only to meet it here.

Then—I thought I heard something. A faint murmur.

I called the others.

Yes. A sound. Distant, soft—but real. Water.

“Running water,” Good whispered. “Come on.”

We moved forward. The sound grew louder—soon, unmistakable swirls, like a river racing through stone.

How could water flow underground?

We were close. Good swore he could smell it.

“Go slow,” Sir Henry said. “We’re near.”

Then—splash!

A cry.

Good had fallen in.

“Good! Where are you?”

A choked reply: “All right. Got hold of a rock. Light a match.”

I lit the last one.

In its dim glow, we saw dark water flowing at our feet. Wide, we couldn’t tell. Ahead—Good clinging to a rock.

“Stand clear,” he yelled. “I’m swimming.”

Splash. Struggle. Then—hands grasping. Sir Henry pulled him up.

“My word,” he gasped, “that was close. If I hadn’t caught that rock… it runs like a mill-race. No bottom.”

We dared not follow the riverbank—too dangerous in the dark. After resting, drinking sweet water, washing our faces, we turned back.

Good dripped ahead.

Then, a new gallery to the right.

“Might as well take it,” Sir Henry said wearily. “All paths are the same. We keep going till we collapse.”

We shuffled on, utterly drained.

I considered dropping the basket again. But no. Why not die rich?

Suddenly, Sir Henry stopped.

We bumped into him.

“Look,” he whispered. “Is my brain failing, or is that light?”

We stared.

Far ahead—a faint glimmer, no bigger than a windowpane. So dim, only eyes used to total darkness could see it.

Hope surged.

We pushed forward.

In minutes, it was undeniable. Light.

Then—air. Real air. Fresh, alive.

We stumbled onward.

The tunnel narrowed. Sir Henry knelt. Smaller. Smaller. Now only a foxhole-sized gap—earth, not rock.

A squeeze. A struggle.

And then—freedom.

Sir Henry emerged. Then Good. Then I, dragging the basket.

Above us—stars! And air, sweet and free, filling our lungs.

Then—crack! We rolled down a slope, tumbling through grass and roots. The basket snagged, and I stopped.

I sat up and shouted.

An answering cry from below—Sir Henry had landed safely.

We found Good stuck in a forked root. Bruised, shaken, but alive.

We sat together on the grass. The emotion was overwhelming. We laughed. We wept. We had escaped the tomb—nearly our grave.

Some higher power must have guided us to that jackal hole at the tunnel’s end.

And there—on the mountains—dawn blushed red, as if welcoming us back.

As light grew, we saw we were at the bottom of the great pit, opposite the cave entrance.

The Colossi loomed on the edge, silent sentinels.

Those endless tunnels—once part of the diamond mine, perhaps.

The underground river—its source, its course—unknown. And I don’t care to know.

Daylight brightened. We could see each other now.

Gaunt, hollow-eyed, filthy, bleeding, our faces etched with terror—yet Good’s eye-glass still sat firm in his eye. Never removed. Not even during the plunge, the fall, the roll.

Then we rose, fearing stiffness, and began the slow climb up the blue clay, using roots and grass for support.

I no longer thought of abandoning the basket. Nothing would separate us now.

At last, we reached the top—standing on the road, facing the huts.

A fire burned. Figures gathered.

We staggered toward them, supporting each other.

One figure rose—saw us—and fell to the ground, shrieking.

“Infadoos! Infadoos! It is you—come back from the dead!”

He rose, ran to us, shaking with joy.

“Oh, my lords! My lords! You’ve returned from the dead!”

And the old warrior threw himself at our feet, clasping Sir Henry’s knees, weeping aloud—for joy.
