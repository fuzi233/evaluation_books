I had barely set the last piece of my hand-thrown earthenware to cool when roiling black storm clouds rolled over the treetops, and gale-force winds screamed across the island, heralding the start of the brutal rainy winter season. The daily expeditions we had grown to love were immediately put on hold, and even inside our spacious, snug rock house, with all our crafts and projects to keep us occupied, the days stretched long and heavy. All of our moods sank under the constant drumming of rain on the rocks, and even the occasional wild gallop on horseback across half-dry stretches of savanna when the storm lulled could not lift our spirits for long.

Fritz noticed the slump as clearly as I did. “Father,” he said one evening, leaning in by the fire, “why can’t we build a canoe faster and lighter than the heavy, clunky craft we’ve got now? I keep daydreaming of a little skiff I can skim across the bay in like a seabird.”

The whole family lit up at the idea—all save my wife, who had never liked any trip out on the unpredictable water, and protested at once. “We already have a pinnace and two canoes, and more than enough risk of drowning when we take them out!” she said. “Why build another tiny, flimsy boat that will tempt you all to sail farther out than you should, chasing adventure?”

Her fears were easy to soothe. I told her we would not be building some thin, fragile cockleshell: I planned to copy the traditional Greenland kayak, a craft so sturdily framed and watertight it could survive rough open sea, and it would keep the boys busy and engaged for weeks. The work was underway before the week was out. We built a light, strong skeleton from scrap whalebone, reinforced the sides with split bamboo canes, and ran a full bamboo deck the full length of the craft, leaving only a single square hatch just big enough for one person to sit inside.

The work absorbed every last bit of our restless energy, so that by the time we sanded the last edge smooth, the rains had cleared completely, and bright, warm sunlight poured down over the island again. We hauled the finished frame out the front door (it was just narrow enough to squeeze through) and stretched cured seal skin tight over every inch of the sides and deck, sealing every seam with rubber sap to make it completely waterproof. The finished kayak was a strange, sleek little thing, so light a single person could lift it one-handed. When we launched it at the beach, it bounced on the waves like an inflatable rubber ball.

Fritz was unanimously declared its rightful owner—but Mother refused to let him climb into the tiny craft until she had finished a custom flotation suit for him. “If that little boat gets punctured by a sharp rock or the fin of a big fish and sinks out from under you,” she said, “you need something to keep you afloat.” The odd garment she sewed was a double-layer waistcoat, coated all over in rubber solution, every seam sealed tight, with one small valve to blow air between the two layers and inflate it. I thought the kayak was far sturdier than she feared, but I agreed to help her finish it anyway.

On the first calm afternoon, we all gathered on the beach to test it. Fritz pulled on the puffy suit, inflated it until he looked round as a balloon, and waded into the water to the raucous laughter of his younger brothers. He paddled so fast he crossed the whole bay to Shark Island before the rest of us could even climb into our larger boat to follow. The test was a complete success, and Ernest, Jack and Franz immediately stopped teasing him to beg Mother for their own matching inflatable suits.

While we were on Shark Island, we checked on the small animal outpost we had built there the previous autumn. All our domestic creatures were thriving, antelope tracks crisscrossed the ground around the shelter we had built for them, and after scattering handfuls of maize and salt for the herd, we wandered across to the far side of the island. The shore was strewn with bright, beautiful shells and delicate pieces of coral, which the boys stuffed straight into their museum sacks. Mother had them gather huge piles of colorful seaweed too, refusing to explain what she planned to do with the odd serrated-edged seaweed leaves she picked out, and drying them carefully in the oven when we got home, grinning and dodging my questions when I asked what they were for.

A few days later, when the boys and I stumbled back to the verandah exhausted after a long hike, Mother called us over holding a porcelain dish heaped with quivering, crystal-clear jelly that glittered in the sunlight. “A little treat for you,” she said. Fritz took the first bite and whooped that it was ambrosia. It was sweet, sharp with citrus, perfectly delicious, and Mother finally laughed and admitted it was the secret from her seaweed: she had learned to make it when she lived with Dutch settlers at the Cape, soaking the serrated seaweed leaves in fresh water for a week, then boiling them with orange juice, citron and sugar. The boys were so eager for more we set out for Shark Island the very next day.

All our plantations there were flourishing, the palms and mangroves had shot up at a fantastic rate, and random seeds we had tucked into rock crevices were already sprouting green over the bare stone. We even found a new sparkling fresh-water spring hidden in the rocks, its muddy edges crisscrossed with antelope footprints. Satisfied, we sailed over to Whale Island to check on our plantations there, and groaned when we saw the damage: the rabbit population had exploded, and they had gnawed every tender new tree shoot down to nubs, leaving only the coconut palms untouched. We quickly built little prickly thorn fences around every remaining tree trunk to keep the rabbits away, then paused by the great bleached whale skeleton we had found earlier, picked clean by seabirds and sun-bleached to brilliant white. The big, round whale vertebrae gave me a brilliant idea: they would make perfect heavy rollers for a grain crushing machine, no heavy stone required. I rolled a dozen of the big bones down to the boat to take home.

The next morning I set out alone to find a hardwood tree to cut the wooden support blocks for my new crusher, riding our stubborn buffalo Storm, since the boys had already snuck off on a hunting trip with their guns and traps. Halfway to the forest I found our entire cassava field completely trampled and eaten down by wild pigs. I tracked the herd for miles, until Floss and Bruno, our two hunting dogs, rounded them up and sent the whole pack of feral hogs grunting and snarling at bay. I fired twice, dropping two fat young porkers, and the rest of the herd fled. I slung the two pigs over Storm’s back, found the perfect hardwood tree, marked it, and headed for home.

Ernest, who had stayed behind at the house, helped me skin the young porkers, and by the time Mother was roasting them for supper, the rest of the boys came thundering back. Jack rode in first on their tamed ostrich, galloping at full tilt, with Fritz and Franz right behind him on their horses, their game bags bulging. They unloaded four wild birds, a full kangaroo, twenty musk-rats, a monkey, two hares, half a dozen beaver rats, and a duck-billed platypus. Jack was bouncing off the walls with excitement, ranting about how fast the ostrich ran, how the wind stung his eyes so bad he had to shut them half the time, begging me to make him a set of glass goggles so he could ride faster. I praised them for the great haul, but reminded them never to run off on a multi-hour expedition without letting us know, so we wouldn’t worry. They also brought back cuttings of cinnamon and sweet-apple trees for Mother’s garden, and a bundle of spiky thistles Fritz had found: they were fuller’s teazle, their sharp hooked thorns perfect for raising the nap on woven cloth.

We feasted on roast pork and spiced mead that night, but the next morning, staring at the huge pile of game hides waiting to be skinned, I decided to find a faster way than scraping every hide off by hand. I rummaged out a large surgeon’s syringe from the old ship’s medical chest, fitted two small valves inside the tube, and turned it into a powerful air pump. The boys howled with laughter when I told them I was going to skin the kangaroo with a “squirt”. I hung the kangaroo by its hind legs, made a tiny slit in the skin of one leg, pushed the nozzle of the pump inside, and forced air in between the skin and the flesh. The hide puffed up and separated completely from the meat in minutes, and I peeled it off clean in one go, with barely a knife cut. The boys were stunned, and we skinned every single one of the animals in a couple of hours flat, a job that would have taken us two full days by hand.

A few days later we felled our huge hardwood tree, sawed it into thick four-foot blocks, and built the whole grain crusher in no time at all using the whale vertebrae as heavy rollers. That evening we noticed all our chickens trotting home full to the brim, completely uninterested in their supper feed—we ran to the wheat field, and realized our grain had ripened completely, a hundredfold more than we had sown. Mother panicked at the sudden mountain of work: we had to reap the grain, thresh it, dig up all the potatoes and manioc, on top of all our usual fishing and preserving. I reassured her we’d use the fast, low-labor Italian farming method, no fancy work required. We flattened a stretch of hard clay into a smooth, rock-solid threshing floor, and went out to reap the wheat without stooping, just grabbing the grain heads in big handfuls and tossing them straight into the basket, wasting no time cutting long straw. Mother was horrified when she saw the tall stubble left in the field, but I told her we’d let the cattle graze on the leftover grain, then mow the regrowing grass and stalks for winter fodder.

We laid the small sheaves of wheat in a big circle on the threshing floor, and the boys mounted our four horses to ride them in circles over the grain, trampling all the kernels loose under their hooves. We didn’t even bother to stop the animals from nibbling at the grain as they worked, remembering the old rule: never muzzle the ox that treads out the corn. When all the grain was loosened, we tossed it high in the air with shovels when the sea breeze blew, and the wind carried all the light chaff away, leaving the heavy clean grain to fall on the floor. By the time we were done, we had harvested more than sixty, even a hundred times the amount of grain we had sown, our storehouse overflowing. We planted rye, barley and oats in the cleared field for our second harvest of the year, right before the next rainy season.

As we wrapped up all our farm work, the annual herring shoals arrived off the coast, and we pickled two full barrels of the fish for winter stores. Right after that, the seal herds turned up on the beaches, and we hunted a large number of them for their thick, durable skins to finish rigging out Fritz’s kayak. We added a small watertight storage compartment on the deck, big enough to hold pistols, ammunition, fresh water and trail rations, and hung two harpoons tied to seal-skin float bladders alongside the little craft, ready for all the adventures we had planned out on the open water.
