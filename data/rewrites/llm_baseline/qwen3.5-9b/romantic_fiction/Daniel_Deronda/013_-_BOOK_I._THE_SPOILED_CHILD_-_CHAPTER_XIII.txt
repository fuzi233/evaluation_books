"Philistia, be thou glad of me!"

Having decided to marry Miss Glanvil, Grandcourt displayed a remarkable talent for aligning his actions with his objectives. For the next two weeks, scarcely a day passed without him arranging a meeting or demonstrating, through emphatic attentions, that she occupied his thoughts. With his cousin, Mrs. Torrington, managing the household affairs, Mrs. Davilow and Gwendolen were invited to a grand gathering at Diplow. There, amidst many witnesses, the host lavished distinction upon the dowerless beauty while showing no concern for the heiress. The social world—including Mr. Gascoigne and all prominent families within visiting distance of Pennicote—grew increasingly confident in the matter. This growing assurance prompted the rector to resolve to perform his duty toward his niece, ensuring that any financial settlements were adequate. Indeed, the wonder shared by Mrs. Davilow and the rector was that an offer, for which so many suitable occasions had arisen, had not yet been made; Grandcourt himself harbored a portion of this surprise. Having told Lush of his resolution, he had expected the affair to conclude swiftly. To his own astonishment, he had repeatedly promised himself in the mornings that he would grant Gwendolen the opportunity to accept him that very day, only to find in the evenings that the necessary formalities remained uncompleted. This delay only hardened his determination for the future. He had never admitted to himself that Gwendolen might refuse him, yet—as heaven help us all!—we are often unable to act upon our certainties. Our aversion to a contrary outcome is so potent it rises like a spectral illusion between us and our truth. We are rationally sure that a blind worm cannot bite us mortally, yet the thought of being bitten is so intolerable, and the creature's gaze so threatening, that we refuse to handle it.

He requested permission to bring a magnificent horse for Gwendolen to ride. Mrs. Davilow was to accompany her in the carriage, and they were to travel to Diplow for lunch, with Grandcourt escorting them. It was a fine mid-harvest season, neither too hot nor too cool for a noonday ride of five miles to be delightful. Poppies glowed along the field borders; a gentle breeze moved softly through the uncut corn like a social spirit, casting clouds' shadows across the soft gray downs. Here stood the sheaves; there, horses strained their muscles under the final loads from vast stretches of stubble. Yet everywhere, the green pasture provided a broad backdrop for the cornfields, while cattle rested beneath wide branches. The road wound through countryside where dairy farms appeared much as they had in the days of our forefathers—a place where peace and permanence seemed to find a home, distant from the frantic changes signaled by the rushing railway trains in the distance.

However, the spirit of peace failed to penetrate poor Mrs. Davilow's mind, overcoming her habitual unease. Watching Gwendolen and Grandcourt canter ahead, then slow to a conversational walk until the carriage caught up, created a gratifying sight. Yet, it primarily fueled the conflict of hopes and fears regarding her daughter's fate. Here was an irresistible opportunity for a suitor to speak and end all uncertainties. Mrs. Davilow trembled, hoping fervently that Gwendolen's decision would be favorable. Certainly, if Rex's love had been repugnant to her, Mr. Grandcourt offered a stark contrast. That he had made a novel impression on her seemed evident in her marked restraint from satire, nay, her total silence regarding his character—a silence Mrs. Davilow dared not break. "Is he a man she would be happy with?" was the inevitable question in the mother's mind. "Well, perhaps as happy as she would be with anyone else—or as most other women are"—was the answer she used to quiet herself, for she could not imagine Gwendolen finding satisfaction in what we traditionally call "mean circumstances."

Grandcourt's thoughts ran in the same direction: he sought to end the uncertainty of his unsaid words. Any further hesitation was baseless, merely a quality in the air acting as an irritant to his desires.

Gwendolen enjoyed the ride, but her pleasure did not burst forth in the girlish, unpremeditated chatter and laughter she had shown that morning with Rex. She spoke a little, even laughed, but with the lightness of a far-off echo. For her, too, there was a peculiar quality in the air. She was certain it was not any subjugation of her will by Mr. Grandcourt, nor the splendid prospects he offered. Gwendolen desired everyone, including that dignified gentleman, to understand that she intended to do exactly as she liked, and that they should not calculate on her pleasing them. If she chose this husband, she wanted him to know she would not renounce her freedom, or, in her favorite formula, "not do as other women did."

Grandcourt's remarks that morning were, as usual, of that brief sort that never fails to command respect when the speaker holds importance in his circle. By stopping so soon, they hinted at a suppressed, formidable ability to say more, while possessing the merit of allowing others to speak longer.

"How do you like Criterion's paces?" he asked after they entered the park and slowed from a canter to a walk.

"He is delightful to ride. I should like to have a leap with him, if it wouldn't frighten mamma. There was a good wide channel we passed five minutes ago. I'd like to gallop back and take it."

"Pray do. We can take it together."

"No, thank you. Mamma is so timid—if she saw me, it might make her ill."

"Let me go and explain. Criterion would take it without fail."

"No—indeed—you are very kind—but it would alarm her too much. I dare take any leap when she isn't by; I do it and don't tell her."

"We can let the carriage pass and then set off."

"No, no, pray don't think of it any more: I spoke quite randomly," Gwendolen said, beginning to feel a new objection to carrying out her own proposal.

"But Mrs. Davilow knows I shall take care of you."

"Yes, but she would think of you as having to take care of my broken neck."

A considerable pause followed before Grandcourt looked toward her and said, "I should like to have the right always to take care of you."

Gwendolen did not turn her eyes to him. It seemed a long while before she first blushed, then turned pale, yet to Grandcourt's rapid judgment, she answered soon enough, with the lightest flute-tone and a careless movement of her head. "Oh, I am not sure that I want to be taken care of: if I chose to risk breaking my neck, I should like to be at liberty to do it."

She checked her horse as she spoke, turning in her saddle to look toward the approaching carriage. Her eyes swept across Grandcourt, but there was no language in them to correct the carelessness of her reply. At that very moment, she realized she was risking something—not her neck, but the possibility of finally halting Grandcourt's advances—and she did not feel content with that possibility.

"Damn her!" Grandcourt thought as he too checked his horse. He was not a wordy thinker, and this explosive phrase represented mixed impressions that eloquent interpreters might expand into sentences full of irritation that he was being mystified, coupled with a determination that this girl should not make a fool of him. Did she want him to throw himself at her feet and declare he was dying for her? Not by that gate could she enter the privileges he could offer. Or did she expect him to write his proposals? Equally a delusion. He would not make his offer in any way that placed him definitively in the position of rejection. As for her accepting him, she had effectively done so by accepting his marked attentions; anything that broke them off would be understood to her disadvantage. Was she merely coqueting?

However, the carriage arrived, and no further tête-à-tête could occur before reaching the house, where abundant company awaited. Clad in riding dress, hat aside, and bearing the reputation of being chosen by Mr. Grandcourt, Gwendolen became naturally a center of observation. Since the objectionable Mr. Lush was absent, this stimulus of admiring attention heightened her spirits and dispersed, for the time, the uneasy consciousness of divided impulses that threatened her with repentance. Whether Grandcourt was offended was impossible to judge: his manners remained unchanged, but Gwendolen's acumen had not penetrated deep enough to discern that his unchanged demeanor offered no clue, leaving her nonetheless afraid of him.

She had visited Diplow before only for dinner. Since certain views from the windows and gardens were worth seeing, Lady Flora Hollis proposed after luncheon, once some guests had departed and the sun was sloping toward four o'clock, that the remaining party explore the grounds. Frequent opportunities arose for Grandcourt to retain Gwendolen apart and speak unheard. But no! He spoke to no one else, and what he said was no more eager or intimate than during their first interview. He looked at her not less than usual; and with some of her defiant spirit returning, she looked full at him in return, not caring—rather preferring—that his eyes held no expression.
