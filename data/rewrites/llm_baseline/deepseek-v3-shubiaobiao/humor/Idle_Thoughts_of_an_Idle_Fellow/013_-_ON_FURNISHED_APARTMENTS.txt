"Oh! You’ve got rooms to let?"

"Mother!"

"Well, what is it?"

"There’s a gentleman about the rooms!"

"Ask ’im in. I’ll be up in a minute."

"Will yer step inside, sir? Mother’ll be up directly."

So you step inside, and after a minute, "mother" emerges slowly from the depths of the kitchen, untying her apron and shouting last-minute potato instructions to an unseen underling.

"Good morning, sir," she says with a smile that suggests she’s washed it too many times. "This way, please."

You protest: "Is it worth the climb? Just tell me—what are they like, and what’s the damage?"

But the landlady insists, so with a resigned sigh (meant to absolve you of any future complaints about wasted time), you trudge upstairs behind her. At the first landing, you kick over a pail, prompting a lecture on the laziness of modern servants and a bellowed summons for Sarah to remove the offending objects *immediately*.

Outside the rooms, she pauses, hand on doorknob, to explain that they’re "a bit untidy"—the last lodger only left yesterday, you see, and today is cleaning day (it always is). Then you enter, and together you survey the scene like two mourners at a funeral.

Furnished apartments in the harsh morning light are not a sight to lift the soul. They have the air of a discarded stage set—lifeless, hollow. It’s only when you’ve colonized them with your own chaos—your pipes strewn like fallen soldiers, your books in drunken piles, your mismatched slippers (one under the coal scuttle, the other atop the piano)—that they become home.

Ah, home! Where the walls are hidden behind familiar pictures, where the mantelpiece is a shrine to lost loves (their photographs smiling bravely through the dust), where the old screen—faded now, nibbled by moths—still stands guard, a relic of your mother’s girlhood. That screen! What a dazzling thing it must have been once, all tulips and roses bursting from a single stem. Now it’s gray and weary, like the hands that stitched it.

Does it remember? Does it recall the girl who bent over it, her laughter as bright as the thread she worked with? And was there ever a boy nearby—a boy who kept interrupting, who kept grabbing those small hands to kiss them, who kept tossing the screen aside in his eagerness? Could that boy—so wild, so ridiculous in his tight trousers and frilled shirt—really have become the stern-faced man you called "father"?

Old furniture holds such ghosts. It whispers stories if you listen. But lodging-house furniture? It’s a stranger, and like all strangers, it shows you its worst side first. The horsehair sofa stabs you with its knobs. The mirror is fogged with age. The carpet is frayed, the wallpaper a crime. The ceiling looks like a coffee-stained manuscript of life’s regrets. And the ornaments—those unspeakable ornaments!

Every lodging-house in Britain has the same hideous knickknacks. The pair of *things* on the mantel (glass danglers eternally on the verge of suicide), the china cow perpetually unsure if it’s a cow or a failed temple, the lump of dough masquerading as Cupid. Add a sampler stitched by a half-blind aunt, a print of *The Huguenots*, and a framed certificate celebrating Father’s vaccination (or was it his membership in the Odd Fellows?), and you have the full horror.

Then comes the rent.

"That’s steep," you say.

The landlady sighs. "To be honest, sir, I *used* to get [higher figure], and before that, [even higher figure]."

One shudders to think what rents were in the Golden Age of Lodgers. Young men of yesteryear must have been either wealthier or more reckless. I’d have been living in the attic.

Ah, the attic! The kingdom of the struggler. In lodgings, the higher you rise in life, the lower you descend in floors. You start in the garret and claw your way down to respectability. And what names have haunted those garrets! Poets, painters, philosophers—the world’s "lumber," stored out of sight until it blazed too bright to ignore.

Haydn, Chatterton, Addison, Goldsmith, Johnson, Dickens—all attic-dwellers. Hans Andersen dreamed there. Burns scribbled there. The garret is genius’s cradle, though a damned uncomfortable one.

Personally, I admire attics—in theory. For living? No. Too many stairs, too many head injuries, too many amorous cats on the roof. But for *thinking*? Give me a garret high above the city’s noise, where I can look down like a disdainful god on the ant-hill below.

The most memorable attic I ever endured was shared with a friend years ago. It defied geometry. Seven corners, walls that sloped like a drunkard’s logic, a window over the fireplace. The bed blocked the cupboard, so retrieving anything meant mountaineering over the mattress, with predictable results. Our coal lived in the cupboard’s depths. Every refill became an expedition, ending in a landslide of fuel, shovel, and curses onto the bed. We slept in a coal bunker and considered it normal.

The whole house was a joke. Doors opened outward, guaranteeing collisions. The ground floor belonged to another building entirely, so the front door dumped visitors straight down the cellar stairs. Nervous callers would vanish mid-greeting with a shriek, convinced they’d been ambushed.

I haven’t seen an attic in years. Life’s flavors haven’t changed much—whether sipped from crystal or chipped china. Happiness and heartache wear the same faces, whether you greet them on velvet or splintered wood. The only difference? In the attic, fried fish and stout were a feast. Now it takes a five-course dinner to stir the same joy.

Progress is overrated.
