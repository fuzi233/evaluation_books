Grandcourt, having resolved to digest the prospect of marrying Miss Harleth, demonstrated a master chef’s meticulous power of adapting raw ingredients to an ambitious menu. During the next fortnight, there was hardly a day on which he did not present himself, folding his emphatic attentions into the daily rhythm of her life like a rich, clarified butter worked into a delicate pastry dough. His cousin, Mrs. Torrington, was now managing the ancestral kitchens and dining halls of his house, allowing Mrs. Davilow and Gwendolen to be invited to a large party at Diplow. Here, amid the fragrant steam of roasted meats and the bright, herbal clatter of the feast, many witnesses could observe how the host distinguished the dowerless beauty. The local world—namely Mr. Gascoigne and all the families within the acoustic basin of Pennicote—felt an assurance on the subject which, in the rector’s mind, crystallized into a firm resolution to oversee the settlements with the exacting eye of an agrarian elder measuring the winter’s grain. 

Indeed, the wonder to the rector and Mrs. Davilow was that the offer had not already been served; Grandcourt himself shared in this mild astonishment. He had promised himself repeatedly that he would allow Gwendolen the opportunity of accepting him, yet evening would fall, the dining table would be cleared of its crumb-scattered linens, and the formality remained unaccomplished. He had never admitted that Gwendolen might refuse him, but the human palate’s objection to a bitter herb is so primal that it rises like a phantom astringency on the tongue before the root is even tasted; rationally, one knows a badly cured olive cannot kill, but the anticipated brine is so intolerable that one simply declines to handle the jar.

He had asked leave to have a beautiful horse of his brought for Gwendolen to ride. Mrs. Davilow was to follow in the carriage, the party making a five-mile pilgrimage to Diplow to partake of luncheon. It was a mid-harvest time, heavy with the mythic, circular rhythms of the generations. The agrarian elders were out in the fields, their scythes whispering through the ancient strains of uncut corn in a soundscape of profound, rhythmic peace. The poppies glowed vibrantly on the borders of the fields like spilled saffron, and the breeze carried the yeast-like, golden scent of the stubble, mingling with the olfactory baseline of damp earth and wild fennel. Cattle rested under the wide branches of oak trees that had shaded three centuries of herds. The road wound through dairy-farms operating much as they had in the days of their forefathers, maintaining a delicate acoustic ecology, a sanctuary of peace and permanence fiercely holding its ground against the jarring, industrial clatter of the railway train that occasionally shrieked in the distance, threatening to tear an iron gash through the valley’s sonic harmony.

But the spirit of this ancestral permanence did not penetrate poor Mrs. Davilow’s mind. Listening to the syncopated canter of Gwendolen and Grandcourt’s horses slackening into a conversational walk, she heard only the uneasy cadence of her own foreboding. The mother hoped with trembling that Gwendolen’s decision would be favorable. Grandcourt had produced a novel impression on the girl, evident in her marked abstinence from satirical observations—a sudden loss of her native dialect of rebellion. This silence between mother and daughter felt like the quiet tragedy of language death, an untranslatable gulf Mrs. Davilow dared not bridge. *Is he a man she would be happy with?* she wondered, trying to digest the thought. *As happy as most women are,* she fed herself, knowing Gwendolen possessed too acute a sensory hunger to ever find sustenance in what traditional society called “mean circumstances.”

Grandcourt’s own thoughts moved in tandem; he wished to be done with the unpalatable uncertainty of not having spoken. 

Gwendolen, for her part, enjoyed the riding, absorbing the rich olfactory landscape of crushed yarrow and warm horseflesh, but her pleasure did not break forth in the bright, unpremeditated chat of her earlier days. She laughed, but with a lightness resembling a hollow acoustic echo. There was a peculiar, oppressive quality in the air—a subtle power dynamic establishing itself. Gwendolen desired everyone to understand that she would not be consumed or molded. If she tasted this marriage, she would have him know she was not going to renounce the vibrant flavors of her own freedom.

Grandcourt’s speeches that morning were delivered in his usual brief, flattened register—a dominant lexicon that conserved its energy, leaving wide, echoing silences that forced others to fill the acoustic void. 

“How do you like Criterion’s paces?” he asked, after they entered the park, the gravel crunching percussively beneath the hooves.

“He is delightful to ride. I should like to have a leap with him, if it would not frighten mamma. There was a good wide channel we passed five minutes ago. I should like to have a gallop back and take it.”

“Pray do. We can take it together.”

“No, thanks. Mamma is so timid—if she saw me it might make her ill.”

“Let me go and explain. Criterion would take it without fail.”

“No—indeed—you are very kind—but it would alarm her too much. I dare take any leap when she is not by; but I do it and don’t tell her about it.”

“We can let the carriage pass and then set off.”

“No, no, pray don’t think of it any more: I spoke quite randomly,” Gwendolen said, her voice dropping a register as she felt a sudden aversion to her own recipe for thrill.

“But Mrs. Davilow knows I shall take care of you.”

“Yes, but she would think of you as having to take care of my broken neck.”

A thick, resonant pause settled between them before Grandcourt looked toward her. “I should like to have the right always to take care of you.”

Gwendolen did not turn her eyes. The silence stretched, a heavy pressure in her ears as she flushed and then paled. To Grandcourt, however, she answered soon enough, deploying a light flute-tone to mask her internal dissonance: “Oh, I am not sure that I want to be taken care of: if I chose to risk breaking my neck, I should like to be at liberty to do it.”

She checked her horse, turning in the saddle to look back at the carriage. Her eyes swept across Grandcourt, but the subjective terror of her experience remained inherently untranslatable to him. She knew she was risking the possibility of checking his advances, a prospect she found surprisingly unappetizing.

*Damn her!* thought Grandcourt. He was not a wordy man; this explosive internal phoneme stood for a complex stew of irritation. He felt he was being mystified by an inferior vocabulary. Did she expect him to write his proposals, or beg? He would not offer himself in any way that risked the sour taste of rejection. She had already accepted his marked attentions, practically partaking of his bread; was she merely coquetting?

The carriage caught up, preserving the social crust until their arrival at Diplow. Here, an abundant company awaited. Gwendolen, still in her riding-habit, became the natural center of observation. 

Luncheon was a meticulous, archival feast, deeply integrated into the estate’s domestic routines. There were tartlets filled with greengage plums preserved from the ancestral orchards, and a centerpiece of roasted venison, basted in clarified butter from heritage-breed cows and scented heavily with wild rosemary and woodsmoke. Gwendolen ate with the acute, passionate appreciation of a food historian, her palate finding profound meaning in the complex, mythic cycles of the soil and sustenance laid before her. Grandcourt, conversely, consumed the meal with the sterile efficiency of a man viewing the heritage purely as utilitarian fuel, indifferent to the gustatory poetry of the preparations. Yet the sheer scale of his culinary dominion heightened Gwendolen's spirits, temporarily dispersing her divided impulses. 

After the meal, when the sun began to slope toward four o’clock, casting long, golden shadows that felt like an extension of the harvest mythos, Lady Flora Hollis proposed an exploration of the grounds. Grandcourt had frequent opportunities to separate Gwendolen, yet he merely looked at her. Her defiant spirit returning, she looked back, her acoustic ecology steadying against the flatline of his unexpressive eyes. 

Eventually, the party stopped by the pool, amused by the dog Fetch retrieving a water lily. The splashing water provided a bright, natural overtone to the afternoon. It was here Grandcourt turned deliberately, fixing his eyes on a knoll artificially planted with exotic American shrubs that choked out the native flora.

“This is a bore,” he drawled, his voice a modern, commercial intrusion into the pastoral symphony. “Shall we go up there?”

“Oh, certainly—since we are exploring,” Gwendolen replied, pleased yet acutely afraid.

The path was too narrow for him to offer his arm, enforcing a stifling, silent ascent. When they reached the small platform at the summit, the air was deadened by the broad, unfamiliar leaves of the imported bushes. 

“There is nothing to be seen here,” Grandcourt said. “The thing was not worth climbing.”

Gwendolen did not laugh. She stood perfectly silent, holding the folds of her robe, gripping the handle of her whip—a tactile anchor in an environment that suddenly felt entirely stripped of her native resonance. 

“What sort of place do you prefer?” he asked.

“Different places are agreeable in their way. On the whole, I think, I prefer places that are open and cheerful. I am not fond of anything sombre.”

“Your place of Offendene is too sombre....”

“It is, rather.”

“You will not remain there long, I hope.”

“Oh, yes, I think so. Mamma likes to be near her sister.”

A short, breathless space of silence followed.

“It is not to be supposed that *you* will always live there, though Mrs. Davilow may.”

“I don’t know,” Gwendolen said, running on nervously, whipping the rhododendron bush so that the sharp, percussive snap of the leather broke the acoustic oppression. “We women can’t go in search of adventures—to find out the North-West Passage or the source of the Nile, or to hunt tigers in the East. We must stay where we grow, or where the gardeners like to transplant us. We are brought up like the flowers, to look as pretty as we can, and be dull without complaining. That is my notion about the plants; they are often bored, and that is the reason why some of them have got poisonous. What do you think?” 

“I quite agree. Most things are bores,” Grandcourt replied. Then, pausing, he resumed his refined, broken drawl—the sound of dominant power exerting its subtle gravity. “But a woman can be married.”

“Some women can.”

“You, certainly, unless you are obstinately cruel.”

“I am not sure that I am not both cruel and obstinate.” She turned her head and looked full at him. 

He stood perfectly still, a lotus-eater's stupor taking possession of his features. “Are you as uncertain about yourself as you make others about you?”

“I am quite uncertain about myself; I don’t know how uncertain others may be.”

“And you wish them to understand that you don’t care?” The new hardness in his tone was an acoustic strike, a sharp discord.

“I did not say that,” she hesitated, turning her eyes away to whip the bush again. She wished she were back on the horse, galloping through the wind that drowned out all human lexicons. 

“You do care, then,” he said, his drawl softening into an insidious, low frequency.

“Ha! my whip!” Gwendolen cried out in a little scream of distress. The gold-handled whip slipped from her grasp—propelled by an agitation she could not translate into words—and crashed forcefully through the foliage, lodging half-way down the knoll in an azalea. The physical sound of breaking branches shattered the tension. She ran down, laughing prettily, forcing Grandcourt to follow her lead to the level ground. She retrieved it before him, her color heightened with the exasperating brightness of a carried triumph, an overt display that Mrs. Davilow immediately noticed as they rejoined the group.

*It is all coquetting,* Grandcourt thought, observing her retreat. *The next time I beckon she will come down.* He anticipated this might happen the very next day at the projected archery picnic in Cardell Chase.

In Gwendolen’s mind, her subjugation to a possible, unpredictable self caused her deep acoustic and psychological dissonance. Her native key of life—doing as she liked—was failing her, drowned out by the dominant lexicon of Grandcourt’s wealth. Yet the prospect of marrying him held the rich, intoxicating aroma of a life she had only ever tasted in imagination. To command those sprawling, multi-generational domestic routines, the deep cellars, the endless pantries of power—it took hold of her senses. And Grandcourt? He was adorably quiet. He had been everywhere, a silent collector of the world’s sensory experiences, yet he seemed to enjoy nothing. His lack of gustatory and emotional appetite meant he would leave her a vast freedom to feast upon life herself. She believed she could manage his flavorless existence thoroughly.

Yet why was she benumbed by his silence? He was formidable—a handsome lizard of an unknown species, unreadable and untranslatable. Ignorance left a wide range of probabilities; perhaps this lizard was merely a suitable boudoir pet. He was so devoid of dramatic overtones that she could not imagine the preceding thirty-six years of his life. Had he ever tasted true passion? Such a vibrant flavor seemed entirely absent from the man who had come to Diplow to alter the epoch of her destiny. Her deliberate intention was still to accept him.

But on the carriage ride homeward, sitting with her back to the horses, the rhythmic clatter of hooves underscored her mounting anxiety. Her independence had stretched farther than necessary, and her own dialect of self-determination felt dangerously close to extinction.

Mrs. Davilow, observing the changeful, shifting light in her daughter’s eyes and her total silence, felt the psychological toll of the afternoon. The shaded, retired bit of road offered an acoustic haven, free from observers, smelling faintly of pine and cooling dust. 

“Something has happened, dear?” the mother asked tenderly, seeking to decode her daughter's silence.

Gwendolen roused herself to her physical senses, pulling off her gloves and hat so the soft breeze might wash over her head, reconnecting her to the natural soundscape. She met her mother’s eyes but offered no translation.

“Mr. Grandcourt has been saying something?—Tell me, dear,” she beseeched.

“What am I to tell you, mamma?” was the perverse, untranslatable answer.

“I am sure something has agitated you. You ought to confide in me, Gwen. You ought not to leave me in doubt and anxiety.” Tears welled in Mrs. Davilow’s eyes, mourning the erosion of their shared understanding.

“Mamma, dear, please don’t be miserable,” Gwendolen said pettishly. “It only makes me more so. I am in doubt myself.”

“About Mr. Grandcourt’s intentions?” 

“No; not at all,” Gwendolen clipped, tossing her head and replacing her hat.

“About whether you will accept him, then?”

“Precisely.”

“Have you given him a doubtful answer?”

“I have given him no answer at all.”

“He *has* spoken so that you could not misunderstand him?”

“As far as I would let him speak.”

“You expect him to persevere?” Mrs. Davilow received no answer to this, so she asked another: “You don’t consider that you have discouraged him?”

“I dare say not.”

“I thought you liked him, dear,” Mrs. Davilow offered timidly.

“So I do, mamma, as liking goes. There is less to dislike about him than about most men. He is quiet and *distingué*.” Gwendolen pouted, then suddenly recovered a flash of her native, mischievous cadence. “Indeed he has all the qualities that would make a husband tolerable—battlement, veranda, stable, etc., no grins and no glass in his eye.”

“Do be serious with me for a moment, dear. Am I to understand that you mean to accept him?”

“Oh, pray, mamma, leave me to myself,” Gwendolen distressed, the pressure of the dominant lexicon forcing her back into silence. 

Mrs. Davilow said no more. 

Upon reaching the house, Gwendolen found her sensory threshold overwhelmed and declared she would not dine, rejecting the evening sustenance entirely to rest in quiet isolation. She knew her uncle would inevitably hear a softened, generalized translation of the day's events. When the mother conveyed her anxious, untranslatable impressions to the rector, the patriarch's response was swift. To Mr. Gascoigne, the preservation of cultural and familial power required strict adherence to the dominant societal grammar; counter-judgments and subtle psychological distress were to him nothing more than fleeting shadows across the solid, agrarian earth to which he had long ago firmly adjusted himself.
