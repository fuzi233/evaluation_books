Letters of fire had recently burned themselves onto the church wall, asking with a look of genuine curiosity why no Mass was being celebrated for the Duke during this time of turmoil. Many had witnessed this supernatural protestation before it gradually faded, leaving the crowd staring in stunned silence. Ultimately, the city council, unwilling to lag behind divine power in their loyalty to a temporal ruler, generously funded the required services. From this arrangement, our old friend, the Cure, derived a substantial sum in hard cash—a fact he certainly appreciated given his refined tastes. Yet, I would not detain you with such a trivial occurrence as a minor miracle if Denys had not urgently requested his weapons.

"Why disgrace him as well as break his heart?" someone asked. "Then swear on the cross of your sword not to leave the Bastard's service until the sedition is crushed." Reluctantly yielding to necessity, Denys recited three solemn vows, thereby recovering his arms and his liberty.

The troops halted at an inn known as "The Three Fish." Upon seeing Denys, Marion exclaimed, "I'm out of luck; who would have thought to see you again?" Noticing his somber expression rather than amusement at her blunt greeting, she inquired what troubled him. He confessed his plight. Marion, however, adopted a bright outlook. Gerard was too handsome and well-mannered to suffer harm; moreover, the women would invariably support him. Things must either go well or ill for him. In the former case, he would join a noble company bound for Rome; in the latter, he would return home, perhaps arriving before his friend, for he had fighting to do in Flanders by all accounts. She then produced her gold pieces, steadfastly refusing to accept a single coin despite his repeated urging.

Denys was somewhat persuaded by her logic, partly because she aligned with his own desires and partly because her honesty cheered him. It made him reflect more kindly on the world in which his poor little friend wandered alone. Meanwhile, foot soldiers in small groups were already heading toward Flanders, many penniless, relying on bailiffs to secure food and lodging from innkeepers along the way. Anthony of Burgundy intercepted numerous stragglers, gathering them under his banner until he entered Flanders at the head of six hundred men. Crossing the frontier, he was met by his brother Baldwyn, who brought men, arms, and provisions. Anthony organized his entire force and marched through several towns in battle array, receiving great acclaim and facing no impediments.

This display of loyalty drew mixed reactions. "This rebellion of ours is a joke," growled a soldier named Simon, who had chosen to be Denys's companion. Denys remained silent, yet he made a private vow to Saint Mars to kill Anthony of Burgundy should this rebellion—which had cost him Gerard—prove to be nothing more than a sham.

That afternoon, they spotted a heavily fortified town. A whisper spread through the small army that it was a disaffected stronghold. However, upon arrival, the great gate stood wide open, and the flanking towers were manned by only a single sentinel each. The advancing force relaxed their formation and marched carelessly. Just as they approached within a furlong, the drawbridge across the moat rose slowly and creakingly until it stood vertically against the fortress. The very moment it settled into this defensive position, the portcullis slammed down at the gate, and the towers and curtain walls bristled with lances and crossbows. A stern murmur rippled through the Bastard's front lines and spread to the rear.

"Halt!" Denys cried. The command echoed down the line, and they stopped. "Herald to the gate!" A pursuivant spurred out, halted twenty yards from the entrance, raised his bugle with the herald's flag trailing behind it, and blew a summons. A tall figure in brazen armor appeared over the gate. Fiery words exchanged between him and the herald were inaudible, yet their meaning was clear: the herald blew a single, keen, threatening note at the walls and galloped back with war in his eyes.

The Bastard stepped out of the line to meet him. Their heads had not been together for two seconds before the stranger turned in his saddle and shouted, "Pioneers, to the van!" Instantly, hedges were leveled, the force took the field, and encamped just out of range of the walls. Mounted officers flew south, east, and west to friendly towns to gather catapults, palisades, mantelets, raw hides, tar barrels, carpenters, provisions, and all materials necessary for a siege.

This bright prospect greatly cheered the drooping spirits of the soldiers. At the first clang of the portcullis, eyes brightened and temples flushed. When the herald returned with battle in his gaze, the effect was immediate; for the first time in many days, a soldier cried out, "Courage, everyone, the devil is dead!" If that great warrior heard, he surely would have grinned.

The besiegers established camp a furlong from the walls and began constructing roads. Pikemen remained in camp ready for assault, while sappers, pioneers, catapultiers, and crossbowmen moved forward. They opened the siege by filling the moat, mining, and attempting to breach the walls. Since much of this work had to be done under heavy fire from arrows, quarrels, bolts, stones, and rocks, these artisans needed a hundred eyes. They operated with a vigilance, individual intelligence, daring, and skill that made the siege fascinating, even amusing, to observers.

First, they advanced their carpenters behind rolling mantelets to erect a high, strong stockade on the very edge of the moat. Some lives were lost, but not many, thanks to a strong force of crossbowmen—including Denys—who rolled their mantelets up and rained shots over the workers' heads at every defender showing his nose or opening a loophole. Protected by this concentrated fire, they soon raised a high palisade between themselves and the ordinary missiles from the pierced masonry.

However, the besieged anticipated this. At night, they ran out wooden penthouses atop the curtains. The curtains featured square holes near the top designed to receive beams supporting these structures—the true defense of medieval forts. From here, the defenders delivered missiles with far greater freedom and range than could be achieved through the oblique, immovable loopholes of the curtain or the sloping crenelets of the higher towers. In response, the besiegers brought up mangonels to hurl huge stones at these woodworks, battering them to pieces. Simultaneously, they constructed a triangular wooden tower as high as the curtain, keeping it ready just out of shot. This was a terrifying sight for the defenders.

These wooden towers had captured many towns. The besiegers began mining beneath the section of the moat where the tower loomed, preparing a warm reception. They also mined elsewhere, aiming to undermine the square barbican and bring it crashing down. Throughout this, Denys stayed behind his mantelet alongside another arbalestrier, protecting the workmen and taking excellent shots. These actions eventually earned him the respect of an unseen archer, who periodically sent a winged compliment quivering into his mantelet.

One arrow struck within an inch of the narrow slit through which Denys was peering. "Peste!" he cried. "You shoot well, my friend. Come forth and receive my congratulations! Shall merit such as yours hide its head? Comrade, it is one of those cursed Englishmen with his half-ell shaft. I won't die until I've had a shot at London Wall."

On the side of the besieged, a figure soon attracted great attention by promenading under fire. It was a tall knight clad in complete brass, carrying a light but prodigiously long lance, which he used to direct the movements of the defenders. Whenever disaster befell the besiegers, this tall knight and his long lance were invariably involved.

"My young reader will ask, 'Why did not Denys shoot him?' Denys did shoot him; every day of his life. Other arbalestriers shot him; archers shot him. Everyone shot him. He was there to be shot, seemingly. But the abomination was that he did not mind being shot. Worse still, he became so demoralized he seemed unaware when he was hit. He walked his battlements under fire, like a sturdy captain pacing his deck in a storm, calmly oblivious to the April rain falling on his woolen armor."

Finally, the besiegers grew spiteful and refused to waste good steel on him, cursing his impenetrable coat of mail instead. He took those missiles like the rest. Gunpowder has since ruined warfare; war was always detrimental to humanity's solid interests. But in old times, it served a purpose: it painted well, sang divinely, and furnished Iliads. Invisible butchery under a pall of smoke a furlong thick leaves no one better off. A poet with a notebook may repeat, *"Suave etiam belli certamina magna tueri"* (It is pleasant even to behold the great contests of war), but the sentiment is hollow and smells of cuckoo. One cannot *tueri* (behold) anything but a horrid row. He didn't say, *"Suave etiam ingentem caliginem tueri per campos instructam"* (It is pleasant to behold vast darkness spread over fields). They managed better in the Middle Ages.

This siege was a small affair, yet a writer or minstrel could witness it and earn an honest penny by singing of it; thus, the sport was reasonable and served an end.
