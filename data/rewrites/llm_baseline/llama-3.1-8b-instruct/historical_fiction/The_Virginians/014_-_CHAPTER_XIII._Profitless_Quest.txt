As Harry Warrington made his way towards Pennsylvania, the tales of the British debacle grew louder and more vivid. Two storied regiments, forged in the fires of Scotland and Europe, had fled before an unseen foe, their vaunted discipline and valor proving no match for a ragtag band of warriors and a handful of French infantry. The ill-fated commander had shown unwavering bravery and resolve, his horse shot from under him four times, twice wounded, and finally succumbing to a mortal blow three days later. Harry's informants painted a gruesome picture of the battle – the river crossing, the long march through the wilderness, the withering fire from the front, the futile attempts to push forward, and the cannon's desperate attempts to clear a path through the enemy lines. Then the ambush, a fusillade that cut down nearly half the expeditionary force, hiding behind every bush and tree. Yet, not all of the general's retinue had fallen. Harry learned that one of his aides-de-camp, a Virginian gentleman, languished with fever and exhaustion at Dunbar's camp.

One of them, but which? Harry hastened to the camp, and after a long journey, he found George Washington, not his brother, stretched out in a tent. A pang of sorrow pierced Mr. Washington's heart as he beheld Harry, and he confessed that he had no news of George. Mr. Washington hesitated to reveal the truth, for three days after the battle, his duty had been to stay close to the general. On that fateful 9th of July, he had watched as George rode to the front with orders from the commander, never to return. After Braddock's death, the aide-de-camp had retraced his steps to the battlefield, where he found the corpses brutally mutilated and stripped. He buried one body, which he believed to be George's, and his own illness deepened, perhaps triggered by the anguish of searching for the missing young soldier.

"Oh, George!" Harry exclaimed, his voice cracking with desperation. "If you had loved him, you would have found him dead or alive." He was insistent that he, too, should investigate the battlefield. With coin, he hired guides to accompany him. He forded the river at the point where the army had crossed, scouring the field from one end to the other.
