The sunlit lawn stretched out like a ribbon of emerald, leading directly to the shimmering expanse of the sea. Along its edges, scarlet geraniums and deep-red coleus formed a vivid border, while cast-iron vases painted in rich chocolate brown stood at intervals along the winding path—each overflowing with trailing petunias and ivy geraniums that spilled over the neatly raked gravel like living lace.

Halfway between the cliff’s edge and the square wooden house—also chocolate-colored, but with a verandah roof striped in yellow and brown to mimic an awning—two large archery targets were set against a backdrop of dense shrubbery. Opposite them, facing the mark, was a real canvas tent, surrounded by garden benches and chairs. A gathering of ladies in light summer dresses and gentlemen in grey frock coats and tall hats stood or sat on the lawn, chatting softly. Every few moments, a slender girl in crisp white muslin would emerge from the tent, bow in hand, and launch her arrow toward the target, drawing quiet attention from the crowd.

Newland Archer stood on the verandah of the house, watching the scene unfold below. On either side of the polished steps, bright yellow stands held large blue porcelain flowerpots, each filled with a spiky green plant. Beneath the verandah, a wide border of blue hydrangeas edged with red geraniums ran along the ground. Behind him, through the French windows he had just passed, lace curtains swayed gently, framing glimpses of glass parquet floors dotted with chintz poufs, tiny armchairs, and velvet tables adorned with silver trinkets.

The Newport Archery Club always hosted its August meeting at the Beauforts’. Though once rivaling croquet as the season’s favorite pastime, archery was now slowly giving way to lawn tennis. Yet tennis, still considered too rough and informal for refined society, left archery as the preferred spectacle—one where elegance could be displayed through graceful posture and fashionable attire.

Archer gazed down at the familiar ritual with a sense of detachment. It amazed him how life carried on as if nothing had changed, while inside himself, everything had shifted. Newport had been the first place to make that clear. In New York, during the winter, after he and May had settled into their new greenish-yellow home with its bow window and Pompeian vestibule, he’d slipped back into his old routine—the office, the daily work—at first with relief. That steady rhythm had felt like a lifeline to the man he used to be.

Then came the excitement of selecting a striking grey carriage horse for May’s brougham (a gift from the Wellands), and the satisfaction of designing his library exactly as he’d imagined: dark embossed wallpaper, Eastlake bookcases, and "sincere" furniture. At the Century Club, he’d found Winsett again; at the Knickerbocker, the young men of his circle. Between law, dinners, home entertaining, and occasional evenings at the opera or theater, his life seemed solid, predictable—even real.

But Newport offered escape from duty, a world entirely devoted to leisure. He’d tried to convince May they should spend the summer on Mount Desert Island—a remote stretch off the Maine coast, where hardy Bostonians and Philadelphians lived in “native” cottages amid woods and water, far from polite society. But the Wellands always went to Newport, and as their son-in-law, he had no good reason to resist. Mrs. Welland had pointed out, with mild sharpness, that it wouldn’t do for May to wear herself out trying on clothes in Paris only to never wear them. And Archer had no rebuttal.

May couldn’t understand his reluctance. She reminded him that he’d always loved Newport in his bachelor days—and since that was true, he could only say he expected to enjoy it more now, being there with her. But standing on the Beaufort verandah, looking out at the animated lawn, he felt a sudden chill. He realized, with quiet dread, that he wouldn’t enjoy it at all.

It wasn’t May’s fault. Poor dear. When they traveled, they sometimes drifted slightly apart—but returning to familiar surroundings always brought them back into harmony. He’d known she wouldn’t disappoint him. And she hadn’t. He’d married her, like so many young men did, because she was charming, and because he’d reached the end of aimless romantic adventures, weary of fleeting passions. She represented peace, stability, companionship—the comfort of knowing one’s responsibilities.

He couldn’t say he’d made a mistake. She had fulfilled every expectation. To be married to one of New York’s most admired and beloved young women—graceful, sweet-tempered, reasonable—was undeniably rewarding. And Archer had never been blind to such advantages. As for the brief madness that had seized him before the wedding—the thought of marrying the Countess Olenska—he’d trained himself to see it as a relic of youth, a discarded dream. The idea that he might ever, in his right mind, have considered such a union now seemed absurd. She remained, in memory, not a woman, but a ghost—poignant, melancholy, haunting.

Yet these thoughts, these dismissals, left his mind hollow, echoing. And perhaps that was why the cheerful, bustling people on the lawn unsettled him—as if children were playing in a graveyard.

A rustle of silk drew his attention. The Marchioness Manson fluttered out of the drawing-room window, resplendent as ever. Her limp Leghorn hat was anchored by layers of faded gauze, and a tiny black velvet parasol, balanced absurdly on a carved ivory handle, rested over the brim.

"My dear Newland," she trilled, "I had no idea you and May had arrived! You came only yesterday, you say? Ah, business—professional duties—I understand. Many husbands find it impossible to join their wives here except for the weekend." She tilted her head, squinting at him with theatrical languor. "But marriage is one long sacrifice, as I often told my Ellen—"

Archer’s heart gave that same strange lurch it had once before, as if a door had slammed shut between him and the world. But it passed quickly. He heard Medora answer a question he must have asked aloud.

"No, I’m not staying here," she said. "I’m with the Blenkers, in their delightful solitude at Portsmouth. Beaufort kindly sent his famous trotters this morning, so I could catch a glimpse of Regina’s garden party. But tonight I return to country life. The Blenkers—those original souls—have rented an old farmhouse where they gather representative people..." She lowered her head beneath her wide brim, blushing faintly. "This week Dr. Agathon Carver is holding Inner Thought meetings there. A stark contrast to this worldly amusement—but then, I’ve always lived on contrasts! To me, the only death is monotony. I always tell Ellen: Beware of monotony—it’s the mother of all deadly sins. But my poor child is going through a phase of exaltation, of disdain for the world. You know, I suppose, she’s declined all invitations to stay in Newport—even with her grandmother Mingott? I barely managed to persuade her to come with me to the Blenkers’! The life she leads is morbid, unnatural. Ah, if only she’d listened when the door was still open... But shall we go down and watch this match? I hear your May is competing."

Beaufort approached from the tent, striding across the lawn—tall, heavy, tightly buttoned into a London frock coat, with one of his own orchids pinned to the lapel. Archer, who hadn’t seen him in months, noticed the change. In the summer heat, Beaufort’s complexion looked swollen, bloated. Without his proud bearing, he might have seemed merely fat and overdressed.

Rumors about Beaufort circulated freely. Earlier that year, he’d sailed to the West Indies on his new steam yacht—built in the Clyde, equipped with tiled bathrooms and other luxuries—said to have cost half a million. Upon his return, he’d presented his wife with a magnificent pearl necklace, the kind of grand gesture meant to silence scandal. His fortune was strong enough to absorb such extravagances, yet whispers persisted—not only in Fifth Avenue, but in Wall Street. Some said he’d lost money in railroads; others claimed he was being drained by a particularly insatiable woman. Each rumor of financial trouble was met with another act of excess: a new row of orchid houses, a fresh string of racehorses, or a new Meissonnier or Cabanel added to his gallery.

Now he stepped forward with his usual half-sneering smile. "Hullo, Medora! Did the trotters do their job? Forty minutes, eh? Not bad, considering your nerves needed protecting." He shook hands with Archer, then turned and placed himself beside Mrs. Manson, murmuring something low and private that she didn’t catch.

She responded with one of her peculiar foreign gestures and a "_Que voulez-vous?_" which deepened Beaufort’s frown. But he managed a courteous smile as he glanced at Archer and said, "You know May’s going to win the prize."

"Ah, then it remains in the family," Medora chimed, and at that moment they reached the tent, where Mrs. Beaufort greeted them in a cloud of mauve muslin and floating veils.

May Welland was just stepping out. Dressed in white, with a pale green ribbon at her waist and a wreath of ivy on her hat, she had the same ethereal grace she’d shown on the night of her engagement. In the time since, not a single thought seemed to have stirred behind her eyes, not a feeling crossed her heart. Though Archer knew she was capable of both, he marveled anew at how effortlessly experience slipped away from her.

She held bow and arrow. Stepping onto the chalk line, she raised the bow to her shoulder and took aim. The pose was so perfectly classical that a murmur rose among the onlookers. Archer felt a familiar warmth—the pride of ownership—that often tricked him into believing he was content.

Her rivals—Mrs. Reggie Chivers, the Merry girls, various Thorleys, Dagonets, and Mingotts—stood behind her, a lovely cluster of anxious beauty, golden hair and rosy cheeks mingling with pale muslins and floral hats like a soft rainbow. All were young and lovely, glowing in the summer light. But none possessed the effortless elegance of his wife, whose muscles tensed, brow furrowed, as she focused her entire being on the task.

"Gad," Lawrence Lefferts muttered, "not one of them holds the bow like she does."

Beaufort replied, "Yes, but that’s the only kind of target she’ll ever hit."

Archer felt a surge of irrational anger. A husband should want to hear such praise. That a coarse man saw her as lacking appeal only proved her refinement. Yet the words sent a shiver through him. What if such perfection were simply emptiness? What if “niceness,” taken to its highest degree, was merely a veil over nothing?

As May returned, flushed but calm, after hitting the bull’s-eye, he realized he had never truly lifted that curtain.

She accepted congratulations with her usual simplicity—the kind that made jealousy impossible. No one could resent her triumph, because she seemed equally serene whether she won or lost. But when her eyes met his, her face lit up with the pleasure of seeing his joy.

Mrs. Welland’s pony carriage waited. They drove off among the dispersing vehicles, May guiding the reins, Archer seated beside her.

The afternoon sun still lingered on the lawns and hedges. Along Bellevue Avenue, a double line of victorias, dog-carts, landaus, and vis-à-vis carriages rolled past—well-dressed people returning from the garden party or heading home after their daily drive along the Ocean Drive.

"Shall we visit Granny?" May suddenly asked. "I’d like to tell her myself I won the prize. There’s plenty of time before dinner."

Archer agreed, and she turned the ponies down Narragansett Avenue, across Spring Street, and out toward the rocky moorland beyond.

In this less fashionable district, Catherine the Great—always independent and frugal—had built herself a many-peaked, cross-beamed *cottage-orné* on cheap land overlooking the bay. Here, among stunted oaks, her verandahs spread above island-studded waters. A winding drive led up between iron stags and blue glass balls embedded in mounds of geraniums to a front door of highly varnished walnut under a striped roof. Inside, a narrow hall with a black-and-yellow star-patterned floor opened into four small rooms with heavy flock-paper walls and ceilings painted by an Italian artist with gods and goddesses of Olympus.

One room had become a bedroom when Mrs. Mingott’s health declined. The adjacent one was where she spent her days—seated in a large armchair between open door and window, perpetually waving a palm-leaf fan, its motion so distant from her body that it stirred only the fringe of anti-macassars on the chair arms.

Since she had helped bring about his marriage, Mrs. Mingott had treated Archer with warm cordiality. She believed his haste stemmed from uncontrollable passion—and since she admired impulsiveness (so long as it didn’t cost money), she always welcomed him with a twinkle and playful innuendos that May, fortunately, never caught.

Now she examined the diamond-tipped arrow pinned to May’s dress, remarking that in her day a filigree brooch would have sufficed, but that Beaufort certainly did things with style.

"Quite an heirloom, really, my dear," she chuckled. "You must leave it to your eldest daughter." She pinched May’s arm, watching the color rise. "Well, well—what have I said to make you flush like that? No daughters? Only boys? Good heavens—look at her blushing again! What—can’t I say that either? Mercy me—when my children beg me to paint out those gods and goddesses overhead, I always say I’m too grateful to have someone around whom *nothing* can shock!"

Archer laughed, and May joined in, crimson to the eyes.

"Now, tell me all about the party," the old lady continued. "I’ll never get a straight word from that silly Medora." When May exclaimed, "Cousin Medora? But I thought she was going back to Portsmouth?" Mrs. Mingott answered calmly, "So she is—but she has to stop here first to pick up Ellen. Ah—you didn’t know Ellen came to spend the day with me? Such nonsense, her refusing to come for the summer. But I gave up arguing with young people fifty years ago. Ellen—_Ellen_!" she called sharply, trying to lean forward to see the lawn beyond the verandah.

No answer. Mrs. Mingott tapped impatiently with her cane on the shiny floor. A mulatto maid in a bright turban reported that she’d seen "Miss Ellen" walking down the path toward the shore.

"Run down and fetch her," Mrs. Mingott said to Archer. "This pretty lady will describe the party to me."

And Archer stood up—as if waking from a dream.

He’d heard the name Countess Olenska often since their last meeting. He knew the details of her life: how she’d spent the previous summer in Newport, entered society, then abruptly sublet her perfect house and moved to Washington. There, during the winter, he’d heard of her—like any pretty woman in Washington—shining in diplomatic circles, praised for her charm and intelligence. He’d listened to reports of her appearance, conversation, tastes—with the detached interest one gives to a story of someone long gone.

Not until Medora mentioned her name at the archery match had Ellen Olenska become real again. The Marchioness’s careless lisp had conjured up the little fire-lit drawing-room, the sound of wheels retreating down a deserted street. He remembered a tale of Tuscan peasants lighting straw in a cave, revealing ancient images in their tombs...

The path to the shore descended from the bank to a walk above the water lined with weeping willows. Through their branches, Archer saw the glint of Lime Rock, its whitewashed turret and tiny house where Ida Lewis, the heroic lighthouse keeper, lived her final years. Beyond lay Goat Island, the bay stretching northward in golden haze to Prudence Island and the faint shores of Conanicut.

From the willow path projected a small wooden pier ending in a pagoda-like summerhouse. And there, leaning against the railing, her back to the shore, stood a woman.

Archer stopped as if struck awake. That vision was a dream. The reality was waiting above: Mrs. Welland’s pony carriage circling the oval, May under the Olympian gods, glowing with quiet hope; the Welland villa at the end of Bellevue Avenue; Mr. Welland already dressed, pacing the drawing-room, watch in hand, with dyspeptic impatience—because in that house, one always knew what was happening at any given hour.

"What am I?" Archer thought. "A son-in-law?"

The figure at the end of the pier hadn’t moved. For a long moment, he stood halfway down the hill, watching the bay, crisscrossed by sailboats, yachts, fishing boats, and coal barges pulled by noisy tugs. The woman in the summerhouse seemed fixed by the same view. Beyond Fort Adams, the sunset split into a thousand flames. A catboat’s sail caught the light as it beat out through the channel between Lime Rock and the shore.

Archer remembered the scene in *The Shaughraun*, Montague lifting Ada Dyas’s ribbon to his lips without her knowing he was in the room.

"She doesn’t know—she hasn’t guessed. Would I know if she came up behind me?" he wondered. Then, suddenly: "If she doesn’t turn before that sail crosses the Lime Rock light, I’ll go back."

The boat glided out on the receding tide. It passed Lime Rock, obscured Ida Lewis’s house, crossed the turret where the light hung. Archer waited until a wide space of sparkling water lay between the island and the boat’s stern. Still, the figure did not move.

He turned and walked up the hill.

"I'm sorry you didn't find Ellen," May said as they drove home through the dusk. "I’d liked to see her again. But perhaps she wouldn’t have cared—she seems so changed."

"Changed?" Archer echoed, voice flat, eyes fixed on the ponies’ twitching ears.

"So indifferent to her friends," May said. "Giving up New York, her house—spending her time with such odd people. How uncomfortable she must be at the Blenkers’! She says she does it to keep cousin Medora out of trouble—to prevent her marrying dreadful men. But sometimes I think we’ve always bored her."

Archer said nothing.

She continued, with a note of hardness he’d never heard before: "After all, I wonder if she wouldn’t be happier with her husband."

He burst out laughing. "_Sancta simplicitas!_" he exclaimed. Seeing her puzzled frown, he added, "I don’t think I ever heard you say anything cruel before."

"Cruel?"

"Well—watching the contortions of the damned is supposed to be a favorite sport of angels. But even they don’t believe people are happier in hell."

"It’s a pity she ever married abroad then," May said, in the calm tone her mother used when addressing Mr. Welland’s quirks. And Archer felt quietly dismissed—as an unreasonable husband.

They drove down Bellevue Avenue and turned in between the chamfered wooden gateposts topped with iron lamps marking the approach to the Welland villa. Lights glowed through the windows. As the carriage stopped, Archer caught a glimpse of his father-in-law—exactly as he’d imagined him—pacing the drawing-room, watch in hand, wearing the expression of pained impatience he’d long since learned was more effective than anger.
