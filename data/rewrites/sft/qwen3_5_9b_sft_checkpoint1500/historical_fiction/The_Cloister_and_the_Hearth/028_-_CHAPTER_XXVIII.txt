A curious equilibrium governs the world, balancing good against evil with a singular, almost universal precision. It was the steel bow strapped to the arbalestrier's back—a burden that had nearly claimed his life—that now served as Gerard's salvation. Suspended above the water, the weapon had drawn the drowning soldier down, yet its position rendered it impossible for anyone else to grasp. Gerard seized it instantly, one hand clutching the shaft, the other wedging firmly between the bowstring and the limb, securing his grip as surely as any master craftsman. With a desperate, jerking motion, Denys was hauled upward, half his torso breaking the surface before the current could claim him again.

"Now, grip me not!" Gerard cried, his voice trembling with the terror of having made a fatal error. "Grip me not!"

"Pas si bete," Denys gurgled, the words bubbling through his gasping lungs.

Instantly, however, Gerard's countenance shifted from panic to a steely resolve. "Lie flat upon thy back," he commanded sharply. Seizing the heavy arbalest, he used it to pull himself forward, creating a bridge for his companion. "Place thy hand upon my shoulder! Beat the water with the other—no, strike downwards! Do exactly as I bid thee." Grasping Denys's long, sodden hair, Gerard twisted it tightly, biting down on the strands to anchor himself, while the powerful muscles of his youth strained against the weight. He propelled them both, striking out vigorously against the torrent. For a fleeting moment, hesitation plagued him regarding which bank to aim for; he knew little of the terrible consequences of such a choice. Yet, spotting the western shore slightly closer, he directed his course toward it rather than toward prison, sparing himself a scandalous capture. Driven by the fierce current, they angled downstream, covering nearly a hundred yards until Denys, buoyed by exhaustion, muttered uneasily, "How much longer?"

"Hold fast," Gerard mumbled, his voice steady despite the strain. "Whatever a duck knows, a Dutchman knows; thou art safe as in thy bed."

Suddenly, the depths yielded to shallow mud, and they waded onto the firm ground of the bank. Upon stepping onto terra firma, they stared at one another from head to toe, eyes wide with shock, as if trying to devour each other with a single gaze. Then, by a shared, instinctive impulse, they flung arms around each other's necks, panting heavily, hearts overflowing with a silence too profound for speech. In this sacred moment, life tasted sweeter than heaven, especially for the exiled lover who had just saved the soul of his friend. What heights of joy have poets yet ascended, or dared attempt? To save a human life—and specifically, a beloved friend—is a reward worth three score years and ten. Calmer now, they clasped hands, strolling along the riverbank like a pair of sweethearts, scarcely aware of their destination or the path beneath their feet.

Meanwhile, the boatmen remained safe upon their vessel, Herr Turnip-face, the "Inverter of things," sitting centrally. The chaos that had erupted on the water had failed to disturb his habitual composure. As he watched them shoot back into the Rhine, and later greet them upon their return, he maintained an air of detached amusement.

"Shall we wait for them to right the boat?" Denys asked.

"No," Gerard replied briskly. "Our fare is paid; we owe them naught. Let us depart."

Denys assented, noting that they could traverse the entire distance to Cologne along this bank.

"I fare not to Cologne," Gerard answered calmly.

"Why, whither then?"

"To Burgundy."

"To Burgundy? Ah, no! That is too good to be true."

"It is truth, coupled with sense. What matters to me the route to Rome?"

"Nay, nay," Denys protested gently. "Thou speakest thus merely to please me. The change is too sudden. Think me not so ill-hearted as to accept thy word blindly. Did I not see thine eyes sparkle at the wonders of Cologne? The churches, the images, the relics..."

"How dull art thou, Denys," Gerard interrupted with a smile. "That was when we were meant to enjoy them together. Churches! I shall see plenty, wherever I go toward Rome. There are bones of saints and martyrs aplenty; alas, the world is full of them. But where on earth's face shall I find another friend like thee? Nay, I will not drive thee further from the road leading to thy dear home, nor will she pine in vain. Nor will I deprive myself of thy company by sending thee away. Since I drew thee from the Rhine, I love thee more deeply than before. Thou art my pearl; I fished thee, and I must keep thee. Gainsay me not, lest thou bring back my fever. Cry courage, lead on, and hey for Burgundy!"

Denys threw up his arms in a joyful caper. "Courage! Va pour la Bourgogne! Oh, soyes tranquille! Cette fois, il est bien décidément mort, ce coquin-là." And turning their backs upon the Rhine, they marched away.

As this decision crystallized, a commotion stirred among the small group watching from the opposite bank. They had not taken many steps before a voice boomed across the water. "HALT!"

Gerard turned, recognizing one of the four figures holding aloft a badge of office and a parchment scroll. His heart sank; he was a citizen of good standing, accustomed to yielding to the voice of the Law that now commanded him to return to Düsseldorf.

Denys, however, shared none of his scruples. A Frenchman by birth, he despised the nations, laws, inhabitants, and customs of others. A soldier by trade, he viewed the situation through a martial lens: superior force opposed, a river as a barrier, and the rear unguarded. Retreat was easily made. Seeing at a glance that the boat still drifted in mid-stream and that no ferry existed nearer than Düsseldorf, he declared, "I shall make a quick retreat to that hill, and once out of sight, I shall move with speed."

They sauntered off casually.

"Halt! In the bailiff's name!" cried a voice from the shore.

Denys spun around, ostentatiously snapping his fingers at the bailiff, and continued his pace.

"Halt! In the archbishop's name!"

Denys snapped his fingers at the prelate and moved on.

"Halt! In the emperor's name!"

With a final, defiant snap of his fingers addressed to the sovereign, Denys proceeded.

Gerard watched this unnecessary pantomime with regret. As soon as they passed the brow of the hill, he declared, "There is now but one course; we must run to Burgundy rather than walk," and he broke into a sprint, maintaining his pace for nearly a league without faltering.

Denys, thoroughly winded, inquired what had become of Gerard's fever. "I begin to miss it sadly," he remarked dryly.

"I dropped it in the Rhine, I trow," Gerard replied.

Soon, they reached a small village, where Denys procured a loaf of bread and a massive bottle of Rhenish wine. "We must sleep in some hollow or corner," he explained. "If we lodge at an inn, we shall be discovered in our beds." This was sound prudence for an old soldier in those times. The official net for capturing lawbreakers, particularly the plebeians, was tight, although the cooperation of the general public on the Continent was nearly non-existent. Innkeepers were subject to intense surveillance regarding their travelers, bearing responsibility not only for their guests' actions but seemingly for their sufferings as well.

Both friends breathed a sigh of relief as the sun dipped below the horizon, and their spirits lifted when, after a long trudge under the starlight—for the moon, if memory serves, did not rise until approximately three in the morning—they arrived at a large barn belonging to a dwelling some distance away. A recent harvest had left the interior filled with threshed grain; a mountain of straw piled on one side of the floor stood nearly as high as the unthreshed corn on the other.

"Here be two royal beds," Denys observed. "Which shall we occupy, the mow or the straw?"

"The straw for me," Gerard decided.

Seated on the heap, they devoured their brown bread and drank their wine. Then Denys covered his friend in layers of straw, piling it high above him, leaving only a narrow aperture for breathing. "Water is said to be death to fevered men," he noted. "Yet, I shall make warm water available, anyhow."

Gerard assured him to ease his mind. "These few drops from the Rhine cannot chill me. I feel sufficient heat within my body now to parch a kennel or boil a cloud if I were situated in one." With this epigram, his consciousness faded rapidly, allowing him to fall swiftly into sleep.

Denys, remaining awake for a time, heard sounds that caused him to draw closer. Hooves rang out from Düsseldorf, approaching the wooden barn. The structure vibrated violently as they rattled past, emitting a howl familiar to the fifteenth century but alien to modern Europe, akin to a Red Indian's war-whoop. Denys shuddered where he lay. Gerard slept soundly, like a spinning top. The storm of horses and howls swept past and died away.
