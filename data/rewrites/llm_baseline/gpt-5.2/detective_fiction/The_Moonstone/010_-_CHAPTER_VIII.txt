Here, for one moment, I must put down my pen, as you might set a lid on a pot to keep it from boiling over too fast.

When I call up my own memory—and when I get Penelope to fetch hers, by the plain, steady help of her journal—I find there is little profit in stirring the days between Mr. Franklin Blake’s coming among us and Miss Rachel’s birthday. They simmered along without any fresh flavour worth naming. So, with your leave, I shall mark only the dates that mattered; and I shall keep the close, minute, day-by-day telling for the time when the Moonstone—like a strong spice dropped into common broth—made everybody’s business in the house taste of nothing else.

Now then. Back we go, as we must, to that bottle of sweet-smelling ink I picked up on the gravel walk at night.

On the next morning—the twenty-sixth—I showed it to Mr. Franklin, and told him what I have told you. He laughed, in his quick, clever way, and said it proved two things: first, that the Indians had been lurking about after the Diamond; and second, that they were simple enough to believe in their own trickery—meaning the signs on the boy’s head and the ink poured into his hand, and then expecting him to see beyond the reach of honest eyes.

“In England,” he said, “there are people who play at the same thing—without the ink; and they give it a French name, something like brightness of sight. Depend on it, Betteredge, the Indians counted on our keeping the Diamond here, and they brought their seeing-boy to guide them to it, if they got into the house last night.”

“Will they try again, sir?” I asked.

“That depends,” says he, “on what the boy can do. If he can see the Diamond through the iron safe at the bank in Frizinghall, we shall have no more visits just yet. If he can’t, we shall likely catch them in the shrubbery before many nights are over our heads.”

I waited—confident as a cook waiting for a second pan to hiss when the first has begun—but, strange as it sounds, the second pan never sang. No Indian shadow crossed our grounds again through all the weeks before Miss Rachel’s birthday.

Whether the jugglers heard in the town that Mr. Franklin had been seen at the bank and took the hint; whether the boy truly “saw” what I, for one, will never believe ink can show him; or whether it was mere chance—that is more than I can season to certainty. The plain truth is all I can serve you: not the ghost of an Indian came near the house again. They stayed in and about the town, practising on fair-day crowds; and Mr. Franklin and I stayed quiet, watching, and resolute not to frighten the rogues off by showing too soon that we had our suspicions. So ends, for the present, what I have to say of them.

On the twenty-ninth, Miss Rachel and Mr. Franklin found a new way of spending the time that would otherwise have sat on their hands like stale bread—filling, but tasteless. There are reasons for noting it. It will have its bearing, later, on what is still to come.

Gentry, as I have observed in my station, have an awkward rock ahead in life: the rock of their own idleness. A man who must earn his dinner knows what the day is for; but a man who has dinner provided often wanders about, hunting for “pursuits,” and ends by making a mess in the house under pretence of improving his mind. I have seen them turn curiosity into cruelty and call it knowledge; or turn cleanliness into filth and call it art. And if you ask what it means, the answer is always the same—time must be got through, time must be got through. A child makes mud-pies; the grown gentlefolk make finer messes, with worse smells.

As for Mr. Franklin and Miss Rachel, they tortured nothing, I am glad to say. They only spoiled a door.

Mr. Franklin’s universal genius—which is a kind of cook’s fire that will heat anything, but is apt to scorch—took to what he named “decorative painting.” He had invented a new mixture for moistening paint, which he called a “vehicle.” What it was made of, I cannot tell you. What it did, I can tell you to the letter: it stank. Not a wholesome kitchen smell—no yeast rising, no peel of orange in hot sugar, no clean scent of herbs bruised between fingers—but a sharp, head-turning reek that got into the curtains and seemed to argue with the very air of the room. When he mixed it, even the dogs sneezed and backed out, affronted in their honest noses.

Miss Rachel, being wild to try her hand, had the materials sent for from London. Mr. Franklin put an apron and bib over her gown as careful as if he were tying up a child for jam-making, and set her to work in her own little sitting-room—called, for want of an English word ready to hand, her “boudoir.” They began with the inside of the door. Mr. Franklin scraped off the decent varnish with pumice-stone—grinding and rasping till the wood looked raw and thirsty—and called it “a surface to work on.” Then, under his directions, Miss Rachel laid her patterns: griffins, birds, flowers, cupids, and such-like, copied from designs by a famous Italian painter—one of the Virgin-Mary makers, and (as I have heard it said) a man with a sweetheart at a baker’s.

The work was slow, and it was dirty—paint and vehicle, grit and rag, the smell hanging about like a bad tune you cannot forget. But they never tired of it. When they were not riding, or seeing company, or taking their meals, or piping their songs, there they were, heads bent close together, busy as bees, spoiling the door with the happiest faces in England. If Satan does indeed find mischief for idle hands, he might have sat down in that little room and taken notes—Miss Rachel with her brush, and Mr. Franklin with his vile-smelling “vehicle.”

The next date worth setting down is Sunday, the fourth of June.

That evening, in the servants’ hall, we debated a domestic question for the first time—one that, like the decoration of the door, points forward to something yet.

Seeing the pleasure Mr. Franklin and Miss Rachel took in one another’s company, and noting what a pretty match they made to the eye, we naturally speculated on whether their heads might be put together for something besides patterns and devices. Some said there would be a wedding before the summer was out. Others—led by me—allowed it was likely enough Miss Rachel might marry, but doubted, for reasons I shall soon show, whether her bridegroom would be Mr. Franklin Blake.

That Mr. Franklin was in love, any person with ears could tell—his voice altered when he spoke to her, and his footsteps altered when he followed her into a room, as if the very boards listened. The difficulty was Miss Rachel. Let me make you acquainted with her, and then you may try your hand at fathoming—if you can.

Her eighteenth birthday was coming, on the twenty-first of June. If you like dark women—and fashion, I am told, has been fickle on that point—Miss Rachel was, for her size, one of the prettiest creatures a man could look on without losing his wits. She was small and slim, but finely made all over. To see her sit down, to see her rise, and above all to see her walk, proved that what people call grace was in her flesh and not hung on her by dressmakers. Her hair was black as night; her eyes matched it. Her nose, I admit, was not quite large enough. Her mouth and chin, Mr. Franklin declared, were morsels for the gods; and her complexion—on the same authority—was warm as the sun, with this advantage over the sun, that it was always in nice order to look at. She carried her head upright as a dart, in a thoroughbred way; her voice rang true; and when she smiled, it began in her eyes before it reached her lips—like a light kindled behind a shutter before the room shows it.

And her disposition? Had she no faults? Just as many as you have, ma’am—no more and no less.

Seriously, with all her charms, she had one defect which impartiality obliges me to confess. She had ideas of her own, and spirit enough to defy the fashions if the fashions displeased her. In trifles, that independence was well enough; but in matters of consequence, it carried her further than my lady thought right—and further than I thought right too. She judged for herself as few women of twice her age judge; she never asked advice; never told you beforehand what she meant to do; never came with confidences to anybody, from her mother down. With those she loved, and those she hated—and she did both heartily—she went her own way, sufficient to herself in her joys and sorrows. Many a time I have heard my lady say, “Rachel’s best friend and Rachel’s worst enemy are—Rachel herself.”

One thing more, and I have done with the portrait.

With all her secrecy and self-will, there was not so much as a shadow of falsehood in her. I never knew her break her word. I never knew her say No and mean Yes. In her childhood, more than once, she took the blame and bore the punishment for a fault done by some playfellow she loved. When it came out, and she was charged after, she never confessed; but she never lied about it, either. She looked you straight in the face, shook her saucy little head, and said plain, “I won’t tell you.” Punished again, she might be sorry for saying “won’t”—but, bread-and-water notwithstanding, she never told. Self-willed—devilish self-willed sometimes, I grant; but the finest creature, for all that, that ever walked this lower world. If you spy a contradiction there, I whisper you a word: study your wife for four-and-twenty hours. If she shows no contradiction in that time, Heaven help you—you have married a monster.

And now, being acquainted with Miss Rachel, we come face to face with her matrimonial views.

On the twelfth of June, my mistress sent an invitation to a gentleman in London to come and help keep Miss Rachel’s birthday. This was the fortunate person on whom I believed her heart to be privately set. Like Mr. Franklin, he was a cousin. His name was Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite.

My lady’s second sister—no fear, I won’t drag you deep into family affairs—had been disappointed in love, and then, taking a husband on the neck-or-nothing principle, made what the world calls a misalliance. There had been terrible work when the Honourable Caroline insisted on marrying plain Mr. Ablewhite, the banker at Frizinghall. He was rich and respectable, and he begot a prodigious large family—much in his favour. But he had presumed to rise from a low station—that was against him. Time, however, and modern enlightenment put things right, and the misalliance passed muster. We are all liberal now; and (provided you can scratch me if I scratch you) what do I care, in Parliament or out, whether you are Dustman or Duke? That is the modern way; and I keep up with it. The Ablewhites lived in a fine house with grounds a little out of Frizinghall; worthy people, respected in the neighbourhood. We shall not be much troubled with them—excepting Mr. Godfrey, who must take his place here, for Miss Rachel’s sake.

With all Mr. Franklin’s brightness and general good qualities, his chance, in my opinion, of topping Mr. Godfrey in Miss Rachel’s estimation was a poor chance indeed.

In the first place, Mr. Godfrey was, for size, the finest man of the two. Over six feet; beautiful red-and-white colour; smooth round face shaved as bare as your hand; and lovely long flaxen hair, falling negligent over the back of his neck. But why do I waste words describing him? If you ever subscribed to a Ladies’ Charity in London, you know Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite as well as I do. He was a barrister by profession, a ladies’ man by temperament, and a good Samaritan by choice. Female benevolence and female destitution could do nothing without him. Maternal societies, Magdalen societies, strong-minded societies—he was vice-president, manager, referee to them all. Wherever there was a table with a committee of ladies sitting round it, there was Mr. Godfrey at the bottom, hat in hand, keeping the temper of the meeting and leading the dear creatures through the thorny ways of business. I do suppose he was the most accomplished philanthropist (on a small independence) England ever produced. As a speaker, he could draw your tears and your money with a handkerchief and a glass of water. The last time I was in London, my mistress gave me two treats: the theatre, to see a dancing woman all the rage; and Exeter Hall, to hear Mr. Godfrey. Crowds for the legs, crowds for the tongue. And with all this—sweet-tempered, simple, pleasant, easy to please. He loved everybody, and everybody loved him. What chance had Mr. Franklin—what chance had any man of average reputation—against such a favourite of the female world?

On the fourteenth came Mr. Godfrey’s answer.

He accepted the invitation from Wednesday, the birthday, to Friday evening, when his charitable duties would call him back to town. He enclosed, besides, a copy of verses on what he elegantly termed his cousin’s “natal day.” Miss Rachel, I heard, joined Mr. Franklin in making fun of the verses at dinner; and Penelope—who was all on Mr. Franklin’s side—asked me in triumph what I thought of that.

“Miss Rachel has led you off on a false scent, my dear,” I said. “But my nose is not so easily mystified. Wait till Mr. Ablewhite’s verses are followed by Mr. Ablewhite himself.”

Penelope replied that Mr. Franklin might strike in and try his luck before the poet arrived. And to do him justice, he left no chance untried.

Though one of the most inveterate smokers I ever met, he gave up his cigar because Miss Rachel said, one day, she hated the stale smell of it in his clothes. He slept badly after the sacrifice—missing, as some men miss their night-cup, the composing comfort he had made a habit of—and came down morning after morning haggard and worn. Miss Rachel herself begged him to take to his cigars again. No: he would take to nothing that could annoy her; he would fight it out, and win back sleep by patience and sheer will. Some downstairs said devotion like that must make its way into any girl’s heart—backed by the daily work, heads together over the door. All very well. But Miss Rachel had a photograph of Mr. Godfrey in her bedroom—taken at a public meeting, hair blown out by the breath of his own eloquence, eyes (most lovely eyes) charming the money out of your pockets. Every morning, as Penelope owned to me, there he was, looking on in effigy while Miss Rachel had her hair combed. Before long, he would be looking on in flesh and blood—that was my belief.

June the sixteenth brought an incident that made Mr. Franklin’s chance look worse to me than ever.

A strange gentleman came to the house that morning, speaking English with a foreign accent, and asked to see Mr. Franklin on business. The business could not have concerned the Diamond—for two reasons: first, Mr. Franklin told me nothing; second, he communicated it, when the gentleman had gone, to my lady. She, in her turn, must have hinted something to her daughter, for Miss Rachel was reported to have said severe things to Mr. Franklin at the piano that evening—sharp little strokes about the people he had lived among abroad, and the principles he had picked up in foreign parts. The next day, for the first time, nothing was done toward decorating the door. I suspected some Continental imprudence of his—with a woman, or a debt, at the bottom; but that is only guesswork. In this matter, not only Mr. Franklin, but my lady too, left me—strange to say—completely in the dark.

On the seventeenth, the cloud seemed to pass. They went back to their work on the door, and were as good friends as ever. If Penelope was to be trusted, Mr. Franklin seized the chance of reconciliation to speak out to Miss Rachel, and had neither Yes nor No in answer. My girl was sure, by signs and tokens she did not trouble to set forth, that her mistress had parried him by saying she could not believe he was in earnest—and had then, in secret, regretted it. But I knew Miss Rachel’s reserve too well to think she would show her mind so freely to anybody. What Penelope told me was—so I suspected—more what she wished than what she knew.

On the nineteenth another matter occurred. We had the doctor in the house, professionally, for a person I have already had occasion to name—our second housemaid, Rosanna Spearman.

That poor girl—who had puzzled me at the Shivering Sand—puzzled me again and again through this interval. Penelope’s notion that Rosanna was in love with Mr. Franklin (a notion I made my daughter keep strictly secret) seemed, at first, as absurd as ever. But what I saw, and what Penelope saw too, began to look mysterious—mysterious, at the very least.

For instance, Rosanna was forever putting herself in Mr. Franklin’s way—quietly, slyly, like a hand stealing spice from the pantry when it thinks no eye observes. Mr. Franklin took no more notice of her than he took of the cat; it never entered his head to waste so much as a glance on her plain face. Her appetite—never hearty—fell away to nothing; and her eyes in the morning told their own tale of wakefulness and crying at night. One day Penelope made an awkward discovery, and we hushed it up on the spot. She caught Rosanna at Mr. Franklin’s dressing-table, secretly taking away a rose Miss Rachel had given him for his button-hole, and putting another rose like it—one Rosanna had picked herself—in its place. After that, she was once or twice impudent to me when I gave her a general hint to be careful; and, worse still, she showed less respect than was decent on the few occasions when Miss Rachel spoke to her by chance.

My lady noticed the change and asked what I thought. I tried to screen the girl, and said I believed she was out of health; and that ended in the doctor being sent for, as I have said, on the nineteenth. He called it her nerves, and doubted if she was fit for service. My lady offered to send her for change of air to one of our farms inland. Rosanna begged and prayed, with tears in her eyes, to be allowed to stay; and in an evil hour I advised my lady to try her a little longer.

As events proved—as you will soon see—this was the worst advice I could have given. If I could only have looked a little way forward, I would have taken Rosanna Spearman out of the house that day with my own hand.
