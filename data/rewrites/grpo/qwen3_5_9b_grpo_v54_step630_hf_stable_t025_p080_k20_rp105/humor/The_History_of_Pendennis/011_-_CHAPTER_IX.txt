In Which the Major Opens the Campaign

Let those who possess a genuine, hearty appreciation for the glittering machinery of London society—the exclusive privilege of slipping into its most select circles—admit that Major Pendennis was a man of extraordinary generosity and deep affection in the sacrifice he now undertook. In May, he surrendered the city. He gave up his newspapers and his mornings, his afternoons spent hopping from club to club, his intimate visits to "my Ladies," his gallops along Rotten Row, his dinners, and his coveted stall at the Opera. He abandoned his rapid escapades to Fulham or Richmond on weekends, the bows exchanged with my Lord Duke or my Lord Marquis at great entertainments, and the inevitable mention of his name in the *Morning Post* the following day. He traded these quieter, more select, and delightful festivals for a solitary retreat to a small country house, sharing it with a simple widow, a greenhorn of a son, a mawkish curate, and a little girl of ten.

He made the sacrifice, and it was the greater because few understood its extent. His letters arrived franked from town, and he presented the invitations to Helen with a visible sigh. It was a scene of beautiful, tragic self-denial to watch him refuse party after party—at least to those who could comprehend the melancholy grandeur of his exile, though Helen merely smiled at the awful pathos with which the Major spoke of the *Court Guide*. Young Pen, however, looked upon the great names on the superscriptions of his uncle's letters with profound respect. He listened with constant interest and sympathy to the Major's stories of the fashionable world.

The elder Pendennis's rich memory was a vault storing thousands of such delightful tales, which he poured into Pen's willing ear with unfailing eloquence. He knew the name and pedigree of everyone in the Peerage and their intricate relations. "My dear boy," he would say, with mournful earnestness and veracity, "you cannot begin your genealogical studies too early. I wish to Heaven you would read in *Debrett* every day. Not so much the historical part—for the pedigrees, between ourselves, are many of them fabulous, and few families can show such a clear descent as our own—as the account of family alliances and who is related to whom. I have known a man's career blasted by ignorance on this all-important subject. Why, only last month, at dinner at my Lord Hobanob's, a young man recently received among us, young Mr. Suckling (author of a work, I believe), began to speak lightly of Admiral Bowser's conduct for 'ratting' to Ministers in the most audacious manner. But who do you think sat next to this Mr. Suckling? Next to him was Lady Grampound Bowser's daughter, and opposite him was Lord Grampound Bowser's son-in-law. The infatuated young man went on cutting jokes at the Admiral's expense, fancying the whole world laughed with him. I leave you to imagine Lady Hobanob's feelings—Hobanob's!—as the wretched intruder exposed himself. He will never dine again in South Street. I promise you that."

With such discourses, the Major entertained his nephew as he paced the terrace for his two-hour constitutional or as they sat over wine after dinner. He grieved that Sir Francis Clavering had not moved to the park to create a local society, and mourned that Lord Eyrie was absent, preventing the presentation of Pen to his lordship. "He has daughters," the Major noted. "Who knows? You might have married Lady Emily or Lady Barbara Trehawk; but all those dreams are over. My poor fellow, you must lie on the bed you have made for yourself."

These words inclined young Pendennis seriously. While printed anecdotes lack the oral magic of delivery, the Major's tales of Great George, the Royal Dukes, and the statesmen and beauties of the day filled young Pen's soul with longing. He found the conversations with his guardian far from tedious, whereas they sadly bored and perplexed poor Mrs. Pendennis.

It cannot be said that Mr. Pen's new guide, philosopher, and friend discoursed on the most elevated subjects, nor treated them with the highest manner. Yet his morality, such as it was, was consistent. It might not advance a man's prospects in the next world, but it was calculated to secure his interests in this one. The Major never doubted that his views were the only practicable ones and that his conduct was perfectly virtuous. He was a man of honor, in a word, with his eyes wide open. He took pity on this young greenhorn nephew and sought to open his eyes as well.

No man attended church more regularly in the country than the old bachelor. "It doesn't matter so much in town, Pen," he explained, "for there the women go and the men are not missed. But when a gentleman is *sur ses terres*, he must set an example to the country people. If I could turn a tune, I'd even sing. The Duke of Saint David's, whom I have the honor of knowing, always sings in the country, and let me tell you, it has a doosed fine effect from the family pew. And you are somebody down here. As long as the Claverings are away, you are the first man in the parish: and as good as any. You might represent the town if you played your cards well. Your poor dear father would have done so had he lived; so might you.—Not if you marry a lady, however amiable, whom the country people won't meet.—Well, well: it's a painful subject. Let us change it, my boy."

But if Major Pendennis changed the subject once, he returned to it a score of times that day. The moral of his discourse was always that Pen was throwing himself away. Now, it requires little coaxing to make a simple boy believe he is a very fine fellow.

Pen took his uncle's counsels to heart. He was glad enough to listen, for the conversation of Captain Costigan had become anything but pleasant. The image of that tipsy old father-in-law haunted him with terror. He could not bring that unshaven, punch-reeking man to associate with his mother. Even regarding Emily, he faltered when the pitiless guardian questioned him. "Was she accomplished?" He was obliged to admit, no. "Was she clever?" Well, she possessed a very good average intellect, but he could not absolutely say she was clever. "Come, let us see some of her letters." So Pen confessed he possessed only three, which were trivial invitations or answers.

"She is cautious enough," the Major said dryly. "She is older than you, my poor boy;" and then he apologized with utmost frankness and humility, flinging himself upon Pen's good feelings. He begged the lad to excuse a fond old uncle who had only his family's honor in view—for Arthur was ready to flame up in indignation whenever Miss Costigan's honesty was doubted, swearing he would never have her name mentioned lightly and would never part from her.

He repeated this to his uncle and friends at home, and also, it must be confessed, to Miss Fotheringay and the amiable family at Chatteris, with whom he still spent some time. Miss Emily was alarmed upon hearing of the guardian's arrival, rightly conceiving that the Major came with hostile intentions. "I suppose ye intend to leave me, now your grand relation has come down from town. He'll carry ye off, and you'll forget your poor Emily, Mr. Arthur!"

Forget her! In her presence, or that of Miss Rouncy, the Columbine and Milly's confidential friend, or even the Captain himself, Pen swore he could think of no other woman but his beloved Miss Fotheringay. The Captain, looking up at his foils hung as trophies on the wall where Pen and he used to fence, grimly declared he would advise no man to meddle rashly with the affections of his darling child. He vowed he would never believe his gallant young Arthur, whom he treated as a son, would ever be guilty of conduct so revolting to every idea of honor and humanity.

He went up and embraced Pen after speaking. He cried, wiping his eye with one large, dirty hand as he clasped Pen with the other. Arthur shuddered in that grasp, thinking of his uncle at home. His father-in-law looked unusually dirty and shabby; the odor of whisky-and-water was even more decided than usual. How was he to bring that man and his mother together? He trembled when he recalled writing to Costigan (enclosing a sovereign, a loan the worthy gentleman needed) and saying that one day he hoped to sign himself his affectionate son, Arthur Pendennis. He was glad to escape Chatteris that day—from Miss Rouncy the confidante, from the old toping father-in-law, from the divine Emily herself. "O, Emily, Emily," he cried inwardly as he rattled homewards on Rebecca, "you little know what sacrifices I am making for you!—for you who are always so cold, so cautious, so mistrustful;" and he thought of a character in Pope to whom he had often involuntarily compared her.

Pen never rode over to Chatteris on a certain errand, but the Major discovered on what errand the boy had been. Faithful to his plan, Major Pendennis gave his nephew no direct hindrance; yet the constant feeling that the senior's eye was upon him, accompanied by an uneasy shame attendant upon the inevitable confession evening conversation would elicit, made Pen visit his charmer less frequently than before. There was no use trying to deceive him; no pretext of dining with Smirke or reading Greek plays with Foker sufficed. Pen felt, upon returning from his flying visits, that everybody knew whence he came, appearing quite guilty before his mother and guardian over their books or game of picquet.

Once, having walked half a mile to the Fairoaks Inn beyond the Lodge gates to catch the *Competitor* coach, a man on the roof touched his hat to the young gentleman. It was his uncle's man, Mr. Morgan, going on a message and taken up at the Lodge. Mr. Morgan returned by the *Rival*, so Pen enjoyed the domestic's company both ways. Nothing was said at home. The lad seemed to have every decent liberty; yet he felt dimly watched and guarded, sensing eyes upon him even in the presence of his Dulcinea.

In fact, Pen's suspicions were not unfounded. His guardian had sent forth to gather all possible information regarding the lad and his interesting young friend. The discreet and ingenious Mr. Morgan, a London confidential valet whose fidelity could be trusted, had visited Chatteris more than once. He delicately cross-examined the waiters, ostlers, and inmates of the bar at the George, extracting what little they knew of the worthy Captain. He was not held in high regard there; the waiters never saw the color of his money and were warned not to furnish him with liquor for which others were not responsible. He swaggered sadly about the coffee-room, consumed a toothpick, looked over the paper, and if any friend asked him to dinner, he stayed. Morgan heard of Pen's acquaintance with Mr. Foker and went to Baymouth to enter relations with that gentleman's man, but the young student was gone to a Coast Regatta, and his servant traveled in charge of the dressing-case.

From the servants of the officers at the barracks, Mr. Morgan learned that the Captain had so frequently and outrageously inebriated himself there that Colonel Swallowtail had forbidden him the messroom. The indefatigable Morgan then communicated with inferior actors at the theatre, pumping them over cigars and punch. All agreed that Costigan was poor, shabby, and given to debt and drink. But there was not a breath against the reputation of Miss Fotheringay. Her father's courage had displayed itself on more than one occasion toward persons disposed to treat his daughter with freedom. She never came to the theatre without her father; in his most inebriated moments, that gentleman kept a watch over her. Finally, Mr. Morgan, from his own experience, added that he had seen her act and was uncommonly delighted with the performance, besides thinking her a most splendid woman.

Mrs. Creed, the pew-opener, confirmed these statements to Doctor Portman, who examined her personally and threatened her with the terrors of the Church one afternoon after service. Mrs. Creed had nothing unfavorable to divulge about her lodger. She saw nobody but one or two ladies of the theatre. The Captain did intoxicate himself sometimes and did not always pay his rent regularly, but he did when he had money, or rather, Miss Fotheringay did. Since the young gentleman from Clavering had arrived to take fencing lessons, one or two more had come from the barracks; Sir Derby Oaks and his young friend, Mr. Foker, often together, driving over from Baymouth in the tandem. But on the occasions of the lessons, Miss F. was very seldom present, generally coming downstairs to Mrs. Creed's own room.

The Doctor and the Major, consulting together as they often did, groaned in spirit over that information. Major Pendennis openly expressed his disappointment; and I believe the Divine himself was ill-pleased at not being able to jack a hole in poor Miss Fotheringay's reputation.

Even regarding Pen himself, Mrs. Creed's reports were desperately favorable. "Whenever he come," Mrs. Creed said, "she always have me or one of the children with her. And Mrs. Creed, marm, says she, if you please, marm, you'll on no account leave the room when that young gentleman's here. And many's the time I've seen him a lookin' as if he wished I was away, poor young man: and he took to coming in service-time, when I wasn't at home, of course: but she always had one of the boys up if her Pa wasn't at home, or old Mr. Bowser with her teaching her her lesson, or one of the young ladies of the theayter."

It was all true: whatever encouragements might have been given him before he avowed his passion, the prudence of Miss Emily was prodigious after Pen had declared himself. The poor fellow chafed against her hopeless reserve, which maintained his ardour as it excited his anger.
